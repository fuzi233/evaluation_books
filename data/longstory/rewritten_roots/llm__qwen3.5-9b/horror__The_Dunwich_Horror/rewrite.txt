When a traveler in north-central Massachusetts mistakes the fork at the junction of the Aylesbury Pike, just past Dean's Corners, they stumble into a landscape that defies expectation. The terrain climbs steeply, and stone walls edged with thorny briars squeeze tighter against the rutted, dust-choked road. The forests encroaching from the frequent belts of trees seem unnaturally vast, their flora—a riot of wild weeds, brambles, and grasses—possessing a lushness alien to settled lands. Yet, the cultivated fields are sparse and barren, and the few scattered dwellings share a startling uniformity of age, squalor, and decay. Instinctively, one hesitates to seek directions from the gnarled, solitary figures occasionally spotted on crumbling doorsteps or lurking in the rocky meadows. Their silence and furtiveness evoke a sense of confrontation with forbidden entities, things best left undisturbed.

As the road ascends, revealing the mountains looming above the deep woods, a profound unease settles in. The summits are grotesquely rounded and symmetrical, lacking the comforting irregularity of nature, and frequently silhouette against the sky the bizarre circles of tall stone pillars crowning their peaks. Deep gorges of uncertain depth slice through the route, crossed by crude wooden bridges that inspire doubt regarding their structural integrity. When the road descends, stretches of marshland appear, instilling an instinctive dread, especially at twilight when unseen whippoorwills chatter and fireflies emerge in abnormal numbers, dancing to the raucous, creepy rhythms of strident bullfrogs. The thin, shimmering ribbon of the Miskatonic River, winding close to the feet of the domed hills, suggests a serpentine motion. As the hills draw nearer, their dark, precipitous wooded sides loom menacingly, offering no escape route.

Beyond a covered bridge lies a small village huddled between the stream and the sheer face of Round Mountain. Here, a cluster of rotting gambrel roofs hints at an architectural era long past, contrasting sharply with the surrounding region. Closer inspection reveals that most homes are abandoned and collapsing, while the broken steeple of the local church shelters the hamlet's sole, slovenly mercantile store. One cannot help but fear the gloomy tunnel of the bridge, yet there is no alternative. Upon crossing, a faint, malignant odor permeates the village street—the scent of massed mold and centuries of decay. Relief comes only in leaving the place, following the narrow road around the hill bases until it rejoins the main pike. Later, travelers often realize they have passed through Dunwich. Outsiders avoid it whenever possible; since a specific season of horror, all signposts directing toward it have been removed.

Judged by standard aesthetic criteria, the scenery is undeniably beautiful, yet it attracts neither artists nor tourists. Two centuries ago, when superstition held sway, people avoided the area citing witch blood, Satan worship, and strange forest presences. In our rational age, following the suppression of the Dunwich horror of 1928, the town is shunned without anyone fully understanding why. Perhaps one reason, applicable perhaps only to the locals, is their repulsive decadence. Having followed a path of retrogression common to many New England backwaters, they have evolved into a distinct race marked by the clear stigmata of degeneracy and inbreeding. Their collective intelligence is woefully low, and their history reeks of open viciousness, hidden murders, incest, and acts of almost unimaginable perversion.

Among the old gentry, descendants of the two or three armigerous families arriving from Salem in 1692, some maintain a marginally higher plane of existence, though many branches have sunk so deeply into the sordid populace that only their names remain as keys to origins they now disgrace. Some Whateleys and Bishops still send their eldest sons to Harvard or Miskatonic University, though these youths rarely return to the moldering gambrel roofs where they and their ancestors were born. Even those aware of the recent horrors cannot pinpoint the exact ailment of Dunwich. However, old legends speak of unhallowed rites and Indian conclaves where forbidden shadows were summoned from the great rounded hills, accompanied by wild orgiastic prayers answered by loud cracks and earth-shaking rumblings.

In 1747, Reverend Abijah Hoadley, newly arrived at the Congregational Church in Dunwich Village, delivered a memorable sermon on the proximity of Satan and his demons. He declared: "It must be allowed that these blasphemies of an infernal train of demons are matters of too common knowledge to be denied; the cursed voices of Azazel and Buzrael, of Beelzebub and Belial, being heard from underground by over a score of credible witnesses now living. I myself caught a very plain discourse of evil powers in the hill behind my house less than a fortnight ago; wherein there were rattling, rolling, groaning, screeching, and hissing, such as no thing of this earth could raise up, and which must needs have come from those caves that only black magic can discover, and only the devil unlock." Hoadley vanished shortly after preaching this sermon, but the text, printed in Springfield, remains extant.

Noises in the hills persisted annually, baffling geologists and physiographers to this day. Other traditions recount foul odors near the hill-crowning stone circles and rushing airy presences heard faintly from specific points at the bottom of great ravines. There is also the tale of the Devil's Hop Yard, a bleak, blasted hillside where no tree, shrub, or blade of grass grows. Furthermore, the natives hold a mortal terror of the numerous whippoorwills, which become vocal on warm nights. It is vowed that these birds are psychopomps waiting for the souls of the dying, timing their eerie cries to the sufferer's labored breathing. Should they catch the fleeing soul, they flutter away in demoniac laughter; if they fail, they sink into a disappointed silence. Of course, these tales are obsolete and ridiculous, relics of ancient times.

Dunwich itself is ridiculously old—far older than any community within thirty miles. To the south, one can still spy the cellar walls and chimney of the ancient Bishop house, constructed before 1700, while the ruins of the mill at the falls, built in 1806, represent the most modern architecture visible. Industry never flourished here; the nineteenth-century factory movement proved fleeting. Oldest of all are the great rings of rough-hewn stone columns atop the hills, generally attributed to Native Americans rather than settlers. Deposits of skulls and bones found within these circles and around the sizable table-like rock on Sentinel Hill support the popular belief that these were once burial grounds of the Pocumtucks, even though many ethnologists, dismissing the absurd improbability of such a theory, insist the remains are Caucasian.

It was in the desolate township of Dunwich, within a sprawling and partially abandoned farmhouse clinging to a precipitous hillside four miles from the village and a mile and a half from the nearest neighbor, that Wilbur Whateley drew his breath at five in the morning on Sunday, February the second, nineteen thirteen. This specific date lingered in local memory not merely for the calendar, but because it coincided with Candlemas—a holiday observed in Dunwich under a forgotten name—and because the night preceding had been shattered by unnatural noises echoing from the hills, accompanied by a persistent, frantic barking from every dog across the countryside. Of lesser note, though significant to those who knew the lineage, was the mother: Lavinia Whateley. She belonged to the decaying branch of the Whateley family, a thirty-five-year-old woman of striking deformity and pallor, an albino whose pale eyes seemed to hold no warmth. She lived with her aged, half-mad father, a man surrounded by whispers of sorcery dating back to his youth. Lavinia had no recorded husband, yet, adhering to the superstitious customs of the region, she made no effort to deny the child. While the townsfolk speculated wildly regarding the boy's unknown sire, Lavinia wore her secret with a strange, grim pride. She regarded the dark, goat-like infant—such a jarring contrast to her own sickly, pink-eyed albinism—with a reverence that bordered on worship, often muttering cryptic prophecies about his unearthly powers and his terrifying destiny.

Such mutterings suited Lavinia perfectly. She was a solitary creature, drawn to wander the hills amidst rolling thunderstorms, seeking to decipher the great, fetid tomes her father had inherited through two centuries of Whateley madness. These books, now crumbling into dust and infested with worms, held the fragmented, disjointed lore Old Whateley had poured into her mind. Having never attended school, she possessed only these eerie scraps of ancient knowledge. The farmhouse itself was a beacon of fear, tainted by Old Whateley's reputation for black magic and compounded by the violent, unexplained death of Mrs. Whateley when Lavinia was just twelve. Isolated by these strange influences, Lavinia indulged in wild, grandiose daydreams and singular obsessions, her domestic life devoid of order or cleanliness.

On the night Wilbur was born, a hideous scream tore through the silence, drowning out the hill noises and the dogs' barking. Yet, no doctor or midwife was present to witness the arrival. The neighbors remained ignorant of the newborn for a week, until Old Whateley himself drove his sleigh through the snow into Dunwich Village. He stopped at Osborn's general store, addressing a crowd of loungers with incoherent fervor. There was a palpable shift in the old man—an added layer of furtiveness in his clouded gaze that transformed him from a mere object of fear into its active subject. Despite this, he displayed a trace of the pride his daughter felt. When asked about the child's father, his words hung heavy in the cold air, etching themselves into the memories of many listeners for years to come. "I don't care what folks think—if Lavinny's boy looked like his pa, he wouldn't look like anything you expect," he rasped. "Ye needn't think the only folks are the ones hereabouts. Lavinny's read some... and has seen some things most of ye only tell about in whispers. I calculate her man is as good a husband as ye can find this side of Aylesbury; and if ye knew as much about the hills as I do, ye wouldn't ask for a better church wedding than hers. Let me tell ye something—*some day ye folks'll hear a child of Lavinny's calling its father's name on the top o' Sentinel Hill!*"

During the first month of Wilbur's life, only two individuals witnessed him: Old Zechariah Whateley of the undecayed line, and Mamie Bishop, the common-law wife of Earl Sawyer. Mamie's visit was driven by frank curiosity, and her subsequent stories did little to soften the community's reaction. Zechariah arrived instead to lead a pair of Alderney cows purchased by Old Whateley from his son, Curtis. This purchase marked the beginning of a peculiar era for the Whateley family, lasting until the horror of 1928, during which they acquired cattle in increasing numbers. Yet, the ramshackle Whateley barn never appeared overcrowded. At times, villagers grew bold enough to sneak up the steep hillside to count the herd grazing precariously near the farmhouse, but they could never tally more than ten or twelve specimens. These animals were anemic, bloodless, and clearly afflicted by some blight or distemper, likely stemming from the unwholesome pasturage or the diseased fungi rotting the barn's timber. Worse still, odd wounds resembling surgical incisions plagued the visible cattle. On rare occasions, visitors claimed to see similar sores around the throats of the gray, unshaven old man and his slatternly, crinkly-haired daughter.

In the spring following Wilbur's birth, Lavinia resumed her customary rambles in the hills, carrying the swarthy child in her misproportioned arms. Once the townsfolk had glimpsed the baby, public interest waned, and few commented on the astonishing rate of his development. Wilbur's growth was truly phenomenal; within three months, he possessed a size and muscular power rarely seen in infants nearing a full year of age. His movements and vocalizations exhibited a restraint and deliberation utterly alien to normal infancy. Consequently, no one was shocked when, at seven months, he began to walk unassisted, stumbling slightly before mastering the feat within another month.

Sometime shortly thereafter, on Hallowe'en, a great blaze erupted at midnight atop Sentinel Hill, illuminating the ancient table-like stone standing amidst its tumulus of bones. Speculation ran high when Silas Bishop, of the undecayed Bishops, mentioned seeing the boy running sturdily up that hill ahead of his mother roughly an hour before the fire was spotted. Silas was rounding up a stray heifer but nearly forgot his task when he fleetingly caught sight of the two figures in the dim lantern light. They darted almost noiselessly through the underbrush, appearing to Silas as entirely unclothed. Later, he could not recall whether the boy wore a fringed belt and dark blue trousers, or if he was naked save for those items. From that point forward, Wilbur was never seen alive and conscious without complete, tightly buttoned attire; the slightest threat to his clothing seemed to fill him with immediate anger and alarm. This stark contrast between his pristine dress and the squalor of his mother and grandfather was noted as particularly notable, until the horrors of 1928 provided the only logical explanation.

By the following January, gossip turned mildly toward the fact that "Lavinny's black brat" had begun to speak at the tender age of eleven months. His speech was remarkable not only for its deviation from regional accents but also for its total absence of infantile lisping—a clarity that many children of three or four would envy. Though not talkative, when he spoke, he seemed to channel an elusive element wholly foreign to Dunwich and its inhabitants. The strangeness lay not in the words themselves, but in his intonation and the internal mechanics producing them. Furthermore, his facial aspect betrayed a premature maturity. While he shared his mother's and grandfather's lack of a chin, his firm, precociously shaped nose combined with the expression in his large, dark, almost Latin eyes gave him an air of quasi-adulthood and preternatural intelligence. Yet, beneath this brilliance lay an extreme ugliness. Something distinctly goatish or animalistic marred his features: thick lips, large-pored yellowish skin, coarse crinkly hair, and oddly elongated ears. He was soon despised even more vehemently than his mother and grandsire, and all conjecture surrounding him became seasoned with references to the bygone magic of Old Whateley, specifically the tale of how the hills once shook when the boy shrieked the dreadful name *Yog-Sothoth* while holding a great book open before a circle of stones. Even the dogs abhorred him, forcing the child to adopt various defensive measures against their relentless barking menace.

Meanwhile, Old Whateley persisted in purchasing cattle, yet the size of his herd remained stubbornly unchanged. He took up the axe to fell timber and commenced repairs upon the neglected sections of his dwelling—a vast structure with a soaring, peaked roof, its rear end swallowed whole by the rocky hillside. For years, only the three most intact ground-floor rooms had sufficed for himself and his daughter, but now, driven by a prodigious reserve of strength, the old man labored tirelessly. Though he still slipped into moments of demented babble, his carpentry revealed a mind engaged in sound, if obsessive, calculation.

The restoration had truly begun the moment Wilbur was born, evidenced by the sudden transformation of a dilapidated tool-shed: it was clapboarded, secured with a heavy fresh lock, and rendered impenetrable. Now, turning his attention to the abandoned upper story, Whateley worked with equal thoroughness. His madness manifested primarily in his decision to board every single window in the reclaimed section shut—many deemed it sheer folly to reclaim the space at all. Yet more inexplicable was his preparation of a new downstairs chamber for his grandson. Several visitors glimpsed this room, though none were ever granted entry to the tightly shuttered floors above. Here, Whateley installed tall, sturdy shelving, along which he methodically arranged the rotting, ancient volumes and fragments of books that had previously been heaped promiscuously in the shadows of various rooms.

"I made some use of 'em," he would murmur, attempting to mend a torn black-letter page with paste boiled on a rusty kitchen stove. "But the boy is fitten to make better use of 'em. He'd orter hev 'em as well sot as he kin, for they're goin' to be all of his larnin'."

By the time Wilbur reached one year and seven months—in September of 1914—his growth and intellect were almost alarming. He stood as large as a four-year-old, speaking fluently and with terrifying intelligence. He roamed freely across the fields and hills, accompanying his mother on her wanderings. At home, however, he pored diligently over the strange illustrations and charts in his grandfather's books, while Old Whateley instructed and catechized him through long, hushed afternoons.

When the house restoration concluded, observers noted with confusion that one upper window in the rear of the east gable, pressed close against the hill, had been converted into a solid plank door. No one could comprehend why a cleated wooden runway had been constructed from the ground up to this inaccessible aperture. Around this same period, the old tool-house, which had been tightly locked and windowless since Wilbur's birth, was abandoned once more. Its door swung listlessly open, and when Earl Sawyer ventured inside after a cattle sale, he was deeply unsettled by the singular odor that greeted him. It was a stench he claimed he had never smelled in all his life, save perhaps near the Indian circles on the hills—an aroma that suggested nothing sane or earthly. Of course, the homes and sheds of Dunwich folk had never been renowned for olfactory cleanliness.

The following months passed without visible incident, save for a slow, steady intensification of the mysterious noises emanating from the hill. On May Eve of 1915, tremors shook the very foundations, felt even by the people of Aylesbury. Then, on the following Halloween, an underground rumbling occurred, strangely synchronized with bursts of flame erupting from the summit of Sentinel Hill—"them witch Whateleys' doin's," the locals whispered.

Wilbur grew with uncanny speed; by his fourth year, he resembled a boy of ten. He read avidly on his own but spoke far less than before. A settled taciturnity was consuming him, and for the first time, neighbors began to speak openly of the dawning look of evil in his goatish face. Occasionally, he would mutter an unfamiliar jargon or chant in bizarre rhythms that chilled listeners with a sense of unexplainable terror. The aversion displayed toward him by dogs became a subject of wide remark; he was forced to carry a pistol to traverse the countryside safely. His sporadic use of the weapon did little to improve his standing among the owners of these canine guardians.

Visitors to the house often found Lavinia alone on the ground floor, while eerie cries and footsteps echoed from the boarded-up second story. She refused to explain what her father and son were doing upstairs, though once she turned pale and exhibited an abnormal degree of fear when a jovial fish-peddler attempted the locked door leading to the stairs. That peddler later told the loungers at Dunwich Village that he thought he heard a horse stamping on the floor above. The loungers pondered the door, the runway, and the cattle that vanished so swiftly, then shuddered recalling tales of Old Whateley's youth and the strange entities summoned from the earth when a bullock is sacrificed to certain heathen gods.

It had long been observed that dogs began to hate and fear the entire Whateley property as violently as they feared young Wilbur personally.

In 1917, the Great War arrived. As chairman of the local draft board, Squire Sawyer Whateley struggled mightily to find a quota of young Dunwich men fit enough to be sent to a development camp. Alarmed by signs of wholesale regional decadence, the government dispatched officers and medical experts to investigate, conducting a survey that New England newspaper readers may still recall. It was the publicity surrounding this inquiry that led reporters directly to the Whateleys, prompting the *Boston Globe* and the *Arkham Advertiser* to publish flamboyant Sunday specials detailing young Wilbur's precocity, Old Whateley's alleged black magic, the shelves of strange books, the sealed second story of the ancient farmhouse, and the weirdness of the region and its hill noises.

At four and a half years old, Wilbur already looked like a lad of fifteen. His lips and cheeks were fuzzy with coarse dark down, and his voice had begun to break. Earl Sawyer accompanied two sets of reporters and cameramen to the Whateley place, drawing their attention to the peculiar stench trickling down from the sealed upper spaces. He described it as identical to the smell found in the abandoned tool-shed and akin to the faint odors he sometimes detected near the stone circles on the mountains.

Dunwich folk read the stories when they appeared and grinned over the obvious errors. They also wondered why the journalists placed such emphasis on the fact that Old Whateley always paid for his cattle in gold pieces of extremely ancient date. The Whateleys received their visitors with ill-concealed distaste, though they dared not court further trouble through violent resistance or outright refusal to speak.

For ten years, the history of the Whateleys dissolved into the grim, stagnant routine of a town accustomed to their peculiarities and desensitized to their macabre rituals. Twice annually, they ignited pyres atop Sentinel Hill, triggering earth-shaking tremors that grew progressively violent; meanwhile, ominous activities persisted within the isolated farmhouse throughout every season. Over time, visitors claimed to hear noises emanating from the sealed upper floors even when the entire family remained below, speculating on the speed or duration required to slaughter a cow or ox. Rumors circulated regarding a potential report to the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, yet such actions never materialized, for the people of Dunwich possessed no desire to draw external scrutiny upon themselves.

Around 1923, when Wilbur was merely ten years old—though possessing the mental acuity, vocal depth, physical stature, and full beard of a grown man—a second major construction project commenced within the ancient house. This work took place entirely behind the locked upper walls. From scraps of discarded timber scattered nearby, neighbors deduced that the boy and his grandfather had demolished every internal partition and stripped away the attic flooring, creating a single, cavernous void spanning from the ground floor to the peak of the roof. They had also razed the massive central chimney, replacing the rusted iron range with a precarious exterior tin stovepipe.

In the spring following these renovations, Old Whateley observed an increasing influx of whippoorwills emerging from Cold Spring Glen to chirp beneath his window at night. He regarded this phenomenon with profound gravity, informing the loafers at Osborn's that his departure was imminent. "They whistle exactly in tune with my breathing now," he rasped. "I reckon they're preparing to catch my soul. They know it's coming and don't intend to miss it. You'll see, boys, once I'm gone, if they succeeded or failed. If they did, they'll keep singing and laughing until dawn. If they didn't, they'll settle down somewhat. I expect those birds and the souls they hunt often engage in rather fierce struggles."

On Lammas Night, 1924, Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was urgently summoned by Wilbur Whateley, who had tethered his sole remaining horse against the dark and dialed Osborn's in the village. He discovered Old Whateley in a critical condition, his heart racing and breathing stertorous, signaling an end approaching rapidly. Beside the bed stood the formless albino daughter and the strangely bearded grandson, while from the empty abyss above descended a disturbing sensation of rhythmic surging, akin to waves lapping against a shore. However, the physician was most unsettled by the chatter of the night birds outside—a seemingly infinite host of whippoorwills crying an endless, repetitive message perfectly synchronized with the dying man's wheezing gasps. It was uncanny, unnatural—an excess that made Dr. Houghton feel as though he had stumbled deeper into a nightmare than he wished to admit.

Toward one o'clock, Old Whateley regained consciousness, interrupting his labored breathing to choke out instructions for his grandson. "Make room, Willy! More space, soon! You grow—and *that* thing grows faster. It will be ready to serve you shortly, boy. Recite the long chant found on page 751 of the complete edition to open the gates to Yog-Sothoth, and *then* set fire to the prison. No earthly flame can burn it!" He appeared utterly deranged. After a pause, during which the flock of whippoorwills outside shifted their cries to match the new tempo and distant rumblings echoed from the hills, he added a final warning. "Feed it regularly, Willy, and watch the quantity carefully. Do not allow it to expand too quickly for the location, for if it bursts the quarters or escapes before you open to Yog-Sothoth, everything is lost and useless. Only those from beyond can cause it to multiply and function... Only them, the old ones seeking to return..."

Speech gave way once more to ragged gasps, prompting Lavinia to scream as the whippoorwills outside seemed to follow the change in rhythm. This cycle repeated for over an hour until the final, throaty rattling ceased. Dr. Houghton gently covered the glazed, gray eyes with shrunken lids as the chaotic bird chorus faded imperceptibly into silence. Lavinia wept, but Wilbur merely chuckled, his heavy bass voice cutting through the quiet as the hill noises rumbled faintly in the distance. "They didn't get him," he muttered.

By this time, Wilbur had become a scholar of staggering, albeit singular, erudition, corresponding quietly with librarians across distant lands who guarded rare and forbidden texts of antiquity. He was increasingly hated and feared in Dunwich due to vague suspicions surrounding certain youthful disappearances attributed to him, yet he invariably silenced inquiries through terror or by deploying his substantial reserve of ancestral gold, which continued to flow outward regularly and in growing amounts for cattle purchases. Now immensely mature in appearance, his height, having reached the standard adult limit, seemed poised to exceed it further.

In 1925, when a scholarly correspondent from Miskatonic University visited and left pale and bewildered, Wilbur stood fully six and three-quarters feet tall. Throughout the intervening years, he had treated his half-deformed albino mother with escalating contempt, eventually forbidding her from accompanying him to the hills on May Eve and Hallowmass. By 1926, the poor creature confessed to Mamie Bishop that she feared him. "There's more about him than I can tell you, Mamie," she whispered. "And nowadays, there's more than I even know myself. I vow by God, I don't know what he wants or what he's trying to do."

That Halloween, the hill noises roared louder than ever, and fires burned on Sentinel Hill as tradition dictated, but villagers paid heightened attention to the rhythmic shrieking of vast flocks of unnaturally late whippoorwills gathered near the unlit Whateley farmhouse. After midnight, their shrill notes erupted into a pandemonium of laughter that filled the countryside, subsiding only at dawn. Then, they vanished, hastening southward despite being nearly a month overdue. The meaning of this event remained uncertain until later. No countryfolk appeared to have perished—but poor Lavinia Whateley, the twisted albino, was never seen again.

In the summer of 1927, Wilbur repaired two sheds in the farmyard and began relocating his books and belongings to them. Shortly thereafter, Earl Sawyer informed the regulars at Osborn's that additional carpentry was underway in the Whateley farmhouse. Wilbur was sealing all doors and windows on the ground floor while removing interior partitions, mirroring the work done upstairs four years prior. Living in one of the sheds, he appeared unusually agitated and trembling. Most suspected he knew something concerning his mother's disappearance, and very few dared approach his vicinity. His height had surpassed seven feet, showing no sign of stopping its relentless growth.

The winter that followed brought an occurrence scarcely less bizarre than Wilbur's initial journey beyond the Dunwich borders. His correspondence with the Widener Library at Harvard, the Bibliothèque Nationale in Paris, the British Museum, the University of Buenos Aires, and the Library of Miskatotic University in Arkham had yielded nothing but silence regarding the loan of the tome he craved. Desperate, he eventually departed in person—shabby, filthy, bearded, and speaking with a rough, uncouth accent—to seek the copy held at Miskatonic, the institution closest to him geographically.

Standing nearly eight feet tall and dragging a cheap, new valise purchased from Osborn's general store, this dark, goatish gargoyle materialized one day in Arkham seeking the forbidden volume secured behind locks and keys within the college library: the abominable *Necronomicon* of the mad Arab Alhazred, rendered in Latin by Olaus Wormius and printed in Spain during the seventeenth century. Having never witnessed a city before, Wilbur possessed no other thought than to locate the university grounds. Indeed, he marched past the great white-fanged watchdog, which barked with unnatural fury and malice while tugging frantically against its heavy chain, paying the beast no mind whatsoever.

Accompanying him was the priceless yet flawed manuscript of Dr. Dee's English translation, bequeathed by his grandfather. Upon gaining access to the Latin original, Wilbur immediately commenced a collation of the two texts, intent on locating a specific passage missing from the seven hundred and fifty-first page of his damaged volume. He could not help but mention this task to the librarian—the learned Henry Armitage (A.M., Miskatonic; Ph.D., Princeton; Litt.D., Johns Hopkins), who had previously visited the farm and now politely interrogated him. Wilbur admitted he sought a particular formula or incantation containing the terrifying name *Yog-Sothoth*. It baffled him to encounter discrepancies, duplications, and ambiguities that rendered the identification process exceedingly difficult.

As he copied the chosen formula, Dr. Armitage involuntarily glanced over his shoulder at the open pages. The left-hand page of the Latin edition displayed monstrous threats against the peace and sanity of the world. Nor should it be assumed that humanity holds the title of Earth's oldest or final master, nor that the common mass of life and substance exists in isolation. The Old Ones exist; they always have, and they ever shall. They do not traverse the spaces we comprehend, but dwell *between* them. They move serenely and primally, undimensioned and invisible to our eyes. *Yog-Sothoth* knows the gate. *Yog-Sothoth* is the gate. *Yog-Sothoth* is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, and future are unified within *Yog-Sothoth*. He knows where the Old Ones breached the barrier in ages gone by, and where they will breach it anew. He knows the paths they have trodden across Earth's fields, where they tread still, and why no mortal eye can behold them in their fullness. Men may sometimes perceive their approach by scent, though no one can discern their appearance except in the distorted features of those whom they have impregnated with mankind; and among these offspring are countless varieties, ranging from shapes resembling man's truest image to forms devoid of sight or substance entirely. They wander unseen and foul in desolate places where their Words have been spoken and their Rites howled forth at their appointed seasons. The wind gibbers with their voices, and the earth mutters with their consciousness. They bow down forests and crush cities, yet neither forest nor city can witness the hand that strikes. Kadath in the cold wastes has known them, and what does any man truly know of Kadath? The ice deserts of the South and the sunken islands of the Ocean hold stones engraved with their seals, but who has beheld the deep-frozen city or the sealed tower draped long ago in seaweed and barnacles? Great Cthulhu is their cousin, yet he can spy them only dimly. *Iä Shub-Niggurath!* Thus shall you know them. Their hand rests upon your throat, yet you see them not; and their dwelling place is identical to your guarded threshold. *Yog-Sothoth* is the key to the gate through which the spheres converge. Man rules now where they once ruled; soon they shall rule where man currently reigns. After summer comes winter, and after winter, summer. They wait patiently and potently, for here they shall reign again.

Dr. Armitage, connecting the text he read with his knowledge of Dunwich and its brooding presences, alongside accounts of Wilbur Whateley and his dim, hideous aura stretching from a dubious birth to a cloud of probable patricide, felt a surge of terror as tangible as the damp chill of a tomb. The bent, goatish giant before him appeared to be the spawn of another planet or dimension; something only partially human, linked to black gulfs of essence and entity stretching like titanic phantasms beyond all spheres of force, matter, space, and time.

Presently, Wilbur raised his head and began speaking in that strange, resonant manner, hinting at vocal organs unlike those of ordinary mankind.

"Mr. Armitage," he said, "I calculate I must take that book home. There are things within it I must attempt under special conditions I cannot achieve here, and it would be a mortal sin to allow bureaucratic rules to hinder me. Permit me to take it along, sir, and I swear no one will notice the difference. I don't need to assure you I will care for it well. It wasn't my doing that placed this Dee copy in its current state..."

He paused, sensing the firm denial on the librarian's face, and his own goatish features shifted into a crafty expression. Armitage, half-ready to suggest copying the necessary sections, suddenly recoiled at the potential consequences. The responsibility of granting such a being access to such blasphemous outer realms was too great. Whateley perceived the situation and attempted to respond lightly. "Well, all right, if you feel that way about it. Perhaps Harvard won't be so fussy as you are." Without further comment, he rose and strode out of the building, stooping low at every doorway.

Armitage heard the savage yelping of the great watchdog and studied Whateley's gorilla-like lope as he crossed the small section of campus visible from the window. Reflecting on the wild tales he had heard and recalling the old Sunday stories in the *Advertiser*, he remembered the lore gathered from Dunwich rustics and villagers during his single visit there. Unseen entities, not of Earth—or at least not of three-dimensional Earth—rushed fetid and horrific through New England's glens, brooking obscenely atop the mountain peaks. Of this, he had long been certain. Now, however, he sensed the close proximity of some terrible fragment of the intruding horror and glimpsed a hellish advance within the black dominion of the ancient, once-passive nightmare.

With a shudder of disgust, Armitage locked away the *Necronomicon*, yet the room remained permeated by an unholy, unidentifiable stench. "As a foulness shall ye know them," he quoted. Yes—the odor was identical to that which had sickened him at the Whateley farmhouse less than three years prior. Thinking of Wilbur, goatish and ominous, he once again mocked the village rumors concerning his parentage. "Inbreeding?" Armitage muttered half-aloud to himself. "Great God, what simpletons! Show them Arthur Machen's *The Great God Pan* and they'll deem it a common Dunwich scandal! But what creature—what cursed, shapeless influence on or off this three-dimensional Earth—was Wilbur Whateley's father? Born on Candlemas, nine months after May Eve of 1912, when whispers of strange earth noises reached clear to Arkham... what walked upon those mountains that May night? What Roodmas horror fastened itself upon the world in half-human flesh and blood?"

During the ensuing weeks, Dr. Armitage dedicated himself to gathering all available data on Wilbur Whateley and the formless presences surrounding Dunwich. He contacted Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury, who had attended Old Whateley in his final illness, finding much to ponder in the grandfather's last words as recounted by the physician. A visit to Dunwich Village yielded little new information; however, a close examination of the *Necronomicon*, specifically in the sections Wilbur had sought so avidly, seemed to provide new and terrible clues regarding the nature, methods, and desires of the strange evil vaguely threatening this planet. Conversations with several students of archaic lore in Boston and letters sent to numerous others elsewhere instilled in him a growing amazement that slowly progressed through varying degrees of alarm until reaching a state of acute spiritual dread.

As summer advanced, he felt dimly that something must be done regarding the lurking terrors of the upper Miskatonic valley and the monstrous entity known to the human world as Wilbur Whateley.

The horror of Dunwich unfolded between Lammas and the autumnal equinox of 1928, a monstrous prologue witnessed firsthand by Dr. Armitage. Even prior to this night, whispers had circulated regarding Wilbur Whateley's grotesque pilgrimage to Cambridge and his desperate, frantic attempts to secure a copy of the *Necronomicon* from the Widener Library. These endeavors had failed utterly; Armitage had issued warnings of such piercing intensity to every librarian guarding the forbidden tome that access remained impossible. Yet, Wilbur returned from Cambridge in a state of shocking nervousness. He was consumed by anxiety for the book, yet equally terrified to remain away from home, as if he sensed that prolonged absence might invite calamity.

Early in August, the anticipated catastrophe arrived. In the small hours of the third day, Dr. Armitage was jolted awake by the wild, ferocious cries of the college's savage watchdog. Deep and terrible, the snarling, half-mad growls persisted, rising in volume until they reached a deafening crescendo, punctuated by hideously significant silences. Then, a scream tore through the darkness—a sound emanating from a throat entirely alien to nature, capable of awakening the slumbering citizens of Arkham and haunting their dreams forevermore. Such a cry could originate from no creature born of this earth.

Armitage scrambled into clothes and sprinted across the street and lawn toward the college buildings. Others were already ahead of him, and the shrill wail of a burglar alarm echoed from the library. Through an open window, he saw a black, gaping void illuminated by the moonlight. The intruder had succeeded in breaching the defenses; the barking and screaming, now fading into a low, guttural growling and moaning, clearly originated from within. A primal instinct warned Armitage that what transpired inside was unfit for unfortified eyes. With authoritative force, he pushed back the gathering crowd and unlocked the vestibule door.

Among the throng, he recognized Professor Warren Rice and Dr. Francis Morgan—men to whom he had previously voiced his deepest conjectures and fears. He motioned for them to enter with him. By this time, the internal commotion had subsided, save for the watchful, droning whine of the dog. Suddenly, Armitage felt a chill as he realized that a chorus of whippoorwills in the nearby shrubbery had begun a damnably rhythmic piping, sounding as if in unison with the final breath of a dying man.

A frightful stench filled the air, one Dr. Armitage knew too well. The three men rushed across the hall to the small genealogical reading-room, the source of the low whining. For a heartbeat, no one dared touch the light switch. Finally, summoning his courage, Armitage snapped it on.

One of the three men shrieked aloud at the sight sprawled before them amidst overturned chairs and disordered tables. Professor Rice claimed he lost consciousness for an instant, though he stood firm. Lying half-bent on its side in a fetid pool of greenish-yellow ichor and tarry slime was a creature almost nine feet tall. The dog had stripped it of all clothing and much of its skin. It was not quite dead; it twitched silently and spasmodically, its chest heaving in monstrous rhythm with the expectant piping of the birds outside.

Scattered around were bits of shoe leather and fragments of apparel. Just inside the window lay an empty canvas sack, evidence of how it had been discarded. Near the central desk sat a revolver, a dented but undischarged cartridge later revealing why it had never been fired. However, the creature itself crowded out all other thoughts. To claim that no human pen could describe it would be both trite and inaccurate; rather, it could not be vividly visualized by any mind whose concepts of form and dimension are strictly bound to the common life-forms of this planet and our three known dimensions.

It was undoubtedly partly human, possessing very man-like hands and a head. Its face was goatish and chinless, bearing the distinct stamp of the Whateley family. Yet, the torso and lower body were teratological fantasies, requiring generous clothing merely to allow it to walk unnoticed on Earth. Above the waist, it was semi-anthropomorphic, though its chest, where the dog's paws still rested vigilantly, displayed the leathery, reticulated hide of a crocodile or alligator. Its back was piebald with yellow and black, faintly suggesting the scaly covering of certain snakes.

Below the waist, humanity vanished completely, replaced by sheer fantasy. Thick coarse black fur covered the skin, and from the abdomen protruded a dozen long, greenish-gray tentacles tipped with red sucking mouths, hanging limp. Their arrangement followed an odd symmetry belonging to some cosmic geometry unknown to Earth or the solar system. Set deep in pinkish, ciliated orbits on each hip were what appeared to be rudimentary eyes. Instead of a tail, a trunk-like feeler hung down, marked with purple rings and showing clear signs of being an undeveloped mouth or throat.

Its limbs, aside from the black fur, roughly resembled the hind legs of prehistoric giants, terminating in ridged, veined pads that were neither hooves nor claws. As the creature breathed, its tail and tentacles shifted color rhythmically, driven by a circulation foreign to human biology. The tentacles deepened in greenish hue, while the tail alternated between yellow and a sickly grayish-white in the spaces between its purple rings. There was no genuine blood, only the foul, greenish-yellow ichor that trickled across the painted floor, leaving a curious discoloration in its wake.

As the presence of the three men seemed to rouse the dying entity, it began to mumble without lifting its head. Dr. Armitage made no written record of its mutterings but asserted with confidence that no words of English were spoken. Initially, the syllables defied all correlation with earthly speech, but toward the end, disjointed fragments emerged, clearly drawn from the *Necronomicon*, the monstrous blasphemy for which the creature had perished. As Armitage recalled them, the phrases sounded something like: *"N'gai, n'gha'ghaa, bugg-shoggog, y'hah; Yog-Sothoth, Yog-Sothoth..."*

These utterances trailed off into nothingness as the whippoorwills shrieked in rhythmic crescendos of unholy anticipation. Then, a halt came to the gasping, and the dog raised his head in a long, lugubrious howl. A transformation swept over the yellow, goatish face of the prostrate thing, and its great black eyes fell in apparent despair. Outside, the shrilling of the birds ceased abruptly. Above the murmurs of the gathering crowd arose the sound of a panic-stricken whirring and fluttering. Against the moonlit sky, vast clouds of feathery watchers rose and raced from sight, frantic at the prey they had found.

Suddenly, the dog bolted upright, gave a frightened bark, and leaped nervously out the window by which it had entered. A cry erupted from the crowd, and Dr. Armitage shouted to those outside that no one must be admitted until the police or a medical examiner arrived. He was grateful the windows were too high to permit prying glances and carefully drew the dark curtains down over each one.

By this time, two policemen had arrived. Dr. Morgan met them in the vestibule, urgently pleading for their own safety to delay entry into the stench-filled reading-room until the examiner could arrive and cover the prostrate thing. Meanwhile, frightful changes were occurring on the floor. No detailed description is needed of the *kind* and *rate* of shrinkage and disintegration witnessed by Dr. Armitage and Professor Rice. Suffice it to say that, aside from the external features of the face and hands, the truly human elements of Wilbur Whateley must have been exceedingly small.

When the medical examiner finally arrived, there remained only a sticky, whitish mass on the painted boards, and the monstrous odor had nearly vanished. It appeared Whateley possessed no skull or bony skeleton—at least, not in any true or stable sense. He had taken somewhat after his unknown father.

Yet all this was merely the prologue to the true Dunwich horror. Official formalities were hurriedly conducted by bewildered bureaucrats; abnormal details were carefully suppressed from the press and the public eye; and men were dispatched to Dunwich and Aylesbury to inventory property and identify potential heirs of the late Wilbur Whateley. They found the countryside in a state of profound agitation, driven not only by the ominous rumbling beneath the domed hills but also by the pervasive, unwonted stench and the surging, lapping sounds emanating increasingly from the great, hollow shell of Whateley's boarded-up farmhouse. Earl Sawyer, who had managed the horses and cattle during Wilbur's absence, had developed a woefully acute nervous condition. The officials devised elaborate excuses to avoid entering the noisome, sealed structure, contenting themselves with a single, brief survey of the deceased's living quarters and the newly mended sheds before filing a ponderous report at the courthouse in Aylesbury. Litigation regarding heirship remains in progress among the innumerable Whateleys—both decayed and undecayed—who inhabit the upper Miskatonic Valley.

An almost interminable manuscript, written in strange characters within a massive ledger and deemed a sort of diary due to its erratic spacing and variations in ink and penmanship, presented a baffling puzzle to those who discovered it on the old bureau serving as the owner's desk. After a week of intense debate, it was forwarded to Miskatonic University alongside the deceased's collection of bizarre books for study and possible translation; yet even the region's finest linguists soon concluded that unraveling its secrets would prove exceedingly difficult. No trace of the ancient gold with which Wilbur and Old Whateley invariably settled their debts has ever been recovered.

It was on the dark night of September ninth that the horror truly broke loose. The hill noises had been particularly pronounced throughout the evening, accompanied by frantic barking from dogs all night long. Early risers on the tenth detected a peculiar, cloying stench hanging heavy in the air. Around seven o'clock, Luther Brown, the hired boy at George Corey's farm situated between Cold Spring Glen and the village, rushed frenziedly back from his morning trip to Ten-Acre Meadow with the cows. He was nearly convulsed with fright as he stumbled into the kitchen, while in the yard outside, the herd pawed the earth and lowed pitifully, having followed the boy back in the shared panic that gripped them. Between ragged gasps, Luther struggled to stammer out his tale to Mrs. Corey.

"Up thar in the road beyond the glen, Mis' Corey... there's something been there! It smells like thunder, and all the bushes and little trees are pushed back from the road as if a house had been dragged along it. And that ain't the worst, nuther. There's *prints* in the road, Mis' Corey—great round prints as big as barrel heads, all sunk deep down like an elephant had walked along, *only they're a sight more than four feet could make*. I looked at one or two before I ran, and I swear every one was covered with lines spreading out from one central point, like giant palm-leaf fans twice or three times the size of any we've seen, hammered down into the mud. And the smell was awful, exactly like what it is around Wizard Whateley's old house...."

Here he faltered, shivering anew with the terror that had sent him fleeing home. Mrs. Corey, unable to extract further information, began calling the neighbors, thereby initiating the overture of panic that heralded the major terrors. When she reached Sally Sawyer, the housekeeper at Seth Bishop's—the nearest dwelling to Whateley's—it became her turn to listen rather than speak. Sally's son, Chauncey, who slept poorly, had been up on the hill toward Whateley's and had dashed back in absolute terror after a single glance at the place and the pasture where Mr. Bishop's cows had been left out all night.

"Yes, Mis' Corey," came Sally's tremulous voice over the party line. "Chauncey just came back a-posting, and he couldn't hardly talk for being scared! He says Old Whateley's house is all blown up, with the timbers scattered around as if dynamite had been inside; only the bottom floor isn't through, but it's all covered with a kind of tarlike stuff that smells awful and drips down often from the edges onto the ground where the side timbers blew away. And there's awful kinds of marks in the yard, too—great round marks bigger round than a hogshead, all sticky with the same stuff on the blown-up house. Chauncey says they lead off into the meadows, where a great swath wider'n a barn is matted down, and all the stone walls tumbled every which way wherever it goes.

"And he says, says he, Mis' Corey, how he sat to look for Seth's cows, frightened as he was, and found 'em in the upper pasture nigh the Devil's Hop Yard in an awful shape. Half on 'em's clean gone, and nigh half of them that's left is sucked most dry of blood, with sores on 'em like they've been on Whateley's cattle ever since Lavinny's black brat was born. Seth's gone out now to look at 'em, though I'll vow he won't care to get very nigh Wizard Whateley's! Chauncey didn't look careful to see where the big matted-down swath led after it left the pasture, but he says he thinks it pointed towards the glen road to the village.

"I tell ye, Mis' Corey, there's something abroad as shouldn't be abroad, and I for one think that black Wilbur Whateley, who came to the bad end he deserved, is at the bottom of this breeding of it. He wasn't all human himself, I always says to everybody; and I think he and Ol' Whateley must've raised something in that there nailed-up house that ain't even so human as he was. There's always been unseen things around Dunwich—living things—that ain't human and ain't good for human folks.

"The ground was a-talking last night, and towards morning Chauncey heard the whippoorwills so loud in Cold Spring Glen he couldn't sleep none. Then he thought he heard another faint-like sound over towards Wizard Whateley's—a kind of ripping or tearing of wood, like some big box or crate was being opened far off. What with this and that, he didn't get to sleep at all till sunup, and no sooner was he up this morning than he had to go over to Whateley's to see what's the matter. He saw enough, I tell ye, Mis' Corey! This don't mean no good, and I think as all the men-folks ought to get up a party and do something. I know something awful's about, and feel my time is nigh, though only Gawd knows just what it is.

"Did your Luther take account of where them big tracks led to? No? Well, Mis' Corey, if they was on the glen road this side of the glen, and ain't got to your house yet, I calculate they must go into the glen itself. They would do that. I always says Cold Spring Glen ain't no healthy nor decent place. The whippoorwills and fireflies there never did act like they was creatures of Gawd, and there's them as says ye can hear strange things a-rushing and a-talkin' in the air down there if ye stand in the right place, betwixt the rock falls and Bear's Den."

By noon, fully three-quarters of the men and boys of Dunwich were trooping over the roads and meadows between the newly made Whateley ruins and Cold Spring Glen. They examined in horror the vast, monstrous prints, the maimed Bishop cattle, the strange, noisome wreck of the farmhouse, and the bruised, matted vegetation of the fields and roadside. Whatever had burst loose upon the world had assuredly gone down into the great sinister ravine; for all the trees on the banks were bent and broken, and a great avenue had been gouged in the precipice-hanging underbrush. It was as though a house, launched by an avalanche, had slid down through the tangled growths of the almost vertical slope. From below, no sound came, save for a distant, undefinable fetor; and it is not to be wondered at that the men preferred to stay on the edge and argue rather than descend to face the unknown Cyclopean horror in its lair. Three dogs accompanying the party had barked furiously at first but seemed cowed and reluctant when nearing the glen. Someone telephoned the news to the *Aylesbury Transcript*, but the editor, accustomed to wild tales from Dunwich, did no more than concoct a humorous paragraph about it—an item soon afterward reproduced by the Associated Press.

That night, everyone went home, and every house and barn was barricaded as stoutly as possible. Needless to say, no cattle were allowed to remain in open pasturage. About two in the morning, a frightful stench and the savage barking of dogs awakened the household at Elmer Frye's, on the eastern edge of Cold Spring Glen. All agreed they could hear a sort of muffled swishing or lapping sound coming from somewhere outside. While the town of Dunwich remained shrouded in silence, a far more terrifying drama unfolded quietly behind the locked doors of a book-lined chamber in Arkham. The manuscript attributed to Wilbur Whateley, recently delivered to Miskatonic University for decryption, had sent waves of confusion and dread through the ranks of linguists and scholars alike. Its script, bearing a vague resemblance to the shadowy Arabic of ancient Mesopotamia, was utterly alien to every known authority. After exhaustive analysis, the consensus among the experts was that the text utilized an entirely artificial alphabet functioning as a cipher. Yet, standard cryptographic techniques yielded nothing, regardless of the assumed native tongue of the author.

Ancient volumes seized from Whateley's residence offered little relief; while fascinating to philosophers and scientists, they held no keys to the mystery. Among these was a massive tome bound with iron clasps, inscribed in a script unlike any other—resembling Sanskrit yet distinct. Ultimately, the burden of solving the riddle fell to Dr. Armitage. Motivated by his unique obsession with the Whateley case and his profound mastery of archaic languages and medieval mysticism, Armitage took charge. He hypothesized that the symbols belonged to a forbidden cult tracing its lineage to the sorcerers of the Saracen world. However, he deemed the origin secondary; if the script served merely as a cipher for a modern language, understanding its roots was unnecessary. Assuming the bulk of the text was in English, Armitage prepared to attack the manuscript.

Aware of his colleagues' previous failures, Armitage knew this was no trivial puzzle. For weeks in late August, he immersed himself in the dense lore of cryptography, devouring works ranging from Trithemius' *Poligraphia* and Porta's *De Furtivis Literarum Notis* to Vigenère's *Traité des Chiffres* and modern treatises by Blair and Klüber. Interspersing his theoretical studies with direct assaults on the document, he eventually realized he faced one of history's most intricate ciphers—a system utilizing multiple letter correspondences akin to a multiplication table, unlocked only by secret keywords known to initiates. The ancient texts proved more valuable than the modern ones, leading Armitage to conclude the code was of great antiquity, passed down through generations of mystical experimenters.

Time and again, Armitage stood on the precipice of success, only to be knocked back by unforeseen obstacles. But as September arrived, the fog began to lift. Specific sequences of letters emerged with undeniable clarity, confirming that the text was indeed English. On the evening of September second, the final barrier crumbled. Dr. Armitage read the first continuous passage of Wilbur Whateley's annals. It was a diary, written in a style that perfectly captured the subject's bizarre blend of occult scholarship and profound ignorance.

The first significant entry, dated November 26, 1916, struck Armitage with chilling force. Written by a creature described as a three-and-a-half-year-old child possessing the mind of a twelve-or-thirteen-year-old boy, the journal read: *"Today learned the Aklo for the Sabaoth, which did not like, it being answerable from the hill and not from the air. That upstairs more ahead of me than I had thought it would be, and is not like to have much earth brain. Shot Elam Hutchins's collie Jack when he went to bite me, and Elam says he would kill me if he dast. I guess he won't. Grandfather kept me saying the Dho formula last night, and I think I saw the inner city at the two magnetic poles. I shall go to those poles when the earth is cleared off, if I can't break through with the Dho-Hna formula when I commit it. They from the air told me at Sabbat that it will be years before I can clear off the earth, and I guess Grandfather will be dead then, so I shall have to learn all the angles of the planes and all the formulas between the Yr and the Nhhngr. They from outside will help, but they can not take body without human blood. That upstairs looks it will have the right cast. I can see it a little when I make the Yoorish sign or blow the power of Ibn Ghazi at it, and it is near like them at May Eve on the Hill. The other face may wear off some. I wonder how I shall look when the earth is cleared and there are no earth beings on it. He that came with the Aklo Sabaoth said I may be transfigured, there being much of outside to work on."*

Morning found Dr. Armitage drenched in a cold sweat, consumed by a frenzied, sleepless concentration. He had not left the manuscript all night, sitting beneath the electric lamp, pages flying past his shaking hands as he deciphered the cryptic words. He had nervously informed his wife he would not return home; when she brought breakfast, he could barely swallow a mouthful. Throughout the day, he continued his relentless reading, halting only when forced to reapply the complex decoding key. Meals were brought to him, but he consumed mere fractions of lunch and dinner.

By midday the following night, exhaustion claimed him, sending him into a slumber broken instantly by nightmares as horrific as the truths he had unearthed. On the morning of September fourth, Professors Rice and Morgan visited him, leaving trembling and pale. That evening, Armitage slept fitfully. By Wednesday, he returned to the manuscript, taking copious notes from both fresh sections and previously decoded passages. He dozed briefly in his office chair around midnight but was back at his desk before dawn. Before noon, his physician, Dr. Hartwell, urged him to stop, but Armitage refused, insisting the completion of the diary was vitally important.

That same evening, as twilight descended, Armitage finished his harrowing review and collapsed, utterly drained. When his wife entered with dinner, he found her in a semi-catatonic state. Though conscious enough to hiss sharply at her gaze drifting toward his notes, he lacked the strength to speak further. Rising weakly, he gathered his scribbled papers, sealing them in a large envelope tucked securely into his coat pocket. He managed to reach home, but his condition was critical, prompting an immediate summons for Dr. Hartwell. As the doctor laid him in bed, Armitage could only mutter repeatedly, *"But what, in God's name, can we do?"*

Armitage slept, though partially delirious, the following day. He offered no explanations to Hartwell, speaking instead of the urgent need to confer with Rice and Morgan. His ravings were terrifying: frantic demands to destroy a boarded-up farmhouse, and fantastical visions of a plan to exterminate all humanity and animal life from the Earth by an elder race from another dimension. He shouted that the world was in peril, claiming the Elder Things sought to strip the planet and drag it from the solar system into some other plane of existence where it had fallen eons ago. At other moments, he screamed for the *Necronomicon* and Remigius' *Dæmonolatreia*, hoping to find a counter-formula. *"Stop them, stop them!"* he cried. *"Those Whateleys meant to let them in, and the worst of all is left! Tell Rice and Morgan we must do something—it's a blind business, but I know how to make the powder... It hasn't been fed since the second of August, when Wilbur came here to his death, and at that rate..."*

Despite his age of seventy-three, Armitage possessed a robust constitution and slept off his disorder without developing a fever. Waking late on Friday, clear-headed but sobered by a gnawing fear and immense responsibility, he waited until Saturday afternoon to visit the library and summon Rice and Morgan. For the remainder of that day and evening, the three men tortured their minds with wild speculation and desperate debate. Ancient, monstrous books were dragged from stacks and secure vaults; diagrams and formulas were copied with feverish haste. Skepticism vanished completely—all three had witnessed the corpse of Wilbur Whateley lying on the floor of that very building, rendering any notion of madness untenable.

Opinions differed regarding notification of the Massachusetts State Police, but the decision ultimately favored secrecy. There were forces involved that could not be comprehended by those who had not seen proof firsthand. Late that night, the conference ended without a concrete plan, yet Sunday saw Armitage busily comparing formulas and mixing chemicals from the college laboratory. The more he contemplated the hellish diary, the less he believed any physical agent could eradicate the entity Whateley had unleashed—the earth-threatening presence destined to emerge within hours as the Dunwich Horror.

Monday mirrored Sunday, requiring infinite research and experimentation. Further consultations of the diabolical journal led to shifting strategies, though Armitage knew uncertainty would persist. By Tuesday, he had mapped a definitive course of action, intending to travel to Dunwich within a week. Then, on Wednesday, the great shock occurred. Hidden obscurely in a corner of the *Arkham Advertiser* was a facetious item from the Associated Press reporting that Dunwich's bootleg whisky had spawned a record-breaking monster. Stunned, Armitage immediately telephoned Rice and Morgan. Deep into the night, they debated furiously; the following day became a whirlwind of preparation. Armitage knew he was meddling with terrible powers, yet saw no other path to undo the deeper, malign interference of others before him.

Friday morning, Armitage, Rice, and Morgan departed by automobile for Dunwich, reaching the village around one in the afternoon. Though the day was fair, a peculiar, suffocating dread hung over the landscape—a silent portent clinging to the strangely domed hills and the deep, shadowed ravines of the scarred region. Occasionally, atop a distant peak, a gaunt circle of stones stood stark against the sky. The atmosphere within Osborn's store was thick with hushed terror; the townspeople knew something unspeakable had transpired. By mid-afternoon, the trio had circled the entire settlement, interrogating locals and witnessing firsthand the horrors of the Frye ruins. They saw the lingering tar-stickiness, the blasphemous tracks marred the earth, the mutilated cattle belonging to Seth Bishop, and vast swathes of vegetation torn apart as if by some unseen giant. To Armitage, the trail winding up and down Sentinel Hill possessed a cataclysmic gravity; he lingered long before the sinister, altar-like stone crowning the summit.

Learning that a detachment of State Police from Aylesbury had arrived earlier in response to initial reports of the Frye tragedy, the visitors resolved to locate them and exchange information. Yet, finding the officers proved easier in theory than in practice. Although five police officers had arrived in a single car, the vehicle was now found abandoned and empty near the Frye ruins. The local inhabitants, who had spoken with the officers, appeared initially as bewildered as the outsiders. Then, Old Sam Hutchins went pale, nudged Fred Farr, and pointed a trembling finger toward the dank, yawning hollow nearby. "Gawd," he gasped, his voice cracking, "I tol'd 'em not ter go down into the glen! I never thought nobody'd do it with them tracks an' that smell an' the whippoorwills screechin' down there in the dark o' noonday..." A cold shudder rippled through both natives and visitors; every ear strained instinctively, listening to the silence that felt too loud.

Armitage, now confronted directly with the horror and its monstrous works, trembled under the weight of the responsibility he felt. Night approached—the hour when the mountainous blasphemy lumbered forth on its eldritch course. *Negotium perambulans in tenebris...* The old librarian recited the formulas he had committed to memory, clutching a sheet of paper containing the alternatives. He verified that his electric flashlight functioned properly. Beside him, Rice retrieved a metal insecticide sprayer from a valise, while Morgan unslung the large-game rifle he trusted, disregarding his colleague's warnings that no physical weapon would suffice. Having read the diabolical diary, Armitage knew precisely what manifestation to anticipate, yet he refrained from offering any clues to the terrified Dunwichites, hoping the creature might be subdued without revealing the full extent of the monstrosity to the world.

As shadows lengthened, the villagers dispersed, retreating indoors despite knowing that locks and bolts were futile against a force capable of bending trees and crushing homes. They shook their heads at the Arkham men's plan to guard the ruins near the glen, expressing little hope of ever seeing the watchers again that night. Under the hills, rumbling sounds echoed ominously, accompanied by the threatening piping of whippoorwills. Occasionally, a wind sweeping from Cold Spring Glen carried a touch of ineffable rot to the heavy night air—a scent all three watchers recognized from standing above a dying thing that had masqueraded as human for fifteen and a half years. Yet, the anticipated terror did not manifest immediately. Whatever dwelt in the glen was biding its time. Armitage advised his colleagues that attacking in the darkness would be suicidal.

Morning broke wanly; the nocturnal sounds ceased, leaving behind a gray, bleak day punctuated by a drizzle. Heavy clouds piled up beyond the hills to the northwest. The men from Arkham debated whether to wait for shelter under one of the few intact Frye outbuildings or to take the offensive and descend into the gloom in search of their nameless quarry. The downpour intensified, distant thunder rolled, and sheet lightning shimmered until a forked bolt flashed dangerously close, seemingly diving straight into the accursed glen. Darkness returned, and the watchers prayed for a brief, sharp storm followed by clarity.

Less than an hour later, a confused babel of voices erupted down the road. Moments later, a panicked group of more than a dozen men appeared—running, shouting, and whimpering hysterically. One leader began sobbing, and the Arkham men stiffened as his words coalesced into a coherent nightmare. "Oh, my Gawd, my Gawd!" the voice choked out. "It's a-goin' agin, _an' this time by day_! In the end, the trio from Arkham—Dr. Armitage, aged and white-bearded; Professor Rice, stocky with iron-gray hair; and Dr. Morgan, lean and relatively youthful—climbed the mountain alone. Following hours of patient instruction on the telescope's operation, they had left the instrument with the terrified locals remaining in the road, watching them intently as the device was passed hand-to-hand. The ascent was grueling; Armitage required assistance multiple times. High above, the great swath of vegetation trembled as its hellish creator moved across it with the slow, deliberate pace of a snail. Soon, it became clear the pursuers were closing in.

Curtis Whateley, representing the undecayed branch of the family, held the telescope as the Arkham party abruptly diverted from the main path. He informed the crowd that the men were attempting to reach a subordinate peak overlooking the swath, positioned significantly ahead of the entity's current location. His assessment proved correct; shortly after the invisible blasphemy swept past, the party gained the minor elevation.

Then Wesley Corey, who had taken the glass, shouted that Armitage was adjusting the sprayer held by Rice, signaling that something imminent was about to occur. The crowd stirred nervously, remembering the sprayer was designed to render the unseen horror momentarily visible. Several men closed their eyes, but Curtis Whateley snatched the telescope back, straining his vision to its absolute limit. He saw that Rice, positioned high above and behind the entity, had a perfect angle to deploy the potent powder with devastating effect. To those without the lens, there was merely an instant flash of gray cloud—a mass roughly the size of a large building—near the mountain's summit.

Curtis, gripping the instrument, suddenly dropped it with a piercing shriek into the ankle-deep mud of the road. He reeled, collapsing where he stood until two or three others seized him to steady him. All he could manage was a half-inaudible moan: "Oh, oh, great Gawd... *that*... *that*..."

Pandemonium erupted in questioning voices, save for Henry Wheeler, who instinctively rushed to retrieve the fallen telescope and wipe the mud from its lenses. Curtis was beyond coherence; even isolated fragments of speech were nearly impossible for him to articulate. "Bigger 'n a barn... all made o' squirmin' ropes... hull thing sort o' shaped like a hen's egg bigger'n anything, with dozens o' legs like hogsheads that haff shut up when they step... nothin' solid abaout it--all like jelly, an' made o' sep'rit wrigglin' ropes pushed clost together... great bulgin' eyes all over it... ten or twenty maouths or trunks a-stickin' aout all along the sides, big as stovepipes, an' all a-tossin' aout... all gray, with kinder blue or purple rings... *an' Gawd in Heaven--that haff face on top*!"

That final image proved too much for poor Curtis, and he collapsed completely before he could speak another word. Fred Farr and Will Hutchins carried him to the roadside, laying him on the damp grass. Trembling, Henry Wheeler turned the rescued telescope toward the mountain to observe further. Through the lenses, three tiny figures appeared, sprinting toward the summit as fast as the steep incline permitted. Nothing else was visible.

Suddenly, a strangely unseasonable noise filled the deep valley behind them, echoing even within the underbrush of Sentinel Hill itself. It was the piping of countless whippoorwills, yet within their shrill chorus lurked a note of tense, malevolent expectancy. Earl Sawyer now took the telescope, reporting that the three figures had reached the highest ridge, standing virtually level with the altar-stone but at a considerable distance. One figure seemed to raise its hands above its head at rhythmic intervals. As Sawyer described this, the crowd seemed to hear a faint, half-musical sound drifting from the distance, as if a loud chant accompanied the gestures. The weird silhouette on that remote peak must have been a spectacle of infinite grotesqueness, yet no observer possessed the mood for aesthetic appreciation.

"I guess he's sayin' the spell," Wheeler whispered, snatching the telescope back. The whippoorwills piped wildly in a singularly curious, irregular rhythm, distinct from the visible ritual below. Suddenly, the sunshine seemed to diminish without any discernible clouds forming. It was a peculiar phenomenon noted by all. A rumbling sound brewed beneath the hills, mixed strangely with a concordant rumble seemingly emanating from the sky. Lightning flashed overhead, and the crowd searched in vain for the usual portents of a storm.

The chanting of the men from Arkham became unmistakable. Wheeler saw through the glass that they were all raising their arms in rhythmic incantation. From a distant farmhouse, dogs began to bark frantically. The quality of daylight shifted, and the crowd gazed at the horizon in wonder. A purplish darkness, born of a spectral deepening of the sky's blue, pressed down upon the rumbling hills. Lightning flashed again, brighter than before, and the crowd fancied it revealed a certain mistiness around the altar-stone on the distant height. Yet, no one had been looking through the telescope at that precise moment.

The whippoorwills continued their irregular pulsation while the men of Dunwich braced themselves tensely against an imponderable menace that seemed to saturate the atmosphere. Without warning, deep, cracked, raucous vocal sounds erupted, forever etched into the memory of the stricken group. These were not born of any human throat, for the human organ cannot yield such acoustic perversions. If one had to assign a source, it would seem to come from the pit itself, had it not been unmistakably the altar-stone on the peak.

To call them "sounds" is almost erroneous, for so much of their ghastly, infra-bass timbre spoke directly to dim seats of consciousness and terror far subtler than the ear. Still, they must be described as such, for their form was indisputably, though vaguely, that of half-articulate words. They were loud—louder than the rumblings and thunder above which they echoed—yet they originated from no visible being. Because imagination might conjure a source in the realm of non-visible entities, the huddled crowd at the mountain's base huddled closer, wincing as if expecting a physical blow.

"_Ygnaiih ... ygnaiih ... thflthkh'ngha ... Yog-Sothoth...._" rang the hideous croaking out of space. "_Y'bthnk ... h'ehye ... n'grkdl'lh...._"

The speaking impulse seemed to falter here, as if some frightful psychic struggle were underway. Henry Wheeler strained his eyes at the telescope but saw only the three grotesquely silhouetted human figures on the peak, moving their arms furiously in strange gestures as their incantation neared its climax. From what black wells of Acherontic fear, from what unplumbed gulfs of extra-cosmic consciousness or obscure, long-latent heredity, were those half-articulate thunder-croakings drawn?

Presently, they gathered renewed force and coherence, growing in stark, utter, ultimate frenzy. "_Eh-ya-ya-ya-yahaah ... e'yaya-yayaaaa ... ngh'aaaa ... ngh'aaaa_ ... h'yuh ... h'yuh ... HELP! HELP! ... _ff--ff--ff_--FATHER! FATHER! YOG-SOTHOTH!..."

And then, silence. The pallid group in the road, still reeling from the *indisputably English* syllables that had poured thickly and thunderously down from the frantic vacancy beside the shocking altar-stone, were never to hear such sounds again. Instead, they jumped violently at the terrific report that seemed to rend the hills—the deafening, cataclysmic peal whose source, whether inner earth or sky, no hearer was ever able to pinpoint.

A single lightning bolt shot from the purple zenith to the altar-stone, and a great tidal wave of viewless force and indescribable stench swept down from the hill to the entire countryside. Trees, grass, and underbrush were whipped into a fury. The frightened crowd at the mountain's base, weakened by the lethal fetor that threatened to asphyxiate them, were almost hurled off their feet. Dogs howled from the distance; green grass and foliage wilted to a curious, sickly yellow-gray. Over field and forest, the bodies of dead whippoorwills were scattered. The stench lingered briefly before fading, but the vegetation never recovered. To this day, there remains something queer and unholy about the growths on and around that fearsome hill.

Curtis Whateley was only just regaining consciousness when the men from Arkham descended the mountain, illuminated by sunlight once more brilliant and untainted. They moved with gravity and quietude, appearing shaken by memories and reflections even more terrible than those which had reduced the native group to a state of cowed quivering. In response to a jumble of questions, they only shook their heads, reaffirming one vital fact.
