Until the enemy fleet met us at the mouth of the gulf, I had not fully grasped my own significance as a recruit for the Empress. But the ambush laid by those ships, the savage desperation with which they fought to capture me—these were signs even the blind could not ignore. It was clear Atlantis expected me to play no small part in the coming storm.

Yet if foes had watched for our arrival, so too had Phorenice’s scouts. From their mountain perches, they had spotted us and raced the news inland. The sea that cradled the great city of Atlantis twisted like a serpent, narrowing in places to a river’s breadth, then swelling into vast lakes where the shores blurred into haze. A swift runner, if he knew the treacherous paths—avoiding fire-streams, quicksands, and beasts—could outpace even the fastest galley.

Phorenice was not one to spare messengers. She had sent twenty to the headlands overlooking the straits; seven survived the brutal journey. But of this we knew nothing as we pressed forward, believing our approach unseen.

The galley reeked of its chained oarsmen, savages from Europe, their stench fouling the air. The wind carried grit from some distant eruption, stinging our eyes and skin, so we did not linger. And though my priestly discipline had taught me stoicism, I confess I was human enough to wonder how Atlantis would receive me. Would I be seized as a criminal, tried for my rule in Yucatan? Or would I step ashore unnoticed, summoned later to some quiet duty? The truth, as it turned out, was neither.

At dawn, the sacred mountain’s fiery peak broke the horizon. The boatswains’ whips cracked harder, driving the slaves to a final effort. No sail could be raised against the wind, but the oars bit deep, and the city’s towers soon rose like ghosts from the gleaming water. Twenty years had passed since I last saw Atlantis, and my chest tightened at the sight.

We anchored outside the harbor walls as custom demanded. Usually, port-captains delighted in making ships wait, but today, a ten-oared boat shot toward us before the chain had fully lowered.

"That should be Dason’s galley?" the port-captain called.

"It was," Tob answered. "I saw his head on your prow."

"You were Tatho’s captain?"

"Still am. Dason sent Tatho’s fleet to the seafloor. This was the only hull left floating."

The port-captain’s eyes swept over us. "A dangerous homecoming, then. Where is Lord Deucalion? If you’ve let him die under your watch, the Empress will flay you alive."

Tob snorted. "Between Phorenice and Tatho, he’s been a risky passenger. But my hide’s safe." He jerked a thumb at me. "Ask him yourself."

The man stared, then bowed deeply. "My lord, forgive my blindness. I expected… grander attire."

"Should my worth depend on cloth and trinkets?" I said. "Spare your apologies and take us in."

His boat led, and we followed. The galley docked at the royal quay, its hemp ropes a stark contrast to the gold rings and posts reserved for the Empress. Tob grinned. "Dason would’ve sprung for silk if he’d known his ship would moor here."

The port-captain paled at his irreverence. I stepped onto the quay, where a crimson pavilion had been erected. Three chamberlains in gaudy silks offered me robes fit for a court peacock.

I refused.

They paled. "The Empress has… particular tastes."

"Did she command my attire?"

"No, my lord."

"Then I keep my own."

They drew their swords, offering the hilts. "Better you kill us now than leave us to the torturers."

I waved them off. "A trifle. Forget it."

But their fear was telling. This was not the Atlantis I had left.

A bath waited inside, tainted with some cloying scent. I washed, then sat in silence, ignoring the chamberlains’ small talk. Outside, music blared, fires spat incense, and the crowd thickened.

Phorenice, it seemed, disdained the habit of making others wait. The moment my ship had touched the quay, she had mounted her steed—a creature that halted my breath.

A mammoth.

Not just any mammoth, but a monster of the breed, tusks gilded, neck garlanded, its shaggy bulk tamed into a royal mount. Upon its back, in a half-castle of gold, sat Phorenice.

The beast was a marvel, but its rider eclipsed it.

She was slight, supple, her hair a ruddy blaze cropped short, her face a mask of cunning beauty. Her eyes—no color could name them. They pinned me as I approached.

"Kneel," a chamberlain hissed.

I walked forward, head high.

She watched, then laughed. "Enough! Deucalion bows to no one. Let all Atlantis know: he stands above any man who dares slight him."

A stair was brought. I climbed, seating myself beside her beneath the canopy of royal snakes.

"You looked at my mammoth before me," she said. "A woman remembers such slights."

"I envied your conquest. I’ve killed mammoths. Never dreamed of riding one."

She smiled. "Bold words. And yet you flatter well. These others nauseate me with their empty praise. But you—you are different."

The mammoth turned, carrying us toward the palace. Around us, the city sprawled in splendor and squalor. Temples and pyramids gleamed; beggars lined the streets, gaunt and hollow-eyed.

"The season has been unkind," Phorenice murmured. "And rebels knock at my gates."

"Rebels?"

She shrugged. "They grant us a truce today. Tomorrow, you may break their engines if it amuses you." Then, with a glance at her attendant: "Ylga, unfasten my dress."

The girl obeyed, revealing a bloodstained bandage beneath Phorenice’s left breast.

"A token of yesterday’s seriousness," she said. "The arrow struck a rib. Had it gone deeper, you’d be standing at my pyre." Her eyes glinted. "But you seem more interested in the mammoth than my wounds."

Ylga fastened the clasp again, her fingers tight with something unspoken.

Phorenice sighed. "Ah, well. At least Deucalion does not sigh for love like the others."

I said nothing.

The mammoth plodded on, carrying us toward the heart of a city—and an empire—on the brink of fire.
