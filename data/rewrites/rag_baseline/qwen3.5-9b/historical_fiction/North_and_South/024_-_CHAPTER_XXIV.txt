The morning dawned with a brittle clarity, stripping away the fever dreams of the previous night. Margaret dragged herself upright, her limbs heavy with exhaustion yet strangely buoyant by the sheer relief that the ordeal was concluded. The house had remained a sanctuary of silence; her mother had stirred but once, a fleeting ripple in the otherwise still water. Outside, a faint breeze began to stir the stagnant heat, carrying with it the promise of movement. Though the garden offered no canopy of leaves to dance in the wind, Margaret's imagination conjured the scene anyway—somewhere beyond the walls, in the wilder spaces of copse or wood, the air must be filled with that familiar, murmuring rush, the chaotic joy of falling foliage. The mere thought of it ignited a distinct gladness within her chest, a warm counterpoint to the chill that still lingered in her bones.

Seated at her embroidery frame in Mrs. Hale's room, she constructed a fortress of routine. Once the morning slumber faded, she would assist her mother with dressing; after dinner, she would visit Bessy Higgins. Her plan was to excise the memory of the Thorntons entirely—to banish them until they physically manifested before her again. Yet, the paradox of suppression proved its own undoing; the harder she tried to push the image away, the more vividly it burned itself into her mind. Periodically, a hot flush would sweep across her pallid cheeks, spreading color as rapidly as a sunbeam breaks through watery clouds to strike the sea below.

Dixon entered silently, his footsteps swallowed by the carpet as he approached the shaded window where Margaret sat. "Mr. Thornton, Miss Margaret," he whispered, his voice barely audible. "He is in the drawing-room."

Margaret dropped her needle, the thread snapping with a tiny, sharp sound. "Did he ask for me?" she queried, her voice tight. "Has Papa returned?"

"He requested you, miss," Dixon replied, bowing slightly. "Master is currently out."

"Very well," Margaret said, her composure returning instantly, though her heart hammered against her ribs. "I will come."

Yet she hesitated, lingering in the doorway as if anchored by invisible chains. Inside the drawing-room, Mr. Thornton stood before a window, his back to the entrance, ostensibly studying the street. In truth, he was paralyzed by his own reflection in the glass—or rather, by the terrifying prospect of hers. His heart thundered, a frantic drumbeat echoing the memory of her arms wrapped around his neck earlier that day. That touch, once felt as an intrusion, now resonated through him with a devastating intimacy, melting his resolve like wax before a flame. He dreaded the approach, terrified that if he moved forward with arms outstretched in silent plea, she would retreat, or worse, accept him with the same unthinking devotion she had shown the day before. The possibility of her acceptance thrilled him, while the specter of her rejection withered his future into a barren wasteland.

Then, the subtle shift in the room's atmosphere alerted him. He turned. She had entered so softly that the distant clamor of the street had drowned out her arrival. Clad in soft muslin, she stood by the table, refusing to sit. Her eyelids were lowered, casting a shadow over her eyes; her lips were parted just enough to reveal the pale line beneath, breathing in slow, shallow rhythms that expanded her delicate nostrils. It was the only motion on her face. Her complexion was wan, stripped of its usual vitality, the heavy curtain of dark hair framing her temples serving to obscure any trace of the blow she had sustained. Despite the drooping eyes, her head was tilted back in that familiar posture of proud defiance. Her long arms hung limp at her sides. She resembled a prisoner awaiting judgment for a crime she despised, too indignant to offer a defense.

Thornton took a hurried step forward, then corrected himself, marching with quiet firmness to the open door to shut it out. Returning to the center of the room, he stood opposite her, absorbing the full weight of her presence before daring to disrupt it.

"Miss Hale," he began, his voice rough. "I was very ungrateful yesterday—"

"You had nothing to be grateful for," she interrupted, raising her eyes to meet his gaze with a gravity that silenced him. "You imply, I suppose, that I ought to thank you for what I did."

Despite her fury, a thick blush surged across her face, burning into her very eyes, yet she maintained her steady, unflinching stare. "It was merely a natural instinct," she said quickly, her hands clasped tightly together. "Any woman would have acted the same. We all feel the sanctity of our sex as a high privilege when faced with danger. I ought, rather, to apologize to you for those thoughtless words which drove you into peril."

"It was not your words; it was the truth they conveyed, expressed with such piercing force," he countered, stopping short of the confession that threatened to spill forth. He forced himself to pause, weighing each syllable, his will triumphing over his passion. "I do not seek to escape anything," she declared, her voice cool. "I simply state that you owe me no gratitude. Furthermore, any expression of it would be painful to me, for I do not feel deserving of it. Nevertheless, if speaking will alleviate even a phantom obligation, proceed."

"I do not wish to be relieved of any duty," he insisted, driven mad by her serene demeanor. "Whether fancied or real, I choose to believe I owe my very life to you—yes, smile if you deem it hyperbole. I believe it, because it imbues this life with value, doubling my joy, sharpening my pride, intensifying my sense of existence until I can scarcely distinguish pain from pleasure in knowing I owe it to one... nay, you must listen," he stepped forward, his voice dropping to a tender, terrifying intensity that made her shiver, "to one whom I love, as I do not believe man has ever loved woman before."

He seized her hand, gripping it tightly, waiting for her response. Instead, he recoiled in indignation as she spoke in an icy tone, her words faltering as if searching for purchase on solid ground. "Your manner shocks me. It is blasphemous. I cannot control my initial reaction. It might differ if I understood the sentiment you describe. I do not wish to vex you; moreover, we must speak gently, for my mother sleeps. Your entire demeanor offends me."

"How!" he exploded. "Offends you! I am truly most unfortunate."

"Yes!" she affirmed, reclaiming her dignity. "I feel offended, and I believe justly. You seem to imagine my actions yesterday were a personal transaction between us, inviting your thanks. Do you fail to perceive, as a gentleman would—that any woman worthy of the title would step forward to shield a man from the violence of the mob with her revered helplessness?"

"And the gentleman thus rescued is denied the relief of thanks!" he retorted, his voice dripping with contempt. "I am a man. I claim the right to express my feelings."

"And I yielded to that right," she replied, her chin lifted high. "Simply stating that you inflicted pain by insisting upon it. But you appear to have misconceived my motives. I was not merely guided by feminine instinct, but..." Passionate tears, suppressed for so long, welled in her eyes, choking her voice. "...but prompted by some particular feeling for *you*! Why, there was not a single man—not a desperate soul in that entire crowd—for whom I did not harbor greater sympathy, nor for whom I would not have done more heartily."

"You may continue, Miss Hale," he said, acknowledging the irony of her misplaced sympathies. "I now realize it was only your innate sense of oppression—yes, I, though a master, may also be oppressed—that compelled such noble action. I know you despise me; permit me to say, it is precisely because you do not understand me."

"I have no desire to understand," she snapped, clutching the table to steady herself. She perceived his cruelty, as he intended, and weakness overwhelmed her indignation. "No, I see clearly. You are unfair and unjust."

Margaret pressed her lips together, refusing to respond to such accusations. Yet, despite his savage words, he could have cast himself at her feet, kissing the hem of her garment. She remained motionless, silent. Hot tears of wounded pride streamed down her face. He waited, hoping for a taunt, a spark of engagement to which he could reply. Silence stretched between them, heavy and suffocating. Finally, he picked up his hat.

"One final word," he said. "You look as though being loved by me taints you. You cannot avoid it. Nor can I, if I wished, cleanse you of it. But I would not, if I could. I have never loved a woman before; my life was too busy, my thoughts consumed by other matters. Now I love, and I will love. But do not fear excessive expression on my part."

"I am not afraid," she replied, rising to her full height. "No one has ever dared to be impertinent to me, and no one ever shall. However, Mr. Thornton, you have been remarkably kind to my father." Her tone shifted, transforming from steel to a soft, vulnerable femininity. "Let us cease making each other angry. Please, do not!"

He ignored her plea, preoccupied for half a minute smoothing the nap of his hat with his sleeve. Rejecting her extended hand and dismissing her grave expression of regret, he turned abruptly and exited
