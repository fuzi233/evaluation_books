“I know very little of that gentleman, sir,” Neville Landless said, as he and the Minor Canon turned back from the riverward path and took the quieter way under the moon.

“You know very little of your guardian?” Mr. Crisparkle repeated, pausing as if the words themselves had clinked oddly, like a spoon against a cracked cup.

“Almost nothing.”

“How came he—”

“To be my guardian?” Neville’s voice tightened, then steadied, as though he had set his jaw on the admission. “I’ll tell you, sir. I suppose you know that we come—my sister and I—from Ceylon?”

“Indeed, no.”

Neville gave a short, surprised breath. “I wonder at that. We lived with a stepfather there. Our mother died there, when we were little children. We have had a wretched existence. She made him our guardian, and he was a miserly wretch who grudged us food to eat, and clothes to wear.” The words came out with the bluntness of a boy remembering hunger: not the grand hunger of romance, but the small domestic one—thin rice, watery curries served as if charity, the smell of cooking always promising more than it ever yielded. “At his death, he passed us over to this man; for no better reason that I know of, than his being a friend or connexion of his, whose name was always in print and catching his attention.”

“That was lately, I suppose?”

“Quite lately, sir.” Neville’s pace quickened for a few steps, his shadow sliding long and sharp across the pale stones. “This stepfather of ours was a cruel brute as well as a grinding one. It is well he died when he did, or I might have killed him.”

Mr. Crisparkle stopped short in the moonlight and looked at his hopeful pupil as if a sudden gun had gone off in the close air.

“I surprise you, sir?” Neville asked, and the fierceness drained into something instantly submissive, like a flame checked by a lid. “You shock me; unspeakably shock me,” Mr. Crisparkle returned.

The young man’s head fell. The town lay hushed around them—old walls holding in the night, and the Cathedral’s great bulk seeming to drink up sound. Even so, Cloisterham had its noises: distant water in the weir, a footfall far down a lane, the soft scrape of their shoes; and Mr. Crisparkle, who loved order in a life as much as in a choir, felt how easily a harsh word could bruise such quiet.

After a little while Neville said, low: “You never saw him beat your sister. I have seen him beat mine, more than once or twice, and I never forgot it.”

“Nothing,” Mr. Crisparkle answered—his voice firm, yet his indignation warming him in spite of himself—“not even a beloved and beautiful sister’s tears under dastardly ill-usage, could justify those horrible expressions that you used.”

“I am sorry I used them, and especially to you, sir. I beg to recall them.” Then, as if he could not bear that even pity should misconceive her: “But permit me to set you right on one point. You spoke of my sister’s tears. My sister would have let him tear her to pieces, before she would have let him believe that he could make her shed a tear.”

Mr. Crisparkle, turning over the notes he had already made of Helena Landless’s face and bearing, was neither surprised nor inclined to dispute it.

“Perhaps you will think it strange, sir,” Neville went on, hesitating as if he had been taught to fear the sound of his own candour, “that I should so soon ask you to allow me to confide in you, and to have the kindness to hear a word or two from me in my defence?”

“Defence?” Mr. Crisparkle repeated. “You are not on your defence, Mr. Neville.”

“I think I am, sir. At least I know I should be, if you were better acquainted with my character.”

“Well, Mr. Neville,” the Minor Canon replied, “what if you leave me to find it out?”

“Since it is your pleasure, sir,” Neville answered, and a sullen disappointment came up swiftly, darkening the deference, “since it is your pleasure to check me in my impulse, I must submit.”

There was something in the tone that pricked the conscience of the man who heard it—something that hinted how a trust, once turned aside, might never return, and how easily a mis-shapen mind, offered its first honest morsel of confession, might snatch it back and go hungry again.

They were within sight of the lights in Mr. Crisparkle’s windows. Laughter and voices, homely and cheerful, floated out into the cool night like warmth from an open oven. The Minor Canon stopped.

“Let us turn back and take a turn or two up and down, Mr. Neville, or you may not have time to finish what you wish to say to me. You are hasty in thinking that I mean to check you. Quite the contrary. I invite your confidence.”

“You have invited it, sir, without knowing it, ever since I came here. I say ‘ever since,’ as if I had been here a week.” Neville gave a quick, uncomfortable smile. “The truth is, we came here—my sister and I—to quarrel with you, and affront you, and break away again.”

“Really?” said Mr. Crisparkle, at a loss for any other word that would not judge too quickly.

“You see, we could not know what you were beforehand, sir; could we?”

“Clearly not.”

“And having liked no one else with whom we have ever been brought into contact, we had made up our minds not to like you.”

“Really?” Mr. Crisparkle said again, more softly, as if to keep the confession from echoing too loud in the night.

“But we do like you, sir; and we see an unmistakable difference between your house and your reception of us, and anything else we have ever known.” Neville’s gaze travelled the still street, the old stones, the high dark line of the Cathedral tower cutting the sky. “This—and my happening to be alone with you—and everything around us seeming so quiet and peaceful after Mr. Honeythunder’s departure—and Cloisterham being so old and grave and beautiful, with the moon shining on it—these things inclined me to open my heart.”

“I quite understand, Mr. Neville. And it is salutary to listen to such influences.”

“In describing my own imperfections, sir, I must ask you not to suppose that I am describing my sister’s. She has come out of the disadvantages of our miserable life as much better than I am, as that Cathedral tower is higher than those chimneys.”

Mr. Crisparkle, for his own part, was not so sure; he had seen in Helena a force that might scorch as readily as it could warm.

“I have had, sir, from my earliest remembrance, to suppress a deadly and bitter hatred. This has made me secret and revengeful. I have been always tyrannically held down by the strong hand. This has driven me, in my weakness, to the resource of being false and mean. I have been stinted of education, liberty, money, dress, the very necessaries of life, the commonest pleasures of childhood, the commonest possessions of youth. This has caused me to be utterly wanting in—I don’t know what emotions, or remembrances, or good instincts—I have not even a name for the thing, you see!—that you have had to work upon in other young men to whom you have been accustomed.”

This is evidently true, Mr. Crisparkle thought; and this is not encouraging.

“And to finish with, sir: I have been brought up among abject and servile dependents, of an inferior race, and I may easily have contracted some affinity with them. Sometimes, I don’t know but that it may be a drop of what is tigerish in their blood.”

As in the case of that remark just now, Mr. Crisparkle thought.

“In a last word of reference to my sister, sir—we are twin children—you ought to know, to her honour, that nothing in our misery ever subdued her, though it often cowed me. When we ran away from it”—his voice faltered, as if he tasted again the grit of dust on the road and the fear of capture like a bitter spice—“we ran away four times in six years, to be soon brought back and cruelly punished), the flight was always of her planning and leading. Each time she dressed as a boy, and showed the daring of a man. I take it we were seven years old when we first decamped; but I remember, when I lost the pocket-knife with which she was to have cut her hair short, how desperately she tried to tear it out, or bite it off. I have nothing further to say, sir, except that I hope you will bear with me and make allowance for me.”

“Of that, Mr. Neville, you may be sure,” returned the Minor Canon. “I don’t preach more than I can help, and I will not repay your confidence with a sermon. But I entreat you to bear in mind, very seriously and steadily, that if I am to do you any good, it can only be with your own assistance; and that you can only render that, efficiently, by seeking aid from Heaven.”

“I will try to do my part, sir.”

“And, Mr. Neville, I will try to do mine. Here is my hand on it. May God bless our endeavours!”

They clasped hands, and the act had the quietness of a contract made without witnesses, under a moon that had looked down upon a hundred such private vows in Cloisterham, and forgotten none.

They stood at the door now, the cheerful sound of voices and laughter within. Mr. Crisparkle, however, held back.

“We will take one more turn before going in,” he said, “for I want to ask you a question. When you said you were in a changed mind concerning me, you spoke not only for yourself, but for your sister too?”

“Undoubtedly I did, sir.”

“Excuse me, Mr. Neville, but I think you have had no opportunity of communicating with your sister, since I met you. Mr. Honeythunder was very eloquent; but perhaps I may venture to say, without ill-nature, that he rather monopolised the occasion. May you not have answered for your sister without sufficient warrant?”

Neville shook his head, and a proud smile touched his mouth. “You don’t know, sir, yet, what a complete understanding can exist between my sister and me, though no spoken word—perhaps hardly as much as a look—may have passed between us. She not only feels as I have described, but she very well knows that I am taking this opportunity of speaking to you, both for her and for myself.”

Mr. Crisparkle looked in his face with some incredulity; yet there was such absolute conviction there that the Minor Canon found his eyes dropping to the pavement, and he mused until they came to the door again, as if the stones held an older language than speech—one the twins had learned early, in silence.

“I will ask for one more turn, sir, this time,” Neville said, colour rising, and something sharper stirring under the civility. “But for Mr. Honeythunder’s—I think you called it eloquence, sir?” There was a faint slyness in it.

“I—yes, I called it eloquence,” said Mr. Crisparkle.

“But for Mr. Honeythunder’s eloquence, I might have had no need to ask you what I am going to ask you. This Mr. Edwin Drood, sir: I think that’s the name?”

“Quite correct,” said Mr. Crisparkle. “D-r-double o-d.”

“Does he—or did he—read with you, sir?”

“Never, Mr. Neville. He comes here visiting his relation, Mr. Jasper.”

“Is Miss Bud his relation too, sir?” Neville asked, and there was a sudden superciliousness in the question that made Mr. Crisparkle’s attention leap, as if a twig had cracked in a hedgerow.

He explained, aloud, what he knew of their little story: that Edwin and Rosa were betrothed.

“O! that’s it, is it?” Neville said. “I understand his air of proprietorship now!”

He spoke it so much to himself—so much as if he were tasting an unpleasant word and could not help letting it out—that Mr. Crisparkle felt it would be like reading over a man’s shoulder to take notice.

A moment after, they re-entered the house.

Mr. Jasper was at the piano as they came into the drawing-room, accompanying Miss Rosebud while she sang. He played without notes, and because she was a heedless little creature, apt to wander off the key as a child wanders from a path to pick flowers, he followed her lips most attentively, with his eyes as well as his hands, and from time to time gave her the key-note—softly, carefully—as if dropping a crumb for her to find her way back.

Standing with an arm drawn round Rosa, but with a face far more intent on Jasper than on the singing, stood Helena. Between her and her brother, as Neville entered, an instantaneous recognition passed—too quick to be called a sign, and yet so bright in its brevity that Mr. Crisparkle saw, or thought he saw, the understanding Neville had spoken of flash out and vanish.

Neville took his admiring station, leaning against the piano opposite the singer. Mr. Crisparkle sat down by the china shepherdess. Edwin Drood, in his easy gallantry, furled and unfurled Miss Twinkleton’s fan; and that lady passively claimed that exhibitor’s proprietorship in the accomplishment on view which Mr. Tope the Verger daily claimed in the Cathedral service, as if art were a thing to be shown, ticketed, and approved.

The song went on—a sorrowful strain of parting. Rosa’s fresh young voice was very plaintive and tender, like milk just beginning to steam before it boils over. Yet beneath it lay the room’s other music: the pressed hush of listeners, the faint stir of fabric, the breathing held and released, and, through it all, Jasper’s guidance, which seemed less accompaniment than possession.

As Jasper watched the pretty lips and again and again hinted the one note—as though it were a low whisper from himself—the voice grew less steady, until all at once the singer broke into a burst of tears, and shrieked out, with her hands over her eyes:

“I can’t bear this! I am frightened! Take me away!”

With one swift turn of her lithe figure, Helena laid the little beauty on a sofa as if she had never caught her up. Then, on one knee beside her, and with one hand upon Rosa’s rosy mouth while with the other she appealed to all the rest, Helena said:

“It’s nothing; it’s all over; don’t speak to her for one minute, and she is well!”

Jasper’s hands had, in the same instant, lifted themselves from the keys, and were poised above them as though he waited to resume. In that attitude he sat quite quiet, not even looking round when all the rest had changed their places and were reassuring one another. It was as if he listened not to their voices but to the room’s altered silence, and found it to his taste.

“Pussy’s not used to an audience; that’s the fact,” Edwin Drood said, with the careless tone of one who treats terror like a small domestic accident—spilled tea, a scorched crust. “She got nervous, and couldn’t hold out. Besides, Jack, you are such a conscientious master, and require so much, that I believe you make her afraid of you. No wonder.”

“No wonder,” repeated Helena.

“There, Jack, you hear!” Edwin went on, laughing. “You would be afraid of him, under similar circumstances, wouldn’t you, Miss Landless?”

“Not under any circumstances,” Helena returned, and if there was fear in her nature it did not live in her voice.

Jasper brought down his hands, looked over his shoulder, and begged to thank Miss Landless for her vindication of his character. Then he fell to dumbly playing, without striking the notes—moving as if sound were beneath him, and only the shape of it mattered—while his little pupil was taken to an open window for air, and otherwise petted and restored.

When Rosa was brought back, his place was empty.

“Jack’s gone, Pussy,” Edwin told her. “I am more than half afraid he didn’t like to be charged with being the Monster who had frightened you.”

But she answered never a word, and shivered, as if the night air had been laid on her too long and too cold.

Miss Twinkleton now opined that indeed these were late hours, Mrs. Crisparkle, for finding ourselves outside the walls of the Nuns’ House; and that we who undertook the formation of the future wives and mothers of England—here dropping her voice, as if the phrase were a preserve to be whispered over—were really bound—voice rising again—to set a better example than one of rakish habits. Wrappers were put in requisition, and the two young cavaliers volunteered to see the ladies home.

It was soon done. The gate of the Nuns’ House closed upon them with a sound that seemed to seal away the last laughter, leaving only the thin night-noises of the town—distant steps, a faint latch, the far-off murmur of water—like a careful kitchen grown quiet after supper.

The boarders had retired. Only Mrs. Tisher, in solitary vigil, awaited the new pupil. Helena’s bedroom being within Rosa’s, very little introduction or explanation was necessary before Helena was placed in charge of her new friend and left for the night.

“This is a blessed relief, my dear,” Helena said, when the door had shut and the house seemed to breathe again in its own stillness. “I have been dreading all day that I should be brought to bay at this time.”

“There are not many of us,” Rosa returned, “and we are good-natured girls; at least the others are; I can answer for them.”

“I can answer for you,” Helena laughed, searching the lovely little face with her dark, fiery eyes, and tenderly caressing the small figure—as if she would reassure herself by touch that this bright, delicate thing was real and within reach. “You will be a friend to me, won’t you?”

“I hope so. But the idea of my being a friend to you seems too absurd, though.”

“Why?”

“O, I am such a mite of a thing, and you are so womanly and handsome. You seem to have resolution and power enough to crush me. I shrink into nothing by the side of your presence even.”

“I am a neglected creature, my dear,” Helena said, and now there was no laughing in it, “unacquainted with all accomplishments, sensitively conscious that I have everything to learn, and deeply ashamed to own my ignorance.”

“And yet you acknowledge everything to me!” Rosa said.

“My pretty one, can I help it? There is a fascination in you.”

“O! is there though?” Rosa pouted, half jest and half earnest, and the pettishness had in it a child’s hunger to be prized. “What a pity Master Eddy doesn’t feel it more!”

Of course her relations with that young gentleman had already been imparted in Minor Canon Corner, as ordinary information is passed with tea and bread-and-butter, and yet can carry the weight of a fate.

“Why, surely he must love you with all his heart!” Helena cried, with an earnestness that threatened to blaze into ferocity if he did not.

“Eh? O, well, I suppose he does,” Rosa said, pouting again. “I am sure I have no right to say he doesn’t. Perhaps it’s my fault. Perhaps I am not as nice to him as I ought to be. I don’t think I am. But it is so ridiculous!”

Helena’s eyes demanded what was.

“We are,” Rosa answered, as if she had spoken aloud already. “We are such a ridiculous couple. And we are always quarrelling.”

“Why?”

“Because we both know we are ridiculous, my dear!” Rosa gave that answer as if it shut the matter up like a box with a clasp.

Helena looked at her for some moments with a masterful intentness, and then, as if she could not keep her need in check, impulsively put out both her hands.

“You will be my friend and help me?”

“Indeed, my dear, I will,” Rosa replied, with affectionate childishness that went straight to Helena’s heart. “I will be as good a friend as such a mite of a thing can be to such a noble creature as you. And be a friend to me, please; I don’t understand myself: and I want a friend who can understand me, very much indeed.”

Helena kissed her, and retaining both her hands said, very quietly:

“Who is Mr. Jasper?”

Rosa turned her head aside in answering, as if the name had a sound that bruised her ear. “Eddy’s uncle, and my music-master.”

“You do not love him?”

“Ugh!” Rosa put her hands up to her face and shook with fear or horror, as if she had tasted something rancid and could not get it from her mouth.

“You know that he loves you?”

“O, don’t, don’t, don’t!” Rosa cried, dropping on her knees and clinging to her new resource. “Don’t tell me of it! He terrifies me. He haunts my thoughts like a dreadful ghost. I feel that I am never safe from him. I feel as if he could pass in through the wall when he is spoken of.”

She actually looked round, as if expecting the shadow behind her to thicken into his shape.

“Try to tell me more about it, darling,” Helena said; and the strength in her voice was like a steady hand upon a shaking cup, preventing the spill.

“Yes, I will, I will. Because you are so strong. But hold me the while, and stay with me afterwards.”

“My child! You speak as if he had threatened you in some dark way.”

“He has never spoken to me about—that. Never.”

“What has he done?”

“He has made a slave of me with his looks. He has forced me to understand him, without his saying a word; and he has forced me to keep silence, without his uttering a threat.” Rosa’s words came in little rushes, breathy and broken, as if she were being pursued. “When I play, he never moves his eyes from my hands. When I sing, he never moves his eyes from my lips. When he corrects me, and strikes a note, or a chord, or plays a passage, he himself is in the sounds, whispering that he pursues me as a lover, and commanding me to keep his secret.”

The very idea made the room feel louder in its quiet: the imagined piano-note, the remembered pressure of his presence, the way a single sound can take over a mind until even silence seems to throb.

“I avoid his eyes, but he forces me to see them without looking at them. Even when a glaze comes over them—which is sometimes the case—and he seems to wander away into a frightful sort of dream in which he threatens most, he obliges me to know it, and to know that he is sitting close at my side, more terrible to me than ever.”

“What is this imagined threatening, pretty one? What is threatened?”

“I don’t know. I have never even dared to think or wonder what it is.”

“And was this all, to-night?”

“This was all; except that to-night when he watched my lips so closely as I was singing, besides feeling terrified I felt ashamed and passionately hurt. It was as if he kissed me, and I couldn’t bear it, but cried out.” Rosa clung harder, as if she would hide inside Helena’s strength. “You must never breathe this to any one. Eddy is devoted to him. But you said to-night that you would not be afraid of him, under any circumstances, and that gives me—who am so much afraid of him—courage to tell only you. Hold me! Stay with me! I am too frightened to be left by myself.”

The lustrous gipsy-face drooped over the clinging arms and bosom; the wild black hair fell down protectingly over the childish form. In Helena’s intense dark eyes there slept a gleam of fire, though they were softened now with compassion and admiration, and with something else—older than the present hour, and as resolute as an oath made under the moon.

Let whomsoever it most concerned look well to it.
