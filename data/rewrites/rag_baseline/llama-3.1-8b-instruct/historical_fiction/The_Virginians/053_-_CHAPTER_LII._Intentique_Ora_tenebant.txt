Silence hung heavy over the fort, a weight that pressed down upon the worn wooden beams. In the flickering candlelight, the shadows of the garrison's makeshift quarters seemed to twist and writhe like living things. Monsieur Museau's asthmatic flute had fallen silent, and I strummed the strings of my guitar with a listless hand, the notes dying away like the last gasps of a dying breath. Fawn, the poor woman, sat nearby, nursing her papoose with a look of stoic resignation, as if she had grown accustomed to the hardships of our isolated existence.

It had been months since the commandant and I had last had a moment to ourselves, free from the petty squabbles and endless routine of life at the fort. The days blended together in a haze of card games, desultory conversations, and the occasional visitor who brought news of the outside world. But today was different. Today, a hunter had arrived with tidings of the Marquis de Montcalm's plans to reinforce the French garrisons and push deeper into British territory. The prospect of reinforcements had set the men of the fort aflame with excitement, but I saw something else in Monsieur Museau's eyes – a glimmer of unease, a hint of doubt.

I leaned forward, my fingers pausing on the strings of my guitar. 'Commandant, it's all well and good to talk of conquest and glory,' I said, my voice barely above a whisper, 'but what of your farm in Normandy? The pot of cider, the trippes a la mode de Caen – will they still be there when you're gone?' I knew I was pushing, probing for a reaction, but I couldn't help myself.

Monsieur Museau's face darkened, his eyes clouding over like a summer storm. 'Ah, garcon,' he growled, 'you'd best be careful what you wish for. The world is a cruel place, and we are but small men, subject to the whims of those who come after us. You'll be lucky to keep your skin intact, let alone your precious farm.' His voice dropped to a whisper, and I felt a shiver run down my spine as he began to recount the tales of torture and suffering he had witnessed among the Senecas, the horrors that had been inflicted upon his own countrymen by the very people we were now fighting.
