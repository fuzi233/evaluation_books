George Warrington never allowed his legal studies to cramp his small pleasures, disrupt his easy comfort, or put his precious health at risk. In her letters, Madam Esmond had reminded him that while he might wear a student’s gown and sit to hall-commons among a crowd of unnamed strangers, he bore an old, distinguished name he was bound to uphold, and could take equal rank with any gentleman of quality at home or across the Atlantic. She bade him study as a gentleman, not a drudging professional hack, and George obeyed her well enough. He never ranked among the law’s rank-and-file pupils, but served rather as a volunteer in her retinue, much like other young men of independent fortune we have encountered before.

A hundred years prior, the law was far less exacting than it is today: it granted its disciples far more leisure, far more merrymaking over punch, far more hours lingering in coffee-houses and away from dreary chambers, than the modern jealous mistress allows her votaries, who are scarcely left time for amusement, instruction, sleep, or a decent dinner. Even then, though, she demanded a good deal of a man’s attention. We are told Murray might have made a name as a second Ovid, but he chose instead to become Lord Chief Justice, wrapping himself in ermine rather than poet’s laurel. Doubtless George Warrington might have risen to a peerage and the Woolsack himself, had he studied long and relentlessly, had he mastered the arts of a dexterous courtier and made himself a favourite of every petty attorney. In short, he might have reached that height had he been anyone other than himself. He treated Themis with decent respect and punctuality, but he loved letters far more than law: the black-letter pages of Chaucer were infinitely sweeter to him than the dense, Gothic tomes of Hale and Coke.

In those quaint old times, letters were deeply cherished, and authors were treated as living authorities. Gentlemen would cite Virgil or Lucan in open court or on the floor of the House of Commons, debating what Statius, Juvenal, not to mention Tully or Tacitus, had said on some point of law or politics. Those good old heathens’ reign is long over now: the worship of Jupiter and Juno is no more out of fashion than the study of Pagan poetry and ethics. We live in an age of economists and calculators, and Tooke’s Pantheon sits deserted, a ridiculous relic of the past. Now and then, a Stanley might sacrifice a kid to the Olympians, a Gladstone might weave them a wreath, a Lytton might burn a little incense in their honour—but what do the crowds of Lambeth, Birmingham, or Tower Hamlets care for those ancient rites and forgotten divinities? Who on earth are the Muses, and what use is all that Greek and Latin rubbish? Where is Helicon, and who cares? Who was Thalia, pray, and how many letters are in her name? Is Melpomene pronounced in three syllables or four? And do you know whose design I stole for that little figure of Tragedy that adorns the initial G at the head of this chapter?

It has been noted before how Mr. George whiled away the long idle hours of his youth at home, and during his long imprisonment in the French fort on the banks of the Monongahela, by paying court to Melpomene, the Tragic Muse. The fruit of their union was a full five-act tragedy, omitted from Bell’s Theatre collections though I dare say it is no worse than half the plays printed in those volumes. Most young literary men pay their first homage to Tragedy first, just as they fall in love with women far older and grander than themselves. Let every candid reader who ever dabbled in writing own the truth: his earliest ambition reached the very highest peaks. In his green unknowing youth, he aimed only at tragedy and epic, and no prize but the very greatest was fit for his hand. Only in riper years did he scale back his pretensions, and decide that translating an ode of Horace, or adapting a song of Waller or Prior into decent alcaics or sapphics, was the full extent of his small talents.

Once settled in London, George attended performances at both patent playhouses, haunted the theatrical coffee-houses to hear the regular critics hold forth, and could be found loitering in the Bedford between the acts, or supping at the Cecil Tavern with wits and actors long after the curtain fell. Gradually he made the acquaintance of every well-known player and popular playwright of the day. The gruff, tough old Macklin, the frolicsome Foote, the vivacious Hippisley, and the sparkling, incomparable Mr. Garrick himself, were all regular faces at those supper tables. George won immense popularity in these circles for his wit, his modesty, and the widely circulated rumour of his large independent fortune: actors would happily share a bowl of punch with him, and critics would sup at his expense with the greatest affability. Many a young man has dreamed of such intimacy with celebrated authors and performers: to hob and nob face to face with the actor who plays Bobadil or Henry the Fifth, to take a pinch of snuff straight from Aristarchus the critic’s own box, to hand Juliet to her coach, or Monimia to her sedan chair—no wonder George Warrington fell so deeply in love with the theatre. It was all the more delightful for being forbidden fruit at home, where Madam Esmond only half approved of playhouses and their raucous denizens. He threw more than one lavish feast for the players, and it was whispered that more than one celebrated genius had condescended to borrow small sums from him.

As he polished his tragic masterpiece, adding new flourishes and tightening scenes, it is no surprise he sought the advice of his theatrical acquaintances, who plied him with no end of lavish praise and friendly counsel. His new Temple acquaintance Mr. Spencer hosted a breakfast in his Fig Tree Court chambers, where George read large portions of his play aloud to a room of guests, who all agreed it held uncommon merit. Even the learned Mr. Johnson, who had been invited as a special guest, was good enough to declare the piece showed remarkable talent. To be sure, he noted, it violated all the classical unities—but many great authors before him had broken those same rules, and Mr. Warrington was fully welcome to follow their example. There was something in the tragedy, he said, that strongly reminded him of both *Coriolanus* and *Othello*. “Two very great works, sir!” George protested, delighted. “No doubt of that,” Mr. Spencer put in, “and your catastrophe is terrible, perfectly just, and drawn in part from real history, making it all the more awful.”

The plot of Mr. Warrington’s tragedy was stuffed full of battle, betrayal, and bloody murder. His grandfather’s favourite old book had been the life of George Frundsberg of Mindelheim, the famous Imperial landsknecht colonel who fought at the Battle of Pavia and served under the Constable de Bourbon. One of Frundsberg’s close military companions, a mercenary named Carpzow or Carpezan, became George’s tragic hero. The first act, as it survives in Sir George Warrington’s original manuscript, opens before a walled convent on the banks of the Rhine, besieged by Lutheran troops under Carpezan’s command. These Lutherans are a rough, godless gang: they have plucked the beards of Roman friars and torn the veils from hundreds of religious women. Two dozen nuns tremble behind the convent walls, and the small garrison has sworn to surrender if no expected reinforcements arrive by midday. A temporary armistice is in place, and the sentries on the walls look down with hungry eyes as the mercenary soldiers and camp followers gamble on the grass outside the gate. Then the convent bell peals twelve: ding, ding, dong. No reinforcements have come. The gate swings open, and the famous Protestant hero, the terror of the Turks on the Danube and Papal troops in the Lombard plains, Colonel Gaspar Carpezan, marches in. He is clad head to toe in polished steel, his massive battle hammer slung over his shoulder—the same hammer he has used to shatter so many fortress sconces and sacred images. His banners fly, his trumpets blare. “No rudeness, men,” he orders. “The wine, larder, and cellar are yours to plunder. The church plate will be melted down. Any of the garrison who wish to take service with me will be welcomed, and paid handsomely. But no insult to the religious ladies! I have promised them all safe conduct, and the man who lays a finger on any of them hangs on the spot. See to that, Provost Marshal!” The giant Provost Marshal, dressed all in a vivid red doublet, nods his assent.

“We shall see a good deal more of that Provost Marshal, who doubles as our on-field executioner,” Mr. Spencer explained to his guests.

“A very agreeable acquaintance, I am sure! I shall be delighted to meet the gentleman again,” Mr. Johnson rumbled, wagging his head over his teacup. “This scene of the mercenaries, the camp followers, and their wild sports is novel and stirring, Mr. Warrington, and I give you my full compliments on it. The Colonel has gone inside the convent, I think? Now let us hear what he does there.”

The Abbess, and two of her oldest senior nuns, step forward to face the conqueror. Fierce as he is on the battlefield, they confront him in their sacred halls, well aware of his reputation for vandalising every convent he has ever sacked. That same battle hammer of his has smashed hundreds of sacred images across Germany, and pounds upon pounds of stolen convent plate are known to have passed through his hands. It is no wonder the Abbess-Princess of St. Mary’s, a lady of noble birth, fierce prejudices, and a sharp, uncurbed tongue, takes an instant dislike to this lowborn heretic who lords it over her house, and proceeds to give Carpezan a full piece of her mind. The scene, where the elderly noblewoman at first gets the better of the rough soldier, was much admired by George’s audience. For all his warlike ferocity, Carpezan is initially shaken by her brisk verbal assault, and for a moment, the great conqueror seems to be vanquished by his own prisoner. But an old veteran like him would never be bested permanently by a woman. “Pray tell me, madam,” he says, “how many ladies live in your convent, so that my men may arrange proper transport for them all?” The Abbess, her face dark with trouble and anger, replies that besides herself, there are twenty-three noble sisters of St. Mary’s. She had been about to say twenty-four, and corrects herself mid-sentence. “Ha! Why that hesitation?” asks Captain Ulric, one of Carpezan’s roguish, lighthearted young officers.

The dark-faced mercenary chief pulls a crumpled letter from his coat pocket. “I demand from you, madam,” he says sternly to the Abbess, “the person of the noble Lady Sybilla of Hoya. Her brother was my favourite captain, slain fighting beside me in the Milanese campaigns. On his death, she became heiress to all his vast lands. We have received word that a greedy uncle tricked her here, and immured her in this convent against her will. The damsel herself shall choose her fate: she may stay here as a cloistered nun of St. Mary’s, or return to the world as a free woman, the Baroness of her brother’s estates.” The Abbess is visibly shaken by this demand, but replies haughtily that there is no Lady Sybilla in the house: every inmate is a professed nun, and all their lands and wealth belong by law to the Order. “Give me the Lady Sybilla of Hoya at once!” Carpezan roars in great wrath. “If you refuse, I will signal my reiters, and give your convent over to full sack.”

“Faith, if I lead the storm, I won’t waste time on the old Abbess,” Captain Ulric jokes, “I’d choose some plump, smiling, red-lipped young nun like—like—” As he leans forward, trying to peer under the veils of the two attendants standing behind the Abbess, the old woman snaps at him: “Silence, fellow, with your ribald talk! The lady you ask for has passed beyond sin, temptation, and vanity. Three days ago, our Sister Agnes died.”

At this announcement, Carpezan is visibly staggered. The Abbess calls on the convent chaplain to confirm her statement, and the ghastly pale old man admits that yes, Sister Agnes was buried three days prior. This is too much. From within the lining of his steel coat, Carpezan pulls out the letter Sybilla herself snuck out to him, in which she wrote that she was soon to be “buried” not in hallowed ground, but in a sealed oubliette beneath the convent floors, to be kept on bread and water or starved to death entirely. He seizes the unflinching Abbess by the arm, while Captain Ulric grabs the chaplain by the throat. He blows a sharp blast on his horn, and his furious landsknechts pour into the convent from outside. Walls are smashed, doors are kicked in, and in the chaos of flames, screaming nuns, and clashing steel, Carpezan himself carries the fainting Sybilla out of her dungeon, slung over his shoulder. It was that same pretty, red-lipped young nun Ulric had been staring at who had shown them the way to the oubliette, and passed Sybilla’s letter to Carpezan in the first place.

“The convent is suppressed with a vengeance,” George smiles. “We end the first act with the whole building in flames, the soldiers roaring in triumph, and the nuns screaming for mercy. They had best change their costumes at once, because in the next act, most of them will be appearing as court ladies.” His guests break into discussion immediately: if the play were staged at Drury Lane, Mrs. Pritchard might not like playing the Abbess, who only appears in the first act, but her daughter Miss Pritchard would make a lovely Sybilla, and Miss Gates a perfect mischievous attendant nun. Mr. Garrick, they note, is not particularly tall, but no one ever thinks of him as anything less than as big as a grenadier when he is performing, so he would make a magnificent Carpezan. Mr. Johnson agrees that the comic actor Woodward would play the roguish Ulric perfectly, as he already excels at Mercutio-style mischievous roles. By the end of the discussion, the whole company has cast every part in the imaginary production.

In Act the Second, Carpezan has married Sybilla. He has grown enormously wealthy from years of plunder in the wars, has been ennobled by the Emperor, and lives in great state and splendour in his castle on the banks of the Danube. For all his wealth, title, and new bride, though, the newly made Lord Carpezan is not happy. Perhaps his long years as a mercenary fighting for every side have left him haunted by the memory of his crimes. Perhaps his rough, unpolished soldier manners clash terribly with the imperious temper of his highborn wife. She makes his life a misery, taunting him endlessly about his low birth, mocking his old war comrades that the veteran still loves to gather around him, until more than once he wishes he had never fished this lovely, quarrelsome, wayward shrew out of that oubliette. After the chaos of the first act, this act is far quieter, consisting mostly of bitter quarrels between the Baron and Baroness, until hunting horns sound, and messengers announce that the young King of Bohemia and Hungary is riding that way on a progress.

Act III unfolds in Prague, where his Majesty has invited Lord and Lady Carpezan to his court, with great promises of promotion for the Baron. He is to be raised from Colonel to General-in-Chief, and made a Count. Sybilla immediately becomes one of the most brilliant, sought-after ladies in the entire court. “Oh, wait a minute—I recognise this story now, sir!” Mr. Johnson interrupts. “It’s in Meteranus’ *Theatrum Universum*. I read it when I was a boy at Oxford. Carpezanus, or Carpzoff—”

“That takes us straight to Act Four,” George says. In the fourth act, the young King’s attentions to Sybilla grow more and more unmistakeable. Her husband struggles against his jealousy, refusing to believe the worst of his wife for as long as he can, until her infidelity is placed beyond all possible doubt. The act closes on the terrible event that actually happened in the real history he drew from: convinced of his wife’s guilt, Carpezan orders his regimental executioner to slay her in her own palace chambers. The curtain falls moments after the murder, in a room lit only by moonlight streaming through a great oriel window, just as the young King arrives outside her door with his lute, strumming the secret tryst song that they agreed on as a signal between them. That very same song, written in the style of Mr. Waller, was sung earlier in Act III at a great royal court festival, and Mr. Johnson praised it as an excellent imitation of the old poet’s manner. The way the bright, cheerful melody echoes right outside the chamber where Sybilla’s corpse lies, deepens the tragic horror of the moment perfectly.

“But what happened to him afterwards?” Johnson asked. “I remember in Meteranus’ account, Carpezan was taken back into favour by Count Mansfeld, and doubtless went on to murder plenty more people on the Protestant side.”

George had deliberately departed from historical fact for his fifth act. King Louis of Hungary and Bohemia, still reeling from the violent end of his affair with Sybilla, receives word that the Emperor Solyman the Magnificent is invading his Hungarian dominions. Two noblemen enter, and report that during the King’s war council, the enraged Carpezan burst into the royal chamber, shattered his sword before the King’s feet, and flung down a glove he dared the King to wear, swearing he would one day come back to claim it. After making that wild challenge, the rebel fled Prague and has not been seen since. Rumour says he has joined the Turkish invader, converted to Islam, and is now in the Sultan’s camp, whose white tents gleam across the river, right in the path of the King’s advancing army. Then the King enters his tent with his generals, draws up his battle lines, and sends all his officers to their posts, keeping only his old, faithful master of the horse at his side.
