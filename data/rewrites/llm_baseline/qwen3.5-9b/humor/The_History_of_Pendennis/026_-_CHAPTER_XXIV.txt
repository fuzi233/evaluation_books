There is a saying that every household harbors a skeleton in its closet, and for the fortunate few, it is a comforting thought to assume that their wealthier neighbors carry heavier burdens of sorrow. In Clavering House, however, the "skeleton" was not a rotting bone hidden in the attic, but a living, breathing, singing entity named Blanche Amory. To the outside world, she was a radiant fairy; inside the house, she was the Nemesis, the bore, and the source of endless disquiet. Just as a single pebble in a shoe can ruin an entire journey, Blanche's presence turned the smooth path of domestic life into a minefield of annoyance. Who could have imagined that such a smiling sprite could be the architect of family discord?

One afternoon, after dinner, the Baronet sat at the billiard table with Captain Strong, puffing on a cigar—the universal confessor of male secrets.

"I say, Strong," the Baronet declared, his tone dripping with malice. "I wish to the doose your wife was dead."

"So do I," Strong replied instantly, laughing. "That's a cannon, by Jove. But she won't; she'll live forever—you see if she doesn't. Why do you wish her off the hooks, Frank?"

"Because then you might marry Missy," the Baronet drawled. "She isn't bad-looking. She'll bring ten thousand pounds, and that's a fortune for a poor old devil like you."

"And gad, Strong, I hate her worse and worse every day. I can't stand her!"

"I wouldn't take her at twice the price," Strong chuckled. "I've never seen such a little devil in my life."

"I should like to poison her," the Baronet said gravely. "By Jove, I should."

"Why, what has she done now?" Strong asked.

"Nothing particular," Sir Francis admitted with a grimace. "Just her old tricks. That girl has a knack of making everyone miserable. Last night she sent the governess sobbing away from the dinner table. Later, I heard the poor little boy howling in the dark because she'd terrified him with stories of ghosts haunting the house. At lunch, she gave my wife a turn. Though my wife is a simpleton, she's a good soul—I'm hanged if she isn't."

"What exactly did Missy do to her?" Strong inquired.

"She started talking about the late Amory, my predecessor," the Baronet explained, grinning wickedly. "She pulled a picture from *The Keepsake* and insisted it looked like her dear father. Then she demanded to know where her father's grave was. Hang her father! Whenever Miss Amory mentions him, Lady Clavering bursts into tears. The little devil does it on purpose to spite her mother. Today, when she started, I flew into a rage. I claimed to be her father, and then... well, she took a shot at me."

"And what did she say about you, Frank?" Strong laughed.

"Gad," the Baronet retorted, "she said I wasn't her father. She claimed I wasn't fit to comprehend her. She insisted her father must have been a man of genius and fine feelings, whereas I had married her mother solely for money."

"Well, didn't you?" Strong teased.

"It doesn't make it any less painful to hear because it's true, you know," Sir Francis snapped. "I'm not a literary man, but I'm not such a fool as she paints me. She always manages to put me in the hole. She turns the whole house around her with her quiet ways and those confounded sentimental airs. I wish she were dead, Ned."

"It was my wife you wanted dead just now," Strong pointed out, maintaining his perfect good humor.

Upon hearing this, the Baronet confessed with his usual candor: "Well, when people bore my life out, I do wish they were dead. And I wish Missy were down a well, with all my heart."

From this candid exchange, it becomes clear that our accomplished little friend possessed peculiarities that made her unpopular. She was a young lady of genuine genius, exquisite sensitivity, and considerable literary talent, living with relatives who couldn't comprehend her. Neither her mother nor her stepfather shared her intellectual depth; the Baronet read *Bell's Life* and the *Racing Calendar*, while Lady Clavering still wrote like a thirteen-year-old schoolgirl with a total disregard for grammar. Feeling keenly that she was surrounded by inferiors, Blanche seized every opportunity to highlight their inadequacies. She played the martyr, ensuring everyone knew she was suffering. If she cried, as she believed she must, was anyone surprised? Without sympathy, life is meaningless. Would it not be dishonest to feign cheerfulness toward those she revered? If a poetess cannot mourn her lot, what use is her lyre? Blanche struck hers only to the saddest tunes, singing elegies over dead hopes and dirges for nipped buds of affection.

Her actual troubles hadn't been severe yet, but her griefs resided entirely in her own soul. Being habitually dissatisfied, why shouldn't she weep? Tears flowed from her eyes on command; she had mastered the art of shedding them, and like the dropsy mentioned by Horace, her ability to cry increased with practice.

Blanche had begun to gush at a very young age. Lamartine was her favorite bard since infancy, followed by a voracious diet of French novels by Balzac and George Sand. By sixteen, she had devoured every romance available. Though she sympathized poorly with her human relatives, she claimed friends in the "spirit-world"—characters like the tender Indiana, the passionate Lelia, the amiable Trenmor, the convict-hero Stenio, and the fiery Djalma. She had fallen in love with fictional princes while still wearing pinafores, settling complex legal questions regarding women's rights alongside Indiana. She played at love with these imaginary lovers just as she once played at maternity with her doll. It is fascinating to watch such poetic spirits shift their affections daily; today the blue-eyed hero is favored, tomorrow the black-eyed villain is neglected until he is deemed odious, only to be dandled again the next day.

History reveals that at eleven, "Mademoiselle Betsi" (as she was then known) fell for a young Savoyard organ grinder she believed to be a displaced prince. At twelve, an old, hideous drawing master captured her heart. By thirteen, attending Madame de Caramel's boarding school near Monsieur Rogron's pension, she corresponded romantically with two students from the College of Charlemagne.

Note that we have used her Christian name, Betsy, here. While known as "Missy" at home, Blanche had chosen the name Blanche for herself. The Baronet's favorite method of discipline was threatening to call her "Betsy" in public, a tactic that occasionally kept the rebellious child in line.

Like her changing dolls, Blanche treated her friendships with similar fickleness. She maintained a treasure chest full of hair locks collected during her sentimental travels. Some friends married, others moved schools, and one beloved sister vanished from the pension only to be rediscovered working the books in her father's grocery store in the Rue du Bac. Blanche viewed these estrangements and disillusionments as the inevitable penalties of genius.

Meanwhile, she successfully tormented her mother and drove her stepfather to wish for her death. Except for Captain Strong, whose good humor was impenetrable, Blanche ruled the house with her tongue. When Lady Clavering mistook asparagus for sparrows or referred to objects as "hobjects," Blanche would calmly correct her, driving the poor woman into nervous fits.

Outside the house, the arrival of the Claverings sparked immense curiosity in the small town. Madame Fribsby, the local milliner, was naturally intrigued. She scrutinized every detail of the ladies' attire, from bonnets to brodequins, ignoring the sermon of Dr. Portman entirely. Soon, she secured an audience with Lady Clavering's housekeeper, presenting business cards claiming she supplied the latest Parisian fashions and crafted dresses for the nobility. These claims were warmly received.

Mrs. Bonner, the lady's maid, quickly became a regular at Madame Fribsby's drawing room, enjoying green tea, scandal, hot Sally-Lunn cakes, and novels. In contrast, Blanche's own maid, Pincott, was rarely allowed a holiday. Worked harder than a factory girl, Pincott spent her days and nights altering Blanche's clothes, rising early and sleeping late to satisfy her mistress's capricious desires. Pincott came from respectable English parents, including a crippled father who relied on her support. Leaving would mean adding to their misery, so she stayed.

With characteristic candor, Blanche often reminded Pincott of her difficult situation. "I should send you away, Pincott, you are too weak and your eyes are failing. But I endure you for your parents' sake," she would say. Other times, she complained that Pincott's wretched appearance gave her migraines and ordered her to wear rouge. Yet, after terrifying the girl with threats to dismiss her, she would settle down at one o'clock, ask Pincott to kiss her goodnight, and demand the pink dress be ready for the morning. Blanche considered herself a superior being, expecting to be waited upon as such.
