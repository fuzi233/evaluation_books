The dinners in Harley Street had a settled order, as regular as the striking of a well-wound clock. Mrs. Lennox provided the handsome room and the glittering appointments; her friends brought their beauty and their airy assurance; Captain Lennox, with his practised, agreeable intelligence, carried the talk lightly over politics and campaigns as if they were maps unrolled upon the table; while Mr. Henry Lennox, and the younger men who came in his train, supplied the sparkle—wit cut fine as lemon-peel, cleverness shaved to a translucent thinness, and learning worn so deftly that it never seemed a burden, only a brilliant accessory.

Margaret sat amongst them as one sits at a feast where every dish is contrived for show rather than nourishment. She had known, in her childhood, what it was to watch an old servant lift a lid and let out a wholesome steam—her mother’s broth, plain but restorative; she had known the honest country silence between spoonfuls, broken only by the small domestic sounds that make a meal feel like shelter: the clink of china, the soft scrape of a chair, the steady tick that seemed to measure safety. Here, the very talents of the company were served up as confections for admiration. Every feeling, every acquisition—nay, even every tendency towards virtue—was turned into fireworks. The hidden sacred fire that should have warmed and steadied life spent itself in sparkle and crackle, leaving no embers for solitude.

They talked of art as if it were only colour laid thick, as if the value of a picture lay solely in the sheen of its surface and the immediate sweetness to the eye. They whipped themselves into enthusiasm before witnesses, and when the room emptied and the lamps were turned down, they had no hunger left for the subject. They had squandered their capability of appreciation into a mere flow of the right phrases—fine words, like elaborate garnishes that smell of nothing and feed no one.

One evening, after the gentlemen had come up into the drawing-room, Mr. Lennox drew near to Margaret and spoke to her with something like intention—almost the first voluntary words he had offered since her return to live in his mother’s house.

“You did not look pleased at what Shirley was saying at dinner.”

“Didn’t I?” said Margaret, startled into a smile that did not soften her. “My face must be very expressive.”

“It always was. It has not lost the trick of being eloquent.”

“I did not like,” she answered hastily, “his way of advocating what he knew to be wrong—so glaringly wrong—even in jest.”

“But it was very clever,” said he, leaning upon the word as if cleverness were absolution. “How every word told! Did you remember the happy epithets?”

“Yes.”

“And despise them, you would like to add. Pray don’t scruple, though he is my friend.”

“There!” Margaret broke off, the blood rising hotly into her cheeks. “That is the exact tone in you, that—” She could not complete the sentence. It was as if the very language she must use had been taken from her and turned into something too sharp to hold. She turned away, and yet, before she had wholly withdrawn herself, she heard him speak again—low, clear, and for once unadorned.

“If my tones, or modes of thought, are what you dislike, will you do me the justice to tell me so, and so give me the chance of learning to please you.”

The words, for all their humility, carried power in them—the power of one who believes himself entitled to request a verdict, and to offer amendment upon his own terms. Margaret’s heart contracted with a distaste she could not easily translate into the neat speech this house prized. She felt, too, that the very cadence of the room—the laughter that never quite sank into silence, the continuous rustle and ring—shaped thought itself. One could hardly hear one’s own conscience in such a climate of sound.

Weeks went by, and there was no intelligence of Mr. Bell’s going to Milton. At Helstone he had spoken of it as a journey he might have to take very soon; but now it seemed he must have transacted his business by writing. Margaret had long thought—had long known—that if he could avoid going to a place he disliked, he would. She, with her private need, her secret importance attached to an explanation that could only be given face to face, understood too well how little weight her urgency must have seemed beside his aversion. He would do what he believed necessary; but whether in summer, autumn, or winter could signify very little to him.

It was now August. No mention came of the Spanish journey he had once alluded to in Edith’s hearing; and Margaret tried to school herself into accepting the fading away of that hope, as one watches a fragrance slip from a room when the door has been left open too long.

Then, one morning, a letter arrived in Mr. Bell’s hand. Next week he meant to come up to town; he wanted to see her about a plan he had in his head; and, moreover, he intended to treat himself to a little doctoring, as he had begun to come round to her opinion—that it would be pleasanter to think that his health was more in fault than his temper, when he found himself irritable and cross.

There was a forced cheerfulness in the lines—Margaret noticed it afterwards, as one recalls an odd aftertaste only when the meal is over. At the moment Edith’s exclamations filled the room.

“Coming up to town! Oh dear! and I am so worn out by the heat that I don’t believe I have strength enough in me for another dinner. Besides, everybody has left but our dear stupid selves, who can’t settle where to go to. There would be nobody to meet him.”

“I’m sure he would much rather come and dine with us quite alone than with the most agreeable strangers you could pick up,” said Margaret. “Besides, if he is not well he won’t wish for invitations. I am glad he has owned it at last. I was sure he was ill from the whole tone of his letters, and yet he would not answer me when I asked him, and I had no third person to whom I could apply for news.”

“Oh! he is not very ill, or he would not think of Spain.”

“He never mentions Spain.”

“No! but his plan that is to be proposed evidently relates to that. But would you really go in such weather as this?”

“Oh, it will get cooler every day. Yes! Think of it.” Margaret’s voice shook with a kind of shame at its own eagerness. “I am only afraid I have thought and wished too much—in that absorbing wilful way which is sure to be disappointed—or else gratified, to the letter, while in the spirit it gives no pleasure.”

“But that’s superstitious, I’m sure, Margaret.”

“No, I don’t think it is. Only it ought to warn me, and check me from giving way to such passionate wishes. It is a sort of ‘Give me children, or else I die.’ I’m afraid my cry is, ‘Let me go to Cadiz, or else I die.’”

“My dear Margaret! You’ll be persuaded to stay there; and then what shall I do? Oh! I wish I could find somebody for you to marry here, that I could be sure of you!”

“I shall never marry.”

“Nonsense, and double nonsense! Why, as Sholto says, you’re such an attraction to the house, that he knows ever so many men who will be glad to visit here next year for your sake.”

The words were lightly spoken; yet Margaret felt them like a hand laid upon her, turning her from a person into a commodity, a pleasant inducement. She drew herself up.

“Do you know, Edith, I sometimes think your Corfu life has taught you—”

“Well!”

“Just a shade or two of coarseness.”

Edith’s face crumpled; she began to sob in a frightened, childish way, declaring vehemently that Margaret had lost all love for her, that she no longer looked upon her as a friend. The sound of Edith’s crying filled the room, displacing any rational order, as loud music drives out thought. Margaret, stung by her own wounded pride and then remorseful at having struck so sharply, ended by yielding—by being Edith’s slave for the rest of the day. Edith lay like a victim on the sofa, heaving a profound sigh from time to time, until at last she fell asleep, exhausted by her own emotion and the attention it drew.

Mr. Bell did not make his appearance even on the day to which he had, for a second time, deferred his visit.

The next morning, at breakfast, a letter came from Wallis, his servant. Margaret’s hand, reaching for it, was steady; her face, as she read, turned suddenly white, as if all warmth had been withdrawn at once.

Wallis wrote that his master had not been feeling well for some time, which had been the true reason of his putting off his journey; and that at the very time when he should have set out for London, he had been seized with an apoplectic fit. It was, Wallis added, the opinion of the medical men that he could not survive the night; and more than probable that by the time Miss Hale received the letter, his poor master would be no more.

Margaret read to the end without a word. Then she placed the paper silently into Edith’s hands and left the room.

Edith, reading, gave a little cry and began to weep—sobbing, frightened, bewildered by the near approach of death as by an unfamiliar taste too strong upon the tongue. Her husband was distressed, and tried to soothe her; Mrs. Shaw, breakfasting in her own room, had to be told. It seemed to Edith, above all, monstrous that a man who was to have dined with them that day should instead be lying dead or dying. It brought the idea of mortality into her pretty, carefully arranged life like an unexpected loud sound in a delicate piece of music.

Only after a while did she remember Margaret. Then she started up and ran upstairs.

In Margaret’s room Dixon was packing a few toilette articles with quick, practised motions; Margaret, shedding tears continually, was putting on her bonnet in such haste that her hands trembled and she could scarcely tie the strings.

“Oh, dear Margaret! how shocking! What are you doing? Are you going out? Sholto would telegraph, or do anything you like.”

“I am going to Oxford,” said Margaret. The words came with the simplicity of necessity. “There is a train in half-an-hour. Dixon has offered to go with me, but I could have gone by myself. I must see him again. Besides, he may be better, and want some care. He has been like a father to me. Don’t stop me, Edith.”

“But I must. Mamma won’t like it at all. Come and ask her about it, Margaret. You don’t know where you’re going. I should not mind if he had a house of his own; but in his Fellow’s rooms! Come to mamma, and do ask her before you go. It will not take a minute.”

Margaret yielded—her habitual deference catching her at the very edge of action—and so she lost her train.

In the suddenness of the event Mrs. Shaw became bewildered and hysterical, and precious time slipped away in exclamations, in questions of what “people” would say, in fluttered directions that led nowhere. At length it was found there was another train in a couple of hours; and after various discussions on propriety and impropriety, it was decided that Captain Lennox should accompany Margaret—the one point on which she remained constant being her resolution to go, alone or otherwise, by the next train.

Her father’s friend—her own friend—was lying at the point of death. The thought came upon her with such vividness that she was surprised herself at the firmness with which she asserted, quietly but immovably, something of her right to act. And five minutes before the time of starting she found herself seated in a railway carriage opposite Captain Lennox.

The railway had its own harsh breath and metallic clamour: the shriek of the whistle, the thud and rush, the vibration that got into one’s bones. Margaret, who had so often felt the moral injury of Milton’s perpetual din, listened now with a strange patience. In such moments, noise became merely a vehicle—an indifferent force carrying her towards what she must do. Captain Lennox spoke little; his composure was of a kind that could sleep when circumstances required it.

At Oxford, the end had come. Margaret had not reached him in time for any last words. Yet it was a comfort—grave, bitter, but real—to have gone, even if only to hear that he had died in the night.

She saw the rooms he had occupied—those scholar’s chambers where books and papers lay about with the quietness of a life lived in thought—and they became for ever after entwined in her memory with her father, and with the idea of the one cherished, faithful friend who had remained when so much had been taken away. She stood and looked around as if she could take the place into herself: the sober walls, the familiar chair, the table at which he must have sat; and all the while it seemed to her that the true language of grief could not be spoken aloud in any dialect she possessed. There are experiences that refuse translation—not into another tongue only, but into any public form at all.

They had promised Edith, before starting, that if all had ended as they feared they would return to dinner. So that long, lingering look had to be interrupted; a quiet farewell had to be taken of the kind old face that had so often lighted at the sight of her, full of pleasant words, merry quips and cranks, as if cheerfulness were a dish he could always set before those he loved. Margaret bent to the stillness, and in her heart thanked God that He had spared the old man a long or painful illness.

On the journey home Captain Lennox fell asleep. The carriage rocked; the wheels beat out their relentless measure. Margaret could cry at leisure, unseen and unobserved, and let memory return in waves—Helstone’s green hush, her father’s voice, the gentler music of a life before this fatal year. No sooner did she grow fully aware of one loss than another seemed to come—not to supersede her grief, but to re-open wounds scarcely healed.

Yet when they arrived, when she heard again the tender voices of her aunt and Edith, when merry little Sholto’s glee rang out at her coming, and she saw the well-lighted rooms—Edith pretty in her paleness, eager, sorrowful, full of a softened attention—Margaret roused herself from a heavy trance of almost superstitious hopelessness. Even here, amid all that was shallow and all that was kind, joy and gladness might still gather, as warmth gathers about a hearth.

She took Edith’s place upon the sofa. Sholto was taught to carry Aunt Margaret’s cup of tea very carefully to her, his small hands intent upon the duty. The familiar domestic sounds—the teaspoon’s faint tap, the soft murmur of women’s voices, the child’s delighted exclamation—fell around her like a protecting cloth laid over a bare table. By the time she went upstairs to dress, her tears had not ceased, but her gratitude had found a place amongst them.
