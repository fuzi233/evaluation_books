If actors and actresses are like children—quick to cry over a dropped prop and just as quick to bounce back when the curtain goes up—then Sally had reason to feel both proud and slightly bewildered. She’d done something so simple, so utterly unremarkable: lent her brother Fillmore twenty thousand dollars. And yet, the world had tilted on its axis. The Primrose Way Company, previously slumped in existential despair like a bunch of wet socks left in the rain, now radiated joy like a freshly polished chandelier.

The sudden rise of the assistant stage manager—from lowly paper-knife wrangler to full-fledged show proprietor—struck them all as dramatically satisfying. It was practically Shakespearean, minus the bloodshed. “She’s been through the fire!” said one actress, wiping away a tear. “And now she reigns supreme!” (Though, to be fair, she hadn’t actually *reigned* yet—just bought a new hat.)

Meanwhile, Miss Hobson, that walking cloud of doom, had vanished into the ether, leaving behind only faint traces of vinegar and ill will. Her absence was like removing a sour lemon from a punch bowl. Suddenly, everyone felt… lighter. More buoyant. As if the universe had finally decided it liked them.

Rumors flew faster than a Broadway producer chasing a good deal. Teddy, the bowler-hatted gentleman with a suspiciously accurate ear for gossip, swore the theatre ban would end Tuesday at the latest—“straight from the lift-boy at the hotel.” The cigar-stand girl at the Pontchatrain, a woman whose word carried the weight of divine revelation among theater folk, told the butler that Toledo and Cleveland were opening tomorrow. “No joke,” she insisted. “I saw the sign.”

Hope bloomed like spring flowers after a long winter. The sun, they agreed, was breaking through. Fate, it seemed, had given up trying to keep decent people down. Why bother? They always came back up anyway.

Fillmore, once a man who looked like he’d been sculpted from leftover cardboard and desperation, was now positively *glowing*. His mode of celebrating success? A waistcoat so shiny it could start a fire, a hundred half-dollar cigars (which he promptly smoked while reading the morning paper), and an intense preoccupation with lunch options. “Not today,” he’d say, waving off the menu. “We’re having steak. No, wait—chicken. No, beef. I’ll decide later.” He’d grown at least six pounds in one day—possibly from sheer optimism, possibly because he’d stopped dieting out of shame.

Now, standing on the sidewalk in front of the theatre, gazing at the posters that declared, *FILLMORE NICHOLAS PRESENTS*, he was so large that pedestrians had to form a detour around him. “It’s not me,” he’d insist. “It’s the energy of the moment.” But the truth was, he’d become a man shaped by triumph—a soft, billowy monument to financial salvation.

And yet, amid all this jubilation, Sally couldn’t help feeling… incomplete. Like a perfectly tuned piano with one key missing. She’d made it all happen. She was the fairy godmother, the silent benefactor, the invisible hand that turned disaster into destiny. And still, she wasn’t happy.

It wasn’t until she was driving with Gerald across Belle Isle—one of those rare afternoons where the air smelled like grass and regret—that the answer came crashing in like a dropped cue card.

Gerald, freed from Miss Hobson’s oppressive shadow, was radiant. His usual moodiness had vanished. He laughed easily, spoke with conviction, and even offered her a cigar without hesitation (which she politely declined). He talked about the future—big plans, grand gestures, the inevitable fame that would follow his play’s success.

“If this thing gets over,” he declared, jaw set like a statue of determination, “I’ll show ’em! One success—that’s all I need—and then watch me go!”

Sally listened, but her mind wandered. The sun had slipped behind the trees. A cold wind blew up from the river, carrying with it the scent of damp earth and inevitability.

And then—like a spotlight turning on—it hit her.

Men didn’t love life. They competed in it.

Success wasn’t just a goal for them; it was their religion. Their identity. Their entire reason for breathing. For Gerald, for Fillmore, for every man she knew, life was a race. Win or lose, but *win*. And anything else—love, laughter, quiet evenings—was just scenery along the way.

But she wasn’t scenery. She was *everything*.

To her, Gerald wasn’t a project. He wasn’t a stepping stone to greatness. He was simply *him*. Without the trappings of fame, without the applause, without even a single dollar to his name—he was enough. And that was the crux of it: she was never going to be enough for him.

A wave of jealousy—futile, sharp, and strangely tender—washed over her. She shivered.

“Cold?” asked Gerald, glancing over. “I’ll tell the driver to turn back.”

“No,” she said, staring ahead at the grey sky. “Just… think.”

“I don’t see any reason why this play shouldn’t run a year in New York,” he continued, undeterred. “Everybody says it’s good. If it does get over, they’ll all be after me. I…”

She didn’t hear the rest.

Because the world outside had turned bleak. The sky was leaden. The wind howled like a disappointed critic. And Sally realized, with a quiet sorrow, that no amount of success could ever make her feel like the prize. She was the prize *before* the game began.

And that, perhaps, was the saddest kind of victory of all.
