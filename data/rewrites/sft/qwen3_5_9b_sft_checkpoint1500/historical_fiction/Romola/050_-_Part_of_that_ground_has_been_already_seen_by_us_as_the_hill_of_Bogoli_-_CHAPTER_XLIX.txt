The winter days slipped past Romola like white ships drifting silently along a distant shore—each bearing a hidden cargo of impending change, indistinguishable from the last in their monotony. Tito's whisper had infused her curiosity about public affairs with a chilling dread, leading her to prefer ignorance over knowledge. Though the menacing German Emperor had vanished and Florence's immediate plight seemed eased, a profound distress lingered. Consequently, Romola's active duties showed little diminution; in fact, they served as her sole sanctuary from a mind plagued by uncertainty.

She could not allow herself to celebrate the relief that had arrived in extremity, nor the apparent vindication it brought to the Friar's faction. Francesco Valori, the fiery leader of the *Piagnoni*, had been elected Gonfaloniere at the start of the year and was already rushing to enact his radical reforms within his brief two-month tenure. On the surface, this looked like a fortification of liberty and a shield for Savonarola. Yet, Romola's intuition, sharpened by fear, sensed that this triumph was merely an unconscious incubation of mixed germs destined to bloom tragically. By Carnival time, shortly after mid-February, her premonition crystallized into reality: the Medici ceased their passivity and began actively maneuvering for the election of Bernardo del Nero as the new Gonfaloniere.

On the final morning of the Carnival, between ten and eleven, Romola ventured forth as promised toward the Corso degli Albizzi to retrieve her cousin Brigida. Together, they intended to depart from the Via de' Bardi in the afternoon, securing a vantage point in the Piazza della Signoria reserved by Tito. They awaited a spectacle of unprecedented novelty, one that demanded the gaze of every Florentine eye. The *Piagnoni* were determined to dictate the spirit of the holiday. Despite the futile efforts of Dolfo Spini and his associates to resurrect traditional masques and pranks seasoned with indecency, the city declared Christ King, rendering such frivolities impossible.

Romola moved with a languid air, the mindset of an adult accompanying a cherished child on a tour of sights. While the day marked an epoch in carnival history, this phase of reform failed to ignite her enthusiasm. She remained unaware that it was simultaneously an epoch in her own life, wherein another destiny was visibly weaving itself into hers, no longer secret but manifest.

Choosing to traverse the vast Piazza alone first, she took in the panorama. Dominating the square stood a monstrous, multi-colored edifice resembling a colossal fir-tree or pyramid, sixty feet tall. Its shelves widened toward the base until they circled eighty yards. The square teemed with life: slender youths clad in white garments, olive wreaths upon their brows, drifted near the base carrying baskets brimming with vibrant objects. Beside them, mature figures—some in monastic robes, others in the loose tunics and crimson caps of artists—assisted, inspected, or retreated to observe the wondrous assembly from afar. A notable cluster, including Piero di Cosimo, stood on the marble steps of Orgagna's Loggia, separated from the scene by an aura of disdain.

As Romola approached, she scrutinized the myriad items arranged in ascending order from the base to the summit. There were tapestries and brocades of scandalous design, paintings and sculptures deemed liable to incite vice. Boards and tables for divers games lay alongside printing blocks, dice, and gaming paraphernalia. Worldly music scores and instruments of every variety—lutes, drums, cymbals, trumpets—were piled together. Masks and costumes from former carnivals mingled with handsome editions of Ovid, Boccaccio, Petrarch, and Pulci, works of vanity and impurity. Implements of female coquetry abounded: pots of rouge, artificial hair, mirrors, perfumes, powders, and sheer veils designed to invite leering glances. At the apex sat an unflattering likeness of a mythical Venetian merchant, rumored to have paid heavily for this collection of "marketable abominations." Soaring above him in grotesque ugliness was the symbolic figure of the ancient, depraved Carnival.

This arrangement prepared for a novel kind of bonfire: the Burning of Vanities. Concealed within the pyramid's core was a bountiful supply of dry fuel and gunpowder. On this final evening, the pile was to be ignited to the sound of trumpets, sending the ugly visage of old Carnival tumbling into the flames amidst chants of reformed triumph.

Such a grand gesture could scarcely have been orchestrated without a unique organization initiated by Savonarola two years prior. The mass of Florentine youth was no longer abandoned to their natural inclinations toward mischief and crude dissipation. Trained under Fra Domenico, Savonarola's lieutenant, these lads and striplings—the hope of Florence—were to speak only pure words. Their zeal for an unseen good was to eclipse the tepidity of their elders, knowing no pleasure but that of an angelic nature: singing divine praises and walking in white robes. These fresh-faced troops were the architects of the regenerated merriment, a sacred parody of the old festivals. Where once bonfires consumed earthly delights, now a fire would consume impurity. Where symbolic processions once paraded worldly vanity, new processions displayed white robes, red crosses, and olive wreaths—emblems of peace and innocent joy. Instead of dancing rings beneath open skies accompanied by loose songs, there would be circles of monks and laity united in divine joy, singing hymns. Collections from the streets, formerly for superfluous feasts, were now gathered to feed the hungry, alongside the gathering of the *Anathema*: the vanities destined for the great pyramidal bonfire.

Regiments of beardless inquisitors scoured the houses, urging the surrender of the *Anathema*. Perhaps, after yielding obvious sins, the Madonna of the household still clutched small red spheres imported from the Levant, meant to fake a sudden bloom upon a sallow cheek? If so, she was to cast them into the basket of doom. Or perhaps she wore ringlets and coils of "dead hair"? Then she must bring them to the street door, holding them in her hands, not upon her head, to publicly renounce the *Anathema* that masked respectable aging with a ghastly mimicry of youth. In return, she would hear fresh, youthful voices bestow a blessing upon her home.

These inquisitors, organized into tiny battalions, likely embraced their work with eagerness. Coercing the populace through shame or spiritual peltage into relinquishing cherished possessions is a form of piety readily adopted by the boyish mind. Even if some stubborn sinners threatened whips or cudgels, such defiance added to the excitement. Savonarola himself seemed acutely aware of the difficulties inherent in training these children, weighing the noble yearning for great ends against an imperfect grasp of means, forcing a reliance on supernatural constraint as the only secure hope. Florentine youth had harbored vile habits and foul tongues; initially, hearing them shout "*Viva Gesù!*" seemed an unmixed blessing. Yet, Savonarola eventually confessed from the pulpit that there was "too much shouting" of the sacred name, lest it fall into contempt, ordering a cessation until the next feast.

Nevertheless, as the long stream of robed youthfulness, adorned with little red crosses and olive garlands, had proceeded to the Duomo at dawn to receive communion from Savonarola's hands, it presented a vision of beauty. Doubtless, many of those young souls were storing memories of hope and awe that would protect them from viewing their civic labor as mere vulgarity. Conscious obedience, after all, represents an advance upon lawlessness, and these boys became the generation of men who fought and endured greatly in the Republic's final struggle. Now, in the interim hours between communion and dinner, they were making their final rounds to collect alms and vanities, explaining why Romola observed those slim, white-clad figures circling the base of the great pyramid.

"What think you of this folly, Madonna Romola?" a brusque voice murmured close to her ear. "Your *Piagnoni* will render hell a pleasant prospect for us if they are permitted to rule the earth as they please. It takes a cudgel dragged from the mountains to witness painters like Lorenzo di Credi and young Baccio aiding in the burning of color from life in such a manner."

"My good Piero," Romola replied, lifting her gaze to smile at the grim man, "even you must find solace in seeing these trifles consumed. Observe those gewgaws, wigs, and pots of rouge: I have heard you speak with as much indignation against them as Fra Girolamo himself."

"What then?" Piero snapped, turning sharply upon her. "I never claimed a woman ought to paint a black patch against the background. *Va!* Madonna Antigone, it is shameful for a woman of your hair and shoulders to indulge in such nonsense—leave it to women unworthy of being painted. What! Has the Most Holy Virgin herself not always been attired with care? That is the doctrine of the Church! Talk of heresy, indeed! And I ask you, what would the excellent Messer Bardo have said regarding the burning of divine poets by these friars, who are no better imitation of men than onions with their bulbs turned upward? Look at that Petrarch sticking beside a pot of rouge: do these fools presume that heavenly Laura was a painted harlot? And Boccaccio?
