Since her son fell ill, Mrs. Pendennis had effectively seized command of the household, turning the young man's private quarters into a fortress of maternal oversight. She controlled access to his drawers, closets, and every scrap of clothing within them. Whether it was shirts missing buttons, stockings needing repair, or—must we admit it?—letters buried among the laundry that demanded answers while Arthur was too weak to handle them, everything flowed through her hands.

Mrs. Pendennis was driven by a mix of genuine concern and a desperate need to solve the "Fanny Bolton Mystery," a topic she had never explicitly broached with her son but which haunted her thoughts with terrifying clarity. To protect her patient from the shock of the outside world, she ordered the brass knocker removed from the inner door. Now, when the postman's jarring double knock echoed through the house, it served only to startle the invalid further. No letter reached Arthur's eyes—not from bootmakers demanding payment, nor from hatters threatening to withhold hats until his accounts were settled next Saturday.

These documents, however, did not vanish entirely. Pen, ever generous and careless with his finances, inevitably received his fair share of debt notices. Though not overwhelming, they were sufficient to terrify his scrupulous mother. Having saved diligently through her own frugality and Pen's surprising self-denial, Mrs. Pendennis had set aside a modest sum specifically to clear her son's debts. Many a respectable young man would happily surrender his mail to his parents under similar circumstances; indeed, the willingness to face the postman is often the truest test of a man's integrity. Blessed is the ear that delights in the *rat-tat* of delivery! The virtuous welcome it; the guilty tremble at its sound. Thus, Mrs. Pendennis performed a double kindness by sparing Pen both the noise and the necessity of replying during his sickness.

A thorough search of Arthur's wardrobe yielded no incriminating evidence regarding Fanny Bolton. Finding nothing, the widow turned to her brother-in-law, Major Pendennis, for guidance. One sunny afternoon at Richmond, while Pen and Warrington lounged on a terrace bench, the Major held court with Helen. She poured out her terrors, though not entirely honestly. Much like a spendthrift hiding ledgers or a fashionable wife concealing dressmaker's bills, she withheld the full extent of her anxieties, offering only a curated selection of her fears to her temporary director.

"Major," she pleaded, "what course should I take regarding this dreadful, horrid affair? Do you know anything?"

The Major puckered his face, his expression unreadable, casting a sidelong glance at her before dropping his eyes to the carpet. "My dear, good creature," he murmured, "I know nothing of it, and I prefer not to know. In my humble opinion, you had best remain ignorant as well. Young men will be young men. Begad, if you suspect our boy is a 'Jo—'"

"Pray, spare me!" Helen interjected, standing tall and dignified.

"My dear creature, I did not initiate this conversation," the Major replied with a bland bow.

"I cannot bear to hear such sins spoken of lightly!" the widow cried, tears pricking her eyes. "To think my boy committed such a crime! I wish he had died before doing it. How do I survive this? It breaks my heart, Major, to imagine his father's son—my child, whom I remember as so honorable—has fallen so dreadfully low, as to..."

"As to flirting with a little grisette?" the Major suggested gently. "Egad, if every mother in England broke her heart over such trifles—" He paused, seeing her distress. "Nay, do not agitate yourself. I cannot bear a woman's tears. But how do we know anything is serious? Has Arthur said anything?"

"His silence confirms it," she sobbed, dabbing her eyes with a pocket handkerchief.

"Not necessarily. There are subjects a young man simply cannot discuss with his mother," the Major insinuated smoothly.

"She has written to him!" the lady gasped, clutching her cambric veil.

"What, before he fell ill? Nothing more likely."

"No, since," the mourner, wearing a mask of grief, stammered. "Not before... that is, I don't think so... I—"

"Only since. And you have intercepted it, haven't you? Since he was too ill to read his own mail, you took charge, did you not?"

"I am the most unhappy mother in the world!" Helen wailed.

"The most unhappy mother in the world because your son is a man and not a hermit!" the Major retorted sharply. "Be careful, sister. If you suppress letters, you may cause a rift between you and Arthur that you will regret forever—a difference far more significant than the trivial cause that sparked it."

"There was only one letter," Helen insisted, her voice trembling. "Just a few words. Here it is—Oh, how can you speak so cruelly?"

At the mention of "only a little one," the Major found himself unable to speak, fighting back laughter despite his pity for the grieving woman. Each viewed the situation through their own moral lens; the Major's morality, as the reader knows, was less rigid than that of an ascetic.

"I advise you," he said gravely, "to seal it up—perhaps with a wafer—and return it to his pile of correspondence. Let him open it when he is well. Or, if we cannot seal it properly, mistake it for a bill."

"I cannot lie to my son," the widow declared firmly.

The letter had been slipped into the box two days prior to their departure from the Temple and delivered to Helen by Martha. Unfamiliar with Fanny's handwriting, Helen recognized the author instantly upon holding the paper. She had been waiting daily for this specific missive, opening other envelopes just to reach it. The offending note currently poisoned her bag. She extracted it and handed it to her brother-in-law.

"Arthur Pendennis, Esq.," the Major read aloud in a shaky, sprawling script, a sneer playing on his lips. "No, my dear, I refuse to read further. But you, having read it, tell me its contents. Prayers for his health in atrocious spelling, and a desire to see him? Well, there is no harm in that. And since you ask for information—" Here the Major adopted a demure expression, feigning ignorance. "As you inquire, my dear, I must inform you that Morgan, my servant, has investigated this affair. Furthermore, Doctor Goodenough looked into it. It appears this young lady was deeply infatuated with Arthur. Morgan learned from an old acquaintance—an Irish gentleman, formerly close to Pen—that she paid for her own entertainment and took her to Vauxhall Gardens. The girl's father, a violent drunkard, beats her mother, who insists on her daughter's innocence to her husband, yet simultaneously told Goodenough that Arthur treated her child like a brute. The story remains a mystery. Shall I clear it up? I need only ask Pen, and he will confirm immediately; he is as honorable a man as ever lived."

"Honorable!" the widow spat with bitter scorn. "Brother, what is this you call honor? If my boy is guilty, he must marry her. I would kneel and beg him to do so."

"Good God! Are you mad?" the Major shrieked. Suddenly, the truth struck him: if Helen prayed for this match, Arthur would undoubtedly comply. He was wild and obstinate enough to commit any folly for a woman he adored. "My dear sister, have you lost your senses?" he asked after a brief, agitated pause. Then, softening his tone, "What right do we have to assume anything passed between them? Let us see the letter. Her heart is breaking. Poor little Fanny—spelled, as you say, with a lack of decorum. But good heavens, what is there here? Only that the little devil continues to make love to him. She didn't enter his chambers until he was delirious and unaware of her presence. Flanagan, the laundress, told Morgan so. She arrived with an old gentleman, Mr. Bows, who kindly brought me away from Stillbrook. By the way, I left him in a cab and never paid the fare; he was very kind. No, there is nothing in this story."

"Do you really think so? Thank Heaven! Thank God!" Helen exclaimed. "I will take the letter to Arthur now. Look at him on the terrace with Mr. Warrington. Talking to children. My boy was always fond of children. He is innocent, thank God! Let me go to him."

Old Pendennis, however, harbored his own doubts. Just moments ago, he had championed the "not guilty" verdict, but privately, he may have judged Arthur by his own standards. If Helen confronted Arthur and he spoke the truth as a rogue usually does, the whole scheme would collapse. He made one final attempt.

"My dear, good soul," he said, seizing Helen's hand and kissing it. "Since your son has not informed you of this affair, consider if you have the right to investigate it. Believing him to be honorable, what right do you have to doubt his honor here? Who is the accuser? An anonymous scoundrel bringing no specific charges.
