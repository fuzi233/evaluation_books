The last day came round upon Helstone as if it had been folded, long ago, into the year’s turning like a pressed leaf—foreseen, yet shocking when at last it lay in the hand.

The parsonage was choked with packing-cases. Men’s boots thudded in and out; straw, shaken loose from hampers, drifted and lodged upon the little lawn as if the house were already shedding itself. Doors were left gaping; windows stood bare of curtains; and the rooms, emptied of their familiar weight, gave back every sound with a hollow reply—boards answering heels, walls answering voices—so that even a spoken word seemed to come from a farther place than the mouth that uttered it. There was the rasp of cord drawn tight round wood, the dull knock of a lid being nailed down, the cart’s rumble at the front, and beyond all that, a country quiet so deep it made those few noises feel intrusive.

Mrs. Hale’s dressing-room was saved for the end, as if delay could keep one chamber faithful. There, she and Dixon moved more like mourners than like women at work. A drawer slid out; linen, sweet with long-locked lavender, breathed up its old, domestic comfort. Each time a small relic rose from the folds—a child’s ribbon, a tiny shoe, some broken toy put away because it could not bear to be thrown—hands paused, and the past came back with such tenderness that the present job of tying parcels became impossible for minutes at a time.

Downstairs Margaret stood where she could see and be seen, calm in her very stillness, receiving questions, directing, judging what must go first and what might stay. She watched the men as she watched everything—exactly, gravely, as if her eyes could keep order in a world that was breaking into pieces. The cook and Charlotte cried as they went about their tasks, and between tears wondered at her. They had settled, with the quick decision of those who feel themselves lesser in such matters, that the young lady must not care as they did; London had blunted her, they thought, or else she was proud.

They did not know that her heart was aching under a pressure that made breathing feel like work; that she had learned, in one long day, to keep pain from mastering her by mastering the smallest necessities of the hour. If she yielded, who would act? Her father had his papers, his registers, his books—things no one else could pack without offending his habits of mind. Strange men were in the house; even friends of the kitchen were witnesses; and Margaret was not one to break down where she must also command.

At last the four hired helpers disappeared to the kitchen for tea, their voices muffled at once by distance and walls. Margaret, stiff from standing, moved away through the dismantled drawing-room, where the light, unsoftened by curtain, lay raw upon stripped spaces. She went out into the garden.

An early November dusk had come, with a soft dull mist that did not hide the world so much as change it, washing every hedge and trunk with a lilac-grey and making the last golds of the leaves glow like embers. A robin sang—thin, distinct, as if the little throat were offering its own brave defiance to departure. Margaret thought, with a pang, that it might be the very robin her father had spoken of as his winter favourite, for which he had made, with his own hands, a small shelter by the study-window. The orchard wall, trained with pear-trees, held a faint scent—damp bark, bruised leaf, earth turned cold—while overhead the branches still wore their splendid dying. The first hard frost would take it all down. Even now, one or two leaves came loosening with a slow, unhurried fall, amber and gold in the last slant of sun.

She went along the walk under the pear-tree wall, a path she had not trodden since she paced it with Henry Lennox. Here, near a bed of thyme—still pungent if you brushed it with your shoe—he had begun to speak of what she must not think of now. She remembered how, as she tried to answer, her eyes had caught at a late rose, and at the delicate, feathery carrot-leaves in the kitchen-bed as if they were brighter truths than speech. Only a fortnight. Yet the days in between had stretched like years.

Where was he now? In London—moving through his circles, dining with his Harley Street acquaintance or laughing with younger friends. She could picture him shutting away his law-books after a day’s satisfied toil, then running out to the Temple Gardens, as he had told her he did, letting the city’s great inarticulate roar beat upon him like surf—thousands of men unseen, near at hand, all intent on appointments and arrivals—while at a turn he might catch a glimpse of lamps rising out of the river’s dark.

Here there was no such roar. The robin’s song ceased, as if it had flown into the great stillness. Now and then, very far off, a cottage door opened and shut with a wooden clap to admit a labourer; the sound travelled thinly, diminished by distance, like a memory half-swallowed. Beyond the garden, from the forest floor, came a stealthy creeping crunch among crisp fallen leaves—so close it seemed within the hedgerow itself.

Margaret knew that sound. More than once in the autumn, sitting up in her room with the candle put out, she had watched the poachers’ noiseless leap over the garden fence, their quick tread across the lawn silvered by dew and moon, and their vanish into black shadow under the trees. Their life—wild, risky, unowned—had had a strange charm for her then; she had wished them success, and felt no fear. But to-night something in her blood changed the rustle into menace. The house, with its open doors and stripped windows, felt less like a home than like a shell.

She heard Charlotte inside, shutting up for the night, fastening latches, drawing bolts—unaware anyone had gone out. Then, in the nearest part of the wood, a small branch fell with a heavy crack—rotten perhaps, or forced. Margaret ran, swift as sudden terror makes a body swift, to the drawing-room window and rapped with trembling haste.

“Let me in! Let me in! It is only me, Charlotte!”

Charlotte started, and in a moment Margaret was inside again, the window secured, the familiar walls closing round her, shutting out the dark and the sound, shutting in her shaken breathing. She sat down upon a packing-case. The room was cheerless and chill; there was no fire, no friendly lamp—only Charlotte’s long candle, unsnuffed, making a wavering, greasy light.

Charlotte looked at her in surprise. Margaret, feeling the look more than meeting it, rose and tried to smile.

“I was afraid you were shutting me out altogether, Charlotte,” she said, half-playfully. “And then you would never have heard me in the kitchen, and the doors into the lane and churchyard are locked long ago.”

“Oh, miss, I should have been sure to have missed you soon. The men would have wanted you to tell them how to go on. And I have put tea in master’s study, as being the most comfortable room, so to speak.”

“Thank you, Charlotte. You are a kind girl. I shall be sorry to leave you. You must try and write to me, if I can ever give you any little help or good advice. I shall always be glad to get a letter from Helstone, you know. I shall be sure and send you my address when I know it.”

The study was ready. A good blaze leaped and settled on the hearth, and the fire’s sound—little snaps and sighs—was as comforting as warmth itself after the garden’s muffled hush. Candles stood unlighted on the table. Tea waited, breathing up that homely smell of comfort—hot leaf, milk, a faint sweetness—one of those domestic scents that seem to promise continuity even when everything else is being taken away.

Margaret sat on the rug close to the grate, partly to warm herself, for damp clung to her dress and over-fatigue had turned her cold to the bone. She clasped her hands about her knees to steady herself. Her head drooped; the posture spoke despondency even while her will kept watch.

When she heard her father’s step on the gravel outside, she sprang up. She pushed her heavy black hair back with hurried fingers, wiped away tears that had come unbidden, and went to open the door before he could touch the latch.

He came in looking older than the morning had left him. His face had the strained gentleness of one who has been doing violence to himself. He scarcely answered her efforts at cheerful talk, and when tea was poured he did not touch it.

“Have you been a very long walk to-day?” she asked at last, watching his refusal with quiet concern.

“As far as Fordham Beeches. I went to see Widow Maltby; she is sadly grieved at not having wished you good-bye. She says little Susan has kept watch down the lane for days past.—Nay, Margaret, what is the matter, dear?”

Little Susan—standing, day after day, in childish faithfulness, looking down the lane for a face that did not come—was the last drop. It was not forgetfulness, not coldness, but sheer imprisonment in duties that had kept Margaret from going; yet the thought of the child’s repeated disappointment struck her with a sharpness she could not bear. Tears broke through at once. She sobbed as if something in her chest had cracked.

Mr. Hale was helpless before it. He rose and walked to and fro, his hands moving nervously, his distress making him restless like a man hunted. Margaret tried to stop, ashamed to add to his pain, but her body would not obey her pride. She heard him speak as if to himself, his voice low and broken.

“I cannot bear it. I cannot bear to see the sufferings of others. I think I could go through my own with patience. Oh, is there no going back?”

“No, father,” said Margaret at last, forcing steadiness into her voice, looking straight at him. “It is bad to believe you in error. It would be infinitely worse to have known you a hypocrite.”

She lowered her tone on the last words, as if even imagining hypocrisy beside his name were irreverent. Then, as tears threatened again, she added more gently, as if she could lay a cloth over the rawest part of the wound.

“Besides, it is only that I am tired to-night; don’t think that I am suffering from what you have done, dear papa. We can’t either of us talk about it to-night, I believe. I had better go and take mamma up this cup of tea. She had hers very early, when I was too busy to go to her, and I am sure she will be glad of another now.”

The morning that followed did not belong to Helstone. It belonged to time-tables.

Railroad time—inexorable, indifferent—wrenched them away. They saw the last of the long, low parsonage, half-covered with China-roses and pyracanthus, homelike beyond endurance in the clear morning sun that glittered upon its windows—each window a remembered room, each pane a witness. Almost before they had settled into the car sent from Southampton to fetch them, they were moving, and Helstone slid backwards into the past.

Margaret’s heart stung to look out for one last glimpse of the old church tower, that at one turn showed itself above a wave of forest trees. She strained for it—then saw her father’s face, and remembered it was his tower more than hers. She silently yielded him the window. She leaned back, shut her eyes, and tears welled and hung for an instant on her lashes, glittering, before they rolled down and dropped upon her dress unnoticed.

They were to stop in London for the night, at a quiet hotel. Mrs. Hale cried in her languid, persistent way nearly all day, as if tears were the only action left to her. Dixon showed sorrow by sharpness: a continual irritable guarding of her mistress’s petticoats from even brushing against Mr. Hale, whom she held, in her mind, to be the cause and author of all this breaking-up.

London received them with its own music—wheels on stone, calls and footsteps, the dense overlapping of voices, a ceaseless clatter that pressed upon the ears and nerves. To those who had come out of Helstone’s great spaces of hush, the sound felt like a storm made of iron and breath. They drove through streets Margaret knew by heart, past houses once familiar, past shops where she had stood impatient by her aunt’s side while some purchase was debated into eternity. They passed acquaintances in the street; for though the morning had been so long to them that it seemed it ought to have ended in darkness and rest, it was the busiest time of a London November afternoon when they arrived.

Mrs. Hale, long absent, roused herself almost like a child at a fair, looking out with startled eagerness.

“Oh, there’s Harrison’s, where I bought so many of my wedding things. Dear! how altered! They’ve got immense plate-glass windows, larger than Crawford’s in Southampton. Oh, and there, I declare—no, it is not—yes, it is—Margaret, we have just passed Mr. Henry Lennox. Where can he be going, among all these shops?”

Margaret started forward, and as quickly fell back, half-smiling at her own involuntary movement. They were a hundred yards away by then, and he was gone among the crowd; but the mere sight—or thought—of him came like a fragment of Helstone transported into the London din, associated with a bright morning and an eventful day. She would have liked to see him without his seeing her; to keep the past intact, not forced into speech under the eyes of strangers.

The evening, with nothing to do, dragged heavily in a high hotel room. Mr. Hale went out to his bookseller’s and to call upon one or two friends; the women remained with their weariness and the muffled city-noise below. Everyone they saw in passages and stairways, in street and doorway, hurried as if drawn by an appointment—expected, or expecting. They alone seemed without place, without claim, without the thread that binds a life into other lives.

And yet Margaret knew—within a mile of where they sat—house after house where she, for her own sake, and her mother, for Aunt Shaw’s, would be welcomed if they came in gladness, or even in peace. But to come sorrowing, asking sympathy for trouble that could not be neatly named or easily shared—this would make them a shadow at the threshold. In London there is too much motion, too much noise, too much perpetual exchange, to admit readily even one hour of that deep, reverent silence with which the friends of Job sat down beside grief too great for speech.
