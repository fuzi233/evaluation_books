The morning after their small dinner party, Shirley leaned against the oak windowsill of the breakfast parlour, gushing to Caroline over how perfectly the evening had unfolded. "I do adore entertaining a room of gentlemen, don't you?" she laughed, sunlight gilding the ends of her dark curls. "It's so delightful to watch them light up over a properly roasted joint or a well-aged bottle of wine. For us, all those fancy dishes and rare vintages are little more than nice treats—but men carry that same sweet, childlike naivete about good food they never quite outgrow, don't they? And it's such a joy to please them, when they carry themselves with that gentle, decent decorum our good rectors do. I've been watching Robert Moore, though, trying to work out what small, trivial little thing might make him smile. He doesn't have that easy softness, that child's openness, at all. Have you ever found his weak spot, Caroline? You've spent far more time in his company than I have."

Caroline flushed, a shy, warm little thrill curling in her chest at the sound of his name—the subject she never dared bring up unprompted, but that she could talk about for hours if given leave. "It's certainly not the same as what charms my uncle or Dr. Boultby," she smiled back, her fingers plucking at the edge of her linen apron. "But I truly don't know what it is, either. Every time I try to watch him, to puzzle him out, I realize all at once he's already watching me, his eyes fixed right on my face."

"There you go!" Shirley exclaimed, clapping her hands. "He never lets his guard down, does he? He won't give you an inch of advantage. Even when he's not looking at you, you can feel his thoughts tracing yours, following every word you say, every move you make, picking apart your motives like he's sorting through spools of thread at his mill. Oh, that sort of character piques my curiosity to no end. Does it do the same for you?"

The question came so sharp and sudden Caroline almost floundered, but she'd long since learned to parry Shirley's quick, unexpected prods. "Pique you? What about him piques you, exactly?"

Shirley never got the chance to answer. Her head snapped toward the drive, and she jumped to her feet so fast her chair scraped across the floor. "Diversion! Right on cue. I never told you about the ridiculous conquest I've made at those parties you always refuse to come to with me, did I? I didn't even try for it, it just happened! There's the bell—wait, there are two of them. Do these curates never hunt for heiresses except in pairs? You get first pick of the pair, Lina. I'm feeling generous today. Listen to Tartar!"

The big tawny bulldog they both loved so much boomed out a bark from the hall, deep and resonant enough to shake the plaster dust off the rafters, followed by a growl so low and menacing it rumbled like distant thunder.

"Listen to him!" Shirley cackled, doubling over with laughter. "They'll think he's about to tear them to shreds. They don't know our old Tartar—all sound and fury, no bite at all, unless someone strikes him first."

Sure enough, the next sounds they heard were a high, imperious voice barking "Down, sir, down!" followed by the sharp crack of a cane hitting fur, then a yelp from the dog, a scuffle, a full-blown tumult.

Shirley bolted for the hall at once. One of the curates was already sprinting up the polished oak stairs, scrambling for the safety of the gallery bedrooms, the other backing away from Tartar, wildly waving a gnarled walking stick and yelling "Down! Down! Down!" at the top of his lungs, while a crowd of bewildered servants poured out of the kitchen. The dog lunged, and the second man turned tail and ran after his friend. The first curate slammed the bedroom door shut behind him, latching it tight, and the second threw his weight against the door screaming to be let in—terror was far less merciful than any dog, it seemed.

"Gentlemen," Shirley called out, her silver voice ringing clear over the chaos, sharp and amused, "pray do not break my locks. Calm yourselves, come down at once. Look at Tartar—he wouldn't hurt a stray cat, I promise."

She knelt to wrap her arms around the dog's thick neck, and he settled at her feet immediately, paws stretched out, tail still thumping a little in irritation, his small bulldog eyes glowering in the direction of the staircase. He was a good, honest, stubborn old thing, loved only Shirley and the man who fed him, didn't care for anyone else at all—until someone hit him, that was, and then a demon came right out.

"Mr. Malone, how do you do?" Shirley called up to the gallery, laughing up at the red-faced man leaning over the balustrade. "That corridor doesn't lead to our oak parlour, that's Mrs. Pryor's apartment. Do ask your friend Mr. Donne to vacate that bedroom, won't you? I should be delighted to receive you both downstairs."

Malone laughed, a hollow, embarrassed rattle, and started making his stately way down the stairs—only to slip on the polished oak, and catch himself on the banisters so hard the whole structure creaked. Tartar rumbled again, low in his throat, but Malone didn't flinch. He stalked past the dog, his face dark with suppressed rage, and shoved into the parlour ahead of Shirley, barely even nodding at Caroline, glowering like he wanted to clutch both ladies by the shoulders and shake them. But Shirley took pity on him, stopped laughing, and Caroline, too, was far too much of a lady to snicker at a man who was that mortified. She sent the servants away, had Tartar taken off to his kennel to calm down, and spoke to him with that soft, warm tone she had that could soothe even a raging bull. Malone was sharp enough to realize he couldn't very well challenge a woman over her own dog, so he forced himself to be polite, and before long he was back to his usual self, determined to be as charming as possible—come in like a lion, go out like a lamb, as the saying went.

He refused the soft armchair near the fire Shirley offered him, refused the spot on the sofa beside Caroline, and dragged a hard chair right up next to the door, where he could make a quick escape if another dog emergency cropped up. He sighed dramatically after every sentence he spoke, sighed in the pauses, sighed even before he opened his mouth to talk, until finally he pulled out a big red-and-yellow silk handkerchief to keep his hands busy. He folded it corner to corner, flapped it open, folded it again into a narrow band—and then, to both ladies' utter astonishment, he tied it tight around his own crossed ankles, winding it back and forth until his legs were bound together. He even did it twice, clearly very proud of his little trick. Shirley had to turn all the way to the window to muffle her silent, shaking laughter, and Caroline turned her head aside, letting her long curls hide the grin spreading across her cheeks. It was so obvious what he was doing, too—he'd stopped even pretending to court Caroline, who he thought only had a tiny five thousand pound inheritance, and had set all his sights straight on Shirley and her great estate at Fieldhead, no subtlety at all.

It took their manservant John nearly twenty minutes to talk Mr. Donne out of the locked bedroom, and when he finally wandered into the parlour, he wasn't even the slightest bit ashamed. Cold, phlegmatic, infinitely pleased with himself, he didn't have a scrap of modesty in his whole body, no fire in his blood at all. He fully believed all he had to do was visit Shirley a few more times, write her a formal marriage proposal, and she'd leap at the chance to marry a curate, be mistress of Fieldhead, wait on him hand and foot. He spoke to her in an injured, haughty tone the second he walked in. "That's a very dangerous dog you keep, Miss Keeldar. I can't imagine why you'd own such a brute. Ugly thing, just a common carter's hound. You ought to hang him, and get yourself a nice little poodle instead—ladies all love lapdogs, everyone knows that."

Caroline almost gasped, but Shirley only smiled, sharp and sweet. "Everyone but me, it seems. I'm very fond of Tartar, and I wouldn't part with him for anything."

Donne was just about to argue when Tartar trotted past the glass French doors to the garden, growling again. Then they heard a cheery, familiar voice call out to him, and old Mr. Hall and little Mr. Sweeting came through the gate, patting the big dog's head like he was a harmless old puppy. Tartar didn't even bark, just sniffed them both over, then wandered off to sun himself on the front steps. The two men had come by to tell Shirley and Caroline their Sunday school subscription drive had gone far better than expected that morning. Caroline ran straight to Mr. Hall, taking both his hands in hers, and the soft, gentle look he gave her was so warm it felt like wrapping up in a wool shawl.

No one wanted to stay inside on such a bright, breezy day, so they all wandered out into the garden, the air sharp with the scent of roses and clover, the wind tangling the girls' curls, their cheeks pinked with cold. Shirley gathered armfuls of the blooming flowers, gave Caroline a handful to make a nosegay for Mr. Hall, and the two of them remembered the upcoming Whitsuntide Sunday school procession and tea, the big combined feast for all three parishes. Mr. Hall teased Caroline about the first time she'd run the tea stand as a tiny twelve year old, trembling so bad her teacups rattled, and he'd sat beside her and helped her pour for an hour. "Someday I'll get to officiate at your wedding," he smiled, "but I'm going to vet the groom first, make sure he's good enough for the little girl who used to follow me all around the churchyard after Sunday school." Caroline laughed and said she'd never marry, she'd stay a spinster just like his sister Margaret, and he promised her if she ever needed a home, Nunnely Vicarage would always have a room waiting for her.

She handed him the nosegay of wild and garden flowers, and made him tuck a tiny blue forget-me-not into the pages of his pocket Testament, while she put the matching one in her little enamelled souvenir book, writing the date in pencil next to it: For the sake of my dearest friend, the Rev. Cyril Hall. Mr. Hall smiled, tucked the flower safe between his pages, and the sun dipped low over the cedar trees, the impromptu picnic of cold chicken and tarts laid out on the lawn long forgotten, all of them wrapped up in the soft, gentle quiet of the afternoon, the silly chaos of the curates' arrival already fading into a joke they'd laugh about for months.
