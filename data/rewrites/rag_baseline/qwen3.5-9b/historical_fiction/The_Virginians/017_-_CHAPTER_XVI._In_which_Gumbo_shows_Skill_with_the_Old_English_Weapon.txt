The air in the drawing room was thick with the scent of brandy and the low hum of calculation. Money changed hands not as currency, but as a fluid exchange between men who viewed the world through a lens of probability rather than morality. My cousin, Harry, had won enough to buy a small estate, yet he felt compelled to return it, a gesture that would surely disappoint his mother and the chaplain. They preferred tea and order; Harry preferred the chaos of the card table. From the moment dinner ended at three, the house shifted gears. Whist and quadrille were polite diversions, but the real business—the high-stakes games involving fortunes—began then. Even Lord Castlewood, usually reserved, had softened toward his kinsman since their last conversation. They gambled openly at backgammon and piquet, a breach of the strict silence maintained by the ladies, who demanded Harry promise never to lead his cousin astray. He gave them his word, a solemn vow broken only when the family retired and the three men slipped into Harry's quarters with dice and rum to play until dawn.

Harry's descriptions of his American home were not lies, nor were they boasts. He spoke honestly of a land where the aristocracy amused itself with hunting, cards, and punch, surrounded by a workforce of enslaved men who cultivated the tobacco and corn. In Virginia, hospitality was a law of physics; every house was a neighbor's, and the idle gentry roamed from mansion to mansion seeking sport and rough plenty. Harry had known a life of straw mattresses and cracked bowls, chasing hounds in the dark, a stark contrast to the sober, bookish nature of his younger brother. Nature divided them cleanly: one loved tea and discipline, the other Burgundy and the gallop. This difference was quickly noted by his English hosts, who found the looseness of colonial manners refreshing compared to the rigid Puritanism they feared. Names were spoken freely, actions taken boldly, things done that would scandalize a modern audience. Yet, amidst this freedom, Harry kept his own counsel. He was a formidable opponent at the gaming table, matching the three gentlemen in skill and holding his own at billiards, a game his grandfather had imported from Europe. While Mr. Will initially dominated, Harry soon became his equal, proving himself superior in many respects. Yet, despite his prowess, Harry frequently lost to the ladies, particularly one. He treated every woman with profound courtesy, a trait inherited from his lineage, and thus he was beloved by all in the Castlewood establishment—from the stern housekeeper to the giggling maids. Madame de Bernstein observed him closely, noting his behavior without speaking.

Then there was Lady Maria Esmond. Her age was a mystery wrapped in the fog of the era; peerage books were scarce, and memory was unreliable. When Will claimed she was thirty-five, he exaggerated, for she was his half-sister, the daughter of a German lady married during Queen Anne's wars. Born perhaps under George I, she claimed to be seventy-two, yet to Harry, she seemed ancient and wise. "I am old, child," she would say, calling him "child" when alone, claiming she might be his mother. Harry, however, saw only vitality. "You don't look twenty," he insisted. Lady Maria possessed a dazzling complexion, abundant fair hair, and arms that gleamed under the candlelight. When she stretched across the billiard table to strike a ball, Harry witnessed a vision: a glimpse of ankle, a clocked stocking, a black satin slipper with a red heel. It was a sight that filled him with rapture, a fixation that made him forget his own mother's feet were far more slender. No doubt she was kind to him in private, mocking him publicly as a simpleton and mimicking his provincial accent until he blushed and corrected himself under the tutelage of his English governesses.

As the seasons turned, Lord Castlewood grew increasingly familiar with his guest. With the harvest complete, the focus shifted to sport. Cock-matches at Winchester and bull-baiting at Hexton Fair provided entertainment, but the true joy lay in the travel. Harry rode with Will to various fairs and races, introduced to the local gentry—the Henleys, the Crawleys, the Redmaynes. Neighbors arrived in heavy coaches, staying for days in country fashion. Fewer guests came than expected due to the presence of Madame de Bernstein, whose rustic company she disliked. "We shall be merrier when my aunt leaves," the young people admitted, acknowledging her power. She had secured the earldom through her influence at court, ruling the household with an iron rod that even the Earl's father had feared. Without her, the house was louder, fuller of life. "Come to us in our gay days, Harry," the Earl urged, extending a soft hand with friendly blue eyes. Harry grasped it with ardour, eager to claim the privileges of cousinship, unaware of his own timidity. He realized his earlier letters, describing a cold reception, had been a mistake. The Earl was kind, promising introductions to London and White's. Castlewood was his English home. In his second dispatch to Virginia, Harry wrote with contrition, adding a clumsy spelling error: "My cousin, the Lady Maria, is a perfect Angle."

"Ille praeter omnes angulus ridet," muttered little Mr. Dempster back in Virginia.
"The child can't be falling in love with his angle!" cried Mountain.
"My niece Maria is forty," said Madam Esmond dismissively, recalling her as a gawky creature with a foot like bellows. Who holds the truth? Is beauty objective, or does it exist only in the squinting eyes of the lover? Perhaps Venus herself has a splay-foot and a crooked back. Let the fairy anoint the eyes, let the mistress's peepers remain clear, so that the beloved object appears as a paragon and the lover's ears receive only fresh roses.

While Harry captivated the drawing room and the servant's hall, his valet, Gumbo, commanded a different kind of admiration. Gumbo possessed a hundred talents: he was a fisherman, huntsman, blacksmith, and hairdresser trained by a Swiss gentleman. He cooked exquisite Virginian dishes, learned French culinary secrets, and sang melodies that made the girls dance. He played the fiddle so well that he earned free ale at the Three Castles whenever he visited. Good-natured and playful with the village children, he was a universal favorite in the Castlewood domain.

Yet, beneath this veneer of competence, Gumbo was a liar. It was undeniable. During church services, he pretended to read from Molly's psalm-book, singing words that bore no relation to the text, which he could not decipher. When the Swiss valet tested his musical knowledge, Gumbo turned the sheet music upside down. These acts of deception were daily occurrences, obvious to everyone in the household. They knew he lied, yet they forgave him, perhaps preferring the comfort of belief to the harshness of reality.

Gumbo kept his true feelings of wonder and envy hidden, observing the splendor of Castlewood with a cool detachment. He noted the horses, saying they were fine, though in Virginia there were six times as many, tended by fourteen or eighteen grooms. He marveled at Madam Esmond's carriages, laden with gold, and her gardens, which produced pineapples and peaches—fruits so common in his homeland they were fed to pigs. He counted twenty or forty gardeners, all black gentlemen like himself. Inside the house, there were twenty or forty gentlemen in livery, alongside a multitude of women-servants, perhaps fifty, all property of Madam Esmond, each worth hundreds of pieces of eight. He calculated her wealth: twenty or thirty thousand guineas a year, rooms full of gold and plate, arriving in ships he could not count, owning estates stretching a week's journey, covered in tobacco and negroes. He asked if Master Harry was the heir, noting that Master George had been killed and scalped by Indians—a tragedy Gumbo had attempted to prevent, having slain many Indians himself. Though dressed in black for his deceased master, Harry was rich beyond measure, with chests of gold, lace, and jewels in Bristol. Harry was the richest man in Virginia, entitled to twenty or sixty servants, yet he traveled with only one, the one who needed him most: Gumbo.

This elaborate tale was not invented spontaneously. It emerged gradually from Gumbo, refined over repetitions in the servants' hall or the butler's private apartment. By the third telling, the narrative was consistent, accurate in its details regarding the number of slaves and the magnitude of Madam Esmond's income. The truth was that in American establishments, four or five blacks performed the work of one white man, meaning the domestic staff swarmed with numbers unseen in England. Like most Virginian mansions, Madam Esmond's home and stables teemed with negroes, a silent army supporting the visible grandeur.
