Approximately three months prior—toward the close of May in the year 1900—the author whose name adorns the title page received a correspondence of such significance that it ranks among the most noteworthy documents he has ever examined. These materials arrived from a distinguished friend, a man whose identity I shall not now conceal: Dr. Arthur Lister Browne, M.A. (Oxon.), F.R.C.P. For the preceding two years, I had been residing largely in France, while Dr. Browne maintained a practice in Norfolk; consequently, despite our frequent visits to London, we had not met. Furthermore, though our bond was one of profound intimacy, we were notoriously inept at correspondence, exchanging only two brief notes throughout that entire period.

Then, last May, the letter and the accompanying packet reached me. The packet contained four notebooks, densely filled with the dizzying script of Pitman's shorthand, a visual ensemble that resembles startled swarms hovering in erratic flight. They were scrawled in pencil, lacking the distinction of stroke weight or vowel markers, rendering their deciphering a laborious ordeal rather than a leisurely pursuit. The letter itself was also penned in this abbreviated code. I now publish this letter, along with the transcription of the second notebook, which bears the mark 'III.'

I must confess that in approximately five instances, sentences were reconstructed based on my best guesswork, and in two cases, the characters were so cryptic and mystical that I was forced to abandon the passage entirely, leaving me with a throbbing headache. However, these discrepancies are immaterial to the overarching narrative.

Below is the text of Dr. Browne's letter:

'DEAR OLD SHIEL,—I have been lying here, lost in thought of you, wishing you were present to give me one final handshake before I—"go": for, by all outward appearances, "going" I am. Four days ago, I began to feel a soreness in my throat. Passing by Old Johnson's surgery in Selbridge, I entered and requested an examination. He muttered something regarding membranous laryngitis, a diagnosis that made me smile, but by the time I returned home, I was hoarse and no longer smiling. Before nightfall, I suffered from dyspnea and laryngeal stridor. I immediately telegraphed to London for Morgan, and between him and Johnson, they have been performing tracheotomies and cauterizing my interior with chromic acid and galvanic instruments. The difficulty in breathing has subsided, and it is astonishing how little pain I endure; yet, I am too experienced a practitioner not to recognize the signs: the bronchi are involved—_too far_ involved—and as a matter of absolute fact, there is no hope. Morgan still, I believe, clings fondly to the possibility of adding me to his statistics of successful tracheotomies, but prognosis has always been my forte, and I say No. The sole consolation of my impending death will be the humiliation of a specialist in his own field. So we shall see.

'I have been arranging my affairs this morning and recalled these notebooks. I intended to send them to you months ago, but my habitual procrastination, combined with the fact that the lady from whom I transcribed the notes was still alive, prevented me. Now she is dead, and as a literary man and a student of life, you should find them interesting, if you possess the skill to read them. You may even discover them valuable.

'I am currently under a slight dose of morphine, propped up in a pleasant state of languor. Since I can write without excessive effort, I will tell you in this old Pitman's shorthand about her. Her name was Miss Mary Wilson; she was about thirty when I first met her and forty-five when she died, and I knew her intimately for those fifteen years. Are you acquainted with the philosophy of the hypnotic trance? Well, that was the nature of our relationship—I was the hypnotist, and she the subject. She had been under the influence of another man prior to my time, but no one ever achieved such mastery over her as I did. She suffered from _tic douloureux_ of the fifth nerve. Most of her teeth had been extracted before I saw her, and an attempt had been made to sever the nerve on the left side via external incision. But it was futile: all the clocks in hell seemed to tick-tock within that poor woman's jaw, and it was the mercy of Providence that she ever encountered _me_. My organization possessed almost complete and effortless control over hers, and with a few passes, I could expel her tormenting Legion.

'Well, you have never seen anyone so singular in personal appearance as my friend, Miss Wilson. As a physician, I could never behold her without a sensation of shock; she suggested so inevitably what we call "the _other_ world," evoking an odor of decay and the distinct feeling that here was a ghost rather than a woman. Yet, I can hardly convey the essence of this impression except through dry anatomical details: her lofty brow, meagre lips, pointed chin, and ashen cheeks. She was tall and deplorably emaciated, her entire skeleton, save for the thigh bones, being fully visible. Her eyes held the bluish hue of cigarette smoke, possessing a strange, feeble, unearthly gaze; while at thirty-five, her scanty wisp of hair was already quite white.

'She was well-to-do and lived alone in the old Wooding Manor-house, five miles from Ash Thomas. As you know, I was establishing my practice in these parts at the time, and soon took up residence at the manor. She insisted that I devote myself exclusively to her; indeed, one patient constituted the most lucrative practice I have ever enjoyed.

'Well, I quickly discovered that, in the state of trance, Miss Wilson possessed remarkably powerful abilities. Remarkable, I mean, not because they were unique in kind, but because they were so constant, reliable, exact, and far-reaching in degree. The novice in psychical science will now sit and discourse finely on the reporting powers of the mind in its trance state, as though it were a brand-new discovery! This simple fact, which the Psychical Research Society admitted only after endless investigation, has been perfectly well known to every old crone since the Middle Ages, and I assume, long before that. What an unnecessary air of discovery! The certainty that someone in a trance in Manchester could report events occurring in London or Pekin was never left to the acumen of an office in Fleet Street; and the Society, in establishing this fact beyond doubt for the general public, has not advanced one step toward explaining it. In fact, they have revealed nothing that many of us did not know with absolute assurance beforehand.

'But speaking of poor Miss Wilson, I say her powers were _remarkable_ because, though not exceptional in genre, they were so special in quantity—so "constant" and "far-reaching." I believe it to be a fact that, _in general_, the powers of trance manifest primarily with regard to space, distinct from time: the spirit roams in the present—it travels over a plain—it does not _usually_ attract the attention of observers through great ascents or descents. I suspect this is true. But Miss Wilson's gift was special in that she traveled in every direction, easily in all but one: north and south, up and down, into the past, the present, and the future.

'This I discovered not at once, but gradually. She would emit a stream of sounds in the trance state—I can hardly call it _speech_, for the utterance was so murmurous yet guttural, mixed with puffy breath-sounds from her languid lips. This state was accompanied by an intense contraction of the pupils, absence of the knee-jerk, considerable rigidity, and a rapt, arrant expression. I fell into the habit of sitting for long hours by her bedside, completely fascinated, trying to catch the meaning of that opiate and visionary language that drifted from her lips in a deliberate monotone. Gradually, over the course of months, my ear learned to detect the words; "the veil was rent" became clear to me also, and I was able to follow somewhat the course of her musing and wandering spirit.

'At the end of six months, I heard her one day repeat words familiar to me. They were these: "Such were the arts by which the Romans extended their conquests, and attained the palm of victory; and the concurring testimony of different authors enables us to describe them with precision..." I was startled: these are lines from Gibbon's "Decline and Fall," which I easily surmised she had never read.

'I said in a stern voice: "Where are you?"

'She replied, "Us are in a room, eight hundred and eleven miles above. A man is writing. Us are reading."

'I may tell you two things: first, that in trance she never spoke of herself as "I," nor even as "we," but, for some unknown reason, in the _objective_ manner, as "_us_": "us are," she would say—"us will," "us went"; though, of course, she was an educated lady, and I do not think she ever lived in the West of England, where they use "us" in that dialect; secondly, when wandering in the past, she always represented herself as being "_above_" (the earth?), and the higher the further back in time she went; in describing present events, she appeared to feel herself _on_ (the earth); while regarding the future, she invariably declared that "_us_" were so many miles "within" (the earth).

'There seemed to exist certain fixed limits to her excursions in this last direction, though I cannot be certain, for I only mean that, in spite of my efforts, she never, in fact, ventured far in this direction. Three or four thousand "miles" were common figures on her lips when describing her distance "above"; but her distance "within" never exceeded sixty-three. Usually, she would say twenty or twenty-five. She appeared, in relation to the future, to resemble a diver in the deep sea, who, the deeper he strives, finds increasing resistance, until, at no great depth, resistance becomes prohibition, and he can no further strive.

'I am afraid I cannot continue: though I had much to tell you about this lady. For fifteen years, off and on, I sat listening by her dim bedside to her murmuring trances! Finally, my expert ear could detect the sense of her faintest sigh. I heard the "Decline and Fall" from beginning to end. Some of her reports were the most frivolous nonsense; over others, I hung in a horror of interest. Certainly, my friend, I have heard amazing words proceed from those wan lips of Mary Wilson. Sometimes I could anchor her repeatedly to any scene or subject I chose by the mere exercise of my will; at other times, the flighty waywardness of her spirit eluded and baffled me: she resisted—she disobeyed: otherwise I might have sent you, not four notebooks, but twenty or forty. About the fifth year, it struck me that it would be wise to record her more connected utterances, since I knew shorthand.

'The notebook marked "I.," [1] which seems to me the most curious, belongs to the seventh year. Its history, like those of the other three, is this: I heard her one afternoon murmuring in the intonation used when _reading_; the matter interested me; I asked her where she was. She replied: "Us are forty-five miles within: us read, and another writes"; from which I concluded that she was some fifteen to thirty years in the future, perusing an as yet unpublished work. After that, during some weeks, I managed to keep her to the same subject, and finally, I fancy, won pretty well the whole work. I believe you would find it striking, and hope you will be able to read my notes.

'But no more of Mary Wilson now. Rather let us think a little of A.L. Browne, F.R.C.P.—with a breathing-tube in his trachea, and Eternity under his pillow...' [Dr. Browne's letter then continues on a subject of no interest here.]

[The present writer may add that Dr. Browne's prognosis of his own case proved correct, for he passed away two days after writing the above. My transcription of the shorthand book marked 'III.' I now proceed to give without comment, merely reminding the reader that the words form the substance of a book or document to be written, or to be motivated (according to Miss Wilson) in that Future, which, no less than the Past, substantively exists in the Present—though, like the Past, we see it not. I need only add that the title, division into paragraphs, etc., have been arbitrarily contrived by myself for the sake of form and convenience.]

(_Here begins the note-book marked 'III.'_)

Well, the memory seems to be getting rather impaired now, rather weak. What, for instance, was the name of that parson who preached, just before the _Boreal_ set out, about the wickedness of any further attempt to reach the North Pole? I have forgotten! Yet four years ago, it was as familiar to me as my own name.

Things which took place before the voyage seem to be getting a little cloudy in the memory now. I have sat here, in the loggia of this Cornish villa, to write down some sort of account of what has happened—God knows why, since no eye can ever read it—and at the very beginning, I cannot remember the parson's name.

He was a strange sort of man surely, a Scotchman from Ayrshire, big and gaunt, with tawny hair. He used to wander London streets in shaggy, rough-spun clothes, a plaid flung from one shoulder.
