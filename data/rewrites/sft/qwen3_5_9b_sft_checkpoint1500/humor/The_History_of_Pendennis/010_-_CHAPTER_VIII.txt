In Which Pen is Kept Waiting at the Door, While the Reader is Informed Who Little Laura Was.

Once upon a time, a certain young gentleman of Cambridge University descended upon a quiet village to spend the long vacation. He sought out the home of young Helen Thistlewood, who resided with her mother—the widow of a lieutenant felled at Copenhagen. This visitor was the Reverend Francis Bell, a fellow of a college renowned for his learning and tutoring skills. By blood, he was the nephew of Mrs. Thistlewood and the cousin of Miss Helen, making his presence in her modest household entirely proper. There he remained, surrounded by three or four pupils who had followed him from the university.

Before long, the two kinswomen discerned that the reverend gentleman was engaged, merely awaiting a suitable college living to formalize his union. His intended bride was the daughter of another parson, who had served as Mr. Bell's private tutor in his youth. Indeed, the romance had begun when Bell, a boy of seventeen or eighteen, had knelt in the garden before Miss Martha Coacher, pledging his eternal affection while helping her pick peas.

Miss Coacher was significantly older than the impetuous youth, and her heart, though wounded by numerous past disappointments, remained open. Her father, the apothecary, had failed to guide her wisely; indeed, three of his pupils and a local dragoon officer had previously tested her affections with varying degrees of success before fleeing or breaking hearts. Yet, despite her skepticism—"Law, Bell, I'm sure you are too young to think of such things"—she secretly harbored hope. A lock of her auburn hair, tied in a blue ribbon, eventually signaled her consent. Though she had gifted similar tokens to faithless suitors before, she believed Francis Bell to be different.

For a time, Francis proved worthy. He labored prodigiously at college, winning honors and prizes, which he sent to old Coacher and the silver declamation cup to Miss Martha. Their correspondence was tender and frequent, and Bell attributed his academic triumphs to her influence. However, by the time Francis returned as a Fellow and Tutor at twenty-six, Miss Coacher was thirty-four. Time had not softened her temper or improved her manners. Having relaxed his studies, Bell found himself bound by a thousand letters to a coarse, ill-favored, and middle-aged woman.

An altercation ensued, leading Francis to refuse a visit to Bearleader's Green, where his former fiancée lived. Instead, he traveled to his aunt's village, a place he had not seen since little Helen sat upon his knee. Now, Helen was a beautiful young woman. For four months, from June to October, the cousins shared walks, mornings, and books read aloud by candlelight. Helen knew everything of his past; he had shown her the portrait of his fiancée, along with letters that were hard, eager, and cruel. Yet, the days grew kinder, filled with more confidence and pity. Then, in October, Francis departed for college, leaving the poor girl with a heart that felt it following him.

Francis, too, awoke from a midsummer dream to the harsh reality of his predicament. He gnashed his teeth against the chain binding him, desperate for freedom. Could he confess, offer his savings, and beg for release? He temporized, knowing few livings would fall for years. Meanwhile, the betrothed woman complained bitterly of his altered tone, while the cousins exchanged sad but fond letters. Finally, the new attachment surfaced. Francis admitted it, rebuking Martha for her violent temper and inferiority. Her response was dramatic: if he broke his promise, she would expose his perjury to the courts and then commit suicide.

Desperate to avoid scandal, Francis arranged a final interview with Helen, whose mother had recently died. They resolved that he must fulfill his duty to honor his vow, effectively making two honest people miserable. The living fell to him sooner than expected, and by the time he was inducted, he was already grey and worn. Helen wrote him a brief, sorrowful letter on his marriage, returning his letters and the lock of hair save for a small fragment.

Years passed. Francis Bell, now a chaplain, secured a position on Coventry Island. He married a colonial lady he loved, but she died in childbirth. Following her death, Bell gave up completely, sending his own daughter home to Helen Pendennis and her husband, John. The child, dressed in black and accompanied by a soldier's wife, wept at parting but quickly found solace under Helen's motherly care. Around her neck hung a locket containing hair Helen had given to the dead Francis decades prior. She named the girl Helen Laura, though John Pendennis, suspicious of the orphan's lineage, insisted she be known simply as "Laura." To everyone, she was Mrs. Pendennis's daughter, a connection viewed with suspicion by both John and his brother, the Major.

Now, let us introduce Mr. Pen, who had been standing outside all this while, steeling his nerves for a confrontation with his formidable uncle. Determined to face the "old man of the world" with the dignity of his famous family, he flung open the door. He entered with a severe, warlike expression, armed cap-a-pie, ready for battle.

However, the Major, observing the scene, could barely suppress a grin at the boy's pompous simplicity. Seeing that the widow was already sympathetic and that threats would fail against Pen's stubborn seriousness, the Major dropped his authoritative demeanor instantly. With a good-humored smile, he shook Pen's passive fingers and said, "Well, Pen, my boy, tell us all about it."

Helen, delighted by the Major's generosity, was caught off guard by Pen's reaction. The boy, expecting a tragedy, felt his grand entrance baulked and ludicrous. Blushing with mortified vanity, he stammered, "I—I—I didn't know that you were come till just now... is—is town very full, I suppose?"

The Major, fighting to keep from laughing, glanced comically at Mrs. Pendennis, who also found the scene absurdly sentimental. After kissing Mr. Pen, the atmosphere lightened. The guardian declared, "What a couple of fools they are," urging them to minimize sentiment and focus on the facts.

Pen, regaining his heroic air, recounted his tale: a most beautiful and virtuous lady of high family, reduced circumstances, whom he loved above all else. He acknowledged the difficulties of age and station but claimed his affection was so great he could surmount them. "She has consented to unite her lot with mine," he declared.

"How much is that, my boy?" the Major asked. "Has anybody left you some money? I don't know that you are worth a shilling in the world."

"You know what I have is his!" cried Mrs. Pendennis.

"Good heavens, madam, hold your tongue!" the guardian muttered, though he maintained his temper. "No doubt, you would sacrifice anything for him. But it is, after all, *your* fortune which Pen is offering, of which he wishes to take possession at eighteen."

"I know my mother will give me anything," Pen said, looking disturbed.

"Yes, my good fellow, but there is reason in all things," the Major interjected gently. "If your mother keeps the house, it is fair that she should select her company. When you transfer her banker's account to support Miss Costigan, don't you think you should have consulted my sister as a principal party? You are her dependent; wouldn't it have been more generous to wait and ask her leave?"

Pen lowered his head, realizing his romantic gesture was perhaps a selfish piece of folly. "I did it in a moment of passion," he admitted, though sincerely. "Now it is said, and I stand to it. I'll die rather than recall it."

"But will she take you on those terms?" the Major pressed. "Don't you suppose your position as a young gentleman of ancient birth makes your address welcome?"

"I'll die, I say, rather than forfeit my pledge!" Pen doubled his fists, turning red.

"Who asks you?" answered the imperturbable guardian. "No gentleman breaks his word freely given. But you can wait. You owe something to your mother, to your family, and to me."

"Oh, of course," Pen said, feeling relieved. "As you have pledged your word to her, give us another, will you, Arthur?"

"What is it?" Arthur asked.

"That you will make no private marriage—that you won't be taking a trip to Scotland."

"That would be a falsehood. Pen never told his mother a falsehood," Helen interjected.

Pen hung his head, tears filling his eyes. How could he explain that the whole intrigue had been a lie to a tender, confiding creature? Giving his uncle his hand, he promised, "On my word of honour, as a gentleman, I will never marry without my mother's consent!" Offering Helen a bright look of unchanging affection, he retired to his study.

"He's an angel—he's an angel," the mother cried.

"He comes of a good stock, ma'am," said the Major, pleased with his diplomacy. "Three years ago, Lady Ferrybridge sent for me regarding her son, Gretna, who had eloped with a Scotch clergyman's daughter. I extracted a promise from Master Pen that he would not repeat such errors."

"He hasn't,—that is one comfort," answered the brother-in-law.
