“Unwatch’d the garden bough shall sway,
The tender blossom flutter down,
Unloved that beech will gather brown,
The maple burn itself away;
Unloved, the sun-flower, shining fair,
Ray round with flames her disk of seed,
And many a rose-carnation feed
With summer spice the humming air;
* * * * *
Till from the garden and the wild
A fresh association blow
And year by year the landscape grow
Familiar to the stranger’s child;
As year by year the labourer tills
His wonted glebe, or lops the glades;
And year by year our memory fades
From all the circle of the hills.”
—Tennyson

The final day had arrived. The house was a scene of quiet chaos—packing cases piled high, their contents carefully wrapped and labeled, waiting to be carted off to the railway station. Even the once-pristine lawn beside the house bore the marks of the move: scattered straw drifted through open windows, clinging to the grass like a forgotten shawl. Inside, the rooms echoed strangely, stripped of their usual warmth and life. Sunlight streamed in unfiltered through bare windows, sharp and unwelcoming, as if the house itself were already preparing to forget its past.

Mrs. Hale’s dressing-room remained untouched until the very end. There, she and Dixon worked slowly, folding garments with trembling hands, pausing now and then to hold up some small treasure—a child’s tiny boot, a faded ribbon, a drawing scrawled in crayon—each one a silent plea from the past. They spoke little, but their silence was thick with emotion. Progress was slow. The memories weighed heavier than the clothes.

Below, Margaret stood calm and composed, overseeing the men who had come to help the cook and Charlotte. She moved with quiet authority, offering directions without hesitation. The two women, weeping between tasks, marveled at her stillness. “How can she keep so steady?” they whispered. “She must not care much for Helstone—she’s been in London so long.” But Margaret, pale and quiet, watched everything—the way the light fell across the floor, the dust motes dancing in the air, the careful placement of each box. Her heart, however, was breaking beneath a weight too deep for words. To let herself feel would be to collapse—and who then would carry on? Her father was absorbed in the vestry, sorting papers and registers with the clerk. When he returned, his own books needed packing—only he could do it properly. And Margaret? She was not one to break down before servants, even those who had served her family for years.

At last, the packers retired to the kitchen for tea. Margaret left her post, moving stiffly through the hall, past the empty drawing-room, into the dimming evening. November twilight draped the garden in a soft, misty veil, blurring the edges of trees and hedges, casting everything in a faint lilac hue. The sun had not yet set, but dusk was gathering fast.

A robin sang—a single, clear note—perhaps the same bird her father had once called his winter companion, the one for whom he had built a shelter by the study window. The leaves, more brilliant than ever in the fading light, shimmered amber and gold as they drifted down, caught in the low slant of the sun. One after another, they fell, like embers from a dying fire.

Margaret walked along the path beneath the pear-tree wall—the same walk where, only a fortnight ago, she had strolled beside Henry Lennox. Here, near the thyme bed, he had begun to speak of things she could no longer think about. Her eyes had lingered on a late-blooming rose as he talked, and she had noticed the delicate feathery leaves of the carrots mid-sentence. Now, the world felt changed beyond recognition. Where was he now? In London, perhaps, dining with old friends in Harley Street, or laughing with younger companions. At this very moment, while she wandered through the damp, quiet garden, he might be closing his law books after a hard day’s work, stepping out into the Temple Gardens for a breath of fresh air—listening to the distant roar of thousands of workers, unseen but ever-present, catching glimpses of city lights rising from the river below. He had often told her of these walks, spoken of them with such warmth and energy that she had found herself drawn to the idea of that bustling life.

But here, there was silence. The robin had vanished. Only the occasional sound of a cottage door shutting in the distance broke the stillness—like a tired man returning home. Beyond the garden, in the forest, a stealthy rustling crept through the crisp leaves. A poacher, perhaps. Margaret had seen them often during autumn nights, sitting up in her room with the candle extinguished, watching their silent leaps over the fence, their swift steps across the moonlit lawn, vanishing into the dark. Once, she had admired their freedom, wished them luck. Tonight, though, she felt fear—though she did not know why.

She heard Charlotte closing the windows, locking the house for the night, unaware that anyone remained outside. Then—a heavy crack! A branch, rotten or broken, fell nearby. Margaret darted forward, swift as wind, to the window, knocking urgently with trembling hands. “Let me in! Let I’m only me, Charlotte!”

Her heart pounded until she was safely inside, the windows bolted, the familiar walls enclosing her once more. She sank onto a packing case, cold and weary. The room was bleak—no fire, no comfort, only the flickering light of Charlotte’s long-unsnuffed candle.

Charlotte looked surprised. Margaret, sensing the glance, rose quickly. “I thought you might shut me out entirely,” she said, half-smiling. “Then you’d never have heard me in the kitchen. And the doors to the lane and churchyard are locked long since.”

“Oh, miss,” Charlotte replied, “I’d have found you soon enough. The men need your direction. And I’ve laid tea in master’s study—it’s the most comfortable room, so to speak.”

“Thank you, Charlotte,” Margaret said softly. “You’re kind. I’ll miss you. Do write to me if I can ever help you—advice, or anything. I’ll always welcome a letter from Helstone. I’ll send my address as soon as I know it.”

The study was ready. A warm fire crackled, candles stood unlit on the table. Margaret sat on the rug, pulling her knees to her chest, clasping her hands tightly around them. Her head drooped slightly, her posture one of quiet exhaustion. Yet when she heard her father’s footsteps on the gravel outside, she straightened up, brushing back her hair, wiping away the tears she hadn’t known had come.

He entered, quieter than usual, more subdued. He refused food, though she tried to draw him into conversation—offering topics she knew would interest him, each attempt costing her strength. “Have you walked far today?” she asked gently.

“As far as Fordham Beeches,” he replied. “I saw Widow Maltby. She’s deeply grieved she couldn’t say goodbye. Little Susan has watched down the lane for days—waiting, hoping.” He paused. “Nay, Margaret, what is it? What troubles you?”

The image of the child—patient, hopeful, disappointed every day—was too much. Margaret broke down, sobbing as if her heart would split. Mr. Hale stood helpless, pacing the room, his face etched with distress. “I cannot bear it,” he murmured, almost to himself. “I cannot bear to see others suffer. I think I could endure my own pain with patience. Oh, is there no going back?”

“No, father,” Margaret said, lifting her eyes to meet his, her voice firm though low. “It is worse to believe you in error than to know you a hypocrite.” She lowered her voice at the last words, as if the very idea of hypocrisy applied to her father was an affront to reverence. “Besides,” she continued, “it’s only fatigue tonight. Don’t think I am suffering because of what you’ve done, dear papa. We can’t talk about it now—I believe we both feel it too deeply.”

Tears came again, despite her efforts. “I should go and bring tea to mamma,” she said, rising. “She had hers early, when I was busy. I’m sure she’ll want another now.”

The next morning, railroad time carried them away from Helstone—its beloved parsonage, half-hidden by China roses and pyracanthus, glowing in the morning sun. Each window sparkled, each room remembered. Before they could settle into the carriage sent from Southampton, they were gone—never to return.

A pang pierced Margaret’s heart as she strained to catch a glimpse of the old church tower at the bend in the road, where it might rise above the forest. But her father remembered the view first. Without a word, she yielded the window to him. She leaned back, closed her eyes—and the tears came, hanging for a moment on her lashes before sliding down her cheeks, unnoticed, onto her dress.

They would spend the night in a quiet London hotel. Mrs. Hale had wept nearly all day, quietly, steadily. Dixon expressed her sorrow through irritation, constantly adjusting her petticoats to avoid touching Mr. Hale, whom she blamed for their misfortune.

Through the streets they passed—familiar houses, shops, faces. Margaret saw people she knew, though she could not stop. The morning had felt endless, yet they arrived in London during the busiest hour of a November afternoon. It was long since Mrs. Hale had been in the city. Now, she stirred with childlike wonder, pointing at shops, exclaiming at carriages. “Oh, there’s Harrison’s—where I bought my wedding things! Look at those windows—larger than Crawford’s in Southampton!” Then, suddenly: “Wait—no, it isn’t—yes, it is—Margaret, we just passed Mr. Henry Lennox! Where can he be going, among all these shops?”

Margaret started forward, then stopped herself, half-smiling at her own impulse. They were a hundred yards behind now—but he seemed a ghost from Helstone, tied to a bright morning, a significant day. She wanted to see him—without being seen, without speaking. Just to know he was there.

That evening, spent in a high room of a hotel with nothing to do, stretched endlessly. Mr. Hale went out to visit his bookseller, to call on friends. Everyone they saw—on the street, in the hotel—seemed bound for somewhere, expected or expecting someone. They alone felt adrift, strangers in a city of motion.

Yet within a mile, Margaret knew houses where they would be welcomed—if they came with joy, or even peace. If they came in sorrow, seeking sympathy for a complex grief, they would be shadows, burdens. London life was too swift, too full, to allow even an hour of the quiet mourning Job’s friends offered—“they sat with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and none spake a word unto him; for they saw that his grief was very great.”
