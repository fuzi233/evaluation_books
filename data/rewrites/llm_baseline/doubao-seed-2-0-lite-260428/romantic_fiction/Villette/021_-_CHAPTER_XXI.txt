Three last days stretched out before me, and then I must return to the Rue Fossette pensionnat. I counted every tick of the clock like it was a bead strung on grief, slow to pass but slipping through my fingers all the same before I was ready, gone before I dared to stop fearing their end.
“Lucy won’t leave us today,” Mrs. Bretton coaxed over breakfast, her hand brushing mine across the table. “We can easily arrange a second respite, you know.”
“I would not beg for another day even if it could be mine for a single word,” I told her, my voice sharper than I meant it to be. “I want to get the goodbye over with, to settle back into the rhythm of the school at once. My trunk is already packed and corded, I must leave this very morning.”
As it turned out, my departure was not mine to schedule: Graham had promised he would walk and ride with me to the door, and he was tied up with patients from dawn until dusk, only stumbling home at twilight. Then a gentle tussle broke out, mother and son pressing me to stay just one more night, their soft, warm kindness pricking at me so sharply I could have cried. I ached to leave the way a man on the scaffold aches for the axe to fall: not because I wanted the pain, but because I wanted it to be over, no more drawn-out lingering in a paradise I had no right to call my own. They could not guess the shape of that restlessness, such a state of mind was entirely outside their soft, sheltered experience.
Dr. John handed me down from the carriage at Madame Beck’s door when the dark was thick. The lamp above the threshold was lit, and a thin November drizzle fell just as it had all day, gilding the wet pavement underfoot with wavering gold. It was the exact same kind of night as the one, less than a year prior, where I had first stood on this very step, alone, heart hammering, a stranger begging for a roof and a crust of bread. I had met him, too, that night, the man who stood at my side now. I had never told him of that old brief encounter, never felt the urge to: it was a little soft, private treasure, stowed away in the dark corners of my heart, too sweet to share.
Graham rang the bell, and the door flew open at once, for it was the exact hour the half-boarders took their leave, and Rosine was hovering on the threshold.
“Don’t come in,” I said to him, but he stepped a moment into the bright vestibule anyway. I did not want him to see the tears brimming over my lashes, for his was too tender a nature not to rush to mend what could not be mended, to soothe a hurt no doctor’s remedy could reach.
“Keep up your courage, Lucy,” he said, squeezing my hand tight. “My mother and I are your true friends, we will not forget you.”
“Nor I you, Dr. John.”
My trunk was hauled inside, we shook hands, he turned to go, but his generous, kind heart would not let him rest. He stepped back after me, voice soft.
“Lucy… will you feel very solitary here at first?”
“I will.”
“Well, my mother will come to call on you soon. And until then? I will write to you — nothing but silly, cheerful nonsense, whatever pops into my head. Would you like that?”
Good, gallant heart, I thought to myself, but I shook my head, smiling. “Never trouble yourself with such a task. You write to me? You will never have the spare time.”
“Oh, I will make the time. Good-by.”
He was gone. The heavy oak door crashed shut behind him. The axe had fallen, the first sharp pang was mine to bear.
I let myself stop for nothing, swallowing my tears like bitter wine, and went straight to Madame Beck’s parlour to pay the required courtesy call. She greeted me with perfectly rehearsed warmth, even a brief show of affection, before dismissing me ten minutes later. I moved on to the dining hall where the rest of the pupils and teachers were gathered for evening study, and their welcome felt far more genuine than hers, soft around the edges, no sharp hidden motive behind the smiles. Then I was free to slip up to the dormitory, sinking tired onto the edge of my narrow bed.
Will Graham really write? I wondered.
That cold, sharp voice of Reason crept up to me through the twilight of the long, dim room, whispering frostily in my ear: He may write once, out of that innate kindness of his. But he cannot keep it up, he will not repeat the effort. Only a fool would build a whole future on that single promise, only a madwoman would mistake one small temporary rain puddle, holding but a single sip of water, for an eternal spring that feeds the land through every season.
I bowed my head, sitting silent for an hour more, while Reason laid her cold, withered hand on my shoulder, nipping at my ear with blue, frosty lips. “If he does write?” she muttered. “What then? Do you dare think you will find pleasure in writing back? Fool, I warn you. Be brief, let yourself hope for no delight, no soft unfurling of your heart, no sweet friendly exchange. Let no feeling in you stretch, not one inch.”
“But I spoke to Graham for hours and you never chided me,” I pleaded.
“Talk is good discipline for you,” she said. “You stammer, you know your place, you never forget how far below him you stand, there is no room for delusion there. But writing?”
“Where speech is faltering and clumsy, can it be wrong to let written words say all the soft, true things my lips cannot stumble through?”
“Cherish that idea at your own peril,” she snapped.
“May I never, never express what I feel?”
“Never.”
I groaned under her stern, bitter weight. Never — what a hard, cruel word! That hag Reason would not let me look up, or smile, or hope, until I was crushed, broken, completely subdued. According to her, I was born only to work for a crust of bread, to wait for death, to despond through every day of my life. She might be right, but is it any wonder we sometimes defy her, run out from under her rod for an hour of truancy, to meet our soft, bright friend Imagination? That sweet, divine hope, the only kindness that never fails us.
Reason had thrown me out into the midwinter snow so many nights before, tossing me a gnawed bone that even dogs had abandoned, vowing she had nothing more to give me — but then Imagination would come, soft as starlight, bringing with her the scent of unfading flowers, the cool breeze of a world that needs no sun to light it. She fed my hunger on sweet, strange manna, soothed my unspeakable grief, gave hope back to my paralyzed despair. That night, she leaned over my bed, and whispered, “Sleep, Lucy. I will gild your dreams.”
She kept her word, watching over me through a full night of rest — but at dawn, Reason took guard again. I woke with a jolt, rain lashing the windowpanes, wind whimpering around the eaves, the night lamp guttering on its stand in the middle of the dormitory. The sharp pain of waking hauled me out of bed like a giant’s hand curled around my collar, and I dressed fast in the raw cold of November dawn, drinking deep of the ice-cold water in my carafe, my usual cordial whenever chagrin left me unsteady.
The school bell soon rang, calling the whole house to rise. I was the first down to the refectory, where the big iron stove burned bright, the only warm spot in the whole drafty building, the rest of the rooms nipping with the sharp bite of an early continental winter. I sat huddled before that dark, comforting hunk of metal, making firm, unbreakable rules for myself: no more lingering over happy memories that were gone forever, walk the hard wilderness of the present patiently, put my faith in the pillar of cloud that guides even as it awes me, don’t let myself long for a far-off promised land I will likely never reach, save for in the dreams of my dying days.
A feeling half strength, half sharp pain coiled tight around my heart, steadying me, holding my wild, unruly throbbings in check, until I was ready for the day’s work. I lifted my head.
Right beside the stove, set into the same wall, was a small window looking out onto the school courtyard. Through that pane, I suddenly saw a tassel of a cap, a furrowed brow, two sharp dark eyes, fixed straight on mine. They had been watching me for who knows how long. I had not known tears were on my cheeks until that moment, but I felt them now, cold and salt against my skin.
This was a strange house, where no corner was sacred, where not a tear could fall, not a private thought could stir, without a spy waiting to note and parse it. And this new, out-of-door, male spy, what was he doing here at such an ungodly early hour? No other professor would dare cross the courtyard before the class bell rang. But Monsieur Paul Emanuel never cared for hours, or rules, or other people’s boundaries. He had come to the library to fetch a reference book, and on his way had spotted me through the little window. He opened the refectory door, and stepped inside.
“Mademoiselle, vous êtes triste.”
“Monsieur, I have every right to be.”
“You are sick at heart and angry,” he went on. “You are half mournful, half mutinous. I see two tears on your cheek, hot as sparks, salt as sea crystals. You look at me like a wild newly-caught creature, the moment the animal trainer steps through the door, half fire half fear.”
It was a rude, unwarrantable thing to say, to a fellow teacher no less. He loved provoking the hot-tempered pupils into outbursts, and he expected me to snap back. But I sat silent, denying him the satisfaction.
“You look like the sort of person who would snatch a draught of sweet poison, and push away wholesome bitter medicine with disgust,” he said.
“I never liked bitters,” I answered, low. “And I do not believe they are always wholesome. What is sweet, even if it is poison, cannot be denied its own sweetness. It is better to die a quick, pleasant death, than drag out a long, charmless one.”
“Then if I had my way, I would force you to swallow your bitter medicine every day,” he said. “And I would break that cup of sweet poison before you could lift it to your lips.”
I turned my head sharply away, his presence chafing at my raw nerves, and I fought to keep my self-command from slipping.
“Come,” he said, his voice softening. “Tell me the truth. You grieve to be parted from your friends, is that not all it is?”
That feigned softness was no more welcome than his prying curiosity. I said nothing, and he sat down on the bench two yards from me, patient for once, trying for ages to draw me into conversation. I could not speak, not a single word, and at last I begged him to leave me alone. My voice faltered, my head sank onto my arms on the table, and I wept quietly, bitterly. He sat a while longer, until I heard the door click shut, and his footsteps retreat down the hall. Those tears were a relief.
I had time to bathe my face before breakfast, and I looked as calm as any other pupil at the table — though not nearly as bright and bubbly as Ginevra Fanshawe, who plopped down opposite me, her small eyes twinkling gaily, and stretched a white hand across the table to shake mine. Her holidays, parties, and flirtations had done her a world of good; she was plumper now, her cheeks round as apples, her golden curls bright against the dark blue, faintly plaided school wrapper that made her fair skin glow all the more.
“I’m so glad you’re back, Timon,” she said. “You have no idea how often I wanted you around in this dismal hole.”
“Oh? Then you must have some task for me, I suppose. Stockings to mend, most likely.” I never gave Ginevra credit for disinterested kindness.
“Still as crabby and sharp as ever!” she laughed. “I expected no less, it wouldn’t be you if you didn’t snub me. Now come, grandmother, I hope you still hate the school coffee and love the rolls: want to barter?”
Ginevra hated the weak, oversweet breakfast coffee the school served, but had a ravenous appetite for the fresh baked morning rolls. I never finished mine, so I always gave her half, and she would slip me a few sips of her own, stronger coffee, the two of us sharing cups as we had on so many long country walks, me always letting her take the lion’s share of whatever treat we found. We bickered every day, but we never grew alienated.
After breakfast, I withdrew to the empty first class to sit and think, as was my custom, before the nine o’clock bell rang and the horde of day pupils and half-boarders poured in, filling the house with noise and bustle until five o’clock. Everyone thought I was some solemn studious bluestocking, so wrapped up in my books I would overwork myself to illness, much to Madame Beck’s frequent mock concern. Only M. Emanuel had somehow guessed that I was not half as learned as the rest imagined, and he would chuckle quietly in my ear, delighted by how little I actually knew. I did not mind, though: I loved nothing better than to sit and think my own quiet thoughts, reading only the books that bore the clear stamp of the writer’s own heart, no matter how unremarked they were.
A soft tap at the door: a young pupil slipped in to fetch a book from her desk, and tiptoed out, murmuring that Mademoiselle looked so very hard at work. No sooner was she gone than the door flew open without a knock, and Ginevra burst in, a huge music book tucked under her arm.
“Go practice at the piano,” I told her at once. “Off to the little salon with you.”
“Not until I talk to you, chérie,” she grinned. “I saw you at the concert the other night, all dressed up like a proper belle! Who is your dressmaker, Lucy?”
“Tittle-tattle, Ginevra, leave me be.”
“Not a chance, wild swan. We all know you’re keeping company with Isidore — you know, our Dr John Bretton. Do tell me how he is. Was he terribly upset after the concert? Wasn’t I cruel to him, flirting so openly with de Hamal?”
“Cruel? I barely noticed you,” I said, lying through my teeth to tease her.
“Oh he noticed! I saw him standing off in the distance, all sulking and dying of jealousy! And his dear old mother, my future mamma-in-law! I do hope Lady Sara and I weren’t too rude, making fun of her.”
“Mrs. Bretton will survive your sneers, never fear,” I told her.
“But that poor son of hers! Tell me what he did after the concert!” she begged, eyes sparkling.
“He said you looked very much like a woman who was already half married to Monsieur de Hamal,” I said.
“Did he?” she squealed, delighted. “He noticed? That’s perfect! I thought he would be mad with jealousy.”
“Ginevra, are you truly done with Dr. Bretton? Do you really want him to give you up entirely?”
“He can’t give me up!” she laughed. “But tell me, how did you get him home that night? Was he absolutely furious?”
“Raving mad,” I assured her, leaning in to spin the tale. “Furious as a March hare. We had to hold him down in the carriage, him fighting the whole time, the coachman got so distracted we lost our way, and he tried to climb out the window to ride on the roof. And once we got home? He refused to touch his supper, the chicken and sweetbread his mother had set out all cold on the table. He would not lie still in his bed, no matter how tight Mrs. Bretton tucked the sheets in. Not even when he was a little boy did she have half as much trouble taming him.”
Ginevra cackled, throwing her head back, and the bell for nine o’clock class rang, cutting off her next request for more details.
