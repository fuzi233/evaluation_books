"And I cannot touch her face,
And I cannot touch her hair,
And I kneel to empty shadows—
Just memories of her grace..."

These words, sung in the wind and the sobbing dawn, haunted me from the first. It was the Joy of the Sunset that first broke the silence between us. I had wandered far from home, lost in a solitary reverie, pausing repeatedly to gaze upward at the piling Battlements of Evening, to feel the strange, sweet gathering of the Dusk envelop the world.

At length, I stood utterly absorbed in the solemn glory of the Coming Night. Perhaps I let a laugh rise in my throat, standing alone amidst the deepening twilight. And, lo! my solitude was answered from the trees bordering the country road on my right. A voice echoed the sentiment, saying, "And thou also!" with a glad understanding. I laughed again, though only half-believing that any true mortal could answer my mirth; it seemed more like some sweet delusion or spirit, perfectly attuned to my melancholy mood.

But then she spoke, calling me by my name. As I stepped aside from the road to catch a glimpse of her—to discern if I knew her—I saw her clearly. She was surely that lady, renowned throughout the sweet county of Kent as Lady Mirdath the Beautiful, a neighbor whose guardian's estates abounded with mine.

Until that moment, however, I had never met her. My life had been spent so often abroad, and so deeply devoted to study and exercise at home, that I possessed of her no more than rumors offered in fleeting moments. I was content with books and physical prowess; I was always an athlete, believing myself unmatched in speed and strength save perhaps in fiction.

Instantly, I stood with my hat in hand, answering her gentle banter as best I could while peering at her intently through the gloom. Truly, rumor had failed to describe the beauty of this strange maiden, who now stood jesting with such sweet spirit, claiming a kinship of cousinhood that proved to be truth as soon as I woke to it. She named me frankly by my boyish name, granting me permission to call her Mirdath, and nothing else—for the time. She bade me climb through the hedge via a gap she confessed was her secret escape, used when she slipped away from her maid for country frolics disguised as a village maid. Though she claimed secrecy, I dared believe the deception was limited.

I climbed through the gap and stood beside her. Tall she appeared when I looked up, and tall she was in truth, though I stood a head taller. She invited me to walk with her to the house, that I might meet her Guardian and confess my sorrow for neglecting to pay court to them. Her eyes sparkled with mischief and delight as she chided me for my amissness.

Suddenly, however, her demeanor sobered. She raised a finger to hush me, having heard something in the wood lying to our right. Indeed, I heard it too—a rustling of leaves, followed by the sharp, clear crack of a dead twig breaking in the stillness.

Immediately, three men burst from the wood toward me. I called sharply for them to keep off, placing the maid to my back with my left hand and gripping my oak staff. The three men uttered no word; they simply charged. I glimpsed the gleam of knives. With a glad, brisk resolve, I attacked. Behind me rang out the shrill, sweet call of a silver whistle—the maid summoning her dogs, perhaps also signaling her household servants.

There was no time for aid to arrive; the need was instant. Unloath to hesitate before my sweet cousin, I stepped forward. I drove the butt of my staff into the body of the man on the left, sending him dropping like a stone. I struck sharply at the head of another, cracking his skull so that he fell instantly to the earth. To the third, I delivered a single, crushing blow with my fist; he required no second strike and joined his companions immediately. The fight ended before it had properly begun, leaving me laughing with a proper pride at the bewilderment I perceived in Lady Mirdath's gaze as she regarded me through the hushed evening.

Before we could linger, three great boar-hounds bounded up, loosed by her whistle. She struggled briefly to keep the brutes from me, while I beat them off the fallen men to prevent further mauling. Directly, shouts rose, lanterns flared, and footmen ran toward us. Uncertain how to treat me, they hesitated momentarily, just as the dogs had, but seeing the men upon the ground and learning my name, they kept a respectful distance. However, regarding me, my sweet cousin displayed the deepest feeling of kinship, surpassing anything she had initially shown.

The servants asked what to do with the footpads, now recovering. I left judgment to them, offering some silver. They administered sound justice, and I heard their cries echo long after we departed.

Upon reaching the Hall, my cousin introduced me to her Guardian, Sir Alfred Jarles, an old and venerable man whom I knew slightly due to our neighboring estates. He thanked me honorably, and I was welcomed as a house-friend thereafter. That evening, I dined and later returned to the grounds with Lady Mirdath. She was more friendly than any woman had ever been, seeming to know me as an old friend. In return, I felt a similar bond, as though we shared an innate understanding of the world's turns, finding common joys without surprise, save for the natural delight of discovering these truths.

One thing captivated her that fore-night: my ease with the three footpads. She asked plainly if I was not truly very strong. Laughing with youthful pride, she seized my arm to test its strength. Suddenly, she released it with a gasp of astonishment, overwhelmed by its hardness. Walking beside me thereafter, she remained silent and thoughtful, staying close. If she derived strange pleasure from my strength, I harbored a constant wonder at her beauty, which the candlelight at dinner had rendered even lovelier.

Further delights awaited in the days that followed. We shared a profound affinity for the Mystery of the Evening, the Glamour of Night, and the Joy of Dawn. On one memorable evening, wandering the park-lands, she half-unthinking remarked that it was truly an elves' night. She stopped abruptly, fearing I lacked understanding. Yet, I was on familiar ground of inward delight and replied calmly that the Towers of Sleep would grow high that night, and I felt in my bones that it was a time to seek the Giant's Tomb or the Tree with the Great Painted Head—or perhaps the Moon Garden, an old fantasy of mine.

As I spoke of the Moon Garden, Lady Mirdath cried out in a strange, low voice, halting me so she might face me. She questioned me earnestly, and I answered with equal fervor, suddenly excited by the realization that she knew these things too. She had believed herself alone in the world with her knowledge of this strange dreamland; now, to find I had traveled there as well! The marvel of it, she repeated, filled her. As we walked, she noted how little wonder she had been drawn to approach me on the road, though she had learned of our cousinship beforehand, having seen me ride by often. Perhaps she had been daintily irked by my lack of heed for Lady Mirdath the Beautiful. Indeed, I had thought of other matters, yet I was human enough to have sought her sooner if I had met her properly before.

Do not think I was not utterly stirred by this shared, dreamful knowledge. Yet, when I probed further, much that was foreign to her in my fancies, and much familiar to her that held no meaning for me, revealed itself. Despite this partial disconnect, time and again, new stories emerged—one told, the other finishing—that brought gladness and amazement to both. Thus, we wandered constantly speaking, growing gladly aged in dear knowledge and sweet friendship.

How time passed, I know not, but presently a hullabaloo erupted—shouts, baying dogs, gleaming lanterns. Lady Mirdath realized with a sweet, strange laughter that we had missed hours in conversation. Her Guardian, uneasy about the footpads, had ordered a search. We had all wandered together in happy forgetfulness. Turning homeward, the dogs found us before we reached the lights. Having grown to know me, they leaped about me, barking friendly, and the men returned to inform Sir Jarles that all was well.

From that time onward, evening by evening, I walked the quiet country road leading to Sir Jarles' estate. I entered by the hedge-gap, often finding Lady Mirdath walking there with her great boar-hounds. I had begged her to keep them for her safety, yet she seemed perverse in diverse matters, striving to plague me as if testing how much I would endure or how far she might go to anger me.

Well do I remember one night when, approaching the gap, I saw two country maids emerge from Sir Jarles' woods. They were naught to me, yet as they passed, the taller one curtseyed with an over-graceful manner unfit for rough wenches. A sudden thought struck me; the taller was surely Lady Mirdath. I could not be sure, for when I asked who she was, she only simpered and curtseied again. Doubt clouded me, yet wonder compelled me to follow them.
