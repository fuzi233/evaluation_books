The sunlight blazed with an intensity that felt unnatural, promising a day that would not last. I glanced down at the milky sapphire ring on my finger, checking the embedded star within. It appeared far less distinct than I had hoped, a faint, wavering point of light. Rising from the breakfast table, I moved to the window, peering at the stone once more. Outside, the aftermath of a heavy night's rain lay scattered across the lawn—a thrush laboriously breaking the shell of a snail.

As I twisted the ring, trying to coax a clearer reflection from the stone, a pair of keen, black eyes seemed to gaze back at me from the milky blue mist of the gem. Startled, I dropped the ring. By the time I bent to pick it up, the eyes were gone. In that exact instant, the sun vanished, swallowed by a dark vapor. Within moments, the sky turned a bruised grey, the air grew suffocatingly hot, and a sudden gust of wind tore through the room. A flash of lightning split the heavens, followed by a sharp crack of thunder, and then the rain began to fall in torrents.

I had flung open the window, standing exposed to the deluge, when I spotted a raven approaching over the sod. He walked with a solemn, deliberate gait, utterly indifferent to the pouring water. Suspecting his identity, I secretly congratulated myself on having stayed on the ground floor, though a sinking conviction settled in my gut that disaster awaited if I remained careless.

He drew near, executed a profound bow, and launched himself with a sudden, winged leap onto the windowsill. Stepping over the ledge, he hopped down into the room and strode toward the door. Assuming he meant for the library, I prepared to follow, vowing not to take a single step if he ascended the stairs. Instead, he turned away from both the library and the staircase, heading instead for a small door leading to a grass patch nestled between two sections of the rambling old house.

Hastening to open it, I watched him step out onto a creeper-covered porch. He stopped, gazing out at the rain, which fell like a massive, thin cataract. Standing in the doorway behind him, I heard the second flash of lightning, followed by a long, rolling boom of distant thunder. The raven swiveled his head over his shoulder, looking back at me as if asking, "You hear that?" Before returning his gaze to the storm, seemingly approving of it. His posture and movements were so undeniably human that I found myself remarking almost without thinking, "Fine weather for the worms, Mr. Raven!"

"Yes," he croaked, using the familiar, gravelly tone I knew so well. "The ground will be lovely for them to emerge from the soil! It must be a grand time on the steppes of Uranus," he added, casting a look upward. "I believe it is raining there, too. It has been doing so all week!"

"Why does that make it a grand time?" I asked.

"Because the creatures there are all burrowers," he explained. "Like the field mice and moles here. They will remain so for ages to come."

"How do you know that, if I may ask?" I pressed.

"As anyone would who has been there to witness it," he replied. "It is a magnificent sight, until you become accustomed to it, when the earth heaves and a beast emerges. You might mistake it for a hairy elephant or a deinotherium—but none of these animals resemble anything we have ever known here. I was terrified the first time I saw the dry-bog serpent wallow out; it had such a head and mane! And SUCH eyes! ... But the shower is nearly over. It will cease directly after the next clap of thunder. Here it comes!"

A flash illuminated the sky, followed swiftly by the thunderclap. Immediately, the rain stopped.

"Now we must depart," the raven announced, stepping to the front of the porch.

"Depart where?" I asked.

"To where we must go," he answered. "Did you truly believe you had arrived home? I told you there is no coming and going at pleasure until you are actually *home*!"

"I do not wish to go," I protested.

"That makes no difference—at least, not much," he countered. "This is the path!"

"I am perfectly content exactly where I am."

"You think so, but you are mistaken. Come along."

He hopped from the porch onto the grass and turned, waiting.

"I will not leave the house today," I declared with stubborn resolve.

"You will enter the garden!" he rejoined.

"I yield for the moment," I replied, stepping off the porch.

The sun pierced through the lingering clouds, causing the raindrops to flash and sparkle on the grass. The raven walked ahead of me.

"You will soak your feet!" I cried out.

"And mud my beak," he answered instantly, plunging it deep into the sod and pulling out a great, wriggling red worm. He tossed his head back, hurling it into the air. As it soared upward, it spread great wings, gorgeously patterned in red and black.

"Tut! Tut!" I exclaimed. "You are mistaken, Mr. Raven: worms are not the larvae of butterflies!"

"Never mind," he croaked. "It will suffice for once! I am not a reader at present, but a sexton at—well, at a certain cemetery, more properly speaking—in... no matter where!"

"I see! You cannot keep your spade still, and when there is nothing to bury, you must dig something up! Yet you ought to consider what you unearth before you set it flying! No creature should be allowed to forget its nature or its origins!"

"Why?" the raven asked.

"Because pride will swell, and it will cease to recognize its superiors."

No one realizes they are acting like an idiot until the damage is done.

"Where DO worms originate?" the raven asked, his curiosity suddenly piqued.

"From the earth, as you have just observed!" I answered.

"Yes, last!" he replied. "But they could not have originated from it first—for that would never return to it!" he added, gazing upward.

I looked up as well, seeing nothing but a small, dark cloud whose edges glowed with the crimson hue of a setting sun.

"The sun cannot possibly be setting!" I exclaimed, struck with amazement.

"Oh, no!" returned the raven. "That red color belongs to the worm."

"You see what happens when creatures forget their origins!" I cried, warmed by indignation.

"It is excellent, surely, if it allows them to rise higher and grow larger!" he responded. "But truthfully, I merely teach them to discover it!"

"Do you intend to fill the air with worms?"

"That is precisely the business of a sexton. If only the rest of the clergy understood it as well!"

His beak plunged back into the soft turf, extracting another wriggling worm. He tossed it into the air, and away it flew.

Turning around, I let out a cry of dismay. I had but moments prior declared I would not leave the house, and now I was a stranger in a strange land!

"What right do you have to treat me thus, Mr. Raven?" I demanded, deeply offended. "Am I, or am I not, a free agent?"

"A man is as free as he chooses to make himself, never an atom freer," the raven answered calmly.

"You have no authority to force me to act against my will!"

"When you possess a will, you will find that no one can."

"You wrong me in the very essence of my individuality!" I persisted.

"If you were an individual, I could not interfere; therefore, since you are not yet one, I do not hesitate. You are only just beginning to become an individual."

Surrounded by a pine forest, I frantically searched my vision for any unaccountable glimmer that might guide me back home. Alas, how could I ever again call that house HOME, when every door and window opened outward, and even the garden I could not contain within!

I suppose I looked disconcerted.

"Perhaps it may comfort you," said the raven, "to be told that you have not yet left your house, nor has your house abandoned you. At the same time, it cannot hold you, or you cannot inhabit it!"

"I do not understand you," I replied. "Where am I?"

"In the region of the seven dimensions," he answered, his throat making a curious noise as his tail fluttered. "You had better follow me carefully for a moment, lest you injure someone!"

"There is no one to harm but yourself, Mr. Raven! I confess I should prefer to hurt you!"

"That you perceive no one is where the danger lies. However, do you see that large tree to your left, approximately thirty yards away?"

"Of course I do: why should I not?" I answered testily.

"Ten minutes ago, you did not see it, and now you do not know where it stands!"

"I do."

"Where do you suppose it stands?"

"Why THERE, where you know it to be!"

"Where is THERE?"

"You bore me with your silly questions!" I cried, feeling weary. "I am growing tired of you!"
