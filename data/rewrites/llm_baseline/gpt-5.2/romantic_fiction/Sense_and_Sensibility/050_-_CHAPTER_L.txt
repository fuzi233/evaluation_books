Mrs. Ferrars resisted first, as if resistance were a duty laid upon her by birth and habit—violent enough to satisfy her own idea of dignity, steady enough to keep her safe from the suspicion of tenderness. Only after that proper battle was fought did she admit Edward to her presence, restore him—by a sentence rather than an embrace—to the rank of son.

Her household, in truth, had lately been as changeable as a kitchen ruled by caprice instead of season. For years she had possessed two sons, as securely as two well-fed hounds under the table. A few weeks ago Edward had been struck out of her life as if he had spoiled the broth beyond redemption; then Robert, by a similar act of “annihilation,” had left her, for a short and mortifying fortnight, without any heir at all. And now—by the odd resuscitation of Edward—she had one again, brought back as if the dish she had thrown away had returned to the platter, still warm.

Edward, though permitted once more to exist, did not feel the continuance of that existence entirely safe until he had served up the truth of his present engagement. It lay on his mind like an untasted sauce whose very name might offend the palate of the house; and he feared that, once announced, it might curdle his mother’s restored affection as abruptly as it had curdled before. He revealed it, therefore, with a caution so anxious it might have been mistaken for guilt; and yet Mrs. Ferrars listened with an unexpected stillness—the kind of calm that belongs to those who mean to disapprove at leisure.

At first she endeavoured, with what she considered reasonable solicitude, to dissuade him from marrying Miss Dashwood. She set before him Miss Morton—higher in rank, heavier in fortune—as if placing a richer joint on the board and expecting him to forget his chosen fare. Miss Morton, she said, was the daughter of a nobleman and had thirty thousand pounds; Miss Dashwood was only the daughter of a private gentleman with no more than three. Edward admitted every figure without flinching; he did not contest the weight of the purse, only refused to let money dictate the heart. When Mrs. Ferrars perceived that truth itself, marshalled into argument, could not move him an inch, she judged it wisest—taught by recent defeats—to submit. And so, after an ungracious delay that she owed to her pride, and which served to keep all suspicion of good-will safely at bay, she pronounced her consent.

What she would do to increase their income came next; and here it was plain that, though Edward was now her only son, he was by no means treated as her eldest. Robert, however inevitably endowed with a thousand a year, encountered no opposition; Edward, to gain at most two hundred and fifty, must take orders—and even then nothing was promised beyond the ten thousand already given with Fanny. It was as much, nevertheless, as Edward and Elinor desired, and more than they had expected; and Mrs. Ferrars herself, by the shuffling air of her excuses, seemed the only person astonished at her own restraint.

With an income sufficient to their wants thus secured, they had nothing to wait for, once Edward was in possession of the living, but the readiness of the house—Colonel Brandon, eager for Elinor’s comfort, improving it with an attention as steady as good husbandry. Yet improvements, like bread, must be allowed their time; and workmen, like careless scullions, have a talent for delay that no watching can cure. There were disappointments enough—boards not come, plaster not dry, a promise made in the morning and forgotten before noon—until Elinor, who had resolved not to marry until everything was ready, did what she so often did when resolution became unreasonable: she broke through it. The ceremony took place in Barton church early in the autumn, when the air had begun to smell of damp earth and late apples, and the lanes carried, in their hedgerows, the soft rustle of leaves turning.

The first month of their marriage they spent at the Mansion-house, that they might superintend the progress of the parsonage and direct every choice on the spot—papers for the walls, shrubberies for the grounds, even the invention of a sweep, as if laying out the courses of a meal and the order in which each comfort should arrive. Mrs. Jennings’s prophecies, though jumbled as her imagination always made them, were chiefly fulfilled; for by Michaelmas she visited them at the parsonage and found—so she firmly believed—one of the happiest couples in the world. In truth they had little to wish for but the marriage of Colonel Brandon and Marianne, and rather better pasturage for their cows.

Nearly all their relations and friends came to see them upon their first settling. Mrs. Ferrars arrived to inspect a happiness she was almost ashamed to have authorised, and the Dashwoods were even at the expense of a journey from Sussex to do them honour. John Dashwood, walking one morning with Elinor before the gates of Delaford House, could not keep his own calculations from seasoning his compliments.

“I will not say I am disappointed, my dear sister,” said he, “that would be saying too much; for certainly you have been one of the most fortunate young women in the world as it is. But I confess it would give me great pleasure to call Colonel Brandon brother. His property here—his place, his house—everything is in such respectable and excellent condition! And his woods—I have not seen such timber anywhere in Dorsetshire as there is now standing in Delaford Hanger! And though perhaps Marianne may not seem exactly the person to attract him, yet I think it would altogether be advisable for you to have them now frequently staying with you; for as Colonel Brandon seems a great deal at home, nobody can tell what may happen when people are much thrown together and see little of anybody else. And it will always be in your power to set her off to advantage, and so forth. In short, you may as well give her a chance: you understand me.”

But though Mrs. Ferrars did come, and always treated Edward and Elinor with a make-believe of decent affection, they were never insulted by her real favour. That was reserved—like the choice cut kept back for the diner she preferred—for the folly of Robert and the cunning of his wife; and it was earned by them before many months had passed away.

Lucy’s selfish sagacity, which had first drawn Robert into his scrape, became the principal instrument of his deliverance. Respectful humility, assiduous attentions, endless flatteries—she brought them out as a practiced cook brings out preserves and cordials when she has found the exact weakness of a guest. As soon as the smallest opening appeared, she slipped herself through it and began to sweeten Mrs. Ferrars’s temper by degrees. The whole of Lucy’s behaviour—and the prosperity that crowned it—may stand as a most encouraging instance of what unceasing attention to self-interest will do in securing every advantage of fortune, with no other sacrifice than time and conscience.

When Robert first sought her acquaintance and privately visited her in Bartlett’s Buildings, he meant only what Edward had imputed to him: to persuade her to give up the engagement. He expected affection—hers and Edward’s—to be the only obstacle; and presumed one or two interviews would settle the matter. In that point, however, and that only, he erred. Lucy soon gave him hopes that his eloquence would convince her in time—yet time always required another visit, another conversation. Some doubt lingered whenever they parted, which could only be removed by another half-hour of discourse. Thus his attendance was secured; and the rest followed in course.

Instead of talking of Edward, they came gradually to talk only of Robert—a subject on which he had always more to say than on any other, and in which she soon betrayed an interest equal to his own. It became speedily evident that he had supplanted his brother entirely. He was proud of the conquest, proud of tricking Edward, and very proud of marrying privately without his mother’s consent. What immediately followed is already known. They passed some months in great happiness at Dawlish; she had many relations and old acquaintances to cut, and he drew several plans for magnificent cottages. From thence returning to town, they procured the forgiveness of Mrs. Ferrars by the simple expedient of asking it—an expedient adopted at Lucy’s instigation.

The forgiveness at first, as was reasonable, comprehended only Robert; Lucy, owing his mother no duty and therefore able (in Mrs. Ferrars’s logic) to have transgressed none, remained some weeks longer unpardoned. But perseverance—humility in conduct and messages, self-condemnation for Robert’s offence, gratitude for the unkindness she received—procured her in time that haughty notice which overcame her by its graciousness, and led soon afterwards, by rapid degrees, to the highest state of affection and influence. Lucy became as necessary to Mrs. Ferrars as either Robert or Fanny. Edward was never cordially forgiven for having once intended to marry her; and Elinor, though superior to Lucy in birth and fortune, was spoken of as an intruder. Lucy, in everything, was considered and openly acknowledged as a favourite child.

They settled in town; they received very liberal assistance from Mrs. Ferrars; they were on the best terms imaginable with the Dashwoods. And setting aside the jealousies and ill-will continually subsisting between Fanny and Lucy—in which their husbands, of course, took a part—as well as the frequent domestic disagreements between Robert and Lucy themselves, nothing could exceed the harmony in which they all lived together.

What Edward had done to forfeit the right of eldest son might have puzzled many to find out; and what Robert had done to succeed to it might have puzzled them still more. The arrangement, however, was justified in its effects if not in its cause; for nothing in Robert’s style of living or talking ever hinted that he regretted the extent of his income as leaving his brother too little or bringing himself too much. And if Edward was judged by the ready discharge of his duties in every particular, by an increasing attachment to his wife and his home, and by the regular cheerfulness of his spirits, he might be supposed no less contented with his lot, no less free from any wish of exchange.

Elinor’s marriage divided her from her family as little as could be contrived without rendering Barton Cottage entirely useless; her mother and sisters spent much more than half their time with her. Mrs. Dashwood was acting from policy as well as pleasure in the frequency of her visits at Delaford; for her wish to bring Marianne and Colonel Brandon together was hardly less earnest—though rather more liberal—than John’s had expressed. It was now her darling object. Precious as her daughter’s company was to her, she desired nothing so much as to give up its constant enjoyment to her valued friend; and to see Marianne settled at the Mansion-house was equally the wish of Edward and Elinor. They each felt Brandon’s sorrows and their own obligations; and Marianne, by general consent, was to be the reward of all.

With such a confederacy against her—with a knowledge so intimate of Brandon’s goodness—with a conviction of his fond attachment to herself which, at last, though long after it had been visible to everyone else, burst upon her—what could she do?

Marianne Dashwood was born to an extraordinary fate: to discover the falsehood of her own opinions, and to counteract by her conduct her favourite maxims. She was born to overcome an affection formed so late as at seventeen, and with no sentiment superior to strong esteem and lively friendship voluntarily to give her hand to another—and that other a man who had suffered no less than herself under the event of a former attachment; whom, two years before, she had considered too old to marry; and who still sought the constitutional safeguard of a flannel waistcoat.

But so it was. Instead of falling a sacrifice to an irresistible passion, as once she had fondly expected—rather like a heroine in a song whose refrain repeats itself until it becomes law—instead of remaining forever with her mother and finding her only pleasures in retirement and study, as afterwards, in calmer judgment, she had determined on, she found herself at nineteen submitting to new attachments, entering on new duties, placed in a new home: a wife, the mistress of a family, and the patroness of a village.

Colonel Brandon was now as happy as all those who best loved him believed he deserved to be. In Marianne he was consoled for every past affliction; her regard and her society restored his mind to animation and his spirits to cheerfulness. That Marianne found her own happiness in forming his was equally the persuasion and delight of every observing friend. Marianne could never love by halves; and her whole heart became, in time, as devoted to her husband as it had once been to Willoughby.

Willoughby could not hear of her marriage without a pang; and his punishment was soon afterwards made complete by the voluntary forgiveness of Mrs. Smith. By stating his marriage with a woman of character as the source of her clemency, she gave him reason to believe that, had he behaved with honour towards Marianne, he might at once have been happy and rich. That his repentance, which thus brought its own punishment, was sincere need not be doubted; nor that he long thought of Colonel Brandon with envy and Marianne with regret. But that he was forever inconsolable—fled from society, contracted habitual gloom, or died of a broken heart—must not be depended on; for he did neither. He lived to exert, and frequently to enjoy himself. His wife was not always out of humour, nor his home always uncomfortable; and in his breed of horses and dogs, and in sporting of every kind, he found no inconsiderable degree of domestic felicity. For Marianne, however, in spite of his incivility in surviving her loss, he always retained a decided regard that interested him in everything that befell her, made her his secret standard of perfection in woman; and many a rising beauty would be slighted by him in after-days as bearing no comparison with Mrs. Brandon.

Mrs. Dashwood was prudent enough to remain at the cottage, without attempting removal to Delaford; and fortunately for Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, when Marianne was taken from them, Margaret had reached an age highly suitable for dancing and not very ineligible for being supposed to have a lover. Between Barton and Delaford there was that constant communication which strong family affection naturally dictates—the easy coming and going, the familiar sounds of carriage-wheels on the same road, voices recognized at the door before the knock is finished, and the quiet domestic music of two households answering one another across short distances, not drowned out by any harsher din.

And among the merits and happiness of Elinor and Marianne, let it not be ranked as the least considerable that, though sisters and living almost within sight of each other, they could live without disagreement between themselves, or producing coolness between their husbands.
