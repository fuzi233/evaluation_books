With General Braddock and the other guests of Castlewood comfortably settled in their rooms, the two young Warringtons retired to their own chamber, eager to share their thoughts on the day’s most momentous event. The idea of their mother marrying the younger son of a colonial family—once trained as a land surveyor—was unthinkable. Could the heir of the noble Marquises of Esmond truly unite with such a man? And what of Castlewood itself, now under the care of a stepfather barely twenty-three years old? It was an outrage! Harry, fired by indignation, declared he would rush straight to his mother’s bedroom—where her black maids were gently removing the jewels and finery she had worn for the feast—and protest with all his might. If the match went forward, he vowed they would leave at once, return to their modest estate in Virginia, and never speak to her again.

George, however, had another plan—one far more daring. “Mother,” he said, “cannot marry a man who has wounded or killed one of us—or whom we have wounded or killed. We must challenge him, Harry.”

Harry saw the wisdom in this instantly. “You’re right, George,” he agreed. “She wouldn’t marry our murderer. And if we wound him, the matter is settled. *Cadit quaestio*, as Mr. Dempster used to say. Shall I send my boy to deliver a challenge to Colonel Washington now?”

“My dear Harry,” replied George, recalling with pride his own duel at Quebec, “you’re not yet accustomed to matters of honour.”

“No,” admitted Harry, sighing as he looked up at his elder brother with admiration and envy.

“We cannot insult a gentleman in our own home,” George continued, speaking with quiet dignity. “The laws of honour forbid it. But we may ride out with him, and once beyond the park gates, we can make our feelings known.”

“That we will!” exclaimed Harry, gripping his brother’s hand. “And that we shall. I say, Georgy…” His face flushed red, and George asked what he wanted.

“This is my turn,” Harry pleaded. “If you go on the campaign, I should be allowed the other affair. Truly, I ought to.”

“Again, the head of the house must lead,” George said with regal composure. “If I fall, Harry, you shall avenge me. But I must fight Colonel Washington. He is mine especially. Was it not he who advised my mother to order that wretch Ward to lay hands on me?”

“Ah, George,” interjected the more gentle Harry, “you ought to forgive and forget.”

“Forgive?” George retorted sharply. “Never, while I live. One cannot command a man to forget. A wrong today remains a wrong tomorrow. I’ve never done harm to another, and I won’t suffer it, if I can help it. I despise Mr. Ward, but I don’t think he’ll ever forgive you for that blow with the ruler. Colonel Washington is our enemy—mine above all. He has already advised one injustice against me, and now plots a greater. We must punish him.”

The old Bordeaux wine had set George’s usually pale face aflame. Harry, ever devoted to his brother, admired his boldness and eloquence, and quietly prepared to follow his lead. They retired to bed, George giving strict instructions to Harry to remain courteous to all guests still under their mother’s roof the next day.

Courtesy and discretion prevent us from revealing which of Madam Esmond’s guests succumbed first to the generous hospitality of Castlewood. The descendants of Messrs. Talmadge and Danvers, aides-de-camp to the General, might prefer not to hear how their ancestors were once overcome by drink; yet the men themselves took no shame in it, and likely indulged just as freely in their own time. Let us imagine them staggering to their beds, supported by sympathetic servants; and the General himself, too robust a drinker to yield to mere bottles of wine, escorted by the young gentlemen to his room, soon asleep under Bacchus’s tender care. Madam Esmond observed the state of her guests without surprise or dismay. She rose early, preparing cooling drinks for their parched throats, which her servants delivered to each chamber.

At breakfast, one English officer teased Mr. Franklin, who refused both wine and the morning toddy, saying he missed two pleasures: the drink and the remedy. The young man laughed and proposed continuing both. Colonel Washington, the General’s new American aide, remained sober and composed. The British officers insisted they must teach him the ways of the English army, but the Virginian replied gravely that he had no desire to learn that part of military education.

Busy with the aftermath of the grand dinner and breakfast, Madam Esmond had little time to observe her sons closely—but she noticed that George was exceptionally polite to Colonel Washington, as to every guest.

Before departing, General Braddock held a private audience with Madam Esmond, formally offering to take her son into his household. When arrangements for George’s departure were finalized between mother and future commander, Madam Esmond showed no signs of fear, though she surely felt the risks her son faced. She knew he must enter the world and meet its trials. “Mr. Braddock is a fine gentleman in the morning,” she told her aide-de-camp, Mrs. Mountain, “and though my father did not drink, many of England’s finest do.” The cheerful General shook hands with George after the interview, welcoming him and instructing him to report to Frederick in three days—shortly before the expedition began.

Now the great coach was summoned again. The General’s escort pranced around it, and the other guests mounted their horses. Madam Esmond saw the General to the steps of the veranda, followed by her sons, who stood beside the coach door. The guard trumpeter blew a sharp note, the negroes shouted “Huzzay, and God save the King,” and General Braddock, graciously thanking his hosts, rolled away toward headquarters.

As the boys ascended the steps, Colonel Washington was once more taking leave of their mother. No doubt she had urged him to protect George. “With my life,” the Colonel assured her. “You may depend on me.” Then, turning to the lads, he said, “Farewell, my dear Harry. With you, George, it is no adieu—we shall meet again in three days at the camp.”

Both young men were headed into danger—perhaps death. Colonel Washington was leaving, and she would see him no more before the war began. It was no wonder the widow was deeply moved.

George Warrington watched his mother’s emotion and interpreted it with a cold, bitter scorn. “Stay yet a moment,” he said steadily, “to comfort our mother. Only time to get booted, and my brother and I will ride with you a short way, George.” His horses had already been saddled. Soon the three young men were riding together, their negro grooms following behind. Mrs. Mountain, knowing she had stirred trouble between them and fearing the outcome, felt relieved that Mr. Washington left without a quarrel—without even a declaration of love to the widow.

No man could appear more courteous than George Warrington to his namesake, the Colonel. The latter was surprised and pleased by the change in his young friend. The shared peril, the necessity of future alliance, the long-standing bond between the Colonel and the Esmond family, and the tender farewells exchanged with the mistress of Castlewood all softened his heart. He chose to overlook past grievances and grew more friendly than ever. George was bright and cheerful; it was Harry who seemed melancholy, riding silently beside his brother, keeping his distance from Colonel Washington, who had once drawn him close.

They discussed the war and the likely outcome—none doubted its success. Two thousand seasoned British troops, led by their commander, could not fail against any force the French could muster, so long as they moved promptly. The passionate young Virginian held deep respect for the discipline and courage of regular soldiers. King George II had no more loyal subject than the General’s new aide-de-camp.

They rode amicably until reaching Benson’s, a rough log tavern where, by custom, guests paid for food and shelter. Here was also a recruiting station, and several officers and men of Halkett’s regiment were gathered. Colonel Washington assumed his young friends would bid him farewell here.

While their horses were watered, they entered the common room, where a simple meal awaited those who wished to eat. George Warrington entered with a particularly lively air, while Harry looked pale and distressed.

“One would think, Squire Harry,” remarked Benson, “that you were the one leaving home to fight the French and Indians, not Mr. George.”

“I may fear for my brother’s safety,” Harry replied, “though I could bear my own well enough. It’s not my fault I stay behind.”

“No indeed, brother,” George added.

“Harry Warrington’s courage needs no proof!” declared Colonel Washington.

“You do the family honor by speaking so well of us, Colonel,” said George, bowing low. “I dare say we can hold our own, if need be.”

Yet as George boasted, Harry looked far from brave. When their eyes met, he read in George’s gaze a terrible certainty. “You’re not going to do it now?” he whispered.

“Yes, now,” George answered, steady as stone.

“For God’s sake, let me have the chance. You’re going to the campaign—you shouldn’t have everything. There might be a misunderstanding, George. We may be wrong.”

“Psha,” George scoffed. “How can we be? It must be done now. Don’t worry. No names will be mentioned—I’ll find a suitable excuse.”

Two officers of Halkett’s regiment, known to the brothers, sat beneath the porch, the Virginian toddy-bowl before them.

“What are you plotting, gentlemen?” called one. “Is it a drink?”

Their voices were thick, their faces flushed—clearly they had already drunk heavily.

“The very thing,” George said cheerfully. “Fresh glasses, Mr. Benson! What, no glasses? Then we’ll drink from the bowl.”

“Many a good man has drunk from it,” Benson said. One by one, the boys bowed to their military acquaintances and touched the bowl with their lips. The liquor seemed undiminished, though George, in particular, acted like a seasoned drinker, declaring it delicious after their ride. He called to Colonel Washington, who stood at the porch, inviting him to join.

The tone was disrespectful—echoing the manner George had lately adopted, which had irritated the Colonel. The Colonel bowed and said he was not thirsty.

“Nay, the liquor is paid for,” George insisted. “Don’t fear, Colonel.”

“I said I wasn’t thirsty,” the Colonel replied, drumming his foot. “I didn’t say the liquor was paid for.”

“When the King’s health is proposed,” George cried, “an officer cannot refuse. I drink to His Majesty, gentlemen. Colonel Washington, you may drink or not. The King!”

This was a point of military honor. Captain Grace and Mr. Waring of Halkett’s both drank “The King.” Harry Warrington drank “The King.” Colonel Washington, eyes blazing, took a small sip from the bowl.

Then Captain Grace proposed “The Duke and the Army”—a toast no one could ignore. The Colonel had to swallow it.

“You don’t seem to enjoy the toast, Colonel,” George remarked.

“I tell you again,” the Colonel replied, “I don’t want to drink. It seems to me the Duke and the Army would serve better if their healths weren’t toasted so often.”

“You’re not yet familiar with regular troops’ customs,” Captain Grace said, voice slurred.

“Perhaps not, sir.”

“A British officer,” Grace continued, “never neglects such a toast. A man who refuses to drink the Duke’s health—hang me, such a man should be tried by court-martial!”

“What means this language to me?” roared Colonel Washington, slamming his fist on the table. “You are drunk, sir!”

“A cursed provincial officer says I’m drunk!” shrieked Captain Grace. “Waring, do you hear that?”

“I heard it, sir!” George Warrington cried. “We all heard it. He came at my invitation—the liquor was mine, the table was mine—and I am shocked to hear such language directed at my esteemed guest, Captain Waring.”

“Confound your impudence, you infernal young jackanapes!” bellowed the Colonel. “You dare insult me before British officers and criticize my words? For months I’ve borne your insolence—if I hadn’t loved your mother—yes, sir, and your grandfather and your brother—I would—”

His words failed him. The Colonel stood speechless, face purple, limbs trembling with rage.

“You would what, sir?” George asked calmly. “If you didn’t love my grandfather, my brother, or my mother? You’re using her affection as an excuse for your behavior. What would you do, sir, may I ask again?”

“I would put you across my knee and whip you, you snarling little puppy!” the Colonel shouted, regaining breath. “That’s what I’d do!”

“Because you’ve known us all our lives and made our home your own,” Harry burst out, rising, “is no reason to insult either of us! What you said, George Washington, is an insult to both of us. You will apologize, sir!”

“Apologize?”

“Or give us the reparation due to gentlemen,” Harry continued.

The Colonel’s heart sank. He realized he might be forced to draw blood from one of the boys he cherished. As Harry stood before him—fair-haired, flushed, voice quivering—a wave of tenderness swept over him. “I—I am bewildered,” he said. “My words may have been hasty. What has George been meaning these past months? Tell me, and perhaps—”

But evil stirred within young George Warrington. His dark eyes burned with hatred. “You’re avoiding the question, sir,” he said, “just as you avoided the toast. I am not a child to endure your arrogance. You publicly insulted me in public, and I demand satisfaction.”

“In Heaven’s name, be it!” the Colonel said, grief etched on his face.

“And you insulted me!” Captain Grace cried, swaying toward him. “What did he say? Confound the militia captain—colonel, what is he? You insulted me! Oh, Waring! To think I should be insulted by a militia captain!” Tears welled in the Captain’s eyes.

“I insult you, you hog!” the Colonel roared, unmoved by humor.

And then a fourth adversary appeared.

“Great Powers, sir!” Captain Waring exclaimed. “Are three affairs not enough? Must I join in too? You have a quarrel with these two young gentlemen.”

“Hasty words, sir!” Harry repeated.

“Hasty words, sir!” Waring echoed. “A gentleman tells another he’ll put him across his knees and whip him, and you call that hasty? If anyone said to me, ‘Charles Waring,’ or ‘Captain Waring, I’ll put you across my knees and whip you,’ I’d drive my cheese-toaster through his body, even if he were as big as Goliath. That’s one affair with young Mr. George Warrington. Mr. Harry, of course, will stand by his brother—that’s two. Between Grace and the Colonel, apology is impossible. And now—run me through the body—you call an officer of my regiment—of Halkett’s, sir—a hog before my face! Great heavens, sir! Mr. Washington, are you all like this in Virginia? Excuse me, I mean no offense, but by Gad, Colonel! I must tell you—you are the most quarrelsome man I’ve ever seen. Call a disabled officer of my regiment—disabled, ain’t you, Grace?—call him a hog before me! You withdraw it, sir—withdraw it!”

“Is this some conspiracy against me?” the Colonel thundered. “It seems I’m drunk, and not you, as you all are. I withdraw nothing. I apologize for nothing. By heavens! I will meet one or half a dozen of you—young or old, drunk or sober!”

“I do not wish to hear more insults,” George Warrington said coldly. “This affair can proceed without further offense from you. When will you give me the meeting?”

“The sooner the better, sir!” the Colonel snapped.

“The sooner the better!” hiccupped Captain Grace, swearing profusely—oaths being the natural seasoning of gentlemanly talk in those days. He staggered to his feet, reached for his sword, and fell as he grasped it. “The sooner the better!” he yelled from the ground, waving his weapon and knocking his hat over his eyes.

“At least this gentleman’s business will wait till tomorrow,” the militia Colonel said, turning to the other officer. “You won’t bring your man out today, Captain Waring?”

“I confess neither his hand nor mine is steady,” Waring admitted.

“Mine is!” George Warrington declared, glaring at his enemy.

The Colonel, equally fierce, replied, “Be it so—what weapon, sir?”

“Not small-swords, Colonel. We can beat you with them. You know that from our old bouts. Pistols had better be the word.”

“As you please, George Warrington—and God forgive you, George! God pardon you, Harry! for dragging me into this quarrel,” the Colonel said, face heavy with sorrow.

Harry hung his head, but George remained calm. “I, sir? It was not I who called names, who spoke of a cane, who insulted a gentleman in public before army officers. This is not the first time you’ve treated me like a slave, talked of the whip for me.”

The Colonel recoiled, face crimson, struck by sudden memory.

“Great heavens, George! Is it that boyish quarrel you still remember?”

“Who made you the overseer of Castlewood?” George spat, grinding his teeth. “I am not your slave, George Washington, and I never will be. I hated you then, and I hate you now. You insulted me, and I am a gentleman, as you are. Is that not enough?”

“Too much, only too much,” the Colonel said, grief written on his face. “Do you bear malice too, Harry? I never thought this of you!”

“I stand by my brother,” Harry said, turning away from the Colonel’s gaze, clasping George’s hand.

The sadness in their adversary’s face did not fade. “Heaven be good to us!” he muttered. “It’s all clear now.” He turned to George. “I’ll write a few letters, and I am at your service, Mr. Warrington.”

“You have your pistols at your saddle. I rode without any, but I’ll send Sady back for mine. That gives you enough time, Colonel Washington?”

“Plenty of time, sir.” Each bowed deeply. George linked arms with his brother and walked away. The Colonel looked at the two helpless captains, one propped by Captain Benson, the tavern keeper.

“It’s not entirely their fault, Colonel,” Benson said grimly. “Jack Firebrace and Tom Humbold of Spotsylvania were here this morning, trading horses with them. Jack and Tom got them to play cards—and they lost. Then Jack and Tom challenged them to drink for Old England’s honor—and they lost that, too. They’re kind-hearted when sober, but they’re a pair of fools now.”

“Captain Benson,” the Colonel said, “you’re an old frontier man, once an officer of ours. Will you help me in this matter with the young gentlemen?”

“I’ll stand by and see fair play, Colonel. I won’t interfere, but I’ll see justice done. Madam Esmond has helped me many times—tended my wife in childbirth, cured our Betty in the fever. You won’t be too hard on those boys, will you? Though I’ve seen both of them shoot—the fair one hunts well, as you know, but the older one’s a wonder at an ace of spades.”
