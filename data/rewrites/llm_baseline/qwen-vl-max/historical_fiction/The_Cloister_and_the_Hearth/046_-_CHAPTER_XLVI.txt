Catherine was a woman of quiet ways, seldom leaving home save when necessity pressed. Even then, some small mishap always seemed to follow—spilled milk, a dropped pot, or a child’s cry that broke the rhythm of her day. She felt keenly aware of this tendency, and so she delayed her planned visit to Sevenbergen from one morning to the next, watching for a lull in the household’s endless demands. But on the very afternoon when everything might have gone smoothly, Peter Buyskens’ mule—a creature of vital importance—was out of commission.

At last, one day, Eli addressed her before the whole family: “Is it true, wife, that you’ve thought of going to see Margaret Brandt?”

“Ay,” she replied, not flinching.

“Then I forbid it.”

“Oh? Do you?”

“I do.”

“And so there’s no more to be said?” she asked, her cheeks flushing with a quiet defiance.

“Not a word,” Eli answered, his voice firm.

Later, alone with her daughter Kate, Catherine spoke with sharper self-criticism than she had ever shown toward Eli.

“It was my own fault,” she confessed. “I should have gone like a cat sneaking after a robin—quiet, swift, unobserved. But no—there I go, blabbing about it as if every soul in the house must know. Like a foolish hen who cackles before laying an egg, calling all the hens to rob her of it. Next time, child, if you and I wish to do anything that might stir talk, let us do it at once, without thought or speech. Then, if we are blamed, we can say, ‘We knew nothing, we meant no harm.’ Who would have guessed such things? And yet, for two pins, I’d march straight into their midst, bold as brass.”

Kate stared, stunned by her mother’s sudden fire. “But mother, surely father will come around in time.”

“And so will Michaelmas,” Catherine retorted, “but when? And I was so set on your seeing this girl. We could have spoken together, weighed her worth. Say what they may, no one judges another rightly unless they’ve seen them with their own eyes. And were I to have fifty sons, I’d never oppose one’s choice until I’d looked upon the woman myself. Had I done so, I might have seen the truth sooner—seen Quicksands clearly—before condemning Gerard’s fancy. Life is a school, and the lesson never ends. We mend one fault, only to fall into another. We stumble here, then there, till we finally stumble into our graves—and that’s the end.”

“Mother,” Kate whispered, her voice trembling.

“What now? No good news, by the look of you. What has frightened you so?”

“A confession,” Kate murmured, barely audible.

“Ah!” Catherine smiled, proud. “A noble word—our Gerard taught you that, I’ll wager. Speak then, out with it.”

“Well,” Kate began, “sooth to say, I’ve seen her.”

“And?”

“And spoken with her too.”

“And never told me?” Catherine gasped. “After this, wonders are dust.”

“Mother,” Kate pleaded, “you were so against her. I waited—until I could speak without making you worse.”

“Ay,” Catherine sighed, half sorrowful, half bitter. “Like mother, like daughter. Cowardice is our curse. I beat the others sometimes—how else would the house hold together? But you, my dear, did I ever harshly speak to you? Did I ever scold or frown? And yet—” She stopped, sensing tears welling. “No, I won no right to weep. You had your reasons. Rise up, brave heart, and tell me all. Better late than never. First—when did you go to Sevenbergen? With your crutches? And I knew nothing?”

“I never went,” Kate insisted. “Never in my life. And though I confess I longed to see her, I never sought her.”

“There now,” Catherine said, relieved. “Did I not say it was unlike you to act without me? Come, tell me—what happened?”

So Kate began. “It came to my ears—how, I cannot say, nor will I tell—only that Gerard was imprisoned in the Stadthouse tower.”

“Ah,” Catherine breathed.

“By father’s order, they claimed.”

Catherine exhaled a sigh that was almost a moan. “Blacker than I thought.”

Giles and I went out at night to comfort him,” Kate continued. “And at the foot of the tower stood a young woman—strange to me, I swear—on the same errand.”

“Look at that now,” Catherine murmured.

“At first, we startled each other—such a place, such a name, and our heads full of devils. We turned pale under the moonlight. But then I said, ‘You are Margaret.’ ‘And you are Kate,’ she replied. Think on it!”

“Did one ever?” Catherine laughed. “It was Gerard! He’s been talking of you to her, and her to him.”

In reply, Kate gave her mother a kiss that held more meaning than words—the composite kiss: one touch, yet saying:

1. Quite correct.
2. Clever mother, for guessing so swiftly.
3. How sweet to be of one mind again, as we always have been.
4. And more.

“Now then,” Catherine said, “speak freely, child. Gerard is not here. Alas, what am I saying? Would that he were.”

“Well, mother,” Kate said softly, “she is fair, and her picture does her little wrong.”

“Eh, dear,” Catherine scoffed gently. “Young people care for looks. I care for deeds. Does she love my boy as he deserves?”

“Sevenbergen is farther from the Stadthouse than we are,” Kate said thoughtfully. “Yet she reached it before I did.”

Catherine nodded slowly.

“More than that,” Kate added, “she had already freed him before I arrived. Down from the tower, where he was captive.”

Catherine shook her head. “The tallest tower for miles! Impossible.”

“But it is true,” Kate insisted. “She brought an old man with her. Together, they sent a rope up to his window. It dangled down. Giles climbed it. When I first saw it hanging, I thought it was magic. But then—her arms around me, her chest beating against mine, her cheeks wet with tears—I knew. It wasn’t magic. It was love. For she is not like me—stronger, bolder. And even I, poor frail creature, feel sometimes I could move mountains for those I love. I love you, mother. And she loves Gerard.”

“God bless her,” Catherine whispered. “God bless her.”

“But—”

“But what, lamb?”

“Her love—can we be sure it is honest? It warms your heart, but it cools mine. Poor soul. She is not a wife, you know, mother, not truly.”

“Humph,” Catherine grunted. “They stood at the altar together.”

“Ay,” Kate said, “but they came as they left—maid and bachelor.”

“The parson said so?”

“No, I don’t know.”

“Then I’ll take no man’s word but his in such a tangled matter.” After a pause, she added, “Still, you’re right, girl. I shall go to Sevenbergen alone. I am a wife, not a slave. We are all in darkness here. She holds the clue. I must question her—alone. Not you. I’ll not bring any lily to a house with a stain, not even to a palace of gold and silver.”

The more Catherine pondered, the more she found herself drawn to Margaret. And curiosity—always a strong force—burned within her, especially in one who had little time for books. At last, on a bright afternoon after dinner, she whispered to Kate, “Keep the house from falling apart, if you can,” and dressed in her best kirtle, hood, scarlet clocked hose, and new shoes. Then she set off briskly for Sevenbergen, without troubling anyone’s mule.

When she arrived, she asked where Margaret Brandt lived. The first man she met shook his head. “Name’s strange to me.”

She walked a bit farther and asked a girl of fifteen standing at a doorway.

“Father,” the girl called into the house, “here’s another seeking the magician’s daughter.”

The man stepped out. “Peter Brandt’s cottage is just outside town, east side. You can see the chimney from here.” He pointed. “But you won’t find them. Father and daughter both left this week—bless you.”

“Say not so,” Catherine urged. “I’ve walked all the way from Tergou.”

“From Tergou? Then you must have met the soldier.”

“What soldier? Ay, I did meet a soldier.”

“Well, that soldier came looking for Margaret.”

“And wasn’t furious when he heard she was gone?” the girl chimed in. “His long beard and her cheek aren’t strangers, I warrant.”

“Hold your tongue,” Catherine snapped. “Too young to slander elders. Tell me more about this soldier, good man.”

“Nay,” the man replied, “all I know is he came seeking Margaret. I told him they’d fled under cover of night, and some say the devil carried them off—magicians, you know. He said, ‘May the devil carry you off for your ill news.’ That was my thanks. Then he said, ‘I doubt it’s a lie.’ I said, ‘If you think so, go and see.’ He did. Burst out with a string of gibberish—my wife thought curses—and rushed to the cottage. Came back singing a different tune. ‘You were right, I was wrong,’ he said, and shoved a silver coin into my hand. Show it to your wife, some of you; then she’ll believe me. I’ve been called a liar once today.”

“It needs not,” Catherine said, inspecting the coin anyway.

“And he seemed quiet, sad, didn’t he?” she asked the girl.

“That he did,” the girl replied warmly. “And, dame, he was the handsomest man I’ve ever seen. Cheeks like a rose, shining beard, eyes like sloes.”

“I saw he was well-bearded,” Catherine said. “But at my age, I don’t scan men as I once did. Still, he was civil—doffed his hat as if I were a queen, and I bowed low in return. Manners beget manners. But little did I know he was her light of love—or most likely the—Who bakes for this town?”

The man blinked, surprised by the sudden turn.

“Well, dame,” he said, “two: John Bush and Eric Donaldson. Both live in this street.”

“Then God be with you,” Catherine said, and moved on. But her step had lost its spring. She no longer tapped the ground with quick jerks and lifted her heel. She asked the bakers if Peter Brandt had left in debt. Bush said they weren’t customers. Donaldson said, “Not a stiver. His daughter paid me the very night they left. Didn’t believe they owed a copper.”

Thus Catherine gathered all she needed with ease.

“Can you tell me what sort of girl Margaret was?” she asked as she turned to leave.

“Well,” said one baker, “a bit too reserved for my taste. I like a chatty customer—when I’m not busy. But she had a good name. A dutiful daughter.”

“That’s no small praise,” Catherine said. “A fair face, they say?”

“Why, where do you come from, wife?”

“Tergou.”

“Oh. Well, judge for yourself. The boys called her ‘the beauty of Sevenbergen.’ The girls mocked it merrily, pulled her to pieces, found so many faults none could agree which was worst.”

“That is enough,” Catherine said. “I see—the bakers are no fools here, and the young women no shallower than elsewhere.”

She bought a manchet of bread—not just for sympathy (for she kept a shop), but to show her household how much better theirs was daily—and returned to Tergou, heavy-hearted.

Kate met her outside the town, eyes bright.

“Well, Kate, lass, it is a happy thing I went. I am heartbroken. Gerard has been cruelly wronged. The child is not ours. Nor shall the mother be welcome from this hour.”

“Alas, mother,” Kate said, puzzled. “I do not understand.”

“Ask no more,” Catherine said sharply. “Never mention her name to me again. That is all.”

Kate sighed humbly and they walked home together.

Inside, they found a soldier seated calmly by the hearth. The moment they entered, he rose and saluted them politely. They stood still—Kate with mild surprise, Catherine with sharp indignation.

“What brings you here?” she demanded.

“I came to seek Margaret.”

“We know no such person.”

“Surely, dame, you know her—Margaret Brandt.”

“We’ve heard of her—enough to regret it.”

“Come, dame, tell me at least where she lives.”

“I know not, and care less.”

Denys sensed the lie. He clenched his jaw. “Well, I expected this place to be enemy ground. But perhaps if you knew all, you wouldn’t be so cold.”

“I do know all,” Catherine snapped. “This morning I knew nothing.” Then, suddenly setting her hands on her hips, she raised her voice, flashing eyes blazing. “How dare you sit by *this* hearth? This very hearth—of all hearths in the world!”

“May Satan carry your hearth to the lake of fire and brimstone!” Denys shouted, fluent in Flemish. “Your servant told me to wait till you came. Else I’d never have troubled your hearth. My curse on it—and on the churlish roof that greets an innocent stranger thus!” He strode to the door, scowling.

“Oh! oh!” Catherine cried, frightened—and slightly ashamed. The fierce woman collapsed, sank into a chair, and burst into tears. Kate followed quietly, but swiftly.

A shrewd writer—now lost to us—once wrote:

She: “I feel all a woman’s weakness.”
He: “Then you are invincible.”

Denys, unknowingly, proved that truth. He stood at the door, staring ruefully at the ruin his words had wrought.

“Nay, wife,” he said, “weep not for a soldier’s rash word. I meant not all I said. Your house is yours. What claim have I to it? There—now, I’ll go.”

“What is to be done?” a deep, calm voice asked.

It was Eli. He had just come in from the shop.

“Here is a ruffian who scolded your women and made them weep,” Denys explained.

“Little Kate, what is it?” Eli asked sensibly. “Ruffians don’t usually call themselves ruffians.”

Before she could answer, Catherine cut in: “Hold your tongue, girl. Muriel told him to sit. I didn’t know. I accused him, and he was leaving, cursing us root and branch. I’ve never been so cursed in all my days—oh! oh! oh!”

“You were both to blame,” Eli said calmly. “You and he. But what the servant says, the master must stand by. We keep no open house, yet we’re not so poor we’d deny a seat to a traveler with a honest face—and, I think, a wounded man. So, end all malice. Sit down.”

“Wounded?” mother and daughter exclaimed together.

“Think you a soldier slings his arm for sport?”

“Nay,” Denys said cheerfully, “just an arrow.”

“But an arrow?” Kate whispered, horrified. “Where were our eyes, mother?”

“Nay, a trifle,” Denys said. “Which, however, I beg you to accept as excuse for my sharpness. These little wounds fret a man. Why, sweeter temper than our Gerard never breathed—yet when the bear tore a piece from his calf, he turned so hot and angry, you’d have sworn he was no son of yours, but born of Sir John Pepper and Dame Mustard. Who is this? A dwarf? Your servant, Master Giles.”

“Your servant, soldier,” roared the newcomer.

Denys started. He hadn’t expected a petard.

Denys’s words surprised his hosts—but not nearly as much as their behavior now. All three crept toward him, lips parted, eyes wide, as if he were some rare creature.

Amazement gave way to growing agitation.

“Hush!” Eli said. “Let none speak but me. Young man,” he said solemnly, “in God’s name, who are you? One who knows us though we know you not? Who speaks of our absent son—our rebellious son—whose heart we break for?”

“What, master,” Denys said, lowering his voice, “has he not written? Has he not told you of me—Denys of Burgundy?”

“He wrote—three lines. Named neither Denys nor any stranger.”

“Ay, I remember—he wrote a long letter to his sweetheart, this Margaret. She’s fled—plague take her—and now I don’t know how to find her.”

“She is not your sweetheart?”

“Who, dame? If you please.”

“Why, Margaret Brandt.”

“How can my comrade’s sweetheart be mine? I know her not from Noah’s niece. I’ve never seen her.”

“Silence, Kate,” Eli said impatiently. “Let the young man answer. How did you come to know Gerard, our son? Think of a parent’s pain. Answer me plainly, like a soldier.”

“And I will. I was discharged at Flushing, bound for Burgundy. On the German frontier, I stayed at the same inn as Gerard. I liked him. I said, ‘Be my comrade.’ He hesitated—then agreed. We walked many weary leagues together. Never truer comrades. First I altered my path to stay with him—then he his to stay with me. We spoke of Sevenbergen, Tergou, this house—countless times. We faced troubles together—but shared them, they were light. I saved his life from a bear. He saved mine in the Rhine—swims like a duck, I like a stone. Once, at an inn in Burgundy, we held a room against seven cutthroats—crippled one, killed two. Your son fought like a man, faced the strongest champion I’ve ever known, and speared him like a sucking pig. Else I would not be here. But just when all was fair, and I was to see him safely aboard ship for Rome—came that son of a—Lord Anthony of Burgundy, with his men, marching to Flanders, in revolt. They tore us apart. Took me—gave me gold, but a broad arrow in my shoulder. Left Gerard alone. At parting, though I’m a soldier, I wept—salt tears. So did he. His last words: ‘Go, comfort Margaret!’ So here I am. Mine to him: ‘Forget Rome. Head for the Rhine, downstream home.’ Now tell me—did I advise well or ill?”

“Soldier,” Eli said, “take my hand.” His lip trembled. “God bless thee. God bless thee.” That was all. But more than words.

Catherine said nothing. She darted from the room, bade Muriel bring the finest food, returned with arms full of wood, piled the fire, took out a snow-white cloth from the press, and hurried to lay it for Gerard’s friend—then suddenly sat down, strength draining from her body.

“Father!” Kate cried, her eyes sharp with love.

Denys leapt up. But Eli waved him back and splashed water sharply on Catherine’s face. Instantly, she gasped. “So sudden. My poor boy!”

Eli whispered to Denys: “Don’t notice. She thinks of him night and day.” They pretended not to see. She shook it off, laid the cloth herself—but her hands trembled, and tears slipped down her cheeks.

They could not do enough for Denys. They fed him, filled his glass again and again. Around the fire—warm with flames, red wine, and eager eyes—he told all I have related, and countless details an artist omits.

But how different the effect on the listeners! To them, the story was already alive before the first word. It was all about Gerard. The man telling it had been warm with him, fought beside him. The flesh and blood around the fire quivered—feeling every danger, every peril.

I ask you, reader, to recall all you know of Gerard’s journey with Denys. Picture it now—in your mind’s eye—told by his comrade to a father stoic outside, torn inside; to a mother and sister, all love and fear. Close the book for a moment. Try to feel it.

Then you will not be surprised when I tell you that Giles crept in and curled before the fire, gazing at Denys with reverence almost dog-like. And when the rough soldier, unaware, revealed his true heart—especially his deep affection for Gerard—Kate, timid as a bird, stole her tiny hand into his huge brown palm. It rested like a spoonful of cream spilled on a platter. Then, gently, she pinched the ball of his thumb—her Kardiometer, a measure of emotion.

Fate is just—even to rival storytellers. Denys had to pay a tax I did not. Whenever Gerard was in danger, the women’s faces paled, their throats tightened. He had to spoil his tale—break suspense—for mercy’s sake. “Now, dame, wait till you hear the end. Ma’amselle, do not blanch so. Courage! It looks ugly, but you shall hear how we prevailed. Had he failed, and I near—would I be alive?”

Meanwhile, Kate’s Kardiometer registered emotion, pinching by scale. It was not a high-pressure engine—but all is relative. Denys soon learned the tender gamut. He knew when to pause, when to stretch suspense.

Once, when he fell into the Rhine and sank, he got pinched—to his surprise and delight. “Oho!” he thought. And, like an anatomist, “experimentum in corpore vili”—he stayed under for a quarter-hour, under pressure. Even when Gerard grabbed him, he lingered, swimming to the east bank first. Then, spotting officers, he trolled them a while, treading water, before turning and swimming back across. Only then, ashamed to admit himself a pike, did he land—exhausted. The pressure released.

It was eleven o’clock—an unheard-of hour—but no one noticed. Denys still had much to tell when the door opened quietly, and in crept Cornelis and Sybrandt, looking sullen. They had drunk the last of their mysterious funds.

Catherine feared Eli would rebuke them before Denys. But he only looked sadly at them and motioned them to sit.

Denys, however, seemed unsettled. He frowned, studied them thoughtfully. Then turned to Catherine. “What say you, dame? The rest tomorrow. I’m weary. It grows late.”

“So be it,” Eli said. But when Denys rose to leave, Catherine stopped him. “And think you to sleep elsewhere? Gerard’s room has been ready for hours. The sheets—I’ll not boast—since I spun the flax and wove the web.”

“Then I’ll lie in them blindfold,” Denys replied gallantly. “Ah, dame, our poor Gerard loved fine linen. He couldn’t forgive the Germans their coarse flax. And when our countrymen erred, he’d excuse them: ‘Well, well—bonnes toiles sont en Bourgogne.’ Good lenten cloths in Burgundy. But he surpassed all in wit and cleanliness.”
