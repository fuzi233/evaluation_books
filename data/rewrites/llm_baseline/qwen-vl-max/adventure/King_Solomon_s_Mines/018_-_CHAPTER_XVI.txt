It was dark by the third day after the events of the previous chapter when we made camp in a cluster of huts at the base of the “Three Witches”—the name given to the trio of jagged peaks that marked the end of Solomon’s Great Road. Our party now consisted of just three of us—Sir Henry, Good, and myself—accompanied by Foulata, who attended to our needs, especially those of Good; Infadoos, the loyal but wary Kukuana warrior; Gagool, the ancient witch carried in a litter where she muttered curses all day long; and a small group of guards and servants.

The mountains rose before us like a monstrous triangle, their mass formed from a single violent upheaval of the earth. One peak stood to our right, another to our left, and the third loomed directly ahead, piercing the sky with its icy crown. I shall never forget the sight that greeted us the next morning as dawn painted the world in gold. High above, the peaks soared into the blue, wrapped in swirling snows like gauzy veils. Below the snowline, purple heather clung to the slopes, covering wild moors that stretched upward toward the heavens. Directly in front of us, Solomon’s Great Road wound uphill like a silver ribbon, leading straight to the foot of the central peak—about five miles distant—and then abruptly ending. This was its final point.

I leave it to the reader’s imagination to grasp the intensity of our excitement as we began our march that morning. At last, after weeks of hardship and peril, we were nearing the legendary mines—the very place that had claimed the life of the old Portuguese Dom centuries ago, my dear friend, his doomed descendant, and now, we feared, George Curtis, Sir Henry’s brother. Would we fare any better? Gagool had warned us: “Evil befell them. Will it also befall you?” And though I usually dismissed such talk as superstition, something about this place stirred a deep unease in me—and I sensed it in Good and Sir Henry too.

For over an hour, we marched swiftly along the heather-lined road, so eager that even Gagool’s bearers struggled to keep pace. From within her litter, she called out, her voice rasping through the grass flaps:
“Walk more slowly, white men! Why run to meet the evil that awaits you? You seek treasure, but what do you truly desire?”

She peered out, her wrinkled face twisted in a grotesque grin, her eyes gleaming with malice. Then came that chilling laugh—dry, hollow, and full of menace—that sent a shiver down my spine and drained the fire from our spirits for a moment.

But onward we went, driven by curiosity and fate. Soon, we saw it—a vast circular pit, three hundred feet deep and half a mile around, its sloping sides rising like a natural amphitheater.
“Can’t you guess what this is?” I asked Sir Henry and Good, who stared in stunned silence.

They shook their heads.
“Then you’ve never seen Kimberley’s diamond diggings,” I said. “This is Solomon’s Diamond Mine. Look at the layers of blue clay clinging to the walls—exactly like the Kimberley pipes. And there,” I pointed to a series of worn stone slabs arranged on a gentle slope beneath an ancient watercourse, “those are washing tables—no doubt about it.”

At the edge of this immense crater—precisely the spot marked on the old Portuguese map—the Great Road split into two paths that curved around the pit. In places, the path was built entirely of stone blocks, clearly intended to reinforce the rim and prevent collapses.

We followed the path, drawn forward by the mystery of three towering figures we could glimpse beyond the chasm. As we neared, we realized they were colossal statues—gigantic seated forms, each about thirty feet tall, perched on dark stone pedestals carved with crude symbols of ancient worship. They were separated by forty paces, facing outward across the plain toward Loo.

There were two male figures and one female. The woman, nude and beautifully sculpted though weather-worn, bore crescent-shaped horns upon her brow. The male figures were draped in robes—one with a serene, almost noble expression, the other with a terrifying, devilish countenance.
“This,” Sir Henry murmured, “is the calm of inhuman cruelty—the kind the ancients attributed to gods who could watch mankind suffer without pity.”

These silent guardians, known to the Kukuanas as the “Silent Ones,” sat in eternal stillness, watching over the land. I could not help wondering who had built them, who had dug the mine, who had laid this road.

Then it struck me—Solomon, according to Scripture, had turned to strange gods: Ashtoreth, Chemosh, and Milcom. Could these statues represent them?
“Perhaps,” said Sir Henry, a scholar of classical languages. “Ashtoreth was Astarte to the Phoenicians, and she was often depicted with horned brows—just like this figure. Maybe a Phoenician overseer carved them during Solomon’s time.”

Before we could explore further, Infadoos arrived, saluting the Colossi with his spear. He asked whether we would enter the “Place of Death” immediately or wait until after midday. If we were ready, Gagool had offered to guide us.

It was only eleven o’clock, and curiosity burned too fiercely to delay. We decided to go at once. I suggested we take food and water, just in case we became trapped. Gagool’s litter was brought forward, and she emerged, leaning on a stick. Foulata, at my request, packed dried meat and water into a reed basket with a hinged lid.

Straight ahead, fifty paces from the backs of the Colossi, rose a sheer rock wall eighty feet high, sloping upward into the mountain’s heart. Gagool cast us one last malevolent grin and limped toward the wall, stopping before a narrow, arched doorway that looked like the entrance to a mine gallery.

She waited, grinning.
“Now, white men from the Stars—great warriors Incubu, Bougwan, Macumazahn the wise—are you ready? Behold! I am here to serve my king and show you the store of bright stones. _Ha! ha! ha!_”

“We are ready,” I replied.

“Good, good! Strengthen your hearts. You will see things that shake the soul. Will you come, Infadoos? You who betrayed your master?”

Infadoos frowned.
“I will not enter. It is not my place. But you, Gagool—restrain your tongue. I will hold you accountable for every harm done to my lords. If a hair is touched, even if you are fifty times a witch, you will die. Do you hear me?”

“I hear,” she chuckled. “I know you—you always loved grand words. Even as a child you threatened your own mother. That was only yesterday. But fear not—I live only to obey the king. I have served many kings… until they served me. _Ha! ha!_ Now I go to see their faces again—and Twala’s too! Come on!”

She pulled a large oil lamp from beneath her fur cloak—a gourd with a rush wick.
“Are you coming, Foulata?” Good asked in his rough Kitchen Kukuana.

“I fear, my lord,” she whispered.

“Then give me the basket.”

“No, my lord. Where you go, I go.”

“The deuce you will!” I thought to myself. That might prove awkward indeed.

Without hesitation, Gagool stepped into the dark passage, wide enough for two to walk abreast. We followed, guided only by the sound of her voice echoing ahead and the sudden flutter of wings overhead.

“Hullo! What was that?” Good shouted. “Something hit me in the face!”

“Bats,” I said. “Keep moving.”

After perhaps fifty paces, the passage began to grow faintly illuminated. Then, suddenly, we stood in one of the most astonishing places ever beheld by mortal eyes.

Imagine the largest cathedral you’ve ever seen—windowless, yet dimly lit from above by shafts of light piercing the roof. That was the cavern we entered—only far greater. The ceiling arched away a hundred feet overhead, and the space was wider and higher than any human-built structure. But the true wonder lay in the rows of colossal pillars lining the chamber—massive stalactites of pure white spar, some twenty feet thick at the base, rising gracefully to the vaulted roof.

Some were still forming. On the floor, broken columns resembled ancient Greek ruins; above, icicle-like points dangled from the ceiling, occasionally dropping water with a soft *plink* onto the growing formations below.
On one pillar, we found a crude carving: a mummy-like figure beside what appeared to be an Egyptian god. It was etched at about five feet from the ground—where idle workers often carve their names. Yet the column itself was only eight feet tall. Given that we heard a drop fall as we watched, and knowing the rate of growth, we calculated it must grow less than an inch per century—proof that this process had been ongoing for thousands of years.

Other stalagmites took curious shapes—a pulpit intricately laced like lacework, beasts frozen in stone, and delicate fanlike patterns resembling frost on glass. Smaller caves branched off like chapels, including one no bigger than a dollhouse—yet inside, water dripped, icicles hung, and tiny spar columns formed just as in the main hall.

We wanted to explore thoroughly, but Gagool had no interest in beauty. She only wanted to reach her destination. I was frustrated—especially because I hoped to discover how light reached this cave. Was it natural, or man-made? Had it been used in ancient times?

We resolved to return later and pressed on.

Gagool led us to the far end of the cavern, where a second doorway stood—square-topped, like an Egyptian temple.
“Are you prepared to enter the Place of Death, white men?” she asked, relishing our dread.

“Lead on, Macduff,” Good said solemnly, trying to appear brave—though we all felt it except Foulata, who clutched his arm.

“This is getting rather ghastly,” Sir Henry muttered, peering into the darkness.

“Come on, Quatermain—_seniores priores_,” he added, stepping aside for me to lead. I inwardly cursed him.

*Tap, tap,* went Gagool’s stick as she hurried ahead, chuckling. I hesitated, gripped by a premonition of horror.

“Come on, old fellow,” Good urged. “Or we’ll lose our guide.”

Reluctantly, I stepped forward. After twenty paces, we entered a gloomy chamber—forty feet long, thirty wide, thirty high—hollowed from solid rock by hand. It was dimmer than the outer cave. At first, I saw only a long stone table, a massive white figure at its head, and smaller white forms seated around it.

Then I noticed a brown shape seated on the table. My eyes adjusted, and I froze—my legs turning to ice. I tried to flee, but Sir Henry grabbed my collar and held me fast.

I am not easily frightened, nor superstitious—but this scene shattered me. If he hadn’t held me, I would have fled, no matter the cost. Even the promise of all Kimberley’s diamonds wouldn’t have brought me back.

Then Sir Henry’s eyes adjusted. He let go, wiping sweat from his brow. Good swore weakly. Foulata shrieked and clung to him. Only Gagool laughed—long and loud.

It was horrifying.

At the head of the table sat a colossal skeleton—fifteen feet tall—holding a great white spear aloft. One bony hand rested on the table, as if preparing to rise. Its skull grinned at us, jaw slightly open, eyes hollow, neck bent forward, vertebrae jutting like daggers. It was Death himself—carved from a single stalactite, perfectly detailed, anatomically flawless.

“Great heavens,” I whispered. “What is this?”

“And those things?” Good asked, pointing at the white figures around the table.

“And what is *that*?” Sir Henry demanded, pointing at the brown object.

“Hee! hee! hee!” Gagool cackled. “To those who enter the Hall of the Dead, evil comes. _Ha! ha!_”

“Come, Incubu, brave in battle—come and see the one you slew,” she croaked, grabbing Curtis’ coat and pulling him forward.

We followed. She stopped and pointed to the brown form on the table.

Sir Henry gasped. There, naked, the severed head resting on its knees, sat the corpse of Twala, the last king of the Kukuanas. His body was gaunt, skin shriveled, vertebrae protruding like spikes—his face a grim parody of Hamilton Tighe.

A thin, glassy film covered the corpse. We couldn’t understand it—until we saw water falling steadily from the ceiling, *drip! drop! drip!* onto the neck, running down the body, and seeping into a hole in the table.

Then I understood—Twala was being turned into a stalactite.

And the others—seated around the table—were the same. Once human, now petrified. Their bodies had been placed under the constant drip for centuries, transformed into ice-like spar. Through the translucent coating, their features remained faintly visible.

Twenty-seven royal corpses lined the table—each preserved forever. The last was Ignosi’s father. The practice must have begun four centuries ago, assuming each king was buried here.

But the giant skeleton—Death himself—was older. Carved from a single stalactite, it bore the marks of the same artist who shaped the Three Witches. Perhaps it inspired the custom. Or perhaps it was meant to frighten intruders away from the treasures beyond.

No one knows. But this is how it stands.

Such was the White Death. Such were the White Dead.
