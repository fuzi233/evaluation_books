Two weeks had passed since Newland Archer had last immersed himself in the intricacies of the law, his mind wandering as he sat in the quiet seclusion of his private office within the venerable firm of Letterblair, Lamson and Low. The soft rustle of papers on his desk and the distant murmur of hushed conversations outside served only to heighten his abstraction. Suddenly, the door slid open and Old Mr. Letterblair, the respected patriarch of the firm, beckoned him to enter.

As Archer rose from his chair, Mr. Letterblair gestured for him to take a seat, his hands steepled together in a gesture of deliberation. The senior partner's countenance was etched with lines of concern, his white whiskers quivering with each deliberate stroke as he sought to frame his words. Archer couldn't help but think of the family physician, struggling to diagnose a patient whose symptoms defied categorization.

"My dear sir," Mr. Letterblair began, his voice measured and calm, "I've summoned you to discuss a delicate matter. One that, for now, I'd rather not share with Messrs. Skipworth or Redwood." His words hung in the air, punctuated by the faint ticking of the clock on the mantle.

Archer's curiosity was piqued, though he knew better than to press for details. Instead, he awaited the older man's next words, his eyes fixed intently on the senior partner's face.

"For family reasons," Mr. Letterblair continued, his brow furrowed in a mixture of concern and puzzlement. "It concerns the Mingott family, specifically the Countess Olenska."

The name was a whispered echo in Archer's mind, a reminder of the evening he had spent in their box at the opera, surrounded by the glittering elite of New York society. He had seen her only once since then, and in that time, she had receded into the background, her presence supplanted by the radiant May Welland.

As he listened to Mr. Letterblair's words, Archer's thoughts turned to the prospect of being drawn into the affairs of the Mingotts, a family whose influence and expectations he had thus far managed to sidestep. He had dismissed the rumors of the Countess's divorce as mere gossip, and now, to be asked to involve himself in the matter, seemed a daunting task.
