Tito walked the ancient Florentine streets with a light, rhythmic step, the heavy dread of recent days momentarily evaporating like morning mist off a sun-baked olive grove. The city’s olfactory landscape—a robust, interwoven tapestry of woodsmoke, crushed juniper, and the rich, yeasty breath of fresh bread from the communal ovens—grounded him in the cyclical comfort of the present. He was going to see Romola. While her life of patient, cloistered devotion to her father’s antiquities had once seemed an irksome fast to him, Tito was still a creature of fresh youth and soft pulses. He possessed a profound, healthy appetite for the ordinary, sensory joys of human existence; he could never view life, or love, as mere utilitarian fuel. He had compromised his soul, yes, but the poison worked slowly, and for now, the vintage of his days tasted much the same. He intended to savor it, to maintain the delicate acoustic and social harmony he had cultivated with those he cared for. And he cared supremely for Romola. There was no woman in all Florence whose presence provided such profound sustenance. When she looked at him with her sincere hazel eyes, he was subdued by a delicious influence, as irresistible and vital as the rhythmic, musical vibrations that govern the seasonal planting of wheat, possessing the body so utterly that when the music ceases, one hungers for it to begin again.

As he trod the worn stone stairs to Bardo’s sanctuary, the thick walls buffering the chaotic noise of the commercial districts, the mere nearness of anticipation felt like the scent of roasting meats before a feast. 

“Welcome, Tito mio,” the old man’s voice resonated through the dim corridor before Tito had even spoken. The blind scholar’s acoustic ecology was meticulously attuned; he navigated the world through the micro-tones of footfalls and the shifting resonance of the air. There was a vital new vigor in his tone, a sap rising in old wood. “You have brought fresh manuscript, doubtless; but since we were talking last night, I have had new ideas: we must take a wider scope—we must go back upon our ancestral footsteps.”

Tito, offering a reverent, silent homage to Romola, went straight to Bardo’s chair. He placed his hand into the waiting palm, seating himself on the cross-legged leather stool that smelled of aged tannin and beeswax. 

“Yes,” Tito said, his voice modulating to match the quiet, measured soundscape of the study. “I have brought the new manuscript, but that can wait your pleasure. I have young limbs, and can walk back up the hill without any difficulty.” 

He did not look at Romola, but he could feel the warmth of her gaze, as palpable as the heat radiating from a well-tended hearth. 

“That is well said, my son,” Bardo replied. “And I perceive with gladness that you do not shrink from labor. It is too often the prize of glory without the dust of the race that attracts young ambition. But what says the Greek? ‘In the morning of life, work; in the mid-day, give counsel; in the evening, pray.’ For my mind was shut up as by a dam over parched agrarian soil; the plenteous waters lay dark and motionless. But you, my Tito, have opened a duct for them, and they rush forward to nourish the roots of my memory. We must go over our preliminary ground again. You mark what I am saying, Tito?”

Bardo’s jealous ear caught the slight rustle of parchment as Tito stooped to retrieve a fallen leaf. 

Tito might have chafed at the prospect of endlessly chewing over old texts, but within this quiet room, insulated from the city's modern industrial clamor, and with Romola near, he was floated along by waves of agreeable sensation. 

“Assuredly,” Tito murmured. “You wish to enlarge your comments on certain passages.”

“I wish to introduce an occasional *excursus*,” Bardo corrected, his voice vibrating with the passion of a meticulous historian fighting the erosion of cultural memory. “This is not a time for well-sifted learning to lie idle, especially when men like Calderino falsify citations to serve the ends of their own vanity. We will turn back to the passage from Thucydides. I wish to go through my notes on the Latin translation made by Lorenzo Valla. Valla is held in high honor, yet his imperial Latin forces the nuanced, subjective experiences of the original Greek into a dominant, sterile lexicon, stripping it of its mythic resonance. My castigations of his text will demonstrate how the subtle power dynamics of language can bury a native dialect. My Romola, thou wilt reach the needful volumes on the fifth shelf.”

Tito rose in unison with Romola, the rustle of their garments harmonizing perfectly. “I will reach them, if you will point them out,” he said, following her into the small adjoining room, an archival pantry lined with the preserved harvest of centuries.

“There they are,” Romola said, pointing upward into the gloom, smelling of dried sage and old vellum. “Every book is just where it was when my father ceased to see them.”

Tito stood close to her, savoring the intimate acoustic isolation of the small room. They had never been in this space alone together. 

“I hope,” she continued, her voice a low, melodic thrum, “I hope he will not weary you; this work provides him such profound sustenance.”

“And me too, Romola—if you will only let me say, I love you—if you will only think me worth loving a little.”

His confession was the softest murmur, an untranslatable breath of subjective experience. His dark face leaned near to hers, offering a beseeching tenderness. 

“I do love you,” she breathed. Her voice had never before sunk to such a quiet, private register, dipping below the dominant lexicon of their scholarly lives into a native dialect of the heart. For a long while, they existed in a pure, silent suspended animation, before she added, “I know *now* what it is to be happy.”

Their faces met. Dark curls mingled with rippling gold, rapid and irreversible as the blending of ancient, complex wines. Quick as lightning after that intoxicating taste, Tito stepped onto a projecting ledge and reached down the volumes. They remained silent, both recognizing that this first blissful experience of mutual consciousness was an exquisite nourishment, perfectly complete without the need for further translation into words.

They returned to the main study, their internal rhythms seamlessly integrated into the domestic routine.

“You have the volumes?” Bardo asked. “Get your pen ready, Romola. As Tito marks off the scholia, copy them without delay.”

They resumed their positions—Tito at the reading desk, Romola at the low table before him. The scratching of the pen formed a rhythmic, deeply comforting soundscape. Romola’s deep, calm happiness encompassed Tito like the rich, quiet evening light that filters through olive branches, dissipating all the harsh, discordant unrest of the outside world.

For two hours, they worked in this acoustic sanctuary, desisting only when the fading light dissolved the ink into shadows. Suddenly, the heavy door opened, shattering the room's delicate equilibrium.

In came a figure who disrupted the environment like a sudden, violent crash of commercial pottery. It was Monna Brigida, a short, stout woman of fifty, wearing a black velvet *berretta* embroidered with pearls. A surprising carmine tint on the upper region of her fat cheeks contrasted with her sallow skin, and her little fat right hand looked exactly as if it had been made of rich pastry dough, risen out of shape under partial baking. Her trailing black velvet gown and rose-colored damask sleeves rustled with an abrasive, ostentatious friction—a sonic testament to the superficial commercialization of Florence's textile trade. 

To Tito, her arrival was a familiar, if jarring, noise pollution; Monna Brigida was an excepted visitor, a cousin to Bardo’s dead wife. Romola smiled affectionately, immediately drawing a leather seat to a due distance from her father’s chair, mindful of protecting the old man's sensitive ears from the coming gush of loud gossip.

“*La cugina?*” Bardo asked interrogatively, detecting the rapid, shallow footfalls and the heavy sweep of drapery.

“Yes, it is your cousin,” Monna Brigida barked in an overly alert voice, raising her doughy fingers at Tito before lifting her face to be kissed by Romola. “Always the troublesome cousin breaking in on your wisdom! If I didn’t bring you news of the world, you’d forget there was anything in life but these moldy ancients. Florence is a wicked city—is it not true, Messer Tito? For what do you think? I’ve been at the Dianora Acciajoli and young Albizzi wedding today. *Cieli!* Such a feast might have been put off till Quaresima for a penance! Half the people invited were *Piagnoni*—that is the new word they use, these new saints of Fra Girolamo’s making. They treat a wedding feast not as a celebration of our agrarian bounty, but as mere utilitarian fuel, stripped of all joy!”

She fanned herself aggressively, the stiff fabric of her veil snapping the air. “I couldn’t stand in my clothes without giving offense. Monna Berta looked at me like a pinched nun and told me to read the Frate’s book about widows. Holy Madonna! And what do you think was the music we had to make our dinner lively? No lutes, no poetry—just a long, dreadful discourse from Fra Domenico of San Marco! He kept marking off doctrines on his fingers till my flesh crept. First, he said Florence shall be scourged by pestilence—which is enough to curdle the very blood in one's veins—and then that it’s all to be in our days! He made me tremble like the very jelly before me. They had jellies, to be sure, molded with the arms of the Albizzi and the Acciajoli in all colors; they haven't completely forgotten how to set a table. But up starts Francesco Valori, making a speech about how we ought to keep to the rules the Signory laid down, dictating what we are not to wear, and not to eat this and not to eat that! As if a man's plate should be governed by political decrees!”

“Stop, cousin!” Bardo commanded imperiously, desperate to arrest the rattling, discordant lengthiness of her monologue. Brigida gave a little start and pursed her mouth.

“Francesco Valori is not altogether wrong,” Bardo intoned, striving to restore the room's philosophical resonance. “It is true, Tito, that our manners have degenerated from that noble frugality which is the parent of true magnificence. Men will now spend sums on the transient, noisy show of a Giostra that would suffice to found a library and preserve the memories of our ancestors. There is a magnanimous sobriety in true Florentine tradition that abhors trivial lavishness. It is like the pure, unadulterated taste of ancient wheat—it needs no garish spices to prove its worth.”

“Laughter, indeed!” Brigida burst forth the instant the acoustic space opened. “If anyone wanted laughter at the wedding, they starved for it. When young Macchiavelli tried to tell stories from Sacchetti’s book—about how we women can make black look white and find new names for forbidden things—the *Piagnoni* looked as dismal as rotting turnips. Save me from going to a wedding again if that’s the fashion; we were as comfortable as wet chickens. It was as terrifying as when I went to hear their precious Frate Girolamo last Quaresima. Oh, Messer Tito, his voice shook the very bench under me like a brazen trumpet—an awful, deafening noise! He called us cows, and lumps of flesh, and wantons! He painted a gruesome picture of us widows as bare-pated vultures cutting off a young corpse's hair to deck our own heads. I was within a hair of sending all my jewels to the Good Men of Saint Martin, but my confessor told me it was the devil's work, that the Dominicans are trying to turn the world upside down. I am not going to be frightened by a Frate again. And all I say is, I wish it hadn’t been the Dominicans that poor Dino joined years ago, for then I should have been glad when I heard them say he was come back—”

“*Silenzio!*” Bardo’s voice erupted—a loud, violently agitated boom that cracked the acoustic tranquility of the room in half. Romola half started from her chair, her hands clasped, looking in mute appeal toward Tito. Monna Brigida gave a tiny shriek and bit her lip.

“*Donna!*” Bardo thundered again, his whole frame vibrating with an ancient, tectonic passion. “Hear once more my will. Bring no reports about that name into this house. And thou, Romola, I forbid thee to ask. My son is dead.”

The silence that followed was suffocating, the shattered sonic environment heavy with unspoken grief. Brigida lifted her shoulders in mute dismay, stole out of the room like a culpable fat spaniel, and left the heavy door to click shut behind her.

Tito’s quick mind combined the ingredients of this revelation with lightning rapidity. Bardo’s son was not dead; he was a Dominican monk. He was “come back.” Fra Luca—yes! The ascetic face that had stared at him in the streets shared the exact structural lineage of Bardo and Romola. If Fra Luca were only to die of his sickness at Fiesole! This importunate, selfish wish thrust itself to the forefront of Tito's palate, souring the evening's sweetness. Bardo’s rigid will might prevent Romola from seeing her brother, but it could not prevent Fra Luca from speaking Tito’s true name, betraying the very identity Tito wore like a forged lexicon. Nothing but Fra Luca’s death could preserve Tito's comfortable, rhythmic life; and falling back on the probability of the monk's fatal illness, Tito let his mind sink back into a tense repose.

They sat in the deepening twilight for many minutes, the darkness pressing in like the silence of a forgotten tongue. 

“Shall I light the lamp, father, and shall we go on?” Romola finally ventured, her voice a fragile lifeline.

“No, my Romola, we will work no more tonight. Tito, come and sit by me here.”

Tito moved from the reading-desk and seated himself close to Bardo’s left elbow. 

“Come nearer to me, *figliuola mia*,” Bardo said softly. Romola seated herself on a low stool, resting her arm on her father’s knee so he could lay his hand upon her hair, a tactile reassurance of their shared, unbroken lineage.

“Tito, I never told you that I had once a son,” Bardo began, his voice tremulous, the rigid dam of his pride finally breached. “But he left me—he is dead to me. He was a ready scholar, but he turned away his eyes from the clear lights of reason and the ancestral texts that nourish our minds. He prostrated himself under the influences of a dim, ascetic mysticism—a language of the soul that eludes all human duty, an untranslatable madness that treats the beautiful, sensory world as ash. And so it ended. The shared language of our family was broken. We will speak no more of him. I wish his face could be permanently blotted from that world of memory, where the distant past seems to grow clearer while the near violently fades.”

Bardo paused. The acoustic space of the study remained perfectly, devastatingly still, save for the old man's ragged breathing. Neither Romola nor Tito dared to speak a word, knowing that in the face of such profound, untranslatable loss, any offering of language would ring hollow. Bardo presently raised his hand, found Tito’s shoulder to rest it upon, and anchored himself in the silent dark.
