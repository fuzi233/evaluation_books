“For never any thing can be amiss
When simpleness and duty tender it.”
—*A Midsummer Night’s Dream*

Mr. Thornton moved through the day with a sharpness and purpose that seemed to erase the shadows of the previous one. The market showed a modest demand for finished goods, and he seized the opportunity, pressing hard in negotiations, his mind clear and decisive. He arrived punctually at the magistrates’ meeting, where his keen judgment and foresight quickly guided the deliberations. Though older men—those long established in the town, whose wealth was rooted in land rather than trade—held positions of influence, they looked to him for direction. He was entrusted with coordinating the police and overseeing essential measures. Yet he took no pride in their quiet respect, as though it were nothing more than the faint breeze that barely stirred the smoke rising from the mill chimneys.

He did not notice the admiration others offered; nor would he have welcomed it, had he known. Such recognition might have been a burden on his path forward. As it was, he focused only on action, on accomplishing what needed doing. It was his mother who gathered the whispers—how Mr. This or Mr. That spoke highly of her son, how things would have gone awry without him. But he dismissed such talk as irrelevant, sweeping through his duties with unwavering efficiency.

By midday, the weight of yesterday’s humiliation had lifted, replaced by a renewed sense of strength. His thoughts ran free, but they turned inevitably to her—not the moment of rejection, but the memory of the day before: her closeness, the warmth of her presence, the way her heart beat against his. He walked through the crowded streets, lost in thought, unseeing of the people around him, longing for that fleeting moment to return.

“Why, Mr. Thornton!” came a voice behind him. “You’re cutting me very coolly, I must say. And how is Mrs. Thornton? Brave weather this! We doctors don’t like it, I can tell you.”

“I beg your pardon, Dr. Donaldson,” he replied, startled. “I didn’t see you. My mother is well, thank you. A fine day, and good for the harvest, I hope. If the wheat comes in well, we’ll have brisk trade next year—whatever troubles may befall you physicians.”

“Ay, ay,” said the doctor with a smile. “Each man for himself. Your bad weather, your bad times—they’re my good ones. When trade falters, health suffers among the Milton folk more than you know.”

“Not mine,” Mr. Thornton answered firmly. “I’m made of iron. News of the worst debt I’ve ever faced never made my pulse falter. This strike—though it affects me more than anyone—doesn’t touch my appetite. You’ll need to look elsewhere for a patient, Doctor.”

“By the way,” the doctor added, “you’ve recommended me a good one—poor lady! I won’t go on in this heartless manner. But I fear Mrs. Hale—the woman in Crampton, you know—hasn’t many weeks left. I never held out much hope, as I told you. But I saw her today, and I think worse now than ever.”

Mr. Thornton paused. For a brief second, his famed composure wavered.

“Can I do anything?” he asked, his voice softer than usual. “You know—I’d want to help. Is there anything she needs? Comforts? Dainties?”

“No,” the doctor replied, shaking his head. “She craves fruit—she has a constant fever. Jargonelle pears will do, and there are plenty in the market.”

“I’ll let you know if there’s anything else,” Mr. Thornton said, already turning away. “I rely on you.”

“Oh, never fear,” the doctor chuckled. “I won’t spare your purse—I know it’s deep enough. I wish you’d give me a carte-blanche for all my patients.”

But Mr. Thornton was not a man given to broad charity. Few believed him capable of deep feeling. Yet he went straight to the first fruit shop in Milton, selecting the finest bunch of purple grapes, the richest peaches, the freshest vine leaves. They were packed into a basket, and the shopkeeper waited for instructions.

“Where shall we send them, sir?”

“No,” Mr. Thornton said. “Give the basket to me. I’ll take it.”

He carried it with both hands, weaving through the busy streets, drawing curious glances from young ladies who found it odd to see him so occupied—like a porter, or an errand-boy. But he did not care. In his mind, he repeated: *I will not be stopped by her. I like to bring this to the poor mother. It is right. She shall not turn me away. What a joke it would be if I refused kindness because of a proud girl. I do this for Mr. Hale. I do this in defiance.*

He moved swiftly, arriving at Crampton in short order. He climbed the stairs two at a time, entering the drawing-room before Dixon could announce him—his face flushed, his eyes bright with genuine concern.

Mrs. Hale lay on the sofa, feverish and pale. Mr. Hale read aloud. Margaret sat beside her, sewing quietly on a low stool. Her heart quickened at his sudden appearance—but he ignored her, even Mr. Hale. He stepped directly to Mrs. Hale, holding out the basket.

“I met Dr. Donaldason, ma'am,” he said, his voice low and gentle—a tone rare from a man so strong and direct. “He said fruit would help you. So I took the liberty—great liberty—of bringing some that seemed fine.”

Mrs. Hale trembled with delight. Mr. Hale, quieter but deeply grateful, nodded. “Fetch a plate, Margaret—anything.”

Margaret rose slowly, standing near the table, afraid to move, afraid to draw attention. She hoped he hadn’t noticed her—though she knew, somehow, that he felt her presence, even if he didn’t look at her.

“I must go,” he said. “I cannot stay. If you’ll forgive this liberty—my rough ways—too abrupt, I fear—but I’ll be more gentle next time. May I bring you more fruit if I find something tempting? Good afternoon, Mr. Hale. Good-bye, ma'am.”

And he was gone—without a word, without a glance toward her.

Margaret stood silent, then reached for a plate. With careful fingers, she lifted the fruit, handling each piece with tenderness. It was kind of him to come—after everything.

“Oh!” murmured Mrs. Hale, weakly. “It is so delicious! How thoughtful of him! Margaret love, taste these grapes! Wasn’t it kind?”

“Yes,” Margaret replied quietly.

“Margaret!” Mrs. Hale said, slightly cross. “You won’t like anything Mr. Thornton does. I never saw anyone so prejudiced.”

Mr. Hale, peeling a peach, cut off a small piece for himself. “If I had any prejudices,” he said, “this fruit would melt them. I’ve not tasted such since I was a boy. Not even in Hampshire. And boys, I suppose, find all fruit delicious. Do you remember the matted currant bushes at the corner of the west wall at home, Margaret?”

She did. Every stain on the old stone, every patch of lichen like a map, the tiny crane’s-bill growing in the cracks—it all flooded back. The past two days had strained her spirit, and now, hearing her father’s words, she rose abruptly, dropped her sewing, and fled into her own room.

Before she could fully grieve, she heard footsteps. Dixon stood at her drawer, rummaging.

“Bless me, miss! How you startled me! Is Missus worse? Is anything wrong?”

“No,” Margaret whispered, tears in her eyes. “Only I’m foolish and need water. What are you looking for? My muslins are in that drawer.”

Dixon kept searching. The scent of lavender drifted into the air.

At last, she pulled something out—what, Margaret couldn’t see. Then she turned.

“I don’t like telling you this,” Dixon said, “because you’ve got enough to bear. I meant to wait till night, maybe. But I couldn’t keep it.”

“What is it? Please, Dixon—tell me.”

“That young woman you visit—Higgins, I mean—died this morning. Her sister’s here, asking for something strange. The girl wanted to be buried in something of yours. That’s why I was looking for a nightcap—not too fine to give.”

“Oh!” Margaret gasped, her tears fresh. “Poor Bessy! I never thought I’d lose her.”

“But there’s more,” Dixon said. “The girl downstairs wants to know—would you like to see her?”

“She’s dead!” Margaret said, turning pale. “I’ve never seen a dead person. No—I’d rather not.”

“I wouldn’t have asked if you hadn’t come in,” Dixon replied. “I told her you wouldn’t.”

“I’ll go down,” Margaret said quickly, afraid that Dixon’s blunt manner might hurt the girl.

She took the cap and went to the kitchen. Mary Higgins sat there, face swollen with grief. She broke into sobs when she saw Margaret.

“Oh, ma'am,” she cried. “She loved you! She loved you! She did indeed!”

For a long while, nothing more could be said. Then, under Margaret’s sympathy and Dixon’s sharp prompting, a few facts emerged.

Nicholas Higgins had left early that morning, leaving Bessy seemingly well. But within an hour, she worsened. A neighbor rushed to call Mary, who had just arrived moments before she passed.

“It were a day or two ago,” Mary said, choking, “she axed to be buried in somewhat o’ yourn. She never tired o’ talking o’ yo’. Said yo’ were the prettiest thing she ever clapped eyes on. She loved yo’ dearly. Her last words—‘Give her my affectionate respects—and keep father fro’ drink.’ Yo’ll come and see her, ma'am. She would ha’ thought it a great compliment.”

Margaret hesitated. “Yes… perhaps I may. Yes, I will. I’ll come before tea. But where’s your father, Mary?”

Mary shook her head and began to rise.

“Miss Hale,” Dixon said quietly, “what’s the use of you going? I’d never say a word against it—if it helped the girl. And I wouldn’t mind going myself. These folks think it’s a sign of respect. Here—let me go instead. Miss Hale is busy. She can’t come, or else she would.”

Mary looked up, pleading. Dixon’s coming might be a gesture, but it wasn’t the same. The sister had once felt jealousy over Bessy’s closeness to the young lady.

“No, Dixon,” Margaret said firmly. “I will go. Mary, you shall see me this afternoon.”

And to silence her own fear, she left the room—resolute, determined not to retreat.
