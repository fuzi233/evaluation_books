For days, the sea had raged against us like an angry beast. Six times, darkness swallowed the sky in thunder and lightning, each dawn bringing no relief—only fiercer winds and waves that crashed over the deck with growing fury. By the seventh day, all hope seemed lost.

We were tossed far from our course, drifting without knowing where we were. The crew had given up. Their strength was gone after endless hours of struggling to keep the ship afloat. Masts snapped and fell into the churning water; leaks sprang open everywhere, and seawater poured in faster than we could bail it out.

No more curses now—only desperate cries to God for mercy, mixed with wild promises made in fear: “If we survive, I’ll give up drinking!” or “I’ll never curse again!” Every man on board turned to prayer, begging for life while also trying to think of how to save himself.

My heart sank as I looked at my family amid this chaos. Our four sons trembled with fear. “Dear children,” I said, “if the Lord wills it, He can save us even now. If not, let us give our lives peacefully into His hands. For those who love one another, even death is not too bitter, because we will be together forever in heaven.”

At these words, my wife lifted her face from her hands, tears still streaming down it, but her eyes bright with courage. As the boys gathered close, she began to comfort them with quiet strength. I felt proud of her bravery—even though my own chest ached with worry.

Together, we knelt and prayed. Each of us spoke from the heart, full of fear yet trusting in God. Fritz, especially, prayed first for his parents and brothers, forgetting himself entirely. In that moment, the storm outside seemed less terrifying. “Ah,” I thought, “the Lord hears us. He will help.”

Then, through the roar of the waves, came a cry: “Land! Land!” At the same time, the ship shuddered violently—a terrible crash sent everyone sprawling. The vessel groaned and split apart. Water surged in from every side.

Above the chaos, the captain shouted: “Lower the boats! We’re doomed!”

“Doomed?” I echoed, and the word pierced me like a knife. But seeing my children’s terror rise again, I forced myself to smile. “Courage, my boys! We’re still above water. Look—land isn’t far. We must do what we can. Stay with your mother. I’ll go see what’s to be done.”

I climbed onto the deck. A wave threw me down. Another followed. Then another. I fought to stand. The ship was shattered—broken on both sides, with a gaping hole in the hull.

The sailors rushed to the lifeboats, ignoring passengers. The last man cut the ropes and launched the final boat into the storm.

My horror deepened when I saw it float away—every sailor aboard, leaving us behind. I called out, pleaded, but my voice was lost in the wind. Even if they’d wanted to return, the waves were mountains high. No boat could come back.

I scanned the wreckage, then slowly realized something: the stern of the ship, where our cabin was, was jammed between two large rocks. It stayed partly above the waves, while the front was being torn apart by the surf. As the rain cleared, I caught sight of a rocky coastline—rough, yes, but land nonetheless. My heart leapt. Help was near.

Still, loneliness weighed on me. I returned to my family and tried to sound calm. “Don’t fear,” I said with a smile. “Though our ship is gone, our cabin is safe. If the storm calms tomorrow, we can reach shore.”

These words lifted their spirits. My children trusted me—and suddenly, the danger didn’t feel so absolute. They laughed, joked, even enjoyed the fact that the ship wasn’t rolling anymore.

But my wife saw through my mask. She knew I was afraid. I told her the truth—how we were abandoned, how the wreck might break apart at any moment. Yet she did not waver. Her faith held firm. Seeing that, I found strength again.

“We need food,” she said. “We must eat well tonight. We’ll need all our strength tomorrow.”

Night fell fast. The storm raged on. Crashes echoed—more parts of the ship giving way. We thought of the lifeboats. Were they all lost?

“God will help us soon, won’t He, Father?” asked my youngest son.

“Silly little thing,” said Fritz sharply. “We don’t get to decide what God will do. We must wait patiently.”

“That’s wise,” I replied gently. “But you speak too harshly to your brothers, even if you don’t mean to.”

Supper was ready. The boys ate hungrily, then slept deeply. Fritz stayed awake with us. After a long silence, he said, “Father, could we make swimming belts for Mother and the others? You and I can swim. With floats, we might all make it to land.”

“It’s a brilliant idea,” I said. “I’ll begin right away.”

We searched the ship. We found empty flasks and tin cans—just enough to link together into buoyant floats. We tied two together, forming pairs strong enough to support a person. My wife and the younger boys each wore one. I packed matches, dry tinder, knives, rope, and anything portable—just in case the wreck broke apart before morning.

Fritz and his brothers fell asleep quickly. All night, my wife and I kept watch, praying and listening for signs of collapse. Every groan of the ship made us tense.

Finally, dawn broke. The storm softened. Blue sky appeared. The sunrise painted the horizon in golden light.

I woke the boys. We gathered on the deck—and froze.

“No one else is here,” said Fritz. “Where are the sailors? Did they take the boats? Why did they leave us?”

“Children,” I said firmly, “we must not despair. Those we relied on have left us. But God has not. He is with us still. Let’s act. Who has an idea?”

“The sea will calm soon,” said Fritz. “We could swim.”

“But only you can swim!” cried Ernest. “What about Mother and the rest? Why not build a raft and all go together?”

“A raft might be hard to make,” I said. “But we must try. First, find everything useful.”

We scattered. I checked supplies—food, water, tools. My wife took Franz to care for the animals, who had been neglected and were suffering.

Fritz went to the arms chest. Ernest hunted for tools. Jack opened the captain’s cabin—and two large dogs burst out, leaping and barking so wildly they knocked him over.

Jack didn’t panic. He recovered, and the dogs licked his face as if asking forgiveness. He grabbed the bigger one by the ears, climbed on its back, and rode toward me like a hero. I laughed. “Good job, Jack—but remember, hungry dogs can be dangerous.”

When we met again, we showed off our finds.

Fritz had guns, powder, bullets, and a shot belt.

Ernest brought nails, scissors, an axe, hammer, pincers, chisels—tools sticking from every pocket.

Even little Franz held a box. “Look!” he said proudly. “Nice sharp little hooks!”

His brothers snickered.

“Well done, Franz!” I said. “Those fishhooks may save us. Fritz and Ernest, you chose well.”

“What about me?” asked my wife. “I have no treasures—but good news. Some animals are alive: a donkey, goats, sheep, a ram, a cow, and a pregnant sow. I just fed them. I milked the goats—but I don’t know how to keep the milk fresh.”

“Wonderful!” I said. “And Jack—your dogs are hungry and useless.”

“Oh, no!” said Jack. “They’ll help us hunt!”

“Maybe,” I said. “But how do we get ashore?”

“Why not use big tubs?” Jack suggested. “I’ve floated in them at home!”

“My child,” I said, “you’ve struck gold! That’s worth trying. Ernest—bring your tools. Everyone, gather tubs!”

We found four large casks—strong, iron-bound—floating in the hold. We pulled them up and placed them on the lower deck. I sawed each in half. It was hard work. We paused to drink goat’s milk, wine, and biscuits.

Now we had eight tubs, lined up near the edge of the wreck. I studied them with satisfaction—until I noticed my wife’s face.

“I’ll never get into one of those,” she said.

“Wait until you see what I build,” I said. “You’ll prefer it to this sinking ship.”

Next, I found a long plank and bent its ends upward to form a keel. I nailed two planks along the sides, shaping them to a point at each end. Together, they formed a narrow boat divided into eight sections. It looked like a small craft—stable in calm water.

But when we tried to move it—nothing. Too heavy. Too clumsy. Even all of us pushing couldn’t budge it.

“I need a lever!” I shouted. “Bring the capstan bar!”

Fritz ran and fetched it. I cut a spar into rollers and raised the front of the boat with the bar. My sons placed a roller under it.

“How does this work, Father?” asked Ernest. “How can one man move more than all of us?”

I explained Archimedes’ principle—how leverage multiplies force. He grinned. “So if I had a long enough lever, I could move the world?”

“Exactly,” I said. “We’ll talk mechanics when we’re safe.”

I tied a long rope to the stern and anchored it to a beam. We added more rollers and pushed again—this time, success! The boat slid smoothly into the water. Had the rope not held, it would have drifted away.

The boys wanted to jump in—but the boat leaned heavily to one side.

We threw in heavy items. Gradually, it balanced. The boys scrambled in, laughing, eager to sit in the tubs.

But I saw it needed more. I added outriggers—long poles across the bow and stern, with empty brandy casks fixed at the ends—to keep it steady.

Then I stepped in. I turned the raft toward the open side of the wreck, clearing debris to make a path. The boys brought oars.

But it was too late to leave. We had to spend another night.

We sat down to supper—biscuits, wine, milk—after a long day of work. We prepared for sleep, better rested than the night before. Still, I warned everyone to wear their float belts again.

I urged my wife to wear a sailor’s outfit. “It will be easier,” I said. “More practical.”

She hesitated—then agreed. She returned in a smart blue uniform, found in a midshipman’s chest. She blushed, but we all admired her. Soon, she forgot her embarrassment.

We lay down in our hammocks. Sleep came easily—ready for the next day.

We rose early. Hope lightens sleep. After prayer, I said, “With God’s help, we escape today. Feed the animals well. Leave plenty of food. We may return. Now, gather everything useful.”

The boys obeyed joyfully. I chose from their haul: canvas for a tent, carpenter’s tools, guns, pistols, powder, fishing gear, an iron pot, soup cakes, biscuit.

We loaded the raft. I added ballast—useful things instead of stones. Still, we had to leave some behind.

We prayed again. Then, we took our places—one in each tub.

Just then, chickens clucked and roosters crowed—as if accusing us.

“Why not bring the fowls?” I said. “If we can’t feed them, they’ll feed us.” Ten hens and two roosters were placed in one tub, covered with wire mesh.

The ducks and geese swam free. Pigeons flew toward the shore.

My wife came last, carrying a bag as big as a pillow. “This is mine,” she said, tossing it to Franz. I thought it was a cushion.

We cast off. My brave wife sat in the first tub. Next, Franz—sweet, gentle, nearly six. Then Fritz—fourteen, bold and quick-witted. The middle tubs held our supplies. Then Jack—ten, daring and impulsive. Next, Ernest—twelve, thoughtful but lazy. I stood at the stern, guiding the raft.

Everyone wore float belts. Each had tools nearby.

The tide helped us. We slipped out of the wreck and into the open sea. All eyes strained for land. The boys rowed hard—but the raft spun in circles.

I adjusted the steering. Then we moved forward—swiftly now!

We had left the dogs, Turk and Juno, on the wreck. But when they saw us go, they howled and jumped into the sea.

I felt sorry. The distance was great. But they swam after us—resting their paws on the outriggers, keeping pace.

Jack wanted to push them away.

“No,” I said. “That would be cruel. Remember: the kind man cares for his beasts. God gave dogs to be our friends.”

Our journey was slow, but safe. As we neared the shore, the rocks looked barren and bleak.

But around us, casks and boxes floated. Fritz and I towed two hogsheads—maybe they held food.

Then, beyond the cliffs, we saw green grass and trees. Tall palms swayed in the breeze. Ernest hoped they were coconut trees—dreaming of sweet milk.

“I wish I’d brought the captain’s telescope,” I sighed.

“Here, Father!” Jack said, pulling out a tiny spyglass.

Through it, I saw the coast to the left—lusher, greener. But a current carried us toward the jagged rocks.

Then I noticed a stream flowing into the sea. Our geese and ducks were heading there.

I steered toward it. We entered a quiet inlet—calm water, shallow depth. The ground sloped gently upward, and the cliffs curved back, forming a small plain.

We landed—everyone except Franz, who had to be lifted out of his tub.

The dogs raced ahead, barking wildly, wagging tails. Chickens, geese, ducks—noisy as ever. Flamingoes and penguins screamed nearby. The noise was loud—but welcome. I smiled. Good dinners awaited.

As soon as we stood on solid ground, we knelt together—thankful, humbled. We praised God for saving us. Then we set to work.

We unloaded the raft. How rich we felt! The poultry wandered free. We found a spot for our tent—using a spar and pole as a frame, stretching the sailcloth over it. We pegged it down and piled chests along the edges. Hooks secured the entrance.

The boys gathered moss and grass for beds. I built a firepit near the brook, using flat stones. Dry twigs and seaweed burned brightly. I filled the iron pot with water. My wife took the portable soup cakes.

Franz watched, curious. “Is Papa making glue?”

“No, my dear,” she said. “This is soup.”

“But it looks like glue!”

“And I wouldn’t want glue soup,” he said. “Do you want beef or mutton, Mama?”

We laughed. And as the fire crackled, I knew—we were safe. For now. And tomorrow? Tomorrow, we would begin our new life.

We would have suffered greatly without the shelter of our tent, for the night turned as cold as the day had been sweltering. Yet we slept soundly, each of us worn out by the day’s labors.

The crowing of our watchful rooster—loud and clear as he greeted the rising moon—was the last thing I heard before sleep, and it was his call that woke me at dawn. I roused my wife quietly so that, while the children still slumbered, we might speak together in peace about our situation and what lay ahead. It was clear to both of us that first we must try to learn the fate of our former companions, and then explore the land on which we had been cast ashore.

So we decided: after breakfast, Fritz and I would set off on a journey to search for signs of them and to learn more about this strange island. My wife would stay near the boat with the three younger boys, keeping watch over the camp.

"Rouse up, rouse up, my boys!" I called cheerfully, shaking the children awake. "Come and help your mother get breakfast ready!"

"As if we could do much," she replied with a smile. "We can only boil more soup."

"Oh, but you’ve forgotten Jack’s fine lobster!" I reminded her. "Where is it, Jack?"

"It’s been safe in this hole in the rock all night, Father," he said. "I thought since the dogs like good things, they might take a fancy to the lobster just as they did the agouti."

"A sensible idea," I said. "Even my careless Jack is learning wisdom. And thank goodness the lobster is large—we’ll need part of it for our journey today."

At the mention of an expedition, the four children leapt with joy, dancing around me and clapping their hands.

"Hold on now, hold on!" I laughed. "Not everyone can go. Such a trip would be too dangerous and tiring for the younger ones. This spot seems safe enough. Fritz and I will go alone, with one dog. The other will stay behind to guard you. Fritz, prepare the guns, and tie up Flora so she doesn’t follow."

At the word “guns,” the boy flushed deeply. He fumbled awkwardly with his rifle, unable to straighten it properly. I left him alone for a moment, but soon saw that he was truly sorry. I let him choose another weapon, pleased to see how shame had stirred a sense of responsibility within him.

A little later, he tried to grab Flora to tie her up—but the dog, remembering the blows she’d recently received, growled and refused to come near. Turk reacted the same way. I had to call them gently, using my voice to coax them forward. Then, in tears, Fritz begged his mother for some biscuit, saying he’d rather skip the rest of his breakfast than remain unkind to the animals.

He carried the biscuits to them, stroking and soothing them, every movement pleading forgiveness. Dogs, more than most creatures—even humans—are quick to forgive kindness and slow to hold grudges. Flora instantly softened, licking the hands that fed her. But Turk, fiercer and more independent, stayed wary.

"Give him a claw of my lobster," Jack called out. "I meant to give it to you anyway for your journey."

With that treat, Turk seemed to accept Fritz. We loaded our gear: each of us took a gun and a game bag. Fritz tucked two pistols into his belt; I slipped a small hatchet into mine. After finishing breakfast, we packed the leftover lobster, some biscuits, and a flask of water—and were ready to go.

"Wait!" I said suddenly. "We’ve missed something vital."

"Surely not," Fritz said.

"Yes," I replied. "We haven’t said our morning prayer. In the rush of life, we’re too quick to forget the One who gives us everything." We paused to pray, asking for His guidance and protection. Then I bid farewell to my wife and children, urging them not to wander far from the boat or tent. We parted with quiet concern—neither of us knowing what dangers might lie ahead in this unknown world.

As we moved along the stream, we found the banks so rocky that we could reach the water only through one narrow path. There was no matching trail on the opposite side. I felt relieved—this meant my family was in a place nearly impossible to reach, protected by steep cliffs on one side and the sea on the other.

Fritz and I followed the stream upward until we came to a waterfall cascading down from a height, where large rocks half-submerged in water allowed us to cross safely. Now the sea lay to our left, and to our right stretched a long ridge of rocky hills dotted with clusters of trees.

We pushed through dense grass, dry and tangled from the sun, when suddenly we heard rustling behind us. Turning, we saw the grass swaying as though something moved through it. Fritz spun around, raised his gun, and stood ready to fire.

I was proud of his composure—his calm under pressure showed he could be trusted in real danger. But instead of a beast, it was Turk, our dog, whom we had accidentally left behind. Our thoughtful wife must have sent him after us.

I praised Fritz for not acting on fear, for waiting to identify the threat before firing. This small incident made us realize how vulnerable we were—how easily a hidden predator could ambush us. So we hurried toward the open shore.

There, the view was breathtaking. Hills rose in the background, green grass waved in the breeze, and trees reached almost to the water's edge. It was a picture of beauty and peace.

On the smooth sand, we searched carefully for any trace of our missing companions—but found nothing. Not a single footprint.

"Should I fire a shot or two?" Fritz asked. "It might bring them if they’re nearby."

"It might," I answered, "or it might attract savages. No—let’s search quietly and thoroughly."

"But why should we care about them at all, Father?" Fritz said. "They abandoned us. I don’t want to see them again."

"You're wrong, son," I replied. "We shouldn't repay evil with evil. They might help us build shelter. And remember—they took nothing from the ship. They may be starving."

"But Father," he argued, "while we waste time searching, why not go back and rescue the animals on board?"

"When many duties call," I said, "we must choose the one that brings the greatest good. Saving a human life is nobler than caring for animals we’ve already fed. Besides, the sea is calm—the ship won great harm before we return."

We walked on, talking, until we reached a lovely grove stretching down to the water. We rested beneath a large tree beside a murmuring brook that flowed into the ocean.

Birds flitted above us, bright and chattering. Fritz suddenly jumped up. "A monkey!" he exclaimed. "I’m sure I saw one!"

He darted around the tree and tripped over a round object, handing it to me. "It’s a bird’s nest," he said. "I’ve read about them."

"Perhaps," I laughed. "But not every fuzzy round thing is a nest. This is a coconut. Remember—coconuts have a hard shell covered by fibrous husk, and outside that, a thick green casing. Time has worn away the outer layer here, revealing the fibers. Let’s crack it open—you’ll see the nut inside."

We struggled to break it, but when we did, the kernel was dry and useless.

"Hullo!" Fritz said. "I always thought coconuts were full of sweet milk, like almond juice."

"They are," I said, "when young. As they age, the milk thickens and turns into solid kernel. When ripe, it dries. But if the nut falls on good soil, the seed inside swells, breaks the shell, takes root, and grows a new tree."

"But how does such a tiny seed push through such a hard shell?" Fritz asked.

"Nature provides," I said, pointing to three small holes near the stem. "The seed pushes out through these. Now let’s find a fresh one."

Since coconuts fall only when fully ripe, it took effort to find one with usable meat. The one we got was oily and slightly sour—but better than nothing. The fruit gave us strength, so we postponed our midday meal and saved our supplies.

Pushing through a thicket thick with vines, we cut a path with our hatchets and emerged once more onto the shore. Before us lay a wide forest receding into the distance, with scattered trees standing apart. Fritz noticed them immediately.

"Oh, Father! Look at those funny trees—what odd bumps on their trunks!"

We approached and recognized them as calabash trees, whose fruits grow directly on the stems. These gourds have tough shells that can be carved into bowls, spoons, or bottles.

"Savages use them cleverly," I said. "They cook food in them."

"But that’s impossible!" Fritz protested. "Fire would burn through the shell."

"Not if they don’t put it directly on the fire," I explained. "After splitting the gourd, they fill it with water and add fish or meat. Then they throw in hot stones until the water boils. The food cooks, and the shell stays intact."

"Ah! A brilliant idea—simple too. I might have thought of that," Fritz said.

"The friends of Columbus thought it easy to stand an egg on end after he showed them how. Now, let’s prepare some calabashes to take home."

Fritz grabbed a gourd and tried to slice it evenly with his knife—but failed. The blade slipped, leaving jagged edges.

"Ugh!" he muttered, tossing it aside. "Ruined. It looked so simple."

"Stay," I said. "Those pieces aren’t useless. Try shaping them into spoons while I make a dish."

I pulled a string from my pocket, tied it tightly around a gourd near one end, then tapped it with the back of my knife. The string pierced the shell. I tightened it further and pulled hard—splitting the gourd cleanly in two.

"Clever!" Fritz exclaimed. "How did you think of that?"

"Savages use this method," I said. "I learned it from travel books."

"Looks like a perfect soup tureen—and even a plate," he said, examining it. "But what if you wanted a bottle?"

"Easier," I said. "Cut a hole at one end, scoop out the insides, drop in some stones or shot. Shake them—any remaining pulp will detach. Then you have a clean bottle."

"That wouldn’t be very handy," Fritz said. "More like a barrel."

"True," I agreed. "For a finer shape, you must act early. Tie a band around a young gourd while it’s still on the tree. That part won’t swell, creating a neck."

I filled the gourds with sand and marked the spot so we could return later.

"Do people in Europe grow bottle-shaped gourds the same way?" Fritz asked.

"No," I said. "They’re a different kind—those shapes are natural."

We continued for three hours, scanning the land for any sign of our companions. At last, we reached a high promontory jutting into the sea. From its rocky peak, we expected a broad view of the island.

We climbed easily and scanned the landscape—yet saw no trace of humans.

Before us lay a wide bay, golden sands curving into the distance, framed by two shadowy headlands. Between them, the water shimmered, reflecting the sun. The land beyond was lush and green—beautiful, yet strangely silent.

Fritz and I both felt a quiet loneliness settle over us.

"Cheer up, son," I said. "We chose this life long ago—settlers, not travelers. We didn’t expect solitude, but what difference does a few people make? With God’s help, we’ll live contentedly. At least we’re not stranded on a barren rock. Come—the heat is unbearable. Let’s find shade before we’re cooked alive."

We descended and headed for a clump of palm trees in the distance. To reach them, we had to pass through a thick reed bed—a difficult task. I feared at every step I might step on a venomous snake.

I sent Turk ahead and cut a reed, thinking it might be safer than a gun against reptiles. As I walked, I noticed a thick liquid oozing from one end. I tasted it—and smiled. Sweet and refreshing. Sugar cane!

I urged Fritz to cut one for himself. He did, and as he beat the ground, the reed split, coating his hand with juice. He touched it to his tongue, then again—more boldly. Then he sprang to me, shouting: "Father! Sugar cane! Taste it! Oh, it’s wonderful! We must take some home!"

"Slow down," I said. "Moderation, remember. Cut some if you like—but not more than you can carry."

He ignored me, cutting a dozen thick canes, stripping the leaves, and carrying them under his arm. We pushed through the cane brake and reached the palms.

As we entered, a troop of monkeys, playing on the ground, scrambled up the trees, chattering and making faces.

Fritz, angry at their mockery, raised his gun. "Stay!" I cried. "Never kill an animal unless necessary. A live monkey is worth more than a dead one. Watch."

I gathered small stones and threw them upward. The monkeys, mimicking instinctively, grabbed nearby coconuts and hurled them down like rain.

Fritz laughed and rushed forward, picking up the best nuts. We drank the milk from them, piercing the shells. It wasn’t tasty, but it quenched thirst. What we enjoyed most was the creamy layer clinging to the shell, which we scraped off with our spoons.

After this feast, we scorned the lobster we’d brought. We tossed it to Turk, who devoured it eagerly—then gnawed the sugar cane ends and begged for more coconut. I slung two coconuts over my shoulder, fastening them by their stalks. Fritz resumed his burden, and we began the return journey.

Soon, Fritz groaned. "I didn’t expect these canes to be so heavy. I feel terrible for the poor slaves who carry loads like this. But I’ll be glad when my brothers taste them."

"Patience, son," I said. "Remember Aesop’s breadbasket—heavy at the start, light at the end. Take a fresh cane and bind the bundle across with your gun."

We did, and moved on. Fritz noticed I kept sucking the end of my cane.

"Ah!" he said. "That’s a good trick. I’ll try it."

He sucked hard—but nothing came out.

"How do you do it?" he asked.

"Think," I said. "You’re capable of figuring it out."

"Oh!" he said. "Like trying to suck marrow from a bone without a hole at the other end."

"Exactly," I said. "You create a vacuum. Air can’t enter the other end, so the liquid won’t flow."

He quickly mastered the technique, learning to cut fresh openings at each joint. He dreamed of teaching his brothers, especially Ernest, who loved coconut milk.

"My dear boy," I said, "you needn’t have brought that flask. It’ll turn to vinegar by the time we return. The heat will ferment it."

"Vinegar?" he gasped. "No! Let me check!" He pulled the cork—*pop!*—and foam erupted like champagne.

"There!" I laughed. "Now you’ll need moderation again, friend Fritz! It may be delicious, but it’ll go to your head."

"Father!" he said, tasting it. "This is amazing! Like fine wine! Vinegar? Never!"

We felt renewed, walking briskly. The journey seemed shorter than expected. We found our gourds dry and light.

Just as we passed the grove where we’d eaten, Turk bolted forward, attacking a troop of monkeys playing nearby. They were caught off guard. The dog, hungry, seized one and tore it apart before we could stop him.

The victim was a mother monkey. Her tiny baby, trapped beneath her when she fled, hid in the grass, watching in terror.

Seeing Turk’s cruelty, Fritz rushed to save the infant, dropping everything—even his hat. Too late for the mother. But as he approached, the little monkey leapt onto his shoulders, gripping his curly hair tightly.

Fritz screamed and thrashed, but the creature held fast, making grotesque faces.

I laughed so hard I could barely help him. "It seems the little one sees you as a father figure," I said.

"Or," Fritz retorted, "he knows I’m a soft-hearted fool who can’t hurt anything that trusts me. But father—he’s pulling my hair! Please help me get rid of him!"

And so, with laughter and a bit of struggle, we finally freed Fritz from his furry passenger—and continued home, lighter in spirit and heart.

"I won’t bore you with a detailed account of our first day," my wife began, "though I confess I spent most of it watching your signals and worrying about your safety. This morning, I rose early and, seeing your reassuring sign that all was well, quickly responded. Then, while the boys still slept, I sat down to think—how could we improve our situation?

“It’s simply impossible,” I said to myself, “to stay here much longer. The sun beats down relentlessly on this bare rock, and our only shelter is this flimsy tent, where the heat is even worse than outside. Why should I and my little ones not do our part? Why shouldn’t we help build something useful?

“If only we could trade this bleak, unhealthy spot for a cool, shady home, we’d all feel better—healthier, happier. Among those lovely woods Fritz and you explored, there must be some perfect place to settle. I’m certain of it—and I’ll find it.”

By then, the boys were awake. I noticed Jack quietly working near where Fritz had left the jackal. Watching closely, I saw him cut two long strips from the animal’s hide, clean and scrape them carefully, then take a handful of large nails from his pocket and drive them through the skin, points outward. Next, he cut wide strips of canvas sailcloth, doubled them over, and laid them across the raw side of the hide so they covered the flat nail heads.

At this point, Master Jack came to me with a polite request: would I please stitch the canvas to the wet hide for him? I handed him needles and thread—but couldn’t bring myself to take the joy away from him.

Yet when I saw how determinedly he struggled with his clumsy fingers, I gave in. Overcoming my disgust, I finished lining the clever dog collars he’d made. Then I was asked to complete a fine leather belt he’d crafted for himself. I suggested he find a way to keep the hide from shrinking.

Ernest, though teasing Jack about his project, offered a sensible solution. Jack immediately put it into action—he stretched the hide flat on a board and placed it in the sun to dry.

My plan for an expedition met with eager approval from the boys. We quickly gathered supplies: weapons, food, water. The older boys took guns, while I carried the water flask and a small hatchet.

Leaving the camp neat and orderly, we set off toward the stream, followed by the dogs. Turk, who had gone with you on your first trip, seemed to understand our purpose at once and proudly led the way.

As I watched my sons—each with a gun—I felt deeply grateful to you, dear husband, for teaching them firearms as children. Their safety now depended on them.

We filled our water jar, crossed the stream, and climbed to the high point you described, where the view opened up like a promise. At the sight of the lush greenery and distant hills, a warm, hopeful feeling stirred in me—one I hadn’t known in a long time.

A small wood caught my eye, and we headed toward it. But soon, thick grass taller than the boys’ heads blocked our path. We turned to follow the open beach on our left, reaching a point closer to the wood before heading back into the reeds.

We hadn’t escaped the tall grass entirely. Just as we strained forward, a loud rushing sound sent us scrambling back. A huge bird burst into flight. Both boys raised their guns, but it was already too far away.

“Oh dear, what a pity!” Ernest exclaimed. “If only I’d had my light gun—and if it hadn’t flown so fast, I’d have brought it down!”

“Ah yes,” I replied gently, “no doubt you’d make a fine hunter—if every creature waited patiently for you to aim.”

“But I didn’t expect anything to rise right under our feet!” he protested.

“A good shot,” I said, “must always be ready for surprises. Birds and beasts don’t send warnings when they’re about to flee.”

“What kind of bird was it?” Jack asked.

“An eagle!” Franz declared. “It was so big!”

“Just because it’s big doesn’t mean it’s an eagle,” Ernest scoffed.

“Let’s see where it was sitting,” I said.

Jack darted forward—and instantly, another bird, even larger, exploded upward with a terrifying cry.

The boys stared, stunned. I laughed. “Well, you’re quite the hunters! You’ll certainly keep my larder well stocked!”

Ernest flushed, close to tears. Jack grinned, pulled off his cap, bowed low, and called after the fleeing bird: “Adieu for now, sir! I hope to meet again!”

After searching, we found a crude nest of dry grass—empty, though broken eggshells lay nearby. We realized the young birds had fled into the grass, which we could see waving gently as they ran.

“Now look here, Franz,” Ernest said, “how could this possibly be an eagle? Eagles never build on the ground. And their young can’t run as soon as they hatch—that’s only true of game birds. The white belly and dull red wing feathers I saw suggest bustards. And the moustache-like feathers above the beak? That’s the Great Bustard.”

“My dear boy,” I said, “your eyes were sharp, even if your hands weren’t ready. And perhaps it’s just as well we didn’t disturb the family.”

Chatting like this, we finally reached the wood. Birds sang and fluttered among the branches, but I discouraged the boys from shooting any. We stood in awe of the trees—so vast, so strange. What we thought was a forest was actually just a cluster of about a dozen trees, each supported by roots that formed arches and braces around the trunk, with the main stem rooted firmly in the center.

I gave Jack some twine. He climbed one of the open-air roots and measured the trunk—it was nearly eighteen yards around. Though I saw no fruit, the foliage was thick and cool, casting deep shade over soft, green grass—free of thorns or brambles. It was the most beautiful resting place imaginable. We enjoyed our midday meal there, refreshed after the heat of our journey.

The dogs joined us later. They’d stayed behind on the shore and now lay down, sleeping peacefully without begging for food—a surprise, since they usually demanded scraps.

The longer we stayed, the more enchanted I became. If only we could live in a shelter among those noble branches, I’d feel safe and content. It seemed foolish to search elsewhere. So I decided to return to the shore and see if the waves had brought anything useful from the wreck.

Before leaving, Jack persuaded me to finish his belt and the spiked collars for the dogs. He’d been carrying the board all along, so the hides could dry properly in the sun.

Now fully dried, I stitched them easily. Jack tied on his belt with pride, holstered his pistols, and marched around like a general. Ernest fitted the collars on the dogs.

Back at the shore, we found many items scattered by the tide—valuable, but too heavy to carry. We rolled casks beyond high tide and dragged chests higher onto the beach. As we worked, the dogs busied themselves among the rocks, diving into crevices and pools, then leaping back with something in their mouths.

“They’re eating crabs,” Jack said. “No wonder they haven’t been hungry lately.”

Sure enough, they were catching tiny green crabs everywhere.

Later, Juno began digging in the sand, uncovering round objects she devoured eagerly.

Ernest examined them and reported calmly: “She’s found turtle eggs.”

“Oh!” I exclaimed. “Then let’s share the treasure!” But Mrs. Juno wasn’t pleased. We had to drive her away to collect a couple of dozen eggs, which we tucked into our bags.

Suddenly, we spotted a sail approaching beyond the cliffs. Ernest insisted it was our raft. Little Franz grew pale—savages, he feared. For a moment, I hesitated.

But we hurried to the stream, crossed the stepping stones, and reached the landing place—where we joyfully met you.

“So, dear husband,” my wife said, “do you approve of our exploration? Tomorrow, will you please pack everything and take us to live among my wonderful trees?”

“Well, little wife,” you replied, “so that’s your idea of comfort—perching on a tree hundreds of feet high, like birds? If only we had wings or a balloon, it might be a fine plan!”

“Laugh all you like,” she said, “but my idea isn’t as absurd as you claim. We’d be safe from jackals at night. And I’ve seen in Switzerland a lovely arbour built high in a lime tree, with a strong floor and a staircase. Why can’t we build something similar—where we can sleep safely?”

“I’ll consider it seriously,” you said. “Perhaps something can come of it. For now, supper is done and night is falling. Let’s pray and rest.”

Under the tent, we all slept deeply—like marmots—until dawn. When I woke, I found my wife already awake, and we discussed our plans.

Regarding her suggestion, I said, “To move would be difficult. We must think carefully. First, I believe we were guided here providentially. This spot is secure—surrounded by cliffs, accessible only through the ford. From here, we can reach the ship and salvage its cargo. Suppose we stay until we bring everything ashore?”

“I agree partly,” she replied, “but the heat here is unbearable. While you and Fritz wander in cool woods, I suffer in the glare. As for the ship’s contents—so much has washed ashore already. I’d rather give up the rest than risk venturing out to sea again.”

“I see your point,” I said. “What if we move to your chosen spot, but keep this rocky area as a storehouse and refuge? I could make it safer by blowing up parts of the rock with gunpowder. But first, we need a bridge—to cross with our belongings.”

“Oh, I’ll fry before we leave if we wait for a bridge!” she exclaimed. “Why not just carry things on our backs and wade across, like we’ve done? The cow and donkey can carry much.”

“That they will,” I said, “but we’ll need baskets and bags. If you focus on making those, I’ll start the bridge. It won’t be used once—there may be floods, or accidents.”

“Well, well,” she sighed, “I submit. But please begin at once—I can’t wait to leave. And your idea of fortifying this place is excellent. Storing gunpowder here, away from our home, will be safer.”

“Gunpowder is both the most dangerous and most useful thing we have,” I said. “So we must guard it carefully. Soon, I’ll carve a chamber in the rock—safe from fire and damp.”

By morning, we’d decided: we would move. Work for the day was assigned. The boys, thrilled, called it our “journey to the Promised Land.” Only the bridge-building seemed a waste of time.

Everyone wanted breakfast so work could begin. We milked the cow and goats, then ate a hearty meal of biscuit boiled in milk. Then I set off for the wreck to gather planks for the bridge.

Ernest and Fritz joined me. We soon reached the current and drifted swiftly out to sea. Fritz steered, and as we passed the islet at the bay’s entrance, we were amazed to see thousands of seabirds—gulls and others—rising like a storm cloud, screaming wildly.

Fritz grabbed his gun, but I stopped him. I was curious—what drew such a swarm? Taking advantage of a fresh breeze, I hoisted the sail and sailed toward the island.

Ernest was delighted by the flying pennant; Fritz scanned the sandy shore where the birds settled.

Then he shouted, “Look! There’s a giant fish—those birds are feasting on it! Let’s get closer, Father!”

We couldn’t land directly, but we managed to moor the raft near the scene. Carefully, we approached.

It was a monstrous shark—its flesh torn apart by the birds. Watching them fight, scavenge, and squabble was a grim spectacle.

“There was nothing here yesterday, Father,” Fritz said. “How did it end up stranded?”

“Why, it’s the shark!” Ernest said. “Your shark! I can see the wound you hit!”

“You’re right,” I said. “Though it’s hard to tell through all that pecking. Look at those jaws—the snout, the rows of teeth! Thank God we escaped them. Let’s see if these birds will spare us a piece of skin. It’s rough—when dried, it could be used like a file.”

Ernest jabbed the ramrod into the crowd, killing several birds and scattering the rest. Fritz cut broad strips of skin, and we returned to the boat.

Seeing boards scattered along the shore—just what I needed—I collected them quickly. We made a raft to tow behind us, returning home without visiting the wreck.

As we sailed, I directed Fritz to nail part of the sharkskin flat on boards to dry in the sun, and the rest around the rounded mast.

“Will that work, Father?” he asked. “It’ll be crooked when dry.”

“That’s exactly what I want,” I said. “It might prove useful bent. It’ll make excellent shagreen—like sandpaper for smoothing wood.”

“Shagreen?” Ernest asked. “Isn’t it made from asses’ hides?”

“And rightly so,” I said. “The finest comes from Turkey, Persia, and Tartary—from horse and ass skins. The roughness is created by pressing corn grains into the hide while it’s still soft. Once dried, the grains are removed, leaving a permanent texture. In France, it’s made from the skin of a hideous fish called the angel-fish.”

“Angel-fish?” Fritz laughed. “What a name for something ugly!”

“There are bad angels too,” Ernest said quietly. “Best let people decide for themselves.”

We were nearing shore. Lowering the sail, we moored the raft and boat safely.

No one was in sight. We called out cheerily—and soon heard a reply. My wife and the boys rushed from behind the rocks, each carrying a bundle wrapped in a handkerchief. Franz held up a landing net.

They were surprised to see us back so soon. We explained our discovery. Then the bundles were opened—revealing dozens of large crawfish, scrambling wildly as they were dumped on the ground. The boys chased them in laughter, capturing one only to see another escape.

“Father!” Jack exclaimed. “Did you ever see such crawfish? Thousands of them—we’ve got at least two hundred! Look at their claws!”

“No doubt you found them, Jack?” I asked.

“No!” he said. “Franz did! He and I went toward the stream while Mother worked. Franz was picking up pebbles and alabaster—some pretty, some to strike sparks, some he swore were gold.”

“Jack! Jack!” Franz called. “Come see the crabs on Fritz’s jackal!” We threw it there, and sure enough, it was crawling with them. Are you glad we found them, Father? Will they be good to eat?”

“Excellent,” I said. “And we should be thankful—food comes to us daily.”

After sharing stories, my wife cooked the crawfish. We then went to haul the planks ashore. Even this simple task required planning. I improvised rope harnesses for the cow and donkey to drag each plank from the water to the streambank.

Jack showed me where he thought the bridge should go. I agreed—it was the best spot: banks close together, steep, and nearly equal in height.

“How can we measure if the planks are long enough?” I asked. “A surveyor’s table would help.”

“Try a ball of string, Father,” Ernest suggested. “Tie a stone to one end, throw it across, pull it back, and measure the line!”

We did just that. The distance was eighteen feet. Adding three feet on each side, I calculated twenty-four feet as the needed length.

How to lay the planks? That was harder. We decided to discuss it at dinner.

While we rested, my wife showed me her sewing. With great effort, she’d made two large canvas bags for the donkey. Without a proper needle, she’d used a nail to punch holes for each stitch. I praised her ingenuity and patience.

Dinner was eaten quickly—we were all eager to resume work. A new idea struck me: how to transport one end of a plank across the water. I began to plan it at once.

The next morning, everyone woke early, and the children bounced around the tree like playful monkeys.

“What should we do today, Father?” they called out. “What do you want us to do?”

“Rest, my boys,” I replied. “That’s what we’ll do.”

“Rest?” they echoed. “Why rest? We’ve got so much to explore!”

“Because it’s the seventh day,” I said. “‘Six days shalt thou labour and do all that thou hast to do, but on the seventh, thou shalt do no manner of work.’ This is a day set apart for quiet, reflection, and worship.”

“Really?” Jack asked. “It’s Sunday? How wonderful! I won’t do any work—but I’ll take my bow and arrow and shoot, and climb the tree all day. That sounds like fun!”

“That’s not resting,” I said gently. “That’s just playing. The Lord’s Day is meant to be different—peaceful and sacred.”

“But there’s no church here,” Franz pointed out. “No priest, no organ, nothing.”

“The shade of this great tree is more beautiful than any church,” I told him. “We can worship God right here, in nature’s own temple. Come down now—let’s turn our dining hall into a breakfast room.”

One by one, the children slid down the ladder.

“My dear Elizabeth,” I said, “today we’ll dedicate our time to honoring the Lord. I’ll tell the children a parable instead of preaching a sermon. But without books or usual Sunday activities, we can’t keep them still all day. Afterward, they may enjoy harmless play. And in the evening, we’ll go for a walk together.”

My wife agreed fully. After breakfast, we gathered beneath the cool green canopy, sitting on the soft grass.

We sang hymns and prayed, giving thanks to the Almighty for all His blessings. Then I told the children I would share a story—a parable—instead of a long lesson.

“Oh, that would be perfect!” Franz exclaimed. “I love the Bible parables. When can we read from the Bible again, Father?”

“I’m ashamed to admit it,” I replied, “but while I’ve been busy salvaging food and supplies from the shipwreck, I’ve neglected the true Bread of Life—the Word of God. I’ll search for a Bible when I return to the wreck. Even if ours were lost, I’m sure one was aboard.”

At those words, my wife stood up, opened her magic bag, and pulled out a copy of the Holy Scriptures. I took it with gratitude, read aloud from its pages, and then spoke:

“A Great King ruled over a vast kingdom of light and love. Within it lay a barren island, which He chose to bless. With all His power and kindness, He transformed it into a thriving land—filled with beauty and life. A group of colonists settled there, tasked not only with farming the soil but also with nurturing love and loyalty in their hearts.

As long as they stayed faithful, the colony prospered. Virtue flourished, and joy filled every heart.

Yet, despite such grace, rebellion crept in. Pride and disobedience brought ruin. The land withered, labor became fruitless, and separation from the King seemed certain.

But mercy came. A message of forgiveness was sent. To those who humbled themselves and followed the King’s will—now revealed in even greater tenderness—was promised restoration: a place in the eternal realm of Light and Blessedness.”

After the story, I turned from allegory to truth, speaking plainly and warmly to each child about faith, obedience, and love. With a short prayer, I closed the service.

Then, quietly, we went our separate ways.

I worked on sharpening arrows using porcupine quills. Franz begged me to make him a bow and arrow, and Fritz asked advice about turning a tiger-cat skin into useful cases. Jack helped with the arrows, inserting sharp spines into reeds and tying them tightly with thread. He wished he had glue to secure them better.

“Oh, Jack!” Franz called. “Mamma’s soup is super sticky! Should I grab some?”

“No, silly!” Jack laughed. “Look in the tool-box. There’s real glue there.”

“And you’ll find it,” I said. “The soup wouldn’t hold. But Jack, don’t mock your brother’s idea. Many great inventions began with thoughts that seemed foolish at first.”

Just then, a shot rang out overhead. Two small birds dropped to the ground. Looking up, we saw Ernest perched in the branches, grinning. “Well hit! Well hit! Wasn’t that a good shot?”

He climbed down, handed me the birds—one a thrush, the other an ortolan dove, prized for its rich flavor.

Since the figs on the tree were just ripening, these birds might soon come in flocks. We could catch them in large numbers and preserve them in casks with melted lard or butter—perfect for the rainy season.

By now, Jack had finished his arrows and practiced archery. I made Franz a bow and arrows, but he quickly wanted a quiver too. So I crafted one from bark, shaped it with a flat back and bottom, added a string, and fastened it to his shoulder. He filled it with arrows and marched off looking, as his mother said, like a tiny Cupid ready for battle.

Soon, dinner was called—and we all obeyed willingly.

During the meal, I suggested naming the places we’d explored.

“It’ll get confusing,” I said, “if we keep saying things like ‘the little island where we found the dead shark’ or ‘the stream near the tent.’ Let’s give names to our landmarks. Start with the bay where we landed. What shall we call it?”

“Oyster Bay!” Fritz said.

“No!” Jack shouted. “Lobster Bay! In memory of the old fellow who bit my leg!”

“I think,” my wife said, “we should name it Safety Bay—to remember how grateful we are to have survived.”

Everyone agreed. It was decided.

Other names followed quickly. Our first camp became Tentholm. The small island in the bay was Shark’s Island. The marshy area with reeds was Flamingo Marsh. Naming our leafy home proved harder. Finally, we called it Falconhurst. Then we named the hill we climbed first—Prospect Hill; the rocky point where we searched in vain for our crew—Cape Disappointment; and the river where we landed—Jackal River. Our map was complete.

In the afternoon, the boys continued their projects. Fritz finished his cases. Jack asked for help making a protective coat for Turk from the porcupine skin. After cleaning it thoroughly, we cut and shaped it to fit the dog. Once dry and strung with leather, Turk wore a strange, effective armor.

Juno avoided him completely, darting away whenever he approached. Clearly, no animal would dare attack him now.

I decided to make a helmet for Jack from the leftover skin. He was thrilled when I finished it.

As evening fell, we walked through fragrant glades, ending our Sabbath with prayer and a joyful hymn. Peace filled our hearts as we retired to rest.

The next morning, I proposed a trip to Tentholm, taking a new route. We left well armed—I and my three older sons with guns and game bags, Franz with his bow and quiver full of arrows. We looked like a curious band: Fritz with his margay-skin belt, Jack in his odd headgear—like young savages.

My wife walked beside me, unarmed, carrying a jar for butter. Ahead of us, Turk led the way in his porcupine armor, Juno keeping her distance.

Master Knips tried to mount his usual horse, but when he saw the strange covering, he hesitated. Touching it with a paw, he grimaced—this wasn’t comfortable! Chattering loudly, he leapt onto Juno’s back instead and settled in contentedly.

The flamingo, used to being petted, joined us. At first, he stayed close to the children, hopping between them. But their wandering path bored him. He soon left them and walked calmly beside me.

We strolled along the stream in the cool air. The boys roamed ahead, exploring.

Suddenly, I heard a shout. Ernest ran toward me, breathless, holding a plant.

“Potatoes! Potatoes, Father!” he gasped.

“Yes!” Jack called. “Acres and acres of potatoes!”

“Ernest,” I said, recognizing the leaves and roots, “you’ve made a wonderful discovery. With potatoes, we’ll never starve.”

“Come see them!” Jack urged. “Thousands of them!”

We rushed to the spot. A wide field stretched before us, covered with potato plants.

“It would’ve been hard,” Jack said, “not to notice such a big patch.”

“Maybe,” Ernest smiled, “but you might not have known it was potatoes.”

“True,” Jack admitted. “You get credit for finding it. I’ll get the first harvest.” He dug up several plants, filling his bag. The monkey copied him, scratching with his paws until he had a pile nearby.

We were so excited, we kept digging until every bag, pouch, and pocket was full.

Some wanted to go back to Falconhurst right away to cook the potatoes. I insisted we continue. We were heavy-laden but happy.

“How can we thank the Giver of all these gifts enough?” I asked.

“Oh,” Franz said, “we can say, ‘We thank thee, O Lord, for all thy goodness and mercy; and bless us for Jesus Christ’s sake. Amen.’”

“That’s not enough,” Fritz said. “Would it be enough to just say ‘Thank you’ to Mom and Dad, without showing it by loving them and doing what pleases them?”

“You’re right,” I said. “Franz missed the point. He should have added, ‘Give me grace to do Thy will, and to obey Thee in all things.’”

As we talked, we reached the head of the stream, where it tumbled over rocks in a sparkling cascade. We crossed and entered tall grass, pushing through thick reeds.

Beyond, the landscape was breathtaking. Lush tropical plants surrounded us—palm trees, ferns, bright flowers, creeping vines, cacti, aloes, jasmine, vanilla, Indian pea, and pineapples*—filling the air with sweet perfume.

The boys rushed to the pineapples, eating greedily. My wife warned them: “No doctors here—if you get sick, you’ll have to cure yourselves!”

But they didn’t listen. They sent Knips to fetch the ripest fruits.

While they ate, I studied the surrounding plants. One caught my eye—the karatas.

“Come here, boys,” I said. “This is worth more than your pineapples. See that plant with long leaves and red flowers? That’s the karatas. Its leaves make strong thread. Crushed, they form a healing salve. The inner pith lights easily—useful for fire or fishing bait.”

“Suppose you were stranded here,” I asked Ernest, “how would you start a fire without matches or flint?”

“Like the natives,” he said. “Rub two sticks together.”

“Try it,” I said. “But only when you have all day. It takes hours. Watch this.” I broke a twig, peeled the bark, placed the pith on stone, struck pebbles together—sparks flew, and the pith caught fire.

The boys cheered. I pulled threads from the leaves and gave them to my wife.

“What about these prickly plants?” Fritz asked. “They seem useless—just annoying.”

“That’s an Indian fig,” I said. “It grows best on dry rock, getting nourishment from the air. Its juice has medicinal uses. The fruit is tasty and healthy.”

Jack rushed to pick some, but the thorns stopped him. He returned with bleeding hands.

I cleaned his wounds, made a wooden skewer, pierced a fig, twisted it off, and sliced it open. The others followed. We enjoyed the fruit—it was delicious.

Ernest examined his carefully. “What are these little red bugs?” he asked. “They’re stuck all over. Could they be cochineal?”

He handed it to me. “Yes,” I said. “That’s real cochineal. It makes a brilliant red dye—worth gold in Europe. But for us, it’s not much use… unless someone wants to wear bright clothes.”

“No thanks,” Jack said. “But we’ll collect some when we go home. Now, let’s find something more useful.” They bombarded me with questions about every plant.

“Stop,” I said finally. “Even the smartest botanist would struggle with these. I’ve never seen most of them—only read about them. I can’t name or explain half of them.”

Still, we discussed the ones I knew, and eventually reached Tentholm. Everything was safe. We collected what we needed. I opened the butter cask, my wife filled her pot. Fritz checked the ammunition. Jack and Ernest ran to the beach to catch geese and ducks.

But the birds, left alone too long, were wary. Nothing would lure them ashore.

Ernest had a clever idea. He tied pieces of cheese to long strings and tossed them into the water. The hungry ducks grabbed them. With careful pulls, he drew them onto shore.

While Jack and Ernest tied the birds together by the feet, we gathered salt and loaded it onto Turk’s back—after removing his armor. We attached the birds to our game bags, locked the tent, and headed home along the shore.

After a cheerful walk, we arrived at Falconhurst. I released the birds, clipped their wings to keep them from flying away, and settled them by the stream. That night, we feasted on potatoes, milk, and butter, then climbed into our tree and slept soundly.

Earlier, I’d noticed driftwood on the beach. I thought it wise to build a sledge—so hauling supplies from Tentholm wouldn’t be so hard.

I woke early, roused Ernest to help—wanting to help him overcome his tendency to laziness. After stretching and yawning, he got up happily, pleased to be part of a secret mission while others slept. We took the donkey, who pulled a large branch I hoped to use as a sled base.

As we walked, I said, “I suppose you’re sorry to leave your warm hammock and dreams so early?”

“Oh, Father, don’t laugh!” he said. “I really want to change. I’m glad to go. I planned to hunt ortolans, but there’s time later. The others will try, but I doubt they’ll succeed.”

“Why not?” I asked.

“They won’t know what kind of shot to use. And they’ll probably aim too high—missed shots because of the thick branches.”

“Well,” I said, “you’re thoughtful and observant—valuable traits. But sometimes, quick action is needed. Train yourself to act swiftly in emergencies. Presence of mind is rare—but can be learned.”

On the beach, we gathered wood, laid it across the broad branch, and with the donkey’s help, dragged a fine load back—including a small chest half-buried in sand.

We heard gunshots as we neared home. The boys rushed to greet us, curious about the chest. I let them open it, then asked my wife to forgive our unannounced absence. After her gentle scolding, I explained the sledge plan—she loved it, already imagining it hauling her butter barrel.

The chest belonged to a sailor—full of wet clothes.

The boys showed off dozens of birds, telling stories of their hunting adventures. Ernest had guessed their mistakes right. Practice was improving them.

But their mother said, “I can’t use more birds. Can’t we trap them instead? You’re wasting powder and shot.”

I agreed. I told the boys to put down their guns. The younger ones made snares from karatas threads, following my instructions. Fritz and Ernest helped me build the sledge.

We were working when chaos erupted among the chickens. Cocks crowed, hens fluttered wildly.

We ran to investigate. Ernest spotted Knips sneaking away—then caught him eating a fresh egg he’d hidden. Several more were buried nearby.

My wife was delighted—eggs at last! From now on, she’d lock Knips up until eggs were collected.

Later, Jack, setting snares, discovered a pair of our pigeons building a nest in the tree. We were glad to have them. I warned the boys not to shoot near the tree or disturb the nests. The snares were only for wild birds.

And so, our day ended—not with hardship, but with purpose, discovery, and growing hope.

The next morning, while breakfast was being prepared, I turned my attention to the beautiful kangaroo hide I had taken. I wanted to preserve it whole and properly, so I carefully set about drying and tanning it. Once that was done, I called Ernest and Jack aside to give them final instructions before we set off for the wreck—Fritz had already made sure everything was ready.

But when I looked for the boys, they were gone. Their mother guessed they might have gone to gather potatoes, since we’d run low. I asked her to scold them upon their return for leaving without permission. Still, knowing they’d taken Turk the dog with them, I felt fairly certain no harm would come to them—even if I did worry a little about leaving my dear wife alone with little Franz. I comforted her with promises that we’d be back soon, loaded with treasures from the shipwreck.

We made steady progress toward the wreck, crossing the bridge over Jackal River. Suddenly, just as we reached the far side, Jack and Ernest burst out from a hiding place they’d been lying in, startling us completely. They’d been waiting in ambush, eager to surprise us—and they succeeded perfectly.

They thought this meant they could now join us on the trip to the wreck. But I quickly put an end to that idea, though I couldn’t bring myself to scold them. They’d only meant to play a joke, and besides, I suddenly realized I needed to send word home. I told them to hurry back, not to leave their mother worrying, but since they were already so far, they could collect some salt on the way.

I instructed them to tell their mother that I expected to stay overnight on the wreck, because the work would take longer than planned. I’d meant to mention it earlier, but I hadn’t had the courage—knowing how much she’d object. Now, thanks to the boys’ unplanned adventure, I had a chance to let her know.

“Goodbye, boys! Take care!” shouted Fritz as I joined him in the tub-boat. We pushed off into the current.

The water carried us swiftly out of the bay, and before long we were moored safely beside the wreck. Clambering up its broken sides, we stood on what remained of the deck and began planning our next steps. My goal was clear: build a raft strong enough to carry heavy cargo—far too much for our small boat.

We found several empty water casks, perfect for a base. We sealed them tightly, dropped them into the sea, and arranged twelve in three rows, binding them together with wooden spars. Then we laid down sturdy planks to form a solid floor, reinforced with a low railing around the edges. Before long, we had a reliable raft, exactly suited to our needs.

By then, we were exhausted from the day’s work and had eaten only the light snacks we’d brought. Returning to land that evening would’ve been impossible. So, grateful that we weren’t expected home yet, we made ourselves a hearty supper from the ship’s supplies. Then we rested on spring mattresses—such a luxury after sleeping in narrow hammocks.

The next morning, we began loading both the raft and the boat. First, we emptied our own cabins, then moved on to the captain’s quarters, taking furniture, doors, window frames, bolts, bars, and locks. We collected the officers’ chests, along with those of the carpenter and gunsmith—their contents too heavy to lift at once, so we had to remove them piece by piece.

One chest held an dazzling array of jewelry—gold and silver watches, snuffboxes, buckles, chains, rings, and all sorts of trinkets. A box of coins caught our eye, too. But more useful right now was a case of plain knives and forks—perfect for everyday use, better than the fancy silver ones we’d already brought ashore.

Then came a discovery that filled me with joy: a number of young fruit trees, neatly packed and labeled. The names on the tags brought a smile to my face—apple, pear, chestnut, orange, almond, peach, apricot, plum, cherry, and vine. These were gifts from Europe, destined for a distant colony—but now they belonged to us.

The entire cargo was a treasure beyond measure. Iron tools, plumbing supplies, lead, paint, grindstones, cart wheels, blacksmith equipment, spades, ploughshares, sacks of maize, peas, oats, and wheat, a hand mill, and even the parts of a sawmill—carefully numbered and ready to assemble. We stood amazed, overwhelmed by the riches around us.

We couldn’t take everything. But if a storm hit, the wreck would be destroyed—and everything left behind would be lost forever. So we selected the most useful items: grain, fruit trees, fishing gear, reels, ropes, two harpoons, and a mariner’s compass.

Fritz remembered our close call with the shark and insisted on having the harpoons ready. He even joked about himself as a whaler, pretending to swing his weapon like a true seaman.

By early afternoon, both boats were fully loaded. We set off for shore, towing the raft behind us. It was tense at first—towing such a large craft posed real danger. But the sea was calm, the wind favorable, and we managed to raise the sail. Progress was smooth and satisfying.

Then Fritz spotted something odd floating in the distance. He handed me the telescope. “Look,” he said. “It’s a turtle—sleeping on the surface!”

“Come on, Father,” he urged. “Steer toward it!”

I did as he asked, curious to see the creature up close. But I didn’t expect what happened next. Fritz was facing away from me, the sail between us. Suddenly, I felt a sharp jolt—then another—as if a line were running through a reel. Before I could react, the boat surged forward.

“Fritz, what are you doing?” I shouted. “You’re pulling us under!”

“I’ve got him!” he yelled triumphantly. “He’s mine!”

To my astonishment, he’d harpooned the turtle. A rope was tied to the barb, and the animal was dragging us across the water.

I grabbed my hatchet and rushed forward to cut the line. “Stop!” I cried. “This is madness! You’ll sink us all.”

“Wait, Father!” Fritz pleaded. “No danger yet. I’ll cut it myself if things get bad. Let’s try to catch it!”

I hesitated. “If this turtle capsizes our boats or spills our goods, it will cost us dearly—even if it doesn’t drown us. Be careful. I’ll wait a few minutes—but the moment it’s unsafe, cut the line!”

The turtle swam hard toward open sea, but the strain proved too much. It changed course and headed back toward shore, dragging us along. It aimed for a stretch of beach well to the left of our usual landing spot. There, the sand sloped gently, and we grounded sharply—but without overturning.

The turtle was clearly spent. It had been pulling two heavily loaded vessels like a steam tug. Its goal was simply to reach land. I leapt into the water, waded to it, and killed it with my axe. Even then, it struggled fiercely until I severed its head.

We were near Falconhurst, so Fritz fired his gun and shouted to warn the family. Soon, we saw them rushing toward us—our return drawing everyone from the house. The sight of the turtle, the loaded boats, and the raft sparked excitement. But my wife’s greatest relief was seeing us safe—she’d been worried sick about our night away, especially after hearing how perilous our ride had been.

We hurried to unload. The boys ran to fetch the sledge. I secured the boats using iron blocks we’d brought—no anchor, but it worked. Together, we hoisted the turtle onto the sledge. It was incredibly heavy—especially with the sapling fruit trees added. But we managed it.

Back home, we chatted happily about our adventures. The first task: preparing turtle meat for supper. My wife thought it would be difficult, but I turned the beast on its back, broke the lower shell with my hatchet, and removed a large piece of flesh. I ordered it cooked with salt, shell and all.

“But look at this green fat,” she said, shuddering. “It’s disgusting. No one could eat that.”

“Leave it!” I insisted. “That’s the best part—the favorite of gourmet cooks. If there’s too much, trim it off. Use it as lard. Give the scraps to the dogs.”

“And the shell!” Fritz exclaimed. “I want to make a water trough from it, near the brook. Keep it full of fresh water—it’d be so useful!”

“Excellent idea,” I said. “We can do it if we find clay to make a firm base.”

“Oh, clay?” Jack said. “I’ve got a big lump under that tree root.”

“You found clay near the river this morning,” his mother said. “You came home covered in mud—I had to scrub your clothes and wash you clean!”

“I’d never have found it,” Jack replied, “if I hadn’t slipped and fallen into it.”

“That’s very believable,” she said, smiling. “Though you sounded like you’d searched for days.”

Just then, Ernest spoke up. “After the clay and shell talk, I’d like to show you something I found today. Roots—drying now. They look like radishes, but the plant was almost a bush. I didn’t taste them, though—our sow was eating them greedily.”

“Wise decision,” I said. “Pigs eat many things harmful to humans. Show me.” I examined the roots closely. “Ernest, this may be manioc! If so, it could save us. In the West Indies, they make cassava bread from it. With potatoes already growing, we’d be nearly self-sufficient if we can process these into flour. But great care is needed—must squeeze out the juice, or it could be poisonous.”

“If we gather enough, we’ll try making bread,” I added. “I think I know how.”

There was still time for another trip, so I went back with the older boys, leaving Franz with his mother. We all looked forward to a grand supper—our work had been grueling.

On the way, Fritz said, “Father, isn’t the turtle shell valuable? Don’t they make combs and boxes from tortoiseshell?”

“This turtle’s shell is worthless for decoration,” I explained. “Its kind is edible, but not prized. The shells used for ornaments come from other species—too tough to eat. Tortoiseshell is heated, the outer layer peels off, revealing a beautiful, translucent pattern that polishes to a high shine.”

We returned with the sledge loaded again—chests, four cart wheels, the hand mill, and smaller items. My wife greeted us warmly. “You’ve been working nonstop,” she said. “Now you can rest. Come see my cellar!”

She showed us a small cask half-buried in the ground, shaded by leaves and branches.

“I found it on the beach today,” she said. “Thought it might be wine. I brought it up for you. The boys are dying to know what it is.”

I tested it with a straw. “Delicious,” I declared. “Canary sack—some of the finest I’ve ever tasted.”

She beamed. “So my efforts weren’t wasted!” The boys crowded around, begging for a sip. I let them take turns, but soon had to stop them—they grew wild and excited, risking drunkenness.

Supper was better: turtle meat, rich and flavorful. We enjoyed it heartily, then hauled the ship’s mattresses into our rooms. That night, we slept deeply and refreshingly.

Next morning, I rose early to check on the boats. The dogs greeted me eagerly; the roosters crowed; kids frolicked. Only the donkey seemed reluctant to start the day—unfortunate, since I needed him.

I woke him up, harnessed him to the sledge. The cow, still un-milked, stayed behind. The rest of us left her snoozing.

My fears about the boats were unfounded—they were fine. I gathered a light load and returned quickly. The donkey trotted willingly, eager for breakfast.

As I approached the tree, silence reigned. No one was awake, though it was broad daylight. My wife jumped when I arrived, startled by the noise.

“What made us sleep so late?” she asked. “Must be the mattresses—they’re wonderful, but too cozy. Look—the children are still asleep.”

The boys finally tumbled down, yawning and rubbing their eyes. Ernest last, as always.

“Come on, boys,” I said. “This won’t do. Your beds were too comfortable.”

I suspected the canary wine had played a role, too. I resolved to ration it strictly from now on.

“Prayers and breakfast first,” I continued. “Then to work. We must finish unloading before the tide rises.”

With hard effort, we completed the task. I boarded with Fritz, while others went home. Jack lingered, pleading so hard I took him along.

I’d intended only to move the boats to Safety Bay, but the calm sea tempted me to visit the wreck again. It took longer than expected. On board, we explored the cargo, collected portable items, and made use of the breeze before it faded.

Jack raced back with a wheelbarrow, thrilled to have found such a handy tool. Fritz followed, bursting with news: he’d discovered the dismantled parts of a pinnace—complete with rigging and even two small brass guns.

“Great find!” I said. But I knew assembling it would be a huge challenge.

We gathered new items: a copper boiler, iron plates, tobacco graters, two grindstones, a barrel of powder, another of flints, and two more wheelbarrows—Jack kept his under strict guard.

As we neared shore, we saw figures lined up along the water’s edge—small, dark-coated, white-waistcoated beings, standing stiffly, arms at their sides. Every now and then, one would stretch out its arms as if to embrace us.

“Ah! Here come the pigmy natives!” I laughed.

“Oh, Father!” Jack exclaimed. “Are they Lilliputians? I read about them once. They must exist!”

“No Lilliputians,” I said. “Penguins. We’ve seen them before, but never in such numbers. Ernest knocked one down shortly after we landed. Excellent swimmers, but helpless on land—they can’t fly or run.”

As we drew closer, Jack leapt from the tub and waded ashore. He surprised the birds with his stick, knocking half a dozen unconscious or dead. The rest dived into the water and vanished.

Penguin meat is oily and unpleasant, so I regretted Jack’s attack. But when we landed, some of the fallen revived and began waddling away. We caught them, tied their feet with grass, and laid them on the sand.

The three wheelbarrows each carried a load. Jack pushed his with the live penguins seated solemnly inside. We raced back.

The dogs barked wildly at our approach, then rushed forward so violently that poor Jack—already struggling—was knocked over, penguins and all.

He snapped at them in frustration. The scene caused great laughter. Everyone puzzled over the tobacco graters and iron plates.

I sent the boys to catch geese and ducks, and told them to tie a penguin to each bird’s leg—to see if we could tame them.

My wife had been busy while we were gone—she’d gathered potatoes and planted manioc seeds. I praised her industry. Little Franz giggled: “Father, wait till mother and I give you Indian corn, melons, pumpkins, and cucumbers!”

“You’ve spoiled the surprise!” she teased.

“I’m delighted,” I said. “Where did the seeds come from?”

“From my magic bag!” she replied. “Every time I dug for potatoes, I planted seeds in the soil. And I planted potatoes too.”

“Well done,” I said. “You’re a model of wisdom and diligence!”

“But,” she added, “those tobacco graters worry me. Are you going to make snuff? Please, focus on food first—don’t think about noses!”

“Don’t worry,” I said. “No snuff here. But these graters will help you enjoy fresh bread—for the first time in ages.”

“How?” she asked. “Bread without ovens? That’s just teasing.”

“Not loaves,” I said. “Flat cakes or scones—on these iron plates. I’ll bake them right away, using Ernest’s roots. First, make me a strong canvas bag.”

She agreed, though she still doubted my baking skills. I noticed she boiled potatoes first—just in case my bread failed.

And so, our day ended with promise, laughter, and the quiet joy of survival turning into prosperity.

Now that we had found a way to make bread for our family, my thoughts turned once more to the wrecked ship and all the treasures still trapped inside. Above all, I longed to claim the beautiful pinnace—our prize vessel—and knew it would take every one of us working together to free it. So I began gently urging my wife to let me lead a full crew, with all the boys except Franz.

She hesitated at first, her worry plain, but finally agreed—only after I promised on my word that I would never spend a single night aboard the wreck. I gave my promise, though reluctantly, and we parted, neither of us entirely satisfied.

The boys were thrilled by the idea of such a grand expedition. They packed their bags with cassava bread and potatoes and set off in high spirits.

We reached Safety Bay without incident. First, we visited the geese and ducks living in the marsh there, fed them well, and checked that they were thriving. Then, each of us fastened on our cork belts, climbed into the tub-boat, towed the raft behind, and sailed straight toward the wreck.

Once aboard, I told the boys to gather anything useful they could find and load it onto the raft, ready for our return that evening. Then, I began a careful inspection of the pinnace.

What I saw filled me with dismay. The little boat was wedged tightly at the far end of the hold, crammed into a space so narrow and confined that assembling it in place was impossible. And the parts were too heavy to move piece by piece to a better location.

I sent the boys off to explore and collect whatever they liked, then sat quietly to think.

As my eyes adjusted to the dim light filtering through cracks in the hull, I noticed something remarkable: every part of the pinnace was carefully labeled with numbers. If only we could find enough room and time, I realized, we might just succeed in putting it together.

“Room! We need room to work, boys!” I called out as they came to see what plan I had hatched. “Bring axes—we’ll break down walls and clear space!”

And so we worked, cutting through wood and clearing debris. But as dusk approached, we saw how much remained to be done. It was clear that reclaiming the pinnace would demand endless effort and unwavering determination.

With haste, we prepared to return to shore. The walk back to Falconhurst felt long and weary, until—surprise and joy!—we found my wife and little Franz waiting at Tentholm. She had decided to stay there while we worked on the wreck.

“In this way,” she said with a warm smile, “you’ll be closer to your work, and I won’t lose sight of you.”

“You’re a wise and kind wife,” I replied, touched by her thoughtfulness. “And we’ll work all the harder so you can return to Falconhurst soon.”

“Come see what we’ve brought you, mother!” Fritz shouted. He and his brothers proudly displayed two casks of butter, three of flour, corn, rice, and other supplies that delighted our careful housewife.

From then on, our days were spent in steady labor aboard the wreck—first carving out space around the pinnace, then fitting its pieces together. We rose early and returned late, each trip bringing valuable goods from the old ship. At last, after incredible effort, the pinnace stood complete—ready to launch—but still trapped within thick wooden walls that defied our strength.

It seemed as if the graceful vessel had awakened from a long sleep, eager to leap into the open sea and feel the wind on her sails. I couldn’t bear the thought of all our hard work ending in failure. Yet no solution presented itself—until an idea struck me.

Without revealing my plan, I fetched a large cast-iron mortar, filled it with gunpowder, placed a block of oak on top, and drilled a hole for a fuse. I positioned this powerful petard so that when it exploded, it would blast open the side of the ship next to the pinnace.

Securing it with chains to prevent damage from recoil, I told the boys I’d be going ashore earlier than usual. I calmly instructed them to get into the boat, then lit a slow-burning match and hurried after them, heart pounding.

We pulled the raft close to shore and began unloading. I left the second boat in the water, ready to depart instantly. My anxiety was hidden, but I listened intently for the sound of the explosion.

Then—*flash!* A thunderous roar! A great burst of smoke!

My wife and children turned toward the sea, frightened, then looked at me, expecting explanation.

“Perhaps,” she said, “you left a candle near some powder?”

“Not unlikely,” I replied calmly. “We had a fire below when caulking the pinnace. I’ll go check right away. Anyone care to come?”

The boys needed no encouragement—they leapt into the boat. I stayed a moment to whisper reassurance to my wife, then joined them. We rowed faster than ever before.

No change was visible where we usually boarded the wreck, so we circled to the other side—and gasped at the sight.

A massive gap tore through the hull. Decks and railings lay shattered, wreckage floated in the water, and chaos reigned. But inside the ruined compartment, the pinnace sat untouched—unharmed.

The boys stared, stunned by the destruction, until I cried out, “Hurrah! She’s ours! The pinnace is free! We can launch her now!”

They turned to me, then suddenly understood. “You planned this, didn’t you? That machine we built—it was meant to blow up the ship!”

They rushed to join me at the breach, where I found everything intact. The explosion had freed the pinnace without damaging it.

The boys studied the wreckage, fascinated, as I explained how a petard worked—its purpose, its dangers, and how to use it safely.

Now, launching the boat was simple. I had already placed rollers under the keel. With levers and pulleys, we used all our strength to push her forward. A rope controlled her descent, and slowly, steadily, she slid from the stocks and glided into the water—where she floated as if born for the sea.

We cheered wildly, waving and shouting.

After securing the pinnace in the safest spot, we returned to Tentholm and explained the explosion: “We blew a hole in the side so we could get the rest of the cargo out in just a few days.”

The next days were spent finishing the rigging, mounting her two small brass guns, and preparing her for sea. The boys grew bold and excited—talking endlessly of savages, fleets of canoes, battles, and glorious victories.

I reminded them gently, “Even if your victories are brilliant, may God keep you from needing to fight at all.”

Finally, the pinnace was ready. Still, my wife knew nothing of our surprise. The boys, sworn to secrecy, were allowed to fire a salute as we entered the bay.

We cast off, unfurled the sail, and began our voyage. The pinnace cut swiftly through the water. I stood at the helm; Ernest and Jack manned the guns; Fritz gave the command: “Fire!”

Bang! Bang! The report echoed across the cliffs, followed by our wild shouts.

My wife and Franz rushed from the tent, alarmed and amazed. But when they recognized us, they waved joyfully and ran to meet us at the shore.

We guided the pinnace close to a rocky ledge, and Fritz helped his mother aboard. Breathless and wide-eyed, she exclaimed, “You dear, horrid, wonderful people—shall I scold or praise you? You frightened me half to death! Seeing a lovely little ship sail in was shocking enough, but the guns made me tremble! Had I not heard your voices, I’d have fled with Franz who-knows-where!”

“But did you really build this yourselves?” she asked, once forgiven. “What a charming little yacht! I wouldn’t mind sailing in it myself.”

After showing off the pinnace and receiving her praise, she said, “Now, come with me—I want you to see what little Franz and I have done while you were gone.”

We landed and followed her upriver to the cascade. There, to our astonishment, we found a neat garden laid out in beds and paths.

“We don’t fire salutes to celebrate our work,” she said, “though someday I’ll need fire—in a peaceful way.” She pointed to rows of lettuce, cabbage, beans, peas. “Imagine the dinners I’ll cook for you! And look—potatoes, cassava, sugar cane, fruit trees—all planted. I’ll need you to help irrigate them with hollow bamboo pipes from the waterfall. On the shaded rocks, I’m growing pineapples and melons—they’ll look splendid. To protect European vegetables from the sun, I’ve planted maize around them. Their shade will shield them from the heat. What do you think?”

“I think it’s brilliant,” I said. “Perfectly planned. Now, as sunset nears, let’s return to the tent. We’re all ready for supper and rest.”

That evening, we talked happily about our new projects. Everyone felt the deep satisfaction of hard work and the joy of surprising others. I reminded my sons that true happiness comes not from selfish pleasure, but from creating good for others.

Next morning, my wife said, “If you can spare time from the sea, dear husband, I’d like you to tend the fruit trees. I fear they’ve been neglected. I’ve watered them and covered their roots, but I couldn’t plant them.”

“You’ve done more than I expected,” I replied. “And if you don’t ask me to give up the sea forever, I’ll go to Falconhurst as soon as the raft is unloaded and everything is secure here.”

Life on land was refreshing. The boys worked eagerly, quickly stacking supplies at the tent, covering them with sailcloth and pegging them down. The pinnace, now anchored, transformed our harbor—no longer dominated by the clumsy tub-boat and flat raft.

Loaded with provisions, we returned to Falconhurst, finding it more peaceful and inviting than ever after our labors.

Several Sundays had passed since we’d left. Now, the Day of Rest returned, observed with heartfelt devotion. I kept my talk short—knowing long sermons would lose their attention—but the Bible reading and simple lessons held them. Their young voices joined sweetly in hymns, sung from memory by my wife.

That evening, I asked the boys to show their skills in running, jumping, wrestling, climbing.

“Keep practicing these,” I said. “Strength and agility mean life in danger. No man can truly be brave without knowing he can endure and act.”

“I want my sons strong—morally and physically,” I added. “That means, little Franz,” I said, looking into his bright blue eyes, “brave to do what’s right, strong to work, hunt, provide, and defend.”

The next day, they were eager to train. I told them I’d make a new weapon—but only if they promised not to neglect archery. As for their guns, I trusted they’d never abandon them.

I took a long cord and tied a lead bullet to each end. Instantly, the boys bombarded me with questions.

“This is a miniature lasso,” I said. “Mexicans, Patagonians, and other tribes use it to hunt. Instead of bullets, they attach stones to long ropes. The hunter swings it overhead, then throws it with precision. Pulling it back, he can strike again—killing or wounding prey.”

“Sometimes,” I continued, “they aim to capture alive—like a wild horse or buffalo. While riding fast, they throw the lasso so the stone wraps around the animal’s neck or legs, stopping it mid-run.”

“Oh, father! What a clever tool!” they cried. “Try it now! There’s the donkey!”

I declined to test it on a live animal, but agreed to aim at a tree stump nearby.

To my surprise, the cord wrapped tightly around the stump—proving the technique possible. The boys begged for their own lassos, and I made them quickly. Soon, lasso practice became daily sport.

Fritz, the most agile and strongest, mastered it fastest.

That night, the weather changed. By dawn, I saw the sea churning violently—a storm was brewing. But I smiled, thinking of our pinnace safely moored.

My attention wasn’t only on the boys. My wife had many things to show—wild pigeons and ortolans preserved in lard, tame pigeon nests, and especially the struggling fruit trees, dry and wilted from neglect.

“We must plant them now,” I said. “Or we’ll lose them.”

So we worked swiftly, planting the orchard before setting off for Calabash Wood—to gather gourds and make vessels of all kinds.

Everyone was eager. The planting finished by evening, we prepared for an early start. My wife and Franz would join us, along with pets and servants.

At sunrise, we set off. The sledge, loaded with ammunition and food and drawn by the donkey, would carry our harvest home.

Turk led the way, armored as always.

The boys followed with guns and game bags. My wife and I walked behind, and Juno trotted last—grumbling, poor dog—because Knips insisted on riding her back.

I carried two guns: one with shot for game, one with ball for defense.

We crossed Flamingo Marsh quickly, and beyond lay a vast, fertile landscape—new to my wife and two boys. The beauty enchanted them.

Suddenly, Fritz and Jack vanished into the bush. Moments later, barking erupted, followed by a gunshot. A large bird burst from the thicket and fell.

But it didn’t die. It scrambled up and bolted at astonishing speed. The dogs chased, Fritz ran after, and Juno darted sideways—flinging Knips onto the sand.

She caught the bird, but it fought fiercely, kicking with powerful legs and sharp claws. Neither boy nor dog could subdue it.

I rushed over. Juno clung to a wing. The bird was a magnificent bustard. Watching my chance, I threw a handkerchief over it and struggled to bind its limbs.

We carried it triumphantly to the others, who had been resting.

“What have you got?”

“A bustard! Amazing!”

“It’s the one we missed before, isn’t it, mother? Ha! Old fellow—you’re caught!”

“I think it’s a hen,” said Ernest. “The mother bird.”

“Oh, poor thing,” my wife said softly. “She had chicks. Now they’re alone. Shouldn’t we let her go?”

“My dear, kind-hearted wife,” I said, “that was weeks ago. Her young are likely grown. Besides, she’s wounded. Let’s try to heal her. If we succeed, she’ll join our flock. If not, you can roast her for dinner.”

We resumed our journey, passing Monkey Grove—the site of Fritz’s adventure with the orphan ape.

As he retold the tale, Ernest stood beneath a towering cocoanut palm, staring up in awe at its height and graceful crown. Below, clusters of nuts hung.

“It’s awfully high,” he sighed. “I wish one would fall.”

No sooner spoken than—*thud!*—a huge nut dropped at his feet.

He jumped back, looking up nervously.

Then—*thud!*—another.

“Why, it’s like the fairy tale of the wishing cap!” he laughed. “My wish came true!”

“I suspect the fairy wants to hit us, not gift us,” I said. “There’s probably a grumpy old ape up there.”

We examined the nuts—they weren’t even ripe.

Curious, we gathered around, peering upward.

“Hollo! I see him!” Fritz yelled. “Oh, a hideous creature! Flat, round, plate-sized, with terrible claws! Here he comes—he’s coming down!”

And so the adventure continued.

The next morning, our first task was to return to Calabash Wood and retrieve the sledge loaded with the dishes, bowls, and baskets we had crafted. I chose to go with only Fritz, leaving the other boys with their mother. My plan was to explore beyond the rocky hills that ran along the coast, and I thought it wiser to travel light for such a venture.

As we passed through the grove of evergreen oaks, we spotted our sow grazing peacefully on acorns. She seemed none the worse for the scare we’d given her the day before—indeed, she appeared friendlier than usual, perhaps seeing us as her rescuers from the wild dogs.

Birds filled the trees around us, undisturbed by our presence—until Fritz fired his gun, bringing down a brilliant blue jay and two parakeets, one glowing scarlet, the other shimmering green and gold. As he reloaded, a strange, dull thumping sound reached our ears—like a distant drumbeat. Instantly alert, we crept forward, hiding behind low bushes and thick vines, until we reached a clearing.

There, perched on a fallen log, stood a magnificent bird about the size of a rooster. Its feathers were rich chestnut brown, mottled with dark browns and grays. On its shoulders grew curious tufts of velvety black feathers, gleaming with hints of green. It ruffled its wings, raised its tail and neck feathers, and strutted in an odd, stately dance.

After some time, it spread its tail like a fan, stiffened its wings, and began beating them rapidly—faster and faster—until a rumbling sound echoed like distant thunder. The whirring motion created a cloud-like effect around it. This was the mysterious noise that had startled us, likely amplified by the hollow log beneath it.

I watched in fascination when suddenly a shot rang out from behind me. The performance ended abruptly—Fritz, eager and impulsive, had fired without my permission. The bird dropped dead from its perch, and the crowd of watching birds scattered in panic.

I was deeply disappointed. Such a rare and beautiful creature—likely a ruffed grouse—had been destroyed senselessly. I scolded Fritz sharply for acting without caution or consent. We placed the bird on the donkey’s back and continued on our way.

The sledge was exactly where we’d left it. With daylight still strong, I decided to press forward and explore the line of cliffs and rocky hills that stretched along the coastline. I hoped to find a pass into the interior, or at least confirm whether we were trapped on this stretch of shore.

Leaving Calabash Wood behind, we moved across land covered in manioc, potatoes, and unfamiliar plants. Gentle streams wound through fertile soil, and the view was open and inviting.

Then I noticed a bush heavy with small white berries, waxy and sticky when picked. I recognized them at once: *Myrica cerifera*, a plant whose berries could be melted and strained to make wax candles. I explained to Fritz how this would solve our problem of having to stop work at sunset. While not as durable as beeswax, the wax would burn well and release a pleasant scent. With the donkey nearby, we quickly gathered enough berries to fill one of his canvas bags.

Soon after, we came upon another marvel—a tree hosting an entire colony of birds, about the size of yellowhammers but with plain brown plumage. Their nests clustered tightly around the trunk and branches, forming a large mass. A roof of grass, straw, and fibers protected them from rain and sun. Multiple openings dotted the sides, resembling rooms in a shared house. Twigs stuck out like perches, and birds darted in and out in constant motion. The whole structure looked like a giant sea sponge.

The birds swarmed everywhere. Among them were bright little parrots, often squabbling over nesting space. Fritz, eager to investigate, climbed the tree hoping to catch young birds. He reached into several empty nests, but when he plunged his hand deep into one, a sharp beak clamped down on his finger. He yelped in pain and pulled away, shaking his hand.

Recovering, he seized the bird firmly, despite its shrieks, and stuffed it into his pocket, buttoning up fast. He slid down quickly, pursued by angry relatives who circled him, screaming and pecking.

His prize wasn’t one of the nest owners—the sociable grosbeak—but a small green parrot. Fritz was thrilled and vowed to tame it and teach it to speak. For now, it stayed locked safely in his coat.

We talked about the bird community as we walked. “It’s amazing how animals cooperate,” I said. “Beavers build dams together. Bees, wasps, ants—all work in harmony. Even coral polyps create vast reefs under the sea through teamwork.”

“I’ve seen ants,” Fritz replied. “They’re always busy, carrying food, moving eggs.”

“Do you know what they do with those?” I asked.

“Oh, those aren’t eggs—they’re larvae, antworms. People call them eggs, but that’s wrong.”

“You’re right,” I said proudly. “And if you liked ants at home, imagine the giant ones in foreign lands! Some build mounds six feet high, divided into streets, chambers, nurseries. Strong enough to use as ovens!”

“But ants are no beauty,” I added. “They’re sticky, smelly, and destructive. In tropical lands, they can ruin crops. But nature balances things—birds, insects, even mammals hunt them.”

“The ant-eater!” Fritz exclaimed.

“Exactly. The tamanoir of South America—six or seven feet long, covered in coarse hair, with a long narrow head and no teeth. Its tongue is like a red worm. It tears apart ant hills with powerful claws, then flicks its tongue inside, coating it with thousands of ants, which stick and get pulled into its mouth.”

“There’s also a smaller kind,” I said, “that lives in trees.”

I was pleased that Fritz listened so intently. Our conversation carried us far down the path.

Eventually, we came to a grove of tall trees with broad, thick leaves. They bore round, fig-like fruits with tiny seeds—bitter and sour. Fritz noticed resin oozing from cracks in the bark. It reminded him of cherry gum from home, so he scraped some off.

He tried to mold it, but it wouldn’t behave like gum. Then he stretched it—and it bounced back! “Father! Look! This is elastic! Could it be india-rubber?”

“India-rubber?” I gasped. “Let me see! You might be right—it’s caoutchouc, a milky sap from certain trees in Brazil, Guiana, and Cayenne. The natives used it to coat clay bottles, then removed the shell and smoked it. They drew designs on it—those old bottles were sold in Europe as erasers.”

“It has many uses,” I said. “We could make boots, waterproof clothes—this is a valuable find!”

Just then, we reached the coconut grove, with the bay and Cape Disappointment ahead. I noticed a smaller palm among the giants—its trunk broken by wind. The pith looked mealy. I knew instantly: this was the sago palm.

Inside, I saw fat worms. “These feed on sago,” I recalled. “In the West Indies, they’re eaten as a delicacy.” I decided to try one.

I built a fire, salted half a dozen worms, skewered them, and roasted them. Soon, fat dripped from them and the smell was delicious. Any hesitation vanished. I ate one like butter on a potato—and found it tasty. Fritz joined in, and we enjoyed this unusual meal with our baked potatoes.

We turned aside from a dense thicket and headed toward the sugar brake near Cape Disappointment. We cut a bundle of sugarcanes and loaded them onto the donkey, adding to his already full load.

At last, we returned to Calabash Wood, unloaded everything onto the sledge, and set off. The donkey pulled it easily—no longer burdened by carrying all the weight himself.

We arrived at Falconhurst in the evening. Our welcome was warm, and everyone listened eagerly to our tales. The green parrot especially delighted the boys.

Supper was ready, and we ate with gratitude. Then we climbed into our tree-house, pulled up the ladder, and rested—well earned after a long day.

The next morning, candle-making was on everyone’s mind. The boys woke up chanting “candles!” Breakfast was all about candles. Afterward, they insisted on starting immediately.

“So be it,” I said. “Let’s become chandlers.” Though secretly, I doubted our success. We lacked animal fat, essential for long-burning candles. And I wasn’t sure I remembered every step.

But I said nothing. Under my guidance, the boys began. We crushed the wax berries into a shallow iron pot over the fire. The green, sweet-smelling wax rose to the surface. We skimmed it off, repeating until we had enough.

I took the wicks my wife had made and dipped them into the wax, handing each to Fritz, who hung them on a bush to dry. After several dips, they thickened into sturdy candles. We stored them in a cool place.

That night, we sat up three hours after sunset—Falconhurst lit brilliantly for the first time. Everyone was thrilled.

“My husband,” my wife said, “you’re clever. Now, can you help me with butter? Cream spoils daily. Can’t you invent something?”

“I think I can,” I replied. “Not my idea—it’s how the Hottentots do it. Jack, bring me a gourd bottle.”

I filled it with cream, sealed it tightly, and gave it to the boys. They tied four posts in the ground, fastened a square piece of sailcloth, and placed the gourd inside. Then, taking turns, they rolled it back and forth for half an hour.

“Open it!” I called. “Take it to your mother.”

They did. My wife stared in delight—there was a large lump of fresh butter.

While they worked, I built a cart using the sledge frame. It was clumsy but strong. It would prove useful for harvests.

Next, we planted fruit trees in a prepared plot. Walnut, cherry, and chestnut trees formed a shaded avenue from Falconhurst to Family Bridge. Between them, we laid a path to reach Tentholm easily.

Vines wrapped around the roots of our great mangrove. Other trees went to spots near Falconhurst and across Jackal River, to beautify Tentholm.

Tentholm had long been on my mind. I didn’t want beauty—I wanted safety. So I planted a thick, thorny hedge to block wild animals and possible attackers. Then we fortified the bridge and mounted two guns from the wreck on hillocks, ready to defend us.

Six weeks passed in hard but joyful labor. Sundays brought rest and thanks to God for our health and safety.

I saw the boys growing stronger. I encouraged running, climbing, swimming. But their clothes—once neat—were now ragged and patched.

So I decided to visit the wreck again—to gather supplies and check its condition. Three boys and I rowed out in the pinnace.

The ship was much the same—just a few more planks gone.

“Rummage her out!” I ordered. “Leave nothing of value.”

They searched every corner. Sailors’ chests, cloth, guns, ball, shutters, bolts, pitch barrels—everything piled on deck. We loaded the pinnace and returned.

We towed the tub-boat back and made several trips until nothing remained.

“One final trip,” I told my wife. “Then the ship will be gone. I left two barrels of gunpowder. We’ll blow her up.”

Before lighting the fuse, I found a large copper cauldron. I tied two empty casks to it so it would float when the explosion came. We placed the barrels, lit the fuse, and retreated.

We dined outside the tent, facing the wreck. Darkness fell. Then—a flash! A roaring blast shook the water. Our ship was gone.

We had planned this. We knew it was wise. Yet that night, we felt a quiet sadness—as though we’d lost a loyal companion.

The next morning, joy returned. The shore was lined with planks and beams from the wreck. I found the cauldron floating, secured by the casks. I hauled it ashore and stored the powder casks beneath it.

As we collected these treasures, my wife brought news: two ducks and a goose had each raised large families in the river reeds. They waddled past us, proud of their broods.

“Hurrah!” Ernest shouted. “Soon we’ll have duck and peas—like civilized people again!”

Seeing the birds reminded me of Falconhurst. I announced we’d visit.

Everyone wanted to come. As we approached, I noticed young trees bent by the wind. I decided to gather bamboo for support the next day.

Since only Fritz and I had explored Cape Disappointment, the others begged to join. I agreed.

The next morning, we set off with the cart—drawn by cow and donkey—loaded with a tent, food, ammunition, tools, and gear. I intended to gather fruits and samples from various trees.

It was a lovely day. We passed through fields of potatoes, manioc, and cassava. The birds’ nests charmed the children.

We stopped at the wax trees to collect more berries. We filled two sacks, hid them among the bushes, and marked the spot.

“Now for the caoutchouc tree,” I said. “Waterproof boots for you, Ernest!”

We found the tree and began tapping the bark, placing vessels below to catch the sap.

The next morning, the boys and I set off with the cart loaded with bundles of bamboo, heading toward our fruit tree avenue. We left the buffalo behind—his services weren’t needed just yet, and I wanted his nose wound to heal completely before putting him back to work.

We hadn’t arrived a moment too soon. Many of the young trees that had looked weak earlier were now fully toppled over, others bent dangerously, and only a few still stood upright. We quickly lifted the fallen ones, dug deep around their roots, and propped them up with sturdy bamboo stakes, securing each firmly with strong, broad fibres.

“Papa,” Franz said as he handed me fibres one by one, “are these trees wild or tame?”

“Oh, they’re wild—ferocious ones!” Jack laughed. “We’re tying them up so they don’t run away. Soon we’ll untie them, and they’ll trot after us, giving us fruit wherever we go. We’ll tame them like the buffalo—put rings in their noses!”

“That’s not true,” Franz replied seriously. “Wild trees grow in forests, like crab-apples. Tame ones grow in gardens, like pears and peaches at home. Which are these, Papa?”

“They’re not wild,” I answered. “They’re cultivated—what you’d call tame. No European tree bears good fruit unless it’s grafted.”

I saw confusion flicker across Franz’s face at the word *grafted*, so I explained.

“Grafting means taking a small branch—or slip—from one tree and inserting it into a knot or hole in another tree. That slip then grows and produces fruit from its own kind, not the original tree. For example, if you have a sour crab apple tree but want sweet apples, you take a slip from a ribston pippin tree, insert it into the crab tree, and in a year or two, you’ll have branches full of delicious apples.”

“But where did those good slips come from?” Ernest asked. “If no good fruit grows without grafting?”

“From foreign lands,” I said. “In colder climates like ours, trees need grafting to produce good fruit. But in warmer regions—like parts of Africa, Asia, or South America—many fruit trees grow naturally, bearing sweet, healthy fruit without any care at all. In England, Germany, and even France, those same trees require expert gardening just to survive.”

“Like when the Romans came to Britain,” Fritz added. “They found only crab apples, nut bushes, and brambles. But by grafting, they grew fine apples, hazelnuts, and raspberries. It was the same in Switzerland—all our fruit trees were brought from elsewhere.”

“Even cherries?” asked Jack. “Can’t we call cherries Swiss? I always thought they only grew here.”

“I’m afraid not,” I said. “Cherries come from Cerasus, a region in ancient Asia Minor. A Roman general named Lucullus brought them to Europe about seventy years before Christ. Hazelnuts also came from Pontus. Walnuts originated in Persia. As for grapes—they’re among the oldest cultivated plants. Remember Noah planting vines? Grapes have been moved from place to place and now grow in most civilized parts of the world.”

As we crossed Jackal River and entered our plantation at Tentholm, Fritz asked, “Do you think all these trees will grow? We’ve got lemons, pomegranates, pistachios, and mulberries.”

“I’m confident they will,” I replied. “We’re clearly in the tropics, where such trees thrive.”

“These pines come from France, Spain, and Italy; olives from Armenia and Palestine; figs originally from Chios; peaches and apricots from Persia; plums from Damascus; and pears from Greece. Our land may not have blessed us with natural fruit, but it gave us wisdom and skill—enough to bring these trees from other lands and grow them well.”

We worked and talked until every tree was secured with a bamboo support. Then, hungry as hunters after a long chase, we returned to Falconhurst.

My wife nearly gasped at how eagerly we devoured the corned beef and palm cabbage she’d prepared. But soon enough, our appetites were satisfied, and we sat back, digesting our meal and discussing our plans.

“I wish you could find another way to get to the nest,” my wife said. “It’s perfect, really—it’s wonderful. But climbing that ladder every time is exhausting. Could you make steps instead?”

I thought carefully. “Stairs outside would be impossible, but inside the trunk… perhaps. I’ve suspected this tree might be hollow or partly so. You told me the other day bees were coming out of a hole in it?”

“Oh yes!” little Franz exclaimed. “I went to look, and one flew right into my face and stung me! I almost cried—but I didn’t.”

“Brave boy,” I said. “Now, if the trunk is big enough to hold a swarm of bees, it might be hollow all the way up. Like a willow back home—it can stay healthy even if the core is rotten.”

Jack, ever practical, jumped up to test my theory. The others followed, scrambling up like squirrels, peering into the hole and tapping the wood to hear how far the cavity extended.

They forgot who lived there—until the bees, disturbed by the noise, erupted with a furious buzz and attacked them. They swarmed around the boys, stinging hands, faces, necks, settling in hair, chasing them screaming to me for help.

We struggled to clear the angry insects and tend to the wounded. Jack, who’d reached the hole first, suffered the worst—his face swollen grotesquely. Only cold earth applied constantly eased the pain. They all wanted to attack the bees immediately, but their pain made them too weak to help.

While they recovered, I began preparing. I took a large calabash gourd and turned it into a beehive. I flattened the bottom half, cut an arched doorway, and added a straw roof to protect it from rain and sun. The hive was ready.

But the bees were still furious, so I waited till dark. When all had returned to the tree, Fritz and I sealed every hole with wet clay so the bees couldn’t escape the next morning.

At dawn, we started. I inserted a hollow cane through the clay into the tree and smoked tobacco down it, using a pipe. The buzzing inside became deafening—the bees confused, frightened.

After finishing the first pipe, I blocked the end with my thumb and passed it to Fritz to refill. I smoked again. The sound softened, then faded into silence. The bees were stunned.

“Now, Fritz,” I said. “Quick—hammer and chisel. Stand beside me.”

He was ready. Together, we cut a small door near the hole, leaving it attached at one corner so we could remove it fast. After one last burst of smoke, we opened it.

Carefully, we removed the bees—clinging in clusters—and placed them in the new hive. Then I scraped every bit of wax and honey from the tree and stored it in a cask.

But I worried: would they return to the old hive when they woke? To prevent that, I lit a board of tobacco inside the trunk, letting the fumes fill the space.

It worked. When the bees regained consciousness, they flew back to the tree—only to find it empty and smoky. Confused, they buzzed between the hive and the tree, finally choosing the new home. By evening, they were calm. We opened the cask.

We separated the honey from the comb and poured it into jars. The rest we boiled in water, strained through a canvas bag under pressure, and collected pure honey. The leftover wax I saved—for candles.

That night, exhausted, we slept.

The next day, we began transforming the tree’s interior. At the base, we carved an opening just wide enough for the door from the captain’s cabin—hinges and all, ready to hang.

We cleared out the rotten wood from the trunk’s center. It took time, but eventually, standing below, we could see straight up the smooth, funnel-like shaft to the sky above.

Now came the staircase. First, we planted a sturdy sapling in the middle as a central pillar. We cut notches into it and into the tree wall to support the steps. The steps themselves were made from wrecked ship planks, nailed securely in place.

We built upward, adding windows along the way for light and air, until we reached the top of the central pole. Then we added a second pole to reach the nest floor, building more stairs around it.

For safety, we ran handrails on both sides—one around the central pillar, the other following the curve of the trunk.

This task took a full month. By the end, we were used to having a major project underway and began wondering what to tackle next.

But we hadn’t ignored daily duties. The goats and sheep had given birth—new lambs and kids bouncing around. To keep them close, we tied small bells around their necks, salvaged from the wreck. The jingle would help us track them.

Juno, our dog, had a litter of puppies. Though the children begged to keep them all, I allowed only two. The rest vanished—just as puppies often do. To comfort her, Jack placed his jackal pup beside the remaining puppies. To everyone’s surprise, the jackal accepted them and cared for them like his own.

Our other animals thrived. The buffalo, once stubborn, was now tamed—strong and reliable. He carried loads and served as a riding beast for the boys. We guided him with a bar through his healed nose, like a horse’s bit. I trained him slowly—first with a hide belt and weights, then with Master Knips riding him, and finally with Fritz himself.

The eagle was also being trained. Fritz hunted small birds daily and placed them near the buffalo or goat, teaching the bird to swoop and catch prey. Soon, the eagle obeyed his whistle and could strike flying birds with precision. We kept him far from the poultry yard—no risk of him attacking our chickens.

Master Knips, the monkey, wasn’t idle either. Ernest, with Jack’s help, made a basket of rushes, strapped it to the monkey’s back, and taught him to climb coconut palms and retrieve fruit.

Jack’s jackal, Fangs, was less cooperative. He caught animals but ate them instantly—leaving only scraps. Still, Jack refused to give up. He believed Fangs could be trained, and he would train him.

These tasks kept us busy while we built the stairs.

I also worked on improving our candles. The first batch had been exciting, but the light was dim and the wicks—made from cotton neckbands—looked rough. My wife urged me to find a better alternative.

I decided to use bamboo moulds. At first, I planned to pour wax in and pull the candle out, but that failed. So I split the moulds lengthwise, greased them, and filled them with wax while tightly bound. Once cooled, the candles came out cleanly.

Next came the wick. My wife refused to let us use our cloth bands. Instead, I used thin strips of flammable wood from an Antillean tree, placing them in the mould centers. She made wicks from karata tree fibers, claiming hers would win.

We tested both. We melted a mix of beeswax and candleberry wax, poured it into the moulds—half with wooden wicks, half with karata—set them in cold water, and let them cool.

When night fell, we lit them. The verdict? Neither matched the cotton wicks. But mine burned bright and steady, while hers flickered weakly—almost useless.

Then I turned shoemaker. I wanted waterproof boots. I filled socks with sand, coated them with clay, dried them in the sun, and layered caoutchouc over them. After drying, I broke out the clay, nailed buffalo-hide soles to the boots, and coated them again. The result? Comfortable, durable, weatherproof boots.

Everyone wanted a pair. Soon, the whole family wore them.

One issue remained: no spring near Falconhurst. The boys had to fetch water daily from the stream—a chore. So we built a dam upstream to raise the water level. From the reservoir, we laid pipes leading to a turtle shell near our home. Excess water drained through the hole Fritz had made with his harpoon.

It was a great convenience. We celebrated by washing a whole sack of potatoes in the trough.

Days passed, each bringing new work, each ending with accomplishment. We had no time to waste or mourn our isolation.

One morning, as we finished the spiral staircase, a terrifying roar echoed through the forest. It was unlike any animal I knew. Jack guessed lion. Fritz thought gorilla. Ernest said hyena—possibly right.

“Whatever it is,” I said, “we must prepare. Up to the nest—quick! I’ll secure the door.”

I armed the dogs, sent them to guard the animals, closed the door, and joined my family. Guns were loaded. Eyes watched.

The sound drew closer—then stopped. Nothing was seen. I decided to investigate. Fritz and I crept down quietly, guns cocked, moving silently through the trees.

Suddenly, the roar sounded again—close by. Fritz raised his gun—then dropped it, bursting into laughter.

There was no mistaking it: *hee-haw, hee-haw!* Our donkey was braying merrily—followed by another animal. Slender and graceful, it looked like a horse, but unmistakably a wild ass—an onager.

“Fritz,” I whispered, “that’s an onager. Go back to Falconhurst—quietly—and bring me a rope.”

While he went, I split a bamboo into pincers—useful if I got close. Fritz returned with rope, oats, and salt.

We tied one end of the rope to a tree, made a running noose at the other. Watching silently, Fritz rose, holding the noose in one hand, oats in the other.

The donkey approached, munching happily. The onager hesitated—then, seeing no danger, stepped forward, sniffing the food.

In a flash, the noose snapped around her neck. She leapt back, the rope tightening—she fell, half-strangled.

I rushed forward, loosened the rope, replaced it with a halter, and secured her with cords between two trees. I left her to recover.

Everyone hurried up to see. She rose, eyes blazing, kicking wildly, snorting in rage. But the cords held. After struggling, she collapsed, exhausted.

I approached calmly. She let me lead her to the base of our tree—our temporary stable—where I tied her beside the donkey, preventing both from wandering.

She was beautiful—wild, fierce, but now under our care. And so, another creature joined our island family.

At last, the storm passed. The wind calmed, the clouds broke apart, and golden sunlight poured down upon the island like a blessing. Rain ceased, and spring had arrived. Stepping out of our winter shelter, we felt as though we’d been set free after years in captivity. The world sparkled with fresh green life, birds sang from every tree, and the air was sweet and warm—alive with the promise of new beginnings.

Our crops were thriving. Seeds we’d planted months before now pushed through the damp earth, sprouting strong and fast. Nature herself seemed to be breathing again.

First, we turned our attention to our nest—the shelter we’d built high in the trees. It had suffered during the storms: leaves scattered, branches broken, the whole structure torn by the wind. We worked hard to repair it, patching holes and reinforcing weak spots. In just a few days, it stood once more as a safe, snug home.

My wife asked me to begin preparing flax for her spinning. I quickly built a drying oven and then crafted a beetle-reel and a spinning wheel. Soon, she and Franz were busy at work—she spinning thread, he winding it neatly onto the reel. The boy’s hands moved with growing skill, and his eyes shone with pride.

I worried about what might have happened to our stores at Tentholm. With Fritz, I made the journey there. What we found was worse than I feared. The tent had been blown flat, its canvas shredded, provisions soaked through, and two casks of powder completely ruined. But the pinnace remained safe, though our beloved tub-boat lay in splinters on the shore.

This loss made me determined: we needed a better shelter for the next rainy season. Something stronger, safer, and permanent. Fritz suggested carving a cave into the rock. At first, the idea seemed impossible—but I saw no reason not to try. Even if we couldn’t dig a full chamber, we could create a cellar to protect our most precious supplies.

A few days later, we loaded the cart with tools—spades, hammers, chisels, pickaxes, crowbars—and set off toward the cliff face near Falconhurst. On the smooth stone wall, I marked the size of the entrance with chalk. Then, with hearts full of hope, we began to chip away.

For six long days, we struck the rock without much progress. The hole barely widened. It looked as though even Master Knips, our smallest dog, wouldn’t fit inside. Yet we did not give up. Then, one morning, the stone beneath us softened. Our tools bit deeper. Progress came faster. Our spirits lifted.

On the tenth day, Jack, hammering fiercely with a crowbar, suddenly shouted:
“Father! It’s gone! My bar has gone through the mountain!”

Fritz laughed. “Run around and get it—maybe it fell into Europe! Don’t lose such a good tool.”

“No, really!” Jack insisted. “It went right through! I heard it crash inside! Come see!”

We rushed to him. I thrust my hammer into the hole. It met no resistance. I could turn it freely. Fritz handed me a long pole. I tested the depth—nothing touched the end. There was only a thin wall between us and a vast, hidden space.

With shouts of joy, the boys swung their tools harder. Rock crumbled. In minutes, the opening grew wide enough to crawl through.

I stepped close, ready to explore—when suddenly, a wave of thick, foul air knocked me dizzy. I gasped and called out:
“Stand back! This air is dangerous!”

When I recovered, I explained: “Stagnant air becomes poisonous. Without fresh oxygen, it can kill. The best way to clean it? Fire. Throw burning hay into the hole. If the flame dies instantly, the air is still too bad.”

We tried. The fire vanished as soon as it entered. We threw in bundles of blazing grass—no change.

Clearly, we needed something stronger. I sent the boys to fetch the chest of signal rockets we’d saved from the wreck. Dozens of fiery serpents shot into the darkness, whizzing deep into the cave. They lit up the walls like stars, revealing a glittering roof and soaring crystal pillars—like a palace carved by magic.

After waiting a while, I threw in lighted hay again. This time, it burned brightly. The air was now safe.

Fritz and I widened the entrance. Jack raced off on Storm, the buffalo, galloping back to Falconhurst with news so astonishing it would surely make everyone leap from their beds.

And indeed, they did. Jack’s story reached them fast. Soon, the bridge echoed with hooves, and the rest of us followed in the cart—excited, curious, eager.

We brought candles, arms, and torches. I led the way, probing the ground ahead with a long pole, careful not to fall into unseen pits.

Silence fell over us all—my wife, the boys, even the dogs—as we entered the cavern. It was breathtaking. A grotto of diamonds. A cathedral of crystal. The candlelight danced on walls that shimmered like gold, while towering columns rose from the floor, branching overhead like trees, their tips dangling with rainbows of stalactites.

The floor was dry, hard sand—perfect for living. I tasted a piece of the crystal: salt. Pure, white rock-salt. An endless supply!

But one thing troubled me: large crystals littered the ground, fallen from above. If more dropped, we’d be in danger. I examined them closely and realized they had broken recently. Likely, the shock of the rockets had shaken the roof.

To test this, we fired our guns from the entrance. Nothing fell. The cave was stable.

We returned to Falconhurst, hearts full of wonder and plans. Now, nothing else mattered but building our new home.

We decided Falconhurst would become a summer retreat. The cave would be our winter fortress—our true stronghold.

Work began at once. We cut windows into the rock, fitting them with frames from the officers’ cabins. The door from Falconhurst was installed. I covered the hollow in the tree trunk with bark, so wild animals or strangers wouldn’t notice it.

Inside, we divided the cave into four sections:
- The front room, open to the entrance, became our sitting, dining, and sleeping area.
- To the right: kitchen and workshop.
- To the left: stables.
- Deep in the rear: storehouse and powder magazine.

We learned from our past mistakes. No more smoke-filled rooms. We built a proper fireplace with a chimney. The stable was larger, the workshop spacious—ready for any project.

Our visits to Tentholm revealed more treasures. Giant turtles came ashore to lay eggs. We caught them by turning them over and tying them to stakes near the water. They stayed healthy for weeks, giving us fresh meat whenever we wanted.

Lobsters, crabs, and mussels lined the shore. But the greatest surprise came one morning.

As we approached Tentholm, the sea beyond the bay churned. The surface flashed like fire under the sun. Hundreds of birds circled, screaming, diving into the waves.

“What is that?” Franz asked.

“It’s a herring bank,” I said. “A massive school of fish, swimming together—thousands deep, stretching across miles. Fishermen call it a ‘bank.’ These herrings are hunted by sharks below and birds above. To escape, they rush to shallow waters where the big fish can’t follow.”

“But how do we catch them?” Jack asked.

“We’ll bale them out like water,” I replied.

Jack and Fritz stood in the surf with baskets. They scooped the fish from the water, tossing them to us on shore. We cleaned them quickly and packed them in casks—layer of salt, layer of fish—until we filled dozens.

We sealed the casks and rolled them into the cool vaults behind the cave.

Then came another gift. After the herring left, seals appeared—drawn by the scraps we’d thrown into the sea.

Though I doubted they’d taste good, we caught several. Their skins we tanned for harnesses and clothes. Their fat we boiled down into oil—useful for lamps, soap, and tanning leather.

These tasks slowed our work on the cave, but soon we returned with renewed energy.

I noticed the salt crystals rested on a base of gypsum—a material I knew could be used as plaster. I broke off pieces, heated them until they turned to powder, then mixed them with water. The result was smooth, white, and strong.

I used it to seal the herring casks—making sure no air could reach them. The rest I saved for later.

To cure more fish, we built a small hut of reeds and branches. We strung the herrings on lines across the roof. On the floor, we lit a fire of brushwood and moss. Thick smoke curled around the fish, curing them in just a few days.

About a month later, another shoal appeared. Jack spotted them first: “Whales! Young whales!” he shouted.

We ran to the river and saw the bay teeming with sturgeon, salmon, and trout—migrating upstream to spawn.

“Now those are real fish!” Jack grinned. “How do you preserve them?”

“First,” I said, “tell me how you’ll catch them.”

“Oh, easy!” he said, dashing off to Rock House.

He returned with a bow, arrow, and a ball of twine. He’d tied a barbed spike to the arrowhead and fastened the string to the shaft.

He fired. The arrow struck a giant sturgeon in the side.

“Help! Father, help!” he cried as the fish dragged him into the water.

I rushed to pull the line. Together, we fought the creature. It thrashed wildly, but we held on. Slowly, it weakened. Blood stained the water. Finally, exhausted, it lay still.

We hauled it ashore. It was magnificent. Fritz and Ernest arrived just in time—jealous of Jack’s triumph.

They didn’t stay idle. Fritz grabbed a harpoon. Ernest took a fishing rod. I armed myself with an iron trident—Neptune himself could not have looked more imposing.

Soon, the beach was littered with great fish. One by one, we pulled them from the water.

Then Fritz harpooned a sturgeon eight feet long. He couldn’t bring it in alone. We all pulled—but it wouldn’t budge.

“Storm!” my wife called. Jack galloped off, returned with the buffalo, harnessed him to the rope, and—pulling steadily—the beast dragged the monster ashore.

We cleaned the fish. Some we salted. Some we dried. Some we cooked in oil, like Mediterranean tuna.

From the sturgeon roe, I made caviar. I removed the membranes, washed the roe in vinegar, salted it, pressed out the moisture, and packed it tightly in small barrels—storing it in the cellar.

I also collected the bladders. When dried and boiled, they produced isinglass—clear glue that could be used as glass substitute. I strained it through cloth, spread it thinly on stone, and let it dry. The result was clear, strong, and perfect for window panes.

Meanwhile, our garden flourished. Peas, beans, wheat, barley, rye, and corn grew year-round. Cucumbers, melons, and vegetables burst forth without care. The success at Tentholm gave me hope for our maize field.

We visited it one morning. As we passed the potato patch, we saw it covered in barley, wheat, rye, and peas—growing wild.

“Where did this come from?” I asked.

“From the seed I sowed,” my wife smiled. “Franz and I scattered it. You tilled the soil. All we did was plant.”

I was amazed—and delighted. Our maize must be doing well too.

We hurried to the field. The crop stood tall and strong. But as we approached, a flock of birds erupted into the sky. Among them, Fritz spotted ruffed grouse. Without hesitation, he unhooded his eagle, launched it, and leapt onto his onager.

The bird soared, then dove, snatching a grouse mid-air. Fritz followed, rescued the bird, hooded it, and returned triumphant.

Jack hadn’t waited. He released Fangs, his jackal. The animal darted among quails and brought back a dozen—faithfully delivered to his master.

We returned to Falconhurst, where my wife served us a refreshing drink: crushed young corn stems, mixed with sugar-cane juice and water.

That evening, I proposed a new plan: establish a farm farther from Falconhurst, to house our growing herd. We loaded the cart with chickens, pigs, sheep, goats, and a pair of hens and a cock grouse. Fritz led the way on his onager.

We cut a path through the forest and emerged onto a wide plain. White flakes covered the ground and bushes.

“Snow!” Franz cried. “Let’s play snowballs!”

But I knew better. “No, son. That’s cotton.”

Fritz nodded. “It’s a dwarf cotton tree—soft wool in pods.”

We gathered armfuls in three bags. My wife rejoiced.

Crossing the field, we climbed a wooded hill. From the top, the view was stunning: rolling green hills, shaded trees, a sparkling brook, and beyond, the endless sea.

Perfect. This was the place.

We pitched our tent, built a fire, and prepared dinner. Fritz and I chose a spot for a shed. A cluster of trees formed a natural rectangle—ideal for posts. I cut deep mortises in the trunks, ten feet up and again higher. I inserted beams, laid laths for a roof, and covered it with bark secured by acacia thorns. Rain would never harm it.

While clearing debris, I noticed a strange smell. I picked up bits of bark—some from the terebinth tree, others from the American fir. The goats had found cinnamon too—fragrant and delicious.

“The fir gives us turpentine and tar,” I told the boys. “We can make pitch for boats, grease for wheels. The terebinth tree produces gum that hardens into amber-like resin. Burn it—it smells wonderful. Dissolve it in alcohol, and you have varnish.”

We had discovered a new treasure—and with it, a future richer than we’d ever imagined.

The next morning dawned bright and early, and we rose with the sun. After breakfast, we set to work right away on making bird-lime—a thick, sticky mixture of caoutchouc oil and turpentine that turned out perfectly. The boys brought long wooden rods, which I coated thoroughly with the adhesive substance and placed high in the trees where fruit was abundant and birds gathered in large numbers.

The number of pigeons this year was astonishing—far greater than ever before. It struck me that last year we had only seen the stragglers at the end of their season; now, entire flocks were descending upon the trees, bending branches under their weight. Even the sweet acorns of the evergreen oaks attracted huge groups, and from the mess beneath the trees, it was clear these birds roosted there at night.

This gave me an idea. I decided to launch a nighttime raid, just as the colonists in Virginia did—using torchlight to flush out the birds and catch them by surprise.

Meanwhile, our bird-lime worked wonderfully. As the pigeons landed, they stuck fast. The more they flapped and struggled, the deeper they sank into the gooey trap, until, crying out in distress, they tumbled to the ground, dragging the limed sticks with them. We quickly removed the birds, cleaned the traps, and reset them for another round.

Soon, the boys could manage the operation without my help, so I left them to it and went to prepare torches—made from pinewood soaked in turpentine—for our planned night attack.

Jack soon came running with a beautiful pigeon unlike any other he’d caught. He didn’t want to kill it. It looked like one of our own European breed—the kind we’d once kept but lost when we first arrived. I took the trembling bird gently, cleaned its feet and wings with oil and ashes to remove the sticky residue, and placed it in a wicker cage. I told Jack to bring me any others like it.

He did, and we collected several pairs. This made me very happy. When we first landed, these birds had flown free and become wild again. Now, thanks to our efforts, we had a small flock we could raise—and enjoy pigeon pie whenever we wanted.

As evening fell, we headed for the grove of sweet acorn trees, armed only with long bamboo poles, torches, and canvas sacks. The children thought our gear looked odd and weak—but soon they saw how effective it would be.

Darkness fell almost as we reached the woods. I lit the torches, and instantly the scene transformed. Every branch was packed with ortolans and wild pigeons, roosting in dense clusters. The sudden glare startled them. Chaos broke out—birds fluttered wildly, blinded and confused. Some dropped straight down before we even touched the branches. We scooped them up and stuffed them into our sacks.

When we began beating the trees with our poles, my wife and Franz could barely keep up with gathering the fallen birds. The sacks filled quickly. We turned back toward Falconhurst, safely stowed our prize, and retired to bed, satisfied and weary.

The next day was spent entirely plucking, boiling, roasting, and stewing the feast. There was no time for anything else.

But the following morning, we prepared for a new mission: a full-scale expedition to Woodlands, where we hoped to stop the monkey raids once and for all. I believed if we could catch the mischievous creatures in the act, we could teach them a lesson they’d never forget.

My wife packed enough food for several days, while she stayed behind with Franz and Turk. I took along extra-strong bird-lime (thicker than what we used for pigeons), short stakes, plenty of string, and a collection of coconut shells and gourds. The buffalo carried everything, plus two of the boys. I rode the ass, and we arrived at a hidden spot deep in the forest near Woodlands, shielded by thick bushes. We pitched a small tent, tied up the animals, and secured the dogs so they wouldn’t wander and give us away.

The cottage stood quiet and empty. I wasted no time setting up our trap. We drove stakes into the ground in a zigzag pattern around the house, weaving strings between them to form a tangled maze. Everything was smeared with the powerful bird-lime. Bowls of the sticky mix were scattered about, laced with rice, maize, and other tempting treats.

Night passed peacefully. With the trap complete, we rested beneath the tent.

Just before dawn, we heard a strange commotion—rustling, cracking, shrieking, chattering. The sound meant one thing: the monkeys were coming.

We grabbed clubs and cudgels, held the dogs on leashes, and crept silently through the underbrush. From our hiding place, we watched as the chaos unfolded.

The forest erupted in noise. A massive horde of monkeys burst from the trees, scrambling, leaping, racing across the clearing toward the cottage. Drawn by the unfamiliar objects, they rushed to the bowls and jars.

They seemed endless. Their frantic movements made it hard to count them. They charged through the palings, some climbing onto the roof, pulling at pegs to break in.

But slowly, one by one, they became trapped. Bird-lime clung to their heads, paws, backs. Some sat down, trying to scrub themselves clean with clumsy, comic gestures. Others got caught in the strings and stakes, dragging them like burdens. Some tried to help each other—only to stick together. The harder they pulled, the worse it got.

Many grabbed the gourds and coconuts, only to have their hands glued to them. One old monkey found a calabash filled with palm wine. He drank greedily—then, as the shell stuck to his face, covering his eyes, he stumbled blindly, spinning and hopping like a madman.

Some fled—but many more became fixed to the nearby trees, hanging from branches, shrieking in terror.

With panic spreading, I released the dogs. They lunged forward, snarling and barking, attacking the helpless apes as if they were hunting criminals.

The scene turned grim. We joined in with clubs, striking where needed. Soon, the noise died—howls, yelps, screams—all silenced. The clearing lay still, littered with bodies.

At least forty monkeys lay dead or dying. The boys grew somber. I shared their sadness and quickly redirected them—helping bury the slain, burn the stakes, strings, and bowls, destroying every trace of our trap.

Then we began restoring the cottage. We searched for the scattered sheep, goats, and poultry, gradually rounding them up and returning them to their pens.

As we worked, we heard a heavy thud from above. Looking up, we found three magnificent birds caught on limed sticks in the trees.

Two were Blue Molucca pigeons. The third, with shimmering green, bronze, and steel-blue feathers, looked like the rare Nicobar pigeon. I smiled at the thought of raising them—making them the first residents of a new home near the cave.

“First tenants, Father?” Fritz asked. “Do you think we’ll catch more?”

“Not exactly catch,” I replied. “I mean to use a secret art. Magic, Fritz!”

I said no more.

Within days, Woodlands was restored. We returned to Falconhurst, welcomed with joy.

Everyone agreed we must go to Tentholm next—to build a proper pigeon house in the rock. There were other tasks too, so we planned to stay longer.

My plan was to carve a spacious chamber into the cliff, facing Jackal River, close to our rocky home. Inside, I’d add partitions, perches, and nesting boxes. A wooden front would cover the opening, with sliding doors, shutters, and a wide platform where birds could walk and rest.

After weeks of work, the dove-cot was ready. I sent the other boys to work far from the cave, then called Fritz.

"Now, my faithful assistant," I said, "it’s time to summon the new settlers. Yes—time to perform a real pigeon-dealer's trick."

Fritz blinked. “You mean… magic?”

“Exactly,” I laughed. “Pigeon dealers are clever. Not only do they protect their own birds—they lure others in. All I need is soft clay, aniseed, salt, and oil. I’ll make a special mixture—delicious to our birds, and scented so strongly that wild ones will come to share it.”

“I can get those things, Father.”

“And I’ll need oil of aniseed,” I added. “To coat the pigeon holes. That way, as the birds pass in and out, their feathers will absorb the scent—drawing others to follow.”

“I’ll bring the mortar and oil,” Fritz promised.

Once the oil was pressed from crushed seeds and strained through cloth, it carried a strong, lasting fragrance—perfect for the task.

All preparations done, we moved the pigeons into their new home. The slides were closed. Our European birds had grown friendly with the three newcomers. When the others returned and climbed the ladder to peek through a glass pane I’d installed, they saw the birds calmly feeding on grain and nibbling at the “magic food,” as Fritz called it—though he kept my secret safe.

On the third morning, I woke Fritz early. I told him to climb the rope ladder and attach a cord to the sliding door, so we could open or close it from below. Then he spread fresh aniseed oil around the entrance.

We returned and woke the family. “If you hurry,” I said, “you might see me release the pigeons.”

Everyone gathered. I waved a wand dramatically, muttered a pretend spell, and signaled Fritz to pull the cord.

One by one, the birds peered out—soft eyes scanning the sky. They retreated, then stepped forward again. Slowly, they walked onto the platform—what little Franz called “the verandah.” Suddenly, they took flight with a sharp whirring sound, circling higher and higher, until they vanished.

We stared after them—then, to our delight, they reappeared, landing softly on the dove-cot.

We cheered—thinking they’d accepted their new home. But then the three blue pigeons rose again, soaring in wide circles, heading straight for Falconhurst.

Their flight was bold, determined. I feared we’d lost them forever.

We tried to comfort ourselves by petting the four remaining birds, but the disappointment lingered. All day, the dove-cot remained the center of attention.

Nothing was seen of the fugitives—until midday the next day.

Jack burst into the cave, breathless. “He’s there! He’s come! He really is!”

“Who? What do you mean?”

“The blue pigeon!” he shouted. “Hurrah!”

“Nonsense,” Ernest scoffed. “You’re joking.”

“Why should it be nonsense?” I asked. “I believe we’ll see them all soon!”

Everyone ran outside. And there, sure enough, stood the blue pigeon—billing and cooing to a mate. She was a stranger, clearly of his kind. He darted in and out of the doorway, bowing and cooing, so charmingly that she finally stepped in.

“Close the door!” the boys yelled, rushing for the cord.

“Wait!” I called. “Don’t frighten them. Besides, the others are coming—would you shut the door in their faces?”

“Here they come!” Fritz cried, spotting them from afar.

And indeed, the second blue pigeon arrived—with a mate. After a playful display of modesty, she followed him inside.

The third and most splendid of the trio came last. Perhaps he’d taken longer to find a partner worthy of him. But we expected him. The boys already spoke of “Mr. and Mrs. Nicobar” as if it were certain.

Late that afternoon, Franz and his mother went out for supper. Franz returned quickly, excited. “Come quick! You’ve got to see something beautiful!”

We all rushed to the dove-cot. There, the third bird had returned—with a lovely bride. Encouraged by the others, they settled in at once.

Soon, nest-building began. Among the materials, I noticed a long grey moss—lichen, possibly the same kind used in the West Indies to stuff mattresses instead of horsehair.

My wife heard about it immediately and sprang into action. “If we gather enough,” she said, “I’ll clean and sort it. We’ll make bolsters, pillows, saddles—anything we need!”

We also discovered nutmegs—thanks to the pigeons. We planted them carefully in our orchard.

For a while, life continued quietly—until Jack, as usual, got himself into trouble.

He set off early on one of his private adventures—always hoping to return with a surprise.

This time, he came back covered in mud and green slime. A bundle of Spanish cane was strapped to his back, muddy and wet. One shoe was gone. He looked miserable—so much so that we couldn’t laugh, though we wanted to.

“Dear boy! What happened?”

“Only the swamp behind the powder magazine,” he said. “I went to collect reeds for baskets and hen-coops. I saw better ones farther out in the marsh—so I jumped from one dry patch to another. Then I slipped—and sank past my ankles.”

“I tried to reach the reeds, but I went deeper and deeper—above my knees in thick mud. I was stuck!”

“I screamed, but no one came. I was terrified.”

“Then Fangs appeared! He knew my voice and came right over the swamp. But all he could do was bark and run around. I wonder you didn’t hear us!”

“In desperation, I cut down all the reeds near me, tied them into a bundle, and used it as a raft. I climbed on top, balanced myself, and kicked and scrambled until I freed my legs.”

“Then I grabbed Fangs’ tail. He pulled, I dragged, crawled, waded—sometimes on the reeds, sometimes dragging them—until we made it back to dry land. I had a close call!”

“A lucky escape,” I said. “And God be thanked. Fangs acted bravely. You showed great courage. Now go wash up—you’ve brought me excellent canes, just what I need.”

I had a plan. I wanted to build a loom for my wife—knowing she understood weaving. I selected two strong reeds, split and bound them tightly so they’d dry straight and firm. Smaller reeds were cut and sharpened for the comb’s teeth.

The boys helped without knowing the purpose—calling the tools “father’s monster toothpicks.”

Eventually, the loom was assembled. I kept it a secret, telling the children it was an “outlandish musical instrument”—one only Mother could play.

When the time came to reveal it, her joy was immense. The children watched in fascination as she “played the loom,” laughing and cheering.

Around this time, a fine foal—son of the onager—was born. Strong and gentle, he was named Swift. He would be trained for my riding.

Inside the cave, progress continued. I began designing an aqueduct to bring fresh water directly to our home. The journey to Jackal River was long—especially on washing days. Hollow bamboo pipes worked well. A large cask served as a reservoir. The supply was steady, and my wife declared she was as pleased with this engineering feat as if we’d built a marble fountain with mermaids.

With the rainy season approaching, I pushed ahead with winter preparations. We gathered roots, fruits, grains—potatoes, rice, guavas, sweet acorns, pinecones. Load after load arrived. My wife sewed constantly, making more sacks and bags.

Casks and barrels were repurposed. Even the raft was dismantled—its tubs now stored in the cellar.

The weather grew stormy. Dark clouds rolled in. Wind, thunder, lightning, and torrential rain swept across the land. The sea surged against the cliffs. Nature itself sang the prelude to the great rains.

It was early June. Twelve weeks of rough weather lay ahead.

We moved some animals into the salt cave—the cow, the ass, Lightfoot, Storm, and the dogs. Knips, Fangs, and the eagle would provide amusement during long evenings.

The boys often rode to Falconhurst to check on things and fetch supplies.

More work remained to make the cave comfortable—especially since we’d be living indoors for months.

The darkness inside bothered me. So I devised a solution. I called Jack and we found a tall, sturdy bamboo pole. We planted it firmly in the floor, wedging it tight.

Jack climbed it, carrying hammer and chisel. He widened a crack in the ceiling and fixed a pulley. Then, descending, I raised a large ship’s lantern—filled with oil and burning four wicks—up through the hole. It cast a warm, steady glow.

Days passed arranging rooms. Ernest and Franz organized the library. Jack and his mother decorated the sitting room and kitchen. Fritz and I handled the workshops—setting up the carpenter’s bench, turning lathe, and hanging tools on the walls.

Our home was becoming a true haven.

"Time to finish this messy job!" I called cheerfully as the sun rose and we all stirred from our sleep. After completing our morning chores, we set to work building a rough platform where we placed large tubs filled with whale blubber. We pressed them down hard so that pure, clear oil dripped into containers beneath.

Later, we boiled the blubber in a cauldron over a fire built far from our home. By skimming off impurities and straining the liquid through coarse cloth, we collected a generous amount of high-quality train oil. We stored it safely in casks and bags made from animal intestines—our makeshift cellar, as the children fondly called it.

The task was unpleasant, and the stench lingered heavily in the air, especially troubling my dear wife. Yet she bore it with her usual calm and grace. Still, she strongly suggested we establish a new outpost on the nearby island. "There," she said, "any animals we leave would be safe from apes and thieves, and you'd have a perfect spot for boiling blubber, filtering oil, and other such tasks."

Her idea met with immediate approval—especially from the boys, who always loved a fresh adventure. They were eager to start right away. But when I reminded them of the rotting whale carcass still lying there, they agreed it would be wise to let wind, rain, birds, and insects do their work first, reducing the beast to bones before we returned.

Meanwhile, an idea had taken root in my mind: a rowing machine. I decided to build one. Using an iron bar laid across the boat’s center—projecting about a foot on each side—I fitted it with ribbed gears in the middle and added four flat paddles at each end, angled like oars. These would mimic human rowing.

I connected a winch mechanism to the central gear so that when wound up, its cogwheel engaged the ribs and spun the bar rapidly, turning the paddles and pushing the boat forward through the water.

Though imperfect, it worked well enough. Fritz and I tested it on a trip across the bay. The boat surged ahead with surprising speed, sending waves splashing around us. The whole family cheered wildly from shore, thrilled by what they saw as a brilliant invention.

Everyone wanted to take a ride, but it was growing late, so I refused. Instead, I promised a full-day excursion the next day—to Cape Disappointment and the little settlement at Prospect Hill.

That evening, we prepared clothes, weapons, food, and supplies, then retired early.

We left the house tidy and set out on our journey, carrying spades and mattocks. I intended to gather young coconut trees and various shrubs, hoping to plant them on Whale Island during our return.

Our course took us across the bay. The sea was calm, and the rowing machine glided smoothly. Leaving Safety Bay and Shark Island behind, we enjoyed the sweeping views of the coastline.

Near Prospect Hill, we landed and walked through the woods to our small farm. Along the way, we collected fresh coconuts and saplings.

Before reaching the cottage, we heard roosters crowing. A sudden wave of emotion washed over me—the sound brought back vivid memories of rides and walks back home, when familiar voices greeted us near friends’ houses. Here, though, only animals lived, and silence ruled. We approached the lonely cottage unnoticed, a quiet family visiting a forgotten place.

Our long absence had caused chaos. The original animals no longer recognized us. To their offspring—lambs, goats, chickens—we appeared as terrifying intruders.

The boys struggled to milk the goats until they used lassos to catch them one by one, tied their legs, and offered salt for them to lick. Soon, the goats calmed down and gave rich milk, which we poured from coconut shells into calabash flasks for later use.

Chickens were lured with grains and rice, and my wife caught several for dinner.

By now, hunger gnawed at us. We unpacked cold food, with the centerpiece being a piece of whale tongue, specially cooked at the boys’ request.

But disaster struck when we tasted it. One after another, faces fell. “It’s terrible!” they moaned, begging for pickled herring to cleanse their palates. Eventually, they handed the prized morsel to Fangs, our dog.

Luckily, we had plenty of other food. Fresh coconuts and goat’s milk soon lifted everyone’s spirits.

While my wife packed up, Fritz and I gathered sugar cane shoots for planting. Then we returned to the boat.

Before heading back to Whale Island, I felt drawn to explore beyond Cape Disappointment. But the promontory lived up to its name. A long sandbar stretched far into the sea, along with hidden reefs and rocks.

Fritz spotted breakers ahead, and we turned quickly. With a light breeze, we steered toward Whale Island.

On landing, I began planting the sugar cane shoots. The boys helped briefly, but soon grew bored and wandered off to collect shells and coral, leaving my wife and me to finish.

Suddenly, Jack burst back shouting:
“Father! Mother! Come quick! There’s a giant skeleton here—an enormous beast, maybe a mammoth!”

I laughed. “Jack, don’t you remember the whale? That’s what it is.”

“No, Father,” he insisted. “This has real animal bones, not fish bones. It’s much bigger than the whale. And the whale must have floated away. This thing is huge!”

Just then, another voice cried out:
“Father! Father! A giant turtle! Please hurry! It’s racing back to the sea, and we can’t stop it!”

That call was more urgent. I grabbed an oar and rushed to help.

Sure enough, a massive turtle was scrambling toward the water, nearly there. Ernest clung to one of its hind legs.

I ran down the bank and used the oar as a lever. Together, we managed to flip the creature onto its back.

It measured fully eight feet long and lay helpless. We left it there and went to inspect Jack’s “mammoth.” Of course, it was just the whale’s skeleton. I showed him the marks of our boots and the broken jaws where we’d cut out the whalebone.

“What made you think it was a mammoth?” I asked.

“Ernest told me,” Jack replied. “He said it looked like an ancient monster. I didn’t think of the whale when I saw no fish bones. Maybe he was joking.”

“Whales are often mistaken for fish,” I explained. “But they’re mammals—like humans, monkeys, dogs, cats, cows, and even sloths. The word comes from the Latin *mama*, meaning ‘breast,’ because all mammals have milk glands to feed their young.”

“The whale’s bones are hollow, filled with air to help it float—just like bird bones. This shows how wisely the Creator designed nature.”

“It’s amazing, Father,” Fritz said. “So many bones! Can we use any?”

“I don’t see anything useful now,” I said. “Let’s leave it to bleach. Later, we might cut it up for chairs or a desk for the museum. But now, we must deal with the turtle.”

We decided to let it swim. I fastened an empty water cask to a rope—one end tied to the boat, the other looped around the turtle’s neck and front legs. We lifted it carefully so it could stand again.

It immediately charged toward the sea, dove in, and the cask floated beside it, keeping it from sinking.

We boarded quickly. The turtle tried to dive but couldn’t. Instead, it swam steadily forward, towing us along. I kept it on course by striking the water with a boat hook, guiding it gently left or right.

The boys were thrilled. “You look like Neptune in his chariot!” they shouted, “drawn by dolphins, with Amphitrite and Tritons!”

We arrived safely near Rockburg. The turtle was soon captured and killed. Its shell—eight feet long and three wide—was cleaned and prepared to become a water trough for the cave. The meat was salted and stored for future meals.

I had planned to prepare land for planting before the next rainy season, but delays and lack of draft animals stopped me. So I shifted focus.

With Ernest’s help, I completed a loom. Though crudely made, it worked well. My past experience in workshops had taught me much about crafts.

We needed paste to coat threads, but we couldn’t spare flour. I used isinglass instead—it kept the warp moist and saved us from setting up the loom in damp places.

I also shaped thin isinglass sheets into windows. They were as clear as horn and, when fixed deep inside the rock, protected from rain, allowed good light into our rooms.

Encouraged, I began making harnesses. Leather from hunted animals provided material. I covered wooden frames with leather and stuffed them with moss or lichen. Soon, our animals wore saddles, stirrups, bridles, yokes, and collars—much to the delight of the young riders.

Next came the annual herring run, followed by other fish and seals. We seized every opportunity and caught many.

The boys begged for another hunting trip, but first, I wanted to make baskets. Our sacks were running low, and we needed containers for roots and fruits. Early attempts were clumsy, but perseverance paid off. We produced baskets of all sizes.

One large basket I fitted with holes for a strong pole, so two people could carry it when full.

The children saw the fun in it instantly. They found a bamboo pole, placed little Franz inside, and carried him triumphantly around.

Fritz got an idea. “Father,” he exclaimed, “can’t we make one like this for Mother? She’d travel much more comfortably than bouncing in the cart!”

They all laughed. Their mother thought it possible, but admitted she didn’t enjoy the idea of sitting in a basket, peeking over the edge.

I promised it would be a proper sedan chair—comfortable and well-built. But how to carry it?

“Use the bull and buffalo!” Jack shouted. “Let’s try them now!”

They raced off. Soon, the basket was hung between Storm and Grumble. Fritz and Jack mounted their horses, Ernest climbed into the “cradle” (as Franz called it), and they set off slowly.

“Mother, it’s wonderful!” Ernest called. “It rocks so gently. Faster, Fritz! Go faster!”

The pace increased. The animals trotted briskly, shaking the basket violently.

Ernest screamed for them to stop. His brothers laughed, enjoying “shaking up the professor.” They circled the field near Rockburg and pulled up dramatically in front of us, like performers awaiting applause.

We couldn’t help laughing. The scene was absurd. But Ernest was furious. He scolded his brothers, who fired back. A fight seemed likely—until I stepped in.

I reminded them how jokes gone too far could lead to quarrels and ruin the harmony that held us together. They listened, and peace returned.

Ernest even helped unharness the animals and fed them salt and barley, saying, “We’ll practice palanquin riding again soon.”

Later, I sat with my wife and Fritz under the verandah, weaving wickerwork and chatting. Suddenly, Fritz stood up, staring down the path from Jackal River.

“I see something strange, Father!” he said. “It looks like coils on the ground, then a mast rises, then sinks again. It’s moving toward the bridge!”

My wife grew alarmed. She called the others and retreated into the cave, where I instructed them to seal the entrances and guard the upper windows.

These openings were high in the rock, reached by steps and a gallery along the front.

Fritz stayed with me while I studied the object through my spyglass.

“It’s a giant serpent!” I said. “It’s coming straight here—and it will cross the bridge. We’re in great danger.”

“Can we attack it, Father?” Fritz asked bravely.

“Only with extreme caution,” I replied. “It’s too powerful. Thank God we’re at Rockburg. We can hide and wait for a chance to destroy it. Go help your mother prepare the guns. I’ll join you soon—but I must watch it.”

Fritz left reluctantly. I continued observing. The serpent was enormous, already too close to remove the bridge. I remembered how easily it could squeeze through walls.

It moved in writhing waves, occasionally raising its head fifteen or twenty feet high, turning slowly as if searching for prey.

As it crossed the bridge, I withdrew and joined my family, now preparing defenses. We barricaded the lower levels and positioned ourselves at the upper windows, unseen but hearts pounding.

The serpent entered our view. It hesitated, coiling and uncoiling, rearing its head repeatedly. Then—without warning—the boys fired, followed by their mother. The shots startled it but did no harm. It sped forward, slipped into the marshy reeds, and vanished.

A great weight lifted from our minds. We talked excitedly about its size and terrifying beauty. I identified it as a boa constrictor—over thirty feet long.

I explained its name: *Boaguacu* in South America, with the Latin suffix *-constrictor*, meaning “to squeeze.” That’s how it kills—by crushing its prey.

The presence of such a creature caused deep anxiety. I forbade anyone to leave without my permission.

For three days, we remained tense,不敢 step far from the door. The snake never appeared—but our geese and ducks grew restless. They swam nervously, flapped their wings, and finally flew to Shark Island.

My worry deepened. I couldn’t attack a hidden, dangerous serpent in thick swamps. Yet staying trapped was unbearable.

Our escape came not from courage—but from the old donkey, Grizzle.

Provisions were low, and hay ran out on the third evening. I decided to send the animals across the river at the ford, guided by Fritz. He would ride Lightfoot, and they’d be tied together for safety.

The next morning, we began tying them in line. My wife opened the door, and Grizzle—fresh and lively after rest—broke free, danced awkwardly, then bolted straight for the marsh.

We called him, but he didn’t stop. Fritz lunged forward, but I held him back.

In seconds, the donkey reached the thicket. Horror struck us as the snake rose from its lair—eyes blazing, jaws wide, tongue flickering. Grizzle’s fate was sealed.

He froze, spread his legs, and let out a heart-wrenching bray.

Like a fencer’s thrust, the serpent struck. It wrapped around him, crushed him tightly, avoiding his desperate kicks.

A cry rose: “Shoot him, Father! Save Grizzle!”

“Impossible!” I said. “Our friend is lost. But perhaps, once it’s full, we can attack.”

“But won{EIF it eats him all at once?” Jack asked.

“Snakes can’t chew,” I explained. “They swallow prey whole. No teeth to tear—just fangs to kill. The entire body, skin, hair, bones—everything goes in. Then it digests slowly.”

“It’s impossible!” Fritz said. “How can those big bones fit down?”

“That’s nature’s wonder,” I said. “And now, we wait. But the snake is full. We may yet have a chance.”

The greatest danger we had faced so far was now behind us, but I still carried a heavy weight of worry in my heart. What if another serpent—unseen, unknown—had slipped into the swamp? Or worse, what if it had come from beyond Falconhurst, slipping through the Gap like a shadow? The thought haunted me.

To ease my mind, I decided on two missions: first, a thorough search of the marsh and thicket; second, a bold journey to the Gap itself—the only path by which such a creature could have entered our land.

I called my sons to join me in the marsh, but Ernest and Jack were less than eager. Jack shivered at the memory of the snake’s tail, imagining how easily it might have crushed his ribs. But I would not be swayed by fear. We must face the truth, no matter how grim. Together, we began crossing the bog, laying planks and wicker hurdles as we went, shifting them forward with each step.

We found nothing but footprints in the reeds and a hollowed-out space where the grass and rushes had been flattened—clearly the serpent’s lair. Beyond that, the ground grew firm again, and we emerged near a steep rock wall. There, glistening in the sunlight, flowed a clear, sparkling brook emerging from a dark opening in the cliffside—a cave, or grotto, large and mysterious.

Inside, the vaulted ceiling dripped with stalactites, and thick columns of stone rose like pillars, holding up the roof. The floor was covered in soft, white earth that felt smooth and slippery underfoot—something I immediately recognized as fuller’s earth, a natural clay used for washing clothes.

“Well,” I said, “this is a fine find! It’s as good as soap—and saves me the trouble of making any.”

Fritz stepped forward, peering into the deeper part of the cave. He soon shouted back, “Father, this passage widens! Come see!”

I followed, leaving the others outside. As I entered, I fired a pistol shot. The echoes rolled back from every side, booming and swelling—proof that the cavern stretched far beyond sight. Lighting the small candle I always carried, we ventured deeper. Though the flame burned weakly in such vastness, it revealed walls that shimmered faintly, reflecting light like glass.

“Look!” Fritz exclaimed. “This must be another salt cave! See how the walls gleam?”

“No,” I replied. “Salt crystals leave marks when water runs over them, and this stream tastes sweet. I believe we’ve found a cave of rock crystal!”

“Oh, how magnificent!” he breathed. “A treasure!”

“A treasure indeed—if we can use it,” I said. “Otherwise, it’s no more valuable than the gold Robinson Crusoe found.”

Still, Fritz insisted on breaking off a piece. “Here’s a good one,” he said, holding up a dull lump. “Only… not transparent. A shame!”

“You’re damaging its shape,” I warned. “Crystal forms naturally into pyramids with six sides. You’ve broken that form.”

We explored further, marveling at the glowing walls and the silence that swallowed sound. When the candle flickered low, we turned back, Fritz firing one last shot just to hear the grand echo fade away.

Outside, we found Jack sobbing uncontrollably. At the sight of us, he rushed forward, throwing himself into my arms.

“Oh, father!” he cried. “I thought you were dead! I heard two terrible crashes—like rocks falling—and I believed you and Fritz were buried beneath them! It was awful!”

I comforted him, explaining the echoes were just our shots bouncing through the cave. Then I asked why he was alone.

“Ernest is over there in the reeds,” he said. “He didn’t hear the guns.”

I found Ernest busy weaving a clever fish trap—shaped like a funnel, wide at one end and narrow at the other, so fish could swim in but not out.

“I shot a young serpent while you were gone, father,” he said proudly. “It’s lying under the rushes. About four feet long, as thick as my arm.”

“A serpent?” I gasped, rushing over—fear rising again. “No, son. That’s an eel. And a fine catch, too. It’ll make a splendid supper. I’m glad you stayed and killed it instead of running.”

“It was hard,” Ernest admitted. “I shot it in the head, but it kept writhing even after. I had to beat it until it stopped.”

“The tenacity of life in eels is remarkable,” I said. “The best way to kill them is to hold them by the neck and strike their tails sharply against a stone.”

We returned more easily, following the cliffs where the ground was firmer. My wife waited at the fountain, washing clothes. She greeted us joyfully and praised the fuller’s earth—soap being nearly gone.

That evening, we feasted on the eel and shared stories of our adventure—the swamp, the cave, the crystal grotto. But I could not rest. The question remained: was there still a serpent lurking in our territory?

So we prepared for the next mission—our greatest yet. We would search the land beyond the river, all the way to the Gap. I wanted the whole family to go. Everyone agreed.

For weeks we’d be away, so we packed the small tent, food, tools, firearms, cooking gear, torches—everything we needed. The cart, drawn by Storm and Grumble, carried most of it. Jack and Franz rode the horses, acting as drivers and riders. My wife sat comfortably in the cart. Fritz led the way on horseback. Ernest and I walked beside. The dogs—Bruno, Fangs, and Juno—guarded our flanks.

We headed toward Woodlands, passing through signs of the serpent’s trail—deep furrows in soft soil, like broad ruts left by a giant beast.

At Falconhurst, we paused. The poultry clucked and fluttered around us, the sheep and goats came to greet us—peaceful as ever.

Then we pressed on to Woodlands, arriving just as night fell. Everything was calm. No sign of the boa. No trace of apes. The little farm thrived.

The next day, we explored nearby, gathering cotton for new pillows and cushions. In the afternoon, Franz joined me, carrying a small gun for the first time.

We took Fangs and Bruno with us, walking slowly along the lake’s left bank. Reeds and thickets often blocked our path, forcing us to detour. The dogs ran ahead, flushing ducks, snipes, herons—all flying across the lake before Franz could get a shot.

He grew impatient. “Can I shoot those black swans, father? They look so graceful.”

Just then, a deep, booming sound echoed through the air. Franz jumped. “Father! Is that Swift, our young onager?”

“No,” I said. “That’s not Swift. Listen closely. I think it’s a bittern—a large bird, like a heron.”

“But how can a bird make such a roar?” Franz asked. “It sounds like an ox!”

“It’s not size that determines sound,” I explained. “It’s the shape of the windpipe, the lungs, and the muscles. A tiny nightingale sings louder than many larger animals. Some say the bittern thrusts its bill into the mud to amplify its cry—like a horn.”

Franz was eager to shoot the bird. We sent the dogs into the woods and waited, hidden.

Suddenly—rustling! Franz fired.

“I hit it! I hit it!” he shouted.

“What did you hit?” I called.

“A wild pig,” he said. “Bigger than Fritz’s.”

“Aha!” I laughed. “You remember the agouti. But this may not be a hog at all. Could it be one of our farm pigs? What will the old sow say?”

I hurried to him. The animal looked like a pig—but its snout was blunt, its body covered in bristles, no tail, yellowish-gray skin. Its feet were webbed. I examined its teeth—odd, flat, strong.

“This,” I said, “is a capybara. A South American animal that lives near water.”

Franz was thrilled. “I shot a new creature!”

Carrying it home was hard. He suggested cutting it open and cleaning it—lighter that way. Then, he asked if Bruno could carry it on his back. The dog agreed, and we reached Woodlands safely.

There, we found Ernest surrounded by dead rats.

“Where did these come from?” I asked.

“We stumbled upon them,” he said. “Knips chased one into a mound among the reeds. It tried to escape into a hole, but Knips held it by the tail. I pulled it out and killed it.”

He broke open the mound—thick with mud and plant stems—and dozens of rats tumbled out. Some escaped into the water. Others swarmed around him, hissing and squealing.

“I thought of Bishop Hatto and the Mouse Tower!” he said. “Knips fought them, but I was scared. Just as I shouted for help, Juno burst through the reeds and drove them all away.”

His mother struggled through the marsh to reach him. Together, they collected the dead rats.

I went to inspect the site. To my surprise, I saw a small dam-like structure—made of clay and lined with reeds and lily pads.

“These are muskrats,” I said. “Also called beaver rats. They build homes like beavers. Webbed feet, flat tails—just like beavers. They also have scent glands—musk. We should skin them quickly. Their pelts will make good caps.”

Back at the house, Fritz and Jack returned—no serpents seen. Jack carried a dozen eggs in his hat. Fritz had shot a cock and hen heath fowl.

Supper was ready. Franz was eager to taste his capybara. Even he wrinkled his nose at the flavor.

“That’s the musk,” I said. “Many animals produce it—musk deer, musk ox, crocodiles, Indian muskrats, even the civet-cat.”

I told them about the civet—a black-and-white animal whose scent was once used as medicine, now mainly perfume. The scent comes from glands near the tail. Dutch traders keep them caged, using a special spoon to scrape the oily substance without harming the animal.

Later, Jack returned with a game-bag full of fruit—pale green apples, unfamiliar to us.

“What are these?” I asked.

“Fritz and I didn’t dare eat them,” he said. “They might be poison, like manchineel. So we brought them to Master Knips.”

I cut one open. Instead of a single seed, it had a ring of pips—different from manchineel.

At that moment, Knips crept up behind me, grabbed half, and began munching happily.

The boys couldn’t resist. A frantic scramble broke out. I managed to save two apples for myself and my wife.

I suspected it was the cinnamon apple of the Antilles.

Everyone was tired. We arranged our beds and settled down.

At dawn, we continued our journey—toward a spot between the sugar brake and the Gap. There, we found an old tree arbor we’d built before. We covered it with a sailcloth and made it comfortable.

Our goal: search for signs of the boa. Fritz, Jack, and Franz went with me to the sugar cane field. We found no trace. We drank fresh cane juice—refreshing and sweet.

Suddenly—barking! Rustling through the canes!

I rushed to the edge, guns ready. From the thicket came a herd of gray creatures—small pigs with sharp tusks—running in single file.

I fired. Two fell. The others kept going, ignoring the bodies. Fritz and Jack shot more.

“They’re peccaries,” I said. “Wild pigs. But we must remove the scent gland from their backs right away—or the meat will spoil.”

We worked fast. While Jack fetched the cart, I cleaned the animals. Ernest arrived with his mother and three more peccaries—caught with Juno’s help.

We loaded the cart. The boys decorated it with flowers and sang triumphantly as they carried the haul to the hut.

After dinner, we singed and scalded the bristles, cut the hams, and salted the meat. The boys built a smokehouse—using damp wood to create steady smoke. The meat hung inside for days, curing slowly.

On the second day, the boys wanted to cook the smallest pig Otaheitean style—baked underground.

They dug a hole, burned dry grass and sticks, heated stones, and placed them around the pit. Fritz cleaned and stuffed the pig with potatoes, onions, herbs, salt, and pepper. He sewed it shut and wrapped it in large leaves.

When the fire died, he placed the pig in the hole, covered it with hot ashes, then earth—making it look like a molehill.

Dinner was anticipated with excitement. My wife, skeptical, prepared plain food “just in case.”

But when Fritz opened the oven, a rich, savory aroma filled the air—roast pork, yes, but with a hint of spice.

“Those leaves,” I said. “They’re ravensara—native to Madagascar. Smells like nutmeg, clove, and cinnamon. The fruit makes oil prized in native cooking.”

The peccary looked perfectly cooked. Fritz received praise—even from his mother.

While curing the meat, we roamed the area—finding no snakes, but discovering towering bamboo—fifty to sixty feet tall. Cut at the joints, they made excellent casks and pots. Their sharp thorns served as nails.

One day, we visited Prospect Hill. The apes had been there again—ruining everything. Animals scattered, cottage torn apart. We left it as it was, promising to fix it later.

Finally, we closed the smokehouse, barricaded it, and set off for the Gap.

We’d cleared a path through the cane thicket during our stay—now it helped us move the loaded cart easily.

Beyond, the land opened. In a few hours, we reached the edge of our coastal domain.

We halted at a small wood. To the right, the cliffs rose steep and forbidding. To the left, the torrent rushed past, carving the narrow pass—the Gap—between the rocks and the sea.

High and safe within gunshot of the pass, I chose this wood as our campsite. We unpacked, set up the tent, and checked the trees for danger. Only wild cats—harmless enough—were found.

By dinner, we were exhausted. The heat was oppressive. We rested until evening, when coolness returned.

All was ready for tomorrow’s journey—into the country beyond the Gap. I planned a longer trek across the savannah than we’d ever attempted.

Early the next morning, we set out. My wife stayed with Franz. The three older boys, all dogs except Juno, went with me.

And so we began our final expedition—into the unknown.

As evening fell, the aroma of stewing bear paws filled the air, rising from the fire like a promise of warmth and comfort. Around it, we sat in quiet anticipation, ears tuned to the distant sounds of returning riders—our sons, still out on their great adventure.

Then came the thunder of hooves, sharp and clear across the open ground. The young explorers appeared in a gallop, dust swirling behind them, and a joyful shout echoed as we rose to greet them.

They leapt from their horses, freeing the animals to graze, and rushed toward us with tales and treasures in hand. What a sight they made! Franz and Jack each carried a young kid slung over their backs, its four legs tied together and dangling beneath their chins like odd little bundles. Fritz’s game bag bulged strangely—round shapes, sharp points, and occasional twitches hinted at living creatures inside.

“Hurrah for the hunt, Father!” Jack exclaimed, grinning from ear to ear. “Nothing beats real chasing! Storm and Grumble flew over that grassy plain like the wind. We wore out those little antelopes and caught them easily!”

“Yes, Father,” said Franz, “and Fritz brought back two angora rabbits. And we found honey—thanks to a clever bird, a cuckoo, who led us right to it!”

“My brothers forget the most important thing,” Fritz said, stepping forward proudly. “We drove a whole herd of antelopes through the Gap into our land. They’re there now—safe, free, and ready for us whenever we want them. Tame them, or hunt them—we’ve got them!”

“Well done!” I said, clapping my hands. “That’s a fine achievement. But more than anything, I’m grateful God brought you home safely. Now, tell me everything—every detail.”

“We rode down Glen Verdant,” Fritz began, “then through the defile in our Rocky Barrier. The morning was cool, and the horses raced almost the whole way. After passing through the Gap, we slowed down and scanned the hills. From the top of a grassy rise, we saw two herds of animals—antelopes, goats, or gazelles? We weren’t sure. But they were grazing near the stream below.

“We were about to charge down for a shot when I had an idea: instead of hunting them one by one, why not drive the whole herd through the Gap into our territory? That way, they’d be trapped in a kind of natural park—free, but ours.

“So we charged down the hill, formed a semicircle behind the larger herd, and with shouts and dogs barking, we drove them along the riverbank toward the Gap. As we neared the opening, they hesitated—just like sheep before the butcher’s door. We nearly lost them, but then one broke through, and the rest followed. In moments, every animal was on our side of the frontier—new citizens of New Switzerland!”

“Excellent,” I said. “But how do you know they’ll stay?”

“Father,” Fritz replied, “we thought of that. We stretched a long line across the defile—feathers, rags, anything that would flutter in the wind. It looked so strange, so unnatural, that I’m certain they’ll never cross it again. Levaillant writes in his *Voyage au Cap de Bonne Esperance* that the Hottentots use this trick to trap antelopes.”

“I’m glad you remember what you read,” I said. “Welcome to New Switzerland, antelopes. But—Fritz—I can’t say the same for your rabbits. They multiply too fast. If you start a colony, you may soon have a plague on your hands.”

“True,” he admitted, “but I plan to release them on Whale Island. There’s plenty of food, and they won’t bother us. Besides, my eagle caught these two. I saw them running, freed him, and in minutes he brought me three—one dead, which I gave him as a reward, and these two alive.”

“Now, Father,” Jack interrupted, “let me tell my part. Or Fritz will go on forever with his long descriptions.”

“Of course, Jack,” I said. “Tell me how you captured those lovely fawns.”

“We chased them,” Jack said. “The dogs sniffed around while Fritz was after the rabbits. Then—pop!—out came the fawns, bounding off like lightning. Storm, Grumble, and the dogs took off after them. They ran at thirty miles an hour, but in no time, we left the dogs behind and closed in.

“They collapsed in the grass, exhausted and terrified. We called Fritz, and—”

“Wait,” I said. “By your account, Fritz must have been seven and a half miles away.”

“Oh, well,” Jack laughed, “maybe we didn’t ride quite that long. And the fawns didn’t run straight. Anyway, Fritz heard us, and we all lashed their legs together. Then we remounted—and suddenly, a sneaky cuckoo appeared, hopping and cooing, leading us far off course. Franz said it was an enchanted princess. I was about to shoot it—but Fritz stopped me. He said it was a ‘Honey Indicator’—that it leads hunters to bee nests. So we let it live. And sure enough, it stopped right beside a hollow tree full of bees!”

“That’s wonderful,” I said. “And then?”

“We couldn’t wait to taste the honey,” Jack continued. “I threw in lit lucifer matches. But instead of killing the bees, they just went wild—stinging me everywhere! I jumped on Storm and galloped for my life. Took ages to shake them off. My face is burning now. I think I’ll ask Mother to bathe it.” With that, he dashed off, leaving Fritz and me to care for the fawns and inspect the rabbits.

I decided to follow Fritz’s idea: move the rabbits to Whale Island. It made sense—not only would they thrive there, but it also fit with my growing plan to turn Whale Island into a fortress. If danger ever forced us to retreat, having a steady supply of food would be vital.

After tending to the antelopes, I tried to share my discovery of the talc deposit with the boys—but just then, Mother called us to dinner.

The main course was bear paws—richly scented, tender, and so tempting that even their resemblance to human hands didn’t stop anyone from eating heartily. Jack muttered a mischievous “Fee-fi-fo-fum,” but no one minded.

After supper, we lit the watchfire, retired to the tent, and slept soundly.

We’d been busy: smoking bear meat, rendering fat, collecting bamboo. But I wanted one last expedition. At dawn, I woke the boys.

Fritz rode the mule, I mounted Lightfoot, Jack and Franz took their usual horses, and we set off with the dogs—first to gather gum from the euphorbia trees, then to check on the ostrich eggs.

Ernest watched us leave without a word, then returned to the tent to help Mother and study.

Our horses carried us swiftly down Green Valley, following the path of our earlier journey. Soon we reached Turtle Marsh, filled our water flasks, and climbed the ridge where Fritz had once seen mounted Arabs.

Jack and Franz wanted to race ahead, so I let them go, while Fritz and I visited the euphorbia trees. A thick red gum had oozed from the cuts I’d made. It had hardened in the sun, so I rolled it into balls and stored it in a bamboo jar.

“Father,” Fritz asked, “you said this juice is poisonous. Why collect so much?”

“I did,” I replied. “It’s deadly. The people of the Cape of Good Hope poison water holes with it to kill wild animals—mainly for hides. But I’ll use it to fight the apes if they return. And it helps preserve animal skins from insects.”

As we rode after the boys, four magnificent ostriches rose from the sand where they’d been sitting.

Jack and Franz spotted them and shouted, driving them toward us. A huge male led the way—black feathers gleaming, tail plume waving. Three gray females followed.

They charged at incredible speed, reaching gun range before noticing us. Fritz had already bound the eagle’s beak so he couldn’t harm the birds.

He released the falcon, which soared up and dove at the lead ostrich’s head. Startled, the bird stumbled. Jack closed in, hurled his lasso, and wrapped the creature’s wings and legs in coils. It crashed to the ground.

The others vanished into the distance. We dismounted and approached cautiously. The bird fought fiercely, kicking wildly in all directions. I feared we’d never bring it home alive.

Then I remembered: cover its eyes, and it might calm down.

I threw my coat and hunting bag over its head. Instantly, the struggling stopped.

We secured it with a wide strip of sealskin, fastened cords to either side so I could lead it, and looped another rope around its legs to prevent sudden bursts of speed. Then we removed the lasso.

“Do you know,” I asked the boys, “how Indian natives tame a new elephant?”

“Oh yes!” Fritz said. “They tie it between two tame elephants. We’ll do the same!”

“The only problem,” Jack added, “is we don’t have tame ostriches. But Storm and Grumble will make fine guards. This beast won’t escape them!”

So we began. Storm and Grumble were led to either side of the resting ostrich. Cords were tied to their girths. Jack and Franz mounted their horses, each armed with a whip. We removed the cloth from the bird’s eyes.

For a moment, it lay still. Then it sprang up—unaware of the ropes—and tried to run. The cords held firm. It fell to its knees. After a brief struggle, it accepted its fate and trotted forward, spurred on by the boys’ laughter.

The noise excited it further, but eventually, Storm and Grumble refused to keep pace. The ostrich stopped.

I told the boys to walk the bird slowly home, while Fritz and I checked the nest.

The eggs were warm—clearly the mother had just left. I left half and packed the rest into a large bag, slung it over Lightfoot’s saddle.

We caught up with the others and arrived at camp without incident.

Mother’s face showed shock and dismay.

“My dear husband,” she said, “do you really need to go raiding deserts for giant beasts? Next you’ll find an iron mine—because ostriches eat iron, don’t they?

“Oh, please, just keep the animals you’ve already collected. This is such a useless monster!”

“Useless?” Jack protested. “You wouldn’t say that if you’d seen it run! It’ll be the fastest horse in our stable. I’m making it a saddle and bridle. It’ll be my only steed. And its diet? Just fruit, grass, and a few stones and nails—it’s very delicate.”

His claim amused his brothers. A cry went up.

“Fine,” Jack said. “Let’s divide the bird. Fritz’s eagle grabbed the head—keep that. Father gets the body. I’ll take the legs. Franz, you get two tail feathers.”

“Hold on,” I said. “Jack has the best claim—he brought it down. If he tames it and makes it a riding bird, it’s his. From now on, he’s responsible for training it.”

Too late in the day to reach Rockburg, we tied the ostrich between two trees and spent the evening preparing for the journey.

At dawn, our colorful caravan moved forward. The ostrich remained stubborn, so we again placed it between Storm and Grumble. The cow pulled the cart, loaded with our treasures. Mother rode in the cart. Jack and Franz mounted Storm and Grumble. I rode Lightfoot, and Fritz brought up the rear on Swift.

At the mouth of the Gap, we paused. We replaced the feathered cord with a strong bamboo palisade. I also gathered pipe-clay—planning to try making china during the coming winter.

At the sugar-cane grove, we stopped to retrieve the smoked peccary hams. Mother asked me to gather seeds of a vanilla-scented plant nearby. I collected a good amount, then we pressed on toward Woodlands, where we planned to rest.

We pitched our tent and slept deeply on cotton beds.

The next morning, we inspected our farm. Everything thrived—the pigs, goats, chickens. Two broods of chicks especially pleased Mother, whom she decided to take back with us.

The antelope herd had settled near the trees. We saw them browsing often.

We repaired the animal stalls and our dwelling—making both safer and more comfortable.

All arranged, we rested. The next morning, we completed our journey to Rockburg.

By midday, we were home. Windows and doors opened wide. Animals settled into their stalls. The cart’s contents were unpacked and stored.

I turned first to the ostrich, tying it between two bamboo posts in front of the house.

Then I focused on the eggs. I built a stove to maintain constant heat and placed the eggs carefully in cotton wool.

Next morning, Fritz and I rowed to Whale Island, released the rabbits, then to Shark Island, where we set free the antelopes.

Back at home, we cured the bear skins and added new supplies to our cellar.

On our way, we met Jack, bursting with joy, carrying a massive eel in a basket—caught the night before with Ernest.

It was delicious. Half went to dinner; the other half was salted and stored.

We returned to household duties.

Thinking the verandah needed greenery, I planted vanilla, pepper, and other creeping vines at the base of each bamboo pillar—adding beauty and shade.

I built hen-coops for the chicks from Woodlands, knowing Knips and Fangs might otherwise investigate them too closely.

Ernest’s rat skins were tied together and hung outside—they smelled so strong that Jack pretended to faint whenever he passed.

The museum gained new items: the condor and vulture, to be stuffed later. Mica and asbestos were stored for future use—china-making and lamp-wicks.

After two days of work, we turned to farming, egg-hatching, and taming the ostrich.

Agriculture, though least appealing, was most important. The animals pulled the plough, but digging and hoeing tested our strength.

We worked two hours in the morning, two in the evening. We truly understood the Bible’s words: *In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread.*

Between shifts, we trained the ostrich. At first, nothing worked. He remained wild.

Then I remembered the eagle—tamed with tobacco smoke.

I applied the same method. The ostrich collapsed, motionless. Slowly, he rose and paced. Calmer, but refusing food.

“Food he must have!” I insisted.

Mother tried an experiment—balls of maize flour mixed with butter. She placed one in his beak. He swallowed. Then another, and another. His appetite returned.

The wildness faded. He began to obey.

After a month, he trotted, galloped, responded to voice, fed from our hands.

Now came the challenge: how to saddle and bridle a bird?

I recalled how darkness subdued him. I designed a leather hood, covering neck to beak, with eye and ear holes. Over the eyes, I attached square flaps—blinkers—with whalebone springs so they closed tightly.

Reins connected to the blinkers. Open both—straight ahead. Close right eye—turn left. Left eye—turn right. Both shut—stop.

I was proud of my invention.

Then came the saddle. After several tries, I made one shaped like an old cavalry saddle—peaked front and back—to prevent falls. I placed it high on the shoulders, secured it with strong girths across the wings and chest.

The contraption was strange—but it worked.

And so, our newest companion was ready. Not just a captive, but a rider’s friend.

The rainy season had arrived with a vengeance—dark clouds rolled in, thunder rumbled across the sky, and heavy downpours turned our usual outdoor adventures into impossible dreams. With the storms came silence and stillness, and even inside our spacious home, where we had so many projects to keep us busy, time seemed to drag. The mood of the family grew heavy; even brief rides during lulls in the rain failed to lift our spirits.

Fritz, ever restless and full of ideas, broke the quiet one afternoon. “Father,” he said, “why don’t we build a canoe? Something light and fast—faster than our pinnace or the old boat. I dream of gliding over the water like a bird on the breeze.”

His idea sparked excitement among us all. But my wife, who always worried when we ventured onto the sea, quickly voiced her concern. “We already have two boats,” she said firmly. “And each one carries enough danger. Why add another craft that might tempt us out into the unpredictable waves?”

I understood her fear, but I reassured her. “This won’t be some fragile shell,” I said. “I’ll build it strong—inspired by the Greenlander’s cajack. It will be tough, safe, and built to last. And while we’re at it, it’ll give the boys something meaningful to do.”

She hesitated, but seeing the determination in my eyes, she finally relented.

Work began at once. We crafted the frame from whalebone, reinforcing the sides with split bamboo canes. A deck ran the full length of the boat, leaving only a square opening for the rider to sit. When completed, we covered the entire structure with seal-skin, sealing every seam with caoutchouc to make it completely watertight. The result was a curious-looking vessel—light enough to carry with one hand, yet sturdy enough to brave the open waters.

When we launched it into the bay, it bounced across the surface like a rubber ball. Fritz was declared its rightful owner, though his mother insisted on one condition: before he could use it, she would make him a swimming dress—just in case.

“Even the strongest boat can fail,” she said. “If it sinks, he must still have a chance.”

So together we designed a strange-looking garment—a double waistcoat made of linen treated with a rubber solution. The seams were sealed with caoutchouc, and a small valve allowed air to be pumped between the layers, inflating the suit like a buoyant bubble.

One bright afternoon, Fritz stood on the shore, fully dressed and inflated, grinning as his brothers roared with laughter. He stepped into the water and paddled easily across the bay toward Shark Island, where we followed in one of our larger boats.

The test was a success. Ernest, Jack, and Franz, still chuckling, begged their mother to make them one too.

On the island, we visited the animals we’d sheltered the previous autumn—the antelopes had used our hut, as their footprints showed. We left behind handfuls of maize and salt, then wandered along the shore, collecting shells and coral for the boys’ museum. Near the water lay a thick layer of colorful seaweed, which the boys gathered under their mother’s guidance, knowing it had uses beyond decoration.

But I noticed something odd—my wife carefully selected certain leaves with serrated edges, washing and drying them in the oven. Curious, I asked if they were meant to replace tobacco.

She smiled. “Not at all. Do you think I’d miss that smelly stuff?” Then, with a twinkle, she added, “A mattress filled with these might be cool in summer.”

I didn’t press further—yet my curiosity only deepened.

Later, after a long hike, we rested on the verandah when my wife appeared with a porcelain dish holding a translucent, shimmering jelly. “Would anyone care for some jelly?” she asked.

We took spoons and tasted it—cool, sweet, and delicately flavored. “Ambrosia!” Fritz exclaimed.

“It’s not just delicious,” she said, “it’s made from those very seaweed leaves.” She explained how, during her time in Cape Town, she’d seen this dish prepared. The leaves are soaked daily for a week, then boiled with orange juice, citron, and sugar. The result is a clear, refreshing gelatin.

We all praised her skill. The boys immediately wanted to return to the island to gather more.

On the next trip, we checked on our plantings on Whale Island. The rabbits had multiplied, nibbling at young shoots—except for the coconut palms, which they left untouched. To protect the trees, we built thorny hedges around their trunks.

I also noticed that some seaweed had been gnawed by the rabbits. Intrigued, I collected a sample to study later.

Nearby, the skeleton of the great whale stood bleached white by sun and wind. I saw potential in its vertebrae—strong, rounded bones perfect for building machinery. I gathered ten or twelve joints and dragged them back to the boat.

Back home, I began planning a crushing machine. Stone would be too hard to shape, but these whale bones could serve as rollers. First, I needed timber. I set out alone, choosing Storm as my mount, since the boys had taken off on their own hunting expedition.

Crossing the bridge, I found the cassava field ruined—trampled and stripped. Examining the tracks, I recognized pig prints. Following the trail, I soon heard barking from Floss and Bruno. They had cornered a wild sow and her litter, standing defiantly in the clearing.

I raised my gun and fired twice. Two pigs fell. The rest fled, chased by the dogs. I retrieved the game and continued to the forest, where I marked a suitable tree for my project.

Ernest stayed behind and helped me skin the pigs. I gave the meat to my wife to prepare for supper, hoping the others would return soon.

That evening, the sound of hooves echoed through the trees. Jack burst in on his ostrich, followed by Fritz and Franz, laden with game. On the ground lay four birds, a kangaroo, twenty musk-rats, a monkey, two hares, half a dozen beaver rats—and a bundle of thistles.

Jack was breathless. “Father! You wouldn’t believe how fast an ostrich runs! It felt like flying. I had to close my eyes—the wind was too strong. I need a mask with glass eyes!”

I laughed. “No mask. You’re capable of making your own. But next time, let me know when you plan such trips. We worry here.”

They nodded, eager to share their tales. Fritz produced the thistles. “I thought this might be useful for wool-making,” he said.

I examined it closely. “You’re right—it’s fuller’s teazle. Its spines are used to raise the nap on cloth.”

We decided to rise early the next day to process the hunt. As the boys grew sleepy, I led the evening prayers.

Skinning so many animals was tedious work. I remembered a tool from the ship’s surgeon’s chest—a large syringe. With a few modifications, I turned it into an air pump. I attached valves and drilled a hole in the stopper.

The boys stared in disbelief when I approached the kangaroo with the device. “Are you going to skin it with a squirt?” they joked.

I ignored the laughter. I hung the kangaroo by its hind legs, made a small cut, and inserted the syringe. With force, I blew air between the skin and flesh. Slowly, the hide swelled, stretching until it separated entirely from the body.

With a few cuts, the skin slipped off.

“What a brilliant idea!” they shouted.

“It’s simple science,” I explained. “Animal skin is held by delicate fibers. Between them are tiny air pockets. By filling them with air, you stretch and break the fibers. The skin detaches naturally.”

“The Greenlanders use this method,” I added. “They inflate seal skins to tow them ashore, then peel them off easily.”

We applied the same technique to all the animals. Within days, every hide was removed and ready for use.

Now it was time to gather wood for the crushing machine. We returned to the marked tree. Fritz and Jack climbed high to cut large branches so the tree would fall safely. Then, using a pit saw, Fritz and I cut through the trunk.

We tied ropes to guide its fall. With a mighty effort, we pulled. The tree groaned, cracked, and crashed to the ground.

We chopped off the limbs and cut the trunk into blocks—four long ones and two smaller. Over the next few days, we transported them home. Using the whale vertebrae as rollers, I assembled the crushing machine in no time.

Meanwhile, I had neglected our grain fields. One evening, the chickens returned with full crops, ignoring their feed. I realized they’d come from the cornfield.

Rushing there, I found the grain perfectly ripe. My wife gasped when I told her. “What about the potatoes and manioc? How will we manage all this work?”

“Don’t fret,” I said. “Manioc can wait. Potatoes are easy to dig here. And we’ll harvest like the Italians—quick and efficient.”

I leveled a large patch of clay soil, watering and tamping it until it became a hard, smooth threshing floor. We slung our largest basket between Storm and Grumble. Armed with reaping hooks, we went to the field.

“Don’t worry about the straw,” I told the boys. “Just grab the stalks wherever convenient. Wind them together and throw them into the basket.”

They loved it. In no time, the basket was full. But my wife was horrified. “You’ve left ears on short stalks! And all that good straw wasted!”

“That’s not waste,” I said. “In Italy, farmers leave the stubble for cattle to graze. Then they let grass grow and mow everything together for winter feed. Now, for threshing—we’ll do it Italian style.”

We piled the sheaves in a circle. The boys mounted Storm, Grumble, Lightfoot, and Hurry, circling the pile at a brisk trot. Dust and chaff flew as the animals stamped out the grain.

My wife and I used hayforks to shift the sheaves, ensuring even coverage. The horses occasionally nibbled at the grain—fair enough, I thought. “Thou shalt not muzzle the ox that treadeth out the corn,” I quoted.

After threshing, we winnowed the grain by tossing it high into the air when the wind blew. The chaff floated away, and the clean grain fell to the ground.

Our poultry visited often, gobbling up stray kernels. “Let them eat,” I said. “We lose some grain, but gain rich poultry. Soon, we’ll have chicken pie, roast goose, and boiled turkey!”

When the harvest was stored, we counted our yield—sixty, eighty, even a hundred times what we’d sown. Our granaries overflowed.

We prepared the field again for planting—mowing the stubble, which attracted quails and partridges. We resolved to catch them next season with nets.

The straw was carried home and stacked. Maize stalks burned into ashes rich in alkali—useful for soap. We planted rye, barley, and oats, hoping they’d mature before the rains returned.

As we finished farming, schools of herring arrived. This year, we pickled only two barrels. But the seals came next—and we hunted them vigorously.

Their skins were essential. I planned to cover the deck of the cajack with seal-skin, creating a waterproof magazine for pistols, ammunition, water, and food. I also rigged two harpoons with seal bladders, hanging them along the side.

Our new boat was ready. Strong, swift, and built with care. And though the stormy season had passed, our adventure had only just begun.

At last, the day arrived for Fritz’s first solo journey in the cajack. Dressed head to toe in his swimming gear—trousers, jacket, and cap—he looked so absurdly bundled up that he could barely fit into the narrow canoe. As he hunched over, puffing and straining like a frog about to burst, laughter erupted from everyone watching. Even his mother couldn’t help but smile, though she had been the one to design the outfit.

I launched our larger boat nearby so my wife could see we were ready to rescue him if needed. The cajack slipped smoothly into the sea, floating like a leaf on glassy green water. Fritz paddled with growing confidence—darting forward like an arrow, turning sharply left and right, then dramatically tipping the canoe onto its side. His mother gasped in horror, but the little vessel held firm, neither capsizing nor sinking. Regaining balance, Fritz shot off again, full of courage.

Encouraged by our cheers, he ventured into the strong current of Jackal River, which swept him swiftly out to sea.

That was more than I’d planned. Without hesitation, I set off after him in the boat, with Ernest and Jack beside me. My wife urged us faster, muttering that “that horrid soap-bubble” was bound to get into trouble.

We soon reached the outer edge of the bay, near the rocks where the old shipwreck once lay. We scanned the horizon anxiously, searching for any sign of the runaway.

After a while, a faint wisp of smoke appeared far in the distance, followed by the sharp crack of a pistol. We fired a signal shot in reply. Moments later, another echoed back. Steering toward the sound, we soon heard Fritz’s cheerful shout. Then, around a bend in the coast, the cajack emerged—and we pulled up alongside.

“Come ashore here!” he called. “I’ve got something to show you!”

With wide-eyed astonishment, we saw a large young walrus lying dead on the rocks, harpooned through the chest.

“You killed this?” I asked, scanning the shore, half expecting a wild tribesman to appear and claim the prize.

“Of course, Father! Don’t you see my harpoon? Why would you doubt it?”

“Well,” I laughed, “the timing is too perfect for an amateur Greenlander. I’m truly impressed. But you’ve scared us half to death going so far out. You should never have left the bay. Your mother was terribly worried.”

“I didn’t mean to go beyond sight,” Fritz said, “but once caught in the current, I couldn’t stop. Then I spotted a herd of walruses, and I just had to try for one. I chased them past the current’s pull until I got close enough to strike. This one swam slower, so I hit him twice. He fled into these rocks and died. I landed, made sure he was really dead with a pistol shot—remember how that serpent attacked you, Jack—and then came to find you.”

“You took a terrible risk,” I said. “Walruses are usually peaceful, but when wounded, they turn deadly. Their tusks can rip apart even a sturdy whaleboat. Thank God you’re safe—that’s worth more than a thousand such creatures. Now, what do we do with this beast? It must be fourteen feet long, even if not fully grown.”

“I’m glad you followed,” Fritz replied. “But we can’t move this whole thing. Let me take the head—with those magnificent white tusks! I want to mount it on the front of the cajack and call it the *Sea-horse*.”

“We’ll definitely take the tusks,” I agreed. “But hurry—this air feels thick and hot. A storm’s coming.”

“But the head! We need the whole head!” Jack insisted. “Just imagine how grand it’ll look on the boat!”

“And how grand it’ll smell when it rots,” Ernest added dryly. “A real treat for the steersman!”

“Oh, we’ll preserve it properly,” Fritz promised. “Soak it, clean it, dry it till it’s as hard as wood. It won’t offend your nose, Ernest.”

“Isn’t the walrus native to the Arctic?” Ernest asked.

“It is,” I said, “though they sometimes wander farther. These may have drifted from the Antarctic seas. There’s also a smaller cousin along Africa’s east coast—the dugong. It has long teeth but no tusks, and looks more like a seal than a walrus.”

As we talked, we worked quickly to remove the walrus’s head and cut strips of hide. It took time—we lacked proper tools—but Fritz decided the cajack must carry a hunting knife and hatchet next time. He also suggested placing a small compass in a glass-topped box at the steersman’s seat. I agreed; it was a smart idea.

Once done, I offered to bring Fritz and the canoe aboard our boat, but he begged to return alone, eager to act as our advance scout and surprise his mother. So he sped away across the water, while we followed slowly.

Meanwhile, dark clouds gathered fast. Soon, a furious storm broke loose. Fritz was already out of sight, beyond our reach.

We fastened on swimming belts and lashed ourselves tightly to the boat, bracing against towering waves that crashed over the gunwales. The sky turned black, wind howled, rain poured down, and lightning split the darkness. Both boys faced the danger bravely, and though I feared for our safety, I was heartened by how well the boat held.

The storm raged, then suddenly cleared—just as abruptly as it began. But the rough seas still threatened our fragile craft.

All hope for myself faded—I feared only for Fritz. I gave him up as lost, and my heart rose in prayer: *Thy will be done.*

Finally, we rounded the point and entered Safety Bay. As we neared the harbor, we saw something astonishing: our wife and Fritz, along with their youngest son, kneeling in prayer, so focused on pleading for our return that they didn’t notice us until we shouted.

Then came a joyful reunion—tears, embraces, and shared thanks for the mercy that saved us all.

Back at Rockburg, we changed into dry clothes and sat down to a warm meal, recounting the storm’s dangers in calm relief.

Later, the walrus head was taken to the workshop. After careful cleaning, embalming, and drying, it was mounted proudly on the bow of the cajack—now truly the *Sea-horse*, looking bold and fearsome.

The hides, tanned and treated, became excellent leather.

The storm had caused damage: floods ruined crops, the bridge over Jackal River was partially destroyed, and the water tanks and pipes needed repair. Days passed in rebuilding.

One day, near the cascade, we found scattered dark-red berries—about the size of hazelnuts, topped with tiny leafy crowns. The boys tried them immediately, but spat them out in disgust. Even Knips refused them. Yet the scent intrigued me. I identified them as cloves.

We planted some in the nursery garden. My wife was delighted—she could now flavor her rice and other dishes with the spice instead of pepper, a welcome change for all.

With plenty of clay from Falconhurst, I proposed making aqueducts. Seeing how recent rains had helped our corn grow, I decided to irrigate the fields using drainage from our crushing mill.

Fishing proved successful again—salmon, sturgeon, and herring filled our nets. Our storehouse bulged once more. Though I wished we could enjoy fresh fish year-round, I had to reject Jack’s whimsical idea of tethering salmon by the gills to the seabed, like we had done with the turtles.

Days passed quietly. I noticed the boys growing restless, tired of farm work. They longed for adventure—either a cruise in the yacht or a trek into the woods.

“Father,” Fritz said one day, “we need more hurdles, but we’re running out of bamboo. Shouldn’t we go to Woodlands? And you mentioned wanting more of that fine clay—we could visit the Gap too.”

I had no objections. Soon, Fritz, Jack, and Franz were set to leave. Ernest, less eager to join, stayed behind with his mother and me to help build a sugar-mill—a project I’d wanted for years.

Before departure, Fritz asked his mother for bear meat to make pemmican.

“And what’s pemmican?” she asked.

“It’s food used by fur-traders in North America,” he explained. “Bear or deer meat, cooked, then pounded into powder. It’s light, durable, and nourishing.”

She agreed—“to humor him,” she said, doubting its value. But within two days, Fritz had made enough pemmican for a polar expedition.

They were ready to go when I noticed Jack slipping a basket of pigeons under the cart’s supplies.

“Oh, oh,” I thought. “He doesn’t trust that pemmican. He thinks a tough old pigeon would taste better.”

The weather was perfect. With warnings of caution from both me and his mother, the three boys set off in high spirits. Storm and Grumble pulled the cart, ridden by Fritz and Franz. Hurry carried Jack ahead, followed by Floss and Bruno, barking at his heels.

The sugar-mill kept us busy for several days. It resembled our other mills, so there was no need to describe it in detail.

On the evening of their first day, as we rested on the porch at Rockburg, we naturally spoke of the boys’ journey.

Ernest seemed secretive. “I might have news tomorrow,” he hinted.

Suddenly, a bird landed on the dove-cot and disappeared inside. In the dim light, I couldn’t tell if it was ours or a stranger. Ernest jumped up to check.

Minutes later, he returned holding a scrap of paper. “News, Father! Latest dispatch by pigeon-post, Mother!”

“Well done, boys! What a clever idea!” I said, reading:

> *Dearest parents and Ernest,*
> *A hyaena killed a ram and two lambs. The dogs attacked it. Franz shot it. It’s dead and skinned. The pemmican isn’t much good, but we’re all safe. Love to all.*
> *Fritz*
> *Woodlands, 15th instant*

“A true hunter’s letter!” I laughed. “But exciting news. When does the next post arrive, Ernest?”

“Tonight, I hope,” he said. His mother sighed, saying she’d rather wait to hear everything when they returned safely.

From then on, the carrier pigeons brought daily updates—brief sketches of adventures later told in full.

Near Woodlands, the boys were startled by repeated sounds of human laughter echoing through the forest. The oxen grew agitated, the dogs growled, and the ostrich bolted with Jack into the rice swamp.

“What’s wrong?” Fritz shouted. “I can’t leave the animals. Franz, take the dogs—move cautiously and see what’s happening.”

Franz crept through the bushes with his gun, followed closely by the dogs. Through a break in the thicket, he saw a massive hyaena—dancing wildly around a freshly killed lamb, laughing hysterically.

It ran back and forth, standing on hind legs, spinning, nodding its head—performing grotesque, almost comic antics.

Franz stayed calm. He waited until the beast calmed, began tearing its prey, then fired both barrels—breaking its foreleg and wounding its chest.

By then, Fritz had unharnessed the oxen and secured them. He rushed to help. The dogs and the dying hyaena fought fiercely. The animal injured both Floss and Bruno badly, but soon collapsed—dead when the boys arrived.

They cheered, drawing Jack to the scene. First, they tended the dogs’ wounds, then examined the creature. It was as big as a wild boar—gray with black markings, stiff bristles forming a mane, powerful jaws, muscular thighs, and sharp claws. Had it not been wounded, it would have overwhelmed the dogs.

After unloading the cart, they brought the hyaena’s body back, skinned it, and sent a pigeon with the news. That night, they slept on bearskin rugs, dreaming of adventure.

The next day, they mapped the shores of Wood Lake, marking paths through the marsh with tall bamboo poles topped with reeds.

They captured three young black swans, which were later brought to Rockburg as decorations for Safety Bay.

A heron peeked from the reeds. Before it could flee, Fritz released his eagle. The bird struggled, but was swiftly bound and subdued—its captors delighted.

Then a strange sound—puffing and whistling—came through the reeds. A large animal surged past, startling them. It vanished before they could call the dogs. From their description, it must have been a tapir—dark brown, rhino-like but without a horn, and with a trunk-like upper lip. Gentle, yet dangerous when provoked. It swims and dives easily, common in tropical American swamps.

Fritz followed in the cajack, but lost sight of it. The others returned via the rice fields and encountered a flock of cranes—five or six, including two demoiselle cranes.

They caught them alive using arrows tipped with loops of cord dipped in birdlime—entangling the birds without harm.

They lived well on peccary ham, cassava bread, fruit, baked potatoes, and milk.

One taste of pemmican was enough—it went to the dogs. But Fritz decided to try again, knowing its value when properly made.

After gathering rice and cotton, they headed to Prospect Hill.

“There,” Fritz later recounted, “we entered the pine wood—and found it overrun by monkeys! They shrieked and threw pine cones at us like demons. We fired a few shots—some fell, the rest fled. But when we reached the hill, we found chaos.

“The cottage was trashed—just like Woodlands last summer! Filth everywhere. We cleaned it up, but ended up sleeping in the tent. I didn’t know how to protect the farm… until I found a bottle of euphorbia poison in the tool chest.

“I mixed it with milk, millet, and anything the monkeys would eat. I put it in coconut shells and hung them high in the trees—out of reach of our animals.

“That night was beautiful—the sea murmured, the moon rose, and silence wrapped the land. Then—suddenly—every beast in the forest woke up. Snorting, snarling, shrieking filled the woods.

“Jackals howled from the hills, answered by Fangs and the others. Far off, deep snorts and neighings echoed—like hippos. Above, mighty roars shook us—lions, elephants?

“We retreated, hoping sleep would erase the terror. But soon, the apes began dying from the poisoned feast. The dogs couldn’t stay quiet. No one slept.

“At dawn, we saw the horror—dozens of dead monkeys and baboons littered the trees. I wished I hadn’t found the poison. We buried some, tossed the rest into the sea. Then we traveled on to the Gap.”

That same evening, a pigeon arrived at Rockburg with a note ending:

> *The barricade at the Gap is broken. Everything destroyed—up to the sugar-brake. The hut is smashed, fields trampled by huge footprints. Come, Father—we’re safe, but we can’t handle this alone.*

I wasted no time. I saddled Swift, rode out under the moonlight, and reached the Gap before dawn. The boys were surprised and thrilled—expecting me the next day.

Early the next morning, I examined the damage. The tracks were unmistakable—elephant prints. Only an elephant could cause such destruction.

Posts snapped like twigs, trees stripped bare, and young sugar cane devoured or crushed. Clearly, not one, but a herd had passed through. The footprints varied in size—many leading to the Gap, and just as many leaving.

We concluded the elephants had raided and retreated—no longer hiding in our lands. We resolved to strengthen our defenses.

That night, sitting by a roaring fire, I listened to stories of their adventures—so vivid and thrilling that none of us wanted to sleep. Instead, we took turns guarding the camp, hearts full of wonder and pride.

"We spend our years as a tale that is told," said King David. These words echoed in my mind again and again as I reflected on the ten years chronicled in the pages of my journal.

Year after year passed—each chapter unfolding like a quiet turning of the page—time slipping away, unnoticed yet certain. Though memory cast a gentle shadow of sadness, it was quickly lifted by gratitude to God for the peace and joy He had granted us, especially in watching our sons grow into strong, capable men shaped by early trials and steady purpose.

And so I pray that any young reader who follows this record may learn how truly formative a life of faith, industry, and family unity can be—how such a home builds character marked by courage, integrity, and kindness. No one enters the wider world more prepared, more loved, or more ready to serve than those raised in such an atmosphere.

Having already described many years of life in New Switzerland—the name we gave our island domain—I need not repeat every detail of our hunting trips, explorations, or mechanical experiments. Such accounts would soon grow tedious, better suited to a scientific ledger than a family’s story.

Yet before closing this chapter of our lives, I wish to share some notable observations about our settlement at the time these events occurred.

Rockburg and Falconhurst remained our winter and summer homes, each improved over the years to become both more comfortable and more beautiful. At Rockburg, fountains sparkled beneath trellised verandas, and flowering vines draped balconies and columns. Trees—both native and brought from Europe—grew thick in groves we planted ourselves. The air was sweet with fragrance, and the place no longer felt harsh or barren as it once had.

In the distance, Shark Island stood guard over Safety Bay, now crowned with graceful palms. Its rocky peak bore the battery and flagstaff, visible from afar. The swamp, drained and cleared, had become a wide lake, ringed by reeds and marshland—an ideal refuge for waterfowl. Black swans glided across its surface like shadows; snow-white geese floated in elegant groups; ducks with bright plumage paddled among the rushes. And sometimes, glimpses of brilliant sultan birds, crimson flamingos, blue-grey demoiselle cranes, and crested herons appeared, all living together in harmony, unafraid of us.

The giant frogs—Grace and Beauty—had grown so large they resembled small rabbits. They knew their names well and would waddle out at Jack’s call to eat a grasshopper or a fat fly.

Old Hurry, the ostrich, still marched through the trees with solemn dignity, as if he ruled the land. Pigeons of every kind nested in the rocks and dove-cots, their soft cooing and shimmering feathers making them beloved pets.

Only one path led to Rockburg: the bridge. Above it, near the waterfall, a dense thicket of orange and lemon trees, figs, prickly pears, and thorny shrubs formed a natural wall—our own barrier against intruders.

On Shark Island, the rabbit warren supplied us with food and fur. The antelopes, which did not thrive on Whale Isle, were moved to live among the shade trees with the rabbits. Their former island became a workshop for tasks like candle-making, tanning, and wool-cleaning—work we preferred to keep separate.

At Woodlands, our farm prospered. Flocks and herds provided mutton, beef, and veal. My wife’s dairy was nearly too busy for her to manage alone.

Our boys still delighted in naming animals. There was Blanche, a creamy-white cow; Stentor, a bull whose bellow could shake the trees; Arrow and Dart, two swift onagers; and Coco, a jackal pup descended from Jack’s old favorite Fangs. Jack insisted that no word could be heard clearly without an "o"—a theory he repeated until we begged him to stop.

Health had been generous to us all during these years. Only occasionally did my wife suffer from mild fevers, and the boys had minor mishaps, nothing serious.

They had grown into fine young men. Fritz, twenty-four, was strong, active, and full of spirit—moderate in height but powerful in build. Ernest, two years younger, was tall and slender, thoughtful and calm, his earlier tendencies toward laziness and selfishness now largely overcome. He had refined tastes and sharp intellect.

Jack, at twenty, looked much like Fritz—though lighter in frame—distinguished by grace and agility rather than brute strength. Franz, seventeen, combined traits of all his brothers—quick-witted, clever, but lacking Jack’s mischievous charm.

All were honest, godly, respectful to their parents, and deeply devoted to one another.

Though we lived in complete isolation, I never lost hope that one day we might return to human society. But as time carried our sons into manhood, it also advanced their parents toward age. Thoughts of what would happen if we were left behind—alone—weighed heavily on my heart.

Yet I never shared these fears with my family. Instead, I turned to prayer, laying my worries before God, and finding strength renewed with each petition.

My older sons often ventured off on long excursions, returning only hours later—unannounced and unharmed. Their joy upon return always eased any anxiety I might have felt, and any rebuke died on my lips.

One day, Fritz had been gone from Rockburg all day. It wasn’t until evening that we noticed his cajack was missing—he must be out at sea.

Anxious to see him back before nightfall, I set off with Ernest and Jack for Shark Island, where we climbed the watchtower. We raised the signal flag and loaded the cannon.

For a long time, we scanned the ocean, glittering under the low sun. Then, far off, a tiny black speck appeared. Through the telescope, we saw it was Fritz’s skiff.

I noted it was moving slower than usual. The cannon fired—a signal he could see—and we hurried back to meet him at the harbor.

As he neared, it was clear why he was delayed. His cajack towed a large sack and carried a heavy load.

"Welcome, Fritz!" I called. "Welcome back, wherever you’ve been! You seem to have quite a cargo!"

"Yes," he replied, "and my journey led to discoveries—both treasures and secrets. Come, let’s carry these things up, and while I rest, I’ll tell you all about it."

We gathered around him as he began.

"I know my absence without permission deserves reproach," he said, "but ever since I got the cajack, I’ve dreamed of exploring the coast beyond the point where I killed the walrus.

So I prepared carefully—provisions, compass fixed before my seat, rifle, harpoon, axe, boat-hook, fishing net—all arranged. And I took Pounce, my eagle. I always will, from now on.

This morning dawned perfectly—calm sea, gentle breeze. I slipped out of the harbor unnoticed. The current carried me swiftly, and I rounded the point, passing over the spot where our wreck lies beneath the waves. And do you know? Through the clear water, I could actually see the guns, cannonballs, iron parts—everything indestructible—scattered on the rocky bottom.

Then I sailed past jagged cliffs and rocks rising from the sea. Sea birds nested in the most inaccessible places. On lower ridges, seals, sea-bears, and walruses basked in the sun, plunged into the water, or hauled themselves up with their tusks.

I admit, I didn’t feel entirely safe. I tried to pass quickly, quietly. It took over an hour and a half to clear the reefs and shoals. Then I came to a high, steep cape jutting far into the sea. Directly opposite, in the rock face, was a magnificent archway—a grand entrance to a vast cave.

I passed beneath it and explored inside.

It was filled with tiny swallows—smaller than wrens. Their nests covered the walls, each resting on a spoon-shaped platform. I collected several. They looked strange—fibrous, gelatinous, almost like coral or fungi. I brought them back in my fishing net."

"If we traded with China," I said, "your find would be valuable. These are edible birds’ nests. The bird is called the esculent swallow. The trade is huge—nests worth their weight in silver when fresh and white. In Java, people risk their lives to harvest them. Over fifty thousand pounds are gathered yearly—worth more than £200,000.

When soaked in water, they swell and soften, then make a nourishing soup. You should try cooking one, mother, just for curiosity."

"I don’t care much for their look," she said, "but I’ll try if they turn to jelly. Though boiling birds’ nests isn一条龙 out of my usual cookery."

"Oh, please, mother!" laughed Jack. "Let’s taste them—though just thinking about it makes me imagine feathers in my mouth!"

"It’s truly fascinating," said Fritz. "Where do these swallows get the gelatin?"

"We’re not sure," I replied. "But it seems glands in their bodies produce a sticky secretion that forms the nest."

After collecting the nests, Fritz continued:

"I went deeper into the cavern. It curved and opened into a quiet bay—so still and sheltered, I thought it must be nearly landlocked. Beyond the rocky entrance, fertile plains stretched toward what looked like a river mouth. Beyond that, marshy ground and a thick cedar forest blocked the view.

The water was crystal-clear. Looking down, I saw beds of shellfish—large oysters—attached to rocks by hairy filaments.

‘If these are oysters,’ I thought, ‘they must be bigger than ours in Safety Bay.’ So I hooked up several clusters and landed on the beach, planning to return for more and tow them behind me.

But the sun was hot. When I came back, the shells were gaping open. I examined them—thought maybe they weren’t delicate at all.

I needed a knife. As I cut into one, I found smooth, round, pearly balls—like peas—of different sizes. Could they be pearls?

Here, I have a box full."

"Oh, show them!" the boys cried. "Look at them—shining, round, glowing!"

"You’ve found treasure!" I exclaimed. "These are real pearls! Worthless now, perhaps—but if we ever return to civilization, they might bring fortune. We must visit your oyster beds soon."

Fritz continued:

"After eating, I resumed my journey, dragging the bag of shellfish. I reached the other side of the bay, enclosed by a headland matching the one I entered through. Between them lay reefs and sandbars—with only one narrow channel to the open sea. So Pearl Bay, as I named it, is completely protected.

The tide was coming in strongly, so I couldn’t cross yet. I searched the cliffs for another archway—but found none, only countless sea birds—from gulls to albatrosses.

They saw me as an intruder. They circled overhead, screaming. I grew impatient and struck wildly with the boat-hook. One blow hit an albatross—it fell stunned into the water.

Then I tried crossing the reef again. I succeeded, and soon found myself in open sea. I saw our flag flying and heard your salute. I was home."

His story ended. But the next morning, Fritz pulled me aside.

"There was something extraordinary about that albatross, father," he said. "I told you I left it, but I didn’t. I carried it onto the canoe. Then I saw a rag tied around its leg. I removed it—and found English words written on it: *Save an unfortunate Englishwoman from the smoking rock!*"

A chill ran through me. "Is this real?" I whispered. "Could someone else be alive here?"

He paused. "I was stunned. But the bird stirred. I tore a strip from my handkerchief and wrote: *Do not despair! Help is near!* I tied it to its leg, replaced the rag, and cared for it. It revived, drank, and flew away—westward."

"Now, father," he said, eyes burning, "I wonder—will my message reach her? Can I find her? Save her?"

I listened, heart pounding.

"My dear son," I said, "you’ve done wisely to tell me alone. Until we know more, we must not alarm the others. This note may be very old. The 'smoking rock' likely means a volcano—but there are none here. Perhaps the woman is already gone."

Fritz wouldn’t accept that. He believed the rag was recent. Smoke could rise from non-volcanic rock. He clung to hope.

I shared his longing, but feared false expectations. After discussion, we agreed: Fritz would search for the woman—but only after modifying his canoe to carry two and stockpiling supplies for a long trip.

He accepted the delay, though restless.

That evening, the boys eagerly opened the oysters, thrilled each time a pearl emerged.

"Can we start a pearl fishery right away, father?" they shouted. "We could build a hut on the shore!"

Soon, everyone was preparing for the trip to Pearl Bay. It would serve as a base for Fritz’s greater mission—one he kept secret.

One day, I spoke seriously to the family:

"I’ve been thinking, dear wife. Our eldest son is now mature enough to act independently. From now on, I will allow him full freedom in his decisions—especially regarding voyages. He need not fear alarming us if he stays away longer than expected. I trust his judgment and his love for us. He will never cause us needless worry."

Fritz looked at me with deep gratitude. His mother embraced him.

"God bless and protect you, my boy," she said, voice trembling.

It took time to build oyster-scraping tools I designed. Meanwhile, Fritz redesigned his canoe with a second seat.

His brothers assumed he’d take one of them along. He let them believe it.

They busied themselves making useful gear and waited patiently.

Finally, the day came. I bid farewell to my wife and Franz, then boarded the yacht with some dogs. Jack proudly sat beside Fritz in the canoe, sharing the honor of leading the way.

We passed safely through the reefs near Walrus Island into calm waters, shielded by cliffs. The sea shone like glass. For the first time, we saw paper-nautilus—delicate, fairy-like shells floating gently.

We couldn’t resist chasing them. Soon, six were caught and handed to us. We preserved them for the museum. That spot became Nautilus Creek.

Beyond, we rounded a flat promontory with a sharp rock at its tip—Cape Pug-Nose. Ahead rose grand cliffs stretching into the sea.

I expected to sail around them. But our pilots held course. Soon, we saw the great archway—our passage into Pearl Bay.

Its pillars, arches, and spires reminded me of a Gothic cathedral. I named it Cape Minster.

A cloud of little swallows darted from the cave mouth, swirling, dividing, then returning swiftly to their nests.

We collected several nests, careful not to disturb those with eggs. Fritz and Jack climbed the broken rocks to reach the highest ones.

The tide helped us glide through the tunnel—its arches and columns forming aisles and chapels in dim, sacred light.

Emerging into sunlight, we floated in the calm expanse of Pearl Bay.

It was even more beautiful than Fritz had said. Romantic islets dotted the water, like smiles on a perfect face.

We cruised, scouting for a landing near the oyster beds. We found a spot beside a sparkling streamlet.

As dusk fell, we lit a watchfire, ate a quick supper, and returned to the yacht—anchoring close to shore.

The dogs and Coco, the jackal, stayed on land.

That night, under stars, we slept aboard, waiting for the dawn—and for what lay ahead.

My wife and Franz were both startled by Fritz’s unexpected absence, but overjoyed to see us return safely. They listened eagerly as we recounted our adventures. My wife shuddered when she heard about our close encounter with the lion and his mate—she could barely suppress a cry at the thought of how near we had come to disaster. Jack’s narrow escape also filled her with fear; he still looked pale, and she could hardly believe he was unharmed.

Tears welled up in Franz’s eyes when he heard of Juno’s death. He asked tenderly if her remains had been brought back so they could be buried near the house where she had lived for so many years. The next day, he oversaw her careful burial. At his request, Ernest composed an epitaph, which was carved onto a stone slab above her grave:

*A servant true lies here:
A faithful friend,
A Dog,
To all most dear;
Who met her end
Fighting right bravely in her master's cause.*

The wild boar meat and truffles were handed over to my wife, who welcomed them with delight, promising delicious meals ahead. She would have liked to take the boar’s head too, but I had promised Ernest it would go to his museum. Though my mouth watered at the thought of roasting it Hottentot-style, I kept my word. So the magnificent head, along with the lions’ hides, was carried to the tannery on Whale Island, where they were cleaned and preserved.

Five days passed, yet Fritz remained missing. My concern grew, and finally I decided to go after him. Everyone supported the idea—even my wife agreed to join us when she learned we’d sail in the pinnace.

We loaded the boat, and on a bright morning with a favorable wind, the five of us, along with the dogs, set off aboard the little yacht toward Cape Minster.

Our small vessel danced across the waves, the sun shining brightly and the sea breeze lifting our spirits. We approached the archway, and I steered steadily forward—then suddenly, just ahead, I saw a dark shape beneath the surface.

“A sunken rock,” I thought. “But why haven’t I noticed it before?”

I turned the helm sharply—but it was too late. We surged forward. A jolt, and then silence. The danger was past.

I glanced behind, expecting to see the rock again—but it was gone. Where moments ago there had been a deep green shadow, now there was nothing.

Before we could recover, Jack shouted, “Father! Another one—starboard!”

Sure enough, another dark mass appeared—this time moving.

“The rock is moving!” cried Franz.

Then, with a mighty splash, a colossal black form rose from the sea. Water erupted from its back in a thunderous column, falling like rain around us. In that instant, I recognized the beast: a cachalot whale, enormous and fierce.

It seemed enraged by the scratches on its back. Retreating slightly, it prepared to charge.

I remembered stories of these whales destroying boats and even sinking ships. With a sense of urgency, I grabbed one of the guns. Jack leapt for the other. We fired almost at the same moment.

Both shots struck. The whale thrashed violently, then plunged beneath the waves—disappearing.

We stayed alert, not wanting to lose such a prize. Reloading quickly, we sailed toward the shore, where the whale seemed to be heading. Soon we spotted it again—near the shallows, lashing its tail wildly, staining the water with blood.

We approached cautiously and fired once more.

For a moment, the creature fought harder than ever—then, with a final tremor from head to tail, it lay still. Dead.

The boys were about to cheer—but held back. From behind a rock, they saw a canoe paddled by a tall, muscular native. He stood upright, staring at us intently. Seeing us approach, he vanished behind the rock.

A chilling thought struck me: There must be a tribe of natives here—and Fritz might be in their hands.

But we would not be taken easily. We loaded our guns and readied ourselves.

Soon, a dark face peered down from a high rock. Then another, lower down. The canoe reappeared, gliding out to spy again.

All of us—Jack included—looked anxiously to me for orders.

“Hoist a white flag,” I said, “and give me the speaking trumpet.”

I took it and spoke in Malay, using the peaceful words I could recall. But neither the flag nor my words made any difference. The man turned to leave.

Jack lost patience. Grabbing the trumpet, he yelled, “Come here, you black son of a gun! Come on board and make friends—or we’ll blow you and your—”

“Stop! Stop!” I warned. “You’ll only frighten him with those wild words!”

“No! Look!” he insisted. “He’s coming toward us!”

And indeed, the canoe was swiftly approaching.

Then Franz screamed, “Look! Look! It’s Fritz’s cajack! I can see the walrus head!”

Only Ernest remained calm. He took the trumpet and called out, “Fritz, ahoy! Welcome, old fellow!”

The moment the words left his lips, I recognized the familiar face beneath the darkened skin.

In seconds, the brave boy was aboard, greeted with hugs and kisses despite his sooty appearance. Questions flew from every side: Where had he been? Why had he been gone so long? And why did he look like a savage?

“The last question,” Fritz replied with a smile, “is the only one I’ll answer now. The rest will wait until I tell my full story.” He explained, “When I heard gunfire, I thought of Malay pirates—I never imagined you’d be here in the yacht. So I disguised myself, came to investigate. When you spoke Malay, I only grew more afraid—you confirmed I was dealing with pirates.”

After we told him about the whale, I asked if he knew a safe place to anchor the yacht.

“Of course,” he said, giving me a knowing glance. “I can show you an island with perfect anchorage—and it’s worth seeing. It holds all sorts of wonders.” After washing off his disguise, he guided us in his canoe to a lovely little island in the bay.

Now that Fritz’s mission had clearly succeeded, I no longer hesitated to tell my wife about his plan. She was deeply moved—almost overwhelmed—by the idea of meeting another human being, especially a woman.

“But why didn’t you tell me earlier?” she asked. “Why wait until the last moment with such wonderful news?”

“I didn’t want to raise hopes that might never come true,” I said. “But now, thank God, he’s found someone—and there’s no need to hide anything.”

The boys couldn’t understand the quiet excitement between us, or why their mother, Fritz, and I were acting so mysteriously. They stared curiously at the island. As soon as the sails were furled and the anchor dropped, they raced ashore. We followed, keeping silent.

Emerging from the thicket, we saw a hut made of branches, with a cheerful fire burning at the entrance.

Fritz slipped inside, leaving his brothers stunned. Moments later, he emerged, holding the hand of a slender, handsome youth dressed like a young English naval officer. Together, they stepped forward.

Fritz, glowing with joy, introduced him as Edward Montrose.

“And,” he added, turning to us, “won’t you welcome him as a friend—and a brother—to our family?”

“Indeed we will!” I exclaimed, stepping forward and extending my hands. “Our life in the wild may have roughened our looks and ways—but not our hearts.”

My wife embraced the young stranger warmly. The boys and even the dogs welcomed him eagerly—the boys thrilled at having a new companion, the dogs drawn to her gentle voice and kind presence.

From Fritz’s words, I realized the girl wished to keep her true identity hidden until my wife could find her proper clothing.

The boys ran to the yacht to bring supplies for supper and prepare a camp. Meanwhile, my wife served a hearty meal. The boys tried hard to make their new friend feel at home. She warmed to them quickly, joining in their games. By supper, she was laughing and chatting as freely as anyone.

She admired our food, tasted our mead, and carried on a lively conversation—never mentioning her past.

Just meeting another person after so many years was thrilling—but that this person should be so beautiful, so cheerful, so charming—well, it nearly drove the boys mad with excitement.

When I signaled the end of the feast, and the new guest was led to her quarters on the yacht, the boys raised a toast to Edward Montrose, drinking fragrant me3ad amid cheers and applause.

Once she was gone and silence returned, Jack leaned forward.

“Well, Fritz,” he said, “now tell us—where did you find this jolly fellow? Did you search for him, or did you stumble upon him by chance? Out with it—while we sit by the fire.”

He tossed more wood onto the blaze and settled down comfortably.

After a pause, Fritz began: “Remember how, on my last trip in the cajack, I shot an albatross? Only Father knew what happened to it. But that very bird led me to the shipwrecked stranger—and I decided it should carry back a message to comfort the one who sent it.”

“I prepared the cajack to hold two people. Then, full of hope, I left you and the yacht, with Pounce sitting before me, and paddled into the open sea. For hours I paddled steadily, until the wind picked up. I decided to stay closer to shore—just in case a storm broke out.”

“It was wise. No sooner had I reached a quiet cove than the sea turned into a fury—waves crashed everywhere. Even in the sheltered bay, I felt danger.”

“I spent the night in the cajack. Next morning, after eating pemmican and drinking from my flask, I ventured out again. The wind had calmed. The sea was smooth. I scanned every direction—searching for smoke, signs of life—until noon.”

“The coastline changed. Sandy shores gave way to dense forests. From within, I heard the roar of beasts, the shriek of apes, the laugh of hyenas, the dying cry of deer. Never had I felt so alone—knowing I was the only human in sight.”

“I rested my paddle and let the cajack drift.”

“Near the shore, I saw strange birds—large, odd-looking creatures. They fluttered around me, then darted back to feed on what looked like pepper plants. They caught berries in their massive beaks, threw them into the air, and caught them again. Their beaks were astonishing—so heavy they must have caused constant pain. But their colors were dazzling, rivaling their plumage.”

“I wish I’d brought one back. But I was too captivated by their antics to think of it. I concluded they were toucans—was I right, Ernest?”

The Professor nodded silently.

“For hours I paddled on, passing rivers and streams. If I’d been exploring, I might have followed one. But now, I had only one goal.”

“Night fell. I moored the cajaka in a sheltered cove and went ashore. You can imagine how good it felt to stretch my legs after so long cramped in the canoe.”

“But I couldn’t sleep on land. After supper, I returned to the cajack and spent the night.”

“Next morning, Pounce and I landed for breakfast. I lit a fire and roasted a plump parrot. Suddenly, I heard rustling in the grass. I turned—and there, eyes blazing, tail swaying, stood a huge tiger.”

“In another second, it would have pounced. I’d have been dead—and our guest doomed to years more of loneliness.”

“My gun was beside me—but I couldn’t reach it in time.”

“Pounce saw the danger. The brave bird lunged at the tiger, blinding it with flapping wings and sharp beak blows. That stopped the spring—giving me time to grab the gun and fire.”

“The beast fell—pierced through the heart. But beside it—poor Pounce—crushed and lifeless. One blow from the tiger’s paw had ended him.”

Fritz’s voice cracked. He paused, then continued quickly.

“I buried Pounce where he died. Then, unable to stay, I returned to the cajack and paddled away, leaving the tiger where it fell.”

“My heart sank. Without my companion, I doubted I could go on. The albatross might have flown hundreds of miles. The stranger might be far away. Every stroke of my paddle could be taking me farther from the signal fire.”

“But my despair didn’t last long. Suddenly, I saw something that banished all fear.”

“A headland loomed ahead. Around it, I found a calm, wooded bay with a rocky reef. From the tip of the reef rose a steady column of smoke.”

“I couldn’t believe my eyes. I stood frozen, then, pulse racing, seized my paddle and rowed as fast as I could.”

“Few strokes carried me across. I secured the canoe and climbed the rock—no sign of anyone.”

“I was about to shout, when I saw a small figure walking along the rocks toward me.”

“You can imagine how I felt.”

“I stepped forward, controlling my emotions, and said in English: ‘Welcome, fair stranger! God has heard your call and sent me to help!’”

“Miss Montrose came forward—”

“Who? What?” the boys shouted. “Who came forward?” Amid chaos, Ernest stood calmly and said, “I didn’t want to say anything until you revealed it, Fritz—but we’ve known all along that Edward Montrose was a disguise.”

Fritz, surprised, regained composure and laughed off the jokes. Then, with three cheers for their new sister, he resumed.

“Miss Montrose grasped my hands warmly. Guessing from my accent I wasn’t used to English daily, she spoke in French: ‘Long have I waited since the bird returned with your message. Thank God, you’ve come at last!’”

“Tears of joy streamed down her face as she led me to the shore, where she’d built a small hut among tree branches—like Falconhurst, but smaller. I was amazed by everything she showed me. Her hut was a marvel—sturdy posts, bamboo beams, reed walls, palm-thatched roof, and clay plaster. A hole in the roof let smoke escape.”

“Inside, a cormorant flew at me, growling. She called it off and explained she’d captured it soon after landing—and trained it to fetch food—fish, birds, rabbits—whatever it could catch. It now brought her everything she needed.”

“Before dark, she showed me all her tools—fishing nets, bone needles, shell plates, knives, hooks, and a chest of clothes—almost all that survived the wreck three years ago.”

“She told me her story: Jenny Montrose, daughter of a British officer born in India. Her mother died when she was three. Her father devoted himself entirely to her—teaching her skills, manners, and love for nature. By seventeen, she rode horses as well as she danced.”

“Her father received orders to return to England. Not wanting her to travel with the troops, he arranged passage for her on the *Dorcas*.”

“They parted sadly. A week after leaving Calcutta, a storm drove the ship off course. More storms followed. Leaks developed. The crew abandoned ship.”

“Jenny got into one of the largest boats. After days at sea, land appeared. The other boats vanished. They tried to land—but the boat capsized. Only she reached shore.”

“For a long time, she lay unconscious. Slowly, she revived, ate shellfish, and regained strength.”

“And so,” Fritz concluded, “she lived here alone—until today.”

Long winter evenings passed in our cozy study, filled with stories told by the boys and Jenny as we drew, wove, and plaited beneath the soft glow of candlelight. It was a season of quiet joy, so peaceful that when spring finally returned—when the rain ceased and the sun warmed the earth once more—we could hardly believe we had been confined within our rocky home for so long.

Now life burst into motion again. The fields, garden, and orchard called to the boys, while their mother and sister busied themselves with poultry and household tasks. Our scattered settlements—Falconhurst, Woodlands, Prospect Hill, Shark Island, Whale Island—each needed attention. Jack and Franz were assigned the task of tending to the island battery.

They spent the entire day repairing the flagstaff, hoisting the flag, and preparing the two cannons. As evening approached, the rest of us strolled along the beach, enjoying the cool sea breeze. We watched as the boys finished their work: they loaded the guns, launched a small tub in the cajack, and floated it out as a target. Then they fired—crash!—and the barrel shattered. With shouts of triumph, they cleaned the guns and pulled them back into place.

No sooner had they done than three sharp gunshots echoed across the water from the west.

We froze. Was it real? A ship? Or had the boys fired again? No—it was clearly not them. Jack and Franz leapt into their canoe and paddled toward us at full speed, having heard the sound too.

A storm of emotions swept through us—fear, hope, doubt, joy—all clashing together. Could this be a European vessel near our shores? Would we soon reconnect with civilization? Or was it a pirate ship, ready to attack? What kind of people would we meet—friends, enemies, or desperate souls in need?

Before anyone could speak, the canoe reached shore. “Did you hear them?” Jack gasped. “What do we do?”

“Father!” cried Fritz, his eyes shining. “It must be an English ship. We’ll find her. We’ll see England again!” He seized his brother’s hands, overwhelmed with emotion.

Until that moment, I hadn’t realized how deeply the presence of their sister, now grown into a young woman, had stirred their longing for home.

All eyes turned to me. What should we do?

“We can do nothing tonight,” I said. “Night is falling. Let’s prepare what we can—and pray.”

Back at Rockburg, excitement buzzed like bees in a hive. No one could sleep. Jack and I took turns watching from the veranda, listening for more signals or signs of danger. But around midnight, the wind began to rise. By morning, a fierce storm raged across the bay. Waves crashed violently against the cliffs. Even if another ship had been nearby, we wouldn’t have heard a shot over the roar of the gale.

For two days and nights, the hurricane pounded the coast. On the third day, the sun broke through. The wind calmed, and the sea subsided. Still anxious, I agreed with the boys’ wish to go to Shark Island and fire the guns again. Perhaps the ship had run aground—or perhaps it had fled into open waters. We couldn’t know.

So I went with Jack and Franz to the fort. One… two… we fired. We waited.

Minutes passed. Then—a distant reply rolled across the water.

There was no doubt now. The strangers were still there. We waved the flag to signal safety, then hurried back.

The family was in a frenzy. I tried to calm them, though I could not answer their questions. I couldn’t hide the truth: the ship might not bring good news.

Fritz and I decided to scout the area. We armed ourselves with guns, pistols, cutlasses, and took a spyglass. In the cajack, we paddled out of the bay, past the high cliffs, heading toward where the shots had come from.

For nearly an hour, we saw only jagged rocks and churning waves. Then we reached Cape Pug-Nose and began rounding the point.

In an instant, all fear vanished. Joy flooded our hearts. There, tucked in a sheltered cove beyond the cape, lay a brig-of-war—her sails furled, anchor dropped, the British flag flying proudly.

Through the glass, I saw figures on deck and tents set up on the shore, smoke curling from fires among the trees. But a sudden unease gripped me. Why was an English ship here, far from known routes? Could it be mutineers hiding out, disguised and ready to raid?

Then Fritz exclaimed, “Father! I see the captain—he’s speaking to an officer. His face is clear. He’s English! I’m sure of it!”

He handed me the glass. I looked. The ship was neat, orderly. Decks gleamed. Brass and steel shone. Discipline reigned. My fears melted away.

We studied the scene carefully, then decided not to reveal ourselves yet. We didn’t want to appear as ragged castaways, but as masters of our land, ready to greet visitors on equal terms.

We returned to Rockburg, where the family waited anxiously. We told them everything. They approved our caution—even Jenny, whose heart had soared at the thought of seeing Englishmen again, agreed to wait until tomorrow.

That day, we prepared. Our little yacht was scrubbed clean, her brass polished, her decks cleared of clutter. The English flag flew proudly from her peak.

My wife sorted through our clothes. Neat uniforms were laid out for the boys and me. Though we had lived simply—wearing wide-brimmed hats, linen shirts, leather belts, and sturdy boots—we knew such attire might shock the sailors. So we dressed properly for the occasion.

At dawn, we gathered in the breakfast room. The meal was eaten quietly, our minds too full for words. Afterward, Fritz and Jack brought baskets of fresh fruit—bananas, oranges, pineapples—from our gardens. These we carried aboard the yacht as gifts.

We weighed anchor. Sails unfurled. The little boat danced over the water, towing the canoe behind. We rounded the Reef, avoided its dangerous rocks, and headed toward the cove.

When we rounded the cape, the strangers were stunned. Here, in this wild, inhospitable land, came a sleek pleasure yacht, manned by a family of settlers, sailing with dignity and grace.

Fritz and I rowed to the brig. The captain greeted us warmly, leading us into his cabin.

“Tell me,” he said, “how did you come to live here? This coast is supposed to be deserted—or ruled by savages.”

I told him our story—the wreck, our survival, our life on the island, and how we had rescued Miss Montrose.

Then he rose, grasping Fritz’s hand. “Let me thank you, on behalf of Colonel Montrose. He has not given up hope of finding his daughter. Three weeks ago, survivors from the *Dorcas* told me of four boats leaving the wreck—only one reached land. I came searching. And now—I’ve found her.”

Fritz humbly accepted the praise. Then the captain asked to meet my wife and Jenny.

“And,” he added, “if it’s not against your rules, I’d like to send a boat for the rest of your party.”

An officer was dispatched. Soon, my wife, Jenny, and the other boys arrived aboard.

The crew welcomed us warmly. Officers vied to honor us. They were gracious hosts. At luncheon, the captain mentioned that he had sailed from Sydney with Mr. Wolston—an invalid gentleman—his wife, and two daughters. The voyage had not helped Mr. Wolston’s health. The storm had worsened his condition, and he longed to rest on land.

We were eager to meet them. That afternoon, we visited their camp under the trees. Mr. Wolston sat by a tent, enjoying the breeze. When we arrived, he and his family were delighted.

We stayed late into the evening. It was too dark to return to Rockburg, so the captain kindly offered tents. The boys slept ashore.

That night, I had a long talk with my wife. Should we return to Europe? It would be foolish to leave just because a chance arose.

We both hesitated—afraid to voice our true feelings. But slowly, we realized neither truly wished to leave. Our hearts belonged to New Switzerland.

She told me she wanted to stay, as long as I and at least two sons chose to remain. If others left, she would accept it—but only if they promised to send skilled emigrants to help build a colony. She suggested the island keep its name, even if future settlers came from England.

I agreed wholeheartedly. We would discuss it with Captain Littlestone, and ask that the island fall under British protection.

Then came the question: who would stay, and who would go?

We decided to let the boys choose.

After breakfast, it was proposed that the captain bring his ship into Safety Bay, so we could host a visit at Rockburg. We invited Mr. Wolston and his family, hoping the change would improve his health.

No sooner was the plan made than Fritz and Jack raced off in the canoe to prepare. The brig and our yacht followed.

When our guests rounded the Rocky Cape, their amazement knew no bounds. Before them lay Safety Bay—our beautiful estate, Rockburg.

And then—eleven cannon boomed from Shark Island. The Union Jack fluttered proudly in the wind.

Faces lit up with wonder and joy. Poor Wolston seemed to revive at the sight of peace and comfort.

He was carried ashore gently and settled into my room. A camp bed was added for Mrs. Wolston, so she could care for him.

At the harbor, excitement ran wild. Guests explored every corner of our home. Dinner was barely noticed.

But later, when a tour of Falconhurst was planned, everyone finally sat down. Yet even at supper, the young ones wandered through lawns and gardens, making the place seem thronged with visitors.

By evening, calm returned. Mr. Wolston joined us, stronger and more hopeful. He declared his desire to stay, at least for a time. His wife and eldest daughter—both fragile in health—would remain. The younger daughter would go to her brother at the Cape.

If he chose to settle permanently, he would send his son to join us.

I welcomed the idea with great joy. “New Switzerland will be our home,” I said.

“Hurrah for New Switzerland!” the company shouted, raising glasses in a joyful toast. “Prosperity to New Switzerland! Long may she flourish!”

Ernest surprised us all. “Long life and happiness to those who make New Switzerland their home!” he said, ringing his glass with mine, my wife’s, and Mr. Wolston’s.

Jenny smiled. “Won’t someone cheer for England and Colonel Montrose?” she asked playfully. “I miss my father, but I’ll feel torn if all the cheers are for staying!”

Fritz laughed. “Three cheers for England! Success and happiness to those returning to Europe!” The hall rang with cheers.

Jenny looked at him gratefully—knowing he understood her longing.

I spoke. “Since Fritz chooses England, he shall carry this dear daughter back to her father—whom I’ve loved as my own. Ernest stays with me. His mother and I rejoice. He shall have every scientific post we can offer.”

Then I asked Jack. “You’re a fine comic actor. You must go to Europe to shine on the stage.”

“No,” Jack replied. “I’m staying. When Fritz leaves, I’ll be the best rider and sharpest shot in New Switzerland. That’s enough for me. I’m afraid of European schools—I’d get caught and forced into one!”

Franz stepped forward. “I want a good school. Among students, there’s competition, progress. I can rise. Fritz may return someday. But one of us should go home—and stay. I’m youngest. I can adapt better. But Father will decide.”

“You may go,” I said. “God bless all our plans. The world belongs to the Lord. Wherever you live faithfully, there is your home.”

Then I turned to Captain Littlestone. “Will you help them fulfill their choices?”

He paused. Then spoke with conviction:

“My orders were to search for survivors. Two wrecks found. Three passengers wish to stay. Three wish to go. It fits perfectly. I gladly take charge of those entrusted to me. Everything has been ordered by Providence. If England gains a thriving colony, it will be the perfect ending to this chain of blessings. Three cheers for New Switzerland!”

Emotion swelled in every heart. Many felt they stood at the edge of a new life. For me, a heavy burden lifted. I thanked God for resolving a worry that had haunted me for years.

From that moment, all thoughts turned to preparations. The departure was brief, but we worked tirelessly. Every comfort was packed—clothes, books, tools. A rich cargo of pearls, corals, furs, spices—enough to give our children a strong start in England.

I gave them private papers, money, and jewels belonging to our ship’s captain—asking them to deliver them to his heirs if possible. I also gave Captain Littlestone a short account of the wreck, a list of the crew.

Our gratitude overflowed. We supplied the *Unicorn* with fresh food, fish, vegetables, and fruit—anything we could spare.

In a long talk with my sons, I warned them of life’s dangers—temptations of wealth, pride, ambition. I urged them to hold fast to faith, duty, and love.

And so, with hearts full of hope and farewell, we watched as the ship set sail—carrying part of our family back to the world they once knew, while we remained, rooted in the soil we had built into a new home.
