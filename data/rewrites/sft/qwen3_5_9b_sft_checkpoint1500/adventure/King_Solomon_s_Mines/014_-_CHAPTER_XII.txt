Fortune, it seemed, was smiling upon our escape. Infadoos and the local chiefs possessed an intimate knowledge of the great town's labyrinthine paths, guiding us sideways through the shadows until we remained unmolested. Despite the oppressive gloom, we pressed forward with steady momentum.

For an hour or more we traversed the darkened landscape, until finally, the eclipse began to wane. The severed edge of the moon, previously hidden, re-emerged. Suddenly, as if summoned by the heavens, a silver streak erupted from it, trailed by a wondrous, ruddy glow. This celestial lamp hung suspended in the blackness, casting a wild and breathtaking radiance. Within five minutes, the stars began to fade, dissolving into the morning light, revealing our surroundings with startling clarity.

We had cleared the town of Loo and were now ascending a large, flat-topped hill. Common to the terrain of South Africa, it was not particularly lofty—its highest point rising merely two hundred feet—but it was shaped like a horseshoe, its sides precipitous and strewn with massive boulders. Upon the grassy plateau at its summit lay ample grounds, formerly utilized as a formidable military cantonment. While its usual garrison numbered a single regiment of three thousand men, our ascent revealed a different reality: several regiments were encamped there, swarming the slopes in the returning moonlight.

Upon reaching the table-land, we found chaos. Crowds of men, roused from their slumber, trembled with fear, huddled together in utter consternation at the unnatural phenomenon they had just witnessed. Passing silently through the throng, we reached a central hut where we were stunned to find two men awaiting us, burdened with our abandoned effects.

"I dispatched them," Infadoos explained, gesturing to the piles of goods. "And also for these," he added, lifting up Good's long-lost trousers.

With an exclamation of rapturous joy, Good lunged for them, instantly donning the garment.

"Surely, my lord, will not conceal his beautiful white legs?" Infadoos lamented, bowing slightly.

Good, however, was a man of profound modesty. Once clad, he refused to remove them, granting the Kukuana people but a fleeting glimpse of his limbs before turning his attention back to his whisker, his transparent eye, and his movable teeth. Yet, as Infadoos gazed with affection at the trousers, he announced his next plan. He had ordered all regiments to assemble at daybreak. Their purpose was twofold: to explain the origins of the rebellion decreed by the chiefs and to formally introduce the rightful heir to the throne, Ignosi.

True to his word, when the sun climbed above the horizon, twenty thousand men—the flower of the Kukuana army—gathered on a vast open plain. Drawn up in three sides of a dense, magnificent square, they awaited our arrival. We took our station on the open flank and were immediately encircled by the principal chiefs and officers.

In silence, Infadoos addressed the assembly. Born an orator, he spoke in vigorous, graceful tones, recounting the tragic history of Ignosi's father, who had been basely murdered by King Twala, leaving his wife and child to starve. He painted a harrowing picture of the people's suffering under Twala's cruelty, citing the previous night's atrocities where nobles, falsely accused, had been dragged out and executed. Then, shifting the tone, he declared that the White Lords from the Stars, perceiving the nation's distress, had undertaken a perilous journey to intervene. They had seized the exiled Ignosi, led him across the mountains, and, exercising their high magic, extinguished the moon and slain the young fiend Scragga—a sacrifice made to save the girl Foulata and to signal the wavering hearts. They stood ready, he proclaimed, to aid the people in overthrowing Twala and restoring the rightful king.

His discourse ended in a murmur of approval. Then Ignosi stepped forward. Reiterating his uncle's words, he delivered a powerful appeal to the assembly.

"Chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people!" Ignosi cried, his voice ringing with authority. "You have heard my words. You must now choose between me and the usurper who sits upon my throne—the uncle who slaughtered his brother and drove his nephew's child to perish in the cold and dark. These"—he pointed to the surrounding chiefs—"know me as the true king. They have seen the snake coiled about my middle. Would these White men, with all their power, stand by a false king? Tremble, therefore! Is not the darkness they wrought upon the land to confound Twala and veil our flight, a darkness falling even in the hour of the full moon, right before your eyes?"

"It is!" the soldiers roared in unison.

"I am the king!" Ignosi shouted, straightening his towering frame and raising his broad-bladed battle-axe high above his head. "If any man among you denies this truth, let him step forward! I will strike him down this instant, and his blood shall be the crimson proof of my honesty!" He shook the great axe, making it flash in the sunlight.

No one dared to rise. Our former henchman, buoyed by this silent acquiescence, continued. "I am indeed the king. Stand by my side in battle, and if I win, you share my victory and honor. I will grant you oxen and wives, and you shall replace all existing regiments. If I fall, I fall with you."

"And I swear to you," Ignosi vowed, "when I sit upon the seat of my ancestors, bloodshed shall cease. Justice shall walk blindfold through the land. No man shall die except for offending the law. The devouring of your kraals ends today. Each of you shall sleep securely within your own hut, fearing nothing. Have you chosen, chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people?"

"We have chosen, O King!" the answer echoed back.

"It is settled. Turn your gaze and see how Twala's messengers rush forth from the great town, east, west, north, and south, gathering a mighty host to slay me and my protectors. Tomorrow, or perhaps sooner, he will bring his entire army against us. Then I shall see who truly stands by me, the man who fears not to die for his cause. Remember, he shall not be forgotten in the spoils of war."

With these final words, Ignosi dismissed the crowd. "Go to your huts and prepare for war."

A moment of silence fell, broken when a chief raised his hand and the royal salute rang out: *"Koom!"* It was the final seal of acceptance. The soldiers marched off in disciplined battalions.

An hour later, we convened a council of war attended by all regiment commanders. It was evident to us that an overwhelming assault was imminent. From our vantage point atop the hill, we could see troops massing below and runners darting in every direction from Loo, undoubtedly summoning reinforcements. We held approximately twenty thousand men, comprising seven of the finest regiments in the country. Twala, according to Infadoos and the chiefs' calculations, currently commanded at least thirty-five thousand loyalists in Loo, with estimates suggesting another five thousand could arrive by midday the following day. While desertion was a possibility, it was not a factor we could count upon. Clearly, Twala was preparing actively to crush us. Strong bodies of armed men were already patrolling the base of the hill, and signs of impending violence were everywhere.

However, Infadoos and the chiefs believed no attack would occur that day. Instead, they argued, the remaining daylight would be spent on preparations and removing the lingering psychological shock of the moon's darkening. The onslaught, they predicted correctly, would commence tomorrow.

Throughout the day, we labored tirelessly to fortify the position. Every able man was put to work. The paths leading up the hill—which functioned more as a sanatorium for sick regiments than a true fortress—were meticulously blocked with tons of stones. Every conceivable approach was rendered as impregnable as time allowed. Piles of boulders were stacked strategically to be rolled down upon any advancing foe, and defensive stations were assigned to each regiment. We exhausted every ounce of our joint ingenuity in defense.

Just before sunset, resting from our toil, we spotted a small company approaching from Loo. One man carried a palm leaf, signaling he was a herald.

Descending to the foot of the mountain, Ignosi, Infadoos, a few chiefs, and I met the approaching figure. He was a gallant-looking youth wearing the regulation leopard-skin cloak.

"Greeting!" he cried as he neared. "The King's greeting to those who wage unholy war against the King! The Lion's greeting to the jackals that snarl around his heels!"

"Speak," I commanded.

"These are the King's words," the herald replied. "Surrender to the King's mercy before a worse fate befalls you. Already the shoulder has been torn from the black bull, and the King drives him bleeding about the camp."
