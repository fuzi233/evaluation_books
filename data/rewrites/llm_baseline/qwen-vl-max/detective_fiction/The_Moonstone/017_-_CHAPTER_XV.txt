The Sergeant said nothing as we moved through the woods of fir trees leading toward the quicksand. He was deep in thought, and it wasn’t until we reached the edge of the forest that he stirred, as if a decision had finally settled in his mind.

“Mr. Betteredge,” he began, “since you’ve chosen to row alongside me—and since I believe you may yet prove useful before nightfall—I see no point in keeping things hidden anymore. I’ll speak plainly, and I hope you’ll do the same. You’re determined not to give me any information that might harm Rosanna Spearman. Why? Because she’s been kind to you, and because you feel for her. That’s noble, I won’t deny it—but in this case, your kindness is misplaced. Rosanna isn’t in danger. Not even if I were to pin her down with evidence so clear it would be obvious to anyone.”

I couldn’t help asking, “Do you mean my lady won’t press charges?”

“I mean she _can’t_,” he replied firmly. “Rosanna is merely a tool in someone else’s hands. And that person—well, they’ll shield her. She’ll be protected, not punished.”

He spoke with such certainty that I had to pause. Yet something inside me resisted. “Can’t you name that person?” I asked.

“Can’t _you_, Mr. Betteredge?”

“No.”

Sergeant Cuff stopped dead. He looked at me with a quiet, almost sorrowful curiosity.

“It always pleases me,” he said, “to show compassion for human weakness. And right now, I feel particularly sorry for you. And you, in turn, are being tender toward Rosanna—for the very same reason. But tell me—has she recently received new linen?”

I blinked. What did that have to do with anything?

She’d arrived with little more than what she wore. In return for her good behavior—yes, I emphasized that—my lady had given her a fresh set of clothes just two weeks ago.

“Ah,” said the Sergeant. “What a world we live in. Life is like a target—misfortune fires from every direction, and always hits. But for that new outfit, we might have found a stained nightgown or petticoat among her belongings. We could’ve pinned her down then. But you know what happened yesterday, after she fell ill, don’t you? You can’t guess? It’s as plain as that patch of light between the trees. At eleven on Thursday morning, Superintendent Seegrave—a man full of flaws—pointed out the paint smear on the door to all the maids. Rosanna suspected her own things were compromised. She seized the chance to get into her room, discovered the stain on her clothing, pretended sickness, slipped away to town, bought materials, sewed a replacement dress that night, lit a fire—not to burn it, but to dry and press it—knowing others were watching outside. Then she kept the stained garment hidden—perhaps even worn beneath her clothes—and is now likely disposing of it somewhere along this beach ahead of us.”

He turned to me. “I followed her trail tonight to the fishing village, to one particular cottage we may need to visit before returning. She stayed there awhile, and came out carrying something under her cloak. A cloak, on a woman’s back, is a symbol of mercy—it hides many sins. I saw her head northward after leaving. Now, Mr. Betteredge—do you consider this coastline a fine example of seaside scenery?”

“Yes,” I answered curtly.

“Tastes vary,” he said. “From my standpoint, I’ve never seen a worse marine view. If you’re tailing someone along here, and they glance back, there’s no cover anywhere. I had a choice: arrest Rosanna now, or let her continue—knowing what she’s doing. For reasons I won’t explain, I chose silence. I returned to the house to ask you to take me around the shore by another route. Sand is one of the finest detectives I know—footprints reveal everything. If we don’t find her directly, the sand will tell us what she’s done—provided the light lasts.”

And so we walked.

Now, if there’s such a thing as *detective-fever*, I was well and truly infected. The Sergeant moved carefully between the dunes, descending to the shore. I followed, heart pounding, waiting to see what would happen next.

As it turned out, I stood nearly where Rosanna and I had spoken the day Mr. Franklin arrived unexpectedly from London. My thoughts drifted back to that moment—the way she had squeezed my hand, how her face had brightened when she saw him. I could almost hear her voice again, whispering about the Shivering Sand pulling her, against her will. The memory made me uneasy. And the desolate bay, with its stillness and gloom, only deepened that unease.

Dusk was fading fast. A heavy calm lay over the place. The ocean heaved quietly against the sandbar, making no sound. The inner sea was flat and dim, untouched by wind. Patches of foul ooze floated yellow-white on the surface. Scum glistened faintly on the rocks jutting north and south. The tide was turning. As I watched, the quicksand began to ripple—its only movement in that eerie stillness.

The Sergeant stiffened when he noticed it. After a moment, he turned back to me.

“A treacherous spot, Mr. Betteredge. And no sign of Rosanna anywhere. Look as hard as you like.”

He led me lower along the shore. I saw then that our footprints were the only ones left in the sand.

“How does the fishing village lie from here?” he asked.

“Cobb’s Hole,” I replied, “lies roughly due south.”

“I saw her walking northward from Cobb’s Hole earlier,” he said. “So she must have come this way. Is Cobb’s Hole beyond that point of land? And can we reach it now—low tide—by the beach?”

“Yes,” I said.

“Then let’s move quickly,” he said. “I want to find where she left the shore before dark.”

We walked perhaps two hundred yards when the Sergeant suddenly dropped to his knees—seeming possessed by some sudden urge to pray.

“There’s something to be said for your scenery after all,” he remarked. “Here are woman’s footprints, Mr. Betteredge. Let’s call them Rosanna’s—until we find proof otherwise. Confused tracks, I notice—deliberately confused, I suspect. Ah, poor soul—she knows the value of sand as well as I do. But didn’t she rush too much to erase them? Look: one footprint heading *away* from Cobb’s Hole, another returning. See how the toe points straight to the water? And further down—two heel marks near the edge. I don’t wish to offend you, but I fear Rosanna is clever. It seems she meant to reach the place we just left without leaving a trace. Could she have waded through the water to the rock ledge behind us, crossed it, and returned? Yes—that fits. And it matches my suspicion: she carried something under her cloak when she left the cottage. Not to destroy it—why go to such lengths then? No. To hide it. Perhaps, if we visit the cottage, we’ll discover what it was.”

At that, my detective fever cooled instantly.

“You don’t need me,” I said. “What use could I possibly be?”

“The longer I know you, Mr. Betteredge,” he replied, “the more virtues I uncover. Modesty—how rare it is! And how much you possess. If I go alone, the villagers will clam up at the first question. But if I go with you—your respected neighbor—you open the door to conversation. That’s what strikes me. What do you think?”

I hesitated. I hadn’t an answer ready. So I asked, “Which cottage?”

When he described it, I recognized it at once. It belonged to Yolland, a fisherman and his family—his wife and two grown children. I’d mentioned earlier that Rosanna sometimes visited friends in Cobb’s Hole. These were the Yollands—decent people, well-respected. Her connection with them began through their daughter, Lucy, who had a deformed foot and was known locally as Limping Lucy. The two girls, both marked by misfortune, seemed to understand each other. They always got on well when they met.

Now, knowing the Sergeant had traced Rosanna to *their* cottage changed everything. She hadn’t gone to a secret hideout—she’d gone to a familiar, trusted place. Proving she’d been with them was as good as proving her innocence so far. Helping the Sergeant now wouldn’t hurt her—it might help her.

So I agreed.

We walked on, following the footprints while the light held.

At the cottage, the fisherman and his son were out at sea. Limping Lucy was resting upstairs. Mrs. Yolland welcomed us alone in her kitchen. When she heard the Sergeant was famous in London, she brought out a bottle of Dutch gin and two clean pipes, staring at him as if she could never look enough.

I sat quietly, watching. I waited to see how he’d bring up Rosanna.

His usual indirect method was even more roundabout than usual. How he managed it, I couldn’t say. But he started with the Royal Family, then Primitive Methodists, then fish prices. From there, he slid—slowly, deliberately—to the Moonstone, the jealous maid, and the unfair treatment Rosanna had endured. By the time fifteen minutes had passed, Mrs. Yolland believed he was Rosanna’s greatest ally—and pressed him to drink from the gin bottle.

I sat back, enjoying the scene like a play. The great Cuff was patient, firing questions like random shots, hoping one would hit. Everything about Rosanna was praised; nothing criticized. Yet, try as he might, he couldn’t draw anything damaging. Mrs. Yolland talked endlessly, trusting him completely.

His final attempt came as we stood to leave.

“I shall bid you goodnight, ma'am,” he said. “And I’ll only add—Rosanna has a true friend in me. But oh dear—she’ll never succeed in her current position. My advice? Leave it.”

“Bless your heart!” Mrs. Yolland cried. “She *is* going to leave!”

(_Note:_ I translate her Yorkshire speech into English. I should mention that even the brilliant Cuff occasionally needed help understanding her. Imagine how confusing it would be if I quoted her in her native tongue.)

Rosanna was leaving? I perked up. It was odd—she hadn’t told my lady or me. A doubt crept in: had the Sergeant’s last shot struck true? Was I involved in something more dangerous than I’d realized?

I tried to excuse myself. “I must go back to the house.”

“I’ll follow shortly,” he said.

I stepped to the door—twice. Both times, I couldn’t bring myself to leave.

“She means to leave?” the Sergeant asked.

“What will she do?” Mrs. Yolland replied. “Sad, sad. She has no one in the world but you and me.”

“But she does!” she insisted. “She came here this evening. After talking with Lucy and me, she asked to go upstairs alone—to write a letter. ‘I want to write to a friend,’ she said. ‘I can’t do it with servants spying.’ Who it was, I don’t know. But she stayed long—must have been a long letter. I offered her a stamp. She didn’t take it. Private, poor girl. But she has a friend—someone she’ll go to.”

“Soon?” the Sergeant asked.

“As soon as possible,” she said.

I stepped forward again. As head of the household, I couldn’t allow such talk about one of my servants.

“You must be mistaken,” I said. “If she intended to leave, she would’ve told me first.”

“Mistaken?” Mrs. Yolland snapped. “Only an hour ago, she bought supplies for travel—from me, in this very room. And that reminds me—something I’ve been meaning to say about her money. Will either of you see her when you return?”

“I’ll deliver a message gladly,” the Sergeant said, cutting me off.

Mrs. Yolland pulled out coins—shillings and sixpences—and counted them slowly, painfully, in her palm. She handed them to him, reluctant to part with them.

“Would you return this to her?” she asked. “With my love. She insisted on paying for the items. Money’s welcome, yes—but I’m uneasy taking a poor girl’s savings. My husband might disapprove when he returns tomorrow. Please tell her the things were a gift. And don’t leave the money on the table,” she added, dropping it there abruptly, as if it burned her. “Don’t, please! Times are hard, and flesh is weak. I might be tempted to put it back.”

“I must go,” I said. “I can’t wait.”

“I’ll follow,” the Sergeant said.

Again, I reached the door—again, I couldn’t cross it.

“It’s delicate, ma'am,” I heard him say. “Returning money. Did you charge her fairly?”

“Fair?” she laughed. “Come judge for yourself.”

She took the candle and led him to a corner. I couldn’t help following. There, piled in the corner, was a heap of salvage—old metal, bits from shipwrecks, unsold yet.

She dug into it and pulled out a japanned tin case—like those used on ships to keep maps dry.

“There!” she said. “She bought the mate to this. ‘Perfect for my cuffs and collars,’ she said. ‘To keep them from getting crushed.’ One and ninepence. Not a penny more!”

“Cheap,” the Sergeant sighed.

He weighed it. I thought I heard a few notes of “The Last Rose of Summer” in his breath. There was no doubt now—he’d uncovered something damning, in the one place I thought Rosanna was safe. All because of me.

“That’s enough,” I said. “We really must go.”

Mrs. Yolland ignored me. She plunged into the pile again and pulled out a dog chain.

“Feel it,” she said. “We had three. She took two. ‘Why do you need dog chains?’ I asked. ‘If I join them, they’ll fit my box perfectly,’ she said. ‘Rope’s cheaper,’ I said. ‘Chain’s safer,’ she said. ‘Who ever heard of a box tied with chain?’ I asked. ‘Oh, Mrs. Yolland, don’t argue!’ she said. ‘Let me have my chains!’ Strange girl—kind as gold, sweet to my Lucy—but odd. Three and sixpence. On my word, three and sixpence for both.”

“Each?” the Sergeant asked.

“Both together!” she insisted.

“Given away,” he said, shaking his head. “Clean given away.”

“There’s the money,” she said, backing toward the silver on the table. “That’s all she bought. One and ninepence, three and sixpence—total five and three. With my love—and I can’t take a poor girl’s savings.”

“I can’t give it back,” the Sergeant said. “You’ve practically given her the things. You have.”

“Is that your honest opinion?” she asked, brightening.

“There’s no doubt,” he said. “Ask Mr. Betteredge.”

I said nothing. All I could manage was, “Goodnight.”

“Bother the money!” she shouted. Then, losing control, she grabbed the coins and shoved them back into her pocket. “It drives one mad to see it lying there, unclaimed!” She sat down hard, glaring at him as if to say, “It’s mine again—get it out if you can!”

This time, I walked out. I felt deeply offended—by one or both of them.

Three steps down the road, I heard footsteps behind me.

“Thank you for introducing me, Mr. Betteredge,” the Sergeant said. “I owe the fisherman’s wife a new experience. Mrs. Yolland has puzzled me.”
