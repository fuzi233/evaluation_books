Not long after Colonel Altamont hit the Epsom races and walked away with enough cash to keep him in brandy and fine waistcoats for months, the very same gentleman finally booked that foreign jaunt he’d been rambling about for weeks. The gossip columnist who loiters down at London Bridge for the sole purpose of waving off rich people leaving the country duly noted the passenger manifest of the *Soho* bound for Antwerp one Saturday: it featured “Sir Robert Hodge, his lady, their three Miss Hodge’s, Mr. Serjeant Kewsy and his wife and daughter, Colonel Altamont, Major Coddy, and several other persons of distinction.”

The Colonel travelled like he owned the whole Channel, too. He turned up in a velvet-lined tailored travelling getup that cost more than a farm labourer made in a year, chugged brandy and water nonstop the whole crossing without turning green and puking over the rail like every other snobby passenger on board, and had his loyal Irish ex-legionary valet trailing after him to tote his bags, light his cigars, and fetch him extra biscuits whenever he grunted. The same valet had been waiting on both Altamont and Captain Strong back in their messy Shepherd’s Inn digs for months, so he was well used to their nonsense.

Chevalier Strong threw Altamont a raucous send-off dinner at Blackwall, along with a couple of random drinking buddies, all of whom toasted the Colonel’s health over and over, entirely at Altamont’s generous expense. Halfway through the feast, Altamont slammed a fat wallet down on the table and shoved a twenty-pound note at his mate. “Strong old boy,” he sloshed, “you need a little extra cash right now? Name your sum, I’m your man. You’ve always been a good bloke to me, and a twenty-pound note more or less doesn’t even register on my bank balance these days.”

Strong turned him down flat, claiming he was totally flush – well, flush enough that he couldn’t pay back the last loan Altamont gave him, but still had enough stashed to keep the lights on for months. The two parted on surprisingly friendly terms, no hard feelings at all. Strong found himself wondering later, as he trundled back to the inn, had all that new money actually made Altamont a better, nicer man, or was he just easier on the eyes now that he wasn’t permanently skint? He decided it was a little of both, and besides: even a former transported convict like Altamont was ten times the gentleman that stuck-up useless Baronet Sir Francis Clavering was. Altamont had pluck! He’d stick up for a mate, and punch an enemy in the face if he had to. Clavering? That coward would run and hide behind a lady’s petticoat before he faced a bar bill, let alone a brawl. What even got Altamont in trouble in the first place? He’d been a little wild once, and signed his father-in-law’s name on a bit of paper. Half the toffs in the West End had done worse and still waltzed around with their noses in the air, no shame at all.

Strong immediately felt a little stupid for lying about being flush, though. He was so broke he couldn’t even afford to hire the valet he’d gotten used to, which meant now the formerly portly, dignified Chevalier was down in his scullery every morning, polishing his own boots and broiling his own mutton chops to a cinder. The Shepherd’s Inn digs that had felt crowded and chaotic with Altamont around were now so quiet they were depressing. He even started daydreaming about hopping a boat to Spain to go visit his ex-wife Juanita, the fiery Spanish lady from Vittoria who’d only lasted a few months of marriage before she brained him with a dinner dish and went back to her family. He was properly “down on his luck”, as he’d put it – the exact kind of dark patch all great heroes go through, right? Like Marius stuck in the mud at Minturnae, or Bonnie Prince Charlie hiding in the Highlands, or Napoleon hanging out right before Elba. No great man avoids the occasional burnt mutton chop dry spell.

Clavering, for his part, was absolutely no use at all for getting extra cash. The twenty-five quid he’d cadged off Altamont had vanished faster than a pub landlord’s missing change. He’d run off with a crowd of blokes who loved illegal bare-knuckle fights, dodged the police, landed in Essex, and bet all his cash on a cabman called Dick, who was winning thirteen whole rounds before he accidentally punched his opponent right in the windpipe and killed him dead. “It’s always my bloody luck, Strong!” Clavering wailed, throwing his hat on the floor. “The odds were three to one on my guy, I thought that thirty quid was already in my pocket! Now I owe my valet Lightfoot fourteen quid that he lent me, and the little blackguard keeps dunning me for it! I’m this close to begging my lady for a handout, c’mon Ned, if you can think up some way to wangle fifty quid out of her, I’ll give you half, I swear on my honour!”

Strong shut him down instantly, stone-faced. He’d given his word of honour he would never help Clavering pull another money trick on his long-suffering wife, and he’d black his own boots every single day for the rest of his life before he broke that promise. He even threatened to tip Lady Clavering off the second he got wind Clavering was planning to lie to her about bills.

Clavering reacted exactly like you’d expect: he screamed, he cried, he cursed up a storm, said death was his only possible escape, begged his “dearest old Ned, best friend in the whole world” not to betray him… then the second he was out on the Shepherd’s Inn stairs, he was spitting and swearing that Strong was the worst traitor, villain, blackguard and coward who ever walked the earth, and he hoped Ned rotted in hell, but not before Clavering got to take his revenge out on him first.

Right as he was storming out, Clavering nearly bowled over young Mr. Samuel Huxter, the medical student from Clavering town who was always popping in to visit Strong. Sam had grown up poaching walnuts in Clavering Park, watching Sir Francis drive through the high street with four horses and powdered footmen, and he thought the Baronet was the grandest, poshest old English gentleman who ever lived. He stumbled through an introduction, blushing so hard his ears turned pink, mentioning how his dad had treated Sir Francis’s gamekeeper when his gun blew up and took off three fingers. Clavering, for once, was affable enough, and Sam left walking on air. Back at his digs in Bartholomew’s, he wouldn’t stop gushing about Sir Francis to his mates, and told his girlfriend Fanny over at the lodge that the Baronet was the perfect example of a thoroughbred old country gentleman. Fanny just cackled. She thought Sir Francis was horrid. “He’s wicked! And cheap! And mean!” she insisted. “Mum lent him seven shillings once, and it took us three whole months to get it back! He even borrowed half a sovereign off you yesterday, dummy!”

“Say ‘her’ not ‘er’, ‘borrow’ not ‘borrer’, Fanny love,” Sam corrected, being the grammar nerd he was.

“Fine, her, borrow, actually – you still a stoopid,” Fanny teased, yanking his messy hair and patting his chin, and Sam was so smitten he would’ve sat through a hundred grammar lessons from her right then and there. Her mum Mrs. Bolton just rolled her eyes in the corner, wiping a dish.

You’ve probably thought about this, right, fancy rich reader? All that respect everyone lavishes on you in your old age – the servants bowing, the tenants doffing their caps, everyone hanging on your every garrulous old story? The second you kick the bucket, all of it moves straight to your heir. They’ll drink your best aged wine, get all the cheers and the deference, and everyone will stop being sad you’re gone by Tuesday. You can’t expect people to grieve you forever before they start enjoying all your stuff. It’s the way of the world.

And speaking of the world, no one should think Arthur Pendennis is being disrespectful to his dead mum now that his mourning band’s faded a little and he’s got a new white hat. That sainted lady’s memory is locked in the most sacred part of his heart, and every time he’s tempted to do something stupid or mean, he can hear her soft voice telling him not to. He and Warrington sit up late after everyone leaves, talking about her, and the rest of the world can snark all it wants – that kind of love for a good woman doesn’t just vanish. But the second Major Pendennis or any other random mate walks in, they both shut up instantly and start gossiping about the opera or the latest Parliament debate, like every other normal bloke.

Major Pendennis, for his part, was furious that his nephew wasn’t parading around like a proper rich landed gentleman now he’d come into his property. He kept hounding Pen to get fancy new quarters, buy a flash showy cabriolet, and stop living in that “horrible garret” in the Temple. Pen laughed him off. “Uncle, I’m so absent-minded I’d crash the cab straight into a bus, or drive the horse’s head right through a lady’s carriage window. You don’t want me looking like a doddery apothecary behind the reins, do you?” Then he teased his uncle about how his own dad, the doctor, was dead proud when he first bought his little gig, and the Major turned bright red, sputtering that of course the old family was ancient and noble, no one needed to go around bragging that the former head of the house was a doctor.

Pen eventually compromised: he moved down one flight to the second floor in Lamb Court, left Warrington in his old garret, and they still shared the same servant, the same dinners, and the same horse, just like two medieval Knights Templar bunking together. Those young adult friendships are the best ones, right? Your heart’s still soft enough to let someone all the way in, unlike later on when you’ve shaken a thousand casual hands and you can barely even be bothered to learn a new acquaintance’s last name.

Warrington, the grumpy old cynic, absolutely hated the Clavering crowd. He’d met them during that disastrous London season, and told Pen straight up they were all bad news. “Those two rakes hanging over Lady Clavering’s carriage in the Park the other day, leering at Blanche under her bonnet? No proper mother would ever let her daughter within ten feet of those blokes, let alone invite them to dinner.”

Pen leapt to Lady Clavering’s defence, the “Begum” as everyone called her. “She’s the sweetest, most innocent woman alive! She’s never heard a single bad story about any of those rakes, you old grump.”

“Would you let Laura Bell hang around those losers?” Warrington snapped, his face turning red. “That woman’s not a lady, Pen, no matter how good her dinners are. It’s not her fault she never learned refinement, but that doesn’t mean we have to hang out with her.”

Pen got a little sulky right back. “Why are we being such snobs? She’s kinder to every single person she meets than half the fancy duchesses with perfect grammar. She pays her awful husband’s debts, spoils her son, gives you the best dinners she can afford. She’s never done a single bad thing to anyone.”

Warrington growled, “Your laundress is nice too, but you don’t invite her over for three courses and silver plates. Are you telling me the best thing in life is good claret and a fancy dinner? I’d rather eat raw turnips and sleep in a hollow tree than degrade myself that much!”

Pen laughed at him. “Oh please, you say that every time we finish one of those dinners, then you go home and smoke a pipe and tell me it was the best beef you’ve ever eaten. I don’t pretend to be best friends with the Claverings! If someone invites me over and gives me a good meal, I show up, I’m polite, and we part ways without any drama. I don’t owe them my eternal soul, I owe them the polite fake friendliness everyone gives each other in society. If I only hung out with my real friends, you’re the only bloke I’d ever see.”

“Ugh,” Warrington said, sadly shaking his head. “You’re your uncle’s pupil through and through. You’re a total worldling.”

“Damn right,” Pen shrugged. “Why would I pretend the world’s something it’s not? I’m not some twenty-year-old revolutionary with a half-baked plan to reform all of humanity. Those guys all end up selling out the second they get a cushy government job, anyway. You think Thiers or Guizot were screaming for change when they got into power? They ended up compromising just like everyone else. No one’s strong enough to march faster than the whole rest of the world.”

Warrington snickered and poured himself another glass of wine. “If you keep talking like that, Mr. Right Honourable Pendennis, they’ll make you First Lord of the Treasury and Chancellor of the Exchequer by the end of the week.”
