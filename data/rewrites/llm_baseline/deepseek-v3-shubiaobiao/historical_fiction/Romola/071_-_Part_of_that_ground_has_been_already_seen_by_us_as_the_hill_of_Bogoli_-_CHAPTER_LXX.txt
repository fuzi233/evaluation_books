On the fourteenth of April, Romola crossed the familiar walls of Florence once more. Restless and uneasy in Pistoja, where conflicting rumors about the Trial by Fire reached her, she had pressed onward to Prato, driven by a gnawing dread that she would be compelled to return to the city she had fled. It was there she encountered the monk of San Spirito, her godfather’s confessor—a man whose words struck her like a blow.

From him, she learned the full weight of Florence’s recent horrors: Savonarola’s arrest, his forced confession under torture, and the grim fate of her husband. The monk, having witnessed the procession of the condemned, recounted what all Florence knew—Tito had escaped the mob’s wrath by leaping into the Arno, only to meet his end on the riverbank at the hands of an old enemy. But Romola understood what others did not. The monk spoke of Savonarola with the disdain of the Frati Neri, declaring the friar had admitted to deceiving the people. Romola could bear no more. That very evening, she was in Florence.

Monna Brigida welcomed her with a storm of tears and exclamations, her joy as effusive as her earlier despair. She had even abandoned her false hair in her grief, though now, with Romola returned, she declared she cared not for gray locks if only her dear child would stay. But Romola was numb to the chatter of trivialities. The weight of Tito’s death, of Savonarola’s broken spirit under torture, left little room for lesser sorrows. Only two thoughts burned through the haze of her grief: she must see Savonarola, and she must find Tessa and the children.

Abruptly, she cut through Brigida’s rambling. *"Cousin, tell me—has there been any word of a young woman with two little children since Tito’s death?"*

Brigida’s hands flew up in shock. *"Cristo! No! Was he truly so wicked? Oh, my poor child, was that why you left?"* Her voice dropped to a scandalized whisper. *"If I’d known, I wouldn’t have thought you so strange. But to leave your husband just because of Bernardo’s execution—I said to myself, ‘Ah, she has her father’s temper.’ But if there was a *hussey* involved—"*

*"No,"* Romola interrupted sharply. *"She is innocent—ignorant of all wrongdoing. I mean to care for her and the children. They have no one else."*

Brigida huffed, crossing her arms. *"Well, if that’s piety, I’ve been praying to the wrong saints. Fra Girolamo said widows ought not to marry again, yet here you are, taking in another woman’s *two* children—Santiddio!"*

Romola silenced her with a look. *"Not now, cousin."*

---

At dawn, Romola sought the house beyond San Ambrogio where she had once found Tessa. But the dwelling was empty. Tessa was gone—likely sent away by Tito before his flight, a thought that twisted in Romola’s chest. If the girl had been taken beyond Florence, finding her would be near impossible. The neighbors knew nothing, only that old deaf Lisa had departed with her belongings.

Days passed in fruitless searching. Every glimpse of a contadina’s dress, every child’s laughter in the street, made Romola’s pulse quicken. She told herself this was no grand act of charity—she *needed* them. The children, at least, might offer something pure to love.

Then, walking the Via San Gallo, she spotted Bratti haggling with a customer—and her breath caught. Dangling from his basket was a familiar necklace.

*"Bratti,"* she demanded, *"where did you get that?"*

The peddler turned, unfazed. *"Ah, you know the little fool who wept when she pawned it? A florin, and it’s yours. My heart’s too soft for business."*

Romola thrust the coin at him. *"Where is she?"*

Moments later, Romola stood at the threshold of a dim room. Tessa sat on the bed, her eyes hollow, while Lillo and Ninna played nearby, Lillo roaring under his skirt to frighten his sister.

Romola stepped forward, the necklace in hand. *"Tessa."*

The girl whirled, screamed, and collapsed into sobs against Romola’s shoulder. The children stared, wide-eyed.

*"God has brought me back to you,"* Romola murmured.

By evening, they were all under Monna Brigida’s roof. Romola had gently told Tessa the truth—Naldo would never return, not from cruelty, but because he was dead.

*"But I will care for you always,"* Romola promised. *"You and the children."*

Brigida watched, torn between awe and the urge to put the girl in her place. *Let be,* she thought. *But one day, she’ll learn who Romola is.*

That night, as she tucked the children in, Brigida sighed. *"You must let me spoil them a little, Romola. You’ve never had a babe, and I—well, I had twins once."* Her voice faltered. *"Though they did not live."*

Romola said nothing. But for the first time in many days, the weight on her heart felt lighter.
