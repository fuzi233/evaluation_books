As the sun-kissed days of late spring gradually gave way to the warmth of summer, Romola found herself suspended in a state of expectant waiting. The streets of Florence, once abuzz with the vibrant energy of the city's daily life, had grown quiet, as if holding its breath in anticipation of some long-awaited news. The absence of any word from Baldassarre hung heavy over her, while the government's watchful eyes seemed to be perpetually scanning the crowds, yet revealing nothing about the suspected conspiracy.

Yet, even as Romola's anxiety lingered, other events began to unfold, weaving themselves into the fabric of her daily life. The plague spread its dark shadow over the city, casting a pall of fear and uncertainty over its inhabitants. At the same time, the Excommunication of Savonarola was solemnly pronounced in the Duomo, sending shockwaves through the community. These developments, though seemingly unrelated, served to rekindle Romola's attachment to the charismatic friar, whose unwavering commitment to principle in the face of profligacy had first awakened her to the possibilities of a life of duty and purpose.

As she watched the solemn procession of monks and clergy file into the cathedral, their faces set in determined lines, Romola felt her own ardour for Savonarola's cause deepen. The stark contrast between the scenes of old – when the Duomo's grand space had been filled with the hopes and aspirations of the people – and the sombre, muted atmosphere of the present moment struck her forcibly. Gone were the radiant faces of the citizens, the vibrant energy of the crowd, and the soaring voice that had thrilled the hearts of all who listened. In their place, the soft clinking of tiny bells and the monotonous reading of a sentence, already familiar to the congregation, hung in the air like a dirge.

The gleeful triumph in the faces of some, and the fearful confusion in others, served only to strengthen Romola's resolve. She knew that the question of obedience versus resistance was a complex and fraught one, particularly when the Church was believed to be a living, breathing entity, imbued with divine power to bless or curse. For most of the pious Florentines, this conviction was not a matter of opinion, but a deeply ingrained impression, like the curve of the sky above.
