Supper-time came, and with it the small, homely ceremony that in that house stood for law: the board drawn close, the trenchers set, the knives laid where each hand expected them, and the steam of good fare rising like a benediction to the rafters.

All were gathered—Eli at the head, Catherine fluttering and fussy with her household dignity, Joan in the place of an honored helper more than a guest, and Jorian, still pale from his late sickness, watching the dishes with that sharpened hunger illness leaves behind. Only Margaret was away.

To Catherine’s surprise, Eli did not give the word to begin.

“We’ll bide a bit for her,” said he.

“Why, I told her you would not wait for the duke,” Catherine returned, half reproach, half pride.

“She is not the duke,” said Eli. “She is a poor, good lass that hath waited—not minutes—but years—for a graceless son of mine. Put the meat on the board all the same. When she comes, we’ll lose no more time.”

So the dishes came in: brown bread with its cracked crust still sighing warmth when the knife went near; a joint that had been turned and basted till the fat ran clear and sweet, and the outside held that deep, savory skin a careful hand coaxes from fire; and a mess of herbs and roots, garden-things lifted from honest earth and stewed till their smell filled the room—leek and onion, maybe, and the green sharpness of parsley crushed between fingers, mellowed now into comfort. The very air was thick with supper; it clung to the tongue before a morsel was swallowed. Beyond the shutters lay the village’s evening sound—distant talk, a dog’s short bark, the soft scuff of feet in the street—acoustic small-wares that make a home feel held in the world.

Eli held out against it as long as he could, then his face softened—hunger and habit conspiring with hope.

“She will come if we begin,” said he; “they always do. Come, sit ye down, Mistress Joan; y’are not here for a slave, I trow, but a guest. There—” He lifted a hand, listening. “I hear a quick step off covers. Withdraw the covers, and fall to.”

Covers were whipped away; steel flashed; the first bread was broken. And then the expected light step did come—quick, urgent—only it was followed by a heavier, harsher tread, and a breathing like bellows. The door flew wide.

Not Margaret.

A Dominican friar burst in, his face grey with fury as if rage had drained it of blood. The whole room, full a moment before of warm smells and neighborly clatter, seemed suddenly to lose its ordinary sounds; the hush fell like a cloth thrown over a dish. He crossed the floor at a bound and was at the table in an instant, in front of Cornelis and Sybrandt.

He flung his long body over the narrow board, and spread his hands above their shrinking heads—hovering, rigid, like talons poised. Then, with a voice that struck the ear like iron on stone, he cursed them.

He cursed them by name; cursed their souls and bodies in this world and the next. The age had a terrible fluency for malediction, and he poured it out with a minute and dreadful craft, each clause fitted like a nail driven home.

“Cursed be the lips,” he shrieked, “which spoke the lie that Margaret was dead; may they rot before the grave, and kiss white-hot iron in hell thereafter! Doubly cursed be the hands that changed those letters—be they struck off by the hangman’s knife, and handle hell fire for ever! Thrice accursed be the cruel hearts that did conceive that damned lie, to part true love for ever! May they sicken and wither on earth, joyless, loveless, hopeless; wither to dust before their time; and burn in eternal fire!”

He cursed the meat at their mouths, and every atom of their bodies, from hair to sole; and while he did it the supper-smell itself seemed to turn strange—good food made suddenly hateful by a word that claimed Heaven for its warrant. Joan’s fingers tightened on her lap; Jorian’s swallow stuck. Catherine made a sound in her throat and could not shape it into speech. Cornelis and Sybrandt bent and cringed, as if the curses were stones hurled at them.

Then the friar tore a letter from his bosom and flung it down before Eli as one flings down a gauntlet.

“Read that, thou hard old man that didst imprison thy son,” he cried. “Read, and see what monsters thou hast brought into the world. The memory of my wrongs and hers dwell with you all for ever! I will meet you again at the Judgment Day; on earth ye will never see me more.”

And as he had come, so he was gone—gone like a gust that slams a door and leaves a house stunned in its wake. The room sat frozen around the steaming dishes, white-faced and rigid as if some Eastern tale had come true and turned living folk to images before untasted food.

That was the sight that met Margaret’s eyes—and Jorian’s—when, the next moment, they entered.

The change was so sharp that Margaret stopped on the threshold as though she had stepped into a chapel after market-noise. Her gaze ran from face to face—Eli standing half risen, Catherine drawn tight with fear and shame, Joan startled and still, the two lads sunk low as if hoping the table itself might hide them—then she caught her breath and cried out, brokenly:

“Oh, joy! all here—no blood hath been shed. Oh, you cruel, cruel men! I thank God he hath not slain you.”

At the sight of her Catherine gave a scream that was all mother and all dread; then turned her head away, as if the very look of Margaret were a judgment.

But Eli had snatched up the letter and cast an eye along it, and the sense of it struck him like a club. He began to understand—began to see the long, filthy work and the pain it had cost. When he looked up and beheld Margaret at that very instant, with her wrongs written in the pallor of her sweet face, his old blood leapt. He sprang to his feet in a transport of rage.

“Stand clear!” he shouted. “Stand clear, and let me get at the traitors. I’ll hang for them!”

In a flash his short sword was out. Steel sang—thin, sharp—a cruelly bright sound in a room that had been meant for bread and peace. He fell upon Cornelis and Sybrandt.

“Fly!” screamed Margaret. “Fly!”

They slipped, howling, under the table and crawled out the far side, knocking trenchers and scraping knees, their terror a clumsy, scrambling thing. But before they could reach the door Eli ran round and barred them, blade lifted. Catherine only screamed and wrung her hands—the great ones of the household, who manage servants and reckon stores and set the table straight, are often the helplessest when the blood rises.

There would have been blood—certainly—had not Margaret and Jorian, with one impulse, seized Eli’s arms and held them with all their strength. Jorian, though not yet fully recovered, set his shoulder and braced as if he had been born for such a moment; Margaret clung like a child to a father who would not listen, her voice breaking on his name.

The pair got clear of the house. Then Margaret and Jorian loosed Eli. He went raging out into the street after the fugitives; but they were already a furlong away, running like hares.

He came back with his breath heaving, and with a single savage stroke hacked down the board on which their names were written—one of those household tablets that keeps the memory of kin and work—and he flung it into the chimney-place as though he would burn the very notion of them out of his hearth.

Catherine sat rocking herself, her apron over her head, as if she could hide from what had been spoken. Joan ran to her husband and caught his hand. Margaret put her arms round Catherine’s neck; she was pale, panting, and yet tried, with a tenderness that had been taught by sorrow, to comfort the woman who had helped wrong her.

But it would not be done. Catherine’s grief burst out in sobs that were more fear than penitence.

“Oh, my poor children!” she cried. “Oh, miserable mother! ’Tis a mercy Kate was ill upstairs. There—I have lived to thank God for that!” And then, with a fresh storm: “It would have killed her. He had better have stayed in Italy than come home to curse his own flesh and blood, and set us all by the ears.”

“Hold your chat, woman,” Eli broke in, anger stiff with pain. “You are still on the side of the ill-doer. You are cheap served. Your weakness made the rogues what they are. I was for correcting them in their youth—for sore ills, sharp remedies; but you still sided with their faults, and undermined me, and baffled wise severity. And you, Margaret—leave comforting her that ought rather to comfort you; for what is her hurt to yours? She never had a grain of justice under her skin; and never will.”

He turned then, with a rough tenderness that was command as much as love.

“So come thou to me, that am thy father from this hour.”

She obeyed, because the voice allowed no choice. She kissed Catherine—softly, as one who parts from the past—and went tottering to Eli. He set her on a chair beside him, and she laid her weak head on his honest breast. No tears came; the ache was too deep for that, like hunger that has gone beyond craving into numbness.

“Poor lamb,” he said, and his voice broke on the words.

Time went on. The supper stood there still, sending up its honest steam into a room where no one could taste it. The smell of roasted fat, which a minute before had promised comfort, now felt like insult—goodness spoiled by human bitterness and by a curse spoken in the Church’s name. It was as though the very language that should guard memory and mercy had been used to strike them deaf.

At last Eli lifted his head and looked across at Jorian and Joan with a kind of shame-faced hospitality.

“Come, good folks,” said he, in a broken voice. “We are in a little trouble, as you see; but that is no reason you should starve. For our Lady’s sake, fall to; and add not to my grief the reputation of a churl. What the dickens!”—and he made a ghastly, brave attempt at heartiness—“the more knaves I have the luck to get shut of, the more my need of true men and women to help me clear the dish, and cheer mine eye with honest faces about me where else were gaps. Fall to, I do entreat ye.”

Catherine, sobbing under her apron, backed his request with a broken “Ay—do, do.”

Poor, simple, antique, hospitable souls—trained from childhood to believe a table was a kind of altar, and that to send a guest away unfed was a sin the house would remember.

Jorian’s appetite, especially since his illness, was keen; the smell of meat and herbs pulled at him like a hand. He moved as if to obey, but Joan leaned close and whispered in his ear—one quick sentence, low as a mouse in the thatch. He drew back at once.

“Nay,” said he. “I’ll touch no meat that Holy Church hath cursed.”

“In sooth, I forgot,” Eli answered, and his plain face took on a dumb wonder. “My son, who was reared at my table, hath cursed my victuals. That seems strange. Well—what God wills, man must bow to.”

So the supper—bread, meat, all that careful turning and basting, all that garden labor and hearth-skill—was flung out into the yard as if it were carrion. The sound of it hitting the wet earth was small, yet it jarred the ear, a wasteful thud that seemed to carry farther than it should into the night.

Jorian took Joan home; heavy sadness reigned in Eli’s house.

Meanwhile, where was Clement?

He lay at full length upon the cold floor of the convent church, his lips pressed to the lowest step of the altar. Terror and misery had him by the throat; penitence crushed him; self-abasement ground him into the stone. And through all that anguish there struggled, like a thin thread of dawn in cloud, gleams of joy—Margaret was alive.

Night fell and found him still there, weeping and praying. The church had its own sound-world: the drip of damp in some unseen corner, the hollow breath of the building, the far footfall of a monk passing like a shadow, and, under all, the pounding within his own ears, the loudest noise of all because it could not be escaped.

Morning would have found him there too, but suddenly another memory rose up, sharper than any curse: in his rage for his own wrongs and Margaret’s, he had committed yet another sin.

He had neglected a dying man.

He sprang up at once, groaning at his accumulated wickedness, and went out to repair the omission. The weather had changed. Rain fell hard, drumming on roof and road, turning earth to black paste; and when he got clear of the town, he heard wolves baying—on the foot, hunting by sound more than sight, their voices travelling through wet air and leafless spaces like a warning.

But Clement was himself again—or nearly. Danger and discomfort were little to him beside the shame of a duty left undone. He went stoutly forward through rain and darkness; and as he went he beat his breast, and cried into the storm, words that were Latin and therefore heavy with the Church’s authority, yet no tongue could fully translate what he felt within them:

“MEA CULPA! MEA CULPA!”
