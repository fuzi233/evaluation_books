The next morning, Archer stepped off the Fall River train into a Boston that seemed to be steaming under the weight of midsummer. The air near the station was thick with the pungent smells of beer, strong coffee, and rotting fruit. Shirt-sleeved crowds shuffled through the heat with the casual intimacy of boarders heading for the communal bathhouse. Archer hailed a cab and instructed the driver to take him to the Somerset Club for breakfast. Even these fashionable districts possessed an air of untidy domesticity that the oppressive heat failed to degrade in European cities. Caretakers in calico lounged on the doorsteps of the wealthy, and the Common looked less like a public park and more like a playground abandoned the day after a massive picnic.

Archer realized that if he had tried to conjure up scenes for Ellen Olenska in this climate, he couldn't have imagined a setting where fitting her would be so difficult. This was a heat-stricken, deserted Boston. Yet, he ate with appetite and method, starting with a slice of melon while reading the morning paper, waiting for his toast and scrambled eggs. Since announcing to May the previous night that he had business in Boston and intended to catch the Fall River boat that evening to reach New York the next, a new sense of energy had taken hold of him. It had always been assumed he would return to town early in the week; indeed, a letter from the office—fatefully placed on a corner of the hall table during his recent trip to Portsmouth—had justified his sudden change of plans. He felt a pang of shame at how easily the deception had worked, reminding him uncomfortably of Lawrence Lefferts' masterful schemes for securing his own freedom. But the feeling passed quickly; he was not in an analytical mood.

After breakfast, he smoked a cigarette and scanned the *Commercial Advertiser*. Two or three acquaintances entered, exchanging the customary greetings. It was the same world, yet Archer harbored a strange sensation of having slipped through the meshes of time and space. Glancing at his watch, he noted it was half-past nine. Rising, he retreated to the writing-room, penned a few lines, and dispatched a messenger to the Parker House to await a reply. Sitting behind another newspaper, he calculated the travel time to the hotel.

"The lady was out, sir," a waiter's voice suddenly interrupted him at his elbow.

"Out?" Archer stammered, the word sounding alien on his tongue.

He jumped up and rushed into the hall. It must be a mistake; she couldn't possibly be out at that hour. Anger flushed his cheeks at his own stupidity. Why hadn't he sent the note immediately upon arrival? Grabbing his hat and cane, he stepped out onto the street. The city suddenly felt vast, strange, and empty, as if he were a traveler from distant lands. Standing hesitantly on the doorstep, he resolved to go to the Parker House himself. Perhaps the messenger had been wrong, and she was still inside.

He began walking across the Common. On the first bench beneath a tree, he saw her. She held a grey silk sunshade over her head—a detail that surprised him, given he had previously imagined her with a pink one. Approaching, he was struck by her listless posture; she sat as though idleness were her only occupation. He observed her drooping profile, the knot of hair fastened low in her neck beneath her dark hat, and the long, wrinkled glove on the hand gripping the shade.

A step closer, and she turned to look at him.

"Oh," she said. For the first time, he noticed a startled look in her eyes, which quickly softened into a slow smile of wonder and contentment. "Oh," she murmured again, on a different note, as he stood gazing down at her. Without rising, she made room for him on the bench.

"I'm here on business—just got here," Archer explained. Unknowingly, he began to feign astonishment at the sight of her. "But what on earth are you doing in this wilderness?" He had no real idea what he was saying; he felt as if he were shouting across endless distances, fearing she might vanish before he could catch up.

"I? Oh, I'm here on business too," she replied, turning her head until they faced each other. Her words barely registered; he was acutely aware only of her voice and the startling realization that not a single echo of it remained in his memory. He hadn't even recalled that it was low-pitched, carrying a faint roughness on the consonants.

"You do your hair differently," he said, his heart hammering as if he'd spoken something irrevocable.

"Differently? No—it's only that I do it as best I can when I'm without Nastasia."

"Nastasia—but isn't she with you?"

"No; I'm alone. For two days it wasn't worth while to bring her."

"You're alone—at the Parker House?"

She looked at him with a flash of her old malice. "Does it strike you as dangerous?"

"No—not dangerous—"

"But unconventional? I see; I suppose it is." She considered a moment. "I hadn't thought of it, because I've just done something so much more unconventional." A faint tinge of irony lingered in her eyes. "I've just refused to take back a sum of money—that belonged to me."

Archer sprang up and stepped back. She had furled her parasol and sat absently tracing patterns in the gravel. Soon, he returned and stood before her.

"Some one... has come here to meet you?"

"Yes."

"With this offer?" She nodded.

"And you refused—because of the conditions?"

"I refused," she said after a pause.

"What were the conditions?"

"Oh, they weren't onerous: just to sit at the head of his table now and then."

Another silence fell. Archer's heart had slammed shut in a peculiar way, leaving him groping vainly for words.

"He wants you back—at any price?"

"Well—a considerable price. At least the sum is considerable for me."

He paused, beating around the question he felt compelled to ask. "It was to meet him here that you came?"

She stared, then burst into laughter. "Meet him—my husband? *Here*? At this season he's always at Cowes or Baden."

"He sent someone?"

"Yes."

"With a letter?" She shook her head. "No; just a message. He never writes. I don't think I've had more than one letter from him."

The allusion brought color to her cheek, reflecting in Archer's vivid blush. "Why does he never write?"

"Why should he? What does one have secretaries for?" The young man's blush deepened. She had pronounced the word as if it held no more significance than any other in her vocabulary. For a moment, it was on the tip of his tongue to ask, "Did he send his secretary, then?" But the memory of Count Olenski's solitary letter to his wife kept him silent. He paused, then plunged forward again.

"And the person?"

"The emissary? The emissary," Madame Olenska rejoined, still smiling, "might, for all I care, have left already; but he has insisted on waiting till this evening... in case... on the chance..."

"And you came out here to think the chance over?"

"I came out to get a breath of air. The hotel's too stifling. I'm taking the afternoon train back to Portsmouth."

They sat in silence, not looking at each other but staring straight ahead at the passersby. Finally, she turned her eyes to his face.

"You're not changed."

He felt like replying, "I was, till I saw you again," but instead, he stood up abruptly and glanced around at the untidy, sweltering park.

"This is horrible. Why shouldn't we go out a little on the bay? There's a breeze, and it will be cooler. We might take the steamboat down to Point Arley."

She glanced up at him hesitantly, and he pressed on: "On a Monday morning there won't be anyone on the boat. My train doesn't leave till evening; I'm going back to New York. Why shouldn't we?" He insisted, looking down at her, and suddenly broke out: "Haven't we done all we could?"

"Oh"—she murmured again.

She stood up and reopened her sunshade, glancing about as if consulting the scene to ensure the impossibility of staying. Then her eyes returned to his face.

"You mustn't say things like that to me," she said. "I'll say anything you like; or nothing. I won't open my mouth unless you tell me to."

"What harm can it do to anybody?" he stammered.

She pulled out a small gold watch on an enamelled chain.

"Oh, don't calculate," he broke out. "Give me the day! I want to get you away from that man. At what time was he coming?"

Her color rose again. "At eleven."

"Then you must come at once."

"You needn't be afraid—if I don't come."

"Nor you either—if you do. I swear I only want to hear about you, to know what you've been doing. It's a hundred years since we've met—it may be another hundred before we meet again."
