He lingered on the curb, submerged in a tide of misery, watching her silhouette shrink until it vanished entirely from view. Yet, the moment she disappeared, a sudden, jarring lightness severed the heavy chain around his spirit. She had granted him his liberty; indeed, technically, he had never truly forfeited it, but the time for semantic squabbles was past. Freedom was absolute, and the path forward, previously obscured, now blazed with terrifying clarity. A swift, intoxicating buoyancy seized him, transforming into a pure, unrestrained rejoicing in his autonomy. By the time he was halfway home, the decision was made.

The vicar of the parish, where his lodging stood, resided a mere ten-minute walk from the square. Oleron turned his steps directly toward the clergyman's residence. Information was vital regarding the dilapidated house, marked by insurance placards and sagging "To Let" signs; the vicar was the most probable repository for such local lore. Having completed this preliminary inquiry, a spark of wicked anticipation ignited within him—a chuckle bubbled up at the prospect of what was to follow.

However, the vicar yielded less data than Oleron had coveted. The house was ancient, the vicar noted, a fact requiring no sermon to establish. More intriguingly, it was rumored to be haunted—an accusation Oleron heard with sharpened attention. Yet, the reverend, speaking with the soothing, evasive cadence of a man who fears offending his listener, dismissed the specter as mere superstition born of the modern world's waning faith. Oleron smiled, recognizing the pastor's discomfort.

"You may leave your nerves out of this equation," Oleron interjected gently. "Simply, how long has the property stood vacant?"

"A dozen years, if I judge correctly," the vicar replied.

"And the final occupant—do you recall him, or perhaps her?" Oleron asked, feeling a distinct, prickling heat crawl beneath his skin as he proffered the gender variable.

"Him," the vicar confirmed. "A gentleman named Madley. An artist, by trade, and a profound recluse who rarely ventured beyond the premises..." The cleric paused, then broke into a sudden, candid rush. "Since you seek this truth, and since it is preferable that facts remain unvarnished rather than distorted by gossip, let me confess it openly. Mr. Madley perished within those walls under circumstances that defied convention. The post-mortem revealed a stomach utterly devoid of sustenance, yet his pockets contained ample coin. His frame was reduced to skeletal ruin. While suicide was considered, deliberate starvation remains, to put it mildly, an aberrant mode of ending one's life. Consequently, an open verdict was rendered."

"Ah!" Oleron breathed. "Do you possess any comprehensive chronicle of this parish?"

"Not a complete one; only fragments. I have maintained various notes on the ecclesiastical history, the registers, and the like, which I would be delighted to display if you wish to consult them. However, this is a vast parish, I am supported by but a single curate, and my leisure hours, as you can easily surmise, are limited..."

The discussion devolved into the geography of the parish and the scarcity of the vicar's free time. Oleron thanked the clergyman, departed, and ambled homeward at a measured pace.

He strolled deliberately, doubling back from the house twice within a stone's throw of the gate, detouring along paths that consumed twenty minutes each. He required a state of absolute mental uncluttering, a vessel emptied of all prior cargo, to perceive the location exactly as he had upon that fateful morning when, having cleared his studio, he had begun the sixteenth chapter of the first *Romilly*.

If he could resurrect that primal impression, he believed he could extract far greater mysteries. Previously, his mind had been encumbered by a mountain of intellectual baggage. Before the influence of the place could penetrate his defenses, it had been stymied by the sheer inertia of those dreary, unfinished chapters. No results had emerged; the process had been a slow, agonizing saturation, filling a cup already near brimming. But now, he was light, unburdened, stripped finally of both *Romilly* and her shadow prototype. Now, the stage was set for the new enigma—the coy, jealous, bewitching Beckoning Fair One.

At 2:30 PM, he inserted his key into the Yale lock, stepped inside, and sealed the door behind him.

His audacious experiment succeeded with startling immediacy. He could have shouted in triumph as he entered the room; it felt as though he had *escaped* into it. Just as in the golden days when his prose possessed a daily freshness and boundless promise, he felt a renewed ease, a masterful exhilaration, a total release. The air of the room seemed charged with extra oxygen; his specific gravity appeared altered, his footsteps falling with a lightness that bordered on the ethereal. The flowers in their bowls, the exquisite proportion of the meadow-sweet-hued panelling and cornices, the gleaming floor, and the lofty, faintly star-speckled ceiling, seemed to laugh a silent, welcoming greeting. Oleron laughed back, his voice ringing aloud.

"Oh, you're quite pretty, quite pretty!" he cooed, flattering the silence.

Then, he collapsed onto the divan.

He spent the afternoon like a convalescent awaiting a cherished guest—in a delicious, hollow vacuum, smiling occasionally as if in a dream, raising drowsy, contented eyes to his seductive surroundings. Thus he lay until the darkness fell, accompanied by the nocturnal symphonies of the ancient house.

Yet, when he awaited a specific manifestation, he waited in vain.

Similarly, the following morning saw him waiting in futile expectation, striving to maintain that sensitized-plate state of mind. Nothing registered. Whatever entity he so patiently courted seemed both shy and fastidious.

It was not until the third day that comprehension dawned. A look of gentle, cunning amusement softened his features, and he chuckled softly.

"Oho, oho! ... Well, if the wind blows from *that* quarter, we shall discover what other measures are requisite. What lies there, now? ... Nay, I shall not summon Elsie; we require no wheel to crush a butterfly upon; we shall not resort to such extremes, my little butterfly...."

Standing there, thumbing his gaunt jaw and casting an oblique glance, he suddenly strode to the hall, retrieved his hat, and descended the stairs.

"My lady is coquettish, is she? Well, we shall observe the effects of a modicum of neglect," he chuckled, stepping out into the street.

He located a railway station, boarded a train, and devoted the remainder of the day to wandering the countryside. Indeed, Oleron considered himself the adept in handling Fair Ones who beckoned, extended invitations, and then retreated into the shadows of shyness and hesitation!

He did not return until well past eleven that night.

"Now," he murmured, pacing the alley and reaching for his keys, "my Fair Beckoner!"

Within his flat, he remained perfectly composed, deliberately calculated, exceedingly cautious lest he betray himself. To suggest an intention for immediate retirement, he lit but a solitary candle; as he embarked on his nightly circuit, he feigned a yawn. First, he entered the kitchen. A full moon hung high, casting a lozenge of moonlight—peacock-blue in stark contrast to the warm glow of his candleframe—upon the floor. The window stood uncurtained, allowing him to catch the reflection of the flame, and faintly, the ghost of his own visage, as he moved. The door of the powder-closet stood slightly ajar; he shut it firmly before sitting to remove his boots from the chair cushioned with the folded harp-bag. Passing from the kitchen, he traversed the bathroom. There, another diagonal slash of azure moonlight cut across the windowsill, illuminating the pipes along the wall. He visited his seldom-trodden study, pausing a moment to gaze at the silvered roofs stretching across the square. Then, proceeding straight through the sitting-room, his bare feet making no sound, he entered the bedroom and deposited the candle upon the chest of drawers. Throughout this ritual, his face bore no expression save a mask of weary resignation. In truth, he had never been more sly, nor more hyper-alert.

His small bedroom fireplace stood opposite the chest of drawers where the mirror rested, while his bed and the window claimed the remaining sides of the chamber. Oleron pulled down the blinds, shed his coat, and stooped to retrieve his slippers from beneath the bed.

He could offer no rational justification, yet he never, for a single second, doubted that the manifestation, withheld for two days, hovered imminently. Nor, though he could not conjecture its shape, did he feel terror. Shocking or surprising it might be; he was prepared for that. But that was all; his scale of sensation had been depressed. His hand moved hither and thither beneath the bed, seeking the slippers....

Yet, despite his caution, his method, and his readiness, his heart leaped and paused with a horror almost physical. His hand had grasped the slippers, but he remained on his knees; absent this circumstance, he would have toppled.
