Dark had loosened its hold, not into day but into that kitchen-hour of the world when the hearth is raked and the first loaf is only beginning to remember it has yeast in its heart. We walked in a dim twilight, and the air had the smell of spring turned inside out—earth damp as washed roots, sap rising, crushed thyme where our feet went, and somewhere far off the bruised sweetness of gorse. It was as if the land had set a pot on the fire in the night and now lifted the lid for us alone.

Or perhaps the marvel was in us. With no honest light in the sky to explain anything, everything was nevertheless clear: every heather-bush with its purple bells, each small shrub, every blade of grass. They did not merely catch light; they seemed to trade it, as neighbors trade starter—each giving a little of what it had, each receiving a little more than it expected. Nothing threw a shadow. The very act of looking was like tasting: my sight had become a tongue, and the world offered itself to it.

Each growing thing showed me its inward recipe—its shape and color declaring the thought that held it together, the way a grandmother’s bread announces its method the moment you break it: not by lecture, but by crumb. The springing green did not say, I am grass; it said, I am the idea of grass made edible to the sun. I stepped barefoot, and my feet—so long acquainted with stone and thorn—loved every plant they trod upon as if each were a familiar herb returned to the pot after long exile. The world’s life and mine were one broth: the macrocosm simmering, the microcosm fed, and at last both tasting of the same hidden fire. To be aware of a thing was to know its life and mine at once; to know whence we came and where we were at home; to know we are what we are because Another is what He is.

Sense after sense woke in me like old dialects remembered at the table—words for tastes you had forgotten could be named, and then discover no common language holds them. I had new doors in my being and everything came in: fragrance like warm grain, texture like cool water over wrist, and other senses that had no human term, because there had never been, in our thin lexicons, room enough for such plenty. I understood then how a language can die—not with a bang but with a shrug—simply because it cannot carry what its people no longer dare to feel.

A little breeze brushed a heather-bush, and its purple bells rang—no harsh clink of metal, but a sweet, quick tin-tinning like tiny spoons tapping the rim of a glass while someone laughs in the next room. The sound wasn’t merely heard; it entered the body as nourishment. I was in the joy of the bells, in the joy of the breeze, in the joy of the hearing that received them. The world’s music was not entertainment sold back to us in bright packets; it was an ecology—each living thing keeping time for the others, each note making a shelter for the mind. No engine roared, no iron shriek cut across the living weave. In that clean soundscape, my thoughts stood up straighter, as if relieved of a constant assault.

I felt like a quiet ocean with a ground-swell of joy continually lifting new waves—always new forms, the same deep sweetness. Life was a holiday, but not a careless one: a feast prepared through long labor, through night work, through hidden kneading. I knew then that life and truth are one; that life, mere and pure, is bliss; and that where being is not bliss, it is not life but life-in-death. Every breath I drew was like steam rising from a pot you have tended for hours: gratitude with a scent. At last I was. I lived, and nothing could touch my life.

My darling walked beside me, and we were on our way home to the Father. So much was ours before even the first sun rose upon our freedom—what then must the eternal day bring with it?

We came to the fearful hollow where once the monsters of the earth had wallowed. It was as I had beheld it in my dream—a lovely lake. The air above it was cool and clean, edged with the mineral tang of deep water. I leaned and gazed into its pellucid depths as into a clear bowl set on a table.

A whirlpool had scoured out the soil in which the abortions once burrowed, and at the bottom lay visible the whole horrid brood. A dim greenish light pervaded the crystalline water and showed every hideous form beneath, as if the lake itself kept a lantern to expose what must not be forgotten. Coiled in spires, folded in layers, knotted on themselves or stretched long and large, they lay in motionless heaps—fantasies of ghoulish dismay, more obscene than any fevered mind could brew. Tentacles and tumid bulges, glaring sepian orbs and wicked heads—each head a flower of wrongness bursting from an abominable stalk—made a garden no one would harvest.

Not one of them moved as we passed. But they were not dead. So long as there exist men and women of unwholesome mind, so long as a dominant tongue can name cruelty “order” and call the stripping of memory “progress,” that lake will be peopled with loathsomenesses. Some evils wait in silence the way rot waits in a bin: it does not need motion; it needs only neglect.

But listen—the herald of the sun: the auroral wind softly trumpeting his approach. The first light came not as glare but as sound and warmth together, like a distant oven-door opening. Heaping before his prow a huge ripple-fretted wave of crimson and gold, the sun rushed aloft as if newly launched into the upper sea, paused, and looked down on the world. White-raving storm of molten metals, he was yet but a coal from the altar of the Father’s never-ending sacrifice to his children.

Every little flower straightened its stalk, lifted up its neck, and stood expectant. Something more than the sun, greater than the light, was coming—coming—none the less surely because long upon the road. What are to-day, to-morrow, ten thousand years to Life himself, to Love himself? He is coming, and the necks of all humanity are stretched out to see Him. Every morning they will thus outstretch themselves; every evening they will droop and wait—until He comes. And I wondered, with a shiver that was half joy and half fear of my own forgetting: is this but a vision drawn in air? When He comes, will He indeed find them watching thus, or will they have traded their longing for some jangling imitation?

It was a resurrection-morning. The night had been spent in preparing it, as a cook spends the dark hours making ready the feast that will look effortless at noon. The children went gamboling before, and the beasts came after us. Butterflies fluttered like colored petals blown from a shaken sieve; dragon-flies darted and hung, blue-green sparks above the damp. They came down upon us like a snow-storm of rainbow flakes, then rose again into the humid air like a rolling vapor of embodied odors—honeysuckle, wet bark, sun-warmed leaf.

It was a summer-day more like itself—more ideal—than ever man who had not died found in any world. I walked on the new earth under the new heaven and found them the same as the old, save that now they opened their minds to me and I saw into them. The soul of everything I met came out to greet me and make friends with me, telling me we came from the same and meant the same. You are going to Him, they said, with whom we always are and whom we always mean; we are lightnings that take shape as we flash from Him to His. Even the dark rocks drank like sponges the rays showered upon them, and then gave back a living warmth, as stones by a fire give out what they have received long after the flame has lowered.

Lona and I were two joy-fires walking side by side. Earth breathed heavenward her sweet-savored smoke—green scent, blossom scent, the clean exhalation of water—and we breathed homeward our longing. For thanksgiving, our very consciousness was that.

We came to the channels that once had been dry and wearyful. Now they ran and flashed and foamed with living water that shouted in its gladness. Far as the eye could see, it was a rushing, roaring, dashing river, made vocal by its rocks. The sound was not noise; it was nourishment. Each plunge and eddy had its own note, each stone a different drumhead, each bank a different hollow for resonance. I thought of the old world where people build cages of clangor, then sell themselves silence in little expensive portions. Here, the river gave freely, and the mind grew quiet under its honest music.

We did not cross it, but walked in glory and in joy up its right bank until we reached the great cataract at the foot of the sandy desert—roaring and swirling and dropping sheer—where the river divided into its two branches. There we climbed the height—and found no desert. Through grassy plains, between grassy banks, flowed the deep, wide, silent river full to the brim, as if the land had been set to proof and found able at last to hold its own abundance.

Then first to the Little Ones was revealed the glory of God in the limpid flow of water. Instinctively they plunged and swam, and the beasts followed them—great bodies entering with the ease of creatures who have never been taught to mistrust their own joy. The desert rejoiced and blossomed as the rose. Wide forests had sprung up; their undergrowth was flowering shrubs peopled with song-birds. Every thicket gave birth to a rivulet, and every rivulet to its water-song—small bright dialects of sound, each untranslatable into the other, yet all belonging to one speech of life. To hear them together was to understand how meaning can be carried without conquest, how difference can be harmony without being flattened into sameness.

The place of the buried hand gave no sign.

Beyond and still beyond, the river came in full volume from afar. Up and up we went, now along grassy margin, now through forest of gracious trees. The grass grew sweeter underfoot, and its flowers more lovely and various as we went—as if the land were laying out course after course, each more delicate, each more itself. The trees grew larger, and the wind fuller of messages—not slogans, not jingles, but the plain speaking of leaves rubbing leaf, bough answering bough, birds stitching their calls into the open air.

At length we came to a forest whose trees were greater, grander, more beautiful than any we had yet seen. Their live pillars upheaved a thick embowed roof, and betwixt their leaves and blossoms hardly a sunbeam filtered—only a green-gold dimness like light passing through a bottle of oil. The air smelled of resin and bruised petals; you could almost taste it on the back of your tongue.

Into the rafters of this aerial vault the children climbed, scrambling and leaping in a land of bloom, shouting to the unseen elephants below and hearing them trumpet their replies. The calls rolled through the timbered hush with a depth that made the chest answer, as if the whole forest were an instrument and the elephants its low strings. The conversations between them Lona understood while I only guessed at them, blunderingly, like a stranger trying to catch the sense of a beloved dialect by rhythm alone.

The Little Ones chased the squirrels, and the squirrels, frolicking, drew them on—always at length allowing themselves to be caught and petted. Often a bird, lovely in plumage and form, would light upon one of them, sing a song of what was coming, and fly away, leaving the air trembling as if it had been touched by a spoon and would keep ringing for a while.

Not one monkey of any sort could they see.
