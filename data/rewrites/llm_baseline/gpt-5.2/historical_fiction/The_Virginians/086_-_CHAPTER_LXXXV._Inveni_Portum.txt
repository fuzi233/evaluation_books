When news reached my mother in Virginia that I had taken employment at home, she received it, I think, as a commander receives a flag of truce—coldly, as if conceding were a kind of defeat. She withdrew her supplies without a word, and I could not but read the intention in the silence: to pinch us, to make our little household feel the edge of want until I should surrender, pack up wife and child, and return across the sea into dependence under her roof and her rule.

We never, to her dying day, spoke plainly of that quarrel in money. She stopped my allowance; I made no appeal; and by shifts, by work, by the patient economies of my wife’s hands, we lived without her aid. Of any repentance on her part I heard nothing directly. Only Harry, in one of those private confidences which he had a brother’s kindness to make, let fall that she had suffered inwardly, and had owned—if not to me, at least to her own conscience—that she had been hard.

In later years, when chance and time brought us face to face again, Madam Esmond’s manner had a new gentleness in it, a studied respect, and—more marked than all—an uncommon, almost ceremonious affection towards my wife. It was as if she wished me to understand, without the humiliation of words, that she knew she had been wrong. She offered no apology. I asked none. Pride stood sentinel between us both; and I am not certain which of us kept the straighter back.

Harry being provided for—his welfare I never grudged—my mother’s savings, her hoardings and schemes, were in the end to my advantage as her heir. There was a time when a handful of guineas would have been more useful to me than the hundreds which might come when I had no need. Yet when the tide turned at last and we had ease, I could afford to remit what I might once have demanded. Remit is the word. Forgive is another thing, and rarer. Some souls, I have seen, forgive as naturally as a herb scents the hand that bruises it. In me there is too much of the old iron. I can pass over; I cannot always soften.

So I made no sign of submission in Virginia yonder; and for years we lived estranged, with only an occasional message passing between the women—some brief announcement of a child’s birth, or a formal courtesy sent at Christmas with a ribboned parcel.

Then came troubles of another sort. The first storms in America about the Stamp Act were not the only ones. In London, too, the air grew sharp. Though in our quarrel with the colonies I have been on the Tory side—as, indeed, I have commonly found myself on the losing side in most controversies—believing the Imperial Government had full right to levy taxes, yet I must needs publish a pert letter to a member of the House of Burgesses in Virginia, in which I handled freely the habitual insolence of the mother-country, and spoke sentiments disagreeable to persons in power. The consequence was prompt: I was deprived of my little place as hackney-coach licenser.

The blow fell with double weight upon my uncle, Sir Miles Warrington. No man ever loved respectability more devoutly, or feared disgrace as if it were a contagion. He had grown to an extreme affection for my wife—who could resist her?—and for my little boy. For myself he entertained a pitying contempt which always infinitely amused me. He had a natural scorn for poverty and a corresponding worship for success. Any opinion that swerved from the beaten track frightened him; truth-telling made him turn pale. Yet, in spite of the shocks I gave him, he continued to come and see Theo and the child, and even me—granting me a mournful recognition, as one gives to a doomed man. He no longer cut me, as he had done at the time of our first differences; he looked on me as a melancholy lost creature, past all worldly help. Never mind. We must cast about for a new scheme of life; and at this juncture the repayment of a debt by Harry enabled me to breathe for some months, even for years. Strange fatuity of youth! How did we dare to be so poor, and yet so little cast down?

In that season his Majesty’s royal uncle of Cumberland fell down and perished in a fit; and, oddly enough, his death occasioned a remarkable change in my fortune.

My poor Sir Miles never missed a court ceremony to which he could thrust himself. Drawing-rooms, christenings, balls, funerals—he was present at all. If ever a prince or princess was ailing, his coach was at their door. Leicester Fields, Carlton House, Gunnersbury—nothing was too far if there was a title to bow to and a hand to kiss. Nothing, therefore, could satisfy him now but to attend the stout Duke’s funeral. He stood bareheaded in the rain through the whole ceremony, caught a great cold and an inflammation of the throat; and one morning—before I had almost heard of his illness—a lawyer waits on me in Bloomsbury, bows, and salutes me by the name of Sir George Warrington.

So party and fear of the future were over. We laid the poor gentleman by the side of his little son, in the family churchyard where so many of his race repose. Little Miles and I were the chief mourners. The tenants bowed and curtseyed with a zeal that had more of prudence than grief. The dowager and her daughter withdrew to Bath presently; and I and my family took possession of the house of which I have now been master these thirty years. Be not too eager, O my son. Have but a little patience, and I too shall sleep under yonder yew-trees, and the people will be tossing up their caps for Sir Miles.

A prosperous country life is easily chronicled, because it repeats itself with the seasons. The steward’s books tell, year by year, what rents were paid and what forgiven; what wheat stood best after beans; what fields were left fallow; how the hedges were laid and the ditches scoured. The house journals note who visited, and how long; what pensioners my wife had, and how she doctored and relieved them, and how they died; what year I was sheriff; how often the hounds met near us.

But those ledgers, though exact as a cook’s scale, can never set down what truly made the place a home: the way Theo governed the kitchen like a small kingdom—quietly, inexorably, and with a tenderness that showed itself in broth and bread long before it found words. In our first years, when we were still learning the estate’s temper as a man learns a horse’s mouth, she would be in the still-room at dawn, listening for the sounds of the house as if the whole building had a pulse: the low crackle of peat; the rooster’s insistence; the soft knock of the dairy-maid; the far, watery music of the pump. There are noises that soothe and noises that harry. In London I had lived with wheels forever grinding the street, with hawkers crying as if they meant to split your skull, with the metallic clang of the city hammering at the nerves. Here the loudest violence was a cart-wheel over a stone, or the gust that set the bare branches creaking. Even the dogs’ bark had a country meaning: not rage for rage’s sake, but warning, welcome, or the simple joy of breath.

We could not afford the fine mansion in Hill Street which my predecessor had occupied; we took a smaller house in town when we must go, and yet contrived to spend more money in it. We made not half the show—fewer liveries, fewer equipages, less plate—than my uncle had been famous for; but our beer was stronger, and my wife’s charities more costly than those of the Dowager Lady Warrington. No doubt her ladyship thought there was no harm in spoiling the Philistines; for she made us pay unconscionably for the goods she left behind her in our country-house; and I submitted to most of her extortions with unutterable good-humour. What a value she imagined her potted plants in the greenhouses bore! What a price she set upon that horrible old spinet in her drawing-room! And the framed pieces of worsted-work performed by the accomplished Dora and the lovely Flora—had they been masterpieces of Titian or Vandyck, she could hardly have rated them higher.

Yet though we paid so generously, though we were—without boasting—far kinder to our poor than ever she had been, for a while we had the very worst reputation in the county. Old stories had been told to my discredit, and they spread like sour yeast in a warm pantry. I thought I might perhaps succeed to my uncle’s seat in Parliament as well as to his land; but I found, I knew not how, that I was voted a person of dangerous opinions. I would not bribe. I would not coerce my own tenants at the election of ’68. A gentleman came down from Whitehall with a pocket-book full of bank-notes; and I discovered that I had no chance against my competitor.

And now that we were at ease in worldly means—now that obedient tenants bowed as we went to church; that we drove to visit friends, or to the neighbouring towns, in the great family coach with the four fat horses—did we not often regret poverty and the dear little cottage at Lambeth, where Want was ever prowling at the door? I found myself, more than once, longing to be bear-leading again, and vowing that translating for booksellers was not such very hard drudgery. When we went to London we made sentimental pilgrimages to our old haunts. I dare say my wife embraced all her landladies. And we asked our friends of those times to share the comforts of our new home.

The Reverend Mr. Hagan and his lady visited us more than once. His appearance in the pulpit at B———, where he preached (as we thought) very finely, caused an awful scandal there. Sampson came too, another unlucky Levite, and was welcome as long as he would stay. Mr. Johnson talked of coming, but put us off once or twice. I suppose our house was dull. I know that I myself would be silent for days; and I fear my moodiness must often have tried the sweetest-tempered woman in the world.

I did not care for field sports. The killing of one partridge was so like the killing of another, I wondered how men could pass day after day in pursuit of that kind of slaughter. Fox-hunting stories began at four o’clock when the cloth was removed, and lasted till supper. I sat silent and listened; day after day I fell asleep. No wonder I was not popular with my company.

What confession is this? Here was the storm over, the rocks avoided, the ship in port—and the sailor not overcontent. Was Susan—whom I had been sighing for during the voyage—not the beauty I had expected to find her?

In the first place, Susan and all the family can look into her William’s logbook; and so, madam, I am not going to put my secrets down there. No, Susan, I never had secrets from thee—so far as concerns fidelity. I never cared for another woman. I have seen more beautiful—Mrs. Carter and Miss Mulso, Mrs. Thrale and Madam Kaufmann, the angelical Gunnings and her Grace of Devonshire, and a host of beauties who were not angelic by any means—and I was not dazzled. I may have led your mother a weary life and been a very Bluebeard over her, but then I had no other heads in the closet.

Only, the first pleasure of taking possession of our kingdom over, I own I began quickly to tire of the crown. When the captain wears it, his Majesty will be a very different Prince. He can ride a-hunting five days in the week and find the sport amusing. He would hear the same sermon fifty times and not yawn more than I do at the first delivery. But sweet Joan, beloved Baucis! being thy faithful husband and true lover always, thy Darby is ashamed of having been testy so often; and, being arrived at the consummation of happiness, Philemon asks pardon for falling asleep so frequently after dinner.

There came a period when, having reached the summit of felicity, I was quite tired of the prospect: I yawned in Eden and said, “Is this all? What, no lions to bite? no rain to fall? no thorns to prick you in the rose-bush when you sit down?—only Eve for ever sweet and tender, and figs for breakfast, dinner, supper, from week’s end to week’s end!”

Shall I make my confessions? Hearken! Well then—if I must make a clean breast of it—

I know the theory and practice of the Roman Church; but, bred of another persuasion, and sceptical and heterodox regarding that, I can’t help doubting the other too, and wondering whether Catholics in their confessions confess all. Do we Protestants ever do so? Does any man confess all?

Does yonder dear creature know all my life—she who has been partner of it for thirty years; who, whenever I have told her a sorrow, has been ready with the best of her gentle power to soothe it; who has watched when I did not speak, and when I was silent has been silent too, or else (with the charming hypocrisy of woman) has worn smiles and an easy face to make me fancy she felt no care, or would not disturb her lord’s secret when he seemed to wish it private?

Oh, the dear hypocrite! Have I not watched her hiding the boys’ peccadilloes from papa’s anger? Have I not known her cheat out of her housekeeping to pay off their little extravagances, and talk to me with an artless face as if she did not know that our revered captain had had dealings with the gentlemen of Duke’s Place, and our learned collegian at term’s end had pressing reasons for sporting his oak against University tradesmen? Why, from the earliest days thou wert forever concealing something from me—this one stealing jam from the cupboard; that one getting into disgrace at school; that naughty rebel (put on the caps, young folks, according to the fit) flinging an inkstand at mamma in a rage, whilst I was told gown and carpet were spoiled by accident.

We all hide from one another. We have all secrets. We are all alone. We sin by ourselves and, let us trust, repent too. Yonder dear woman would give her foot to spare mine a twinge of the gout; but when I have the fit, the pain is in my slipper. The poets end their tales at marriage or death, huzzahing for the couple till the postchaise turns the corner, or fetching the hearse and plumes and shovelling them underground. But when Mr. Random and Mr. Thomas Jones are married, is all over? Are there no quarrels at home—no Lady Bellastons abroad—no constables to outrun—no temptations to conquer, or be conquered by?

The Sirens sang after Ulysses long after his marriage, and suitors whispered in Penelope’s ear; and he and she had weary days of doubt and care. So have we all. As regards money I was put out of trouble by the inheritance; but does not Atra Cura sit behind baronets as well as equites?

My friends in London used to congratulate me on my happiness. Who would not like to be master of a good house and a good estate? But can Gumbo shut the hall-door upon blue devils, or lay them always in a red sea of claret? Does a man sleep the better who has four-and-twenty hours to doze in? Do his intellects brighten after a sermon from the dull old vicar; after ten minutes’ cackle and flattery from the village apothecary; after Sir John and Sir Thomas and their ladies, who come ten moonlight muddy miles to eat a haunch and play a rubber?

There are pleasures in precedence—room made for you at quarter-sessions; tradesmen bowing at your carriage door; my lady wife taken down the second or third to dinner. But these pleasures fade—nay, have their inconveniences. For seven years after we came to Warrington Manor, our two what they called best neighbours were my Lord Tutbury and Sir John Mudbrook. We are of an older date than the Mudbrooks; consequently my Lady Tutbury always fell to my lot at table—deaf and asleep after dinner; or if I had Lady Mudbrook, she chattered with a folly so incessant and intense that even my wife—consummate hypocrite—could scarce keep her complacency, knowing the rage with which I fumed at the other’s clatter.

I came to London; I showed my tongue to Dr. Heberden; I poured out my catalogue of complaints. “Psha, my dear Sir George!” says the unfeeling physician. “Headaches, languor, bad sleep, bad temper—” (“Not bad temper: Sir George has the sweetest temper in the world, only he is sometimes a little melancholy,” says my wife.) “—Bad sleep, bad temper,” continues the implacable doctor. “My dear lady, his inheritance has been his ruin; and a little poverty and a great deal of occupation would do him all the good in life.”

No—my brother Harry ought to have been the squire, with remainder to my son Miles, of course. Harry’s letters were full of gaiety and good spirits. His estate prospered; his negroes multiplied; his crops were large; he was a member of the House of Burgesses; he adored his wife; could he but have a child his happiness would be complete. Had Hal been master of Warrington Manor in my place, he would have been beloved through the whole country: steward at the races, gayest of jolly huntsmen, the bien venu at every mansion round about—where folk scarce cared to perform the ceremony of welcome at sight of my glum face. As for my wife, all the world liked her, and agreed in pitying her. I know not how the report got abroad, but it was generally agreed that I treated her with awful cruelty, and that for jealousy I was a perfect Bluebeard.

Ah me! It is true I have had many dark hours; that I pass days in long silence; that the talk of fools and whipper-snappers makes me rebellious and peevish; that when I feel contempt I sometimes do not know how to conceal it—or did not. I hope, as I grow older, I grow more charitable. Because I do not love bawling and galloping after a fox like the captain yonder, I am not his superior; in this respect I humbly own he is mine. He has perceptions denied me; enjoyments I cannot understand. Because I am blind, the world is not dark. I try now and listen with respect when Squire Codgers talks of the day’s run. I do my best to laugh when Captain Rattleton tells garrison stories. I step up to the harpsichord with old Miss Humby (our neighbour from Beccles) and try to listen as she warbles her ancient ditties. I play whist laboriously. Am I not trying to do the duties of life? And I have a right to be garrulous and egotistical, because I have been reading Montaigne all the morning.

I was not surprised, knowing by what influences my brother was led, to find his name among the Virginia burgesses who declared that the sole right of imposing taxes on the inhabitants of the colony is, and ever hath been, vested in the House of Burgesses, and who called upon the other colonies to pray for royal interposition in favour of the violated rights of America.

And it was now—after we had been some three years settled in our English home—that a correspondence between us and Madam Esmond began again. It was my wife who, upon some pretext such as women always know how to find, re-established relations. Mr. Miles must needs have the small-pox; he miraculously recovered, without losing any portion of his beauty; and upon his recovery the mother wrote her prettiest wheedling letter to the grandmother of the fortunate babe.

She coaxed with modest phrases and humble offerings of respect and goodwill. She narrated anecdotes of the boy’s precocious genius (what hath since happened, I wonder, to stop the growth of that gallant young officer’s brains?), and she must have sent a lock of the darling’s hair; for the old lady, in her reply, acknowledged receipt of such a present. I wonder, as it came from England, they allowed it to pass the custom-house at Williamsburg.

In return for these peace-offerings and smuggled tokens of submission came a tolerably gracious letter from my Lady of Castlewood. She inveighed against the dangerous spirit pervading the colony; she lamented to think her unhappy son was consorting with people who, she feared, would be no better than rebels and traitors. She did not wonder, considering who his friends and advisers were. How could a wife taken from an almost menial situation be expected to sympathise with persons of rank and dignity who had the honour of the Crown at heart?

If evil times were coming for the monarchy—for the folks in America appeared disinclined to pay taxes, and required everything should be done for them without cost—she remembered how, to monarchs in misfortune, the Esmonds (her father the Marquis especially) had ever been faithful. She knew not what opinions—though she might judge from my newfangled Lord Chatham—were in fashion in England. She prayed, at least, she might hear that one of her sons was not on the side of rebellion.

When we came, in after days, to look over old family papers in Virginia, we found “Letters from my daughter Lady Warrington” neatly tied up with a ribbon. My Lady Theo insisted I should not open them; and the truth, I believe, is that they were so full of praises of her husband that she thought my vanity would suffer from reading them.

When Madam began to write she gave us brief notices of Harry and his wife. “The two women,” she wrote, “still govern everything with my poor boy at Fannystown (as he chooses to call his house). They must save money there, for I hear but a shabby account of their manner of entertaining. The Mount Vernon gentleman continues to be his great friend, and he votes in the House of Burgesses very much as his guide advises him.

“Why he should be so sparing of his money I cannot understand: I heard of five negroes who went with his equipages to my Lord Bottetourt’s; only two had shoes to their feet. I had reasons to save, having sons for whom I wished to provide, but he hath no children, wherein he certainly is spared from much grief; though, no doubt, Heaven in its wisdom means our good by the trials which, through our children, it causes us to endure. His mother-in-law has been ailing.

“Ever since his marriage, my poor Henry has been the creature of these two artful women, and they rule him entirely. Nothing, my dear daughter, is more contrary to common sense and to Holy Scripture than this. Are we not told, Wives, be obedient to your husbands? Had Mr. Warrington lived, I should have endeavoured to follow up that sacred precept, holding that nothing so becomes a woman as humility and obedience.”
