Loves of my youth, where have you fled? Tubby, with hair like spun gold; Langley, sporting a nose permanently indented; Shamus, stout of spirit yet frail of leg, easy to knock down but impossible to coax into admitting defeat; Neal, the lanky one; and Dicky, "Dicky Dick" the portly; Ballett, keeper of the weeping eye; Beau Bunnie, master of countless neckties who invariably clashed in black kid gloves; and all the rest, whose names escape me now though I cherish the memory of our bond, where do your ghostly forms haunt? Could anyone, gazing at us then, foresee a day when I would never again witness your merry countenances, never again hear your shrill, wild greetings, never again feel the warm grip of your ink-stained fingers, never again fight, quarrel, hate, or love you? If told this prophecy, could I have endured the thought?

I think I saw you recently, Tubby. You and I, so often, had mapped the North Pole and probed the mysteries of the Nile's source. Have you forgotten, Tubby, our secret campsite by the lonely waters of the Regent's Park canal? There, amidst our frugal meal of toasted elephant's tongue—often mistaken by the uninitiated for jumbles—the hungry roar of a lion or tiger would break upon our hunters' ears, mingled with the melancholy, long-drawn growl of a polar bear, swelling in volume and impatience until precisely half-past four. Then we would snatch our rifles and, with stealthy tread and every sense alert, march through the jungle... until halted by the spiked fencing of the Zoological Gardens. I felt sure it was you, despite your side-whiskers and the greyness and thinness that had replaced your once-clustering golden locks. You were hurrying down Throgmorton Street, shackled to a small black bag. I should have stopped you, but time was short; I needed to catch a train at Liverpool Street and get shaved on the way. Did you recognize me? You stared at me a little hard, I thought.

Gallant, kindly-hearted Shamus, who once fought for half an hour to save a frog from being skinned, they say is now an Income Tax assessor. A man, it is rumored, possessing a power of disbelief unusual even among Inland Revenue circles, of little faith, lacking the charity that thinks no evil. May Providence guide you to districts other than mine.

Time, Nature's busy handyman, bustles about the many rooms, tidying everything, covering burnt volcanoes with sweet earth, turning age-old debris to use, smoothing the ground above the dead, healing beech bark marred by lovers.

In the beginning, I was far from a favorite with my schoolmates, and trouble first dwelt upon me. Later, men and women generally convince themselves that, whatever else we miss, we secure at least a measure of popularity, for without it, few dare face existence. But where the child suffers more acutely than the adult is in discovering oneself exposed to the cold truth, stripped of the protective clothes of self-deception. My ostracism was painfully plain, and, true to my nature, I brooded over it in silence.

"Can you run?" asked one day a most important personage whose name I have forgotten. He headed the Lower Fourth, a tall youth with a beak-like nose and the manner of one born to authority. Son of a draper in the Edgware Road, his father's failure meant he settled for a lower clerkship in the Civil Service. But to us youngsters, he appeared a Duke of Wellington in embryo, and under different circumstances might perhaps have become one.

"Yes," I answered. It was, in fact, my sole accomplishment, and rumor likely reached his ears.

"Run round the playground twice at your fastest," he commanded. "Let me see you."

I clinched my fists and charged off. How grateful I was to him, the outcast of the class, for speaking to me publicly, I could only demonstrate by my exertions to please. When I drew up before him, panting hard, I saw satisfaction in his gaze.

"Why don't the fellows like you?" he asked bluntly.

If only I could step out of my shyness and speak my real thoughts! "Oh, Lord of the Lower Fourth! Upon whom success—the only success in life worth having—has fallen as from the laps of the gods! You to whom all Lower Fourth hearts turn! Tell me the secret of this popularity. How may I acquire it? No price is too great for me to pay. Vain little egoist that I am, it is the sum of my desires, and will remain so until the long years teach me wisdom. The lack of it embitters all my days. Why does silence fall upon their chattering groups when I approach? Why do they drive me from their games? What shuts me out, repels them? I creep into corners and shed scalding tears of shame. I watch with envy all you to whom this wondrous gift is given. What is your secret? Is it Tommy's swagger? Then I will swagger, too, with an anxious heart, mixed with fear and hope. But why—why, seeing that Tommy admires it, do they wait for me with imitations of cock-a-doodle-do, strutting beside me mimicking a pouter pigeon? Is it Dicky's playfulness? Dicky, who runs away with their balls, snatches caps from heads, springs upon backs when least expected?

"Why should Dicky's reward be laughter, and mine a bloody nose and a widened circle of dislike? I am no heavier than Dicky; if anything, a pound or two lighter. Is it Billy's friendliness? I too would fling my arms about their necks, but from me they angrily wrench themselves free. Is indifference the best plan? I walk apart with a step I try so hard to render careless, but none follows, no friendly arm slips through mine. Should one win by kind offices? Ah, if one could! How I would labor for them. I could do their sums—I am good at sums—write their impositions, gladly take their punishments, would they but return my service with a little love—and more importantly—a little admiration."

But all I could utter, sulkily, was: "They do like me, some of them." I dared not, aloud, acknowledge the truth.

"Don't tell lies," he answered. "You know they don't—not one of them." And I hung my head.

"I'll tell you what I'll do," he continued in his lordly tone. "I'll give you a chance. We start hare and hounds next Saturday; you can be a hare. You needn't tell anybody. Just turn up on Saturday, and I'll arrange it. Mind, you'll have to run like the devil."

He walked away without waiting for my answer, leaving me to meet Joy running toward me with outstretched hands. The great moment comes to all of us: to the politician when the Party whip slips from confabulation to congratulate him on his admirable speech; to the youthful dramatist reading the manager's note asking him to call that morning at eleven; to the subaltern beckoned to his chief's stirrup—the moment when the sun breaks through the morning mists, and the world stretches before us, our way clear.

Obeying orders, I gave no hint in school of the great fortune that had befallen me; but hurrying home, I exploded in the passage before the front door could close.

"I am to be a hare because I run so fast. Anybody can be a hound, but there are only two hares, and they both want me. And may I have a jersey? We begin next Saturday. He saw me run. I ran twice round the playground. He said I was splendid! Of course, it's a great honor to be a hare. We start from Hampstead Heath. And may I have a pair of shoes?"

The jersey and shoes my mother and I purchased that very day, for fear that unless we hurried, the last blue-and-white striped jersey in London might sell, and the market empty of running shoes. That evening, before bed, I dressed in full costume to admire myself in the glass; and from then until the end of the week, to the terror of my mother, I practiced leaping over chairs, and my method of descending stairs became perilous and roundabout. But, as I explained, the credit of the Lower Fourth was at stake, and banisters and legs were of small account compared with fame and honor. My father nodded support, backing me with manly argument; but my mother added another line to her prayers.

Saturday arrived. The hunt members were mostly local boys, so the arrangement was to meet at the turnpike gate outside Spaniards at half-past two. I brought lunch and ate it in Regent's Park, then took the bus to the Heath. One by one, the others arrived. Beyond mere glances, none paid me any notice. I wore my ordinary clothes over my jersey. I knew they thought I had come merely to watch them start, and I hugged to myself the dream of the surprise I held for them, wherein I should be the hero. He came, one of the last, our leader and chief, and I sidled up behind him, waiting while he organized and constructed.

"But we've only got one hare," cried one of them. "We ought to have two, you know, in case one gets blown."
