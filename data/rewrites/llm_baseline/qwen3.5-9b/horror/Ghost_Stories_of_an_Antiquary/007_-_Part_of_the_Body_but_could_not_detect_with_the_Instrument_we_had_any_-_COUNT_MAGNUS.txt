By what means the papers out of which I have constructed this narrative came into my possession remains the final mystery these pages intend to solve. However, before presenting the extracts themselves, it is imperative to establish the provenance of the documents.

They comprise, in part, a series of travelogues typical of the mid-nineteenth century—volumes akin to Horace Marryat's *Journal of a Residence in Jutland and the Danish Isles*. Such works typically chronicled obscure districts on the Continent, illustrated with woodcuts or steel engravings, and offered practical details regarding hotel accommodations and transport, alongside anecdotes gleaned from intelligent foreigners, spirited innkeepers, and loquacious peasants. In essence, they were chatty affairs.

My own collection, initially conceived as raw material for such a publication, gradually evolved into a continuous record of a singular personal experience, maintained until the very eve of its conclusion.

The author was Mr. Wraxall. My knowledge of him rests solely on the testimony of his writings, from which I infer he was a man of mature years, possessing private means yet existing in profound solitude. He held no permanent domicile in England, drifting instead between hotels and boarding houses. While he presumably harbored hopes of eventual settlement, these never materialized. It is highly probable that the catastrophic fire at the Pantechnicon in the early 1870s consumed significant portions of his background, for he makes occasional references to property stored at that establishment.

Furthermore, Mr. Wraxall had previously published a work detailing a holiday in Brittany. Beyond this, bibliographical research suggests his output was either anonymous or penned under a pseudonym.

Regarding his character, a superficial assessment reveals an intelligent and cultivated intellect. He appears to have been a candidate for Fellowship at Brasenose College, Oxford. His fatal flaw, however, was an insatiable inquisitiveness—a virtue in a traveler, but a vice that ultimately cost him dearly.

On his final expedition, Mr. Wraxall plotted a new book. Scandinavia, then little known to the English public, struck him as a fertile ground. Having discovered old Swedish histories and memoirs, he conceived the idea of a travelogue interspersed with episodes from the annals of great Swedish families. Securing introductions to persons of quality in Sweden, he departed for the region in the early summer of 1863.

Details of his northern travels and his extended stay in Stockholm need not detain us. Suffice it to say that a local scholar directed him toward a significant collection of family papers belonging to the proprietors of an ancient manor-house in Västergötland, granting him permission to inspect them.

The manor, known locally as Råbäck (pronounced roughly "Roe-beck"), though bearing a different formal title, stands as one of the finest examples of its kind in the countryside. An engraving in Dahlenberg's *Suecia antiqua et moderna* (1694) depicts it virtually unchanged from the present day. Constructed shortly after 1600, it resembles an English house of the same era in both material—red brick with stone facings—and architectural style. Its founder was a descendant of the illustrious House of De la Gardie, whose lineage retains ownership to this day.

The De la Gardies received Mr. Wraxall with immense kindness and hospitality, urging him to reside within the manor house throughout his research. Yet, preferring independence and distrusting his limited Swedish conversational abilities, he chose to settle at the village inn, which proved comfortably adequate during the summer. This arrangement necessitated a daily walk of slightly less than a mile to and from the manor.

The house stood within a park, surrounded by ancient timber. Adjacent lay a walled garden, beyond which stretched a dense woodland bordering one of the numerous small lakes scattered across the landscape. Further on rose the boundary wall of the demesne, guarding a steep knoll lightly draped in soil. Crowning this elevation stood the church, enclosed by tall, dark trees. To the English eye, it presented a peculiar visage: the nave and aisles were low, packed with pews and galleries. In the western gallery rested a handsome, brightly painted organ with silver pipes. The ceiling was flat, adorned by a seventeenth-century artist with a grotesque and terrifying "Last Judgment"—a scene teeming with lurid flames, crumbling cities, burning vessels, screaming souls, and grinning brown demons. Brass crowns hung from the rafters; the pulpit resembled a dollhouse covered in tiny painted wooden cherubs and saints; even the preacher's desk featured a stand holding three hourglasses. While such features are common in Swedish churches today, this edifice possessed a unique addition: at the eastern end of the north aisle, the manor's builder had erected a mausoleum for himself and his kin.

This structure was a substantial octagonal building, illuminated by a row of oval windows, capped by a domed roof surmounted by a pumpkin-like spire—a favorite motif of Swedish architects. The exterior roof was copper, painted black, contrasting sharply with the stark white walls shared with the church. Access to this mausoleum was strictly separate from the church; it possessed its own portal and steps on the northern side.

Beyond the churchyard lies the path to the village, a mere three or four minutes' walk to the inn.

On his first day at Råbäck, Mr. Wraxall discovered the church door ajar. He recorded observations of the interior, noting the fine marble effigies and copper sarcophagi visible through the keyhole of the mausoleum, along with abundant armorial bearings, which ignited a fierce desire to investigate further.

The family papers he sought were precisely what he required. They included correspondence, journals, and meticulously kept account books of the estate's earliest owners, rich in amusing and vivid detail. The first Count Magnus appeared in these records as a formidable and capable leader. Following the construction of the mansion, the district suffered a period of unrest; peasants rose in rebellion, attacking several castles. The owner of Råbäck played a prominent role in suppressing the uprising, resulting in the execution of ringleaders and brutal punishments meted out without mercy.

A portrait of this Magnus de la Gardie hung prominently in the house. Mr. Wraxall studied it with keen interest, noting that the face impressed him not by beauty or benevolence, but by sheer power. Indeed, he described Count Magnus as an almost phenomenally ugly man.

That evening, Mr. Wraxall dined with the family and returned to the inn in the late, yet still bright, twilight.

"I must remember," he wrote, "to ask the sexton if he can grant me entry to the mausoleum. Evidently, he possesses access, for I observed him tonight standing on the steps, seemingly locking or unlocking the door."

Early the following morning, Mr. Wraxall engaged in a lengthy conversation with his landlord. The extent of his recording surprised me initially, but I soon realized these documents served as the foundation for the book he contemplated—a quasi-journalistic work blending observation with dialogue.

His objective was to ascertain whether traditions concerning Count Magnus de la Gardie persisted in the regions of his former dominion and whether the popular estimation of the count remained favorable. He learned that the Count was decidedly unpopular. Tenants arriving late to their labor faced the wooden horse, flogging, or branding in the manor yard. There were accounts of individuals whose lands encroached on the lord's domain having their homes mysteriously burned down on winter nights, with the entire family inside. However, what weighed heavily on the innkeeper's mind—and which he revisited repeatedly—was the rumor that the Count had undertaken the "Black Pilgrimage" and returned with something, or someone, in tow.

One naturally wonders what the Black Pilgrimage entailed. Yet, as Mr. Wraxall experienced, this curiosity must remain suspended for the time being. The landlord, evasive and reluctant to provide a full explanation—or indeed any answer whatsoever—excused himself abruptly, claiming an urgent summons to Skara, promising to return only in the evening.

Thus, Mr. Wraxall departed unsatisfied. His attention shifted immediately to the papers awaiting examination, specifically the correspondence between Sophia Albertina in Stockholm and her married cousin, Ulrica Leonora, at Råbäck, spanning the years 1705–1710. These letters offered exceptional insight into the cultural milieu of that era in Sweden.

By afternoon, having completed his review of these missives and returned the storage boxes to their shelves, Mr. Wraxall selected volumes adjacent to them for his next focus. The shelf contained primarily account books penned by the first Count Magnus. Among them, however, lay not an account book, but a volume of alchemical and esoteric tracts in a sixteenth-century hand. Unfamiliar with such literature, Mr. Wraxall devoted considerable space cataloging the titles: *The Book of the Phoenix*, *The Book of the Thirty Words*, *The Book of the Toad*, *The Book of Miriam*, *Turba Philosophorum*, and others. Eventually, he announced with evident delight his discovery, on a formerly blank leaf near the center of the volume, of a passage written by Count Magnus himself, titled *"Liber nigræ peregrinationis"* (Book of the Black Pilgrimage). Though brief, the fragment sufficed to confirm the landlord's earlier allusions to a belief dating back to the Count's time, likely shared by him. The translation reads:
