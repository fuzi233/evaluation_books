Descending the steps to the front door, I encountered Sergeant Cuff. It chafed against my nature, following the friction we had recently shared, to display any semblance of concern for his inquiries. Yet, despite my better judgment, a compelling interest took hold of me that I could not stave off. My sense of personal dignity crumbled, and the words escaped me unbidden: "Any news from Frizinghall?"

"I have spoken with the Indians," Cuff replied, his tone steady. "I have also uncovered what Rosanna purchased privately in the town last Thursday. The three men will be released on Wednesday next week. There is no shadow of doubt in my mind, nor in Mr. Murthwaite's, that these men descended upon this estate with the intent to steal the Moonstone. Of course, their plans were thwarted by the events within the house on Wednesday night; consequently, they bear no responsibility for the actual loss of the gem, any more than you do. However, I must impart a singular truth, Mr. Betteredge: if _we_ fail to locate the Moonstone, _they_ certainly will not. You have not heard the final word regarding the trio of jugglers."

Just as these startling words hung in the air, Mr. Franklin returned from his walk. Governing his curiosity with a discipline I lacked, he passed us silently and retreated into the house.

Having already surrendered my pride, I resolved to extract the full value of this revelation. "Enough concerning the Indians," I pressed. "Pray, what of Rosanna?"

Sergeant Cuff shook his head slowly.

"The mystery surrounding her has grown denser," he observed. "I have traced her to a shop in Frizinghall, maintained by a linen draper named Maltby. She visited no other drapery, millinery, or tailor establishments; at Maltby's, she purchased nothing save a length of coarse fabric. She was notably particular in selecting a specific grade. As for quantity, she acquired sufficient material to construct a single nightgown."

"Whose nightgown?" I demanded.

"Her own, undoubtedly," the Sergeant affirmed. "Sometime between noon and three o'clock on Thursday morning, she must have slipped down to your young lady's chamber to finalize the concealment of the Moonstone while the household slept. Upon returning to her quarters, her gown inevitably brushed against the wet paint on the door. She could not cleanse the stain, nor could she safely destroy the garment without first procuring a replacement to balance her laundry inventory."

"What evidence suggests it was Rosanna's gown?" I countered.

"The material she bought for the substitute dress," the Sergeant explained. "Had it been Miss Verinder's gown, she would have required lace, frills, and countless other embellishments; she would not have possessed the time to fashion it overnight. This plain, unadorned cloth signifies a servant's nightgown. Every detail is clear, Mr. Betteredge. The crux of the matter remains: why, after securing a replacement, did she conceal the stained gown rather than destroy it? If the girl refuses to speak, there is but one recourse. The hiding-place at the Shivering Sand must be searched, and the truth revealed therein."

"How do you intend to locate the spot?" I inquired.

"I regret to inform you," the Sergeant said, "that this is a secret I intend to retain."

(To spare your curiosity, as I spared his, I may note that he had returned from Frizinghall armed with a search warrant. His extensive experience indicated that Rosanna likely carried a memorandum of the location, intended to guide her should she return under altered circumstances. Possession of this document would furnish the Sergeant with all necessary leads.)

"Now, Mr. Betteredge," he continued, "let us abandon speculation and engage in business. I instructed Joyce to monitor Rosanna. Where is Joyce?"

Joyce, the local constable assigned to Cuff's service, was expected. The clock struck two precisely as the query was posed, and punctually, the carriage arrived to escort Miss Rachel to her aunt's residence.

"One step at a time," the Sergeant interjected, halting me before I dispatched a search party. "I must attend to Miss Verinder first."

Since the rain remained a threat, the enclosed carriage designated for Miss Rachel had been arranged. Sergeant Cuff signaled Samuel to descend from the rumble seat behind.

"You will observe a friend of mine concealed among the trees, near the lodge gate," the Sergeant directed. "He awaits you. Without halting the carriage, he will climb into the rumble with you. Your sole duty is to remain silent and close your eyes. Any deviation will bring trouble."

With this instruction, he dismissed the footman to his station. I suspected what lay ahead, though Samuel's thoughts remained unknown. It was evident that Miss Rachel was to be surveilled privately from the moment she departed—if she indeed departed. A watch placed on my charge! A spy lurking in the shadows of her mother's carriage! I felt a sharp pang of remorse for having allowed myself to speak to the Sergeant in that manner.

The first to emerge from the house was my lady. She positioned herself on the upper step, observing silently. She offered no words to the Sergeant or to me. With lips sealed and arms crossed over the light garden cloak she wore, she stood as motionless as a statue, awaiting her daughter's appearance.

Moments later, Miss Rachel descended. She was attired in a soft yellow fabric that complemented her dark complexion, tailored tightly around her waist. Upon her head sat a neat straw bonnet adorned with a white veil, paired with primrose gloves that fit her hands like a second skin. Her jet-black hair gleamed smoothly beneath the hat, and her ears, resembling rosy shells, bore pearls. She moved swiftly and straight as a lily, her limbs as supple as a young cat. Nothing in her features had altered, except for her eyes and lips. Her gaze burned with a fierce intensity I disliked to witness, and her lips had lost all color and smile, rendering her almost unrecognizable. She kissed her mother briefly and abruptly on the cheek, muttering, "Try to forgive me, mamma," before violently pulling her veil down over her face, tearing it in the process. In an instant, she fled down the steps and rushed into the carriage as if seeking refuge.

Sergeant Cuff acted with equal swiftness. He reseated Samuel and stood before Miss Rachel, the carriage door open, the moment she settled inside.

"What do you wish?" Miss Rachel asked, her voice muffled by the veil.

"I desire to utter a single word before you depart, miss," the Sergeant responded. "I cannot presume to prevent your visit to your aunt. However, I venture to suggest that your departure, given the current circumstances, obstructs my efforts to recover your Diamond. Please comprehend this, and then decide whether you will go or remain."

Miss Rachel offered no reply. "Drive on, James!" she commanded the coachman.

Without further hesitation, the Sergeant closed the carriage door. At that exact moment, Mr. Franklin ran down the steps. "Farewell, Rachel," he called, extending his hand.

"Drive on!" Miss Rachel shrieked, her voice louder than before, paying no heed to Mr. Franklin any more than she had to the Sergeant.

Mr. Franklin stepped back, thunderstruck. The coachman, unsure of his course, glanced toward my lady, who still stood immobile on the upper step. Anger, sorrow, and shame warred across her countenance; she signaled the horses to start, then hastened back into the house. As the carriage rolled away, Mr. Franklin recovered his speech, calling out, "Aunt! You were entirely right. Accept my gratitude for your kindness—and let me depart."

My lady turned as if to respond, but then, seemingly distrustful of herself, waved her hand gently. "Allow me to see you before you leave, Franklin," she said in a fractured voice, retreating once more to her chambers.

"Do me a final favor, Betteredge," Mr. Franklin implored, turning to me with tears in his eyes. "Escort me to the train immediately!"

He too withdrew into the house. For the moment, Miss Rachel had utterly disarmed him. To judge from this, how deeply he must have loved her!

Left face-to-face at the base of the steps, the Sergeant gazed fixedly toward a gap in the trees, commanding a view of a winding path leading from the house. His hands were in his pockets, softly whistling "The Last Rose of Summer."

"There is a season for all things," I said savagely. "This is not a time for whistling."

At that precise instant, the carriage appeared in the distance through the gap, heading toward the lodge gate. Another figure, besides Samuel, was clearly visible in the rumble seat behind.

"All correct!" the Sergeant murmured to himself. He turned to me. "There is no time for whistling, Mr. Betteredge, as you rightly noted. It is time to seize this business, sparring no one. We commence with Rosanna Spearman. Where is Joyce?"

Both of us called for Joyce, receiving no answer. I dispatched a stable-boy to seek him.

"You heard my instructions regarding Miss Rosanna," the Sergeant remarked while we waited. "I bade Joyce keep watch without revealing himself. Do you imply that you have allowed her to escape?"
