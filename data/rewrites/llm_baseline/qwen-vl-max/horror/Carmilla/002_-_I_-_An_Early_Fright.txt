I. An Early Fright

In Styria, we live in a castle—though not a grand one—nestled deep within a wild and ancient forest. It’s called a *schloss*, though the word sounds more noble than our modest circumstances warrant. Here, money stretches far; eight or nine hundred pounds a year does wonders. Back home in England, such an income would barely keep a household going, but here, in this quiet, forgotten corner of the world, it provides comfort without extravagance. My father is English, and I bear an English name, though I’ve never seen England. He served in the Austrian army, retired on a pension and family inheritance, and bought this old feudal estate—a bargain, they said. The land is small, but the place itself is hauntingly beautiful.

The castle sits atop a gentle rise, surrounded by trees that stretch endlessly in every direction. A narrow, weathered road winds past its drawbridge, which has never been raised in my memory. Below, the moat holds perch and swans gliding through still water, their reflections mingling with clusters of white water lilies. Above it all rises the schloss—its many windows staring out like eyes, its towers reaching toward the sky, and its Gothic chapel standing solemn and silent. Before the gate, the forest parts into an irregular glade, and to the right, a steep stone bridge crosses a dark stream that curls through the woods like a serpent in shadow.

This is a lonely place. So lonely that when you stand at the hall door and look down the road, the forest spreads fifteen miles to the right and twelve to the left. The nearest village lies seven English miles away, and the closest inhabited *schloss* with any history is General Spielsdorf’s, nearly twenty miles off. I say “inhabited” because just three miles westward, near Spielsdorf’s estate, lies a ruined village—its little church roofless, its aisle filled with crumbling tombs of the Karnstein family, long since vanished. Their chateau stands abandoned in the heart of the forest, overlooking the silent ruins below. There is a legend about why the village was deserted—dark and strange—and I will tell it to you later. But for now, let me speak of what lives inside these walls.

Our household is tiny. Not counting servants or those who dwell in the outbuildings, only four people make up the family: my father, kind-hearted and aging; myself, then nineteen; Madame Perrodon, my governess from Berne, whose gentle face has been part of my life since I can remember; and Mademoiselle de Lafontaine, a finishing governess who taught me French and German. We spoke in a jumble of tongues—French, broken English, and fluent English from my father and me—creating a chaotic mix that strangers found amusing. And sometimes, there were young friends who visited for weeks or months—girls close to my age, whom I occasionally returned to see. But beyond that, our social world was limited. Neighbors came rarely, and only from within five or six leagues. So, though I had company, I lived much alone.

My governesses had little real control over me—what child could resist when her only parent indulged her every whim? And so I grew up sheltered, unafraid of the things others feared. I knew nothing of ghosts or witches, no tales of spirits that crept through the night. That ignorance, I believe, made the first true horror of my life all the more terrible.

It happened when I was no older than six. I awoke one night in the nursery—a large room high in the castle, beneath a steep oak ceiling. The chamber was dim, lit only by moonlight filtering through the lattice. I looked around and saw neither the nursery maid nor my nurse. I thought myself abandoned, and felt a surge of anger—not fear. I began to whimper, preparing to cry out loud—when suddenly, I saw her.

A woman knelt beside my bed, her face pale yet beautiful, her hands tucked under the coverlet. She smiled gently, and reached out to stroke my cheek. I stared, not afraid, but strangely enchanted. She lay down beside me, drew me close, and I felt warmth and calm wash over me. I fell asleep again.

But I woke abruptly—shocked by a sharp, piercing pain in my chest, as if two needles had driven deep into my flesh. I screamed.

She flinched back, eyes wide, then slipped silently to the floor and vanished beneath the bed. Panic seized me. I shrieked until the house erupted in motion. Nurse, the maid, the housekeeper—all burst in, breathless, alarmed. They laughed softly at my tale, soothing me, but I saw the pallor on their faces, the tension in their movements. They searched the room—under the bed, behind furniture, in cupboards—then whispered urgently.

“Feel here,” the housekeeper murmured, pressing her hand into the hollow where she’d lain. “It’s still warm. Someone *was* there.”

They examined my chest where I pointed. No mark, no wound. Nothing visible. Yet I knew—I *knew*—it wasn’t a dream.

That night, the servants stayed awake in the nursery, watching over me. From then on, someone sat with me every night until I was nearly fourteen. I became nervous, trembling at shadows, unable to endure even daylight solitude. A doctor came—pale, elderly, with a long face pitted by smallpox and a chestnut wig. He prescribed medicine, which I loathed, and visited every other day.

The next morning, I was terrified. My father came up, stood by my bed, asked questions, laughed at the nurse’s reply, and kissed my forehead. “It was only a dream,” he said kindly. “Nothing to fear.”

But I knew better. It was not a dream. I remembered every detail—the woman’s face, her touch, the pain. The nursery maid insisted it had been her, that I must have been half-asleep. But I didn’t believe her. I remembered the old priest too—the venerable man in black robes who arrived with the nurse and housekeeper. His face was sweet, his voice soft as he took my hands and told me to pray: “Lord, hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus’ sake.” I repeated those words often after that. I remember him kneeling in the dim room, the women beside him, praying aloud in hushed tones, their voices trembling with earnestness.

Before that night, my memories are vague. Afterward, they blur again. But that moment—those scenes—remain sharp, vivid, like images from a nightmare that refuses to fade. I know now, looking back, that something unnatural entered my room that night. Something that should not have been there. And though I did not understand it then, I feel its echo still—cold, quiet, waiting.
