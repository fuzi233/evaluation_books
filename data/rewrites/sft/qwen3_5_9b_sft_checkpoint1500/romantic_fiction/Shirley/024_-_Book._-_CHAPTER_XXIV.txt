Sometimes the future whispers a low, trembling warning, like a distant storm gathering in the sky, announcing a blast strong enough to scatter wreckage across the sea. At other times, it bursts forth with sudden violence, as if a tombstone had cracked open to release a sleeper from the grave—a new Lazarus emerging from the dark.

Caroline Helstone returned home from Hollow's Cottage feeling robust, as she believed. Yet, the next morning, a heavy, unfamiliar languor pressed down upon her. Breakfast, and every meal thereafter, tasted of ash and sawdust; she lost all desire to eat.

"Am I ill?" she asked, staring into the mirror. Her eyes burned brightly, her pupils wide, her cheeks flushed with a rosy, unusual fullness. "I look well; why cannot I eat?"

A frantic pulse beat in her temples, and her mind raced with strange, brilliant, broken thoughts. A warm glow suffused her skin. Then came a hot, parched, restless night. Toward dawn, a terrifying nightmare seized her like a tiger. When she awoke, she knew with certainty that she was sick.

She could not recall how the fever had taken root. Perhaps, on her final walk home, a sweet, poisoned breeze—laden with honey-dew and miasma—had slipped into her lungs. Finding there a fever born of mental excitement and the lingering fatigue of long, habitual sadness, it had fanned a spark into a roaring fire.

Yet, for two days, the fire burned gently. Neither her uncle, nor Fanny, nor the doctor, nor Miss Keeldar, when she visited, feared for her life. Everyone believed a few days would restore her.

But the days passed, and the recovery did not begin. Mrs. Pryor, who had visited daily, was present in Caroline's chamber one morning after a fortnight of illness. She watched silently for some moments, then took Caroline's hand, placing a finger on her wrist. Quietly, she left the room and retreated to Mr. Helstone's study, where she remained sequestered for nearly half a morning.

Returning to the bedside, she removed her shawl and bonnet. Standing by the bed, hand clasped within hand, she rocked gently, a motion habitual to her. Finally, she spoke. "I have sent Fanny to Fieldhead to fetch things I shall need for a short stay here. It is my wish to remain with you until you are better. Your uncle kindly permits my attendance. Will it be acceptable to you, Caroline?"

"I am sorry for the trouble," Caroline replied, her voice weak. "I do not feel very ill, but I cannot refuse. It will be such comfort to know you are here. Please, do not confine yourself on my account, dear Mrs. Pryor. Fanny nurses me well."

Mrs. Pryor leaned over the pale sufferer, smoothing the hair beneath her cap and lifting her pillow. Smiling, Caroline lifted her face to kiss her.

"Are you free from pain? Are you at ease?" Mrs. Pryor asked in a low, earnest voice.

"I think I am almost happy."

"You wish to drink? Your lips are parched."

Mrs. Pryor offered a glass of cooling liquid.

"Have you eaten today?"

"I cannot eat."

"But soon your appetite will return; it *must* return—that is, I pray God it may."

As she laid Caroline back on the couch, she encircled her with her arms. In a movement that seemed scarcely voluntary, she drew the girl close to her heart, holding her tightly for an instant. "I shall hardly wish to get well," Caroline whispered, "so that I may keep you always."

Mrs. Pryor did not smile. A tremor ran over her features, which she struggled to suppress. "You are more used to Fanny than to me," she remarked eventually. "My attendance must seem strange, officious?"

"No; quite natural, and very soothing. You must have been accustomed to waiting on the sick, ma'am. You move so softly, and speak so quietly, and touch me so gently."

"I am dexterous in nothing, my dear. You will often find me awkward, but never negligent."

Negligent, indeed, she was not. From that hour, Fanny and Eliza became ciphers in the sickroom. Mrs. Pryor claimed the domain, performing every duty, living there day and night. The patient remonstrated faintly at first, then ceased altogether. Loneliness and gloom vanished; protection and solace took their place. Caroline and her nurse coalesced in a wondrous union. Caroline, who usually pained to require so much care, found herself as willing to be cherished as the nurse was bent on giving it. There was no sign of weariness in the latter that ever reminded the former of any burden.

Despite this devoted care, the sick girl did not improve. She wasted like a snowdrift melting in spring, faded like a flower in drought. Miss Keeldar, who rarely feared for anyone, began to alarm when she saw Caroline sink from time to time. She urged Mr. Helstone to acknowledge that his niece was ill of something more than a migraine. When Mrs. Pryor quietly requested a physician, he relented, allowing her to summon two if she wished. One came—an oracle who delivered a dark prophecy, wrote prescriptions, pocketed his fee, and departed, knowing he could do little good.

Still, no rumor of serious illness reached the neighborhood. At Hollow's Cottage, it was believed Caroline had only a severe cold, a notion reinforced by a note she sent to Hortense. Mademoiselle responded with currant jam, a tisane recipe, and advice. Mrs. Yorke, upon learning of the summons, sneered at the hypochondriac fancies of the rich and idle. Meanwhile, Caroline fell fast into a state of prostration, puzzling everyone except one, who understood how easily an undermined structure sinks into ruin.

Sick people often have fancies inscrutable to ordinary attendants. Caroline had one even Mrs. Pryor could not initially explain. Once a week, at a specific hour, she would entreat to be dressed and sit by the window. She would remain there until noon. Despite her exhaustion, she softly resisted all persuasion to rest until the church clock tolled twelve. When the bell rang, she grew docile, lying down to bury her face in the pillow, pulling the covers tight as if to shut out the world and the sun, of which she was weary. More than once, a slight convulsion shook the bed, accompanied by a faint sob.

One Tuesday morning, as usual, Caroline sat wrapped in her white dressing-gown, gazing steadily from the lattice. Mrs. Pryor knitted nearby, watching intently. A change crossed Caroline's pale brow; a light revived her faded eyes. She looked out, and Mrs. Pryor, moving softly, glanced over her shoulder. Beyond the churchyard, on the road, a horseman rode sharply. The figure was not too remote for recognition. Mrs. Pryor knew Mr. Moore. Just as a rising ground concealed him, the clock struck twelve.

"May I lie down again?" asked Caroline.

Her nurse assisted her to bed. Laying her down, Mrs. Pryor stood listening near. The couch trembled, a suppressed sob stirring the air. A contraction of anguish twisted Mrs. Pryor's features; she wrung her hands, a groan escaping her lips. She realized Tuesday was Whinbury Market Day; Mr. Moore must pass the rectory just before noon.

Caroline wore a slender braid of silk around her neck, attached to a trinket. Mrs. Pryor had glimpsed a bit of gold but never seen it clearly. Hidden in her bosom when dressed, held in her hand when bedridden, Caroline never parted with it. That Tuesday afternoon, a transient doze—more like lethargy than sleep—stole over her. Turning in febrile restlessness, she pushed the coverlets aside. Mrs. Pryor bent to replace them. The small, wasted hand lying nerveless on Caroline's breast claspéd the treasured braid. Those attenuated fingers, painful to behold, relaxed in sleep. Gently, Mrs. Pryor disengaged the braid, revealing a tiny locket. Under its crystal face appeared a curl of black hair, too short and crisp to belong to a female head.

An agitated movement caused a twitch of the chain. The sleeper started, waking with scattered thoughts and a wandering gaze. Half-rising, as if in terror, she exclaimed, "Don't take it from me, Robert! Don't! It is my last comfort; let me keep it. I never tell any one whose hair it is; I never show it."

Mrs. Pryor had already disappeared behind the curtain. Reclining in a deep armchair by the bedside, she was withdrawn from view. Caroline looked around the chamber; she thought it empty. As her stray ideas returned, each folding its weak wings on the mind's sad shore, she believed herself alone. Words escaped her lips unawares.

"Oh, I *should* see him once more before all is over! Heaven *might* favour me thus far!" she cried. "God grant me a little comfort before I die!"

"But he will not know I am ill till I am gone, and he will come when they have laid me out, and I am senseless, cold, and stiff."
