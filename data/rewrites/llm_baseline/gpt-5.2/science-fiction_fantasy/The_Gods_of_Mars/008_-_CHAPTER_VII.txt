For an instant the black pirate and I held ourselves like two statues carved out of the same hard night—his face above the rail, mine below it, the thin anchor-chain biting my palm and forearm as though it meant to saw me from the world. The air was salt-cold and metallic with the ship’s oil, and the nearer moon had already slipped away; only the farther one washed the cruiser’s deck in pale light, turning every rivet into a pinprick of frost.

His handsome lips curled. Over the deck’s edge slid an ebony hand, unhurried, as if he were reaching for a spice-jar he knew by heart. The cold hollow eye of a revolver found the center of my forehead.

My free hand shot up, not for the weapon, but for his throat—warm, living, and corded as a rope. His hiss—“Die, cursed thern”—caught and broke in his windpipe under my fingers. His trigger tightened; the hammer fell; the chamber answered with a futile click.

Before he could roll to another round I dragged him so far over the edge that he had to drop the revolver and clutch the rail with both hands. My grip on his throat throttled any outcry. We fought in savage silence: he scrabbling to tear free, I hauling him toward the black drop beneath us. His face darkened to a livid bruise; his eyes bulged, pleading not for mercy but for air.

With a final effort he threw himself back upon the deck, and in that same fraction of time let go the rail to claw at my fingers. That was the moment I had been waiting for. I surged downward with all my weight. He swept clear of the deck, falling so close to my body that his plunge nearly tore me from my single hold on the chain and sent us both tumbling to the Lost Sea.

I did not loosen my grip. A single shriek from him, flung upward, would have been enough to rouse the deck. So I held on—choking, ever choking—while his frantic struggles dragged me lower along the chain, the iron links clinking softly like a miser counting coins. His contortions turned spasmodic, then smaller, then ceased. I released him only when he was utterly quiet. He vanished into the shadows below, swallowed without a splash I could hear.

I climbed again until my eyes cleared the rail.

The farther moon’s clear effulgence lay across the deck, bringing into sharp relief six or eight black men sprawled in sleep, their harness straps loose, their swords and pistols tossed near their hands the way a tired cook leaves knives too close to the board. The deck smelled of sweat and powder residue and old ship’s grease—human odor braided with machinery—so strong that even the cold could not thin it.

Huddled tight to the base of a rapid-fire gun sat a young white girl, securely bound. Her eyes were wide with the kind of fear that anticipates pain before it arrives. They fixed on me as my head rose above the edge.

Then relief flooded them—instant, unutterable—as though they had fallen upon the mystic jewel that sparkled in the center of the headpiece I had stolen. She did not speak; instead, with a tiny motion of her gaze, she warned me of the sleepers around her.

Noiselessly I gained the deck, letting my boots find the planks between scattered weapons. The girl nodded for me to come. When I bent close she whispered, urgent and practical as a housekeeper in a fire.

“Release me. I can aid you—and you will need all the aid available when they awaken.”

“Some of them will awake in Korus,” I replied, and I smiled as grimly as the night itself.

She understood at once. The cruelty of her answering smile struck me like a slap—cruelty on a beautiful face is an abomination, like blood spilled into milk. I cut her bonds quickly.

“Give me a revolver,” she breathed. “I can use that upon those your sword does not silence in time.”

I placed one in her hand. The metal looked too heavy and too dark against her skin, as if a soot-stained pot had been set on a lace cloth. Then I turned toward the ugly work waiting for us.

There was no room here for fine compunctions, no time for the delicate courtesies that these demons would neither value nor return. Stealthily I approached the nearest sleeper. When he woke he was already far on his journey to the bosom of Korus. His piercing shriek, born as consciousness returned, came faintly up from the depths as he fell—thin as steam escaping a kettle in the distance.

The second woke as my hand touched him. I succeeded in hurling him overboard, but his wild cry of alarm jerked the rest to their feet.

Five of them—five blades flashing up at once in the moonlight.

The girl’s revolver spoke in sharp staccato. One sank back to the deck and rose no more.

The others rushed me with drawn swords. She dared not fire again with me in the mêlée, but I saw her move—silent, cat-like—seeking an angle on their flank as one might circle a crowded hearth to reach the salt.

Then they were upon me.

The deck was too cramped for footwork; it was stand fast and pay in steel. For a few minutes it was the hottest fighting I had ever known. At first I took more than I gave—cuts that burned cold, a jar up the arms as blades met and skidded. The crashing raised a din that seemed obscene in the vast quiet of the night, as if some great factory had been dropped into a sleeping valley. Sparks leapt at each impact. Then I got beneath one fellow’s guard and saw him crumple to the planks.

The others redoubled their efforts. Steel on steel, a terrible clanging—noise without music, the kind that fouls the mind and makes even courage taste like ashes. Somewhere in it came the dull, sickening sound of bone parting under my blade, as unmistakable as cleaver meeting joint.

Three faced me now, and the girl was nearly in position to cut them down with lead.

What happened next came so fast I can scarce order it even now. The three rushed me together, plainly meaning to drive me back the few steps that would send me over the rail into the void. At the same instant the girl fired, and my sword arm made two swift moves.

One man dropped, a bullet through his brain. Another’s sword flew clattering across the deck and toppled over the edge as I disarmed him. The third went down with my blade buried to the hilt in his breast, three feet of it protruding from his back; and when he fell he wrenched the sword from my grasp.

Disarmed, I faced the last of them—bare-handed now, for his own sword lay somewhere thousands of feet below, lost in the Lost Sea. The new conditions pleased him. He smiled, teeth gleaming, and rushed at me with empty hands, trusting in the great muscles rolling under his glossy skin. He did not even trouble to draw the dagger at his harness.

I let him come almost upon me. Then I ducked under his arms and sidestepped right. Pivoting on my left toe, I drove a terrific right to his jaw. He dropped like a felled ox, the sound of his skull on the deck a final punctuation.

Behind me rang a low, silvery laugh—light as a spoon tapping crystal.

“You are no thern,” said the sweet voice of my companion, “for all your golden locks or the harness of Sator Throg. Never lived there upon all Barsoom before one who could fight as you have fought this night. Who are you?”

“I am John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium,” I said.

“And whom,” I added, “has the honor of serving been accorded me?”

She hesitated—only a breath—and then asked, as if the question mattered more than my name.

“You are no thern. Are you an enemy of the therns?”

“I have been in the territory of the therns for a day and a half,” I replied. “During that entire time my life has been in constant danger. I have been harassed and persecuted. Armed men and fierce beasts have been set upon me. I had no quarrel with the therns before, but can you wonder that I feel no great love for them now? I have spoken.”

She studied me for several minutes, gaze drawn-out and searching, as though she were weighing my words against some private lexicon where mercy and weakness were spelled the same. At last she seemed satisfied.

“I am Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, Holy Hekkador of the Holy Therns, Father of Therns, Master of Life and Death upon Barsoom, Brother of Issus, Prince of Life Eternal.”

As she spoke I saw that the black I had felled with my fist was stirring back toward consciousness. I sprang to him, stripped his harness away with quick hands, and bound his wrists behind him, then his ankles, and tied him to a heavy gun carriage. His skin was warm under the straps; he smelled of sweat and leather and the sour tang of fear held in check.

“Why not the simpler way?” Phaidor asked.

“I do not understand. What ‘simpler way’?” I said.

With a slight shrug of her lovely shoulders she made a gesture—hands casting an imaginary body over the side as carelessly as one might toss refuse from a kitchen pail.

“I am no murderer,” I told her. “I kill in self-defence only.”

She looked at me narrowly, divine brows puckering, and shook her head as though my words were in a dialect she refused to learn. She could not comprehend. My own Dejah Thoris had once found such restraint foolish and dangerous, but with her there had been tenderness behind the practicality—a sorrow for stern necessity. With Phaidor, I sensed something colder: not regret for danger spared, but disappointment at a pleasure withheld.

The bound man watched us now, fully awake. He was handsome—clean-limbed, powerful, an intelligent face with features so finely chiseled that even Adonis might have envied him. His eyes were alive with calculation.

The vessel, unguided, had been moving slowly across the valley. It was time to take the helm.

Only in the roughest way could I guess our location. I knew the Valley Dor lay far south of the equator—constellations told me so—but I lacked the charts and delicate instruments with which, in the Heliumite Navy, I had once reckoned position. Still, one conclusion was plain: northward would bring us quickest toward the more settled portions of the planet.

Beneath my hand the cruiser swung about with graceful obedience. I pressed the control of the repulsive rays and we soared up into space. With the speed lever pulled to the last notch, we raced north as we climbed ever farther above that terrible valley of death.

Below, as we passed at a dizzy height over the narrow domains of the therns, flashes of powder winked like angry fireflies, mute witness to battle along that cruel frontier. No sound came up to us. In the rarefied air, sound-waves died before they could reach our ears. The silence at that height was not peace; it was absence—like a world where every familiar voice has been erased and only the harsh instruments remain.

Cold seized us. Breathing grew difficult, each inhale thin and grudging. Phaidor and the bound black pirate stared at me; the girl’s gaze clung to my hands on the levers.

At length the girl spoke, calm as if announcing that a pot was about to boil over.

“Unconsciousness comes quickly at this altitude. Unless you are inviting death for us all you had best drop—and that quickly.”

There was no fear in her tone. Merely certainty.

I dropped the vessel to a lower level—and not a moment too soon. The girl swooned. The black, too, went slack and senseless. I held on, I think, only by sheer will; responsibility itself can be a kind of air.

We swung along low above the foothills of the Otz. Warmth returned, and air thickened enough to fill starved lungs. Soon the black opened his eyes, and a moment later the girl’s lids fluttered.

“It was a close call,” she said.

“It has taught me two things,” I replied.

“What?” she asked.

“That even Phaidor, daughter of the Master of Life and Death, is mortal,” I said, smiling.

“There is immortality only in Issus,” Phaidor answered. “And Issus is for the race of therns alone. Thus am I immortal.”

A fleeting grin passed across the black man’s features at her words—wicked, knowing. I did not then understand it. Later I was to learn, and she too, in a manner too horrible for any boast to survive.

“If the other thing you have just learned,” she continued, “has led to as erroneous deductions as the first you are little richer in knowledge than you were before.”

“The other,” I said, “is that our dusky friend does not hail from the nearer moon. He was like to have died at a few thousand feet above Barsoom. Had we continued the five thousand miles that lie between Thuria and the planet he would have been but the frozen memory of a man.”

Phaidor looked at him in astonishment.

“If you are not of Thuria, then where?” she demanded.

He shrugged and looked away, silent.

The girl stamped her little foot, imperious as a priestess at a feast table when a servant dares delay.

“The daughter of Matai Shang is not accustomed to having her queries remain unanswered,” she said. “One of the lesser breed should feel honored that a member of the holy race that was born to inherit life eternal should deign even to notice him.”

Again the black smiled.

“Xodar, Dator of the First Born of Barsoom, is accustomed to give commands, not to receive them,” he replied. Then, turning his eyes to me, “What are your intentions concerning me?”

“I intend taking you both back to Helium,” I said. “No harm will come to you. You will find the red men of Helium a kindly and magnanimous race, but if they listen to me there will be no more voluntary pilgrimages down the river Iss, and the impossible belief that they have cherished for ages will be shattered into a thousand pieces.”

“Are you of Helium?” he asked.

“I am a Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium,” I replied, “but I am not of Barsoom. I am of another world.”

Xodar regarded me intently. “I can well believe that you are not of Barsoom,” he said at length. “None of this world could have bested eight of the First Born single-handed. But how is it that you wear the golden hair and the jewelled circlet of a Holy Thern?” He emphasized holy with a touch of irony that tasted like smoke.

“I had forgotten them,” I said. “They are the spoils of conquest,” and with a sweep of my hand I removed the disguise.

When his eyes fell on my close-cropped black hair they widened in surprise. He had expected the bald pate of a thern.

“You are indeed of another world,” he murmured, awe threading his voice. “With the skin of a thern, the black hair of a First Born and the muscles of a dozen Dators it was no disgrace even for Xodar to acknowledge your supremacy. A thing he could never do were you a Barsoomian.”

“You are travelling several laps ahead of me,” I said. “I glean that your name is Xodar. But whom, pray, are the First Born? What is a Dator? And why, if you were conquered by a Barsoomian, could you not acknowledge it?”

“The First Born of Barsoom,” he explained, “are the race of black men of which I am a Dator—Prince, as the lesser Barsoomians would say. My race is the oldest on the planet. We trace our lineage, unbroken, direct to the Tree of Life which flourished in the center of the Valley Dor twenty-three million years ago.

“For countless ages the fruit of this tree underwent gradual change, passing by degrees from true plant life to a combination of plant and animal. In the first stages the fruit possessed only the power of independent muscular action while the stem remained attached to the parent plant; later a brain developed in the fruit, so that hanging there by their long stems they thought and moved as individuals.

“Then, with the development of perceptions came comparison; judgments were reached and compared, and thus reason and the power to reason were born upon Barsoom.

“Ages passed. Many forms of life came and went upon the Tree of Life, but still all were attached to the parent plant by stems of varying lengths. At length the fruit tree consisted in tiny plant men—such as we now see reproduced in huge dimensions in the Valley Dor—still hanging to limbs and branches by the stems that grew from the tops of their heads.

“The buds from which the plant men blossomed resembled large nuts about a foot in diameter, divided by double partition walls into four sections. In one section grew the plant man; in another a sixteen-legged worm; in the third the progenitor of the white ape; and in the fourth the primeval black man of Barsoom.

“When the bud burst the plant man remained dangling at the end of his stem, but the three other sections fell to the ground, where the efforts of their imprisoned occupants to escape sent them hopping about in all directions.

“Thus, as time went on, all Barsoom was covered with these imprisoned creatures—hopping and skipping about the broad planet, falling into rivers, lakes, and seas to be spread farther still. Countless billions died before the first black man broke through his prison walls into the light of day. Prompted by curiosity, he broke open other shells, and the peopling of Barsoom commenced.

“The pure strain of the blood of this first black man has remained untainted by admixture with other creatures in the race of which I am a member; but from the sixteen-legged worm, the first ape, and renegade black man has sprung every other form of animal life upon Barsoom.

“The therns”—and he smiled maliciously—“are but the result of ages of evolution from the pure white ape of antiquity. They are a lower order still. There is but one race of true and immortal humans on Barsoom. It is the race of black men.

“The Tree of Life is dead, but before it died the plant men learned to detach themselves and roam the face of Barsoom with the other children of the First Parent. Now their bisexuality permits them to reproduce themselves after the manner of true plants, but otherwise they have progressed but little. Their actions are largely instinct. The brain of a plant man is but a trifle larger than the end of your smallest finger. They live upon vegetation and the blood of animals, and their brain is just large enough to direct them toward food and translate the sensations carried to it from eyes and ears. They have no sense of self-preservation and so are without fear. That is why they are such terrible antagonists in combat.”

As he spoke at such length—bound at my feet, yet discoursing like a lecturer in some ancient hall—I wondered at his purpose. It seemed too convenient, too leisurely for a captive.

Then I caught it: the faintest wandering of his gaze past me, the barest fraction of a second. He lay slightly forward of where I stood at the levers and thus faced the stern. At the end of his description his eyes fixed momentarily on something behind me, and a swift gleam of triumph lit them like a match in the dark.

We had slowed earlier; the Valley Dor was far astern, and I had let myself feel, for a breath, comparatively safe. Now I turned, sudden apprehension tightening my chest more than thin air ever had.

Close astern, forging silent and unlighted through the night, loomed the bulk of a great battleship.
