An hour after Grandcourt had ridden away from Offendene, the air of the rectory seemed to change—like a kitchen when the first word goes round that there is to be a wedding feast. By evening, Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne arrived with Anna, and the house filled itself with voices and the small domestic rustlings that make even great news feel like it must submit to cups of tea and a decent fire.

“My dear,” said the rector, taking Gwendolen’s hand with an approving gentleness, “let me congratulate you. You look grave—and no wonder. A life-long union is solemn. But from the way Mr. Grandcourt has acted, I think we already see good rising out of our adversity. It has enabled you to observe your future husband’s—hm—delicate liberality.”

He alluded, with the calm satisfaction of a man quoting a comforting text, to Grandcourt’s manner of letting it be understood that Mrs. Davilow would be provided for—an item of the courtship which Gwendolen had repeated to her mother with exactness, as if she were copying down a recipe that must not fail.

“But I have no doubt he would have behaved handsomely even if you had never gone away to Germany,” Mrs. Gascoigne put in, feeling the need to salt the sweetness with duty. “And now there is no more room for caprice. I trust you have no inclination to any. A woman owes a debt of gratitude to a man who perseveres in making her such an offer. No doubt you feel properly.”

“I’m not at all sure I do, aunt,” said Gwendolen, with that saucy gravity which let her mock propriety while seeming to ask for it. “I don’t know everything it is proper to feel on being engaged.”

The rector patted her shoulder, smiling as if she were merely a child who had spilt sugar and made it pretty. Mrs. Gascoigne, watching his indulgence, allowed herself not to be offended. Anna kissed Gwendolen and murmured, “I do hope you will be happy,” and then, as if her own breath would betray her, withdrew into stillness and struggled with tears.

For Anna, lately, had been nursing a shy romance of her own—imagining Rex’s longing enduring, and trouble softening Gwendolen at last into a love that would make amends. That romance had dwindled into a prayer—only that she might rejoice like a good sister, and, if Rex was not rich, still be useful. And now she had to find grace to rejoice in something else altogether.

Miss Merry and the four girls were there too—Alice with her high shoulders; Bertha and Fanny whispering; Isabel listening with that devout attention which makes listeners seem wiser than speakers. It was as if the household had arranged itself, without a word said, into an audience for a heroine whose life had taken on the splendid interest of a novel, and every cup and chair was merely a prop for “the honor and glory of Gwendolen.”

The evening went chiefly in Mr. Gascoigne’s decisive remarks, answering the conjectures of the elder ladies. On the question of settlements—those domestic contracts that can make love sound like law—he declared the case was not one in which he could think it his duty to mention them: all must, and doubtless would, be safely left to Mr. Grandcourt.

“I should like to know exactly what sort of places Ryelands and Gadsmere are,” said Mrs. Davilow.

“Gadsmere, I believe, is a secondary place,” the rector replied; “but Ryelands I know to be one of our finest seats. The park is extensive; the woods valuable. The house was built by Inigo Jones, and the ceilings are painted in the Italian style. The estate is said to be worth twelve thousand a year, and there are two livings—one a rectory—in the gift of the Grandcourts. There may be burdens on the land. Still, Mr. Grandcourt was an only child.”

“It would be most remarkable,” said Mrs. Gascoigne, counting the grandeur on her fingers as if she were portioning out fruit at a dessert-table, “if he were to become Lord Stannery in addition to everything else. Only think—there is the Grandcourt estate, the Mallinger estate, and the baronetcy, and the peerage—though they say there will be no land coming with the peerage.” She paused on the fourth finger, as if regretting that providence provided no fifth.

“The peerage,” said the rector, smoothing the matter as one smooths dough that threatens to crack, “must be regarded as a remote chance. There are two cousins between the present peer and Mr. Grandcourt. It is a serious reflection how death and other causes sometimes concentrate inheritances on one man. But excess is to be deprecated. To be Sir Mallinger Grandcourt Mallinger, I suppose that will be his style, with corresponding properties, is talent enough for any man to have committed to him. Let us hope it will be well used.”

“And what a position for the wife, Gwendolen!” Mrs. Gascoigne said. “A great responsibility. But you must lose no time in writing to Mrs. Mompert, Henry. It is a good thing you have an engagement to offer as an excuse, else she might feel offended. She is rather a high woman.”

“I am rid of that horror,” thought Gwendolen, for the name Mompert had become to her like a conjuring-word—one of those noises that make the mind recoil before it even attaches meaning.

She had been very silent, and that night, in her little white bed, she could hardly sleep at all. Wakefulness was rare for her robust youth; rarer still was this care—almost stealthy—that her mother should not know she lay staring into the dark. But everything within her was new. She, who had always felt sure of herself, always ready to manage others, had taken a decisive step she had beforehand believed she would not take—nay, perhaps, must not.

There was no retreat now. She liked much of what lay before her; and behind her there seemed nothing to like—only the dull humiliation of insignificance and servitude. Yet the resolution she had just made was dogged by the shadow of her former resolve, when her whole being had first started back from Grandcourt with repugnance.

Lying with wide-open eyes—“looking on darkness which the blind do see”—she was appalled by the notion that she was going to do what she had once turned from as wrong. It was new to her that the question of right or wrong should rouse terror; she had been used to little stings of conscience being soothed by caresses, gifts, distractions—like a bitterness covered over with sugar. But now some other consciousness had awakened, raw and importunate. It was as if she stood on the verge of adopting, deliberately, for all her life, the bitter sentence she had flung out when her discovery had driven her to Leubronn—that it did not signify what she did; she had only to amuse herself as best she could.

That lawlessness—that casting away of the need for justification—suddenly frightened her. It came with a shadowy array of calamities behind it; and calamity had ceased to be a mere word. The old, half-forgotten influences of religious teaching she had disregarded, and the deeper impression that something inexorable might wrap itself round her, gathered into the vague idea of avenging power.

The brilliant position she had longed for, the freedom she imagined marriage would grant her, the deliverance from the flatness of girlhood—all stood close, ready. And yet they came to her hunger like food tainted with sacrilege: something she must snatch with trembling, and then pretend it nourished her without poisoning her.

In that loneliness, her resistant self could not rise against the first onslaught of dread after her irrevocable decision. The unhappy-faced woman and her children—Grandcourt and his relations with her—returned and returned, sticking in her imagination like the taste of smoke that clings after a kitchen fire has been put out. Gradually they blotted out all other thought, leaving only one consciousness: she had taken those scenes into her life.

Her long wakefulness became like fever. A faint light crept in beside the curtain; the chill increased. She could bear it no longer.

“Mamma!” she cried.

“Yes, dear,” Mrs. Davilow answered at once, in a voice that had not slept.

“Let me come to you.”

She went, and soon fell asleep on her mother’s shoulder, as if that familiar warmth could quiet all the unseen terror. She slept late; and when she opened her eyes again she was dreaming of a lit ball-room, only to find her mother standing by the bed with a small packet in her hand.

“I am sorry to wake you, darling, but I thought it better you should have this at once. The groom has brought Criterion—he has come on another horse, and says he is to stay here.”

Gwendolen sat up and broke the packet. Inside was a delicate enamelled casket; inside that, a splendid diamond ring. There was a letter too, with a folded slip of colored paper, and these words:

Pray wear this ring when I come at twelve in sign of our betrothal. I enclose a check drawn in the name of Mr. Gascoigne, for immediate expenses. Of course Mrs. Davilow will remain at Offendene, at least for some time. I hope, when I come, you will have granted me an early day, when you may begin to command me at a shorter distance.—Yours devotedly,

H. M. GRANDCOURT.

The check was for five hundred pounds. Gwendolen turned it toward her mother with the letter.

“How very kind and delicate!” said Mrs. Davilow, with real feeling. “But I should like better not to be dependent on a son-in-law. I and the girls could get along very well.”

“Mamma, if you say that again, I will not marry him,” Gwendolen said, sharply—anger rushing up like heat from a stove someone has opened too suddenly.

“My dear child, I trust you are not going to marry only for my sake,” Mrs. Davilow said, deprecatingly.

Gwendolen tossed her head away on the pillow and let the ring lie untouched. She was irritated at her mother’s attempt to take away a motive—as if someone had moved the very board on which she meant to play her game. Perhaps the deeper cause was the knowledge that she was not marrying solely for her mother’s sake—that she was drawn toward it by other forces, and that even her mother’s renunciation was not strong enough to stop them.

She had woken to the signs that she was irrevocably engaged; and the ugly visions, alarms, arguments of the night had to be met in daylight, where they would likely seem feeble—like ghosts that are bold only in dark corners.

“What I long for is your happiness, dear,” Mrs. Davilow continued, pleading. “I will not say anything to vex you. Will you not put on the ring?”

Gwendolen did not answer at once; but thoughts moved quickly in her as hands move when a cook, resolved not to fail, begins again with fresh precision after a moment’s discouragement. At last she raised herself with a sudden determination—like one who, having started on horseback, will go on with spirit whatever wild fancies gallop beside her.

“I thought the lover always put on the betrothal ring himself,” she said, laughing as she slipped it on and turned her head with a charming movement to see its fire. “I know why he has sent it,” she added, nodding at her mother.

“Why?”

“He would rather make me put it on than ask me to let him do it. Aha! he is very proud. But so am I. We shall match each other. I should hate a man who went down on his knees and came fawning on me. He really is not disgusting.”

“That is very moderate praise, Gwen.”

“No, it is not—for a man,” Gwendolen said gaily. Then, with the swift change of a girl who can turn from defiance to caress as if it were the same motion: “But now I must get up and dress. Will you come and do my hair, mamma dear,” she coaxed, drawing her mother’s face down and rubbing cheek to cheek, “and not be so naughty any more as to talk of living in poverty? You must bear to be made comfortable even if you don’t like it. And Mr. Grandcourt behaves perfectly now, does he not?”

“Certainly he does,” said Mrs. Davilow, encouraged, and persuading herself—as mothers do—that her daughter must be fond of her betrothed. She herself judged Grandcourt as many judge suitors: by the figure they make in society, by the look of their attentions, by the quiet authority of their wealth. Her anxiety lay less in his character than in Gwendolen’s mood—whether it would hold steady.

That mood necessarily shifted this morning. Even while she made her toilet, she drew on all the knowledge she had—like a housewife searching her shelves—to find grounds that would justify her marriage. What she dwelt on most was a determination: when she was Grandcourt’s wife she would urge him to liberal conduct toward Mrs. Glasher’s children.

“Of what use would it be to her that I should not marry him? He could have married her if he liked; but he did not like. Perhaps she is to blame for that. There must be a great deal about her I know nothing of. And he must have been good to her in many ways, else she would not have wanted to marry him.”

But that last argument began at once to taste doubtful—like a sauce that turns on the tongue. Mrs. Glasher naturally wished to exclude other children who would stand between Grandcourt and her own; and Gwendolen’s comprehension of that feeling opened another way of reconciling claims.

“Perhaps we shall have no children. I hope we shall not. And he might leave the estate to the pretty little boy. My uncle said Mr. Grandcourt could do as he liked with the estates. Only when Sir Hugo Mallinger dies there will be enough for two.”

Then Mrs. Glasher appeared unreasonable in demanding that her boy should be sole heir; and the double property seemed a sort of safety—proof that Grandcourt’s marriage need do her no wrong, when the wife was Gwendolen Harleth, armed with proud resolution not to be fairly accused. She had been accustomed to regard herself as blameless: it was other people who were faulty.

Strikingly, as this argument took hold—that she was doing no wrong—her repugnance toward the idea of Grandcourt’s past sank into something secondary. The terror of overstepping the border of wickedness dulled her emotions about his conduct. She thought of him now, whatever he might be, as a man she was going to have indefinite power over; and since loving him had never been a question, any agreeableness he had was so much gain.

Poor Gwendolen had no awe of unmanageable forces in marriage. She regarded it as a matter of management, and believed she knew how to act. As to Grandcourt’s past, she encouraged herself with doubts—perhaps he differed little from other men; perhaps men in general were expected to have such histories. She even devised little schemes for learning, without seeming to ask, what was allowed by the dominant language of “the world”—that language which can turn suffering into a mere “indiscretion,” and make a woman’s life into an unspeakable footnote.

But whatever else might be true, her hair was dressed suitably for riding, and she went down in her habit so that there might be no delay before mounting. She wanted her blood stirred again with the intoxication of youth; she wanted to recover the daring with which she had always imagined her course in life. A load already seemed lifted: in daylight and movement, it was less oppressive to have doubts than to have no choice but insignificance.

As she went down-stairs she turned suddenly.

“Go back and make yourself look like a duchess, mamma. Put your point-lace over your head. I must have you look like a duchess. You must not take things humbly.”

At twelve Grandcourt came. When he raised her left hand gently and looked at the ring, she said gravely, “It was very good of you to think of everything and send me that packet.”

“You will tell me if there is anything I forget?” he said, still keeping her hand softly within his own. “I will do anything you wish.”

“But I am very unreasonable in my wishes,” said Gwendolen, smiling.

“Yes, I expect that. Women always are.”

“Then I will not be unreasonable,” she retorted, taking her hand away and tossing her head. “I will not be told I am what women always are.”

“I did not say that,” Grandcourt answered, looking at her with his usual gravity. “You are what no other woman is.”

“And what is that, pray?” Gwendolen moved a little away, with an air of pretty menace.

He paused before answering, as he always did, as if even tenderness must submit to his measure. “You are the woman I love.”

“Oh, what nice speeches!” she laughed, letting his words fall on her like music she did not mean to believe in, yet liked to hear. The sense of that love he must once have given another woman under strange conditions had begun to feel familiar—as if the mind, like a household, can grow used to any sound that repeats often enough.

“Give me a nice speech in return. Say when we are to be married.”

“Not yet. Not till we have had a gallop over the downs. I am so thirsty for that I can think of nothing else. I wish the hunting had begun. Sunday the twentieth, twenty-seventh, Monday, Tuesday—” She counted on her fingers, nodding prettily as she looked at him, then swept one palm over the other with triumph. “It will begin in ten days!”

“Let us be married in ten days, then,” said Grandcourt, “and we shall not be bored about the stables.”

“What do women always say in answer to that?” she asked, mischievously.

“They agree to it,” he said, a little off his guard.

“Then I will not!” She took up her gauntlets and began to draw them on, her eyes on him with gathering fun.

The scene pleased them both. A cruder lover would have spoiled it with heavy caresses, breaking the fine surface of her play; but Grandcourt preferred the drama, and she, left at ease, felt her spirits rise as she played at reigning. There was in her—when she was not trying, when she was simply alive—a kind of unconscious acting, more persuasive than any theatrical effort.

After they had had their gallop—a glorious one, with the clean rush of air and the ground sounding under hoof like a deep drumbeat in the open world—she was exhilarated into thinking well of hastening the marriage that would make her life all of a piece with this splendid enjoyment. She would not debate any more about an act she had committed herself to; and she consented to fix the wedding for that day three weeks, despite the difficulty of fulfilling the customary laws of the trousseau.

Lush, of course, read the engagement in a hundred signs without being formally told: the altered movements in the house, the brief orders, the new silences, the sense of a door closing on old habits. He expected communication as a consequence, and after a few days grew impatient under Grandcourt’s quiet. He was sure the change must affect his own prospects and wanted to know precisely how.

His tactics no longer included opposition; he did not love it for its own sake. He could annoy Grandcourt, certainly—but it would injure himself, and mere annoyance was not his motive. Miss Gwendolen he would not have been sorry to thwart a little; but one never knew what might come. Grandcourt’s perverse wilfulness was not new, yet in this freak about the girl he struck Lush as something like a man who was fey—led on by an ominous fatality. That one born to fortune should make worse business of his life than was necessary seemed, to Lush, genuinely pitiable.

Grandcourt had even taken pains to write letters and give orders himself instead of employing Lush; he appeared to ignore his usefulness, and, against the habit of years, chose sometimes to breakfast alone in his dressing-room. But in a house empty of guests a tête-à-tête cannot be wholly avoided. One day after dinner—difficult enough, since Grandcourt did not regularly dine at Offendene—Lush seized his chance.

“And when is the marriage to take place?”

Grandcourt, who drank little, had left the table and lounged near the hearth, smoking in an easy chair while a fire of oak boughs gaped into glowing depths and edged itself with delicate ash. The chair’s red-brown velvet brocade set off the pale, well-cut features and the exquisite long hands. Without the cigar he might have been a portrait by Moroni—impenetrable gaze, air of distinction; and such a portrait would have been as lively a companion as Grandcourt was disposed to be. But he answered without unusual delay.

“On the tenth.”

“I suppose you intend to remain here.”

“We shall go to Ryelands for a little while; but we shall return here for the sake of the hunting.”

There was that languid inarticulate sound Grandcourt often made when he meant to continue. Lush waited, hearing in that faint noise the promise of his own future. Nothing followed. Lush was about to question again when the inarticulate sound began once more and introduced, mildly uttered:

“You had better make some new arrangement for yourself.”

“What! I am to cut and run?” said Lush, prepared to be good-tempered.

“Something of that kind.”

“The bride objects to me. I hope she will make up to you for the want of my services.”

“I can’t help your being so damnably disagreeable to women,” Grandcourt said, by way of apology, as if soothing a horse he was turning out.

“To one woman, if you please.”

“It makes no difference since she is the one in question.”

“I suppose I am not to be turned adrift after fifteen years without some provision.”

“You must have saved something out of me.”

“Deuced little. I have often saved something for you.”

“You can have three hundred a year. But you must live in town and be ready to look after things when I want you. I shall be rather hard up.”

“If you are not going to be at Ryelands this winter, I might run down there and let you know how Swinton goes on.”

“If you like. I don’t care a toss where you are, so that you keep out of sight.”

“Much obliged,” said Lush; the terms were easier than he had feared. Underneath, he nursed a secret belief that by-and-by he would be wanted as much as ever.

“Perhaps you will not object to packing up as soon as possible,” Grandcourt added. “The Torringtons are coming, and Miss Harleth will be riding over here.”

“With all my heart. Can’t I be of use in going to Gadsmere?”

“No. I am going myself.”

“About your being rather hard up. Have you thought of that plan—”

“Just leave me alone, will you?” Grandcourt said in his lowest audible tone, tossing his cigar into the fire and rising to walk away.

He spent the evening in solitude in the smaller drawing-room. Various new publications lay on the table—political, comic, cynical, romantic—the sort a gentleman likes to have in view without touching, as if the presence of books could stand in for the trouble of reading. He employed himself in sitting meditatively on the sofa and abstaining from literature. In this way hours pass surprisingly soon—not from love of thought but from hatred of effort: a premature inward age, where the need for action lapses into a mere image of what has been, is, and might be; where impulse is born and dies in a phantasmal world, rejecting even shadowy fulfilment.

That condition often comes with whitening hair; and sometimes with it comes an intense obstinacy and tenacity of rule—an exorbitant egoism standing out more starkly as the susceptibilities of youth are stripped away.

But Grandcourt’s hair, though not abundant, was of a fine sunny blond; and his moods could not be explained solely by ebbing energy. There is a strange chemistry within us: a lazy stagnation, even a cottony milkiness, may be preparing—one knows not what—biting or explosive material. The navvy who wakes and without malice crushes his sleeping comrade is understood to lack trained motive; yet by roundabout courses even a gentleman may make of himself a chancy personage—raising, in those who live near him, an uncertainty as to what he may do next, which spoils companionship as surely as any harsh sound spoils a quiet room.
