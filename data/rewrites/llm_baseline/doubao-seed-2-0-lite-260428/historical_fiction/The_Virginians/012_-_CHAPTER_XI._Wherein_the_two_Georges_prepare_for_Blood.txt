A thick, heavy hush hung over the rough log walls of Benson’s tavern that crisp Virginian morning. Shut in one bedchamber, Colonel George Washington, newly appointed aide-de-camp to General Braddock, sat polishing his duel pistols with slow, grim strokes, every line of his strong handsome face pulled tight with unspoken sorrow over the coming useless bloodshed. In the opposite chamber, young George Warrington paced restlessly, equally convinced he would not walk away from the meeting, and insisted on drafting his final farewells before the hour appointed for the ground.

He dictated to his weeping younger brother Harry, acting as his reluctant secretary, a grandiloquent letter to their mother, framed with a sharp, bitter edge. He made no mention of her name tied to the quarrel, he told Harry, recalling their grandfather’s memoir of the old duel between Lord Castlewood and Lord Mohun, where no hint of the lady at the heart of the conflict ever appeared in the formal record. Harry’s eyes overflowed so freely with honest, boyish tears that he blotted half the page, misspelling the final phrase “in view” as “in vew”, and George laughed half-heartedly before insisting the boy could not be trusted to write his last wishes at all.

He pushed Harry aside, took a fresh sheet of paper, and filled it with all the grandest flourishes, Latin quotes, and pointed satire his young scholar’s wit could conjure. His will set free his loyal enslaved boy Sady, left his prized copy of Horace, the rest of his book collection, and a small steady stipend to his gentle, impoverished tutor Mr. Dempster. He bequeathed his silver fruit-knife, his sheet music, and his beloved harpsichord to little Fanny Mountain, and asked Harry to tuck a lock of his hair into a locket and wear it always, in memory of his fond, loyal older brother. He sealed the document with his grandfather’s old heraldic signet, pulled out the heirloom gold watch his ancestor had carried, and realized with a jolt that two and a half hours had already slipped by. Sady should long have returned with the matched duelling pistols he had been sent to fetch.

Harry threw his arms around George’s neck, sobbing that if the Colonel killed him, he would step up next and put a bullet straight through Washington himself. The boy poured out so many wild, impious oaths that the recording angel posted at the celestial chancery was sent darting off half a dozen times, his ledger smudged, to add the transgressions to the public record.

Across the hall, Washington had already penned five crisp, firm letters in his steady, bold hand: one to his mother at Mount Vernon, one to his brother, one signed only to “M.C.”, one addressed to General Braddock, and one, the tavern landlord later revealed, for Madam Esmond herself.

Old Captain Benson, the tavern keeper who wore his faded militia uniform for the occasion, popped his head in to inform them the Colonel was already pacing the kitchen garden waiting for them, and most of the redcoat officers who had been drunk the night before were almost sober at last. The patch of ground was ringed with rough shingle palings, all the enslaved servants crowded along the top to crane over and watch, and a handful of squires and neighbouring farmers had wandered over, drawn by the gossip of a duel. Washington paced the dirt path, hands clasped behind his back, head bowed, hating every moment of the predicament he had landed in. He knew he had spoken sharp, insulting words to the hot-headed young Warrington, and was bound by the strict honour code of the colony to answer the boy’s challenge, no matter how much he regretted that his own hasty temper had sparked the whole mess.

A distant roar of whooping, the wild full-throated yell that enslaved Virginian riders gave when galloping full tilt across country, cut through the quiet. Sady came careening into the yard a minute later on a lathered, foaming horse, fired one pistol in the air, then a second, and was immediately surrounded by a crowd of his hooting, cheering fellow servants. He ignored Harry and George’s shouts to bring the pistols over, grinning, snapped the pistol locks shut, pointed one at a panicking farm pig that darted squealing through the fowls scattered in the yard, and nodded down the road. “Comin’, mas’r,” he called. “Everybody a-comin’.”

A minute later the hooves of other horses thundered into view: scrawny little Mr. Dempster spurring his pony so hard its flanks were streaked with foam, and stout, breathless Mrs. Mountain riding Madam Esmond’s grey mare, her riding habit all askew from her wild gallop. They shoved through the crowd of gawking servants, bolted across the porch where the bleary redcoat officers sat staring in confusion, and burst down the steps into the garden where Washington still paced opposite the two Warrington boys.

Before George could even demand what on earth she was doing there, Mrs. Mountain threw her arms around his neck, sobbing. “It’s all a mistake! All my fault! That paper I picked up out of Colonel Washington’s portfolio, the one that said he meant to marry a widow with two children—we all thought it was your mother! But it’s not! It’s young Widow Custis, Martha Custis, the rich one with two little ones. He told your mother that this very morning, before he left the house! She flew into such a temper I thought she’d explode. When Sady came tearing home to fetch the pistols and blabbed the whole duel plan, I told him to fire those guns off to warn everyone, and I galloped after him as fast as my old bones could carry me.”

George huffed that he had half a mind to give Sady a sound thrashing for blabbing his private plans, but Harry grabbed his arm, crying that he thanked God the boy had run his mouth. Before George could protest further, Harry went bounding over the cabbage rows straight towards Washington, his boots crunching through the loose garden soil, and poured out the whole artless, foolish tale of misplaced jealousy and misunderstood private correspondence.

Washington hauled the boy straight into a tight, warm hug, his usually steady voice cracking with relief. “Thank God,” he said, again and again. “Thank God this bloodshed is spared us.” Harry blurted out that he wished he could ride off to the frontier campaign with him and Braddock, and Washington squeezed both his hands hard in a firm grasp of friendship that would never loosen.

Then he stepped forward to young George Warrington, and held out his hand. “I owe you an apology, sir,” George said, stiff but sincere. “I acted under a terrible misapprehension, and behaved very ill to you because of it.”

“The fault was mine,” Washington replied gravely. “I chose to keep a private secret locked away that needed no one else’s eyes on it. All this pain, all this risk, could have been avoided if I had not been foolish enough to leave my personal papers where other folks might read them without my leave. I will say no more, lest my temper get the better of me again. Take a true friend’s advice, George: do not be so quick to think ill of the people around you. Save your powder, and your will to fight, for the King’s enemies on the frontier, not your countrymen.”

He gave the gaping, half-sober redcoat officers a stately formal bow, turned, and walked to his waiting horse, mounted, and rode off down the road to join Braddock’s expedition, leaving the whole relieved, dishevelled party staring after him, the spectre of completely unnecessary bloodshed banished before it could ever draw a drop.
