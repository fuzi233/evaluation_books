CHAPTER L. The Temple Gardens, once a haunt of roses and romantic quarrels—those innocent white and red blooms that sparked civil wars in Shakespeare’s day—have long since been abandoned by fashion. According to the learned author of *The Handbook of London*, even the hardiest rose has given up on this smog-choked air. Few who live in the surrounding buildings care whether any flowers bloom there anymore; most pass through only to reach their chambers, their briefcase clutched tighter than a lover’s hand, their thoughts as dry as legal briefs.

The clerks don’t carry bouquets, nor do they pause for poetry. Only the occasional lawyer with a constitutional walk might glance at the garden—but not out of reverence for York or Lancaster, nor for the ghostly echoes of Sir Roger de Coverley or Mr. Spectator strolling along the path. No, these days, it's mostly antiquarians and literary dreamers who imagine Goldsmith musing under the summer-house roof, perhaps pondering his next *Citizen of the World* or the tailor’s latest ill-fitting suit. Or maybe he’s just trying to ignore the dunning letter from Mr. Newbery.

And then, like a sudden burst of inspiration—or perhaps just a man trying to avoid thinking about money—comes Dr. Johnson, rolling forward in a snuff-coloured suit and a wig that needs both powder and a good scolding from a barber. His Scotch lackey trails behind, slightly unsteady from port wine consumed at the Mitre. “Come home,” says the Great Doctor, “and take tea with Miss Williams.” Ah, kind fancy! Sir Roger and Mr. Spectator may be fictional, but they’re no less real to us than the actual men who once walked here. Poets live longer than politicians, especially when they're remembered fondly.

Arthur Pendennis, being of a poetic and literary turn (and decidedly not interested in law), was likely lost in such musings one evening after the events of the previous chapter. He found himself wandering into the Temple Gardens, not because he’d ever planned to, but because the quiet Sunday night seemed to whisper: *Walk. Think. Escape.*

On Sundays, the Temple is serene. Chambers are empty. The bigwigs are off hosting dinner parties in Belgravia or Tyburnia, sipping claret and flirting with daughters named Ermine. Young barristers attend these feasts, while the uninvited settle for joint and half-pint at the Club, trading Circuit jokes like seasoned veterans. Only poor Mr. Cockle remains, sick in bed, fed gruel by his laundress. Mr. Toodle plays flute solos from the second floor. And young Tiger, student extraordinaire, emits cigar smoke like a volcano and leaves dishes piled outside his door—clearly a man of refined taste.

But wait—vacation time! Everyone’s gone. Except Arthur Pendennis.

Perhaps solitude drew him in. Or perhaps it was just that he’d had a rather lonely dinner at a tavern nearby and fancied some fresh air. So, off he went, walking slowly down the gravel paths, past groups of contented citizens enjoying the calm, the river, the sunset. After a few minutes, he grew tired and ducked into one of the summer-houses at the end of the main walk.

What did he think about?

The sky was clear. The Thames sparkled. The chimneys across the way were silent—no smoke, just peace. Warehouses glowed rosy in the light, as if they’d taken a bath for the Sabbath. Steamers rushed up and down, full of holiday folk. Church bells rang out, summoning prayers. It was the kind of evening Arthur might have known as a child—walking with his mother on the terrace at home, the sun setting behind the Clavering elms and the village church.

Was it memory? Or just the blush of twilight? He tapped his foot to the bells, dusted his boots with his handkerchief, stood up, stamped his foot, and declared: “No, by Jove, I’ll go home.”

That decision suggested an internal battle—whether to stay, or flee. A temptation, perhaps.

He stepped out of the summer-house—and nearly tripped over two tiny children, barely knee-high, trotting along with long blue shadows stretching eastward.

“Oh!” said one.

“Missa Pendennis!” chirped the other, with a mischievous chuckle.

Arthur looked down—and saw them: Mesdemoiselles Ameliar-Ann and Betsy-Jane, his little friends from the day before.

He blushed. Then, impulsively, he picked up Ameliar-Ann, tossed her gently into the air, and kissed her.

She screamed.

Which brought forth two ladies—Mrs. Bolton in a scarlet Caledonian Cashmere and black silk, and Miss Fanny Bolton in yellow scarf, sprigged muslin, and parasol—looking every inch the genteel matron and lady.

Fanny didn’t speak. But her eyes lit up—brighter than any window in Paper Buildings.

Mrs. Bolton, after scolding Betsy-Jane, said: “Lor sir—how very odd we should meet you here! I hope you’ve got your health well, sir. Ain’t it odd, Fanny, that we should meet Mr. Pendennis?”

Ah, coincidence. That’s all it was.

Earlier, Arthur had casually mentioned how pleasant the gardens were on Sundays. And now, lo and behold, fate had arranged a reunion—just like those polite encounters in novels where lovers meet in shrubberies or at seaside piers. Wasn’t it funny? Hadn’t you seen it happen before? At the Hall? At the Lodge?

Arthur looked dashing—white trousers, white hat, multicolored neckcloth, gold chains, shirt-studs gleaming. He was practically a prince of the blood. How he carried himself! How he blushed! Some unseen observer might have whispered: *Has anyone ever looked so fine?*

And indeed, the children had been playing at being him. Ameliar-Ann, sticking her fingers into her pinafore like Pen did with his waistcoat, had announced: “Now, Bessy-Jane, I’ll be Missa Pendennis.” Fanny laughed until she cried, kissing her sister for the performance.

Arthur was touched—especially when he saw how happy Fanny was to see him embrace the child.

But Fanny looked pale. Worn. Arthur asked why.

“I was awake all night,” she said, blushing.

“Put out her candle,” Mrs. Bolton interjected. “Hordered her to sleep and leave off readin’.”

“Reading?” Arthur asked, amused. “What was so interesting?”

“Oh, it’s so beautiful!” Fanny sighed. “‘Walter Lorraine.’ I hate Neaera—Neaera—I don’t know how to say it. But I love Leonora, and Walter… oh, how dear he is!”

How had Fanny discovered the novel—and that Arthur wrote it?

She remembered everything he’d said the night before: that he wrote books and articles. What books? She became determined to find out.

First, she charmed Captain Costigan—smiling, helping with dinner, offering to mend his shirts (which had holes so large she could stick her face through one). She danced around his room, mixed him a tumbler of whisky (and tasted a drop), made him sing an Irish song in his rich, quavering voice—fancying herself the little siren.

Then came her question: “Do you know Arthur Pendennis? Does he write a book called ‘Walter Lorraine’?”

Costigan, though not a literary type, knew the name. “Oh yes,” he said. “They call him ‘Walter Lorraine’ at the Back Kitchen. Bludyer calls him a confounded coxcomb. Hoolan wonders why Doolan doesn’t kick him.”

So Fanny went to the library—several libraries, actually. Some had the book. One wouldn’t let her borrow it without a sovereign. She didn’t have one. She returned crying.

“I was so afraid someone would take it while I was away,” she said, cheeks glowing.

Arthur was flattered—deeply moved by her innocence, her passion.

“Do you like it?” he asked. “If you come to my chambers—no, I’ll bring you one. No, I’ll send you one. Good night. Thank you, Fanny. God bless you. I mustn’t stay. Good-bye.”

He pressed her hand, nodded to her mother, and strode out—quickening his pace, talking to himself.

“Dear, dear little thing,” he murmured. “Darling little Fanny! You’re worth them all. I wish Shandon were back. I’d go home to my mother. I mustn’t see her. I won’t. I won’t, so help me—”

As he ran, people turned to watch. He collided with a small old man—Mr. Bows.

“Your very umble servant, sir,” Bows said, bowing sarcastically.

“I wish you a good day,” Arthur replied, sulkily. “Don’t detain me. I’m in a hurry. Good evening.”

Bows thought something was wrong. “Where are your rooms?” he demanded. “They told Bolton they were going to church. They weren:’t there. They’re in your chambers. They mustn’t stay there, Mr. Pendennis.”

“Damn it, sir!” Arthur snapped. “Come and see if they are. Here’s the court. Come in.”

Bows bowed and followed.

They weren’t there.

Later, as the gardens closed, the women walked away sadly, the children dragging behind. They passed under the lamp-post in Lamb Court, looked up at Pendennis’s chamber window—where a light soon appeared—and then left.

Bows inspected the room—bare, littered, unchanged since last seen. “Shall I look in the bedrooms?” Arthur asked bitterly. “See if I’ve hidden the girls in the coal-hole?”

“Your word is sufficient,” Bows said solemnly. “You say they’re not here. I believe you. And I hope they never were.”

Arthur bristled. “You’re choosing my acquaintances for me, sir. You suppose I’d ruin someone’s reputation. I remember you, Mr. Bows. I owe you kindness. But this persecution—this watching me—”

He paused, realizing he’d slipped.

“You came to steal something,” Bows said quietly.

“To visit poor old Bows? Or Mrs. Bolton?” Arthur scoffed. “O fie! Such a fine gentleman as Arthur Pendennis doesn’t condescend to sit in a laundress’s kitchen unless he has reasons of his own. My belief is—you came to steal a girl’s heart, ruin it, and cast it aside.”

“It’s sport to you,” Bows continued, “but what is it to the poor? Toys you play with, then throw away. I know your kind. Selfish. Arrogant. Proud. What does it matter to your lordship if a poor man’s daughter is broken? You must have your pleasures. We must pay for them. What are we made for, but that?”

Arthur laughed—a little too sharply. “Yes, I’m an aristocrat. In a palace three flights up. With a carpet nearly as handsome as yours, Mr. Bows. My life is grinding the people? Ruining virgins? This is comedy. Job Thornberry slapping his chest, asking my Lord how dare he trample on an honest man. But in real life, Mr. Bows—when I work as hard as you do—how can you talk about tyranny?”

“You were different once,” Bows said. “Honest. Impetuous. You liked me because I was ready to ruin myself for a woman.”

“And now?” Arthur asked.

“And now,” Bows replied, “you want a woman to ruin herself for you.”

He spoke of Fanny—how she’d become obsessed with novels, how her mind was filled with romance, how she barely noticed she lived in a kitchen. “I’ve taught her,” Bows said. “She’s talented. Winning. I’m fond of her. I’m a lonely old man. I have only her. Don’t take her from me, Mr. Pendennis. Please.”

His voice cracked.

Arthur felt it—more than sarcasm, more than threat.

“Indeed,” he said kindly, “you do me wrong. I never meant harm to Fanny. I met her by chance. I have no intentions—none.”

“None?” Bows said. “You know she’s foolish. Her mother is foolish. You met her in the gardens. She scorned me yesterday when she read your book. What am I good for but to be laughed at? A deformed old fiddler, wearing threadbare clothes, earning bread at an alehouse? You wear scent. You wear rings. You dine with great people. Who gives a crust to old Bows? I might have been great. I wrote plays, poems, music. Nobody listened. I loved a woman—she laughed at me. Now I’m alone. Don’t take this girl from me.”

Arthur sighed. “I swear, I never meant to affect her. I didn’t realize I had—until tonight. And then, I was running from temptation. When I heard the bells, I thought of home—of my mother, of pure women there. I was fleeing danger, seeking strength from God.”

Silence.

Then Bows softened. They shook hands. He apologized for misunderstanding. Arthur squeezed his hand warmly.

As they parted, Arthur said: “I’ve given a promise. I hope you trust it.”

“Amen to that prayer,” Bows said, and walked slowly down the stairs.

And so the evening ended—not with scandal, but with sorrow, honesty, and a quiet vow.
