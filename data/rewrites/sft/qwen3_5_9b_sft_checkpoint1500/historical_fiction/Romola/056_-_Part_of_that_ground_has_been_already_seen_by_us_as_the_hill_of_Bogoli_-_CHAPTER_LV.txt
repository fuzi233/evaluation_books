The sun stretched its golden arms across the long days, yet it offered neither the reunion Romola craved nor the finality she feared. There came no word from Baldassarre. Despite the government's vigilant eyes, the shadowy conspiracy remained unexposed. Yet, in lieu of answers, the season delivered two calamities that cut close to her heart: the ravaging Plague and the formal Excommunication of Savonarola.

These twin disasters served to halt Romola's drifting away from the Friar and to bind her anew to the man who had unveiled to her the vocation of duty. How could she traverse the streets of pestilence and sorrow carrying the buoyant joy of her own existence, forgetting that she owed this transcendent moral awakening solely to Fra Girolamo? And how could she stand before the silencing and banishment of a soul whose greatness lay not in error, but in the fierce energy to make Christianity a living reality, without feeling pulled irresistibly to his defense?

Deep into the sweltering heat of June, the Edict from Rome arrived, and weeks later, it was proclaimed solemnly within the Duomo. Romola attended the ceremony, seeking to fortify the sympathy for Savonarola that had become a pillar of her strength. It stood in stark, somber contrast to the vibrant mornings she had known. Gone were the upward-turned faces of citizens, the youthful crowds rising tier upon tier to form a garland of hope around the elders, and the mighty voice that thrilled the heart with visions of struggle. Instead, the great hall echoed with the gloom of evening tapers casting shadows on bare walls. A black and grey throng of monks and clerics stood with bowed, expectant countenances. Only the faint, metallic chiming of small bells broke the monotony of a voice reciting sentences long hung over the churches. Then, the lights faded, and the heavy, shuffling footsteps of the friars departed into a dim silence.

Romola's fervor for the Frate was doubled by the gleeful triumph etched onto the rough features of the crowd and the terrified confusion visible in the eyes of many of Savonarola's staunchest allies. The boundary between obedience and resistance, always a treacherous line, became nearly impassable here. To the devout Florentines, the Church was not a political compromise but a living organism, imbued with divine power to bless and to curse. This conviction was not merely an opinion held by the faithful; it was an inalienable impression, as undeniable as the curve of the sky. Thus, Savonarola's written arguments—that the Excommunication was unjust and therefore void—did not reassure them. On the contrary, they caused them to tremble, perceiving his defiance as a sacrilege cast against a mystical image, a power before which there was no shield.

For Romola, however, whose spirit had never drunk from the traditional wells of communal faith, the Church existed only through Savonarola. His moral force was her sole authority. In his exile, she saw only the menacing thrust of hostile vice: on one side, a man dedicated to public virtue and spiritual purity; on the other, an assault led by alarmed, greedy, and murderous self-interest, personified by Rodrigo Borgia, now elevated to the throne of infamy as Pope Alexander VI. While neutral observers might see nuance, Romola needed the clarity of conviction. This Excommunication stripped away the petty antagonisms of local politics, presenting Savonarola simply as a champion appealing to Christendom against a tyrannical abuse of ecclesiastical power. He became a standard-bearer leaping into the breach. Life appeared clearest when the heart beat fast at the sight of generous self-sacrifice; in those moments, doubt vanished, and the soul believed in its capacity to achieve the highest prize. A new current of this enthusiasm carried Romola through the difficult summer.

She had said nothing to Tito regarding her recent meeting with Baldassarre or the terrible revelations he had made. What purpose would agitated words serve? Would they yield the truth, or merely a cold sneer at her sympathy for an assassin? Baldassarre was clearly helpless. Her true fear was not that he would harm Tito, but that Tito, driven by terror of the injured man who haunted his dreams, would devise a new scheme to eliminate Baldassarre. Until she saw him again and learned the whole truth about his "other wife"—whether she were indeed the woman to whom Tito was first bound—she could take no decisive action.

The possibilities surrounding that other wife, striking at the very core of her hereditary pride, mixed with a newly embittered suspicion. These thoughts ate away at the lingering tenderness of her past with Tito, poisoning the memory of their union. An irresistible belief in the veracity of Baldassarre's tale caused her to shrink from Tito with a horror so profound it might have compelled a passionate confession if he had not been away from home. Like many wealthy citizens during the plague, Tito spent his leisure hours in the countryside. The charming villa of Melema welcomed guests, and since Romola had refused to flee the city, he had found no necessity to establish a country residence of his own.

Finally, in the waning days of July, the weight of these public troubles began to lift. The Plague had receded, and Savonarola's cause gained hope through a favorable magistracy that wrote urgent letters to Rome, pleading for the revocation of the Excommunication. Yet, though Romola's robust frame succumbed to the languid exhaustion following her continuous exertion, her restless mind refused stillness save during the sultry hours. In the cool of the morning and evening, she walked constantly, varying her path with a vague, desperate hope: if Baldassarre lived, perhaps she would find him. Maybe some sickness had paralyzed his memory, causing him to forget where she dwelt, or even her existence itself. That was her most sanguine explanation for his absence. But the probability that weighed heaviest on her soul was far darker: that he had perished of the Plague.
