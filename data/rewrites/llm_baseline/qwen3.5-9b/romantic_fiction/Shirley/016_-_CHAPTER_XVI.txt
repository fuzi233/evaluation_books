The local fund was thriving. Through Miss Keeldar's inspiring example, the energetic efforts of the three rectors, and the quiet, efficient assistance of their spinster aides—Mary Ann Ainley and Margaret Hall—a substantial sum had been raised. Managed wisely, this money was already alleviating the suffering of the unemployed poor. The neighborhood seemed to settle down; for two weeks, no cloth had been burned, and no violence had been committed against mills or homes in the three parishes. Shirley was optimistic that the disaster she sought to prevent was narrowly avoided, believing the threatened storm had finally passed. With summer approaching, she was certain trade would revive—it always did—and this exhausting conflict couldn't last forever. Peace must eventually return, and with it, a surge in commerce.

This was the standard tone of her conversations with her tenant, Gérard Moore, whenever they crossed paths. Moore listened with a silence that deeply frustrated her. Impatiently glancing at him, she demanded further explanation or at least a comment. Smiling in his peculiar way—with a sweetness that softened his mouth while his brow remained grave—he replied that he, too, believed the war was finite. That belief was the anchor of his hope. "You know," he continued, "I now operate Hollow's Mill entirely on speculation. I sell nothing; there is no current market. I manufacture for a future day, preparing to seize the first opportunity. Three months ago, this was impossible; I had exhausted both credit and capital. You remember who rescued me, and from what source I received the loan that saved me. On the strength of that loan, I can continue this bold gamble which, recently, I feared I could never play again. Total ruin awaits failure, and profit remains uncertain, yet I remain cheerful. As long as I can act, as long as I can strive, as long as my hands are free, depression is impossible. One year—no, perhaps six months—of the 'reign of the olive,' and I shall be safe. As you say, peace will stimulate commerce. You are right about that, but regarding the restored tranquility of our neighborhood and the lasting benefit of your charity fund, I harbor doubts. Alms-giving has never truly pacified the working classes; it never makes them grateful, nor is it human nature to expect it. Ideally, people shouldn't need such humiliating relief, yet they do, and they feel it acutely. We would feel it too if placed in their situation. Furthermore, to whom should they owe gratitude? Perhaps to you or the clergy, but certainly not to us mill-owners. They hate us more than ever. Moreover, the dissatisfied here correspond with agitators elsewhere—Nottingham, Manchester, and Birmingham serve as their headquarters. Their subordinates receive orders from their leaders; they are disciplined, and no action is taken without careful planning. Like sultry skies threatening thunder daily yet clearing each night, the danger persists even when the clouds vanish; the storm is merely delayed. The long-threatened tempest will surely break eventually. There is a direct analogy between the moral and physical atmospheres."

"Well, Mr. Moore," these meetings invariably concluded, "take care of yourself. If you believe I have ever aided you, repay me by promising to preserve your own life."

"I do; I will exercise close and vigilant care. I wish to live, not to die. The future unfolds like Eden before me; yet, when I gaze deeply into the shadows of my paradise, I perceive a vision I prefer to any seraph or cherub gliding across distant horizons."

"Do you? Pray, tell me what vision?"

"I see—"

At that moment, the maid bustled in with the tea service.

Early May had been fine, the middle wet, but during the last week, coinciding with the change of the moon, the weather cleared. A fresh breeze swept away the heavy, silver-white rainclouds, carrying them mass upon mass toward the eastern horizon until they vanished, leaving the sky a vault of pure blue, ready for the summer sun. That sun rose broad and brilliant on Whitsuntide. The gathering of the schools was marked by splendid weather.

Whit-Tuesday was the grand day. In preparation, the two large schoolrooms of Briarfield—built by the current rector largely at his own expense—were scrubbed, whitewashed, repainted, and adorned with flowers and evergreens sourced from the rectory garden, two cartloads from Fieldhead, and a wheelbarrow-full from the more parsimonious estate of De Walden, home to Mr. Wynne. Inside, twenty tables, each seating twenty guests, were arranged among benches and draped in white cloths. Suspended above were at least twenty cages housing canaries, a tradition cherished by the district and specifically favored by Mr. Helstone's clerk, who loved their piercing songs and knew they sang loudest amidst the cacophony of other tongues. It must be noted that these tables were not for the twelve hundred scholars arriving from the three parishes, but solely for the patrons and teachers. The children's feast was to be held outdoors. At one o'clock, the processions would arrive; by two, they would be marshaled; by four, they would parade the parish, followed by the feast, and finally, the meeting within the church accompanied by music and speeches.

Briarfield was chosen as the rendezvous point not because it was the largest or oldest parish—Whinbury surpassed it in population, and Nunnely's ancient temple and mossy parsonage predated it still—but simply because Mr. Helstone decreed it. His will outweighed that of either Boultby or Hall; the former lacked the power to object, and the latter refused to challenge his brother's authority. They allowed him to lead and rule.

Historically, this anniversary had been a trying ordeal for Caroline Helstone. It forced her into public view, compelling her to face the wealthy, respectable, and influential families of the neighborhood. Without the kindly countenance of Mr. Hall, she would have appeared utterly unsupported. Obligated to be conspicuous, to lead her regiment as the rector's niece and head teacher, to serve tea at the front table for a motley crowd of ladies and gentlemen, and to perform all this without a mother, aunt, or chaperone—while being a naturally timid person who dreaded publicity—it is understandable why she trembled at the approach of Whitsuntide.

However, this year Shirley was to accompany her, transforming the trial entirely. It ceased to be a burden and became almost an enjoyment. Miss Keeldar, alone, was superior to a host of ordinary friends. Self-possessed, spirited, and easygoing, conscious of her status yet never presumptuous, her mere presence inspired courage. The only anxiety was whether the heiress would be punctual. Shirley often lingered carelessly, and Caroline knew her uncle would not wait a second. At the precise moment the church clock tolled two, the bells would clash and the march begin. Caroline realized she must ensure Shirley's arrival, or her companion would fail her.

Whit-Tuesday saw Caroline rise almost with the dawn. She, Fanny, and Eliza spent the entire morning arranging the rectory parlors in impeccable order and setting out a collation of cooling refreshments—wine, fruit, and cakes—on the dining-room sideboard. Then, she dressed in her finest white muslin, a costume deemed appropriate and even required by the perfect clarity of the day and the solemnity of the occasion. Her new sash, a birthday gift from Margaret Hall (which she suspected Cyril himself had purchased, in exchange for a set of cambric bands she had given him), was tied by Fanny's deft fingers, who took great pleasure in adorning her young mistress. Her simple bonnet was trimmed to match, and her inexpensive white crape scarf complemented her dress perfectly. Ready, she formed a picture: not dazzling, but interesting; not strikingly brilliant, yet delicately pleasing. Sweetness of tint, purity of air, and grace of demeanor compensated for the lack of rich color or magnificent contours. Her brown eyes and clear forehead reflected a mind modest, gentle, and harmoniously pensive. Neither lamb nor dove needed to fear her; instead, they would welcome her look of simplicity and softness, sensing a sympathy with their own natures.

Ultimately, she was an imperfect human being, beautiful in form and attire, but, as Cyril Hall remarked, neither so virtuous nor so great as the withered Miss Ainley, who was currently donning her best black gown, Quaker drab shawl, and bonnet in her narrow cottage chamber.

Caroline departed, crossing secluded fields and hidden lanes to Fieldhead. She glided swiftly beneath green hedges and across lush leas. There was no dust or moisture to soil her pristine garment or damp her slender sandals. Following the recent rains, everything was clean, and under the glowing sun, everything was dry. Walking fearlessly over daisies and turf through thick plantations, she reached Fieldhead and entered Miss Keeldar's dressing room.

Fortunately, she had arrived in time, for Shirley would otherwise have been late. Instead of hurrying, Shirley lay stretched on a couch, absorbed in reading. Mrs. Pryor stood nearby, vainly urging her to rise. Caroline wasted no words; she immediately took the book and began undressing and redressing Shirley with her own hands. Indolent from the heat and buoyant with youthful joy, Shirley wanted to talk, laugh, and linger.
