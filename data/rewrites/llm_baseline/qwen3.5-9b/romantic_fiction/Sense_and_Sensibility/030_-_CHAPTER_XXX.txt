Upon returning home, Mrs. Jennings hastened straight to the sisters' chamber. Without awaiting permission, she pushed open the door and entered, her face etched with genuine alarm. "How do you do, my dear?" she asked, her voice thick with compassion, addressing Marianne, who had turned her face away and offered no reply. "How fares she, Miss Dashwood? Poor creature! She looks dreadfully ill. No wonder, truly. Alas, it is all too true. He is to be wed very shortly—to a woman of questionable character! I harbor no patience for such a scoundrel. Mrs. Taylor informed me of this mere half-hour past, having learned it from a close confidante of Miss Grey's; otherwise, I daresay I would never have credited it. Indeed, I nearly fainted upon hearing the news. 'Well,' I exclaimed, 'if this holds water, then this young gentleman has treated a lady of my acquaintance with abominable cruelty. May his new wife torment him until his dying day!' And so I shall always maintain, my dear; rely on it. I cannot comprehend why men behave thus. Were I to encounter him again, I would administer a beating he hasn't known in years. Yet, there is solace, my dear Miss Marianne: he is not the sole eligible bachelor in existence. With your radiant visage, you shall never lack suitors."

Having delivered this torrent of sentiment, she departed, moving on tiptoes lest her footsteps disturb the grief of her young friend, assuming that noise might exacerbate her sorrow. To her sister's astonishment, Marianne resolved to dine with the household. Elinor counseled against it, yet Marianne insisted, declaring she could endure it and preferred the distraction of the bustle to solitude. Elinor, relieved to see her sister momentarily governed by such practical motives—though doubting her ability to sustain the meal—offered no further objection. She adjusted Marianne's gown as best she could while the latter remained prostrate on the bed, preparing to escort her to the dining room upon summons.

At table, despite her wretched appearance, Marianne consumed more food and appeared calmer than Elinor anticipated. Had she attempted speech or been fully cognizant of Mrs. Jennings's well-intentioned yet clumsy attentions, such composure would have been impossible. Instead, she spoke not a single word; the trance-like absorption of her mind shielded her from awareness of the surrounding events. Elinor, acknowledging the kindness of Mrs. Jennings despite its frequent distressing and occasionally absurd nature, managed the social exchanges that her incapacitated sister could not. Observing Marianne's unhappiness, Mrs. Jennings felt compelled to offer every conceivable comfort to alleviate her pain. She treated her with the indulgent affection of a parent toward a beloved child on the final day of vacation. Marianne was granted the prime seat by the fire, tempted with every delicacy the house possessed, and bombarded with the latest gossip. Had Elinor not witnessed the tragic pallor of her sister's countenance, she might have found amusement in Mrs. Jennings's efforts to cure a broken heart with sweets, olives, and a roaring fire. However, once the relentless repetition of these gestures became unbearable to Marianne, she could endure no longer. With a sudden cry of "Misery!" and a frantic signal to her sister not to follow, she rose abruptly and fled the room.

"Poor soul!" exclaimed Mrs. Jennings immediately after her departure. "It breaks my heart to witness her plight! I declare she has vanished without finishing her wine! And the dried cherries! Good heavens, nothing seems to benefit her. If I knew of anything she desired, I would scour the entire town for it. Truly, it is the strangest thing that a man should treat such a lovely girl so poorly! But when vast wealth resides on one side and penury on the other, Lord bless you, they care naught for such trifles!"

"The lady, then—Miss Grey, I believe you termed her—is exceedingly wealthy?"

"Fifty thousand pounds, my dear! Have you ever beheld her? They say she is sharp and stylish, though not particularly handsome. I recall her aunt, Biddy Henshawe, who married a man of considerable fortune; the entire family is affluent. Fifty thousand pounds! And, by all accounts, it will not materialize until absolutely necessary, for they claim he is utterly ruined. No wonder! Dashing about in his curricle and hunting horses! Well, it serves no purpose discussing it, but when a young man, whoever he may be, courts a beautiful girl and pledges marriage, he has no right to abandon his vow merely because he falls into poverty and a richer suitor appears. Why, in such a circumstance, should he not liquidate his assets, dismiss his staff, and enact a complete reformation instantly? I wager Miss Marianne would have waited patiently for circumstances to improve. But that is not the fashion nowadays; nothing regarding pleasure can ever be relinquished by the youth of this era."

"Do you know the character of Miss Grey? Is she reputed amiable?"

"I have heard no ill of her; indeed, she is rarely spoken of, save that Mrs. Taylor remarked this morning that Miss Walker hinted to her she believed Mr. and Mrs. Ellison would welcome Miss Grey's marriage, as the two women could never agree."

"And who are the Ellisons?"

"Her guardians, my dear. Now that she is of age, she may choose her own path; and what a choice she has made!—But pray, after pausing a moment—"Your poor sister has retired to her chamber, I presume, to lament in solitude. Is there nothing we can do to console her? Poor dear, it seems cruel to allow her to remain alone. Well, soon we shall have visitors, which might amuse her slightly. What shall we play? She detests whist, I know; but does she favor any other game?"

"My dear madam, this kindness is entirely superfluous. Marianne, I trust, will not leave her room again tonight. I shall endeavor to persuade her to retire early, for I am certain she requires rest."

"Aye, I believe that is best for her. Let her dictate her own supper and retire. Good Lord! No wonder she has looked so pale and despondent for the past week or two; this matter has undoubtedly hung over her head for that duration. And so the letter arrived today to seal her fate! Poor soul! I am sure had I suspected it, I would not have teased her about it for all my wealth. But then, how could I possibly guess such a thing? I assumed it was merely a common love letter, and you know young people enjoy being laughed at regarding such matters. Good Lord, how distressed Sir John and my daughters will be upon hearing the news! If I possessed my wits, I might have stopped at Conduit Street on my way home to inform them. Yet, I shall see them tomorrow."

"It would be unnecessary for you to caution Mrs. Palmer and Sir John against mentioning Mr. Willoughby or alluding to our situation before my sister. Their inherent goodness will surely recognize the cruelty of feigning knowledge when she is present. Furthermore, the fewer words spoken to me concerning this subject, the greater relief my feelings will experience, as you, my dear madam, will readily understand."

"Oh! Lord! Yes, I do indeed. It must be dreadful for you to hear it discussed; and regarding your sister, I assure you, I would not utter a word about it to her for the world. You observed I paid no heed during dinner. Neither would Sir John, nor my daughters, for they are all remarkably thoughtful and considerate, especially if I provide them with a hint, which I certainly shall. For my part, I believe the less said about such matters, the better; the sooner it blows over and is forgotten. What good does talking accomplish, you know?"

"In this instance, it can only cause harm; perhaps more so than in many similar cases, for it has been accompanied by circumstances that render it unfit for public discourse for the sake of everyone involved. I must concede this much to Mr. Willoughby: he has broken no formal engagement with my sister."

"Law, my dear! Do not attempt to defend him. No formal engagement indeed! After touring Allenham House with her and selecting the very rooms they were to inhabit henceforth!"

For Marianne's sake, Elinor could not press the matter further, hoping it was not required for Willoughby's benefit; although Marianne might suffer greatly, he stood to gain little by enforcing the harsh reality. After a brief silence, Mrs. Jennings, with her characteristic vivacity, erupted anew.

Mark my words, if they are not married by midsummer! Good Lord, how he will chuckle over this tidings! I hope he arrives tonight. Delaford is a charming estate, I can attest; precisely the sort of quaint, old-fashioned place filled with comforts and conveniences.
