When Sally departed Detroit on the following Saturday, flanked by Fillmore—who was heading back to the city to snag some offices and establish his dominance on Broadway—her spirits had fully rebounded. She felt a pang of guilt for having been so fanciful, perhaps even morbid. *Naturally*, men sought advancement; it was their profession. She reminded herself that her fate was tied to Gerald's success, and complaining about his tireless energy was the last thing she should do, given that they would share the rewards. This renewed optimism was fueled by the recent excitement. Detroit, that haven of polite audiences, had adored "The Primrose Way." True to Teddy's prophecy, the theater opened on Tuesday to a packed house starving for diversion, welcoming the play with gusto. The press, usually skeptical of first-night crowds, unanimously hailed Gerald as the rising author and Elsa Doland as the upcoming star. There was even a fleeting nod to Fillmore as the future manager.

Yet, amidst these triumphs, a tiny irritant marred the perfection. Fillmore's victory was nearly undone because the sole mention of Gladys Winch appeared in a critic's list of supporting players, misspelled as "Wunch."

"One of the greatest character actresses on the stage," Fillmore grumbled later that morning, recounting the slight to Sally. However, his buoyant spirit quickly rallied. Life offered so much brightness that dwelling on one dark spot would be churlish. Business boomed; Elsa improved nightly. A frantic telegram from Mr. Cracknell begging to repurchase the rights (Miss Hobson seemingly having cooled) was a welcome bonus. Best of all, Ike Schumann—the tycoon owning half of New York's theaters—had visited, inspected the production, and stamped it with his approval.

Sitting across from Sally on the train, Fillmore glowed with satisfaction.

"Yes, do," Sally said, breaking a long silence.

"Eh?" Fillmore snapped out of his reverie.

"I said, 'Yes, do.' I think you owe it to your position."

"Do what?"

"Buy a fur coat. Wasn't that what you were contemplating?"

"Don't be a chump," Fillmore protested, though a blush betrayed him. He had indeed toyed with the idea; a fellow needed warmth.

"With an astrakhan collar," Sally pressed.

"As a matter of fact," Fillmore declared loftily, his noble soul unaccustomed to such banter, "what I was truly considering was something Ike said."

"Ike?"

"Ike Schumann. He's on this train. I met him just now."

"We call him Ike!" Sally exclaimed.

"Of course I call him Ike," Fillmore heatedly corrected. "Everyone does."

"He wears a fur coat," Sally whispered.

Fillmore bristled. "Please stop harping on that dreadful coat. And anyway, why shouldn't I wear one?"

"Fill..." Sally gasped. "How can you be so cruel to imply I ever suggested otherwise? I'm a staunch supporter of the fur coat! Big cuffs! You must roll up Fifth Avenue in your car, and I'll point proudly: 'That's my brother!' 'Your brother? No!' 'He is, really.' 'You're joking. That's the great Fillmore Nicholas.' 'I know. But he really is my brother. And I was with him buying that coat.'"

"Do drop the coat talk!"

"'And it isn't just the coat,' I'll declare. 'It's what lies beneath. Tucked inside that mass of fur, dodging behind that dollar cigar, stands a man worthy of our pride...'"

Fillmore checked his watch sternly. "I must go see Ike Schumann."

"We consult with Ike hourly."

"He wants to see me about the show. He suggests launching it in Chicago before New York."

"Oh no!" Sally cried, dismayed.

"Why not?" she recovered, realizing that while she feared separation from Gerald, he wouldn't need to stay with the company long. "You're worried we lack a New York reputation for Chicago. Still, a Chicago run helps New York. Well, I'll ponder it."

"All wrong," Sally interjected.

"Eh?"

"Not a bit of it. Compress your lips and press your right index finger against your temple in a worried manner. You have much to learn, Fill."

"Oh, stop it!"

"Fillmore Nicholas," Sally said seriously, "if you knew the pain of teasing my only brother, you'd regret it. But it's for your own good. Go now and relieve Ike of his anxiety. Remember his last words to me: 'Will he come, Miss Nicholas?' Oh, Fill, the longing in his voice. 'Of course he will,' I assured him. 'My brother is kindness incarnate.' 'If only I could believe that,' he gulped. 'Managers are busy; they brood over fur coats and forget everything.' 'Fear not, Mr. Schumann,' I promised. 'Fillmore Nicholas keeps his word.'"

She would have continued the charade, for she spared no effort entertaining her kin, but Fillmore was already striding down the aisle, his stiff back a silent rebuke to her jests. Sally watched him vanish, picked up a magazine, and began reading. She tracked a gripping jungle adventure only to discover it was split into two parts, leaving her hanging.

"How do you do, Miss Nicholas?"

Into the seat beside her, freshly vacated by the manager, slid none other than Bruce Carmyle. He inserted himself with that familiar, well-bred air of deferential restraint that never abandoned him.

Sally was startled. While travelers often encounter acquaintances abroad, finding a man in America whom she presumed was in Europe felt like rewinding time and reopening a closed chapter.

"Mr. Carmyle!" she exclaimed.

Even if Sally had been constantly on Carmyle's mind since their parting on the Paris express, he occupied very little space in hers. She searched her memory briefly to identify him.

"We meet on trains frequently, don't we?" she replied, regaining her composure. "I never expected to see you in America."

"I came over."

Sally hesitated to ask why, but a sudden embarrassment silenced her. She recalled her previous rudeness toward him—a sin she invariably followed with remorse. She settled for a bland "Yes."

"Yes," Carmyle agreed, "it has been many years since I took a proper holiday. My doctor thought I was run down. Visiting America seemed ideal. Everyone," he stated oracularly, attempting to convince himself as he often did since sailing from England, "ought to visit America at least once. It is part of one's education."

"And what are your impressions of our glorious nation?" Sally teased.

Carmyle seemed pleased to lecture on neutral ground. Though his face showed no sign, he was initially embarrassed. The sound of his own voice restored him.

"I visited Chicago," he said after a brief travelogue.

"Oh!"

"A magnificent city."

"I've never seen it. I came from Detroit."

"Yes, I heard you were in Detroit." Sally's eyes widened. "You heard I was in Detroit? Good heavens! How?"

"I... ah... called at your New York address and made inquiries," Carmyle admitted awkwardly.

"But how did you know where I lived?"

"My cousin—er—Lancelot told me."

Sally fell silent. Like a detective realizing he is being tailed, she felt uneasy. Whether Carmyle had pursued her directly or not, it was clear he found her highly interesting. It was flattering, yet disturbing to realize she held such significance for him. Seizing the mention of Ginger as a distraction, she steered the conversation away.

"How is Mr. Kemp?" she asked.

Carmyle's dark face grew slightly darker.

"We have received no news of him," he said curtly.

"No news? Do you mean he's vanished?"

"He has disappeared!"

"Good heavens! When?"

"Shortly after I saw you last."

"Disappeared!" Carmyle frowned.

Watching him, Sally felt her initial dislike resurface. There was an inherent hardness about the man.

"But where has he gone?"

"I don't know."

Carmyle frowned again. The topic of Ginger was clearly painful.

"And I don't wish to know," he added heatedly, a flush rising in cheeks Sally knew required double shaving. "The family has washed its hands of him. Let him manage as best he can. I believe he is off his head."

Sally's rebellious temper flared, but she suppressed it. She felt compelled to defend Ginger, yet she recognized she must placate Carmyle to learn more.

"What happened? What was the trouble?"

Carmyle's eyebrows knit together. "He insulted his uncle, Donald. The one man he should have respected—"

"By keeping in touch with?"

"Yes. His future depended on it."

"But what did he do?" Sally cried, fighting to keep a mischievous gleam from her eyes.

"I heard no specifics. My uncle is reticent. He invited Lancelot to dinner to discuss plans, and Lancelot defied him. Defied him! He was rude and insolent. My uncle refuses to see him again. Apparently, the young fool won some money at Roville, which turned his head. My uncle insists he is mad. I agree. Since that dinner, nothing has been heard of Lancelot."
