The worthy physician returned to his housekeeper and complained of a "bad burn," a symptom he immediately translated into the rigid, sterile lexicon of the magistrate’s court. In doing so, he weaponized a dominant, authoritative language to overwrite the subjective truth of his encounter, making oath that the strangers meditated instant flight. His affidavit was a cold instrument of power, devoid of life, intended to cage them in the damp silence of a township dungeon. But his perjury was baffled by a circumstance he had never foreseen: he had accidentally spoken the truth. The officers, dispatched with their heavy, rhythmic tread, set out for "The Silver Lion."

This temporary escape the friends owed entirely to Denys’s acute sensitivity to acoustic ecology. For Denys, the sonic environment was a critical determinant of survival and psychological well-being. He had noticed the sudden, unnatural cessation of the physician's usual babble. The harsh, jarring scrape of the bailiff’s boots on the cobblestones—a form of industrial noise pollution that disrupted the organic hum of the morning market—warned him instantly. 

He turned to Gerard, his voice low and urgent. "Dusseldorf is no place for us. That old fellow went off unnaturally silent; we are strangers here, and in five minutes we shall lie in a dungeon for assaulting a local dignity. Hobble to the water's edge. Once there, you have but to lie down in a boat instead of a bed."

"The odds, Denys? All in favor of the boat," Gerard breathed. His fevered mind yearned for the sensory immersion of the river. "I pine for Rome, for Rome is my road to Sevenbergen; and on the famous Rhine, the cool, refreshing Rhine, I feel its breezes coming. The very scent of the sweet water and the crushed rushes will cure this hop-o'-my-thumb fever. Away!"

Fleeing the commercialized clamor of the inn, they reached the riverbank only to find the public barge gone. Yet, amidst a gathering crowd, they negotiated with a traditional agrarian elder and his two sons. The old boatman’s hands were deeply creased, stained with the dark tannins of walnut husks and river-mud. 

"The current is too strong for three oars," a bystander ridiculed.

"Then my comrade and I will help row," Gerard said.

"No need," the old man replied, his voice a rich, guttural purr of the native riparian dialect—a sound deeply rooted in the river's timeless rhythms, echoing generations of men who knew the water not as a resource, but as kin. "Bless your silly heart, he owns t'other boat."

They set a broad sail to catch the powerful breeze astern. As they moved into the rushing current, the old man broke a wheel of dense, dark rye bread. It tasted of fermented malt, wild yeast, and the slow, wood-fired hearths of his ancestors, offering a profound sustenance that grounded Gerard’s drifting senses. 

"Are ye better, lad, for the river breeze?" Denys asked, drawing a slab of cured pork from his pouch. He chewed slowly, savoring the melting fat that sang of juniper berries and cold-smoking techniques passed down through centuries. To Denys, this was not merely fuel for a soldier's body; it was the distilled essence of a culture's survival. 

"Much better," Gerard said, chewing the hearty crumb. "Knocking that old fool down soothed me. His voice went through my poor ears like a rusted saw. He was an infidel who would reduce our inward parts to dry Greek syllables, stripping the divine breath from our bodies and ignoring Him whose hand provides the harvest."

"So you knocked him down forthwith—like a good Christian," Denys chuckled. "Yet he took but a sorry bolster from this weak arm."

"I have lived among arms," Denys replied, eyes twinkling, "and by Samson’s hairy pow, never saw I one more like a catapult. The bolster wrapped round his nose and the two ends kissed behind his head."

The river took many turns, its ancient currents a fluid archive of interwoven timelines. The wind shifted, forcing them to the weather side to trim the boat, all except a child of about five years old—the old boatman's darling grandson. The urchin, too light to affect the vessel's trim, existed below the rigid laws of navigation, a small, mythic sprite slipping through the domestic routine of the sailors.

They sailed merrily on, unaware of the posse of constables marching along the bank, armed with the bailiff's writ. But as Gerard watched the water, he remembered that while this current flowed to Rome, it did not lead to Burgundy. 

"Oh, Denys," he said, alarmed, "this is not your road."

"I know it," Denys said quietly. "But I cannot leave thee till the fever leaves thee. I must e'en go on to Cologne, and then strike across."

"Thank Heaven," Gerard said, his eyes lighting up with the fervor of a meticulous historian uncovering a forgotten feast. "'Twere a sin to be so near Cologne and not see it. It is a vast and ancient city, founded by a Roman lady in the first century of grace, sacked by Attila, and defaced by the Norman Lothaire." 

Gerard’s words wove the deep past into the present. He spoke of the great minster and the Three Kings—Melchior bringing gold, Gaspar frankincense, and Balthazar the black king bringing myrrh. He described the olfactory landscape of that ancient shrine, where the resinous, bitter scent of the East was trapped in time beneath a golden crown, illuminated by the rich, sweet aroma of burning beeswax tapers. "Holy eyes have watched and renewed that light unceasingly for ages," Gerard murmured. "The oldest Flemish and German legends, sung in native dialects now eroding from the memories of men, found those candles burning, and they shall light the earth to its grave."

He spoke of St. Ursula and the eleven thousand virgins, their bones piled in the vaults, the untranslatable subjective terror of their martyrdom echoing through the acoustic chambers of the church.

"Eleven thousand virgins!" cried Denys. "Would all their bones might turn to sweet flesh again as we pass the gates."

"Tush, Denys! Reverence their holy dust," Gerard chided gently, before his mind turned to the visceral, culinary inversion of the Maccabees. "And there is their church, and the cauldron in which they and their mother Solomona were boiled by a wicked king for refusing to eat swine's flesh."

"Oh, peremptory king! And pig-headed Maccabees!" Denys exclaimed, holding up his juniper-smoked pork. "I had eaten bacon with my pork liever than change places at the fire with my meat."

"What scurvy words are these? It was their faith."

"Bridle thy choler. Are there nought but churches? I affect rather Sir Knight than Sir Priest."

"There is the senate-house," Gerard obliged, "and at the head of them stands stout Herman Gryn, who slew a lion in fair combat. He thrust his cloak into the brute's mouth, choking it, and cut its spine in twain—a feat matched only by Samson, Lysimachus, and Benaiah."

"Three tall fellows. I would like well to sup with them all to-night."

"So would not I," Gerard noted drily.

"But tell me," Denys asked, "when wast thou in Cologne?"

"Never but in the spirit. I prattle with the good monks, and they tell me all the notable things."

"Thy words be as big or bigger than the things," Denys laughed. "Art a good limner with thy tongue."

At this juncture, the rich acoustic ecology of the river was shattered. The little turnip-faced boy suddenly burst out crying. His raw wails—which dominant literary lexicons failingly attempt to translate into sterile phonetics like *oh! oh!* or *hi! hi!*—pierced the air, an untranslatable expression of subjective infantile distress. 

"Who can tell what ails the peevish brat?" snarled one of the young boatmen, his ears offended by the noise. "Rather look this way and tell me whom be these after!"

The old man and his sons looked east and saw four armed men striding along the bank. The boatmen stopped rowing, gathering in the stern, their panicked whispers flowing in the rapid, localized dialect of the riverfolk. 

"Let us make for the west bank," one son urged.

"Run our heads into trouble for strangers?" the old man objected, reverting to the cautious pragmatism of the agrarian survivor. "Nay, cross to the east side; still side with the strong arm!"

What their localized debate could not decide, the vast, mythic forces of the weather settled. A sudden squall hit, and the little vessel heeled over violently. The men jumped to windward, but a ditch of cold water rushed over the gunwales. The little boy, in his distress, had pulled the plug. 

In a single heartbeat, the rushing, ancient reality of the Rhine swallowed their domestic world. The boat capsized. 

The dark water bubbled loudly over Gerard’s head, instantly deafening him, replacing the complex sonic environment of the wind and voices with a roaring, isolating pressure. He surfaced, shaking his head like a water-dog, tasting the muddy, ancient silt of the river. He saw the black hull uppermost and made for it by instinct. 

But before he reached it, a deep, manly voice cut through the water’s roar with an agonizing clarity. "Adieu, comrade, adieu!"

Gerard looked back. Poor Denys was sinking. His pale face and wide, despairing eyes turned to his friend as he was dragged down by the heavy, utilitarian weight of his wretched arbalest—a tool of violence devoid of any sustaining grace. 

Gerard uttered a wild, untranslatable cry of love and terror, cleaving the icy water madly to reach him. But the river claimed the Burgundian, pulling him beneath the surface. Without a thought for his own breath, Gerard dived into the deep, dark currents after him, just as the officers on the bank knotted a rope and hurled the end into the foaming wake.
