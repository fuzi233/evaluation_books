Major Pendennis, being a man who could keep a promise as neatly as he kept a crease, contrived to satisfy his own conscience and, what was harder work, to soothe poor Helen’s. He let her understand—by nods, hems, and the kind of sentences that end before they begin—that all connexion between Arthur and the odious little gatekeeper had been clipped off as clean as parsley with a sharp knife. Helen might sleep, then, without fearing that her son would be carried off by imprudence, or—worse in her creed—by a degrading marriage.

Arthur’s mind, too, after the first smart to his vanity (that tender part of a gentleman which bruises easiest), recovered its ease in a very comfortable reflection: Miss Fanny was not going to die of love for him; the world would not be scandalized by any tragic sequel; no inconvenient child, no lawsuit, no melodrama would pop out from behind the scenes. The luckless little episode, like an overdone cutlet, might be forgotten, and the family might travel.

So they went; and Pen, now styled by himself a “rentier,” and by his passport a personage of weight and height, set forth “voyageant avec Madame Pendennis and Mademoiselle Bell,” with George Warrington, “particulier, age de 32 ans, taille 6 pieds (Anglais),” and other marks by which men are reduced at frontiers to hair-colour and inches. They procured passports at Dover; crossed to Ostend; and made their way with that leisurely gravity which English people assume on the Continent as if time were a waiter they had hired—through Bruges and Ghent to Brussels and the Rhine.

It is not our business to paint every gable and canal; but it would have been worth any man’s while to observe how the very air altered to Helen Pendennis. In those old cities it was not only the bells that were strange, but the silences between the bells; the hush in a beguinage court where a woman’s step seemed an impertinence; the sudden roar of an organ that shook the ribs like thunder in a kitchen-flue; the muttering of prayers in a tongue that was not her own, and therefore sounded to her like conspiracies. The Catholic ceremonies, with their black veils and outstretched arms, their candles and incense, their crowned images bowing to crowned images, pressed on her mind as heavily as a hot lid on a boiling pot. Barefooted friars padding along the stones; confessionals like dark cupboards; theatres open on Sundays; dancers scraping merrily while her conscience sat rigid as a pew—all these novelties bewildered the simple country lady more than any foreign grammar.

At their lodgings, when the young men came back from drive or promenade, they found always the same domestic little altar of habit: Helen’s books of devotion laid open on the table; Laura reading softly, and breaking off as they entered, as if their footfall had startled the very words. The late troubles connected with Arthur had shaken Helen cruelly. Laura, tender as bread fresh from the oven and as watchful, too, observed every change in her friend’s face—those faint blanchings and flushes which tell you, if you have any eyes, that the heart is being pinched somewhere out of sight. Pen, for his part, was constant in the outward duties; he waited upon his mother with affectionate punctuality. Yet between them a cloud hung always, as if a curtain had been drawn across a familiar room. Helen’s love yearned towards him; and yet there was a secret—an anguish, almost a rage—at finding recesses in her son’s heart which she dared not enter. She sickened thinking of the sacred boyish days when Arthur had poured into her lap his triumphs and childish griefs as naturally as a child pours milk into a bowl; when home had been his nest; before selfishness, nature, fate—call the cook by what name you please—had sent him forth to range, to sing his own song, to seek his own mate.

Once Laura, with that honest feminine logic which goes straight to the wound, said, “If Pen had loved me as you wished, I should have gained him; but I should have lost you, mamma—I know I should. And I like you to love me best. Men do not know what it is to love as we do, I think.” Helen sighed, and agreed to the last part, protesting against the first; and perhaps Miss Laura was right in both. Love with a man is too often an hour snatched between taxes and tailors; with a woman it is breakfast, dinner, supper, and the long night between—the continual simmering of the heart, which never is taken off the fire.

Old Major Pendennis had only escorted them to the water’s edge, placed them aboard as one stows away valuables in a carriage, and handed over the chief charge of the expedition to Warrington. The Major himself was bound for a brief visit to the house of a great man—an establishment where pheasants were still plentiful though the best partridges had been thinned off, and where the guests were of that rich variety which makes a man feel important for having eaten the soup in their presence. At Stillbrook, where the Marquis of Steyne entertained dukes, foreigners of note, and illustrious statesmen, the Major disported with comfort and deserved complacency. It warmed his heart to see his own name peeping in the Morning Post among the distinguished company, as modestly as a truffle among mushrooms.

He was invaluable in such a house: full of anecdotes that young men laughed at, and with, in the smoking-room; obsequious with the ladies in the morning as if each were a duchess and he her waiting-maid; a guide who could point out the best view of the lake, the place where the old road went before the new bridge, the very patch in the wood where old Lord Lynx discovered Sir Phelim O’Neal on his knees before her ladyship—etcetera, etcetera. He knew lodge-keepers and gardeners by name, the number of domestics who sat down in the housekeeper’s room, and how many ate in the servants’ hall; he had a word for everybody and a little against everybody. A country-house is a great kitchen of talk: the Major was an excellent cook at the dish.

Nor was he ill-pleased, perhaps, at having shifted the Continental command to Warrington, thus keeping that stiff gentleman in the service of the ladies—a servitude George undertook willingly for his friend’s sake, and for the sake of a society that daily grew more delightful. Warrington, being a good German scholar, gave Miss Laura lessons; she drank in new words as eagerly as she would have sipped a new sauce. Pen, for his part, was too weak or lazy to resume his own studies, preferring the more practical education of sleep and meals.

Warrington acted as courier and interpreter; saw baggage in and out of inns and boats; managed money matters; marshalled the little troop. He found the English church, and, if Mrs. Pendennis and Miss Laura were inclined that way, walked with proper decorum beside them. He walked by Mrs. Pendennis’s donkey when she took her evening excursions, as gravely as if she rode in state; he procured ‘Galignani’; he invented comfortable seats under lime-trees where the bath guests paraded after dinner while the Kursaal band played—music floating and settling in the leaves, making the air itself seem to have a tune.

Many a fine-whiskered Prussian or French dandy, in town for Trente-et-quarante, cast longing glances at the fresh-coloured English girl beside the pale widow, and would have liked to whirl her at a galop or waltz. But Laura seldom appeared in the ballroom, except once or twice when Pen vouchsafed to walk with her; and Warrington, that rough diamond, had not been polished by any dancing-master, and did not know how to waltz—though he would have liked to learn, if the partner had been Laura. Such a partner! Psha—what had a stiff bachelor to do with partners? What was he about, drinking in pleasure with the risk of lonely longing to follow? Yet he stayed on.

You might have taken him for the widow’s son from his constant care; or for an adventurer sniffing after fortune—though the truth is most people, generously or meanly, are occupied about Number One. Warrington devoted himself to Helen; Helen devoted herself to Pen; and Pen, at this present period, devoted himself chiefly to himself—except for a real disquiet about his mother’s health. They sat together often; they did not talk much; the cloud stood between them.

Every day Laura looked for Warrington and welcomed him with frank eagerness. He found himself talking to her as he had not known he could. He performed small gallantries and afterwards stared at himself as if surprised to find his own hands had done them. He began to look blankly in the glass at the crow’s-feet round his eyes, at the streaks of white in his hair, at the intrusive silver bristles in his grim blue beard. He looked at the young bucks—the tight-waisted Germans, the capering Frenchmen with lacquered moustaches, the English dandies, Pen among them, calm and insolent in their languor—and envied each some excellence of youth. And every night, when he quitted the little circle, he went away with greater reluctance, and felt his own lodging emptier than before.

The widow could not help seeing his attachment. She understood now why Major Pendennis (always a tacit enemy of her darling project) had been so eager Warrington should be of their party. Laura frankly owned her enthusiastic regard for him; and Arthur made no movement. He did not choose to see; or did not care to prevent; or actually encouraged it. Helen remembered his saying he could not understand how a man proposed to a woman twice. She was in torture—at secret feud with her own son—averse to Warrington, who was good and generous, and yet threatened her schemes; in doubt about Laura in a way she dared not name. No wonder the healing waters of Rosenbad did her little good, and that Doctor von Glauber found the poor lady made no progress.

Meanwhile Pen recovered rapidly; slept with immense perseverance twelve hours out of the twenty-four; ate huge meals with the steady application of a man repaying the body for its late inconvenience; and by the end of a couple of months had almost regained his former strength and weight.

After they had passed about fifteen days at Rosenbad, a letter came from Major Pendennis announcing his speedy arrival; and soon after, the Major himself appeared, accompanied by Morgan—faithful valet and indispensable apparatus. The old gentleman travelled in a jaunty, juvenile costume; from the back you might have taken him for one of those slim youths whom Warrington was beginning to envy. Only when he moved did you see that Time had weakened his knees, and unkindly interfered with the action of the natty varnished boots which still pinched his toes.

Rosenbad that autumn was full of magnates. The Major read the strangers’ list with gratification on the night of his arrival, delighted to find acquaintances among the great folks, and promised himself the honour of presenting his nephew to a German Grand Duchess, a Russian Princess, and an English Marquis. Pen was not averse: he liked polite life and its splendours. That very evening the Major, leaning on Arthur’s arm, made his appearance in the Kursaal and lost or won a napoleon or two at Trente-et-quarante. He did not play to lose or win, he said; he played because other folks played; and betted his napoleon with an air of virtue.

He pointed out Russians and Spaniards gambling for heaps of gold, denouncing their eagerness as sordid and barbarous. An English gentleman should play where the fashion is to play, but should not elate or depress himself at the sport. He told of seeing his friend the Marquis of Steyne, when Lord Gaunt, lose eighteen thousand at a sitting and break the bank three nights running at Paris without ever showing emotion. “That’s what I call being an English gentleman, Pen, my dear boy,” the old man said, warming as he prattled—“the great manner only remains with us and with a few families in France.”

And as Russian Princesses passed whose reputations had long ceased to be doubtful, and damaged English ladies with their faithful attendant for the time being, the Major, with mischievous relish, poured into Pen’s ear wonderful particulars, seasoning the evening with scandal as cooks pepper a stew. Egad, he felt young again, he said, as Princess Obstropski—rouged, grinning, her enormous chasseur behind her carrying her shawl—smiled, recognized, and accosted him. He remembered her in ’14, when she was an actress on the Boulevard; and how Obstropski, the Emperor Alexander’s aide-de-camp (a man of talents, who knew a great deal about the Emperor Paul’s death, and was a devil to play), married her. The Major asked leave to call upon her, and to present his nephew, Mr. Arthur Pendennis; and pointed out half a dozen other famous personages whose histories were, to his mind, equally “satisfying.”

What would poor Helen have thought, had she heard those tales, or known to what sort of people her brother-in-law was presenting her son? She had passed once through the room where the green tables were prepared, and the croaking croupiers called their fatal Rouge gagne and Couleur perd. She had shrunk out as from a kitchen full of smoke and knives, imploring Pen, extorting a promise on his word of honour that he would never play. The pandemonium that stifled her amused the worldly old veteran and made him younger. Her right was not his right; his food was her poison. Men breathe different airs and call them health.

To Pen’s credit, he kept his promise honestly, and stoutly told his uncle he would abide by it.

Yet the Major’s arrival cast a damp upon at least three in the little party: upon Laura, who had anything but respect for him; upon Warrington, whose manner showed involuntary haughtiness and contempt; and upon timid Helen, who dreaded his interference with her desperate projects. Indeed, the Major, unknown to himself, was the bearer of tidings that would bring about a catastrophe in their affairs.

Pen and the two ladies had apartments in the town; Warrington lodged hard by; the Major, as befitted his dignity, took quarters at one of the great hotels—the Roman Emperor or the Four Seasons—where two or three hundred gamblers, pleasure-seekers, and invalids sat down daily and over-ate themselves at an enormous table-d’hôte, as if trying to drown their maladies in gravy.

On the morning after the Major’s arrival, Pen went dutifully to pay respects. He found his uncle’s sitting-room set out by Morgan with the precision of a well-ordered pantry: hats brushed, coats laid out; despatch-boxes, umbrella-cases, guidebooks, passports, maps—all trim as if still in Jermyn Street. Even the medicine bottle was fresh filled from the pharmacien’s; and the old fellow’s prayer-book lay ready, for he made a point of appearing at the English church in every place he honoured with a stay. “Everybody did it,” he said; “every English gentleman did it,” and he would as soon have omitted the national worship as he would have failed to call upon an ambassador.

The Major had gone to take one of Rosenbad’s famous baths and was not yet finished with his toilette when Pen arrived. The elder called out cheerily from the inner room. Presently Morgan came in bearing a little packet addressed to Pen—Mr. Arthur’s letters and papers, brought from his chambers in London. Chiefly numbers of the Pall Mall Gazette, which Mr. Finucane, in his benevolent tyranny, thought his collaborateur would like to see. The papers were tied together; the letters lay in an envelope in Finucane’s handwriting.

Among them was a little note addressed, as a former one had been, to “Arther Pendennis, Esquire.” Arthur opened it with a start and a blush, and read with a keen pang of interest, sorrow, and regard. Fanny Bolton wrote that she had come to his house and found he was gone—gone away to Germany without leaving a word for her, or an answer to her last letter, in which she prayed but for one word of kindness—or the books he had promised her in happier times, before he was ill, which she would like to keep in remembrance. She would not reproach those who had found her at his bedside in the fever, when he knew nobody, and who had turned the poor girl away without a word. She thought she should have died of that; but Doctor Goodenough had kindly tended her and kept her life, though perhaps the keeping of it was of no good. She forgave everybody; and as for Arthur, she would pray for him forever. When they cut off his hair, she had made so free as to keep one little lock for herself, and that she owned; and might she still keep it, or would his mamma order that that should be given up too? She was willing to obey him in all things, and could not but remember that once he was so kind—oh, so good and kind—to his poor Fanny.

When Major Pendennis came out fresh and smirking from his toilette, he found Arthur with the note before him and a savage anger on his face. The elder gentleman started, surprised.

“What news from London, my boy?” he asked rather faintly. “Are the duns at you, that you look so glum?”

“Do you know anything about this letter, sir?” Arthur asked.

“What letter, my good sir?” said the other, dry at once, and perceiving what had happened.

“You know what I mean—about, about Miss—about Fanny Bolton—the poor dear little girl,” Arthur broke out. “When she was in my room—was she there when I was delirious? I fancied she was—was she? Who sent her out of my chambers? who intercepted her letters to me? Who dared to do it? Did you do it, uncle?”

“It’s not my practice to tamper with gentlemen’s letters, or to answer damned impertinent questions,” Major Pendennis cried, trembling with indignation. “There was a girl in your rooms when I came up at great personal inconvenience, daymy—and to meet with a return of this kind for my affection to you is not pleasant, by Gad, sir—not at all pleasant.”

“That’s not the question, sir,” Arthur said hotly—“and I beg your pardon, uncle. You were, you always have been, most kind to me: but I say again, did you say anything harsh to this poor girl? Did you send her away from me?”

“I never spoke a word to the girl,” the uncle said. “I never sent her away from you, and know no more about her, and wish to know no more about her, than about the man in the moon.”

“Then it’s my mother that did it,” Arthur broke out. “Did my mother send that poor child away?”

“I repeat I know nothing about it, sir,” the elder said testily. “Let’s change the subject, if you please.”

“I’ll never forgive the person who did it,” said Arthur, bouncing up and seizing his hat.

The Major cried, “Stop, Arthur, for God’s sake, stop;” but before the words were well out Arthur had rushed from the room. The next minute the Major saw him striding rapidly down the street towards home.

“Get breakfast,” said the old fellow to Morgan; and he wagged his head and sighed as he looked from the window. “Poor Helen—poor soul! There’ll be a row. I knew there would: and begad all the fat’s in the fire.”

When Pen reached home he found only Warrington in the ladies’ drawing-room, waiting their arrival to conduct them to the room where the little English colony held their Sunday church. Helen and Laura had not appeared yet; Helen was ailing, and her daughter was with her. Pen’s wrath was too hot to stand over like porridge; it must boil over at once.

He flung Fanny’s letter across the table to his friend. “Look there, Warrington,” he said. “She tended me in my illness; she rescued me out of the jaws of death; and this is the way they have treated the dear little creature. They have kept her letters from me; they have treated me like a child, and her like a dog, poor thing! My mother has done this.”

“If she has,” Warrington said quietly, “you must remember it is your mother.”

“It only makes the crime the greater, because it is she who has done it,” Pen answered. “She ought to have been the poor girl’s defender, not her enemy: she ought to go down on her knees and ask pardon of her. I ought! I will! I am shocked at the cruelty which has been shown her. What? She gave me her all, and this is her return! She sacrifices everything for me, and they spurn her.”

“Hush!” said Warrington. “They can hear you from the next room.”
