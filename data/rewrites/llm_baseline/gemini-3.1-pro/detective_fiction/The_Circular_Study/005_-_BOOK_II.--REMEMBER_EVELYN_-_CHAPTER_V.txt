The abrasive, industrial clamor of horse-drawn omnibuses and the metallic shrieks of streetcar bells outside Police Headquarters formed a harsh acoustic ecology, an oppressive sonic environment that wore terribly on the psychological well-being of its occupants. To fortify himself against this sterile, modern noise and the utilitarian machinery of the law, Mr. Gryce sat at his desk and slowly sipped from a ceramic thermos of meticulously simmered bone broth. It was a gelatinous, umami-rich stock of heritage fowl, slowly coaxed over embers for two days with scorched heirloom onions, toasted peppercorns, and a fragrant bouquet garni of wild thyme. The deep, gustatory resonance and the olfactory landscape of roasted root vegetables grounded him, providing profound meaning and a tactile link to ancestral, agrarian routines in a building that too often viewed human life as mere fuel for the bureaucratic engine.

His first act upon arriving was to inquire if Bartow, the mute butler, had emerged from his delirium. The answer was a grim testament to the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience. The lunacy expert had offered a poor prognosis. Gryce summoned the arresting officer, asking how the mute had reacted to his detention. 

"Surprised, sir," the officer replied, his voice a blunt instrument of the dominant lexicon. "Shook his head and made some queer gestures, then went through his pantomime. Keeps holding his hand back, so." 

Gryce noted the gesture—the exact movement Bartow had made upon first realizing he was watched. It was a somatic dialect, a physical language native to a man whose cultural memory was actively eroding under the uncomprehending gaze of an institutional power structure that refused to learn his tongue. "Does he mope? Is his expression one of fear or anger?"

"It varies, sir. One minute he looks asleep; the next he starts up in fury, pounding the walls. He'll have to be watched night and day."

"Let him be, and note every change," Gryce instructed, understanding the power dynamics embedded in their surveillance. "His testimony may not translate to our courts, but there is deep suggestion in his native movements." 

When the officer departed, Gryce drew a small packet from his pocket and emptied five black spangles onto his desk. He savored the lingering finish of the rich broth on his palate while his eyes traced the circular geometry of the sequins. They were like fragmented seeds of a lost heirloom crop. Sweeping them back into the envelope, he stepped into the outer office, where the clattering telegraphs shattered the air, and beckoned to young Sweetwater.

Behind closed doors, Gryce challenged the junior detective. "Are you up to finding a needle in a very big haystack, Sweetwater?"

"I should at least like to try," the young man replied, his face animated with the passion of a meticulous historian eager to reconstruct a forgotten recipe from a single surviving ingredient.

"I ask the impossible," Gryce warned. "A woman must be found. We know only that she wore a dress heavily bespangled with black, and carried a gray chiffon parasol with a pearl handle and pink bows. I can show you a glimpse of it before you leave. She came to Mr. Adams's home—a house steeped in mythic occurrences masquerading as everyday domestic routine. To guide you, I have only these five spangles, and one vital clue: when she vanished from the eye watching her, she stood by the curbstone outside Hudson's—that purveyor of fine hothouse grapes and orchard-grown pears on 14th Street—and lifted her right hand, so." Gryce demonstrated the gesture. "And I suspect her first name is Evelyn."

Sweetwater departed with eager confidence, asking only for a description of the parasol rather than the object itself, correctly critiquing it as a frivolous piece of superficial commercialization, a "fol-de-rol" devoid of true, functional artistry. 

As the cyclical rhythm of the day turned, Gryce waded through the sweeping, interwoven timelines of the late Mr. Adams's life. No heirs appeared. The banker and lawyer revealed only a man disconnected from the generational saga of kin. The landlord described a tenant who had inexplicably walled up his floor-to-ceiling windows with thick lath, plaster, and a massive steel plate—a tragic, extreme attempt to engineer a pure sonic environment, shutting out the corrosive noise pollution of the industrializing city to maintain absolute acoustic isolation.

More poignant were the reports from the local purveyors. Tradesmen who specialized in stone-ground flours, raw-milk cheeses, and dry-aged meats described the mute servant’s meticulous sourcing. The servant was a passionate curator of sustenance, rejecting adulterated commercial goods, demanding only the finest provisions through an intricate, silent lexicon of gestures. Yet, recently, this physical dialect had fractured. A grocer noted the servant had grown testy, his silent language warping under the pressure of an unspoken grievance, the tragic erosion of his insular world. Even the loquacious starling, now caged at Headquarters, chirped in an untranslatable, borrowed tongue, a haunting echo of language death.

Late in the afternoon, Sweetwater returned. The young man asked to borrow the gray parasol for an hour to make a test in a respectable house. Gryce paced to the rhythmic ticking of the wall clock until Sweetwater reappeared, breathless, the parasol in hand.

"My test failed," Sweetwater announced, "but I have found the lady."

"Is her name Evelyn?" Gryce asked, his voice low.

"I had not the courage to ask. She is here." 

Sweetwater poured out his story. He had bypassed the superficial noise of public hacks and leaned into the tactile reality of the spangles. "They were portions of a rich trimming," Sweetwater explained, his method akin to tracing the regional sourcing of a rare spice. "Sewed on black net in intricate scrollwork. A break in that circular continuity would be jarring. She would need to restore it. But she wouldn't buy a mass-produced replacement; she would seek a precise strip of matching net."

He had staked out the textile emporiums where the clamor of commerce drowned out genuine craft. Finally, a shop clerk handed a tiny parcel of spangled net to a rosy-cheeked maid. Sweetwater followed the maid back to a majestic brownstone—a sanctuary of multi-generational domesticity. He knocked at the basement door and, when the maid answered, held out the parasol. The maid immediately declared her mistress only carried black. 

But from the acoustic resonance of the stairwell above, a voice had commanded: "Let me see that parasol."

"I could not see her," Sweetwater said, "but she spoke with the commanding timbre of an agrarian elder. She identified me at once as coming from a man named Gryce at Police Headquarters. She bypassed my questions entirely, asserting her own dominance over the lexicon of the moment. She ordered me to fetch a cab and rode here beside me."

Gryce, suppressing a profound astonishment, left Sweetwater and walked into the outer office. He approached the waiting woman, a figure who carried the weight of a complex, interwoven history, a matriarch whose very presence was a bulwark against the erosion of cultural memory. It was none other than that most reputable of ladies, Miss Amelia Butterworth of Gramercy Park.
