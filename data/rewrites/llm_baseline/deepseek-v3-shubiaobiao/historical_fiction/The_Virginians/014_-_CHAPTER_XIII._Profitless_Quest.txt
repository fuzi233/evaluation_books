As Harry Warrington pressed deeper into Pennsylvania, the grim echoes of the British defeat grew louder. The vaunted regiments, seasoned in European wars, had crumbled before unseen foes—wilderness sharpshooters and a handful of French regulars. Tales of the disaster spread like wildfire: the river crossing, the slow advance through tangled woods, the ambush that erupted from the very trees themselves. The commanding general had fought valiantly—four horses shot beneath him, his body riddled with wounds—but bravery could not stem the slaughter.

Harry’s heart clenched with each new account. Survivors spoke of the fallen, of officers cut down in the chaos. Among them, perhaps, his brother. One name surfaced again and again: *George Washington*. The young Virginian aide had been at the general’s side, yet emerged unscathed while others perished.

At Dunbar’s camp, Harry found Washington himself—pale, fever-wracked, but alive. The man’s face darkened at Harry’s desperate questions. He had searched the field after the battle, burying the mutilated dead. One body, clad in George’s uniform, had been laid to rest. But Harry refused to believe. "If you loved him, you’d have found him!" he cried, raw with grief.

Against all advice, Harry retraced the army’s path. The battlefield was a graveyard of carrion and crows. He gagged at the stench, the sightless eyes of the fallen. No trace of George remained—only the silent accusation of the woods.

By the time he staggered back to camp, fever had him in its grip. Delirium blurred days into nights. When he woke, weak but lucid, Washington and the steadfast Dempster helped him home to Castlewood.

The estate was a house of shadows. His mother, Madam Esmond, greeted him with trembling hands and hollow eyes. She clung to a spectral hope: George was not dead. A prisoner, perhaps. A vision of her father had assured her. But her welcome for Washington was ice. "You promised to guard my son," she whispered, her voice a blade. "Yet here you stand—whole—while *he* is lost."

Harry watched, torn. Washington, stiff with regret, accepted her scorn. He had done his duty—fought, searched, mourned. But duty rang hollow to a mother’s grief.

Castlewood festered with unspoken accusations. Madam Esmond’s faith in George’s survival became obsession. She spurned Washington, forbade his name. Harry, caught between loyalty and love, met the colonel in secret, their friendship now a guilty thing.

The house itself seemed to sicken. Harry’s strength waned; the doctors urged a sea voyage. His mother, brittle with unresolved anger, let him go. As his ship sailed from the James River, he donned mourning—not for a brother confirmed dead, but for one vanished into the wilderness’s embrace.

Behind him, Castlewood stood empty of joy. Madam Esmond moved to Richmond, filling her days with cards and sermons, her heart with ghosts. The great estate, once vibrant with family strife and laughter, echoed only with absence.

And Harry? He sailed toward Europe, leaving behind a home that could no longer hold him.
