Margaret returned to Rotterdam long before Gerard stirred from his sleep, leaving her son behind. She sent Reicht—faithful, sturdy Reicht—off to Gouda at once, carrying the vicar’s grey frock and large felt hat, along with detailed instructions on how to care for her new master.

Then she went to Jorian Ketel. “He is the closest I’ve ever known,” she told herself, “so he is the one for me.” Together, they undertook two secretive acts—acts so clever, yet not cruel, in my view, though Margaret believed them to be bold. But if you ask what these deeds were, I must say: since she, a woman, kept them hidden until her death, and I, a man, am bound by silence—*Verbum non amplius addam.*

She stayed away from the Gouda parsonage.

Sometimes, small things unnoticed during passion grow sharp in memory later. When Margaret reflected on what had passed, she was wounded by Gerard’s assumption that she could forget the priest who had once been her lover. “What did he take me for?” she asked aloud. That thought sparked a quiet shyness she wouldn’t have felt otherwise—she was innocent, after all—but pride joined modesty, whispering, “Do not go back to Gouda.”

So she left little Gerard there, trusting him to complete the work her mother’s heart claimed as his own—not hers, nor her wit or wisdom—but to bind his father forever to humanity.

Yet this act of grace cost her deeply. She had never been parted from her boy even for an hour. Without him, she felt strange, desolate. After the first day, it became unbearable. What did she do? At dusk, she crept up to the parsonage, lurking like a thief until she saw Reicht Heynes alone in the kitchen. Then, softly tapping at the window, she whispered, “Reicht, for pity’s sake, bring him out to me—unseen.” With Margaret, whoever occupied her thoughts lost their name and became only “him.”

Reicht soon found a reason to bring the child out. There followed a scene of joy, then tears when mother and son parted again.

They arranged that Reicht would take the boy halfway to Rotterdam each day at a fixed hour, where Margaret would meet them. At these meetings, after the laughter and play—mother and child tumbling together like a young cat and her kitten—the boy sometimes played quietly at their feet, while the two women seized the moment to talk seriously about Luke Peterson.

“Reicht,” said Margaret, “I nearly promised him to marry Luke Peterson. ‘Say the word,’ I told him, ‘and I’ll wed him.’”

“Poor Luke!”

“Why poor Luke?”

“To be tossed between yes and no.”

“But Reicht, you haven’t ever looked at him with affection, so why side with him?”

“Me?” she replied, tossing her head.

“Oh, forgive me. Well then, you can do me a favor.”

“Whisht! Whisper! That little darling is listening—eyes like saucers.”

At that, both women ducked their heads under one cap.

Two women plotting against one boy? Oh, you great cowardly serpents!

But after five days of these secret meetings, Margaret began to feel the injustice of it—irritated, unhappy.

She was weeping when a cart arrived at her door. In it sat the Vicar of Gouda, clean as a new coin, beard freshly shaved, hands white as snow, a faint blush in his pale face—dressed in the grey frock and broad hat she had sent him.

She rushed upstairs, washed away her tears, put on a cleaner cap (theoretically), and came down.

He took both her hands, kissed them, and a tear fell upon them. She turned her head to hide her own.

“My sweet Margaret,” he said, “why this distance? Why keep aloof from your own good deed? We’ve waited for you every day—and no Margaret.”

“You said things.”

“What? When I was a hermit, a donkey?”

“Aye. No matter—you said things. And without cause.”

“Forget what I said. Who listens to a madman’s ravings? For now I see I’d have become a gibbering fool within months. No one could have saved me but you. Oh, what wisdom and kindness you showed! But I cannot thank you here—I must do so in the home you gave me, among the sheep you made my flock. Already I love them dearly. There, I shall bless ‘the truest friend a man ever had.’ So now I say to you, as you once said to me—come to Gouda manse.”

“Humph. We’ll see.”

“Margaret, think you I’d have kept your dear child so long unless I knew you’d return daily? He curls around my very heartstrings. But what right have I to him compared to yours? Confess—you’ve had hard thoughts of me.”

“No, no. Ah! You’re yourself again—always thinking of others. I’m half tempted to go to Gouda manse just for that.”

“Then come with half your mind—it’s worth more than most people’s whole.”

“Well, perhaps I will—but no rush,” she said coolly (though she burned to go). “Tell me first how you get along with your people.”

“Oh, already the poor are rooted in my heart.”

“I knew it.”

“And there are such good souls among them—simple, rough, superstitious, yet wonderfully kind.”

“Oh, I leave you to find good in a bushel of bad.”

“Whisht! Whisht! And Margaret—two men had been enemies for four years. Each came to the manse to win my favor. But give glory to God—I won their hearts, made them friends, and made them laugh at their folly.”

“But are you truly their vicar? Answer me.”

“Certainly. I’ve gone to the bishop, taken the oath, rung the church bell, touched the altar, missal, and holy cup before the wardens. They handed me the parish seal—here it is. Yes, a real vicar invites a true friend to Gouda manse.”

“Then my mind is eased. Tell me more.”

“Well, sweet one, nearest to me is a poor cripple—my guardian angel and his (you know her name by the way you turn your head)—placed beneath my roof. Sybrandt and I are brothers now—closer than ever before. It would gladden and sadden you to hear how we kissed and forgave each other. He speaks of you in praise, lives in piety. He says he’s happier now than he ever was in his strength. Out of my house, he shall never go—only to heaven.”

“Now tell me something that happened to you, poor soul! All this is good, but not news to me. Do I not know you well?”

“Well… at first, I was dazzled by sunlight and couldn’t go out—like an owl—but that’s past. And good Reicht Heynes—humph!”

“What of her?”

“This to your ear only—she is a diamond. Her voice cuts through me like a knife, and all voices seem loud except yours—so mellow, so sweet. Wait—now I’ll give you tidings. Yesterday I spoke with an old man who thinks himself ill-tempered, tries to appear so, but oh—so threadbare, yet the best heart beneath.”

“Why, it’s a parish of angels,” said Margaret, ironically.

“Then why do you stay out?” retorted Gerard. “Well, he said no parish in Holland has such power of the devil as Gouda. Among his examples: ‘We had a hermit—the holiest in Holland. But being Gouda, the devil came this week and took him—bag and baggage. Not a ha’porth left, save a piece of skin—just like a hedgehog’s—and a bit of old iron, polished up.’”

Margaret smiled.

“But,” continued Gerard, “the strangest thing is—the cave has truly collapsed. Had I resisted you, it would have buried me where I buried myself alive. Therefore, I see the hand of Providence—condemning my past, blessing my present life. What say you?”

“Can I understand such mysteries? I am but a woman.”

“Somewhat more, I think. This very tale proves you my guardian angel. All else confirms it. Come to Gouda manse.”

“Well, go on—I’ll follow.”

“No—ride with me.”

“Not so.”

“Why?”

“Can I say why? Being a woman, I only know I seem—to feel—to wish—to come alone.”

“So be it. I leave the cart to you, as you say, a woman. I’ll walk, as a man again—with joyful news of your coming.”

When Margaret reached the manse, the first sight was the two Gerards together—the son playing freely on the lawn, the father slumped in a chair, hat pulled low, pencil in hand, trying patiently to sketch him.

After a warm greeting, he showed her his drawings. “But I fail to fix him,” he said. “He is incarnate quicksilver. Yet note his changes—infinite, yet none ungracious. All is supple, easy. How he melts from one pose to another!” He paused. “Woe to illuminators! Looking at you, sir baby, I see how awkward, lopsided, ugly toads I and my fellows painted in missals—calling them cherubs and seraphs.” Finally, he threw the paper aside in despair. Margaret slipped it into her bosom.

That night, by the peat fire, he pointed to the brass candlesticks and other gleaming metal. “See how beautiful they glow,” he said.

Catherine’s eyes sparkled. “And oh, the sheets I lie in here,” he added. “Often my conscience pricks me: ‘Who art thou to lie in linen like snow?’ Dives was never so flaxen as I. And to think there are people in the world who possess all these fine things—and still are not content. Let them spend six months in a hermit’s cell, seeing no human face—then they’ll find how lovely and pleasant this wicked world is. And that men and women are God’s fairest creatures. Margaret was always fair—but never so bright as now.” Margaret shook her head, doubtful.

Gerard continued, “My mother was ever kind and good—but I did not notice her beauty till now.”

“Nor I,” said Catherine. “A score years ago I might pass in a crowd—but not now.”

Gerard declared each age had its own beauty. “See this mild grey eye—filled with motherly love for so many of us. All that love has left its shadow—and that shadow is a beauty beyond time. See this delicate lip, these pure white teeth. This brow—well-shaped, where comeliness turns into reverence. You are beautiful to me, mother dear.”

“And that is enough for me, my darling. Time for bed, child. You preach tomorrow.”

Reicht Heynes and Catherine exchanged a glance: *We have an amiable maniac to supervise—one who calls everything beautiful.*

The next day was Sunday. They heard him preach in his own church. The place was packed—curious at first, devout by the end. Never had his gift shown so powerfully. Deeply moved by seeing his people, his heart swelled over the flock he had long neglected. In a sermon lasting two hours—yet seeming but twenty minutes—he unveiled the whole scripture: he terrified the careless, strengthened the wavering, comforted the afflicted, uplifted the poor. When he finished, the congregation stood rapt, unwilling to believe the divine music of his voice had ceased.

Need I say two poor women in a corner sat entranced, tears streaming?

“Where did he learn all this?” whispered Catherine, her apron to her eyes. “By our Lady, not from me.”

As soon as they were alone, Margaret threw her arms around Catherine’s neck and kissed her.

“Mother, mother—I am not quite happy—but oh, I am proud.”

And she vowed on her knees never, by word or deed, to let her love stand between this young saint and Heaven.

Reader, have you ever stood by the sea after a storm, when the wind suddenly dies? The waves do not stop with the cause—they lash harder at first. Yet we know calm has begun, gently rocking them to sleep. So it was with these lovers, from that night when they walked hand in hand beneath the stars—from hermitage to manse.

At times, a wave would roar—but it was only memory’s echo of the tempest. The storm itself was over. The boiling waters began to fall—down, down, gently, but surely.

This image takes the place of endless details that would bore. What matters now is the general state, and the strange tangle of feeling that arose. History, bolder than romance, offers few things stranger than the footing on which Gerard and Margaret now lived—for many years.

United by love, familiarity, and a marriage irregular yet legal. Separated by Holy Church and their own consciences, which sided wholly with the Church. Yet bound by a living pledge—lawful at its birth.

Living near each other, she calling his mother “mother.” For years, she brought her boy to Gouda every Sunday, returning at dark. Wherever she went, it was a festival at the manse. She was received like a queen. Catherine was often with her, Eli frequently. Tergou held little charm compared to Rotterdam—and eventually, they left it altogether, settling in the capital.

Years passed—quiet, uneventful, free from great hopes or fears, so alike that without dates I could scarcely mark their passage.

But early in 1471, the Duchess of Burgundy—openly opposed, yet secretly aided by the Duke—raised troops to help her deposed brother, Edward IV of England, invade the realm. Our old friend Denys enlisted. Passing through Rotterdam to the ships, he learned that Gerard was a priest and Margaret alone. He told her that marriage was not his habit—but since his comrade had broken his vow, he felt bound to honor it for him. “A comrade’s honor is as dear to us as our own,” he said.

She stared—then smiled. “I prefer to remain your she-comrade,” she said. “Closer, we might not agree so well.” As her she-comrade, she equipped him with a new sword from Antwerp and a double handful of silver. “I give no gold,” she said, “for it vanishes as fast as silver—and harder to reclaim. May Heaven keep you safe from peril. There are famous fair women there to beguile you with their faces—and men to strike you with axes.”

He was hurried aboard at La Vere and never saw Gerard at that time.

In 1473, Sybrandt began to decline. His fragile life had been sweetened by his brother’s tender care and his own rare contentment—remarkable given his past. Gerard spent two hours daily with him—reading, singing, praying, drawing him in a soft carriage they had built together. When death drew near, Sybrandt begged Margaret be summoned. She came at once. With his last breath, he sought forgiveness—again—though she had granted it long ago. She stayed until the end. He died, blessed and blessing, cradled in the arms of the two true lovers who had parted him from life. *Tantum religio scit suadere boni.*

In 1474, there was a wedding in Margaret’s house—Luke Peterson and Reicht Heynes.

This may seem odd. Let me explain the interrupted conversation.

Margaret continued: “Then you can easily make him fancy you. And for my sake, you must—for my conscience pricks me. I must provide him with a wife—the best I know.” She instructed Reicht to be always kind and cheerful toward Luke, while she would be deliberately irritable. “But do not be so simple as to criticize me,” she said. “Leave that to me. Make excuses for me—I’ll make myself black enough.”

Reicht accepted the task like a command to sweep a room—and obeyed exactly.

For two years, they subjected poor Luke to this dual pressure. He came to see Margaret as fog and wind, Reicht as sunshine. Gradually, his affections shifted—without knowing how or when.

On the wedding day, Reicht embraced Margaret, nearly weeping. “He was always my fancy,” she said. “From the first moment I saw him.”

“Heyday! You never told me that. Are you as sly as the rest?”

“Nay, nay,” Reicht insisted. “But I never thought you’d really give him to me. In my country, the mistress expects service before the maid.”

Margaret settled them in her shop and gave them half the profits.

1476 and 1477 were years of deep trouble for Gerard. His conscience forced him to oppose the Pope. His Holiness, siding with the Grey Friars in blurring the distinction between Mary and Christ, offered indulgence to all who added this clause to the Ave Maria: “And blessed be thy Mother Anna, from whom, without stain of sin, proceeded thy virgin flesh.”

Gerard, like many northern clergy, called this flat heresy. He refused to utter it in church, warned his flock against using it privately, and rejected the new feast the Pope invented—the Feast of the Miraculous Conception of the Virgin.

This earned him bitter enmity from the Franciscans, who had the power to place him in serious danger and inflict many petty humiliations. In crises, he consulted Margaret. She either said, “I do not see my way,” refusing to guess—or gave advice that proved remarkably wise. He had genius; she had extraordinary tact.

When affection blinded her judgment, he stepped in. She knew she spoiled little Gerard, and Catherine ruined him—yet neither would part with him. He remained at home, talents neglected. A shrewd nine-year-old, instead of learning obedience, played and learned selfishness from their endless devotion, ruling two intelligent, spirited women with an iron rod.

Gerard saw this with pain. He intervened gently but firmly. After much struggle, he succeeded in sending the boy to the finest school in Europe—run by Haaghe in Deventer—in 1477. Many tears were shed, but the boy’s progress reconciled Margaret somewhat. Reicht, now her business partner, allowed her to visit him often.

And so the years flowed on. These two, faced with one of the strongest temptations imaginable, became each other’s guardians—not tempters.

Of course, the polished morality of the next century—teaching that vows to God are sacred only if reasonable—had not yet entered any mind. Their choice was clear: self-denial or sacrilege.

It was strange to hear them speak with open tenderness of their son—while a frozen wall stood between themselves.

Eight years had passed. Gerard, compared to most men, was happy.

But Margaret was not.

Her usual expression was gentle pensiveness—but sometimes she grew irritable, petulant. She snapped at Gerard now and then. If she visited and found a monk with him, she would turn and go home. She hated monks for separating her from Gerard—and passed that hatred to her son, whose contempt for them lasted until death.

Gerard bore it like an angel. He knew her heart was gold—and hoped the bitterness would pass.

He, now the right man in the right place, loved his parishioners and was loved in return. From dawn to dusk, he labored in good works, regaining his natural cheerfulness. To be honest, some of his humor was a mask—he was the greatest peace-maker since Mr. Harmony in the play. In ten years, he reconciled more enemies than his predecessors had in three centuries. One trick? Making quarrelers laugh at their dispute—thus undermining discord. But beyond that, he genuinely enjoyed harmless jokes.

He was also a master of animals—squirrels, bears, fawns. Once, a farmer brought him a mule believed possessed. “Tame this vagabond, parson, if you can.” Six months later, Jack not only obeyed—Jack ran to his whistle like a dog.

One day, sheltering from rain outside a tavern, he heard a drunk inside boast he could drink more at a draught than any man in Gouda. Gerard marched in: “What, lads? None take him up for Gouda’s honor? Shall a stranger claim we’re weaker than he? No—your parson takes him up. I’ll find a parishioner who drinks more.”

A bet was made. Gerard whistled—Jack clattered in, calm as ever, nose in his hand.

“A pair of buckets!” shouted Gerard. “Let’s see which of these two sons of asses drinks more!”

Another time, two farmers argued whose hay was better. “Ask the parson,” they said. By now, he was their referee.

“How lucky you thought of me!” he said. “I have a guest staying with me—the best judge of hay in Holland. Bring me a double handful each.”

They arrived. He placed the bundles on chairs. Then whistled.

Jack entered.

“Lord a mercy!” said one farmer.

“Jack,” said the parson, casually, “just tell us—which is the best hay?”

And so the years passed—peaceful, full of quiet duty, and profound, unspoken love.
