Darkness began to seep over the meadows with the manner of a house-servant who dislikes interrupting a party but has been told to draw the curtains all the same. Beyond the trees, the west still held a thin smear of apricot light, and above the big barn the new moon hung like a clean, sharp sickle laid out on a butcher’s block for tomorrow’s work.

Sally stepped out onto the porch and—because the house had its own small rites, as steady as setting dough to rise—bowed gravely three times for luck. The gravel was cool through the soles of her shoes. The air smelled of clover and sun-warmed boards, of damp earth under the hedges, and of the kitchen’s lingering ghosts: soap, onions, and the faint sweet-fat note of bacon that had been everywhere this last year, even when there wasn’t any. She stood a moment with her hands loosely folded, listening.

Stillness had its own music here. Not the brassy modern racket that cities threw at you—motor horns and shrieking brakes, the sort of noise that made a mind clench like a fist—but the good domestic orchestra: crickets tuning up in the grass, a far-off hinge giving one sleepy complaint, the barn timbers settling with a sigh. Across the meadow, windows winked on one by one, each a small square of lamplight like a page being opened.

From the kennel direction came a solitary, elderly bark, as if some dog had remembered a duty and then immediately regretted it. Toto—the small white woolly visitor who had attached himself to Sally’s heels like a burr—halted in the drive and emitted his challenging squeak, a sound halfway between a squeal of indignation and the protest of a kettle that thinks it’s boiling.

Then, along the state road, came another rhythm: Ginger’s footsteps, steady and unmistakable, thudding in time on the hard surface as he returned from the village. Even before he turned in at the gate, you could hear him as plainly as if the road itself carried him home on a drum. Sally faced the sound and felt, as she had felt every evening for nearly a year, that snug, astonished warmth that marriage had turned into a habit.

“Ginger!” she called.

“What ho!” came the answering hail, cheerful and slightly winded.

Toto, taking the responsibility of the household upon his compact shoulders, scuttled down the drive to investigate and was met, as usual, with a chilly reception. Ginger was fond of dogs in the abstract, but Toto had never managed to look like a dog in the concrete. He looked like a muff that had learned to walk and had all the moral purpose of a blancmange.

“It’s wonderful what you’ve done for Toto, angel,” said Sally as Ginger came up, sidestepping the creature’s leaps of welcome with the dexterity of a man avoiding a thrown sponge. “He’s a different dog.”

“Bit of luck for him,” said Ginger. “In all the years I was at Mrs. Meecher’s I never knew him move at anything more rapid than a stately walk. Now he runs about all the time.”

Sally’s eyes softened. “Country air.”

“The blighter had been overeating from birth,” said Ginger. “That was all that was wrong with him. A little judicious dieting put him right. We’ll be able”—his voice brightened with the pleased finality of someone predicting the end of a minor plague—“to ship him back next week.”

“I shall quite miss him,” said Sally.

“I nearly missed him—this morning—with a shoe,” said Ginger. “He was up on the kitchen table wolfing the bacon, and I took steps.”

“My cave-man,” murmured Sally, as if this were a compliment of the highest order. “I always said you had a frightfully brutal streak in you. Ginger, what an evening!”

“Good Lord!” Ginger stopped short at the open kitchen door, where lamplight spilled out onto the threshold and turned the air to honey. He stared at her as if she had altered while he was away, as if she had somehow slipped out and been replaced by a newer, lovelier Sally. “Now what?”

“What what?”

“Do you know you’re looking prettier than you were when I started down to the village!”

Sally gave his arm a small, satisfied hug. “Beloved!” she said, and then, with the practical directness of a woman who can admire a sunset and still remember the pantry, added, “Did you get the chops?”

Ginger froze in his tracks with the expression of a man who has just seen the trapdoor open beneath his feet.

“Oh, my aunt! I clean forgot them!”

“Oh, Ginger, you are an old chump. Well, you’ll have to go in for a little judicious dieting, like Toto.”

“I say, I’m most awfully sorry. I got the wool.”

“If you think I’m going to eat wool—”

“Isn’t there anything in the house?”

“Vegetables and fruit.”

“Fine!”

Sally lifted her chin with mock spirituality. “Not at all. I’m spiritual. Besides, people say that vegetables are good for the blood-pressure or something. Of course you forgot to get the mail, too?”

“Absolutely not! I was on to it like a knife.” Ginger fished in his pocket. “Two letters from fellows wanting Airedale puppies.”

“No! Ginger, we are getting on!”

“Pretty bloated,” agreed Ginger, with that serene complacency of a man whose prosperity is still in its early, shining stages. “Pretty bloated. We’ll be able to get that two-seater if things go buzzing on like this. There was a letter for you. Here it is.”

“It’s from Fillmore,” said Sally, turning the envelope over as they went into the kitchen. “And about time, too. I haven’t had a word from him for months.”

The kitchen smelled of the day’s honest work: potatoes in their bin, apples in a bowl, the green snap of vegetables waiting their turn, and under it all the clean, comforting scent of scrubbed boards. On the table lay Ginger’s evening paper, already wanting to be unfolded with importance. Sally sat, slit the envelope, and drew out the letter.

Ginger heaved himself onto the table and wriggled into comfort, a red-headed domestic monarch taking his throne. He began with the sporting page, because a man must keep in touch with the larger movements of civilization. But after a moment he lowered the paper and let his eyes rest on Sally’s bent head.

Marriage had not worn off. It had not turned into a duty, or a system, or a thing you did because you had started doing it. It remained, for Ginger, a sort of continual miracle, like bread rising when you have followed the recipe exactly and still can’t quite believe it. He watched her with the contentment of a man who has finally found a thing in life that does not produce a catch when you lift it.

Sally, reading, made a sound that began as a snort and ended as laughter.

“Ginger, look at this!”

He reached down and took the slip of paper she held out. In bold letters it read:

POPP’S OUTSTANDING SUCCULENT—APPETIZING—NUTRITIOUS.
(JUST SAY “POP!” A CHILD CAN DO IT.)

Ginger stared at it as if it were a code message from a foreign power.

“What is it?” he asked.

“It’s Fillmore.”

“How do you mean?”

Sally gurgled again, delighted and appalled in the same breath. “Fillmore and Gladys have started a little restaurant in Pittsburg.”

“A restaurant!” The note of shock in Ginger’s voice suggested that Fillmore had been caught selling bootlaces on a street corner. However often he had seen Fillmore’s grand schemes burst like overfilled puddings, Ginger retained a reverence for his brother-in-law’s air of destiny. That Fillmore Nicholas, the man who always spoke as if the world were waiting for his instructions, should descend to running a small eating-place struck him as almost indecent.

Sally, for sisters are constitutionally incapable of worshipping their brothers properly, beamed. “It’s the most splendid idea,” she said. “It really does look as if Fillmore was going to amount to something at last. Apparently they started on quite a small scale, just making pork-pies—”

“Why Popp?” interrupted Ginger, still grappling with the slogan. “Why not Fillmore’s Outstanding—whatever it is?”

“Just a trade name, silly. Gladys is a wonderful cook, you know.” Sally’s eyes took on the pleased, hungry sparkle of a woman imagining pastry. “And she made the pies and Fillmore toddled round selling them.”

In Sally’s mind, Gladys was already there at some flour-dusted table in a hot little back room, sleeves rolled, patient as an elder on harvest day. You could almost hear the crisp scrape of a knife cutting cold lard into flour, the soft patter as water went in by spoonfuls, not a drop more than necessary. You could smell pepper and pork and the quiet authority of salt. A proper pie did not need shouting. It needed good meat, a firm hand, and an oven that held its heat without complaining.

“And they did so well,” Sally went on, “that now they’ve started a regular restaurant, and that’s a success, too. Listen to this.” She turned the page, still laughing. “‘…sound financial footing. In fact, our success has been so instantaneous that I have decided to launch out on a really big scale. It is Big Ideas that lead to Big Business. I am contemplating a vast extension of this venture of ours, and in a very short time I shall organize branches in New York, Chicago, Detroit, and all the big cities, each in charge of a manager and each offering as a special feature, in addition to the usual restaurant cuisine, these Popp’s Outstanding Pork-pies of ours. That done, and having established all these branches as going concerns, I shall sail for England and introduce Popp’s Pork-pies there…’”

She lowered the letter, glowing. “Isn’t he a little wonder!”

“Dashed brainy chap,” said Ginger, loyally, though the words “New York” and “branches” and “manager” made him flinch inwardly, as one does on hearing a promising sauce being threatened with an unnecessary final flourish.

“I must say I was rather uneasy when I read that,” Sally added, practical even in her sisterly pride. “I’ve seen so many of Fillmore’s Big Ideas. That’s always the way with him. He gets something good and then goes and overdoes it and bursts. However, it’s all right now that he’s got Gladys to look after him.”

She glanced down again, her smile widening. “She has added a postscript. Just four words, but oh! how comforting to a sister’s heart. ‘Yes, I don’t think!’ is what she says, and I don’t know when I’ve read anything more cheering. Thank heaven, she’s got poor dear Fillmore well in hand.”

Outside, the night had deepened, and the house’s sounds became clearer for it: the faint tick of the clock, the small rustle of paper, Toto’s offended breathing. In the distance, some motorcar passed on the state road, briefly tearing a harsh note across the quiet—an ugly, mechanical intrusion that vanished as quickly as it came, leaving the crickets to stitch the calm back together.

“Pork-pies,” said Ginger thoughtfully, and at the same time his stomach, which had been expecting chops and had instead been offered noble abstractions like “fruit,” began to speak up in a language too old for modern advertising to improve. “I wish he’d sent us one of the outstanding little chaps. I could do with it.”

Sally rose and ruffled his red hair, her fingers smelling faintly of yarn and envelope paper and the day’s work. “Poor old Ginger! I knew you’d never be able to stick it. Come on—it’s a lovely night. Let’s walk to the village and revel at the inn. We’re going to be millionaires before we know where we are, so we can afford it.”

And with that—without tragedy, without fireworks, without any noise but the good, human sort—they stepped out into the clean, star-pricked dark, leaving Toto to squeak his opinions to the empty kitchen and the moon to hang above the barn like a promise of another harvest, another meal, another story that would come back round again.
