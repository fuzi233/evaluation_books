*"If there are ranks in suffering, Israel takes precedence of all the nations—if the duration of sorrows and the patience with which they are borne ennoble, the Jews are among the aristocracy of every land—if a literature is called rich in the possession of a few classic tragedies, what shall we say to a National Tragedy lasting for fifteen hundred years, in which the poets and the actors were also the heroes?"*
—Zunz, *Die Synagogale Poesie des Mittelalters*

Daniel Deronda had turned that passage over in his chest for days, so it rose sharp and unbidden in his mind as he walked toward the Cohens’ pawnshop, a bright brass-trimmed storefront that bore not a single trace of the sublime, age-worn tragic nobility he’d been reading of. Ezra Cohen cut no figure as a suffering martyr; his knack for turning every shilling he touched into profit was exactly the kind of exasperating, thriving good fortune that had marked Jewish tradespeople for centuries of petty, bitter resentment. This cheerful, unpretentious pawnbroker was no grand symbol of 1500 years of national grief—yet Deronda could not shake the quiet, striking pathos of it: Mordecai, that frail, breath-thin living vessel of his people’s collective consciousness, nested right here in the Cohens’ loud, warm, unthinking little bubble of hard-won prosperity.

The whole family’s faces lit up like lit lanterns the second Deronda stepped over the threshold. Cohen himself made a point of saying he could have made more interest by holding onto Deronda’s diamond ring a few days longer, but he would not trade that profit for anything—his wife, his mother, the children had done nothing but chatter about his first visit since he left. Young Mrs. Cohen first fretted that the baby was fast asleep, then brightened that Adelaide was still up, and begged him not to linger in the drafty shop front, but come straight back to the parlor to sit with the family. Deronda accepted gladly: he had tucked small gifts in his coat pockets on the walk over, a set of cut paper dancing figures for Adelaide, an ivory cup and ball for Jacob.

The old grandmother was hunched over the table teaching the children card games, and had just landed a perfect “card plate” that did not shatter.
“Stop!” Jacob yelped, skidding to a halt at Deronda’s feet the second he walked in. “Don’t step on my plate. Watch me toss it up again.”
Deronda laughed and obliged, exchanging a soft, knowing smile with the grandmother as the flimsy card plate spun three times in the air before it finally crumbled. He noticed the door Mordecai had stepped out of during his last visit was shut, but he did not rush to ask after their singular boarder first: he wanted to show the Cohens, the rough, unrefined family that had sheltered him, that their kindness mattered too.

He settled Adelaide on his knee, setting the paper figures to twirl across the tablecloth while Jacob fumbled eagerly with his new cup and ball, before he asked gently, “Is Mordecai in just now?”
Cohen, popping in from the shop to watch the fun, turned to his wife. “Where is he, Addy?”
“Back in the workroom there,” she said, nodding toward the closed door.
“Truth is, sir,” Cohen said, tapping his forehead lightly, “he’s always been a touch odd, you know? Not quite as grounded as you or me, but steady as clockwork otherwise—works hard, dotes on that boy Jacob more than anything. But these past two days? He’s wandered about like a sleepwalker, or sat stock still like a wax figure for hours.”
“It’s that consumption, poor dear soul,” the grandmother said, her voice soft as down. “I doubt he’ll hold out much longer against it.”
“Oh no, I think it’s something caught fast in his head,” young Mrs. Cohen cut in. “He’s pored over his old papers nonstop. When I call his name it takes him minutes to hear, to answer.”
“You might think we’re a little soft for keeping him,” Cohen added, apologetic, “but me wife and mum wouldn’t part with him even if he was twice the burden. It’s not that I can’t count costs—I’m no fool to lose money on purpose. It’s principle, see?”
“Mordecai carries a blessing right inside him,” the grandmother said, firm.
Jacob trotted over, determined to correct her. “No, grandma, he’s got a sick thing inside him. Said he couldn’t play with me yesterday, wouldn’t even have a bite of my bun.”

Deronda told them then, that far from judging their fondness for the man, he had already grown attached to Mordecai himself, met him at Ram’s bookshop some weeks prior, and had promised to come fetch him here so they could walk out together.
Cohen slapped his knee, delighted. “That’s it! He’s been waiting for you. That’s what’s got him all stirred up. Real kind of you, sir, to pay him mind—can’t be much use in all that old learning of his, else he wouldn’t be living here with us, no.” A customer’s bell jingled from the shop front, and he hurried back out.
Jacob leaned in helpful as ever, offering to go call Mordecai for him, but his mother shushed him, telling him to turn the knob quiet so Deronda could step in unannounced.
Jacob turned the doorknob so slow not a single creak sounded, and Deronda stepped behind him to the threshold. The tiny back workroom was lit only by a dying fire and one shaded candle, with half-finished pieces of jewelry scattered across the work board under the window, and dog-eared books stacked in the far corner. Mordecai was perched on a tall stool at that board, back to the door, hands folded over the wood, a watch propped up on a stand right in front of him. He had been waiting, sick with the tight, desperate hope of a prisoner listening for his long-delayed pardon, when Deronda’s voice wrapped around him soft: “I am come for you. Are you ready?”

He did not speak a single word. He just spun around, grabbed his furred cap off the edge of the board, and moved to Deronda’s side in two short steps. They were back in the sitting room in an instant, and Jacob, gawking at how bright Mordecai’s hollow eyes had gone, shoved his new cup and ball up near his face, yelling “Watch me catch it!” as if he was cheering up a man who had only just woken from a near-death fever. Mordecai smiled, thin and wobbly but real, and said “Fine, fine!” before young Mrs. Cohen reminded him he had forgotten his wool overcoat and thick comforter, and he ducked back to the workroom to grab them.

Cohen had stepped back into the parlor, whispering to Deronda under his breath, “See? He’s come alive again, didn’t I tell you? I’m usually right about these things.” Then he spoke in his normal tone, “Well, sir, I won’t keep you, but I do hope this isn’t the last time you stop by.”
Jacob ran over, holding the cup and ball out to him. “Will you come back? I bet I can catch this every single time, no misses, if you come visit again.”
Deronda smiled at the grandmother, noting how clever the boy’s small hands moved. “That boy has wonderfully deft hands. Which side of the family does he get that talent from?”
The grandmother nodded toward her son, who puffed up proudly. “My side! My wife’s family’s not the handy sort. Ours? It’s clever as gutta percha—twist it any way you want. There’s nothing us old lot can’t turn our hands to if we set our minds to it.” He winked at Jacob, who cackled nasally and stamped his feet, singing “Old gentlemen, old gentlemen!” all around the room.

Deronda realized then that he would soon have to ask Cohen straight out if he had lost a little sister named Mirah when she was six years old—but the decisive moment still felt too fragile to grasp. For the first time, the faint repulsion he had once felt at the family’s rough, unpolished manners melted away into warm, soft affection. They might not speak in refined, well-turned sentences, but their steady, tender care for a sick, half-mad boarder whose grand visions they barely understood was a quiet moral refinement far rarer than any polished drawing room manners.
“The Cohens seem to have a great affection for you,” Deronda said as soon as they stepped off the doorstep into the crisp evening air.
“And I for them,” Mordecai answered immediately. “They carry the heart of the Israelite inside them, even if they are like the horse and mule, who see no further than the narrow path they walk each day.”
“I fear I made you anxious,” Deronda said, apologetic. “I meant to come yesterday, but I could not get away.”
“I trusted you,” Mordecai said, his voice thin and wobbly with barely contained emotion. “But it is true: my youth’s old spirit woke up in me, and this weak, sick body of mine can not bear the beating of its wings. I am like a man bound in prison for decades, suddenly set free, brought face to face with the one person he has longed to speak to. He weeps, he staggers, the joy in him threatens to burst the fragile flesh that holds his soul.”

“Do not speak too much out in this cold night air,” Deronda said, his chest tight with the weight of the trust this man laid on him. “Wrap your scarf over your mouth. We are heading to the *Hand and Banner*, are we not? We shall have a private nook there?”
“Ah, that is my trouble,” Mordecai said. “I wished you had come yesterday. Tonight is the night of that little club I told you of. We might not get a minute alone until late, when everyone else has left. Perhaps we should go somewhere else, but I do not know any other place well. Strange places make the outer world press in too hard on me, blur the visions I hold inside my head. But the people there know my face.”
“I do not mind the club at all,” Deronda said, relieved. “If it is the place you are most comfortable, that is where we will go. We can talk as long as we can, and I will come back to finish our conversation if we run out of time. What is this club like?”
“It is called The Philosophers,” Mordecai explained. “Only a handful of us, all as rare and scattered as the cedars of Lebanon. Poor working men who love to think. None poorer than I am. Sometimes people of higher station have come as guests. We are allowed to bring a friend who cares about our subjects. Everyone buys a pint of beer or a drink, that pays for the use of the room. Most of them smoke. I go as often as I can, because there are other men of my people there, and sometimes I can speak, say the things that burn in my chest. I like to think of these poor, quiet thinkers as faint echoes of the great Masters of our race, the men who worked with their hands for crusts of bread, but preserved and passed down our people’s memory, kept the soul of Israel alive like a seed buried safe among the tombs. A heart can find such small resemblances so dear.”

Deronda agreed eagerly to join them, glad for the small reprieve before the heavy, private conversation he knew waited between them. Three minutes later, they pushed open the glazed door with the red curtain, and stepped into the tiny parlor barely fifteen feet square. Gaslight glowed through a thin haze of pipe smoke, and Deronda saw a scene he had never witnessed before: half a dozen men, ages from twenty to fifty, all in worn, shabby clothes, most holding clay pipes, leaning in completely focused on a broad-chested man in a pepper-and-salt suit with blonde hair, who held his pipe up in one hand and thumped his knee with the other, finishing a line from Shelley’s *Prometheus Unbound*:
“As thought by thought is piled, till some great truth
Is loosened, and the nations echo round.”

Their entrance broke the hush, and everyone shifted around the cramped semicircle of chairs around the fireplace to make room. The voices that greeted Mordecai were warm, laced with gentle, long-standing affection for the sick man, before all eyes turned to Deronda.
“I have brought a friend who cares for our subjects,” Mordecai said. “He has traveled far, studied much.”
“Is the gentleman a mystery man? A Great Unknown?” the broad Shelley quoter joked.
“My name is Daniel Deronda,” Deronda said, a soft, gentle smile tugging at the corner of his grave face, warming the whole room. “I am unknown, but not great.”
“Your name is a good one, sir, you are very welcome,” the broad man said, nodding at Mordecai. “C’mon, take this corner seat next to me—it is the warmest.”
Deronda sat across from them, looking around the motley group of sharp, thoughtful faces: Miller the bookseller who read every book he sold, Buchan the sharp-tongued Scotch saddler, Pash the tiny dark vivacious Jewish watchmaker, Gideon the red-haired Jewish optical instrument maker, Croop the dark-eyed shoemaker, plus three plain English working men: the wood inlayer, the laboratory assistant, the pale copying clerk. These were no cheap caricatures of working-class poverty to laugh at—they were men who had scrabbled for knowledge with every spare minute they could steal, the select of the select among the poor, drawn together by a love of ideas almost no one in their station shared. Deronda pulled out his cigar case (he never smoked himself, but always carried it for guests, hating to seem stiff and priggish) and offered it around, and the group relaxed, no less at ease than they had been before he walked in.

Miller, the unofficial moderator, explained they had been debating the nature of progress, arguing over whether new ideas were the primary force that transformed societies. The conversation twisted and turned, until the watchmaker Pash joked that the idea of nationalities was little more than a ghost, soon to fade away entirely. Mordecai turned to him, his face soft with quiet sadness, and said “An idea has no force unless there is a feeling behind it.” Deronda pushed back gently, saying that a sentiment that seemed dead could wake up stronger than ever, that nations could rise again, that the Arabs were even now being stirred by a new, bright zeal. At his words, Mordecai’s whole frame straightened, his worn, haggard face lighting up like the first break of dawn. The conversation spiraled on, until Mordecai spoke, his voice ringing soft but with a fire no one in the room had heard from him before:
“Woe to the men who see no place for resistance in this generation! I believe in a growth, a passage, a new unfolding of life whereof the seed is more perfect, more charged with the elements that are pregnant with diviner form. The life of a people grows, it is knit together and yet expanded, in joy and sorrow, in thought and action; it absorbs the thought of other nations into its own forms, and gives back that thought as new wealth to the world. But there may come a check, an arrest; memories may be stifled, and love may go faint for lack of them. Who dares say the fountain of their life is dried up, they shall forever cease to be a nation? Not he who feels the life of his people stirring within his own breast. His very soul is resistance, and is as a seed of fire that may enkindle the souls of multitudes, and make a whole new pathway for events.”
