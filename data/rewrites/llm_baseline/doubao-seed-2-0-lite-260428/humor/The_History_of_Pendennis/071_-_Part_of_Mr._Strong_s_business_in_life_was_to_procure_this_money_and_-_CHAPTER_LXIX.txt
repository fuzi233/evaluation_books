Bright and early the next morning, Morgan the valet swung open the Major’s bedroom shutters with a perfectly grave, respectful face—you’d never guess they’d had a screaming falling out 12 hours prior—hauling in hot water, fancy grooming tools, and the old man’s crispest dressing gown like nothing had ever happened.
“Ugh, it’s you, is it?” grumbled the Major from under his covers. “Don’t get ideas, I’m not taking you back.”
Mr. James Morgan drew himself up so stiff he nearly creaked, full of the kind of dignity only a long-serving upper-servant who runs a secret side loan-shark ring can pull off. “I wouldn’t dream of coming back, Major Pendennis, sir! I’m done serving you, or any man. But since you’re still camped out in my house, I figured I’d make sure you didn’t look a mess while you’re here.”
He went through the whole tedious old valet routine one last time, laying out the Major’s silver razor case like he was arranging a state funeral wreath, then leaned in with the kind of solemnity reserved for announcing a duchess has run off with a stable boy. “Figured you’d need a temporary new valet while you hunt for a permanent one, so I rounded up a chap last night. Lived in all the best families, I can vouch for him.”
“Truly? You are far too polite,” the Major snickered, because he knew exactly what Morgan had been up to the night before: he’d hotfooted it straight to the Wheel of Fortune, the valets’ own private club where they traded gossip, pilfered cufflinks, and job leads, and found Frosch, a courier fresh back from dragging young Lord Cubley around the Continent who was currently at loose ends. Morgan had spun him a whole dramatic tale about blowing up his own job and quitting service forever, so Frosch jumped at the temp gig.
“Er, he’s worked for Lord De La Pole! He was with young Lord Cubley on his grand tour, and he doesn’t want to go up to Fitzurse Castle because his chest can’t handle Scottish cold, so he’s free as a bird,” Morgan huffed, getting tetchy because he could tell the Major was ribbing him.
“Marvelous, send him in, he’ll do perfectly,” the Major said. “Morgan, would you be so kind as to—”
“Show him all your little quirks and routines, sir, of course,” Morgan said. “Breakfast here or at the club, Major?”
“Here, thanks, then we’ll sort out our little accounts.”
“Right you are, sir.”
“Now please leave the room.”
Morgan stomped off, seething. The Major’s over-the-top politeness was way more infuriating than any insult he could have yelled. While the Major faffed about doing his toilet, we’ll leave him to it, no peeking.

After breakfast, the Major and new guy Frosch set to work packing. The Major was the kind of neat old bachelor who never accumulated junk, so he didn’t have 30 years of random memorabilia to sift through. His entire collection of books and art amounted to his late mom’s Bible, a road atlas, Pen’s calf-bound novel, every volume of the Duke of Wellington’s dispatches, plus a handful of prints of Wellington, the other generals he’d served under in India, and random portraits of British kings and queens. He could be packed and ready to move in two hours flat, and the same old crates he’d arrived with 15 years prior were still rotting in the attic, perfect to hold everything. The overworked “Slavey” maid dragged them down, dusted them off under Morgan’s glowering supervision. Morgan hadn’t said a word about the blackmail he’d threatened the night before, but he looked so menacing poor widow Mrs. Brixham was trembling in her kitchen waiting for the axe to fall.
The Major prodded along with his cane, overseeing Frosch packing everything up, while the maid burned all the old useless papers the Major didn’t want hanging around. Soon every closet, every drawer was empty, except his desk, waiting for Morgan to bring over the final bill.

Morgan swanned in with his ledger. “Want that new valet out of the room, sir, I need to talk private.”
The Major sent Frosch off to hail two cabs and wait downstairs. Morgan made sure he was gone, then pulled out his books, which were totally easy to settle up. He pocketed the Major’s cheque, scribbled his signature with a fancy flourish, and puffed out his chest. “Now that our accounts are squared away, Major Pendennis, I reckon we can talk man to man. I’ve got some very valuable information, see.”
“Oh? What sort of information?” the Major asked, already guessing.
“I know of a so-called marriage that’s not a real marriage! A baronet who’s as good as unmarried, whose wife was already hitched to someone else before she married him! And you know all about that, sir!”
The Major snorted, looking down his nose. “Ah, that explains why you’ve been acting so weird. You were lurking at the keyhole at that public house the other night, weren’t you? Should’ve checked for eavesdroppers before I went in.”
“Hey, I’m allowed to get information same as you!” Morgan squawked. “A poor servant can have a lucky break same as a fancy gentleman, right? Don’t pull that aristocrat haughty rubbish on me, it’s all nonsense! I’m an Englishman, I’m as good as you are!”
“Cut to the chase, man,” the Major said, all regal. “What’s this got to do with me?”
“What’s it got to do with your nephew Pen? His seat in Parliament? The whole mess with suborning people over this bigamy secret? Why should you lot be the only ones making money off it? I want half, Major! Make it worth my while, and I’ll zip my lips forever. 500 quid a year, first quarter 125 quid right now, hand it over. Your chequebook’s right there on the desk.”
“And this,” the Major growled, yanking open the desk drawer to pull out the old double-barrelled pistol that had belonged to his old Indian army commander, “you scoundrel. One more word and I’ll shoot you like a rabid dog. Kneel down and say your prayers, you lying coward, I swear I’ll blow your brains out!”
Morgan screamed so loud the windows rattled: “MURDER! POLICE!” and made a flying leap for the open window, right above a copper who happened to be walking his beat.
To Morgan’s absolute shock, the Major wheeled his heavy table out of the way, walked over to the other open window, and waved at the copper. “C’mon up here, officer!” he called, then planted himself in front of the door. “You pathetic little sneak,” he snickered at Morgan. “That pistol hasn’t been loaded in 15 years. You’d know that if you weren’t such a coward.”
The copper trundled up, followed by a terrified Mrs. Brixham and the Slavey maid, who’d been holding back a crowd of nosy passersby trying to crane their necks to see the drama. The Major launched straight into his story: “This drunk rogue I sacked last night tried to assault me! I picked up that old unloaded pistol to defend myself, and he screamed for help before I even pointed it at him. I’m charging him with stealing my property—search his trunks, he’s got piles of my stuff stashed there.”
Morgan was losing his mind. “You gave ’em to me! The velvet cloak you haven’t worn in three years! The old waistcoats! The fancy shirts! The opera glass I was gonna put back, I swear!”
“Confessed he’s a thief, right in front of you,” the Major said calmly. He knew Morgan had all sorts of embarrassing garbage stashed in his own trunks too: the dodgy snuffboxes, fake jewelry, bad cigars and cheap French prints he peddled to spendthrift young aristocrats as part of his side hustle. If this went to the station, his whole secret racket would get exposed, and his reputation as a respectable valet would be totally ruined. He looked like he was about to faint.
The Major paused for a second, glancing at poor Mrs. Brixham’s terrified face. If he dragged Morgan off to jail, the desperate man might blurt out all the bigamy secrets they were all trying to keep quiet, so no point pushing him too far. “Hold on, officer,” he said. “I’ll sort this out with him alone for a minute. Wait outside, will you?”
The copper stepped out. Morgan whispered, “Thank you, sir, thank you so much.”
“You thought you had the winning hand, didn’t you?” the Major cackled. “You lost, mate. I found out your little side gig a week ago, by the way! Young De Boots from the Blues recognised you peddling your dodgy goods at the barracks, one part cash, one part eau de cologne, one part cheap French prints. I never missed any of those old shirts and trinkets, I didn’t care! But I saw a perfect chance to take you down, and I did.”
“What do you want from me?” Morgan whimpered.
“Simple. You’ve been bleeding poor Mrs. Brixham dry for five years. You lent her 60 quid, she and her son the insurance clerk paid you back 250 quid total, and you still hold a bill of sale over all her furniture for another 120 quid. You’re gonna burn that bill right now, and give her her note of hand back.”
“No! That’s my property!” Morgan yelled.
“OFFICER!” the Major shouted, turning to the door.
“Fine! Fine, I’ll give it back!” Morgan yelped.
The Major made him sit down at the desk and write out a whole humiliating confession, signed and dated, saying he’d been wildly overpaid in his 15 years of service, had made his fortune running loans and dodgy goods, stole all the Major’s old trinkets, and any lies he told about the Major or his family were totally untrustworthy. Morgan fumed the whole time, but he had no choice but to sign it, and get the copper and Mrs. Brixham to witness it.
Half an hour later, all the Major’s trunks were loaded into the cabs, he bade a grateful Mrs. Brixham a quick goodbye, and strolled off to his temporary rooms at Cox’s Hotel in Jermyn Street, just a few minutes’ walk away. Morgan glowered at him from the parlour window, steaming mad, unable to do a single thing about it.

A few hours later, who should turn up at the door but Arthur Pendennis, our very own hero. “My uncle in, Morgan?” he asked, already amused to see the valet loitering on the steps smoking a cigar—something the strict old Major would never have allowed while he was living there.
“Major Pendennis is out, sir,” Morgan said, bowing stiffly but not bothering to take his cap off. “He moved out today, sir, and I’m no longer in his service. He’s staying temporarily at Cox’s Hotel in Jermyn Street, sir.” He paused, a weird glint in his eye. “Are you in town for a while, sir? Staying at your chambers? I’d be ever so grateful if you could spare a quarter of an hour of your time, I’ve got some important business to discuss that concerns you.”
Arthur assumed the man was begging for a reference to get a new job, so he said fine, wandered inside the dining room, and had no idea he was about to get an earful of every chaotic secret Morgan had dug up.
