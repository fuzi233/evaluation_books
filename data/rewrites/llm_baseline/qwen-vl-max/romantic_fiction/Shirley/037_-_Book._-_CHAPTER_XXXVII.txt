Yes, dear reader, it is time to draw the story to its close. I have only a few final threads to weave in the lives of those we’ve come to know so well—then our journey ends, and we part with a quiet nod, grateful for the moments shared.

Let us begin with the curates—those humble souls who often go unnoticed, yet whose quiet virtues deserve recognition. Come forward, modest merit! And indeed, Malone answers first. He knows himself when he hears his own description.

No, Peter Augustus—there’s no room for you here. We cannot speak of your fate. It would be too raw, too real. Do you not understand, Peter, that people crave beauty, not truth? That unvarnished facts are often unwelcome? The squeal of a real pig is no more palatable now than it ever was. If I were to tell your tale—the wildness, the chaos, the mess—you’d find the public fleeing in panic, calling for smelling salts and feathers. “Impossible!” they’d cry. “Untrue!” others would shout. “Inartistic!” some would declare solemnly. Yet the very lie, the imagined tale, the polished fiction—that is what they embrace, adore, call sweet and natural. The honest child gets all the slaps; the false one, all the sweets. Such is the world, Peter. And since you are the honest one—rude, unpolished, untamed—you must step aside.

Now make way for Mr. Sweeting.

Here he comes, arm in arm with his wife—the most radiant and dignified woman in Yorkshire: Mrs. Sweeting, once Miss Dora Sykes. Their marriage was blessed from the start: Mr. Sweeting had just been appointed to a comfortable living, and Mr. Sykes provided Dora with a generous dowry. They lived long and happily, beloved by their parish and by many friends. Their home became a beacon of grace and peace.

There! I believe the polish has been applied just right.

Now, advance, Mr. Donne.

He turned out better than anyone could have hoped. He married a gentle, sensible woman—quiet, refined, and full of quiet strength. The union transformed him. He became an ideal husband and a diligent priest (though he never claimed to be a pastor, insisting on the title of "clergyman" alone). He cared deeply for the outward order of things—the church, the parsonage, the school—all were built under his hand and stood as models of neatness and care. He was especially gifted at one thing: begging. With remarkable skill, he raised funds for every project, appealing to all ranks—from the barefoot child to the crowned duke. His letters went far and wide—to Queen Charlotte, to royal princes, to ministers of state—and astonishingly, he extracted something from each. Records show he secured five pounds from the famously frugal Queen Charlotte and two guineas from her profligate son. When he set out on his missions, he wore metaphorical armor of brass. You gave him a hundred yesterday? Then two hundred today was no impossibility. He’d say so plainly, and nine times out of ten, he’d get it. People gave simply to be rid of him. Still, he did good with the money. He served his generation well.

I should mention that after Mr. Malone's sudden and unexpected departure from Briarfield parish—how it happened, you may never know, for curiosity must yield to taste—another Irish curate took his place: Mr. Macarthey. I am glad to say, with truth, that this man brought honor to his country where Malone had brought shame. He was decent, respectful, and deeply conscientious—everything Peter was not. I’ll refrain from naming the opposite trait, lest I spoil the mystery. He worked faithfully, and under his care, both Sunday and day schools flourished like green bay trees. Being human, he had flaws—but they were the kind many would call virtues. A tea invitation from a Dissenter could unsettle him for days. Seeing a Quaker wear his hat in church, or thinking of burying an unbaptized soul with Christian rites—these could disturb his mind and body alike. Otherwise, he was steady, thoughtful, charitable, and true.

Now, I suspect a justice-loving reader might wonder why I’ve said nothing about the attempted murder of Mr. Robert Moore. Here was a chance to lead you through a thrilling chase—a dance of law and justice, of dungeons and trials, of suspense and retribution. You might have enjoyed it. But I wouldn’t. I would have lost my balance. My subject and I would have clashed. Fortunately, truth spared me the burden. The murderer was never caught—not because he escaped, but because he was never pursued. The magistrates made half-hearted gestures, as if preparing to act bravely, but Moore himself, instead of pressing them, lay quietly in his cottage, laughing inwardly, mocking with every line of his pale, foreign face. They reconsidered, completed the necessary formalities, and wisely let the matter rest.

Mr. Moore knew who fired the shot—and so did all Briarfield. It was Michael Hartley, the half-mad weaver mentioned before—fanatic in faith, radical in politics. The poor man died of delirium tremens a year later, and Robert gave his grieving widow a guinea to bury him.

* * * * *

Winter has passed. Spring came with light and shadow, bloom and rain. Now we stand in the heart of summer—in mid-June, 1812.

The air glows with azure and gold. It fits the age, the moment, the spirit of nations. The nineteenth century strides into adolescence, bold and strong. The Titan boy uproots mountains in play, hurls rocks in sport. This summer, Bonaparte rides across Russia with six hundred thousand men—French, Poles, Italians, Rhinelanders—marching toward Moscow. Beneath Moscow’s walls, the Cossack waits. Stoic, fearless, he trusts in snow and storm. The wilderness, wind, and hail are his allies. His companions are the elements—air, fire, water. These are the archangels before God’s throne, holding vials of divine wrath. Their time is vengeance. Their signal: the voice of the Lord, thundering with majesty.

“Have you entered the storehouses of snow? Have you seen the treasures of hail, kept for the day of battle?”

“Go forth. Pour out the vials of God’s wrath upon the earth.”

It is done. The earth burns. The sea turns red as blood. Islands flee. Mountains vanish.

This year, Lord Wellington took command in Spain. They made him generalissimo—for their own survival. He captured Badajos, fought Vittoria, stormed San Sebastian, won Salamanca. He shaped the war.

Men of Manchester, forgive me this brief history. It matters little. Lord Wellington is, to you, merely an old man now. Some of you have called him a “dotard,” mocked his age, his frailty. What heroes you are! Men like you can scorn what is mortal in a demigod. Laugh freely—your scorn will never break his noble heart.

But come, friends—whether Quakers or cotton-printers—let us hold a peace congress. Let us release our venom gently. We’ve spoken too fiercely of war and slaughter. Now comes a triumph in your domain. On June 18, 1812, the Orders in Council were repealed. Blockaded ports opened. You remember—those of you old enough—how Yorkshire and Lancashire erupted in joy. In Briarfield, the bell rang so hard it cracked—still dissonant today. The Association of Merchants dined in Stilbro’ and returned home in such states their wives wished never to see again. Liverpool roared like a river-horse awakened by thunder. American merchants feared apoplexy, bled themselves, eager to dive into speculation, risking futures they might never reclaim. Stocks that had gathered for years vanished in an instant. Warehouses emptied. Ships filled. Work multiplied. Wages rose. The good times seemed real. Perhaps fleeting—but brilliant. In that one month, many fortunes were made.

* * * * *

When a whole province rejoices, even the smallest soul feels the joy. Public bells stir the quietest home, as if calling everyone to celebrate. So it was with Caroline Helstone, who dressed carefully that day—her finest muslin—and went to Fieldhead to oversee wedding preparations. She chose the wreath, the veil, the dress for the altar. She selected gowns for other occasions, mostly ignoring the bride’s opinion—since the bride was in a mood both stubborn and dreamy.

Louis had foreseen trouble—and found it. His mistress delayed her wedding day week after week, month after month. At first, she teased him with soft excuses. Then, slowly, his calm, resolute nature rebelled against her charm and tyranny—sweet, yet unbearable.

It took a crisis to bring her to terms. But finally, she was fixed—bound by love, sealed by vow.

Thus conquered, she pined like any prisoner. Only her captor could comfort her. Only his presence could ease the loss of freedom. In his absence, she wandered, spoke little, ate less.

She ignored wedding plans entirely. Louis had to manage everything. Weeks before the wedding, he was already master of Fieldhead—not by title, but by necessity. The kindest, least presumptuous master ever known, yet absolute in his rule. She surrendered without protest. “Ask Mr. Moore,” she’d say when asked for orders. Never had a suitor of a wealthy bride been so completely freed from the role of subordinate, so inevitably elevated to leadership.

Miss Keeldar partly yielded to her nature, but a comment she made a year later revealed deeper purpose: “Louis would never have learned to rule if she had not ceased to govern. Her weakness made his strength.”

It had been planned that Miss Helstone would serve as bridesmaid. But fate had another role for her.

She returned home in time to tend her plants. She finished watering them. The last was a rosebush blooming in a quiet green corner behind the house. She paused, resting a moment. Near the wall stood a carved stone relic—perhaps once the base of a cross. She climbed it to see better. She still held the watering pot in one hand; with the other, she lifted her dress lightly, avoiding drips. She looked over the wall—into lonely fields, beyond three dark trees standing together against the sky, beyond a lone thorn at the head of a distant lane. She watched the moors, where bonfires were lit. The evening was warm. Bell music rang joyfully. Blue smoke curled softly, flames burned bright. Above, where the sun had set, a silver star sparkled—the star of love.

Caroline was not sad that night—far from it. But as she gazed, she sighed. And as she sighed, a hand slipped around her waist, resting gently.

“I’m looking at Venus, mamma. See how beautiful she is. How white her light, compared to the red fires!”

The reply was a tender embrace. Caroline turned—and saw not Mrs. Pryor’s face, but a dark, manly visage. She dropped the watering pot and stepped down.

“I’ve sat with ‘mamma’ for an hour,” said the man. “We’ve talked long. Where were you?”

“At Fieldhead. Shirley is as difficult as ever, Robert. She won事 neither yes nor no. She sits alone. I don’t know if she’s sad or indifferent. If you try to rouse her, she gives you a look—half longing, half reckless—and sends you away as confused as she is. I don’t know what Louis will do with her. For my part, if I were a gentleman, I’d never dare take her on.”

“Don’t worry. They’re meant for each other. Strangely, Louis likes her all the more for her moods. He’ll manage her—if anyone can. It’s been a stormy courtship for such a calm man. But look—he’s won. Caroline, I’ve come to ask you something. Why are the bells ringing?”

“For the repeal of your hated law—the Orders in Council. You’re pleased, aren’t you?”

“Last night at this hour, I was packing books for a sea voyage. They were the only things I felt free to take—clothes, seeds, tools—on my way to Canada. I was leaving England.”

“Leaving me? Leaving _me_?”

Her fingers clutched his arm. She looked frightened.

“Not now. Not now. Look at my face. Is there despair written there?”

She looked—and saw a face glowing with hope, warmth, joy. Though dusk fell, his features shone with light.

“Will the repeal help you—much? Immediately?”

“It saves me. I won’t go bankrupt. I won’t abandon business. I won’t leave England. I won’t be poor. I can pay my debts. All the cloth in my warehouses will sell. New commissions will come. Today lays the foundation for my life—firm, broad, secure.”

Caroline listened, breathless. She held his hand.

“You’re saved? Your burdens are gone?”

“They are. I breathe. I can act.”

“At last! Oh, Providence is kind! Thank Him, Robert.”

“I do.”

“And I, for your sake!” She looked up, devout.

“Now I can hire more workers, pay better wages, plan wiser. I can do good. Be less selfish. _Now_, Caroline, I can have a home—truly mine—and _now_—”

He paused. His voice faltered.

“And _now_,” he continued, “I can think of marriage. I can seek a wife.”

She did not speak. No words were needed.

“Will Caroline—who forgives as she is forgiven—pardon all I’ve made her suffer? All the pain I caused? The sickness of body and mind she bore because of me? Will she forget my ambition, my petty schemes? Will she let me make amends? Will she let me prove that, though I once deserted cruelly, trifled wantonly, injured basely—I can now love faithfully, cherish deeply, treasure tenderly?”

His hand remained in hers. A soft pressure answered him.

“Is Caroline mine?”

“Caroline is yours.”

“I will prize her. The sense of her worth lives in my heart. My need for her is woven into my life. I’ll guard her happiness as I guard my own blood.”

“I love you too, Robert. I’ll take faithful care of you.”

“Will you? Faithful care? As if that rose could shelter this gray stone from storm. But she _will_ care for me—in her way. These hands will bring me comfort. I know the woman I seek to bind to myself will give me solace, charity, purity—things I’ve never known in myself.”

Suddenly, Caroline trembled. Her lip quivered.

“What troubles you, my dove?” Moore asked, as she leaned into him, then pulled back.

“Poor mamma! I am all she has. Must I leave her?”

“I thought of that. I’ve spoken with ‘mamma.’”

“Tell me what you wish. I’ll consider if it’s possible. But I cannot leave her—even for you. I cannot break her heart.”

“She was faithful when I was false—wasn’t she? I never visited your sickbed. She watched it night and day.”

“What must I do? Anything but leave her.”

“At my wish, you never shall.”

“She may live near us?”

“With us—she’ll have her own rooms, her own servant. She insists on it.”

“You know she has income—enough to live independently?”

“She told me, with a quiet pride that reminded me of someone.”

“She’s not interfering. She doesn’t gossip.”

“I know her, Cary. But even if she were the opposite, I wouldn’t fear her.”

“Yet she’ll be your mother-in-law?” The speaker smiled.

“Louis and I aren’t men who fear mothers-in-law. Our enemies have never been in our homes. I doubt my mother-in-law will bother me.”

“She will—in her quiet way. She’s not expressive. When she’s silent, don’t think she’s angry. It’s just her manner. Always let me interpret for her, Robert.”

“Oh, absolutely. Jesting aside, I feel we’ll suit—_on ne peut mieux_. Hortense is sensitive—by our French meaning—and not always reasonable. Yet I’ve never wounded her deeply, never quarreled seriously with her.”

“No. You’re wonderfully kind, tender, indulgent. And you’ll be kind to mamma. You’re a gentleman through and through, Robert—especially at home.”

“A compliment I welcome. I’m glad my Caroline sees me this way.”

“Mamma thinks of you as I do.”

“Not quite, I hope?”

“She doesn’t want to marry you—don’t be vain. But she said the other day, ‘My dear, Mr. Moore has pleasing manners. He’s one of the few gentlemen I’ve met who combine politeness with sincerity.’”

“‘Mamma’ is rather cynical about men, isn’t she?”

“She doesn’t judge them all, but she admires certain ones—Louis, Mr. Hall, and lately, you. She didn’t like you once. I knew it—because she never spoke of you. But Robert—”

“Well? What now?”

“You haven’t seen my uncle yet?”

“I have. ‘Mamma’ brought him in. He consents—conditionally. If I prove I can support a wife, I may have her. And I _can_—better than he thinks.”

“If you grow rich, will you do good with your wealth?”

“Of course. I’ll build, help, teach. I’ll use it wisely.”
