In the weeks after Sally’s reunion with Ginger Kemp, life took on the glossy finish of a well-buttered pie-crust: everything smoothed, everything behaving, the whole world apparently rolled out with a pin and dusted with just enough flour to keep it from sticking.

Gerald, it was true, remained inconveniently a thousand miles away—Chicago had grabbed him by the collar and was refusing to let go. He had gone out to superintend the opening of The Primrose Way (now being treated, from Fillmore’s letters, less like a comedy and more like a civic religion). Fillmore, taking Ike’s advice, had produced it there first; then some frantic manager had begged Gerald to patch up a brother dramatist’s ailing play, and patching, like pastry, was a job that always took longer than advertised. Sally had hoped for him back in New York within days. Instead she received dates described in airy phrases like “soon” and “before long,” which in theater talk mean anything from Tuesday to the Second Coming.

But Sally refused to be gloomy. Everything else had aligned itself with a domestic kind of bliss. Fillmore was flourishing. Ginger was no longer a weight on her conscience. She had found an apartment. Her new hat made her look as if she belonged to a better-organized universe. And The Primrose Way—so Fillmore reported—had hit Chicago like a fire, only friendlier. Chicago, according to him, had abandoned national questions, local questions, and even questions about the weather. The only subject under discussion was how to get seats. If you wanted to start a riot, you didn’t shout “Fire!”—you murmured, “I’ve got two in the fifth row,” and waited for civilization to fall apart.

Best of all, Ginger’s future had been solved neatly, like tying up a roast with string. Ginger had entered the service of the Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. (Managing Director, Fillmore Nicholas). Fillmore would have made it longer, but the brass plate had refused to stretch. Ginger was now to be found daily in the outer office, where his duties consisted chiefly of reading the evening papers and preventing the populace from storming the throne room in which Fillmore sat in semi-regal state, brooding over schemes as if he expected the Muse to arrive with a fountain pen and a checkbook.

What, precisely, Ginger was—secretary, gatekeeper, executive, mascot—remained unclear even to Ginger. Sometimes he felt like the man at the wheel. Sometimes he felt like an office boy with a longer leash. But there was nothing unclear about his gratitude to Sally for having pulled whatever strings needed pulling. He thanked her whenever he could; and lately he could often, because he had appointed himself, with cheerful violence, to the job of furnishing her new apartment.

“And what I mean to say is,” Ginger said, pausing mid-haul with a massive easy chair that seemed to have been carved out of a small respectable iceberg, “if I didn’t sweat a bit after the way you got me that job—”

“Ginger, desist,” said Sally.

“Yes, but honestly—”

“If you don’t stop it, I’ll make you move that chair into the next room.”

“Shall I?” Ginger adjusted his grip, hands blistered, face freckled and shining, as if he had been lightly basted. “Anything you say.”

“Silly! Of course not. The only other rooms are my bedroom, the bathroom, and the kitchen. What on earth would I want a great lumbering chair in them for? All the same, I believe the first we chose was the best.”

“Back she goes, then, what?”

Sally frowned with the profound seriousness of someone deciding between thyme and tarragon.

“No,” she said at last. “By the window is better.”

Ginger, accompanied by chair, staggered obediently to the window. Sally watched, feeling tired on his behalf. It was remarkable how another person’s exertion could leave you longing to lie down and be fanned with a magazine.

“I’m giving you a lot of trouble,” she said, remorseful.

“Trouble!” Ginger panted, depositing the chair and wiping his freckled forehead. “The way I look at it is this. You got me that job, and—”

“Stop!”

“Right ho… Still, you did, you know.”

Sally stretched in the armchair and surveyed the room. It was small, but it had begun to look like a real home—pictures up, carpet down, furniture arranged as if it meant to stay arranged. For the first time in her life she had that restful sensation of being behind her own locked door, alone, safe, not subject to boarding-house smells and voices and the thin wall through which somebody always seemed to be learning scales. Here the sounds were softer: the shift of a chair-leg on carpet, the distant hush of the street like a sea heard through shells, Ginger’s occasional grunt as if he were wrestling furniture into submission.

She looked about, pleased—until a tiny uneasiness pinched.

“Hullo!” she said. “Where’s that photograph of me? I’m sure I put it on the mantelpiece yesterday.”

Ginger peered, red-faced from effort and embarrassment.

“No. No photograph here.”

“I know there isn’t. But it was there yesterday. Or was it? I know I meant to put it there. It’s the most beautiful thing you ever saw. Not a bit like me; but what of that? They touch ’em up in the dark-room, you know. I value it because it looks the way I should like to look if I could.”

“I’ve never had a beautiful photograph taken of myself,” said Ginger solemnly, with gentle regret.

“Cheer up!”

“Oh, I don’t mind. I only mentioned—”

“Ginger,” said Sally abruptly, because the chair had begun to trouble her like a badly placed comma, “pardon my interrupting your remarks, which I know are valuable, but this chair is—not—right. It ought to be where it was at the beginning. Could you give your imitation of a pack-mule just once more? And after that I’ll make you some tea. If there’s any tea—or milk—or cups.”

“There are cups all right. I know, because I smashed two the day before yesterday. I’ll nip round the corner for some milk, shall I?”

“Yes, please nip. All this hard work has taken it out of me terribly.”

Over tea—made with the sort of improvisation that would have horrified an elder of any farm kitchen, for Sally’s cupboards were still in the raw stage and her spoons had a habit of hiding—Sally grew inquisitive.

“What I can’t understand about this job of yours, Ginger—which, as you are just about to observe, I was noble enough to secure for you—is the amount of leisure that seems to go with it. How is it you’re able to spend your valuable time—Fillmore’s valuable time, rather—juggling my furniture every day?”

“Oh, I can usually get off.”

“But oughtn’t you to be at your post doing—whatever it is you do? What do you do?”

Ginger stirred his tea as if the answer might rise to the surface.

“Well, I sort of mess about, you know.” He considered. “I interview divers blighters and tell ’em your brother is out and take their names and addresses and… oh, all that sort of thing.”

“Does Fillmore consult you much?”

“He lets me read some of the plays that are sent in. Awful tosh most of them. Sometimes he sends me off to a vaudeville house of an evening.”

“As a treat?”

“To see some special act, you know. To report on it. In case he might want to use it for this revue of his.”

“Which revue?”

Ginger blinked. “Didn’t you know he was going to put on a revue? Oh, rather. A whacking big affair. Going to cut out the Follies and all that sort of thing.”

“But—my goodness!” Sally sat up. Her peace, which had been lying purring on her lap, suddenly grew claws. It was exactly like Fillmore to go launching into expensive fireworks when he ought to be putting away pennies for winter. All his life he had thought in millions where sensible people thought in tens. Optimism bubbled in him like fermenting dough, rising whether you watched it or not. “That’s rather ambitious,” she said.

“Yes. Ambitious sort of cove, your brother. Quite the Napoleon.”

“I shall have to talk to him,” said Sally, firmly. She felt annoyed, not only because he might lose money, but because he had disturbed her new, snug order of things. “And I’ll tell you another thing that won’t do. This armchair. Of course it ought to be over by the window. You can see that yourself, can’t you.”

“Absolutely!” said Ginger, resignedly preparing for action once more.

In the midst of these domestic campaigns Sally received, like a pebble through a window, a telegram from Miss Winch in Chicago:

Have you been feeding Fillmore meat?

Sally couldn’t have said she understood it. Yet there was something ominous in the way the words sat together, as if they belonged to a private dialect spoken by sensible people when the world began to wobble. It decided her. Furnishing could wait; emergencies had no respect for curtains.

She went at once to the headquarters of the Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. (Managing Director, Fillmore Nicholas).

In the outer office, Ginger was absent from his customary chair. In his place sat a boy of tender years and pimply exterior, whose expression thawed as if somebody had set him near a stove when Sally gave her name. He told her to walk right in.

Sally walked right in and found Fillmore with his feet on a desk that looked as if it had fought in wars. He was studying what appeared to be costume designs, and wearing the weary, distracted air of a man who has appointed himself Providence.

“Ah, Sally!” he said, in a voice heavy with vast concerns. Prosperity was still doing its soft, silent work upon him. What had once been merely a pleasant smoothness at the jaw was now frankly a double chin, as if success had folded itself neatly and tucked it in. He wore a new waistcoat, unbuttoned. “I am rather busy. Always glad to see you, but I am rather busy. I have a hundred things to attend to.”

“Well, attend to me,” said Sally. “That’ll only make a hundred and one. Fill, what’s all this I hear about a revue?”

Fillmore looked like a small boy discovered with jam on his fingers—except that the jam, in his case, was ambition and it had got into his hair. He picked up a pen and put it down; buttoned his waistcoat and unbuttoned it; scratched his ear with one of the costume designs as if seeking inspiration under the paper.

“Oh yes, the revue!”

“It’s no good saying ‘Oh yes’! You know perfectly well it’s a crazy idea.”

“Really… these business matters… this interference…”

“I don’t want to run your affairs for you, Fill, but that money of mine does make me a sort of partner, I suppose, and I think I have a right to raise a loud yell of agony when I see you risking it on a—”

“Pardon me,” said Fillmore loftily, brightening. “Let me explain. Women never understand business matters. Your money is tied up exclusively in The Primrose Way, which, as you know, is a tremendous success. You have nothing whatever to worry about as regards any new production I may make.”

“I’m not worrying about the money. I’m worrying about you.”

A tolerant smile moved about the lower slopes of Fillmore’s face.

“Don’t be alarmed about me. I’m all right.”

“You aren’t all right. You’ve no business, when you’ve only just got started as a manager, to be rushing into an enormous production like this. You can’t afford it.”

“My dear child, as I said before, women cannot understand these things. A man in my position can always command money for a new venture.”

“Do you mean to say you have found somebody silly enough to put up money?”

“Certainly. I don’t know that there is any secret about it. Your friend, Mr. Carmyle, has taken an interest in some of my forthcoming productions.”

“What!”

Sally had been uneasy; now she was stunned. Bruce Carmyle again—sliding into her life like a tide that found every crack. There seemed no escaping him. Wherever she turned, there he was, and all she could do was rage helplessly at the way the world kept offering him opportunities to be useful.

Fillmore mistook her tone.

“It’s quite all right,” he assured her. “He’s a very rich man. Large private means, besides his big income. Even if anything goes wrong—”

“It isn’t that. It’s…”

Sally stopped. How could you explain to Fillmore the particular sort of discomfort that came from a man’s money and attention, when the man himself was precisely what you wanted at a distance? It wasn’t the arithmetic of it; it was the pressure. Some things, like certain tastes and certain memories, refused translation into another person’s vocabulary.

She was still struggling with this when voices rose in the outer office. The pimply boy seemed to be attempting to defend the sanctity of the inner room against an invader. He failed. The door burst open and Miss Winch sailed in as if propelled by outrage and railway schedules.

“Fillmore, you poor nut,” said Miss Winch—who, while her telegrams might be cryptic as ancient runes, spoke in person with all the subtlety of a frying-pan. “Stop picking straws in your hair and listen to me. You’re dippy!”

The last time Sally had seen Miss Winch she had been calm as a lake. This new agitation—this crackle—felt ominous.

“Ah! Here you are!” said Fillmore, indignation springing up like a bearded lion until he recognized the intruder and remembered affection. “My dear—”

“Yes, here I am!” Miss Winch dropped into a swivel chair with despairing energy and attempted to restore herself with chewing gum, as if mastication were the first aid for human folly. “Fillmore, darling, you’re the sweetest thing on earth, and I love you, but on present form you could just walk straight into Bloomingdale and they’d give you the royal suite.”

“My dear girl—”

“What do you think?” demanded Miss Winch of Sally.

“I’ve just been telling him,” said Sally gratefully, glad of an ally, “I think it’s absurd at this stage of things for him to put on an enormous revue—”

“Revue?” Miss Winch froze with the gum halfway to her mouth. She flung up her arms. “I shall have to swallow this gum. You can’t chew with your head going round. Are you putting on a revue too?”

Fillmore buttoned and unbuttoned his waistcoat with the hunted air of a man trying to escape his own genius.

“Certainly, certainly,” he said, feverishly. “I wish you girls would leave me to manage—”

“Dippy!” said Miss Winch again. “Telegraphic address: Tea-Pot, Matteawan.” She swung back to Sally. “Listen! This boy must be stopped. We must form a gang in his best interests and get him put away. What do you think he proposes doing? I’ll give you three guesses. Oh, what’s the use? You’d never hit it. This poor wandering lad has got it all fixed up to star me—me—in a new show!”

Fillmore lifted one hand from his waistcoat in protest.

“I have used my own judgment—”

“Yes, sir!” said Miss Winch, rolling right over him as if he were a bump in the road. “That’s what he’s planning to spring on an unsuspecting public. I’m sitting peacefully in my room at the hotel in Chicago, pronging a few cents’ worth of scrambled eggs and reading the morning paper, when the telephone rings. Gentleman below would like to see me. Oh, ask him to wait. Business of flinging on a few clothes. Down in elevator. Bright sunrise effects in lobby.”

“What on earth do you mean?” said Sally, bewildered.

“The gentleman had a head of red hair which had to be seen to be believed,” explained Miss Winch. “Lit up the lobby. Management had switched off all the electrics for sake of economy. An Englishman he was. Nice fellow. Named Kemp.”

“Oh, is Ginger in Chicago?” said Sally. “I wondered why he wasn’t on his little chair in the outer office.”

“I sent Kemp to Chicago,” said Fillmore, stiffly, “to have a look at the show. It is my policy, if I am unable to pay periodical visits myself, to send a representative—”

“Save it up for the long winter evenings,” advised Miss Winch. “Mr. Kemp may have been there to look at the show, but his chief reason for coming was to tell me to beat it back to New York to enter into my kingdom. Fillmore wanted me on the spot, he told me, so that I could sit around in this office here, interviewing my supporting company. Me! Can you or can you not tie it?”

“Well…” Sally began diplomatically, and failed.

“Don’t say it! I know it just as well as you do. It’s too sad for words.”

“You persist in underestimating your abilities, Gladys,” said Fillmore reproachfully. “I have had a certain amount of experience in theatrical matters—I have seen a good deal of acting—and I assure you that as a character-actress you—”

Miss Winch rose like a spring, kissed him energetically, and sat down again with the briskness of someone administering medicine.

“You’re a darling old thing to talk like that,” she said, “and I hate to wake you out of your daydreams, but honestly, Fillmore, dear, do just step out of the padded cell for one moment and listen to reason. I know exactly what’s been passing in your poor disordered bean. You took Elsa Doland out of a minor part and made her a star overnight. She goes to Chicago, and the critics and everybody else rave about her. As a matter of fact,” she said to Sally, honest admiration lighting her face, “you can’t realize, not having seen her play there, what an amazing hit she’s made. She really is a sensation. Everybody says she’s going to be the biggest thing on record. Very well then, what does Fillmore do? The poor fish claps his hand to his forehead and cries, ‘Gadzooks! An idea! I’ve done it before, I’ll do it again. I’m the fellow who can make a star out of anything.’ And he picks on me!”

“My dear girl—” murmured Fillmore, wounded.

“Now, the flaw in the scheme is this. Elsa is a genius, and if he hadn’t made her a star somebody else would have done. But little Gladys? That’s something else again.” She looked to Sally. “You’ve seen me in action, and let me tell you you’ve seen me at my best. Give me a maid’s part with a tray to carry on in act one and a couple of ‘Yes, madam’s’ in act two, and I’m there! Ellen Terry hasn’t anything on me when it comes to saying ‘Yes, madam,’ and I’m willing to back myself for gold, notes, or lima beans against Sarah Bernhardt as a tray-carrier. But there I finish. That lets me out. And anybody who thinks otherwise is going to lose a lot of money. Between ourselves the only thing I can do really well is to cook…”

“My dear Gladys!” cried Fillmore, revolted.

“I’m a heaven-born cook,” said Miss Winch simply, “and I don’t mind notifying the world to that effect. I can cook a chicken casserole so you’d leave home and mother for it. Also my English pork-pies! One of these days I’ll take an afternoon off and assemble one for you. You’d be surprised! But acting—no. I can’t do it, and I don’t want to do it. I only went on the stage for fun, and my idea of fun isn’t to plough through a star part with all the critics waving their axes in the front row, and me knowing all the time that it’s taking money out of Fillmore’s bankroll that ought to be going towards buying the little home with stationary wash-tubs… Well, that’s that, Fillmore, old darling. I thought I’d just mention it.”

Sally couldn’t help feeling for Fillmore. He sat with his chin in his hands, staring moodily ahead—Napoleon at Elba, only with better tailoring. It was plain this dream of flinging Miss Winch to glory had been very near his heart.

“If that’s how you feel,” he said at last, in a stricken voice, “there is nothing more to say.”

“Oh, yes there is,” said Miss Winch briskly. “We will now talk about this revue of yours. It’s off!”

Fillmore sprang up. He thumped the desk with a well-nourished fist. A man can stand only so much feminine committee-work in his private office.

“It is not off! Great heavens! It’s too much! I will not put up with this interference with my business concerns. I will not be tied and hampered. Here am I, a man of broad vision and—and—broad vision—I form my plans… my plans… I form them… I shape my schemes… and what happens? A horde of girls flock into my private office while I am endeavouring to concentrate… and concentrate… I won’t stand it. Advice, yes. Interference, no. I… I… I… and kindly remember that!”

The door slammed. A fainter detonation suggested his whirlwind passage through the outer office. Footsteps faded down the corridor.

Sally stood staring at Miss Winch, stunned. A roused and militant Fillmore was new—like discovering your mildest relative has opinions about revolution.

Miss Winch, unperturbed, unwrapped her gum at last and began chewing.

“Isn’t he cute!” she said. “I hope he doesn’t get the soft kind,” she added thoughtfully.

“The soft kind?”

“He’ll be back soon with a box of candy,” explained Miss Winch, “and he will get that sloshy, creamy sort, though I keep telling him I like the other. Well, one thing’s certain. Fillmore’s got it up his nose. He’s beginning to hop about and sing in the sunlight. It’s going to be hard work to get that boy down to earth again.” She sighed, gently. “I should like him to have enough left in the old stocking to pay the first year’s rent when the wedding bells ring out.” She chewed. “Not that it matters. I’d be just as happy in two rooms and a kitchenette, so long as Fillmore was there. You’ve no notion how dippy I am about him.” Her freckled face glowed with a kind of stubborn tenderness. “He grows on me like a darned drug. And the funny thing is, I keep right on admiring him though I can see all the while he’s the most perfect chump. He is a chump, you know. That’s what I love about him. That and the way his ears wiggle when he gets excited. Chumps always make the best husbands. When you marry, Sally, grab a chump. Tap his forehead first, and if it rings solid, don’t hesitate. All the unhappy marriages come from the husband having brains. What good are brains to a man? They only unsettle him.”

She broke off and examined Sally with sudden intensity, as if changing subjects were a stage entrance.

“Say, what do you do with your skin?”

Sally laughed. “What do I do with my skin? I just carry it around with me.”

“Well,” said Miss Winch enviously, “I wish I could train my darned fool of a complexion to get that way. Freckles are the devil. When I was eight I had the finest collection in the Middle West, and I’ve been adding to it right along. Some folks say lemon-juice’ll cure ’em. Mine lap up all I give ’em and ask for more. There’s only one way of getting rid of freckles, and that is to saw the head off at the neck.”

“But why do you want to get rid of them?”

“Why?” said Miss Winch. “Because a sensitive girl, anxious to retain her future husband’s love, doesn’t enjoy going about looking like something out of a dime museum.”

“How absurd! Fillmore worships freckles.”

“Did he tell you so?” asked Miss Winch eagerly.

“Not in so many words, but you can see it in his eye.”

“Well, he certainly asked me to marry him knowing all about them,” said Miss Winch, comforted. “And what’s more, I don’t think feminine loveliness means much to Fillmore, or he’d never have picked on me. Still, it’s calculated to give a girl a jar when she picks up a magazine and reads an advertisement of a face-cream beginning, ‘Your husband is growing cold to you. Can you blame him? Have you really tried to cure those unsightly blemishes?’—meaning what I’ve got. Still, I haven’t noticed Fillmore growing cold to me, so maybe it’s all right.”
