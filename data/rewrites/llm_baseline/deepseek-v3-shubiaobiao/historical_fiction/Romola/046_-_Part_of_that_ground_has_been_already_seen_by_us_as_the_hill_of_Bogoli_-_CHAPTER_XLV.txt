Tito’s arrival in Florence as the bearer of peace had been a stroke of luck, but his true business lay in more calculated schemes. After delivering his report to the Ten at the Palazzo Vecchio—a performance that even the skeptical Bernardo del Nero could not help but admire—Tito left with the cold courtesy they now exchanged. Bernardo’s distrust had deepened since the sale of Bardo’s library, though Romola’s silence on the matter bound him to discretion. He watched her fading spirit with a grimace, blaming her unhappiness on Tito’s smooth, hollow charm.

Yet politics had no room for moral scrutiny. Tito was useful, and in Florence, usefulness forgave much.

Exhausted from riding, Tito lingered in the streets, feigning idle wandering while his mind worked. The Piazza del Duomo buzzed with post-procession energy, the air thick with restless joy. He avoided eager crowds by directing them toward another messenger—Meo di Sasso, who surely knew more than he did.

At Nello’s barber shop, a knot of men lounged, their laughter too loud to be unforced. Among them stood Dolfo Spini, leader of the Compagnacci, his red plume a defiant splash against the city’s newfound sobriety. Spini’s grin was brittle; his faction had spent weeks decrying Savonarola’s influence, only to be undone by the day’s miraculous news.

Niccolò Macchiavelli, sharp-eyed and sharper-tongued, dissected Spini’s pride with a smirk. "A man who laughs hardest after a defeat," he mused, "is either a fool or a liar." His gaze flicked to Tito as the Greek took his seat in Nello’s chair. "Ah, the secretary who steals my opportunities. How does luck cling to you like perfume?"

Tito played the weary traveler, lamenting his disheveled state. Nello scolded him for neglecting Romola—pale as a moonbeam, he claimed, worn by fasting and fervor. Tito sighed. "What can I do? The Frate’s voice drowns mine."

The talk turned to Savonarola’s precarious hold. Domenico Cennini defended him; Macchiavelli countered with cool logic. "A prophet’s power lasts only as long as his miracles do. The people’s faith is fickle—and Florence has three kinds of enemies waiting for him to stumble."

Francesco Cei sneered. "A hypocrite, nothing more. He belongs in Dante’s hell, walking backward."

Macchiavelli chuckled. "You poets demand purity even in lies. The Frate merely draws his own line between truth and tale."

Tito listened, smiling faintly, as Nello fussed over his appearance. "There! The handsomest scholar in Florence—though time etches even you, my friend."

As he left, the whispers followed. Cei muttered about old rumors of stolen jewels; Nello dismissed them as dead gossip. Macchiavelli, ever amused, deflected the venom. "Bad precedent, Francesco. Next, they’ll accuse *you* of stealing bad verse."

Outside, Spini’s red feather vanished into the crowd—a man who’d gladly see the city burn for spite. Macchiavelli watched him go. "Imagine him as Capo d’Oca, trumpeting his pride only to slink home in disgrace."

Tito walked away, the gold ring in his pouch a silent weight. Pucci was out of the city—good. The Medici’s message could wait. For now, he had other threads to weave.
