The next evening, old Mr. Sillerton Jackson arrived for dinner at the Archers’ quiet West Twenty-eighth Street home.

Mrs. Archer, a shy widow who shrank from most large gatherings, lived for the quiet thrill of knowing every small, unspoken detail of New York society’s doings. Her old friend Jackson pursued the tangled affairs of their shared acquaintance with the patience of a rare artifact collector and the precise eye of a naturalist cataloguing new species, while his unmarried sister Miss Sophy—invited to every dinner that couldn’t secure her far more sought-after brother—trotted home with scraps of minor gossip that filled every tiny gap in his meticulously curated store of lore. So whenever a story was stirring that Mrs. Archer ached to unpack, she called Jackson to dine; and since she extended those invitations to precious few, and she and her spinster daughter Janey made such rapt, unchallenging audiences, Jackson almost always came himself instead of sending his sister along in his place.

If he could have dictated every condition of the visit, he would have chosen nights when Newland was out of the house. Not that the young man was unpleasant company—the two got on famously at their club, after all—but the veteran anecdotist had long noticed that Newland had a tiresome habit of weighing and questioning his tales, a critical streak the two Archer ladies never showed. If perfection were possible on earth, Jackson would also have asked for Mrs. Archer’s cooking to be a little less bland. But every old New Yorker knew the great fundamental divide stretching back as far as anyone could remember: on one side were the Mingotts, Mansons, and all their sprawling clan, who lavished care on fine food, elegant clothes, and ample fortunes; on the other was the Archer-Newland-van-der-Luyden tribe, devoted to travel, horticulture, and the finest contemporary fiction, who looked down on all gross, bodily forms of pleasure as rather vulgar. You couldn’t have everything, after all. Dine with Lovell Mingott, and you got canvasback duck, terrapin, and decades-old vintage wine; dine with Adeline Archer, and you could debate Alpine scenery and *The Marble Faun* for hours—and luckily, the Archer family’s prized Madeira had rounded the Cape, so even the worst meal was not entirely without a saving grace. So whenever Mrs. Archer’s friendly summons arrived, the eclectic Jackson would shrug and tell his sister, “I’ve had a touch of gout since my last dinner at the Mingotts’—a light diet at Adeline’s will do me a world of good.”

Mrs. Archer, Janey, and Newland divided their home gently: Newland claimed the entire upper floor for his own use, while the two women tucked themselves into cozy, compact quarters on the ground level. Their shared tastes and interests wrapped the house in unclouded harmony: they grew ferns in glass Wardian cases, twisted macramé lace, stitched wool embroidery on linen, collected Revolutionary-era glazed pottery, subscribed to the wholesome magazine *Good Words*, and read Ouida’s novels purely for the lush Italian atmosphere. (They favored her stories of peasant life, for the sweeping scenery and soft, sweet sentiments, though they preferred novels set among their own social set, whose motives and habits felt far more comprehensible. They spoke severely of Dickens, who they insisted “had never drawn a proper gentleman,” and thought Thackeray was far less at home in high society than Bulwer-Lytton, even if the latter was beginning to be dismissed as old-fashioned.)

Mother and daughter were both great lovers of landscape, the first thing they sought out and admired on their rare travels abroad; they considered architecture and painting proper subjects for men, and only for the learned sort who pored over Ruskin’s writings. Born a Newland herself, Mrs. Archer and Janey looked so much alike people often mistook them for sisters: tall, pale, slightly round-shouldered, with long elegant noses, soft sweet smiles, and a kind of drooping, faded distinction straight out of a Reynolds portrait. Their resemblance would have been total if not for the soft embonpoint of middle age that stretched Mrs. Archer’s black brocade gowns, while Janey’s brown and purple poplin dresses hung looser and looser on her unmarried frame as the years passed.

Mentally, though, they were less alike than their shared little mannerisms suggested. Their long, mutually dependent life together had given them the exact same vocabulary, and the same habit of prefacing any new opinion with “Mother thinks” or “Janey thinks,” depending on which of them was voicing the thought. But where Mrs. Archer’s serene, unimaginative mind rested easy on all the accepted, familiar rules of their little world, Janey was prone to sudden, wild bursts of fancy welling up from long-suppressed springs of romance. They adored each other, and revered Newland entirely, and he loved them back with a tender, apologetic fondness softened by their exaggerated admiration for him, and his own quiet secret satisfaction in being the head of their little household. He thought it a very good thing for a man to have his authority respected in his own home, even if his sense of humor sometimes made him question how much real power he actually wielded.

That evening, Newland was perfectly sure Jackson would have preferred him out at the club. But he had his own reasons for staying home. Of course old Jackson wanted to talk about Ellen Olenska. Of course Mrs. Archer and Janey were burning to hear every bit of gossip he had. All three of them would be faintly awkward with Newland there, now that his engagement to May Welland had made him an official relative of the Mingott clan, and he waited, amused and curious, to see how they would dance around the subject.

They began obliquely, talking of Mrs. Lemuel Struthers.
“It’s such a pity the Beauforts asked her to their ball,” Mrs. Archer said gently. “But Regina always does exactly what he tells her, and Beaufort—”
“Certain nuances escape Beaufort,” Jackson said, poking cautiously at his broiled shad, wondering for the thousandth time why Mrs. Archer’s cook always burned the roe to a cinder. Newland, who had long shared that exact wonder, could spot the familiar melancholy disapproval in his face.
“Oh, he’s vulgar, by definition,” Mrs. Archer said. “My grandfather Newland always told my mother, ‘Whatever you do, don’t let that fellow Beaufort near the girls.’ But at least he’s associated with gentlemen, even in England, they say. It’s all so mysterious—” She glanced at Janey and paused. She and Janey knew every fold of the Beaufort backstory, but in front of an unmarried girl, Mrs. Archer always pretended such subjects were not fit for discussion.
“But this Mrs. Struthers,” she continued smoothly. “What did you say her past was, Sillerton?”
“Sprung from a mining camp saloon, to be precise. Then she toured New England with a traveling wax works show, until the police shut that down. After that, they say she lived—” Jackson glanced at Janey now, whose eyes bulged a little under her prominent lids, still eager to fill the remaining gaps in Mrs. Struthers’s story. “Then Lemuel Struthers came along. They say his advertising man used her face for the shoe polish posters, you know—her hair’s that deep black, Egyptian style. Anyhow, eventually he married her.” The space between each syllable of that “eventually” was packed full of unspoken innuendo.
“Oh well, at this point in time, it hardly matters,” Mrs. Archer said indifferently. The ladies were not truly interested in Mrs. Struthers just then—Ellen Olenska was far too fresh, far too absorbing a topic. Mrs. Archer had only named Struthers so she could pivot seamlessly to what they all really wanted to talk about.
“And Newland’s new cousin, Countess Olenska? Was she at the ball too?”
The faint touch of playful sarcasm was obvious, and Newland had expected it. Even Mrs. Archer, who rarely approved of most people’s choices, had been over the moon about his engagement. (“Especially after that silly business with Mrs. Rushworth,” she’d murmured to Janey once, referring to the old affair that had once felt to Newland like a permanent, searing scar on his soul.) There was no better match in all New York than May Welland, no matter how you looked at it. Of course such a marriage was only what Newland was owed—but young men were so foolish and unpredictable, and some women so ensnaring and unscrupulous, that it felt nothing short of a miracle to see her only son safe past the siren isle of reckless passion, anchored in the calm harbor of blameless domesticity.

Newland knew she’d been perturbed, though, by how the engagement had been announced prematurely, all because of the Mingotts’ obsession with shielding Ellen Olenska. It was partly out of tenderness for his mother, the indulgent head of the household, that he’d chosen to stay home that night. “I don’t object to the Mingotts’ fierce family loyalty,” he’d heard her grumble to Janey when they were alone, “but I can’t see why Newland’s engagement has to be mixed up with that Olenska woman’s comings and goings.” She’d behaved perfectly, of course, on their formal call on the Wellands—but Newland had noticed how both she and Janey had nervously scanned every doorway for Ellen Olenska to appear the whole visit, and after they left, she’d allowed herself to murmur, “I’m so thankful Augusta Welland received us alone.”

Newland knew her quiet, lawful little revenge for that small stress would be to draw Jackson out on every last detail about the Countess, and now that he’d done his public duty as the Mingotts’ soon-to-be relative, he had no objection to hearing the lady discussed in private—even if the subject was already starting to bore him a little.

Jackson helped himself to a slice of tepid filet the mournful butler handed him with a look as skeptical as his own, rejected the mushroom sauce after a barely perceptible sniff, and leaned back in his chair, glancing up at the candle-lit portraits of Archers, Newlands, and van der Luydens hung in their heavy dark frames on the walls.
“Ah, your grandfather Archer loved a good dinner, my dear Newland!” he said, nodding at the portrait of the plump young man in a blue coat and starched stock, a white-columned country house visible behind him. “Well, well… I wonder what he would have said to all these foreign marriages!”
Mrs. Archer ignored the jab at her family’s ancestral disregard for fine food. “So she wasn’t at the ball?”
“Ah—” Mrs. Archer murmured, the tone heavy with relief that Ellen had retained at least that much decency.
“Maybe the Beauforts don’t even know her,” Janey suggested, with artless little malice.
Jackson took a faint sip of his wine, as if tasting a vintage Madeira. “Mrs. Beaufort may not. But Beaufort certainly does. Half of New York saw her walking up Fifth Avenue with him this afternoon.”
“Mercy,” Mrs. Archer moaned, clearly giving up on the hope that a foreigner like Ellen would follow the unspoken rules of delicacy.
“I wonder if she wore a round hat or a bonnet in the afternoon,” Janey mused. “At the opera, you know, she had on that dark blue velvet, perfectly plain and flat—just like a nightgown.”
“Janey!” her mother chided, and Janey blushed, trying to look audacious.
“At any rate, it was far better taste not to go to the ball,” Mrs. Archer continued.
A perverse little urge to push back made Newland speak up. “I don’t think it was a matter of taste for her. May said she planned to go, then decided the dress she had wasn’t smart enough.”
Mrs. Archer smiled, relieved that lined up with her own assumption. “Poor Ellen,” she said softly. “We have to remember what an eccentric upbringing Medora Manson gave her. What can you expect of a girl who was allowed to wear black satin to her own coming out ball?”
“Ah, I remember her in that exact dress!” Jackson said. “Poor girl,” he added, in the tone of a man who’d thoroughly enjoyed the spectacle at the time, and knew exactly what it had portended.
“It’s odd,” Janey said. “She kept such an ugly name as Ellen. I would have changed it to Elaine.” She glanced around the table to see how the line landed.
Her brother laughed. “Why Elaine?”
“I don’t know. It sounds more… more Polish,” Janey said, blushing.
“It sounds more conspicuous,” Mrs. Archer said distantly. “That can hardly be what she wants right now.”
“Why not?” Newland broke in, suddenly argumentative. “Why shouldn’t she be conspicuous if she chooses? Why should she slink about as if she’s the one who disgraced herself? She’s poor Ellen, all right—she had the rotten luck to make a terrible marriage. But that’s no reason for her to hide her head like she’s the culprit.”
“I suppose that’s the line the Mingotts have decided to take,” Jackson said speculatively.
Newland reddened. “I didn’t need the Mingotts to tell me that, if that’s what you’re implying, sir. Madame Olenska has lived a very unhappy life. That doesn’t make her an outcast.”
“There are rumors,” Jackson began, glancing pointedly at Janey.
“Oh, I know—the secretary,” Newland cut him off. “Nonsense, Mother, Janey’s grown up. They say, don’t they, that the secretary helped her get away from that brute of a husband who kept her practically a prisoner? Well, what if he did? I hope there isn’t a man among us who wouldn’t have done the exact same thing.”
Jackson turned to the sad butler hovering nearby. “Actually… that sauce, just a little after all,” he said, and once the man had served him, he added casually, “I’m told she’s looking for a house. She’s planning to settle here for good.”
“I hear she’s planning to get a divorce,” Janey said, boldly.
“I hope she will!” Newland exclaimed.

The words landed like a bombshell in the quiet, pure, tranquil air of the Archer dining room. Mrs. Archer lifted her delicate eyebrows in that very specific curve that meant “the butler is still in the room”—and Newland, suddenly mindful of how vulgar it was to discuss such intimate family matters in front of staff, hastily steered the conversation over to his recent visit with old Mrs. Mingott, the famously matriarchal invalid.

After dinner, following their ancient, unchanging custom, Mrs. Archer and Janey dragged their long silk skirts up to the drawing room, where the gentlemen stayed behind to smoke. They sat beside a carved glass Carcel lamp, facing each other across a rosewood worktable with a green silk bag tucked under it, stitching at opposite ends of a tapestry band of wild field flowers meant to adorn an occasional chair in the future drawing room of young Mrs. Newland Archer.

Down in the Gothic library, Newland settled Jackson in an armchair by the fire and handed him one of his own fine cigars. Jackson sank into the chair with deep satisfaction, lit his cigar knowing it would be perfectly good, and stretched his thin, gouty old ankles out toward the flames.
“You say the secretary only helped her escape, my dear fellow? Well, a year later he was still helping her, then. Someone spotted the two of them living together in Lausanne.”
Newland flushed bright red. “Living together? Well, why not? Who on earth has the right to run her life for her, if she hasn’t? I’m sick of this hypocrisy that would bury a woman of her age alive just because her husband chooses to live with his mistresses.” He turned away angrily to light his own cigar. “Women ought to be free—as free as we are,” he declared, making a grand new discovery he was far too riled up to measure the terrible consequences of.

Sillerton Jackson stretched his ankles even closer to the fire, and let out a sardonic little whistle.
“Well,” he said after a pause, “it seems Count Olenski agrees with you. For I’ve never once heard of him lifting a single finger to get his wife back.”
