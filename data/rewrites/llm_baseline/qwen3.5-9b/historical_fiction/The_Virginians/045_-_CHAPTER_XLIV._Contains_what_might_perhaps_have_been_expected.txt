Upon the rejection of his peace offerings, our warlike young American chief resolved to harbor a profound wrath—not merely against Colonel Lambert, but against the entire family of that gentleman. "He has humiliated me before the girls!" the young man fumed internally. "He and Mr. Wolfe, who were constantly preaching morality to me while posturing with airs of superior virtue and protection, have once again held me up to the family as a mere scapegrace and prodigal. They are so sanctimonious they refuse to even shake my hand; and when I attempt to demonstrate a shred of common gratitude, they simply fling my gifts back in my face!"

"Why, sir, the things must be worth a small fortune!" exclaimed Parson Sampson, casting a greedy eye upon the two morocco boxes resting on white satin cushions, filled with Mr. Sparks's golden trinkets.

"They cost some money, Sampson," replied the young man. "Though I would not grudge tenfold the sum to anyone who has shown me kindness."

"No, faith, sir, not if I know your honor!" interjected Sampson, seizing every opportunity to praise his young patron directly to his face.

"The repeater, they told me, was a magnificent bargain, worth a hundred pounds in Paris," Harry continued. "Little Miss Hetty mentioned she longed for a repeating watch."

"Oh, what a lovely piece!" cried the chaplain. "With a small circle of pearls set on the back and a diamond knob for the handle! Why, 'twould win any woman's heart, sir!"

"There passes an apple-woman with a basket," shouted Mr. Warrington fiercely. "I have a mind to fling the thing out to her!"

When Harry ventured out on business, taking him to the City and the Temple, his parasite did not follow him far into the Strand; turning away instead, citing a terror of Chancery Lane, its inhabitants, and its precincts. Mr. Warrington proceeded to his broker, and they walked to the Bank, conducting some minor transactions. After signing a few trivial documents, Harry departed with a handful of crisp banknotes in his pocket. His broker escorted him to one of the great dining-houses for which the City was renowned, and subsequently guided him along the Virginian Walk on 'Change. Through this thoroughfare, Harry passed with considerable shame. *What would a certain lady in Virginia say,* he wondered, *if she knew that he was carrying off in that bottomless gambler's pocket a significant portion of his father's patrimony?* *Those are all Virginia merchants,* he thought, observing the bustling crowd, *all talking among themselves about me, declaring, 'That is young Esmond of Castlewood, on the Potomac, Madam Esmond's son; and he has been squandering his money at play, selling out so much, so much, and so much.'*

His spirits did not lift until he had passed beneath the Traitors' Heads of Temple Bar and emerged fully from the City. From the Strand, Mr. Harry walked home, glancing briefly into St. James's Street en route, though the houses were empty; the company did not arrive at the Chocolate-House until a later hour.

Once arrived home, Mr. Harry produced his bundle of banknotes. He placed three of them inside a sheet of paper, sealing it carefully after writing within: *"Much good may they do you. H. E. W."* Directing this packet to the Reverend Mr. Sampson, he left it on the chimney-glass, instructing his servants to deliver it to the clergyman upon his arrival.

Now, his honor's phaeton was brought to the door, and he stepped in, intending to drive around the park. However, driven by the onset of rain, an east wind, or some other unforeseen circumstance, he turned his horses' heads down St. James's Street and returned to White's Club by approximately three o'clock. Scarcely anyone had arrived yet; it was the hour when patrons were still dining. Nevertheless, there sat his cousin, Lord Castlewood, lounging over the *Public Advertiser*, having just returned from his duties at Court nearby.

Lord Castlewood yawned over the newspaper. "What shall we do?" he asked. "Shall we play a little piquet?" Harry had no objection to a brief game. "Just for an hour," said Lord Castlewood. "I dine at Arlington Street at four." "Just for an hour," agreed Mr. Warrington, and they summoned the cards.

"Or shall we move upstairs?" suggested my lord. "Away from the noise?"

"Certainly, out of the noise," replied Harry.

By five o'clock, a half-dozen gentlemen had entered after dinner, engaging in cards, coffee, or conversation. The regulars from the ordinary had not yet cleared their tables. Thus, the gentlemen of White's often remained seated until well past midnight.

From behind the coffee-house blinds, a toothpick pointed into the street. "Whose phaeton is that?" asked Toothpick Number 1 of Number 2.

"The Fortunate Youth's," replied Number 2.

"Not so fortunate the last three nights. Luck confoundedly against him. He lost thirteen hundred to the table last night. Has Mr. Warrington been here today, John?"

"Mr. Warrington is in the house now, sir. In the little tea-room with Lord Castlewood since three o'clock. They are playing piquet," answered John.

"What fun for Castlewood!" remarked Number 1, shrugging.

The second gentleman growled an execration. "Curse the fellow!" he spat. "He has no right to be in this club at all. He doesn't pay if he loses. Gentlemen ought not to play with him. Sir Miles Warrington told me at court the other day that Castlewood has owed him money on a bet for three years."

"Castlewood," said Number 1, "doesn't lose if he plays alone. A large company flurries him; that is why he avoids the table." The facetious gentleman grinned, displaying teeth polished to perfection.

"Let's go up and stop them," growled Number 2.

"Why?" asked the other. "It is much better to look out the window. See the lamplighter climbing the ladder—famous sport. Look at that old quiz in the chair: have you ever seen such a specimen?"

"Who is that just leaving the house? By my life, it's Fortunatus! He seems to have forgotten his phaeton has been waiting here all this time. I bet you two to one he has been losing to Castlewood."

"Jack, do you take me for a fool?" demanded one gentleman of the other. "Pretty pair of horses the youth has got. How he is flogging them!" They watched Mr. Warrington gallop up the street, causing terrified coachmen and chairmen to clear the path. Presently, Lord Castlewood was seen entering a hackney carriage and departing.

Harry drove up to his own door. It was but a few yards, and those poor horses had beaten the pavement all this while in the rain. Mr. Gumbo was engaged at the door in conversation with a country-looking lass, who curtsied and hurried away. Mr. Gumbo was perpetually occupied with some pretty maid or other.

"Gumbo, has Mr. Sampson been here?" asked his master from the driving seat.

"No, sar. Mr. Sampson have not been here!" answered Mr. Warrington's servant. Harry instructed him to go upstairs and retrieve a letter addressed to Mr. Sampson.

"Addressed to Mr. Sampson? Oh yes, sir," said Mr. Gumbo, unable to read.

"A sealed letter, stupid! On the mantelpiece, in the glass!" snapped Harry. Gumbo leisurely retired to fetch the document. As soon as Harry secured it, he turned his horses toward St. James's Street. Meanwhile, the two gentlemen still yawning out the window at White's beheld the Fortunate Youth return in an instant.

As they passed out of the little tea-room where he and Lord Castlewood had played piquet, Mr. Warrington observed that several gentlemen had entered the playroom and established a bank. Some were already steadily working, wearing their gaming jackets—a garment the club kept for members wishing to engage in serious nightly play.

Mr. Warrington approached the clerk's desk, settled his account for the previous night, and sat down at the table, requesting fresh counters. This had been decidedly an unlucky week for the Fortunate Youth, and tonight proved no more fortunate than the preceding nights. He called for more counters, and then more. He appeared slightly pale and silent, though remaining easy and polite when spoken to. Yet, he could not win.

Finally, he rose. "Hang it! Stay and mend your luck!" urged Lord March, sitting beside him with a heap of green and white counters. "Take a hundred of mine and go on!"

"I have had enough for tonight, my lord," said Harry, rising and departing. He ate a broiled bone in the coffee-room and walked back to his lodgings sometime around midnight.
