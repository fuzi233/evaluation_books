It was toward Candlemas-tide, on the fifteenth day of February in the year of grace 1587, when the dusk came early and the west wind smelled of salt and wrack, that Mistress Leigh walked, as she had walked at every evening high-water for three full years, the terrace at Burrough.

Below her the river uncoiled, slate-colored under the failing light; beyond it the sand-hills lay hazed and low, and farther still the broad western sea, which had swallowed so many brave men as quietly as a housewife takes in her breath and goes on kneading. The same sight, night after night; the same patient watching, until watching had become as much a household duty as setting out bread, or warming a little broth for the sick in the cottages.

Three years and more, and no word—neither of Frank nor of Amyas, nor of the good ship which had borne them out. In Bideford and Appledore, Clovelly and Ilfracombe, eyes had grown hollow with the same hunger. Once there had been a flare of hope: Sir Richard, unknown to her, had sent a rider hard to Plymouth when Drake and the rest came home from the Spanish Main; but the news, though great—Cartagena and San Domingo, Saint Augustine, and Raleigh’s poor Virginians relieved—brought no whisper of the Rose. Then Mistress Leigh had bowed her head as she did when bread failed or fever came, and spoken the old words as if they were a prayer learned before she had teeth: the Lord gave; the Lord hath taken away.

Her hair, once dark and orderly under her coif, was now grey as sea-fret. Her cheeks had lost their orchard-color. She moved more slowly; but she never missed the tide, any more than she missed church on a Sunday, or the round of cottages where she carried simples and kind speech. She did not speak her sons’ names. She would not let them be spoken in her hearing, as if to name them were to season a wound and make it smart anew. Yet every day, when the tide rose high and the red flag upon the sand-hills told there was water over the bar, she went out under the wind-clipped trees and stared westward with eyes that ate the horizon.

Ships still came and went as of old. White sails hovered off the bar, waiting; and many a time, year after year, men on the terrace had been deceived by a trick of light, or by longing. But in her there was an instinct as sure as a baker’s hand that knows, without looking, when the loaf is proved. She knew the cut of that ship, every line of her; she could have plucked her out miles away among a fleet—and the sail she sought never came.

So she went to and fro among the poor, and they, seeing her steadfast gaze and her quiet mouth, whispered that God had brought her very near to Himself. Lone women, bed-ridden men, took strength from the calm of her presence; for though she spoke little of her own sorrow, they felt she had walked deep in the furnace and was not burned. And all the while she blamed herself at night for that “earthly” pull which drew her, each high tide, back to the terrace as faithfully as a bell draws folk to prayer.

But this evening, Northam would not be quiet.

Down on the pebble ridge the surf thundered as it always did, like a cartload of stones poured without end; yet the village had noise enough without the sea’s voice. The tower shook with pealing bells. Men and boys were in the streets, singing and shouting about bonfires, drinking to the Queen’s health, crying, “So perish all her enemies!” Effigies were burned with a stink of pitch and singed cloth. The hills answered with their own red blazes from every hamlet, and far off Bideford’s bells replied, as they had replied years ago when Amyas had come home, brown from the sun and bright with the madness of the great sea-road.

For this day the tidings had run through England like fire through dry heath: Mary of Scotland had been beheaded at Fotheringay. After seventeen years of dread and plots and whispering treasons, the land sprang up as from a nightmare and shouted itself hoarse.

Mistress Leigh, Protestant as she was, found no relish in the shouting. She breathed a prayer—brief as the crossing of oneself, and as involuntary—that God would have mercy on that soul, clear as diamond and as hard, as Mary had once said of herself. The woman in Mary—whatever else had been in her—rose for a moment, pitiful and haunting, among the smoke and bells and zeal; and Mistress Leigh, who had learned in sorrow what no court could teach, shuddered to think of what training a girl might have had amid the Medicis and the Guises, and how little is asked where little is given. Still the bells would not cease.

Then, cutting across their clangor, came a sound from the outer dark—a flash, and after it the deep voice of a gun at sea.

Mistress Leigh stopped short upon the terrace.

The flash had been close outside the bar. It was no distress-signal; the wind was light and westerly, and the spring tide was making full. Another flash, another gun. At once the revel in Northam faltered, as if a hand had clapped over a mouth. Men ran, the way men run when fear sobers them: up into the churchyard that looks down upon the flats and the river.

Out beyond the bar, riding the dim water, was a tall ship running in with all her canvas spread. Near a thousand tons she might be, and yet the rig did not look English. For a breath the old dread bit: a Spanish cruiser come to strike for Drake’s mischief on the Cadiz shore? And yet—no. Spaniards did not play at such lone ventures if they could help it. If the Don came, he came with his proper order and ceremony: admiral and vice, transports and avisos, a whole book of war made flesh.

The strange ship slipped between the sand-hills on the flowing tide, bold as a hawk, and still she did not veil her topsails. That alone made men’s hands itch for match and powder; for every foreigner knew what it meant to show full canvas within sight of an English port. There were old seamen at Appledore who had not forgotten John Hawkins’s lesson to a proud Spanish admiral at Plymouth Sound—how a shot between the masts had taught manners, and a second had near “lackt” him through and through, until down came the flag and apologies with it. And if Hawkins had been so jealous in peace, then now—now—there was more than one sea-dog who would have found an iron pot to burn powder in.

The ship passed behind the hill of Appledore and vanished for a while; and then, up from the river came a cheer, sudden and great, like geese rising. Mistress Leigh stood as if turned to stone, listening. Another gun boomed among the hills. Another cheer answered it.

Time passed—twenty minutes, it might be; no clock in that moment had any use. She never moved, her eyes fixed where the dark rock of the Hubbastone should show.

At last the vessel came again, rounding the Hubbastone as she turned up the Bideford river. Music floated from her decks—drums and fifes, shawms and trumpets—waking echoes from slate and wooded knoll, so that the very land seemed to answer. And as she opened full upon Burrough House, her crew sent up a cheer that rolled along the silver water and climbed the hills.

Mistress Leigh turned, walking quickly toward the house.

“Grace,” she called, and her voice was not steady, “bring me my hood. Master Amyas is come home.”

Her maid, a faithful soul whose hands knew all household tasks from scouring to salving, stared and then cried out with a broken laugh.

“No, surely? O joyful sound! Praised be the Lord! But, madam—how ever did you know?”

“I heard his voice on the river,” Mistress Leigh said; and then the truth in her struck like cold iron. “But I did not hear Mr. Frank’s with him, Grace.”

“Oh, madam,” Grace hurried on, clinging to hope as to a warm pan in winter, “where the one is, the other is. They’d never part company. Both come home or neither, I’ll warrant. Here’s your hood.”

Hooded, with Grace behind her, Mistress Leigh went down toward Bideford at a pace her feeble step had not kept for years.

Was it truth? Or a dream wrought by long watching—some mercy, or some cruelty, of the mind? Grace asked herself the same, and crossed herself once in spite of being a Protestant, as folk do when fear is too old for doctrine.

On the quay it answered itself.

Down Bridgeland Street they came—where afterward tobacco-warehouses would stand for the Virginia trade, but where then rope-walks ran and sailmakers’ shops smelled of tar, hemp, and wet canvas. They saw the strange ship already at anchor. And as they reached the lower end of the street, a great mob swept round the corner—sailors, women, ’prentices—crying, questioning, laughing, weeping in the same breath.

Mistress Leigh stopped, and the crowd stopped too, as if a wave had met a rock.

“Here she is!” someone shouted. “Here’s his mother!”

His mother. Not their mother. The thought turned her skin white; but her heart, long trained to endurance, did not break.

Then the giant head and shoulders of Amyas rose above the press and came round the corner, moving like a man who has marched through worse crowds than this.

“Make a way! Make room for Madam Leigh!” he cried; and in the next moment he was on his knees at her feet.

She put her arms about his neck and bent her grey head over his dark one. Rough harbor-women, loud lads, weather-bit sailors—all were hushed as if a psalm had begun. A ring formed round them in silence.

Mistress Leigh asked no question. She saw, with a knowledge that needed no words, that Amyas was alone.

At last he whispered, close to her so that none but she might hear, “I would have died to save him, mother, if I could.”

“You need not tell me that, Amyas Leigh, my son.”

Silence again, heavy as a shut door.

“How did he die?” she whispered.

“He is a martyr. He died in the—”

The word would not come.

“The Inquisition?” Mistress Leigh said, and though her voice was low it had a hard edge, as a knife has even when laid down.

“Yes.”

A shudder went through her frame as if winter had passed inside her; and then she lifted her head, forcing her face to steadiness.

“Come home, Amyas. I little expected such an honor—such an honor—ha! ha! and such a fair young martyr, too; a very St. Stephen! God have mercy on me, and let me not go mad before these folk, when I ought to be thanking Thee for Thy great mercies! Amyas—who is that?”

She pointed. Close behind Amyas stood a girl, dark-eyed, watchful, slight as a young doe, looking upon the whole scene as one listens to an unknown forest: alert for danger, for kindness, for snares.

“She is a poor wild Indian maid,” Amyas said. “My daughter, I call her. I will tell you her story hereafter.”

“Your daughter? My grand-daughter, then. Come hither, maiden, and be my grand-daughter.”

The girl came obediently and knelt, because she had seen Amyas kneel.

“God forbid, child; kneel not to me. Come home, and let me know whether I am sane or mazed, alive or dead.”

She drew her hood closer, as if the cloth could hold in what her face might betray, and turned to go back, taking Amyas by one hand and the girl by the other.

For some twenty yards the crowd let them pass in a reverent hush; then, as if breath could be held no longer, it burst into a cheer that made the old town ring from slate to slate.

Mistress Leigh stopped suddenly.

“I had forgotten, Amyas. You must not let me stand in the way of your duty. Where are your men?”

“Kissed to death by this time,” Amyas answered, with a sound that tried to be mirth and failed, “all of them, that is, who are left.”

“Left?”

“We went out a hundred, mother, and we came home forty-four—if we are at home. Is it a dream? Is this you? and this old Bridgeland Street again? As I live, there stands Evans the smith at his door, tankard in hand, as he did when I was a boy!”

The brawny smith did indeed come striding across; but when he saw Amyas and no Frank, he stopped, and his comfort was clumsy as a sailor’s knot.

“Better one than neither, madam,” he said.

Amyas gripped his hand as he passed. Mistress Leigh neither heard nor saw him nor any man there.

“Mother,” Amyas went on, when they were past the causeway and the noise fell behind them like surf, “we are rich for life.”

“Yes,” she said, and the word cut, “a martyr’s death was the fittest for him.”

“I have brought home treasure untold.”

“What, my boy?”

“Treasure untold. Cary has promised to see to it to-night.”

“Very well. I would that he had slept at our house. He was a kindly lad, and loved Frank. When did he—?” Her voice faltered on the unspoken seeing of Frank, as if her mouth could not shape the past without tasting it.

“Three years ago and more. Within two months of our sailing.”

“Ah. Yes. He told me so.”

“Told you so?” Amyas stopped, bewildered.

“Yes. The dear lad has often come to see me in my sleep; but you never came.” She spoke as one speaks of bread rising or a cow calving—plain, without ornament, as if the world of spirits were only another room in the house. “I guessed how it was—as it should be.”

“But I loved you none the less, mother!”

“I know that too. But you were busy with the men, sweet; so your spirit could not come roving home like his, which was free.” She turned her head slightly toward the girl, as if drawing her into the circle of speech. “My maid—do you not find it cold here in England after those hot regions?”

The girl’s English was broken and small, but her meaning came clear.

“Ayacanora’s heart is warm. She does not think about cold.”

“Warm?” Mistress Leigh said softly. “Perhaps you will warm my heart for me, then.”

“Would God I could do it, mother!” Amyas answered, and there was a half-reproach in him, as of a man who has carried a burden too long and cannot set it down gently.

Mistress Leigh looked up full into his face. Something in it—what he had suffered, what he had feared from her—broke the last crust of her long-held composure, and she wept, violently, as if the tears were a flood at last allowed to run.

“Sinful! sinful that I am!”

“Blessed creature!” Amyas cried, and his own voice cracked with the strain. “If you speak so I shall go mad. Mother, mother—these months I have dreaded this meeting. It has hung over me like a black thunder-cloud; like a great cliff miles high, with its top hid in the clouds, which I had to climb and dared not. I have longed to leap overboard and flee from it like a coward into the depths of the sea—the thought that you might ask me whether I was not my brother’s keeper; that you might require his blood at my hands—and now, now, when it comes, to find you all love, and trust, and patience—mother, mother, it is more than I can bear!” And he wept as one who has held himself iron through battle and is broken only by kindness.

She knew him too well not to hear, in that outburst from a usually quiet nature, the sound of long wrestling with despair. At once she forgot her own pain in the one old habit of a mother: to soothe.

And she did soothe him. Home they went, arm in arm. Ayacanora clung like a child to the skirt of Mistress Leigh’s cloak; the fearless forest creature had shrunk into trembling modesty, cast suddenly upon a shore where every face was strange and every sound carried meanings she did not yet know.

“Will your mother love me?” she whispered to Amyas at the threshold.

“Yes,” he whispered back. “But you must do what she tells you.”

Ayacanora pouted, quick as a flame.

“She will laugh at me, because I am wild.”

“She never laughs at any one.”

“Humph,” said Ayacanora, and glanced sideways, measuring. “Well, I shall not be afraid of her. I thought she would have been tall like you; but she is not even as big as me.”

It did not sound promising for obedience. Yet before a day was gone, Mistress Leigh had won her utterly—not by threats, nor even by soft indulgence, but by that quiet power which rules a household without noise. Ayacanora, trying to explain in her own fashion, said she had thought so great a man must have a great mother; she had expected some dreadful princess with a frown like Juno, and had found instead—so her halting words made it—one who healed.

Her story was soon told; and Mistress Leigh, woman-like, would suffer no doubt of the girl’s right to the name Amyas had given her. And never did she imprint a prouder, tenderer kiss upon her son’s forehead than when he said simply that he had kept, unspotted like a gentleman and a Christian, the soul which God had put into his charge.

“Then you have forgiven me, mother?” he asked, like a boy again.

“Years ago,” she answered. “I said in this same room, What shall I render to the Lord for having given me two such sons? And in this room I say it once again. Tell me all about my other son, that I may honor him as I honor you.”

And then—with that iron nerve which belongs to good women—she made him give her every detail: Lucy Passmore’s tale; the long wandering; and all that had befallen them from the day they sailed until that luckless night at La Guayra. She did not flinch; she asked where she must; she forced the words, and when they were spoken she gathered them up and set them away inside her, as a housewife lays up grain against famine.

When it was done she led Ayacanora out and began to busy herself, quietly, with the girl’s comforts, as if Frank and Amyas had been sleeping in their cribs in the next room and the world had never altered. She saw to fire and water; she called for clean linen. The house filled with homely sounds—the clack of a pot-lid, the scrape of a trencher, the soft hurry of feet. The very scents of a lived-in place rose up: smoke in the rafters, wool warmed by a body, rosemary crushed when one brushed past it. After the great sea, the small household noises fell like blessings.

She had hardly gone up when the door below was struck with a loud knock—no timid visitor’s tap, but a command. It opened hastily; and into the hall burst, regardless of all manner, Sir Richard Grenville.

Tall, stately, his face cut as if from iron and his eyes like a winter sky, he came in as a storm comes. Amyas dropped on his knees by instinct, as he might have done before his captain on a deck.

But the stern warrior was unmanned. He bent over his godson; and a tear dropped from that iron cheek onto the weathered cheek of Amyas Leigh.

“My lad! my glorious lad! Where have you been?” he cried. “Get up and tell me all. The sailors told me a little, but I must hear every word. I knew you would do something grand. I told your mother you were too good a workman for God to throw away. Now—let me have the whole story. Why, I am out of breath! To tell truth, I ran three parts of the way hither.”

So they sat down, and Amyas spoke far into the night. Sir Richard, his stateliness returning as he listened, gave a grim smile here, a stern nod there, at each deed of daring; and when all was ended he answered with something like a sigh that betrayed the hunger of action in him.

“Would God that I had been with you every step! Would God, at least, that I could show as good a three-years’ log-book, Amyas, my lad!”

“You can show a better one, I doubt not.”

“Humph! With the exception of one paltry Spanish prize, I know not that the queen is the better, or her enemies the worse, for me, since we parted last in Dublin city.”

“You are too modest, sir.”

“Would that I were. I got on in Ireland no better than my neighbors, and so came home again to find that while I had been wasting time in that land of misrule, Raleigh had done a deed to which I can see no end. For he has found—his two captains, Amadas and Barlow, found for him—between Florida and Newfoundland, a country the like of which, I believe, is not on earth for climate and fertility. Whether there be gold there, I know not, and it matters little; for there is all else on earth that man can want: furs, timber, rivers, game, sugar-canes, corn, fruit; every commodity which France, Spain, or Italy can yield, growing wild in abundance. The savages civil enough for savages. In a word, a jewel fit for her majesty’s crown. The people call it Wingandacoa; but we—after her majesty—Virginia.”

“You have been there, then?” Amyas asked, and the new name tasted strange and promising in his mouth, like a spice brought from oversea.

“The year before last; and left there Ralf Lane, and Amadas, and some twenty gentlemen, and ninety men—and money of my own and of old Will Salterne’s, which neither of us may see again. For the colony, I know not how, quarrelled with the Indians—though I fear I was over-sharp with some for stealing; if I was, God forgive me!—and could not keep themselves alive for twelve months. Drake, coming back from his last West Indian voyage, after giving what help he could, had to bring the whole faint-hearted party home. And if you will believe it, they had not been gone a fortnight before I was back again with three ships and all they could want. Never was I more wroth than when all I found was the ruins of their huts, already full—so rich is the growth—of great melons, and wild deer feeding thereon. Pretty sight enough; not what I wanted then. So back I came, and being in no overgood temper, vented my humors on the Portugals at the Azores and had hard fights and small booty. So there the matter stands—but not for long. Shame it were if such a paradise, once found by Britons, should fall into any hands but her majesty’s. We will try again this spring, if men and money can be found. Eh, lad?”

“And the prize?” Amyas asked, for he knew Grenville would not have called it paltry if it had not cost him.

“Ah! That was no small make-weight to our disasters. I sighted her six days’ sail from the American coast; but ere we could lay her aboard it fell dead calm. Never a boat had I on board—they were all lost in a gale—and the other ships were becalmed two leagues astern. There was no use lying there and pounding her till she sank; so I called the carpenter, got up old chests, and with them and some spars we floated ourselves alongside—only just in time. The last of us had hardly scrambled into the chains when our crazy Noah’s ark went all aboard and sank at the side, so that if we had been minded to run away, Amyas, we could not. Whereon, judging valor the better part of discretion—as I usually do—we fell to with our swords and had her in five minutes; and fifty thousand pounds’ worth in her, which set up my purse again, and Raleigh’s too—though I fear it has run out again since as fast as it ran in.”

So ended Sir Richard’s tale; and the night wore on with the kind of talk that binds men who live by wind and steel, while in the house above, the household settled into uneasy sleep, and the river outside made its soft sucking sounds against the mud like a creature breathing.

The next day Amyas went to Salterne.

The old man had heard the bare outlines already; yet he bade Amyas sit down and tell all from the beginning, and listened with chin upon hand, elbows on knees, as if he were weighing coin. His cheek did not blanch; his lips did not quiver. Only when Amyas came to Rose’s marriage, he drew one long breath as if a weight had been lifted from his heart.

“Say that again, sir,” he said.

Amyas said it again, and then went on; and at the manner of her death his voice faltered. He hinted, as if any gentleness could soften truth.

“Go on, sir!” Salterne snapped. “Why are you afraid? There is nothing to be ashamed of there, is there?”

So Amyas told the whole with downcast eyes, and then dared a glance at the old man’s face. There was no sign of grief in it—only a proud curl at the corner of the iron mouth, as if something in him had been satisfied.

“And her husband?” Salterne asked at last, after a pause.

“I am ashamed to have to tell you, sir, that the man still lives.”

“Still lives, sir?”

“Too true, as far as I know. That it was not my fault, my story bears me witness.”

“Sir, I never doubted your will to kill him. Still lives, you say? Well, so do rats and adders.” He leaned back a little, and his eyes narrowed. “And now, I suppose, Captain Leigh, your worship is minded to recruit yourself on shore a while with the fair lass whom you have brought home—as I hear—before having another dash at the devil and his kin!”

“Do not mention that young lady’s name with mine, sir,” Amyas answered, and there was a stiff pride in him which even grief could not soften. “She is no more to me than she is to you; for she has Spanish blood in her veins.”

Salterne smiled grimly, as if that suited him well enough.

“But I am minded at least to do one thing, Mr. Salterne,” Amyas went on, “and that is, to kill Spaniards, in fair fight, by land and sea, wheresoever I shall meet them. And therefore I stay not long here, whithersoever I may be bound next.”
