Mr. Moore returned to the others after sending off the delegation, and Mr. Helstone and Mr. Sykes greeted him with cheerful congratulations on his firmness and resolve. But Moore remained quiet, his face as still and dark as a cloudless night—no warmth in it, no motion. The rector glanced at him sharply, then tucked away his praise as if it were too much for the moment, and turned to Sykes, who seemed unaware that his presence was unwelcome. “Come along,” he said. “Our paths lie together. Let’s walk home together. We’ll bid Moore good morning and leave him to whatever thoughts he chooses to dwell upon.”

“And where is Sugden?” Moore asked, looking up.

“Ah!” Helstone exclaimed. “I’ve been busy while you were talking to that solemn-looking man—Farren, I believe. While you were negotiating, I opened the back window, called to Murgatroyd in the stable to bring round Mr. Sykes’s gig, and slipped Sugden and brother Moses—wooden leg and all—through the opening. I saw them climb into the carriage, of course, with Mr. Sykes’s full approval. Sugden took the reins—he drives like a storm—and within twenty minutes Barraclough will be safely locked up in Stilbro’ jail.”

“Very well,” Moore replied. “Thank you. Good morning, gentlemen.” He courteously escorted them to the door and watched them depart before closing it behind them.

For the rest of the day, Moore was silent and serious. He didn’t even exchange a witty remark with Joe Scott, who kept his words brief and businesslike but often glanced at his master from the corner of his eye. Joe came by to stoke the fire more than once, and when Moore was locking up for the evening—trade had slowed, so the mill was running short hours—he remarked, “It’s a fine night, sir. You might take a walk up th’ Hollow. It’d do you good.”

At this, Moore broke into a short laugh. “What’s all this concern about?” he asked. “Do you think me a child or a woman?” He snatched the keys from Joe’s hand and gently pushed him out of the room. But just as Joe reached the yard gate, Moore called him back.

“Joe,” he said, “do you know the Farrens? They’re not doing well, are they?”

“They can’t be,” Joe answered. “They haven’t worked in three months. You’d see it yourself—William’s changed, looks worn down. They’ve sold most of what they had in the house.”

“He wasn’t a bad worker?”

“Never better since you started trade, sir.”

“And decent people—the whole family?”

“Niver dacenter. The wife’s clean as a whistle—ye could eat your porridge off the floor. They’re hard hit. I wish William could get a job as gardener or something—he knows the craft well. Once lived with a Scotchman who taught him all the secrets.”

“Well, you may go now, Joe. No need to stand there staring.”

“You’ve no orders, sir?”

“None, except to take yourself off.”

And Joe did.

* * * * *

Spring evenings can be sharp and biting, even after a warm day. Though the sun had shone brightly, the air grew cold at sunset, the ground crusted over, and by dusk a thin frost crept across the grass and budding plants. It whitened the pavement in front of Briarmains—Mr. Yorke’s home—and silently damaged the tender greenery in his garden and on the mossy lawn. The great tree near the road, strong-trunked and wide-armed, stood defiant against the frost, as did the bare walnut trees rising behind the house.

In the dim, starlit night, lights glowed warmly from the windows. This was no lonely or silent scene. Briarmains stood close to the highway, an old house built before the road was cut, when a winding lane through fields was the only way to reach it. Briarfield was barely a mile away—its hum could be heard, its glow seen clearly. Nearby rose Briar Chapel, a large, new Wesleyan church, just a hundred yards distant. Inside, a prayer meeting was underway. Its windows lit the road, and a hymn—unusual in rhythm and fervor—echoed through the countryside. A Quaker might have danced to such a tune. The lyrics drifted through the night in fragments:

“Oh! who can explain
This struggle for life,
This travail and pain,
This trembling and strife?
Plague, earthquake, and famine,
And tumult and war,
The wonderful coming
Of Jesus declare!

“For every fight
Is dreadful and loud:
The warrior's delight
Is slaughter and blood,
His foes overturning,
Till all shall expire:
And this is with burning,
And fuel, and fire!”

Then came a burst of passionate prayer, followed by groans. A shout rang out: “I’ve found liberty!” “Doad o’ Bill’s has fun’ liberty!” And the congregation erupted again.

“What a mercy is this! What a heaven of bliss!
How unspeakably happy am I!
Gathered into the fold,
With Thy people enrolled,
With Thy people to live and to die!

“Oh, the goodness of God
In employing a clod
His tribute of glory to raise;
His standard to bear,
And with triumph declare
His unspeakable riches of grace!

“Oh, the fathomless love
That has deigned to approve
And prosper the work of my hands.
With my pastoral crook
I went over the brook,
And behold I am spread into bands!

“Who, I ask in amaze,
Hath begotten me these?
And inquire from what quarter they came.
My full heart it replies,
They are born from the skies,
And gives glory to God and the Lamb!”

After another long pause filled with shouts, cries, and groans, the final verse rose in a thunderous crescendo:

“Sleeping on the brink of sin,
Tophet gaped to take us in;
Mercy to our rescue flew,
Broke the snare, and brought us through.

“Here, as in a lion’s den,
Undevoured we still remain,
Pass secure the watery flood,
Hanging on the arm of God.

“Here——”

(The last line burst forth in a strained, ear-splitting shout.)

“Here we raise our voices higher,
Shout in the refiner’s fire,
Clap our hands amidst the flame,
Glory give to Jesus’ name!”

The chapel roof did not collapse—a testament to its sturdy slate.

Yet if the chapel pulsed with energy, so too did Briarmains, though in quieter fashion. Lights glowed in several windows. Lower casements opened onto the lawn, curtains partly veiling the candlelight inside, yet not muffling the sound of laughter and conversation. We step inside—into the heart of the home.

No guests fill the house. Only the Yorke family gathers in the farthest room to the right—the back parlour. This is their evening sanctuary.

By daylight, the windows would reveal stained glass—purple and amber hues surrounding medallions of Shakespeare and Milton. On the walls hang Canadian scenes—green forests, blue rivers—and in the center, a painting of Vesuvius erupting at night, glowing fiercely against cool waterfalls and dark woods.

The fire blazing here is one southern readers rarely see in private homes. High, clear coal flames fill the wide chimney. Mr. Yorke insists on such fires—even in summer. He sits beside it, book in hand, a small candlestand at his elbow. But he isn’t reading. He watches his children.

Opposite him sits his wife—a woman whose features I could describe in detail, but I hesitate. Yet I see her plainly: tall, grave, burdened with care—not crushing, but constant, like a shadow she chose to carry. She believed sorrow was noble, joy profane. She disapproved of cheerfulness, especially in women. To her, mirth was frivolity, laughter was irreverence. She never made distinctions. Still, she was a devoted wife, attentive mother, loyal to her husband. Her flaw? She wanted him to have no friend but herself. His relatives she distrusted, kept at bay.

Yorke and she got along well enough, though he was naturally sociable, fond of family ties, and once preferred lively, spirited women. Why he married her, how they suited each other—puzzling, but perhaps explained by his own dual nature. His darker side found comfort in her unrelenting seriousness. She was strong-minded, never weak or trite, skeptical of society, cynical of humanity, convinced of her own perfection and the world’s failure. Her greatest fault? An endless, unshakable suspicion of men, ideas, beliefs—everything. It clouded her vision, misled her steps.

Their children, shaped by such parents, were no ordinary souls. Six of them.

The youngest, a baby, rests on her mother’s lap. Still entirely hers, untouched by doubt. She feeds from her, clings to her, loves her above all. The mother feels certain of this bond—because the child lives by her, it must love her. So she loves in return.

Next are two girls—Rose and Jessy—now seated at their father’s knee. They rarely approach their mother unless forced. Rose, twelve, resembles her father—but softer, like marble carved from stone. Her face is gentle, childlike, cheeks rosy. Her gray eyes hold something deeper—a serious soul already stirring. Young, yes, but destined to grow stronger, purer, more noble than either parent. She is quiet, sometimes stubborn. Her mother wants to mold her into a dutiful, somber woman, but Rose carries seeds of thought her mother never knew. It hurts her to have those thoughts crushed. She hasn’t rebelled yet—but if pressed too far, she will. And once, it will be forever.

She loves her father. He doesn’t rule her harshly. He is kind. And he fears she won’t live—so bright are the sparks of intelligence in her gaze. That fear makes him tender, almost fearful.

Jessy, younger and livelier, is his favorite. Gay, talkative, mischievous, affectionate when praised. Passionate when provoked, fearless—especially of her mother, whom she often defied. With her bright face and charming ways, she’s meant to be loved. And she is.

Now imagine, reader, if a mirror showed you both daughters twenty years hence. First, Jessy.

You recognize this place—though never seen. Cypress, willow, yew. Stone crosses, eternal flowers. Green sod, gray marble. Here lies Jessy. She lived one April day—loved deeply, loving much. She wept, she suffered, but smiled often, bringing light wherever she went. She died peacefully in Rose’s arms, in a foreign land. The soil there holds her now.

Now Rose, two years later. The crosses seem strange, but the landscape—hills, woods—feels wilder still. This is far from England. A virgin land. Birds unknown flutter near the forest. A river unlike any European one winds beneath her. The quiet Yorkshire girl is alone, an emigrant in the southern hemisphere. Will she ever return?

Three boys—Matthew, Mark, Martin—sit together in the corner, playing. Their heads look alike at first glance, but closer inspection reveals differences. All have dark hair, dark eyes, red cheeks, English features, a blend of both parents. Yet each bears a distinct face, a unique spirit.

Matthew, the eldest, draws attention. Jet-black hair, white brow, high-cheeked, quick eyes. Handsome, yes—but his face echoes something else: the eruption of Vesuvius. Flame and shadow—no sunlight, no peace. He seems English in form, but not in mind. Like an Italian blade wrapped in British leather. He scowls—crossed in the game. Mr. Yorke sees it and whispers, “Mark, Martin—don’t provoke your brother.” This is their constant tone. They claim no favoritism, yet Matthew is never challenged. They avoid angering him as if he were a powder keg. “Concede, conciliate”—their motto. The republicans unknowingly crown a tyrant. The younger boys sense this injustice, feel it deeply. Rebellion simmers beneath the surface. Seeds of discord are sown among Yorke’s olive branches.

Mark, calm, steady. Regular features, shrewd smile. Can say the sharpest things in the quietest voice. His brow hints at temper—smooth waters can hide danger. Too still, too unmoved. He will never truly enjoy life. By twenty-five, he’ll wonder why people laugh. Poetry? Rant. Enthusiasm? Ridiculous. He’ll be middle-aged in spirit by fourteen. His body young, his soul aged.

Martin, the youngest, is different. Life may be short—or long—but it will be vivid. He’ll pass through illusions, half-believe, wholly enjoy, then outlive them. Not handsome now—plain, even rough. A shell around him. But it will shed. Near twenty, he’ll become striking. His manners clumsy, clothes plain—yet the chrysalis holds the power to transform. Then he’ll be vain, eager, thirsty for pleasure and knowledge. He’ll drink deep from every source. After that? Unknown. He might become remarkable. Or not. No vision reveals the path.

Together, these six children hold more mental power, originality, and vigor than half a dozen ordinary families combined. Mr. Yorke knows this. He takes pride in them. Yorkshire breeds such families—racy, strong-blooded, proud, untamed. Lacking polish, yes, but true-hearted, bold, like eagles on cliffs or horses on steppes.

A soft tap sounds at the door. The boys have been noisy, little Jessy singing a sweet Scotch song—her father adores such music, and has taught her many. The outer ring went unnoticed.

“Come in,” Mrs. Yorke says, her voice solemn, always modulated to a funereal tone—no matter the subject. “Come in.”

Robert Moore enters.

Moore’s quiet demeanor and abstemious habits have earned him some favor with Mrs. Yorke. She hasn’t yet accused him of secret affairs or hidden motives—unlike most of her husband’s friends, whom she dismissed early in marriage. She’s not yet discovered his secret entanglement, nor that he may wear a sheep’s skin over a wolf’s heart.

“Well, is it you?” she asks, as he approaches and offers his hand. “Roving about at this hour? You should be at home.”

“Can a single man truly call a place home?” he replies.

“Pooh!” she answers, scornful of pretense. “Don’t play games with me. A man can have a home if he wants. Does your sister not make one for you?”

“No,” Mr. Yorke adds. “Hortense is honest, but when I was Robert’s age, I had five or six sisters—just as proper. Yet I still sought a wife.”

“And sorely regretted it,” Mrs. Yorke says, cracking a dry joke. “Repented in sackcloth and ashes. Look at him now—look at the punishment” (she gestures to the children). “Who would choose such a brood? Not just bringing them into the world—feeding, clothing, raising, settling them. Young sir, when you think of marrying, look at our four sons and two daughters. Think twice before you leap.”

“I’m not tempted now,” Moore says. “These are not times for marriage.”

Mrs. Yorke nods gravely. Such sentiments pleased her. But then she adds, “I don’t trust the wisdom of a Solomon your age. One fancy and it’s all undone. Sit down, sir. You can talk as well seated as standing.”

That was her way of inviting him to sit. As soon as he does, little Jessy leaps from her father’s knee and runs into Moore’s arms—held ready.

“You talk of marrying him,” she says to her mother, indignant, as she’s lifted onto his knee. “But he’s already married—or as good. He promised last summer, the first time he saw me in my new white frock and blue sash. Didn’t he, father?” (These children never said “papa” or “mamma”—their mother forbade such “namby-pamby” terms.)
