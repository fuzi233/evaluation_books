I was still reeling from the events of the day, my thoughts in turmoil and my nerves frayed, when my daughter Penelope appeared—just as her mother once did, always seeming to materialize at the worst possible moment. She came with that quiet insistence only a daughter can muster, demanding to know every detail of the meeting between Mr. Franklin Blake and myself. In such circumstances, there is but one course: silence, swift and decisive. I therefore told her that Mr. Franklin and I had spent our time discussing foreign affairs until we were both overcome by the heat and fell asleep under the sun. Try it yourself next time your wife or daughter corners you with an inconvenient question—trust in the natural grace of women; they’ll soon forgive and kiss away the lie.

The afternoon passed slowly. My lady and Miss Rachel returned, and their astonishment was immediate and profound upon learning that Mr. Franklin had arrived and then vanished again on horseback. Naturally, they questioned me—and this time, my flimsy tale about “foreign politics” and “falling asleep” would not hold water. With no better excuse at hand, I attributed his sudden arrival to one of Mr. Franklin’s well-known whims. When pressed whether his abrupt departure on horseback was another such whim, I nodded and said, “Yes, it was,” and slipped away—thinking myself rather clever for managing the escape.

But the troubles were far from over. Back in my room, Penelope returned—not with anger, but with that gentle sweetness women possess when they seek reconciliation. She kissed me, then, with the curiosity only daughters seem born with, asked about Rosanna Spearman, our second housemaid.

It seemed Rosanna had returned from the Shivering Sand in a most peculiar state. According to Penelope, she had gone through every emotion imaginable—laughing without cause, weeping without reason, asking endless questions about Mr. Franklin, then snapping at Penelope for daring to suppose the gentleman could interest her. One moment she was scribbling his name inside her workbox, the next staring at her deformed shoulder in the mirror, tears streaming down her face. She had been surprised, smiling, confused, angry—all within minutes. Had she known Mr. Franklin before? Impossible. Had they ever heard of each other? Still impossible.

Yet both I and Penelope could attest to the sincerity of their reactions—the man’s genuine shock at seeing her, and her own unmistakable fascination with him. The conversation became increasingly uncomfortable until Penelope, suddenly serious, broke the silence with a statement so outrageous I nearly choked on my own laughter.

“Father,” she said, “there’s only one explanation. Rosanna has fallen in love with Mr. Franklin Blake—at first sight.”

Now, you may have heard of beautiful young ladies falling in love at first glance—it’s romantic, even expected. But consider: a plain-faced girl, raised in a reformatory, with a twisted shoulder, smitten instantly by a stranger visiting her mistress’s home? That, I thought, surpassed absurdity. I laughed—loudly, uncontrollably—until tears ran down my cheeks.

Penelope did not laugh. Instead, she looked at me with quiet sorrow. “I never knew you cruel before, father,” she said softly, and left.

Her words struck me like ice water. I felt ashamed—ashamed of my mirth, ashamed of my lack of understanding. And yet, I couldn’t help but feel uneasy. We shall change the subject now, if you please. I regret mentioning it—though the reason will become clear soon enough.

That evening, the dinner bell rang, but Mr. Franklin had not yet returned from Frizinghall. I took his hot water up to his room myself, expecting news—some tidings of danger, perhaps, or discovery. To my disappointment, nothing had occurred. He had not encountered the Indians, either going or returning. He had safely deposited the Moonstone in the bank, describing it simply as a valuable object of great worth, and had secured the receipt in his pocket.

I descended the stairs, feeling the day’s excitement had ended in anticlimax. What transpired between Mr. Franklin and his aunt and cousin remains unknown to me. I would have given much to have served at table that night—but as the head servant, such a role was beneath my dignity, especially in my lady’s eyes, who already suspected me of lowering myself too often among the staff.

Later, news filtered down from above. Penelope mentioned that Miss Rachel had been unusually meticulous with her hair, and looked more radiant than ever as she descended to greet Mr. Franklin in the drawing-room. The footman, Samuel, added that serving Mr. Franklin while maintaining proper composure had tested his training more than any task he’d faced before.

By nightfall, music drifted out from the open windows: Mr. Franklin singing high, Miss Rachel higher still, and my lady accompanying them on the piano, guiding their voices like a shepherd leading sheep across rough ground—melodious, unexpected, and strangely comforting.

As midnight approached, I made my usual rounds with Samuel, locking the doors and securing the house. All was fastened except the side door opening onto the terrace. I sent Samuel to bed and stepped outside for a breath of air.

The night was still, the moon full and bright. The sea murmured faintly in the distance, its swell rolling gently onto the sandbar near our cove. The terrace side of the house lay in shadow, but the gravel path along the adjacent wall glittered under the moonlight.

Looking upward, I saw a figure’s shadow cast forward from behind the corner of the house. Old and cautious, I refrained from shouting. But old and heavy-footed too, my steps crunched loudly on the gravel.

Before I could move silently around the corner, I heard lighter feet—more than one pair—retreating hastily. By the time I reached the spot, the intruders had vanished into the shrubbery on the far side of the walk, hidden among thick trees and bushes. From there, they could easily cross our fence and disappear into the road.

Had I been forty years younger, I might have pursued them. As it was, I returned to summon Samuel. Without waking anyone, we fetched two guns and searched the entire property—house, garden, shrubbery—finding no trace of anyone.

Returning past the gravel path, I noticed something small and shiny lying in the moonlight. I picked it up—a tiny bottle filled with a thick, black liquid, sweet-smelling and viscous. I said nothing to Samuel.

But then I remembered what Penelope had told me about the jugglers—the boy whose palm was filled with ink. A cold certainty settled over me. I had disturbed the three Indians. They had come back—not to steal, but to search—for the Diamond. And now, I had found their clue.
