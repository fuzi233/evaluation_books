"As is the race of leaves, so is that of men." In the quiet, unassuming shadow of a tailor's workshop in Rotterdam, a great soul budded unnoticed this year, while simultaneously, a colossal figure crashed to earth with deafening fanfare. At Bruges, Philip, Duke of Burgundy, Earl of Holland, and myriad other titles, lay wasting away in fever. Here, nature allowed the poor to fall ill and recover with equal indifference; but woe betide the wealthy in an era where a single Mr. Malady could decimate three, while Dr. Remedy struggled to save one. The Duke's affliction, unnamed in those days, is now known as diphtheria—a cruel, weakening scourge that claimed the old man. Consequently, Dr. Remedy resorted to the ancient, bloody art of leeching. The Duke turned icy cold, a testament to the failure of mercy. Desperate, the physician turned to the arcane sciences of his time. "Ho! This is grave," he cried, demanding, "Flay an ape incontinent and clamp it to the Duke's breast!" State officers scrambled in every direction, seeking a primate to counteract the bloodthirsty folly of their own kind. Perdition! No apes remained. Buffaloes, lizards, Turks, and leopards roamed free, but none were the requisite beast. "Why, there used to be an ape about," one officer muttered. "If I stood here, I saw him." Indeed, there had been; but a mastiff, driven by hunger, had mauled the sprightly creature for stealing its supper, fulfilling the grim human precept: *Be of your season*. In this emergency, the seneschal cast his despairing eyes around the room, and hope flickered. "Here is this," he whispered, holding up a specimen. "Surely this will serve: 'tis altogether apelike, doublet and hose apart." "Nay," snapped the Chancellor peevishly, "the Princess Marie would hang us. She dotes on this." Thus spoke our friend Giles, strutting unconsciously in cloth of gold, oblivious to the gravity of the moment. Dr. Remedy, growing impatient with the bureaucracy of life and death, commanded, "Flay a dog." "A dog is next best to an ape," he reasoned, adding the condition, "only it must be a dog of one color." They skinned a liver-colored hound and pinned its still-palpating heart to the sovereign's chest. The Duke died. Philip the Good, thus scientifically disposed of, left behind thirty-one children; of these, one, by some miracle of lineage, was legitimate and ascended to rule. The benevolent duke had provided for nineteen of the others; the remainder were forced to shift for themselves. According to the Flemish chronicles, the deceased prince descended from the kings of Troy through Thierry of Aquitaine, Chilperic, Pharamond, and the ancient monarchs of Franconia. Yet, this genealogy held no true distinction. Not a single prince of his day can be discovered who does not claim descent from Troy. "Priam" was merely medieval code for "Adam." The Duke's body was carried into Burgundy and interred in a noble mausoleum of black marble at Dijon. Holland rang with mourning for his passing, little dreaming that within its borders, something as famous as a new sun was being born that same year. That judgment has since been utterly reversed. All eyes now turn to the tailor's house, the site of the great birth of the fifteenth century. As for the house where the good duke breathed his last, "no one knows and no one cares," as the song goes. Why? For Dukes like Philip the Good come and go, leaving mankind neither a halfpenny wiser, nor better, nor different than they found it. But when, once in three hundred years, such a child is born to the world as Margaret's son, behold! A human torch ignited by fire from heaven; and "FIAT LUX"—Let there be Light—thunders from pole to pole.
