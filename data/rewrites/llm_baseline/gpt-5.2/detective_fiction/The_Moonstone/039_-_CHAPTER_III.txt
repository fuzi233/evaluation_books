My remembrance of what passed at Hotherstone’s Farm is no better than a taste lingering after a meal—strong in one instant, gone the next, leaving only impressions that the senses can swear to, while the mind cannot marshal the order of them.

I recall a welcome as hearty as a kitchen fire newly stirred; a supper of such prodigality that it might have victualled a parish—cold roast and hot, bread with a crust that spoke of honest flour, butter with the clean sweet smell of churn-work, and the sharp comfort of pickled things that bite the tongue awake. I recall, too, the farmhouse quiet—boards that creaked with age instead of machinery, a draught sighing under the door, and, underneath all, the living sound of the place: distant cattle shifting in their stalls, a bucket set down, a hinge complaining once and then being still. They were country noises—each one with its proper interval of silence—and they rested the ear like a familiar dialect rests the heart.

In my bedroom there was cleanliness enough to shame London lodging-houses. Only one article in it awakened my indignation: a feather-bed. That soft, smothering contrivance—made, I suppose, in the days when our forefathers mistook suffocation for comfort—kept me waking, striking match after match and nursing the little candle as if it were a witness I dared not lose. When daylight came, and with it the first bird-notes outside—small, clear syllables in the morning air—I felt a bodily relief at the mere prospect of rising, as if my lungs had been given back to me.

Over-night I had arranged with Betteredge that I should call for him, on our way to Cobb’s Hole, as early as I chose. Interpreted by my impatience to possess Rosanna Spearman’s letter, “as early as I chose” meant as early as I could. I would not wait for breakfast at the Farm; I took a crust in my hand—dry, with the faint nutty scent of yesterday’s baking—and set out, half fearing I might surprise the excellent Betteredge still among his blankets.

To my relief he was ready before me, and waiting with his stick.

“How are you this morning, Betteredge?”

“Very poorly, sir.”

“Sorry to hear it. What do you complain of?”

“I complain of a new disease, Mr. Franklin, of my own inventing. I don’t want to alarm you, but you’re certain to catch it before the morning is out.”

“The devil I am!”

“Do you feel an uncomfortable heat at the pit of your stomach, sir? and a nasty thumping at the top of your head? Ah! not yet? It will lay hold of you at Cobb’s Hole, Mr. Franklin. I call it the detective-fever; and I first caught it in the company of Sergeant Cuff.”

“Aye, aye! and the cure in this instance is to open Rosanna Spearman’s letter, I suppose? Come along, and let’s get it.”

Even at that early hour we found the fisherman’s wife astir in her kitchen. There were the domestic smells of a life that begins with the hands: damp wood warming near the hearth; yesterday’s fish-boards rinsed clean; tea-leaves newly scalded; and, under all, the salt breath that crept in with every opening of the door, bringing the sea into the house as surely as if it were a member of the family.

On Betteredge presenting me, good Mrs. Yolland performed a ceremony I learned afterwards to be reserved for strangers of distinction. She set a bottle of Dutch gin and a couple of clean pipes upon the table, and began, with an amplitude of inquiry that might have covered the whole kingdom:

“What news from London, sir?”

Before I could find an answer to so comprehensive a question, something moved—no, not something: an apparition advanced out of a dark corner of the kitchen.

A wan, wild, haggard girl, with remarkably beautiful hair and a fierce keenness in her eyes, came limping on a crutch to the table. She fixed her gaze on me with an intensity that made me feel less a man than an object set down for inspection—an object that roused in her at once curiosity and disgust, and fascinated her in spite of both.

“Mr. Betteredge,” she said, never taking her eyes from my face, “mention his name again, if you please.”

“This gentleman’s name,” answered Betteredge—laying a stress on the word gentleman as if it were a weapon—“is Mr. Franklin Blake.”

She turned her back on me and left the room, as suddenly as she had come.

Mrs. Yolland, I believe, offered apologies for her daughter’s odd behaviour; Betteredge, no doubt, translated them into the politeness of his own vocabulary. I cannot answer for either; my attention had been caught by the sound of the crutch, as a listener’s ear is caught by a wrong note in a familiar tune.

Thump—thump, up the wooden stairs. Thump—thump, across the room above our heads. Thump—thump, down again. And then the girl stood in the open doorway with a letter in her hand, beckoning me out.

I left more apologies in the middle of their course than I care to remember, and followed her—limping on before me faster and faster—down the slope of the beach. The village behind us made its small morning music: a gull’s harsh cry, a net being shaken, a child calling, the low talk of fishermen. But she led me behind some boats, out of sight and hearing, to where the shore lay bare and private, and there she stopped and faced me for the first time.

“Stand there,” she said. “I want to look at you.”

There was no mistaking her expression. She regarded me with abhorrence so plain that it needed no interpreter. It is a hard thing for any man to be examined as if he were foul; harder still to endure it in silence.

To shift the subject—like a child trying to escape a scolding—I ventured:

“I think you have got a letter to give me. Is it the letter there, in your hand?”

“Say that again,” was the only answer.

I repeated my words.

“No,” she said to herself, rather than to me, still holding me in the merciless net of her eyes. “I can’t find out what she saw in his face. I can’t guess what she heard in his voice.”

Then, as if a weight had pressed her down, she looked away and rested her head wearily on the top of her crutch.

“Oh, my poor dear,” she said—softly now, in the first gentle tones I had heard from her. “Oh, my lost darling! what could you see in this man?”

In an instant she flung up her head again and looked at me with returning fierceness.

“Can you eat and drink?” she asked.

I did my best to keep my gravity. “Yes.”

“Can you sleep?”

“Yes.”

“When you see a poor girl in service, do you feel no remorse?”

“Certainly not. Why should I?”

She thrust the letter into my face, as if it were something that burned her fingers.

“Take it!” she cried. “I never set eyes on you before. God Almighty forbid I should ever set eyes on you again.”

With that she limped away at the top of her speed, leaving me alone with the sea-wind and my bewilderment.

The interpretation that rose first in my mind will have risen, no doubt, in every other. I could only suppose her to be mad. And having arrived at that convenient conclusion, I turned to the more interesting object in my hand.

The address ran:

“For Franklin Blake, Esq. To be given into his own hands (and not to be trusted to anyone else), by Lucy Yolland.”

I broke the seal. The envelope contained a letter, and the letter contained a slip of paper. I read the letter first:

“Sir,—If you are curious to know the meaning of my behaviour to you, whilst you were staying in the house of my mistress, Lady Verinder, do what you are told to do in the memorandum enclosed with this—and do it without any person being present to overlook you.

“Your humble servant,
“ROSANNA SPEARMAN.”

I turned to the slip of paper. It was headed “Memorandum,” and I copy it word for word:

“To go to the Shivering Sand at the turn of the tide. To walk out on the South Spit, until I get the South Spit Beacon, and the flagstaff at the Coast-guard station above Cobb’s Hole in a line together. To lay down on the rocks, a stick, or any straight thing to guide my hand, exactly in the line of the beacon and the flagstaff. To take care, in doing this, that one end of the stick shall be at the edge of the rocks, on the side of them which overlooks the quicksand. To feel along the stick, among the seaweed (beginning from the end of the stick which points towards the beacon), for the Chain. To run my hand along the Chain, when found, until I come to the part of it which stretches over the edge of the rocks, down into the quicksand. And then, to pull the chain.”

The last words were underlined in the original.

As I finished them, Betteredge’s voice rose behind me—breathless with the very disease he had named.

“I can’t stand it any longer, Mr. Franklin. What does her letter say? For mercy’s sake, sir, tell us, what does her letter say?”

I handed him the letter and memorandum. He read the letter with little show of interest; but the memorandum worked on him at once, as a scent works on a hound.

“The Sergeant said it!” he cried. “From first to last, sir, the Sergeant said she had got a memorandum of the hiding-place. And here it is! Lord save us, Mr. Franklin, here is the secret that puzzled everybody, from the great Cuff downwards, ready and waiting, as one may say, to show itself to you! It’s the ebb now, sir, as anybody may see for themselves. How long will it be till the turn of the tide?”

He looked up and hailed a lad at some distance mending a net.

“Tammie Bright!”

“I hear you!”

“When’s the turn of the tide?”

“In an hour’s time.”

We both looked at our watches.

“We can go round by the coast, Mr. Franklin,” said Betteredge, “and get to the quicksand in that way with plenty of time to spare. What do you say, sir?”

“Come along!”

As we walked, I made Betteredge revive my memory of the circumstances connected with Rosanna Spearman at the time of Sergeant Cuff’s inquiry. The sequence came back, linked like the very chain we were going to seek: Rosanna’s journey to Frizinghall, when the whole household believed her ill in her room; her locked door at night, her candle kept burning till morning; her suspicious purchase from Mrs. Yolland of the japanned tin case and the two dog’s chains; the Sergeant’s conviction that she had hidden something at the Shivering Sand, and his complete ignorance of what that something might be.

The whole story, with its domestic facts and its secret hours, returned as plainly as if it had been spoken in my ear, when we reached the place and walked out together on the low ledge of rocks called the South Spit.

With Betteredge’s help I got myself into the right position to see the beacon and the coast-guard flagstaff exactly in a line. We laid my stick on the uneven rock, as neatly as we could, following the memorandum’s direction.

Then we looked at our watches again. There were nearly twenty minutes yet before the turn of the tide.

I proposed we should wait on the beach rather than on wet rocks slick with weed. When we reached the dry sand, I prepared to sit down—and, to my surprise, Betteredge prepared to leave me.

“What are you going away for?” I asked.

“Look at the letter again, sir, and you will see.”

A glance reminded me I was charged to make the discovery alone.

“It’s hard enough for me to leave you, at such a time as this,” said Betteredge. “But she died a dreadful death, poor soul—and I feel a kind of call on me, Mr. Franklin, to humour that fancy of hers. Besides,” he added, lowering his voice, “there’s nothing in the letter against your letting out the secret afterwards. I’ll hang about in the fir-plantation, and wait till you pick me up. Don’t be longer than you can help, sir. The detective-fever isn’t an easy disease to deal with, under these circumstances.”

He went.

The waiting—short by the clock—lengthened by the mind, until each minute felt like a separate trial. The only comfort I had, and it was no small one, was the habit of smoking. I lit a cigar and sat on the slope of the beach.

The morning had the clean brilliance of a well-scoured pan. Sunlight poured over sea and sand; the air, sharp with salt and weed, made the mere act of breathing feel like nourishment. The bay, lonely though it was, carried its own order of sounds: the small lick of water at stones, the wind threading the rigging of distant boats, the gulls’ cries—wild, untaught phrases belonging to no human grammar. There was no machinery here to grind one note without mercy; each sound appeared, spoke its piece, and fell silent again, leaving space for thought.

Even the quicksand, bare at low water, glittered with a false golden brightness, as if it could smile. And yet, as I watched, it betrayed itself.

The turn of the tide came before my cigar was finished. I saw first the preliminary heaving, and then the awful shiver that ran over the surface—like a living thing struck by fear. It was as if some spirit moved below, and the sand could not keep still over it. I threw away my cigar and went back to the rocks.

I followed Rosanna’s directions. Beginning at the end of the stick nearest the beacon, I felt along its line among the weed, touching rock after rock. More than half-way, nothing. Then, in a narrow fissure within reach of my forefinger, I felt metal—cold, harsh, unmistakable. The chain.

Trying to follow it by touch towards the quicksand, I found my hand stopped by a thick growth of seaweed fastened into the crevice—no doubt tightened in the time since Rosanna chose her hiding-place. I could neither pull up the weed nor force my hand through it.

Marking the spot where the stick’s end lay nearest the sand, I changed my plan. I would “sound” immediately under the rock, on the chance of striking the chain where it dipped down into the quicksand.

I took up the stick and knelt at the brink.

In that position my face was within a few feet of the quicksand’s surface. Nearness made it more hateful. Now and again the shivering fit ran through it, and the sight of the thing so close shook my nerves. A fancy seized me—horrible and absurd—that the dead woman might appear here to aid my search; that she might rise through the heaving sand and point. The notion chilled me in the warm sunlight. I own I shut my eyes at the moment the stick’s point entered the quicksand.

The next instant, before the stick could have sunk more than inches, terror fell away—and excitement took me all at once, like a hand tightening on the throat.

At my first blind attempt I had sounded right. The stick struck the chain.

Gripping the roots of the seaweed with my left hand, I stretched myself over the brink and felt with my right under the overhanging rock. My fingers found the chain. I drew it up without difficulty, and there, fastened to the end, was the japanned tin case.

The sea had rusted the chain so thoroughly that I could not unfasten it from the hasp. I braced the case between my knees and, with all my strength, forced off the cover.

A white substance filled the interior. I put in my hand: linen. As I drew it out, a crumpled letter came with it. I looked at the direction, saw my name, and put it at once in my pocket.

Then I pulled out the linen completely. It came in a thick roll, moulded to the shape of the case and preserved by its close confinement. I carried it to the dry sand and unrolled it, smoothing it out with my hands.

It was an article of dress. A nightgown.

On the upper side there were only folds and creases. I turned it. And there—instantly—was the smear of paint from the door of Rachel’s boudoir.

My eyes held to that stain as if it were a word in a language I had once spoken and forgotten; and my mind leapt back, from the brightness of the beach to that dark time of suspicion and inquiry. Sergeant Cuff’s very voice came to me, repeating his conclusion as though he stood at my shoulder.

“Find out whether there is any article of dress in this house with the stain of paint on it. Find out who that dress belongs to. Find out how the person can account for having been in the room, and smeared the paint between midnight and three in the morning. If the person can’t satisfy you, you haven’t far to look for the hand that took the Diamond.”

The words travelled over my memory again and again with a mechanical persistence, like a refrain one cannot stop hearing.

A voice broke in upon it, calling to me. I looked up. Betteredge, his patience conquered, was visible between the sandhills returning to the beach.

The sight of him brought me back sharply to the present and to the one remaining question. I had found the stain. To whom did the nightgown belong?

My first impulse was to take the letter from my pocket—the letter from the case. But a shorter way occurred to me. The nightgown itself would tell its owner, if it were marked.

I lifted it and searched for the mark.

I found it.

And I read—MY OWN NAME.

There were the familiar letters; there was the sun; there were the waters glittering; there was Betteredge coming nearer, step by step. I looked down again.

My own name—plainly confronting me. I had left London saying, “If time, pains, and money can do it, I will lay my hand on the thief who took the Moonstone.” I had penetrated the secret the quicksand had kept from every other living creature. And now, on the unanswerable evidence of the paint-stain, I had discovered myself as the thief.
