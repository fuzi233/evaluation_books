Sunlight stabbed through my window like an overeager houseguest, announcing that the day had begun without consulting me. I sat up, squinting at the empty space where my watch usually resided on the wobbly dressing table. My clothes lay scattered across the floor like casualties of war. One boot had claimed the chair by the fireplace as its throne; the other had vanished entirely, possibly staging a rebellion.

My head, meanwhile, had apparently expanded overnight to twice its usual size. For a delirious moment, I wondered if I’d accidentally swapped skulls with Minikin. If so, I wanted mine back. This borrowed model ached like it had been used as a bowling ball.

Then last night’s memories crashed into me like a drunken uncle at a wedding. Church bells chimed nearby. I counted carefully. Eleven. I leapt from bed—or rather, I attempted to. My legs had other plans. I sat down hard on the floor.

The room, which I dimly recalled waltzing with me in a spirited but uncoordinated manner last night, was still swaying gently, as if recovering from its own hangover. I crawled to the washstand, missed the basin twice, and finally dunked my head in like a man trying to drown his regrets.

Clutching the easy-chair like a lifeline, I took stock of my situation, working backward from the present like a detective who already knows he’s the culprit.

**Fact One:** I felt like death warmed over.
**Fact Two:** Something had disagreed with me violently.

*Ah yes,* I thought, *that cigar. Clearly the culprit. And the stuffy room. And overwork. Definitely not the three bottles of champagne. No, no—it was the cigar.*

Then my own brain, unsympathetic and relentless, cut through the delusion: *You were drunk, you idiot.*

I winced. *Not drunk. That’s such a crude word. Some people can’t tolerate sweet champagne. It’s a liver thing.*

*Hopelessly, vulgarly drunk,* my conscience shot back. *Like an ‘Arry on Bank Holiday.*

*It was my first time!* I protested weakly.

*And your first opportunity,* came the merciless reply. *How old are you?*

*Old enough to know better. What if this is the start of a downward spiral? What if you end up a confirmed sot, staggering home to frighten small children?*

Temperance pamphlets I’d skimmed on dull Sundays flashed before my eyes. I saw my future self—haggard, red-nosed, begging coins outside pubs. My father’s disappointment, my mother’s tears. Barbara’s scorn. Hal’s withering contempt. Shame and terror gripped me.

*Never again!* I vowed aloud, with the dramatic fervor only a repentant teenager can muster. *I’ll leave this place! Wipe the slate clean! Start anew! I’ll—*

Then, like a specter at the feast, Miss Rosina Sellars materialized in my mind’s eye.

*What’s she doing here?* I demanded of myself.

*Your affianced bride,* my brain reminded me with a smirk.

*Exactly. And now you’re stuck with her. Two cramped rooms. Her family. A lifetime of listening to her drop every ‘h’ this side of the Thames. How’s that for a bright future, Mr. Ambition?*

I groaned. *What do I do?*

*No idea,* my inner voice admitted cheerfully.

---

The day passed in a haze of self-loathing. Too ashamed to face Mrs. Peedles, I barricaded myself in my room. At dusk, I slunk out for a penitent meal of unsweetened tea and a kippered herring at a nearby coffee-house. When another patron ordered buttered toast, the smell alone sent me fleeing.

At eight o’clock, Minikin arrived, took one look at me, and said, *Thought as much. Looks like you had an A1 night of it.*

*I’m glad it happened,* I lied. *A lesson I’ll never forget.*

*Nothing like a proper drunk for making you feel virtuous,* Minikin agreed. *Better than a sermon.*

Desperate for advice, I confessed the full extent of my folly.

*Engaged?* Minikin blinked. *You quiet ones go hard when you do go. Nice girl?*

Minikin polished his glass eye thoughtfully. *Artful type?*

Minikin glanced around. *Must be fond of you.*

I nodded miserably.

*Let her wait,* Minikin advised. *Plenty of time for disaster later.*

Just then, Jarman barged in without knocking. He shook my hand solemnly. *Friend of yours?* he asked, eyeing Minikin.

After introductions:

*Proud to meet you,* said Jarman.

*Glad to hear it,* said Minikin. *Don’t look like you’ve got much else to be proud about.*

Jarman, instead of taking offense, grinned. *Wonderful gift, that. Ever think of hiring yourself out to ruin parties?*

Thus began a lengthy debate between the two on how to extract me from my predicament, treating me as though I were an inanimate object with poor judgment.

*She’s a hundred and sixty pounds of determination,* Jarman explained. *Bit much for our Paul here.*

*Some chaps have all the luck,* Minikin deadpanned.

After much deliberation, Jarman slapped his knee. *The stage!*

*Can do anything that doesn’t require sense,* Jarman declared. *No questions, no character needed. Sanctuary!*

*Sing something,* he demanded.

I croaked out *Sally in Our Alley,* every mention of the name conjuring Miss Sellars’ formidable silhouette.

*Not grand opera material,* Jarman mused, *but you’ll do for a chorus where cheap is the main requirement.*

Their plan: I would appeal to the O’Kelly for help breaking into theater. *Lose the girl in the footlights,* Jarman advised.

---

The next evening, alone in my room, gloom set in. The empty parlor below yawned like a tomb. Even the scratch of my neighbor’s pen sounded accusatory.

Then—a knock. A pink, scented note.

*Dr mr. Paul—I herd you was took hill. You must not work so hard or I shall be cros. Come for a wark if you are good. With much love. ROSIE.*

Against all reason, my pulse quickened. My first love letter. For a fleeting moment, Miss Sellars’ face softened in my mind’s eye, her arms outstretched.

I dressed in a fever, then hesitated at her door.

*Come in, dear!* she trilled.

Inside, the illusion shattered. Powder dusted her cheeks like flour on a loaf. Her dress crinkled like gift wrap. A wart on her hand held my horrified gaze.

*You’re a naughty boy,* she scolded. *Why didn’t you visit me?*

She offered her cheek. I pecked it, tasting stale pastry.

As we walked, she narrated her entire life story—her *refined* instincts, her *shameful* lineage (a waiter father!), her determination to marry *a gentleman* (me, apparently).

*I could never abide anything common,* she declared, while I prayed for a tram to intervene.

By the time we reached Newington Butts, I knew two things:

1. I was trapped.
2. The stage was my only hope.
