Between the silver thread of dawn and the emerald shimmer of the sea, a boat drifted to Harwich, releasing a cloud of humanity like a swarming insect colony. Amidst this throng, the figure we must track remained unremarkable—indeed, he wished to remain so. To the casual observer, he presented a curious juxtaposition: the festive, holiday cheer of his attire clashed violently with the grim, official gravity etched onto his countenance. His garments comprised a pale grey jacket, a stark white waistcoat, and a silver straw hat adorned with a slate-blue ribbon. His lean visage was dark, framed by a short, black beard that evoked a Spanish monk or an Elizabethan ruffian. Yet, as he smoked a cigarette with the heavy deliberation of a man who had nothing to lose, there was no outward sign that the grey jacket concealed a loaded revolver, nor that the white vest hid a police warrant, nor that the straw hat sheltered one of the sharpest minds in Europe. This was Aristide Valentin, the head of the Paris Police and the world's preeminent investigator, arriving from Brussels to London for the capture of the century.

His quarry, Flambeau, was already in England. The combined forces of three nations' police services had finally narrowed the trail of the great criminal from Ghent to Brussels, then to the Hook of Holland. Rumors suggested Flambeau intended to exploit the confusion surrounding the Eucharistic Congress currently convening in London. Most likely, he would pose as a minor clerk or secretary; yet, Valentin knew better than to rely on probability. Nobody could truly predict the moves of Flambeau.

It had been many years since this colossus of crime had ceased his reign of terror, a period marked by a profound global quietude following the death of Roland. In his prime, Flambeau was a figure as statuesque and international as the Kaiser himself. Daily newspapers chronicled his escapes, often noting that he committed a fresh, extraordinary crime immediately after avoiding the consequences of the previous one. Of gigantic stature and terrifying physical daring, Flambeau was a Gascon legend. Tales circulated of his athletic humor—toppling judges upside down "to clear his mind" or sprinting down the Rue de Rivoli with a policeman cradled under each arm. While his physical feats were often theatrical, his true crimes were those of ingenious, wholesale robbery. Each theft was a unique sin, a story unto itself. From running the phantom Tyrolean Dairy Company in London, serving thousands of subscribers with no cows or milk, to maintaining a secret correspondence with a young lady by photographing his letters onto microscopic microscope slides, Flambeau's methods were a study in sweeping simplicity. He had once repainted street numbers in the dead of night merely to lure a traveler into a trap, and invented portable pillar-boxes in quiet suburbs to catch unsuspecting strangers. Furthermore, despite his massive bulk, he possessed the agility of an acrobat, leaping like a grasshopper and vanishing into tree tops like a monkey. Valentin, therefore, knew that finding Flambeau was merely the beginning of a perilous adventure.

But how, then, was Valentin to locate him? His strategy was still forming. There was one trait Flambeau could not disguise: his exceptional height. Had Valentin spotted a towering apple seller, a grenadier, or even a tall duchess, the arrest would have been immediate. Yet, scanning the passengers on his train, Valentin found no candidate resembling a disguised Flambeau; there was no one who could pass for him any more than a cat could masquerade as a giraffe. Among those he had encountered, only six individuals warranted scrutiny. There was a short railway official, three market gardeners of moderate height, a very short widow from Essex, and finally, a diminutive Roman Catholic priest also traveling from Essex. Upon examining this last individual, Valentin almost chuckled. The priest was the epitome of rural flatness, possessing a face as round and dull as a Norfolk dumpling and eyes as vacant as the North Sea. He carried several brown paper parcels, seemingly unable to manage them, and struggled with his large, shabby umbrella, which constantly slipped from his grasp. He displayed a childlike confusion regarding his return ticket and explained to every passenger in the carriage that he had to protect his parcels, containing items made of "real silver with blue stones." This peculiar blend of provincial naivety and saintly simplicity amused the Frenchman endlessly. Eventually, the priest arrived at Tottenham with his parcels intact, only to realize he had forgotten his umbrella. Valentin, displaying unexpected kindness, gently advised him not to advertise the silver contents to others. However, beneath this surface amusement, Valentin kept a vigilant eye, searching relentlessly for anyone of significant height, for Flambeau stood four inches above six feet.

Valentin alighted at Liverpool Street, convinced he had not yet missed his prey. He proceeded to Scotland Yard to formalize his position and coordinate assistance, then lit another cigarette and wandered the streets of London. Strolling past Victoria, he paused in a quaint, quiet square that seemed frozen in accidental stillness. The surrounding flat houses appeared simultaneously prosperous and uninhabited, while a central shrubbery looked as deserted as a green islet in the Pacific. One side of the square rose significantly higher than the rest, broken by a restaurant that seemed to have wandered from Soho. It was an unreasonably attractive establishment, featuring dwarf potted plants and long, striped blinds of lemon-yellow and white. Perched unusually high above the street, a flight of steps ascended directly to its front door, resembling a makeshift fire escape. Valentin stood before the yellow-and-white blinds, smoking and contemplating them for a long time.

"The most miraculous aspect of miracles is that they occur," Valentin mused, observing the quirks of fate. "Clouds coalesce into the shape of an eye; trees stand in the landscape of a doubtful journey like a question mark. Nelson dies at the moment of victory; a man named Williams accidentally murders a man named Williamson—it sounds like a kind of infanticide. Life possesses an element of elfin coincidence that those calculating on the prosaic invariably miss. As Edgar Allan Poe wisely noted, wisdom must account for the unforeseen."

Aristide Valentin was profoundly French, and the French intelligence was singular in its nature. He was not a "thinking machine"—a term belonging to the materialists—but a thinking man, a plain man nonetheless. All his successful sleights, appearing like magic tricks, were achieved through plodding logic and clear, commonplace French thought. The French electrify the world not by inventing paradoxes, but by executing truisms to their absolute limit. Because Valentin understood reason, he understood its boundaries. Lacking strong first principles to guide him, as Flambeau had been missed at Harwich, Valentin adopted a unique methodology. Where the reasonable path offered no clues, he followed the unreasonable. Instead of visiting banks or police stations, he systematically visited the wrong places, knocking on empty houses, turning down dead ends, and traversing lanes choked with refuse. He defended this erratic course with cold logic: if one possessed a clue, the direct route was best; but with no clue at all, the indirect path was superior, for any oddity catching the eye of the pursuer might be the same anomaly that had captured the gaze of the pursued.

Something about the flight of steps leading to the restaurant, coupled with its quiet, quirky atmosphere, ignited Valentin's romantic imagination. He resolved to strike at random. Ascending the steps, he sat at a window table and ordered a cup of black coffee. It was mid-morning, and he had not eaten; the remnants of other breakfasts littered the table, reminding him of his hunger. Adding a poached egg to his order, he musings shook sugar into his coffee, his thoughts drifting inevitably to Flambeau. He recalled Flambeau's past escapes: by a pair of nail scissors, by a burning house, by unpaid postage stamps, and by directing a crowd to stare at a comet believed to destroy the world. Valentin considered his detective intellect equal to the criminal's, yet he recognized his disadvantage. "The criminal is the creative artist; the detective is merely the critic," he murmured with a sour smile. He lifted his coffee cup to his lips slowly, then lowered it abruptly. He had put salt into the coffee.

Staring at the vessel from which the silvery powder had spilled, Valentin realized it was undoubtedly a sugar bowl, designed with the same intent as a champagne bottle for champagne. He wondered why salt had been dispensed there. Glancing around for orthodox containers, he found two salt cellars completely full. Curiosity piqued, he tasted the contents of the sugar bowl; it was indeed sugar. Refreshed by this discovery, he surveyed the restaurant with renewed interest, searching for other traces of this singular, artistic reversal of customs. Aside from a dark stain on a white-painted wall, the establishment appeared neat and ordinary. Ringing the bell, he summoned the waiter.

When the official hurried over, his fuzzy hair and bleary eyes suggesting fatigue, Valentin, appreciative of simple humor, asked him to taste the sugar and assess whether it matched the hotel's high reputation. The result was immediate: the waiter yawned, then instantly awoke, alert and confused.

"Do you play this delicate joke on your customers every morning?" Valentin inquired. "Does swapping the salt and sugar never tire of the jest?"

The waiter, realizing the gravity of the situation, stammered assurances that no such intention existed; it must be a bizarre mistake. He examined the sugar bowl, then the salt cellar, his expression growing increasingly bewildered. Finally, he abruptly excused himself and returned seconds later with the proprietor.
