Edward remained at the cottage for seven days, a duration that felt agonizingly brief given his evident reluctance to depart. Mrs. Dashwood pressed him daily to extend his stay, yet he moved with the grim determination of a soldier accepting a transfer to a hostile front, seemingly resolved to leave precisely when his morale among his hosts peaked. For the final forty-eight hours, his spirits, though volatile, showed signs of stabilization; he grew increasingly attached to the cottage and its grounds, sighing heavily at the mere suggestion of departure, claiming his schedule was entirely free, and expressing genuine confusion regarding his destination once he vacated their presence. Despite this professed uncertainty, his exit was non-negotiable. No week had evaporated so rapidly; he insisted repeatedly on its fleeting nature, uttering sentiments that betrayed a profound emotional shift while his actions remained stubbornly fixed on departure. He harbored no fondness for Norland and despised the prospect of returning to London, yet he was trapped in a binary orbit between the two, compelled to choose one over the other regardless of his personal cost. He valued their kindness above all earthly rewards, finding his greatest solace in their proximity, yet he was destined to sever those ties within a week, defying their pleas and his own inclinations, stripped of any temporal constraints.

Elinor attributed the inexplicable rigidity of Edward's behavior to his mother's influence, a convenient scapegoat whose character remained sufficiently obscure to explain any eccentricity in her son's conduct. Though disappointed and occasionally irritated by his inconsistent demeanor toward her, she maintained a charitable disposition, readily granting him the leniency and understanding that had previously been wrung from her with difficulty in service to Willoughby. His lack of vitality, transparency, and consistency was dismissed as symptoms of his compromised independence and his acute awareness of Mrs. Ferrars' manipulative designs. The brevity of his visit and the steely resolve with which he planned his departure stemmed from the same shackles: a fettered inclination and an unavoidable necessity to appease his mother. The ancient, grinding conflict between filial duty and personal will, pitting parent against child, was the root cause of all his distress. She yearned desperately to know when this struggle would conclude, when Mrs. Ferrars would reform and her son would finally possess the liberty to pursue happiness. Denied such answers, she sought refuge in renewing her faith in Edward's enduring affection, recalling every gesture of regard he had offered during his stay at Barton, and particularly the constant, tangible token he wore upon his finger—a flattering testament to his devotion.

"I believe, Edward," Mrs. Dashwood remarked over breakfast on the final morning, "that you would derive greater happiness from adopting a profession to occupy your time and lend substance to your ambitions. While this might impose certain inconveniences on your friends, limiting the hours you can dedicate to them, you would gain a significant advantage in at least one regard: you would know precisely where to go when you leave us."

"I assure you," Edward replied, "that I have contemplated this very issue with the frequency you suggest. It remains, and likely will forever remain, a crushing misfortune that I possess no obligatory duties, no profession to command my attention or grant me a semblance of autonomy. Unfortunately, my own fastidiousness, compounded by the expectations of my family, has rendered me what I am: an idle, helpless entity. We could never reconcile our choices regarding a career path. I consistently favored the clergy, a preference I hold to this day. Yet, it lacked the requisite flash for my family's tastes. They advocated for the military, a path far too ostentatious for my constitution. The legal profession was deemed sufficiently genteel; numerous young men residing in the Temple cut a fine figure in high society, traversing the city in sophisticated carriages. However, I possessed no aptitude for the law, even in its less abstract applications, which my family endorsed. Regarding the navy, it certainly enjoyed fashionable cachet, but I was already too advanced in age when the discussion initially arose. Ultimately, since there was no pressing necessity for me to acquire a profession—I could be equally dashing and extravagant without a uniform as with one—idleness was declared the most advantageous and honorable course. A young man of eighteen is rarely so driven by the desire for occupation as to resist the solicitations of his friends to accomplish nothing. Consequently, I was enrolled at Oxford and have remained properly idle ever since."

"The logical conclusion of which," Mrs. Dashwood countered, "is that since leisure has failed to cultivate your own contentment, your children will be raised to pursue as myriad careers, employments, and trades as Columella's catalog suggests."

"They shall be raised," he stated with a somber gravity, "to be as dissimilar to myself as humanly possible. In sentiment, in action, in circumstance, in every conceivable aspect."

"Enough, enough," interjected Mrs. Dashwood, dismissing his gloom. "This is merely an overflow of immediate despondency, Edward. You are in a melancholic frame of mind and assume that anyone unlike yourself must necessarily be miserable. Remember that the agony of separation from friends is universal, transcending education and station. Recognize your own source of happiness. You require nothing but patience—or, if you prefer a more seductive term, call it hope. Your mother will secure for you, in due course, the independence you crave; it is her duty, and it shall inevitably become her joy to ensure your entire youth is not squandered in discontent. Who knows what a few months might achieve?"

"I suspect," Edward replied flatly, "that I may challenge many months to produce any positive outcome for me."

This despondent turn of mind, though concealed from Mrs. Dashwood, cast a pall over the impending farewell, leaving an unsettling residue on Elinor's psyche that demanded considerable effort and time to suppress. Determined to master her emotions and avoid appearing more devastated than the rest of her family, she rejected the strategy employed by Marianne on similar occasions: the amplification and fixation of sorrow through seclusion, silence, and idleness. Their methods differed radically, yet both were perfectly calibrated to their respective objectives.

The moment Edward crossed the threshold, Elinor retreated to her drawing table, immersing herself in work until late afternoon. She neither sought nor avoided mentions of his name, displaying an engagement with the household's general affairs that rivaled her previous intensity. While this conduct may not have diminished her internal grief, it successfully prevented its escalation and spared her mother and sisters undue anxiety.

Such behavior, diametrically opposed to Marianne's, held no merit in the latter's estimation, just as Marianne's apparent faultlessness seemed lacking to Elinor. The discipline of self-command proved effortless for Marianne; with passions of such ferocity, restraint was impossible, whereas with calm affections, it carried no virtue. That her sister's affections were indeed tranquil, Marianne dared not deny, though she burned with shame to admit it. Conversely, she provided striking evidence of the potency of her own feelings by continuing to love and respect her sister, despite the mortifying realization of their fundamental differences.

She did not isolate herself from her family, nor did she retreat into the house to avoid them, nor did she lie awake indulging in nocturnal rumination. Instead, Elinor discovered that every day afforded sufficient leisure to contemplate Edward and his enigmatic conduct, exploring every nuance her fluctuating mood permitted—from tender concern and pity to approval, censure, and doubt. There were frequent intervals, often dictated by the absence of her mother and sisters or the nature of their occupations, where conversation was effectively silenced, producing the effects of solitude. Her mind was invariably liberated; her thoughts could not be tethered elsewhere. Thus, the past and future concerning a subject of such profound interest inevitably occupied her foreground, forcing her attention and monopolizing her memory, reflection, and imagination.

One morning, shortly after Edward's departure, a reverie interrupted by the arrival of guests pulled her from her musings. She happened to be entirely alone. The closing of the small gate leading to the green court in front of the house caught her eye, directing her gaze to the window. She observed a substantial procession approaching the door. Among the group were Sir John and Lady Middleton, along with Mrs. Jennings, accompanied by two strangers—a gentleman and a lady—completely unfamiliar to her. Seated near the window, she watched as Sir John, spotting her, abandoned the remainder of the party to perform the formalities of knocking. Stepping directly onto the lawn, he compelled her to unlatch the casement to address him, despite the narrow distance between the door and the window making simultaneous speech nearly impossible without cross-hearing.

"Well," he announced cheerfully, "we have procured some strangers for you. How do you fare with them?"

"Hush! They will overhear you."

"Never mind if they do. It is merely the Palmers. Charlotte is remarkably lovely, I can assure you. You may observe her if you glance in this direction."

Certain she would glimpse her within moments without granting such permission, Elinor politely requested exemption.

"Where is Marianne? Has she fled because we have arrived? I perceive her instrument is open."

"She is walking, I believe," Elinor replied.
