Arthur stumbled back to Lady Rockminster’s dinner table looking like he’d just run into a ghost that owed him money, and the old battleaxe was already mid-rant about tardy young people having zero respect for good roast beef. But Laura spotted her cousin’s face was paler than a badly set blancmange, cut her imperious patroness off mid-scold, and demanded in a panicky whisper if he’d come down with some terrible wasting disease. Arthur just chugged an entire bumper of sherry like it was cheap pub tap water, hissed that he had the wildest story he’d ever heard but would not spill it in front of footmen who’d run straight to the town’s gossip mill before dessert.

He fidgeted so hard for the rest of the meal that he tramped right on tiny Fido the lapdog’s paws, sent his saucer of gravy flying, and made Lady Rockminster snap that he was the only person she’d ever met who could turn a perfectly good dinner into a bouncy castle test run. By the time the fruit platter came out, she’d had enough. “This meal is drier than a three-day-old biscuit,” she announced, tossing her head like a very posh swan. “It’s obvious you two want to whisper about boring secret business, so I’m off for my nap. Don’t bother bringing tea later, I won’t touch it. Good night, Mr. Warrington, do come back when there’s no scandal hanging over the table.” And she glided out of the room with all the dignity of a queen who’d just caught her footman stealing her jam.

Warrington was already half out his chair to say goodbye when Arthur called him back, wide-eyed. “Don’t go, George. I need a neutral third party who won’t either sob or throw a teacup when I tell you this, I haven’t the faintest clue how to act.”
Laura’s heart jumped so hard she almost spilled her wine. “It’s Blanche, isn’t it?” she blurted, cheeks flushing so bright she matched the port on the table.
“Worse,” Arthur said. “When I popped over to my uncle’s old lodgings earlier, Morgan – that servant who’s been with him for 20 years skimming every spare shilling off his master’s side table – told me he quit that morning, bought the whole house, and is now basically a secret-holding millionaire. Do you know what he had to blackmail me with? Blanche’s dad is not dead. The guy everyone thought drowned in New South Wales? He’s alive and kicking. That means Lady Clavering’s marriage to Sir Clavering is 100% fake.”
“Wait, so Blanche is technically her grandfather’s heir?” Warrington said, frowning.
“Maybe, but her dad’s an escaped convict,” Arthur groaned. “Clavering knows it, my uncle knows it, and the old rascal was waving that secret over Clavering’s head to force him to hand over his parliamentary seat to me, no questions asked.”
“Blanche doesn’t have a clue, does she?” Laura said, voice tight. “Or poor Lady Clavering?”
“Not a bit,” Pen said. “Blanche thought her dad was a ship’s captain who drowned. Lady Clavering told me once she was so relieved when she heard he was dead, she cried for a whole hour – said it was the worst kind of shame to be happy your husband kicked the bucket. But now this louse Morgan has the whole secret, and he tried to bribe me 10 minutes ago with a private annuity if I helped him keep it quiet. He thought I was in on the whole blackmail scheme! He threatened me! As if our family – our poor mom’s son – would ever trade on that poor lady’s misfortune for a crummy seat in Parliament.”
Laura grabbed his hand and kissed it immediately. “I never thought that for a second, Arthur.”
“Damn right you didn’t,” Warrington rumbled, his deep voice a little wobbly. “Arthur Pendennis doesn’t marry a convict’s daughter and sit in Parliament like he works for the prison board. You should break the engagement, Pen. Don’t explain a single thing, say family reasons make it impossible. It’s far better Blanche and her mom think you’re a lying cad than they ever learn their whole life was a fraud.” He paused, and he couldn’t quite bring himself to look Laura in the face. He was already waving away his last tiny sliver of hope that the engagement might fall apart, he knew it.
But Laura was already shaking her head, going back to Arthur’s side to wrap her arm around him. “Her dad’s crimes aren’t her fault, Arthur,” she said, fast and bright and pale as a sheet. “Would you abandon her if you were already married, just because something that’s not her fault went wrong? You promised her when she had a good name, you can’t leave her when she’s fallen on hard times. Our mom would never have done that.” She buried her face on his shoulder, and Pen sobbed that she was the best person who ever lived, and he’d do whatever she thought was right.
“Amen,” Warrington groaned, head in his hands. “She’s right. That girl never does a single wrong thing.”
Laura wiped her eyes, smiled, and patted the table. “Right. Now we’re all going to have tea.”
Before anyone could pour a single cuppa, though, a servant popped in to say Major Pendennis was back at the hotel and waiting very impatiently for his nephew. Laura gave Arthur a very pointed look that screamed *Don’t be an idiot, stand your ground, be nice to your old uncle but don’t let him bully you into a life of blackmail* before she trundled off to bed. Warrington grumbled about the bad timing, he’d been looking forward to that tea, and wandered off to his club, where a bunch of loud drunks were yelling about horse races, bad opera girls, and how the committee was the most tyrannical group of thieves alive. He sat there smoking his cigar, carrying their huge sacred secret around like a heavy backpack, and thought it was very funny that every single one of those yelling idiots almost certainly had a weird, private secret they’d never tell a soul.

Arthur’s anger was bubbling up to boiling point by the time he reached his uncle’s hotel room. The old Major was hunched over the fire, sniffling, grumbling that his new German servant was a total lout who couldn’t even hand him his dressing gown right, and cursing Morgan for being the ungrateful thief who’d stolen all his secrets and quit. “He got drunk last night, challenged me to a duel!” the old man whined, coughing so hard his whole body shook. “I could have stabbed him! That rascal’s got ten thousand pounds now, mark my words, but I wish he’d stuck around till I kicked the bucket. He knew exactly how I liked my tea made. Speaking of – hey, Arthur, I’m getting very old. I found my poor mom’s old bible in my books today, I want you to have it. I hope you’ll find it when I’m under the sod, yeah?”
The old man’s wobbly kindness almost deflated all Arthur’s anger. He knew he was about to smash every single last one of the old guy’s air castles to smithereens.
“Hey, I still want to see you give your first speech in the Times!” the Major cackled. “‘Unaccustomed as I am to public speaking’! And then you can buy the old family estate out in the west, become a baronet, rise right up the social ladder like a rocket!”
Arthur’s heart sank. He had to pop that balloon. “Uncle. I went to your old lodgings earlier. I talked to Morgan.”
The Major’s face flushed bright red immediately. Oh no, he thought, the cat’s fully out of the bag.
“He told me that story about Blanche’s father,” Arthur said grimly.
“Awkward, that,” the Major said, rubbing his nose very fast. “I’ve known about that little thing for a while, yeah. But Amory can’t show his face! The second he does, he’s either dead or shipped back to the hulks for life. We’ve got him completely trapped, it’s no trouble at all! Miss Amory’s a perfectly nice girl with fifty thousand pounds, Clavering likes you, you get his seat in Parliament! What could be simpler?”
“Simple?” Arthur burst out. “I’d rather go move into the prison hulks with my future father-in-law than profit off this blackmail scheme! I’d rather steal the silver spoons off the table than take a parliamentary seat that’s a bribe for keeping quiet about their secret! You almost made me prostitute my honour for a petty title and a pile of cash! I’m not going to do it.”
The old man shrieked so loud the windowpanes rattled. “WHO’S GOING TO TAKE IT AWAY FROM YOU? Clavering will give you every penny of that eighty thousand pounds, I’ll make him!”
“No,” Arthur said. “I’m going to keep my promise to Blanche, because she didn’t do anything wrong to deserve being dumped over her father’s crimes. But I’m not taking Clavering’s free seat. I’m not taking a single extra penny beyond the original settlement that was already written down for Blanche. We’re not doing any more blackmail.”
The Major’s knees gave out, he sank right down on the floor, grabbing Arthur’s hands and weeping. “Arthur! Your dad was the proudest man alive, he cared so much about the family’s honour! I’ve always been so fond of you! Don’t throw this chance away, it’s the making of you! I’m begging you, on my knees, don’t do this.”
Arthur almost cried too, hauling the old man up. “Fine. I’ll marry her. But I’m not taking any of the bribes. Don’t ask me to.”
The old man wiped his tears, coughing so hard he could barely talk. “Ring the bell for me. I said everything I had to say. I’m a broken old idiot. Shakespeare was right, Cardinal Wolsey was right, if I’d served God half as hard as I served you, I wouldn’t be on my knees begging my own nephew to take a fancy to a parliamentary seat. Good night. Don’t bother coming to see me tomorrow.”
His hand was cold and clammy when Arthur shook it. It looked like the fight had aged him ten years. The next day, he stayed in bed, and refused to let Arthur through the door at all.
