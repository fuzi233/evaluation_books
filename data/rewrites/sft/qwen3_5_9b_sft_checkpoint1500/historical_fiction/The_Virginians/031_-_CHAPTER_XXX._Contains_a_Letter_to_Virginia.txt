One afternoon, Mr. Harry Warrington repaired to his customary lunch at the White Horse Ordinary, finding the scene far livelier than he anticipated. Seated around the table was Parson Sampson, a jolly, handsome figure whose countenance seemed permanently illuminated by the fires of wit and claret. The chaplain regaled the gathering of country squires with a rapid-fire stream of *bons-mots* and scandalous gossip, eliciting roars of laughter from his audience. Though absent from London for several months, the parson possessed the freshest intelligence available to the local gentry: the latest intrigues at Kensington and Pall Mall, the behavior of Mr. Byng within the confines of prison, the shifting odds at Newmarket, and the current reigning toast of Covent Garden.

Parson Sampson's chronicles, however, were less concerned with historical accuracy than with moral vitality. To the rustic ears of his listeners, the precise identity of a lover mattered little; whether it was Lady Jane or Lady Mary harboring a secret flame, or whether the duel at Marybone Garden was fought by Harry Hilton or Tommy Ruffler, the essential truth remained the same—the stories were wicked, lively, and utterly captivating. The parson chatted and laughed with boundless energy, draining bottle after bottle, his spirited discourse keeping the company in a state of amused intoxication. In an era where the "Abbe Parson"—a clergyman unburdened by the strictures of modern piety, equally at home in the theater, the tavern, and the gaming table—was a fixture of high society, Sampson stood as a quintessential example. His voice boomed across the hunting-fields, and his songs rang clear through the halls of Covent Garden.

When Mr. Warrington finally entered, the atmosphere shifted. Whether driven by modesty or the effects of the vintage, the parson's rosy complexion deepened into a crimson flush. He leaned toward the squire in the drab coat seated nearby, whispering a Latin phrase, *"Maxima debetur,"* before rising abruptly. With a mixture of haste and stumbling, he rushed to greet the Virginian.

"My dear sir! My very dear sir!" Sampson cried, his voice thick with emotion. "My conqueror of spades, clubs, and hearts! How delightfully fresh and well you look, Sir Harry!"

Harry returned the greeting with genuine warmth, pleased to see the clergyman and quick to compliment his rosy cheeks. The squire in the drab coat, recognizing Harry, pulled up a chair beside him and urged the parson to resume his seat, promising to continue his tale of Lord Ogle and the grocer's wife. However, his sentence was cut short by a shout and an oath directed at the parson, who had inadvertently stepped on the gouty toe of a neighbor.

Apologizing profusely, Sampson turned back to Harry, bombarding him with news from Castlewood. My Lord Castlewood sent his affectionate greetings and had specifically commissioned Sampson to monitor the young gentleman's morals at Tunbridge Wells. Lady Viscountess and Lady Fanny had departed for Harrogate, Mr. Will had triumphed at Newmarket, and Molly, the housemaid, was weeping uncontrollably over Gumbo, Harry's faithful valet. Harry listened with a benevolent smile, for he was beloved throughout the region, and Sampson, with characteristic flair, wove the names of the highest nobility into his narrative of Harry's successes.

As the guests began to drift away one by one, Harry and the chaplain remained, the latter pouring toast after toast to the amusement of his host. By the end of the repast, Harry's nature had softened; the sharp edges of his youth melted into a warm, generous hospitality. Why should Mr. Sampson endure the expense of an inn when Harry's lodgings offered ample space? The chaplain's trunk was dispatched to the quarters, and Gumbo was summoned to ensure his comfort. Nothing satisfied Harry more than seeing the horses in the stable. Upon recognition, Sampson's mount—a steed Harry had once won from him—whinnied in joyful reunion, nuzzling his old master's coat. Harry, struck by the animal's loyalty, vowed by Jupiter to restore the horse to its rightful owner. Sampson, overcome with gratitude, seized Harry's hand to bless him, flung his arms around the horse's neck, and shed tears that mixed the richness of Bordeaux wine with pure emotion.

Arm in arm, the two friends made their way to Madame Bernstein's apartments, carrying with them the lingering scents of the stables. Madame Bernstein, an elderly woman of keen appetite for both conversation and entertainment, received the chaplain with open arms. She relished Sampson's broad jokes and rapid speech, much as she savored a newly invented dish. Her sole addiction, she confessed, was the game of cards, for unlike life, the cards did not cheat; a bad hand spoke the truth, and a strong suit of trumps was a form of divine flattery. Though she usually refused to play with anyone who lacked funds, the sight of Sampson's empty pockets prompted a momentary hesitation. Yet, it was evident that Harry's overflowing heart and wallet had already procured a small reserve of coin, enabling the chaplain to sit at the green baize table.

Harry had adapted to his new milieu with remarkable speed. His attire, though still black, was of the finest cut and quality, adorned with stars and ribbons. His stockings were drawn, his hair flowed over his shoulders, and he possessed the dashing air of a young Hamlet. Aunt Queensberry noted that he was likely the death of a dozen Ophelias, both here and in the colonies, yet she viewed his romantic pursuits with fond indulgence. His linens and lace were exquisite, and he indulged in shaving plates, magnificent brocade bedgowns, and the finer accoutrements of the fashionable world: French watches with painted backs, enamel snuff-boxes, and a retinue of grooms, jockeys, and tradesmen who waited in his anteroom.

Furthermore, Harry diligently cultivated the arts expected of a gentleman. Under the tutelage of a French fencing-master and a dancing-master, he acquired considerable skill and grace. Lady Maria, herself an elegant dancer, eventually conceded that no gentleman at court could execute the minuet with greater poise than Mr. Warrington. On horseback, too, he proved superior to the finest jockeys and nobles, declaring that none could ride better than his friend, Colonel George Washington of Mount Vernon.

The obsequious but affable Sampson found himself in better quarters than he had known in years. He became Harry's confidential leader, sharing the wisdom of the world and recounting tales, whether real or fancied, that delighted the younger Virginian. It was a classic dynamic of the age: the man of twenty looking up to the man of thirty, admiring the latter's seasoned jests, stale puns, and anecdotes polished by a century of dinner tables. To Harry, Sampson's college pleasantries and town stories were endlessly charming.

In the spirit of the times, there existed a symbiotic relationship between the experienced mentor and the aspiring youth. Just as the "toads" of the literary and social world had supposedly vanished in later eras, leaving behind an island free of parasites, so too did the reign of the Great Grandfather provide a sanctuary for figures like Sampson. These men, who accompanied lads to the gaming table and drank lemonade to replace Burgundy for "Master Hopeful," flourished in an environment where folly was celebrated and vice was merely a subject of mirth.

Thus, Harry's circle expanded. He had fencing and dancing masters, a tutor, a chaplain, and a secretary. This arrangement was detailed in a letter preserved in the Warrington correspondence, dated August 25th, 1756, from Mrs. Bligh's Lodgings at Tunbridge Wells to his mother, Mrs. Esmond Warrington, at Richmond, Virginia.

*Your honoured letter of 20 June, per Mr. Trail of Bristol, has been forwarded to me duly, and I have to thank your goodness and kindness for the good advice which you are pleased to give me, as also for the remembrances of dear home, which I shall love never the worse for having been to the home of our ancestors in England.*

*I writ you a letter by the last monthly packet, informing my honoured mother of the little accident I had on the road hither, and of the kind friends who I found and whom took me in. Since then I have been profiting of the fine weather and the good company here, and have made many friends among our nobility, whose acquaintance I am sure you will not be sorry that I should make. Among their lordships I may mention the famous Earl of Chesterfield, late Ambassador to Holland, and Viceroy of the Kingdom of Ireland; the Earl of March and Ruglen, who will be Duke of Queensberry at the death of his Grace; and her Grace the Duchess, a celebrated beauty of the Queen's time, when she remembers my grandpapa at Court.
