The silence inside the Nuns' House was not empty; it was a suspended weight, thick with the memory of a drowning girl. Rosa remembered her mother as a small, pale thing, barely older than herself, laid out in a summer dress that seemed to hold the humidity of the sea. Wet hair, scattered petals, the specific, terrible color of a life cut short at a party of pleasure. Her father had followed her to the grave a year later, broken by the arithmetic of loss. From that equation, a new variable emerged: Edwin Drood. He had been the solvent for her father's grief, a widower matching a widow, until he too vanished into the quiet road where all pilgrims eventually fade.

Time had altered the texture of Rosa's isolation. Once, the pity surrounding her was a grey fog; now, it shimmered in hues of gold, rose, and azure, a soft, artificial light that kept her perpetually young. The nuns, driven by a collective need to console, treated her as a perpetual child, a dynamic that bred a peculiar rivalry among them. Who would bring the ribbon? Who would write the letter? These petty contests masked a deeper, harder strife beneath their veils. Rosa, spoiled by this endless well of affection, had grown giddy and wilful, unaware that the depth of her reservoirs had never truly been tested.

Then, the news arrived. It did not knock; it seeped. Perhaps carried by the wind through the open casements, or kneaded into the morning bread, or deposited on the dust-beaten mats by the town's atmosphere, the rumor permeated every gable before Miss Twinkleton had even risen. Through Mrs. Tisher, while the matron was still sacrificing to the Graces in her dressing gown, the report crystallized: Miss Landless's brother had attacked Mr. Edwin Drood.

The narrative expanded with the chaotic logic of gossip. First, a bottle. Then, a knife, which suggested a fork. Finally, the cook confirmed the trifecta: bottle, knife, and fork, plus a decanter flying aimlessly at everyone's head. The psychological imperative was clear: one must know the motive. Why throw the implements? Because Miss Landless's brother admired Miss Bud, and Mr. Drood had denied him the right to do so. The altercation was simple, a collision of pride, yet the aftermath was a minefield.

Rosa retreated, plugging her ears, seeking refuge in a corner. Miss Landless, however, demanded clarity. She bypassed the filter of discretion, striking directly for Mr. Crisparkle's office. When she returned, her cheeks flushed, she offered Rosa only the tip of the iceberg—the provocation, the final insult—carefully omitting the earlier, more complex exchanges regarding her lover's easy temper. She delivered her brother's petition for forgiveness with sisterly earnestness, closing the door on the subject.

It fell to Miss Twinkleton to manage the public consciousness. Entering the "apartment allotted to study," she summoned the girls with a forensic air. With Mrs. Tisher standing behind her like a queen at Tilbury, Miss Twinkleton invoked Shakespeare, the Bard of Avon, specifically his depiction of Rumour in *The Merchant of Venice*. She declared that the recent fracas between two young gentlemen, occurring within a hundred miles of their peaceful walls, had been grotesquely exaggerated by the voice of Rumour. Citing responsible inquiries, she dismissed the affair as an "airy nothing," urging the students to return to their labors.

Yet the subject survived. By dinner, Miss Ferdinand had donned a paper moustache and attempted to lob a water-bottle at Miss Giggles, who defended herself with a spoon. Rosa watched this performance with an uneasy knot in her stomach. She felt implicated, a passive participant in a drama she did not fully understand, trapped in a false position regarding her engagement. The distance from her fiancé amplified her anxiety, and today, with Helena avoiding the topic as a delicate wound, Rosa was cast alone with her thoughts.

The bell rang, announcing the arrival of her guardian. Mr. Grewgious entered, a man composed of aridity and sand. His hair was a mangy yellow tippet, so unlike natural growth that it must have been a wig, unless Nature had simply abandoned the project mid-chisel. His face was carved from hard curves, his forehead notched as if the sculptor had lost patience. He walked with a shambling hesitation, his near-sightedness failing to notice the expanse of white cotton stocking contrasting with his black suit. Despite this awkward machinery, he possessed a strange capacity to make an agreeable impression.

He found Rosa discomfited in Miss Twinkleton's presence, dim forebodings weighing on him. He greeted her warmly, offering a chair, while Miss Twinkleton, with charming grace, maneuvered her desk to the corner window, effectively removing herself from the immediate circle. "You will be under no restraint," she assured them, retreating to the periphery.

Left alone by the fire, Mr. Grewgious repeated his greeting, waiting for Rosa to settle. "My visits," he said, "are like those of the angels—not that I compare myself to an angel."

"No, sir," Rosa replied.

"Not by any means," he agreed, smoothing his head as if pressing water from a dive. "I merely refer to my visits, which are few and far between. The angels are, we know very well, up-stairs." He glanced meaningfully at Miss Twinkleton, who resumed her writing with a stiff stare.

"I refer," he continued, placing his hand on Rosa's, "to the other young ladies."

Miss Twinkleton dipped her pen, waiting for inspiration from the Celestial Nine. Grewgious smoothed his head again, pulled out a pocket-book, and a stub of pencil. "I made a guiding memorandum... 'Well and happy.' Truly. You are well and happy, my dear?"

"Yes, indeed, sir," Rosa answered.

"For which," Grewgious noted, bending his head toward the window, "acknowledgments are due to the maternal kindness of the lady before me." This was a lame departure, a circuitous route that never reached its destination, as Miss Twinkleton bit the end of her pen, looking upward for an idea.

Grewgious lined out "well and happy" and moved to the next line: "'Pounds, shillings, and pence.' A dry subject, but important. Life is pounds, shillings, and pence. Death is—" He paused, a sudden recollection of her parents stopping him, and softened his tone. "Death is *not* pounds, shillings, and pence." His voice was as hard and dry as his own constitution, yet somehow conveyed kindness. If Nature had finished him, his face might have shown it, but the notches in his forehead refused to fuse.

"You find your allowance sufficient?" he asked.

Rosa wanted for nothing. "And you are not in debt?"

She laughed at the notion, finding it a comical vagary of the imagination. Grewgious stretched his near sight to confirm her view. "Ah!" he commented, lining out the currency notes. "I spoke of having got among the angels! So I did!"

Rosa sensed the direction of his next query before he voiced it, blushing and folding a crease in her dress. "Marriage," Grewgious murmured, carrying his smoothing hand down over his eyes, nose, and chin. He leaned closer, speaking confidentially. "I now touch upon the point that is the direct cause of my troubling you. Otherwise, being a particularly Angular man, I should not have intruded. I am the last man to intrude into a sphere for which I am so entirely unfitted. I feel, on these premises, as if I was a bear—with the cramp—in a youthful Cotillon."

His ungainliness validated the simile, setting Rosa off in hearty laughter.
