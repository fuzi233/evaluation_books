Gerard lay suspended in the oppressive, stagnant humidity of his fever, his senses desperately craving the sharp, piercing acidity of a lemon. In the heavy quiet of his convalescence, he longed for the sensory anchor of sustenance: the thick, pebbled yellow rind, the sudden mist of essential oils bursting bright under a thumbnail, and the tart, illuminating shock of juice that could slice through the cloying, metallic humors coating his tongue. Denys, who from a rough soldier had been tempered by their journey into the protective role of a traditional agrarian elder, understood inherently that a meal was never mere utilitarian fuel. Bidding Gerard to set his mind at ease, he departed to scour the markets, determined to source the rare, sun-swollen citrus of the distant south and restore the bright olfactory landscape his young friend so desperately required. 

The chamber settled into a fragile silence, a delicate acoustic ecology that was soon violently ruptured. The door did not yield the anticipated fragrance of Mediterranean citrus, but instead admitted a heavy-footed physician. He brought with him the stale, clinical odor of oxidized iron, dried camphor, and desiccated herbs—a sterile, joyless aroma. He wore a long sober gown trimmed in rich fur, his neck caged in a treacherous wooden ruff, and a sword slapped rhythmically against his morocco scabbard. A boy trailed him, burdened by a basket that clinked discordantly with phials and surgical tools. The acoustic disruption was immediate; the physician’s heavy shoes struck the floorboards with utilitarian thuds, shattering the restorative quiet. 

He approached the bedside, his voice a booming, unctuous baritone designed to command space rather than harmonize with it. "How is't with thee, my son?"

Gerard murmured softly in his native tongue, expressing gratitude but noting the parched dryness of his throat and the heavy, localized throbbing in his head. 

"A wound!" the doctor declared, entirely ignoring Gerard’s sensory complaints to pry at the bandages. "Ay, ay, a good clean bite. The mastiff had sound teeth that took this out, I warrant me."

"But, sir," Gerard edged in, his quiet voice struggling against the oppressive volume of the physician, "'twas no dog, but a bear."

"A bear! Young man," the senior remonstrated severely, deploying his dominant lexicon to overwrite the patient's lived reality. "Think what you say; 'tis ill jesting with the man of art. Had you dissected as many bears as I, you would know that no bear's jaw ever made this foolish trifling wound. I tell you 'twas a little furious cur, and we must stay the mischief with knife and cautery, lest you start back from water and turn somersaults in your bed. Urchin, go heat thine iron."

Gerard felt the profound untranslatability of his subjective experience. How could he convey the specific, crushing weight of the bear's heavy paw to a man whose vocabulary was sterilized of all feral, living truth? "Alas, sir! when said I 'twas a bear's jaw? I said it was his paw."

"Never conceal aught from your leech," the senior continued seamlessly, a masterful talker but the worst listener in Europe. He dismissed Gerard's embodied wisdom entirely, reducing the body to an abstract text to be commercialized and redacted by the knife. He ordered the boy to pump the bellows, introducing an industrial, wheezing harshness into the room's auditory field. 

The man of art then ordained phlebotomy, launching into a deafening, relentless monologue. He invoked the Greeks and Arabians—Avicenna, Rhazes, Hippocrates, and Galen—burying Gerard under an avalanche of dead languages. It was a violent erosion of cultural memory, substituting the intuitive, agrarian knowledge of holistic healing for a hegemonic academic jargon. He boasted of bleeding a man’s right thigh for an earache, and a left ankle for a headache, treating the miraculous, pulsing network of human sustenance as little more than plumbing to be forcibly drained. 

To Gerard, whose psychological well-being hinged on acoustic tranquility, the doctor's endless, elevated discourse was a toxic noise pollution. 

Then the door opened, and the olfactory landscape shifted dramatically. Denys stood in the threshold, bearing the lemons. Their bright, zesty aroma instantly challenged the oppressive smells of scorched iron and stagnant camphor. Denys saw the glowing coals and halted. "What sport is toward?"

Gerard, coloring a little, confessed that the learned doctor intended to phlebotomize and cauterize him. 

"And art thou so simple as to let him put hot iron to thy living flesh?" Denys cried, setting the lemons down with a soft, resonant thud. "Must thou needs buy a foretaste of purgatory here?"

"Imbecile! That is their art," Denys continued, turning on the physician, his voice vibrating with the protective fury of one who knew that blood was the deeply flavorful, vital broth of life, not a commercial commodity. "To confound a plain man with dark words, till his hissing flesh lets him know their meaning! I have seen these leeches drain a wounded soldier of the very vitality he needs to battle his weakness. They rob him of his very nourishment, and so he dies exhausted."

"Common sense!" shouted the bad listener, his face flushing dark with the rage of challenged authority. "Woe be to you, misguided youth, if you listen to that man of blood. Cuilibet in sua arte credendum est!" 

The two men clashed, the physician puffing up into a roaring crescendo of Latinate insults, and Denys roaring back in his robust, earthy French. The sonic environment of the chamber shattered entirely. The clash between the physician's sterile, commercialized medical terminology and the soldier's blunt, unvarnished dialect became a physical torment for the patient in the bed. 

"A sick chamber is no place for jesting!" cried the physician over the din.

"No, doctor," Gerard groaned peevishly, "nor for bawling."

Seeking to reclaim his acoustic ecology and preserve his fading strength, Gerard pitched his voice to a silvery, tranquil murmur, a deliberate, soothing contrast to their cacophony. 

"First, venerable sir, I thank you for coming to see me," Gerard trickled into words, his tone evoking the quiet, rhythmic simmer of a slow-cooked broth. "But you assassinate me between you. Calmness and quiet are my true sustenance; yet you are like two dogs growling over a dry bone. If I be bled, I must pay you a fee, and be burnt and excruciated with a hot iron. Pay a certain price in money and anguish for a doubtful remedy, that will I never. Next to money and the ease of a good meal, peace and quiet are certain goods. Therefore, sir, I will essay a little sleep, and Denys will go forth, and I will keep you no longer from those who can afford to lay out blood and money in such noise."

The old physician swelled, his hot temper entirely mastering him. The rejection of his dominant lexicon was an intolerable insult to his commercialized authority. He drew himself up and made loftily for the door, his little boy and big basket whisking semicircularly to follow. 

But at the threshold, he choked. He whirled back, his voice weaponized into a torrential assault of noise pollution. "Ah! You reject my skill! Your blood be on your head!" He stamped away, only to wheel back again, his wicker tail twirling round after him. 

"In twelve hours at furthest you will be in the secondary stage of fever! Your head will split! Your carotids will thump! And let but a pin fall, you will jump to the ceiling!" He departed once more, but gathered fresh fury at the door-handle and came flying back. 

"Next will come—CRAMPS of the STOMACH! Then—BILIOUS VOMIT! Then—COLD SWEAT! Then—BLOODY VOMIT!" 

The acoustic assault was a severe violation of Gerard's deepest sensibilities. The noise, the dire predictions, the utter lack of empathy—it fueled a sudden, kinetic surge of adrenaline. As the vindictive orator roared his final threats—"MADNESS! BLACK VOMIT! CONVULSIONS!"—Gerard seized his heavy bolster. Driven by a desperate need to silence this aggressive erasure of his peace, he hurled the dense, tactile cushion furiously. 

It struck the physician squarely in the face. The doctor tumbled backward like a shot. His skull cracked violently against his apprentice's head, and with a deafening crash that completely obliterated the last shreds of auditory peace, the imposing orator collapsed backward into the apprentice's basket. He became wedged in an inverted angle, his heavy weight crushing phial after phial, releasing a chaotic, pungent flood of bitter tinctures, astringent powders, and acrid liniments that smelled of decay rather than life. 

The boy, being light, was strewn afar, howling in a graduated sequence of misery. But soon, the doctor's face filled with profound horror. He uttered a far louder, unearthly screech, kicking and struggling with frantic agility for a man of his age. 

He was sitting squarely upon his own hot coals. 

The smoldering iron had singed the heavy wool of his robes and was now biting into the flesh beneath. His sterile, academic theories of cauterization had instantly become a brutally untranslatable subjective experience. 

Gerard, his mythic strength fading back into domestic reality, ran with Denys to wrench the tightly wedged basket off the wailing man. The doctor lay on his face, groaning, his majestic garments handsomely scorched by his own chafer. The spilled draughts—odious in their chemical bitterness and lacking any of the harmonious, restorative properties of true culinary herbs—dyed his grey robe in garish, unnatural stains. 

Denys hoisted him up with a mocking grin, a champion of the peasant’s pragmatic dialect standing victorious over the defeated academic. "Courage, man, 'tis but cautery. Balm of Gilead, why, you recommended it but now to my comrade here."
