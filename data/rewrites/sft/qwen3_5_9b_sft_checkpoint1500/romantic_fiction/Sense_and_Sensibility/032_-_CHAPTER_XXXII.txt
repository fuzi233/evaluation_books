When Miss Dashwood recounted the details of her interview with Colonel Brandon to her sister, the impact was not entirely what she had anticipated. Marianne did not question the truth of a single word; she listened with a steady, submissive attention, offering no objection, no defense for Willoughby, and shedding tears that betrayed her absolute conviction of his guilt. Yet, though Elinor saw the sting of this knowledge taking root, causing Marianne to stop avoiding Colonel Brandon and to address him with a compassionate, respectful air, she also saw that her sister remained deeply wretched. While Marianne's spirit had lost its violent agitation, it had settled into a gloomy dejection. The revelation of Willoughby's character weighed heavier on her than the loss of his heart. The memory of his seduction of Miss Williams, the misery inflicted upon that poor girl, and the chilling suspicion of what his intentions toward herself truly were, preyed relentlessly upon her mind. So profound was her sorrow that she refused to speak of it even to Elinor, brooding in silence and inflicting a deeper, more silent pain than any open confession could have caused.

As for Mrs. Dashwood, to describe her reception of Elinor's letter would merely repeat the anguish already shared between the sisters. Her letters, arriving in rapid succession, poured forth her suffering, her anxiety for Marianne, and her desperate pleas for fortitude. How terrible must Marianne's affliction be, her mother reasoned, when she could speak of strength? How humiliating must the cause of these regrets be, that her daughter could not bear to indulge them?

Despite her own discomfort in leaving Barton, Mrs. Dashwood resolved that Marianne would find it easier to endure her grief anywhere else than at home, where every object constantly recalled Willoughby as she had always seen him there. She urged her daughters to extend their stay in town, hoping that the varied occupations, sights, and company of London might, at least occasionally, divert Marianne's thoughts from herself and perhaps even provide a fleeting amusement, however spurned by her current state of mind. From the perspective of safety, Mrs. Dashwood believed London offered equal protection from Willoughby as the country did. Since his acquaintances would now sever ties with anyone connected to her, design could not bring them together, negligence could not expose them by chance, and the crowded streets of London held less opportunity for accidental encounters than the quiet seclusion of Barton. Furthermore, a letter from her son-in-law indicated that he and his wife would arrive in town before mid-February, making it prudent for them to maintain their proximity.

Marianne, though inwardly believing her mother's plan flawed and contrary to her wishes, submitted without opposition. She felt it was entirely wrong to prolong her stay in London, depriving her of her mother's direct sympathy and condemning her to a social circle that would allow her no moment of rest. However, a glimmer of solace remained: what brought ruin to herself might bring restoration to her sister. Elinor, anticipating that she could not fully avoid Edward Ferrars, found comfort in the thought that their extended stay, while potentially hindering her own happiness, was necessary for Marianne's sake.

Elinor's vigilance in shielding her sister from the mention of Willoughby proved effective. Neither Mrs. Jennings, Sir John, nor Mrs. Palmer ever spoke his name before Marianne. Yet, Elinor wished that same restraint could have been applied to herself, for she was compelled to listen daily to the clamors of indignation surrounding the scandal. Sir John, incredulous, declared that he could not have imagined it, recalling Willoughby as a bold rider and a good-natured soul. "He did not believe there was a bolder rider in England!" he exclaimed, wishing the scoundrel at the devil. "Such a deceitful dog!" he added, lamenting that the meeting at Allenham had ended so tragically.

Mrs. Palmer, equally indignant, resolved to drop all acquaintance with him and vowed never to utter his name again, calling him a "good-for-nothing." Her sympathy manifested primarily in her eagerness to gather and disseminate gossip regarding the impending marriage. She detailed where the new carriage was being built, which artist painted Willoughby's portrait, and where Miss Grey's wedding attire could be purchased.

In stark contrast, Lady Middleton's calm and polite unconcern provided a rare relief to Elinor, whose spirits were often overwhelmed by the fervent kindness of others. It was a profound comfort to know that at least one person in their circle could greet her without curiosity about particulars or anxiety for her sister's health. Elinor sometimes found herself rating good-breeding higher than good-nature under the pressure of officious condolences, valuing Lady Middleton's ability to express shock—"It is very shocking, indeed!"—while maintaining a dignified distance. This allowed Lady Middleton to support the dignity of her own sex, voice her censure, and then turn her attention to the interests of her assemblies, deciding to call on Mrs. Willoughby the moment she was married, despite Sir John's reservations.

Colonel Brandon's inquiries, always delivered with delicacy and unobtrusive concern, were never unwelcome to Miss Dashwood. Having earned the privilege of discussing Marianne's disappointment through his zealous efforts to soothe her, they conversed with complete confidence. For Brandon, the reward for exposing his own past sorrows and present humiliations lay in Marianne's pitying gaze and the gentle tone of her voice when addressing him. These signs assured him that his efforts had fostered goodwill, giving Elinor hope for further growth. Meanwhile, Mrs. Jennings, unaware of the depth of their connection, grew increasingly convinced that the marriage would not occur until Michaelmas, or perhaps not at all, as the "good understanding" between the Colonel and Miss Dashwood seemed to suggest that the pleasures of the mulberry tree and the yew arbour would fall to her alone.

By early February, scarcely a fortnight after receiving Willoughby's letter, Elinor had the painful duty of informing her sister of his marriage. She ensured the news reached Marianne privately, sparing her the agony of reading it in the newspapers, which she examined with eager, trembling hands. Marianne received the tidings with resolute composure, shedding no tears initially. However, after a brief interval, the dam broke, and for the remainder of the day, she existed in a state of pitiable desolation hardly less severe than when she first feared the event.

With the Willoughbys departed from town immediately following the wedding, Elinor hoped to persuade her sister to venture out gradually, as she had done previously. About this time, the two Miss Steeles returned to the residence of their cousins in Conduit Street, greeted with great cordiality. Only Elinor felt sorrow at their presence, for their arrival invariably caused her pain, especially Lucy Steele's delighted exclamation of finding her still in town.

"I should have been quite disappointed if I had not found you here *still*," Lucy remarked, emphasizing the word. "Though you *told* me at Barton that you would not stay above a *month*, I thought you would change your mind when it came to the point. It would have been such a pity to have gone away before your brother and sister came. And now, surely, you will be in no *hurry* to be gone?"

Elinor understood the implication perfectly and struggled to maintain her self-command, feigning indifference to the insinuation. When Mrs. Jennings asked how they traveled, Miss Steele proudly declared they had come post with a "smart beau," Dr. Davies, who had treated them with the utmost gentility. Though Mrs. Jennings suspected the Doctor was the man behind the affection, Miss Steele denied it with affected earnestness, vowing to contradict anyone who claimed otherwise—a promise Mrs. Jennings happily accepted.

Later, returning to the charge, Lucy asked if Miss Dashwood would accompany her and her sister to stay with her family in town. Elinor, refusing to humor further opposition, simply stated, "No, I do not think we shall." Lucy pressed, and when Mrs. Jennings interjected that their visit was barely begun, Lucy was silenced. When Mrs. Steele expressed regret that they could not see Miss Dashwood's sister, noting Marianne's absence due to nervous headaches, Elinor politely declined the invitation, explaining that her sister was confined to bed or her dressing gown and unable to receive visitors. However, when Miss Steele suggested they could simply go to see her, Elinor found the impertinence becoming too much for her temper. Fortunately, Lucy's sharp reprimand saved her from a heated exchange, curbing her cousin's forwardness, albeit without adding sweetness to their mutual manners.
