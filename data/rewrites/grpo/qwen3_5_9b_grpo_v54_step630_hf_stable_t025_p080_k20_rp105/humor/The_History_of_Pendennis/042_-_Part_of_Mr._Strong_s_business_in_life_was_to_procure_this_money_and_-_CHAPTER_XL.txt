Since that fateful, delightful night in Grosvenor Place, the heart of Mr. Harry Foker had been in a state of such violent agitation that it would have been impossible to imagine a man of his supposed philosophical depth enduring such turmoil. One must recall the excellent counsel he had previously dispensed to Arthur Pendennis, the early wisdom that marked this gifted youth, and the fact that a life of constant self-indulgence, befitting a gentleman of his means, should theoretically have hardened him into a cynic who cared for no one but himself. Yet, here was Harry Foker, a man who had supposedly mastered the world, suddenly falling victim to the universal plague that strikes us all once or twice in our lives: the disquieting power of a woman. Though early wise, Foker was still merely a man. He could not escape the common lot any more than Achilles, Ajax, Lord Nelson, or Adam himself. Now, his time had arrived, and young Harry became a prisoner to Love, the All-Conqueror.

That night, when he retreated to the Back Kitchen after bidding farewell to Arthur Pendennis at the staircase door in Lamb Court, the gin-twist and deviled turkey held no charm for him. The jokes of his companions fell flat on his ears. Even when Mr. Hodgen, the chronicler of 'The Body Snatcher,' launched into a new chant even more dreadful and humorous than his famous composition, Foker, bound by politeness and his status as a chief of the Back Kitchen, offered a perfunctory "Bravo, Hodgen," yet he failed to hear a single word of the song, later immortalized as 'The Cat in the Cupboard.' Exhausted and late, he slipped into his private apartments, but the fever of his soul kept him awake. The moment he stirred from his agitated sleep, the image of Miss Amory materialized, declaring, "Here I am, your princess and beauty; you have discovered me, and shall care for nothing else henceforth."

Suddenly, his former pursuits and friendships seemed stale and distasteful. Until now, he had rarely associated with women of his own rank, dismissing them as "modest women"—a virtue that, he felt, failed to compensate for the lack of lively qualities found in his relatives. His mother, though tender, did not amuse him; his cousins, the daughters of the Earl of Rosherville, wore him thin. One was a blue geologist, another a cigar-smoking horsewoman, and the third a High Church fanatic who turned her room into an oratory and fasted every Friday. Their paternal home, Drummington, was a place he swore he would rather endure on the treadmill than visit. To Lord Erith, his cousin, Harry was a vulgar low-life; to Harry, Erith was a dull prig, the Speaker's opprobrium, and a dreary philanthropist. The memory of an evening when Harry poked his uncle in the side with a billiard cue, declaring, "Well, old cock, I've seen many a bad stroke, but never such a bad one as that," remained a source of deep shame for the Earl, who shunned his nephew thereafter, though he dared not banish him entirely to spare his sister Agnes's heart.

Furthermore, there was the matter of Lady Ana Milton, the fifth daughter of Lord Gravesend, whom fate had destined for Harry since childhood. From her nursery days, it was ordained that when Harry reached maturity, he would wed Lady Ann. Both young people had acquiesced without protest. When Harry's father declared, "Harry, your uncle and I have agreed you'll marry Lady Ann. She has no money, but she is good blood and looks well. Refuse, and you'll have your mother's jointure and two hundred a year," Harry, trusting his sire implicitly, had replied, "Well, sir, if Ann's agreeable, I say ditto. She's not a bad-looking girl." He had hung a small, insignificant portrait of his cousin in his sitting-room, a poor figure beside the French prints of dancing nymphs and opera stars that adorned his walls.

But now, the instant Foker awoke the morning after Lady Clavering's dinner, Blanche's image glared upon him with clear grey eyes and a winning smile. Her tune rang in his ears: "Yet round about the spot, ofttimes I hover." Opposite him, a French print depicted a Turkish lady and her Greek lover caught by a venerable Ottoman; on another wall, a gentleman and lady rode and kissed at full gallop. The room was a gallery of gauzy nymphs, lovely illustrations, and signed autographs of Miss Calverley in tight pantaloons and Miss Rougemont as Venus. These were the treasures of honest Harry, an idle, jovial fast man about town. Yet, a letter from Miss Calverley, scrawled in a degage style calling him "Hokey-pokey-fokey," lay on his bedside table amidst keys, sovereigns, and a sprig of verbena from Miss Amory. Suddenly, the prospect of dining at the Elephant and Castle with that old woman filled him with dreary disgust.

When his fond mother, Lady Agnes, beheld her son's pallor and gloom, she fretted over his billiards at Spratt's and the Back Kitchen. Harry, however, revealed a perfect confidence to her, bringing home choice anecdotes from clubs and billiard rooms that the simple lady relished. He artfully praised the Claverings' magnificent display of plate and Sir Francis's agreeable nature, selecting a date only a fortnight hence for their invitation to Grosvenor Street. But as Lady Agnes wrote the card, Harry noticed an omission. "You have not asked Miss Whatdyecallem—Miss Amory," he insisted. Reluctantly, his mother added the name. Triumphant, Harry embraced his parent and retired to his ottoman, brooding on schemes to bring Miss Amory under his roof.

His polyglot valet, Anatole, noted the disturbance in his master's spirits. That morning, Harry was impossible to please, cursing his pantaloons and boots. In a moment of blushing hesitation, he asked Anatole if he could dress hair. Anatole twisted Harry's locks until his head was as curly as a negro's. Dressed in splendor, Harry ordered his drag to Pelham Corner, but then hesitated. "Confound her! Put the dinner off—I can't go!" he cried, though he immediately reversed himself, ordering the carriage to Pelham Corner at six.

For what purpose? Why did he need to see Pendennis so urgently? Because, as the narrator notes, when this mishap befalls a young lady, people who were once indifferent become fascinating, while former loves turn insipid. Harry, suddenly valuing Pendennis because he had been near the "fragrant flower of love," rushed to Lamb Court. Warrington informed him that Pen was gone to the printing office. Disconsolate, Foker visited the *Pall Mall Gazette*, only to find Pen had left again. With no courage to face Lady Clavering directly, he delivered two engraved cards to Jeames, who bowed his powdered head and closed the varnished doors.

Heartbroken, Harry ordered his pony to Tattersall's. Just as he mounted the fiery animal, Lady Clavering's splendid barouche drove up. Dodging the carriage, Harry followed it through the Green Park until he saw Lady Clavering enter a shop with Miss Amory, clad in a gossamer dress and pink bonnet. The carriage proceeded to Madame Rigodon's, Mrs. Wolsey's, and finally to Hunter's for ices. Harry, on his pony with a red jacket, dodged them until they entered the Park. He dashed forward, hoping for a nod of recognition across the ditch, but received none.

As Miss Amory returned to her carriage, Harry joined a group of young men in the Park. They mocked the "Indian widow" and speculated on Lady Clavering's debts and gambling habits. When they mentioned Miss Amory's singing, comparing her to a mermaid, Harry turned red with tears in his eyes, protesting his ignorance of classical literature. "I never said I was a good scholar," he stammered. Alone, he lamented his wasted youth, realizing he was a "poor, ignorant little beggar" fit only for Foker's Entire, while she could compose music as easily as he drank beer.

As he cantered out of Rotten Row, he nearly collided with a large family carriage. A cheery voice cried, "Harry, Harry!" It was his aunt, Lady Rosherville, and her daughters, including his betrothed, Lady Ann. Harry started back, pale and scared, the truth crashing over him: his fate sat in the back seat. "What is the matter, Harry?" Lady Ann asked. He muttered a greeting, waved a hurried goodbye, and fled on his pony as the great carriage rolled away, leaving him behind in the dust.
