It was a languid afternoon on the seventh of September, more than two months since Romola and Tito had confessed their love to one another. Tito stepped into Nello’s shop, finding the barber sprawled across his bench, cap pulled low over his eyes, one leg drawn up, the other dangling toward the floor—dragging with it a manuscript volume of verse, its pages crushed beneath the weight of slumber. In the corner, Sandro sat alone, absorbed in a game of *mora*, his fingers moving slowly, deliberately, as if counting time itself.

With quiet steps, Tito picked up the lute and bent over the sleeping man, plucking the strings gently as he sang:

“Quant’ è bella giovinezza,
Che si fugge tuttavia!
Chi vuol esser lieto sia,
Di doman non c’è certezza.”

[“How fair is youth in bloom,
Yet it slips away!
Who would be joyful, let him be,
For tomorrow brings no surety.”]
—A Carnival song by Lorenzo de’ Medici.

Nello stirred instantly, like a bird roused from dreams. His cap flew off, and he sprang upright. “Ah, my Apollino! I’ve overslept my siesta on this sweltering day. That’s what happens when you don’t sleep naturally, but take a draught of potent poetry instead. Hear me—how I begin to echo words with the same first letter, like a Troubadour? It’s one of my ill signs: I fear my fine wine of reason may run out through the spigot of authorship, leaving me an empty cask with only the scent of dregs—like so many brilliant minds I know. What is it, my Orpheus?” He stretched his arms wide, then swept them around Tito, playfully grasping his curls. “What do you want from your well-tamed Nello? I can hear the coaxing tone in your melody. Let me see the smallest thread of your desire, as the great poet says, so I may thread it.”

“That’s just a tailor’s image,” Tito replied, letting his fingers dance lightly across the strings. “But you’ve guessed why I’m so eager to wake you. I need a little extra care—not on my chin, no—but on my hair, which is as tangled as Florence’s politics. You have a way with your comb that pleases the skin and stirs the spirits nicely. And perhaps a touch of your finest orange-scented oil wouldn’t hurt—I’m bound for the Scala palace, where I’ll meet radiant company. Young Cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici will be there, along with Bernardo Dovizi of Bibbiena, whose wit moves so swiftly I can think of no defense except the fragrance of orange blossoms.”

Nello had already seized his comb, guiding Tito backward into the chair and wrapping the cloth about him. “No talk of rivalry, my dear young man. Bernardo Dovizi is sharp enough—like a weasel, though not quite as sly as Ser Piero, whom Piero de’ Medici keeps handy to slip through narrow cracks. But you—you surpass all others. Soon you’ll reach the stars with a single finger. They say you’ve even won over the sour Messer Angelo, who now admits you’re less of an ass than expected—especially given your close friendship with the Secretary.”

“And between us, Nello mio,” Tito said, smiling faintly, “Messer Angelo has more genius and learning than all the other Florentine scholars combined. It’s true they praise me now, while Poliziano is down—grief-stricken, ill, or something. I could spar with Pietro Crinito, a mere sparrow-hawk, but Poliziano—he’s an eagle with a broad beak who’d swallow me whole, feathers and all, and never notice.”

“I won’t argue with your modesty,” Nello said, “but we Florentines don’t live by truth alone. We cry down Dante, praise Francesco Cei—just for variety. And when we call you the new Poliziano, heaven sees to it that it’s not quite the lie it might be. And aren’t you a model of virtue in this wicked city? Your ears are double-waxed against every siren song that might lure you from the Via de’ Bardi and the great work meant to astonish future generations?”

“Future generations, indeed,” Tito answered. “They’ll likely be astonished—as the universe is—by the impossibility of seeing how it was ever planned.”

“Exactly,” Nello said. “That was the very prophecy here the other day. Cristoforo Landino claimed the excellent Bardo is like a knight in heavy armor, toppled over, then angry because he’s been trampled—though I suspect that quote wasn’t his own. For all men who feed you with empty promises and gag you with endless talk, Messer Cristoforo is the pearl. Ecco! You’re perfect now.” He removed the cloth. “Nothing could improve upon this. But love isn’t always fed on learning, is it? I’ll have to style your hair again soon—for the betrothal, perhaps?”

“Perhaps,” Tito replied, smiling. “Unless Messer Bernardo suggests Bardo wants me to yoke a lion and a wild boar to the Zecca’s chariot before I win my Alcestis. Still, he’s right to say I’m unworthy of Romola. She is a Pleiad—she might dim if she marries any mortal.”

*Gnaffè*, Nello said, “your modesty is perfectly placed. Yet fate seems to have carved you for the niche left empty by the old man’s son—who, by the way, Cronaca told me, is now back at San Marco. Did you know?”

A sudden jolt ran through Tito, though he showed nothing. He stooped to pick up the fallen book, smoothing its crumpled pages. “No. I thought he was still at Fiesole. Are you certain he’s returned to San Marco?”

“Cronaca is my source,” Nello said, shrugging. “I don’t frequent that place, but he does. Ah,” he added, taking the book from Tito’s hands, “poor Nencia da Barberino! It pains your scholarly soul to see her pages dog-eared. I fell asleep under the charm of her rustic verses—‘prettier than the turnip-flower,’ ‘with cheeks sweeter than cheese.’ But to truly smell such a contadina, one must lie on velvet cushions in the Via Larga—not go to watch the Fierucoloni trudge into the Piazza della Nunziata after sunset.”

“And who are the Fierucoloni?” Tito asked, adjusting his cap.

“The peasant women from the mountains of Pistoia, Casentino, God knows where—they come to keep vigil at the church of the Nunziata and sell yarn and dried mushrooms at the little Fair, as we call it. A strange sight, with paper lanterns, chanting hymns to the Virgin on this eve of her nativity—if you had the time to see. No? Then I’ve seen enough. There’s chaos in the square. One might get hit by stones without asking for it, and I’ve never liked that kind of attention. Addio.”

Tito left with a quiet uneasiness. His visit ended sooner than expected—the young cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici, later Pope Leo X, had been detained by hunting and failed to appear. Sunset was still half an hour away when Tito stepped from the Scala palace, intending to go straight to the Via de’ Bardi. But scarcely had he walked far when he saw Romola approaching from the Borgo Pinti.

She wore a thick black veil and mantle, yet there was no mistaking her figure or stride. Beside her walked Monna Brigida, her usual finery replaced by plain, unassuming clothes.

Romola had never been raised in piety, and her outings beyond the roof loggia were rare, always carefully arranged—because Bardo disliked being left alone. Tito knew something urgent must have caused this unexpected departure.

She saw him through her veil and quickened her pace.

“Romola,” he said, turning to walk beside her, “has anything happened?”

She did not answer at once. Monna Brigida spoke instead. “Ah, Messer Tito, you do well to turn around—we’re in haste. And isn’t it unfortunate? We must go round by the walls and up the Via del Maglio because of the Fair. The peasants block the road near the Nunziata, which would have taken us to San Marco in half the time.”

Tito’s heart leapt, pounding hard. “Romola,” he whispered, “are you going to San Marco?”

They had passed the Borgo Pinti and were now beneath the city walls, gardens stretching to their left, the air calm and still. Romola lifted her veil to breathe freely, and Tito saw the quiet turmoil in her face.

“Yes, Tito mio,” she said, looking directly at him with sorrowful eyes. “For the first time, I am doing something unknown to my father. I’m glad I met you—now I can tell you. But if you’re going to see him, please don’t say you saw me. He thinks I’m visiting my cousin, who sent for me. I left my godfather with him—he knows where I’m going and why. Do you remember the evening when my brother’s name came up, and my father spoke of him to you?”

“Yes,” Tito murmured.

A storm of emotions surged within him. His hopes sank at the thought of a great change, yet his mind raced through what he might do when it came. And still, he looked at her with a desperate hunger—this might be the last time she gazed at him with full trust.

The cousin had heard he had returned. The night before—San Giovanni’s Eve—he had been seen by Maso near their house. When Maso checked at San Marco, Dino—her brother, baptized Bernardino after their godfather, now known as Fra Luca—had been moved to Fiesole due to illness. But this morning, a message arrived: he was back at San Marco. Maso went to him.

He is gravely ill, and he begged me to come. I cannot refuse, though I hold him guilty. I still remember loving him as a child, before I knew he would abandon my father. Perhaps he has words of repentance for me to carry. It cost me much to act against my father’s wishes—which I’ve always believed just. I’m almost certain you’ll think I’ve done right, Tito, because I’ve noticed your nature is softer than mine. You don’t grow angry easily. Forgiveness comes easier to you. Though—if you had seen your father abandoned by one to whom he gave his deepest love, one in whom he planted all his labor and hope—abandoned when he needed him most—even you, Tito, would find forgiveness difficult.”

What could he say? He couldn’t pretend such betrayals should be forgiven. Nor could he speak against her choice.

“You are right, my Romola. You are always right—except in thinking too well of me.”

There was truth in those words. Tito looked pale, his lip trembling slightly as he spoke. Romola, seeing everything magnified by her high ideals, felt a tearful brightness in her eyes—pleased that he felt so deeply as she did.

Yet she did not pause. “Now, Tito, I ask you to leave us. My cousin and I will be less noticed if we enter the piazza alone.”

“Yes,” Monna Brigida said, “it would be better. To tell the truth, Messer Tito, everyone watches you. No matter how Romola hides herself, people want to see what lies beneath her veil—she walks like a procession. Not that I blame her—only, it doesn’t suit my pace. And honestly, I’d rather not be seen going to San Marco. That’s why I’m dressed like one of the Piagnoni themselves, as old as Sant’ Anna. For if it weren’t poor Dino—someone who ought to be forgiven if dying—what good is holding a grudge against the dead? Make them feel it while they live, I say.”

No one interrupted Monna Brigida. Tito had just kissed Romola’s hand and whispered, “I understand. I obey you.” Then he turned away, lifting his cap—a gesture of reverence rarely made by Florentines, which Bernardo del Nero would scorn as Greek fawning, but which Romola, who cherished honor, found deeply noble.

He was half glad to be dismissed, half wishing to cling to her until the last moment she loved him without suspicion. For he knew—this brother would surely want to know everything. Romola would tell him of her father’s condition, of her own life, of the years that had changed both. She would speak of her coming betrothal to a young scholar meant to fill the role long left vacant by a wandering son.

And when she named the husband… Fra Luca would reveal all he knew, all he suspected. Tito saw no way to lie now—no falsehood could save him.

His future in Florence was over. Messer Bernardo del Nero would rejoice, confirming his advice to delay the betrothal until Tito’s character and standing were proven. And the tale of the young Greek professor whose patron was enslaved would spread beneath every loggia.

For the first time, Tito felt too agitated to control himself. He abandoned his visit to Bardo and wandered beneath the city walls, pacing until the western light faded entirely. Then, without purpose, he turned down the first street—the Via San Sebastiano—leading directly toward the Piazza dell’Annunziata.

He stood at one of those reckless moments when desire rules, and the path ahead suddenly closes. He was ready to follow any impulse that offered him an immediate purpose.
