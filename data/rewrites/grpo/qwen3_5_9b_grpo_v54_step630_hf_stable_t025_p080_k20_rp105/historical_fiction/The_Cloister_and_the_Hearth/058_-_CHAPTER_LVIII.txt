Gerard walked in silence beside Teresa, his mind wandering as artists often do, speculating on her intentions rather than daring to ask. Finally, she spoke of her own volition. A friend had informed her of a goldsmith's wife seeking a writer; her shop was nearby.

Soon they arrived at the goldsmith's dwelling.

"Madama," Teresa said, "Leonora tells me you require a writer. I have brought you a fine one; he saved my child from the cruel waves. Pray, look upon him with favor."

The goldsmith's wife, in a sense, complied. Her gaze lingered on Gerard's handsome face, reluctant to withdraw. Yet her reply was disappointing. "Nay, I have no use for a writer. Ah! Now I recall, it was my gossip, Claelia the sausage-maker, who sought one. She told me, and I passed it to Leonora."

Teresa offered a polite remark and withdrew.

Claelia lived further away. Upon reaching her home, she was absent. Teresa remained calm, stating, "I shall wait for her return," and sat so still, so dignified, that Gerard began to draw her sketch furtively until Claelia appeared.

"Madama, I hear from the excellent Olympia, the goldsmith's wife, that you need a writer," Claelia said, taking Gerard's hand and leading him forward. "I have brought you a beautiful one; he rescued my child from the treacherous sea. For our Lady's sake, look kindly upon him."

"My good dame, my fair Sir," Claelia replied, "I have no need for a writer; but now that you remind me, it was my friend Appia Claudia, a tailor living in the Via Lepida, who requested one recently."

Teresa retired quietly.

"Madama," Gerard interjected, "this seems likely to be a tedious pursuit for you."

Teresa opened her eyes. "What has ever been accomplished without a measure of patience?" she added gently. "We shall knock at every door in Rome, and you shall receive justice."

"But, madama, I believe we are being followed. I noticed a man trailing us, sometimes at a distance, sometimes close."

"I have seen it," Teresa said coldly, though her cheeks flushed faintly. "It is my poor Lodovico."

She paused, turned, and beckoned with her finger. A figure approached them, somewhat unwillingly.

Upon seeing him, Teresa gazed steadily into his face, and he looked sheepish.

"Lodovico mio," she said, "know this young Sir, of whom I have spoken so often. Know him and love him, for he is the one who saved your wife and child."

At these words, Lodovico, who had been bowing and grinning artificially, suddenly transformed into an expression of heartfelt gratitude and embraced Gerard warmly.

Yet, there was something in the man's initial demeanor, and the fact that he had followed his wife in secret, that made Gerard uneasy under this embrace. Nevertheless, he asked, "Shall we have your company, Sir Lodovico?"

"No, signor," Lodovico replied, "I do not go that way across the Tiber."

"Addio, then," Teresa said significantly.

"When shall you return home, Teresa mia?"

"When I have completed my errand, Lodovico."

They continued their journey in silence. Teresa now wore a sad, almost gloomy expression.

Briefly, Appia Claudia proved merciful. She did not send them across the river again, but merely a hundred yards down the street to Lucretia, who kept a glove shop. Lucretia also required a writer, though Appia Claudia could not fathom the purpose. Lucretia was a cheerful little lady who received them warmly, declaring she needed no writer as she kept all her accounts in her head. "It was for my confessor, Father Colonna; he is mad after them," she explained.

"I have heard of his excellence," Teresa remarked.

"Who has not?"

"But, good dame, he is a friar; he has taken a vow of poverty. I cannot allow the young man to write without payment. He saved my child at sea."

"Did he now?" Lucretia cast an approving glance at Gerard. "Well, rest assured; a Colonna never lacks funds. The good father need only speak the word, and the princes of his lineage will pour a thousand crowns into his lap. And what a confessor, dame! The finest in Rome. His mind is leagues and leagues away all the while; he never heeds what you say. Why, I confess my sins to him no more than I would tell them to that wall. Once, to test him, I confessed, along with the others, that I had killed my lodger's little girl and baked her in a pie. Well, when my voice ceased confessing, he startled out of his dream, gathered a gloom, and said, 'My erring sister, say three Paternosters and three Ave Marias kneeling, and eat no butter nor eggs next Wednesday, and pax vobiscum!' Then off he went with his hands behind him, looking as though I did not exist."

Teresa waited patiently, then calmly steered this discursive lady back to the point: "Would you be so kind as to accompany this good youth to the friar and speak for him?"

"Alack! How can I leave my shop? And what need? His door is always open to writers, painters, and scholars, and all such cattle. Why, once he refused to receive the Duke d'Urbino because a learned Greek was closeted with him; the friar's head and the Greek's were so close over a dusty parchment from Greece that you could have placed one cowl over both. His wench Onesta told me. She mostly comes here for a chat when she runs an errand."

"This is the man for thee, my friend," Teresa said.

"All you must do," Lucretia continued, "is go to his lodgings—my boy shall show you—and tell Onesta you come from me and are a writer, and she will take you up to him. If you place a piece of silver in the wench's hand, it will do you no harm; that stands to reason."

"I have silver," Teresa said warmly.

"But stay," Lucretia cautioned, "mind one thing. Whatever the young man claims he can do, he must be able to do, or let him shun the good friar like poison. He is a very wild beast against all bunglers. Why, 'twas but the other day, someone brought him an ill-carved crucifix. He said, 'Is this how you present "Salvator Mundi"? Who died for you in mortal agony, and you grudge him careful work? This slovenly trinket, a crucifix? But since it is a crucifix of some sort, and I am a holy man, I would dust your jacket with your crucifix,' he declared. Onesta heard every word through the keyhole; so mind."

"Have no fears, madama," Teresa said loftily. "I will answer for his ability; he saved my child."

Gerard was not subtle enough to appreciate this conclusion; indeed, he was far from sharing Teresa's confidence and begged for a respite. He would rather not go to the friar today; would tomorrow not suffice?

"Here is a coward for ye," Lucretia said.

"No, he is not a coward," Teresa snapped, "he is modest."

"I am afraid of this high-born, fastidious friar," Gerard admitted. "Consider he has seen the handiwork of all the writers in Italy, dear dame Teresa. If you would but let me prepare a better piece of work than I have done yet, and then tomorrow I will face him."

"I consent," Teresa said.

They walked home together.

Not far from his lodging was a shop selling vellum. A beautiful white skin hung in the window. Gerard looked at it wistfully but knew he could not afford it, so he hurried past. However, he soon decided where to obtain vellum. Parting with Teresa at his own door, he ran hastily upstairs, took the bond he had brought all the way from Sevenbergen, and laid it with a sigh on the table. He then prepared his chemicals to erase the old writing; but since this was his last chance to read it, he overcame his deadly repugnance to bad handwriting and proceeded to decipher the deed despite its detestable contractions. It appeared that Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was to advance money to Floris Brandt on a piece of land, to be repaid from the rent.

On this, Gerard felt it would be imprudent and improper to destroy the deed. On the contrary, he vowed to decipher every word at his leisure. He went downstairs, determined to buy a small piece of vellum with his share of the card-money.

At the bottom of the stairs, he found the landlady and Teresa talking. At the sight of him, the former cried, "Here he is. You are caught, donna mia. See what she has bought you?" and whipped out from under her apron the very skin of vellum Gerard had longed for.

"Why, dame! Why, donna Teresa!" He was speechless with pleasure and astonishment.

"Dear donna Teresa, there is not a skin in all Rome like it. However did you hit upon this one? 'Tis glamour."

"Alas, dear boy, did not thine eye rest on it with desire? And didst thou not sigh in turning away from it? And was it for Teresa to let thee want the thing after that?"

"What sagacity! What goodness, madama! Oh, dame, I never thought I should possess this. What did you pay for it?"

"I forget. Addio, Fiammina. Addio, Ser Gerard. Be happy, be prosperous, as you are good." And the Roman matron glided away while Gerard hesitated, thinking how to offer such a stately creature payment for her purchase.

The next afternoon, he went to Lucretia, and her boy took him to Fra Colonna's lodgings. He announced his business, fed Onesta, and she took him up to the friar. Gerard entered with a beating heart. The room was large, strewed and heaped with objects of art, antiquity, and learning, lying in rich profusion and confusion. Manuscripts, pictures, carvings in wood and ivory, musical instruments; and amidst this glorious chaos sat the friar, poring intently over an Arabian manuscript.

He looked up a little peevishly at the interruption. Onesta whispered in his ear.

"Very well," he said. "Let him be seated. Stay; young man, show me how you write?" He threw Gerard a piece of paper and pointed to an inkhorn.

"So please you, reverend father," Gerard said, "my hand trembles too much at this moment; but last night I wrote a vellum page of Greek, and the Latin version by its side, to show the various characters."

"Show it me?"

Gerard brought the work to him in fear and trembling, then stood heart-sick, awaiting his verdict.

When it came, it staggered him. For the verdict was a Dominican falling upon his neck.

The next day, an event occurred in Holland whose effect on Gerard's destiny no mortal at the time, nor even my intelligent reader now, could foresee.

Marched up to Eli's door a pageant brave to the eye of sense and to the vulgar judgment noble, but to the philosophic, pitiable more or less.

It looked like one animal, a centaur; but on severe analysis proved two. The human half was sadly bedizened with those two metals with which man clothes his carcass and lines his pouch, occasionally disposing of his soul; still, the horse was the vainer brute of the two. He was far worse beflounced, bebonneted, and bemantled than any fair lady reigning in crinoline. For the man, under the color of a warming-pan, retained Nature's outline. But it was *subaudi equum*! Scarce a pennyweight of honest horse-flesh to be seen. Our crinoline spares the noble parts of women and makes but the baser parts gigantic (why this preference?); but this poor animal from stem to stern was swamped in finery. His ears were hidden in great sheaths of white linen tipped with silver and blue. His body was swaddled in stiff, gorgeous cloths descending to the ground, except just in front, where they left him room to mince. His tail, though dear to memory, no doubt, was lost to sight, being tucked in heaven knows how. Only his eyes shone out like goggles through two holes pierced in the wall of haberdashery, and his little front hoofs peeped in and out like rats.

Yet did this compound, gorgeous and irrational, represent power; absolute power. It came straight from a tournament at the Duke's court, which, being on a progress, lay the previous night at a neighboring town—to execute the behests of royalty.

"What ho!" cried the upper half, and on Eli emerging, with his wife behind him, saluted them. "Peace be with you, good people. Rejoice! I am come for your dwarf."

Eli looked amazed and said nothing. But Catherine screamed over his shoulder, "You have mistaken your road, good man; here abides no dwarf."

"Nay, wife, he means our Giles, who is somewhat small of stature: why gainsay what gainsayed may not be?"

"Ay!" cried the pageant, "that is he, and discourseth like the big taber."

"His breast is sound for that matter," said Catherine sharply.

"And prompt with his fists though at long odds."

"Else how would the poor thing keep his head in such a world as this?"
