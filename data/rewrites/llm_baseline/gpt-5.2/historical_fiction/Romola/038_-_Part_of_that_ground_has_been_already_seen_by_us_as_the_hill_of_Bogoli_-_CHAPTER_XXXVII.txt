Romola was wakened by a light tapping—timid as a moth’s wing—at her chamber-door. The cold, unseasoned light of early morning lay flat in the room, turning every surface to a pale truth. Maso had come for the travelling-wallet.

When she opened, the old man started as if a draught had struck him. He had been used to the graceful outline crowned with her shining hair; now there were only the thick folds of a grey mantle and, beneath the dark cowl, a face blanched and shadowed, as if night had not quite released it.

“It is well, Maso,” she said, forcing steadiness into her voice, as one might hold a knife firm while the hand trembles. “Here is the wallet—quite ready. You will go on quietly, and I shall not be far behind you. When you get out of the gates you may go more slowly, for I shall perhaps join you before you get to Trespiano.”

She closed the door on him gently, as though even a latch might make too loud a sound in a house still asleep. Then her fingers sought the key she had taken from the casket as the last act of the night.

It was the original key of the little painted tabernacle. Tito had forgotten to drown it in the Arno. Such small things lodge themselves where the eye does not think to look—caught in a corner of the embroidered scarsella he had worn with his purple tunic. Long after their marriage Romola had found it there; she had put it by, not using it, but keeping a quiet, inward satisfaction that the means of opening was within reach.

The cabinet on which the tabernacle stood had been moved close to a window; the wan morning made the painted forms just discernible to her, who knew them by heart: triumphant Bacchus with his clusters and vine-clad spear, clasping crowned Ariadne; Loves showering roses; a wreathed vessel; dolphins with cunning eyes; and the rippled sea, all circled by a border of flowers like a bower promised by some gentler world.

She looked at those familiar images and felt a new bitterness, sharper because of the chill. The pictures were not a tomb where sorrow might lie quiet; they were a screen that had lied. Foolish Ariadne—gazing up as if that bright face with hyacinthine curls, tendril-like among the vines, held the deep secret of her life.

“Ariadne is wonderfully transformed,” Romola thought. “She would look strange among the vines and roses now.”

She took up the mirror and looked once more at herself. The morning light made the sight so strange—so like the outline of a figure already withdrawn from its former name—that she set the mirror down again, shrinking, almost as strongly as she had shrunk from the joyous Ariadne.

For the cowl around her face brought back a dread that had haunted her since childhood: the dread of being drawn, at last, into fellowship with what she had always despised—into the company of howling fanatics and weeping nuns. She had wrapped herself in their outward sign, and felt it like a garment that did not belong to her skin.

Hastily—too hastily to allow herself a second thought—she thrust the key into the tabernacle. It turned. She opened it and took out the crucifix without looking; her fingers trembled as they found the little ring. She passed a cord through it, hung it round her neck, and hid it within the bosom of her mantle as one hides a spark under ashes.

“For Dino’s sake,” she said inwardly. That name, like an old dialect word spoken in the dark, carried meanings no dominant speech could translate.

Still, there were letters to be written—letters Maso was to carry back from Bologna. She wrote them briefly, as if too many words would only make a louder echo.

The first was to Tito:

“Tito, my love for you is dead; and therefore, so far as I was yours, I too am dead. Do not try to put in force any laws for the sake of fetching me back: that would bring you no happiness. The Romola you married can never return. I need explain nothing to you after the words I uttered to you the last time we spoke long together. If you supposed them to be words of transient anger, you will know now that they were the sign of an irreversible change.

“I think you will fulfil my wish that my bridal chest should be sent to my godfather, who gave it me. It contains my wedding-clothes and the portraits and other relics of my father and mother.”

She folded the ring inside that letter—as if sealing a vow turned to ash—and wrote Tito’s name outside.

The next letter was to Bernardo del Nero:

“Dearest Godfather,—If I could have been any good to your life by staying I would not have gone away to a distance. But now I am gone. Do not ask the reason; and if you love my father, try to prevent any one from seeking me. I could not bear my life at Florence. I cannot bear to tell any one why. Help to cover my lot in silence. I have asked that my bridal chest should be sent to you: when you open it, you will know the reason. Please to give all the things that were my mother’s to my cousin Brigida, and ask her to forgive me for not saying any words of parting to her.

“Farewell, my second father. The best thing I have in life is still to remember your goodness and be grateful to you.

“Romola.”

Romola placed the letters, along with the crucifix, within the bosom of her mantle. The paper lay against her as bread might lie against a traveller’s ribs—thin, necessary, carrying the last taste of home. Now everything was done. She was ready to depart.

No one stirred in the house. She went down the stairs almost as quietly as a grey phantom and out into the silent street. Her heart beat violently, yet there was a strange enjoyment in the firmness of her tread on the broad flags—in the swiftness of movement, like a chained resolution at last released.

Anxiety to carry through the act, and dread of hindrance, kept sorrow at bay. When she reached the Ponte Rubaconte, she felt less that Santa Croce was near than that the yellow seam of morning, splitting the grey, was widening; and unless she hastened, she must meet faces—voices—questions. She was keenly aware, too, of the city’s waking sounds beginning to thicken: a shutter lifted, a distant hinge complaining, the first hollow roll of a cart somewhere far off. Florence, even before it spoke, prepared to be loud.

Her simplest road led on to the Borgo Pinti and then along by the walls to the Porta San Gallo. That path took her by the Piazza di Santa Croce. She walked through it steadily, rapidly, not trusting herself to look towards the church. It was not devotion that pulled at her, but the fear of being seen: the thought that eyes might turn on her with curiosity and recognition, and that indifferent minds might begin to speculate on her private sorrows. The very idea felt like a physical torture. She felt degraded even by that act of her husband from which she suffered helplessly—degraded because the shame was public the moment it became known, and Florence was a city where talk moved like wind through streets.

But no windows opened to watch the tall grey sister with the firm step and the proud carriage of the cowled head. Her way lay aside from the early traffic; and when she reached the Porta San Gallo it was easy to pass, for a dispute was going forward about the toll on panniers of eggs and other market-produce just entering. The argument rose and fell—voices sharp, then muffled—like the cluck and scold of a waking farmyard. It made a veil of sound behind which she slipped unnoticed.

Out. Once past the houses of the Borgo she would be beyond Florence’s last fringe; the sky would widen above her, and she would have entered on her new life: loneliness and endurance, but freedom. She had been strong enough to snap the bonds she had accepted in blind faith. Whatever befell, she would no more feel the breath of soft hated lips warm upon her cheek; no longer feel the breath of an odious mind stifling her own.

The bare wintry morning and chill air were welcome in their severity. Leafless trees and sombre hills did not wear the masks of beauty and joy—the gods she had forsaken for ever. Yet presently the light burst forth with sudden strength, flinging shadows across the road, as if the sun would chase away all greyness by force.

In such moments light is felt like a divine presence—not as a thought, but as something that stirs the deepest, inarticulate sensibilities. It awakens the shadows instantaneously, and with them a kind of awe. So the great momentousness of her act became more conscious as she walked in the impalpable golden glory—and saw, stretched before her, the long shadow of herself that could not be escaped.

Hitherto she had met no one but an occasional contadino with mules; and the many level turnings had prevented her from seeing that Maso was not far ahead. But when she had passed Pietra and began to rise, she lifted the hanging roof of her cowl and looked eagerly forward.

She let it drop again at once.

She had seen, not Maso, but two monks approaching within a few yards. The edge of the cowl had made a pent-house on her brow; for the last minutes she had been looking only at the brightness on the path and her own tall shrouded shadow like a dread spectre. Now she wished she had not looked up.

Her disguise made her especially dislike encountering monks. They might expect pious passwords, some accustomed phrase, some dialect of devotion she did not know how to speak; and she walked on with a careful appearance of unconsciousness until the black skirts passed by her. Their footsteps went down-hill and faded quickly; but the meeting had set her heart beating in a new, uneasy way. There was shame in this studied concealment, and it grew sharper under actual glances when she was striving to seem unaware.

Seeing a great flat stone against a cypress that rose from a projecting green bank, she yielded to the desire—born of the slight shock—to sit and rest. She turned her back on Florence, not meaning to look that way until the monks were quite out of sight. When she was seated she raised the edge of her cowl again and discerned Maso and the mules at a distance where it was not hopeless to overtake them; the old man would likely linger, expecting her.

Meanwhile she might pause a little.

She was free and alone.
