The utility of a Colonel without a regiment is nil. A mathematical zero. The Governor and Council who had performed their ceremonial gratitude for endowing me with this title were absent, skulking aboard ships, engaging in occasional piracy and arson on the shore. My Lord Dunmore's Black allies had frightened away those of his own blood; furthermore, rumors circulated that he had dispatched envoys to the Southern Indians, promising them numbers and tomahawks to reduce our people to good behavior. "And these are to be our allies!" I said to my mother, exchanging ominous glances with her, recalling with ghastly clarity the savage whose face had glared over mine, whose knife had been at my throat when Florac struck him down on Braddock's Field. We placed Castlewood in a state of defense as good as we could devise; yet, in truth, it was the Red Men and the Blacks we feared, not the Rebels. I had never seen my mother lose courage except once, when she recounted the particulars of our father's death in a foray of Indians more than forty years prior. Seeing figures move before our house one night, nothing persuaded the good lady but that they were savages, and she sank to her knees, crying, "The Lord have mercy upon us! The Indians—the Indians!"

My Lord's Negro allies vanished onto his ships or wherever pay and plunder could be found; but the painted heroes from the South never appeared, though I confess to looking at my mother's grey head, my wife's brown hair, and our child's golden ringlets with a horrible pang of doubt lest these should fall victims to ruffian war. It was we who fought with such weapons, and enlisted these allies! Yet I dare not set myself up as an interpreter of Providence, pointing out the special finger of Heaven as many are wont to do. I would simply say that our employment of these Indians and the German mercenaries brought their own retribution in this war. In the field, where the mercenaries were attacked by the Provincials, they yielded, and it was triumphing over them that raised the spirit of the Continental army; and the murder of one woman, Miss McCrea, by a half-dozen drunken Indians, did more harm to the Royal cause than the loss of a battle or the destruction of regiments.

Once the Indian panic subsided, Madam Esmond's courage returned. She became seriously, and not unjustly, uneasy at the danger I ran myself, and the danger I brought upon others, by remaining in Virginia.

"What harm can they do me," she said, "a poor woman? If I have one son a colonel without a regiment, I have another with a couple of hundred Continentals behind him in Mr. Washington's camp. If the Royalists come, they will let me off for your sake; if the rebels appear, I shall have Harry's passport. I do not wish, sir, I do not like that your delicate wife and this dear little baby should be here, and only increase the risk of all of us! We must have them away to Boston or New York. Do not talk about defending me! Who will think of hurting a poor, harmless, old woman? If the rebels come, I shall shelter behind Mrs. Fanny's petticoats, and shall be much safer without you in the house than in it." She said this partly because it was reasonable, and more so because she wished to remove me and my family from danger; yet, regardless of the peril, she felt determined to remain in the land where her father was buried and where she was born. She was living backwards, so to speak. She had seen the new generation, blessed them, and bade them farewell. She belonged to the past, to old days and memories.

While we debated the Boston scheme, news arrived that the British had evacuated that luckless city altogether, never having ventured to attack Mr. Washington in his camp at Cambridge (though he lay there for many months without powder at our mercy). Instead, they waited until he procured ammunition, seized and fortified Dorchester Heights, which commanded the town, forcing the whole British army and colony to beat a retreat. That the King's troops won the battle at Bunker's Hill is no more doubtful than that they beat the French at Blenheim; yet throughout the war, their chiefs seemed constantly afraid of assaulting entrenched Continentals afterward. Else, why, from July to March, hesitate to strike an almost defenseless enemy? Why the hesitation at Long Island, when the Continental army was in our hand? Why that astonishing timidity—of Howe before Valley Forge, where the relics of a force starving, sickening, and in rags could scarcely man the lines they held before a great, victorious, and perfectly appointed army?

As the hopes and fears of the contending parties rose and fell, it was curious to mark the altered tone of the partisans. When news of the evacuation of Boston reached us in the countryside, every little Whig in the neighborhood made his bow to Madam and advised her to a speedy submission. She did not carry her loyalty quite so openly as heretofore, nor flaunt her flag in the faces of the public, but she never swerved. Every night and morning in private, poor Hagan prayed for the Royal Family in our own household; and on Sundays, any neighbors were welcome to attend the service, where my mother acted as a very emphatic clerk, delivering the prayer for the High Court of Parliament under our most religious and gracious King with stout conviction. The brave Hagan was a parson without a living, as I was a Militia Colonel without a regiment. Hagan had continued to pray stoutly for King George in Williamsburg long after His Excellency, our Governor, had run away. But on coming to church one Sunday to perform his duty, he found a corporal's guard at the church-door who told him that the Committee of Safety had put another divine in his place and requested he keep a quiet tongue in his head. He told the men to "lead him before their chiefs"—our honest friend always loved tall words and tragic attitudes—and accordingly was marched through the streets to the Capitol, with a chorus of white and colored blackguards at the skirts of his gown. He had an interview with Messrs. Henry and the new State officers, confronting the robbers, as he said, in their den. Of course, he intended to make an heroic speech before these gentlemen (he was one of many men who perhaps would have no objection to being made martyrs so that they might be roasted *coram populo*, or tortured in a full house), but Mr. Henry was determined to give him no such chance. After keeping Hagan three or four hours waiting in an anteroom in the company of negroes, when the worthy divine entered the new chief magistrate's room with an undaunted mien and began a prepared speech—"Sir, by what authority am I, a minister of the----" "Mr. Hagan," said the other, interrupting him, "I am too busy to listen to speeches. And as for King George, he has henceforth no more authority in this country than King Nebuchadnezzar. Mind you that, and hold your tongue, if you please! Stick to King John, sir, and King Macbeth; and if you will send round your benefit-tickets, all the Assembly shall come and hear you. Did you ever see Mr. Hagan on the boards, when you was in London, General?" So saying, Henry turned round upon Mr. Washington's second in command, General Lee, who was now come into Virginia upon State affairs, and our shamefaced good Hagan was bustled out of the room, reddening, and almost crying with shame. After this event, we thought that Hagan's ministrations were best confined to us in the country, and removed the worthy pastor from his restive lambs in the city.

The selection of Virginians to the very highest civil and military appointments of the new government bribed and flattered many of our leading people, who, otherwise, and but for the outrageous conduct of our government, might have remained faithful to the Crown and made good head against the rising rebellion. But, although we Loyalists were gagged and muzzled, though the Capitol was in the hands of the Whigs, and our vaunted levies of loyal recruits were so many Falstaff's regiments for the most part, the faithful still kept intelligences with one another in the colony and with our neighbors. Although we did not rise, and although we ran away, and although, in examination before committees, justices, and so forth, some of our frightened people gave themselves Republican airs and vowed perdition to kings and nobles, yet we knew each other pretty well. According as the chances were more or less favorable to us, the master more or less hard, we concealed our colors, showed our colors, half-showed our colors, or downright apostatized for the nonce, crying, "Down with King George!" Our negroes bore about, from house to house, all sorts of messages and tokens. Endless underhand plots and schemes were engaged in by those who could not afford the light. The battle over, the neutrals came and joined the winning side, shouting as loudly as the patriots. The runaways were not counted. Would any man tell me that the signers and ardent well-wishers of the Declaration of Independence were not in a minority of the nation, and that the minority did not win? We knew that a part of the defeated army of Massachusetts was about to make an important expedition southward, upon the success of which the very greatest hopes were founded.
