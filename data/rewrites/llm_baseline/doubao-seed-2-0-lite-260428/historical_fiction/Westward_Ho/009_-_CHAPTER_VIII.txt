“It is virtue, yea virtue, gentlemen, that maketh gentlemen; that maketh the poor rich, the base-born noble, the subject a sovereign, the deformed beautiful, the sick whole, the weak strong, the most miserable most happy. There are two principal and peculiar gifts in the nature of man, knowledge and reason; the one commandeth, and the other obeyeth: these things neither the whirling wheel of fortune can change, neither the deceitful cavillings of worldlings separate, neither sickness abate, neither age abolish.” -- LILLY's *Euphues*, 1586.

It falls to me now to tell the story of the founding of that chivalrous, storied Brotherhood of the Rose: a band that within a handful of years would win renown across their native Devon, and strike fear into enemies across Ireland, the Netherlands, the Spanish Main, and the uncharted heart of South America. Should any reader dismiss this chapter as quixotic, over-fantastical nonsense, let them pause to remember: the generation of Elizabethan men who spoke and acted this way over matters of love and honor were also seasoned, fearless soldiers, shrewd and far-sighted politicians. The man who wrote *Arcadia* was, for all his youth, one of the sharpest, most subtle diplomats in all of Europe. The poet who penned *The Faerie Queene* also drafted the sober, hard-headed *A View of the Present State of Ireland*. If any sneer and quote Lily’s *Euphues* at me like it is nothing but foppish nonsense, I will only ask: have they ever read it? If they have, they would find it, for all its occasional tedious pedantry, as brave, righteous, and pious a book as any man could hope to read. I ask no better proof of the nobility of the Elizabethan age than the simple fact that *Euphues* and *Arcadia* were the most popular romances of the day.

Sir Walter Scott may have used the foppish Sir Piercie Shafton to mock the Euphuists and the performative courtliness that travelled Englishmen of the era adopted, but that character is riddled with anachronisms, not least that the Euphuist knight in Scott’s story references Sidney’s duel with Lord Oxford a full ten years before it ever happened. Even more to the point: that absurd fop bears no resemblance to the earnest readers of Lily’s work. His true spiritual ancestors are the quarrelsome Gabriel Harvey and Lord Oxford, not the earnest young Devon lads of our story.

Let us stop sneering at these old Elizabethan gallants, and see them for what they are: our own ancestors, wild with the fire of youth, already bearing all the core virtues that make a true Englishman. Their daring on the sea and on the battlefield, their sharp political wit, their deep reverence for the law, their solemn belief in the great purpose of the English nation: all these are the ancestors of those same traits that live in us still. Their famous chivalry is nothing but a different, older wrapping for that same tenderness and mercy that still stands as the twin of English valor today. Even their eagerness to learn from Continental manners and literature speaks to that old Anglo-Norman curiosity and open-heartedness that has let us absorb the wisdom and civilization of every age and every land, never losing our own distinct national character in the process.

So on with the story. If any reader does not care for it, they are welcome to turn the page to a tale that suits their fancy better.

Amyas could not sail the next day, nor the day after that: a stiff southwester gale was blowing dead against the bay, and the crew had only just finished moving his ship *Mary Grenville* down the river to Appledore Pool, anchored and ready to cast off the second the wind shifted. He went home quietly, and when his mother set off riding pillion behind their old serving-man to visit Clovelly where Frank lay wounded, he escorted them as far as Bideford. There on the High Street he met a procession of horsemen led by Will Cary, clad head to toe in shining polished armor, his sword at his thigh and his helmet slung from his saddlebow: as gallant a young gentleman as any Bideford housewife had ever gawked at through a door or window. Behind him on shaggy country ponies rode four stout serving-men, carrying lances, baggage, longbows, swords, and bucklers, and at the very back, much to Mrs. Leigh’s joy, Frank himself, lying comfortable in a horse-litter.

Frank insisted his wounds were only shallow flesh cuts, the attacker’s dagger having glanced off his ribs. He refused to go home to Burrough, and said he would lodge instead with Will Cary at the Ship Tavern, right at the foot of Bideford Bridge, so he could see Amyas off on his voyage before he left. No sooner was he settled on a couch there than he held open court, gossips of the whole town filing in to call on him, spinning all kinds of little white lies about who could have possibly put him in such a sorry state.

While the crowd milled around them, Frank and Amyas hatched a plan that they put into action the very next day, market day. First Amyas gave the innkeeper orders to start a bustle of roasting, boiling, and frying the likes of which the Ship Tavern had never seen in all its history. Then Amyas wandered out into the market, and invited every single one of Rose Salterne’s known suitors to a private, merry supper, one by one, each man thinking he was the only honored guest.

Each man arrived expecting an intimate, exclusive feast, and found himself seated at the same table with half a dozen rivals for the Rose’s hand, men he had not spoken to in half a year out of petty jealousy. All were far too well-bred to let the Leighs see how furious they were, though. Frank propped himself up on his couch at the head of the table, Amyas took the seat at the foot, and the two of them plied their guests with such heaps of good food that for a long no one had to make awkward small talk. The two hosts joked, swapped tales, and carried the whole conversation so easily that Will Cary, who never could resist a good laugh, cracked the first jest, then another, and soon he was teasing the tavern waiters so mercilessly that the men stumbled out of the room holding their sides, bursting with laughter.

By the time the table was cleared and the sack and sugar were passed around, the whole company had drunk the toast “Queen and Bible” with all the proper ceremony. Frank made his first move.

“Gentlemen, I have a toast for you. To the gentlemen of the Irish wars! May Ireland never lack a St. Leger to stand by a Fortescue, a Fortescue to stand by a St. Leger, and a Chichester to stand by both!”

The toast broke the ice further: the three young men from those families each drank to each other’s houses, returned thanks, and the conversation flowed freely. Young Fortescue then raised a toast to “Amyas Leigh, and all bold Devon mariners,” and Amyas replied in his plain, blunt way that he could ask for no better fortune than to sail all the way around the world again with every man at that table as his fellow adventurer, and give the Spaniards another taste of what Devon men were made of.

The wine flowed sweet, every man’s tongue loosened, and the last of the awkward frost melted away. This was the exact moment Frank had been waiting for.

“Gentlemen,” he said, “let me give you a health I know none of you will refuse to drink with all your hearts. A lady whose beauty and virtue are so great that no one notices the lowliness of her birth beside them. A lady I would call peerless in loveliness, if not for the fact that every gentleman here has sisters who could easily challenge her for the prize of Venus’s girdle — and yet those very same fine ladies, who know far more of beauty than I, have openly given her the golden apple in my hearing. If the goddesses themselves vote on the fairest, Paris himself has no business sitting on the judge’s seat. Gentlemen, your hearts already know who I speak of. Drink to the Rose of Torridge!”

Had Rose herself walked through the tavern door, she could not have caused more shock. Every guest turned red, then pale, then red again, and stared at the man next to him as if to say, “What right have you to even speak her name? Lift that glass and I will dash it out of your hand.” But Frank drank on, unflappable.

“To the health of the Rose of Torridge! And a double health to that worthy gentleman, whomever she shall choose to grace with her love!”

“Well played, cunning Frank Leigh!” boomed the blunt, hearty Will Cary. “None of us can quarrel with you now, even if we are all still sulking at each other. Every last one of us here is fool enough to think she likes us best. You just toasted all of us, you rascal!”

“And so I have,” Frank smiled. “What better thing can you do, gentlemen, than drink each other’s healths in turn, and prove yourselves true gentlemen, true Christians, and true lovers? For what is love, but the very breath of that God whose name is Love? The ancients called Eros the eldest of all the gods, the power that turned the clashing chaos into harmony and order. Why then would lovers make strife out of love? The soul cannot be filled with that divine love and then burn with envy and jealousy, division and distrust. The rose has thorns, but it leaves poison and stings to the nettle. Cupid has his arrow, but he hurls no scorpions. Surely anyone who loves rightly will not only find love lovely, they will grow more lovely themselves.”

His rapid, passionate speech had kept every man so stunned none had time to grow angry — until he paused, out of breath from his wounds. Mr. Coffin, the eldest of the country squires, who had sat bolt upright staring at the wall this whole time, got quietly to his feet and made for the door, fed up with what he saw as Frank’s impertinent interference. One more minute, and the old tale of the Lapiths’ bloody wedding feast might well have repeated itself.

But Frank never faltered.

“Mr. Coffin!” he called, in a tone that made the man stop in his tracks and turn around. His face was so tender, so earnest and pleading that no man could have looked at it for five minutes and held a grudge. “My dear Mr. Coffin! If my eagerness made me forget the bounds of courtesy, I beg you to forgive me. Do not add the grief of losing a friend to the heavy griefs I already carry. Only hear me out patiently, and if you are still angry after that, I will beg your forgiveness a second time.”

Coffin, who had never been to court, had always been a little jealous of Frank’s polished court manners, and a little nettled at being lumped in with boys barely eighteen years old. But when Frank humbled himself so openly and threw himself on Coffin’s mercy, the honest, good-natured squire — who had known Frank since they were both toddlers — had no choice but to sit back down again and listen.

“Believe me, gentlemen,” Frank went on, “I do not think myself better than any of you, any more than a sailor washed ashore by the surf thinks he is better than the comrade still fighting the waves. I, too, love her. The sun that dazzled you dazzled me. The lodestone that drew you drew me. But I have learnt to love you all for loving what I love, to admire you for admiring that which I admire. Why should worship of the same beautiful woman not be a bond of friendship between us, just as loyalty to the same queen binds subjects, or devotion to the same God binds worshippers? Why treat each other like enemies, when we are all fellow worshippers? Shall we make ourselves unworthy of her by the very jealousy we think will help us win her? A lady will never favor the man whose hands are stained with the blood of the people she likes. Our only chance of winning her, if she is worth winning, is to honor who she honors, to love who she loves. If we must compete, let us compete in virtue, in loyalty to our queen, in courage against her enemies, in kindness to the afflicted and the oppressed. And why not make this shared love for her the sacrament of our shared friendship? We will vow to abide by her choice, to rejoice in her joy, and whoever she picks for her husband, we will swear to be his loyal, faithful friend. As I do now, openly, before all of you.”

There was a long pause. At last young Fortescue spoke.
“That sounds like a very generous bargain for you, sir — you seem far more likely to win her than any of us country oafs.”

“Set your mind at rest,” Frank laughed. “I have not a scrap more idea what she thinks of any man than you. For my own peace of mind, I have sworn a vow not to see her, or even hear news of her, for three full years. Dixi — I have spoken.”

Coffin stood up again.
“Gentlemen, I may not be able to match Mr. Leigh’s fine words, but I will not be outdone in generosity. If he is leaving this part of the country for three years, so am I.”

“And I am off to Ireland, as Amyas knows, to cool my hot temper in a bog,” Will Cary cried, holding out his hand to Coffin. “Give me your hand, old man! You are heart of oak, not some soft wishy-washy elm plank. I was planning to fight you today, you know —”
“I would have been delighted to oblige you, sir,” Coffin said, bowing.
“But now I am all love and charity to the whole world! Does anyone want to pull my nose? Send me on an errand? Borrow five pounds off me? Buy a horse from me that’s worth twice the asking price? Come on! All of you, join hands, swear eternal friendship. We need a name for this sacred order, Frank Leigh — you’re the clever one, christen us!”

“The Rose,” Frank said quietly, seeing his plan had worked perfectly, determined to seal the moment before anyone could change their mind.

“The Rose!” Cary yelled, grabbing Coffin’s right hand and Fortescue’s left. “Come on, lads, all of you!”

One by one, every man in the room gave in, whether it was Frank’s chivalrous speech, Cary’s infectious good humor, Amyas’s spiced sack, or just the goodness inherent in every young man’s heart if someone will take the trouble to call it out. They joined hands all round, and swore on the hilt of Amyas’s sword that they would no longer make fools of themselves over petty jealousy. They would stand by each other, honor the Rose, and never grumble no matter who she chose to dance with, flirt with, or marry. To spread the fame of the Rose and their brotherhood across the whole world, they swore to get their fathers’ permission (easy enough to win in those bold days) to go off to wars wherever there was fighting to be done abroad, to emulate the courage and courtesy of great knights like Bayard, Sidney, and Walter Manny.

Frank smiled his soft, quiet smile — the kind where his eyes crinkled even when his lips did not. “Gentlemen,” he said, “you will never, ever regret this day.”

“Regret?” Cary snorted. “I already feel as saintly as you look, Saint Silver-Tongue. Wait — was that a sneeze? The cat?”

“More like a lion, by the sound of it,” Amyas said, lunging for the heavy arras hanging on the wall behind him. “There’s a door behind here!”

He ducked through the hidden gap in the tapestry, and dragged out by the scruff of his neck one very sheepish Mr. John Brimblecombe.

You may well remember him: that fat schoolmaster’s son Sir Richard Grenville punished for tale-bearing three years before, sending him off to Oxford instead of Coventry. He was twenty-one now, a Bachelor of Arts, hanging about Bideford before he went back to university after Christmas to study divinity, planning to come home one day and serve as parson for his home parish.

Jack was built exactly like a prize show pig: plump mulberry complexion dotted with a few sparse black bristles, sleek skin that looked like it might burst any second, short waddling legs, a little curved back, a squeaky cracked voice, tiny twinkling eyes, and a little upturned nose that was always sniffing the air for the nearest savory smell, perfectly content to make do with even the worst meal if a better one wasn’t on offer. He’d snuck in behind the arras earlier to beg a bite of the feast, and gotten so caught up in the ceremony he’d accidentally sneezed loud enough to give his hiding place away.
