This is the strangest twist of fate in all our wild, sun-scorched African adventures—proof that the world works in far more wondrous, unguessable ways than any man could dream up.

I was tramping a good twenty yards ahead of Sir Henry and Good, following the thin, gurgling stream that trickles out of the hidden oasis before vanishing completely into the ravenous desert sand. The heat hung thick enough to chew, and I was half in a daze when I stopped dead, rubbed my stinging eyes hard, and thought the sun must have addled my wits. There, perched on a grassy bank under the dappled shade of a wild fig tree, facing out over the water, was a neat little hut woven from withes and grass in the Kafir style, but with a full-sized wooden door instead of the usual tiny, bee-hole entry you find on native huts out in the wilds.

“What in tarnation is a hut doing all the way out here?” I muttered to myself. No hunter would ever wander this far off known trails, much less settle down here. Even as the thought crossed my mind, the hut door creaked open. Out limped a white man, clad head to toe in sewn animal skins, his face half hidden behind a huge, matted black beard.

I stared until my eyes burned. The two men behind me stopped short when I called out, “Hey, you two—tell me I’m not seeing things. Is that a white man, or have I fried my brains in this heat?”

Sir Henry and Good both squinted, then the bearded stranger let out a roar of shock and joy, hobbling as fast as his lame leg would carry him toward us, before crumpling unconscious right at our feet. Sir Henry cleared the distance in one bound, his voice cracking. “Great God in heaven—*that’s my brother George*!”

At the sound of the commotion, a second figure in skins bolted out of the hut, a rifle held high. When he spotted me, he screamed so loud the birds took off from the fig tree. “Macumazahn! Don’t you know me, Baas? I’m Jim the hunter! I lost the note you gave me to pass to Mr. Curtis nearly two years back, and we’ve been stranded here ever since.” He fell to my feet, rolling on the sand sobbing so hard he could barely breathe for happiness.

“You reckless scoundrel,” I said, clapping him on the shoulder instead of giving him the sjambok lashing he deserved.

George Curtis had come round by then, and he and Sir Henry were pumping each other’s hands so hard their knuckles went white, too choked up to get more than half a sentence out at a time. Whatever old spat they’d fallen out over years before (I’d wager it was some lady, though I never asked) was long forgotten, washed away by two years of fear and no hope of ever seeing each other again.

“I thought you were dead,” Sir Henry said at last, clapping his brother so hard on the back he nearly fell over. “I trekked all the way across Solomon’s Mountains to find you. I’d given up every last scrap of hope, and now I find you camped out in this desert like some scrawny old aasvogel.”

George laughed, his voice rough and halting from two years of barely speaking to anyone but Jim. “I tried to cross the range almost two years ago. But once I got here, a loose boulder fell right on my leg and crushed it. I couldn’t go forward, and I couldn’t make the trek back to civilization, either.”

That night, huddled around our campfire, he told us the full story: how he’d left Sitanda’s Kraal two years prior to hunt for the fabled diamond mines, how Jim had lost my warning note before he could ever hand it over. He’d followed native advice to take the steep, ladder-like mountain pass we’d just scrambled down (a far better route than the one marked on old Dom Silvestra’s tattered map) only to get lost in the desert, half dead of thirst, until he stumbled on this hidden oasis. He was sitting on the stream’s edge the day they arrived, Jim scrambling up the bank above him to raid a stingless bee’s honey nest, when Jim dislodged a ton-weight boulder that crushed his right leg to pulp.

They’d survived two whole years on game that came to the oasis to drink at night, shooting antelope and trapping wild ostrich in pitfalls, eating the meat and tanning the hides to replace their worn-out clothes. “Last night we finally agreed Jim would leave me at dawn to try to trek back to Sitanda’s Kraal for help,” George said, shaking his head like he still couldn’t believe we were there. “I never thought he’d make it, and I’d have died here alone. And then you three show up, out of the middle of the empty desert. It’s the kindest, most impossible thing that ever happened to a man.”

We sat up till nearly dawn, telling him of our own adventures: the witch Gagool, the savage king Twala, the hidden cave stacked with uncut diamonds, the trap that nearly sealed us inside forever. When I pulled a handful of the rough stones out of my pocket to show him, he whistled. “Well, you lads got something for your trouble besides dragging my useless hide out of here.”

Sir Henry laughed, and explained our bargain that the diamonds belonged to me and Good. But after a quick word between the two of us, we told him we’d not take the whole haul—we insisted he take a full third share for himself, and the third share that was meant for Sir Henry would go straight to George, who’d suffered more than any of us chasing the mines. We wore him down until he agreed, though we kept the full arrangement secret from George for months after.

The trek back across the desert to Sitanda’s Kraal was brutal, slow going, with us taking turns supporting George’s weak leg that kept spitting out shards of broken bone. But we made it, eventually, and found our old cache of rifles and trade goods completely untouched, much to the disgust of the greedy old native overseer who’d long written us off as dead and planned to steal every last thing. Six months later, we were all safe and sound at my little cottage on the Berea, outside Durban, where I’m sitting now writing this down. It’s the strangest adventure I’ve ever lived through, in all my decades of hunting across southern Africa.

I’d just put the last full stop on the last line of this page when a Kafir runner came up my avenue of orange trees, carrying a letter tucked in the cleft of a stick from the nearest outpost post. It was from Sir Henry, and I’ll print it exactly as he wrote it:

> October 1st, 1884
> Brayley Hall, Yorkshire
>
> My Dear Quatermain,
>
> We all made it back to England safe and sound: George, Good, and I. We docked at Southampton, and you’d barely recognize Good the day after we hit London—shaved so clean you could see your reflection in his chin, brand new frock coat that fits like a second skin, shiny new monocle he’s absurdly proud of. I dragged him for a walk in Hyde Park last week, ran into a crowd of old friends, and told them the whole story about his famous bleached white river-bleached legs. He’s been absolutely furious ever since, especially now some mischief-maker printed the whole silly story in a society paper.
>
> To business: Good and I took all the diamonds to Streeter’s to get them valued. I daren’t even tell you the number they quoted, it’s so staggeringly huge. They say no one’s ever seen a haul of uncut stones like ours hit the market before, all but the two largest gems are perfect water, just as fine as the best Brazilian diamonds out there. They won’t buy the whole lot off us outright, said it would crash the market if we dumped all those stones at once—they recommend selling them slowly, a handful a year, over a whole decade or more. Even for a tiny fraction of the haul, they offered us one hundred and eighty thousand pounds.
>
> You have to come home to England, Quatermain, to sort all this out, especially if you’re dead set on giving that third share of the diamonds to George, who doesn’t have two pennies to rub together. Good’s no help at all right now, he’s too busy primping and preening. He’s still broken up over Foulata, too—said he hasn’t seen a single woman here in England with half her grace, or half her sweetness.
>
> There’s a house for sale a mile down the road from me that’s perfect for you, plenty of space to hang your hunting trophies, room for you to putter about. You’ve more than earned your rest, you old rogue, and you’ve got more than enough money now. Come stay for Christmas—Good’s coming, George is coming, and your boy Harry’s down here too. I took him out shooting last week, he accidentally put a pellet right through my calf, dug it out with a penknife, and then had the cheek to say every shooting party ought to bring a medical student along for first aid. He’s a cool hand, that lad.
>
> We’ve refused to tell anyone our whole story till you write it up properly, no one will believe us otherwise. The sooner you sail the better.
>
> Goodbye, old friend. I know you’ll come.
>
> Yours ever,
> Henry Curtis
>
> P.S. The tusks of the great bull elephant that killed poor Khiva are hung in my hall right now, above the pair of buffalo horns you gave me, and they look magnificent. The axe I chopped Twala’s head off with is nailed above my writing desk. I wish we could have carried those chain mail suits out of the cave with us. Don’t you lose that little basket Foulata wove that you carried the diamonds out in!

Today’s Tuesday. There’s a steamer sailing for Southampton on Friday. I think I’ll take Henry at his word, book my passage, and sail for England. It’s high time I saw my boy Harry again, caught up with my old comrades, and saw this whole strange story printed proper, so no one can ever call it a lie.

Allan Quatermain
