Two weeks of grueling labor had finally yielded results, though the toil was no greater than what they had endured countless times before. Having bid a final farewell to the verdant oceans of the East, the expedition had crossed the Cordilleras and cast a lingering gaze upon Santa Fe, nestled amidst its wealthy gardens upon a lofty plateau. They had wisely recognized the city's magnitude as too formidable for their immediate designs. Yet, their efforts had not been wasted. Their Indian guide had revealed the existence of a gold-train descending from Santa Fe toward the Magdalena River. Thus, they had established a camp beside a miserable rut serving as a road, hidden within a forest of oaks that, save for the ferns forming the undergrowth and the deep gorges yawning beneath them, might have transported them back to Europe. Here, amidst the cool breezes of a temperate zone sweeping their brows, they peered down into the eternal vapor baths of the tropics, where the majestic forms and vibrant hues of the jungle lay shrouded in mist.

Pitched among the tree-ferns, overlooking a steep hillside where a forgotten road descended into a muddy ditch, they sat in wait. Once, perhaps, this path had served the civilized kingdom of Cundinamarca, leaving behind only crumbling steps and the ruins of aqueducts and bridges, swallowed by the frantic search for gold. Civilization had decayed everywhere save in architecture, surviving only in the splendid cathedrals rising over the temples of the Sun—a "milder Pantheon" that nonetheless demanded human sacrifice, a grim contrast to the gentle nature-worship of the Incas. Now, the rapid tropical vegetation had reclaimed its domain, leaving Amyas and his crew as utterly isolated as they would be in the solitudes of the Orinoco, despite being mere miles from a Spanish settlement.

Attempts to procure sulfur and nitre had failed, forcing them to rely on swords and arrows, a contingency Yeo resented, though Amyas reminded him that Drake had taken Nombre de Dios with no better weapons. With the road blocked by a felled tree, the men chewed coca, lacking food and drink, listening to a mysterious roar that had haunted their nights since leaving the Meta. It was neither the snap of a branch nor the cry of a bird or monkey.

"At last!" Yeo exclaimed as a sharp crack echoed above, followed by a loud cry. "That was a whip's crack, and a woman's wail. They are close!"

"A woman?" Amyas asked. "Do they drive women in their gangs?"

"Why not, the brutes? See their basnets glitter!"

"Do not shoot till I give the word," Amyas commanded quietly. "Then one arrow, then swords. Pass the word along."

Slowly, the column emerged from the gloom. About twenty soldiers followed, half on foot, the others borne in chairs upon the backs of single Indians. Those marching had handed their heavy armor and arquebuses to attendant slaves, who were goaded forward by the pikes of the soldiers.

"The men are mad to let their ordnance out of their hands," a soldier noted.

"An Indian will pray to an arquebus not to shoot him," Yeo replied. "Surely their artillery is safe enough."

"Look at the proud villains," whispered another. "Making dumb beasts of human creatures!"

Amyas counted ten shot and ten pikes, assigning tasks to his men. Last came an inferior officer, also seated, who lit cigars at regular intervals to inspire his followers with pious ejaculations to gods, humans, wood, and textiles—an unholy mix that earned the Spaniards the reputation of being both the most fetish-ridden idolaters and the most abominable swearers of Europe.

"But the blasphemous dog!" Yeo muttered, fumbling his bowstring. Amyas restrained him.

Then followed a procession that froze the hearts of the ambushers: a sad and hideous sight, common in those remote districts where humane edicts were ignored. A line of Indians, Negroes, and Zambos—naked, emaciated, scarred, and chained by their left wrists—trudged upward, panting under the burden of baskets strapped across their foreheads. Among them were old men, youths, and women, including slender girls and mothers with children clinging to their knees. Indignation rose from the Englishmen, echoing the free and righteous spirits of Raleigh, who appealed to man and God for the outraged heathens of the New World.

Yet, the first forty bore a burden that overshadowed the misery of their carriers. Each basket held a square package of corded hide, recognizable to Amyas as gold.

"What's in there, captain?" someone asked.

"Gold!" Amyas whispered, his eyes straining forward. "Be men, or you will spoil all!"

The last twenty carried lighter loads of manioc and maize bread, followed by twenty more soldiers and the officer in charge, who twirled his mustaches, oblivious to the English arrows itching to pierce his ribs. The ambush was complete.

Amyas hesitated, shrinking from drawing his bow in cool blood against men so utterly defenseless, even as they committed devilish cruelty. Whips lashed the staggered line, cracking like pistol-shots against naked limbs, punishing every stumble.

Suddenly, the *casus belli* arose of its own accord. The last-but-one in the chain was an old, gray-headed man, followed by a slender, graceful girl of about eighteen. Amyas's heart yearned for them. As they passed the bend where the road narrowed, a voice shouted, "Halt, señores! There is a tree across the path!"

"Halt?" the officer bellowed, addressing the Mother of Heaven and various saints in a frenzy. The trembling line surged upward, driven by blows below, until the old man fell groveling. The officer leaped down and hurried up, finding the prostrate wretch.

"Grandfather of Beelzebub, is this a place to lie worshipping your fiends?" he screamed, pricking the man with his sword.

The old man tried to rise, but the weight on his head crushed him back down. He lay motionless. The driver applied the manati-hide across his loins with fearful force, declaring him "used up."

"Gastado, Señor Capitán," the driver shrugged. "Forward there!"

"Clear away the tree, señores, and I'll soon clear the chain," the officer ordered, flourishing a Toledo blade whose beauty made Amyas feel he had broken the Tenth Commandment. The officer stepped back, intending to sever the chain at one stroke, displaying the strength of his arm and the temper of his blade.

However, the adventurer's fancy proved recondite. The blade gleamed, fell—not on the chain, but on the wrist it fettered. A shriek, a crimson flash, and the chain and its prisoner were parted.

For one moment, Amyas's arrow would have pierced the murderer's throat, but vengeance did not come from him. Quick and fierce as a tiger-cat, the girl sprang on the ruffian. With the intensity of passion, she clasped him and leaped with him from the narrow ledge into the abyss below.

A rush, a shout, and all faces bent over the precipice. The girl hung by her chained wrist; the officer was gone. An awful silence followed, broken only by Amyas hearing his body crash through the tree-tops far below.

"Haul her up! Hew her in pieces! Burn the witch!" the driver screamed, pulling at the chain while the Spaniards scrambled from their chairs.

"Now!" Amyas cried. Swift and sure, his arrow rushed through the driver's body at ten yards, and then, roaring like a leaping lion, he sprang into the midst of the astonished ruffians.

His first thought was for the girl. By sheer strength, he jerked her safely into the road, while the Spaniards recoiled, imagining him a supernatural foe. A cry of "English! Lutheran dogs!" arose too late. The men of Devon followed their captain's lead; a storm of arrows left five Spaniards dead and a dozen wounded, and Salvation Yeo leaped down with twenty swords, and the work of death began.

The Spaniards fought like lions, but they had no time to fix their arquebuses on crutches, and no room to use their pikes on the narrow path. The English had the wall, and holding the wall was holding the enemy's life at mercy. In five desperate minutes, not a living Spaniard stood upon those steps; none lay in the green abyss below. Two, fully armored, escaped down the hill.

"After them! Michael Evans and Simon Heard!" Amyas cried. The two lean Clovelly men ran two feet for every step the Spaniard took, returning in ten minutes with the job done. Amyas and his men hurried past the Indians to aid Cary and the party, where shouts and musket shots announced a sharp affray. Their arrival settled the matter. All the Spaniards fell but three or four, who scrambled down the cliffs.

"Let not one escape! Slay them as Israel slew Amalek!" Yeo cried. Ere the wretches could reach shelter, an arrow quivered in each rolling body.

"Now then! Loose the Indians!"
