"Sir, let us consult the taste of marriageable men. Consider the world's store: iron, cotton, wool, chemicals—all rendered to our plastic skill. Every shape is wrought responsive to demand; the market's pulse sets the law, by some sublime rule. Our daughters must be wives, and to the wives must be whatever men will choose. A man's taste is a woman's test. Observe the phrasing? 'Tis good, I think? Well-winged and poised with those letters."

"Nay, turn it round," interjected the second gentleman. "Give us the test of taste. A fine *menu*—is it today what Roman epicures insisted a gentleman must eat to earn the dignity of dining?"

At Brackenshaw Park, where the archery meeting was held, the landscape unfolded from gentle heights overlooking the valley, stretching eastward toward the downs and westward into a broad, cultivated plain that hung like a vast curtain. Upon the highest platform of the clustered hills stood the castle, constructed of rough-hewn limestone, its surface textured with the dark dust of lichens and the washings of rain, casting a play of lights and shadows. Masses of beech and fir sheltered it on the north, spreading down the green slopes like flocks seeking the water that gleamed below. At the farthest end of the park lay the carefully kept archery ground, a table-land protected by tall elms and a thick screen of hollies, ensuring the gravel walks and the freshly mown turf where targets stood remained in agreeable afternoon shade. Ahead, the Archery Hall with its arcade rose like a white temple against the surrounding greenery.

What could serve as a finer backdrop for the flower-groups of ladies moving, bowing, and turning their necks with the leisurely grace of lilies given motion? Even after the military band from Manchester ceased playing, the atmosphere retained a pleasant resonance: musical laughter in all registers, a harmony of friendly speeches rising to mild excitement before sinking back into an agreeable murmur. No open-air amusement could claim such freedom from the noisy, crowding conditions that often spoil modern pleasures. This gathering was highly selective; the number of guests accompanying the members was restricted by ticket awards to ensure comfort for the ensuing dinner and ball within the castle. Within the enclosure, no plebeian spectators were admitted save Lord Brackenshaw's tenants and their families. Among these, it was chiefly the feminine members who exercised the privilege, bringing small children or younger siblings. While the young males relieved the slight insipidity of the event with imaginative betting—"anything you like"—being their stake, the young maidens applied a different principle of discrimination, deliberating over which of the sweetly dressed ladies they would choose to emulate if given the power of selection. Though perhaps the rustic souls would have striven for a tabernacle unlike Gwendolen's—one flushed with more pink cheeks and hair of the most fashionable yellow—the male judges surrounding her unanimously pronounced her the finest girl present.

No wonder she reveled in that July day. Pre-eminence is sweet to those who cherish it, even amidst mediocrity. Perhaps it was not purely mythical that a slave has been proud to be bought first; nor unlikely that a barn-yard fowl, though unaware of his condition, might possess a self-aware consciousness of his relative importance and strut accordingly. Yet for complete enjoyment, the outward and inward must concur. And for Gwendolen, this concurrence was absolute.

Who could deny that bows and arrows are among the fairest weapons in the world for feminine forms to wield? They invite attitudes of grace and power, liberating the fine concentration of energy inherent in marksmanship from bloody associations. The ancient British resource of "killing something" was no longer carried out with bow and quiver; bands defended their passes under shades other than clouds of arrows, and poisoned darts existed only as harmless survivors in rhetoric or distant regions. Archery possessed no ugly scent of brimstone, broke no shins, and bred no athletic monsters. Its sole peril was failure, a prospect sufficient to mold skilled action in generous hearts. Moreover, the prizes awarded by the Brackenshaw Archery Club were of the nobler symbolic kind: not parcels to be hoarded, degrading honor into gain, but the gold and silver arrows, the stars to be worn upon the breast for a long time before transfer to the next worthy achiever. These signs of pre-eminence offered the virtue of wreaths without their inconvenience, avoiding any melancholy effect in the heat of the ballroom. Indeed, the club was framed with impeccable taste, seemingly immune to ridiculous incidents.

Today, every incalculable element favored the occasion. The air was mildly warm, devoid of wind to disturb hair, drapery, or the flight of the arrow. All preparations met fair play, and when the general march to retrieve arrows commenced, the promenade of joyful young creatures, speaking lightly and laughing, moving gracefully toward a common object, formed a spectacle worth viewing. Here, Gwendolen seemed a Calypso among her nymphs. It was in her attitudes and movements that everyone was compelled to admit her surpassing charm.

"That girl is like a high-mettled racer," observed Lord Brackenshaw to young Clintock, one of the invited spectators.

"First chop! Tremendously pretty, too," remarked the elegant Grecian, who had paid her assiduous attention; "I never saw her look better."

Perhaps she had never looked so well. Her face beamed with young pleasure, free from any malignant ray of discontent. Being satisfied with her own chances, she felt kindly toward all and content with the universe. Lacking the highest distinction in rank or the spotlight of an heiress like Miss Arrowpoint granted her an added triumph in eclipsing those advantages. Regarding personal recommendation, she would not have traded her family group for any other: her mother's appearance suited an amiable duchess, while her Uncle and Aunt Gascoigne, alongside Anna, made equally gratifying figures. Gwendolen was so full of joyous belief in herself that she felt no jealousy whatsoever regarding Miss Arrowpoint, despite the latter's prowess as an archeress.

Even the unexpected reappearance of the formidable Herr Klesmer, causing surprise among the company, aligned perfectly with Gwendolen's inclination to be amused. Short of Apollo himself, what greater musical maestro could cut a figure at an archery meeting? A satirical light flickered in Gwendolen's eyes as she glanced toward the Arrowpoint party upon their entrance. The contrast between Klesmer and the average assembly of English country folk reached its zenith in close proximity to his hosts—or patrons, as Mrs. Arrowpoint would prefer them to be called, thereby denying the lingering notion of patronizing genius, whose royalty was universally acknowledged. Such a contrast might have amused a graver soul, but Gwendolen found it diverting. The English, a miscellaneous people, display varied architectures of animal and facial ornament in chance fifties; however, the prevailing expression of the pure English gentleman is the easy style of his figure and clothing. He objects to marked ins-and-outs in attire and, equally, to appearing inspired.

Imagine an assembly where men bore the ordinary stamp of the well-bred Englishman, watching the entrance of Herr Klesmer. His mane floated backward in massive inconsistency with a chimney-pot hat, seemingly donned for a joke atop his pronounced, well-modeled features and powerful, clear-shaven jaw. His tall, thin frame was clad in a manner that, lacking strict adherence to English norms, emphasized intention to an almost comical degree. Draped in a loose garment with a Florentine beretta, he would have stood beside Leonardo da Vinci; yet how did he fare presenting himself in trousers that defied English sensibilities regarding the knee? How did the fire evident in his glances and the curious movements of his head transform into comedy under the tyranny of a hat mandating well-cropped hair and staid demeanor, such as that of Mr. Arrowsmith, whose nullity of face and perfection of tailoring passed everywhere without ridicule? One understands why it is often better for greatness to be dead, shedding the outward man.

Many present knew Klesmer, or had heard of him, but only in candle-lit settings where he appeared merely as a musician. He had not yet attained that supreme, world-wide celebrity that renders an artist great to the most ordinary through knowledge of his expense. It was a novel light for them to perceive him in—an unexpected presentation on this July afternoon within an exclusive society. Some were inclined to laugh; others felt a pang of disgust at the lack of judgment displayed by the Arrowpoints in utilizing an introductory card.

"What extreme guises those artistic fellows usually adopt!" cried young Clintock to Gwendolen. "Behold the figure he cuts, bowing with hand upon heart to Lady Brackenshaw—and Mrs. Arrowpoint's feather just reaching his shoulder."

"You are one of the profane," retorted Gwendolen. "You are blind to the majesty of genius. Herr Klesmer smites me with awe; I feel crushed in his presence; my courage oozes away."

"Ah, you understand all about his music."

"No, indeed," Gwendolen replied with a light laugh. "It is he who understands all about mine and thinks it pitiable." Klesmer's verdict on her singing had become an easier jest since he had been struck by her *plastic*.

"It is not addressed to the ears of the future, I suppose. I'm glad of that: it suits mine."
