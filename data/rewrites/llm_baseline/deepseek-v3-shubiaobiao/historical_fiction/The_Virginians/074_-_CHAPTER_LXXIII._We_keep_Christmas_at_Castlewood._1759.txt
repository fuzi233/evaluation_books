My dear children, we know from our beloved fairy tales how at every feast—be it christening or wedding—some discontented soul nurses a grudge and swears vengeance. So it was no surprise when our fiery cousin Will erupted in curses upon hearing of his brother’s betrothal to the colonial heiress. At first, he stormed from the house, vowing never to return. But Will’s oaths were as fleeting as his tempers, and within days, the prodigal slunk back to the family table. The aroma of roasted meats and spiced wine proved too tempting; he could not forsake Castlewood’s bounty, even in his wrath.

He drank deeply, toasting the bride with venom beneath his breath. As the chapel bells chimed and my lord pledged to love and cherish her, Will muttered responses fit for a tavern brawl. Yet he was not alone in his disdain. Mr. Warrington heard no end of grievances against the new Countess—how she had enchanted and discarded suitors like a siren luring ships to wreck. The City merchants, the Dissenting preacher, even poor Jack Lambert, who had hoped to save her soul, all spat her name like a curse. The lawyer, once so eager to tally her estates, now scoffed, "No such place as Virginia! A scheme, a damned colonial trick!"

As for Will, he nursed his grievance like a wound. "That little Cerberus!" he snarled. "A she-devil with a dowry!"

Warrington, ever amused, replied, "Then you ought to thank the gods for sparing you such a wife. If she poisons Castlewood, better him than you."

Will clenched his fists. "Aye, but the fortune—*my* fortune—stolen by that grinning Judas!"

Warrington arched a brow. "And how, pray, did you risk your life for it?"

Here Will grew red as a beet, confessing how Lydia herself had provoked their near-duel—how she’d mocked Warrington as a coward, taunted Will for enduring his cousin’s jibes. "Damme, I near skewered you in Tottenham Fields!"

Warrington laughed. "Lucky for me the constables came. You looked ready to spit me like a capon."

Now, the new Countess ruled Castlewood with a scepter of iron. She mastered chapel protocol, her footmen bearing her prayer book as solemnly as a bishop’s procession. She lectured the parson on divinity, the bailiff on crops, and the village midwives on childbirth—though she’d yet to bear a child herself. She even decreed the fate of old Lockwood, the ancient porter, until Warrington intervened.

"Respect his years, my lady," he chided.

She scoffed. "Why? Old folks ain’t handsome, nor amusing. Look at Aunt Bernstein—once a beauty, they say. Pfah! When *I* grow shriveled, I hope someone puts me out of my misery!"

Aunt Bernstein arrived for Christmas, sharp-tongued and imperious. She had orchestrated this marriage, and she expected gratitude. "The girl has fire," she mused. "A bit savage, but we’ll polish her."

Yet Lydia was no puppet. She dismissed the dowager’s airs, commanded the servants like a general, and declared old portraits "musty relics." When Bernstein praised her, the Countess retorted, "I ain’t here to be *formed*, madam. I’m Lady Castlewood now."

And so Christmas passed—amid feasts, quarrels, and the unspoken question: *Who truly rules here?* The old order or the new?
