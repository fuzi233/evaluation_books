To unravel the labyrinth of my own guilt, I required a guide whose judgment was as sharp as it was seasoned. Thus, I departed for the railway station, accompanied by Gabriel Betteredge. Clutched within my breast pocket was the incriminating letter, and tucked securely inside a small leather bag was the nightgown—the very garment that had become the focal point of my torment. Both items awaited the forensic scrutiny of Mr. Bruff before I retired to sleep that night, a sleep I feared would be haunted by the specters of my own actions.

We emerged from the house into the twilight silence, a profound quietude that marked a departure from our usual dynamic. For the first time in my acquaintance with him, I found old Betteredge walking beside me without uttering a single word. The weight of the unspoken hung heavy between us until we crossed the threshold of the lodge gates, beyond which the familiar rhythm of our banter might resume. Seizing the initiative, I broke the silence immediately.

"Before I depart for London," I began, my voice steady despite the turmoil beneath, "I have two inquiries to pose to you. They concern myself, and I anticipate they may catch you somewhat off guard."

"If these questions succeed in expelling that poor creature's letter from your mind, Mr. Franklin, then they may accomplish anything else they desire with me. Pray, begin to surprise me, sir, at your earliest convenience."

"My first question, Betteredge, is this: Was I intoxicated on the night of Rachel's Birthday?"

"_You_ drunk!" the old man exclaimed, his tone laced with incredulity. "It is the great flaw in your constitution, Mr. Franklin, that you consume alcohol solely with your dinner, never touching a drop thereafter!"

"But the birthday was a singular occasion. Might I not have abandoned my customary restraint on that specific night?"

Betteredge paused, his brow furrowing in recollection.

"You did deviate from your habits, sir," he admitted slowly. "Allow me to recount the details. You appeared wretchedly ill, and we persuaded you to partake of a drop of brandy and water to restore your spirits."

"I am not accustomed to brandy and water. It is entirely possible—"

"Hold fast, Mr. Franklin. I knew your limitations equally well. I poured you out half a wineglass of our fifty-year-old Cognac; and, let me confess to a momentary lapse in judgment on my part, I drowned that noble spirit in nearly a tumbler-full of ice-cold water. No child could have become intoxicated on such a mixture, let alone a grown man!"

Relief washed over me. I knew I could rely on his impeccable memory regarding matters of substance. It was physically impossible that I had been rendered unconscious by such a diluted concoction. Satisfied on this front, I pressed forward to my second query.

"Prior to my departure abroad, Betteredge, you observed me frequently during my childhood. Tell me plainly: do you recall anything peculiar about my behavior after retiring at night? Did you ever witness me wandering in my sleep?"

Betteredge halted, fixing his gaze upon me for a fleeting moment before nodding solemnly and resuming his pace.

"I perceive your trajectory now, Mr. Franklin!" he declared. "You attempt to rationalize the presence of paint on your nightgown without acknowledging your agency. It will not suffice, sir. You remain leagues removed from the truth. Wandering in your sleep? You never engaged in such a practice in your entire life!"

Once again, I felt compelled to concede. My existence, both domestically and overseas, had never been one of solitude. Had I been a somnambulist, hundreds of witnesses would have noted the phenomenon, and concerned relatives would undoubtedly have intervened to curb the habit for my own safety.

Yet, driven by an obstinacy that I deemed both natural and excusable given my dire circumstances, I clung to one of only two plausible explanations for the untenable predicament in which I now found myself. Sensing my dissatisfaction, Betteredge astutely redirected the conversation toward subsequent events in the chronicle of the Moonstone, scattering my theories to the winds forever.

"Let us approach this differently, sir," he proposed. "Retain your hypothesis and observe how far it carries you toward the truth. If we are to accept the premise of the nightgown—which I certainly do not endorse—you not only smudged the paint from the door unknowingly, but you also appropriated the Diamond without consciousness. Is that accurate thus far?"

"Absolutely correct. Proceed."

"Excellent, sir. We posit that you were either drunk or sleepwalking when you seized the jewel. This explains the events of the night and morning following the birthday. But how does this theory account for the aftermath? The Diamond has been transported to London since then. It has been pledged to Mr. Luker. Did you perform these acts unknowingly as well? Were you intoxicated when I saw you off in the pony-chaise on that Saturday evening? And did you wander in your sleep to Mr. Luker's premises once the train deposited you at your destination? Pardon me for stating the obvious, Mr. Franklin, but this ordeal has so unsettled you that you are currently unfit to adjudicate your own fate. The sooner you align your thoughts with Mr. Bruff's, the sooner you will discern a path out of this deadlock."

We arrived at the station with barely a minute to spare.

I hastily provided Betteredge with my London address, instructing him to correspond if necessary, while promising to relay any developments on my end. As I prepared to bid him farewell, my gaze drifted involuntarily toward the stall selling books and newspapers. There stood Mr. Candy's distinctive assistant once more, conversing with the keeper. Our eyes locked simultaneously. Ezra Jennings removed his hat in acknowledgment; I returned the gesture and hastened into a carriage just as the train lurched forward. It was a psychological balm, I suppose, to focus on subjects seemingly devoid of personal consequence. Regardless, I embarked on this momentous journey back to Mr. Bruff, wondering—though I admitted the absurdity of the thought—that I had encountered the man with the piebald hair twice within a single day.

My arrival in London occurred late enough to extinguish any hope of locating Mr. Bruff at his office. I drove directly to his private residence in Hampstead, interrupting the elderly lawyer as he dozed alone in his dining room, his favorite pug resting on his lap and a bottle of wine within reach.

I shall best convey the impact of my narrative upon Mr. Bruff by detailing his subsequent actions after hearing my tale in its entirety. He commanded that lights and strong tea be brought to his study and dispatched a message to the ladies of his household, strictly forbidding any disturbance under any pretext. Once these formalities were concluded, he first inspected the nightgown and then immersed himself in the reading of Rosanna Spearman's letter.

Upon finishing the correspondence, Mr. Bruff addressed me for the first time since we had secluded ourselves in his chamber.

"Franklin Blake," the old gentleman intoned, "this is a grave matter, encompassing complexities in multiple dimensions. In my assessment, it implicates Rachel as profoundly as it does you. Her extraordinary conduct ceases to be a mystery _now_. She believes you have stolen the Diamond."

I had recoiled from reasoning my way to such a repugnant conclusion, yet it had inevitably forced itself upon me. My resolve to secure a personal audience with Rachel had indeed rested, fundamentally and truly, on the ground Mr. Bruff had just articulated.

"The initial step in this investigation," the lawyer continued, "is to appeal to Rachel. She has maintained silence hitherto due to motives which I, who know her character intimately, can readily comprehend. It is impossible, after these events, to sustain such silence indefinitely. She must be persuaded—or compelled—to disclose the grounds upon which she bases her conviction that you took the Moonstone. The probability is high that the entirety of this case, however serious it appears, will disintegrate if we can penetrate Rachel's inveterate reserve and induce her to speak freely."

"That is a remarkably comforting sentiment for _me_," I remarked. "I confess I should like to know——"

"You would like to know how I can justify it," Mr. Bruff interjected sharply. "I can elucidate this in two minutes. Comprehend, first and foremost, that I view this matter through the lens of a legal practitioner. It is a question of evidence. Well. The evidence collapses at the outset on one critical point."

"On what point?"

"You shall hear. I concede that the mark of the name confirms the nightgown belongs to you. I acknowledge that the paint stain proves the gown touched Rachel's door. Yet, what evidence exists to demonstrate that you were the wearer of that garment on the night the Diamond vanished?"

The objection struck me with renewed force, echoing a doubt I had harbored internally.
