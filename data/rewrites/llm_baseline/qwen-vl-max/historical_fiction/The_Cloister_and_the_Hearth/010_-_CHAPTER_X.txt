The banns of marriage were to be proclaimed three times, as they are even today—but in those days, they were usually read on weekdays, and the young couple had little trouble persuading the curate to complete all three readings within a single day. He was new to the parish, and their earnest looks spoke more eloquently than words ever could. On Monday morning, the banns were announced at matins and again at vespers—and to their great relief, no one from Tergou was present in the church.

The next morning, both Gerard and Margaret arrived at the church, hearts pounding with anxiety. But just as the curate prepared to speak, a stranger rose abruptly and declared the banns invalid—on the grounds that neither party had reached the age of consent, and that parental permission had not been granted.

Outside the church, trembling and nearly overcome with despair, they whispered urgently to one another. Before they could decide what to do, the man who had blocked their union approached them. With a sly smile, he confessed he regretted interfering—he truly wished the young lovers well. But, he added, his livelihood depended entirely upon such interruptions. “A crown,” he said, “and I’ll undo it all. Tell the curate I was misinformed. The matter will be settled.”

“A crown?” Gerard exclaimed. “I’ll give you a golden angel for that.” The man agreed instantly and went with Gerard to the curate, claiming he had made a grave error—one corrected by seeing the couple in person. The curate, satisfied, consented to marry them the following day at ten o’clock. The man returned home with Gerard’s golden angel.

But this clever rogue was also a fool. He drank the coin at a tavern in Tergou, near a green where men practiced archery and other pastimes. There, half-drunk, he boasted of his exploit. And who should overhear him but Sybrandt, a notorious ne’er-do-well and frequent visitor to the place.

Sybrandt rushed home to tell his father—but the man was absent, having gone to Rotterdam to purchase cloth. Instead, Sybrandt signaled his elder brother, who stepped outside. These two were Gerard’s black sheep—the reckless, idle sons of a once-honest family. Idleness breeds vice; waiting for an inheritance breeds cruelty. Their thoughts revolved around nothing but the wealth they believed awaited them after their parents’ deaths. While their parents’ frugality stemmed from love and hard work, in these brothers it had twisted into greed—a root of endless crime.

Together, they decided not to inform their mother—her feelings were too unpredictable. Instead, they would go directly to the burgomaster. They sensed his opposition to the match, though they did not know why. Ghysbrecht Van Swieten saw through them immediately, but played along. He listened gravely, then assumed an air of authority. “Since your father is away,” he said, “the duty falls upon me, as father of the town. I know your father’s mind. Leave everything to me. And above all—do not tell a woman a word of this. Not even one in your own house. Chattering tongues ruin the wisest plans.”

He dismissed them with a faint air of superiority—ashamed of their company.

When they returned home, they found Gerard seated on a low stool beside their mother’s knee. She was gently stroking his hair, speaking kindly, promising to stand by him and no longer oppose his love. Her change of heart was rooted in her nature. It was she who, in a fit of anger, had torn Margaret’s portrait to pieces. She had watched Gerard turn pale, motionless, clutching the fragments until tears blurred his vision. Then remorse struck her—she wept in secret, yet dared not confess. “I’ll say nothing,” she told herself, “but I’ll make it up to him.” Her heart softened. Her harshness died. She was turning toward Gerard when the brothers burst in.

Gerard knew nothing of the reason for her sudden kindness. In fact, her tenderness only deepened his shame—he had suspected her of betraying him. Now he kissed her again and again, and retired to bed, joyful as a prince, believing his mother had finally become his mother once more—at the very moment his fate hung in the balance.

The next morning, at ten o’clock, Gerard and Margaret stood together in the church at Sevenbergen. He glowed with joy; she blushed with emotion. Peter and Martin Wittenhaagen were there—no others. Secrecy was essential. Margaret had refused to leave for Italy. She could not abandon her father—he was too learned, too helpless. But they had agreed to flee to Flanders for a few weeks, until the storm passed in Tergou.

The curate did not keep them waiting long—though it felt like an eternity. Then he stepped forward, calling them to the altar. They walked hand in hand, the happiest pair in Holland. The curate opened his book—then, before he could utter a word, a harsh voice cried: “Forbear!”

The constables of Tergou surged down the aisle and seized Gerard in the name of the law. Martin’s knife flashed out instantly.

“Hold!” shouted the priest. “Draw your weapon in a church? And you, who interrupt this holy sacrament—what madness is this?”

“There is no impiety, father,” replied the burgomaster’s servant respectfully. “This youth seeks to marry against his father’s will. His father has requested our burgomaster to act under the law. Let him deny it if he can.”

“Is this true, young man?” the curate asked.

Gerard bowed his head.

“We take him to Rotterdam,” the servant declared, “to await the Duke’s judgment.”

Margaret let out a cry of despair. The two lovers, so radiant moments before, now clung to each other, sobbing helplessly. Even the officers of the law hesitated—ashamed. One, kind-hearted, stepped forward under pretense of separating them, and whispered: “Rotterdam? That’s a lie. We’re taking him to the Stadthouse.”

They led Gerard away on horseback, heading toward Rotterdam. After several stops and devious turns, they turned back toward Tergou. Just outside the town, a crude canvas-covered cart waited. Gerard was placed inside and, by dusk, secretly delivered to the prison of the Stadthouse.

He was taken up many flights of stairs and thrown into a small room lit only by a narrow window with a vertical iron bar. The only furniture was a massive oak chest. Imprisonment in that age was often a death sentence. Cold, isolation, starvation, torture—sometimes poison—were its companions. Gerard felt himself in the hands of an enemy.

“Oh, the look that man gave me on the road to Rotterdam,” he murmured. “There’s more here than my father’s wrath. I fear I shall never see daylight again.”

He knelt and prayed. Then, rising, he lunged for the iron bar, gripping it tightly. Pressing his knees against the wall, he managed to peer outside.

For just a moment—yet enough. He saw Martin Wittenhaagen’s back. Martin sat quietly by the brook near the Stadthouse, fishing.

Gerard whistled sharply.

Martin turned instantly—his eyes sharp, alert. He saw Gerard, made a signal, then picked up his bow and line and slipped away.

Gerard watched him go—relief flooding through him. The sight of his friend was comfort enough. Yet he wished Martin had stayed.

As the soldier vanished, Gerard pulled with all his strength—wrenching the rusted iron bar from the stone, held only by corroded nails. At that moment, the door creaked open. Ghysbrecht Van Swieten entered silently.

The burgomaster’s gaze fell upon the broken bar, then swept to the window—so high above the ground. He said nothing.

He brought a brown loaf and a pitcher of water, set them on the chest, and left in silence.

Gerard’s first impulse was to strike him with the bar and flee. But the burgomaster caught the flash in his eye and coughed softly. Instantly, three armed men appeared at the door.

“My orders,” the burgomaster said coldly, “are to hold you here until you swear to abandon Margaret Brandt and return to the Church, to which you have belonged since birth.”

“Death sooner,” Gerard replied.

“With all my heart,” the burgomaster answered, and withdrew.

Martin raced to Sevenbergen. There he found Margaret—pale, agitated, but resolute. She had just finished a letter to Countess Charolois, appealing against the injustice and treachery of Ghysbrecht.

“Courage!” Martin called as he entered. “I’ve found him. He’s in the haunted tower—the very top. I know the place. Many a man has gone up there… and come down feet first.”

He described how he had looked up and seen Gerard’s face at a narrow slit in the wall.

“Oh, Martin! How did he look?”

“What do you mean? He looked like Gerard Eliassoen.”

“But was he pale?”

“A little.”

“Anxious? Like one doomed?”

“No, no—bright as a pewter pot.”

“You mock me. Wait—then it must have been at sight of you. He counts on us. Oh, what shall we do? Martin, good friend, take this letter to Rotterdam at once.”

Martin reached for the letter.

Peter had been silent, but watching closely—more attentive than usual.

“Put not your trust in princes,” he said.

“Alas! What else have we to trust in?” Margaret sighed.

“Knowledge.”

“Father, your learning won’t help us now.”

“How do you know? Wit has overcome iron bars before.”

“Ay, father—but nature is stronger than wit, and nature is against us. Think of the height! No ladder in Holland could reach him.”

“I need no ladder. I need only a gold crown.”

“Nay, I have money. Nine angels. Gerard gave them to me to keep. But what use are they? The burgomaster won’t be bribed.”

“What use? Give me one crown, and the young man shall sup with us tonight.”

Peter spoke with such confidence that Margaret felt a flicker of hope. But Martin’s eyes lingered on him with gentle mockery.

“It passes the powers of man’s invention,” she breathed.

“Invention!” Peter snapped. “A fig for invention. Everything that can be said or done has already been said and done. Let me tell you of a Florentine knight shut in a tower higher than Gerard’s. His faithful squire stood below and freed him—with no machine but the bow in your hand, Martin, and some trinkets I’ll buy for a crown.”

Martin stared at his bow, turning it slowly. Still, he remained unconvinced.

Then Peter recounted the tale—the squire’s clever plan. Simple, yet brilliant. So simple, in fact, that now they wondered how they could have thought it impossible.

The letter never reached Rotterdam. They trusted Peter’s wisdom—and their own courage.

It was nine o’clock on a clear moonlit night. Gerard senior was still in Rotterdam. The rest of the household slept.

A figure stood beside Giles’s bed. Pale, glowing in the moonlight. With a sound halfway between a yell and a snarl, the gymnast rolled off his bed and under it in one fluid motion.

A soft voice followed: “Why, Giles, are you afraid of me?”

Giles peeked out cautiously—and saw it was his sister Kate.

She pressed a finger to her lips. “Hush! Lest Cornelis or Sybrandt hear us.”

Giles climbed back with a single gymnastic leap.

Kate revealed she had overheard Cornelis and Sybrandt mention Gerard’s name. Anxious about his absence, she had listened at their door—and learned a terrible truth: Gerard was imprisoned in the haunted tower of the Stadthouse. By their father’s order. But that couldn’t be—Gerard’s father was in Rotterdam. There must be treachery.

She begged Giles to accompany her to the tower—to speak to Gerard, to tell him their father was away and would surely release him upon return.

“Dear Giles, I’d go alone—but I’m afraid of the spirits said to haunt the tower. But with you, I’ll not fear.”

“Nor I with you,” Giles replied. “I don’t believe in spirits. I’ve never seen one. The closest thing I’ve seen was you, Kate, after all.”

Within half an hour, Giles and Kate slipped out of the house. She made him carry a lantern—though the night was bright.

“The lantern gives me courage against the evil spirits,” she said.

The first night in prison is the hardest—especially when loneliness adds to the horror. Even today, many commit suicide within the first twenty-four hours of solitary confinement. Perhaps that is why modern jailers avoid watching during that critical time.

As the sun sank, Gerard’s spirit sank with it. Hope faded with the light. He was weak from hunger—he dared not eat the food Ghysbrecht had brought. Hunger breaks men.

He sat on the chest, arms and head drooping—utterly despondent.

Suddenly, something struck the wall behind him sharply, then rattled at his feet. An arrow—white feathered.

A chill ran through him. Assassination from outside?

He crouched. No more came.

Crawling forward, he picked up the arrow. No head. A cry of hope escaped him—could it be a friendly hand?

He examined it carefully. A soft substance attached. Using his tinder-box, he struck a light.

Two things revealed themselves—filling his heart with joy, though vague.

A skein of silk tied to the shaft—and words written on the arrow:

*Well beloved, make fast the silk to thy knife and lower to us: but hold thine end fast: then count an hundred and draw up.*

Gerard grabbed the chest and dragged it to the window—using superhuman strength. Moments ago, he could not have moved it.

Standing on the chest, he looked down. Figures at the base of the tower—blurred, like one giant shadow.

He waved his bonnet.

Then he untied the silk, fastened one end to his knife, and lowered it until it stopped drawing.

He counted a hundred.

Then pulled.

The silk grew heavier.

At last, a knot. Attached—thick whipcord.

What could it mean?

Then, from below, Margaret’s voice rose—low, clear:
“Draw up, Gerard, till you see liberty.”

He drew the whipcord upward—then another knot. A thicker cord replaced it.

He began pulling. The weight increased. Sweat poured down his face.

Then it dawned on him.

He pulled harder—until the rope emerged from the shadows below, coiling like a serpent.

A shout of joy burst from him.

More pulls—and a strong new rope touched his hand.

He hauled it in, threaded it through the chest handles, and knotted it firmly.

Then paused—breathing heavily, gathering courage.

First, test the chest.

He jumped—hard.

On the third jump, the side burst open. Parchments tumbled out.

Startled, he realized the chest hadn’t broken—it had opened. He had likely triggered a hidden spring.

Still, doubt crept in. He reinforced it—fastening the iron bar across the rope and window with a thin cord.

Now, he mounted the chest, put his foot through the window, and sat half in, half out, one hand gripping the inner rope.

In the silence, he heard his own heartbeat.

The free air touched his face—giving him the courage to risk what all must lose one day—liberty.

Many dangers lay ahead. The greatest—stepping onto the rope outside.

He reflected.

Finally, he positioned himself like a swimmer—body in, legs out.

Holding the inner rope with both hands, he felt with his feet for the outer rope.

Found it.

Tucked it between his palms.

Prayed briefly.

Then, calmly, placed his left hand on the sill and wriggled out.

He grasped the iron bar—hung suspended by one hand—while his left searched for the rope at his knees.

Too tight against the wall.

But he gripped it.

Let go of the bar.

Seized the rope with his right.

His body dropped a yard.

A stifled cry came from below.

Gerard hung in midair.

He clenched his teeth, squeezed the rope with his feet, gripped it with his hands—and descended slowly, hand below hand.

Rough stones passed beneath him.

Moss clung to one.

He looked up—the moon shone into his window.

Looked down—figures below seemed miles away.

Dizziness threatened.

So he fixed his eyes on the wall.

Down—down—down.

The rope burned his hands.

Another glance upward—the window was far away.

Now he dared look down.

Thirty feet below—Margaret and Martin, hands raised, ready to catch him.

Their eyes and teeth gleamed in the moonlight.

Mouths open—breathing hard.

“Take care, Gerard! Look not down!”

“Fear me not!” he called joyfully—and focused on the wall—descending faster.

In seconds, his feet touched their hands.

They seized him before he reached the ground.

All three embraced—silent, trembling.

“Hush! Away in silence, dear one.”

They crept along the shadow of the wall.

But suddenly—light shot from a corner of the building, blazing across their path like fire.

Whispers. Footsteps.

“Back!” hissed Martin. “Keep in the shade.”

They retreated—passed the dangling rope—raced toward a small projecting tower.

Just as they rounded it, the light flickered past, vanishing into the distance.

“A lantern!” Martin groaned. “They’re after us.”

“Give me my knife,” whispered Gerard. “I’ll never be taken alive.”

“No!” Margaret murmured. “Is there no way out?”

“None! None!” Martin said. “But I carry six lives at my shoulder.”

He strung his bow—fitted an arrow.

“In war, never wait to be struck. I’ll kill one or two before they know where death comes from.”

He motioned them to silence—drew his bow—ready to loose the arrow the moment a target appeared.

Gerard and Margaret held their breath.

A wild hope stirred in Gerard—perhaps the watcher was the burgomaster himself.

But the bow wavered—shook violently.

The soldier staggered back—knees knocking, face white.

He dropped his arrow—clutched Gerard’s shoulder.

“Let me feel flesh and blood,” he gasped. “The haunted tower! The haunted tower!”

His terror spread.

They gasped—not spoken.

“Hush!” he cried. “It will hear you up the wall! It is going up the wall! Its head is on fire. Up the wall, as mortal creatures walk on grass. If you know a prayer, say it—for hell is loose tonight.”

“I have power to exorcise spirits,” Gerard said, trembling. “I will venture forth.”

“Go alone,” Martin said. “I have looked on it once—and lived.”
