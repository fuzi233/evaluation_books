His Excellency the Commander-in-Chief departed for Madam Esmond's residence with a magnificence befitting the highest authority in His Majesty's North American dominions. A formidable escort of dragoons led the way from Williamsburg, thundered over by the raucous cheers of a devoted, predominantly African, populace. The General rode within a ponderous, heavily decorated coach. At its entrance stood Captain Talmadge, the Master of the Horse, who accompanied the vehicle throughout the journey. Seated inside, alongside the aide-de-camp Major Danvers, was Mr. Franklin, the diminutive postmaster from Philadelphia. Once merely a printer's apprentice, Franklin had evolved into a man of astonishing acumen, a fact acknowledged with respect by the General and his circle. "Extraordinary," observed His Excellency, tipping his hat gracefully, "how a man of such humble origins has acquired such breadth of knowledge and refinement!"

Franklin bowed politely. "It has been a fortunate chance, sir, to fall into the company of gentlemen like yourself. I have diligently studied their manners to adapt myself accordingly. Regarding formal education, I cannot boast; my father's means were narrow, and the schools in New England offered little. Yet, I have labored to the utmost of my ability, gathering whatever wisdom I could. I confess, however, I know nothing comparable to what you possess in England."

Braddock erupted in laughter. "Education! By George, there are gentlemen in the army who cannot decide whether 'bull' takes two 'b's or one! I've heard the Duke of Marlborough was hardly a proficient penman. Though I have not the honor of serving under such a noble commander—he predated my time—I have thoroughly thrashed the French, despite his lack of scholarship."

"I am fully aware of both facts," Franklin interjected calmly.

"Nor is my Duke a scholar," Braddock continued, feigning surprise at Franklin's knowing glance. "Aha, Mr. Postmaster, you heard that too—I see the wink in your eye." Instantly, Franklin withdrew the mischievous glint, replacing it with orbs innocent as a child's. "He lacks learning, but he stands as a match for any French general who has ever attempted to fry a frog in English style. He secured the Crown for the best of kings, Your Gracious Majesty, King George."

Franklin removed his hat, sending a cloud of powdered hair drifting from his voluminous wig. "He is the soldier's truest friend and the unyielding foe of those beggarly, red-shanked Scotch rebels and cunning Jesuits who seek to strip us of our liberties and faith. My gracious master, the Prince, shares this lack of academic polish, yet remains one of the finest gentlemen alive."

"I have seen His Royal Highness at a review of the Guards in Hyde Park," Franklin noted. "The Duke is indeed a magnificent rider."

"To your health today, Postmaster!" Braddock declared warmly. "He is the best of masters, the best of friends, the most dutiful son, and the finest gentleman to ever wear an epaulet."

"Epaulets are entirely out of my sphere, sir," Franklin chuckled. "I reside in a Quaker city."

"Certainly, every man to his trade," Braddock agreed, settling deeper into the swaying carriage. "You and your fellows belong to books; we to battle and governance. Look at these roads, and how this infernal coach plummets! Who are these two negroes in liveried livery?"

"That is Mr. Washington," Major Danvers replied.

"I'd wager him a corporal in the Horse Grenadiers," Braddock mused. "He carries himself well on horseback, and knows this terrain intimately, Mr. Franklin."

"Yes, indeed."

"And a remarkably genteel young man, considering his background. One would expect the polish of Europe."

"He does his best," Franklin observed, studying the stout chief with innocent eyes. Braddock sat slumped, his face flushed crimson beneath his coat, swearing at nearly every sentence. Ignorant of everything save the virtues of liquor and women, yet absurdly proud of his non-existent lineage, he was brave as a bulldog, savage, lustful, prodigal, yet generous. Gentle in soft moments, easily won, dull of wit, and utterly uneducated, he believed his nation supreme and himself equal to any gentleman. "He is mighty well suited for a provincial, truly. He was beaten at Fort What-d'ye-call-it last year, near the Thingamy River."

"The Lord knows the name, sir," Talmadge admitted, "and I suspect the Postmaster laughs at us both."

"Oh, Captain!"

"A classic trap," Franklin explained. "Militia and Indians alone. Good day, Mr. Washington. Fine mount, sir. Was that your maiden engagement last year?"

"At Fort Necessity?" The young gentleman saluted gravely as he approached, flanked by two sleek negro grooms in velvet caps. "I began poorly, sir, having never seen combat prior to that unfortunate day."

"You were all raw levies, my good fellow. I recall seeing our militia flee from the Scots, cursing them. You needed proper troops."

"It is my deepest passion to serve with them, Your Excellency," Washington stated earnestly.

"By George, we shall endeavor to grant you your wish!" Braddock boomed, using one of his customary oaths. As the heavy carriage rolled toward Castlewood, Washington requested permission to gallop ahead to announce the General's imminent arrival.

The Commander-in-Chief's progress was so glacial that lesser dignitaries, invited to greet him, found themselves trailing in a dusty procession behind the great wheels. First came Mr. Dinwiddie, Lieutenant-Governor, accompanied by his servants and the jovial Parson Broadbent. Soon joined by Little Mr. Dempster, the schoolmaster, sporting a grand Ramillies wig reserved for state occasions, and Mr. Laws, the judge, riding a mule with Madam Laws perched behind him, her capbox carried by a negro man. The spectacle was so comical that Major Danvers and Mr. Franklin burst into laughter, though quietly enough not to disturb the sleeping General. They signaled the rear guard to proceed, leaving the Commander and his dragoons to follow at their leisure. Castlewood awaited them all with ample space.

Inside, provisions were abundant: meat, drink, and the finest tobacco for the soldiers; laughter and revelry for the negroes; and a lavish welcome for their masters. The honest General required assistance with nearly every dish and constantly raised his glass for another drink. He praised Nathan's sangaree highly, having consumed it liberally before dinner. The spread included cider, ale, brandy, and copious amounts of Bordeaux wine, some brought directly from England by Colonel Esmond itself, deemed fit for pontiffs' banquets according to Little Mr. Dempster. Parson Broadbent, a clergyman of questionable reputation who had spent time in Fleet Prison and various English gaming dens, returned a wink to Dempster and drank the vintage without concern for Latin phrases. Broadbent, a gambler and cock-fighter, had shared countless adventures with young George Esmond—hunting roe, hauling shad, slaughtering geese, and poaching ducks. Rumors circulated that Broadbent was the midnight poacher whom Washington had once caught and caned by the river at Mount Vernon; though Broadbent escaped, he returned two Sundays later with a black eye and a new collar. George and Broadbent played all the card games together, hunted every bird and beast, and fished every sea. Indeed, when the boys attended Mr. Dempster for lessons, I suspect Harry stayed behind to learn from the other professor, while George read his own books and told no tales of his younger brother.

Every bird of the Virginia air and every fish of the sea were laid before Madam Esmond's board to feed Her Excellency and the English and American gentlemen. Gumbo was declared perfection—a delicious discovery that earned the name of young George's black servant, found behind a door with his head buried in a bowl of the hotch-potch. The shad were rich and fresh; the stewed terrapins were worthy of London aldermen, a dish the General claimed the Duke himself would enjoy. Negro women possessed a genius for cookery, nurtured under the keen eye of the late and present Madam Esmond. Certain dishes, particularly sweets and flans, were prepared by Madam Esmond herself with remarkable dexterity. Carving principal pieces, rolling up the laced lappets of her sleeves, she revealed prettily rounded arms and slender wrists, performing an ancient rite of hospitality far superior to modern languid standards. The old law of the table dictated that the mistress press her guests with eager warmth, encouraging further enjoyment, mastering culinary anatomy, executing carving operations, and cheering her guests to fresh efforts. She whispered to her neighbor, Mr. Braddock, "I have kept for Your Excellency the jowl of this salmon—I take no denial!" She turned to Mr. Franklin, noting his water consumption despite a cellar of wholesome wines, and to Mr. Justice, asking if he favored woodcock pie.

"My dear lady," Mr. Laws responded with a profound bow, "we lack such a happy knack for pastry at home. I often tell my wife, 'My dear, I wish you had Madam Esmond's hand.'"

"It is a very pretty hand; surely others would admire it too," chimed the Postmaster of Boston, earning a half-smile from Mr. Esmond.
