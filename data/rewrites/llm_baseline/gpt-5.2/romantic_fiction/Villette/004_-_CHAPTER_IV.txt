When I left Bretton—only a few weeks after little Paulina had been carried away from it—I did not suspect that the quiet streets I had come to know by heart would close behind me for ever. I went home after six months’ absence; and if it be agreeable to imagine that I entered the familiar door with a grateful cry, and that kindred arms made a harbour of my return, the fancy may stand. Let the reader, if it pleases, lay a calm cloth over the next eight years: picture me as a vessel moored in fair weather, rocking softly, the deck warm, the air mild, the steersman asleep to all alarms.

Yet I remember too sharply that I did not live always in sunshine. There was a long season when the sea rose, when it struck salt into the throat and iced the lungs, when night after night gave neither star nor compass, and hope went down like a lantern drowned. I kept my own counsel through that storm. To whom, indeed, could I speak?

Mrs. Bretton had slipped away from my sight long before. Others had set impediments between us, and time completed what they began. I heard, through stray rumours, that the property she held for her son had shrunk—melted, as people say of money, as if it were butter left too near a fire—through some joint-stock venture. Graham had taken a profession; both mother and son had gone from Bretton, and were now in London. There was no hand to lean on, no old hearth to claim me. I had, willy-nilly, to become my own resource.

I do not pretend I was naturally fitted for enterprise. But necessity is a stern tutor, and its lessons are plain. When Miss Marchmont—an unmarried lady in our neighbourhood—sent for me, I went, because I hoped she might set me to some work I could do, and so give my days shape and bread.

She was rich, and lived in a handsome house; but for twenty years she had been a rheumatic cripple, powerless in hand and foot. Her world was upstairs. Her drawing-room opened into her bed-room, and there she sat—always—like a figure set in a niche, with the same walls for horizon, the same air for weather.

I had heard of her “eccentricities,” but had never seen her. She was grey, furrowed, and severe: solitude had made her grave; long pain had made her stern; and pain, when it has been kept too long without relief, will sometimes sharpen itself into irritability. A companion who had served her was about to marry. Hearing of my desolate position, she had thought I might take that place.

After tea, she told me so, frankly, as we sat by her fire.

“It will not be an easy life,” she said. “I require much attention, and you will be much confined; yet, compared with the life you have lately led, it may seem tolerable.”

I tried to make that word—tolerable—sound like safety, like shelter; but it fell on my mind with a dull weight. To spend youth in two heated rooms, watching suffering; sometimes bearing temper; with the past already behind me and not kindly behind—it was a prospect to chill the blood. Still, I forced myself to look at it steadily, neither adorning nor exaggerating it. I had too much plain sense, perhaps, to make even misery romantic.

“My doubt is whether I should have strength for the undertaking,” I said.

“That is my own scruple,” she returned. “You look a worn-out creature.”

So I did. In the glass, in my mourning, I saw a face faded and hollow-eyed, a mere pale reflection; but the look of it did not greatly alarm me. I felt, under the blight, a stubborn pulse still beating at life’s sources.

“What else have you in view—anything?” she asked.

“Nothing clear as yet; but I may find something.”

“So you imagine. Perhaps you are right. Try your own method then; and if it fails, test mine. I will leave my offer open to you for three months.”

It was kind—kind with her kind of kindness, which came as a concession from a proud nature. I thanked her, and while I spoke pain seized her suddenly—an attack sharp enough to blanch her lips. She dictated what must be done, with the cool precision of one long used to command even in suffering. I did as she directed: warmed what needed warming, applied what she required, moved with a promptness my own nerves hardly understood. Before the paroxysm had quite passed, a link had formed between us: a certain trust on her side, a certain pity and respect on mine.

She sent for me next day, and the next; for five or six successive days she claimed my company. Closer acquaintance showed faults and oddities, but it uncovered also something solid—a character made rigid by endurance, yet not base; harsh in sound, yet with reason running like a steady thread through passion. When she scolded—and she did scold, tartly at times—it stung less than one would think. There was nothing mean in it; it was more like an irascible mother rating a daughter than a mistress mortifying a dependant. She could storm, but she could not lecture: she did not preach my inferiority. Even in her fiercest moments, she was logical.

By degrees, the thought of remaining with her changed colour. In a week it was no longer a sentence but a duty I could accept. Before the three months were out I had agreed to stay.

Two hot, close rooms became my world; and a crippled old woman became—by a strange swift narrowing—my mistress, my friend, my all. Her service was my task; her pain my daily shadow; her relief my hope; her displeasure my punishment; her regard my reward. Outside the steam-dimmed panes there were fields and woods and running water, and the large, changeful sky; but I forgot them. Habit tamed me, destiny disciplined me. I did not ask for walks; my appetite grew content with the tiny trays brought for the invalid—thin broths, delicate jellies, custards beaten smooth, toast cut in narrow fingers, and the perpetual faint scent of warmed milk that clung to linen and curtains as if the room itself were forever nursing an illness. The house had its own hush: no wholesome kitchen clamour, no rattle of pans like cheerful bells—only the muted foot of servants, the subdued crackle of coal, the occasional clink of a spoon in a cup.

Yet even in that narrowed life there was something to feed upon. She had originality to study; steady virtues to respect; passions with power enough to command attention; and feelings true in their very severity. For these I attached myself to her. I would have crept on beside her for twenty years more, had her long endurance been so long prolonged.

But another decree stood written, and it seemed the intent of it was to drive me, not to let me rest. The little morsel of affection I held—held as one might hold a pearl—was to melt in my fingers like hail. The small adopted duty that suited my quiet conscience was to be snatched away. I had tried, in my heart, to bargain with Fate: to escape rare great agonies by submitting to a whole life of privation and small pains. Fate would not be so appeased.

One February night—I remember it with a strange bodily distinctness—there came a voice near Miss Marchmont’s house, heard by all who were under that roof, but interpreted, perhaps, by one alone. After a calm winter, storms were beginning to usher in spring. I had put Miss Marchmont to bed; I sat by the fire sewing. All day the wind had wailed at the windows, but as night deepened it changed tone. It grew keener, more searching, almost articulate—an utterance rather than a sound; a plaint that set the nerves listening against their will. In each gust there seemed a long sobbing cry.

“Oh, hush! hush!” I said to myself, dropping my work, raising a hand as if I could physically ward it off. That sound had visited my life before; and unwilling observation had fastened on my mind a belief about it. Three times I had known it to precede an atmosphere unpropitious to life. It seemed to me that epidemic disease is often heralded by just such a gasping, tormented east wind, lamenting for hours as if the very air were sick. Hence—so my fancy ran—might have sprung the old legend of the Banshee: not a spirit in a white dress, but the world’s own breath, foretelling a loss.

And with that eerie cry there came, to my thought, other disturbances—reported from far off—like echoes in the same body of earth: volcanoes restless under distant skies, rivers swelling beyond their banks, strange tides lifting and charging upon low coasts. As if the globe, at such times, were out of order—its pulse irregular—and the feeble amongst us withered in its distempered exhalation.

I listened and trembled. Miss Marchmont slept.

Near midnight, in half an hour, the storm fell into a dead calm. The fire that had been burning low glowed up suddenly, vivid and clear, as though the room drew a new breath. The air changed: it sharpened. I rose, lifted blind and curtain, and looked out. Stars sparkled with a hard brilliance in a sky rinsed clean; the keen glitter told of frost.

I turned, and met Miss Marchmont’s eyes. She was awake, her head lifted on the pillow, looking at me with an earnestness I had not seen in her before.

“Is it a fine night?” she asked.

“Yes—very fine.”

“I thought so,” she said slowly; “for I feel so strong—so well. Raise me. I feel young to-night—young, light-hearted, happy. What if my complaint were about to take a turn, and I were yet destined to enjoy health? It would be a miracle!”

“And these are not the days of miracles,” I thought; yet I wondered to hear such words from her lips.

She began to talk—not of medicines, not of aches, not of household minutiæ—but of the past; and she recalled it with a vividness that made the close room seem to widen into old scenes.

“I love Memory to-night,” she said. “I prize her as my best friend. She is giving me a deep delight. She brings back realities—not dead notions, but what once lived and warmed me—what I long thought dissolved and mixed with grave-mould. I have again the hours, the thoughts, the hopes of my youth. I renew the love of my life—its only love—almost its only affection; for I am not a particularly good woman: I am not amiable. Yet I have had feelings, strong and concentrated; and these feelings had their object. In him I gathered what others scatter over a hundred points. While I loved, and while I was loved, what an existence I enjoyed! What a glorious year!—how bright it returns! A living spring, a warm summer, soft moonlight over autumn evenings, and hope strong even under winter’s ice. Through that year my heart lived with Frank’s heart. O my noble Frank—my faithful Frank—my good Frank! Better than I—his standard higher in all things. I can see and say it now. If few women suffered as I did in his loss, few enjoyed as I did in his love. It was not common love: I never doubted him. It honoured, protected, elevated, as much as it gladdened. And now—now, when my mind is so strangely clear—let me ask why it was taken. For what crime was I condemned, after twelve months of bliss, to thirty years of sorrow?”

She paused, as if listening inwardly for an answer, and then shook her head faintly.

“I do not know,” she said. “I cannot—cannot—see the reason; yet at this hour I can say, sincerely, what I never tried to say before: Inscrutable God, Thy will be done! And at this moment I can believe that death will restore me to Frank. I never believed it till now.”

“He is dead, then?” I asked, softly.

“My dear girl,” she answered, “one happy Christmas Eve I dressed and decorated myself, expecting my lover—soon to be my husband—would come that night to visit me. I sat down to wait. I see it now: the snow-twilight stealing through the window—for I left the curtain undropped that I might watch him ride up the white walk; the soft firelight warming me, playing on my silk dress, and showing, fitfully, my own young figure in the glass. The moon of a calm winter night floated full, clear, and cold over the black shrubbery and the silvered turf. I waited, my pulse quick, but no doubt in my breast. The fire sank, but was still a bright mass; the moon mounted higher, but I could still see her from the lattice. The clock neared ten; he rarely tarried later, though once or twice he had been delayed.

“Would he fail me—once? No—not even once. And now he was coming—coming fast—to atone for lost time. ‘Frank! you furious rider,’ I said inwardly, listening gladly yet anxiously to the approaching gallop; ‘you shall be rebuked. It is my neck you put in peril, for whatever is yours is—dearer and more tenderly—mine.’

“There he was: I saw him—but my sight was confused; I think tears were in my eyes. I saw the horse; I heard it stamp; I saw a mass; I heard a clamour. Was it a horse? What heavy, dragging thing was it, crossing so darkly the lawn? How can one name such a thing in moonlight? How can one utter what rises in the soul?

“I could only run out. A great animal—Frank’s black horse—stood trembling, panting, snorting before the door; a man held it—Frank, as I thought.

“‘What is the matter?’ I demanded. Thomas, my servant, answered sharply: ‘Go into the house, madam.’ And calling to Ruth, who came hurrying as if instinct had summoned her: ‘Take missis into the house directly.’

“But I was kneeling in the snow beside something that lay there—something I had seen dragged along the ground—something that sighed, that groaned against my breast as I lifted it to me. He was not dead; he was not quite unconscious. I had him carried in; I refused to be thrust from him. I was collected enough to be mistress of myself and others. They would have treated me like a child—people always do when God’s hand strikes—but I gave place to none except the surgeon. And when he had done what he could, I took my dying Frank to myself. He had strength to fold me in his arms; power to speak my name. He heard me when I prayed softly over him; he felt me when I comforted him.

“‘Maria,’ he said, ‘I am dying in Paradise.’ He spent his last breath in faithful words for me. When the dawn of Christmas morning broke, my Frank was with God.

“And that,” she added, “was thirty years ago. I have suffered since. I doubt whether I made the best use of calamity. Soft, amiable natures it would have refined to saintliness; strong, evil spirits it would have made demons. As for me, I have only been a woe-struck and selfish woman.”

“You have done much good,” I said; for she was known for liberal almsgiving.

“I have not withheld money where it could assuage affliction. What of that? It cost me no effort, no pang, to give. But I think from this day I am about to enter a better frame of mind—to prepare for reunion with Frank. You see I still think of Frank more than of God; and unless it be counted that in loving the creature so much, so long, so exclusively, I have not blasphemed the Creator, small is my chance of salvation. What do you think, Lucy, of these things? Be my chaplain, and tell me.”

I could not answer. No speech came. Perhaps my silence served her as a reply, for she went on, as if I had spoken wisely.

“Very right, my child. We should acknowledge God merciful, though not always comprehensible to us. We should accept our lot, whatever it be, and try to render happy that of others. Should we not? Well, to-morrow I will begin by trying to make you happy. I will endeavour to do something for you, Lucy—something that will benefit you when I am dead. My head aches with talking; still, I am happy. Go to bed. The clock strikes two. How late you sit up—or rather, how late I, in my selfishness, keep you up. But go now; have no more anxiety for me. I feel I shall rest well.”

She settled herself as for sleep. I went to my little crib—a narrow closet within her room—where even the air seemed charged with the mingled scents of fire, camphor, and warmed linens.

The night passed quietly. Quietly, too, her doom must have come at last—peacefully, painlessly. In the morning she was found without life, nearly cold, her face calm and undisturbed. The strange lift of spirits, the softened mood, the clear talk of the past, had been but the prelude to the stroke: one blow sufficed to sever the thread of an existence long fretted by affliction.
