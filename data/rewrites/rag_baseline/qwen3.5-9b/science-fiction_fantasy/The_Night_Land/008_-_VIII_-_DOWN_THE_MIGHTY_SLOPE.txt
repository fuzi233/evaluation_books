The descent was a slow, deliberate erosion of the self. I moved downward into a darkness so absolute it seemed to press against the ribcage, a physical weight that threatened to crush the soul into silence. In this void, the boundary between traveler and abyss blurred; for moments at a time, I walked not on earth, but in a dreamlike suspension, stepping off the solid plane into the infinite Nothing. Each time the mind drifted toward this unreality, the body corrected the error with violent necessity—a boot striking a protruding ridge, a shoulder colliding with a hidden boulder—shaking me back into the harsh, undeniable fact of gravity.

Progress was agonizingly slow. Without sight, distance became meaningless; I covered perhaps a mile in two hours, my stride hampered by the terror of the unseen. To navigate this suffocating blackness, I activated the Diskos. Its spinning generated a faint, glistening cone of light, a tiny island in an ocean of night. This artificial beacon allowed me to trace a path, fixing a direction in my memory before the darkness swallowed it again. The light appeared impossibly bright, a testament to the sheer magnitude of the surrounding void. I relied on it sparingly, using it only when the pain of stumbling demanded clarity, conserving the power for moments of extremity.

After six hours of this bitter labor, past the twentieth hour since my last sleep, I halted on the Mighty Slope. Sitting amidst the eternal dark, I consumed two tablets and drank water, checking the rhythm of my survival. Wrapping my cloak tight, I used my satchel as a pillow, the Diskos resting nearby, and succumbed to sleep. My thoughts were vague, drifting toward Naani, the name anchoring me to a future that might never arrive.

I slept roughly six hours before waking abruptly, as if pulled from the depths by a touch. Gripping the Diskos, I listened intently into the silence. Nothing answered. No sound, no movement, only the heavy stillness of the night. Reassured, I ate and drank again, fumbling in the dark until my stomach was satisfied. I stood, gear secured, Diskos in hand, and resumed the descent.

A strange unease persisted throughout the day, a whisper in the spirit suggesting a presence following quietly behind. My ears detected nothing, yet the feeling remained. I continued downward, fighting the pull of the slope. Early in the seventh hour, disaster struck. My foot slipped into a small depression, pitching me forward. The impact shook me violently; the rock beneath me had torn my clothing, though my armor held.

Recovering my strength, I abandoned upright walking. Crawling on hands and knees became my mode of travel. This posture allowed me to feel the terrain directly, reducing the need for the Diskos, which I realized I was using too frequently. The journey was grueling, a series of sore miles through the Night Land. After eighteen hours of crawling, eating, and drinking three times, I found a relatively flat spot to rest. Clearing away loose stones, I settled in for sleep, haunted again by the sensation of a watcher in the dark. Twice I woke to listen, finding only silence, convincing myself it was paranoia before drifting into a restless slumber where I listened even in dreams.

The cycle repeated. Waking suddenly at dawn, gripping the Diskos, listening to the void—and finding nothing. The next day followed suit, except near the eighth hour, when I nearly plunged into a monstrous pit. Catching myself on the edge, I crawled around the precipice to reach the lower side, shaken and terrified, unsure if I had discovered a monster or merely imagined one.

Over-caution dictated my movements for days. Eventually, I devised a plan to move faster and safer. Lacking a rope, I improvised: I buckled my satchel and pouch together, extracting a long, thin strap. Attaching a stone to one end, I cast it ahead of me while crawling, holding the other end taut. If the line went slack, I knew a drop awaited; if it tightened, I knew the ground was firm. This cumbersome method offered a modicum of security, allowing me to traverse the slope with greater confidence than I had in days.

At the eighteenth hour, I slept again, only to wake strangely before the sixth hour. This recurring pattern added a layer of wonder and discomfort to my ordeal. Harm did not come, but the certainty of a presence lingered. I believed something was always near, though I could not determine if it was malevolent; it simply did not attack.

For three more days, I crept downward, weary and exhausted, the Diskos strapped to my hip. Six days had passed on the Great Slope, a descent into a dire destination unknown. The air itself had changed; the biting cold had receded, replaced by a heaviness that pressed against my chest. I reflected on the atmosphere of the Night Land, noting its thinness compared to the air of the present age. Unlike the dense air trapped within the Upper Cities or the Underground Fields, the air here lay low, near the earth, insufficient to support flight.

This scarcity of oxygen explained the absence of flying creatures in the Night Land, despite ancient records of monstrous flyers. It also rendered the idea of using a flying ship futile. Even if a machine existed in the Great Museum, constructed of indestructible grey metal, I lacked the skill to pilot it after a century of disuse. More importantly, the air was too weak to sustain lift, and the silence of the night would be shattered by the roar of engines—a noise that would invite the Forces of Evil. Furthermore, ascending into the night to escape the darkness felt like a form of madness, hanging suspended for the Evil to behold.

Why did the Road Builders of the distant past choose to construct a road rather than fly down the valley? Perhaps the air of the upper world had thinned so greatly over eons that humanity forgot the art of flight. Or perhaps the prospect of descending a hundred miles into a chasm filled with unknown monsters was too terrifying to contemplate. The Great Valley, formed when the earth split and oceans rushed inward, represented the ultimate catastrophe—the End of the World. Fire, storm, and chaos marked its birth. Yet, paradoxically, this destruction was the seed of a new eternity, a transition from death to life, from the old order to a strange, enduring future.
