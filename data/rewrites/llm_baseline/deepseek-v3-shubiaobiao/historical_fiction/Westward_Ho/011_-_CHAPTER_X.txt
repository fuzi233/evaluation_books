—Palamon and Arcite

So it befell to Chaucer’s knight in prison, and so it befell to Don Guzman de Soto—though his fate came not by steel or dungeon, but by the snares of his own pride and passion.

He had settled at Bideford under Grenville’s parole, lodged more comfortably than in many a campaign, and bore his captivity with the indifference of a soldier. Yet idleness bred restlessness, and restlessness, in time, a dangerous curiosity. When old Salterne, the mayor, came to dine at Stow, Don Guzman endured the merchant’s presence with the condescension of a nobleman forced to sup with his social inferior. But Salterne was no common tradesman. His shrewd wit and worldly wisdom soon pierced the Spaniard’s disdain, and before the evening ended, Guzman found himself drawn into conversation—first by Grenville’s courtesy, then by the merchant’s own cunning.

By the night’s close, Salterne extended an invitation to his own home. Don Guzman, amused by the novelty, accepted. There, he discovered a table well-stocked and a host eager to ply him with questions about the Spanish Main, the Moluccas, and the Indies—questions he parried with the skill of a man long practiced in guarding secrets.

Then entered Rose Salterne.

Had Don Guzman been a colder man, he might have laughed at himself for the stir she caused in him. She was a merchant’s daughter, a heretic, a girl of no rank—yet her freshness, her quick wit, and the artless admiration in her eyes ensnared him. He told himself it was mere diversion, a pastime to fill the dull hours of exile. But the heart is a traitor, and his pride was no match for her charm.

Soon, he found excuses to visit the Salterne household—to stroll in the garden, to linger over supper, to regale Rose with tales of his travels. He spoke of Peru, where he had been born; of Italy, where he had studied; of the Levant, where he had fought. He painted for her the siege of Famagusta, the horrors of Turkish captivity, the splendor of the East. And she, innocent of his designs, drank in every word.

Old Salterne, blinded by ambition, saw only opportunity. The Spaniard’s knowledge of trade routes, of Spanish ports and policies, was a treasure worth mining. He encouraged the visits, never suspecting the danger to his daughter.

But Don Guzman was no fool. He knew the gulf between them—knew that to love Rose Salterne was to forsake his name, his lineage, his faith. Yet love her he did, with a passion that startled even him. One evening, emboldened by solitude, he cast aside restraint.

*"Your cheek is not for me?"* he demanded when she refused his kiss. *"Yet it is free enough to every tradesman in Bideford!"*

*"They are my equals,"* she replied, trembling.

For a moment, pride warred with desire. Then, with a Spaniard’s fire, he fell to his knees.

*"I love you,"* he swore. *"Forget rank, forget creed—I am nothing but your slave."*

Rose fled. Yet in her heart, doubt lingered. Had his words been true? Or was she but another conquest, a pastime for an idle captive?

Days passed without his return. She watched from her window, torn between relief and despair. Had she driven him away too harshly? Or had she, in her innocence, been but a pawn in some deeper game?

Meanwhile, beyond the narrow world of her fears, greater tides were turning. Sir Humphrey Gilbert dreamed of new worlds, and England’s destiny stirred in the balance. But Rose Salterne, in her quiet torment, knew nothing of empires. She knew only that a Spaniard’s eyes had burned into her soul—and that she could not forget.
