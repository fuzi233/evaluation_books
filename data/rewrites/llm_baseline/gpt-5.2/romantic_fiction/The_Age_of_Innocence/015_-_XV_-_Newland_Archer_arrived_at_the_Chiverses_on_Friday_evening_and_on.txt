Newland Archer reached the Chiverses’ place on Friday evening, and on Saturday submitted, as if to a liturgy, to every rite a country week-end at Highbank demanded.

In the morning he skimmed the ice in a little boat that hissed like a knife along a plate, the wind singing in the rigging and the runners ringing on the hard surface; there was a clean, mineral smell where the lake had frozen, and the distant cry of a crow stitched itself into the white air. His hostess laughed too loudly, as people did when the cold made them feel alive and watched at once, and Newland smiled when he ought, answering in the accepted phrases.

In the afternoon Reggie Chivers took him “over the farm,” past stalls as polished as drawing-rooms, where the sweet ammoniac warmth of horses and the sharp bite of leather rose and fell with every breath; the clink of a currycomb against a buckle had the exactness of music, and Reggie’s voice, droning on about bloodlines and feed and the virtues of a particular bit, seemed almost part of the stable soundscape, as necessary as the stamping and the patient, heavy exhalations. Archer listened, nodded, asked the proper questions, and felt the hours packed around him like wool.

After tea—tea that tasted of over-steeped leaves and propriety—he talked in a corner of the hall, firelight making a false dusk on gilded cornices, with a young lady who had once announced herself broken-hearted at his engagement and now, with the zeal of the newly consoled, wanted him to be moved by her own matrimonial prospects. The house itself was never quiet. Doors opened and shut, feet crossed corridors, a servant appeared with coals, another with the tray, and the constant, careful rustle of comfort made its own kind of noise—an upholstered, well-fed noise that left no room for a private thought.

At midnight the week-end’s spirit of regulated disorder took possession of the guests. Archer helped put a gold-fish into one visitor’s bed; he assisted in dressing up a burglar in the bath-room of a nervous aunt; and, toward dawn, he was drawn into a pillow-fight that ranged from nurseries to basement, the shrieks and thuds and flurried steps ricocheting through stairwells and passages until the whole place sounded like a holiday market hastily shut in under a roof.

But Sunday after luncheon he borrowed a cutter and drove over to Skuytercliff.

People had always been told the house there was an Italian villa. Those who had never been to Italy believed it; so did some who had. It had been built by Mr. van der Luyden on his return from the grand tour, in anticipation of his marriage to Miss Louisa Dagonet: a large square wooden structure painted pale green and white, with a Corinthian portico and fluted pilasters between the windows—Italy translated into the stiff grammar of New York carpentry, a dialect that tried for melody and achieved only correctness.

From the high ground a series of terraces, bordered by balustrades and urns, descended in the steel-engraving style to a small irregular lake edged with asphalt and overhung by rare weeping conifers. Right and left the famous weedless lawns, studded with specimen trees—each a different variety, each labelled by its mere existence—rolled away to long ranges of grass crested with elaborate cast-iron ornaments. Below, tucked into a hollow like an older sentence surviving in a new language, lay the four-roomed stone house the first Patroon had built on the land granted him in 1612.

Against the uniform sheet of snow and the greyish winter sky the villa loomed up grimly; even in summer it kept its distance, and the boldest coleus bed had never ventured within thirty feet of its awful front. Now, when Archer rang, the long tinkle seemed to run through the place as through a mausoleum. It took time for the sound to be answered. When at length the butler appeared, his surprise was such as if he had been called back from final sleep.

Happily Archer was of the family; irregular though his arrival was, he was entitled to information.

“The Countess Olenska is out, sir,” the butler said, with the grave relief of a man delivering a message to which his whole house was subordinate. “She drove to afternoon service with Mrs. van der Luyden exactly three quarters of an hour ago. Mr. van der Luyden is in, sir; but my impression is that he is either finishing his nap or else reading yesterday’s Evening Post. I heard him say, sir, on his return from church, that he intended to look through the Evening Post after luncheon; if you like, sir, I might go to the library door and listen—”

Archer thanked him and said he would go and meet the ladies. The butler, obviously relieved that he was not to be sent as a spy to a library door, closed the great door on him with majesty. A groom took the cutter away, runners crunching on packed snow like bones underfoot, and Archer struck through the park toward the high-road.

Skuytercliff village was only a mile and a half away; but Mrs. van der Luyden never walked, and he knew he must keep to the road to meet the carriage. Presently, however, coming down a footpath that crossed the highway, he caught sight of a slight figure in a red cloak, with a big dog running ahead.

He hurried forward. Madame Olenska stopped short and smiled as if he were the answer to something she had asked of the air.

“Ah, you’ve come!” she said, drawing her hand from her muff.

The red cloak made her look gay and vivid, like the Ellen Mingott of old days—something bright and alive moving across the disciplined whiteness. He laughed as he took her hand.

“I came to see what you were running away from.”

Her face clouded, but she answered: “Ah—well. You will see, presently.”

It puzzled him. “Why—do you mean you’ve been overtaken?”

She shrugged, with a little movement that reminded him of Nastasia, and at once lightened her tone. “Shall we walk on? I’m so cold after the sermon. And what does it matter, now you’re here to protect me?”

The word protect set his blood beating like a drum. He caught a fold of her cloak, as if to hold her where she stood.

“Ellen—what is it? You must tell me.”

“Oh, presently—let’s run a race first: my feet are freezing to the ground,” she cried; and, gathering her cloak, she fled across the snow, the dog leaping about her with challenging barks.

For a moment Archer stood watching, his gaze delighted by the flash of that red meteor against the white. The snow itself seemed to throw sound back at him—the quick tap of her boots, the dog’s excited bark, the faint creak of branches—so that her laughter, when it came, landed on his nerves like warmth. Then he started after her, and they met, panting and laughing, at a wicket that led into the park.

She looked up at him. “I knew you’d come!”

“That shows you wanted me to,” he said, and took an almost shameful joy in their foolishness.

The white glitter of the trees filled the air with its own strange brightness, and as they walked on the ground seemed to sing under their feet—snow protesting softly, a clean, crisp sound that made their steps feel like the only human thing in an immense quiet.

“Where did you come from?” she asked.

He told her, and added: “It was because I got your note.”

After a pause she said, with a chill barely perceptible: “May asked you to take care of me.”

“I didn’t need any asking.”

“You mean—I’m so evidently helpless and defenceless? What a poor thing you must all think me! But women here seem not—seem never to feel the need: any more than the blessed in heaven.”

He lowered his voice. “What sort of a need?”

“Ah, don’t ask me! I don’t speak your language,” she retorted, petulant as a child who has been corrected.

The sentence struck him with the bluntness of a blow. He stopped in the path and looked down at her, and the cold air seemed suddenly to have no fragrance, only a thin, metallic taste.

“What did I come for, if I don’t speak yours?”

“Oh, my friend—!” She laid her hand lightly on his arm, and it was as if she were trying to soften the very words she had thrown.

He pleaded, the need in him louder than any discretion: “Ellen—why won’t you tell me what’s happened?”

She shrugged again. “Does anything ever happen in heaven?”

They walked on a few yards without speaking. Then, as if the house itself had answered her with its perpetual, muffled vigilance, she broke out:

“I will tell you—but where, where, where? One can’t be alone for a minute in that great seminary of a house, with all the doors wide open, and always a servant bringing tea, or a log for the fire, or the newspaper! Is there nowhere in an American house where one may be by one’s self? You’re so shy, and yet you’re so public. I always feel as if I were in the convent again—or on the stage, before a dreadfully polite audience that never applauds.”

“Ah, you don’t like us!” Archer exclaimed.

They were passing the old Patroon’s house—squat walls, small square windows grouped around a central chimney. The shutters stood wide, and through one of the newly washed panes Archer caught the glow of a fire. That little place seemed to breathe differently from the big villa: the crackle of burning wood, the quiet that held it, the sense that the sound belonged to the room and not to an audience.

“Why—the house is open!” he said.

She stopped. “No; only for today, at least. I wanted to see it, and Mr. van der Luyden had the fire lit and the windows opened, so that we might stop there on the way back from church this morning.” She ran up the steps and tried the door. “It’s still unlocked—what luck! Come in and we can have a quiet talk. Mrs. van der Luyden has driven over to see her old aunts at Rhinebeck and we shan’t be missed at the house for another hour.”

He followed her into the narrow passage.

His spirits, which had dropped at her words about “your language,” rose with an irrational leap. The homely little house stood there, its panels and brasses shining in firelight as if it had been waiting through centuries to shelter just such an hour. A bed of embers still glowed in the kitchen chimney, under an iron pot hung from an ancient crane. The faint smell of woodsmoke and iron and old stone—something like the taste of rye bread baked close to a hearth—wrapped itself around him with an intimacy the great villa could never permit. Rush-bottomed chairs faced each other across the tiled hearth; rows of Delft plates stood on shelves against the walls, blue designs repeating themselves like a family story told in the same words from generation to generation.

Archer stooped, chose a log, and set it on the embers. The fire answered with a little crack, a soft settling sigh, and then the quicker, brighter speech of flame. Madame Olenska dropped her cloak and sat down. He leaned against the chimney and looked at her.

“You’re laughing now; but when you wrote me you were unhappy.”

“Yes.” She paused. “But I can’t feel unhappy when you’re here.”

“I shan’t be here long,” he said, his mouth stiff with the effort to offer no more than the fact.

“No; I know. But I’m improvident: I live in the moment when I’m happy.”

The words moved through him like temptation given a voice. He stepped away from the hearth and stood at the window, gazing out at black tree-boles scored against the snow. But it was as if she had shifted with him; between him and the trees he still saw her, bent toward the fire, smiling in that indolent way that seemed to hold a whole history he could not translate.

His heart beat out of step with his reason. What if she had been running from him—what if she had made him come for that? What if she had waited until they were alone in this secret, modest room, with its honest fire and its quiet?

“Ellen, if I’m really a help to you—if you really wanted me to come—tell me what’s wrong, tell me what it is you’re running away from,” he insisted.

He spoke without turning. If the thing was to happen—whatever it was—let it happen with the width of the room between them, his eyes fixed on the snow, as if he could keep himself from it by refusing to look.

For a long moment she was silent. In that moment he imagined—almost heard—her stealing up behind him, those light arms coming around his neck, warmth against the cold glass of his restraint.

While he waited, soul and body throbbing, his eyes mechanically took in the figure of a heavily coated man advancing along the path, fur collar turned up. The man was Julius Beaufort.

“Ah—!” Archer cried, bursting into a laugh that hurt him as it came out.

Madame Olenska had sprung up and moved to his side, slipping her hand into his; but after a glance through the window her face went pale and she shrank back, as if the air itself had changed key.

“So that was it?” Archer said, with a derision that was meant for his own folly as much as for anything else.

“I didn’t know he was here,” she murmured.

Her hand still clung to his; but he drew away, went out into the passage, and flung open the door.

“Hallo, Beaufort—this way! Madame Olenska was expecting you,” he called, and his voice sounded too loud in the little house, as if the old walls resented being made a stage.

On the way back to New York next morning Archer relived, with exhausting vividness, his last moments at Skuytercliff.

Beaufort, clearly annoyed at finding him there, had carried off the situation as he always did—high-handedly, by behaving as though whatever inconvenienced him scarcely existed. His talent for making the inconvenient feel invisible had a brutal efficiency, and Archer, sensitive enough to feel it, suffered from that strange sense of nonexistence. Yet the humiliation gave him the ghostly advantage of watching without being watched.

Beaufort had entered the little house with easy assurance, but he could not smile away the vertical line between his eyes. It was clear Madame Olenska had not known he was coming, though her talk to Archer had hinted at some looming pursuit; at any rate she had not told Beaufort where she was going when she left New York, and her unexplained departure had exasperated him. The ostensible reason of his appearance was the discovery, the night before, of a “perfect little house”—not in the market—which was exactly the thing for her and would be snapped up instantly if she didn’t take it. He was loud in mock reproaches for the dance she had led him, running away just as he’d found it.

“If only this new dodge for talking along a wire had been a little nearer perfection I might have told you all this from town,” he grumbled, “and been toasting my toes before the club fire at this minute instead of tramping after you through the snow.”

Madame Olenska, taking the opening as if it were a rope thrown across a chasm, twisted the talk away to the fantastic possibility that people might one day converse from street to street—or, incredible dream, from one town to another. The notion drew from all three the inevitable allusions to Edgar Poe and Jules Verne, and the platitudes that rise when one is pressed for time and faced with an invention one is afraid of seeming too naïve to believe. So the telephone—this new instrument that would carry voices while leaving bodies behind—brought them safely back to the big house, where sound was always managed and supervised, and nothing human was ever allowed to ring too nakedly.

Mrs. van der Luyden had not yet returned. Archer took his leave and walked off to fetch the cutter, while Beaufort followed the Countess indoors. Little as the van der Luydens encouraged unannounced visits, Beaufort could count on being asked to dine and then sent back to the station to catch the nine o’clock train; but more than that he would not get. It would never occur to his hosts that a gentleman travelling without luggage might wish to spend the night; and it would be distasteful to them to propose such a thing to a person for whom their cordiality was so carefully limited. Beaufort knew all this, must have foreseen it. His willingness to travel so far for so small a reward measured his impatience.

He was undeniably in pursuit of Madame Olenska; and Beaufort had only one object in view when he pursued pretty women.

His dull, childless home had long ago palled on him; besides more permanent consolations he was always in quest of amorous adventure in his own set. This was the man from whom Ellen was, by her own admission, flying. The question was: had she fled because his importunities disgusted her, or because she did not wholly trust herself to resist them? Or—most disturbing possibility—had her talk of flight been only a blind, her departure a manoeuvre?

Archer did not really believe that. Little as he had actually seen of her, he was beginning to think he could read her face, and if not her face, her voice. Both had betrayed annoyance, even dismay, at Beaufort’s sudden appearance.

And yet—if she truly feared him—was that not worse than if she had gone to meet him? If she had chosen him knowingly, she would simply have “classed” herself, irretrievably, among the vulgarest of dissemblers; she would cease to be mysterious, and therefore to be—he hated himself for the word—interesting. But it was worse a thousand times if, judging Beaufort and likely despising him, she was still drawn by what made him different from the other men around her: his habit of two continents and two societies, his easy familiarity with artists and actors and people in the world’s eye, his careless contempt for local prejudices.

Beaufort was vulgar, uneducated, purse-proud; yet circumstances and a certain native shrewdness made him, in talk, more various than many men morally and socially his betters, whose horizon was bounded by the Battery and Central Park. How could any one who had known a wider world not feel the difference, and feel, in spite of herself, its pull? Ellen had flung at Archer, in irritation, that they did not speak the same language; and he knew that in some respects it was true. But Beaufort understood every turn of her dialect and spoke it fluently: his view of life, his tone, his posture toward experience, were a coarser reflection of what Count Olenski’s letter had revealed. It might seem a disadvantage with Olenski’s wife; but Archer was too intelligent to imagine that a young woman would recoil from everything that reminded her of her past. She might revolt against it; yet what had once charmed her could still charm her, even against her will—like a taste remembered, impossible to explain to someone who had never eaten the dish, impossible to forget once it had melted on the tongue.

Thus, with painful impartiality, he made out the case for Beaufort, and for Beaufort’s possible victim. He longed to enlighten her; there were moments when he imagined all she asked was to be enlightened. Yet what did “enlighten” mean, when she had said he did not speak her language? Perhaps the very words he would use were part of the trap: the accepted terms, the dominant lexicon of his world, the phrases that could classify and condemn without ever understanding.

That evening he unpacked his books from London. The box was full of things he had waited for impatiently: a new volume of Herbert Spencer, another collection of Alphonse Daudet’s brilliant tales, and a novel called Middlemarch, of which the reviews had lately been saying interesting things. He had declined three dinner invitations in favour of this feast, but though he turned the pages with the sensuous joy of a book-lover, he did not know what he was reading. One book after another slipped from his hand. Even the smell of paper and cloth and fresh-cut pages—usually as bracing as a draught of wine—could not bring his mind to heel.

Suddenly, among them, he lit on a small volume of verse he had ordered because the name had attracted him: The House of Life.

He took it up and found himself plunged into an atmosphere unlike any he had breathed in books: warm, rich, and yet ineffably tender, giving a new, haunting beauty to the most elementary human passions. All night he pursued through those enchanted pages the vision of a woman with Ellen Olenska’s face—her quick laughter, her sudden chill, the untranslatable accent of her pain.

But in the morning, when he woke and looked out at the brownstone houses across the street, and thought of his desk in Mr. Letterblair’s office and the family pew in Grace Church, his hour in the park at Skuytercliff became as far outside probability as the visions of the night. The city’s daily roar—the wheels, the shouted calls, the muffled thunder of ceaseless commerce—closed around him like a lid, flattening mystery into routine.

“Mercy, how pale you look, Newland!” Janey commented over the coffee-cups at breakfast.

His mother added: “Newland, dear, I’ve noticed lately that you’ve been coughing; I do hope you’re not letting yourself be overworked?”

For both ladies were convinced that, under the iron despotism of his senior partners, his life was spent in exhausting professional labours—and he had never thought it necessary to undeceive them.

The next two or three days dragged heavily. The taste of the usual was like cinders in his mouth; at moments he felt as if he were being buried alive under his future. He heard nothing of the Countess Olenska, nothing of the perfect little house. When he met Beaufort at the club they merely nodded across the whist-tables, as if what had happened in that small stone house belonged to a language neither of them would use aloud.

It was not till the fourth evening that he found a note awaiting him on his return home.

“Come late tomorrow: I must explain to you. Ellen.”

Those were the only words.

He was dining out. He thrust the note into his pocket, smiling a little at the Frenchness of the “to you,” at the faint foreign tilt of it—like a spice one could taste without naming. After dinner he went to a play; it was not until he returned home after midnight that he drew the missive out again and reread it slowly, a number of times, as if repetition could make its meaning safer.

There were several ways of answering it. He weighed each one through the watches of an agitated night. And when morning came, the decision he finally made was to pitch some clothes into a portmanteau and jump aboard a boat leaving that very afternoon for St. Augustine.
