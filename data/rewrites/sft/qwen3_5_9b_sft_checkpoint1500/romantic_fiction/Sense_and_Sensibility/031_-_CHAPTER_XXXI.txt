Marianne awoke the following morning with a renewed, suffocating awareness of her misery, a state of soul she had barely dared to close her eyes against the night before. Elinor, gentle yet firm, pressed her to articulate the turmoil within, and before breakfast was served, they had traversed the landscape of Marianne's grief again and again. With Elinor offering steady counsel and affection, Marianne oscillated between impulsive despair and fleeting moments of hope. At times, she clung to the delusion that Willoughby was merely unfortunate and innocent, sharing her own tragedy; at others, the sheer impossibility of defending his actions stripped her of every consolation. Her moods swung violently: from absolute indifference to the scrutiny of the world, to a desire to vanish from it forever, and finally to a fierce resistance against any observation. Yet, in one matter, she remained constant: she rigorously avoided the presence of Mrs. Jennings, and when forced to endure it, she fell into a determined, icy silence. Her heart had grown hard against the possibility that Mrs. Jennings felt anything more than a casual interest in her sorrows.

"No, no, no!" Marianne cried, her voice trembling with indignation. "She cannot feel! Her kindness is not sympathy; her goodness is not tenderness. She craves nothing but gossip, and she cherishes me solely because I provide it."

Elinor, observing her sister, recognized the injustice of these judgments. Though Marianne possessed an excellent disposition and formidable abilities, she lacked reason and candor. Like many of the clever and virtuous, she projected her own internal standards onto others, measuring their motives by the immediate impact of their actions on herself. A specific incident later that morning, occurring in the privacy of their chamber after breakfast, drove this judgment of Mrs. Jennings further into despair. Through a weakness of her own, Marianne had inadvertently become the vessel for fresh pain, though Mrs. Jennings acted with pure goodwill.

Mrs. Jennings entered with a letter held outstretched, her countenance bright with the promise of solace. "Now, my dear," she announced, "I bring you something certain to do you good."

Marianne heard the fatal words. In an instant, her feverish imagination conjured a letter from Willoughby, brimming with tenderness, contrition, and explanations that would absolve him entirely, swiftly followed by Willoughby himself, rushing to kneel at her feet. The vision shattered as quickly as it formed. Before her lay the handwriting of her mother, a sight never previously unwelcome, now a source of agonizing betrayal. The cruel sting of Mrs. Jennings' deception, born of misguided confidence, overwhelmed Marianne. Tears streamed from her eyes with violent passion, a reproach that Mrs. Jennings, oblivious to the depth of her offense, could only interpret as excessive emotion. Despite Marianne's weeping, Mrs. Jennings withdrew, still insisting on the comfort of the letter. But when Marianne finally read it, calm enough to process the words, she found little solace. Every page echoed with Willoughby's name. Her mother, still blindly confident in their engagement and trusting in his constancy, had pleaded only for Marianne to be more open, speaking with a tenderness and affection for Willoughby that caused Marianne to weep with agony throughout the reading.

An overwhelming impatience to return home seized Marianne. Her mother, despite her errors, became dearest to her now, cherished precisely because of the tragic excess of her misplaced faith in Willoughby. Marianne was wild to depart. Elinor, torn between the wisdom of staying and the need to obey, offered no counsel save patience until their mother's reply arrived. Eventually, Marianne consented to wait.

As Mrs. Jennings prepared to leave, earlier than usual, she refused Elinor's company, needing the Middletons and Palmers to share her sorrow. Alone, Elinor sat down to write to her mother, her heart heavy with the knowledge of the pain she was about to reveal, knowing her previous efforts to prepare the ground had failed. Marianne, entering the drawing-room as her aunt departed, stood fixed beside Elinor, watching the movement of her pen. She grieved for the burden of the task and for the inevitable heartbreak it would cause her mother.

For about fifteen minutes, silence hung over them, broken only by the scratching of the pen, when a sharp rap at the door startled Marianne. Her nerves, already frayed, could not withstand the sudden noise.

"Who can this be?" cried Elinor. "So early! I thought we were safe."

Marianne hastened to the window. "It is Colonel Brandon!" she exclaimed, her face flushing with vexation. "We are never safe from *him*."

"He will not enter, as Mrs. Jennings is absent."

"I will not trust in that," Marianne retreated to her own room, her voice sharp. "A man who possesses no occupation for his time has no conscience regarding his intrusions into the leisure of others."

Her conjecture proved correct, though founded on error. Colonel Brandon did enter. Elinor, seeing the genuine solicitude in his disturbed, melancholy gaze and his anxious inquiry after Marianne, could not forgive her sister's shallow estimation of him.

"I met Mrs. Jennings in Bond Street," Colonel Brandon began, after the initial salutations. "She urged me to come. I thought it probable I might find you alone, a desire I hold strongly. My sole wish—to express it clearly—is to offer conviction, lasting conviction, to your sister's mind. Not merely temporary comfort, but the truth. My regard for her, for yourself, and for your mother... will you permit me to prove it by relating some circumstances? Nothing but sincere regard and an earnest desire to be useful justify me, though after spending so many hours convincing myself of my rightness, am I not afraid I may be wrong?" He paused, breathless.

"I understand," said Elinor gently. "You have something to tell me of Mr. Willoughby that will fully reveal his character. To disclose this is the greatest act of friendship for Marianne. My gratitude is immediate, and hers will follow in time. Please, let me hear it."

"You shall," Brandon replied, composing himself. "To be brief, when I left Barton last October... I must go further back. I am an awkward narrator, Miss Dashwood; I hardly know where to start. A short account of myself seems necessary, and it shall be brief. On such a subject..." He sighed heavily. "...can I have little temptation to be diffuse."

He hesitated, gathering his thoughts, then continued with another sigh. "You likely forgot a conversation—an evening at Barton Park, during a dance—in which I alluded to a lady I once knew, noting her resemblance to your sister, Marianne."

"I have not forgotten it," Elinor admitted. He looked pleased. "If I am not misled by the bias of tender memory, the resemblance is striking, both in mind and person. The same warmth of heart, the same eagerness of spirit. This lady was my nearest relative, an orphan from infancy, under my father's guardianship. Our ages were alike, and from childhood we were playmates and friends. I cannot recall a time when I did not love Eliza. As we matured, my affection deepened into something profound, perhaps so intense that, judging by my current forlorn gravity, you might deem me incapable of feeling it. Hers, for me, was fervent, equal to the attachment of your sister for Mr. Willoughby, and equally unfortunate. At seventeen, she was lost to me forever. She was married, against her will, to my brother. Her fortune was substantial, but our estate heavily encumbered. And I fear this is all that can be said of the conduct of a man who was both her uncle and guardian. My brother did not deserve her; he did not even love her. I had hoped her regard for me would sustain her, and for some time it did. But eventually, the misery of her situation, marked by great unkindness, overcame her resolve. Though she had promised me otherwise, blind as I was in relating it, the truth emerged. We were within hours of eloping to Scotland. The treachery—or folly—of my cousin's maid betrayed us. I was banished to a distant relation's house, and she was denied liberty, society, or amusement until my father's demands were met. I had relied too heavily on her fortitude, and the blow was severe. If her marriage had been happy, so young as I was, a few months might have reconciled me, or at least I would not mourn it today. But it was not. My brother held no regard for her; his pleasures were base, and from the start, he treated her unkindly. For a mind so young, lively, and inexperienced as Mrs. Brandon's, the consequence was natural. She submitted initially to the misery, but if she had not lived to overcome the regrets caused by my memory, it would have been fortunate. But she did. Can we wonder that, with a husband provoking inconstancy and no friend to advise or restrain her—for my father died shortly after their union, and I was stationed in the East Indies—she should fall? Had I remained in England, perhaps... but I sought to promote her happiness by removing her, for which purpose I secured an exchange. The shock of her marriage hurt me, but it was trifling compared to what I felt hearing of her divorce two years later. It was *that* which cast this gloom—the recollection of my suffering..."
