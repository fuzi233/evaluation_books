The Mother of Sorrows rose without haste, as if she would not wake the grief too suddenly, drew her veil closer about her face, and went to call the Little Ones.

They slept like dough left to prove in a warm corner—still, deep, and whole; yet at her first word they started up at once, bright-eyed and ready, as if the night had been no more than the turning of a page. Down the stair they went after her in a little patter of bare feet, their sounds small and soft in the house’s hush, and she led them to where the princess lay.

Lilith slept, and in her sleep her tears had not ceased. They ran steadily, thread after thread, along the hollows of her cheeks, gathered at her jaw, and fell—clear drops with a faint salt tang that seemed to sharpen the air. The boys’ faces, that had come in laughing, stilled into wonder.

“The sky is falling!” whispered one, staring out at the rain beyond the window.

“The white juice is running out of the princess!” cried another, not mocking but awed, as though he had found a spring where no spring should be.

Odu leaned close, peering at the thin, glistening tracks. “Is it rivers?”

“Yes,” answered Mara, “the most wonderful of all rivers.”

Odu frowned, working at the word as if it were a crust too hard for his teeth. “I thought rivers was bigger, and rushed—like a lot of Little Ones, making loud noises!” He glanced toward me, for from me alone he had heard the thing described.

“Look at the rivers of the sky,” said Mara, and her voice seemed to tune itself to the rain’s steady drumming. “See how they come down to wake up the waters under the earth. Soon will the rivers be flowing everywhere—merry and loud, like thousands and thousands of happy children. Oh, how glad they will make you, Little Ones! You have never seen any, and do not know how lovely is the water.”

“That will be the glad of the ground that the princess is grown good,” said Odu. “See the glad of the sky!”

“Are the rivers the glad of the princess?” asked Luva. “They are not her juice, for they are not red!”

“They are the juice inside the juice,” said Mara.

Odu touched a finger to his own eye, tasted the wet on his skin by instinct though not with his tongue, and shook his head as if the matter would not come right in his understanding.

“Princess will not bite now!” said Luva, keeping her gaze fixed on Lilith’s face as though watching a beast that might wake.

“No; she will never do that again,” replied Mara. “But now we must take her nearer home.”

“Is that a nest?” asked Sozo.

“Yes; a very big nest. But we must take her to another place first.”

“What is that?”

“It is the biggest room in all this world.” Mara’s eyes went far away for a moment, as if she saw the room already crowded. “But I think it is going to be pulled down: it will soon be too full of little nests. Go and get your clumsies.”

“Please are there any cats in it?” asked a boy, anxious in the practical way of children.

“Not one. The nests are too full of lovely dreams for one cat to get in.”

“We shall be ready in a minute,” said Odu, and ran out, followed by all except Luva.

Lilith’s eyelids fluttered; she was awake now, listening. A sad smile lay on her mouth, as if she heard herself spoken of like a sick creature to be carried. Luva stood close beside her.

“But her rivers are running so fast!” Luva said, distressed, watching the wet darken the pillow cloth. “Her robe is all—I don’t know what. Clumsies won’t like it!”

“They won’t mind it,” answered Mara. “Those rivers are so clean that they make the whole world clean.”

I had fallen asleep by the fire; I had been awake for some time, hearing their voices and the rain’s steady cooking of the silence into something bearable. Now I rose.

“It is time to mount, Mr. Vane,” said our hostess.

“Tell me,” I said, “is there not a way by which to avoid the channels and the den of monsters?”

“There is an easy way across the river-bed, which I will show you,” she answered; “but you must pass once more through the monsters.”

“I fear for the children.”

“Fear will not once come nigh them,” she said, and there was something in her tone like a remembered lullaby—older than words, older than the fear itself.

We left the cottage. The beasts stood waiting about the door, patient as beasts that know their work is not hunting but bearing. Odu was already astride the neck of one of the two that were to carry the princess.

I mounted Lona’s horse. Mara brought her body and placed it in my arms—so light, so still, like bread cooled and gone stale before its time. When she came out again with Lilith, a cry of delight broke from the children: the princess was no longer muffled. Her face, uncovered, shone pale under the morning’s clean light, her lashes dark against her skin, her mouth set as if she feared even her own breath. The boys, entranced, forgot to help.

But the great elephants did not forget. With trunks gentle as hands that have kneaded dough and learned tenderness, one looped about her body, the other under her knees; with Mara’s guiding touch they laid her along between them as if setting a fragile platter between two steady boards.

“Why does the princess want to go?” asked a small boy. “She would keep good if she staid here!”

“She wants to go, and she does not want to go: we are helping her,” answered Mara. “She will not keep good here.”

“What are you helping her to do?”

“To go where she will get more help—help to open her hand, which has been closed for a thousand years.”

“So long?” The child pondered, like an elder weighing a seed against a season. “Then she has learned to do without it: why should she open it now?”

“Because it is shut upon something that is not hers.”

“Please, lady Mara, may we have some of your very dry bread before we go?” asked Luva suddenly, with that sharp turn children make from dread to hunger.

Mara smiled and went back. She returned with four loaves—so hard and brown they looked as if the oven had kissed them longer than kindness would advise—and a great jug of water. The loaves gave off a scent of scorched grain and honest heat; the water smelled of stone and depth.

“We will eat as we go,” they said; but the jug went from hand to hand at once. They drank with bright astonishment, as if water were a festival.

“I think,” said one, wiping his mouth, “it must be elephant-juice! It makes me so strong!”

We set out. The Lady of Sorrow walked with us—her beauty not loud like sunlight on metal, but steady like fire in a hearth—and the white leopardess followed, silent as a thought.

At first I thought our hostess meant only to show us the way across the channels and then return. Soon I knew she would go with us to the end. I would have dismounted that she might ride, but she would not.

“I have no burden to carry,” she said. “The children and I will walk together.”

It was the loveliest of mornings; the sun poured out his brightest, the wind brought his sweetest, yet the desert took no comfort, for it had no water to answer. The world sounded thirsty. Even the wind’s voice seemed to search for a creek-bed that would sing back.

We crossed the channels without difficulty. The Little Ones gambolled about Mara, their small talk and scuffing feet making a kind of domestic music in that bare place, as if they walked a kitchen floor rather than a waste. But we did not reach the ridge above the bad burrow until the sun was already slipping away.

Then I made the Little Ones mount their elephants, for the moon might be late, and I could not help my anxiety. The Lady of Sorrow came by my side and led the way; the elephants followed, the two bearing Lilith in the centre; the leopardess kept behind.

Just as we reached the frightful margin, the moon looked up and showed the shallow basin lying before us—smooth, quiet, deceitfully settled, like a pan of milk with a skin upon it.

Mara stepped into it. Nothing answered her tread, nor the hoofbeats of my horse: no crack, no sigh. But the moment the elephants carrying Lilith touched that ground, it woke like something sleeping ill. The seeming earth began to heave and boil, and the whole brood of the nest was commoved. It was not only sight; the place had its own brutal acoustics—the wet suck of shifting soil, the hard clap of something snapping shut, the hollow cough of buried cavities opening. Noise with no music in it, noise like a machine grinding bone.

Monsters uprose on all sides—every neck stretched, every beak and claw thrust out, every mouth gaping. Long-billed heads, horribly jawed faces, knotty tentacles without number reached toward Lilith. She lay rigid in an agony of fear; she dared not move so much as a finger.

Whether those hideous things even saw the children, I doubt. Not one touched a child. Not one foul member passed the living rampart of her body-guard to lay hold of her.

“Little Ones,” I cried, “keep your elephants close about the princess. Be brave; they will not touch you.”

“What will not touch us? We don’t know what to be brave at!” they called back, and I understood then: they were unaware. They could not see one of the deformities around them.

“Never mind then,” I returned. “Only keep close.”

They were panoplied in their blindness. Incapacity to see was their safety; what they could not be aware of could not hurt them. I, who saw, felt every shape like a taste in the mouth—a bitterness, a rancid taint—things that did not belong in the world’s stew, yet rose here like scum.

Mara kept a few paces in front of me when a solitary head—bodiless, obscene in its independence—bounced on the path between us. The leopardess came rushing under the elephants from behind and would have seized it, but with frightful contortions and a loathsome howl it twisted away, spun like a thrown pot, and buried itself in the ground.

The dead in my arms—Lona, dear weight of love and loss—seemed to absolve me from fear. I looked on unmoved, though never had I beheld such a crew accursed.

The leopardess shivered often now; the cold in that basin was not honest night-cold but something drawn from the absence of living breath. Then, to my left, the ground began to heave. A low wave of earth came slinking toward us; it rose, and out of it slouched a dreadful head with fleshy tubes for hair. It opened an oval mouth and snapped at me. The leopardess sprang—and fell baffled beyond it, finding nothing solid to seize, as if the thing were a lie given teeth.

Almost under our feet shot up the head of an enormous snake, eyes wallowing with a lamp-like glare. Again she rushed—again nothing. At a third horror she darted with the same fury and met the same emptiness; at last she ceased to heed the phantom-horde and paced on, sullen, wise, as if she had learned that some evils cannot be bitten.

I hastened the crossing, for the moon was carrying herself strangely. Even as she rose she seemed ready to sink back, and once I saw her drop her own breadth as if she had been struck. Her arc was low; now she began to descend with alarming speed, as if the sky itself were tired of holding light.

We were almost over when between us and the border of the basin rose a long neck, topped by what seemed the head of a corpse, its mouth half open and crowded with canine teeth like a butcher’s rack. I went on; it retreated, then drew aside.

The Lady stepped onto firm land. But the leopardess, between us, roused once more, turned, and flew at the throat of that terror.

I remained where I was until the elephants, with Lilith and the children, were safe upon the bank. Then I turned to look after the leopardess.

That moment the moon went down.

For an instant I saw leopardess and snake-thing convolved in a cloud of dust; then darkness swallowed them. My horse trembled, wheeled, and in three bounds overtook the elephants.

As we came up with them, a shapeless jelly dropped upon Lilith—cold and wet as fat scraped from a pan. Immediately a white dove fell upon it, stabbing with its beak. It made a squelching, sucking sound, and fell off, as if the foulness had weight even for a bird of peace.

Then I heard the voice of a woman speaking with Mara in the dark, and I knew the voice.

“I fear she is dead!” said Mara.

“I will send and find her,” answered the Mother. “But why, Mara, shouldst thou at all fear for her or for any one? Death cannot hurt her who dies doing the work given her to do.”

“I shall miss her sorely; she is good and wise. Yet I would not have her live beyond her hour!”

“She has gone down with the wicked; she will rise with the righteous. We shall see her again ere very long.”

“Mother,” I said—though I could not see her—“we come to you many, but most of us are Little Ones. Will you be able to receive us all?”

“You are welcome every one,” she answered. “Sooner or later all will be little ones, for all must sleep in my house! It is well with those that go to sleep young and willing! My husband is even now preparing her couch for Lilith. She is neither young nor quite willing, but it is well indeed that she is come.”

After that I heard no more. Mother and daughter moved away through the dark, their steps quiet as careful hands in a sleeping room.

But we saw a light in the distance, and toward it we went stumbling over the moor. Adam stood in the door holding a candle to guide us, talking with his wife; behind him she set bread and wine upon the table as if receiving travellers were as common as setting supper. The bread’s smell met us even outside—warm grain, crust browned; and the wine held a sweetness like fruit kept through winter, a memory preserved against forgetting.

“Happy children,” I heard Eve say, “to have looked already on the face of my daughter! Surely it is the loveliest in the great world!”

When we reached the door, Adam welcomed us almost merrily. He set the candle on the threshold and went to the elephants. He would have taken the princess to carry her in; but Lilith repulsed him. With a sudden pride she thrust the elephants apart and stood erect between them. They stepped away obediently, leaving her alone with him who had been her husband—gaunt now, ashamed of her uncomeliness, yet unsubmissive.

He stood with welcome in his eyes, shining through severity. “We have long waited for thee, Lilith!” he said.

She made no answer.

Eve and her daughter came to the door. “The mortal foe of my children!” murmured Eve, radiant in her beauty.

“Your children are no longer in her danger,” said Mara; “she has turned from evil.”

“Trust her not hastily, Mara,” answered her mother; “she has deceived a multitude!”

“But you will open to her the mirror of the Law of Liberty, mother, that she may go into it, and abide in it! She consents to open her hand and restore: will not the great Father restore her to inheritance with His other children?”

“I do not know Him!” murmured Lilith, and her voice shook as if the words were not in her native tongue, as if she spoke a name that would not fit her mouth.

“Therefore it is that thou art miserable,” said Adam.

“I will go back whence I came!” she cried, wringing her hands, and turned to depart.

“That is indeed what I would have thee do, where I would have thee go—to Him from whom thou camest! In thy agony didst thou not cry out for Him?”

“I cried out for Death—to escape Him and thee!”

“Death is even now on his way to lead thee to Him. Thou knowest neither Death nor the Life that dwells in Death! Both befriend thee. I am dead, and would see thee dead, for I live and love thee. Thou art weary and heavy-laden: art thou not ashamed? Is not the being thou hast corrupted become to thee at length an evil thing? Wouldst thou yet live on in disgrace eternal? Cease thou canst not: wilt thou not be restored and BE?”

Lilith stood silent, head bowed, as if the words pressed on her shoulders.

“Father,” said Mara, “take her in thine arms, and carry her to her couch. There she will open her hand, and die into life.”

“I will walk,” said the princess.

Adam turned and led the way. Lilith followed him into the cottage with a slow, stiff step, like someone moving through a room where every object remembers her crimes.

Then Eve came out to me where I sat with Lona in my bosom. She reached up her arms, took her from me, and carried her in with a tenderness that made the dead seem almost fed, almost warmed. I dismounted; the children also.

Outside, the horse and the elephants stood shivering. Mara patted and stroked every one. At her touch they lay down and fell asleep, as if she had found the hidden place behind their ears where peace lives.

She led us into the cottage. The Little Ones gathered at the table as naturally as chicks to scattered grain, and she gave them the bread and wine Eve had set. They took the bread in both hands, broke it with a crackle of crust, and chewed with the solemn delight of those who truly taste. The wine they sipped as if it were a story older than their words—sweet, dark, and warm in the throat, setting a quiet humming in their small bodies that steadied them after the basin’s harsh, unnatural noise.

Adam and Lilith stood together, both silent.

Eve came from the chamber of death where she had laid Lona down, and offered bread and wine to the princess.

“Thy beauty slays me! It is death I would have, not food!” Lilith said, and turned away.

“This food will help thee to die,” answered Eve.

But Lilith would not taste.

“If thou wilt nor eat nor drink, Lilith,” said Adam, “come and see the place where thou shalt lie in peace.”

He led the way through the door of death, and she followed submissive. But when her foot crossed the threshold she drew it back and pressed her hand to her bosom, struck through by that cold which is not of weather. A wild blast fell roaring on the roof and died away in a moan, as if some great creature had thrown itself against the house and slid away, thwarted.

Lilith stood ghastly with terror.

“It is he!” said her voiceless lips; I read their motion.

“Who, princess!” I whispered.

“The great Shadow,” she murmured.

“Here he cannot enter,” said Adam. “Here he can hurt no one. Over him also is power given me.”

“Are the children in the house?” asked Lilith, and at the word Eve’s heart began to move toward her, as if even such fear could be a doorway.

“He never dared touch a child,” said Eve. “Nor have you either ever hurt a child. Your own daughter you have but sent into the loveliest sleep, for she was already a long time dead when you slew her. And now Death shall be the atonemaker; you shall sleep together.”

“Wife,” said Adam, “let us first put the children to bed, that she may see them safe!”

He came back to fetch them.

As soon as he was gone, Lilith sank to her knees before Eve, clasped her knees, and spoke like one pleading in a language she barely commands. “Beautiful Eve, persuade your husband to kill me: to you he will listen! Indeed I would but cannot open my hand.”

“You cannot die without opening it. To kill you would not serve you,” answered Eve.

“But indeed he cannot! no one can kill you but the Shadow; and whom he kills never knows she is dead, but lives to do his will, and thinks she is doing her own.”

“Show me then to my grave; I am so weary I can live no longer. I must go to the Shadow—yet I would not!”

She did not understand; she could not. She struggled to rise and fell at Eve’s feet.

The Mother lifted her and carried her inward as one carries a child too exhausted to walk. I followed Adam and Mara and the Little Ones into the chamber of death.

We passed Eve with Lilith in her arms and went farther in.

“You shall not go to the Shadow,” I heard Eve say as we went by. “Even now is his head under my heel!”

Adam’s dim light glimmered on sleeping faces, and as he moved, the darkness closed over them again. The air itself seemed dead—perhaps because none of the sleepers breathed it in the way of the waking. Profound sleep filled the wide place. It was as if not one had stirred since last I was there: the forms I had then marked lay still. My father was as I had left him, only nearer to perfect peace; the woman beside him looked younger, as if the cold were not decay but a keeping, like fruit stored in a root-cellar against a future spring.

The darkness, the cold, the silence, the still air, the faces of the lovely dead made the children’s hearts beat softly, yet their tongues would talk—low, hushed, as if sound here mattered, as if a loud word would be a kind of violence.

“What a curious place to sleep in!” whispered one. “I would rather be in my nest!”

“It is SO cold!” said another.

“Yes, it is cold,” answered Adam; “but you will not be cold in your sleep.”

“Where are our nests?” asked more than one, looking about and seeing no couch unoccupied.

“Find places, and sleep where you choose,” replied Adam.

Instantly they scattered, fearless beyond the candle’s reach; yet we could still hear their gentle voices, and it was plain they saw where I could not.

“Oh,” cried one, “here is such a beautiful lady!—may I sleep beside her? I will creep in quietly, and not wake her.”

“Yes, you may,” answered Eve’s voice behind us.

We came to the couch while the little fellow was still creeping under the sheet, slow and careful as if sliding into a warm oven without disturbing the loaves. He laid his head beside the lady’s, looked up at us once, and was still. His eyelids fell; he slept.

We went a little farther. Another child had climbed onto the couch of a woman. “Mother! mother!” he cried, kneeling over her, face close to hers. Then, looking up at us: “She’s so cold she can’t speak—but I will soon make her warm!” He lay down, pressed close, and put his little arm over her. At once he too was asleep, smiling an absolute content, as if the warmth he promised had been given him.

We came to a third Little One: Luva. She stood on tiptoe, leaning over the edge of a couch.

“My own mother wouldn’t have me,” she said softly, as if ashamed of the words. “Will you?”

Receiving no reply, she looked up at Eve.

The great mother lifted her to the couch; Luva slipped under the snowy covering without more ceremony, like a tired kitten finding a place beside a hearth.

By this time each of the Little Ones—save three boys—had found at least an unobjecting bedfellow, and lay still and white beside a still, white woman. The little orphans had adopted mothers.

One tiny girl had chosen a father to sleep with—and that was mine. A boy lay beside the beautiful matron with the slow-healing hand.

On the middle one of the three couches hitherto unoccupied lay Lona. Eve set Lilith down beside it.

Adam pointed to the vacant couch on Lona’s right and said, “There, Lilith, is the bed I have prepared for you!”

Lilith glanced at her daughter—lying like a statue carved in semi-transparent alabaster—and shuddered from head to foot.

“How cold it is!” she murmured.

“You will soon begin to find comfort in the cold,” answered Adam.

“Promises to the dying are easy!” she said.

“But I know it: I too have slept. I am dead!”

“I believed you dead long ago; but I see you alive!”

“More alive than you know, or are able to understand. I was scarce alive when first you knew me. Now I have slept, and am awake; I am dead, and live indeed!”

“I fear that child,” she said, pointing to Lona. “She will rise and terrify me!”

“She is dreaming love to you.”

“But the Shadow!” Lilith moaned. “I fear the Shadow! He will be wroth with me!”

“He at sight of whom the horses of heaven start and rear, dares not disturb one dream in this quiet chamber!” said Adam. “When the Shadow comes here, it will be to lie down and sleep also. His hour will come, and he knows it will.”

“I shall dream then?”

“You will dream.”

“What dreams?”

“That I cannot tell, but none HE can enter into.”

“How long shall I sleep?”

“You and he will be the last to wake in the morning of the universe.”

Lilith lay down, drew the sheet over her, stretched herself straight, and lay still with open eyes.

Adam turned to Mara. She drew near.

“Lilith,” said Mara, “you will not sleep, if you lie there a thousand years, until you have opened your hand, and yielded that which is not yours to give or to withhold.”

“I cannot,” Lilith answered. “I would if I could, and gladly, for I am weary, and the shadows of death are gathering about me.”

“They will gather and gather, but they cannot infold you while yet your hand remains unopened. You may think you are dead, but it will be only a dream; you may think you have come awake, but it will still be only a dream. Open your hand, and you will sleep indeed—then wake indeed.”

“I am trying hard, but the fingers have grown together and into the palm.”

“I pray you put forth the strength of your will. For the love of life, draw together your forces and break its bonds!”

“I have struggled in vain; I can do no more. I am very weary, and sleep lies heavy upon my lids.”

“The moment you open your hand, you will sleep. Open it, and make an end.”

A tinge of colour rose in Lilith’s parchment face. The contorted hand trembled with agonised effort, as if the flesh remembered its own hardness and would not yield. Mara took it to help her.

“Hold, Mara!” cried Adam. “There is danger!”

Lilith turned her eyes upon Eve, beseeching. “There was a sword I once saw in your husband’s hands,” she whispered. “I fled when I saw it. I heard him who bore it say it would divide whatever was not one and indivisible!”

“I have the sword,” said Adam. “The angel gave it me when he left the gate.”

“Bring it,” pleaded Lilith, “and cut me off this hand that I may sleep.”

“I will,” he answered, and gave the candle to Eve. He went.

Lilith closed her eyes as if hiding from her own request.

In a few minutes Adam returned with an ancient weapon. The scabbard looked like vellum darkened by years; the hilt shone like gold that no handling could tarnish. He drew the blade. It flashed like a pale blue northern streamer—cold fire—and the light of it made Lilith open her eyes.

She saw the sword, shuddered, and held out her hand.

Adam took it. The sword gleamed once; there was a small gush of blood, startlingly red in that white place, and he laid the severed hand in Mara’s lap.

Lilith gave one moan and was already fast asleep.

Mara covered the arm with the sheet, and the three of them turned away.

“Will you not dress the wound?” I asked.

“A wound from that sword,” answered Adam, “needs no dressing. It is healing and not hurt.”

“Poor lady,” I said. “She will wake with but one hand!”

“Where the dead deformity clung,” replied Mara, and her voice was as certain as seedtime, “the true, lovely hand is already growing.”

We heard a childish voice behind us and turned again. Eve’s candle shone on Lilith’s sleeping face and the waking faces of three Little Ones grouped on the other side of her couch.

“How beautiful she is grown!” said one.

“Poor princess!” said another. “I will sleep with her. She will not bite any more!” He climbed into her bed, and sleep took him at once.

Eve drew the sheet over him with a tenderness that seemed to lay a quiet seal on the promise.

“I will go on her other side,” said the third. “She shall have two to kiss her when she wakes!”

“And I am left alone!” said the first, mournful as if loneliness were hunger.

“I will put you to bed,” said Eve. She gave the candle to her husband and led the child away.

We turned to go back toward the cottage. I was very sad, for no one had offered me a place in the house of the dead—no couch, no warm adoption into that hush where even fear is taught to speak softly.

Eve joined us and walked before with Adam. Mara walked beside me, carrying Lilith’s severed hand in the lap of her robe as one carries something that must not be dropped—not for horror, but for meaning.

As we stepped into the cottage we heard Eve’s voice: “Ah, you have found her! I sent them with the lantern,” she said to her husband, “to look for Mara’s leopardess: they have brought her.”

The door stood open. Two elephant-trunks came through it out of the night beyond, careful as servants bearing a dish too precious to jolt.

I followed Adam to the threshold. Between us we took the white creature from the elephants and carried her to the chamber we had just left, the women preceding us—Eve with the light, and Mara still with the hand.

There we laid the leopardess, beautiful and battered, across the feet of the princess: her fore-paws outstretched, her head couched between them, like a guardian laid down at the edge of a new sleep.
