Gerard walked in silence beside Teresa, his mind wandering in the contemplative fashion of an artist, speculating on what she intended to do with him, rather than daring to ask. Finally, she broke the quiet herself. "A friend has informed me," she explained, "of a goldsmith's wife seeking a writer. Her shop is nearby; you will not travel far."

Soon they arrived at the goldsmith's dwelling. "Madam," Teresa began politely, "Leonora tells me you require a writer. I have brought you a most handsome one—a man who saved my child from drowning at sea. Pray, look upon him with favor."

The goldsmith's wife, Madama Olympia, complied in spirit if not in action. She fixed her gaze upon Gerard's fine features and seemed unable to look away. Yet her verbal response was disappointing. "Nay, I have no need for a writer," she declared. "Ah! Now I recall, it was my gossip, Claelia the sausage-maker, who requested one. She told me, and I passed the message to Leonora."

Teresa offered a graceful excuse and withdrew. Claelia lived some distance away, and by the time they reached her home, she was absent. Teresa remained calm. "I shall wait for her return," she stated, sitting so still, so dignified, and so statuesque that Gerard began to sketch her furtively. Just then, Claelia returned.

"Madam, I hear from the excellent Olympia that you seek a writer," Claelia announced, taking Gerard's hand and leading him forward. "I have brought you a beautiful one; he rescued my child from the cruel waves. For the sake of our Lady, look kindly upon him."

"My good dame, my fair Sir," Claelia replied, "I have no use for a writer. But now that you remind me, it was my friend Appia Claudia, a tailor living in the Via Lepida, who asked for one recently."

Teresa retreated quietly. "Madam," Gerard interjected gently, "this seems likely to become a tedious pursuit for you."

Teresa opened her eyes wide. "What has ever been accomplished without a measure of patience?" she added softly. "We shall knock at every door in Rome, and you shall receive justice."

"But, Madam, I believe we are being shadowed," Gerard whispered. "I noticed a man following us, sometimes at a distance, sometimes close behind."

"I have seen him too," Teresa said coolly, though her cheeks flushed slightly. "It is my poor Lodovico." She paused, turned, and beckoned with her finger. A figure approached them, moving somewhat reluctantly. Upon reaching them, she stared him directly in the face; he looked sheepish.

"Lodovico mio," she said, "acquaint yourself with this young Sir, of whom I have often spoken. Know him and love him, for he is the one who saved your wife and child."

At these final words, Lodovico, who had previously bowed and grinned artificially, instantly transformed into an expression of heartfelt gratitude and embraced Gerard warmly. Yet, there was something unsettling in his initial demeanor—his secret following of his wife—that made Gerard uneasy under this affectionate greeting. Nevertheless, he asked, "Shall we have your company, Sir Lodovico?"

"No, Signor," Lodovico replied, "I do not go that way across the Tiber."

"Then addio," Teresa said meaningfully.

"When shall you return home, Teresa mia?" Lodovico asked.

"When I have completed my errand," she answered.

They continued their journey in silence. Teresa now wore a somber, almost gloomy expression. Briefly, Appia Claudia proved merciful; she did not send them further across the river, but merely directed them a hundred yards down the street to Lucretia, who kept a glove shop. Lucretia also required a writer, though Appia Claudia could not imagine why.

Lucretia was a cheerful little woman who welcomed them warmly. "I do not need a writer myself," she exclaimed, "for I keep all my accounts in my head. But it was for my confessor, Father Colonna. He is utterly mad for them!"

"I have heard of his Excellency," Teresa remarked. "Who has not?"

"But, good dame, he is a friar; he has taken a vow of poverty. I cannot allow this young man to write without payment. He saved my child at sea."

"Did he now?" Lucretia asked, casting an approving glance at Gerard. "Well, rest assured; a Colonna never lacks funds. The good father need only speak the word, and the princes of his lineage will pour a thousand crowns into his lap. Such a confessor, dame! The finest in Rome. His mind is leagues and leagues away constantly; he pays no heed to what you say. Indeed, I confess my sins to him no more readily than I would tell them to that wall. Once, to test him, I confessed alongside everyone else that I had murdered my lodger's little girl and baked her in a pie. Well, when my voice ceased confessing, he startled out of his trance, gathered a grave expression, and said, 'My erring sister, recite three Paternosters and three Ave Marias kneeling, abstain from butter and eggs next Wednesday, and pax vobiscum!' Then off he went with his hands behind him, looking as though I did not exist."

Teresa waited patiently, then calmly steered this digressive lady back to the point. "Would you be so kind as to accompany this worthy youth to the friar and plead his case?"

"Alas! How can I leave my shop? And what necessity is there? His door is always open to writers, painters, scholars, and all such creatures. Why, once he refused to receive the Duke of Urbino because a learned Greek scholar was closeted with him. The friar's head and the scholar's were pressed so closely together over a dusty parchment newly arrived from Greece that you could fit both cowls over the pair. That is what Onesta told me. She mostly comes here for a chat when running errands."

"This is the man for you, my friend," Teresa said. "All you need do," she continued, "is go to his lodgings (my boy will show you) and tell Onesta you come from me and are a writer. She will take you up to him. If you place a piece of silver in the wench's hand, it will do you no harm; it stands to reason."

"I have silver," Teresa said warmly.

"But wait," Lucretia interrupted. "Mind one thing. Whatever this young man claims he can do, he must be capable of doing it, or let him avoid the good friar like poison. He is a very wild beast against all bunglers. Why, just the other day, someone brought him an ill-carved crucifix. He said, 'Is this how you present the *Salvator Mundi*, who died for you in mortal agony? And you dare grudge Him careful work? This slovenly trinket, a crucifix? But since it is a crucifix of some sort, and I am a holy man, I would dust your jacket with your crucifix!' Onesta heard every word through the keyhole. So beware."

"Have no fears, Madam," Teresa said loftily. "I will answer for his ability; he saved my child."

Gerard was not subtle enough to grasp this conclusion. Far from sharing Teresa's confidence, he begged for a delay. "Pray, let me postpone this until tomorrow. Would it not be better?"

"Here is a coward for you," Lucretia chided.

"No, he is not a coward," Teresa snapped, flaring up; "he is modest."

"I am afraid of this high-born, fastidious friar," Gerard admitted. "Consider, Dame Teresa, he has seen the work of every writer in Italy. If you would only let me prepare a superior piece of work than I have thus far produced, then tomorrow I will face him."

"I consent," Teresa agreed.

They walked home together. Not far from Gerard's lodging was a shop selling vellum. A magnificent white skin hung in the window. Gerard gazed at it wistfully, knowing he could not afford it, so he hurried past. However, he quickly decided where to procure vellum. Parting ways with Teresa at his own door, he ran hastily upstairs, retrieved the bond he had carried all the way from Sevenbergen, and laid it on the table with a sigh. He then prepared his chemicals to erase the old writing; but since this was his last chance to read it, he overcame his deep aversion to illegible script and proceeded to decipher the deed despite its detestable abbreviations.

The document revealed that Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was to advance money to Floris Brandt regarding a piece of land, to be repaid from the rent. On this discovery, Gerard felt it would be imprudent and improper to destroy the deed. Instead, he vowed to decipher every word at his leisure. Descending the stairs, he resolved to buy a small sheet of vellum with his share of the card-money.
