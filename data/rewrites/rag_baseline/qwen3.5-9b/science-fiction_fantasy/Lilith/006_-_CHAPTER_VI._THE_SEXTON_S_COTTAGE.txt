"Here we are at last!" the raven announced, his voice cutting through the unnatural chill. "What a trek! In half the time I could have flown to Paradise and visited my cousin—the one who never returned to Noah! Dear heavens, it feels almost like winter!"

"Winter?" I gasped, struggling against the biting wind. "It seems but half a day since we departed home!"

"That is because you travel by clockwork," the raven replied, circling slightly. "In your world, you are shackled to gravity, tethered to a spinning sphere that dictates your pace. Here, time bends to our will." He gestured toward the distant structure. "This is my wife's dwelling. She is generous enough to let me reside here, calling it the Sexton's Cottage."

"But where is the churchyard? Your cemetery? Where do you inter the dead?" I demanded, scanning the barren expanse. All I saw was flat, desolate heath.

The raven extended his neck, holding his beak horizontal, then rotated it slowly through every point of the compass. He remained silent. I followed the direction of his beak with my eyes, and suddenly, the landscape transformed. Without a single stone marker, without a cross or crypt, the entire heath was a graveyard. Wherever the dreary wind swept, it marked a burial site. He was the sexton of all he surveyed, the lord of everything cast aside. I stood within the burial ground of the universe itself; its boundaries defined by the unenclosed moor, its walls formed by a low, starless gray horizon. I had abandoned spring, summer, autumn, and sunlight, only to arrive at a winter that awaited me in ambush.

I had set out in the prime of my youth, only to find myself already aged—but I was mistaken. The day here was vast, containing the entirety of the seasons. Winter slept there, wrapped in a winding sheet of ice throughout the night; with a childlike smile, Spring awakened at dawn; at noon, Summer blazed forth in her terrifying brilliance; and as the afternoon waned, old Autumn crept in, dying at the first breath of the vaporous, ghostly night.

As we neared the cottage, the clouded sun plummeted down the steepest slope of the western sky, sinking completely before we reached the door. The instant I crossed the threshold, I was assaulted by a cold so dense it felt like a physical weight, a material presence pressing against my lungs. I stumbled across the floor, fighting off the sensation of icy death. Outside, a wind swelled on the moor, battering the door as I finally managed to latch it shut.

Silence descended instantly. Inside, a candle flickered on a rough wooden table. The first object to catch my eye was what appeared to be a coffin lid propped upright against the wall. Yet, it yielded to touch; it was a door, and a woman stepped through.

She was clad entirely in white—purer than fresh snow. Her face matched her dress, yet unlike the cold sterility of frost, hers radiated a profound, living warmth. Her features were flawless, but her eyes stole my attention. The vitality of her countenance and her entire being converged in those orbs, transforming them into sources of light. It might have been the approach of death that rendered her face luminous, but her eyes possessed a fierce, expansive life—a darkness deepening endlessly as I gazed into them. A whole night's sky seemed condensed within each pupil; countless stars flashed in their black depths, framed by an iris of eternal twilight. No mortal eye could comprehend such intensity; these must have emerged directly from the divine source. Her still face suggested a primeval perfection, but her eyes represented a continuous creation.

"He is Mr. Vane, wife!" the raven called out.

"He is welcome," she answered, her voice low, rich, and gentle, resonating with treasures of immortal sound.

I stared, speechless.

"I knew you would be glad to see him!" the raven added.

She stood before the door she had entered, refusing to advance further.

"Will he sleep?" she asked.

"I fear not," the raven replied. "He is neither weary nor burdened."

"Why, then, have you brought him?"

"My fears suggest it may be premature."

"I do not quite understand," I said, an uneasy foreboding tightening my chest, though a vague hope for escape lingered. "Surely a man must perform a day's labor first!"

I fixed my gaze on the woman's white face, and my heart hammered against my ribs. She met my stare in silence.

"Let me depart and return once I have found, forged, invented, or at least discovered something!" I insisted.

"He has not yet learned that the day commences with sleep!" the woman retorted, turning to her husband. "Command him to rest before he attempts any action!"

"Men," the raven corrected, "are so obsessed with the notion of accomplishment that they collapse into their own shells. They cannot crack an egg without immediately lying down within it!"

His words tore my gaze away from the woman and locked onto the speaker. For a fleeting moment, I saw no bird, but rather the Librarian—the same slender, elderly man in a tattered black coat, broad-bodied and long-tailed. I had previously viewed only his back; now, for the first time, I beheld his face. It was gaunt, the bone structure visible beneath the skin, hinting at the skulls his former profession had exposed him to. Yet, in truth, I had never witnessed a face so vivid, a gaze so keen and friendly as the one reflected in his pale blue eyes, which held a haunting haze, as if saturated with tears.

"You knew I was not a raven!" he said, smiling.

"I knew you were Mr. Raven," I replied. "But somehow, I assumed you were avian as well!"

"What prompted that assumption?"

"You looked like a raven, and I saw you digging worms from the earth with your beak."

"And then?"

"Tossing them into the air."

"And then?"

"They metamorphosed into butterflies and fled."

"Have you ever observed a raven performing such a feat? I told you I am a sexton!"

"Does a sexton toss worms into the air, transforming them into butterflies?"

"Yes."

"I have never seen one do that!"

"You saw me do it!—Yet I remain the Librarian in your house, for I was never dismissed, nor did I relinquish the post. Now, I serve as both Librarian and Sexton here."

"But you just claimed to be the Sexton!"

"So I am. It is essentially the same vocation. With you, books are mere corpses, and a library is nothing but a catacomb!"

"You confuse me!"

"That is perfectly acceptable!"

A brief silence ensued. The woman remained motionless beside the coffin-door, a statue of white porcelain.

"On occasion," the Sexton said eventually, "it proves more expedient to project one's avian persona. Everyone, as you should know, possesses a bestial self, a bird-self, a foolish fish-self, yes, and a creeping serpent-self—all requiring significant effort to suppress! Indeed, he also harbors a tree-self and a crystal-self, along with countless others, all needing harmonization. One can discern a man's character by observing which creature most frequently emerges to the forefront."

He turned to his wife, and I scrutinized him anew. He towered above average height, standing with an erect posture unseen before. Like his wife, his face was pallid; his nose elegantly housed the beak that had receded within it. His lips were exceedingly thin, devoid of color, yet their curves were exquisite, trembling with a shadowy smile that blended humor, love, and pity.

"We require sustenance and refreshment, wife," he stated. "We have traveled a considerable distance."

"You know, husband," she responded, "we can offer only to those who ask."

She turned her immutable face and radiant eyes toward me. "Please provide me with something to eat, Mrs. Raven," I pleaded, "and something—to name whatever you choose—to quench my thirst."

"Your thirst must intensify before you can receive what satisfies it," she replied. "However, whatever I can provide, I shall willingly give."

She moved to a cupboard embedded in the wall, retrieved bread and wine, and placed them upon the table. We seated ourselves for a perfect repast. As I consumed the provisions, they penetrated deeper than mere hunger or thirst could account for. Anxiety and physical discomfort evaporated, replaced by pure anticipation. I grew drowsy, and for the first time, I felt genuine weariness.

"I have earned neither food nor sleep, Mrs. Raven," I admitted. "Yet you have granted the former freely, and now I pray you grant the latter, for I desperately need it."

"Sleep is too precious a commodity to be earned," the Sexton declared. "It must be bestowed and accepted, for it is a necessity. However, utilizing this house as a halfway inn—for a night's rest, that is, merely—would be perilous."

A wild-looking black kitten leaped onto his lap as he spoke. He stroked it with the tenderness one reserves for a child coaxing it to sleep; to me, it resembled patting down the soil upon a grave, a loving gesture accompanied by an internal lullaby.
