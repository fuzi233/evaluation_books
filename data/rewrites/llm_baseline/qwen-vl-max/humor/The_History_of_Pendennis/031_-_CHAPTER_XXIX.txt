Our dear reader, if you’ve been enjoying the tranquil woods, seaside breezes, and cozy gossip of Clavering, or the quiet misery of little Fairoaks, now it’s time to pack your bags and hop aboard the *Alacrity* coach with Arthur Pendennis—because London is calling, and he’s off to conquer it. No more country lanes, no more idle dreams: this is the big city, where fortunes are made, reputations are lost, and young men either become men or disappear into the fog like a poorly planned alibi.

As the coach rattles through the night, leaving behind the friendly gates of home (and the lingering scent of his mother’s lavender), Arthur is already busy planning his future. He’ll be wise. He’ll be prudent. He may even achieve fame—though he hasn’t quite decided whether he wants that yet. His first failure has taught him humility, but not defeat. It hasn’t broken his spirit, nor crushed his belief in himself—only made him slightly more aware that he might not be as brilliant as he once imagined. Still, he’s got ideas—hundreds of them—racing through his head like a herd of runaway squirrels. How much older he feels than when he last took this road, twelve months ago, on his way to Oxbridge! Then he was a boy. Now? He’s a man… or at least, he’s pretending to be one.

His thoughts drift tenderly toward his mother—blessed, trusting, loving her son despite all his past escapades. “Blessings be upon her!” he whispers to the stars. “Give me strength to work, to endure, to be honest, and avoid temptation.” He imagines she’s awake too, praying for him from the safety of Fairoaks, sending up purer petitions than any he could ever muster. Her love is his talisman, his anchor, his excuse for not completely falling apart. And then there’s Laura—whose affection he’d dearly loved to carry with him, but whom he knows he doesn’t deserve. He confesses it with shame: she’s better, nobler, cleaner than he’ll ever be. He feels like a wild thing drawn near a church—not out of hatred, but because he knows he doesn’t belong inside. He draws back from her purity like a drunkard from a hymn.

With such noble reflections, Pen finally dozes off just before dawn, only to wake refreshed at the old breakfast stop at B——, where he’s eaten many a merry meal since he was knee-high to a horse. As they leave, the sun bursts through, the coach picks up speed, the horn blares, and Pen, feeling gloriously alive, chats with the guard and fellow passengers. The world grows busier, livelier—until finally, the greys gallop out at H——, and the coach rolls into London.

Ah, London! What young man hasn’t felt a shiver run down his spine at the sight of it? Carriages clatter, crowds swarm, and the air hums with possibility. “Here it is,” thinks Pen. “My battlefield. My chance. I’ve been a dawdling boy—now I must prove I can be a man.” From his perch on the roof, he surveys the city like a soldier about to charge into battle—except instead of swords, he’s armed with ambition and a hundred pounds.

On the journey, he strikes up a conversation with a fellow traveler in a shabby cloak—Mr. Doolan, reporter for *The Star*, who claims to know every literary giant in England personally. Tom Campbell? Hood? Sydney Smith? He’s dined with them all. As they pass Brompton, Doolan points out Mr. Hurtle, the famed reviewer, strolling with an umbrella. Pen cranes over the coach, hoping for a glimpse. “A Boniface man,” says Pen. “Faith, he is,” replies Doolan, “and I’ve known him well.”

Pen feels honored to have seen the great Hurtle. He still believes in authors, editors, and literary greatness. Even Wagg, whose books seem rather pedestrian, is respected by Pen—especially when Doolan reveals he earns three hundred pounds per novel. Pen does some quick math: five thousand a year? Why, that’s possible! With the right book—and maybe a few more dinners with influential people—he might just make it.

At the Gloster Coffee-house, Pen meets his first London acquaintance: Harry Foker, prancing down Arlington Street in a cab pulled by a horse so large it looks like it might eat the carriage. Foker wears white kid gloves, white reins, and a respectable chin tuft. A tiny cab-boy, half his size, swings behind like a dangling ornament. Foker spots the dusty *Alacrity*, waves his whip, and shouts, “Hullo, Pen!”—a greeting so enthusiastic it makes Pen feel instantly at home.

Doolan, impressed by Pen’s connection to such a grand cab, decides he must dine with him. Pen, thrilled to be free and in London, invites him to the Covent Garden Coffee-house, where he’s booked a room. He calls a cab, rattles away, and arrives buzzing with energy. He greets the waiter, bows to the landlord, asks for the landlady, and mourns the absence of the old Boots. He’s got a hundred pounds in his pocket—enough to live like a king… for a week.

He dresses in his finest clothes, dines modestly on a pint of sherry (determined to be economical), and heads to the theatre next door.

The lights, music, crowd, and general gaiety enchant Pen. He laughs at jokes, applauds songs, and delights the weary regulars in the boxes, who’ve long since stopped caring about anything. After the first act, he struts through the lobby like a man who belongs in high society—though really, he’s just excited to be anywhere other than Fairoaks.

And there’s Foker again, this time with Grandy Tiptoff (of the Household Brigade) and Lord Colchicum, Captain Tiptoff’s uncle—a venerable peer who’s been chasing pleasure since the French Revolution began. They rush over to Pen, insisting he join their private box, where Miss Blenkinsop, the celebrated actress, sits with shoulders like moonlight and ringlets like rope. Behind her, snoozing in a wig, is her father—old Blenkinsop, the veteran actor known for playing “heavy fathers” both on stage and in life.

It’s eleven o’clock. At Fairoaks, Mrs. Pendennis lies awake, wondering if her son is safe. Laura, too, is awake—though what she’s thinking, we don’t know. Meanwhile, the farce continues, and Mrs. Leary, in a hussar jacket and braided pantaloons, enchants the audience with her charm and ballads.

Pen would love to watch her—but the box is full of chatter. Tiptoff knows where her maillots came from. Colchicum saw her debut in ’14. Miss Blenkinsop declares Mrs. Leary sings “out of all tune,” which shocks Pen, who thinks she sings like a nightingale and looks like an angel.

Then Hoppus appears as Sir Harcourt Featherby, and the men in the box complain he’s getting stale. Tiptoff suggests throwing Miss Blenkinsop’s bouquet to him.

“Not for the world!” she cries. “Lord Colchicum gave it to me.”

Pen remembers the name—and with a bow and a blush, he tells the peer he owes him thanks for proposing him at the Megatherium Club, at his uncle’s request.

“What, you’re Wigsby’s nephew?” says the peer. “We always call him Wigsby.” Pen blushes again. “We balloted you in last Wednesday. Your uncle wasn’t here.”

Delightful news! Pen thanks Lord Colchicum profusely, while the peer peers through his opera-glass like a hawk inspecting prey.

“Don’t be always looking at that box, you naughty creature,” says Miss Blenkinsop.

“She’s a dev’lish fine woman, that Mirabel,” says Tiptoff. “Though Mirabel was a d——d fool to marry her.”

“A stupid old spooney,” says the peer.

“Mirabel!” cries Pendennis.

“Ha! ha!” laughs Foker. “We’ve heard of her before, haven’t we, Pen?”

It’s his first love—Miss Fotheringay, now Lady Mirabel, wife of Sir Charles Mirabel, G.C.B., former envoy to Pumpernickel. She once captured his heart. Now, seeing her again—through the lens of experience—Pen wonders how he ever thought she was worth loving.

So, on his very first night in London, Arthur Pendennis is introduced to a club, an actress, a heavy father of the stage, and a glittering circle of jovial blades—old and young alike. Lord Colchicum, though ancient and bald, still drinks claret like a champion and hosts dinners at Richmond and Greenwich. He loves the theatre, welcomes actors from every nation, and entertains young men of fashion at his Thames-side villa—especially the ladies of the greenroom, whom he finds more polished than their male counterparts.

The next day, Pen pays his entrance fee at the Megatherium Club—exactly one-third of his hundred pounds—and enjoys lunch with immense satisfaction. He sinks into an easy chair, tries to read magazines, and wonders if the members are staring at him. He writes a letter on the club’s paper, declaring how comforting it will be after a hard day’s work.

Then he visits his uncle’s lodgings in Bury Street, trembling slightly, as requested by his mother. But the Major isn’t home. The apartment is empty—brown holland covers the table, bills and letters wait on the mantelpiece. The Major is abroad, in Badnbadn, with the Marcus of Steyne. Pen leaves his card—still bearing the address “Fairoaks.”

When the Major returns in November, he finds another card—this one reading “Lamb Court, Temple”—and a note from Arthur and his mother saying he’s come to town, joined the Upper Temple, and is studying hard for the bar.

Lamb Court? The Major recalls hearing of it—some fashionable ladies dined with Mr. Ayliffe, a barrister “in society.” Mr. Deuceace, Lord Crabs’s son, had lived there too. He sends Morgan to investigate.

“What sort of place is it, Morgan?” the Major asks, peering out of bed-curtains in the thick yellow fog.

“I should say rayther a shy place, sir,” Morgan replies. “The lawyers live there, names on the doors. Mr. Harthur lives three pair high. Mr. Warrington too.”

“Suffolk Warringtons? Good family,” thinks the Major. “Comfortable rooms, eh?”

“Honly saw the outside, sir. Nasty black staircase. Wonder how a gentleman can live in such a place.”

“Who taught you where gentlemen should live, Morgan?” the Major retorts. “Arthur’s studying law. That’s good enough for me.”

“Boys will be boys,” the Major muses later. “He wrote a decent letter. Colchicum says he’s a gentlemanlike lad. His mother’s a saint. If he sows his wild oats and sticks to his work, he might do well. Think of Charley Mirabel marrying that Fotheringay! Foolish old man. He doesn’t want to come here until I give permission—that’s manly. I was angry with him before. Gad, I’ll go see him.”

And so, the Major takes a city omnibus to the Upper Temple.

Arriving at the dingy gate, he’s led through dark alleys and melancholy archways until he reaches Lamb Court. If Pall Mall is dim, Lamb Court is pitch-black. Candles burn in rooms—pupils scribble declarations under tallow lamps, clerks chat patronizingly, and wigmaker Curling displays judges’ wigs like ghostly serpents. Two clerks toss halfpennies under a lamp. A laundress in pattens enters. A newspaper boy exits. A porter paces slowly. The whole place reeks of damp, despair, and legal ambition.

The Major shudders. “Good Ged! The poor boy mustn’t live on here.”

He climbs the abominable black stairs, groping along damp banisters, until he reaches the third floor. A candle burns in the passage. Doors open. Names: Mr. Warrington, Mr. A. Pendennis.

An Irish charwoman opens the door.

“Is that the beer?” booms a voice.

A man sits on a table, unshaven, smoking a pipe. Pen sits in a chair, legs near the fire, cigar in hand. A little boy grins at the Major, mistaking him for beer.

“Pen, my boy—it’s I,” says the Major, choking on smoke. “Your uncle.”

Warrington rises, Pen jumps up. “Beg your pardon,” says Warrington, loud and frank. “Will you take a cigar? Clear those things off the chair, Pidgeon.”

Pen throws his cigar into the grate. The Major shakes his hand warmly.

After greetings, Pen introduces Warrington—an old Boniface man, younger son of Sir Miles Warrington of Suffolk. The Major nods approval. He served with Warrington’s uncle in India and New South Wales.

“Took a sheep-farm there,” says Warrington. “Made a fortune—better than law or soldiering. Think I shall go there too.”

Beer arrives in a tankard with a glass bottom. Warrington laughs, takes a long draught, wipes his wrist across his beard. He’s dressed in a ragged shooting jacket, blue-bearded, drinking like a coalheaver—but unmistakably a gentleman.

He leaves, letting the Major and Pen talk.

“Rough and ready, your chum seems,” says the Major.

“Times are altered,” says Pen, blushing. “Warrington’s just called, no business, but he knows law. Until I can afford a pleader, I use his books.”

“Is that one of the books?” the Major asks, smiling.

A French novel lies at Pen’s feet.

“This is not a working day, sir,” says Pen. “We were out late last night—at Lady Whiston’s. Everybody in town was there except you—Counts, Ambassadors, Turks, Stars and Garters. My name’s in the paper too.”

He laughs. “I met an old flame—Lady Mirabel. She shook hands, was gracious. I may thank you for being out of that scrape. She presented me to the husband—an old beau in a star and blonde wig. He doesn’t seem very wise. She asked me to call. I may go now without fear of losing my heart.”

“What, new loves?” asks the Major, chuckling.

“Two or three,” says Pen. “But I don’t put on my grand serieux anymore. That goes off after the first flame.”

And so, Arthur Pendennis begins his life in London—not with grandeur, but with smoke, beer, and the quiet hope that someday, he might actually become someone worth remembering.
