The afternoon sun cast long, skeletal shadows across the floorboards of the Hale residence, a visual prelude to the shadow of death that now settled over the household. Dr. Donaldson arrived to conduct his first formal consultation with Mrs. Hale, a visit that immediately reinstated the barrier of professional secrecy Margaret had hoped to dismantle through their previous intimacy. While the physician entered the sickroom, Margaret was relegated to the periphery, confined to the adjacent chamber.

She paced the narrow confines of her mother's bedroom, the rhythm of her footsteps a frantic counterpoint to the silence. Every pause was seized by her ear, straining to catch the faintest tremor of pain—a moan that seemed to vibrate through the walls. Clenching her fists until her nails dug into her palms, she held her breath, convinced she had heard the sound of suffering. When the room finally fell silent, followed by the rustle of chairs and the murmur of departure, she rushed out, her heart hammering against her ribs.

In the hallway, she intercepted Dr. Donaldson, her demeanor shifting instantly from anxious daughter to authoritative mistress of the house. She bypassed the old servant, Dixon, with a dignified stride that silenced the man's usual officious interjections. This sudden adoption of regal authority provided her with a fleeting respite from her terror, a momentary oblivion granted by the ridiculous grandeur of her posture. Yet, as she descended the stairs, the weight of the task crashed back upon her, stealing the air from her lungs.

Confronting the doctor in the hall, she demanded the truth with an imperious tone. "What is the matter with mamma?" she commanded. "You will oblige me by telling the simple truth." When the physician hesitated, citing her mother's explicit wishes and the presence of a devoted servant, Margaret cut him down. "I am her daughter, sir," she declared, her voice steady despite the turmoil within. "I am not good or patient enough to submit to prohibition. Besides, you are too wise to have promised to keep a secret that must inevitably surface."

Dr. Donaldson, a man whose diagnostic acumen relied on piercing the soul rather than just the flesh, observed her reaction with clinical precision. He saw the dilation of her pupils, the flash of black horror in her eyes, and the livid pallor spreading across her cheeks. He understood that withholding the truth would inflict a torture more agonizing than the knowledge itself. With a low, grave voice, he delivered the diagnosis in two short sentences.

The effect was instantaneous. Margaret's color drained completely, her lips compressing into a line of steel. "I thank you most truly, sir, for your confidence," she whispered, her voice breaking. "That dread has haunted me for many weeks. It is a true, real agony. My poor, poor mother!" Tears welled, unchecked for a brief moment, before her formidable will reclaimed control. She pressed further, asking about suffering and prognosis. The doctor assured her that while the outcome depended on countless variables, modern medicine offered significant means of alleviation.

When Margaret trembled, asking about her father, the doctor advised caution. He suggested bearing the burden alone until the shock wore off, noting that Mr. Thornton's recent sacrifices proved his devotion, even if his judgment was flawed. He spoke of future visits, framing his role not merely as a healer but as a confidant worthy of years of social calling. As he departed, Margaret wrung his hand with such desperate force that he later marveled at the strength of her grip, remarking on the "immense power" of her well-set bones. He admired her resilience, noting how she had forced him to speak the truth with her head thrown back, only to bend eagerly forward to receive it. "Such a girl as that would win my heart," he mused, though age rendered the sentiment moot. "Another, who had gone that deadly colour, could never have come round without either fainting or hysterics. But she wouldn't do either—not she! And the very force of her will brought her round."

Back in the study, Margaret collapsed inwardly, praying for strength before ascending to her mother. "Oh, my God, my God! but this is terrible," she sobbed silently. "How shall I bear it? Such a deadly disease! no hope!" She lamented her absence during her childhood, wishing she had remained by her mother's side, and prayed that her mother's final moments would not be marked by unbearable agony. She resolved to shield her father from the immediate blow, fearing it would kill him, yet she refused to waste another second of her mother's remaining time.

Upon entering the sickroom, she found Mrs. Hale reclining in an easy chair, draped in a soft white shawl and wearing a neat cap. Despite the gravity of the situation, her mother appeared remarkably calm, her face flushed with a faint, peaceful color. Margaret approached, startled by the tranquility. Her mother, sensing the unasked questions, grew displeased, accusing Margaret of avoiding the doctor.

"I made him," Margaret confessed, kneeling beside her mother's chair and seizing her hand. "It was I—blame me." She kissed the hand fervently, her hot tears soaking the fabric. Mrs. Hale protested, claiming it was wrong for Margaret to know, but her resistance crumbled under the weight of her daughter's affection. Exhausted by the emotional contest, she allowed her hand to remain in Margaret's grasp.

"Let me be your nurse," Margaret pleaded, her voice thick with emotion. "I will learn anything Dixon can teach me... I do think I have a right to do everything for you." Mrs. Hale shuddered, warning her of the magnitude of the request, but Margaret insisted, promising a dedication surpassing anyone else's.

"You don't know what you are asking," Mrs. Hale warned, referencing Dixon's belief that Margaret would shrink from the reality of illness. Margaret scoffed at the notion, defending her capacity for love against the servant's patronizing assumptions about weak-willed women. "Don't let Dixon's fancies come any more between you and me, mamma," she implored.

Mrs. Hale softened, urging peace regarding Dixon, and Margaret agreed to humility and learning. They shared memories of their time at Helstone, contrasting the makeshift comforts of their youth with the polished luxury of Harley Street. Mrs. Hale tearfully declared she would never see Helstone again, viewing her impending death as a just punishment for leaving it. Suddenly, the mention of "Frederick" shattered her composure. A wild, passionate cry erupted from her—"Frederick! Frederick! Come to me. I am dying!"—as the thought of her husband overwhelmed her exhaustion, triggering violent hysteria.

Margaret summoned Dixon in terror. The servant entered, scolding Margaret for over-excitement, but the young woman bore the rebuke meekly, hoping her father's return might be delayed. Following Dixon's instructions with prompt obedience, she smoothed her mother's distress. Once Mrs. Hale slept, Dixon led Margaret to the drawing-room, forcing her to drink a cup of coffee while standing over her with a sour, commanding air.

"You shouldn't have been so curious," Dixon chided, predicting that Margaret's curiosity would only hasten her grief. "And now, I suppose, you'll tell master..."

"No, Dixon," Margaret replied sorrowfully, bursting into fresh tears to prove her fortitude. "I will not tell papa. He could not bear it as I can."

Dixon, moved by the display of spirit, revealed the depth of her own hidden love for Mrs. Hale, recounting stories of the Beresford family's fierce loyalty and justice. She praised Margaret's resemblance to her father, Frederick, noting how her temper mirrored his stormy looks. As Margaret prepared to leave, she lingered, then suddenly kissed the old servant, exiting the room with a light step. Dixon watched her go, blessing her sweetness and listing the three people she loved above all others: her mistress, Master Frederick, and the young lady. She reflected bitterly on Frederick's obsession with reading and thinking, believing it had cost him his wife, and watched Margaret disappear into the night, her shabby clothes a stark contrast to the finery she had worn a year prior.
