I pressed forward, deliberately refusing to glance backward. To look behind would be to invite despair; instead, I forced my heart and spirit to steel themselves. What lay ahead demanded every ounce of my manhood and courage—either to reach that Maiden waiting in the distant darkness of the world, or to face my inevitable death.

Thus, I marched steadily, acutely aware of the heavy emotions swelling within me: the pain of leaving my mighty Home, and the profound love and wisdom that had governed our society with such strict care. My heart warmed toward Master Monstruwacan for the honor he bestowed upon me; he had descended in armor to join the Full Watch, standing beside me to bolster my resolve at this critical hour of departure.

As I walked, the aether of the night swirled around me, charged with the chaotic thoughts and desperate wishes of the millions I had just abandoned. Soon, however, I steadied my emotions enough to notice the physical reality of my surroundings: the biting cold of the night air. It tasted different on my tongue, sharper and keener than the atmosphere of the Thousandth City where I had lived. It felt denser, heavier in my lungs, suggesting a composition vastly different from the regulated air of the plateau. Indeed, the air varied greatly depending on altitude; many peoples had migrated to levels that offered better health, guided strictly by scientific calculation. Not everyone suffered from poor air quality, for some were constitutionally resistant, but the differences were undeniable.

Deep within the fields below, the air was said to be wonderfully varied, capable of charming one soul while saddening another, depending entirely on where they wandered and the company they kept.

I continued forward, filled with a mix of new thoughts, ancient memories, and breaking wonders, though doubt and fear lingered in the corners of my mind. It was profoundly strange to finally step onto the "outward earth" of the Night Land—a realm my imagination had visited a thousand times, yet which I had never physically touched until this moment. This realization would likely seem quaint to readers of today.

Eventually, I approached the Circle surrounding the Redoubt. To my surprise, it was not the massive structure I had anticipated based on logical deduction. Instead, it was a small, clear tube barely two inches thick, yet it emitted a brilliant, intense light that appeared enormous when viewed from a distance.

This detail seems minor, yet it captures the essence of the new world I entered. It reminded me vividly of my childhood, when we would spend holidays watching through the embrasures of the great glass towers. We eagerly scanned the darkness, hoping to be the first to spot a Monster peering over that tiny ring of light toward the Mighty Pyramid. They came frequently, only to slink back into the shadows, clearly afraid of the illumination. We took pride in identifying the ugliest, most horrific creatures, treating it like a game, unaware that our childish joy stemmed from the safety of the light keeping those terrors at bay.

Those memories surged back in that moment, causing me to hesitate instinctively, fearing what might emerge from the dark beyond. But soon, my heart cleared, and I gained the courage to bid farewell. I turned to gaze upon the wondrous Home of the Last Millions. The sight was breathtaking: a mountain of metal rising measurelessly into the night, spreading across the earth to hold the entire world in its grasp.

On the nearest slopes stood three hundred thousand great embrasures. In total, the four sides of the Redoubt held twelve hundred thousand windows, as recorded in the schoolbooks and the covers of the *Atlas*. Half a mile upward began the lowest tier, followed by countless layers stretching higher and higher. A great light poured from them into the void. I looked up for miles, seeing distinct rows of glowing openings, each separated from its neighbor, until they faded into a distant, shimmering peak piercing the black heavens. This was the Everlasting Monument.

I realized that the tiny clusters huddled against the embrasures were the countless millions of the Redoubt. From this distance, they looked like insects trapped within a colossal bulk. I knew they watched me through their spyglasses. Raising my gaze again up the gray metallic slope, past the glimmering tiers, I fixed my eyes on the single star crowning the wonder—the Tower of Observation. I knew Master Monstruwacan was bending his great spyglass upon me, the instrument through which I had spent my life spying. I raised my Diskos in salute and farewell, though I saw nothing but the vast expanse between us.

My heart was full, yet my soul felt stronger. Then, I heard a murmur in the night, faint and distant. Seeing the tiny shapes in the lower embrasures moving constantly, I understood instantly that the multitudes were taking my salute, crying out and waving their farewells—or perhaps calling for me to return.

Alone before that mountain of metal and life, I knew I faced danger if I lingered. I raised my Diskos, reversing it to signal my departure to all the millions, then looked swiftly upward through eight miles of night toward the Final Light, ensuring my friend knew I thought of him in my final moments. Perhaps the invisible millions high above interpreted this gesture as a farewell to themselves too; a faint, wind-like sound drifted down from the monstrous heights.

Lowering my Diskos, I turned away. I struggled against the resistance of the Air Clog and stepped across the Circle into the loneliness of the Night Land. I refused to look back; witnessing my home weakened my resolve, so I vowed not to glance behind for a long time.

Yet, as I walked, the world's aether surged constantly, carrying the prayers, wishes, and perpetual watching of my people and kin. This gave me a sense of companionship, but it also terrified me. If this disturbance reached an Evil Force, they would know I was abroad. How could I stop it? Even if I explained the danger to the masses, their collective focus alone would create a psychic ripple impossible to suppress.

At first, I walked blindly, intent only on putting distance between myself and the ache in my heart. But soon, I slowed and applied reason. I decided to avoid the path trodden by the Youths, suspecting an over-watchfulness in that sector. Instead of heading directly north, I planned a circuitous route: northwest, circling behind the North-West Watcher, then approaching the Plain of Blue Fire from the rear, before finally striking due north. This detour would take me far from the House of Silence, which terrified me more than anything else in the land.

It was a longer journey, but better to move slowly toward success than rush toward destruction. My certainty in heading north came from an inner knowing and from studies of old books within the Pyramid. Recently, I had discovered a strange, ancient metal book hidden in the Great Library, forgotten for perhaps a million years. While much of its content resembled fairy tales dismissed by modern skeptics, it spoke with a grave authority that compelled belief.

The book recounted a cataclysmic event from a time long past: a massive earthquake that rent the world for a thousand miles, creating a bottomless chasm into which an ocean rushed. The earth shook, mists rolled in, and rains fell heavily. History records this, but the people of the Pyramid treated it as mere entertainment for children. However, this specific book quoted eyewitnesses, lending it credibility.

Its conclusion described a valley emerging from the west, turning southeast, then north—a thousand miles wide, with sides a hundred miles deep. At its western end, the setting sun cast a red gloom. Within lay great seas, strange beasts, and terrible poisons.

I reasoned that this was not fiction. Long ago, when the world was still warm, a great earthquake had split the globe along a weak curve. An ocean fell into the earth's furnace, flashed into steam, and tore the surface apart. Mist, confusion, and rain followed.

In the book's final section, written by someone living eons later when the sun was dying and the upper world had become cold and uninhabitable, the narrative shifted. The great chasm had settled into a deep, wondrous valley holding seas, hills, forests, and areas of fire and sulfur clouds. Here, in the warmth at the bottom, nature had reborn primal monsters.

Man, softened by centuries of ease, eventually found himself facing a freezing world. A hardier race emerged, drawn to the warmth of the valley. They climbed down the steep heights, building houses along the way, becoming known as "The Road Makers." Generations passed, climbing endlessly, unaware of the destination until they finally reached the bottom. There, amidst the monsters, they built cities and connected them with roads, navigating the shadowed turns where the sun couldn't reach.

However, the valley became infested with horrors. Driven back by the terror of the West, the Road Makers retreated to their cities in the red-lit western valley. For a hundred thousand years, they thrived, experimenting with harmful forces in ignorance, opening doors to disaster. When the sun finally died completely, plunging the world into utter twilight, madness seized the people. Cities were attacked by monsters from the west, leading to an age of sorrow and conflict.

From this crucible rose a determined leader who united the sound millions. He declared that the growing darkness required a refuge. The people acclaimed the idea, and a Great House was begun—but deemed insufficient. The leader led the wandering masses to the Bight, where the Great and Mighty Pyramid was finally constructed.
