CHAPTER VII. In Which the Major Makes His Grand Entrance (With a Side of Snoring, Rum, and Regret)

Major Arthur Pendennis arrived at Fairoaks like a storm cloud after a particularly grueling night—though, to be fair, it wasn’t the weather that had him looking like he’d been dragged through a hedge backwards by a bad-tempered donkey. No, it was the mail-coach. That infernal, rattling, jolting tin can of a vehicle that once upon a time passed for travel in England.

The journey had been a symphony of discomfort: a man with the build of a barrel and the social grace of a startled badger had squashed him into the corner like a piece of unwanted luggage; said gentleman then proceeded to snore like a foghorn on full blast, punctuated by occasional grunts that suggested he might be dreaming of wrestling bears or perhaps just digesting a very large meal. Opposite sat a widow lady whose idea of fresh air seemed to involve sealing all windows shut and then dousing the cabin in the fumes of Jamaica rum-and-water, which she sipped from a bottle concealed in her reticule like a secret agent delivering poison. Every sip sent waves of aromatic despair through the coach.

And when the Major managed to steal a moment’s sleep? Ah! Then came the horn at the turnpike gate—blaring like a trumpet announcing the arrival of doom—or the neighbour wedging himself even tighter, or the widow’s feet, which seemed to have a personal vendetta against his toes. Poor Major Pendennis emerged at Fairoaks looking less like a nobleman and more like a man who’d survived a siege and lost the battle for dignity.

But such were the joys of the jolly old days! Eight miles an hour for twenty hours, a hard seat, gouty limbs, grumbling coachmen, and fellow-passengers who believed spirits-and-water were essential to good health—why, one could only marvel at how anyone endured it, let alone enjoyed it. And yet they did. They laughed. They ate pie. They complained. And somehow, they made it home.

Among the passengers were two schoolboys returning for midsummer holidays, their enthusiasm as bright as their cravats. The Major watched in mild astonishment as they devoured a feast at Bagshot Inn—ham, eggs, pickles, boiled beef, tea, coffee—you name it, they loaded it into their saddlebags like modern-day gluttons with no sense of moderation. Meanwhile, the Major sipped a cup of tea so weak it might have been brewed from yesterday’s dreams.

But the boys’ cheerfulness struck a chord. One of them, it turned out, was the son of a lord—someone the Major had met in London’s glittering circles. The boy slept through the entire ordeal, undisturbed by the chaos around him, and woke up as fresh as a daisy (and proudly declared as much), while the Major looked like he’d been dipped in mud and left out in the rain.

At last, the Major reached Fairoaks. He descended at the lodge gate, yellow-faced, wig askew, beard bristling like a porcupine, and every joint screaming in protest. The porteress and gardener’s wife greeted him with reverence. Mr. Morgan, his manservant, received even greater deference—because, as we shall see, Morgan was not merely a valet but a master of mystery.

Helen, ever watchful, saw the Major arrive from her window. But she didn’t rush to meet him. She knew better. The Major disliked surprises. He required preparation—like a king before a coronation, or a man before facing a room full of relatives who might ask awkward questions about his wardrobe choices.

Ah yes—the famous morocco box incident. When Pen was a boy, he had committed the unforgivable sin of stealing the Major’s false teeth from the dressing table. A crime so grave it nearly caused a family rift. The Major would never appear without his teeth, nor without his wig—Morgan guarded both like sacred relics, curling the wig in secret corners, introducing it to the Master’s room with the solemnity of a priest bringing forth holy water.

So the Major retreated to his chamber, groaning, moaning, wheezing, and cursing Morgan under his breath—much like an old bear who’s just been woken from hibernation by a sudden cold snap. After what felt like an eternity of grooming, he finally emerged—belted, curled, and properly dignified—descending into the drawing-room with the air of a man who had seen things, done things, and still expected everyone to bow.

But Pen was nowhere to be found. Only Helen and little Laura, sewing quietly at her knees. The Major offered her a single forefinger—a gesture of greeting so formal it might have been a diplomatic protocol. Laura took it trembling, dropped it, and fled from the room.

The Major didn’t care. In fact, he preferred it. Laura was not his favourite personage. He had private reasons for disliking her—one day, perhaps, we’ll reveal them. For now, let us say she disappeared into the house, searching for Pen.

She found him in the orchard, pacing back and forth in deep conversation with Mr. Smirke, the tutor. So absorbed was Pen that he didn’t hear Laura’s voice until Smirke tugged his coat and pointed toward her. She ran up, hand outstretched.

“Come in, Pen,” she said. “There’s somebody come—Uncle Arthur’s here.”

“He is, is he?” Pen replied, gripping her hand tightly. He turned to Smirke with an expression that said, *I’m ready. Bring it on.* Mr. Smirke sighed gently, raised his eyes to heaven, and muttered something about the perils of youth.

“Lead on, Laura,” Pen said, half-fierce, half-laughing. “Lead on, and say I wait upon my uncle.”

But beneath the bravado, he was terrified. These past two days, he’d confided everything to Mr. Smirke—his passion for Miss Emily Fotheringay, his belief in love, his determination to marry her despite all obstacles. And Mr. Smirke, himself hopelessly in love with the idea of love, had listened with rapt attention, nodding sagely, occasionally muttering, “Yes, yes, quite right,” though he’d never actually seen the woman in question.

Pen had spoken of Emily as if she were a goddess—pure, refined, brilliant, kind-hearted, and utterly deserving of a life beyond the stage. He described her wit, her grace, her genius, her devotion to her father—a nobleman fallen on hard times but still respected in high society. He insisted he wouldn’t marry until he was twenty-one, but also swore—face solemn as a funeral—that this was the one true passion of his life, and death itself would be preferable to failure.

Helen, naturally, had tried to reason with him. She’d reminded him of long engagements, of tragic outcomes, of her own cousin—Laura’s father—who had married above his station and died young, broken-hearted. But Pen dismissed her fears with a mix of fiery logic and poetic flair, convincing her almost completely that if God had ordained this match, then it was meant to be.

And so, during those two days, Pen had practically won over his mother—though not without effort. He’d written verses late into the night, composing odes to Emily so grand and ridiculous that even the most romantic soul might have blinked. One night, Helen passed by his door and heard him mumbling poetry in bed. She peeked in—only to find him biting a pencil, papers scattered everywhere, his face flushed with passion.

“Arthur,” she whispered.

He jumped, shoved papers under the pillow, and mumbled, “I couldn’t sleep. I—I was writing.”

Then, suddenly, he flung his arms around her neck. “O mother! I love her, I love her!”

How could any mother resist? She held him close, remembering when he was a child, when she used to pray over his bed. Now he was a man—madly, dangerously in love.

When the Major finally appeared, dressed and presentable, he found Helen already distressed—not just about the marriage, but about the possibility that Pen might suffer terribly if it were denied. She begged him to be gentle. “He has a high spirit,” she warned. “Dr. Portman spoke harshly to him the other night—and it frightened me. Please, be careful.”

The Major took her hand, kissed it gallantly, and smiled with the air of a man who knew exactly what was going on. “Bon Dieu!” he thought. “The boy has talked the woman round. She’d give him a wife like a toy if he cried for it. Where are the lettres-de-cachet when you need them?”

He pressed her hand, placed it gently on the table, and looked her straight in the eye. “Confess,” he said, “you’re wondering how you can possibly justify letting him have his way.”

She blushed. “I’m thinking he’s unhappy—and I am too.”

“To contradict him or to let him have his wish?” he asked.

“I’m thinking… that he’s formed such a foolish, cruel, fatal attachment,” she said, “one that will end in pain, whatever happens.”

“No,” said the Major firmly. “We are not going to have a Pendennis marry a strolling mountebank from a booth. No, no—we won’t marry into Greenwich Fair, ma’am.”

“If the match is broken suddenly,” Helen interposed, “I don’t know what may happen. Arthur’s temper is ardent. His affections are intense. I tremble at the thought.”

“My dear madam,” said the Major, with deep sympathy, “I have no doubt Arthur will suffer terribly. But is he the only one who has suffered such a disappointment?”

“No,” Helen whispered, her mind drifting back to her own youth.

“And I,” said the Major, lowering his voice, “have known such sorrow myself. A young woman—fifteen thousand pounds, niece to an Earl—most accomplished. Half her money would’ve promoted me to lieutenant-colonel by thirty. But it wasn’t to be. I was penniless. Her parents interfered. I went to India. I served Lord Buckley. Without her. We exchanged letters. We returned our hair. We suffered—but we recovered. She’s now a baronet’s wife with thirteen children. Altered, yes—but her daughters remind me of what she was. The third is to be presented next week.”

Helen said nothing. She was seventeen again.

“Look at my brother,” the Major continued. “He too had a disappointment. Miss Balls—daughter of a doctor in large practice. Nearly succeeded. But difficulties arose. And look—he ended up with *you*.”

He gently squeezed her fingers.

“Marriages between people of different rank,” Helen said softly, “are often sad. Laura’s father—my cousin—was brought up with me. He married above his station. It ended badly.”

“Most injudicious,” the Major cut in. “Imagine marrying a woman of low birth, and having your house filled with her vulgar relations! Imagine your wife clinging to a mother who drops her h’s or calls Maria Marire! How do you introduce her into society? I’ve seen men ruined—cut, lost—by their wives’ connections. Lady Snapperton refused to receive Lady Brouncker because she was a druggist’s daughter. Tom Wagg said she never needed medicine—she never had an ‘h’ in her life. Good Ged, what would a brief separation be compared to a lifetime of misalliance?”

“What, indeed,” Helen murmured, half-laughing, half-sighing.

“And this woman is ten years older than Arthur. What happens in such cases? Misery. Undeviating misery. Look at Lord Clodworthy—his wife looks like his mother. Lord Willowbank—twice he’s cut her down when she’s hanged herself out of jealousy. Mark my words—next time, he won’t cut her down. No, my dear madam, you’re not in the world. I am. You’re sentimental. You must leave this to my experience. Marry this woman? Marry an actress at eighteen? Bah bah! I’d sooner send him to the kitchen and marry the cook.”

“I know the evils of premature engagements,” Helen sighed—for the third time in the conversation.

And so, with these thoughts swirling, the chapter ends—just as we begin to wonder: who is little Laura? Why does the Major dislike her? And what secrets lie behind her quiet presence in the house?

Perhaps the answer lies in the next chapter.
