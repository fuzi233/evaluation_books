The journey to Rome had been decided in a single evening—an abrupt departure that swept aside the obstacles to Romola’s own flight. Tito had left for the supper at the Rucellai Gardens in high spirits, anticipating delicate meats, rare wines, and the company of Florence’s elite. The host, Bernardo Rucellai, was no ordinary nobleman. His very name carried the weight of history, derived from the lichen *oricello*, whose purple dye had once enriched his merchant ancestors. Now, generations later, Bernardo himself stood as a man of letters, politics, and influence—a patron of philosophy, a builder of palaces, and a diplomat whose presence lent gravity to any gathering.

Tito had been invited through Lorenzo Tornabuoni, and the urgency of the summons suggested matters of state. Florence was restless, and the Twenty—the council that now held the reins of power—were men whose favor Tito could not afford to ignore. The prospect of politics flavored with fine dining was far more appealing than the dreary philosophic suppers he had endured in these same gardens, where men lectured at length on the cosmos as if it were a tract to be memorized.

The night was dark, broken only by the occasional glow of a lamp before some street-corner Madonna. Beneath one such light, a figure paused—tall, cloaked, unmistakably Tito—before vanishing through the gate of the Rucellai Gardens. Behind him, unnoticed, another man halted: thick-set, older, clad in rough serge.

*"Your name?"* the porter demanded as the stranger moved to follow.

A hesitation. *"Are you his servant?"*

There was a quiet authority in the answer, and the porter yielded. The door closed behind Baldassarre, and he too disappeared among the torchlit hedges.

Months ago, this man would have faltered, his mind adrift in shadows. But tonight, he walked with purpose. The change had come suddenly—on the very evening Tito had last stood before him, when the dagger meant for treachery’s heart had shattered in his grip.

That night, Baldassarre had sat in the aftermath of rage, his body trembling, his thoughts unnervingly clear. The past had unspooled before him, vivid as a fresco newly restored. He remembered the name *Tessa*, the revelation of Tito’s double life. He had gathered the broken blade and the forgotten book beside him—Pausanias, lying open to the page headed *ΜΕΣΣΕΝΙΚΑ*.

Moonlight had struck the letters, and suddenly, they were no longer marks on parchment but gateways. Messenia rose in his mind—its revolt, its traitors, its justice. The words of the ancient historian had rushed back, and with them, memory itself.

That night, he had walked the hills under the cold stars, the city spread below him like a map he could read anew. Names, places, histories—all returned, sharp as the edge of a knife. And with them, one unwavering purpose: vengeance.

Since then, he had stalked Tito like a shadow. He learned his routines, his alliances, the delicate threads of his standing in Florence. He avoided those too close to him—Nello’s shop, Piero di Cosimo—lest warning reach Tito too soon. He wanted no hurried flight, no desperate ploy. He wanted his moment to be perfect: when Tito stood at his most secure, flanked by the powerful, certain of his own invincibility.

Twice, Baldassarre had felt his mind waver—a dizzying lapse, as if the light within him might flicker out. But tonight, there was no uncertainty. He had followed Tito here, into the heart of the Rucellai Gardens, where the great men of Florence gathered.

Tonight, the reckoning would come.
