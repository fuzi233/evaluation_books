Barton Park stood roughly half a mile from the cottage. Though the ladies had crossed its grounds earlier while traversing the valley, the house remained hidden from their view back home, concealed by the projecting ridge of a nearby hill. It was a structure of considerable size and grandeur, inhabited by the Middletons, who maintained a lifestyle marked by both generous hospitality and refined elegance. This dual display served distinct purposes: the former catered to Sir John's desires, while the latter satisfied his wife. Their household was rarely empty; they entertained guests constantly and hosted a wider variety of social gatherings than any other family in the district. Such activity was essential to their contentment. Despite their stark differences in temperament and demeanor, they shared a profound similarity: a complete lack of intellectual or artistic cultivation that restricted their pursuits outside of social obligations to a very narrow scope. Sir John was a devoted sportsman, while Lady Middleton was solely defined by her role as a mother. He spent his days hunting and shooting; she devoted hers to doting on her children. These were their only true passions. Lady Middleton held the advantage of being able to indulge her offspring year-round, whereas Sir John's independent amusements were available only seasonally. Nevertheless, a relentless schedule of engagements—both local and distant—compensated for their natural and educational shortcomings. These activities sustained Sir John's high spirits and provided ample opportunity for Lady Middleton to practice her impeccable manners.

Lady Middleton took immense pride in the refinement of her dining table and the perfection of her domestic arrangements; indeed, this vanity constituted her greatest joy during any gathering. Conversely, Sir John derived a far more genuine satisfaction from society itself. He delighted in filling his home with as many young people as possible, finding the louder the noise, the greater his pleasure. He was a boon to the youth of the neighborhood: in summer, he organized endless outdoor feasts of cold ham and chicken, and in winter, his private balls were frequent enough to satisfy any young lady not yet overwhelmed by the voracious appetites typical of age fifteen. The arrival of a new family in the countryside was always a cause for celebration with him. Consequently, he was utterly charmed by the inhabitants he had recently secured for his cottage at Barton. The Dashwood sisters were young, beautiful, and possessed a natural simplicity. To Sir John, this combination was sufficient to win his approval; for a pretty girl, lacking pretense was the ultimate asset in making her spirit as captivating as her appearance. His kindly disposition made him eager to accommodate those whose circumstances, compared to his own, might seem unfortunate. Thus, in showing kindness to his "cousins," he experienced the authentic fulfillment of a benevolent heart. Furthermore, by hosting a family consisting entirely of women in his cottage, he enjoyed the specific satisfaction of a sportsman: a man who values only those of his own sex who share his love for the chase is seldom inclined to encourage their tastes by inviting them to reside within his own estate.

Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters were greeted at the door by Sir John, who welcomed them to Barton Park with sincere warmth. As he escorted them to the drawing-room, he reiterated the regret he had expressed the previous day regarding his inability to arrange a gathering of lively young gentlemen. He explained that aside from himself, there would be only one other gentleman present—a close friend staying at the park who was neither notably young nor particularly vivacious. He hoped they would forgive the smallness of the party, assuring them that such an occurrence would never repeat itself. He had visited several households that morning hoping to expand their numbers, but the moonlit night meant everyone was already fully engaged. Fortunately, Lady Middleton's mother had arrived at Barton less than an hour prior. Described as a cheerful and agreeable woman, Sir John hoped the young ladies would not find the evening as dreary as they might anticipate. Both the sisters and their mother were perfectly content with having two complete strangers among their party and desired no additions.

Mrs. Jennings, Lady Middleton's mother, was a good-natured, jolly, plump, elderly woman who spoke incessantly, appeared perpetually happy, and leaned heavily toward vulgarity. Full of jokes and laughter, she managed to utter numerous witty remarks concerning lovers and husbands before dinner concluded. She joked that they must not have left their hearts behind them in Sussex, pretending to detect blushing cheeks regardless of whether they actually felt shy. Marianne found these comments irritating on her sister's behalf, turning her gaze toward Elinor to observe how she endured such banality, with an intensity that caused Elinor far more distress than the triviality of Mrs. Jennings's teasing warranted.

Colonel Brandon, Sir John's friend, seemed equally ill-suited to friendship as Lady Middleton was to matrimony, or Mrs. Jennings to maternal duty. He was silent and grave. Yet, despite being deemed by Marianne and Margaret an absolute old bachelor (though he was merely past fifty-five), his appearance was not displeasing. While his face lacked conventional handsomeness, his countenance was intelligent, and his bearing was distinctly gentlemanly. Nothing in the assembled group recommended them as ideal companions for the Dashwoods; however, the cold insipidity of Lady Middleton was so uniquely repellent that, in comparison, the gravity of Colonel Brandon—even the boisterous mirth of Sir John and his mother-in-law—appeared engaging.

Lady Middleton seemed capable of enjoyment only after dinner, once her four noisy children entered the room. They tugged at her dress, tore at her clothing, and silenced every form of conversation save that which concerned themselves. Later that evening, upon discovering Marianne's musical ability, she was invited to perform. The piano was unlocked, and everyone prepared to be enchanted. At their request, Marianne, who sang beautifully, performed the majority of the songs Lady Middleton had introduced into the family upon her marriage. Perhaps these melodies had lain untouched on the keyboard ever since, for her ladyship had abandoned music entirely upon marrying, despite accounts from her own mother claiming she played exceptionally well and her personal admission that she loved it. Marianne's performance received enthusiastic applause. Sir John shouted his admiration at the conclusion of every song and conversed loudly with others throughout the duration of each piece. Lady Middleton frequently chided him, marveling at how anyone could divert their attention from music for even a moment, and insisted Marianne sing a specific tune she had just heard completed.

Of the entire party, Colonel Brandon alone listened to her without falling into rapture. He offered her only the courtesy of attentive silence. On this occasion, Marianne felt a sense of respect for him that she believed the others had reasonably forfeited through their shameless lack of taste. His appreciation for music, though it did not reach the ecstatic heights required to truly sympathize with her own feelings, was commendable when contrasted against the horrifying insensitivity of the rest. She was sensible enough to acknowledge that a man of fifty-five might well have outgrown all acute sensitivity and every exquisite capacity for enjoyment. She was entirely willing to grant every allowance for the Colonel's advanced years that humanity demanded.
