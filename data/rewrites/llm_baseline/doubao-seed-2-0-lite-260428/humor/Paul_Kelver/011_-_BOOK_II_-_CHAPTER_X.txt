IN WHICH PAUL GETS SHIPWRECKED AND Tumbled Headfirst Into The Deep, Murky Waters Of Grown-Up Life

My father, displaying the kind of absurd punctuality that only the very dead can pull off, breathed his last on the exact morning of his own birthday. None of us had been expecting the final curtain to fall for another few weeks at least, so for the first quarter hour we hadn’t the foggiest the show was over.

“I left him dozing,” my mother whispered, slipping back into the parlour in her dressing gown, soft as a mouse. “Washburn gave him a sleeping draught last night. No use rousing him now.”

So we huddled round the breakfast table, talking in tones so quiet the toast nearly fell asleep, too. Our little Poplar house was about as soundproof as a paper bag – you could hear someone dropping a teacup three doors down, never mind through our own thin partitions. Later my mother crept upstairs, me tiptoeing two paces behind her, and eased the bedroom door open a crack.

The blinds were still drawn, the room dim as a coal cellar. My mother stood there for what felt like half an hour, ear pressed to the doorjamb, listening. The first costermonger of the day drifted past – a girl, by the sound of her – yelling at the top of her lungs: “Watercreases! Fine fresh watercreases, going with your breakfast, a penny a bunch, watercreases!” And further off, a hoarse teenage lad was wailing like a lost seagull: “Mee-ilk, mee-ilk-oi!”

Inch by inch my mother pulled the door wider, and we snuck inside. He was lying there eyes still closed, lips just parted. I’d never seen death before, and I couldn’t wrap my head around it at first. All I noticed was that he looked younger than I’d ever seen him look when he was alive, like he’d shed ten years overnight. Slowly, slowly it sank in: he was gone. For days, weeks afterwards, I’d spot his step behind me on the high street, hear his voice calling my name, catch a glimpse of his face in a crowd, and sprint over to greet him – only to stand there gawping, confused, as some random bearded grocer or pipe-smoking carpenter stared back at me like I was a lunatic. At first, funny enough, I felt no pain at all.

For my mother, it barely registered as a separation at all. Her quiet, unshakable faith had never once entertained fear or doubt. He was just waiting for her, that was all. In God’s good time they’d meet again, no tears needed. The days did drag on, of course – the house always feels a bit emptier when the man who tells all the bad jokes and fixes the wobbly chairs isn’t around – but that was the worst of it. She’d talk about him constantly, smiling at the memory of his daft little quirks and silly habits, not as if he was some fossil stuck in the past, but as if he was leaning over the garden fence just out of sight, waving her over.

We stayed on in that old house, forever muttering about moving out, even as that great greedy brick monster that was London crept closer every year, crunching up every nice thing between its ugly cement teeth. Fields, gardens, oak trees, cosy old cottages that looked like they had stories spilling out of their windows, soft hedgerows that hid the grime beyond – every last little spot that still smelled like sunshine got gobbled up one by one, leaving nothing but endless rows of smoky, sooty terraces, the East End’s little ghetto for the working poor that just kept spreading, dreariness piling on top of dreariness until it felt like the whole place was one big grey catacomb. On misty afternoons, when we knew the sun was shining somewhere outside that maze of slums, we’d trudge out house-hunting in the suburbs that had not yet been eaten alive – back when Highgate was a tiny little market town connected to civilisation only by a single rickety coach that trundled through leafy Holloway; back when Hampstead had rows of ancient red brick houses, and from the windy heath you could see London’s domes and spires peeking over distant farms and woods; back when Wood Green was surrounded by pastures, where labourers in smock coats argued about politics and ale under spreading elm trees; back when Hornsey was little more than an ivy-covered church and one single grass-lined lane. Every time we’d find the “absolute perfect place for us”, pick it apart from every angle, decide it was flawless… and then dither.

“We’ll sleep on it,” my mother would say. “No rush. For all kinds of silly little reasons, I’ll be sorry to leave Poplar.”

“What reasons, mum?”

“Oh, no particular reason, Paul. We’ve lived here so long, that’s all. It’ll feel like yanking a tooth, leaving this old house.”

We’re all of us just clingy vines, aren’t we? We twist our tendrils round whatever wall is closest, no matter if it’s a castle keep or a pigsty, and the rain and sun only make the grip tighter. You remember old Sir Walter Scott, dying in Italy, hurrying home as fast as he could for fear he’d draw his last breath without breathing the damp mist off his beloved Scottish hills? I knew an old woman once, who got carted out of her slum attic to the seaside to get fresh sea air… and just pined away for her little hovel. She was a wife, a mother, a widow, lived there her whole life, till the alley’s smoky reek smelled sweeter than lavender, and the neighbours yelling at each other at 2am sounded like the voices of her family. Who are we to argue with that, save the God who made us?

So the old house held us tight, till first my aunt, then my mother, were gone, and I’d said goodbye to Amy, crying in the middle of half-packed crates, before I finally walked out the front door for good.

My aunt died exactly as she lived: grumbling the whole way.

“You’ll all be glad to be rid of me!” she huffed, dropping for the first and only time in my memory straight talk, no snarky detours. “And I can’t say I’m all that gutted to go myself. This life never was what I’d ordered.”

Poor old soul! That was only two weeks before the end. I don’t reckon she realised how close the finish line was, or she might’ve bothered being a bit sentimental.

“Don’t be daft,” my mother said. “You’re not dying.”

“Why talk such rubbish?” my aunt snapped back. “I’ve got to die some time. Why not now, when all the arrangements are already sorted? It’s not like I’m having a grand old time.”

“I’m sure we do all we can for you,” my mother said.

“I know you do,” my aunt replied. “I’m a burden. Always have been.”

“Burden’s not the word for it,” my mother corrected her.

“Well what word would you use?” my aunt shot back. “Sunbeam? Have I been a sunbeam in this house? I’ve been a nuisance to everyone. That’s what I was born for. It’s my speciality.”

My mother wrapped her arms round the grumpy old thing and kissed her forehead. “We’d miss you terribly,” she said.

“Good! I hope you do!” my aunt said. “It’s the only half-decent thing I’ve got to leave you lot.”

My mother laughed.

“Might even have been good for you, Maggie,” my aunt mumbled, softening a little. “If there weren’t cantankerous old devils like me knocking about, gentle patient sorts like you would never get any practice at being patient. For all you know, I’ve been a blessing in disguise.”

I can’t honestly say I ever caught myself wishing she was back – but we absolutely did miss her. Missed all the little jokes we’d crack about her odd habits, missed all the laughs at her grumpy, prickly little ways. It takes all sorts to make a world, as the saying goes. If all of us perfect, easy-to-get-along types were left, we’d all be so bored we’d start picking fights just to liven things up.

Amy, for one, truly did regret her passing.

“You never know what you’ve got till it’s gone,” Amy sighed.

“I’m so glad you grew fond of her,” my mother said.

“See, mum,” Amy explained, “I was one of seven kids. A little bit of yelling every now and then does a body good. I reckon I’ll lose the use of my tongue soon if no one around here picks a fight with me.”

“You’re getting married in a few weeks, remember,” my mother reminded her.

Amy just glumly shook her head. “Men are useless things, aren’t they? The best of ’em can’t stand a bit of backchat. Half the time you’re just getting into a good argument, you turn round and find they’ve put their hat on and snuck out the door.”

My aunt gone, Barbara off to finishing school abroad (old Mr Hasluck called it “learning how to ape the nobs proper”), mum and I were left all alone. I’d left school by then, and taken a no-salary job with Mr Stillwood, the idea being I’d study to be a solicitor.

“You’ve hit the jackpot, my boy,” old Mr Gadley assured me. “Starting your career at Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal is a golden ticket anywhere. It’ll mark you out as a proper respectable sort, no question.”

Mr Stillwood himself was so old and frail he looked like he might blow over in a strong gust of London smog. It was a wonder a man as rich as he hadn’t retired decades earlier.

“I keep meaning to!” he explained to me a few days after I started at the office. “When your poor father came to see me, he told me straight he hadn’t got more than a few years left. ‘Mr Kelver,’ I told him, ‘don’t you worry on that score. I’ve got a few loose ends tied up in the office I’d like to sort out first, and you can help me with those. The second they’re done, I’m retiring!’ And here I still am, plodding along. I’m like that old hackney coach horse Mr Weller tells us about, you know? If you pulled the shafts out from under him he’d just collapse on the spot. So I jog on, one slow step at a time.”

He’d married late in life, to a woman much younger than him, a common sort of girl who’d dragged in her wake a whole horde of greedy, broke relatives who all thought they were owed a fancy living. No wonder he preferred the quiet peace of Lombard Street to his own home, where the noise never stopped. We’d see his wife every now and then. She was the sort of loud, bossy woman who went through life convinced that if she yelled loud enough and made enough of a fuss, she was “putting things to rights”. She was absurdly ashamed of her humble origins, and tried to hide them under a posh duchess act that her sycophantic friends told her was perfectly aristocratic – but in reality, it just came off like a barmaid on a Sunday trying to pretend she’d never served a pint in her life. Old Gadley was the only person alive who wasn’t scared of her. On the contrary, he kept her terrified of him, speaking to her with a brutal honesty that was always extremely refreshing. He’d known her back when she was a urchin playing in the gutter, and he’d always taken it as a personal insult that she’d wormed her way into the old, respected firm of Stillwood & Co.

Her story was a strange one. Mr Stillwood, a bored, well-off bachelor pushing 40, had first spotted her when she was a scrawny blonde little kid, caked in dirt, playing in the gutter. To this day he’s kicking himself over it, but it was young Gadley, walking home with his boss from Westminster, who drew Stillwood’s attention to her by boxing her ears – because the little brat had slapped him across the face with a sprat she’d grabbed off a passing fishmonger’s cart. On a random impulse, Stillwood took the kid by the hand, dragged her off kicking and screaming to her father’s place of business – a tiny little coal shed down Horseferry Road. The arrangement he made there amounted to buying the child off her parents, practically. He sent her off to boarding school abroad, and the coal shed was closed up. Ten years later, when she came back a tall, handsome young woman, he married her – and learned the hard way what everyone always says about what’s bred in the bone. You can scrub it, dress it up in silk, teach it to speak posh, but it always comes back out.

Her constant complaint about her husband was that he was “only a solicitor”, a profession she considered terribly common. Nothing riled old Gadley more than hearing her spout that nonsense.

“It’s not fair on the girls!” I heard her yell at him once. I was working in the next room, the door ajar, and she talked so loud at all times you could hear her in the next postcode. “What proper gentleman is ever going to marry the daughter of a City attorney? I told him years ago, he ought to retire and get himself a seat in the House of Lords.”

“Funny, that’s exactly what your old dad was going to do every time he lost a sack of coal or a bag of potatoes in his little shop,” Gadley grunted back.

Mrs Stillwood called him a “low brute” in her poshest duchess tone, and swept out of the room in a huff.

Old Stillwood himself was never all that fond of the law, to hear him talk.

“I’m not at all sure you made a wise choice going into law, Kelver,” he said to me once. “It makes you look at humanity the same way a doctor looks at a patient – like everyone’s fundamentally sick. You start suspecting every single person you meet is a rogue. The only people that ever cross our desks are the ones that are up to no good – all roguery, trickery, hypocrisy. It rots your character a bit, Kelver. We breathe nothing but sin all day. I sometimes wonder if it’s catching.”

“It doesn’t seem to have infected you, sir,” I told him. The firm of Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal was famous across the whole legal world for being as honest as the day is long, old-fashioned rectitude and all.

“I hope not, Kelver, I hope not,” the old man smiled. “Though, you know, sometimes I catch myself wondering… what if I’m a scamp and I don’t even know it? Rogues always manage to talk themselves into believing they’re perfectly honest, don’t they? No scamp ever thinks he’s a scamp.”

His words gave me a little jolt then, because on Gadley’s advice I’d talked my mother into putting all our small savings into Mr Stillwood’s hands to invest, and it had boosted our tiny income no end. But when I looked into his soft, smiling eyes, the silly fear vanished instantly.

He laughed, and laid a hand on my shoulder. “There’s one person you should never trust, Kelver – yourself. Nobody on this whole planet can do you half as much harm as you can do yourself.”

We saw more and more of Washburn, after that. We both called him Hal, now. He’d slipped past my mother’s shyness with his soft, strong hands, and settled right into our family like he’d always been there. Mum treated him like some absurdly bearded overgrown boy who’d somehow wandered into her care without noticing, and I looked up to him like the coolest, most chaotic big brother anyone ever had.

“You rest me, Mrs Kelver,” he’d say, lighting his pipe and sinking into the big worn leather armchair we kept waiting for him by the fire. “Your even voice, your soft eyes, your quiet hands – they soothe the savage beast in me.”

“A man ought to test all his grand ideas against two things,” he’d say, drifting smoke curling around his head as he looked between the two of us. “The face of a good woman, and the ear of a young man – sorry Paul, not quite a child anymore. A good woman’s face is bright sunlight. Under the gaslamps, you can’t tell a diamond from a bit of paste. Hold it up to the sun, and you know instantly if it’s real. And the kids! They’re the soft waiting earth, and we fling all our silly grand thoughts onto them. Is it chaff and dust, or real living seed? Wait and watch. I dump all my cleverest thoughts on you, Paul. To me they seem brilliant, deep, original. You swallow them, and then forget them instantly. Turns out they were rubbish. Then I say something that sticks with you, that grows. Ah! That one was alive, that was a real seed. The earth only keeps what’s true.”

“You ought to get married, Hal,” my mother would say. It was her cure for every ill a man ever had.

“I would, Mrs Kelver,” he told her once, “I’d march down the aisle tomorrow if I could marry half a dozen women. I’d be the perfect husband for six different wives. I’d neglect one for five straight days, and be a total nuisance to her on the sixth.”

From anyone else, my mother would have taken that as an insult to the whole female sex, but Hal’s smile could sugar any kind of pill.

“I’m not one man, Mrs Kelver. I’m six different men rolled into one. If I married just one woman, she’d end up married to six separate husbands. That’s way more than any poor woman can handle.”

“Have you never fallen in love, even once?” my mother asked.

“Three of me have. But every single time, the other five of me outvoted him.”

“Are you sure six is enough?” my mother asked, grinning.

“Just the right number.
