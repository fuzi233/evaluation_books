Within the drawing-room at The Pool, Durrance bid farewell to Ethne. He had meticulously arranged for their parting to be brief; already, the carriage waited at the steps of Guessens, laden with his luggage, and his servant stood ready at the door. Ethne accompanied him onto the terrace, where Mrs. Adair awaited them at the top of the stone steps. Durrance extended his hand to her, but she turned instead toward Ethne.

"I must speak with Colonel Durrance before he departs," Mrs. Adair said.

"Very well," Ethne replied. Then, turning to Durrance, she added, "Let us say our goodbyes here. You will write from Wiesbaden? Please, do so soon."

"The moment I arrive," Durrance promised.

He descended the steps alongside Mrs. Adair, leaving Ethne standing alone on the terrace above. The final act of pretense had concluded; months of tense surveillance had finally ended, and both men and women breathed freely. Yet, as Durrance walked briskly across the lawn beside Mrs. Adair, showing relief in his stride, she lagged behind. Her voice, when it came, was heavy with despondency.

"So you are going," she murmured. "In two days, you will be in Wiesbaden, and Ethne at Glenalla. We shall all be scattered. This place will feel terribly empty."

She had achieved her aim; she had successfully driven a wedge between Ethne and Durrance, sparing herself the torture of witnessing their union. Yet, strangely, her victory offered her no solace.

"The house will seem so hollow after you are gone," she remarked, turning to walk further into the garden with him.

"We shall return, undoubtedly," Durrance reassured her.

Mrs. Adair cast her eyes around the grounds. The vibrant flowers were gone, the sunlight dimmed, and grey clouds stretched across the sky. The grass beneath their feet lacked its usual verdancy, the stream running murky through the tree line, and autumn leaves—russet and yellow—drifted across the lawns.

"How long will you remain in Wiesbaden?" she asked.

"I cannot say," Durrance answered. "Only as long as it is advisable."

"That tells me nothing," she snapped, sensing his evasion. "I suppose it was intended not to tell me anything."

Durrance said nothing, and her silence bred resentment. She knew nothing of his future plans, unsure if he intended to honor his engagement to Ethne or abandon it entirely. Curiosity gnawed at her, threatening to consume her. The prospect of seeing him again so soon faded, replaced by the dread of a long separation filled with uncertainty.

"You distrust me?" she challenged, her voice rising with anger.

"Why did you tell me of Captain Willoughby's arrival? Why did you interfere?" Durrance asked gently.

"I thought you ought to know," she insisted.

"But Ethne wished that secret kept. I am glad to know, truly glad. Yet, you were Ethne's friend."

"And yours, I hope," Mrs. Adair countered, her tone sharp. "Don't you understand why I couldn't keep silent?"

"No." Durrance might have understood, but he had rarely considered her perspective, and she resented that indifference. His simple denial stung her deeply.

"I spoke brutally, didn't I?" she declared. "I gave you the truth as harshly as I could. Does that not help you comprehend?"

Again, Durrance merely said, "No." The monosyllable pushed her past the bounds of prudence. Suddenly, she found herself spilling out thoughts she had previously suppressed. Standing apart from herself, she watched her own speech spiral into madness, yet she could not halt it.

"I told you the truth brutally *on purpose*," she confessed, her voice trembling. "I was stung because you refused to see what was plain to anyone else. I wanted to hurt you. I am a bad, bad woman, I suppose. Remember those nights? You and she in the room, talking in the dark, while I stood alone on the terrace. It happened today again. You and Ethne inside; I outside. I wonder if it will always be so. But you will not say—you will not admit it."

She clapped her hands together in a gesture of despair, but Durrance walked silently ahead, quickening his pace toward the stile that marked the boundary between the garden and the fields. His haste was a silent rebuke, but Mrs. Adair, seized by her frenzy, matched his speed.

"I would not have minded so much," she raged, "if Ethne truly loved you. But she never did; she cared only as a friend cares. And what is friendship worth?"

"It prevents Ethne from shrinking from her friend," Durrance stated calmly.

"She shrinks from you!" Mrs. Adair cried. "Why? Because you are blind! She is terrified. As for me—I will tell you the truth—I was glad. When the news of your blindness arrived from Wadi Halfa, I was glad. Seeing you in Hill Street, I was glad. Ever since, I have been glad. Because I saw her shrink. From the start, she feared her life would become hampered and fettered." Her voice dropped to a whisper, laced with scorn. "I am not afraid. I am not afraid. I repeat it: I am not afraid."

To Durrance, this outburst from the woman who called herself Ethne's friend seemed utterly horrifying and unforeseen.

"Ethne wrote to you at Wadi Halfa out of pity," Mrs. Adair continued relentlessly. "She wrote out of fear, and lacking the courage to confess her fear, she hesitated. You would not have blamed her if she had been honest; you would have remained her friend. But she lacked the fortitude."

Durrance knew there was another, truer explanation for Ethne's timidity. He also knew that explanation was correct. Tomorrow, he would depart the Salcombe estuary, and Ethne would journey toward the Irish Channel and Donegal. Arguing against Mrs. Adair's slanderous ravings served no purpose. He was nearing the stile; once across, he would be free of her influence.

"You are not just to Ethne," he said quietly.

At those words, the madness drained from Mrs. Adair. She recognized the futility of her boasts and her attacks. Whether her words were true or false, they achieved nothing. Durrance remained perpetually in the room with Ethne, never on the terrace with her. Realizing her degradation, she faltered into excuses.

"I am a bad woman, I suppose," she whispered, her voice pitiful. "But my life has not been happy. Perhaps there is something to be said for me."

Even to her own ears, it sounded weak, yet they had reached the stile. Durrance turned to face her, his stern expression softening. He recalled the dignity and reticence she possessed in his youth, making the current transformation seem impossible, yet compelling him to listen.

"Will you tell me?" he asked gently.

"My marriage was arranged almost immediately after school," she began, her voice gaining a strange simplicity. "I was a child, ignorant of everything, wedded to a stranger chosen by my mother. She sought security, comfort, and protection from poverty for me. Blinded by filial duty and accustomed to obedience, I accepted her choice without question. It is a common tragedy, yet its frequency makes it no less painful to endure."

"But Mr. Adair?" Durrance prompted.

"He was kind," she replied softly. "He cared for me. That love was the only link holding me together. Initially, I was content. I had homes in town and here. But it was dull. Oh, how dull! Can you imagine the backstreets of a manufacturing town? Rows of identical, ugly houses, choked by smoke, with dirt-stained plants in the windows? Any charlatan promising color to such grey lives finds eager followers. When I reflected on my existence, those streets haunted me. Many women find joy in managing large estates and London society, but I was not one of them. I was oppressed by the sheer monotony."

She paused, unable to stop her confession.

"I married believing that was my lot, destined for all women. But soon, doubts arose. I learned that life offered more than mere dullness—that there existed something greater, though perhaps not for me. Passing a couple riding together, or sharing a conversation with a stranger, I would catch a glimpse of profound happiness in another's face. I knew, with absolute certainty, that there was 'ever so much more.' My mother had denied me that chance."

All traces of earlier violence vanished from her demeanor. She spoke simply, without seeking pity.

"And then you came," she continued. "I met you, and met you again. You departed on your duties and returned, and I learned—not that there was 'ever so much more,' but exactly what it was. Still, it was denied to me. Yet, I felt happier. I thought I could be content watching your progress, proud of you. But then Ethne appeared, and you turned to her. Immediately—if only you had been slower to turn to her! In a short time, I grew sorrowful that you had entered my life at all."
