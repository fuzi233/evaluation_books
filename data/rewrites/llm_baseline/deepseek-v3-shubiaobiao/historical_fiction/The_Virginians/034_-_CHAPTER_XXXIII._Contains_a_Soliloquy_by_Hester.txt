Colonel Lambert’s first impulse upon discovering his younger daughter’s secret was indignation—not at her, but at the young man who had unwittingly stolen her heart.

“Confound all reckless youths, English or otherwise!” he exclaimed to his wife. “Must this one stumble into *our* lives of all places?”

Mrs. Lambert, ever the peacemaker, offered a gentler perspective. “Perhaps Providence guided him here, my dear. If not Heaven’s doing, then whose? His arrival may yet prove a blessing.”

The Colonel groaned. “We raise them, nurse them, fret over every ache and fancy—only for some stripling to sweep in and eclipse us in an instant. A lifetime of devotion undone by a pair of blue eyes!”

His wife’s hand rested softly on his arm. “And did I not leave my own father for you, Martin? Is it not the way of things?”

He sighed. “Aye, human nature is a fickle jade. But mark my words—when our girls take husbands, I shall despise them as your father despised me!”

“What unnatural talk!” chided Mrs. Lambert. “Would you deny them happiness?”

“Happiness? Bah! It’s the parting I dread. Already you speak of Virginia as if she were packed and gone.”

His wife’s eyes grew distant. “Thousands of miles… I shall have to cross the sea. Oh, how I loathe ships!”

“Shall we order lace for the christening gown while we’re at it?” Lambert teased.

She ignored him, lost in thought. “I do hope Rachel Esmond and I shall reconcile. She was ever proud-spirited.”

---

Meanwhile, Hetty lay abed, flushed with feverish misery—not of body, but of heart. Shame and fury warred within her. She had betrayed herself, and the knowledge burned like a brand.

“What folly!” she stormed to her pillow. “As if he’d spare me a thought! He prefers painted actresses or silly milliners—as he should! I’m a greater fool than he is, and he’s dull enough.”

She railed against her own weakness, vowing never to forgive herself. When Theo tried to soothe her, Hetty turned fiercer still.

“Suppose he scorns me? I’ve given him cause enough! Called him goose, mocked his looks—though God knows his eyes are… No! I’ll not pine for a man who’s merely tall and handsome. Better to stay with Papa.”

Yet long after Theo slept, Hetty lay awake, the truth lancing her heart anew. “A punishment,” she whispered, “for my pride. But why must I suffer so? I’d have stood aside for Theo—helped her elope, even! Now I must bear this alone.”

Dawn found her at last in exhausted slumber, tear-stained but resolute. Youth’s wounds cut deep, yet seldom prove mortal. Time would tell whether this arrow, loosed by Love’s careless hand, had struck too deep—or whether the barbs might yet work free.

---

At tea that evening, Hetty’s manner toward Harry was all frost and thorn. When he invited them to a dance at the Assembly Rooms, Theo clapped her hands in delight.

“How splendid! Might we go tonight?”

Lambert chuckled. “For a colonial, Mr. Warrington, you’ve the manners of Versailles. My dear—” He bowed theatrically to his wife. “Shall we lead the minuet?”

Only Hetty sat sullen. “I’ve no taste for dancing,” she muttered.

Harry’s face darkened. “Have I offended again?” he asked Mrs. Lambert afterward.

The kind matron could only sigh. Later, Theo scolded her sister: “Why be so cruel when he meant only kindness?”

Hetty’s eyes flashed. “To vex myself, Theo! Don’t you see? I must drive him away before I disgrace us all.”

Yet when darkness fell, her defiance crumbled. Alone, she wept into her pillow. “Let him think me a shrew. Better that than pity.”

Outside, the moon silvered the eaves where Love, relentless marksman, kept his watch.
