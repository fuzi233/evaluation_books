For reasons that will become clear as this story unfolds, the ancient Cathedral town at the heart of our tale must bear a name other than its own. Let it be known here as Cloisterham—a city steeped in silence and shadow, where time seems to have paused long ago. Once, perhaps, it was known by the Druids under another name; later, by Romans, Saxons, and Normans—each leaving behind their mark, yet none lasting enough to matter much in the dust-laden pages of its history.

Cloisterham is not a place for those who crave the clamor of modern life. It breathes slowly, like an old man in deep repose. Its streets are narrow, almost too quiet to be real—so still that even the summer wind hesitates before stirring the sunblinds of shopfronts. The air carries a faint earthiness, drawn from the Cathedral crypt below, and everywhere you look, there are traces of monastic days: graves half-buried beneath gardens, stone markers worn smooth by centuries. Children grow salad in the soil once trod by abbots and abbesses; they bake dirt-pies with the bones of nuns and friars. And out in the fields, every ploughman pays tribute to forgotten lords—Archbishops, Treasurers, Bishops—grinding their relics into bread, just as the ogre in fairy tales would grind his guest’s bones.

It is a drowsy, dreamlike city, where people believe change has already passed them by. As if the past were all there ever could be. Yet irony lies in that belief: for while Cloisterham clings to memory, it remains strangely unchanged—not because it’s preserved, but because it has ceased to live.

The houses lean close together, some built around fragments of ancient walls or ruined chapels, as though the city itself were made up of half-remembered dreams. Even the pawnbroker offers no pledges—his shop holds only unredeemed relics: tarnished sugar-tongs, dimmed watches sweating in slow decay, and books so grim they seem to whisper secrets better left unspoken.

Life in Cloisterham stirs only in the quiet growth of vegetables—its most vibrant signs of continuity. Even the tiny theatre, drooping and disused, has a garden where the devil, when he vanishes from stage, descends into hell among scarlet beans or oyster shells depending on the season.

At the very center of this stillness stands the Nuns’ House—a brick edifice older than most stories told within it. Its current purpose is far removed from its legendary past, but the name persists. A polished brass plate gleams upon the gate, announcing: “Seminary for Young Ladies. Miss Twinkleton.” The contrast between the weathered building and the shiny sign has led visitors to compare it to a battered gentleman wearing a modern eye-glass over a blind eye—awkward, yet oddly dignified.

Who were the nuns who once lived here? Did they bow their heads low to avoid beams in cramped chambers? Did they sit by the windows, counting beads not for adornment but penance? Were any walled alive in hidden corners, victims of nature’s restless spirit? Such questions may haunt ghosts—but not Miss Twinkleton. They do not appear in her accounts, nor in her recitals. Her poetry teacher never includes such themes—too dark, too impractical.

Yet Miss Twinkleton herself lives in two worlds. By day, she is the stern, proper headmistress, overseeing studies and manners. But each night, after the girls retire, she transforms—curls smoothed, eyes brightened, voice lightened. Then she speaks of Tunbridge Wells (“The Wells,” she calls it), of scandals whispered only in candlelight, and of one Mr. Porters—“Foolish Mr. Porters,” she sighs, as if remembering a lost romance.

And through both phases, constant and dutiful, is Mrs. Tisher—widow, caretaker, keeper of wardrobes, and rumored former hairdresser. She moves with a soft step, a chronic sigh, and a voice barely above a whisper. The servants believe her husband was once a barber—a fact passed down like folklore.

Among the pupils, none is more cherished than Miss Rosa Bud—known affectionately as Rosebud. She is lovely, childlike, whimsical. There is a peculiar tension around her, however, for it is known that she has been promised to a husband by her late guardian’s will. He is to come of age soon—and then she must marry him.

Miss Twinkleton pretends to mourn this fate, shaking her head over Rosebud’s dimpled shoulders. But the girls see through her: “O, what a pretending old thing Miss Twinkleton is, my dear!” they whisper in bedchamber.

And indeed, the house trembles with excitement whenever the fiancé arrives. It is understood—by all—that he has a right to visit. Should Miss Twinkleton object, she’d be instantly arrested and shipped off to Australia.

On the afternoon following the dinner at the gatehouse, the bell rings again. “Mr. Edwin Drood to see Miss Rosa,” announces the parlour-maid.

Miss Twinkleton, grave-faced, turns to Rosebud: “You may go down, my dear.”

Rosebud descends, watched by every eye. In the parlour, Edwin waits—a young man of sharp features and restless energy. The room is modestly furnished, with only two globes hinting at academic rigor. They suggest that even in private, Miss Twinkleton might be summoned to journey across the world in search of knowledge.

A new maid, unused to seeing the future husband, peeks through the door. She slips down the stairs guiltily as Rosebud enters—her face hidden behind a silk apron.

“Oh! It _is_ so ridiculous!” she exclaims, stopping short. “Don’t, Eddy!”

“Don’t what, Rosa?”

“Don’t come any nearer, please. It _is_ so absurd.”

“What is absurd?”

“The whole thing. To be an engaged orphan… to have girls and servants scuttling about like mice in the wainscot… to be called upon!”

She has a thumb tucked into the corner of her mouth as she speaks.

“You give me an affectionate reception, Pussy, I must say.”

“Well, I will in a minute, Eddy, but I can’t just yet. How are you?” (briefly.)

“I am unable to reply that I am much the better for seeing you, Pussy, since I see nothing of you.”

At this, a bright, pouting eye emerges from under the apron—then vanishes again.

“Oh good gracious! You’ve had half your hair cut off!”

“I should have done better to have had my head cut off,” Edwin says, ruffling his hair fiercely, glaring at the mirror, and stamping impatiently.

“Shall I go?”

“No, not yet. The girls would ask why.”

“Once for all, Rosa, will you uncover that ridiculous little head of yours and give me a welcome?”

The apron comes off. “You’re very welcome, Eddy. There! That’s nice. Shake hands. No, I can’t kiss you—I’ve got an acidulated drop in my mouth.”

“Are you glad to see me, Pussy?”

“Oh yes, dreadfully glad. Go and sit down. Miss Twinkleton.”

It is customary for Miss Twinkleton to make appearances every few minutes—either in person or via Mrs. Tisher—to pretend to search for something. This time, she glides in and out, saying: “How do you do, Mr. Drood? Very glad indeed to have the pleasure. Pray excuse me. Tweezers. Thank you!”

“I got the gloves last evening, Eddy, and I like them very much. They are beauties.”

“Well, that’s something,” Edwin replies, half-grumbling. “The smallest encouragement thankfully received. And how did you pass your birthday, Pussy?”

“Delightfully! Everyone gave me a present. We had a feast. And a ball at night.”

“A feast and a ball, eh? These occasions seem to go off tolerably well without me, Pussy.”

“De-lightfully!” Rosa cries, genuinely delighted.

“And what was the feast?”

“Tarts, oranges, jellies, and shrimps.”

“Any partners at the ball?”

“We danced with one another, of course. But some of the girls made game to be their brothers. It _was_ so droll!”

“Did anybody make game to be—”

“To be you? Oh dear yes!” Rosa laughs. “That was the first thing done.”

“I hope she did it pretty well,” Edwin says, doubtful.

“Oh, it was excellent!—I wouldn’t dance with you, you know.”

Edwin doesn’t catch the point. “Why not?”

“Because I was so tired of you.”

But seeing his displeasure, she adds quickly, pleadingly: “Dear Eddy, you were just as tired of me, you know.”

“Did I say so, Rosa?”

“Say so? Do you ever say so? No, you only showed it. O, she did it so well!”

“It strikes me that she must be a devilish impudent girl,” Edwin remarks.

“And so, Pussy, you’ve passed your last birthday in this old house.”

“Yes!” Rosa clasps her hands, looks down, sighs. “Somehow, I feel as if it would miss me when I’m gone so far away, so young.”

“You seem sorry, Rosa.”

“I am sorry for the poor old place.”

“Perhaps we had better stop short, Rosa?”

She looks up—bright-eyed—then shakes her head, sighs, and looks down again.

“That is to say, is it, Pussy, that we are both resigned?”

She nods.

Then, after a pause, she bursts out: “You know we must be married, and married from here, Eddy, or the poor girls will be so dreadfully disappointed!”

For a moment, there is more compassion in Edwin’s face than love—for both her and himself. He checks it and asks: “Shall I take you out for a walk, Rosa dear?”

Rosa hesitates, then brightens. “Oh yes, Eddy! Let us go for a walk! And I tell you what—we’ll pretend. You shall pretend you’re engaged to someone else, and I’ll pretend I’m not engaged to anyone. Then we shan’t quarrel.”

“Do you think that will prevent our falling out, Rosa?”

“I know it will. Hush! Pretend to look out of window—Mrs. Tisher!”

Through luck, Mrs. Tisher appears—rustling like a ghost in silken skirts. “I hope I see Mr. Drood well; though I needn’t ask, if I may judge from his complexion. I trust I disturb no one; but there _was_ a paper-knife—O, thank you, I am sure!”—and vanishes.

“One other thing you must do, Eddy,” says Rosebud. “As soon as we’re in the street, you must put me outside, and keep close to the house—squeeze against it.”

“By all means, Rosa, if you wish. Might I ask why?”

“Oh! Because I don’t want the girls to see you.”

“It’s a fine day; but would you like me to carry an umbrella?”

“Don’t be foolish, sir. You haven’t got polished leather boots on,” she says, pouting, one shoulder raised.

“Perhaps that might escape notice,” Edwin mutters, looking down at his boots with distaste.

“Nothing escapes their notice, sir. And then I know what would happen. Some of them would start reflecting on me—saying (for _they_ are free) that they’ll never engage themselves to lovers without polished leather boots.”

“Hark! Miss Twinkleton. I’ll ask for leave.”

Outside, Miss Twinkleton is heard asking aloud, as if conversing with no one: “Eh? Indeed! Are you quite sure you saw my mother-of-pearl button-holder on the work-table in my room?”

Rosa requests permission to walk—and it is granted.

Soon, the pair leave the Nuns’ House, taking care to hide Edwin’s ill-fitting boots. Let us hope these precautions serve peace in the future household of Mrs. Edwin Drood.

“Which way shall we take, Rosa?”

“To the Lumps-of-Delight shop.”

“To the—?”

“A Turkish sweetmeat, sir. My gracious me, don’t you understand anything? Call yourself an Engineer and not know _that_?”

“How should I know, Rosa?”

“Because I’m very fond of them. But oh! I forgot what we’re to pretend. Never mind—you needn’t know anything about them.”

So he is dragged gloomily to the shop. Rosa buys sweets, offers some to Edwin (who refuses sharply), then eats them with relish—first removing pink gloves like rose petals, occasionally bringing her fingers to her lips to wipe away the Dust of Delight.

“Now, be a good-tempered Eddy, and pretend. And so you are engaged?”

“And so I am engaged.”

“Is she nice?”

“Charming.”

“Tall?”

“Immensely tall!”

“Must be gawky, I should think,” Rosa comments quietly.

“I beg your pardon; not at all,” Edwin replies, defensive. “What is termed a fine woman; a splendid woman.”

“Big nose, no doubt,” she says again.

“Not a little one, certainly,” he answers quickly—Rosa’s nose being small.

“Long pale nose, with a red knob in the middle. I know the sort,” she nods, enjoying her treat.

“You _don’t_ know the sort, Rosa,” he insists, warming. “Because it’s nothing of the kind.”

“Not a pale nose, Eddy?”

“No.”

He refuses to yield.

“A red nose? Oh! I don’t like red noses. However—she can always powder it.”

“She would scorn to powder it,” Edwin says, growing heated.

“She would? What a stupid thing she must be! Is she stupid in everything?”

“No; in nothing.”

After a pause, Rosa says, watching him closely: “And this most sensible of creatures likes the idea of being carried off to Egypt, does she, Eddy?”

“Yes. She takes a sensible interest in triumphs of engineering skill—especially when they change undeveloped countries.”

“Oh!” Rosa shrugs, laughing softly. “Do you object, sir, to her feeling that interest?”

“Object? My dear Eddy! But really—doesn’t she hate boilers and things?”

“I can answer for her not being so idiotic as to hate Boilers,” he returns sharply. “Though I cannot answer for her views about Things—really not understanding what Things are meant.”

“But don’t she hate Arabs, Turks, Fellahs, and people?”

“Certainly not.”

“Then she must hate the Pyramids? Come, Eddy?”

“Why should she be such a little—tall, I mean—goose, as to hate the Pyramids, Rosa?”

“Oh! You should hear Miss Twinkleton,” Rosa says, nodding, “bore about them. Tiresome old burying-grounds! Isises, Ibises, Cheopses, Pharaohses—who cares? And Belzoni, or somebody, dragged out by the legs, half-choked with bats and dust. All the girls say: Serve him right, and hope it hurt him, and wish he had been quite choked.”

They wander side by side through the Cathedral Close, not arm-in-arm now, each stepping slowly, leaving deeper marks in the fallen leaves.

“Well,” Edwin says after a long silence, “according to custom. We can’t get on, Rosa.”

Rosa tosses her head. “I don’t want to get on.”

“A pretty sentiment, considering.”

“Considering what?”

“If I say what, you’ll go wrong again.”

“You’ll go wrong, you mean, Eddy. Don’t be ungenerous.”

“Ungenerous! I like that!”

“Then I _don’t_ like that, and so I tell you plainly,” Rosa pouts.

“Now, Rosa, I put it to you. Who disparaged my profession, my destination—”

“You’re not going to be buried in the Pyramids, I hope?” she interrupts, arching her eyebrows.

“You never said you were. If you are, why haven’t you mentioned it to me? I can’t find out your plans by instinct.”

“Now, Rosa, you know very well what I mean, my dear.”

“Well then, why did you begin with your detestable red-nosed giantesses? And she would, she would, she would, she WOULD powder it!” she bursts out comically.

“Somehow or other, I never can come right in these discussions,” Edwin sighs, resigning himself.

“How is it possible, sir, that you ever can come right when you’re always wrong? And as to Belzoni, I suppose he’s dead—I’m sure I hope he is—and how can his legs or chokes concern you?”

“It is nearly time for your return, Rosa. We have not had a very happy walk, have we?”

“A happy walk? A detestably unhappy walk, sir. If I go upstairs the moment I get in and cry till I can’t take my dancing lesson, you are responsible, mind!”

“Let us be friends, Rosa.”

“Oh!” she cries, shaking her head and breaking into real tears. “I wish we _could_ be friends! It’s because we can’t be friends that we try one another so. I am a young little thing, Eddy, to have an old heartache; but I really, really have, sometimes. Don’t be angry. I know you have one too often. We should both have done better, if What is to be had been left What might have been. I am quite serious now, and not teasing you. Let each of us forbear, this one time, on our own account, and on the other’s!”

Disarmed by this glimpse of depth in the spoilt child—even if momentarily offended by its implication—he watches her sob gently, hands clutching a handkerchief to her eyes. Then, as she calms, even begins to laugh at herself, he leads her to a bench beneath the elm trees.

“One clear word of understanding, Pussy dear. I am not clever out of my own line—now I come to think of it, I don’t know that I am particularly clever in it—but I want to do right. There is not—there may be—I really don’t see my way to what I want to say, but I must say it before we part—there is not any other young—”

“Oh no, Eddy! It’s generous of you to ask me; but no, no, no!”

They are near the Cathedral windows. Suddenly, the organ swells, the choir sings—sublime, solemn music fills the air.

Edwin remembers last night’s confidence, thinks how unlike this harmony is to the discord between them.

“I fancy I can distinguish Jack’s voice,” he murmurs.

“Take me back at once, please,” Rosebud urges, laying her hand on his wrist. “They will all be coming out directly. Let us get away. Oh, what a resounding chord! But don’t let us stop to listen—let us get away!”

Her urgency fades once they leave the Close. Now they walk arm-in-arm, gravely, along the High Street toward the Nuns’ House.

At the gate, the street empty, Edwin leans down to Rosebud.

She laughs, remonstrates. “Eddy, no! I’m too sticky to be kissed. But give me your hand, and I’ll blow a kiss into that.”

He gives it. She breathes lightly into it, then holds it, looking into it.

“Now say, what do you see?”

“See, Rosa?”

“Why, I thought you Egyptian boys could look into a hand and see all sorts of phantoms. Can’t you see a happy Future?”

For certain, neither sees a happy Present. The gate opens. One goes in. One goes out.
