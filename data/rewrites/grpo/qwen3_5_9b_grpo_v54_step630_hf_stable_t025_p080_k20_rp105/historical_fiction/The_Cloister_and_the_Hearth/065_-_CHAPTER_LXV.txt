Two days after the turmoil that had shaken his spirit, Gerard wandered through the squalid arteries of Rome, seeking refuge from a sudden, violent thunderstorm. He sought shelter in a low hostelry, where he ordered wine and, as the rain lashed against the roof, drank himself into a stupor. With his head resting in his hands upon the table, he drifted into a heavy doze.

Soon, the room filled with the clamor of rough guests, their noise slowly pulling him back to consciousness. It was then that he became aware of two figures leaning close together, whispering in a hushed tone near his elbow. One was a pardoner, a man of the church's shadowy trade. The other, though dressed in clean but modest garments that suggested a decent tradesman, wore his hat slouched low over his brow, obscuring every feature save his beard. This was the mark of a man who shuns the gaze of honest folk and the reach of the law. They were negotiating a bargain in the black market of sin, sealing a celestial contract for a crime that had not yet been committed.

The man with the slouched hat grumbled about the rising cost of salvation. "If prices climb any higher," he complained, "we poor fellows shall be shut out of heaven altogether."

The pardoner denied the accusation flatly. "Indulgences have never been cheaper for good husbandmen."

"Who were they?" the buyer asked.

"Why, those who sin by the market, like reasonable creatures," the pardoner replied. "But if you are so perverse as to pick out a crime the Pope has set his face against, blame yourself, not me!"

To demonstrate that such sins were within the reach of all but the very dregs of society, the pardoner read aloud from a parchment scroll. It was a curious list, one too dark for print, yet familiar to any great library. Suffice it to say that the price for the murder of a layman was far less than many offenses my readers might consider trivial. The transaction proceeded with a little trifling concession on each side; the money changed hands, and the aspirant to heaven's favor was absolved in advance for the removal of one layman. The cost for eliminating a clerk, however, bore no such proportion. Not once did the word *assassination* leave either man's lips.

All this buzzed in Gerard's ears, yet he never lifted his head from the table, listening only with a dull, drunken stupor. Only when the parties rose and separated did he half-lift his head, eyeing the retreating figure of the purchaser with a scowl.

"If Margaret were alive," he muttered, his voice thick with grief and rage, "I would take thee by the throat and throttle thee, thou cowardly stabber. But she is dead; dead; dead. Let the whole world perish; it matters nothing to me. So let me die among the first."

When he finally returned home, a man in a slouched hat paced briskly to and fro on the opposite side of the street.

"Why, there is that cur again," Gerard thought.

But in his shattered state of mind, the sight made no impression upon him whatsoever.
