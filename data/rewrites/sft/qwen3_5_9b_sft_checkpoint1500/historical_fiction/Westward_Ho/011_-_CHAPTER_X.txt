So spake Chaucer's knight of his fellow-prisoners; and so it befell to Don Guzman. It happened thus.

Having secured his parole, the Spaniard settled quietly into lodging at Bideford, quarters far superior to those he had known for many days. He adopted the stoic demeanor of a seasoned soldier of fortune, accepting circumstances with a calm resolve. But after scarcely a month in the household of Sir Richard Grenville, the local authority, Old Salterne the Mayor, arrived to invite him to supper.

Though initially perplexed by the peculiar English custom of inviting "burghers" and tradesfolk to dine above the salt alongside nobility, Don Guzman possessed sufficient gentility to recognize that Sir Richard Grenville acted with unerring propriety. Shrugging aside his initial surprise, the Spaniard submitted to the arrangement, sitting at the same table as a man who sat at a ledger, managed apprentices, and conducted business. Yet, finding Salterne speak wisely and without vulgarity, the Don quickly condescended to engage him in conversation. He discovered the mayor to be a prudent and courteous soul, acutely aware of the disparity in their ranks, yet holding his ground with the confidence of a wise elder—a manner of address reminiscent of the merchant princes of Genoa and Venice.

At supper's end, Salterne extended a formal invitation for the following night. Turning to the Don, he admitted frankly that it required a profound condescension for a nobleman of Spain to dine with a simple merchant, but promised that the fare would prove worthy of any station. Graciously accepting the offer, hoping for amusement, Don Guzman returned, and indeed found the repast excellent, accompanied by wine of considerable quality.

Mr. Salterne, a merchant of keen intellect, was naturally eager for adventures abroad, a trait later proven by his tireless efforts in settling Virginia. Consequently, he was equally eager to probe the minds of guests who appeared knowledgeable regarding distant lands. His strategy twofold: first, to loosen the guest's tongue with generous measures of sack, and second, to interrogate him with carefully crafted inquiries about the Spanish Main, Peru, the Moluccas, China, and the Indies.

The first scheme failed miserably; the Spaniard was as abstemious as a monk, drinking little save water. The second met with limited success; the Don was as cunning as a fox, offering answers that amounted to little more than wind.

Into this intellectual stalemate stepped the Rose of Torridge, radiant as ever. Hearing the subject matter, she artlessly interjected her own questions. To the Don, silence was impossible; without revealing significant commercial secrets, he furnished his host and his host's daughter with a remarkably entertaining evening.

Little Eros, though the eldest of the gods and son of Jove and Venus, is reported by authorities such as Burton to be ultimately the child of idleness and satiety. This slander struck a responsive chord in Don Guzman's heart. Being idle, as captives inevitably must be, and full of bread thanks to Sir Richard's bountiful table, he had already cast about for amusement. Lady Grenville, being nearest, was initially considered, but the Spaniard, a man of honor, and Sir Richard, his gracious host, placed boundaries around his thoughts. With a self-denial on which he greatly prided himself, he dismissed the idea of courting Lady Grenville, despite acknowledging that the sinfulness of such a pursuit mattered little to Southern Europeans of his era, or indeed to the English in the days of the Stuarts.

Thus, space opened to consider Rose Salterne. He did not approach her with a specific intent to wrong her, but rather half to amuse himself and half because he could not resist. There existed an innocent freshness about the Rose of Torridge, coupled with her evident fondness for admiration, which was entirely new to him and deeply attractive. "The train of the peacock," he mused, "and yet the heart of the dove." Such a combination was so charming that, if he could persuade her to love no one else, perhaps he might foolishly follow suit. However, a sudden panic of prudence seized him, resolving him to keep aloof. Yet, the days dragged on slowly. When Lady Grenville, content to discuss only her husband, departed for Stow, leaving the Don alone in a corner of the great house, what choice remained but to lounge toward the butt-gardens to display his fine black cloak and feather, indulge in games of rackets with the youth and bowls with the elders, and eventually receive an invitation back to supper?

There, naturally, he ruled supreme. Not only did his rank command respect, but he possessed knowledge of value, for those were the dawn days of English commerce. Every merchant in Bideford and England burned with imagination fueled by dreams of discovery, companies, patents, and colonies. They spoke with fervor of Sir Edward Osborne's Turkish ventures, the privileges granted by Sultan Murad Khan, the Levant voyages of Roger Bodenham, John Fox, Lawrence Aldersey, and John Rule. Hope swelled for Mediterranean trade, shattered Venetian power at Famagusta having opened new doors via the alliance between Elizabeth and the Grand Turk.

News from Africa, carried by Edmund Hogan's embassy to Morocco and the voyages of John Hawkins and George Fenner trading for musk, civet, gold, and grain, rivaled West Indian tales in value. Two months prior, Captain Hare had sailed for Brazil in the *Minion*, carrying Sheffield hardware and textiles to trade with an ingenio and slaves owned by John Whithall, a man who had married a Spanish heiress. Meanwhile, Drake had demonstrated not only the feasibility of plundering American coasts but of establishing an East Indian trade; Frobisher and Davis were boring through northern ice seeking a passage to China; and Anthony Jenkinson had penetrated deep into Russia, Persia, Armenia, and the frozen wastes of Siberia and Tartary, echoing the tragic fate of Sir Hugh Willoughby.

Everywhere, under Queen Elizabeth's wise rule, English commerce spread its roots. As Don Guzman conversed with his new friends, he realized with shrewd insight that they belonged to a race destined for extinction if Spain sought to remain mistress of the world. Seizing the New World in the Pope's name, crushing the Dutch, enslaving the Venetians, and absorbing Portuguese trade was insufficient. These fierce islanders remained to assert, through cunning policy, scripture, and if necessary, sharp shot and cold steel, the principles of free seas and free trade.

It was clear to the Don and his countrymen alike: the Spanish Armada was coming. And the Spaniard knew, by hard experience, that these islanders, who sat in Salterne's parlors speaking broad Devon with nasal intonation, were not mere hawkers of goods. Though they detested combat and preferred profit, they could fight, when occasion demanded, with a dogged and terrible ferocity rivaling the finest blood of Spain. Their merchant ships sailed armed to the teeth, manned by crews trained from childhood to wield those arms, ordered to use them without mercy against any who dared impede their money-making.

One evening, driven to madness, Don Guzman civilly hinted that if Englishmen ventured where they had no right, they might find themselves returned. The response was immediate: "We'll see that, sir." "Depends on who says 'No right.'" "You found might makes right when you claimed the Indian seas," countered another. "We may find right makes might when we try." "Try them, gentlemen!" challenged the Don. "Find the sacred flag of Spain as invincible as ever was the Roman eagle."

"We have," replied the merchants. "Did you ever hear of Francis Drake?"
"Or of George Fenner and the Portugals at the Azores, one against seven?"
"Or of John Hawkins, at St. Juan d'Ulloa?"

"You are insolent burghers!" cried Don Guzman, rising to depart.
"Sir," interposed old Salterne gently, "as you say, we are burghers and plain men, and some of us have perhaps forgotten ourselves a trifle. We beg forgiveness for our lack of manners, attributing it to the strength of my wine. Insolence was never our intent, especially toward a noble gentleman and a foreigner."

Yet the Don refused to be pacified. Walking out, he cursed himself as an ass and a fool for demeaning himself to such a company, forgetting that he had provoked them.

Next day, Salterne, prompted by the great devil Mammon, approached the Don again, begging pardon and promising that the rude individuals involved would never be asked to meet him again, provided the Don would honor his house once more. Appeased, the Don returned the following evening, sneering internally at his own submission.

"Fool that I am!" he chided himself. "That girl has bewitched me, I believe. Must I go, and eat my share of dirt, for her sake?"

And so he went. Cunningly, he hinted to Salterne that he had developed a liking for the host and felt bound by his courtesy and hospitality, suggesting he might reveal certain matters he would not disclose to others. He explained that Spaniards were not jealous of single traders, but of any general attempt to deprive them of their hard-earned wealth; however, opportunities for individual enrichment abounded.
