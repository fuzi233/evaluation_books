We weave for ourselves fantasies of grand design,
But life itself is woven in the loom, where passion drives the shuttle,
Imposing its own pattern on our deeds.

Gwendolen's note, arriving with the awkward timing of a bird caught between dawn and dusk, reached Klesmer just as he prepared to depart Quetcham. Driven by a desire to honor her plea, he spent the night in Wanchester, though staying at Quetcham held no appeal for him. The magnificent estate, built with extravagant care, had become stiflingly hot for his spirit. Its owners, much like politicians shocked by an uprising they had ignored until it was too late, were bewildered by the rebellion brewing beneath their very noses.

The house was filled with guests, including one whom Miss Arrowpoint viewed as a formidable rival: a political man of high birth who confidently anticipated a peerage and believed he required a vast fortune to sustain such a title. Heiresses are often treated as appendages to their wealth, expected to marry where society dictates, regardless of their personal nature. Yet, nature occasionally endows the "only child" with a clear mind and a will of iron. The Arrowpoints had grown anxious regarding Catherine's refusal to accept the conventional path of marrying a needy nobleman or a commoner climbing the social ladder. They were strangely unconcerned about her potential attachment to Klesmer—a blindness common to parents who assume their sons' ignorance is secured by their own efforts, or that their daughters' affections are safe so long as marriage remains inconceivable.

To the Arrowpoints, hiring a first-rate musician was a privilege of wealth, and Catherine's talent demanded the finest environment for study. Klesmer, though admired across Europe, was not yet a Liszt, and no honorable musician would presume to propose to an heiress. Furthermore, Catherine was convinced that no man would dare seek her hand. The large check Mr. Arrowpoint drew in Klesmer's name seemed to render him as harmless as a footman. Where marriage is deemed impossible, a girl's sentiments are considered secure.

However, Klesmer was a man of honor, and passions rarely begin with formal declarations. Catherine's definition of the conceivable differed from her mother's. Outsiders might have deemed Klesmer's position dangerous if Miss Arrowpoint were a conventional beauty, failing to recognize that the most powerful allure is that which reveals itself only through shared sympathy. There is a charm in the eyes and lips that certify delicate perception, in the unostentatious smile that shows a heart awake to others. Such a presence restores confidence; no gesture is more satisfying than entering a room where one feels fully seen and understood. Obvious beauty, often an apology for folly, eventually grows tiresome through repetition. Klesmer's passionate attachment to Miss Arrowpoint was no paradox, but rather a triumph of deep, manifold sympathy over monotonous attraction.

Klesmer possessed a versatility akin to a youthful Ulysses, enhanced by nature's gift of music as a dominant power. Like Mendelssohn, he expressed this not only in technical mastery but in a fervor of creative belief that pierced the future with devoted purpose. His arrogance and vanity were minor foibles found in the best English families, clashing with Catherine's cool firmness. Yet, she was a creature of discovery, whose integrity inspired a desire to know her thoughts and witness her actions. Their companionship created a rhythm of expectation and fulfillment, a systole and diastole of bliss. Soon, they realized they were deeply interested in one another, though the extent of their feelings remained a mystery. Klesmer did not believe Catherine saw him as a lover, nor did she expect to stir more than friendly regard in a man not enamored of her fortune. Both were content to endure a shared sense of denial for the sake of loving the other's company too well. Under these conditions, Klesmer's visits had remained unrestricted for a year. He knew that if Catherine were poor, he would have made ardent love; she knew that if he wished for her hand, she would have found reasons to give it. This was the safety of full cups, secure from overflow unless disturbed.

Yet, silent feelings shift like the hands of a clock. During this visit, Klesmer began to think he would not return, while Catherine resented his frequent brusqueness, interpreting it as an unnecessary assertion of superiority.

Enter Mr. Bult, the expectant peer. An esteemed party man with strong opinions on the Niger districts and the Brazils, he spoke decisively of the South Seas and possessed the solid, suffused pinkness of a healthy Briton. Catherine, aware of the tacit agreement that he was a suitable husband for an heiress, found him thoroughly tiresome. Mr. Bult was amiably confident, unaware that his insensitivity to counterpoint was a flaw. He barely regarded Klesmer as a serious human being and dismissed Catherine's musical addiction as trivial as her interest in antique lace. Consequently, he was amazed by Klesmer's after-dinner outburst regarding the lack of idealism in English politics. To Klesmer, the crusades had at least the excuse of a banner of sentiment; otherwise, everyone rallied around the advertisement van of "Buy cheap, sell dear."

Klesmer's eloquence, gesticulatory and impassioned, burned like stray fireworks before sinking into silence. Mr. Bult, unsurprised by Klesmer's flighty opinions but astonished by his command of English idiom, approached him at the piano while Miss Arrowpoint stood nearby.

"I had no idea before that you were a political man," Bult said.

Klesmer folded his arms, pursed his lips, and stared.

"You must be used to public speaking. You speak uncommonly well, though I disagree. From what you said about sentiment, I fancy you are a Panslavist."

"No; my name is Elijah. I am the Wandering Jew," Klesmer replied, flashing a smile at Miss Arrowpoint before rushing backward and forward on the piano like a gust of wind. Bult found the buffoonery offensive and Polish, yet, with Catherine present, he did not move away.

"Herr Klesmer has cosmopolitan ideas," Catherine interjected, smoothing the situation. "He looks forward to a fusion of races."

"With all my heart," Bult said graciously. "I was sure he had too much talent to be a mere musician."

"Ah, sir, you are mistaken," Klesmer fired back. "No man has too much talent to be a musician. Most men have too little. A creative artist is no more a mere musician than a great statesman is a mere politician. We are not ingenious puppets living in a box, waiting for the world to gape for amusement. We help to rule nations and shape the age as much as any legislator. We count ourselves on level benches with them. And a man who speaks effectively through music is compelled to something more difficult than parliamentary eloquence."

With these words, Klesmer wheeled away from the piano.

Miss Arrowpoint blushed, and Mr. Bult observed with his usual phlegm, "Your pianist does not think small beer of himself."

"Herr Klesmer is something more than a pianist," Catherine apologized. "He is a great musician in the fullest sense. He will rank with Schubert and Mendelssohn."

"Ah, you ladies understand these things," Bult remarked, convinced that these matters were frivolous precisely because Klesmer had revealed himself as a coxcomb.

The next day, in the music-room, Catherine asked, "Why were you so heated last night with Mr. Bult? He meant no harm."

"You wish me to be complaisant to him?" Klesmer asked fiercely.

"I think it is hardly worth your while to be other than civil."

"You find no difficulty in tolerating him? You have a respect for a political platitudinarian as insensible as an ox to everything he cannot turn into capital. You think his monumental obtuseness suits the dignity of the English gentleman."

"I did not say that."

"You mean that I acted without dignity, and you are offended."

"Now you are slightly nearer the truth," Catherine smiled.

"Then I had better put my burial-clothes in my portmanteau and set off at once."

"I don't see that. If I have to bear your criticism of my operetta, you should not mind my criticism of your impatience."

"But I do mind it. You would have wished me to take his ignorant impertinence about a 'mere musician' without letting him know his place. I am to hear my gods blasphemed as well as myself insulted. But I beg pardon. It is impossible you should see the matter as I do. Even you can't understand the wrath of the artist: he is of another caste for you."

"That is true," Catherine admitted, betraying her feeling. "He is of a caste to which I look up—a caste above mine."

Klesmer, who had been reviewing scores, started up and walked to a distance. "That is finely felt—I am grateful. But I had better go, all the same. I have made up my mind to go, for good and all. You can get on exceedingly well without me: your operetta is on wheels—it will go of itself. And your Mr. Bult's company fits me 'wie die Faust ins Auge.' I am neglecting my engagements. I must go off to St. Petersburg."

There was no answer.

"You agree with me that I had better go?" Klesmer asked, irritated.

"Certainly; if that is what your business and feeling prompt. I have only to wonder that you have consented to give us so much of your time in the last year. There must be treble the interest to you anywhere else. I have never thought of you consenting to come here as anything else than a sacrifice."

"Why should I make the sacrifice?" Klesmer asked, sitting at the piano and playing a delicate echo of a melody he had set to Heine's "Ich hab' dich geliebet und liebe dich noch."

"That is the mystery," Catherine said, agitated, tearing a piece of paper into minute morsels as if performing a task imposed by a cruel fairy.

"You can conceive no motive?" Klesmer asked, folding his arms.

"None that seems in the least probable."

"Then I shall tell you. It is because you are to me the chief woman in the world—the throned lady whose colors I carry between my heart and my armor."

Catherine's hands trembled so violently she could no longer tear the paper, nor could her lips form a word. Klesmer continued, "This would be the last impertinence in me, if I meant to found anything upon it. That is out of the question. I meant no such thing. But you once said it was your doom to suspect every man who courted you of being an adventurer, and what made you angriest was men's imputing to you the folly of believing that they courted you for your own sake. Did you not say so?"

"Very likely," she murmured.

"It was a bitter word. Well, at least one man who has seen women as plenty as flowers in May has lingered about you for your own sake. And since he is one whom you can never marry, you will believe him. There is an argument in favor of some other man. But don't give yourself for a meal to a minotaur like Bult. I shall go now and pack. I shall make my excuses to Mrs. Arrowpoint."

As he rose to leave, Catherine suddenly made a desperate effort. "You must take this heap of manuscript." She had risen to fetch it from another table. Klesmer returned, and they stood facing each other across the length of the folio sheets.

"Why should I not marry the man who loves me, if I love him?" Catherine asked. To her, the effort felt like leaping from a ship's deck into a lifeboat.

"It would be too hard—impossible—you could not carry it through. I am not worth what you would have to encounter. I will not accept the sacrifice. It would be thought a *mésalliance* for you and I should be liable to the worst accusations."

"Is it the accusations you are afraid of? I am afraid of nothing but that we should miss the passing of our lives together."

The decisive word had been spoken. There was no doubt concerning the end willed by each; only the method remained. Catherine determined to take the straightest path. She went to her father and mother in the library and announced that she had promised to marry Klesmer.

Mrs. Arrowpoint's state of mind was pitiable. Imagine Jean-Jacques Rousseau, after writing his essay on the corrupting influence of the arts, waking among children of nature who offered him raw bones to grill with a flint knife; or Saint-Just, after denouncing all recognition of pre-eminence, receiving a vote of thanks for the unbroken mediocrity of his speech. Something similar befell the author of "Tasso" when what she had safely demanded of the dead Leonora was enacted by her own Catherine. It is hard to live up to our own eloquence while treading solid earth and liable to heavy dining. Besides, the proprieties of literature are not those of practical life. Mrs. Arrowpoint naturally wished for the best. She liked to feel herself at a higher level of literary sentiment than her associates and wished not to be behind them in social consideration. While Klesmer was seen as a patronized musician, his peculiarities were picturesque and acceptable; but to see him suddenly flash into the light of her son-in-law gave her a burning sense of what the world would say. Having represented Catherine as a model of excellence, she was now shattered.

Under the first shock, she forgot everything but her anger, snatching at any phrase that could serve as a weapon.

"If Klesmer has presumed to offer himself to you, your father shall horsewhip him off the premises. Pray, speak, Mr. Arrowpoint."

The father took his cigar from his mouth and rose to the occasion. "This will never do, Cath."
