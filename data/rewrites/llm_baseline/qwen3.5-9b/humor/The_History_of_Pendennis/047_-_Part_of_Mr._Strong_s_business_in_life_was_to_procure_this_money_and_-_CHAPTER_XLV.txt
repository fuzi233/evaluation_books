After the lavish entertainments at Grosvenor Place and Greenwich, Major Pendennis's devotion toward the Clavering family seemed to deepen daily. This worthy old gentleman, a fixture of London society, possessed the uncanny ability to gain entry into households where a woman of Lady Clavering's stature was expected. "Would Her Ladyship not delight in attending the grand gala at Gaunt House?" he would inquire eagerly. "There is a delightful breakfast ball at Viscount Marrowfat's in Fulham tomorrow. Everyone will be there—even august figures of the highest rank—and there will be a Watteau quadrille in which Miss Amory is certain to look ravishing." To these and countless other diversions, the obsequious elder kindly offered to escort Lady Clavering, while simultaneously positioning himself as a tireless utility to the Baronet.

Despite his current status and fortune, the world maintained a chilly demeanor toward Clavering. Suspicious rumors trailed him like shadows. He was blackballed from two clubs in quick succession. Within the House of Commons, he found solace only among a select group of the most disreputable members, possessing a rare talent for selecting bad company and adapting to it with natural ease, much as others adapt to the company of their superiors. To list every senator Clavering consorted with would be invidious; suffice it to name a few. There was Captain Raff, the Member for Epsom, who retired after the last Goodwood races under the guise of a mission to the Levant; there was Hustingson, the patriotic Member for Islington, whose voice ceased its denunciations of corruption upon becoming Governor of Coventry Island; and there was Bob Freeny of the Booterstown line, a marksman of note. Of all these gentlemen, with whom Mr. Hotspur was professionally compelled to converse, none inspired in the hot-headed Whip a deeper contempt than Sir Francis Clavering, the representative of an ancient lineage who had sat for their own borough time immemorial. "If that man is needed for a division," Hotspur grumbled, "ten to one he is lurking in a hellscape. Educated in the Fleet and still smelling of Newgate, he'll muddle away the Begum's fortune at thimble-rig, get caught pickpocketing, and finish his days on the hulks." Yet, if the high-born Hotspur could maintain civility for professional reasons, why should Major Pendennis lack his own motives for attending to this unluckily situated gentleman?

"He possesses a magnificent cellar and a superb cook," the Major observed. "So long as he remains silent, he is harmless, and he speaks very rarely. If he chooses to frequent gambling tables and lose his coin to blacklegs, what concern is it of mine? Do not pry too deeply into anyone's affairs, Pen, my boy. Every fellow has a cupboard in his house, begad, which he would prefer you and I not peek into. Why bother trying when the rest of the house is wide open to us? Besides, it is a devilishly good house, as you and I know. And if the master of the family falls short of perfection, the women are excellent. The Begum is not overly refined, but she is the kindest woman alive and remarkably clever. As for little Blanche, you know my opinion, you rogue; you know I believe she is sweet on you and would have you for the asking. But you are becoming such a great man, I suppose you won't settle for a Duke's daughter—hey, sir? I recommend you ask one of them and try."

Perhaps Pen was slightly intoxicated by his rising success in the world; perhaps, too, his uncle's perpetual hints encouraged the notion that Miss Amory was favorably disposed to renew the flirtation begun by the banks of the rural Brawl. However, he was currently disinclined toward marriage. Adopting some of his uncle's worldly cynicism, he spoke contemptuously of matrimony in favor of bachelorhood.

"You are very fortunate, sir," he remarked, "and you thrive splendidly alone, as do I. With a wife at my side, I would lose my footing in society; and I do not particularly fancy retreating to the countryside with a Mrs. Pendennis, nor dragging my wife into lodgings to be served by a servant-of-all-work. My period of youthful illusions is over. You cured me of my first love, who was certainly a fool and would have made a fool of a husband, and a very sulky, discontented one at that. We young fellows live fast, sir; and I feel as aged at twenty-five as many of the 'old fogies' I see in the bow-windows at Bays's. Do not take offense; I merely mean I am blasé regarding love matters. I could no more fan myself into a flame for Miss Amory now than I could adore Lady Mirabel again. I wish I could; I actually like old Mirabel for his infatuation, and consider his passion the most respectable aspect of his life."

"Sir Charles Mirabel was always a theatrical man, sir," the Major snapped, annoyed that his nephew should speak lightly of someone of Sir Charles's rank. "He has been immersed in theatricals since his early days. He acted at Carlton House when he was Page to the Prince; he has been involved in that sort of thing. He could afford to marry whomever he chose, and Lady Mirabel is a most respectable woman, received everywhere—*everywhere*, mind. The Duchess of Connaught receives her, Lady Rockminster receives her—it does not become young fellows to speak lightly of persons in that station. There is no more respectable woman in England than Lady Mirabel. Those 'old fogies,' as you call them, at Bays's, are some of the first gentlemen in England, of whom you youngsters had best learn a little manners, a little breeding, and a little modesty." The Major began to suspect that Pen was growing exceedingly pert and conceited, and that the world was making far too much of him.

The Major's anger only amused Pen. He studied his uncle's peculiarities with constant relish, remaining perpetually in good humor with his worldly mentor. "I am a youngster of fifteen years' standing, sir," he replied adroitly. "If you deem us disrespectful, you should observe the present generation. A protégé of yours came to breakfast with me the other day. You instructed me to ask him, and I did so to please you. We spent the day sightseeing, dined at the club, and attended the play. He remarked that the wine at the Polyanthus was inferior to Ellis's at Richmond, smoked Warrington's Cavendish after breakfast, and when I presented him with a sovereign as a farewell token, he said he had plenty but would accept it to demonstrate he wasn't proud."

"Did he? Did you ask young Clavering?" cried the Major, instantly appeased—"Fine boy, rather wild, but a fine boy. Parents appreciate that sort of attention, and you cannot do better than pay it to our worthy friends of Grosvenor Place. So you took him to the play and tipped him? That was right, sir, that was right!" With this verdict, the Mentor dismissed Telemachus, convinced the young men were not so terrible after all and that he could make something of that fellow eventually.

As Master Clavering matured in years and stature, he became too robust for the authority of his fond parents and governess, governing them rather than allowing himself to be led. With his father, he was silent and sullen, rarely appearing near the gentleman; with his mother, he roared and fought whenever contests arose regarding the gratification of his appetite or other desires; and in disputes with his governess over books, he kicked her shins so fiercely that she was entirely overpowered. He would have treated his sister Blanche similarly, attempting to dominate her on one or two occasions, but she displayed immense resolution and spirit, boxing his ears so soundly that he refrained from molesting Miss Amory, as he did the governess, his mother, and his mother's maid.

Eventually, when the family arrived in London, Sir Francis declared, "The little beggar had best be sent to school." Thus, the young son and heir of the house of Clavering was dispatched to the Reverend Otto Rose's establishment at Twickenham, where young noblemen and gentlemen were prepared for admission to the great English public schools.

It is not our intention to chronicle Master Clavering's scholastic career; the paths to the Temple of Learning were paved easier for him than for many of our predecessors. He advanced toward that fane in a carriage-and-four, halting for refreshments whenever he pleased. From the earliest days of youth, he wore varnished boots, cambric handkerchiefs, and lemon-colored kid gloves of the smallest size ever manufactured by Privat. At Mr. Rose's, the young gentlemen dressed formally for dinner, donned shawl dressing-gowns, enjoyed fires in their bedrooms, exercised with horses and carriages occasionally, and had oil applied to their hair. Corporal punishment was entirely abolished by the Principal, who believed moral discipline sufficed to guide youth. Consequently, the boys were so rapidly advanced in various branches of learning that they acquired the art of drinking spirits and smoking cigars before reaching the age for public schools. Young Frank Clavering stole his father's Havannahs, conveying them to school or smoking them in the stables at a surprisingly early age, and at ten years old drank champagne almost as stoutly as any whiskered cornet of dragoons.
