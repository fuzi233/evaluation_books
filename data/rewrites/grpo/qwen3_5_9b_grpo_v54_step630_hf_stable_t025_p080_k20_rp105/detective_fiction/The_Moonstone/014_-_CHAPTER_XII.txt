Thursday night slipped by in silence, unbroken by incident. But Friday dawned with two distinct shocks to the household. First, the baker's boy reported seeing Rosanna Spearman the previous afternoon, veiled heavily, walking toward Frizinghall across the moor. It was a baffling error; Rosanna's broad shoulders were unmistakable, and she had been confined to her sickbed all Thursday. The boy must have been mistaken. Second, the postman delivered news of Dr. Candy. Despite his waterproof coat, the rain had seeped through, bringing on a fever. He was now delirious, babbling nonsense in his high state. While we pitied the little doctor, Mr. Franklin seemed chiefly concerned for Miss Rachel's welfare, fearing that without the Moonstone's fate soon revealed, she desperately needed the finest medical attention available.

Before breakfast concluded, a telegram arrived from Mr. Blake, the elder. He had secured the aid of the Commissioner to find the right man for us: Sergeant Cuff, arriving by the morning train from London. The name caused Mr. Franklin to start; he recalled strange anecdotes about the detective from his father's lawyer. "I begin to hope we are seeing the end of our anxieties," he declared. "If half the stories are true, there is no equal in England to Sergeant Cuff!"

Excitement mounted as the hour approached. Superintendent Seegrave retreated to a room to draft his report, eager to hand it over. I wished to fetch the Sergeant myself, but Lady Verinder's carriage was unavailable, and the pony-chaise was reserved for Mr. Godfrey, who delayed his departure until the last train to hear the London officer's thoughts, though he must return to town by Friday night for a charity consultation.

When the time came, I stood at the gate. A fly pulled up, and out stepped a grizzled, elderly man so lean he seemed to have no flesh on his bones. Dressed in somber black with a white cravat, his face was sharp as a hatchet, his skin yellow and withered like an autumn leaf. His steely-grey eyes possessed a disconcerting habit of expecting more from you than you offered. His walk was soft, his voice melancholy, his long fingers hooked like claws. He looked less like a police officer and more like a parson or an undertaker—a stark, unsettling contrast to the bustling Superintendent Seegrave.

"Is this Lady Verinder's?" he asked.
"Yes, sir."
"I am Sergeant Cuff."

As we walked toward the house, I introduced myself to establish trust. Yet, Cuff said not a word of business. Instead, he admired the grounds, remarking on the brisk sea air. I privately wondered how such a man had built his reputation. Upon reaching the house, he asked for my lady, who was in a conservatory. While we waited, Cuff wandered into the rosery, displaying a surprising, almost childish fascination with the flowers.

"Ah, you've got the right exposure here," he said, stroking a white musk rose as if it were a child. "Gravel walks are too hard for them. Grass, Mr. Gardener—grass between your roses."

"You seem fond of roses, Sergeant," I ventured.
"I haven't much time for anything," he replied, his voice softening. "But when I do have a moment, the roses get it. I began my life among them, and I shall end it there, if I can. One day I shall retire from catching thieves and try my hand at growing roses. There will be grass walks between my beds."

"It seems an odd taste for a man in your line," I noted.
"If you look about you," Cuff countered, "you'll see a man's tastes are often the opposite of his business. Show me two things more opposite than a rose and a thief. I'll correct my tastes accordingly."

Just then, Lady Verinder appeared. Her presence seemed to unsettle her, causing a rare embarrassment. Cuff, however, immediately put her at ease. He asked if anyone else had been consulted regarding the robbery and, upon learning that Superintendent Seegrave was present, requested to speak with him first.

The two officers retired together. When they emerged, Seegrave was agitated, while Cuff was yawning.
"The Sergeant wishes to see Miss Verinder's sitting-room," Seegrave announced pompously. "Attend the Sergeant!"

I led the way upstairs. Cuff moved softly through the Indian cabinet and the boudoir, asking questions that drifted between the two of us, largely unintelligible. Finally, he stopped before the decorative painting on the door, laying a lean finger on the small smear beneath the lock that Seegrave had previously blamed on the servants' petticoats.

"That's a pity," Cuff murmured. "How did it happen?"
He asked me. I explained that the servants had crowded in and that Seegrave had ordered them out before further damage occurred.
"Right!" Seegrave interjected militantly. "The petticoats did it."
"Did you notice which petticoat?" Cuff asked, ignoring the superintendent and addressing me.
"No, sir."
"You noticed, I suppose?" Cuff turned to Seegrave.
"I can't charge my memory, Sergeant," Seegrave stammered, dismissing it as a mere trifle.

Cuff's expression darkened. "I made a private inquiry last week, Mr. Superintendent. At one end a murder, at the other a spot of ink on a tablecloth. In all my experience of this dirty world, I have never met a 'trifle.' Before we proceed, we must see the petticoat and know for certain when the paint was wet."

Seegrave bristled, offering to summon the women. Cuff sighed and shook his head. "No. Let us take the paint first. It is a question of Yes or No. The petticoats are a question of length. What o'clock was it when the servants were in here? Eleven. Is there anyone who knows if the paint was wet or dry then?"
"Her ladyship's nephew, Mr. Franklin Blake, knows," I said.

Franklin entered promptly. "That door was painted by Miss Verinder under my inspection. The vehicle I used dries whatever color is applied within twelve hours."
"Do you remember when the smeared bit was done?"
"Perfectly," Franklin answered. "It was the last piece finished on Wednesday afternoon, around three o'clock."
"Today is Friday," Cuff noted. "Three o'clock Wednesday plus twelve hours is three o'clock Thursday morning. At eleven Thursday morning, you held your inquiry. Take three from eleven... the paint had been dry for eight hours."

It was a devastating blow to Seegrave. From that moment, Cuff dismissed his colleague as incompetent and turned his attention to Franklin.
"It is quite on the cards, sir," Cuff said, "that you have put the clue into our hands."

Suddenly, the bedroom door opened and Miss Rachel emerged. She addressed Cuff directly, ignoring his strangeness.
"Did you say," she pointed at Franklin, "that *he* had put the clue into your hands?"
"This is Miss Verinder," I whispered to Cuff.
"That gentleman, miss," Cuff replied, his steely eyes studying her face intently, "has possibly put the clue into our hands."

She glanced at Franklin, then quickly looked away. A strange disturbance clouded her mind; she flushed, then paled, a new, startling look entering her eyes.
"Having answered your question, miss," Cuff continued, "I beg leave to inquire. There is a smear on your door. Do you know when it was done, or who did it?"
Instead of answering, she asked, "Are you another police-officer?"
"I am Sergeant Cuff, miss, of the Detective Police."
"Do you think a young lady's advice worth having?"
"I shall be glad to hear it, miss."
"Do your duty by yourself—and don't allow Mr. Franklin Blake to help you!"

She spoke with such savage ill-will toward Franklin that, despite knowing her since infancy and loving her next to my lady, I felt ashamed of her. Cuff's gaze remained immovable. "Thank you, miss. Do you happen to know anything about the smear? Might you have done it by accident?"
"I know nothing about the smear."
Turning away, she retreated to her room, where I heard her burst into tears.

I looked at Franklin, who appeared more distressed than I. "I told you I was uneasy about her," he said. "And now you see why."
Cuff remarked casually, "Miss Verinder appears to be a little out of temper about the loss of her Diamond. It's a valuable jewel. Natural enough!"
Here was the excuse I had made for her before Seegrave, now repeated by a stranger. A cold shudder ran through me. I realized then that Cuff had seen something in Miss Rachel that cast a new, horrid light on the case.

"A young lady's tongue is a privileged member, sir," Cuff told Franklin. "Let us forget what has passed. Thanks to you, we know when the paint was dry. The next thing is to discover when the paint was last seen without that smear. *You* have a head on your shoulders—you understand what I mean."
Franklin composed himself. "The more we narrow the question of time, the more we narrow the field of inquiry."
"Did you notice your work here after you finished it on Wednesday?"
"I can't say I did."
"Did *you*?" Cuff turned to me.
"I can't say I did either, sir."
"Who was the last person in the room the last thing on Wednesday night?"
"Miss Rachel, I suppose."
Franklin corrected him gently, "Or possibly your daughter, Betteredge."

Cuff took me aside to the window. "Your Superintendent has set the servants' backs up. It is vital to smooth them down. Tell your daughter and the others: First, I have no evidence the Diamond has been stolen, only that it is lost. Second, my business is simply to ask them to help me find it."
I relayed the message, adding a third point: that they were free to move about the house. This calmed the household instantly.

Cuff approved of Penelope. Her evidence was given prettily: she had helped mix the colors, noticed the unfinished spot under the lock, and confirmed she had left the room at midnight without a smear. She held up her new dress, a gift from Miss Rachel, and swore there was no stain. Her father verified the dress. No trace of paint was found.

Next, Cuff questioned about large dogs, ruling them out. He then produced a magnifying glass. The smear showed no skin marks, only signs of a loose article of clothing brushing against the wet paint. Combining Penelope's testimony with Franklin's timeline, the culprit must have been in the room between midnight and three o'clock on Thursday morning.

Summarizing for Seegrave, Cuff outlined three discoveries needed: identify the stained garment, find its owner, and determine how that person accounted for being in the room during those specific hours. "If the person can't satisfy you, you haven't far to look for the hand that has got the Diamond," Cuff declared, dismissing Seegrave to his regular duties.

Seegrave, humiliated, retorted that Cuff was making a mountain out of a molehill.
"There is also such a thing as making nothing out of a molehill, in consequence of your head being too high to see it," Cuff replied coolly.

Left alone, Cuff stood by the window, whistling softly "The Last Rose of Summer"—a melancholy tune that suited his character and his obsession with roses. Turning from the window, he stared at Miss Rachel's bedroom door, nodded to himself, and requested ten minutes with my mistress. As I left, Franklin asked, "Can you guess yet who has stolen the Diamond?"
Cuff answered simply, "Nobody has stolen the Diamond."
We both stared, begging for explanation.
"Wait a little," Cuff said. "The pieces of the puzzle are not all put together yet."
