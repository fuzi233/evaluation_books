The sharp, rude thud against the cottage back door hit the hush of the storm-wracked night like a prearranged signal. They were trapped, hemmed in on all sides. At the alarming sound, Margaret’s rigid panic shattered long enough for her to grab Gerard’s arm, her voice a frantic, breathless whisper against his ear: “Tell them he was here, but already gone.” She all but dragged him up the rough, splintered wooden steps leading to her father Peter’s garret sleeping chamber, her own small bedroom just a little further down the narrow upper landing.

The blows on the lower door came again, harder, rattling the oaken planks in their frames.
“Who knocks at this ungodly hour?” Martin Wittenhaagen called, from his seat by the peat fire.
“Open the door, and you’ll see well enough!” the voice boomed back.
“I do not throw my door open to thieves. All honest folk are abed and asleep at this hour.”
“Open in the name of the law, Martin Wittenhaagen, or you’ll rue your stubbornness before dawn.”
Martin grunted, recognition pricking through his feigned confusion. “Why, if that isn’t Dirk Brower’s voice all the way from Tergou! What brings you tramping so far out of your jurisdiction on a night fit to drown a duck?”
“Open the door, and I’ll explain all your questions soon enough.”

Martin drew back the bolt slow as molasses, and Dierich Brower burst through the threshold with four hard-faced men at his heels. They waved their waiting companion in from the back path to join them.
“Right then, Martin. Where is Gerard Eliassoen?” Dierich demanded, planting his boots on the packed earthen floor by the fire.
“Gerard Eliassoen? Why, he was here not half an hour ago!” Martin said, feigning wide, innocent surprise.
“Was here?” Dierich’s face fell. “And where has he run off to now?”
“Folks say he’s bound for Italy. What’s all the ruckus about, man?”
“Don’t play the fool with me. You’ll not make me believe he tramped off through that lashing gale, out in the pitch dark.”

Martin huffed, feigning scorn at the whole commotion, lit a tallow candle, and settled himself coolly back by the hearth, winding fine silk thread around the nicked section of his bowstring where arrow fletchings wore thin. “I’ll tell you the whole truth, then. You know that rascal of his brother Giles? The little misbegotten imp, all head and gangling arms? He came tearing over here on a stolen mule, yelling at the top of his lungs the whole ride. I was out by the byre and too far off to make out his squawking, but there was no mistaking he was raising a terrible ruckus. No sooner had he galloped off than there was all this crying and clinging, and off Gerard went. The girls round here say he’s heading for Italy—mayhap you’ve a better idea where that far-off country lies than I do.”

Dierich’s face darkened further and further the longer Martin spoke, and he could find no flaw in the tale. A cleverer, more cunning man might have overplayed his hand, added extra lies that would rouse suspicion, but Martin stuck strictly to the single falsehood he and the others had agreed on, inventing no extra tales of his own.
“Mates,” Dierich said, turning to his men, “I think he’s telling the truth. I warned the burgomaster this is exactly how it would go. I saw that dwarf Giles galloping past on Peter Buyskens’s mule from Sevenbergen an hour back, and I said straight off, ‘That little imp’s been sent to warn Gerard, so Gerard’s at Sevenbergen.’ But did they listen? No! They wasted half the night rummaging every ratty corner of Tergou hunting for those cursed parchments, and forgot all about Sevenbergen till that imp had time to give the whole game away. Gerard’s leagues away by now, long gone before we even set foot on this path. That chalk-faced girl Margaret’s outwitted all of us bearded men, and I told the burgomaster we’d get nothing for this errand but wet jerkins and cold shoulders.” Martin grinned slow and smug right in Dierich’s face.

“Still,” Dierich added, sighing, “to satisfy the old burgomaster when we get back, we’ll search the whole house top to bottom.”
Martin’s grin vanished instantly, his face turning grave. Dierich spotted the shift in his expression at once, and paused to think. “Two of you, stand guard outside, one on each side of the cottage. No one jumps out an upper window and slips past us. The rest, come on with me.” He snatched the tallow candle from the table and started up the stairs, three of his men tramping heavy at his heels.

Left alone at the foot of the stair, Martin’s broad soldier’s shoulders slumped. Everything had gone so perfectly up till that moment, and now this fatal, terrible turn. Then a wild idea struck him—all was not lost yet. Gerard was hiding in one of the two upper chambers, not far off the ground. He’d leap for the window the second the men found him, Dierich had posted only one man down below to catch him. For half a minute, that would make Martin and Gerard two against one, and in that small window of time, anything could happen.
He propped the back door ajar and waited, watching. The light of the candle glowed bright in Peter’s bedroom. “Curse the fool,” Martin growled under his breath, “is he going to let them march him off like a frightened milkmaid?”
The light moved past the landing and into Margaret’s bedroom, and still no window sash was thrown open. Martin saw at once Gerard would never have waited till the men were in the room to make his escape out the glass. He’d begun to creep to the foot of the stair, half convinced Gerard had already slipped out a window while all the law men were crowded inside the lower cottage—until a faint, trembling cry drifted down from Margaret’s bedroom.
“They’ve got him,” Martin groaned, “they’ve caught him for sure.”

The thought flashed through his mind like a lightning bolt: if they dragged Gerard off to Tergou, his life wasn’t worth a brass farthing. And if Gerard was killed, Margaret’s heart would break right in her chest. He glanced wildly around the little room, like a cornered wild boar hunting a way out, and in an instant settled on a resolution as fierce and unyielding as the iron men of his day, a soldier driven straight to bay. He stepped to the front door, then the back, mimicked Dierich Brower’s sharp, commanding voice perfectly, and called “Watch the windows, don’t leave your posts!” Then he quietly slid the bolts home on both doors, locking the four law men inside the cottage with him.
He grabbed his old war bow and six arrows, nocked one tight to the string, slipped the rest in his quiver. He set his hunting knife on the chair behind him, hilt turned outward for easy grabbing, and settled himself at the foot of the stairs, calm as stone, determined to kill all four men before they could take Gerard, or die trying. He knew he could put two arrows through their chests before they could reach him, then he and Gerard would fight the remaining two hand to hand. He’d seen men break and run in sudden, unexpected ambushes. If Brower and his men hesitated even a single heartbeat before rushing him, he could bring down three with his arrows, and the fight would be on his side.

He didn’t have long to wait. Heavy boots thudded down the upper landing, coming closer and closer. The glow of the tallow candle drew near, and the murmur of loud voices. Martin’s stout soldier’s heart thudded hard against his ribs, thinking he was about to send four men to their graves on no more than the chance to save his friend. But this same man who’d trembled at the sight of Giles prancing in a lanthorn mask never wavered for a second in that awful, breathless moment. He stood braced, eye glowing bright, ready to take lives and lose his own if need be. It was a desperate game: win, and he’d be exiled for life instantly. Lose, and he’d drop dead on that very earthen floor before the hour was out.

Upstairs, Dierich and his men had first stumbled into Peter’s room while the old man was deep in his first sleep. They rummaged through every cupboard, ran their daggers through the stuffed alligator pelt Peter had nailed to his wall as a curio, poked under the bed, but the large room, full of crannies and nooks, held no sign of Gerard.
They moved on to Margaret’s bedroom, and the sight of it gave them pause: small, bare, not a single cupboard in sight, nothing to hide a full grown man. Only a wide fireplace and tall stone chimney. Dierich’s eyes locked on the chimney straight away. There lay Margaret, the beauty of Sevenbergen, pretending to sleep on a low old oak chest bed no more than a foot off the ground, her linen sheets snow white, her shift peeking out equally bright, looking for all the world like a pure white lily that had fallen into the muddy rut of their rough, uninvited intrusion.
She woke after a moment, sat up in the bed like a woman dazed from a deep dream, spotted the four armed men looming over her, and began to scream soft, trembling pleas for mercy. Her distress shamed Dierich Brower right out of his bluster.
“Easy now, lass, we’re not here to hurt you,” he said, flustered. “Lie still, shut those pretty eyes, and dream of your wedding night while I poke up this chimney to see if Master Gerard is hiding there.”
“Gerard? In my bedroom?” Margaret cried, her voice rising. “Cruel men! You all know full well they drove him away from me, drove him out of his own native land! This is nothing but a lie. You’re thieves, you’re wicked brutes, you’re no men of Sevenbergen, or you’d know Margaret Brandt better than to dare look for her lover in this room of all places in the world. Oh, brave, brave thing! Four great hulking armed men tramping in at midnight to insult one poor honest girl! The women in your own houses must be good for nothing, or you’d respect your own wives and daughters too much to dare shame a girl of good name like this.”
“Right, come away, lads, before she gives us an earful we’ll never live down,” Dierich said, hastily beating a retreat from the bedroom. “He’s not hiding in the chimney, that’s plain. You can plaster over a crack a cudgel makes easy, but a woman’s sharp tongue is a double-edged dagger, and a girl that young still has her mother’s milk in her bones, and she’ll carve you to shreds before you know what hit you. I told the burgomaster this whole fool’s errand would come to nothing.”
