The stormy winds had finally stilled. Golden sunlight pierced the scattered clouds, and the rains ceased—spring had arrived. Stepping out of our winter shelter, we breathed in the crisp, fragrant air, our spirits lifting at the sight of fresh greenery and the cheerful songs of birds. Nature had renewed itself, and so had we.

Our crops flourished, the seeds we had sown now sprouting vigorously. But our first task was to repair Falconhurst, our treehouse, which had been battered by the winds. We patched the torn canopy and reinforced the structure, making it habitable once more. My wife, eager to begin spinning, soon had flax drying in an oven I built, while little Franz helped reel the thread she spun.

Yet Falconhurst was not our only concern. Tentholm, our storage site, had suffered worse. The tent lay in ruins, provisions were soaked, and two barrels of gunpowder were ruined. The pinnace remained intact, but our trusty tub-boat was shattered. The losses convinced me—we needed a sturdier refuge before the next rains.

Fritz suggested carving a cave into the rock. Though the idea seemed daunting, I agreed. Even if we couldn’t create a full living space, we could at least secure a cellar for our most vital supplies. Armed with tools, we set to work, chiseling away at the cliffside. Days of labor yielded little progress, but we pressed on, determined.

Then, on the tenth day, Jack’s excited cry rang out: *"Father! My crowbar went right through the rock!"* We rushed to his side, testing the hole with poles. To our astonishment, only a thin wall separated us from a vast cavern. But as I leaned in, a rush of foul air sent me staggering back—stagnant air, deadly to breathe.

To purify it, we launched signal rockets from the wreck into the cavern. Their fiery arcs illuminated a breathtaking sight—a crystal grotto, its walls glittering like diamonds under the torchlight. Stalactites shimmered overhead, and the sandy floor was bone-dry, perfect for habitation.

More astonishing still—the crystals were salt. Pure, endless rock salt. Our discovery was beyond anything we’d imagined. But danger lurked; some crystals had fallen, likely shaken loose by our rockets. To test the stability, we fired guns near the entrance. When no more fell, we knew our new home was safe.

Falconhurst, once our pride, now paled in comparison. We designated it as a summer retreat, while the cave—*Rock House*—would be our winter fortress. We set to work, carving windows, fitting doors, and dividing the space into living quarters, a kitchen, stables, and storage.

Meanwhile, Tentholm offered its own bounty. Turtles hauled themselves ashore to lay eggs, and we captured them, keeping some alive for fresh meat. Then came the herrings—a massive shoal churning the sea into silver. We harvested them by the barrel, salting and smoking the catch. Seals followed, drawn by the scraps, and we took them for their oil and hides.

But the greatest surprise came later—a surge of sturgeon and salmon, so thick in the bay that Jack could spear them with arrows. We hauled in giants, preserving their roe as caviar and rendering their bladders into isinglass for makeshift windowpanes.

Our gardens thrived untended, yielding crops year-round. At Falconhurst, the fields my wife had secretly sown now waved with grain. Even the maize plantation flourished—though it also drew flocks of thieving birds, which Fritz and Jack hunted with eagle and jackal.

Eager to expand, we set out to establish a farmstead beyond Falconhurst. Crossing a plain, we stumbled upon a field of cotton bushes, their white fluff like snow. Delighted, we gathered sacks full before pressing on to a lush hillside—perfect for our new outpost.

There, amidst cinnamon-laden trees and terebinth bark that promised resin for varnish, we built a sturdy shelter. As night fell, we rested on beds of cotton, our minds alight with plans. The wilderness was no longer just our refuge—it was becoming our kingdom.
