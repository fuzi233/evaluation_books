Our readers have already heard Sir Francis Clavering’s brutally unvarnished hot take on the wife who dumped her entire fortune on him to drag him home from abroad and install him as a big shot in his native shire, and the man was not even slightly off. Lady Clavering was never going to win any awards for being the sharpest or most erudite lady in the county. She’d notched exactly two years of formal education in a London suburb she swore was called “Ackney” until her dying breath, before being dragged off at 15 to rejoin her wealthy dad in Calcutta.

On that voyage out aboard the *Ramchunder* East Indiaman, under command of grumpy, no-nonsense Captain Bragg, she met her first husband Mr. Amory: third mate, professional troublemaker, and all-around chaos gremlin. We won’t drag you through every boring detail of her early life, but suffice it to say Captain Bragg got so fed up with the mate’s nonsense he locked him in irons, dumped him at the Cape of Good Hope, and delivered his ward safely to her Calcutta dad after a voyage so stormy half the cargo nearly ended up at the bottom of the Indian Ocean. Amory, ever the resilient scammer, worked his way back to Calcutta as a common deckhand, married the rich attorney’s daughter over her dad’s loud protests, and then proceeded to fail at literally every business he touched: indigo planter? Broke. Import agent? Broke. Editor of the *Sunderbund Pilot*? So broke the paper only ran for three issues before creditors seized the printing press. He fought nonstop with his wife and father-in-law the entire time, until one last very illegal bit of forgery meant he had to hightail it to New South Wales faster than you can say “debts owing.”

At that point, the now very miserable Mrs. Amory grabbed her four-year-old daughter Blanche (called Betsy back then) and her loyal maid Mrs. Bonner (the same lady you met last chapter, now Lady Clavering’s head confidante) and hightailed it back to Europe. She moved into a house near grumpy Captain Bragg in Pocklington Street, who was so awkward about the whole Amory mess he acted like she carried the plague, and she quickly decided the whole of London was whispering about her husband’s scandals (they weren’t) and the King, Queen, and entire East India Company board were keeping a file on her (they absolutely weren’t). So she bolted for the continent, and proceeded to have the time of her life.

She gallivanted through every major European city, paid her boarding house bills months in advance, turned every picnic she attended into a feast with three times more champagne and cold ham than anyone else brought, made curries and pillaws for every boarding house guest, gave away every spare play ticket and masked ball pass she could get her hands on, and bailed out every broke, mustachioed aristocrat and stranded dowager whose remittance was late. No one cared that she said “Ackney” instead of Hackney or dropped half her H’s – she was the most popular guest in every boarding house in Brussels, Paris, Milan, Naples, and Rome. It was in Rome that she got the news Amory had finally kicked the bucket, at the exact same time deadbeat Captain Clavering and his equally broke buddy the Chevalier Strong were stuck in the city unable to pay their hotel bill. The good-natured widow married the fancy “ancient baronet heir” without shedding a single tear for her dead scapegrace first husband, and soon found herself mistress of Clavering Park.

Little Blanche tagged along for every single one of her mum’s European adventures, so she learned a whole lot more about life by 14 than most girls her age. She had a governess, a stint at Madame de Caramel’s fancy Paris boarding school in the Champs Elysees, and then the family moved back to England – at which point her whole world turned upside down. Granddad died, little baby Frank was born, and suddenly everyone in the Clavering household acted like the squawking toddler who spilled his dinner on every available surface was the greatest heir the British aristocracy had ever produced. If it hadn’t been for him, Blanche would’ve been the heiress to the whole estate! She didn’t care much about money, but it was still pretty obvious that her stepdad was a dull dimwit who only cared about smoking his cigars, her mum was way less “refined” than the other posh ladies in the county, and little Frank was a spoiled monster who kept ruining her nice dresses. Naturally, none of these three people could possibly understand her deep poetic soul, right? So she went looking for someone to dump all her dramatic unspent affection on.

Unsurprisingly, Lady Clavering and Sir Francis were just as eager to get Blanche married off as she was to find a suitable crush – Sir Francis had even weirdly daydreamed that Mrs. Strong would conveniently drop dead so he could marry Blanche off to Chevalier Strong, but when that didn’t look likely, any nice smart young man with a pulse was welcome to try for her hand. Our own pal Arthur Pendennis, who checked every box of handsome and educated, could’ve sauntered up to the house, proposed, and been greeted with open arms before he even finished his sentence. But Pen was being a total dork about it: he was mortified by his recent failures, his empty bank account, and the fact that his stupid romantic follies had left his mum scrimping at Fairoaks to make ends meet. There was equal parts vanity and guilt holding him back – how could he, the broke loser who couldn’t even make rent, possibly end up with a dazzling creature like Blanche Amory who lived in a mansion with a dozen footmen? So he just moped around, half-heartedly daydreaming about romance but too shy to actually do anything about it, the way so many silly young men do.

Pen’s mum Helen, though? She’d already seen right through Blanche, no effort required. The poor widow had watched enough of her son’s romantic nonsense to spot a frivolous, self-absorbed girl a mile off, and she was convinced Blanche was full of worldliness and selfishness hidden behind pretty verses and sweet lines, with no respect for her parents or anything even remotely sacred. At first Pen and Laura argued with her, Pen laughing off her objections and saying “Pshaw mum, all women are jealous over Laura, that’s all this is!” and Laura being completely starstruck by her new fancy poet friend. But the more Helen watched the two of them traipsing between Fairoaks and Clavering Park every single day, the more her heart sank – her secret dearest wish was that Pen and Laura would end up married, the two kids she loved more than anything in the world, and she was terrified Blanche would snatch Pen right out from under her. One day, she broke down and told him so, tearful, saying she was sure she didn’t have long left to live and all she wanted was to see the two of them settled together before she went.

Pen gave her that big patronising soothing hug he was so good at, and got a little smug jolt of self-satisfaction when he realised: wait a minute, I could have Laura? The sweet, kind, honest girl I’ve known my whole life, who’s always been there? When she walked in from the garden a minute later, cheeks flushed, holding a basket of roses to give to Helen, Pen looked at her and thought, wow, that really is a prize I could just ask for. For a second he felt all giddy and triumphant, the idiot.

But it didn’t take Laura more than a few weeks to realise Helen was 100% right about Blanche. The “Muse” spent all their private time complaining about how no one at home understood her, how embarrassing her mum’s Ackney accent was, how dim Sir Francis was, how her horrible little baby brother had stolen all her mother’s love. But none of that explained the time little Frank spilled a glass of water on her drawing, and she boxed his ears so hard he screamed, while yelling very rude insults at him in both English and French. Or how she ordered his governess around like a personal servant, demanding she run across the house to fetch her books or handkerchief any time she couldn’t be bothered to stand up. Laura, who always thanked every servant and helper who did anything for her, was horrified. The final straw was when Blanche read her this absurdly weepy original poem about how she’d love her baby brother forever, no matter what, how she’d cling to his feet if he ever pushed her away – and then 10 minutes later was the one shoving him and boxing his ears, not the other way around. That was Laura’s first big life lesson: poets don’t always practice what they preach, not unlike that silly boy Pen, who was also prone to grand dramatic speeches he didn’t follow through on. But Pen was a man! That was different! He had a good heart under all his nonsense! Blanche was just… faking all the sentiment.

Laura’s former gushy admiration for her new friend quickly curdled into quiet, amused tolerance, which Blanche hated. No one likes getting found out to be a fraud, especially when they’ve been acting like they’re too good for everyone around them. The situation finally exploded one terrible afternoon, when Blanche was in a truly foul mood: she’d been rude to her mum, yelled at little Frank, been horrid to his governess, and then drove her own poor maid Pincott out of the room in tears for no reason at all. Laura, who’d been watching this all build up, absolutely snapped. She gave Blanche a full on loud, indignant dressing down, telling her it was positively wicked to go around torturing everyone weaker than her just to make herself feel big, then grabbed her bonnet and ran all the way home across the park, leaving Blanche gawping.

When she got back to Fairoaks, she told Helen the whole story, and declared she was done being Blanche’s friend forever. “Oh mamma, you were right!” she said. “She seems so soft and sweet, but she’s all selfishness through and through. Anyone who talks constantly about how full of love they are and treats their servants and family like that can’t possibly have a real heart.” Helen was absolutely over the moon, secretly – that meant Blanche couldn’t possibly marry Pen now, right? He’d see how horrible she was any day now, and turn back to nice, steady Laura.

But Laura didn’t even admit to herself the real first crack in the friendship that day had happened long before Blanche started bullying her maid. The two of them had been sitting in Blanche’s bedroom, and Blanche had very slyly brought up Pen, making snide little comments about his old romance with that actress. When Laura jumped to his defence, sputtering that Pen had acted perfectly honorably the whole time, Blanche decided to mess with her even more. She ran to her fancy mother-of-pearl desk, pulled out a handful of crumpled, slightly green-stained love poems Pen had written her, all about Naiads and rivers and Undine, and showed them to Laura like they were a huge top secret. Laura stared at them for ages, barely able to see the words through the funny blur in her eyes. Blanche acted all coy, insisting she’d never dreamed of writing a reply. Then she sat down at the piano and belted out a few flashy Rossini arias, while Laura sat there in silence, barely listening. They went down to lunch with their arms around each other’s waists ten minutes later, Laura even giving her a sisterly kiss on the cheek, but the damage was done. By the time Blanche started her reign of terror over the rest of the household later that afternoon, the friendship was already well and truly dead.
