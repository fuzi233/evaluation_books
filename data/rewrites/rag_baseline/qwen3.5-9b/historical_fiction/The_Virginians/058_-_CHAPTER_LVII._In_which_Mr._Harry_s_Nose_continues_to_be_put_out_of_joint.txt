The air in the drawing room was thick with the scent of stale tobacco and the dust of settled grievances. Madame de Bernstein sat, her posture rigid, watching the aftermath of a battle fought not with swords, but with silence and withheld glances. The game was over. The pieces were reset. Yet, the board remained scarred.

George Warrington entered, bringing with him the weight of a victory that tasted of ash. He spoke to his aunt of the interview with Lady Maria, detailing the transaction with a casual brutality that suited the man. He had seen the move clearly: the sacrifice of Harry, the acceptance of the lesser prize, the preservation of the family line through the elder son. It was logic stripped of emotion, a clean equation where Harry was the variable eliminated to balance the sum.

"My sister," George said, his voice smooth, devoid of tremor, "has secured her future. She will not waste another moment on a dream that cannot feed itself. I stood by my blood, as duty demands."

Madame de Bernstein nodded, her expression unreadable. "Your candour is... adequate."

"Sans rancune," George replied, offering a bow that was more of a dismissal than a greeting. "We lost the wager, but the stakes remain ours."

They dissected the scene with the clinical detachment of surgeons examining a corpse. The visit to the spunging-house, the desperate offering of gifts, the tearful farewell—it was all analyzed as a tactical error, a necessary concession to circumstance. "Had George not appeared," Eugene mused, taking a pinch of snuff as if to dry the sweat of the conversation, "we might have held the field longer. But nature abhors a vacuum, and the elder brother filled it."

The discussion drifted to the specifics of the loss. George's admission that he had advised his sister to see Harry, only to withdraw support when the older brother emerged, hung in the air. It was a confession of calculated cruelty, wrapped in the language of familial necessity. "No bread except at his brother's side-table," George explained, his tone matter-of-fact. "That was the reality. The elder said as much."

"How hard-hearted!" Madame de Bernstein exclaimed, though her voice lacked conviction.

"Audacity," George corrected gently. "Everyone plays the role assigned to them. Even Molly admitted it once the initial shock wore off. She retreated with dignity, yes—a masterly withdrawal—but she was starved out of her position."

The narrative shifted to the memory of the encounter itself. The image of the young man, muddy-stocked, hair disheveled, throwing himself among the diners with a frankness that bordered on desperation. He had offered himself, naked and vulnerable, to a woman who had already chosen the path of least resistance. The comparison to Mrs. Woffington in the new Scotch play served to elevate the tragedy into something theatrical, something distant enough to be observed without pain. They had lost, but they had lost with the honors of war.

Yet, beneath the surface of this polite exchange lay a current of genuine bitterness. The reconciliation was superficial, a diplomatic maneuver to restore the facade of the great world. Differences were smoothed over; estranged branches of the family were allowed to reunite as if no chasm had ever existed. A stray prodigal, a stray puppy-dog, welcomed back under the blanket of amnesty, despite the ugly company he had kept.

For six months, the Castlewoods and Madame de Bernstein had been locked in a silent war for possession of Harry Warrington. Now, the question was settled. The coach rolled to Lady Castlewood's rout, and the Esmond ladies smiled at Madame de Bernstein's drums, pretending that the previous animosity had never tainted the air.

"So, sir," the Baroness said, her voice rising to a playful menace, brandishing her fan like a weapon, "you acted like a hard-hearted monster regarding your poor brother."

"I acted upon your ladyship's hint," George replied, his cheeks flushing a shade of red that betrayed his composure. "To test whether it was for himself or his money they sought him."

"Nay, Maria could not marry a pauper," George insisted, the argument rehearsed and delivered with practiced ease. "And so thou didst, O thou hypocrite!" the old lady cried, her French slipping through the cracks of her indignation.

"Hypocrite?" George drew himself up, adopting a pose of mock grandeur. "Why? I know all, my infant! Thou art very like thy grandfather. Come, embrace me! Harry has told me all—that thou hast divided thy little patrimony with him!"

"It was natural," George said, his eyes filling with tears that were clearly manufactured. "Common hearts and purses since birth. I feigned hardness to try those people yonder."

"And Virginia?" the Baroness pressed.

"The land goes to the eldest," George conceded, the logic unyielding. "Though half of what is mine is his. He was extravagant because he was mistaken about his station."

Madame de Bernstein retired to her chambers, whispering to her attendant, "A knight of old! A Bayard! The grandfather come to life!" That evening, when the lads finally left, she bestowed her favors: two fingers to Harry, a rouged cheek to George. George blushed, a deep, artificial crimson, at the mark of approval from his ancient kinswoman.

Harry Warrington, however, felt none of this warmth. Though he honored his brother as his guide and superior, a profound sense of humiliation settled over him. He had been deposed from the throne of the Fortunate Youth, stripped of the boundless Virginian territories that had been his inheritance. His friends at Kensington promised love in his fall, but Harry saw the truth: George was the center of the universe. All the talk, the compliments, the *petits soins* belonged to the elder brother. Only Maria followed him, her eyes conveying a silent, dismal reproach. She had rejected him, yet she could not quite forgive him for accepting her rejection.

"Oh, my young friends," the narrator reflected, observing the absurdity of it all, "how delightful the beginning of love, and how undignified the end! Young Damon and Phillis twine their arms, surveying the *pays du tendre*, believing they can cross any river, slay any dragon. They imagine resting together under the stars, safe in the woods. But they have not walked a mile before they hit a great wall. They argue with the post-boy at Barnet; Papa appears with a whip; the girl is locked in the schoolroom crying. Years later, when Strephon meets Delia again, is it not ridiculous? Still, one should not fall in love unless one believes in the eternity of passion. Some women expect their hearts to break from eighteen to eighty-five. When the King of France marches up the hill with twenty thousand men and colors flying, only to march down again, he and you are in an absurd position."

This was Harry's position. He felt the melancholy of looking into a house once lived in, now dark and empty. He passed No. 13, where someone else grinned from the window, on friendly terms with the landlady. The sight of other boots on the sofa where he had once reclined provoked a visceral rage. He kicked the air, imagining the boots as muddy, vulgar highlows.

His period of occupation was over. Maria's rooms were to be let. He found himself unable to converse with her; her words were dull, commonplace, far less agreeable than the red lip of a white-necked pipe of Virginia. When George went to Kensington, Harry stayed behind, pleading other engagements.

At his uncle's house in Hill Street, the treatment was colder still. The virtuous people there paid him scant attention. The Ladies did not scruple to deny themselves when he arrived, a betrayal that shocked the naive Harry. Sitting with the Lamberts, he watched his aunt's chairmen arrive with an empty seat. She stepped out, entered the vehicle, and did not even blush when he bowed from the opposite window.

"To be denied by one's own relations!" Harry cried, his impetuosity breaking the tension. "To have that door slammed in my face! Did you ever see such cold-heartedness, Aunt Lambert?"

"Do we make any difference to you, my dear Harry?" Aunt Lambert asked, casting a sidelong glance at her youngest daughter. "The world may look coldly at you, but we do not belong to it. You may come to us in safety."

"In this house, you are different," Harry replied, seeking solace in the familiar. "I always feel quiet and happy."

*"Quis me uno vivit felicior? aut magis hac est Optandum vita dicere quis potuit?"* General Lambert quoted, addressing his son from college who had come to spend Easter.

"You got them out of Catullus, sir," the scholar answered.

"I got them out of no such thing," the General declared proudly. "Out of my favorite Democritus Junior—old Burton, who has provided many indifferent scholars with learning; and Montaigne."

The General's voice boomed, filling the room with the authority of a man who believed he understood the world, unaware that outside, the world had quietly closed its doors on the young man standing in the corner, holding a pipe that burned brighter than any conversation.
