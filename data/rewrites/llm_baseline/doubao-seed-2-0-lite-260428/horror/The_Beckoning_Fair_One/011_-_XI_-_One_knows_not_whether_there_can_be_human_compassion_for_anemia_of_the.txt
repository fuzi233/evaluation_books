No human heart, no matter how soft or how steeped in love, can hold compassion for the anemia of the soul. When life’s pitch drops so far it snaps, when the spirit is twisted inside out until all that once tasted sweet, bright, and ordinary curdles into loathing, the ties that bind people to each other dissolve. The sane recoil, appalled, lest their own sanity bleed away too. We are not exorcists. We cannot cast out devils. We are selfish, and right to be, to make sure those devils do not crawl under our own doors—even when love swells so hot in our veins we fool ourselves into thinking we are half divine. Talk of honor, of duty, is useless. The note slipped under the dark threshold will lie unread in the dust, or if it is glanced at for a single, fumbling second between two bouts of unspeakable oblivion, it will be dropped and forgotten. The telegram will never be answered. The whistling messenger, trundling down the steps afterward, will never notice the drawn blind creaking open an inch behind him, a trembling finger prodding its edge before snatching it back to hide.

Let the lost man wrestle his own shadows. Let him, if his madness runs that far, cling to the succubus he has conjured, curl around her, feed her. But let him do it in a house no breath of heaven ever touches, no ray of sun ever pierces to cut through its filthy, thick twilight. The lost belong lost. Humanity has other, brighter things to tend to.

The handwriting on the two envelopes Oleron had found kicked across his kitchen floor, the day he crept in to dump an armful of fetid, rotting blooms he’d stashed in his sitting room, meant less to him than the characters of some half-remembered dream. When the telegraph boy rapped on the front door, only a few feet from the bed he was sprawled in, Oleron gnashed his teeth and stuffed his knuckles in his ears. He pictured the boy standing just outside his partition, between stacks of forgotten parcels and bundles of flowers already dead and oozing brown slime. His whole outer landing was heaped with them. He’d been too afraid to open the door and drag them in. After a week, the errand runners marked his address down as a mistake, and never came back. Inside, in that thick red twilight, the old blooms turned brown, crumbled, and rotted where they fell.

His strength was draining away, drop by drop. The Abomination fastened to him, feeding on every spark, and the steady sapping left him prone for hours at a time, staring vacantly up at his rose-tinged ceiling, letting random, unbidden fancies crawl all over him. Even his sharpest memories could barely cling to his attention. Sometimes a half-flitting memory teased him: the novel he was supposed to write, the one his contract demanded he finish, flickering for a few seconds before winking out entirely. Sometimes whole perfect, timeless novels bloomed in his head out of nowhere, vivid enough to touch. Sometimes the memories were tiny, trivial, decades old: the garret he’d lived in at twenty-two, the moldy lodgings he’d rented after his first book got a bad review. He’d once known how to fight these slow, creeping blurs, but that was all over now.

He’d found a place he never intended to leave until they pried his unconscious body out by force. A place some might call fever-sick, reeking too hard of long-dead, morbid things for a living man to lock himself inside— but God, it was irresistible. It had a will of iron, a pull no one could fight, a companion no sane man would ever trade away. What delights it held, once you stopped straining against its inexorable tug! A novel? Somebody ought to write a novel about a house like this! There was a million stories tangled in its walls, if you just dug deep enough. That painter Madley, who’d died here, had probably painted every inch of it… but Oleron didn’t know Madley, and he was sure he’d hate the man. Hated the very thought of him, in fact. (Aha, that was a fine joke!) He was half tempted to march over and tell that long-nosed prude of a curate next door what a rotten, ungodly life Madley had led— but he suspected the curate already knew, that he’d been praying loud through the thin walls for Madley’s soul all these years. He and Oleron had even fought once, about some girl… Elsie Bengough, that was her name.

He had moments of deep, jittery unease about Elsie. Not unease for her, exactly— restless, that she kept shoving herself uninvited into his head. The second she popped up, he tried to kick her out. She wasn’t just a bore, never had been. Now, her very image in his mind was poison, ruining the bright, burning raptures the house gifted him. She had no tact. She ought to know no one wants every random thought parading through their head at all hours. She ought to leave him alone for hours at a time, when his veins hummed with fire and power, when he had every right to shut out prying fools the way he’d shut that nosy Barrett out. But no— there she was, popping up, and the towering, glorious visions he was on the cusp of grasping vanished instantly, as if a fog had snuffed out a star, as if a pit had gaped open right on the threshold of a golden door, as if a flapping bat shadow had snuffed out the dawn and dragged everything back to murk. He fought to snuff even the first faint spark of any thought of her.

But one night, he couldn’t. He didn’t know what hour it was— only the faint glimmer of a streetlamp seeping around his drawn blind told him it was dark, that it had been weeks since he’d thought of her at all. She was just there, unannounced, her face fixed in his mind no matter how hard he tried to scrape it away. She was haunting him, and of all moments to show up, when he was right on the edge of the triumph of his rival— the fair, dark succubus who called to him from the walls— Good God, it was monstrous! How dare she stand there, peering in on it? The blood rushed to the roots of his hair, hot with rage. “Damnation take her!” he choked.

The next second, his petty anger dissolved into a cold, shearing sweat of unnameable fear. Panic coiled in his chest. He knew he’d done something fatal, irreparable. That blaze of fury flooding his murky twilight wasn’t his. That white, searing infernal light that split the dark wasn’t his. That rift yanking open to hell wasn’t his. His rage had been no bigger than a child’s fist, ready to throw a tiny, harmless blow— but that other, horrific hand, pulling back to strike in the exact same spot? Had he set that in motion? Had his tiny spark lit the fuse on the full, accumulated power of this relentless, hungry house? He didn’t know. He only knew his own small spark had been blown out, that the soft, clinging kiss he’d been chasing for months had curdled on his lips to a ravenous, tearing bite, and that for the first time he felt terrible pity for the woman he’d just been screaming about. He had to warn her. He had to.

“LOOK OUT!” he shrieked aloud.

The revulsion hit like a cold, weighty wave crashing over his head. He jolted awake, flat on his back in his bed, the thick mist of horror that had wrapped around him for months suddenly stripped away. He was Paul Oleron. Tired novelist. Past the peak of his talent. Sick, naked, helpless, unutterably alone. His mind was clear, for the first time in longer than he could remember, and he understood he was a failure. He’d missed every prize: the easy, warm domestic happiness other men loved, and the shining, wild prize he’d chased that was supposed to justify all his reckless choices. There was no do-over. Fate gives no second morrows. His future stretched out, empty, nothing but day after day of grinding out bad, unwanted work, paying forfeit to his publishers for the half-finished novel he’d never finish. He was spent. Old age was creeping close. The rest of his life would be nothing but sad, quiet wisdom, if he was lucky. If only he’d chosen the wife, the children, the friend at the fireside, and let the will-o’-the-wisp he’d chased burn itself out without him.

It struck him then how weak he was, how the whole room reeked of rot, how the scratch of a thick, weeks-grown beard rasped against his palm when he dragged a hand over his face. “Extraordinary,” he muttered. “Have I been ill? Why did no one call a doctor? Why am I left alone?”

He thought he heard a soft sound from the kitchen. He propped himself up on one elbow, listening. He wasn’t alone, then. Of course they wouldn’t leave him sick all by himself. If everyone else abandoned him, Elsie Bengough would never leave him. His dearest, most faithful friend. Bless her heart.

A short, stifled, spluttering cry rang out, sharp as a cracked bell, from the other side of the door. “Paul!”

In that same second, a tiny, forgotten sound clicked into place in his memory, one he’d ignored minutes earlier: the soft scrape of metal on metal, exactly the sound his key made turning in his front door lock.

“Hello? Who’s there?” he called, his voice hoarse. No answer. He called louder. “Hello? Who is it? Elsie? Is that you?” He thumped on the wall beside his bed. “Elsie! You called, didn’t you? Come in, whoever you are!”

He heard the soft thud of a closing door, then dead silence. Alarm pricked at him. Elsie must have hired a nurse for him, that made sense. She’d stay as long as she could, then leave the nurse to watch over him. But that voice had been exactly hers. “I have to get up,” he mumbled, throwing one leg over the edge of the bed. His legs were like water. He reached out for the wall to prop himself up.

He froze before he could put his other leg down. He was suddenly, horribly certain he dared not step foot in the kitchen. It was miles away. Any sort of unnameable horror could leap on his back the second he crossed the threshold. When every bone in your body screams to crawl back to bed, you listen. Besides— why should he go? If that spoilsport Elsie had gotten herself into trouble, that was her problem. He wasn’t going to drag some clinging, hungry horror back onto his defenceless self for her sake. Let her find her own way out. He didn’t care. He had more important things to do. Tomorrow, he was going to write a novel with a heroine so beautiful, so wicked, so hungry and adoring and cruel, every man who read it would lose their mind. He could feel her near now, the air in the room thrumming with that soft, giddy bliss that only came when she was there,beckoning,beckoning.

He let go of the wall, and fell back limp into the bed, as the other half of that ravenous kiss the bite had interrupted pressed to his lips, stealing every last breath from his chest.
