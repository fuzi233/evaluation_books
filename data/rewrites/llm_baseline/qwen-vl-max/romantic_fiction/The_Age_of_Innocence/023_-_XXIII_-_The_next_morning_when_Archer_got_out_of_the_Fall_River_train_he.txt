XXIII. The next morning, when Archer stepped off the Fall River train, he was met by a Boston sweltering under the weight of midsummer heat. The air near the station hung thick with the mingled scents of coffee, beer, and rotting fruit, while shirt-sleeved men and women moved through the streets like guests returning from an evening’s indulgence—unconstrained, unguarded, as if they’d just come from a shared bathhouse. He hailed a cab and rode to the Somerset Club for breakfast. Even in its most refined quarters, the city wore the look of domestic disorder that no amount of warmth could ever impose on European capitals. Caretakers in calico lounged on the doorsteps of grand homes, and the Common sprawled beneath the sun like a neglected garden after a festival. If Archer had tried to imagine Ellen Olenska in any improbable setting, this overheated, half-deserted Boston would have been the hardest to picture her in.

He ate his breakfast with quiet precision—first a slice of melon, then toast and scrambled eggs—while scanning the morning paper. A new energy hummed inside him, born from the decision he’d made the night before: to tell May he was going to Boston on business, take the Fall River boat that night, and return to New York the following evening. It had always been assumed he’d be back early in the week, so when he returned from Portsmouth and found a letter from the office waiting on the hall table—a letter fate seemed to have placed there deliberately—it gave his sudden departure all the justification it needed. He even felt a pang of shame at how easily it had all come together, a fleeting memory of Lawrence Lefferts’s deft maneuvers to free himself from obligation. But the thought didn’t linger; he wasn’t in the mood for analysis.

After breakfast, he lit a cigarette and glanced over the *Commercial Advertiser*. A few familiar faces drifted into the club, exchanging their usual greetings. It was still the same world, yet he felt as though he’d slipped through time and space, arriving in a place both familiar and utterly alien. He checked his watch—half past nine—and rose to go to the writing room. There, he scribbled a short note and sent a messenger with a cab to the Parker House, instructing him to wait for a reply. Then he sat again, newspaper in hand, trying to guess how long the ride might take.

“The lady was out, sir,” came a waiter’s voice beside him.

“Out?” he stammered, as if the word were foreign.

He stood up, heart pounding. It couldn’t be true—she wouldn’t be out at this hour. He flushed with irritation at his own delay. Why hadn’t he sent the note the moment he arrived? He grabbed his hat and stick and stepped back into the street. The city suddenly loomed vast and strange, as if he’d wandered into a distant land. For a moment he hesitated on the doorstep, then decided to walk to the Parker House. Maybe the messenger had been wrong—maybe she was still there.

He crossed the Common and saw her sitting on the first bench, beneath a tree. She held a grey silk parasol over her head—how could he ever have imagined her with a pink one? As he approached, he noticed her stillness, her posture relaxed as if she had nowhere else to be. Her profile drooped slightly, her hair tied low at the nape of her neck beneath a dark hat, her long glove wrinkled where it gripped the parasol. She turned as he neared.

“Oh,” she said.

For the first time, he saw surprise flash across her face—but only for a second. Then it softened into a slow, knowing smile. “Oh,” she murmured again, quieter now, as if she’d been waiting for him. Without rising, she shifted on the bench to make room.

“I’m here on business—just arrived,” he explained, though he didn’t know why he was pretending to be surprised to see her. “But what on earth are you doing in this wilderness?”

He didn’t really know what he was saying. It felt as if he were shouting across miles, afraid she might vanish again before he could reach her.

“I? Oh, I’m here on business too,” she replied, turning her head so they faced each other.

Her words barely registered. All he heard was her voice—the low timbre, the faint roughness on the consonants—something he’d never remembered, something that now struck him as entirely new.

“You do your hair differently,” he said, heart thudding as if he’d spoken something irreversible.

“Differently? No—only because I do it myself without Nastasia.”

“Nastasia? Isn’t she with you?”

“No. For two days, it wasn’t worth bringing her.”

“You’re alone—at the Parker House?”

She looked at him, a spark of old mischief in her eyes. “Does it strike you as dangerous?”

“No—not dangerous—”

“But unconventional? I suppose it is.” She paused. “I hadn’t thought of it, because I’ve just done something far more unconventional.”

A hint of irony lingered in her gaze. “I’ve just refused to take back money that was mine.”

Archer jumped up and took a step back. She had folded her parasol and was absently drawing patterns in the gravel.

He returned slowly, standing before her. “Someone came here to meet you?”

“Yes.”

“With this offer?”

She nodded.

“And you refused—because of the conditions?”

She waited a moment. “I refused.”

He sat beside her again. “What were the conditions?”

“Oh, nothing harsh—just to sit at the head of his table now and then.”

Silence fell between them. His heart had shut down, that peculiar way it did when he was close to her. He fumbled for words.

“He wants you back—at any price?”

“Well—yes. A considerable price. At least, it’s considerable for me.”

Another pause. He circled around the question he knew he had to ask. “Was it to meet him that you came here?”

She stared, then laughed. “Meet him—my husband? _Here_? At this season, he’s always at Cowes or Baden.”

“He sent someone?”

“Yes.”

“A letter?”

She shook her head. “No—just a message. He never writes. I don’t think I’ve received more than one letter from him.”

The mention of it brought color to her cheeks—and then to his. “Why does he never write?”

“Why should he? What are secretaries for?”

He blushed deeper. She said the word as if it carried no weight, no implication.

For a moment, he almost asked: “Did he send his secretary, then?” But the memory of Count Olenski’s single letter to his wife flashed through his mind, and he stopped.

Then he tried again. “And the person?”

“The emissary?” she replied, still smiling. “The emissary might, for all I care, have left already. But he insisted on waiting until this evening… in case… on the chance…”

“And you came out here to think about it?”

“I came out to breathe. The hotel is stifling. I’m taking the afternoon train back to Portsmouth.”

They sat in silence, staring ahead at the people passing along the path. Finally, she turned her eyes to his face. “You’re not changed.”

He wanted to say, “I was—until I saw you again.” Instead, he stood abruptly and looked around at the park, steam rising from the hot ground. “This is terrible. Why shouldn’t we go out on the bay? There’s a breeze—there’ll be cooler. We could take the steamboat down to Point Arley.”

She looked up at him, hesitant. He pressed on. “On a Monday morning, there won’t be anyone. My train doesn’t leave till evening—I’m going back to New York. Why shouldn’t we?”

He leaned down, urgent. “Haven’t we done all we could?”

“Oh,” she murmured.

She stood, reopened her parasol, and glanced around as if confirming the impossibility of staying. Then her eyes met his.

“You mustn’t say things like that to me.”

“I’ll say anything you want—or nothing. I won’t open my mouth unless you tell me to. What harm can it do? All I want is to listen to you,” he stammered.

She pulled out a small gold watch on an enamelled chain. “Oh, don’t calculate,” he broke in. “Give me the day! I want to get you away from that man. When is he coming?”

Her cheeks flushed. “At eleven.”

“Then you must come now.”

“You needn’t be afraid—if I don’t.”

“Nor you—if you do. I swear I only want to hear about you—to know what you’ve been doing. It’s been a hundred years since we met—maybe another hundred before we do again.”

She hesitated, her eyes searching his. “Why didn’t you come down to the beach to fetch me, the day I was at Granny’s?”

“Because you didn’t look round—because you didn’t know I was there. I swore I wouldn’t unless you looked.”

He laughed, realizing how childish it sounded.

“But I didn’t look on purpose.”

“On purpose?”

“I knew you were there. When you drove in, I recognized the ponies. So I went down to the beach.”

“To get away from me as far as you could?”

She repeated softly, “To get away from you as far as you could.”

He laughed again, this time with boyish satisfaction. “Well, you see it’s no use. I may as well tell you—the business I came here for was just to find you. But look—we must start, or we’ll miss our boat.”

“Our boat?”

She frowned, then smiled. “Oh, but I must go back to the hotel first—I must leave a note—”

“As many notes as you please. You can write here.” He pulled out a note-case and a stylographic pen. “I’ve even got an envelope—see how everything’s predestined! Here—steady the paper on your knee, and I’ll get the pen working.”

He tapped the pen against the bench. “It’s like jerking down mercury in a thermometer—just a trick. Now try—”

She laughed and bent over the sheet he’d laid on the case, beginning to write.

Archer walked a few steps away, staring blankly at the passersby, who in turn paused to stare at the sight of a well-dressed woman writing a note on her knee in the middle of the Common.

She slipped the letter into the envelope, wrote a name, and tucked it into her pocket. Then she stood.

They walked back toward Beacon Street, and near the club, Archer spotted the plush-lined cab that had delivered his note. Its driver sat cooling his brow at a hydrant.

“I told you everything was predestined!” Archer said. “Here’s a cab for us. See!”

They laughed, amazed at the miracle of finding a public conveyance at that hour, in such an unlikely place, in a city where cab-stands were still considered foreign.

Looking at his watch, Archer saw they had time to drive to the Parker House before heading to the steamboat landing. They rattled through the hot streets and stopped at the hotel.

He held out his hand for the letter. “Shall I take it in?”

She shook her head and stepped inside.

It was barely half-past ten. But what if the emissary, impatient and unaware of how to fill his time, had already settled among the travelers sipping drinks at the bar—those very travelers Archer had glimpsed as she entered?

He waited, pacing before the cab. A Sicilian youth with eyes like Nastasia’s offered to shine his boots; an Irish matron sold peaches. Every few moments, the doors opened, letting out men in straw hats, their faces weary, their movements sluggish. They all looked alike—like every other overheated traveler in America at that hour, passing endlessly in and out of hotel doors.

Then, suddenly, a face broke the pattern.

He caught only a flash—his pacing had carried him to the far end of his beat—and as he turned back, he saw it among the familiar types: the lank, the tired, the wide-eyed, the mild. This face was different—pale, yes, dimmed by heat or worry, but sharper, quicker, more aware. Or perhaps it seemed so because it stood apart.

Archer clung to a thread of memory—but it snapped, drifting away with the disappearing figure. Just a foreign businessman, looking doubly foreign in that setting.

He vanished into the crowd, and Archer resumed his patrol.

He didn’t want to be seen watching the clock. His own sense of time told him that if she delayed much longer, it could only mean she’d met the emissary and been detained.

At the thought, his anxiety tightened into anguish.

“If she doesn’t come soon, I’ll go in and find her,” he muttered.

The doors swung open again—and she was at his side.

They climbed into the cab. As it pulled away, he checked his watch: she had been gone exactly three minutes.

In the clatter of loose windows, talk was impossible. They bumped over cobblestones to the wharf.

Seated side by side on the nearly empty boat, they found themselves speaking little—what they had to say passed best in silence, in the relief of being alone, in the hush of escape.

As the paddle-wheels began to turn, and the docks and ships faded behind a veil of heat, Archer felt the old world slipping away. He longed to ask her if she felt the same—the sense of embarking on a journey from which they might never return.

But he dared not speak. Not a word that might shake the fragile trust she placed in him.

In truth, he didn’t want to betray it.

There had been nights when the memory of their kiss burned on his lips. The day before, even, on the drive to Portsmouth, her presence had coursed through him like fire.

But now, with her beside him, drifting into this unknown sea, they seemed to have reached a depth of closeness that no touch could disturb.

As the boat turned seaward, a breeze stirred, and the bay broke into long oily waves, then into ripples tipped with spray. The fog still clung to the city, but ahead lay fresh waters and distant headlands, lighthouses gleaming in the sun.

Madame Olenska leaned back against the rail, breathing in the cool air through parted lips. Her veil wound loosely around her hat, leaving her face uncovered. Archer was struck by the calm joy in her expression. She accepted their adventure as natural, neither fearful nor overly excited by the possibility of danger.

In the bare dining-room of the inn—where he’d hoped they’d have privacy—they found a loud group of young teachers on holiday. Archer’s spirits sank.

“This is hopeless—I’ll ask for a private room,” he said.

She made no objection as he went to find one.

The room opened onto a long wooden verandah, the sea visible through the windows. It was plain and cool, with a coarse checkered cloth on the table, a bottle of pickles, and a blueberry pie under a cage.

No more innocent-looking private room could have sheltered a clandestine couple. Archer thought he saw the comfort of it in the faint, amused smile on her face as she sat opposite him.

A woman who had fled her husband—and rumored to have done so with another man—would surely know how to take things for granted.

Yet there was something in her quiet composure that softened his irony. By being so calm, so unsurprised, so simple, she had brushed aside the rules and made it feel natural—natural for two old friends, with so much to say, to seek a moment of solitude.
