The new Pope favored the Dominican Order. From the Vatican came a summons requesting a capable friar to teach at the University of Basel. Now, Clement was the very monk suited for this task: well-versed in languages and, in his former secular life, a student of Guarini the Younger's lectures. Consequently, his planned return to England was postponed, though not abandoned. Instead, he was dispatched to Basel; however, lacking immediate need there for three months, he was instructed to preach along the way.

He departed through the northern gate with his gaze lowered, the entire figure of the man wrapped in pious contemplation. *Oh, if one could paint a mind and its history,* the observer thought, *what a living fresco was this barefooted friar!* Hopeful love, bereavement, despair, impiety, vice, suicide, remorse, religious despondency, penitence, and finally, death to the world and total resignation—all compressed into merely twelve short months. And now, the traveler was on foot again. Yet, everything had changed. There were no longer perilous adventures. Even thieves and robbers bowed before him; instead of stripping him, they forced stolen coins into his hands and begged for his prayers. Thus, this journey offered few picturesque incidents.

However, I have readers in mind who care little for melodrama and prefer a quiet glimpse into the workings of a human heart. To such observers, the undramatic is often the most vocal, revealing the slow progress of a mind. The first Sunday of Clement's journey bore witness to this internal shift. He prayed for the soul of Margaret. He had never done so before. Not because her eternal welfare was not dearer to him than anything on earth—it was his own humility. The terrible blasphemies that had burst forth from his lips upon hearing of her death had horrified his well-meaning contemporaries, but upon reflection, they horrified him far more.

For a long duration during his novitiate, Clement had been crushed by religious despair. He believed he must have committed the sin against the Holy Spirit, a transgression that doomed the soul forever. Gradually, aided by Anselmo, that dark cloud lifted; yet, a profound self-abasement remained. He felt his own salvation precarious and reasoned that it would be mocking Heaven for him, a soul so deeply stained, to pray for one as innocent and comparatively pure as Margaret's. Thus, he coaxed good Anselm and another kindly monk to intercede for her. They refused not, nor did they offer half-hearted aid. Generally, the good old monks—though every convent held a mix of good, bad, and indifferent souls—possessed a pure, tender affection for their younger brethren, a bond truly not of this world.

Clement, having preached on Sunday morning in a small Italian town and feeling mightily carried onward, was greatly encouraged. That day, a balmy sense of God's forgiveness and love descended upon him. From that hour, he prayed for the welfare of Margaret's soul. It became his daily habit, the single purified tie connecting his heart to the earth. For his family were to him as if they had never existed. The Church would not share its domain with the earth, nor could it heal the great love without annihilating the lesser attachments.

Throughout most of this journey, Clement felt no spring of life within him, except when standing in the pulpit. Other exceptions occurred when he relieved a fellow creature in distress. Once, he encountered a young man suffering from what appeared to be a tarantula bite—or perhaps, like many others, merely imagined it. Whether fancy or reality, the youth had slept for two days and suffered extraordinary convulsions, leaping, twisting, and battering the walls. Village musicians had only exacerbated his condition with their frantic tunes. Exhaustion and death awaited if the malady gained further hold.

Clement stopped to inquire. Summoning a psaltery, he attempted to soothe the patient with gentle melodies. If other tunes drove the man mad, Clement's seemed to crush him. The youth groaned and moaned, eventually collapsing onto the floor. Finally, the friar noticed that at intervals, the patient's lips moved silently. Applying his ear, he discovered the man was whispering a tune—a singular melody that had no existence outside that mind. Clement learned the song and played it. The patient's face brightened miraculously. He danced lightly across the room, enjoying the rhythm, until Clement's fingers ached from the continuous playing. Only then did the young man sink complacently into sleep, lulled by this strange creation of his own mind, for he was no musician and had never composed a tune before or after. This sleep saved his life. After teaching the melody to another, in case it were needed again, Clement moved forward with a heart slightly warmer.

On another occasion, he found a mob dragging a decently dressed man who struggled and shouted in a strange tongue. This individual had entered the town erect and sprightly, waving a mulberry branch overhead. The locals first stared in stupefied disbelief, then pounced, dragging him before the podestà. Clement accompanied them but quietly approached the prisoner, speaking to him in Italian. Receiving no answer, he switched to French, then German, then Dutch—to no avail. The man then tested Clement in tolerable Latin, albeit with a sharp accent.

"I am an Englishman," the man explained, "oppressed by the heat of Italy. I plucked a bough from the nearest tree to save my head. In my country, anyone is welcome to what grows on the highway. Confound the fools; I am ready to pay for it. But here, all of Italy rises in arms over a mere twig and a handful of leaves!"

The stubborn podestà intended to imprison the dogged islander, but Clement intervened. With some difficulty, he helped the prisoner understand that silkworms—and consequently, mulberry leaves—were sacred, falling under the protection of the Sovereign and serving as his source of income. Clement urged the podestà that ignorance of these mulberry laws was natural in a distant land where the very tree might be unknown. The opinionated islander tipped the scales by producing a long purse and reiterating his original stance: the matter was purely commercial.

*"Quid damni?"* he asked. *"Dic, et cito solvam"* ("What damage? Speak, and I will quickly pay"). The podestà sniffed the gold, fined the man a ducat for the Duke—roughly the value of the entire tree—and pocketed the coin. The Englishman shed his ire instantly, laughing heartily at the absurdity, yet remaining deeply grateful to Clement.

"You are too good for this hole of a country, father," he said. "Come to England! That is the only place in the world. I was an uneasy fool to leave it and wander among mulberries and their idiots. I am a Kentish squire, educated at Cambridge University. My name is Rolfe; my place is Betshanger. The man and the house are at your service. Come over and stay until Doomsday. We sit down forty to dinner every day at Betshanger; one more or one less makes no difference. You shall spend your days with me and my heirs if you will. Come now! What an Englishman says, he means." He gave Clement a hearty grip of the hand to confirm his sincerity.

"I will visit thee some day, my son," Clement replied, "but not to weary thy hospitality."

The Englishman then begged Clement to absolve him. "I know not what will become of my soul," he confessed. "I have lived like a heathen since leaving England." Clement consented gladly, and soon the islander knelt by the roadside, confessing the sins of the last month. Finding him so pious a son of the Church, Clement revealed he was indeed returning to England. He then asked if it were true the country was overrun with Lollards and Wickliffites. The other flushed slightly.

"There be black sheep in every land," he admitted. After a moment's reflection, he spoke gravely: "Holy Father, hear the truth regarding these heretics. None are better disposed toward Holy Church than we English. But we are ourselves, and by ourselves. We love our own ways, and above all, our own tongue. The Normans could conquer our bill-hooks, but not our tongues; and hard they tried it for many long years by law and proclamation. Our good foreign priests speak God to plain English folk in Latin, or in some French or Italian dialect, like the bleating of a sheep. Then comes the fox Wickliffe and his crew, reading Scripture out loud in plain English, so that all men's hearts warm to it. Who can withstand this? God forgive me, I believe the English would turn deaf ears to Saint Peter himself if he did not speak to them in the tongue their mothers taught them alongside their mother's kisses."

He added hastily, "I say not this for myself; I am Cambridge-bred, and good words come amiss to me in Latin; but for the people in general. *Clavis ad corda Anglorum est lingua materna.* ('The key to the hearts of the English is the mother tongue.')"

"My son," said Clement, "blessed be the hour I met thee; for thy words are sober and wise. But alas! How shall I learn your English tongue? I possess no book."
