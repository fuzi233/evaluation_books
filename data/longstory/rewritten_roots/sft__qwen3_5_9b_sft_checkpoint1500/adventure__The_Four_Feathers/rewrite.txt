The first guest to arrive at Broad Place was Lieutenant Sutch. He reached the house at five o'clock on a sun-drenched afternoon in mid-June. The old red-brick mansion, nestled on the southern slope of the Surrey hills, glowed like a rare jewel warmed by the deep, dark shadow of surrounding pine forests. Sutch, favoring a cane due to a limp, crossed the grand hall—where portraits of the Feversham lineage climbed endlessly toward the ceiling—and stepped out onto the stone-flagged terrace at the rear. There, seated upright as a young boy, was his host, General Feversham, gazing southward toward the Sussex Downs.

"How fares the leg?" General Feversham asked abruptly as he rose from his chair. He was a small, wiry man, sharp-eyed despite his white hair, though his alertness seemed purely physical. His face was bony, dominated by a high, narrow forehead and steel-blue, impenetrable eyes that suggested a barrenness of the spirit.

"It troubled me throughout the winter," Sutch replied. "But that was inevitable." Feversham nodded, and for a moment, silence hung heavy between them. From the terrace, the land plunged steeply into a vast plain of brown earth and emerald fields, dotted with dark tree clusters. Through the sunlight, faint but piercing, voices drifted up from below. In the distance, near Horsham, a coil of white smoke from a passing train snaked rapidly through the trees; on the horizon, the Downs rose, patched with stark white chalk.

"I thought you'd be here," Sutch observed.

"This was my wife's favorite corner," Feversham answered, his voice devoid of emotion. "She would sit here for hours. She possessed a peculiar affinity for wide, empty spaces."

"Yes," Sutch agreed softly. "She had imagination. Her thoughts could fill those emptinesses."

Feversham glanced at his companion with a look of mild confusion, as if the concept eluded him entirely. Being a man who habitually dismissed the uncomprehensible as unworthy of attention, he shifted topics immediately.

"There will be a vacancy at our table tonight."

"Certainly," Sutch said. "Collins, Barberton, and Vaughan departed this winter. Well, we are all permanently shelved on the world's half-pay list anyway. "I am pleased you arrived before the others," Feversham continued. "I wish to hear your opinion. Today holds greater weight for me than the mere anniversary of our assault on the Redan. At the exact moment we stood under arms in the darkness..."

"To the west of the quarries, I recall," Sutch interrupted, drawing a deep breath. "How could one forget?"

"At that precise moment, Harry was born in this house," Feversham stated. "Therefore, if you do not object, he shall join us tonight. He is currently at home. Naturally, he will enter the service; perhaps he will learn something useful along the way—one never knows."

"By all means," Sutch replied with alacrity. Since his visits to General Feversham were restricted to these annual dinners, he had never previously met Harry. For many years, Sutch had been puzzled by the qualities in General Feversham that had captivated Muriel Graham—a woman renowned for the refinement of her intellect as much as for her beauty. He could never decipher the mystery of why such a refined woman had married a man so much older and so fundamentally unlike her in character. To Sutch, personal courage and indomitable self-confidence were the sole, glaring traits of the General. Now, sitting in his garden chair, Sutch reflected back twenty years, recalling a season in London following his return from the China station, driven by a curiosity to see if the boy resembled his mother or his father. He told himself this was merely the natural curiosity of a man, disabled in youth, who had made the study of human nature his hobby.

That night, Harry Feversham took his seat at the dinner table, listening to the tales spun by his elders, while Lieutenant Sutch watched intently. The stories all concerned that dark winter in the Crimea, and a new narrative was always launched before the previous one concluded. They were accounts of death, hazardous exploits, the gnawing hunger of famine, and the biting cold of snow. Yet they were delivered in clipped, matter-of-fact tones, as though the storytellers viewed these tragedies as distant, abstract concepts. There was rarely a comment sharper than a casual "That's curious," nor an exclamation more significant than a dry laugh.

However, Harry Feversham sat listening as though the incidents being carelessly narrated were occurring in real time, within the walls of that room. His dark eyes—so strikingly like his mother's—turned from speaker to speaker, wide-open and fixed, waiting until the final word was spoken. He listened, entranced. So vividly did the shifts of expression ripple across his face that it appeared to Sutch the boy must truly hear the drone of bullets overhead, truly withstand the stunning shock of a charge, truly ride down into the thick of a squadron where cannons screeched out tongues of flame through the fog. When a Major of Artillery described the agonizing suspense of the hours between parading troops and the first order to advance, Harry's shoulders worked under an intolerable strain.

Yet he did more than tense his shoulders. He cast a single, furtive, wavering glance backward. Lieutenant Sutch was startled, indeed pained. For this was Muriel Graham's boy.

The look was too familiar to Sutch. He had witnessed it on the faces of recruits during their first exposure to battle too frequently to misunderstand it. A specific image rose instantly before his mind: an advancing square at Inkermann, a tall, broad soldier rushing forward from the line in the fervor of attack, then stopping abruptly as if suddenly realizing he was alone, facing a charging Cossack alone. Sutch recalled the fatal, wavering glance that big soldier had thrown backward toward his comrades—a glance accompanied by a strange, sickly smile. He remembered, with equal vividness, the consequence. Though the soldier carried a loaded musket with a bayonet locked to the muzzle, he had succumbed to the Cossack's lance-thrust in the throat without an ounce of resistance.

Sutch glanced hurriedly around the table, fearing General Feversham or another guest had noticed the same look and smile on Harry's face. But no one paid the boy any heed; every visitor was too eager to recount their own story. Sutch drew a breath of relief and turned to Harry. The most cursory, least graphic descriptions of the biting days and nights in the trenches caused the lad to shiver. Even his face grew pinched, as though the iron frost of that winter were actually eating into his bones. Sutch touched him lightly on the elbow.

"You renew those days for me," he said. "Though the heat drips down the windows, I feel the chill of the Crimea."

Harry roused himself from his trance.

"The stories renew them," he replied.

"No. It is *you* listening to the stories."

Before Harry could respond, General Feversham's voice broke sharply from the head of the table: "Harry, look at the clock!"

Immediately, all eyes turned to the boy. The hands of the clock formed the acute angle of midnight. From eight o'clock, without a word or a question, he had sat at the dinner table listening. Yet even now, he rose with evident reluctance.

"Must I go, father?" he asked, and the General's guests interjected in a chorus. "Conversation is clear gain to the lad," the Major of Artillery argued. "A first taste of powder which might stand him in good stead later."

"Besides, it is the boy's birthday," the Major added. "He wants to stay; that is plain. You would not find a fourteen-year-old sit these hours without kicking the table leg unless the conversation entertained him. Let him stay, Feversham!"

For once, General Feversham relaxed the iron discipline under which the boy lived.

"Very well," he said. "Harry shall have an hour's furlough from his bed. A single hour makes little difference."

Harry's eyes turned toward his father, resting upon his face with a curious, steady gaze. It seemed to Sutch they uttered a question, and rightly or wrongly, he interpreted it into words: "Are you blind?"

But General Feversham was already speaking to his neighbors, and Harry quietly sat down, again propping his chin upon his hands, listening with all his soul. Yet he was not entertained; he was enthralled. He sat quiet under the compulsion of a spell. His face became unnaturally white, his eyes unnaturally large, while the flames of the candles shone ever redder and more blurred through a blue haze of tobacco smoke, and the level of the wine in the decanters grew steadily lower.

Thirteen years had rolled by, yet the calendar remained fixed upon June. Where once Harry Feversham had basked under the relentless sun of India, he now dwelt in a shadowed, quiet solitude within a city that felt vast and ancient. His lodging was perched high atop a sprawling, fortress-like edifice looming over Westminster, a structure so immense and irregular that a traveler crossing St. James's Park beneath the moon might mistake it for a mountain range inhabited by gnomes. Here, on the tenth floor, Harry had secured a flat during his year-long furlough, and within its dining room, a ceremony of sorts was unfolding.

The chamber was draped in somber tones, designed for rest, yet a cheerful fire crackled in the hearth, defying the chill air outside. Through an uncurtained bay window, the sprawl of London unfolded like a circuit board of gaslights. Around the table sat four men, smoke curling from their lips like ghosts.

Harry Feversham had changed little save for the growth of a fair moustache, a stark contrast to his dark hair, and the broadening of his frame. Now middle-height, long-limbed, and built like an athlete, he carried the same features scrutinized by Lieutenant Sutch so many years prior. Beside him sat two brother-officers also on leave: Captain Trench, a diminutive man whose bald crown and sharp, resourceful face betrayed a mind of remarkable speed and activity; and Lieutenant Willoughby, a figure of quite a different order. With a round forehead, a thick snub nose, and vacant, protruding eyes, Willoughby possessed an appearance of invincible dullness. He spoke rarely, and then only to wander into points long forgotten, twisting a moustache whose ends curled up toward his eyes with ridiculous ferocity. To the casual observer, he seemed of no consequence, yet he was stubborn as stone and, being slow-witted, unable to admit error. He clung to his few ideas with a tenacity that made persuasion futile.

The fourth guest was Durrance, a lieutenant of the East Surrey Regiment, summoned by a telegram.

It was June, 1882. The air was thick with the scent of impending storm. Civilians watched Egypt with anxious foreboding, while soldiers viewed it with eager anticipation. Down south, the clouds swelled like thunderheads. Mohammed Ahmed, the Dongolawi prophet, had risen a year prior, marching through the villages of the White Nile with the fervor of a revivalist preacher, promising a Savior amidst the whispers of the wind and the holy names stamped upon the eggs of the poor. "I am delighted you could join me tonight," Harry said, addressing Durrance. "I sent word to Castleton as well—an officer of ours—but he is dining with a senior official in the War Office and departs for Scotland shortly. However, I have news."

The three men leaned forward, their minds preoccupied with the East, yet Harry's news concerned neither war nor politics.

"I returned to London this morning from Dublin," Harry admitted, a faint flush of embarrassment coloring his cheeks. "I spent several weeks there."

Durrance raised his gaze from the tablecloth, meeting his friend's eyes with steady calm.

"Yes?" he asked.

"I have returned engaged to be married."

Durrance lifted his glass, a toast offered swiftly and curtly, yet it carried a weight of genuine goodwill. Between these two friends, affectionate platitudes were unnecessary. They were bound by a bond stronger than words, a tool forged for hardship and sacrifice, understood fully by both yet never spoken aloud. If duty called, it would demand everything, and they would give it without complaint.

"Thank you, Jack!" Feversham replied. "I am grateful for your good wishes. You were the one who introduced me to Ethne; I shall never forget it."

Durrance lowered his glass, pausing as if searching for a deeper sentiment. He sat silently, hands resting on the table edge, his eyes fixed on the cloth. He seemed poised to say something profound, perhaps to acknowledge the gravity of the moment, but Captain Trench's sharp, pragmatic voice cut through the hesitation.

"Will this alter your plans?" Trench asked, cutting straight to the bone.

Feversham tapped his cigar back into place. "Do you ask whether I shall resign?" he murmured slowly. "I am not certain."

"I am uncertain," he repeated. "It requires reflection. Considerations weigh heavily on either side. On one hand, there stands my father, my duty, and my career. On the other, there lies her father, Dermod Eustace."

"Trench laughs at the notion that the Irishman objects to authority," Trench quipped. "Yet does Feversham subscribe to it?"

"It is not merely that," Feversham insisted, directing his speech still toward Durrance's retreating form. "Dermod is old; his estates are crumbling. There are complications. You know, Jack?"

The appeal hung in the air, met with an absent nod. "Yes, I know," Durrance muttered, reciting a familiar phrase. "If you require whiskey, tap twice on the floor with your boot. The servants understand."

"Precisely," Feversham confirmed. He weighed his words carefully, his gaze fixed over his friends' shoulders onto the back of his oldest ally. "Furthermore, there is Ethne herself. Dermod named her appropriately. She is of her land, deeply rooted in it, specifically in County Donegal. The spirit of that country runs in her veins. I fear she could find no happiness away from Donegal—far from the peat fires, the streams, and the brown kindness of its hills. One must consider that."

He awaited a response, receiving none, and pressed on. Durrance, however, harbored no intent of reproach. He desired for Harry to speak, to pour out his heart, yet he paid no heed to the words. Standing rigid before the window, he faced the glaring lights of Pall Mall rising toward the sky, hearing the phantom rumble of a million carriages. Far below, St. James's Park lay silent and black, a quiet pool amidst the glitter. Durrance longed to flee the room into that darkness, but he dared not move without drawing attention. Instead, he rested his forehead against the glass, hoping his friend would continue talking, facing a sacrifice that must remain unspoken, betraying no sign.

Feversham did continue, and though Durrance remained deaf, Captain Trench listened with rapt attention. It became evident that Harry was laying out serious reasons, not mere excuses. Eventually, Trench was content.

"Well, I drink to you, Feversham," Trench declared, "with all due sentiments."

"I too, old man," Willoughby echoed, following suit.

They clinked glasses, honoring their comrade, and as the empty vessels rattled, a knock resonated at the door. The two officers looked up; Durrance turned from the window. Feversham bid, "Enter," and his servant placed a telegram upon the table.

Feversham tore the envelope open with careless speed, scanned the contents, and then sat utterly still. His eyes locked onto the pink paper, his face transforming instantly into grave seriousness. He sat for a considerable time, not stunned, but profoundly thoughtful. A heavy silence descended upon the room. His guests averted their eyes. Durrance returned to the window; Willoughby twisted his moustache, staring upward at the ceiling; Trench shifted his chair and gazed into the glowing embers. Each posture radiated a suspended breath. It seemed that disaster, riding close upon the heels of good fortune, had knocked at the door.

"There is no answer," Harry whispered, falling silent again. Twice he raised his head to meet Trench's gaze, as if seeking counsel, but thought better of it, returning his focus to the message. Moments later, the silence shattered, but not by any internal movement. The interruption arrived from without.

From the parade grounds of Wellington Barracks, the shrill cry of drums and fifes sounding the tattoo pierced the open window with startling clarity, like a sharp summons, before fading as the band marched away across the gravel, only to swell again. Feversham did not shift his stance, but his expression hardened into that of a listener, thinking deeply, just as he had read thoughtfully. In the years to come, this moment would haunt the memory of every guest. The warm room, the bright fire, the view of London's myriad lamps, Harry Feversham seated with the telegram spread before him, and the military beat calling loudly before dwindling into a small, pretty music that beckoned instead of commanding—all these elements formed a picture whose colors would never fade, though its meaning remained hidden in the dark.

Alone, Jock Durrance walked the length of the street toward his lodging, his mind drifting back nearly two years. It was a memory sparked by a peculiar whim of Old Dermod Eustace, the man who had once dragged the reluctant youth to a cottage by the Lennon River in Donegal. There, amidst the quiet of the countryside, Durrance had met Dermod's daughter, Ethne. To everyone else, she was merely a girl; to Durrance, she was a revelation. He had spent a night in her father's house, and throughout the evening, she had played her violin. Seated with her back to the room, she turned away from any audience, refusing to let a glance or a gesture break the spell of her concentration.

Those melodies still echoed in his mind. The girl possessed a rare gift; her bow seemed to speak directly to the strings, drawing forth sounds that transcended ordinary emotion. He remembered an overture, *Melusine*, that carried the very sob of the waves. That night, sitting in silence while Ethne's face remained turned away, Durrance had felt the music tell stories of journeys too perilous for mortal tongues, of silver paths across moonlit seas, and of voices calling from the desert's edge. It had spoken of unattainable joys and griefs so profound they became noble in their scale, of longings that defied words—all without a single note of complaint.

And now, as he walked alone, knowing that Ethne's back would forever remain turned to him, that same truth resonated. *True music does not complain.* It simply exists, pure and unburdened.

The following morning, the sun broke over London with the same warmth as ever. Durrance mounted his horse near the cluster of lilacs and laburnums at the end of the Row, expecting his friend. It was the hour and place where Harry Feversham and he had met every chance they had since leaving Oxford. But Harry did not appear. Nor did he show up for the next three weeks. While rumors swirled about the coming war, growing steadily from gossip into grim certainty, Durrance felt a hollow confusion. It seemed, to him, as if he had lost his friend entirely.

Then, finally, Harry kept the appointment, but the mood between them had shifted irrevocably.

"I told you luck might look my way," Harry said, his voice brimming with an exhilaration that cut sharply through the air. "Well, it has. I'm going to Egypt on General Graham's staff. There's talk we'll follow him down the Red Sea to Suakin after."

A spark of unmistakable envy flared in Harry's eyes, startling even Durrance. It was strange, and yet strangely pleasant, to see such a glint in his friend's gaze, especially given how little Harry wanted. But Durrance read the expression through the lens of his own ambition.

"It's rough on you," Durrance said gently, trying to soften the blow. "That your regiment has to stay behind."

Harry rode beside him in silence. As they reached the shaded benches beneath the trees, he finally spoke.

"That was expected. The day you dined with me, I sent in my papers."

"That night?" Durrance asked, turning in his saddle. "After we'd gone?"

"Yes," Harry admitted, accepting the correction. A shadow crossed his face, and for a moment, Durrance sensed a reproach in his friend's silence—but then he realized he was misreading it. Suddenly, Harry laughed, a hearty sound that broke the tension.

"It's been a long time since we bathed in Sandford Lasher," Durrance remarked.

"And frozen in those Easter vacations in the big snow-gully on Great End," Harry replied.

Both men felt a heavy sense of finality settle over them. This morning marked the closing of a volume in their shared book of life. It had been a pleasant chapter, full of laughter and easy company, but they couldn't say whether the pages that followed would hold the same promise. They looked backward through the leaves of memory before putting the book away for the last time.

"You must stay with us, Jack, when you come back," Harry urged.

Durrance schooled his features carefully, masking any sting at the word "us." If his left hand tightened slightly on the reins, it was invisible to his friend.

"If I come back," Durrance said, his voice steady. "You know my creed. I could never pity a man who dies on active service. I'd very much like to come by that end myself."

It was a simple creed, born of the simplicity of the man who spoke it. To die decently was worth far more than a lifetime of living. He said it without melancholy, without a tremor of fear. Yet, a part of him worried that Harry might interpret these words differently. He glanced quickly at his friend, searching for understanding, but found only that same puzzling look of envy in Harry's eyes.

"You see, there are worse things that can happen," Harry continued, shaking off the thought. "Disablement, for instance. Some clever men might manage to live with it, but what in the world should I do if I had to sit in a chair all my days? Just thinking of it makes me shiver." He shook his broad shoulders as if to dislodge the chill. "Well, this is the last ride. Let us gallop."

He spurred his horse forward. Harry followed suit, and side by side they raced down the sandy path. At the bottom of the Row, they stopped, shook hands, and exchanged the briefest, kindest nod before parting ways. Long ago, as a boy in Southpool, Devonshire, sitting on a wooded creek by the Salcombe estuary, Durrance had felt a restless pull. Later, Ethne recounted a local legend that Feversham had heard before, though it never failed to thrill him anew. Years ago, standing at a door high on the hill leading to the river, her horse had spooked at the sound of a pail clattering nearby. The reins slipped from her grasp, falling to the ground just as the terrified animal bolted downhill toward the sharp curve near the bridge. Seated helpless in the cart, with the horse tearing toward disaster, Ethne acted with terrifying coolness. Feversham stared ahead, his jaw tight. "You need not apologize," he rasped.

"Why?" she pressed gently. "How can I help it? I blame myself because I do not see things like other women. If you had gone and the worst had happened, I would have been lonely, yes, but I would have known that eventually, when those days were over, you and I would see much of one another."

Her words were delivered without emotion, as if stating an obvious fact. Feversham caught his breath, his chest tightening, yet he remained still, unable to trust himself to speak.

"You see, I can endure the absence of those I care for better than most," she continued, searching for the right words. "Life moves along in a dull sort of way, and then suddenly a face springs out from the crowd. You recognize it instantly as a friend, as if you had been waiting for them all along. "Unless," Feversham interjected slowly, "one has made a mistake. What if the face in the crowd is a mask? What if one recognizes the wrong person?"

Ethne shook her head decisively. "Not that kind. One may seem to err, perhaps for a long time, but in the end, the truth proves otherwise."

Her unwavering faith tightened around Feversham like a vice. "Ethne," he cried, breaking his silence, "you don't know—"

At that moment, Ethne reigned in her horse, laughing and pointing her whip forward. They had crested a hill overlooking open fields and a wood of oaks. Built into the stone wall to their left was a scarlet letter-box.

"I wanted to show you that," she interrupted cheerfully. "That is where I used to post my letters to you during the anxious times."

Feversham let slip the confession he had been preparing. "The letter-box is very convenient."

Biographers of Lord Dermod Eustace required a wary mind. Though he had died less than twenty years prior, he had swollen into a legendary figure, his anecdotes growing like barnacles upon his name. Anecdotes could be invented and sold to anyone with a knack for storytelling, and they were readily believed. However, the facts were undeniable: Dermod practiced a tyrannical hospitality, forcing strangers to stay, and was a man of a bygone century who glowered angrily at the modern world.
