It is one of those facts which a biographer records with the modesty of a cook confessing he once burnt a sauce: disgraceful to the hero, mortifying to the narrator, but indispensable to truth. Arthur Pendennis, having taken to his bed with fever and a sentimental inflammation besides, was duly submitted to the whole pharmacopoeia—bled, blistered, shaved, lectured, and dosed—until, when the bodily furnace at last cooled, the other heat went out too; and the passion which had made him talk poetry over a porter’s daughter departed as briskly as steam when the kettle is taken off the fire.

He lay on his pillow, and actually laughed—softly, for invalids must not split stitches, even when their hearts have been mended. He could not for the life of him remember what particular spice had seemed so intoxicating in Fanny Bolton. Not her wit, not her breeding, not her beauty—London could show prettier faces at any greengrocer’s stall, and better manners at any respectable dinner-table. The change, he perceived with the calm of a food-historian dissecting yesterday’s pie, was not in the dish but in the appetite. She was as she had been; only the palate that had raved over her was altered. It is a humiliating comfort to find you are cured.

Yet he was grateful too—grateful in that selfish way young men have of being thankful chiefly that they have escaped consequences. He reviewed, from the safe high ground of convalescence, the precipice he might have fallen down. At least—so he told himself—he had not tumbled. He had resisted when he was weakest; and whatever foolishness had been simmering in his head, he had not let it boil over into open shame.

Meanwhile, the quiet presence at his bedside was like warm broth after a long illness: Mrs. Pendennis’s tenderness, her watchfulness, her readiness to execute any caprice as though it were an order from the Privy Council, filled him with a peace he had not felt since childhood. Her love wrapped him round as a shawl wraps an invalid who fancies every draught a hurricane.

As for the early days of the fever—those hours in which Fanny had indeed been near him—he had only dim misty scraps, like half-remembered tunes heard through a wall. Delirium had muddled them. He could not separate what had actually happened from what his heated brain had invented. And because he had never spoken of Fanny to his mother when he was well, he could not suddenly begin to confide in her now. Silence on both sides—such an excellent precaution, and such a fatal one—kept each ignorant of what the other suffered.

For Mrs. Pendennis, seeing Miss Bolton installed as nurse, had made up her mind—without the tiresome ceremony of inquiry—that the worst stories of Arthur were true. There are tales against a man which the women who love him most are sometimes the keenest to believe; jealous affection has a sharp nose, and sniffs scandal like smoke. That pure and tender creature sat by her boy, smiling when she could, and feeling within her, under the forced calm, a horror far worse than the fever she watched—believing him stained by a sin she dared not name, and compelled to nurse him while nursing her suspicion too.

While Lamb Court kept its sick-room hush, Boulogne went on clinking glasses. Captain Shandon, reading the Pall Mall Gazette there, observed to Mrs. Shandon that Jack Finucane’s hand no longer showed in the leading articles, and that Warrington must have taken the whip again.

“I know his cut,” Shandon said, with that affectionate brutality of journalists. “Jack Bludyer hacks like a butcher. Mr. Warrington lays on neat, straight, scientific lashes—draws blood in every line.”

“Law, Charles!” cried Mrs. Shandon. “How can you talk so? Mr. Warrington always seemed a kind gentleman. I’m sure he was most kind to the children.”

“He’s kind to the children,” Shandon conceded, “and savage to the men. But never mind. Mix another tumbler, my dear. There’s little good comes of newspapers. Better here—wine plenty, brandy two francs. ‘Cras ingens iterabimus aequor’—bad luck to it.”

In truth, Warrington did Pen’s portion of the Gazette with all his might and malice. He wrote criticism, he attended theatres and concerts, and treated them as if they were political enemies. His hand was too heavy for light trifles; yet he took a grim pleasure in telling Mrs. Pendennis and Major Pendennis and Laura that Arthur’s pen was the most graceful and elegant in the whole fraternity.

“These people don’t know what style is, ma’am,” he would say. “If they did, they’d see our young one’s merit. I call him ours, for I bred him. And, bating a little wilfulness, a little selfishness, a little dandification—he’s as loyal and gentle a creature as lives. His pen can be wicked, but he would not harm a fly.”

Helen—who carried her own secret ache—sighed deep at this; and Laura, wounded as she was, felt grateful nevertheless, and loved Warrington the more for loving Pen. Major Pendennis, for his part, grew enthusiastic—almost indecently so, for him.

“He is a gentleman,” the Major told Helen, swelling like a man reciting a recipe from memory and improving it as he goes. “Every inch. The Suffolk Warringtons—Charles the First’s baronets. What could he be but a gentleman? Sir Miles—friend of the Prince of Wales—very known in London—beg your pardon, Miss Bell. Talents, accomplishments—sure to get on if he had but the motive.”

Laura blushed, partly because the Major praised so loudly, and partly because Warrington’s face—manly, worn, with those dark melancholy eyes—had set her fancy working. She had already decided, with the confidence of youth and romance, that he must have been the victim of some unhappy attachment. Catching herself at the thought, she blushed again.

Warrington took chambers close by—Grenier’s in Flag Court—and after hammering out copy in the mornings, came in the afternoons to sit in the sick-room company during those mild, sunny autumn evenings. More than once he had the honour of giving Miss Bell his arm for a turn in the Temple Gardens. When Laura asked Helen’s leave, the Major burst in, eager as a beadle for propriety.

“Yes, yes, begad—of course. It’s like the country. Everybody walks with everybody in the Gardens. There are beadles, you know—perfectly safe.”

If the Major, that self-appointed inspector of morals, saw no danger, Helen could hardly object; and besides, she was glad her girl should breathe air off the river and return with a little colour, as if the Thames—muddy old fellow—could still do a kindness now and then.

For Helen and Laura, in these days, had come to an explanation. When news arrived of Pen’s alarming illness, Laura had insisted on travelling to London with the terrified mother. Helen, still angry, refused; Laura refused the refusal. And when it seemed the poor lad might die, and when it was known that his conduct made any union hopeless, Laura—through tears that came as naturally as breath—confessed what every attentive spectator had long guessed: how fondly, truly, entirely she had loved him. She could not marry him now; must she be denied even the poor consolation of owning her love? Their tears mingled; their grief shared itself; and the journey’s terrors were softened by companionship.

Now consider poor Fanny Bolton’s case. What could she expect when dragged, as it were, into court before two such judges—mother and sweetheart? She could expect nothing but condemnation, sentence, and expulsion. Women, in such matters, are critics without mercy; and we, being Turkish in our hearts (the present narrator declares himself turbaned and bearded, and ready—Bismillah!—for the sack), applaud their severity—provided only they execute the right culprit. Let the barge be ready, by all means; only do not clap the wrong Fatima into the sack and pop her into the Bosphorus merely because you are in a hurry to get back to supper. That is all one pleads—no more, by the Prophet’s whiskers.

So the Major, far from objecting, encouraged Warrington’s intimacy with Laura as if it were an exhibition to be visited with proper tickets. Any sight in town? Warrington should escort her. Vauxhall itself? The Major would probably have said, “Perfectly harmless—beadles everywhere.” And in truth there was no harm between two honourable creatures: Warrington, who for the first time found himself in close companionship with a woman pure, artless, and high-minded; and Laura, who for the first time was thrown daily with a gentleman of strong natural gifts, varied acquirements, enthusiasm, simplicity, and humour—so different from Pen’s languid, dandified sovereignty.

In Warrington’s very roughness there was a kind of refinement. His energy, his hearty laughter, his desire to please, his respect—how unlike Sultan Pen, yawning graciously over the homage women had piled upon him until it cloyed him like too much sugar on a child’s tongue. At home he had been a despot because the women had spoiled him—fed him too continually on sweet attentions, until he grew weary of the very sweetness. Abroad, like many men, he had found his appetite sharpened by change. Does any reader dare interpret that as advice to revolt? Never! The narrator, still Turkish, still turbaned, still sentimental about slavery—would sooner cut off all heads in Christendom or Turkeydom than be henpecked. There: the domestic constitution is saved.

And yet—Pen being what he was—why had Laura loved him so? Why had she talked of him all the way from Fairoaks to London in that post-chaise, as if his childhood toothaches and bird’s-nests were epics?

Because she had seen scarcely anybody else. Because Helen praised Arthur constantly. Because he was gentlemanlike, good-looking enough, witty. And because it is in a girl’s nature to attach herself to Somebody. She does not, like an Arabian princess, fall in love with a dream; she does not usually plight her heart to a portrait in an exhibition or an engraving in a newspaper. She meets Somebody. She hears Somebody praised. She walks, rides, waltzes, talks, sits in the same church pew with Somebody. Again, again—and then “marriages are made in Heaven,” mamma says, pinning on orange-flowers, and there is a wedding breakfast. Or else the affair breaks, and poor heart—why then it twines itself round Somebody Else. We love partly for the beloved, and partly because we have within us that hunger to love. Do you suppose you would eat if you were not hungry?

So Laura had nursed Pen’s image through long solitudes, clasped it, brooded over it; and when she came to London, and Mr. George Warrington appeared daily—odd, original, powerful, amusing—what was to prevent her from finding him agreeable?

Long afterwards—when Fate, with its clumsy hand, had put each of these people into their appointed place—some of them perhaps looked back and thought those Lamb Court days strangely happy: their evening talks, their little walks, their simple recreations round Pen’s convalescent sofa. Major Pendennis ever after spoke of September in London as “doosid pleasant, begad,” and would go home to Bury Street wondering how the evening had slipped away. He climbed the Temple stairs daily with a benevolent perseverance, and—being a man to whom feeding was a species of virtue—he made interest with the chef at Bays’s to prepare little jellies, clear soups, aspics, and dainty invalid trifles, as carefully clarified as a good consommé and almost as consoling.

Doctor Goodenough allowed Pen a glass or two of pure sherry; whereupon the Major, with tears almost in his eyes, related how his noble friend the Marquis of Steyne, passing through London, had ordered any quantity of his precious Amontillado—gift of King Ferdinand himself—to be placed at Mr. Arthur Pendennis’s disposal. The widow and Laura tasted it with reverence (and disliked its bitterness, as good women often do strong wine), but Pen declared himself invigorated, Warrington pronounced it superb, and on the first day it appeared, made a mock after-dinner speech proposing the Major’s health—and Lord Steyne’s—and the aristocracy in general.

Major Pendennis returned thanks with the gravity of a man addressing the House, and used the phrase “the present occasion” the proper number of times. Pen cheered faintly from his armchair. Warrington taught Laura to cry “Hear! hear!” and rap the table with her knuckles. Pidgeon the attendant grinned, and Doctor Goodenough, arriving for his faithful gratuitous visit, found them merry as if sherry were medicine and laughter part of the prescription.

Warrington knew Sibwright, who lived below; and Sibwright, informed that his chambers had been lent for the ladies’ use, replied with a letter so polite and flowery that one might have thought the very carpet had bowed. His bed was at their disposal, his rooms at their service. Kindness gathered round the sick man like neighbours bringing broth to a cottage door. Pen’s heart melted at it; and his mother’s too, we may be sure.

In Sibwright’s chambers stood a piano—an instrument upon which that gallant lover of the arts performed himself, and excellently ill, and to which a song had been dedicated (words by Sibwright, air by his devoted friend Leopoldo Twankidillo). At this “music-box,” as Warrington called it, Laura, at first trembling and blushing—very prettily—would play and sing of an evening, simple airs and old home-songs. Her voice was a rich contralto, warm as brown bread. Warrington, who could scarcely tell one tune from another and whose whole repertoire consisted of a discordant bray of “God save the King,” sat rapt as if she were feeding him with invisible loaves. He could follow the rhythm if not the harmony; and day by day his enthusiasm grew for the pure, tender creature who made the music.

And I wonder—how did that poor pale girl in the black bonnet, who sometimes stood by the lamp-post in Lamb Court looking up at the open windows, like a hungry child outside a baker’s—how did she like to hear it?

When Pen’s bedtime came, the songs were hushed. Lights moved in the upper room—his room—whither the widow helped him. Then the Major and Warrington, and sometimes Laura, would play écarté or backgammon; or she would sit working at a pair of slippers in worsted—gentleman’s slippers. They might have been intended for Arthur, or for George, or for Major Pendennis; any one of the three would have given a great deal for those slippers, and not merely for the warmth of wool.

While this snug business went on within, the shabby old gentleman—Fanny’s father—would appear and lead away the pale girl in the black bonnet, who had no right to be out in the night air. The Temple porters, a laundress or two, and other amateurs who had listened to the concert, would drift off as well; the evening’s little domestic theatre closed, the audience dispersed into darkness and damp.

Just before ten came another musical performance: the chimes of St. Clement’s in the Strand, which played a clear cheerful psalm before striking its ten fatal strokes. As they rang, Laura folded up the slippers. Martha from Fairoaks appeared with a bed-candle and a constant smile. The Major started as if time were a surprise invented to insult him.

“God bless my soul! is it so late?”

Warrington and the Major rose, left their unfinished game, and shook hands with Miss Bell. Martha lighted them through the passage and down the stairs; and as they descended they heard her bolt and lock “the sporting door” after them upon her young mistress and herself. If there had been danger, grinning Martha said, she’d have fetched “that thar hooky soord as hung up in gantleman’s room”—meaning the Damascus scimitar with the Prophet’s names engraved upon the blade and a red velvet scabbard, which Percy Sibwright had brought from the Levant along with an Albanian dress, and had worn with such effect at Lady Mullingar’s fancy ball—where it entangled itself in Miss Kewsey’s train (she appearing in the dress in which she had been presented to their Sovereign), and led to events which have nothing to do with this history. Is not Miss Kewsey now Mrs. Sibwright? Has not Sibwright a county court?—Good night, Laura, and good night, Fairoaks Martha. Sleep well and wake happy, pure and gentle lady.
