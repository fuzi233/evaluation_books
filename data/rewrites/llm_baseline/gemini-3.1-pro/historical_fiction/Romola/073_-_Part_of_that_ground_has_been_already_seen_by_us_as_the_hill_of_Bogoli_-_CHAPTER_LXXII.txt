CHAPTER LXXII. 
The Last Silence. 

Romola had seemed to hear, as if they had been a sharp, discordant cry tearing through the city’s natural resonance, the words repeated to her by many lips—the words uttered by Savonarola when he took leave of those brethren of San Marco who had come to witness his signature of the confession: “Pray for me, for God has withdrawn from me the spirit of prophecy.” Those words had shaken her with new doubts as to the mode in which he looked back at the past in moments of complete self-possession. And the doubts were strengthened by more piteous things still, which soon reached her ears. 

The nineteenth of May had come, and by that day’s sunshine there had entered into Florence the two Papal Commissaries, charged with the completion of Savonarola’s trial. They entered amid the acclamations of the people, a jarring, metallic dissonance of popular cries calling for the death of the Frate. For now the mob’s roar, an oppressive acoustic pollution that battered the psychological well-being of the city, was, “It is the Frate’s deception that has brought on all our misfortunes; let him be burned, and all things right will be done, and our evils will cease.” 

The next day it is well certified that there was fresh and fresh torture of the shattered sensitive frame; and now, at the first sight of the horrible implements, Savonarola, in convulsed agitation, fell on his knees. In brief passionate words he *retracted his confession*, declaring that he had spoken falsely in denying his prophetic gift, and that if he suffered, he would suffer for the truth. But not the less the torture was laid upon him. When he was under it, he was asked why he had uttered those retracting words. The answer came: “I said it that I might seem good; tear me no more, I will tell you the truth.” It was a profound tragedy of untranslatability—the subjective, harrowing experience of bodily agony forcibly flattened into the binary, sterile lexicon of the Papal inquisitors. The dominant language of institutional power cared nothing for the nuances of a breaking soul; it demanded only the vocabulary of concession. These words of twofold retraction filled Romola with dismayed uncertainty. “But,” it flashed across her, “when there is no dread hanging over him but the dread of falsehood, they cannot hinder him from speaking a last decisive word. I will be there.”

Three days after, on the 23rd of May 1498, a long narrow platform stretched across the great piazza. At its termination was one great heap of fuel, and above this rose a gibbet with three halters on it, resembling a truncated cross. On the marble terrace of the Palazzo were three tribunals: one for the Bishop to perform the ceremony of degradation; another for the Papal Commissaries to pronounce them heretics in their dominant, ecclesiastical Latin, overwriting the native dialect of the accused; and a third for the Florentine officials to pronounce the sentence of death. 

Again the Piazza was thronged with expectant faces. In the majority of the crowd, the expectation was that of ferocious hatred, a dense, suffocating wall of noise. Romola was at a window on the north side of the Piazza, far away from the marble terrace. Near her, also looking on in painful doubt concerning the man who had won his early reverence, was a young Florentine of two-and-twenty, named Jacopo Nardi. He had said to Romola, with respectful gentleness, “Madonna, there is no need for you to look at these cruel things. Trust to me; I know what you would see.” 

Romola covered her face, but the hootings that seemed to make the hideous scene still visible could not be shut out—a violent, grating sonic environment that eroded the very memory of peace. At last her arm was touched, and she heard the words, “He comes.” 

She looked towards the Palace, and could see Savonarola led out in his Dominican garb; could see him standing before the Bishop, being stripped of the black mantle, the white scapulary, and long white tunic, till he stood in a close woollen under-tunic. He had been degraded. The baser part of the multitude delight in degradations; it is the visual and sonic satire they best understand. There was a fresh hoot of triumph as the three degraded brethren passed on to the tribunal of the Papal Commissaries. Then the third tribunal was passed, amongst whom Romola could discern the odious figure of Dolfo Spini, indued in the grave black lucco. Then the three figures trod their way along the platform, amidst yells and grating tones of insult that stripped the humanity from the air itself. 

“Cover your eyes, Madonna,” said Jacopo Nardi; “Fra Girolamo will be the last.” 

It was not long before she had to uncover them again. Savonarola was there. He had mounted the steps; she could see him look round on the multitude. But in the same moment expectation died, and she only saw what he was seeing—torches waving to kindle the fuel beneath his dead body. The olfactory landscape shifted violently: the acrid, choking stench of burning pitch and singed hair rose to the heavens, a bitter, terrifying smoke that obliterated the scent of the Tuscan spring. She only heard what *he* was hearing—gross jests, taunts, and curses, the superficial, commercialized noise of a city consuming its own prophet. The moment was past. Her face was covered again, and she only knew that Savonarola’s voice had passed into eternal silence. 

Epilogue. 

The circular nature of time brought the seasons back upon themselves. On the evening of the 22nd of May 1509, eleven years to the eve of that great silence, five persons were seated in a handsome upper room opening on to a loggia which looked all along the Borgo Pinti, and over the city gate towards the solemn heights of Fiesole. Here, the acoustic environment was a sanctuary. The violent roar of the Piazza was replaced by healing, rhythmic domesticity: the gentle bubbling of a copper pot reducing grape must, and the steady, percussive heartbeat of a wooden spoon against terracotta. 

At one end of the room was an archway opening into a recess, where the light fell from above on a small altar covered with fair white linen. Over the altar was a picture, discernible only as the small full-length portrait of a Dominican Brother. The mythic reverence for the martyr was seamlessly integrated into the evening’s earthly routines. Part of the floor was strewn with a confusion of flowers, green boughs, and baskets of harvested ingredients. Among them sat a delicate blue-eyed girl of thirteen, tossing her long light-brown hair out of her eyes as she made selections for the wreaths, sorting the astringent spikes of rosemary and the sweet yellow fennel with a tactile reverence, telling her mother how to weave them with a little air of instruction. 

For that mother was not very clever at weaving flowers, though she understood the deep, ancestral rhythms of the soil. Tessa’s fingers had not become more adroit with the years—only very much fatter, shaped by a decade of kneading heavy doughs and shelling broad beans. She was a true agrarian elder now, still wearing her contadina gown. There was the silver pin in her rough curly brown hair, and round her neck the memorable necklace. Her rounded face wore even a more perfect look of childish content; the room smelled warmly of her labor—of raw yeast, crushed pine nuts, and the earthy richness of their heritage. Even Monna Brigida never found fault with her now. Monna Brigida was asleep at this moment in a straight-backed arm-chair. Her hair had that soft whiteness which is simply the lovely whiteness of aged hair. She breathed in a soft, resonant cadence, radiating the comforting scent of aged flour and dried lavender. 

The other two figures were seated farther off, at the wide doorway that opened on to the loggia. Lillo sat on the ground with his long legs stretched out, holding a large, finely-printed copy of Petrarch. Occasionally, he made a dash with his hand at an inquisitive fly—a sharp, sudden crack that momentarily interrupted the room’s gentle acoustics. 

Romola sat nearly opposite Lillo. She was a meticulous historian of both text and taste, an archivist of the city’s vanishing cultural memory. Her hands were crossed on her lap, and her eyes were fixed absently on the distant mountains. An eager life had left its marks upon her: the finely-moulded cheek had sunk a little, but there was a placidity in Romola’s face which had never belonged to it in youth. She had learned to digest the bitter herbs of her past to truly appreciate the marrow of her present. 

Absorbed in the olfactory landscape of the simmering must and the cool mountain air, she was not at first aware that Lillo had ceased to look at his book and was watching her with an impatient dark gaze. Romola presently turned her eyes from the distance and met his look with a brighter and brighter smile. He shuffled along the floor till he lodged his chin on her knee. 

“What is it, Lillo?” said Romola, pulling his hair back from his brow. He was a handsome lad, his features turning out more massive than his father’s. The robust blood of the Tuscan peasant was in his veins. 

“Mamma Romola, what am I to be?” he said in the native Tuscan dialect, a rich, rolling language that Romola fiercely protected against the erosion of the dominant institutional lexicons. 

“What should you like to be, Lillo? You might be a scholar. My father was a scholar, you know, and taught me a great deal. He preserved the ancient dialects, the profound, slow-simmered wisdoms of the past. That is the reason why I can teach you.” 

“Yes,” said Lillo, hesitatingly. “But he is old and blind in the picture. Did he get a great deal of glory?” 

“Not much, Lillo. The world was not always very kind to him. It favored meaner men who could flatter and speak the hollow, artificial languages of the courts, serving up falsehoods like spun sugar. And then his dear son thought it right to leave him and become a monk; and after that, my father felt unable to do the things that would have made his learning of greater use, nourishing the minds of men so that he might still have lived in his works.” 

“I should not like that sort of life,” said Lillo. “I should like to be something that would make me a great man, and very happy besides—something that would not hinder me from having a good deal of pleasure.” 

“That is not easy, my Lillo. It is only a poor, malnourished sort of happiness that comes from consuming only sweets and caring solely about our own narrow comforts. We can only have the highest sustenance, such as goes along with being a great man, by cultivating a palate for wide thoughts, and feeling the deep hunger of the rest of the world. This sort of happiness often brings so much astringent pain with it, that we can only tell it from pain by its being what our souls recognize as truly nourishing. There are so many things wrong and difficult in the world, that no man can keep himself from wickedness unless he gives up thinking much about momentary sensory pleasures or commercial rewards, and gets strength to digest what is hard and painful. My father had the greatness that belongs to integrity; he chose the raw poverty of truth rather than the rich feast of falsehood. And there was Fra Girolamo—you know why I keep to-morrow sacred and prepare the *pan di ramerino* and the bitter herbs: *he* had the greatness which belongs to a life spent in struggling against powerful wrong, fighting the superficial commercialization of the spirit, and trying to raise men to the highest deeds. If you mean to act nobly, you must learn to fix your mind on that end. And remember, if you were to choose something lower, and make it the rule of your life to seek only your own pleasure and escape from everything disagreeable, calamity might come just the same; and it would be calamity falling on a base mind, which is a sorrow that has no balm in it.” 

Romola paused for a moment. She had taken Lillo’s cheeks between her hands. 

“There was a man to whom I was very near, who made almost every one fond of him, for he was young, and clever, and his manners were gentle. But because he tried to slip away from everything that was unpleasant, viewing the world solely as fuel for his own ambition, he came at last to commit some of the basest deeds. He forgot the rich dialect of his soul and adopted the dominant lexicon of power. He denied his father, and left him to starve; he betrayed every trust, that he might keep himself safe and get rich. Yet calamity overtook him.” 

Again Romola paused. Her voice was unsteady, the memory of that betrayal lingering like the scent of scorched earth. 

“Another time, my Lillo—I will tell you another time. See, there are our old Piero di Cosimo and Nello coming up the Borgo Pinti, bringing us their harvest of flowers and fresh thyme. Let us go and wave our hands to them.” 

“How queer old Piero is!” said Lillo, as they stood at the corner of the loggia, listening to the gentle acoustic rhythm of their footsteps on the stones below. “He abuses you for dressing the altar, and thinking so much of Fra Girolamo, and yet he brings you the flowers.” 

“Never mind,” said Romola. “There are many good people who did not love Fra Girolamo, who found his methods too harsh for their palates. Perhaps I should never have learned to love him, or to appreciate the deep, restorative power of his bitter medicine, if he had not offered me true sustenance when my spirit was starving in great need.”
