As the General’s last words still trembled in the chapel’s stale air, a figure entered by the same shadowed door through which Carmilla had so often passed—gliding in and out like a thought you cannot keep from returning.

He was the strangest old man I had ever seen: tall and spare to the point of gauntness, narrow-chested, bent as if years had pressed him into that angle, his shoulders rising like two black hills beneath a suit the colour of extinguished coals. His face was sun-browned and dried, seamed with furrows like ploughed fields left too long without rain. A hat of odd fashion sat upon him with a broad leaf-like brim. Long grizzled hair fell to his shoulders, and over his eyes—eyes that never quite met anything directly—rested gold spectacles that caught the dim light and returned it in small, insect-bright flashes.

He moved with a slow, shambling gait, as if listening to some interior metronome. At moments his chin tipped upward, as though he were taking counsel of the roof-beams and broken saints; at others he bowed toward the floor, studying the graves beneath our feet. A perpetual smile lay on his mouth—neither kindly nor cruel, only fixed, as if the muscles had learned it long ago and forgotten how to stop. His thin arms swung loosely, and his hands, lost in old black gloves far too wide, kept tracing and cutting the air in busy, abstract gestures.

“The very man!” cried the General, his voice suddenly alive with relief. He stepped forward as if the chapel were his own drawing-room and took the newcomer by the hand. “My dear Baron—how happy I am to see you. I had no hope of meeting you so soon.”

He signed to my father, who had returned to us, and drew the Baron toward him. Formal introductions were made, and at once the three fell into that earnest, low-voiced conversation in which one hears more in what is withheld than in what is said.

The Baron produced from his pocket a roll of paper and uncurled it upon the worn lid of a tomb. He held a pencil-case delicately, like a cook’s tasting spoon when he is exacting about a sauce, and began to trace lines from point to point. Their eyes moved between his paper and the fabric of the chapel, measuring the building as one measures a household—where each door opens, where each wall might conceal a cupboard, a larder, a secret. Now and then he opened a dirty little book, its yellow leaves crowded with cramped writing, and read aloud a sentence or two in a voice so thin it seemed to whistle through the cracks in his teeth.

As they sauntered down the side aisle opposite where I stood, their steps made the only steady sound: the soft scuff of boots on dust, the occasional tap of a stick. Outside, the world had its own evening noises—the far-off complaint of a bird, the wind worrying the ivy—but here the chapel seemed to swallow every natural sound and keep only what men forced into it. Even the silence had a weight, like a lid pressed down.

They began to measure distances by pacing, counting under their breaths. At last they halted before a portion of the side-wall and examined it with minute care. They pulled away the ivy that clung there, strand by strand, as a careful housekeeper strips away spoiled herbs from a bunch so the good may remain. Their sticks rapped the plaster: dull, dead knocks; then, at a certain point, a sharper answer came back, as if the wall had a hollow behind its skin. They scraped here, tapped there, and I watched their concentration with a sickening sense that the whole chapel was a body being diagnosed.

The woodman returned, and with his help they worked more roughly. Plaster fell in pale crumbs. A broad marble tablet emerged, its surface coldly smooth, letters carved in relief upon it. Beneath grime and the green veining of age, there was an escutcheon, and an inscription, and names that once would have been spoken with ceremony and now returned like a curse remembered.

It was the long-lost monument of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein.

The General—who, I fear, was not often disposed to prayer—raised his hands and eyes to heaven and stood in mute thanksgiving for some moments, as if his body could not contain the surge of triumph and dread together. Then, lowered and hoarse, I heard him say, “Tomorrow. The commissioner will be here, and the Inquisition will be held according to law.”

He turned and seized the Baron warmly by both hands. “Baron, how can I thank you? How can we all thank you? You will have delivered this region from a plague that has scourged its inhabitants for more than a century. The horrible enemy—thank God—is at last tracked.”

My father led them a little apart, out of my hearing. The General followed. I saw them glance, again and again, quickly toward me; each look was like the flick of a knife in lamplight. They spoke, and though I could not catch the words, I felt with helpless certainty that my own story—my shameful weakness, my nightly terror, the marks and languors that had made my bed a place of dread—was being laid out among them as plainly as the Baron’s paper plan lay on the tomb.

When my father returned to me, he kissed me again and again with a tenderness that was half apology and half fear. He took my hand, and his fingers were cold.

“It is time to return,” he said. “But before we go home, we must add to our party the good priest who lives but a little way from this. We must persuade him to accompany us to the schloss.”

We succeeded in that quest. The priest—grave, worn, and smelling faintly of wax and old stone, like a pantry where the bread has been stored too long—came with us. By the time we reached home I was unspeakably fatigued, my limbs heavy as though I had been kneading some dense, unwilling dough for hours and could not feel my hands.

Yet the moment I crossed the threshold my satisfaction turned to dismay.

There were no tidings of Carmilla.

No one offered me any explanation of what had occurred in the ruined chapel; it was clear my father meant, for the present, to keep it from me. But Carmilla’s sinister absence made every fragment of that scene more horrible. The air of the house had changed: even the familiar passages seemed to listen. The sounds that ought to have soothed—servants moving, a distant door closing, the faint rattle of crockery—now arrived muffled, as though some thick cloth had been hung over the world to deaden it, leaving only my own pulse too loud in my ears.

The arrangements for the night were singular. Two servants and Madame were to sit up in my room. In the adjoining dressing-room my father kept watch with the ecclesiastic. The priest performed certain solemn rites whose purport I did not understand. I smelled the acrid sweetness of incense, like resin warmed too close to a flame, and heard the measured cadence of words—Latin, I suppose—rolling on with the inevitability of a mill-wheel, each syllable striking the ear and retreating into darkness. The effect was less comfort than a new kind of unease, for the prayers did not restore the natural sounds of safety; they imposed their own soundscape, stern and vigilant, as if even God must be summoned as a guard against what moved in quiet.

I slept—or rather, drifted in and out of a strained stupor—under the weight of so many waking eyes. Yet in the days that followed, I saw the change plainly: Carmilla’s disappearance was followed by the discontinuance of my nightly sufferings. The little horror that had become my domestic routine—going to bed with dread, waking with that peculiar faintness and the memory of pressure at my throat—ceased as abruptly as if a hand had closed a tap.

In this country you have heard, no doubt, of the appalling superstition that prevails in Upper and Lower Styria, in Moravia, in Silesia, in Turkish Serbia, in Poland, even in Russia: the superstition—so we call it—of the Vampire. It is easy for those who live amid the neat noise of cities, where iron and steam and commerce make a constant din and every old tale is sold back as entertainment, to laugh at such beliefs as though they were the crude produce of rustic fear. But the countryside keeps memories differently. It does not print them; it speaks them. They pass in dialect from elder to child like a starter passed from kitchen to kitchen—alive, souring, strengthening, altered by each household, yet recognizably the same thing. And when the dialect falters, when the old words are replaced by the polite vocabulary of officials and travellers, something more than speech is lost: not only the tale, but the exact flavour of dread that taught people how to survive.

If human testimony—taken with every care and solemnity, judicially, before commissions innumerable, each consisting of many members chosen for integrity and intelligence, with reports more voluminous perhaps than exist upon any other class of cases—is worth anything, it is difficult to deny, or even to doubt, the existence of such a phenomenon. For my part, I have heard no theory by which to explain what I myself witnessed and experienced other than that supplied by the ancient and well-attested belief of the country. There are sensations for which no dominant language has adequate words; they remain untranslatable, lodged in the body—like a taste you cannot describe to anyone who has never eaten the thing itself.

The next day the formal proceedings took place in the Chapel of Karnstein.

The grave of the Countess Mircalla was opened.

The commissioner was present, and the witnesses, and the two medical men—one officially appointed and the other attending on behalf of the promoter of the inquiry. The air in the chapel was altered by their presence: not only more crowded, but more sonically cruel. Tools rang against stone. Orders were spoken and repeated. The scrape of metal on wood, the grunt of labour, the hush that fell suddenly when something resisted—each sound bit sharply, as if the building’s old silence resented being broken and answered with a deeper, colder quiet.

When the coffin was reached and opened, the General and my father each recognized—without hesitation—his perfidious and beautiful guest in the face disclosed to view.

Though a hundred and fifty years had passed since her funeral, her features were tinted with the warmth of life. Her eyes were open. No cadaverous smell exhaled from the coffin—no honest stink of corruption, no release of the earth’s long work. Instead there was only the close, sweetish odour of blood shut up too long, like iron and salt and something faintly animal. The doctors attested the marvellous fact that there was a faint but appreciable respiration, and a corresponding action of the heart. Her limbs were perfectly flexible, her flesh elastic; and the leaden coffin floated with blood, in which—seven inches deep—the body lay immersed.

Here, then, were all the admitted signs and proofs of vampirism.

The body was raised. The prescribed ancient practice was followed with that dreadful domestic thoroughness with which people, in the country, dispatch what threatens the household. There was no flourish, no theatricality—only the method of men who have slaughtered animals and cut wood and dug graves, who know where force must be placed to do its work.

A sharp stake was driven through the heart.

At the moment it entered, the vampire uttered a piercing shriek—such a sound as might escape from a living person in the last agony. It cut through every ear and seemed to strike the bones of the chapel itself, waking old echoes that had slept under stone for generations. For an instant, all the lesser noises—breath, shifting feet, the whisper of cloth—vanished beneath that single cry.

Then the head was struck off, and a torrent of blood flowed from the severed neck.

Afterward they placed the body and head upon a pile of wood and reduced them to ashes. The smoke rose thick and greasy, carrying that same iron-sweet taint, and the crackle of burning seemed almost too cheerful a sound for such work, like a hearth-fire mocked into the service of horror. When all was done, the ashes were thrown upon the river and borne away, and that territory has never since been plagued by the visits of a vampire.

My father possesses a copy of the report of the Imperial Commission, with the signatures of all who were present attached in verification of the statement. It is from this official paper that I have summarized my account of that last shocking scene.
