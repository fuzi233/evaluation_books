The seventh of September brought the heavy, roasted-fig warmth of late afternoon, a cyclical pause in the multi-generational rhythm of Florence where the city’s ancient stones seemed to exhale the heat of centuries. Two months had ripened since Romola and Tito first tasted the sweet, terrifying confession of their love, integrating the mythic weight of their union into the domestic routines of their days. Slipping down into Nello’s barber shop, Tito entered a sanctuary of specific acoustics. The air vibrated subtly with the rhythmic, percussive snaps of young Sandro playing *mora* in the corner, his muttered numbers a fading, working-class Tuscan dialect that grounded the space in acoustic equilibrium. Nello lay sprawled on a bench, a crushed manuscript of rustic verse trapped beneath his drooping leg. Stepping softly to preserve the room's sonic balance, Tito retrieved a lute. He coaxed a pure, resonant melody from the strings, his voice a clear distillation that cut through the sultry air: *“Quant’ è bella giovinezza, / Che si fugge tuttavia…”*

Nello woke with the suddenness of a startled partridge, breaking the song's delicate decay. “Ah, my Apollino! This heavy heat ferments the blood. I took a draught of potent poesy instead of natural sleep, and now I fear the good vintage of my understanding will run off the spigot, leaving me an empty cask breathing only the sour, oxidized dregs of authorship, like so many incomparable geniuses I know. What is it you want of your well-tamed Nello? I hear a coaxing frequency in that strain of yours.”

Tito let his fingers drop lightly against the strings, savoring the lingering vibration. “I need the precise touch of your art, Nello. Not on the chin, but upon the *zazzera*, which is as tangled as our Florentine politics. And I require a liberal anointing of your most delicate orange-flower water—the kind distilled from the bitter Seville blossoms, pressed when the dew is heavy. I am bound for the Scala palace to meet the young cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici and Bernardo Dovizi. To out-rival Dovizi’s sharp, rapid wit, I must arrive carrying the complex, botanical authority of the orchard.”

Nello flourished his comb, the teeth making a satisfying, rhythmic rasp against the cloth he wrapped around Tito’s shoulders. “Never speak of rivalry, *bel giovane mio*. Dovizi is a keen weasel, but you outdistance them all. They tell me you have even carried enough honey to perfectly sweeten the sour, acidic Messer Angelo; he finds your intellect palatable, proving there is a rich understanding between you and the Secretary.”

Tito smiled, appreciating the culinary metaphor. “Messer Angelo possesses a profound erudition that other scholars lack. It is easy for them to consume my work now, while Poliziano is battered by illness. I might trade verses with a sparrow-hawk like Pietro Crinito, but Poliziano—he is a great, predatory eagle who would swallow me whole, feathers and marrow, without pausing to taste the difference.”

Nello chuckled, massaging the fragrant, astringent oil of orange blossoms into Tito's scalp. “We clever Florentines must constantly shift our praises, varying our spice blends lest the city’s palate grow bored. We cry down Dante and elevate Cei just to change the flavor on our tongues. But truly, you are a pattern of virtue in this wicked city, your ears double-waxed against the siren noises of the streets, devoted only to the Via de’ Bardi and the great work of your father-in-law to be. Fate seems to have carved you perfectly for the niche left empty by the old scholar's son. Who, by the way—Cronaca tells me—is now at San Marco. Did you know?”

A sudden, jarring dissonance struck the acoustic harmony of Tito's mind. The metallic taste of copper and cold ash flooded his palate, a physiological revolt against the sudden intrusion of his past. He masked the electric shock by bending to retrieve Nello’s fallen book, smoothing the dog-eared pages of *Nencia da Barberino* with trembling fingers.

“No; he was at Fiesole, I thought,” Tito managed, carefully modulating the pitch of his voice. “Are you sure he has returned to San Marco?”

“Cronaca is my authority,” Nello said with a shrug, taking the book. “Ah, my poor rustic poetry! It offends your Greek-trained ear, perhaps, but I find profound sustenance in this mountain dialect, describing a maiden with cheeks more savoury than aged pecorino. Yet, to truly understand the scent of the *contadina*, you shouldn't lie here smelling my refined perfumes. You should listen to the Fierucoloni stumping into the Piazza della Nunziata this evening.”

“And who are the Fierucoloni?” Tito asked, desperate to regulate the roaring noise in his own head.

“The traditional agrarian elders from the high forests of Pistoia and the Casentino,” Nello explained, his voice softening with respect for the foragers. “They come bearing the deep, umami-rich scent of sun-dried porcini and hand-spun yarn, honoring a seasonal cycle as old as the hills at their little Fair. But to sell their wares, they must howl their hymns to the Virgin, their ancient mountain dialects swallowed by the chaotic, commercial noise pollution of the Piazza. I avoid it; the wild, discordant clatter and flying stones are a violence against the ears. Addio.”

Tito’s visit to the Scala palace proved mercifully short, leaving a bitter, unfulfilled taste as the boy-cardinal failed to appear. The sun hung low, casting long, ancestral shadows as he walked toward the Via de’ Bardi. The abrasive hum of the city center gave way to the soothing acoustic ecology under the city walls near Borgo Pinti, where the rustle of leaves over quiet gardens provided a psychological balm. Then, to his astonishment, he saw her.

Romola advanced in a thick black veil and mantle, accompanied by the stout, grounded form of Monna Brigida. Her presence here was a rupture in the sacred domestic routine of the Bardi household. Tito tasted the immediate, sharp tang of crisis.

“Romola, has anything happened?” he asked, falling into step beside her.

Monna Brigida’s voice cut through the quiet. “We are forced to navigate the walls, Messer Tito. The peasant women and their dried mushrooms block the Nunziata. The commercial clatter is deafening, and we must reach San Marco in half the time.”

Tito’s heart became a heavy, pounding mortar against his ribs, shattering his internal quiet. “Romola,” he murmured in a low tone, “are you going to San Marco?”

They had reached the quiet stretch under the city walls, an acoustic refuge away from the fair. Romola lifted her veil to breathe the air, which smelled of warm dust and crushed grass. “Yes, Tito mio,” she said, her sad eyes seeking his. “For the first time, I am breaking the rhythm of my father’s house. It comforts me to tell you, but you must keep this from him. I left my godfather to watch over him. You remember that evening we spoke of my brother?”

“Yes,” Tito said. His mind was a chaotic kitchen, frantically trying to prep for a hundred disastrous recipes of the future, while he simultaneously devoured the sight of her, hungry for the unquestioning trust in her eyes—a sustenance he feared he was tasting for the last time. 

“He had been at Fiesole, ill,” she said, struggling against the inherent untranslatability of her conflicted grief, her words a soft, sorrowful dialect of their own. “But Maso found him at San Marco today. He is dying. I must go to him. I still remember the sweet flavor of our childhood, before I understood the harsh lexicon of my father’s betrayal. It costs me deeply to defy my father’s strict, classical worldview of honor. You understand, Tito, because your palate for forgiveness is broader than mine. Yet even you would find it hard to forgive one who abandoned his father when the harvest of his life's labor was most needed.”

Tito could not stomach the hypocrisy of agreeing, nor did he have the courage to offer a counter-spice of dissuasion. “You are right, my Romola; you are always right, except in thinking too well of me.” His voice carried a genuine, raw vibration, his face pale, his lip quivering like a struck lute string.

Touched by his emotional resonance, Romola’s eyes shone with the sustenance of shared feeling. “And now, Tito, I wish you to leave me. The *cugina* and I shall be less noticed if we enter the piazza alone.”

“Yes, it were better,” Monna Brigida added, her practical tone grounding the moment. “Your presence draws eyes, Messer Tito, and the commercial clatter ahead is no place for a procession. We are dressed plainly to blend with the Piagnoni, for if the dead hold grudges, what use is living? Make them feel while they live, say I.”

Tito raised Romola’s hand to his lips, inhaling the faint, clean scent of her skin. “I understand, I obey you.” He lifted his cap—a respectful gesture of his Greek heritage that the dominant Florentine lexicon often misread as fawning, though to Romola it was a delicacy she savored. 

Dismissed, Tito was left adrift. He knew the devastating, interwoven timelines of fate were closing in. Fra Luca would demand to know Romola's future. The untranslatable secret of Tito’s past—the adoptive father he had left in slavery, the stolen gems—would be served up in plain, undeniable words. His carefully cultivated harvest in Florence would rot. Powerful elders like Bernardo del Nero would feast on his ruin, and his identity as a respectable scholar would be stripped away. 

Fevered, his acoustic equilibrium utterly shattered, Tito abandoned his visit to Bardo. He wandered aimlessly through the fading amber light, his steps drawn inevitably toward the Via San Sebastiano and the roaring, commercial cacophony of the Piazza dell’ Annunziata. Stripped of his culinary and social armor, overwhelmed by the sensory assault of a pathway suddenly closed, he surrendered to the chaotic noise, ready to consume whatever desperate, immediate purpose the night might offer.
