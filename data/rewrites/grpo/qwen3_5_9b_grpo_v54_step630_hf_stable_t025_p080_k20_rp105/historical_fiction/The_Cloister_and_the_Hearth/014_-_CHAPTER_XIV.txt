The back door crashed open with a violence that echoed through the quiet house, signaling the end of their sanctuary. In an instant, the room was hemmed in by the threat of intrusion. Yet, amidst the chaos, Margaret found a sudden reserve of composure. With a whisper that cut through the panic, she commanded, "Tell them he was here, but is gone." Seizing Gerard by the arm, she nearly dragged him up the rough wooden steps leading to her father's chamber, her own room lying just beyond.

The pounding on the door resumed, relentless and demanding.

"Who dares knock at this hour?" a voice called from within.

"Open, or you shall answer for it!"

"I will not open to thieves; honest men are all asleep."

"Open to the law, Martin Wittenhaagen, or you shall rue the day!"

"Why, that is Dirk Brower's voice," Martin muttered, recognizing the tone. "What brings you so far from Tergou?"

"Open, and you will know."

Slowly, deliberately, Martin drew the bolt. The door swung inward, and Dierich and four burly companions surged in, letting in the man who had entered through the rear.

"Now, Martin, where is Gerard Eliassoen?" Dierich demanded, his eyes scanning the room.

"Gerard Eliassoen? Why, he was here but moments ago!" Martin replied, his voice steady despite the tension.

"Was he here?" Dierich's face fell. "And where is he now?"

"They say he has fled to Italy. What is to be done?"

"No matter. When did he leave? Do not tell me he vanished in such a storm!"

A sneer curled Martin's lips. "There is a fuss about Gerard Eliassoen," he said with mock contempt. He lit a candle and sat calmly by the fire, beginning to wind fine silk around his bowstring where the arrow's notch had worn it thin. "Let me explain," he said casually. "Do you know his brother Giles? A little misbegotten imp, all head and arms? Well, he tore over here on a mule, screaming something I could not hear from my distance, only the noise. It startled Gerard. Immediately after he departed, there was much crying and kissing, and then Gerard left. They tell me he has gone to Italy—perhaps you know where that is, for I do not."

Dierich's expression darkened further at this account. There was no flaw in Martin's tale; a more cunning man might have lied too freely and raised suspicion, but Martin had done his task perfectly. He had spoken only the single falsehood he was instructed to utter, inventing nothing else.

"Folks," Dierich said, his voice grim, "I fear he speaks the truth. I warned the burgomaster of this very possibility. He met the dwarf galloping Peter Buyskens's mule from Sevenbergen. 'They have sent that imp to Gerard,' he said, 'so Gerard must be at Sevenbergen.' 'Ah, master!' I replied, 'it is too late now. We should have thought of Sevenbergen before wasting our time hunting the odd corners of Tergou for those cursed parchments we shall never find until we find the man who took them. If he was at Sevenbergen, and they sent the dwarf to him, it must have been to warn him we are after him. He is leagues away by now,' I told him. Confound that chalk-faced girl! She has outwitted us bearded men. So I told the burgomaster, but he would not hear reason. A wet jerkin apiece is all we shall get from this job."

Martin grinned coolly in response to Dierich's frustration.

"However," Dierich added, "to satisfy the burgomaster, we must search the house."

Martin's demeanor shifted instantly to grave seriousness. Dierich noted the change immediately. After a moment's reflection, he ordered, "Post two of you outside, one on each side of the house, to ensure no one jumps from the upper windows. The rest come with me."

Taking the candle, Dierich ascended the stairs, followed by three of his comrades, leaving Martin alone in the hallway.

The stout soldier hung his head, the initial success of their mission now seeming like a cruel illusion. Suddenly, a spark of hope ignited within him. Gerard must be in Peter's room or Margaret's; neither was high above the ground. Gerard would leap out. Dierich had left a guard below, but what then? For a fleeting half-minute, Gerard and Martin would be two against one, and in that brief span, anything might happen.

Martin held the back door ajar and watched. Light spilled from Peter's room. "Curse the fool!" he muttered. "Is he going to let them take him like a girl?"

The light then shifted to Margaret's bedroom. Still, no window was open. If Gerard had intended to escape that way, he would not have waited until the men were inside. Martin realized this immediately and left the door, descending to the foot of the stairs to listen.

Silence stretched, growing heavier with every passing second. He began to believe Gerard must have slipped out the window while the men occupied the rooms. But this conviction was suddenly and rudely shattered.

Faint cries drifted from the inner bedroom—Margaret's.

"They have taken him," Martin groaned. "They have caught him."

It flashed across his mind that if they removed Gerard, his life would be forfeit, and if harm came to him, Margaret's heart would break. Casting his eyes wildly around the room like a savage beast seeking an exit, he formed a resolution characteristic of those iron times and a soldier driven to the brink. He stepped to each door in turn, mimicking Dierich's voice sharply, "Watch the window!" Then, quietly, he closed and bolted both doors. He picked up his bow and six arrows, fitting one to the string while stowing the others in his quiver. He placed his knife on a chair behind him, hilt facing him, and waited at the foot of the stairs with a calm determination to slay those four men or be slain by them. He knew he could dispatch two with his arrows before they could reach him, leaving Gerard and himself to fight hand-to-hand with the remaining pair. Besides, he had seen men panic-stricken by such a sudden assault. Should Brower and his men hesitate even an instant before closing with him, Martin would shoot three instead of two, tipping the odds in their favor.

He need not wait long. Heavy footsteps echoed in Margaret's room, growing closer. The light advanced, accompanied by voices.

Martin's heart, though stout, hammered hard at the sound of men marching toward their deaths—and perhaps his own. Four against one in a battlefield of ten feet square was long odds, especially if Gerard was bound and powerless to aid. Yet this man, who had once trembled in his shoes at the sight of a lantern, never wavered in this awful moment of real danger. He stood there, his body braced for combat, his eye glowing, equally ready to take life and lose it. It was a desperate gamble; to win meant instant and lifelong exile, while to lose meant death upon that very floor.

Dierich Brower and his men found Peter in the first stages of sleep. They ransacked his cupboards, drove their knives into an alligator skin nailed to his wall, and searched beneath his bed. It was a large room, seemingly full of hiding places, yet Gerard was nowhere to be found.

Next, they moved to Margaret's room. The sight of it was discouraging—it was small, bare, and devoid of cupboards. However, there was a large fireplace and chimney. Dierich's gaze fell immediately upon these. There, sleeping on an old chest not a foot high, with no attempt to cover it, lay the beauty of Sevenbergen. The sheets were snowy white, as was Margaret's own linen. She lay there, looking like a lily fallen into a rut.

Soon, she awoke, sitting up in bed in amazement. Upon seeing the men, she began to scream faintly, praying for mercy.

Her presence made Dierich Brower ashamed of his errand.

"This is a to-do," he said, slightly confused. "We are not going to hurt you, my pretty maid. Lie still, shut your eyes, and think of your wedding-night while I look up this chimney to see if Master Gerard is there."

"Gerard! In my room?"

"Why not? They say that you and he—"

"Cruel! You know they have driven him away from me—driven him from his native place. This is a blind. You are thieves; you are wicked men; you are not men of Sevenbergen, or you would know Margaret Brandt better than to look for her lover in this room of all others in the world. Oh, brave! Four great hulking men, armed to the teeth, to insult one poor honest girl! The women that live in your own houses must be naught, or you would respect them too much to insult a girl of good character."

"There! Come away, before we hear worse," Dierich said hastily. "He is not in the chimney. Plaster will mend what a cudgel breaks, but a woman's tongue is a double-edged dagger, and a girl is a woman with her mother's milk still in her." He beat a hasty retreat. "I told the burgomaster how 'twould be."
