Behind Madame Beck’s house on Rue Fossette lay a garden—surprisingly large for the heart of a crowded continental city, and soft with remembered sweetness even now, across the stretch of all the years since. Time, like distance, wraps quiet, overlooked places in a tender haze, and set all about by blank stone walls and sun-baked pavement, one small shrub, one patch of planted, green earth, feels like a stolen miracle.

A very old tale clung to the house, too, that it had once been a convent, centuries back, when the land around was still tilled fields and shaded avenues, the kind of deep, leafy seclusion a religious order ought to hide within. Some horror had unfolded on this site, so the story went, something terrible enough to leave a ghostly legacy: a vague, half-forgotten rumour of a nun in black and white robes, seen wandering the grounds on certain dark nights. The ghost itself must have been built out generations ago, the city closing all around, but relics of that old convent orchard remained: gnarled, ancient fruit trees, their branches twisted like old knuckles. The oldest of them all was a patriarch of a pear tree, almost entirely dead, save for a few loyal boughs that every spring still burst into drifts of perfumed white blossom, and every autumn bore clusters of honey-sweet fruit. Scrape away the mossy earth between its half-exposed roots, and you could catch a glint of a smooth, hard black slab. The legend, never proven, never quite fading, said that slab was the lid of a vault, where a medieval conclave of monks had bricked a young girl alive beneath the grass and flowers for breaking her vows. It was her shadow the timid had feared for hundreds of years after her bones turned to dust; her black robe and white veil that moonlight and shifting shadow had teased the eye with, fluttering through the garden thickets on windy nights.

But far more than ghost stories, the old garden had its own quiet charms. Summer mornings I would rise early to wander it alone, summer evenings I would linger long after the pupils had been called indoors, to meet the rising moon, catch one soft brush of the evening breeze, or almost taste the fresh fall of dew before it fully settled. The turf was bright green, the gravelled walks pale and clean, and sun-bright nasturtiums clustered in glowing banks around the bases of the gnarled old orchard trees. There was a wide arbour draped in the shade of a tall acacia, and a smaller, far more secluded bower tucked into vines that ran all along a high grey wall, twisting their tendrils into a soft, tangled knot of green, hanging heavy with sweet clusters of fruit right where jasmine and ivy wove their branches together.

At midday, of course, when the whole of Madame Beck’s school poured out noisily to play, day students and boarders alike yelling and chasing, vying for volume with the boys from the college next door, the garden was nothing but a trampled, ordinary, unremarkable place. But at sunset, after vespers, when all the day pupils had gone home and the boarders were quiet over their books, it was a paradise to wander the empty alleys, and hear the bells of St. Jean Baptiste peal out soft and sweet over the rooftops.

One evening I stayed later than usual, held fast by the deepening hush, the mellow cool air, the faint scent of flowers that never saw direct sun, blooming only for the dew and the dark. I could see a light glowing in the oratory window, where the Catholic household were gathered for their evening prayers— a ritual I, as a Protestant, was sometimes exempt from attending. “Stay just a little longer,” the quiet and the thin young moon whispered to me, “no one will notice you’re gone for another quarter hour. The day’s heat and noise have worn you thin. Enjoy these precious, quiet minutes.”

The garden was walled in by the windowless backs of other houses, one full side bordered by the rear of the neighbouring boys’ college: all blank stone, save for a few high attic loopholes that opened out from the servants’ bedrooms, and one lower casement that was said to belong to a master’s study. The alley that ran parallel to that high stone wall was strictly forbidden to all pupils: l’allée défendue, they called it. Any girl who dared step foot there faced the harshest penalty Madame Beck’s gentle house rules allowed. Teachers could walk there with impunity, but the path was narrow, the neglected shrubs had grown so thick on either side they wove a roof of leaves overhead that sunlight only pierced in rare, dappled flecks. Even in the day it was almost empty, and after dark no one but me ever dared venture near.

I was drawn to it, from the first. The seclusion, the soft gloom, felt made for someone as quiet as I was. For a long while I held back, afraid of seeming strange, but slowly, as everyone got used to my quiet, peculiar little habits, I began to go there regularly. I tended to the pale little wild flowers that grew between the bushes, cleared out the dead autumn leaves that choked a weathered old rustic seat at the far end, borrowed a pail of water and a scrub brush from the cook Goton, and scrubbed every spot of mould and moss off the wood until it was clean. Madame Beck saw me at work and smiled her approval, sincere or not, I could never be sure, but she seemed kind enough. “See how neat she has made it, our Miss Lucy?” she said. “You like this little alley, then?” I told her yes, it was quiet and shady. “Quite right,” she agreed, all gentle goodwill, and said I was welcome to use it as much as I liked, since I had no duty to supervise the pupils. She only asked that I let her small children join me there sometimes to practice their English.

That night in question, I was sitting on that hidden seat, listening to the far-off hum of the city. It was not truly far, of course: the school sat in the very centre of the city, a five minute walk from the public park, ten from the grand, palatial avenues. Just beyond the walls, wide brightly lit streets teemed with life, carriages rolling past on their way to balls and the opera. The same hour that dimmed all our lamps and drew the curtains around every bed in our quiet pensionnat was the hour that summoned the rest of the city out to feast and celebrate. I barely thought of that contrast. I had few gay, worldly instincts: I had never seen a ball or an opera, and while I sometimes wondered what they looked like, it was no hungry, burning wish to shine in that bright world, only the soft, calm curiosity of someone who just wanted to peek at something new.

A thin crescent moon hung in the sky, visible through a gap in the leaves overhead. The stars beside her were no strangers, even in this foreign city: I had known them since childhood. I had seen that same golden crescent, with the dark faint outline of the full moon cradled in its curve, hanging over an old thorn bush on the edge of a field back in England, hundreds of miles and years away, just as it hung now over the spire of St. Jean Baptiste. Oh, my childhood. Passive as I lived, quiet as I was, cold as I seemed to most people, when I thought of those old days I could feel, sharp and bright, all the feelings I spent most of my time lulling to sleep. The present called for stoicism, the future I could imagine for myself called for complete stillness, and I held the quick, beating core of my nature carefully, almost buried, to keep it from waking and aching.

I remembered well how some things, thunderstorms for example, used to fill me with dread, because they woke that sleeping self and stirred a craving so deep I could never satisfy it. Once, a terrible thunderstorm had raged through the city, a hurricane that shook the dormitory windows while all the Catholic girls rose in panic to pray to their saints. The tempest seized me too, roused me roughly back to full, throbbing life, and I crept out of bed, climbed out the casement window onto the narrow ledge that jutted over the lower roof, sitting there in the wild dark and rain, watching the clouds split open to pour down white, blinding sheets of lightning. For 24 hours after that I ached so badly for something, anything, to pull me out of my small, narrow life and carry me forward, that I had to snuff that longing out like a flame— figuratively driving a nail through its head, like Jael did to Sisera. But those longings never truly die, they only lie stunned, and every so often they twist against the nail, and my whole being aches.

That night, though, there was no pain. My Sisera lay quiet, slumbering in his tent, and something soft, like an angel, knelt beside him, dropping balm on his temples, holding a magic glass that showed soft, sweet, hopeful visions. I was filled with a gentle, unnameable peace, no specific hope attached, only a quiet feeling of courage, of lightness in my chest. Such a soft, rare mood could only be a harbinger of something good, I thought. But I was wrong. The crude, rude real world burst in all too soon.

I heard a faint creak of a casement hinge somewhere above me, over the high wall. Before I could even look up to find where it had opened, something hit the tree branches over my head and dropped light at my feet. The clock of St. Jean Baptiste struck nine. It was not fully dark yet, the last gold of the sunset still glowing low in the sky, enough light that I could have read small print if I held it close. The thing at my feet was a small casket, carved from white and coloured ivory, its lid half open. Inside, a cluster of sweet violets lay wrapped around a closely folded sheet of pink paper, a note addressed in looping script: Pour la robe grise. To the grey dress. I was wearing my plain French grey dress that night, of course. A love letter? I had heard of such things, but never once dreamed of receiving one.

Not a moment did I believe it was meant for me. No man had ever looked at me that way. All the other teachers had their little romantic daydreams, every girl over fourteen knew of some future suitor most of them were already promised to by their parents as children. I had never once dared step foot in that world. All the other girls and teachers swore that Dr. John’s bright blue eyes had rested on them with an admiring glance at one time or another. But I knew, for me, those eyes were always calm, blank as the summer sky they matched. No one ever noticed me. So this could not be for me. I opened the note and read it, translated from the gushing French:
“Angel of my dreams! A thousand, thousand thanks for keeping your promise! I scarcely dared hope you would agree. You said the enterprise was so dangerous, the hour so late, this alley so strictly guarded by that terrible dragon, the English teacher— that Britannic prude you described, a brute as gruff as an old grenadier corporal, as sour as a cloistered nun! Little Gustave, sick with fever, was moved to a master’s chambers in the college, the very room whose window overlooks your little garden. I am his favourite uncle, you see, so I was allowed to visit. How my heart pounded when I leaned out the window and saw the glint of your straw hat, the sweep of your grey dress through the leaves! But why would you not look up, cruel one? One glance of your beautiful eyes would have revived me entirely. I write this in the greatest haste, while the doctor tends to Gustave, tucking it into this little casket with the sweetest violets that grow, none half so sweet as you, my Peri. Ever yours, you know who!”

“I wish I did know who,” I thought to myself, and even more than the writer, I wished I knew who the note was addressed to. Half the women in the house owned a grey dress that same season, it was the height of fashion: Madame Beck wore a grey day dress, another teacher, three of the older boarders, and even Rosine the portress. The straw hat was nothing special either, a dozen of us had identical plain ones for working in the garden.

I knew I had to go in soon. Lights were bobbing in the dormitory, the pupils getting ready for bed. Half an hour more and all the doors would be locked, all the lamps snuffed. The front door still stood open, letting the cool night air into the overheated house. Then a soft, cautious little bell tinkled, not loud at all, just a warning whisper. Rosine darted out of her lodge to open it, stood talking to whoever was on the doorstep for a minute or two, before she picked up her lamp and stepped out onto the garden steps, lifting it to look around. “What nonsense!” she laughed, coquettish. “There’s no one out here.”

“Let me pass,” I heard a familiar voice plead. “I only need five minutes.” And tall, broad-shouldered Dr. John, whom everyone in the pensionnat thought so handsome and imposing, stepped past her and strode out into the garden, his boots soft on the grass. A man in the garden after dark was almost sacrilege, but he moved like he knew he was privileged, trusting the night to hide him. He wandered the alleys, looking this way and that, trampling flowers, snapping branches in his search, until he pushed through the foliage and found me in l’allée défendue.

“Dr. John,” I said, holding up the ivory casket. “It’s found.”

He did not even ask who had it. His sharp eye had spotted it the second he rounded the bush. “Do not betray her,” he said, looking at me almost as if I really was that dragon the note had described.

“I cannot betray what I do not know,” I told him. “Read that note, you will see it gives away no names at all.”

He took it from my hand and read, and his face flushed bright red, hot with shame. “This is too much,” he said. “Cruel. Humiliating.” I agreed, seeing how upset he was.

“Will you tell Madame Beck what you found?” he asked. “Start a whole scandal, an esclandre?”

I said I had thought I ought to, that Madame was far too prudent to make a public fuss over something like this happening in her school. He stood meditating, far too proud and honourable to beg me to keep a secret that I thought was my duty to report. I wanted to do right, but I could not bear to see him grieve or get in trouble. Then I spotted Rosine standing in the doorway, her own grey dress glowing in the faint lamplight. Of course. No pupils were involved here.

So I held out the casket, the violets, the note, and said: “If you can assure me none of Madame Beck’s pupils are mixed up in this, I will happily forget the whole thing. Take it all. I will not breathe a word.”

“Look there,” he whispered suddenly, his hand closing over the casket as he pointed through the branches.

I looked. There was Madame Beck, wrapped in her shawl, her nightgown, soft slippers on her feet, creeping down the steps as quiet as a cat, about to round the corner and catch us both in two seconds. But Dr. John was as light on his feet as a leopard when he wanted to be. He waited until she turned her back, then bounded across the garden in two silent strides, Rosine yanking the front door shut behind him the very second he slipped inside, before Madame could even turn around again.

I did not run. I stayed right there, and met her openly. It was well known I liked to linger in the garden at twilight, but never once had I stayed this late. I expected a reprimand. But Madame was all soft sweetness, no surprise, no rebuke. With her legendary, perfect tact, she claimed she had only stepped out to “taste the evening breeze.” “What a beautiful night!” she cried, looking up at the stars. “How fresh the air is!” Instead of sending me inside immediately, she walked a few slow turns with me up the main alley, leaning gently on my shoulder for support up the steps when we finally went in, and kissed my cheek goodnight. “Sleep well, my dear good friend,” she said.

I lay in my bed later, awake and smiling, thinking of her. That overdone, over-sweet kindness was a dead giveaway. She had caught some glimpse through the branches, heard the low murmur of a man’s voice drifting through the quiet house, picked up some strange shadow or unusual footstep. She knew something odd had happened that night, even if she did not know what, and she had a new little tangled mystery to unpick. And there I was, plain quiet Miss Lucy, caught right in the middle of it, like a silly little fly stuck in a web I could not yet see all the threads of.
