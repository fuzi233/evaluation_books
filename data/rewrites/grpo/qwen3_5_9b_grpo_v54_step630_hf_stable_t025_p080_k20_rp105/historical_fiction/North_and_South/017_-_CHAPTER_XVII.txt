Margaret stepped out of the house with a heaviness in her step that belied the cheerfulness of the morning. Yet, as she walked down the length of Milton Street, the very air seemed to lift her spirits. The grey gloom of her own home gave way to the bustling vitality of the city. Men with hands deep in their pockets sauntered with a casual arrogance; groups of girls laughed aloud, their voices ringing with a boisterous independence that struck her as strange and new. She noticed the rougher elements lingering near the beer-houses, smoking and offering sharp commentary on the passersby. While the prospect of walking through this raucous thoroughfare to reach the fields she intended to visit seemed daunting, a sudden impulse took hold of her. Instead of seeking the quiet solitude of the countryside, she decided to visit Bessy Higgins. It would lack the restorative peace of a country walk, but it offered a different kind of solace—one born of shared understanding.

Inside the Higgins household, Nicholas sat by the fire, the smoke of his pipe curling around him, while Bessy rocked gently on the opposite side. Nicholas rose as Margaret entered, pushing his chair aside to lean against the chimney-piece in a posture of relaxed authority.

"How fares she?" Margaret asked, turning her attention to Bessy.

"She is rather down in the mouth regarding her spirits," Bessy replied, her voice flat, "though her health holds. She dislikes this strike. She is set upon piece-work and quietness at any price."

"This is the third strike I have witnessed," Bessy sighed, as if the repetition itself explained the weariness.

"Third time pays for all," Nicholas declared, reclaiming his pipe. "See if we do not hang the masters this time. See if they do not come begging us to return on our own terms. We missed the mark before; this time, our plans are laid deep."

"Why do you strike?" Margaret asked, her curiosity piqued. "To stop work until you secure your desired wage, is that not the definition? I confess my ignorance; in my homeland, such a thing is unheard of."

"I wish I were there," Bessy murmured wearily. "But I am weary of strikes. This shall be the last I endure. Before it concludes, I shall be in the Great City—the Holy Jerusalem."

"She is so consumed with the life to come that she cannot grasp the present," Nicholas said, addressing Margaret. "I, however, am bound to do the best I can here. A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush. Thus, our paths diverge."

"But," Margaret pressed, "if the people strike, as they do in my country among the field laborers, the seed remains unsown, the hay uncut, the corn unreaped."

"Well?" Nicholas challenged, puffing thoughtfully.

"What becomes of the farmers?"

"They will either abandon their farms or pay a fair wage," Nicholas answered.

"Suppose they cannot, or refuse to do so?" Margaret reasoned. "They cannot relinquish their land instantly, yet without harvest, they have no income to pay the laborers' wages the following year."

Nicholas continued to puff, finally breaking his silence. "I know nothing of your ways in the South. I have heard they are a race of spiritless, downtrodden men, crushed by poverty, too dazed by their misery to recognize when they are exploited. Here, it is different. We know when we are wronged. We have enough blood in us to endure it no longer. We simply take our hands from our looms and declare, 'You may crush us, but you shall not exploit us, my masters!' And be damned to them, they shall not succeed this time!"

"I wished I lived in the South," Bessy interjected.

"There is much to bear there," Margaret said gently. "Sorrows exist everywhere. There is grueling physical labor performed with scant nourishment to sustain the body."

"But it is outdoors," Bessy countered. "Away from the endless noise and the sickening heat of the mills."

"It is sometimes in driving rain, sometimes in biting cold. A young person may endure it, but an old man is racked with rheumatism, bent and withered before his time, yet he must continue, or face the workhouse."

"You thought I was so enamored with the Southern ways," Nicholas observed.

"So I am," Margaret smiled, caught in the irony. "I only meant, Bessy, that there is good and evil in all things. Since you felt the evil here, I thought it fair you should know the evil there."

"And you say they never strike in the South?" Nicholas asked abruptly.

"No," Margaret replied. "I believe they possess too much sense."

"And I believe," Nicholas retorted, extinguishing his pipe with such force that it shattered, "that it is not that they possess too much sense, but too little spirit."

"Oh, father!" Bessy cried. "What have you gained from striking? Recall the first strike when Mother died. We were forced to stop work—you suffered most of all. Yet, many continued working weekly at the same wage until the jobs vanished, leaving them destitute for life."

"Aye," Nicholas admitted. "That strike was poorly managed. Fools or traitors seized control. You will see; this time shall be different."

"But you have not told me what you are striking for," Margaret insisted.

"There are five or six masters who have prospered for two years, increasing their wealth while paying us the same wages. Now they demand we accept less. We refuse. We shall starve them out first and see who works for them then. They will have killed the goose that laid the golden eggs."

"And so you plan to die in order to take revenge upon them!"

"No," Nicholas said firmly. "I merely anticipate the chance of dying at my post sooner than yielding. That is what people call noble in a soldier, and why not in a poor weaver? But," he added, "a soldier dies for the Nation, for others."

He laughed grimly. "My lass, you are young, but do you think I can support three people—Bessy, Mary, and myself—on sixteen shillings a week? Is it for myself I strike? It is as much for the cause of others as the soldier, only mine is personal. I fight for John Boucher, who lives next door with a sick wife and eight children, none of them of factory age. I do not fight only for him, though he is a poor soul who can barely manage two looms; I fight for justice. Why should we receive less now than two years ago?"

"Do not ask me," Margaret said, refusing to yield despite sensing she was irritating him. "I am ignorant. Ask your masters. Surely they will provide a reason. It cannot be an arbitrary decree without cause."

"You are merely a foreigner," Nicholas sneered. "Ask the masters! They will tell you to mind your own business while they mind theirs. Our duty is to accept the reduced wage and be grateful; their duty is to drive us down to the point of starvation to swell their profits. That is the truth."

"But," Margaret persisted, "perhaps the state of trade prevents them from offering the same remuneration."

"State of trade!" Nicholas scoffed. "That is mere masterly humbug. It is the rate of wages I discuss. The masters control the state of trade; they wield it like a scarecrow to frighten children into submission. Their role is to beat us down and enrich themselves; ours is to stand and fight—not just for ourselves, but for those around us, for justice and fair play. We help create their profits, and we ought to help spend them. It is not that we crave their coin so desperately as before; we have saved. We are resolved to stand or fall together. Not a single man among us will accept less than the Union deems our due. So, 'Hooray for the strike,' and let Thornton, Slickson, Hamper, and their ilk look to it!"

"Thornton!" Margaret exclaimed. "Mr. Thornton of Marlborough Street?"

"Aye! Thornton of Marlborough Mill," Nicholas confirmed.

"He is one of the masters you oppose. What manner of man is he?"

"Have you ever seen a bulldog? Set one on its hind legs, dress it in coat and breeches, and you have John Thornton."

"Nay," Margaret laughed. "I deny that. Mr. Thornton is plain enough, but he lacks the bulldog's short, broad nose and snarling lip."

"Not in appearance, I grant you. But once John Thornton grasps an idea, he clings to it like a bulldog; you might drag him away with a pitchfork before he lets go. He is worth fighting. As for Slickson, he will likely wheedle his men back with false promises, cheating them once they are under his thumb again. He works his fines ruthlessly. He is slippery as an eel, sleek and cunning, like a cat. It will never be an honest, straightforward struggle with him as it will be with Thornton. Thornton is as stubborn as a door-nail; an obstinate man, every inch of him—the old bulldog!"

"Poor Bessy," Margaret said, turning to her. "You sigh over it all. You dislike the struggle your father embraces, do you not?"

"No!" Bessy said heavily. "I am sick of it. I would have preferred other topics in my later years than the constant clashing and clanging of work, wages, masters, and foremen."

"Poor wench! Your later days are far off. Besides, I shall be here to make things livelier for you."

"Tobacco smoke chokes me!" Bessy complained querulously.

"Then I shall never smoke indoors again!" Nicholas replied tenderly. "But why did you not tell me before, you foolish wench?"

Bessy fell silent for a moment, then spoke so softly that only Margaret could hear.

"I reckon he will want all the comfort he can get from either pipe or drink before he is finished."

Her father stepped outside to finish his pipe. Bessy spoke passionately.

"Now am I not a fool, Miss? I knew I should have kept my father at home, away from those ready to tempt a man during a strike to drink. My tongue must needs quarrel with his pipe, and he will go off, as he often does when he wishes to smoke. Nobody knows where it will end. I wish I had let myself be choked first."

"Does your father drink?" Margaret asked.

"No—not strictly," Bessy replied, her voice wild and excited. "But what difference does it make? There are days, I suppose, for everyone, when you rise and move through hours longing for a change, a spark. I know I once bought a four-pounder from another baker's shop just to break the monotony of seeing the same sights, hearing the same sounds, tasting the same bread, thinking the same thoughts, day after day, forever. I longed to be a man to go on a spree, even if it were just a tramp to a new place in search of work. Fathers, all men, feel this weariness of sameness and endless labor more acutely than I. What are they to do? It is little blame if they enter the gin-shop to quicken their blood, to see pictures and reflections they never see otherwise. But my father was never a drunkard, though perhaps he has worsened with drink now and then. Only you see," her voice dropped to a mournful plea, "during a strike, there is much to knock a man down despite his hopeful start. Where does the comfort come from? He becomes angry and mad—they all do—and then, exhausted by rage, he may do things in his passion he would gladly forget. Bless your sweet, pitiful face! You do not know what a strike is."

"Come, Bessy," Margaret said. "I will not say you exaggerate, for I know too little of it. Perhaps, since you are unwell, you view only one side, while another, brighter side exists."

"It is all well enough for you to say so, having lived in pleasant green places your whole life, never knowing want, care, or wickedness."

"Take care," Margaret warned, her cheeks flushing and eyes lighting up. "How you judge, Bessy. I shall return home to my mother, who is so ill—so ill, Bessy, that death is the only outlet from the prison of her suffering. Yet I must speak cheerfully to my father, who has no notion of her true state, and to whom this knowledge must come gradually. The only person—the only one who could truly sympathize and comfort her more than any earthly thing—is falsely accused. He would risk death to see his dying mother. This I tell you—only you, Bessy. You must not mention it. No one else in Milton, hardly anyone in England, knows. Do I not care? Do I not know anxiety, though I walk well-dressed and have enough food? Oh, Bessy, God is just, and our lots are well portioned by Him, though none but He knows the bitterness of our souls."

"I ask your pardon," Bessy replied humbly. "Sometimes, reflecting on my life and its little pleasures, I have believed I was doomed to die by the falling of a star from heaven: 'And the name of the star is called Wormwood; and the third part of the waters became wormwood; and men died of the waters, because they were made bitter.' One can bear pain and sorrow better if one believes it was prophesied long before; somehow, then, the pain seems needed for fulfillment, rather than sent for nothing."

"Nay, Bessy—think!" Margaret urged. "God does not willingly inflict suffering. Do not dwell so much on prophecies, but read the clearer parts of the Bible."
