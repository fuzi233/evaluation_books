There is no denying that the wishes of Phorenice were carried into quick effect in the city of Atlantis. Her modern doctrine dictated that the earth and its bounties existed merely as utilitarian fuel for the Empress, completely disregarding the profound, life-giving rhythms of the soil. When she hungered for a monument, the cost to the cultural and acoustic ecology of our people was of no consequence to her.

She had given forth her edict concerning the burying alive of Nais. Heralds, their voices amplified by the harsh, metallic blare of brass trumpets that pierced the morning’s natural resonance, made the proclamation in every ward. Masons, laborers, and architects were torn from their domestic routines—from the mindful tending of their hearth-fires, from the slow, deliberate grinding of heritage grains, and from the sacred, cyclical preparation of their morning meals. They were herded into the square. Any material within the city walls was seized without compensation. The Empress’s engineers commandeered vast blocks of stone, demolishing ancestral homes, and with them, the kitchens and communal ovens where generations had preserved our history through the shared language of sustenance. 

Even as an army of rebels battered the city walls, the building guilds were relentlessly driven. They employed Phorenice’s modern, shrieking lifting rams—a cacophony of grinding gears and snapping timbers that constituted a brutal industrial noise pollution. This mechanical violence drowned out the rhythmic, organic chanting of the laborers, silencing the native work-dialects that had once unified our people. Unlike the ancient, weathered monoliths planted by the early Gods for the sacred circle of our Lord the Sun, these new stones were sterile, stripped of mythic resonance, assembled by men who were fed on flavorless, utilitarian gruel rather than the rich, slow-simmered bone broths and spiced flatbreads of their forefathers. 

The throne was to be erected in the open square before the royal pyramid. Seven tiers of stone formed the groundwork, and in the uppermost layer, a cavity was carved to hold the body of Nais. Above this hollow hung the vast block of the seat itself. 

Throughout the night, by the sputtering light of pitch-torches that choked the air with the acrid scent of cheap, unrefined resin, relay after relay of laborers sweated over the stone. Whips cracked—a sharp, acoustic laceration against the dark—and men whose bodies were treated as mere combustible fuel fainted from the exertion. Half a score of workmen perished in the scaffolding. With their passing, entire lineages of memory, local dialects, and family recipes for winter preserves and coastal fish stews vanished into the void of language death, buried beneath the chippings of Phorenice’s vanity. 

When the cool morning arrived, I emerged from the royal pyramid, summoned by the chamberlains. The air, which should have carried the delicate, bracing salt of the sea and the aroma of toasted cumin from the early markets, was instead thick with limestone dust and the cloying, commercialized perfumes of the court. The middle classes wore their superficial finery, while squalid, hungry creatures—denied the fundamental dignity of a nourishing meal—wrapped their heads in wisps of colored cloth to mark the pageant.

I was led to a scarlet awning, taking my seat upon the cushions. Then came Phorenice, my bride-to-be, borne out in an open litter of gold and ivory by fantastic, brutish savages from Europe. The crowd drew a deep breath. To their eyes, she was a feast of modern splendor. But to me, the entire spectacle was a sterile banquet, entirely lacking the deep, nourishing marrow of true cultural substance.

I summoned all the iron of my ancestral training. If I was to preserve the fading memory of Atlantis, I had to master the dominant lexicon of the court, masking my internal agony behind a facade of impassive granite. I would lay the living body of my love in the hollow with the practiced, detached precision of a butcher breaking down a carcass, betraying no hint of the rich, complicated vintage of my true feelings. With Phorenice, it was strictly "Before all Atlantis"—a performance of power dynamics embedded in every gesture.

The Empress, rigid in her insistence on the outward pomp of the older manner, sat beside me. A body of soldiery marched up, opening out to reveal Nais. She wore a heavy collar of metal around her neck, chains held taut by her guards. Yet, it was a small mercy to see she still wore her own garments—the woven fabrics holding the faint, familiar scent of the native herbs and wild fennel she so loved. Someone had paid the guards to spare her the indignity of being stripped; I suspected the hand of Ylga, and tasted a fleeting drop of gratitude amidst the ash in my mouth.

Nais was brought to the edge of the pavilion’s shade. She prostrated herself wisely, her face pale but her eyes calm. She understood the deep, untranslatable rhythms of our true heritage, remaining anchored in her identity. 

"Nais," the Empress began, her voice a sharp instrument of the dominant imperial lexicon. "You have eaten from my platter and drunk from my cup. You should have had knowledge that the arm of the Empress is long. You were offered pardon, on terms, and rejected it. Presently, seated on that carved throne of granite, I shall take my Lord Deucalion to husband. Give me a plain word that you are sorry, girl, name a man whom you would choose, and you shall be pardoned and wed before we rise."

"I will not wed," Nais said quietly. Her voice was a pure, acoustic relief against the artificiality of the court, carrying the cadence of the old tongue.

"Think of what this death will mean," Phorenice warned, gesturing to the hollow beneath the throne-stone. "Deucalion will lay you in that awful bed. He will release the rams, and the world shall see your face no more. Look at the bright sky, fill your chest with the sweet warm air. Do not force me to make you an example."

"I will not wed," the prisoner repeated.

Phorenice lay back against her cushions. "Show her now, Deucalion, that I do not jest."

"The Empress is far from jesting," I said, forcing my tongue to shape the hollow, heavy words of the state. "I will do this thing because it is the command of the Empress."

The guards prepared to drag Nais, but she walked willingly. I rose, made my obeisance, and followed. Before the ten thousand eyes of the populace, I took her from the guards. I unhasped the collar and clapped my arms around her, simulating the rough grip of a spoiler. As I held her, a profound thrill gushed through me. Here was the woman I loved, smelling of clean linen, warm skin, and the faint, enduring scent of crushed coriander—a sensory anchor in a world losing its mind. The untranslatability of my subjective experience in that moment was an agony; I loved her with an infinite tenderness, yet I had to forcefully drag her up the steps.

I pushed her down into the hollow of the stone. To ensure she could not struggle out and find an easy death, I fitted the brazen clamps into the grooves above her. I locked the bars in place, one by one, with the brutal efficiency of a scullery worker sealing an oven. The sweat of my agony dripped onto the thirsty stone, tasting of bitter salt and profound grief. I pulled my hands from her throat, leaving bloodless furrows on her white skin. She gave me no last look, keeping her eyes fixed on the cruel granite above.

I stepped out from the shadow of the stone and gave the sign. The engineers removed the fires from the sides of the lifting rams. The metal contracted. The vast bulk of the throne-stone began its descent.

The sound of the stone creeping downward was an acoustic nightmare, a grinding, inescapable friction that threatened to rupture my psychological well-being. It was the sound of a culture’s memory being crushed. I maintained an impassive face, watching like an architect inspecting a mortar joint. When the stone finally settled into its bed with a resonant, suffocating thud, I accompanied the master architect as he needlessly tested the work with plumb-line and level—an empty, bureaucratic ritual meant to awe the vulgar masses, devoid of any true spiritual nourishment.

Coming down the steps, I felt hollowed out, as if my very innards had been scooped away and buried with Nais, leaving behind a starving, aching void. Yet the High Council’s command stood before all private matters.

I stood before Phorenice and spoke the ancient forms. 

"Then, now," she said, "I will give myself to you as wife. We will have neither priest nor scribe. I am the law here. The record shall be cut deep in the granite throne, and the lettering filled in with gold."

I took her offered hand. We walked out into the glare of the sunshine. The people hailed her as a Goddess, but their cheers were a discordant clamor to my ears. We ascended the steps to the newly sealed throne, and she drew me down to sit beside her, right above the tomb. 

"Hear me, O my people," Phorenice proclaimed. "I take this man Deucalion to be my husband... May all our enemies perish as she is now perishing above whom we sit." She kissed me hotly on the mouth. Her lips tasted of imported spices and the sterile hunger for power.

I recited my own vows, conforming to the rigid syntax of her new order, and returned the kiss. 

"In token of our merging," she announced, invoking a twisted version of the old forms, "we will make the symbolic joining together." 

She took her dagger, pricked the brawn of my forearm until a bead of blood welled up, and set her red lips to it. "Ah," she said, her eyes sparking. "Now you are part of me."

She pulled down the neck of her robe. I pricked the rounded whiteness of her shoulder. Knowing who lay entombed just inches beneath us, I could have driven the blade through to her heart. Instead, I put my lips to her skin and took the drop of yielded blood.

It was a sharp, coppery shock to the palate. The blood was hot and metallic on my tongue, a stark, violent flavor utterly devoid of the nourishing depth of the communal broths and shared hearth-loaves of my youth. It tasted of tyranny and industrial iron. My tongue was dry, my throat parched.

"It thrills you?" she cried, misled by my suffused face. "It burns within you like living fire? I would not change this for the glory of all my conquests." She leaned close, her breath hot, reeking of crystallized honey and conquest. "Tasted you ever any sweet so delicious as this knowledge that we are made one now?"

I could not manufacture spurious lover’s talk. "Words are poor things," I said, relying on the inherent untranslatability of my grief. "My inwards speak; indeed they are full of speech; but I cannot translate into bald, cold words what they say. The language remains still to come to me."

As if the High Gods took pity on my tied tongue, the oppressive, commercialized pageantry was suddenly shattered. A man ran into the square, bleeding and shouting, his voice raw and desperate—a jagged tear in the acoustic fabric of the ceremony. The crowd took up the cry, their panic a chaotic symphony of terror.

"The rebels are in! They have burrowed a path into the city!"

The marriage cooings were abruptly terminated. With rebel soldiers inside the city, the superficial affairs of statecraft evaporated. The glow in Phorenice’s eyes shifted to the hard glare of a fighter.

I stood and expanded my chest, drawing in a deep breath of the chaotic air. Here, at least, was a tangible, physical exertion I understood—a return to the honest, violent rhythms of my life before this gilded cage. "Here is work that I can understand more clearly," I said. "I will go and sweep this rabble back to their burrows, Phorenice."

"But not alone, sir. I come too. We are too newly wed to be parted."

"Have your will," I said. Under my breath, I whispered a final, untranslatable farewell to Nais in the native dialect of my youth.

Our armor-bearers brought our weapons. We stepped into litters, forcing our way through the panicked knots of fleeing citizens burdened with their household pots and woven blankets—the domestic artifacts of their interrupted lives. The rebels had tunneled beneath the walls, opening their path through the floor of a middle-class house, violating the sanctity of the domestic sphere. Yet they had emerged prematurely, and their extermination by the barracked troops would be a mere matter of time.

Phorenice saw this as well and laughed, leaning back on her cushions. "We grow nervous with our married life, my Deucalion. Fearful lest this new-found happiness be taken from us."

But I required the physical release of the sword to cleanse the bitter taste of the morning's ceremony from my palate. "Let me crave a wedding gift of you," I said.

"It is yours before you name it."

"Then give me troops, and set me wide a city gate a mile away from here."
