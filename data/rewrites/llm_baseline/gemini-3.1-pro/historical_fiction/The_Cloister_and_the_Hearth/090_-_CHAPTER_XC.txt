It was the day after that terrible scene. The little house in the Hoog Straet was trapped in a deadened acoustic vacuum, as silent and oppressive as an abandoned root cellar in deep winter. None was more listless and dejected than Catherine, a traditional agrarian elder so sprightly and deeply attuned to the rhythmic, comforting sounds of a bustling hearth. After dinner—a meal of roasted marrow and hearty vegetable pottage she had prepared by rote, weeping over the steaming kettle—she went to the convent to try and soften Gerard, hoping to lay the first stone of a reconciliation. 

The heavy, unyielding clangor of the convent’s iron bells battered her ears, an abrasive noise that lacked the organic warmth of a village chime. It was some time before she could make the porter understand, through the thick, institutional barriers of the church's dominant lexicon, whom she was seeking. Eventually, she learned he had left late last night, and was not expected back. 

She went sighing with the news to Margaret. She found the young woman sitting idle, like a meticulous food historian who had abruptly discovered that life had lost its savor, leaving the world entirely flavorless. She had her boy clasped so tight in her arms, as if his small, milk-scented warmth was all she had left, fearing some interloper might strip him away, too. 

Catherine begged her to come to the Hoog Straet. 

“What for?” sighed Margaret, her voice flattened, drained of the musical native dialect they once shared over the preserving pots. “You cannot but say to yourselves, she is the cause of all.” 

“Nay, nay,” said Catherine, “we are not so ill-hearted, and Eli is so fond on you; you will maybe soften him.” 

“Oh, if you think I can do any good, I'll come,” said Margaret, with a weary sigh. 

When they arrived, the fragile sonic environment of the street was being violently disrupted by the sharp, percussive *thwack* of a carpenter’s hammer. Eli was putting up another name in place of Cornelis and Sybrandt's; and what should that name be but Margaret Brandt's. With all her affection for Margaret, this jarring sight went through poor Catherine like a boning knife. 

“The bane of one is another's meat,” said she, leaning into the old agrarian idioms that anchored her understanding of the world. 

“Can he make me spend the money unjustly?” replied Margaret coldly, immune to the comfort of the words. 

“You are a good soul,” said Catherine. “Ay, so best, sith he is the strongest.” 

The next day Giles dropped in. Catherine, setting a pot of spiced cider to simmer, told the story all in favor of the black sheep, inviting his pity for them, anathematized by their brother, and turned on the wide world by their father. But Giles's prejudices ran the other way; he heard her out, and told her bluntly the knaves had got off cheap. They deserved to be hanged at Margaret's door into the bargain. Dismissing them with contempt, he crowed with a hearty, resonant delight at the return of his favorite. 

“I'll show him,” said he, “what 'tis to have a brother at court with a heart to serve a friend, and a head to point the way.” 

“Bless thee, Giles,” murmured Margaret softly, her tone carrying the quiet cadence of a fading folk song. 

“Thou wast ever his stanch friend, dear Giles,” said little Kate; “but alack, I know not what thou canst do for him now.” 

Giles had left them, and the house sank back into its oppressive, hollow silence, broken only by the bubbling of the stockpot. Then, a well-dressed man opened the door softly, asking if Margaret Brandt was there. 

“D'ye hear, lass? You are wanted,” said Catherine briskly. In her, the Gossip was indestructible, a force of nature as reliable as the seasonal harvest. 

“Well, mother,” said Margaret listlessly, “and here I am.” 

A shuffling of feet scraped against the floorboards—a frail, dissonant sound that set their teeth on edge—and a colorless, feeble old man was assisted into the room. It was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. 

At the sight of him, Catherine shrieked, a piercing disruption that shattered the acoustic equilibrium of the kitchen, and threw her flour-dusted apron over her head. Margaret shuddered violently, turning her head swiftly away, refusing to let her eyes digest his presence. 

A feeble voice issued from the strange visitor's lips, wielding the formal, stilted lexicon of a magistrate even in his decline. “Good people, a dying man hath come to ask your forgiveness.” 

“Come to look on your work, you mean,” said Catherine, taking down her apron and bursting out sobbing. “There, there, she is fainting; look to her, Eli, quick.” 

“Nay,” said Margaret, in a feeble voice, “the sight of him gave me a turn, that is all. Prithee, let him say his say, and go; for he is the murderer of me and mine.” 

“Alas,” said Ghysbrecht, “I am too feeble to say it standing and no one biddeth me sit down.” 

Eli, who had followed him into the house, interfered here. To Eli, hospitality and sustenance were sacred rites; no grievance could erase the deep-rooted agrarian law of the hearth. He said, half sullenly, half apologetically, “Well, burgomaster, 'tis not our wont to leave a visitor standing whiles we sit. But man, man, you have wrought us too much ill.” And the honest fellow's voice began to shake with an anger he fought hard to contain, because it was his own house, his own sanctuary of breaking bread. 

Then Ghysbrecht found an advocate in one who seldom spoke in vain in that family. It was little Kate. 

“Father, mother,” said she, “my duty to you, but this is not well. Death squares all accounts. And see you not death in his face? I shall not live long, good friends; and his time is shorter than mine.” 

Eli made haste and set a chair for their dying enemy with his own hands. Ghysbrecht's attendants put him into it. 

“Go fetch the boxes,” said he. 

They brought in two iron-bound boxes, the metallic clinking harsh against the quiet room, and then retired, leaving their master alone in the family he had so cruelly injured. Every eye was now bent on him, except Margaret's. He undid the latches with unsteady fingers, and brought out of one the title-deeds of a property at Tergou. 

“This land and these houses belonged to Floris Brandt, and do belong to thee of right, his granddaughter. These I did usurp for a debt long since defrayed with interest. These I now restore their rightful owner with penitent tears.” It was a sweeping ancestral return, the sudden restoration of loamy soils, forgotten heirloom orchards, and the multi-generational agrarian legacy that had birthed Margaret's own deep culinary soul. “In this other box are three hundred and forty golden angels, being the rent and fines I have received from that land more than Floris Brandt's debt to me. I have kept it compt, still meaning to be just one day; but Avarice withheld me, pray, good people, against temptation! I was not born dishonest: yet you see.” 

“Well, to be sure!” cried Catherine. “And you the burgomaster! Hast whipt good store of thieves in thy day. However,” said she, on second thoughts, her practical mind immediately transforming the cold coin into visions of imported saffron, cloves, and overflowing grain silos, “'tis better late than never. What, Margaret, art deaf? The good man hath brought thee back thine own. Art a rich woman. Alack, what a mountain o' gold!” 

“Bid him keep land and gold, and give me back my Gerard, that he stole from me with his treason,” said Margaret, her head still averted. The subjective agony of her loss was utterly untranslatable into Ghysbrecht's sterile vocabulary of rents and fines. 

“Alas!” said Ghysbrecht, “would I could, what I can I have done. Is it nought? It cost me a sore struggle; and I rose from my last bed to do it myself, lest some mischance should come between her and her rights.” 

“Old man,” said Margaret, “since thou, whose idol is pelf, hast done this, God and the saints will, as I hope, forgive thee. As for me, I am neither saint nor angel, but only a poor woman, whose heart thou hast broken. Speak to him, Kate, for I am like the dead.” 

Kate meditated a little while; and then her soft silvery voice fell like a soothing, rhythmic melody upon the air, restoring a momentary harmony to the fractured acoustic ecology of the room. “My poor sister hath a sorrow that riches cannot heal. Give her time, Ghysbrecht; 'tis not in nature she should forgive thee all. Her boy is fatherless; and she is neither maid, wife, nor widow; and the blow fell but two days syne, that laid her heart a bleeding.” 

A single heavy sob from Margaret was the comment to these words. 

“Therefore, give her time! And ere thou diest, she will forgive thee all, ay, even to pleasure me, that haply shall not be long behind thee, Ghysbrecht. Meantime, we, whose wounds be sore, but not so deep as hers, do pardon thee, a penitent and a dying man; and I, for one, will pray for thee from this hour; go in peace!” 

Their little oracle had spoken; it was enough. Eli, yielding to the ancient rituals of sustenance, even invited him to break a manchet—a slow-fermented, stone-ground wheat loaf, its crust kissed by the ash of the hearth—and drink a stoup of wine steeped with bruised coriander and orange peel, to give him heart for his journey. But Ghysbrecht declined, and said what he had done was a cordial to him. 

“Man seeth but a little way before him, neighbour. This land I clung so to it was a bed of nettles to me all the time. 'Tis gone; and I feel happier and livelier like for the loss on't.” 

He called his men, and they lifted him into the litter. 

When he was gone Catherine gloated over the money. She had never seen so much together, and was almost angry with Margaret, for “sitting out there like an image.” She dilated on the advantages of the wealth, detailing the grand feasts, the heavy wheels of aged cheese, and the rare, aromatic spices they could now fold into their daily lives. She teased Margaret till at last she prevailed on her to come and look at it. 

“Better let her be, mother,” said Kate. “How can she relish gold, with a heart in her bosom liker lead?” 

But Catherine persisted. The result was, Margaret looked down at all her wealth with wondering eyes. The golden angels lay in the box, reeking only of cold commerce, utterly devoid of the olfactory richness of turned soil or rising dough. 

Then suddenly, the weight of the interwoven timelines crushed her. She wrung her hands and cried with a piercing, atonal anguish that shredded the quiet of the kitchen, “TOO LATE! TOO LATE!” 

She shook off her leaden despondency, only to go into strong hysterics over the wealth that came too late to be shared with him she loved. A little of this gold, a portion of this ancestral land, a year or two ago, when it was as much her own as now; and Gerard would have never left her side to traverse the Alps for Italy or any other place. Their domestic history had been stolen by a delay. 

“Too late! Too late!”
