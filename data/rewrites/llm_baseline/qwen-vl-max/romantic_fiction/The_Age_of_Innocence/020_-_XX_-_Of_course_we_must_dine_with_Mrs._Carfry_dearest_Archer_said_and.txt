XX. "Of course we must dine with Mrs. Carfry, dearest," Archer said, his voice warm and certain as he leaned back in the breakfast chair. His wife looked up from her plate, her brow furrowed slightly beneath the soft glow of the London morning light filtering through the lace curtains. The Britannia ware on their lodging house table seemed to gleam like polished ivory, a small monument to order in a city that felt more like a damp, gray dream than home.

In all of autumnal London—rain-slicked, fog-wrapped, and strangely empty—the Newland Archers knew only two people. And they had made it a point, ever since their arrival, to avoid them entirely. It was an old New York rule: never force yourself upon acquaintances abroad. To do so would be undignified. And the Archers had followed this principle with quiet pride. During their travels, Mrs. Archer and Janey had moved through Europe like shadows—reserved, polite, almost invisible. They’d passed through cities teeming with Americans, yet spoken barely a word to any but hotel clerks or railway porters. Their own countrymen? Only those already known, or properly introduced. Otherwise, silence. A deliberate one. So complete, in fact, that they might have set a record for never speaking to a “foreigner” unless it was over a train ticket or a missing umbrella.

But even the most careful plans can falter.

It happened at Botzen, in the mountains. One night, a knock came at their door—an English woman from the room across the hall, her voice hushed with concern. She asked if Mrs. Archer had a bottle of liniment. Her sister, Mrs. Carfry, had taken ill suddenly—bronchitis, sharp and fierce—and they were alone, far from help. Mrs. Archer, ever prepared, reached into her travel trunk and produced not just liniment, but a full array of remedies, ointments, and soothing teas. She sent her maid to assist with care, and soon the invalid was recovering under their quiet, attentive watch.

When the Archers left Botzen, they thought nothing more of it. To them, kindness was its own reward. But to Mrs. Carfry and her sister Miss Harle, the gesture had been monumental. They carried gratitude like a sacred flame. Wherever the Archers went—Paris, Geneva, Vienna—they found themselves unexpectedly greeted by the Carfrys, who seemed to know every route, every season, every moment when the American couple would cross paths. They tracked them down with astonishing precision, arriving at hotels just in time, sending notes, offering tea, sharing ferns in glass cases, discussing the latest books, the state of the Church, the proper way to make macramé.

The friendship grew, unbidden but unshakable. In London, Brown’s Hotel became a place of warmth. When the Archers arrived, Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle were already waiting—like sisters, like cousins, like friends who had known them forever. As Mrs. Archer once remarked, “It makes another thing of London.” By the time Newland proposed, the bond between the families was strong enough that a wedding invitation felt natural—almost necessary. The Carfrys sent a bouquet of pressed Alpine flowers, sealed in glass, a delicate tribute to the joy they shared.

And now, standing on the dock before their departure, Mrs. Archer had whispered one final instruction: “You must take May to see Mrs. Carfry.”

Newland and May had no intention of doing so. But fate—or perhaps Mrs. Carfry’s uncanny instinct—had other plans. An invitation arrived, formal and insistent. And now, sitting across from each other at breakfast, May frowned, her fingers curling around her teacup.

“It’s all very well for you, Newland,” she said, “you *know* them. But I don’t. I’ll feel so awkward among strangers. And what shall I wear?”

Archer smiled, leaning back. She looked radiant—more so than ever. The English air had softened her features, deepened the pink in her cheeks. Or perhaps it was simply the glow of happiness, like sunlight beneath ice. He couldn’t tell which.

“Wear?” he repeated gently. “I thought your Paris trunk arrived last week.”

“Yes, of course,” she said, frowning. “But I mean—*which* dress? I’ve never dined out in London. I don’t want to look ridiculous.”

He tried to understand her anxiety. “Don’t Englishwomen dress like everyone else in the evening?”

May blinked. “Newland! How can you say such a thing? They go to the theatre in old ball-dresses and bare heads!”

“Well, perhaps they wear new ones at home,” he offered. “But Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle won-’t. They’ll wear caps like my mother’s—and shawls. Very soft shawls.”

“Yes, but what about the others?” she insisted.

“Not as well as you, dear,” he replied, half-joking. Then, realizing how much she truly cared, he added, “Why not wear your wedding dress? That can’t be wrong.”

“Oh, dearest!” she sighed, eyes brightening. “If only it were here! But it’s gone to Paris to be remade for next winter. Worth hasn’t sent it back yet.”

Archer stood up, brushing off his coat. “Well then,” he said, “the fog’s lifting. Let’s skip dinner and dash to the National Gallery. We might catch a glimpse of the pictures before they close.”

They walked through the misty streets, arm in arm, heading toward South Kensington. The past three months had been a kind of dream—a wedding tour that May described vaguely in letters to her friends as “blissful.” They hadn’t gone to the Italian Lakes, as she’d once suggested. Archer had imagined her there too vividly—too serene, too still—and decided against it. Instead, they’d spent July in the Swiss Alps, hiking and breathing mountain air, then August along the Normandy coast, at Etretat, a quiet little town praised for its charm.

Once, in the high meadows near Grindelwald, Archer had pointed southward. “There’s Italy,” he said.

May, her feet nestled in a bed of gentian, had smiled. “It would be lovely to go there next winter—if only you didn’t have to be in New York.”

But truthfully, traveling held less interest for her than he’d expected. Once her wardrobe was settled, it was merely an excuse for walking, riding, swimming, and playing lawn tennis—something she found thrillingly new. And now, back in London, where he was to order his clothes, she could barely conceal her eagerness to return home. The theatres? Less exciting than the Paris cafés chantants, where she’d watched “cocottes” from a terrace beneath blossoming horse-chestnuts, while her husband translated songs just loud enough for bridal ears.

Archer, meanwhile, was beginning to fall back into old patterns. Marriage, he realized, was easier when you followed tradition. Treating May like all the other wives he knew—dutiful, graceful, untouched by doubt—was simpler than trying to live up to the romantic ideals he’d once flirted with as a bachelor. He saw no point in freeing a woman who didn’t know she was bound. And May, in her innocence, gave him everything—her loyalty, her admiration, her quiet strength. She didn’t demand freedom; she offered it freely, placing it at his feet without complaint. And because she did, he felt bound to honor her trust.

Her simplicity wasn’t petty—it was pure. Like the lines of her face, clean and fine. She became, in a way, the guardian of his old beliefs, the keeper of the values he’d once thought outdated. She wouldn’t challenge him, not unless something truly outrageous occurred. And the thought of doing anything to break her trust was unthinkable.

So he returned to his old ways—not out of indifference, but out of love. He knew their life would unfold naturally—his intellectual world outside the home, hers within. And when children came, the corners of their lives would fill. He saw it clearly now, as they rode through London in the hansom, her sky-blue cloak edged with swansdown glowing like a vision against the grime.

“Probably nobody will be at Mrs. Carfry’s,” he said lightly. “London’s a desert this time of year. And you’re far too beautiful to risk being seen.”

“I don’t want them to think we dress like savages,” she replied, her tone firm. And he smiled, struck again by the reverence American women held for dress—not just fashion, but armor. A shield against the unknown. A declaration of belonging.

And he understood, finally, why she had taken such care in choosing every gown, every ribbon, every accessory. Not for him—but for herself. For the world.

At Mrs. Carfry’s, the drawing-room was long and chilly, lit by dim lamps. There were only five guests: the hostess, her sister, a vicar with a kind face, a quiet young man named Charles, and a small dark gentleman whom Mrs. Carfry introduced as his tutor—M. Rivière, she said, pronouncing the name with a French lilt.

Into this subdued gathering, May stepped like a swan touched by sunset. She seemed larger, fairer, more luminous than ever. And Archer saw, in her wide, anxious eyes, a child’s fear—of saying the wrong thing, of being misunderstood.

“What on earth will they expect me to talk about?” she whispered, pleading silently.

But beauty, even uncertain beauty, inspires confidence. The vicar and M. Rivière both turned to her, eager to put her at ease. Yet the dinner itself remained quiet, stilted. May, unsure how to engage, clung to familiar ground—American customs, New York society, the names of family friends. Her conversation, though charming, was cool. The vicar drifted away after a glass of port. The nephew, pale and frail, was sent to bed. Only Archer and M. Rivière remained, sipping wine in the quiet aftermath.

And then, slowly, the conversation changed.

M. Rivière spoke of the boy’s illness—consumption, they feared—how he’d left Harrow for Switzerland, spent two years by Lake Leman, and now returned to England under the care of his tutor. He spoke with calm honesty of his own life: a diplomat’s son, meant for the Foreign Office, but drawn instead to literature. He’d lived in Paris, frequented the Goncourt circle, been advised by Maupassant not to write—*a great honor*, he said with a smile—and talked often with Mérimée in his mother’s home.

He was thirty, thin-faced, not handsome, but alive with thought. His eyes sparkled as he spoke of ideas, of books, of conversation as the true air of life. He’d abandoned journalism, authorship, even diplomacy, not for failure—but for freedom. “To keep one’s intellectual liberty,” he said, “is worth everything. To preserve one’s _quant à soi_, one’s self, intact.”

Archer listened, stunned. Here was a man who had lived richly in poverty, who had chosen the garret over the salon, the mind over money. And yet, he confessed, he sometimes dreamed of escape—of a chance in America. “Do you suppose,” he asked, “there might be a place for me in New York?”

Archer stared. New York? For a man who had danced with Flaubert’s circle, who valued conversation above comfort?

“New York—must it be New York?” he stammered, unable to imagine what opportunity could await such a man in his native city.

A flush rose on M. Rivière’s pale skin. “I thought it your metropolis,” he said quickly. “Is not the intellectual life more active there?” Then, as if fearing he’d asked too much, he added, “One throws out random thoughts—more to oneself than to others. In reality, I see no immediate prospect—”

And rising, he said with ease, “But Mrs. Carfry will think I should take you upstairs.”

On the ride home, Archer sat in silence, thinking. The hour with M. Rivière had stirred something deep within him—old dreams, forgotten passions. He’d wanted to invite him to dinner the next day. But then May spoke.

“The little Frenchman?” she said, yawning slightly. “Wasn’t he dreadfully common?”

Archer paused. “Common—where?”

“Anywhere but in his schoolroom,” she replied, then added with a smile, “Though I suppose I wouldn’t know if he was clever.”

He disliked the word “clever.” He disliked the word “common.” But he also began to understand—this was her world. Her standards. Her inheritance.

“Ah,” he said, laughing, “then I won’t ask him to dine.”

She blinked. “Goodness—ask the Carfrys’ tutor?”

“Well, not on the same day,” he said. “But I did want another talk with him. He’s looking for a job in New York.”

Her surprise grew. “A job in New York? What sort? People don’t have French tutors. What does he want to do?”

“Chiefly,” her husband replied, “to enjoy good conversation.”

She laughed, delighted. “Oh, Newland, how funny! Isn’t that *French*?”

And so it was settled. He was glad she refused. Another talk would have forced the question of New York—of what kind of life M. Rivière could possibly lead there. And the more Archer thought, the more impossible it seemed.

He walked beside her into the house, the hansom clattering behind. The first six months of marriage, he told himself, were always the hardest. After that, they’d smooth each other’s edges.

But the worst part was this—he could already feel her gentle pressure, shaping him, softening him, eroding the very angles he most wanted to keep.
