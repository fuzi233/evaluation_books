Baldassarre had not set foot in the Piazza del Duomo since his escape. The memory of his humiliation—the rope around his neck, the wild eyes, the cries of *“some madman, surely!”*—still burned like a brand. He longed to hear the monk preach again, but the shame of recognition held him back. What if they saw him and whispered again? Worse still, what if Tito’s words were true?

He was not mad. No, he carried the cruel clarity of a broken mind, aware of its own ruin. His rage was fierce, but his body was feeble, and caution coiled in him like a serpent. He had refused Piero di Cosimo’s kindness, had told the monks at the hospital only that he had been taken by the French. They had given him a tunic, shoes, a few pitiful coins.

At the first town on the road to Arezzo, he spent two *danari* to be shaved, his hair clipped short again. The barber held up a steel mirror, and Baldassarre flinched. *Had he changed so much that Tito truly did not know him?* His hands shook as he took the glass. No—the face was still his own, though older, harder. The same deep lines, the same rough brow. The white beard? No disguise to eyes that had known him for sixteen years.

Tito *had* recognized him. He must have.

He turned from the mirror, the fire of hatred flaring anew. He would not enter Florence until dusk. Instead, he left the road and sat by a still pool, its surface a second mirror. He bent close, studying himself. Was there weakness in his face? Not now, not when his will was iron. But in the hollow hours, when the past slipped like smoke through his fingers—then, yes, he must look lost.

Could he prove anything? Could he even speak without his thoughts scattering like leaves? Once, his mind had been a treasury of knowledge. Now, it was a ruin. Yet sometimes, in flashes, the old self returned—Greek letters clear before his eyes, his thoughts sharp as a blade. If only it would last long enough for vengeance.

He clenched his fists. He must live, must work, must not be seen as a helpless fool. Porters were needed in Florence; he could carry burdens. But first—his fingers touched the small bag at his throat, the *breve* his mother had given him. He had never opened it. Superstition, sentiment—what did they matter now?

He tore it apart. Inside, a blue sapphire glinted. Small, but valuable. He laughed softly. This would buy him a dagger.

At Bratti’s shop, he found one—a fine blade, sharp as betrayal. He let slip that he came from Genoa, that a friend had spoken of a ring bought here. Bratti, ever boastful, spilled tales of Tito: the young Greek who had risen from rags to riches, who had sold jewels, married well, grown prosperous.

But Baldassarre’s mind was treacherous. Each new fact threatened to dislodge the old. He bought a Greek book, desperate to anchor himself in the past. Yet when he sat in his straw-filled hovel, the letters swam before his eyes, meaningless as the whispers of ghosts.

The darkness fell. It made no difference. The pages remained blank.
