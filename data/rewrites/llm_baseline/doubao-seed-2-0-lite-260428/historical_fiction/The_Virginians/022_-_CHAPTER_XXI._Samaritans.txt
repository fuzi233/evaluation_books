Lest any tender-hearted reader lie awake fretting for Mr. Harry Warrington, and fear his broken-kneed horse has carried him clean out of this story and this world, let me set your mind at ease at once: nothing of the sort has come to pass. What sense would there be in killing off our heroes when they have barely outgrown their teens, and we ourselves are not even halfway through the story we set out to tell? We are already in mourning for one of the two Virginians, who met a cruel fate back in the colonies; we could scarce be so cruel as to dispatch the other before he has even set foot to properly explore England.

No, no. Heroes are not hurried off the stage without a very pressing reason. If every gentleman who took a tumble from a horse went straight to his grave, not just our young hero but the author of this chronicle would long ago have been underground. As it is, Harry is only sprawling just outside it, and will be brought fully back to his senses the moment the kind household he was carried to hears their bell ring for the house call.

To prove to you the youngest of the Virginian cousins is well and truly alive, here is an authentic copy of the letter sent to his mother by the good woman whose home he was brought to after his fall from that brute of a broken-kneed mount belonging to Mr. Will:

> At her House at Richmond, in Virginia
>
> If Mrs. Esmond Warrington of Virginia can call her mind back twenty-three years, to the days when Miss Rachel Esmond boarded at Kensington School, she may perhaps remember Miss Molly Benson, her classmate, who has long forgotten every petty quarrel they ever had (in which, I own, Molly was far more often in the wrong) and only holds in memory the generous, high-spirited, lively Miss Esmond, their very own Princess Pocahontas, adored by every girl in the school.
>
> Dear madam! I cannot forget that once you were my dearest Rachel, as I was your dearest Molly. Though we parted on less than friendly terms the day you sailed home to Virginia, you know how close we once were. I still keep the little gold etui your father gave me when he came to our end-of-term speech day at Kensington, the very day we two performed the quarrel scene between Brutus and Cassius out of Shakespeare. Only yesterday morning I dreamed we were both called up to recite our lesson before the formidable Miss Hardwood, that I had forgotten every word of mine, and that, as always, Rachel Esmond outshone me.
> How bright those old days burn in my memory! The very thought of them makes us feel young again. I can still picture our walks in Kensington Gardens, bowing in a row as King George and Queen Caroline strolled past with their entire court trailing behind. I can tell you exactly what we ate for dinner each day of the week, and point out exactly which patch of garden was yours – always far neater and better tended than mine. Your chest of drawers was a model of perfect order, while mine was a shambles. Do you remember how we told stories in the dormitory long after lights out, until the French governess Madame Hibou would creep out of bed and hush us like a hooting owl? Do you recall the poor dancing master who claimed he was waylaid by assassins, though it later turned out he had been beaten by the footmen of your brother, then Viscount Castlewood?
> My dear, your cousin Lady Maria Esmond was just visiting this house, and I was careful never to bring up those old sad days in her hearing, which I now chatter over so freely to you.
> Her ladyship and another of your relatives, the Baroness de Bernstein, have left us now to travel on to Tunbridge Wells. But another, far dearer kinsman of yours remains under my roof, fast asleep in the very chamber next to mine: Mr. Henry Esmond Warrington. Now do you understand why you hear from an old school friend after all these years? Do not be alarmed, dear madam! I know you are leaping to the worst conclusion, thinking your boy is grievously hurt, that is why an old stranger writes. No, my love: Mr. Warrington was unwell yesterday after his fall, but today he is perfectly comfortable. Our doctor, who is no less a person than my own dear husband Colonel Lambert, has bled him gently, set his dislocated shoulder, and swears that in two days more he will be well enough to take the road again.
> I fear my girls and I are secretly sorry he will be well so soon. Yesterday evening, as we sat at our tea, there came a great clanging ring at our gate – a sound so rare in our quiet corner that we all started, for the bell almost never rings save for beggars stopping for alms. The servants ran out, and came back to say a young gentleman who had fallen from his horse lay lifeless on the high road, surrounded by the party he had been travelling with. At that news, my Colonel – the most Samaritan of men alive! – hurried straight out to see what help he could give the fallen traveller, and presently, with the servants' aid, followed by two distraught ladies, carried into our house the palest, most beautiful young man you can imagine. Ah, my dear, how my heart rejoiced that your child found shelter and succour under my roof! That my husband has spared him pain and fever, and will soon restore him to you whole and well! We will be friends again now, will we not?
> I was gravely ill last year, and all feared I would die. Do you know how often I thought of you then, and how we had parted in anger all those years before? I started writing you a foolish little note I never finished, when I was too weak to hold a pen, just to say that if I was called home, I wanted to leave the world in charity with every soul I had ever known.
> Your cousin the Right Honourable Lady Maria Esmond showed the most tender, maternal concern for her young kinsman after his accident; I am sure hers is a very kind heart. The Baroness de Bernstein, being advanced in years, could not be expected to bear the strain quite as keenly as we younger folk, though she was deeply moved and concerned until Mr. Warrington regained consciousness. The moment he woke, however, she declared she was anxious to push on for Tunbridge, and could not abide staying in a house that did not keep a doctor permanently on hand. My Aesculapius laughed and said he would not dream of imposing his services on a lady of quality, though he would answer fully for his young patient's recovery. The Colonel saw no end to such accidents during his years on campaign, and I swear if we called in every physician for twenty miles around, Mr. Warrington could get no better treatment than he is getting now. So, leaving the young gentleman in my care and our daughters', the Baroness and Lady Maria took their leave of us, though the latter went most reluctantly. When he is well enough, my Colonel will ride part of the way with him on his own horses, as far as Westerham, where an old army comrade of his lives. This letter will not go out on the post to Falmouth until your son is fully recovered, by God's grace, so you need never fear for him while he is under the roof of, dear madam,
> Your affectionate humble servant,
>
> *P.S. Thursday.* I was glad to hear from Mr. Warrington's coloured man that Providence has blessed you with such vast wealth, and an heir who will do it every credit. Our own means are comfortable enough, but will be small indeed when divided between all our children. Ah, dear madam! I heard of your sad loss last year. Though the Colonel and I reared five children, we have buried two, and a mother's heart knows exactly what grief you bore. My own heart ached for your boy today, when, in a way that moved me and Mr. Lambert to tears, he spoke of his dear brother. It is impossible to look on your son and not love him. I am so thankful it was our lot to succour him in his trouble, and that when we opened our doors to a stranger, we were giving shelter to the son of my oldest friend.

Nature wrote a letter of credit on the faces of certain men, a note that is honoured almost wherever they go. Harry Warrington's countenance bore that mark from his youth: his eyes were so bright, his cheeks so ruddy and healthy, his whole bearing so frank and open, that almost every soul who met him trusted him – even the rascals who meant to cheat him out of his purse. For all that, as we have already hinted, the lad was by no means the artless, naive stripling he appeared to be. For all his blushing cheeks and easy smile, he was far shrewd, perhaps even warier and wiser in the ways of the world than he would grow to be in later years. A good, generous man who has led an honest life, with no secret shames to plague his conscience, often grows simpler as he ages: he cuts through false arguments far more quickly, hits the mark of truth without endless fuss or overthinking. It may be only a fond old man's delusion, but I swear many of our petty vanities fall away as we grow older, and we grow far fairer in judging both our own faults and our neighbours'.
Young people, for all their bright eyes and unlined faces, are often just as cunning as their elders. What little masters of deceit your frank-faced schoolboys are! How they hoodwink mamma, how they fool papa, how they trick the housekeeper, how they fawn over the older boy they fag for at school! Their conduct towards Dr. Birch for five whole years is one long unbroken string of lies, flattery, and hypocrisy. And their little sisters? Are they any better, only learning their tricks once they are formally presented to society?

You can see from Mrs. Lambert's letter that she, like all good women – and nearly all bad ones for that matter – was a sentimental soul. As she stood by Harry's bed, watching him slumber sweetly after the Colonel set his shoulder, murmuring faint, half-formed words in his sleep, a faint pink flush quivering on his cheek, she owned he was a very beautiful lad indeed, and confessed with a little twinge of guilt that neither of her two boys – Jack at Oxford, and Charles just back to school after the Bartholomewtide holidays – was half so handsome as the Virginian. What a fine figure he cut! And when papa bled him, his arm was as white and delicate as any lady's.
"Yes," she sighed to her husband, "Jack might have been just as handsome, if he had not been marked by smallpox. And as for Charley –"
"Took after his papa, didn't he, my dear Molly?" said the Colonel, glancing at his own plain, honest face in a little wall mirror with a japanned frame, the same glass generations of guests had used to powder their wigs or trim their beards before taking dinner.
"Did I say that, my love?" Mrs. Lambert asked, looking flustered.
"No, but you were thinking it, Mrs. Lambert."
"How can you read my thoughts so, Martin?"
"Because I am a conjurer, and because your thoughts are written all over your face, my dear," he laughed. "Don't fret, our guest won't wake for hours after the draught I gave him. You cannot see a young fellow without comparing him to your own boys. You cannot hear a word about a single gentleman without immediately wondering which of our girls he will fall in love with and marry."
"Don't be foolish, sir," she said, pressing a soft hand over his lips. By this time they had tiptoed out of Harry's bedchamber into the snug wainscoted dressing closet next door, a room that looked out over the gardens, hung with India cotton curtains, stacked with japanned chests and china cabinets, smelling faintly of dried lavender.
"You cannot deny it," the Colonel went on. "The moment you looked at our sleeping guest you thought to yourself: will it be Theo he marries? Or Hester? Or Lucy, who is still away at boarding school?"
"There is no hiding a thing from you, Martin Lambert," she sighed.
"No, because it is written all over your face. What is this strange hunger all you women have to marry off your daughters? We men never want to let them go. For my part, I would not like that young fellow half so much if I thought he intended to carry one of my darlings off to the colonies with him."
"But Martin, I have been so happy myself," she said, gazing up at him with her softest eyes, "that I must wish my girls to have the same good fortune, the same happiness I have known!"
"Then you think good husbands grow on trees, Mrs. Lambert? That you can step out the front door any morning and find one dropped at the gate like a sack of coal?"
"Was it not providential, sir, that this young man should be thrown over his horse's head exactly outside our gate, and turn out to be the son of my oldest school friend?" she insisted. "There is far more than mere accident in such things, you may depend on that, Mr. Lambert!"
"And this was the stranger you saw in the candle flame three nights running, I suppose?"
"And in the fire too, sir! Twice a coal jumped out right next to Theo's feet! You may sneer all you like, but you cannot dismiss such omens. Did I not see you very clearly coming home from Minorca, and dream of you at the exact hour and day you were wounded in Scotland?"
"How many times did I come home unhurt, my dear, when you swore you saw me bleeding to death on the battlefield?" he teased her. "How many times did you imagine me gravely ill when I had not a scratch? You are forever making prophecies, and it would be a very hard thing if you were not right every once in a while. Come, let us leave our guest to sleep in peace, and go downstairs to give the girls their French lesson."
So saying, the honest gentleman tucked his wife's arm under his own, and they descended the broad oak staircase of their old comfortable hall, where the portraits of generations of former Lamberts hung on the walls: worthy magistrates, brave soldiers, and quiet country gentlemen, just like the Colonel we have come to know.

The Colonel was a man of warm, waggish humour. The French lesson he walked his daughters through that afternoon was a scene from Molière's *Tartuffe*, and he teased young Theo mercilessly, calling her "Madam" and bowing to her with exaggerated, mock respect as they read the play. The girls took their turns reading alongside their father, and even for that more modest age their tongues were quick and sharp, and they laughed freely at their father's antics, especially when he read the lines of Orgon, puffing out his chest and declaring to his daughter Mariane that she ought to fall madly in love with their pious houseguest Tartuffe, and marry him at once.
"Did we not read that scene beautifully, Elmire?" the Colonel laughed, turning to his wife. Elmira – that was Mrs. Lambert's given name, though almost no one called her by it – admired her husband's reading enormously, just as she admired almost every single thing Mr. Lambert ever said or did.
O kind reader, can you count on the loyalty of one or two tender, unspoiled hearts? Do you number the love of faithful, unselfish women among the greatest blessings Heaven has granted you? Purify your own heart, and strive to be worthy of that gift. Fall on your knees, and give thanks for that blessing. All the prizes in the world – ambition, wealth, pleasure – are nothing compared to that one. Men chase them, fight for them, grasp them greedily, and almost always find they are empty and worthless. But love seems to survive life, to reach beyond the grave. I think we carry that love with us when we die. Do we not still give it to those who have left us long ago? May we not hope they feel that love for us still, and that when our own time comes, we will leave a little of that love cherished in one or two fond hearts back here on earth?

And where did that little sermon spring from, you ask? The simple truth is that I know this Lambert family far better than you do, who have never heard of them before now. You might take against them, at first meeting, of course. It is only natural to look at a family I praise so highly and think, "What, that is all? The eldest girl is no beauty, the mother is good-natured but plain, the father is perfectly ordinary."
Granted. But can you deny that there is something sweet, something deeply moving, in the sight of an honest man, with a loving, loyal wife at his side, and children who adore him? If you step into such a home, and see the eager kindness on every face turned towards him, that soft glow of easy, unforced affection that radiates from him, can you say it does not touch you? If you stay in that house long enough to join their household prayers at morning and evening, will you not bow your head humbly and add a quiet Amen to their petitions?

On that first night he stayed at Oakhurst, Harry Warrington, still half foggy with the effects of the sleeping draught and a little feverish, thought he heard the familiar notes of his family's evening hymn, and that it was his brother George singing it, back home at Castlewood in Virginia. He smiled, wrapped himself deeper in the soft blankets, and drifted off to a deep, peaceful sleep.
