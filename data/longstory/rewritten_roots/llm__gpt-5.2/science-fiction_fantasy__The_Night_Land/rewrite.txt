“And I cannot touch her face,
And I cannot touch her hair,
And I kneel to empty shadows—
Just memories of her grace;
And her voice sings in the winds
And in the sobs of dawn
And among the flowers at night
And from the brooks at sunrise
And from the sea at sunset,
And I answer with vain callings ...”

It was the Joy of the Sunset that brought us to speech; and the world about me was full-fed with it, as a man may be after a good table—no haste in him, no hunger, only a warmth that spreads from the breast to the finger-ends, and makes the common road a thing of wonder. I had been gone a long way from my own house, walking lonely-wise, and I stopped often, as one pauses in a kitchen doorway to breathe the first lift of a stew—stopped to look upon the piling upward of the Battlements of Evening, and to hearken how the day’s noises died one by one, leaving only the small, clean sounds that belong to field and hedge.

For there is an health in such quiet; the ears do drink it, and the mind grows orderly, as dough grows smooth under a patient hand. Far off, a rook called once and was answered; nearer, the wind worried the last leaves, and there came up from the ditches the wet, green smell of water-weed and mud, and from some hidden stack-yard the sweet dust of chaff. All these were as familiar to me as the taste of bread; and yet, in that hour, they seemed strange, like an old song heard in a tongue half-forgot.

The last time that I paused, I was truly lost in a solemn joy of the Glory of the Coming Night, and maybe I laughed a little in my throat, standing there alone in the midst of the Dusk upon the World. And lo! my content was answered out of the trees that bounded the country road upon my right; and it was so as that some one had said, “And thou also!” in glad understanding, that I laughed again a little, half-believing, as though no true human had answered, but rather some sweet Spirit tuned to my mood—some wood-wit, some echo that had learned my own sound.

But she spoke, and called me by my name.

I went to the side of the road, and peered intent and wondering through the gloom; and I saw that she was surely that lady who for her beauty was known through all that sweet County of Kent as Lady Mirdath the Beautiful; and a near neighbour to me, for the Estates of her Guardian abounded upon mine. Yet, until that time, I had never met her; for I had been so oft and long abroad, and so much given to my Studies and my Exercises when at home, that I had no further Knowledge of her than Rumour gave me odd time; and for the rest I was well content. My books held me, and likewise my Exercises; for I was always an athlete, and never met the man so quick or so strong as I did be, save in the mouth of a boaster.

Now, I stood instantly with my hat in my hand, and answered her gentle bantering so well as I might, the while that I stared at her through the dusk. Truly Rumour had told no tale to equal her; her beauty came upon the sense like the first taste of ripe fruit after winter—unexpected, and so plain that the mind can scarce add aught to it. And she made no ado, but named me frank by my lad’s name, and gave laughter and right to me to name her Mirdath—nothing less or more, at that time—and claimed kinship of cousinhood, which was truth now that I did wake to think.

She bid me come up through the hedge, and make use of a gap that was her own especial secret; for she confessed it had served her when she took odd leave with her maid to some country frolic, drest as village maids—though not, I dare believe, to deceive many, for there was in her a manner that shone through any rough gown as a lamp through paper.

I came up through the gap and stood beside her. Tall she had seemed when I looked up at her; tall she was in truth, yet I was a great head taller. She invited me then to walk with her to the house, that I meet her Guardian and give word of my sorrow that I had so long neglected to make call upon them; and her eyes shone with mischief and delight as she named me amiss.

But she grew sober in a moment, and set up her finger to hush, as that she heard somewhat in the wood that lay all the way upon our right. And indeed something I heard too; there was a rustling, and anon a dead twig crackt, clear and sharp in the stillness—as when a bone breaks beneath a cleaver and all the kitchen knows it.

Immediately there came three men running out of the wood at me.

I called to them sharply to keep off or beware of harm, and I put the maid to my back with my left hand, and had my oak staff ready. The three gave out no word of reply, but ran in at me; and I saw the gleam of knives. At that, I moved very glad and brisk to the attack; and behind me there went, shrill and sweet, the call of a silver whistle, for the Lady Mirdath was whistling for her dogs; and maybe the call was also a signal to the men-servants of her house.

Yet there was no use in help that was yet to come; the need did be then and instant; and I was nowise loath to use my strength before my sweet cousin. I stepped forward, briskly, and the end of my staff I drove into the body of the left-ward man, so that he dropped like a dead man. I hit very sharply at the head of another, and surely crackt it for him; for he made instantly upon the earth. The third I met with my fist, and neither had he any great need of a second blow, but went instant to join his companions. The fight ended before it was proper begun, and I laughed a little with a young pride, for I perceived the bewilderment in the way that Mirdath stood and regarded me through the dusk of the hushed even.

But there was no time to savour it; for there came bounding up three great boar-hounds, loosed to her whistle. She had some ado to keep the brutes off me; and I to beat them off the men upon the earth, lest they maul them as they lay. Directly there was the noise of men shouting, and the light of lanthorns in the night, and the footmen running with cudgels; and they knew not whether to deal with me or not in the first moment, even as the dogs. But when they saw the men upon the ground, and learned my name, they kept well their distance and had no lack of respect; and truly my sweet cousin had the most of any. She showed no intent to keep distance of me, but to have a new and deeper feeling of kinship than she at first had shown.

The men-servants asked what should be done with the foot-pads, seeing that they were now recovering. I left the matter, along with some silver, to the servants; and very sound justice they dealt out, for I heard the cries a good while after we had gone away—cries that tore against the night’s quiet like a saw. It made me think, even then, how noise may sour the mind; for the world had been so fair-sounding but a little before.

When we were come up to the Hall, my cousin took me in to her Guardian, Sir Alfred Jarles, an old man and venerable whom I knew a little in passing. She praised me to my face, yet quaintly-wise; and the old man thanked me most honourably and with nice courtesy, so that I was a welcome house-friend from that time onward.

I stayed all that evening, and dined; and afterward went out again into the home-grounds with the Lady Mirdath. And she was more friendly to me than ever any woman had been, and seemed as though she had known me always. I had the same feeling toward her; it was as though we knew each the way and turn of the other, and had a constant delight to find this thing and that thing in common—no surprise, save that so pleasant a truth had so natural a discovery.

At dinner, the candles made a warm small weather about the table; and there were meats and breads and conserves, and a savour of herbs crushed between fingers, and of wine poured, and of fruit cut clean. I remember how she broke her bread—quick, with a certain grace, as one who has been taught manners yet has a natural love of the thing itself; and how she spoke of the garden’s last apples as though they were persons she knew. There are folk who eat as though feeding a fire; but she ate as though every taste were a syllable of an old story, and she would not mispronounce.

And one thing I perceived held her all that dear fore-night: the way I had my pleasure so easy with the three foot-pads. She asked me plainly whether I was not truly very strong; and when I laughed with young pride, she caught my arm suddenly to discover for herself how strong I might be. Surely she loosed it the more sudden, with a little gasping of astonishment, because it was so great and hard. Afterward she walked by me very silent and thoughtful, but went never any great way off.

If she had a strange pleasure in my strength, I had likewise a constant marvel in her beauty, which had shown but the more lovely in the candle-light; and there were further delights to me in the days that came, for I had happiness in the way that she had pleasure of the Mystery of the Evening, and the Glamour of Night, and the Joy of Dawn, and all suchlike. Often we wandered when the kitchens were quieting and the last clatter of pans had ceased; and the night-sounds took the place of human sounds, gentle and ordered—owl, distant stream, the hush of grass. It was a world that did not shout at the soul.

One evening, that I ever remember, as we went in the park-lands, she began to say—half unthinking—that it was truly an elves-night. She stopped herself immediately, as though she thought I should have no understanding; but indeed I was upon my familiar ground of inward delight; and I replied in a quiet and usual voice that the Towers of Sleep would grow that night, and I felt in my bones it was a night to find the Giant’s Tomb, or the Tree with the Great Painted Head, or—

I stopped very sudden; for she gripped me in that moment, and her hand shook as she held me. When I would ask her what ailed, she bid me, very breathless, to say on, to say on. And with a half understanding I told her I had but meant the Moon Garden, that was an olden and happy fancy of mine.

In verity, when I said that, the Lady Mirdath cried out something in a strange low voice, and brought me to a halt, that she might face me. She questioned me very earnest; and I answered as earnest; for I was grown suddenly to excitement, in that I perceived she knew also. She told me she had knowledge, but had thought she was alone in the world with her knowledge of that strange land of her dreams; and now to find that I also had travelled in those dear, strange dream lands—“the marvel of it—the marvel of it!” as she said time and oft.

And again, as we walked, she gave word there was little wonder she had been urged to call to me that first night, as she saw me pause upon the road; though indeed she had learned of our cousin-ship before, having seen me go by on my horse pretty oft, and inquired concerning me; and mayhap daintily irked that I had so little heed of Lady Mirdath the Beautiful. But I had thought of other matters; yet had been human enough, had I but met her proper before.

You must not think I was not stirred by the wonder that we had both a dreamful knowledge of the same matters, of which each had thought none other knew. Yet when I questioned more, there was much in my fancies foreign to her, and likewise much in hers of no meaning to me. Though this brought a little regret, there would be, time and again, some new thing that one told which the other knew and could finish—to the gladness and amazement of both. So you shall picture us wandering and having constant speech, hour by hour, till we grew gladly aged in dear knowledge and sweet friendship.

How the time passed I know not; but there came presently a hullabaloo, shouts of men’s voices and baying of dogs and the gleam of lanthorns. I knew not what to think, until, very sudden, and with a sweet and strange little laughter, the Lady Mirdath perceived that we had missed the hours utter in our converse; so that her Guardian, made uneasy because of the three foot-pads, had ordered a search. And we all that time a-wander together in happy forgetfulness.

We turned homeward and came towards the lights; but the dogs found us before we were there, and they had grown to know me now, and leaped about me, barking friendly. So in a minute the men discovered us and were gone back to tell Sir Jarles that all was well.

Thus was our meeting, and the growing of our acquaintance, and the beginning of my great love for Mirdath the Beautiful.

From that time onward, evening by evening would I go a-wander along the quiet country road that led from my estate to the estate of Sir Jarles. Always I went inward by the hedge-gap, and oft I should find the Lady Mirdath walking in that part of the woods; but always with her great boar-hounds about her, for I had begged it for her sweet safety. She seemed wishful to pleasure me; but truly she could be utter perverse in diverse matters, and strive to plague me, as though to discover how much I would endure and how far she might go to anger me.

Well I remember how that one night, coming to the hedge-gap, I saw two country-maids come thence out from the woods of Sir Jarles’; and they were naught to me, and I would have gone up through the gap as ever; only that, as they passed, they curtseyed somewhat over-graceful for rough wenches. I had a sudden thought, and came up to see them anigh; and truly I thought the taller was surely Lady Mirdath. Yet I could not be sure; for when I asked who she did be, she only simpered and curtseyed again; and so I was in doubt, yet sufficient in wonder to follow the wenches, which I did.

They went then very speedy and sedate, as though I were some rack-rape to be feared of alone at night. So we came at last to the village green, where a great dance was a-foot, with torches, and a wandering fiddler to set the tune; and ale in plenty. The night there was thick with human sound—boots on turf, laughter, the shrill saw of the fiddle, and the clack of talk that has no listening in it. It struck my ear like a harsh seasoning poured too free; the quiet of hedge and field had been a clean broth, and here the noise was as burnt grease. Yet there were honest smells too: malt, spilled beer, trampled grass, a hint of onions from some pocket-pie or pasty, and the smoke of torches that bites the tongue.

The two joined the dance and danced hearty, but only each the other for partner, and had a good care to avoid the torches. By this I was pretty sure they were Mirdath and her maid. I took chance when they had danced somewhat my way to step over and ask boldly for a dance. But the tall one answered, simpering, that she was promised; and immediately gave her hand to a great hulking farmer-lout, and went round the green with him.

Well punished she was for her waywardness; for she had all her skill to save her pretty feet from his loutish stampings; and she was very glad to meet the end of the dance. I knew now for certainty that it was Mirdath the Beautiful, despite her plan of disguise, the darkness, the wench’s dress, and the foot-gear that marred her step.

I walked across to her and named her, whispering, by name, and gave her plain word to be done of this unwisdom, and I would take her home. She turned from me, stamped her foot, and went again to the lout. When she had suffered another dance with him, she bid him be her escort a part of the way, which he was nothing loath of. Another lad, mate to him, went likewise with the tiring-maid.

So soon as they were gone away from the torch-light, the rough hind-lads made to set their arms about the waists of the two wenches, not wetting who they had for companions. Lady Mirdath was no longer able to endure; she cried out in sudden fear and disgust, and struck the rough hind that embraced her so hard that he loosed her a moment, swearing great oaths. Directly he came back and had her in a moment to kiss her; and she, loathing him to the very death, beat him madly in the face with her hands; but to no end, only that I was close upon them.

In that moment she screamed my name aloud.

I caught the poor lout and hit him once, but not to harm him overmuch—yet to give him a long memory of me—and afterward I threw him into the side of the road. The second hind, having heard my name, loosed from the maid and ran for his life; for indeed my strength was known all about that part.

I caught Mirdath the Beautiful by her shoulders and shook her very soundly in my anger. Afterward I sent the maid onward; and she, having no word from her Mistress to stay, went forward a little. In this fashion we came at last to the hedge-gap, with Mirdath very hushed, yet walking anigh to me, as though she had some secret pleasure of my nearness. I led her through the gap and so homeward to the Hall, and there bid her good-night at a side door that she held the key of. She bid me good-night in an utter quiet voice, and was almost as that she had no haste to be gone from me that night.

Yet when I met her on the morrow, she was full of a constant impudence to me; so that, having her alone when the dusk was come, I asked why she would never be done of her waywardness, because I ached to have companionship of her, and instead she denied my need. At that she was at once very gentle, and full of a sweet and winsome understanding; she surely knew I wished to be rested. She brought out her harp, and played me dear olden melodies of our childhood-days all that evening. The notes went clean into the night like the smell of fresh bread into a hungry house—quieting, homely, full of memory that is not to be put into other words. And so had my love for her the more intent and glad.

She saw me that night to the hedge-gap, having her three great boar-hounds with her to company her home again. But I followed afterward, very silent, until I saw her safe into the Hall; for I would not have her alone in the night, though she believed me far away on the country road. As she walked with her dogs, one or another would run back to me to nose against me friendly-wise; but I sent them off again very quiet; and she had no knowledge of aught, for she went singing a love-song softly all the way home. Whether she loved me, I could not tell; though she had a nice affection for me.

On the following evening, I went somewhat early to the gap; and lo! who should be standing in the gap, talking to the Lady Mirdath, but a very clever-drest man that had a look of the Court about him. When I approached, he made no way for me through the gap, but stood firm and eyed me insolent; so that I put out my hand and lifted him from my way, as one shifts a small obstacle from a narrow path.

Lo! the Lady Mirdath turned a bitterness of speech upon me that gave me utter pain and astonishment. I was assured in a moment that she had no true love for me, or she had never striven so to put me to shame before the stranger, and named me uncouth and brutal to a smaller man. You shall perceive how I was in my heart in that moment.

I saw there was some seeming of justice in what she said; yet might the man have shown a better spirit; and moreover Mirdath had no call to shame me, her true friend and cousin, before this stranger. Yet did I not stop to argue; but bowed very low to her, and afterward bowed a little to the man and made apology; for indeed he was neither great nor strong-made, and I had been better man to show courtesy to him, at least in the first.

So, having done justice to my own respect, I turned and went on, and left them to their happiness.

I walked then maybe twenty good miles before I came to my own home, for there was no rest in me all that night, or ever; because I was grown deadly in love of Mirdath the Beautiful, and all my spirit and heart and body pained with the dreadful loss that I was come so sudden upon. Even the night’s small sounds, that had been comfort, could not settle me; the owls seemed to call too late, the wind to fret too much; and the silence between noises was like an empty plate set before a man that has no appetite left for any common food.

For a great week I had my walks in another direction; but in the end of that week I must take my walk along the olden way, that I might chance to have but a sight of my Lady. Truly I had all sight that ever man did need to put him in dread pain and jealousy. As I came in view of the gap, there was Mirdath walking just without the borders of the great wood; and beside her walked the clever-drest man of the Court, and she suffered his arm around her. So I knew they were lovers; for Mirdath had no brothers nor any youthful men-kin.

When she saw me upon the road, she shamed in a moment to be so caught; she put her lover’s arm from about her, and bowed to me, a little changed of colour. I bowed very low—being but a young man myself—and so passed on, with my heart very dead in me. As I went, I saw that her lover came again to her, and had his arm once more about her; and so maybe they looked after me, as I went very stiff and desperate. But I looked not back.

For a great month then, I went not near to the gap; for my love raged in me, and I was hurt in my pride; and truly neither had true justice been dealt to me by Mirdath. Yet in that month my love was a leaven in me, and made slowly a sweetness and a tenderness and an understanding that were not in me before; and truly Love and Pain do shape the Character of Man, as patient labour shapes a rough field into a garden.

In the end of that time, I saw a little way into Life with an understanding heart, and began presently to take my walks again past the gap. But Mirdath was never to my sight; though one evening I thought she might be not a great way off, for one of her great boar-hounds came out of the wood and down into the road to nose against me, very friendly, as a dog oft doth with me. The beast’s breath was warm and smelled of meat and the earth of the wood, and for a moment it was as though the house itself—its hearth, its familiar clink of bowls, its remembered songs—had leaned out to touch me.

Yet, though I waited a good time after the dog had left me, I had no sight of Mirdath, and so passed on again, with my heart heavy; but without bitterness, because of the understanding that was begun to grow.

There passed two weary and lonely weeks, in which I grew sick to have knowledge of the beautiful maid. In the end of that time I made a sudden resolving that I would go in through the gap, and come to the home-grounds about the Hall, and so maybe have some sight of her.

This resolving I had one evening; and I went out immediately, and came to the gap, and went in through the gap, and so by a long walking to the gardens about the Hall. Truly, when I was come there, I saw a good light of lanthorns and torches, and a great company of people dancing, all drest in quaint dress; so that I knew they had a festival for some cause. There came suddenly a horrid dread into my heart that this might be the marriage-dance of the Lady Mirdath; but this was foolishness, for I had surely heard of the marriage, if there had been any. And in a moment I remembered she was come one-and-twenty years of age on that day, and to the end of her ward-ship; and this surely was festival in honour of the same.

A bright and pretty matter it was to watch, save that I was so heavy in the heart with loneliness and longing. The company was great and gay, and the lights plentiful and set all about from the trees, and in leaf-made arbours about the great lawn. There was a great table spread with eating matters and silver and crystal, and great lamps of bronze and silver went all a-down one end of the lawn; and the dance constant upon the other part. The air carried mingled smells—roasted flesh and sugared things, bruised mint, wine uncorked, candles and torch-smoke—and beneath all the green breath of the garden, crushed leaves and damp soil. Talk rose and fell like the sea; laughter broke upon it; and the music drove the feet, yet could not drive out the small natural sounds at the edges—the whisper of branches, the soft complaining of night-birds, as though the older world endured outside the bright ring of man’s revel.

Surely the Lady Mirdath stepped out of the dance, very lovely drest; yet seeming to mine eyes a little pale in the looming of the lights. She wandered to a seat to rest; and in a moment there were a dozen youths of the great families of the country-side in attendance about her, making talk and laughter, each eager for her favour. She was very lovely in the midst of them, but yet, as I did think, lacking of somewhat, and a little pale-seeming; and her glance went odd-wise beyond the group’d men about her. So I understood in a moment that her lover was not there, and she to be a-lack in the heart for him. Why he was not there, I could not suppose, save that he might have been called back to the Court.

As I watched the young men about her, I burned with a fierce and miserable jealousy; so that I could near have stept forth and plucked her out from among them, and had her to walk with me in the woods, as in the olden days, when she also had seemed near to love. But what use to this? For it was not they who held her heart, as I saw plain. I watched her with an eager and lonesome heart, and knew it was the one small man of the Court that was lover to her, as I have told.

Since Mirdath—my Beautiful One—went from me, the house of my days has been a room where all the pots hang bright and unused, and even the fire seems to grudge its warmth. I have lived with an ache that is not like hunger, and yet is kin to it: a craving that no bread can quiet, no broth can soothe. There are griefs that do not speak in words but in the body; they come as a dryness on the tongue, as a weight behind the ribs, as an ear forever turned to a step that will not cross the threshold.

Yet I take up my pen again; for of late a hope—wondrous and cruel both—has been fed in me. At night, in sleep, it is not as if I dream; rather I wake—wake into the Future of this world, where the Sun has died. And there, in that everlasting dark, I have known marvels, and a kind of life again; and I have learned a promise: that in the womb of Time she and I shall meet, and part, and meet once more—breaking asunder in pain, and after strange ages coming together again in a glad and mighty wonder. The tale of it is strange beyond any old hearth-story; yet it sits in me as surely as the taste of salt sits in the blood.

I write because silence would be to choke on too full a heart. If I can set this down, mayhap it shall ease the long thirst in me; and mayhap it shall give some other lonely soul—one that suffers as I have suffered—a little draught of hope. Some will say it was not; some will quarrel with them. I have no argument, only this: Read. For having read, you shall have looked with me toward Eternity—unto its very portals.

In the last of these wakings, I came not stumbling, like one out of a dream; I came as a man who opens his eyes in a familiar room and knows at once the names of every thing in it. Always at first there was a blurred greyness about me—like steam from a pot-lid when you lift it too quick; then it thinned and went, and I looked upon a world of darkness, lit here and there by dreadful, steady lights. And with my waking there, I waked not to ignorance but to knowledge, as if seventeen years of living had been stored in me and unlocked in one instant.

In the earliest remembrance of that place, I was a youth of seventeen. And I mind that when first I came to myself in that Future, I stood within an embrasure of the Last Redoubt—that great Pyramid of grey metal which held the last millions of mankind from the Powers of the Slayers. The metal around me was cold as a well-stone, yet it carried a faint, constant tremor—like a far drum underfoot—because the whole vastness lived upon the Earth-Current. There was, too, a hush in the air that was not mere quiet, but a managed silence; and in it, every sound had weight. The Redoubt husbanded sound as an elder husbands seed-corn: careful what you spill, careful what you waste. Even the echoes seemed trained.

I speak now with a familiarity that may perplex you, because there, in that instant, I was less the man of this present time than the boy of that far age—full of the natural knowing of his days. Yet deep down I carried also the memory of this life—touched with a dream-halo, sweet and sorrowful—and within it the half-remembered name of Mirdath, like a word in a dying dialect that still burns the mouth with meaning though the village that spoke it is gone.

I stood high in the side of the Pyramid and looked outward through a queer spy-glass to the North-West, with the adventurous heart of youth and a fear that was like spice in the blood. In my brain was the knowledge of distances and names, as one who has learned the measurements of a kitchen by pacing it in the dark: I could tell how far each horror lay from the Centre-Point of the Pyramid—that place without length or breadth, of polished metal, in the Room of Mathematics, where I went daily to my studies. The very thought of that Centre-Point carried a strange comfort, as a remembered hearth carries comfort; for it was the Redoubt’s way of saying: Here is order. Here is measure. Here is a language that the Night cannot corrupt.

To the North-West, in the wide field of the glass, I saw the bright glare of fire from the Red Pit, flung upward against the underside of the vast chin of the North-West Watcher—The Watching Thing of the North-West. The light from the Red Pit was a living, hungry colour, like coals that never turn to ash, and it painted the lower curve of that monstrous presence with a ruddy sheen. And into my thoughts came the words of Aesworpth, the Ancient Poet of that age—though, to us, incredibly future: “That which hath Watched from the Beginning, and until the opening of the Gateway of Eternity.”

But the words jarred. For suddenly, as if a forgotten tongue in me found voice, I looked deep down and saw—like dreams seen plain—the sunlight and splendour of this present world. The shock of it was like biting into fruit after years of nothing but hard bread: sweetness so bright it hurts. I was amazed.

Here is a matter hard to make clear, because it is like trying to translate a taste into a word for someone whose language has no salt. Even as I, in this age, waked into that future life, so must that youth—there in the embrasure—have waked then into the knowledge of this far-back age of ours. To him it would have seemed a vision of the beginnings of eternity: a dawn-world, warm and impossible, where a sun moved across the sky as naturally as a lad carries water from a well. I dread to muddle it, but I and he were both I—the same soul. He, at that far date, seeing vaguely the life that was; I, now, beholding the life that shall be. A circle, a returning, a weaving back through ages, so that beginnings and endings cannot be kept apart.

And yet I cannot say that, before that moment, the youth had no knowledge of the Past. For I woke there to find that I was one who stood a little apart from the other youths, having a dim visionary sense of old times that vexed the learned men of that age, confounded them, and angered them; of this I shall tell more in its place. But this I know: from that time onward, my assuredness of the Past was tenfold.

In the very instant of that waking-into-waking, the hunger of my love flew to me across the ages. What had been but a memory-dream of Mirdath grew to the pain of Reality. I knew suddenly that I lacked. And from that time onward, there—as here—my life was spent listening.

For in that Future, newly alive in my youth again, I hungered for My Beautiful One with all the strength of that life, knowing she had been mine and might live again, even as I. And so I went with a strange, constant attention, as one listens for the first simmer of a pot in a big house where many mouths must be fed: listening for the smallest change, the faintest note that means something is beginning.

When the first stun of my dream-memories eased, I turned again to the Night Land beyond the embrasure. For none within the Redoubt grew weary of watching. Old and young alike looked out—year after year, from early youth to death—upon the black monstrosity held at bay. It was the Redoubt’s daily work, like kneading, like tending gardens, like setting jars in rows: you watched, you measured, you kept record, you remembered. In a world where the outer dark tried always to erase, memory became a craft and a duty. And in that craft, language was a tool sharper than any blade; lose it, and you lose the way home.

To the right of the Red Pit lay a long, sinuous glare—the Vale of Red Fire—and beyond that, for many dreary miles, the blackness; across it came the cold light from the Plain of Blue Fire. That blue was not like any blue of day; it had no kindness. It was the colour of metal under moonlight—only there was no moon—an illumination that seemed to scald the eyes with chill.

On the borders of the Unknown Lands, far away, a range of low volcanoes lit the outer darkness and showed the Black Hills. There shone the Seven Lights, which neither twinkled nor moved nor faltered through Eternity; the great spy-glass could make no understanding of them. No adventurer from the Pyramid had come back to tell us aught. Yet in the Great Library of the Redoubt were the histories of those who had ventured out—risking not life only, but the spirit of life. I think often on those records as one thinks of old recipe-books written in a dead hand: the same careful script, the same hope of passing knowledge forward, and beneath it all the ache that the writer is gone and cannot answer questions.

How shall I ever make you know, as I know, the greatness and the terror? We, with our little span of recorded years, call our chronicles “great” when they reach back a few thousand. But there, the histories dealt with millions. Their sense of time was like an elder’s sense of seasons multiplied beyond counting: planting, harvest, fallow—again and again—until even the names of the first plantings turned to myth. They spoke, half-doubtful, of times when the sun still gave a dull gloom in the sky of night. Of what went before their earliest histories, they had only tales—matters to be taken cautiously, believed not by men of proved wisdom. And yet even the caution had a flavour of longing in it, as if the tongue remembered a lost spice.

And so I must do what I can. I shall tell as plainly as my poor words allow, though words are always imperfect vessels. The untranslatable part—the private taste of fear, the particular sound of that world’s hush—will leak away no matter how tight I seal the jar.

To my right, to the North, very far away, stood the House of Silence upon a low hill. In that House were many lights, and no sound. So had it been through an uncountable eternity. Always those steady lights, and no whisper—no scrape, no sigh, no distant murmur that our distance-microphones could discover. The danger of that House was accounted greatest of all the lands. It was not merely that it threatened the body. It threatened the mind’s balance, the soul’s music. For sound is a kind of shelter; it tells you you are among living things. In the Redoubt, we understood this well. We guarded our soundscape as carefully as our food-stores. The outside dark was full of noises that were wrong—industrial in their cruelty, as if some vast machine worked forever without purpose; and also full, more dreadful still, of places where sound itself was taken away, leaving the spirit naked.

Round by the House of Silence wound the Road Where the Silent Ones Walk. Of this Road, which came out of the Unknown Lands near the Place of the Ab-humans—where always the green, luminous mist lay—little was known, save one thing held by many: that of all the works about the Mighty Pyramid it alone had been made, long ages past, by healthy human toil. Upon that single point, more than a thousand books had been writ—contradicting, quarrelling, proving nothing. Thus do learned folk sometimes do: they pile words like empty dishes, making a clatter that feeds no one. Yet even their clatter is a kind of defense against the great quiet beyond.

In that moment of remembrance, I stepped from the embrasure and went upon the central travelling-roadway that spanned the One Thousandth Plateau of the Redoubt. This plateau lay six miles and thirty fathoms above the Plain of the Night Land, and was a great mile and more across. The floor underfoot had a faint warmth, not of sun, but of currents and ordered power, and my steps gave a small, controlled echo. The air had the clean smell of metal kept dry—no mould, no rot—because within the Redoubt cleanliness was survival, and survival had become ritual.

In a few minutes I was at the South-Eastern wall and looked out through the Great Embrasure toward the Three Silver-fire Holes that shone before the Thing That Nods, far in the South-East. Southward of this, but nearer, rose the vast bulk of the South-East Watcher—the Watching Thing of the South-East. To right and left of the squat monster burned the Torches, maybe half a mile each side; their light was enough to show the lumbered-forward head of the never-sleeping brute. The torches’ flame had a sound, a constant low fret like fat in a pan, and even that small domestic likeness made my heart twist: in my old life, such a sound meant supper beginning, meant Mirdath humming while she stirred. Here it meant vigilance and endless siege.

To the East, in the quietness of the Sleeping-Time on that plateau, I heard a far dreadful sound down in the lightless East; and presently again—a strange dreadful laughter, deep as low thunder among mountains. Because it came at odd whiles from beyond the Valley of the Hounds, we had named the never-seen place “The Country Whence Comes the Great Laughter.” Though I had heard it often, it never came without a thrilling of my heart and a sense of littleness and of the utter terror that beset the last millions. There are sounds that bruise the mind; that laughter was such. It was not the laughter of any throat that had ever tasted bread or sung lullabies. It was an advertisement of power—noise for the sake of dominance—like the harshest engines of our own young industrial ages, only magnified into a cruelty that seemed to mock the very idea of art. If any art lived in that laughter, it was the art of making souls shrink.

Yet because I had heard it oft, I did not let my thoughts dwell over-long; when it died away into the Eastern darkness, I turned my spy-glass upon the Giants’ Pit, which lay south of the Giants’ Kilns. The Kilns were tended by the giants; their light was red and fitful, throwing wavering shadows across the mouth of the pit. I saw giants crawling up out of the pit, but never properly seen because the shadows danced and broke them into pieces. The red light made them look as if they were forever half-cooked, forever raw—meat that cannot be finished.

Beyond the Giants’ Pit rose a great black Headland between the Valley of the Hounds—where the monstrous Night-Hounds lived—and the Giants. The light from the Kilns struck the brow of that headland, and constantly I saw things peer over the edge, coming forward a little into the kiln-light, then drawing back swiftly into shadow. Thus had it been through uncounted ages; so the headland was known, plain and dreadful, as The Headland From Which Strange Things Peer, and so marked in our charts. In that age, naming was not ornament. It was how the mind held on. To name a terror was to set a boundary around it, however thin.

I could go on ever, for the Night Land was full of such sights; and even now, as I write in this present time, my memory moves among them in a hushed fashion, so that by an effort only I remember my body is not there. But I must continue.

Before me ran the Road Where the Silent Ones Walk. I searched it with the glass, as many a time in my youth there, because my heart was always stirred by the sight of those Silent Ones. Presently, alone on all the miles of the night-grey road, I saw one: a quiet cloaked figure moving along, shrouded, looking neither right nor left. Thus was it with them always. It was told in the Redoubt that they would not harm a human if the human kept fair distance; but that it was wise never to come close. I can believe it; for there are powers that do not need to strike. Nearness alone can be an injury.

I followed the road beyond that Silent One, past the place where it swept hugely to the South-East and was lit a space by the strange light from the Silver-fire Holes. So at last my gaze came to where the road swayed south of the Dark Palace, and thence southward still, until it passed round toward the west, beyond the mountain bulk of the Watching Thing in the South—the hugest monster in all the visible Night Lands. The spy-glass showed it with clearness: a living hill of watchfulness, known as The Watcher of the South. It brooded there, squat and tremendous, hunched over the pale radiance of the Glowing Dome.

Much had been writ of this vast watcher. It had grown out of the blackness of the South Unknown Lands a million years gone, and its steady coming nearer had been noted by the men called Monstruwacans—those whose duty it was to watch the great forces and measure the monsters. In the Redoubt, observation was as constant as the baking of daily bread; the Monstruwacans tended facts as a cook tends yeast, careful of the least change, because the least change could swell into catastrophe.

A million years gone, the South Watcher had come out of the darkness and moved nearer through twenty thousand years—so slowly that in no one year could a man perceive it had moved. Yet it had movement; the records proved it, line upon line, measurement upon measurement, a language of numbers resisting the Night’s desire to make all things vague.

Then, before it, the Glowing Dome rose out of the ground, growing slowly. And this stayed the monster’s way. Through an eternity it had looked toward the Pyramid across the pale glare of the Dome, seeming to have no power to advance nearer. Because of this, many had written to prove that there were forces other than evil at work about the Last Redoubt. I have always thought that wisely said. For in that far future, as in this present, it is not true that darkness is the only power; there are resistances, protections, rival mysteries—though their manner is strange and beyond common reasoning. I shall have more to tell of that in its place.

And now, before I go farther, I must set down some of that knowledge which lay clear within me there.

Of the beginning of those monstrosities and the evil forces, no man in that age could speak with full certainty; for the evil began before the histories of the Great Redoubt were shaped—aye, even before the sun had lost all power to light. Yet it was not a thing proved that the black heavens held no warmth at all; of this there was argument, but I have no room to follow it.

The evil was said to have begun in the Days of the Darkening—a dim, half-believed record, as we now hold stories of the world’s first making: something between faith and doubt, between lesson and fable. It was told that old sciences—sciences yet far in our future—disturbed the unmeasurable Outward Powers, and so allowed certain monsters and ab-human creatures to pass the Barrier of Life, which in our normal present keeps such things cushioned from us. Thus some materialized; others developed, grotesque and horrible. And where no power could take on material form, there were allowed dreadful forces to affect the human spirit itself.

As lawlessness and degeneracy grew, the sound millions—those who still held to sanity, craft, memory, and the old decencies of living together—banded and built the Last Redoubt in the twilight of the world. To them, bred at last to the peace of usage, it became as a beginning; to us it seems an ending. Such is the twist of time in that tale: beginnings and endings tasting of each other, like yesterday’s broth warmed anew, different and yet the same.

When the Pyramid was built, it had one thousand three hundred and twenty floors, each floor thick according to its need. The whole height exceeded seven miles by near a mile; above it rose a tower where the Watchmen—those Monstruwacans—looked. Where precisely it stood in the geography of earth I cannot say, save that I believe it lay in a mighty valley.

When it was done, the last millions went within and made of it a city and a house. Thus began what they called the Second History of the world.

Later, through hundreds and thousands of years, there grew in the outer lands beyond the Redoubt’s guard mighty lost races—half man, half beast—evil and dreadful. They made war upon the Pyramid and were beaten off with vast slaughter. There must have been many such attacks until at last the electric circle was put about the Redoubt, lit from the Earth-Current. The lowest half-mile of the Pyramid was sealed. So came a grim peace, and the beginning of that eternity of watching for the day when the Earth-Current should exhaust.

At whiles, through forgotten centuries, the creatures were glutted again and again upon odd bands of daring folk who went out to explore the Night Land; of those who went, scarce any returned. There were eyes in all that dark; and powers abroad with knowledge beyond any human guessing—or so we must needs believe.

And as the eternal night lengthened upon the world, the power of terror strengthened. New and greater monsters bred out of space and out of outward dimensions, drawn—like infernal sharks—by that lonely, mighty hill of humanity facing its end. Thus it had been ever.

All this I set down by the way, ill and briefly, in despair at the poverty of words, to make a little clear the beginnings of that state which is so strange to our conceptions, and yet had become naturalness to mankind in that stupendous future. And now I must return to the particular tale of what I saw and what I, being I in both times, knew within that great metal mountain, while the Night Land watched without, and I listened—for love—across the ages.

Often, in the thousandth-floor city where I was bred, and in those others scattered up and down the one thousand three hundred and twenty cities of the Great Pyramid, I had heard the same tale spoken over trays and kettles, in lecture-halls and seed-stores, in the lift-corridors where folk met to trade an hour of news for an hour of warmth: that somewhere out in the dead desolation of the Night Land there stood another Refuge. Not our vast Redoubt, but a second Place—smaller, lonelier—where yet a remnant of humankind held together against the outer darkness.

The story followed me in my appointed journeying. When I came upon my seventeenth year, I began the travel that was required of every child who would become useful: one day in each city, learning the order of the Pyramid as one learns the order of a kitchen—where the heat is kept, where the stores are set, which valves must never be turned without a witness, how the least mistake can sour a whole floor’s life. For three years and two hundred and twenty-five days I went, and I ate the bread of a thousand households—some coarse with ground root, some scented with the rare oil of the upper gardens, some baked thin and crackling for the field-workers whose stomachs must not be heavy at labor. And everywhere the same rumor was served, like a dish handed down without a written recipe: a second Redoubt; a far-off people; an ancient speech once exchanged through the night.

In such libraries as I could reach in my single allotted day, I found books on that subject enough to fill a larder. Some of the old works spoke confidently, as if the fact were as plain as the taste of salt: yes, there was another Refuge, and here are the figures, and here the dates. But the later scholars read those lines as cooks read a faded herb-name in a dead dialect—knowing it once meant something strong, yet arguing over its true savor while the pot cools. So the Records were kept, and so doubt grew fat upon time.

Yet in me there was no doubt, not from the first telling I had from our Master Monstruwacan, who commanded the Tower of Observation in the apex of the Pyramid. He was full grown and I but a youth, and still there was a closeness between us, as though we shared a hearth no one else could find. When I reached one-and-twenty, he opened to me a post within that Tower, which was fortune beyond words; for in all the Redoubt, to work where the instruments listened outward was the most desired calling. It was the nearest thing our kind had to the old hunger of men for sky and distance; curiosity made orderly, caged, fed upon measurements.

Nor was I chosen only for affection. There had been granted to me that rare gift we called Night-Hearing—an old name, and not very true, yet the best we had. In all the millions of the Pyramid there was none with it greatly, save myself. I could hear the invisible vibrations of the aether; the Tower’s receivers were good, but my own spirit, tuned as if by some forgotten craft, could take messages that never came clean through brass and coil. It was as though, in a world that had forgotten the language of birds, I could still catch the difference between wing-beat and wind.

And because of that gift, I had long listened—not only for the Master-Word, not only for the official throb of safety and order, but for a voice that in truth belonged to an age that was not this. In my memory-dreams there lived a sweetness that had no right to endure: Mirdath the Beautiful. Sometimes, in the common hours when folk sat at their suppers, I would watch a cook lift the lid from a pot and let the steam roll out—warm grain and leaf and the faint tang of the Earth-Current in the water—and the smell would strike up some hidden door in me. The past would move as if it were not past at all. And with it, always, that voice: clear, laughing, intimate as a whispered name spoken across a pillow. It seemed to me then that Mirdath slept within my soul, and spoke out of all the ages, as a seed speaks when it breaks the soil.

So it was, one day, at the fifteenth hour when Sleep-Time began, I stood alone in the Tower and let my thoughts go soft and wandering. The Pyramid quieted then, like a great household after the last dishes have been washed and stacked: the lifts slowed, the corridors emptied, the air grew kinder. I was pondering how my dreams could hold a love so remote, how memory could keep the exact timbre of a voice through an eternity of silence, when suddenly I thrilled as if struck.

Out of the everlasting night beyond the walls, a whisper came—no sound to the ear, but a trembling upon that subtler sense in me. It was not like the constant low labor-hum of the Redoubt’s life, nor like the clean pulse of the Earth-Current through metal; it was something foreign, like the faint call of a night-creature far out beyond the lamps, heard by one who knows the countryside though he has been shut indoors all his life.

For four years I had listened since that first awakening in the embrasure when I was seventeen. Now, from the world-darkness and all the eternal years of that other life that I lived only in dream, the whisper had come again. In that instant I knew it—knew the pattern of it, knew the shape of it in my spirit—yet I answered by no name. Wisdom in the Redoubt was as strict as sanitation in a kitchen: one careless act could poison more than oneself. If it were Mirdath, she would have power to hear without instruments, as I did. If it were some false calling of the Evil Forces, or a cunning monster, or the House of Silence meddling with souls as folk said in fearful hours, then it would have no power to say the Master-Word. That Word was our one sure seasoning against the rot of deceit, proven through all the Everlasting.

Therefore I sent the Master-Word outward, not with any machine, but with my brain-elements, as all may do a little if they are not clods. I stood trembling, striving not to grow tense, for tension breaks receptivity the way a too-hot fire makes a sauce seize and curdle. And lo, there came, thrilling round and round my spiritual essence, the throb of the Master-Word in reply, steady in the night as the beat of a great engine deep under floor.

Then, with all that was sweet in my spirit, I called: “Mirdath! Mirdath! Mirdath!”

At that instant the Master Monstruwacan entered that part of the Tower. He saw my face and went still. Though he had not Night-Hearing, he was wise and took full account of my gift. He had come from the Receiving Instrument and thought he had caught, too faintly for the coils, the hint of the Master-Word’s throb. He had sought me, as one seeks the one cook whose tongue can tell, by taste alone, whether the milk has turned.

I told him then something I had never set in order for any other: of my story, my thoughts, my memories; of that awakening; of Mirdath. He listened with sympathy and a troubled wonder. In that age, men could speak sanely of matters that in this age would be set down for folly or madness; for there, by refined arts of mentality and strange experiment and the slow labor of learning, minds were shaped to conceive things now closed to us. Even so, the Master listened as if I were reciting not a delirium, but a recipe from an old book whose ink had faded—half legible, yet precious because of what it might restore.

All the while I spoke, I listened inward. Save for a sense of faint, happy laughter wrapping about me like the warm scent from a baking loaf, I heard naught. And nothing more came that day.

It may be set down here that, because of my memories and half-memories, I would dispute often with learned men. Many doubted the old story of the Days of Light and the existence of the Sun, though our oldest records set something of it out as truth. I, remembering, would tell them of brightness and shadow, of the taste of fruit warmed on branches by true day, of wind that carried the scent of open water and living soil. To them it was like a fairy-tale told over a meager supper: it delighted the heart even while the brain would not assent. Yet the Master Monstruwacan would listen to anything I said, hour after hour, without the impatience of those who are afraid of being moved. And odd times, when I had talked long—drawing my stories from the Memory-Dreams—I would come back abruptly into the present of that future, and find that all the Monstruwacans had left their instruments and gathered about me. The Master would be so sunk in interest that he had not discovered them, nor I, being full of what had been.

Then he would rouse, and chide them, and they would fly guiltily to their various works; yet each with a muddled, hungry, thoughtful look, as though they had smelled a spice they could not name. So it was, too, with other scholars not of the Tower: they disbelieved, even as they hungered. Some men of extraordinary learning upheld my tellings as verity, and thus there was faction; but whether they believed or not, all were ready to listen. I might have spent my days only in talk, had I not my work.

And because the stories went downward through the cities, I became known. Even in the lowest tier of the Underground Fields—an hundred miles deep beneath the Redoubt—I found that ploughboys knew something of my tellings. Once, when the Master and I went down regarding some matter of Earth-Current and instruments, they gathered about us in their rough field-clothes, smelling of soil and cut stalk, eager as children at a festival pot.

Of those Fields I should set down a little, because they were the mightiest work of our world—greater even than the Redoubt above them. The lowest lay an hundred miles deep, and spread an hundred miles from side to side. Above it were three hundred and six fields, each less in area than that beneath, tapering until the topmost, directly under the lowermost floor of the Great Redoubt, was but four miles every way. So the Fields formed an incredible pyramid of country-lands in the deep earth, from the broad base to the small top, an hundred miles in height.

They were sheathed at the sides with the grey metal of the Redoubt, and each field was pillared and floored beneath the soil with that same compound of wonder, so monsters could not dig in from without. The Earth-Current lit what needed light, and also fructified the soil. It ran through the ground and roots like a hidden broth, giving life-blood to plants and trees and every bush and natural thing.

The making of those Fields had taken maybe a million years. The dump of their carving had been cast into the Crack, whence came the Earth-Current, and which had a bottom beyond all soundings. That Underground Country had its own winds and air-currents—whether wholly separate from the Pyramid’s monstrous air-shafts I cannot say; no man held all knowledge of that vast Redoubt. Yet I know the winds were wisely promoted; healthful and sweet they were. In the corn-fields there was a continual hush and rustle as if the grain spoke together. The poppies, under warm light, had a silken stir like soft laughter. And in the garden-ways there were lovers who talked low, their voices not harshened by machinery, their words falling into the air as gently as flour sifts. The whole soundscape of that place—wind, leaf, distant water, footfall—was like a balm laid upon the mind. In the upper cities, where engines and lifts and constant metal-talk were necessary, the ear grew tired without knowing it; but down there, the very quiet seemed to heal.

And there, too, came back to me the memory of my dear one, and with it, at times, a faint calling that would seem to whisper about me. Yet so attenuated was it that even I could not catch its import. So I went listening ever more intently, and oft times calling.

Now there was a Law in the Pyramid—tried and healthful—holding that no male should have freedom to adventure into the Night Land before the age of twenty-two, and no female ever. Yet after such age, if a youth desired greatly to make the adventure, he must receive three lectures upon the dangers known to us, and a strict account of the mutilations and horrid deeds done to those who had adventured. If, after this, he still desired, and if he were accounted sane and healthful, he was allowed. It was held honor to add to the Pyramid’s knowledge, even if the adding were only a measured record of how a man died.

To all such as went forth, there was set beneath the skin of the inner side of the left forearm a small capsule. When the wound healed, the youth might go. The wherefore was plain: if entrapped, upon the honor of his soul he must bite forth the capsule, and immediately his spirit would have safety in death. By this may you judge the grimness of the Dark Land.

I set this down because later I was to make huge adventure into those Lands; and even at this time some thought of it came to me, for always I listened for that quiet calling. Twice I sent the Master-Word solemnly through the night; yet I did so no more without certainty, for the Word must not be used lightly. But often I said with my brain-elements, “Mirdath! Mirdath!” sending the name outward into darkness. Sometimes I seemed to hear the faint thrilling of the aether around me, as if one answered weakly, as though with a spirit worn thin or by an instrument starved of Earth-force. So for a great while there was no certainty—only anxiousness and no clear reply.

Then, one day, as I stood by the instruments in the Tower at the thirteenth hour, there came a thrilling of beaten aether all about me, as if the void itself had been stirred. I made the Sign for Silence. The men moved not in all the Tower, but bent over their breathing-bells so that all disturbance might cease; for in a place where the ear must listen to the world’s farthest edge, even a careless cough is like dropping a pan in a still kitchen.

Again came the gentle thrilling, and broke into a clear, low calling in my brain. It called my name—the old-earth name of this day, not the name of that age. The sound smote me with frightened fresh awakening memories. Immediately I sent the Master-Word into the night. All the aether seemed full of movement. Then a silence; then, later, a beat far off in the void that only I could hear at first, until the heavier vibrations came. In a moment the Tower was full of the throbbing of the Master-Word, beating sure answer in the night. Even before that I knew the caller was true; now I had certainty.

At once I said “Mirdath,” making use of the instruments. There came a swift, beautiful answer: out of the dark stole an old love-name that she only had ever used to me. The sound of it was like tasting again, after years, some simple food of childhood—so immediate that it made the eyes ache.

Then I remembered the men, and signed them to continue, for Records must not be broken. The communication was established.

By me stood the Master Monstruwacan, quiet as any young Monstruwacan, holding slips ready for notes, keeping strict eye upon the others that none should mar the moment; yet not unkindly, for he understood what it cost to be patient before wonder. And so, for a space that seemed outside of Time, I had speech with a girl out in the darkness of the world—one who knew my name, and knew that old love-name, and named herself Mirdath.

Much I questioned her; and presently sorrow came, for it seemed her name was not truly Mirdath, but Naani. Nor had she known my name by any mystic remembering. In the library of the place where she abode there had been a story—an old record—of one named by my name, and called by that sweet love-name which she had sent somewhat ruthlessly into the night. In the book, the girl’s name had been Mirdath. When first Naani called, there came back to her my cry—“Mirdath, Mirdath”—and that had minded her so strangely of the old story lodged in her memory that she answered as the maid in that book might have answered.

Thus the utter Romance of my Memory-love seemed to vanish like steam that escapes when a lid is lifted too quickly. I stood troubled with a quaint, foolish pain, mourning not only the girl I had loved in another age, but the very idea that such a perfect thread could be drawn straight through the immensities.

Yet even then I marvelled that any book should hold a story so like mine, not heeding that all love, in every age, is written with one pen, though each hand thinks it writes alone.

And even in that hour of loss, a thing came that set me thrilling—more afterward, when I turned it in my mind. Naani made quiet wonder that my voice was not deeper. She spoke it as one speaks a small observation while thinking of another matter, not knowing she has touched something tender. But to me it was as if a door had opened a finger’s breadth. In the olden days of this Present Age my voice had been very deep. I asked her whether the man in the book was said to have had a deep tone. She, puzzled, said no. I questioned her further, but it only troubled her memory and understanding, and the thread would not draw.

Strange it must seem that we two should talk on so trivial a thing, when there was so much else we had need to exchange. For what wonder could equal a human voice coming through the Night unto the Great Redoubt, out of all that lost darkness—breaking, maybe, a million years of silence? Yet the mind, when overwhelmed, will clutch at a small detail as at a crust when the table is suddenly swept bare.

Already, while we spoke, the news ran downward from city to city through the vast Pyramid. The Hour-Slips would be full of it; each city eager and excited, waiting. In one moment I was more spoken of than in all my life before. That earlier calling had been vague, easily set down as fancy blown on by half-dreams. But now there was verification, clean as a measured weight.

Concerning Naani’s voice, I set down what she told; and it verified, in the main, the oldest Records long treated too lightly.

Somewhere out in the Outer Lands, at what distance none could discover, there stood indeed a second Redoubt. It was a three-sided pyramid, moderately small—no more than a mile in height, and scarce three quarters of a mile along its bases.

When first it was built, it had stood upon the far shore of a sea—where now no sea was. It had been raised by wandering humans grown weary of wandering and of night attacks by tribes of half-human monsters, which began to inhabit the Earth even early, in the days when the half-gloom was upon the world. The planner of it was one who had seen our Great Redoubt, having lived there in the beginning. He had escaped because of a correction set upon him for irresponsibility; he had caused disturbance among the orderly ones in the lowest city. Yet in time he too was tamed by the weight of fear, as wild blood is tamed by hunger. Being a master-spirit, he planned and built the smaller Refuge, aided by four million who were likewise weary of harass, though until then they had been wanderers, restless as wind in a door-crack.

They chose that place because they discovered there a sign of the Earth-Current in a great valley leading to the shore; for without the Current no Refuge can exist. While many built and guarded and cared for the Great Camp in which all lived, others worked within a great shaft. In ten years they made it to a distance of many miles and tapped the Earth-Current—not a great stream, but sufficient, as they believed.

Presently, after many years, they built their pyramid and took refuge there. They made instruments and ordained Monstruwacans and had speech daily with the Great Pyramid for many long ages. Thus, for a time, the two hearths of mankind called to each other across the dark.

Then the Earth-Current began to fail in that smaller Redoubt. Though they labored for many thousands of years, they found no better resource. So they ceased to have communication with us, for the Current lacked power to work the instruments; the receivers grew dull, and our messages could no longer be recorded.

Thereafter came maybe a million years of silence, with the continual domestic round—birthing and marrying and dying of those lonesome humans. They grew fewer. Some held it to be because the Earth-Current dwindled slowly through the centuries of that Eternity, so that bodies and spirits alike were underfed, as a people grows listless on thin broth and forgets what fullness feels like.

Once in a thousand years, maybe, there would be born among them one Sensitive, able to hear beyond ordinary. Such an one would perceive odd vibrations afloat in the night, and go listening; sometimes catch half-messages, stirring great interest in their pyramid. They would turn up old Records and write much and attempt to send the Master-Word through the night. Doubtless sometimes they succeeded; for our Great Redoubt held in its Records certain occasions when the Master-Word came, arranged and made holy between the two places in the early days of that second life of the world.

Yet for an hundred thousand years, Naani said, there had been no Sensitive among them. In that time their people had become no more than ten thousand. The Earth-Current was weak and could not put joy of life into them. They went listlessly and deemed it not strange, because of so many aeons of use. Their days had the thin sound of a house where no one sings and all footsteps are careful, as if even laughter might cost too much.

Then, to the wonder of all, the Earth-Current put forth new power. Young people ceased to be old over-soon. There was happiness and a certain joy in living, and a strange birthing of children, such as had not been through half a million years. The very air of their halls, Naani said, seemed to carry more tone—less dull, more alive; as if their world, long muffled, had found again some lost resonance.

And then came the new thing that touched us: Naani, daughter of the Master Monstruwacan of that smaller Redoubt, showed she was Sensitive. She perceived the odd vibrations afloat in the night and told her father. Because their blood moved afresh in their bodies, they had heart to seek out the ancient plans of the instruments; for those instruments had long rusted and been forgotten, as old words rust when no tongue uses them.

So they built anew an instrument to send forth a message. For they had no memory then that brain-elements had power to do thus; and perhaps, too, their brain-elements had been weakened through so many ages of starvation of the Earth-Current, and could not have obeyed even had their masters known what ours knew.

And in this wise, across the huge quiet, her calling had found me.

Toward the end of the Sixteenth Hour, when the lamps were turned low and the long corridors of the Mighty Pyramid fell into that hush which is not silence, but the careful quiet of a million sleepers breathing within one vast, warm shell, I made my preparations for rest. I had eaten little—only a thin cake of meal and a cup of hot water steeped with a bitter herb, for my mind had been running so hard that even the smell of food could not tether it. Yet I kept the small habit of order: I folded my outer garment, laid my knife where my hand might find it in the dark, and set by the bed the close-lidded jar of grease with which we anointed the joints against chill. The domestic things steadied the heart, as they always had, even in that last age of the world.

Then the aether thrilled.

It came not like the usual faint stirring which sometimes brushed the mind as a draught might brush the skin, but like a harp-string plucked too near the ear. The very air in the chamber seemed to ring without sound, and in my soul a voice rose clear—Naani’s voice, and yet with a strange authority in it, as though another breath pushed behind her words.

I did not answer at once. For all the tenderness I had for her, I had learned caution in the Night Land. So I sent out, steady and sure, the Master-Word—our sign that the mind which spoke was human and known.

Straightway the answer beat back, solemn as a heart: the Master-Word, true and paced.

I questioned her then—why she used the Instrument at this hour, when the Monstruwacans in the little Pyramid had long been in their sleep-time since the Eleventh Hour; why she was awake, and in the Tower of Observation without her father. I supposed she spoke through their sending-gear, for her voice struck into my brain with a clearness that in those days belonged to the Instruments.

Yet she did not answer my question.

Instead, into the midst of my careful enquiry, she poured words that were not of our ordinary speech—words that came dressed as a poem, and the first line smote me as a hand might smite the breast:

“Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night—”

My whole being trembled. For memory, long buried under the weight of ages, burst like a seed splitting at last in water. I saw—no, I was—myself in that ancient time, in that other life that lay behind the black gulf of the world, bending over Mirdath the Beautiful, and speaking those very words when she died and left me alone. The room of that old existence—its air, its scent, its warmth—rose up in an instant so sharp that it had taste. My mouth went dry. I gripped the edge of the bed as though the stone might keep me from falling through time.

With all the strength of my brain-elements I called to Naani, urgent for an explanation, for any small tether back to the present.

But again she made no direct answer. The line came once more, borne over the dark, repeated as if by one who can do nothing else but sing the one phrase she remembers.

And then a sudden knowledge struck me cold and bright: it was not Naani who spoke.

It was Mirdath.

I cried out into the night with my mind, “Mirdath! Mirdath!” and the far, faint voice came again through the eternity, giving me the same beginning, as if the rest of the verse lay beyond a closed door.

Yet it was both voices—the voice of Mirdath, and the voice of Naani braided together as two streams braid when they meet. In that moment I knew with the terrible gentleness of certainty that my spirit had been born again into the living time of the Only One with whom it had mated across the ages; that the long sorrow I carried like an old wound had not been meaningless, but a circle whose end touched its beginning.

I called to Naani with everything in me. No answering came. I called through hours, until thought itself felt ragged, until the effort wore my mind as grinding wears a stone. There was nothing but the vastness, and the throb of my own blood.

At last I fell into utter exhaustion. Still I could not find quiet, nor sleep—yet presently sleep took me, as it will take even the desperate.

When I waked, the first thing that stood before me, clearer than the walls, clearer than my own hands, was that line of verse. None in all this age could have known it. The phrase belonged to that lost room, to that old grief. The voice had been Mirdath’s; and Mirdath’s voice had been Naani’s. There was nothing to be said of such a thing except what the heart says when the mind has no language.

I called to Naani—once, and then again, and a third time.

In a little moment the Master-Word throbbed about me in the night, solemn and regular. I sent it back for assurance, and then Naani’s voice came, weaker than when she used the Instrument, for now she sent only with her brain-elements, and her speech had that faintness that is like hearing a melody through walls.

I questioned her eagerly about what had passed in the sleep-time—about those words, about why she had spoken them.

She disclaimed it utterly. She had slept, she said, through all that span. She had no knowledge of sending to me. But she had dreamed—and the dream clung to her mind as burrs cling to cloth.

As she spoke, I became aware again of the aether’s delicate shivering about her presence, the way her thought made a small weather in the vast night.

In her dream, she told me, she had seen a man: tall and dark, built very big, wearing clothing strange to her—an elder fashion, perhaps, or one from some other city of the far past. He had been in a small room, and in that room the air of sorrow lay thick, as if sorrow had a smell. He was lonesome beyond any common loneliness, the kind that makes a person speak to objects and listen for answers.

Naani in the dream had gone near to him.

Then the man made to write, as though the act of setting words down might take some weight from his heart. She could read what he wrote, though waking she would not have known the language. Dream had made her a food-historian of the spirit, tasting meaning in letters she had never learned, as one may taste salt in a dish without knowing the sea it came from.

But she could not remember all. It had flowed away on waking like steam from a pot. Only one thing remained fixed: he had written the name “Mirdath” above.

She spoke of the strangeness of that—how she could dream a name she had never heard; but she supposed I had planted it in her by my first callings.

With a tremble I asked her then—what line did she remember, if any, from the writing?

In a little moment her far voice said, very softly, as if afraid of its own weight:

“Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night—”

No more could she recall.

Yet it was enough. Something in me rose, wild and strange—a triumph that was also a fear, as though the universe had leaned close and spoken my true name. With my brain-elements I sent to her the remainder of the verse, the words I had spoken in that other time, the words which were mine and not mine.

I felt them strike upon her spirit and jolt her memory awake as a blow wakes a sleeper. For a little while she stumbled in silence, as if her mind had been opened and there was too much light. Then her spirit wakened fully, and she near wept—not from pain alone, but from fright at wonder, from the sudden sense of a life behind life.

Her voice thrilled about me then, and in it were tears—not tears of the body, but of the soul, cleansing and brightening the sound. And her voice was the voice of Mirdath, and the voice of Naani, not by confusion but by joining. She asked me, as one who stands at the Gates of Memory and sees beyond them, whether she might be truly Mirdath.

I was weak and shaken, and could not answer at once. My blood thudded strangely in my ears, so that even in that careful quiet I heard my own body as a drum. She asked again, using my old love-name from that dead time, and there was a sureness in her far tone as though she had always known it.

Still I was dumb, and then speech came swift, breaking like water that has been held too long behind a dam.

So was that meeting of our spirits, across the everlasting night.

If you would hold a picture of it, see her standing in her far refuge, in that lesser Pyramid out in the darkness, her eyes perhaps open in sleep, her hands perhaps clenched on a blanket—yet her spirit turned and looking back through the part-opened gates of her own memory into the past of our life and Age, as a cook looks back to the first starter that soured, the first bread that rose, and knows all later loaves are kin to that beginning. She saw more than I had ever been given in that ancient day. She had glimpses of other comings-together, other half-meanings, other circles that touched and went on. But of this present life, we spoke as of some yesterday—hallowed, and homely, and dreadfully dear.

The wonder of that surety stirred me fiercely toward completion. My whole heart cried out to be with her—not merely to hear her mind in the aether, but to stand where she stood, to break bread at her side, to share the small domestic rituals that keep a human soul from freezing in the Night Land: the warming of water, the saving of crumbs, the telling of old words so they do not die.

Yet how could it be won? None among all the learned men of the Mighty Pyramid knew the position of the Lesser Redoubt. The Records and Histories held no true bearing, only the general thought—passed about in talk and Hour-Slips—that it lay somewhere between the North-West and the North-East. No man had surety. None could conceive the distance: it might be thousands of miles, or only hundreds—either way, the Night Land between was hunger, desolation, and peril.

I spoke much with Naani of their position; she and her father—Master Monstruwacan of that Refuge—had no knowing of ours either, only that their Builder had come out of the Southward World in the Beginning, as their Records said.

Her father set an ancient Compass to bear, and explained through the Instrument that so great a power of Earth-Current must be ours that perhaps our force drew upon the pointer. For indeed, their needle swung helplessly within the Westward arc, wavering between North and South; it had ever done so for them, and was no guide. Yet we reasoned—on nothing firmer than yearning—that if the Earth-Current from our “Crack” had any pull upon it, then the Lesser Redoubt might lie to our North. They made the same reckoning and set us to their South. But it was guess-work built on sand, not a thing to lay life and soul upon.

Out of curiosity—though a million times it had been done in ages past—we set a compass before us, fetched from the Great Museum, a relic handled like a sacred spoon whose bowl has fed forgotten mouths. But it spun if we stirred the needle, and would stop nowhere with surety; the flow of the Earth-Current beneath the Pyramid tugged at it and set it wandering. It was strange to think that once such a thing had pointed steadfastly and led men across open land under the Sun.

We knew the positions of the Land only by tradition, from that ancient time when in the Half-Gloom they had built the Pyramid and still had sight of the Sun to name the directions. We in the far future had forgotten the beginnings of those names and used them because our fathers had, and their fathers before them, a million years and more. The same was true of our days and weeks and months and years—names for markings we no longer saw. Yet in that age it felt natural, as kneading feels natural though one has never seen the grain standing in a field.

Naani, hearing my constant questions, craved with a keen hunger that I might come to her—yet she forbade it, because she loved me. Better, she said, to live and commune in spirit than to risk my soul and perhaps die in the folly of seeking her in the dead world’s darkness. But had I known with surety the direction, had I possessed even a rough measure of distance, I would have heeded no forbidding. Love is a hunger, and hunger makes its own laws.

One other thing must be told: when I sent my speech into the night with my brain-elements, I came to know that whether or not I held the names of direction in mind, my body and spirit would turn often with a sure instinct toward the North. The Master Monstruwacan took great note of this. He had me experiment many times, screened about, eyes bandaged, so I could have no outward clue. Yet I would turn northward frequently by a certain inward feeling, and if I were turned otherwise by force I seemed unable to speak, as if the words could not find their path.

We asked Naani if she had such an unusualness; she could discover none. We could only note the thing and wonder at it. I set it to the attracting of her spirit, believing she lay somewhere that way in the darkness. But it was still only suppose.

The Master Monstruwacan wrote a study of my Northward turning, and it was set out in the Hour-Slips of the Tower of Observation. It was copied into the Hour-Slips of the great cities, and made much comment. Many called to me through the home instruments, eager, meddlesome, asking if I could hear their dead kin, or locate lost vaults, or tell them if the Earth-Current would last. I was diversely pleased and oft angered by such attention, for their voices made a kind of noise-pollution in the mind—cheap and constant, like the clatter of tins in a market—spoiling the careful inward listening that true hearing needs.

While I pondered how I might reach Naani, a terrible thing befell.

At the Seventeenth Hour, when the millions of the Mighty Pyramid slept, I was with the Master Monstruwacan in the Tower of Observation, taking my due turn. The tower was a place of disciplined quiet: instruments dimly glowing, watch-lamps hooded, the air smelling faintly of oil, metal, and the dry dust of old stone. Outside, beyond the lenses, the Night Land lay soundless in its own fashion; yet within the Pyramid the acoustic ecology of our living was always present—the distant hum of the Earth-Current, the soft sighs of ducts, the occasional far footstep of a watcher.

Suddenly the aether thrilled about me, and Naani’s voice entered my soul.

I sent the Master-Word into the darkness. The answer beat back, steady.

I called to Naani with my brain-elements, asking what troubled her in her sleep.

Her voice came weak, far, faint—so faint that I had to hold my whole mind still as one holds a spoon steady when pouring a thin sauce, lest the precious thing spill. In time I gathered her meaning: the people of the Lesser Redoubt were in deadly trouble. The Earth-Current had failed, suddenly and mightily. They had called her from sleep to listen whether we answered their callings by the Instrument; but no call had come to us.

Those who had been joyful with the fresh coming of power were grown old with sorrow in an hour. They feared the new strength had been only a final flicker before the end.

Even as we spoke, her voice seemed to draw further off, as if distance itself thickened. I felt as though my heart would break from the strain of listening.

Through all that remained of that sleep-time I conversed with her, as two lovers might speak who know they must part forever: saying little, listening much, trying to press into each word the taste of comfort. When the cities awoke, the news went throughout them, and our millions were in sorrow and trouble. The Hour-Slips became a flood of grief and speculation. In the markets and food-halls, where people usually spoke of rations and the month’s allotment of dried greens, they spoke in low voices of failing force and the end of warmth. Even the common cooking-smells seemed muted, as if fear had closed the nostrils of the whole Pyramid.

So it was for, maybe, a little month.

In that time Naani’s voice grew so weak and far that even I, who had the Night-Hearing, could scarce make real its meaning. Every word was a treasure; each syllable struck my soul like a fingertip laid on a bruise. Grief drove me so that I could not eat, nor rest. The Master Monstruwacan chided me—tenderly, with sternness that had love at its root—for if any help were to be given, how should it be done if I fell into ill-health?

Because of this, and such wisdom as was mine, I forced myself to eat and to order my life that my powers might not fail. I took broth thickened with meal, swallowing though my throat resisted; I chewed dried strips of meat until taste returned; I made myself drink the salted water that keeps a body from trembling. Yet all was beyond my strength. For I knew the people of the Lesser Pyramid were threatened by the monsters that beset them; and later, in faint far whispers in my brain, I learned there had been a fight with an outside Force that harmed many in their minds. In madness they opened the gate and ran out into the darkness, and there their bodies fell to the monsters of those lands. Of their souls—who may know?

We set this assuredly to the failure of the Earth-Current. Robbed of force, they were robbed of power, and in a few weeks all life and joy of living left them. Hunger and thirst grew strange in them, dulled; desire for living thinned—yet fear of death rose new and mighty. It is a cruel trick of the mind: when the sustaining current fails, the world becomes tasteless, yet terror sharpens like vinegar.

In the Great Redoubt, people thought newly of our own Earth-Current that issued from the Crack beneath the Pyramid, and of our latter end. Much was written in the Hour-Slips, mostly to assure the millions and keep hearts from disturbance. Some, as ever, spoke foolishly of immediate danger and spread panic. The truth lay perhaps between comfort and alarm, as it so often does.

The Hour-Slips were full also of imaginings of those poor humans in the Lesser Redoubt facing the end that must come upon all—even upon our mighty Pyramid—though most believed it so far away as to be no cause for present trouble. There were sad poems written to them, and plans proposed to rescue them—fine words, airy as steam, with no method beneath. It showed how loosely people speak when they live in over-security, warmed by a power they did not earn.

But in me there came a certain knowledge: I must make the adventure, though I achieved nothing save my own end. Better a quick ceasing than the slow gnawing I endured.

That same night, in the Eighteenth Hour, there was a great disturbance in the aether about the Mighty Pyramid. I was awakened suddenly by the Master Monstruwacan, who shook me from sleep with urgency, for the Monstruwacans thought they had heard, vaguely through the Instruments, the throbbing of the Master-Word. None of them was sensitive enough to say with surety.

As I sat up, the sound came plain to me: the Master-Word beating in the night about the Pyramid.

And immediately there was a crying in the aether all about me: “We are coming! We are coming!”

My inward parts leaped, and then sickened for a moment, so shaken was I by sudden belief. The message seemed to come from near—near enough to touch the outer wall. For an instant, mad hope rose: that Naani and her people, somehow, impossibly, had crossed the Night Land and were at our very boundary.

I sent the Master-Word out at once.

No answer came for a while. Then a faint thrilling about me, and the weak pulse of the Master-Word, sent by a far voice—strangely distant.

I knew it was Naani.

I asked whether she was within the Lesser Redoubt and safe thus far.

Presently her small voice spoke in my soul, weak and from an infinite distance. She had heard the strange pulse too, and that message: “We are coming! We are coming!” It had disturbed her mightily, waking her in her sleep. She did not know what to think, except that perhaps we had devised some method to come to them.

I cut that hope away, gently, though it hurt me to do it. I would not have her tortured twice—once by danger, and again by vanity. I told her she must not build on such believing. I spoke what little comfort I could, putting my words as carefully as one lays food into a sick person’s mouth, small and slow. Then I bade her sleep, as gently as I might, though I knew sleep in such a time is thin.

I turned to the Master Monstruwacan, who waited in quiet patience, hearing none of what I had heard and sent, for his hearing was normal. Yet his brain and heart were such as made me love him.

As I put on my clothing, I told him what had passed: that there had indeed been the calling of the Master-Word, but not by the Lesser Redoubt; that to my belief it came from nigh about the Great Pyramid; and moreover it was sent by no Instrument, but by the brain-elements of many calling in unison. There was something in that—something like a mob’s chant, the way many voices together can become one great noise that drowns out sense.

Fear and trouble moved in me, and yet a blind expectation too, for who would not feel it? But I no longer believed it had anything to do with Her nearness.

I said we should go to the Tower of Observation and search the Night Lands with the great spy-glass.

We did so. The tower received us with its familiar smell of metal and lamp-oil, and the faint tremor of power in the walls. We set our eyes to the glass and swept the outer darkness.

Presently we saw a great number of men pass over the Electric Circle that went about the Pyramid, where the boundary glowed faintly like a ring of pale fire. Yet they came not to us. They went outward—toward the blackness, toward the strange fires and hideous mysteries of the Night Land.

We ceased from spying and looked swiftly at one another, and knew in our hearts that some had left the Mighty Pyramid in the sleep-time.

The Master Monstruwacan sent word at once to the Master Watchman that his wardership had been outraged: people had gone out in the sleep-time, against the Law. None ever went out into the Night Land except when the Full Watch was posted to the Great Door, at a due time when all were wakeful. The Opening of the Door was made known to all the millions, so that all might be aware and no folly done without the people’s wotting.

Moreover, ere any might leave, they must pass the Examination and be Prepared, as the ancient rules demanded. The Law was framed sternly enough that there were still metal pegs upon the inner side of the Great Gate, where in the early days the skin of one disobedient had been stretched as warning. The tradition remained vivid; we lived close to the place, and Memory had no room to escape.

The Master Watchman, angered, went hastily with some of the Central Watch from the Watch-Dome to the Great Gate. There he found the men of the sleep-time watch, with the Warder of the Gate, all bound and stopped in the mouth so none could make outcry.

They were freed, and it was learned that nigh five hundred young men—judged from the bigness of their chests to be from the Upper Cities—had come upon them suddenly, bound them, and escaped into the night through the Eye-Gate in the top of the Great Gate.

The Master Watchman demanded why none had called by the instruments of the Watch House. Some had tried, but found they could not wake the recorders that lay in the central Watch-Dome. There had been tampering.

Afterward, new rules were made, and tests of the lesser instruments of the inward Pyramid were ordered nightly upon the coming of the sleep-time—called, even then by tradition, the Night, though always it was night without upon the world. And in the telling of this, I kept to the names that belonged to that age, for all our lives were bound in such old words, and words, once lost, do not return; and the loss of them is a kind of starving that no ration can cure.

After the ruin that had fallen upon the Ten-thousand, and after the fresh proving of what the Night Land would do to any who trusted it, there could be no more talk of succour. Those youths that went now upon the Road Where The Silent Ones Walk were already beyond our reach, as surely as if the dark had shut its hand about them.

Yet there were some who said we should call to them with the Home-Call—the old, great Voice that issued from the Machine above the sealed base of the Mighty Pyramid. But the Master Monstruwacan would not suffer it at first; for the Home-Call was not a whisper to a friend, but a proclamation to all the wilderness. It was a sound that, in elder ages, had guided the flying-ships; and in our age it had no common use, save to shout our living presence into a world that listened with hunger. To send it out was to tell every Beast and Power that some were abroad from the Great Redoubt.

And still there lay upon the Land an Influence—quiet, strange, like a pressure in the mind that the Instruments scarce knew how to name. It did not clang upon the measures as a storm does, nor flare as the gross energies of the night; it was rather as a hush laid cunningly across all hearing, so that the watchers grew wary of their own breathing. We had long held belief that this Influence issued from the House of Silence, that squat and dreadful building upon the low hill north of the Great Road. Many feared it was bent upon the youths, though none could swear it. In that place, certainty was a rare food; we lived mostly on guess and dread, and the careful saving of hope, as one saves seed-corn against a lean year.

So we waited and watched.

Through one day and one night, there ceased not multitudes from peering forth. The Pyramid seemed to have put aside its household quiet; even common talk in the ways and rooms had a guarded tone, as though the walls themselves listened outward. And ever, behind it all, the Land lay waked and unquiet. There were low roars that rolled across the black, not like thunder that promises rain, but like the grumbling of immense things moving their weight in darkness. Yet worse than the roaring was the way the sounds shaped the mind: each bellow or far bay set the heart measuring distance, and the ear straining for the next note, until the soul tired with listening.

The watchers at the Great Spy-Glass affirmed that the youths had not slept. They had eaten but once, and that as men do who forget taste and swallow only because the body threatens to fail. Their pace was a woeful speed—too steady for a band that meant to live, too urgent for any natural fear. They hastened north along the Great Dismal Road as though a command moved within their bones.

This did strengthen our belief that the House had laid hold on them, and drew them by some spell. For presently the Influence thickened, as if the air itself had been steeped in a cold infusion. A Monstruwacan came to the Master with report that the Instruments had taken sudden record of a mighty increase; and in the same moment, looking through the Glass, I saw the youths break swiftly from the Road Where The Silent Ones Walk and begin to run—run hard and straight—toward the House of Silence.

Then the Master Monstruwacan hesitated no more. He sent the Home-Call abroad.

It burst out across the night with that ancient bellowing; and the sound was so huge that it seemed not only to strike the ear, but to shove at the very chest, like the first shock of a great wave. All within the Pyramid fell into a silence that was not peace, but foreknowledge. The Master sent also a message to the natural eye, in set language, warning the youths that they were being drawn by a Force from within the House. He besought them to strive with their spirits and do battle for their souls; and if they could not win against that which drew them onward, then—ere they went through that doorway—he bade them slay themselves quickly, rather than enter to utter destruction.

And as his words went outward, there went also something else—something made not of Metal or Voice, but of the gathered wills of millions. From near five hundred thousand embrasures, and from the View-Tables besides, the people stared into the night until their eyes burned; and under their staring rose prayers, pleadings, dumb soul-wishes. It was as if the Pyramid itself became a great listening instrument; and the soundscape of the place—normally full of regulated hums, soft tread, the distant tick and breath of mechanisms—was overborne by a vast inward noise that my Night-Hearing knew.

It beat through the aether like the surge of a sea made of supplication. I had the sense of it as a pressure upon my spirit, and as a tone without pitch that yet had a rhythm; it stunned me with its might. And trembling, I looked again through the Spy-Glass.

The youths ceased from their running. They came together in a crowd, confused, with a staring manner, as people who wake suddenly from sleep to find themselves far from their beds. Then from the embrasures rose a roar of triumph—too early, as it proved—for the counter-force of our gathered yearning brake, and the Evil Force from the House drew them again. They turned once more and ran.

The Pyramid shook with a silence, and then with lamentation; for the mothers and fathers among us had seen their own children’s bodies obeying a will not theirs. Yet in that same moment there came a fresh wonder.

Before the youths, billows of mist rose suddenly—like pure white fire, shining very chill, yet casting no light upon them. It had the look of flame, but without heat; the look of fog, but with a hard gleam, as if it were a substance of cold radiance. It stood in their path and stayed their way.

We knew then that some sweet Power of Goodness fought for their souls. The thing was visible to all; to some with clearness, to many with doubt, for though the people of our age had more inward seeing than normal folk of elder times, none among them had Night-Hearing such as mine—to know a soul speaking in the aether half across the world.

A Monstruwacan came again to the Master to report that the Influence had ceased to work upon the Instruments; and by that sign we understood that the Force from the House was cut off—from us and from the youths. Hope moved through the Pyramid like a restrained breath, and voices fell low, as in a kitchen when a long-simmering pot is lifted and all wait to learn whether it has taken salt or spoiled.

The youths wavered. I saw it through the Glass and I knew it also by inward sense, by the natural wit that reads a crowd’s motion as one reads the set of a wind in corn. The Master sent the Home-Call once more, and with it a pleading: for their souls’ sake, and for the love of their mothers, to come swiftly homewards while the shielding Power held, and sweet sanity was allowed them.

Some seemed to look toward the Pyramid, as though the great Voice had hooked their hearts and turned them. For an instant, it was as if the long road of the world had looped back upon itself, and the lost might yet step again into the circle of the living.

But in a moment they faced about with obedience to one who always led them. I inquired and learned the leader to be Aschoff, a great athlete of the Nine-Hundredth-City. In bravery and boldness of heart he made—unwitting—to destroy them all. For he went forward and leapt into the billows of the bright, cold fire that barred the way.

Immediately the shining ceased. The barrier gave way and sank and grew to nothingness, as foam collapses when the sea draws back. Aschoff began again to run toward the House; and all that were with him followed faithfully, and ceased not to run.

They came presently to the low hill whereon stood that horrid House. Two hundred and fifty they were—wholesome of heart, innocent, save for the natural waywardness of spirit that makes youth desire the forbidden gate. They went up swiftly to the great open doorway, that had been open since the Beginning. Through it poured the cold steadfast light and the inscrutable silence of evil. The uncased windows gave out that same light and that same silence—an unholy desolation so absolute it seemed almost a substance.

Aschoff ran in through the doorway of silence; and they that followed. They never came out, nor were seen again by any human.

The mothers and fathers of those youths watched from the embrasures and saw that thing come to pass. The people held their tongues. Some said presently that the youths would come forth again; yet all knew in their hearts they had gone in to destruction. For there was something in the night that spoke horror to every soul, and there came a sudden utter quiet in all the Land.

To me, who had the Night-Hearing, there came a great fear that agony might be whispered into my spirit out of that quietness. Yet there came no sound—not then, nor in all the years after. It was as if they had passed into a Silence of which the heart cannot think.

That strange quiet which filled the Land, brooding within the night, was more horrid than all the roarings that had gone before. I had known the far-echoing low thunder of the Great Laughter; the whining that sometimes sounded in the night from the South-East where lay the Silver-fire Holes before the Thing That Nods; the baying of the Hounds; the roaring of the Giants. Those dreadful noises, though they fretted the nerves, were at least part of the world’s wild music—terrible, yet intelligible as danger. But that time of silence was an assault upon the mind’s need for any honest sound. It was like a land stripped of birdsong and wind, where one’s own pulse becomes an enemy because it is the only drum left.

After this, none had idle speech, even, that any should have power to succour the Peoples of the Lesser Redoubt. Neither had we knowledge of the place where it stood. It was made plain that those people must suffer and come unhelped and alone to their end. The Great Pyramid had already paid in sorrow for attempts at such rescue: only failure, loneliness of wives, and the grief of mothers. And now this fresh horror upon us, the loss of those youths, as though the night had taken a tithe from our very future.

For me, the sure knowledge that we could give no succour to the Lesser Redoubt lay heavy. I had held, perhaps foolishly, vague hopes of a secret expedition into the Night, of discovering that lesser Pyramid and bringing out those poor thousands. And above all, I had cherished the thought of a moment: that I should step forward out of the dark and put forth my arms to Naani, saying, “I am That One,” and she—she that had been mine in that bygone Eternity—would know me at once, and cry out, and come swiftly, and be again unto me in this age as she had been in that other.

To think that it might never be; to know that even while I stood safe within the ordered life of the Great Redoubt, she might be suffering horror under some foul Power—this was like madness. Often my hand would go to the Diskos, and my body would lean toward the doors, as though it were the simplest domestic errand: to fetch a loved one home, as one goes to the store-room for grain. Yet the errand was into the Night Land.

I called to Naani again and again, sending the Master-Word beating through the night, so she might know it was I that spoke, and no Monster laying lies upon her. I instructed her in all ways I could: never to be tempted forth from the shelter of her Redoubt by any message out of the night, but always to await the Master-Word; and to hold certain knowledge that no friend would ever seek to lure her into darkness.

Thus I spoke with my brain-elements into the black; yet it was weariful always to cast words into a void and never receive the answering beat. And still, once and again, I had knowledge that the aether thrilled about me, weakly; and to mine inward hearing it would seem the Master-Word beat faint in the night. After such moments my heart had a little comfort, as a man comforted by the remembered scent of bread when hunger has made him sharp with longing.

Constantly I put forth my soul to hark, until my health failed with the effort. I chided myself for lack of control and made a fight to be sane; yet day by day my heart grew more restless. Life seemed a small matter against so great a loss.

At times a Voice would speak plainly out of the night, purporting to be Naani. Ever I answered with the Master-Word; and the Voice had no power to return it. I did not jeer, for contempt is a kind of conversation, and therein lies danger. I let it bide, and the Voice would fall silent a time and call again; but never did I make speech with it—only the Master-Word, to its silencing. Then I shut the thing from memory and turned my thought to what was sweet and holy: Truth and Courage, but more often Naani, who was both sweet and holy to my spirit.

So it was plain there were Monsters without that tormented me—either to lure me to destruction or, having no hope, to plague me with malice. And all this trouble, and the giving of my strength through the night so that she might have comfort, worked upon me. I grew thin, plainly to the eye.

The Master Monstruwacan, who loved me as though I were his son, chid me gently and spoke wisely, and I loved him the more; yet my health did not return, for my heart destroyed me, as love will if it be held back and made always to weep.

It may seem strange my mother and father did not speak with me; but I had neither those many years, which I should have set down earlier. The blame is to my telling.

Then there befell a thing that forced decision. One night I woke from a sore troubled sleep, and it seemed Naani called my name—my old love-name—in a voice of utter anguish, beseeching. I sat upright and sent the Master-Word into the Night, and presently there was the solemn beat of it answering—weak, faint, scarce to be heard. I called again, speaking to give her assurance and begging her to tell what was wrong. My spirit shook with eagerness, for long it had been since her voice had spoken clear within my soul, and now—behold—her voice.

Yet though I called many times into the everlasting night, there came no more speech of Naani within my spirit; only at times a weak thrilling of the aether about me, like the last tremor of a struck string.

At last sorrow maddened me. I stood upon my feet, raised my hands, and gave word and honour to Naani through all the blackness: that I would no more abide within the Mighty Pyramid to my own safety whilst she, that had been mine through Eternity, came to horror by the Beasts and Evil Powers of that dark world. I bade her be of heart; until I died, I would seek her. But out of the darkness came naught but silence.

Then I clothed myself swiftly and went up to the Tower of Observation, to speak at once with the Master Monstruwacan, for my heart burned with intention and haste.

I came to him and told all—of the calling in my sleep, of the faint Master-Word, of my resolve that I would suffer no longer in quiet to no end. I would make adventure into the Night Land to find Naani, or else find a swift peace from this long troubling.

When the Master heard it, the matter sat heavily upon him. He besought me long and many times to refrain, for none might achieve so great a task; I should be lost in my youth within days. To all he said I answered only that this thing was laid upon me; as I had promised, so I must act.

In the end he saw I was set and could not be moved. He urged that I had grown lean with trouble and should wait awhile to put on full strength. But even as I was, so would I go. I told him gently that it was meet and helpful to my soul’s safety; my strength still was in me, and my leanness seemed to me a kind of cleansing, as though dross and littleness had been burned away. Fear was not in me. I laid it all to the count of love, which purifies and makes fearless the human heart.

Because I was thus prepared in spirit, I was the less troubled by evil Forces; long and sore had my preparing been, though I had not named it so. None, I wot, had gone forth into the Darkness so long withheld from that which weakens and taints.

Now it must be known that the Three Days of Preparation, proper to those who would go forth into the Night Land, were chiefly for cleansing of the spirit, that the Powers of Evil might have less hold. But also none should go out in ignorance of the full dreadfulness of the Night. In the Preparation were made known certain horrors not told to the young—tales of mutilations, abasements of the soul—things that, if whispered only, could shake the heart with fear. These were written in no book that might be lightly come by, but were warded and locked by the Master of the Preparation in the Room of Preparation.

When I heard what I was to hear, I wondered that ever any went out into the Night Land; that the Room of Preparation had any who meant to depart, and not only students seeking grim knowledge at safe distance. Yet this is the way of the heart: adventure is the lust of youth; to leave safety is the waywardness of the spirit. Who shall wholly reprove it? It would be sorrowful if that Spirit of Man should cease. Yet I uphold not fightings to the death between man and man; rather I sorrow upon such thought.

When the morrow came—if thus I may name it, for outwardly all was night though within the Pyramid time moved in its measured fashion—I went to the Room of Preparation. The door was closed upon me, and I underwent the Full Preparation, to have full aid toward success through all the terror of the Night Land.

Three days and three nights I abode within. Upon the fourth day my armour was brought. The Master of the Preparation stood away from me, silent and sorrowful, touching me not, coming not near to aid, having no speech; for none might crowd upon me nor cause me to answer.

Presently I was clad in the grey armour; and beneath it a close-knit suit of special shaping and texture, fitted to the armour’s form, to keep me from dying of the cold of the Night Land. I took also a scrip of food and drink, prepared to sustain life a great time, and stitched about with the Mark of Honour, so that I knew loving women had sped me with their hands—setting careful stitches as housewives do when they mend a child’s coat against winter, and thereby putting a little of the warmth of the Pyramid into what I must carry into the world’s dead chill.

Now went I forward a space, and made my mind to look not backward; for if a man would carry help unto a Maid that calleth from afar in the darkness, he must needs go with the whole strength of his heart, and not let tenderness pull him as a hand upon the shoulder.

Yet tenderness did pull me, all the same, not with force, but with the quiet weight of a house wherein one hath eaten and slept safe since childhood. I carried the savor of my Home with me, though there was no kitchen-smoke in that place, nor any hearth upon the outer earth. It was a memory upon the tongue more than upon the mind—bread and salt and the faint sweetness of warmed milk—those plain daily kindnesses that, set together by custom through a million years, become a kind of prayer.

And my thought was warm toward the Master Monstruwacan, because he had come down in armor to stand with the Full Watch at my going. He had made a ceremony of it, not for show, but that my spirit should be held up straight; and I understood then that rule, even when it feeleth hard, hath often a hidden gentleness, as a careful hand that bindeth up meat for keeping, lest spoil come.

All the while as I went, the aether about me was in a turmoil—no visible thing, but a pressure upon the nerves, a continual washing as of far surf—made by the blind wishings of the great millions that I had but now left. It was as though the whole Pyramid breathed after me, and the breath carried voices that had no words, only longing. The air itself seemed troubled with them, and it came to me, even in that first while, that such a great thinking-together must needs be like a beacon unto any Evil that hearkeneth in the Night Land.

Presently, as I steadied from the sharpness of parting, I became aware of the exceeding coldness of the outer air, and of the different taste of it upon my lungs and in the mouth. It had a keen sharping upon the palate, like metal touched to the tongue; and it filled the lips more heavily in breathing, as though it were thicker, and had more body. Each draught was a thing to be chewed, in a manner—dry, biting, and clean as frost. So I knew again, as the books did say, that the air of every height and place was different; and how the folk of the Cities did migrate, under rule and measured guidance, to the levels that best fitted their health. There were always some contrary, that would choose ill air for pride or whim, but most did take that which kept the blood sweet and the mind clear.

And I thought, too, of the deeps of the Fields, where the air is utter varied and wonderful—some places with a damp that smelleth like old cellars and mushrooms, some with a bitterness of sulphur, some with a faint resin as of broken pine in a long-dead world. There might be airs that would charm one man and make another sorrow, according to the body’s liking and the soul’s habit; and I had a strange desire then, as one that loveth the histories of bread and seed, to know what grasses and fruits might have grown once in those several airs, when the world was yet lit. But this was a thought to put by; for my business was life and death.

Thus went I, full of new thoughts and olden memories, and fresh-breaking wonders; and not without doubts and fears. It was a cunning strange thing to be out in the Night Land—not yet far, yet truly out—where often my fancies had walked while my body lay safe within metal walls. Until that hour I had never set foot upon the outward earth. In this present age, when men go abroad as they list, such a thing would seem a quaint conceit; but there, it was as though I had stepped out of the womb of the last human world.

So came I at last nigh to the Circle that went about the Redoubt, and presently I was come to it. I was astonished that it had no great bigness; for I had reasoned it mighty, as a wall of light. Yet, lo, it was but a clear tube, not two inches in thickness, and it sent out so bright and strong a shining that, seen from afar, it deceived the eye into believing it broad as a roadway.

This is a little matter to tell, and yet it held much; for I had seen, as child and man, Things and Beast-Monsters peer over that same small tube of light, their faces thrusting forward out of the black. We children, upon holiday-times, would take watches by hours through the great glasses of the embrasures. There was a game among us, each hoping to be first to cry out that a monster looked inward across the Circle’s shining. They came oft enough; and presently would slink away, having in truth no liking for that light.

We took pride to perceive the monsters that had most ugliness and horror to commend them; for thereby one won the watching-game, until some other child discovered a brute yet more fearsome. Yet always—so it seemeth to me now—there was beneath our rejoicing a half-known shudder, and a child’s gladness in safety, not understood. And now, at the Circle, all those memories rose quick in me, as a smell will bring back a whole room and a whole season; and I gave back a little, unthinking, having suddenly the imagining of some great face coming out of the Dark beyond.

I stood a brief moment, and then grew free in my heart to have courage of farewell. So at last I turned me to the viewing of that wondrous Home of the Last Millions. The sight did uplift and astonish; for indeed it was a mighty thing upon the earth.

Well might monsters and forces gather about that Hill of Life out of all the Darkness; for the Redoubt was as a mountain that went up measureless into night, and spread out upon the earth to right and left of me, seeming to hold all the world in its grip. In the nearer slope were three hundred thousand great embrasures, as I knew; and in all the four sides twelve hundred thousand, set down in school-books and upon the covers of the old Atlas-books, and in many other places.

Half a mile upward was the lowest tier of the great window-places, and above that countless tiers. A great light came out from them into the darkness. I looked up many miles, and still saw the shining rows, each embrasure separate from his fellow for a long way; but higher yet they thickened into a constant glimmering fire, shaping a bright peak into the blackness, dwindling at last into the utmost height. Thus stood that Everlasting Monument.

Then it came to me that the little things clustering against the embrasures were in truth the countless millions within. In the lower embrasures I could make it out more plain; they were set against the light within, yet were as insects in that distance, within so huge a bulk. And I knew they looked out upon me and watched through their spy-glasses.

I sent my gaze upward along the great slope of grey metal, up and up where it strode glimmering into the black, and at last to the little star that crowned the Wonder in the eternal night. For a little I stared toward that far point; it came from within the Tower of Observation where so lately I had spended my life; and I knew in my heart that the dear Master Monstruwacan bent the Great Spy-Glass upon me, through which so oft I had spied. I raised the Diskos to him in salutation and farewell, though I saw him not through that vastness.

My heart was very full; yet my soul the stronger. And then I was aware of a murmur in the night, dim and far; and I saw the little shapes of the people in the lower embrasures in constant movement. I knew upon the instant that the multitudes took my salute unto themselves, and cried out and waved their farewells, or cried to me to come back, as may be. It was as if a whole city were a single body, and the body had found its voice.

Indeed I was but a lonesome person looking up at that great mountain of metal and life. I knew I had danger if I let myself dwell. So I raised the Diskos reversed, as was meet from one young man unto all the millions; and I looked swiftly upward through those eight great miles of night unto that final light, that my friend should know I thought of him in that last moment. And it may be the unseen millions far above, in the Upper Cities, took it also as farewell; for there came down out of the monstrous height a far faint murmur, like a vague wind.

Then I lowered the Diskos, and turned me about. I breasted against the Air Clog, and stepped forward across the Circle into the lonesomeness of the Night Land. I looked no more behind; for Home, if looked on overmuch, doth soften a man until he break. I made determination not to turn my head again for a great while.

Yet about me, as I went, there was a constant surging in the aether, telling how my people and kin had continual mind of me, in prayer and wishings and perpetual watching. It gave me a feeling as of being companied; and then, in a time, it came to me that this same disturbance might tell to some evil force that I was abroad. But how to stop it? Who hath power to still the thinking of millions? Even had I stood among them and explained the danger, they would have been powerless to cease; for to have such great multitudes a-think upon one matter is of itself a cry in the dark.

At the beginning I walked outward somewhat blindly, without sure direction, being intent only to set a good space between me and the ache of leaving. But after a while I ceased from overswiftness and put thought to my going.

I reasoned that I should try a new way through the Land; for it might be there was an over-watchfulness upon that part trod by the Youths. So I began to practice this thought, and went not direct toward the North, but toward the North and West; meaning in the end to circle round to the back of the North-West Watcher, and thence to the North of the Plain of Blue Fire, and afterward, as might be, take a straight way to the North. By this planning I should come a long way clear of that House of Silence, which put more fear upon me than all else horrid in the Land. This would make me a greater journey, yet better to go slow and come to success than haste toward destruction. For surely destruction would be mine, if I went unwary.

It may be wondered that I went so assuredly to the North. Yet I went partly by an inward knowing, and partly by much latter studying of olden books within the Pyramid, and by reasoning upon things I had observed that seemed to bear truth. I had long had a liking for the old tales—those that most folk counted fairy-stuff—because I had learned, like a careful keeper of recipes, that even a corrupted copy may hold the taste of the original, and that the true thing, though changed by many tongues, doth sometimes shine through.

Thus it was that, but a while back, I had found a little book of metal, strange and ancient, hid in the Great Library, forgot through ten hundred thousand years, maybe, or more or less. Much in it was common knowledge, and set mostly to the count of nursery romances; even as we of this age do not take over-surely any belief in myths. Yet that book spoke as though it quoted from the pens of those that had witness, and it held a gravity that made one listen as to an elder speaking in a dialect that is dying, wherein certain words cannot be said in the newer tongue without losing their strength.

It told, again, what I had often heard, how once—monstrous far back from our time, yet utter future to this present age of yours—the world brake upward in a vast earth-quaking that rent it for a thousand miles. There came a mighty chasm, so deep that none might see the bottom; an ocean rushed therein; and the earth burst afresh with a sound that shook all cities; and a great mist lay for many days, and there was a mighty rain. Such things were set in certain histories and old records, but the people of the Pyramid took them not seriously—only as quaint studies, or tales to entertain nurseries, and sometimes wise men, who love to play with wonders as cooks with spices, for the pleasure of the smell more than for nourishment.

But in this book, the telling had the savor of truth. It was as if a food historian, writing for those not yet born, had set down not only the list of ingredients, but the exact smell of the pot at the moment it turned, and the sound of the boil, and the manner of stirring. There were touches that cannot be forged by one that never stood by the fire.

Further, at the ending, it spoke of a time afterward—maybe an hundred thousand years, maybe a million; who shall say—of a huge valley coming out of the West toward the South-East, turning thence Northward, a thousand miles both ways; its sides an hundred miles deep; the sun standing in the western end making a red gloom for a thousand miles; and in the bottom great seas, and beasts strange and plentiful. This, at first seeming, was mere romance. Yet I turned my wits to their natural end.

For I had in my heart a belief, grounded upon many hints, that the world had once been light. So I conceived that in a bygone eternity there had been indeed that great earthquake; and that along a curve of weakness the earth burst, and one of the oceans fell into the yawning furnace and became steam, and brake upward again, tearing the land with its uprising; and so came mist and confusion and rain. This was seemly, and no light tale.

Then, in the book’s ending, there wrote one living in a vast later age, when the sun had come nigh to his dying, and the upper earth was grown quiet and cold and not good to live upon. In that time the mighty chasm, calmed by the weight of an eternity, was now a deep wondrous valley holding seas and hills and mountains; and in it were forests, and lands good and healthful; and places given over to fire, steamings, sulphur clouds, and poisons ill for man. Great beasts were there in that far depth, such as none might see save by strong spy-glass—old kinds bred anew by the inward warmth, as though the primal world were born again in the ending, to breed monsters both ancient and peculiar.

The latter part was constrained and difficult, not like the plain speech of the earlier tellings; as if written in a speech already changing, with old words failing and new words too thin. I must set it out in my own manner.

It seemed that man had once come to softness through ages of over-ease. But the world grew cold as the sun ceased. Then there rose a race hardy upon the earth, made to fight for life, who perceived that the mighty valley was a place of warmth and living. They adventured down those wondrous heights, and were many ages coming safe to the bottom; yet found safe places on the downward way where they built houses and lived and begot children. Those children grew to a life of continual climbings and hard workings upon the Road, which was the one intent of that people; so the book called them always the Road Makers.

Thus they made downward through long years and ages. Many generations lived and died and saw not the reaching of the Road into that great vale. But in the end they came there with it. They were very hardy; they fought the monsters and slew many; they built many cities through great years in the mighty valley; and they made the Road from city to city along that valley even unto the Bight. There they found constant darkness and shadow, because the sun could not shine around that great corner. Yet they ceased not, but took the Road around, and a mighty way unto the North, passing it among strange fires and pits that burned from out of the earth.

But presently there was such power and horror of monsters and evil things in that valley of shadow, that the Road Makers were made to go backwards into the red light filling the westward valley from the low sun. They lived in their cities mayhaps an hundred thousand years, and grew wise and cunning; and their wise people dealt and experimented with forces distasteful and harmful unto life, not for evil, but ignorance, thinking only to know more. Yet they opened a way for those forces, and much harm and pity came thereby; and then all had regret, too late.

After an hundred thousand years, or more, there came the utter twilight of the world as the sun died further, until it gave but a gloomy light. Wildness grew upon many; shameful things were done that had been unthinkable in the light; there were wanderings and consortings with strange outward beings; and many cities were attacked by monsters from the West, and there was pandemonium.

Then was an age of sorrows and fightings, and a hardening of spirit for those of good fibre. There grew a leader, and he took all the sound millions and made a mighty battle upon foulness and all that troubled them, driving enemies down the valley and up the valley, scattering them utterly. Then he called the people together and made plain how darkness grew upon the world and how the foul powers abroad would be more horrid with greater gloom. He urged that they build a mighty refuge. They acclaimed, and built a great house; but it was not proper. So that man took the people to wander; they came to the Bight; and there was built at last the great and mighty Pyramid—our Redoubt.

This was the sense of the book. But late had I read it, and spoken somewhat with the Master Monstruwacan of it, though not overmuch; for a sudden mind to go had seized me, and all else fell away like husks. Yet to us it seemed clear there was no life in the invisible upper world; and surely the great Road whereon the Silent Ones walked must be the same Road the hardy peoples made. So it seemed wise that if any should find the Lesser Redoubt, they must do so somewhere within that mighty valley. Whether the Road leading into the West, where was the place of the Ab-humans, should bring me to it, I had no knowing; nor whether it lay on the Northward way beyond the House of Silence. I might wander a thousand miles wrong—if, indeed, I came not into dreadful trouble before.

No reason of value was there to give hope that the Lesser Pyramid lay either to the West or on the Northward road. Yet I felt it somewhere to the North, and made determination to search that way a great distance first; and if I found naught, then soberly to consider it might lie Westward. In the valley, someways, I had assurance it must be; for the book’s bottom sense was sound. How should any live in the utter bleak deadly chill of the silent upper world that lay an hundred miles up, hidden and lost? The very thought was like trying to keep dough alive in a frost-room—no leaven would stir.

Strange it was to think of the mighty cliffs that girt us about, yet were held from us by the dark, so that I had not known them save by that book; though it was always supposed we lived in a deep place of the world. Yet most had believed it rather an ancient sea-bed sloping gradual away, not an abrupt savage uprising of rock and height.

Here I make clear, as I may, that the general people had no fixed thought upon such matters. Something was taught in the schools, but it was divers conclusions, as different teachers reasoned. One, lacking imagination, would scoff; another would take it staidly; some would build fancy upon records and make foolish what had grounding in truth. So it is ever. To most within the Pyramid, there was no deep conviction nor steady thought of a great hid world in darkness. They believed what lay before their eyes; and many could not even imagine there had ever been any other condition.

To them it seemed meet that there should be strange fires from the earth, an ever-abiding night, monsters, and mysteries. Most were content, save those with yeast of imagining or pimples of fancy; the first might be called back to sanity, and the second would talk much foolishness. Yet with children, as ever, the old tales had more believing; and the simplicity of the wise did mate with the beliefs of the young; and between them, in that narrow place where speech faileth and experience standeth alone, did lie the truth.

And I, going solitary into the Night Land, carried both: the book’s grave old telling, and the child’s half-known shudder; and above these, the continual soundless murmur of the millions behind me, troubling the aether like wind over a field of ripe grain that cannot be reaped.

Now as I went toward the North and West, I steered myself with a carefulness that was near to superstition; for the Great Watcher of the North-West had its own region, and I had no wish to give it knowledge of me, nor to have it cry out my passing to those other Powers that do hunt in the Night. Therefore I made my going crooked and patient, choosing shadow and low ground, and that dull cover of the moss-bushes, and the stony places where a man’s footfall is deadened. And in all that while, I kept a constant reckoning within me, like an housewife counting meals and hours, for I had before me a journey not to be measured by any sure yard; and it seemed to me that the least folly with my body would be paid for, later, in blood and in failing.

So I set rules upon myself, even as one sets a kettle to the fire in due time, or turns bread at the right moment; for such small orderly doings are the very saving of a man when all the world about him is disorder. I made it my purpose to go forward, and at every sixth hour to eat and drink, and at the eighteenth hour to sleep until the twenty-fourth. Thus should I have three eatings in the circuit, and six hours’ rest, and be the less broken when need should arise for strength and quickness.

Yet, as is common with human nature, I broke mine own good rule at the very start; for the fear of that Watcher and the thought of the open spaces made me to push on one-and-twenty hours without cease, now walking, now creeping, now lying close as a beast lies when a hawk is abroad. And when, once or twice, the thought of food came, it turned sour in my mouth, as if I had chewed brimstone; so that I put it aside, saying within me, “Presently—presently,” and went on, because the Night Land drives a man’s mind into odd corners.

But when the one-and-twenty hours were gone, weariness took me, and a faintness also, and I knew I must have a rest, though it were but a poor one. Then, after a little, I saw afar off a small fire-hole such as I had passed at odd times, even so early in my journey. I made toward it; for there is comfort in warmth, and I thought to find a dry place where I might lay down, out of the chill.

And when I came near, it seemed, for all its evil smell, almost cheerful in that waste. The hole was but a few paces wide, and full of a dull red glow, with a bubbling that was like thick stew at the first simmer, and it threw out a little sulphur-smoke, thin yet biting, that set my nostrils to smarting. The air there had a taste, as if a knife had been rubbed on a stone and then put to the tongue. I sat down at no great distance, where the warmth reached me without my being too close, and I laid the Diskos on the rock by my hand, as one lays a trusted tool within easy reach.

For a while I moved not. My limbs were heavy, and in my belly there was a hollow weakness; yet my mouth had no desire. I sat and turned me, and looked back to the Mighty Pyramid. Though I had come a good space, it still stood so huge that it seemed close upon me, and I was both comforted and put out of heart: comforted because it was Home and humanity, and put out of heart because its very nearness, by reason of its greatness, made my journey seem the more impossible, as if I had scarce begun. It is a strange thing: a mountain comforts a man by showing him where he belongs, yet it can also crush his courage by showing him how small he is.

I knew, too, that within that great mass there were countless millions, and that many would be watching. I made no sign; for I had resolved not to be ever turning to wave farewell, as a child doth at a door, but to go. Yet it was a queer feeling to sit so near to all mankind, and behave as if I were already beyond all speech and fellowship. And I had a thought—warm and homely—that the dear Master Monstruwacan might even then have me in the Great Spy-Glass; and that perhaps he watched me in that moment, as a careful elder watches a young one set out, making a show of sternness to hide his concern.

Then shame came on me that I should sit like a sulky invalid and refuse my vittles; and I thought of how foolish it would look, even to that far eye, and how I should need strength. So I opened my scrip and took three of the tablets, and chewed them and swallowed. They were strong food, all virtue and little bulk; yet they were poor comfort to the mouth—dry, with a dull tang like ground meal kept too long—and they filled not the belly. Therefore I would drink well, to have at least the sense of something within me.

To that end, I took one of the especial tubes and shook forth a dust into a little cup. The air took hold on it at once, as if an unseen hand stirred it; it boiled and fizzed like yeast in warm mash, and rose and changed, until the cup was filled with water—plain water, yet made by a thing that looked like grey flour. It is a wondrous provision, and I had been used to it; but there is ever a little strangeness in seeing thirst answered by so small a conjuring, with no stream, no well, no living sound of pouring—only the chemical whisper and the sharp smell that dies away.

Thus had I in a little scrip such keeping of life as might serve for a long time. Yet it was a comfortless manner of feeding, lacking the honest savor of bread, and the fat smell of meat, and the common sweetness of a cooked thing. In that hungry world, though, a man is thankful even for cold devices that give the body continuance.

Afterward I went over the things I carried, as one checks a pack before a long day’s march. There was the Diskos and the food-scrip, and besides these a pouch with various matters. And I took out the small compass that the Master Monstruwacan had made for me with his own hands, from an olden one out of the Great Museum. He had told me that I might go so far into the Night Land that I should lose sight of the Pyramid amid the darkness and the vastness; and if the ancient principle still lived within the machine—though turned no more to the North, but to the Pyramid—it might guide me Homeward, by that old obedience men once trusted upon the seas of the world.

I set it upon the ground; but it had no certainty, and did spin and waver. This I considered, remembering I was yet above that part where, far down, the greatness of the Underground Fields spread; and I was, likely, not far from the “Crack,” though a mighty way above it. The earth there had a different pull and a different secret life, and it made the little needle fret and tremble, as a creature will when it cannot find its path.

I wondered whether the Master, watching through the Glass, could see me make this test, for the fire-hole gave a good light, and the Glass had great strength. Yet there is no surety in that searching: the strange fires waver, and smoke and mist rise in this place and that; now the sight is clear where you would swear it must fail, and anon it dulls where you think it should go gaily. The Night Land is a false lantern, and plays tricks with the eyes of men.

Presently I put the compass away and made to compose myself to sleep. Yet here I will set down—though it be out of strict order—how later, after many days outward, I found indeed that the needle’s northward part did draw toward the Great Redoubt, which was a comfort to my spirit and would have been a matter of interest to the Master if I had returned. And after a yet greater time, far away, fearing to lose Home entirely in the Darkness, I pulled it out again and found it pointed no more to the Pyramid but westward, as if some great Power in the Night did sway it. Then came upon me a childlike wonder whether it might be that ancient Northward Force of which the books and my Memory-Dreams had told. Truly there should have been no doubt; yet in that age, to know with the brain is one matter, and to know with the heart is another. I had known always that such a Force existed; but I had not known it, with the true meaning of knowledge, until a little needle in my palm obeyed it.

And there was yet another cause for doubt: perhaps, I thought then, the power of the Lesser Redoubt had begun to pull upon the machine, even as the Earth-Current of the Great Pyramid pulled it still. If so, then might I be near the end of my journey; for that lesser power could not tug, save I had come close. Yet now I know it was the North that drew. I make much telling of this small thing, because it shows the manner of my inward mind in those days; and the lack of sure knowledge that is like a hunger, gnawing always.

But now, in that first time, I wrapped the cloak-matter—crossed shoulder to hip—about me and lay down beside the fire-hole. I laid the Diskos within the cloak, close to my side, and it gave me a queer comfort, as if I had a living companion. The fire-hole made a low bubbling and a faint hiss, and the sulphur-smoke came and went. In those drowsing moments when the soul goes outward like breath toward sleep, I had a half-sense that the aether surged about me; and I doubt not but that many within the Great Redoubt watched my every doing, and were stirred humanly at my commending of myself unto slumber. Their unity of sympathy seemed to shake the very air of the world, and perhaps I knew it dimly, and slept the better for it.

I was worn to the bone, and slept heavy and strong. Yet my last thin thoughts were of the Maid I went to find; and in dreams I had speech with her, and a strange happiness, as if all were touched with fairy-light and freed from sorrow.

From such sleep I was waked sudden by a great and mighty sound. My senses came instant, as a blade comes out of a sheath. I knew the Voice of the Home-Call went howling across the Night. Swift and silent, I slipped the cloak away and took the haft of the Diskos into my hand.

I looked toward the Pyramid for a message, knowing surely that a great need had arisen and some Terror moved toward me out of the Dark; else they would not have waked all the Night Land to knowledge that an human went abroad beyond the Refuge. Yet even as I peered, I could not keep my gaze wholly that way; fear made my eyes sweep all around. I saw nothing; only the vast blackness above, and, high up, the Final Light of the Tower of Observation, thin as a star and steady as duty. I crouched, holding the Diskos, glancing over my shoulders, waiting—listening with all of me, as one listens for a foot upon a stair in a quiet house.

Then, far up in that prodigious height, I saw quick darting flashes of strange green fire. I knew the Set-Speech. The warning came sharp: a Grey Man—a great grey monster—had made scent of me and even then crawled toward me through the low moss-bushes beyond the fire-hole, at my back. The message bade me leap into the bushes at my left and hide, that I might take it with advantage.

I had scarcely read the tale before I was gone among the shadows of a clump of moss-bush. Terror made sweat cold upon me, and my heart shook; yet my spirit was set hard to conquer. And there, crouched and hidden, I saw something come very quiet out of the bushes beyond the fire-hole. It was great, creeping, all grey in every part, as if it were cut from ash and old bone. The glare from the fire-hole troubled it. It laid its head to the ground and peered along the earth, spying under the glare as a brutish thing might do to see past a lantern. It crept back into the bushes and came out again at another place by the edge of the hole; this it did thrice to my left and thrice to my right, each time laying its head down, hunching its shoulders, thrusting forward its jaw in a wanton nasty manner, turning its neck as a beast turns when it scents prey.

This fashion shook my courage; for each time it went in among the moss-bushes I thought it had discovered me and would take me from behind. A man may be brave enough in the open; but to be hunted in silence, with the dark full of possible hands, is a different proving.

Then, in truth, the swift sense of my Hearing saved me. The thing went back into the bushes after that last coming out, and for perhaps a minute my mind would have it that it had failed to discover me and meant only to return to the Night. And then, lo, within my soul a voice spoke plain and warned me: it made a great compass among the bushes about the fire-hole; it had found me, and went warily to take me from the other side.

When I heard that voice within my spirit, I knew the Master Monstruwacan watched from the Tower and sent the speech with his brain-elements, having in mind that I had the Night-Hearing. I trusted it; for in the same instant the solemn throb of the Master-Word beat all about me in the Night, to give assurance. I leapt quick from that clump of moss-bush to another, crouched, watched all ways, and kept the ears of my spirit open, knowing he watched with me.

Suddenly I saw a little stirring of a bush to the back of where I had hid. Out came a great grey hand, feeling into the moss of the clump I had left, as if the thing peered from behind the leaves. Then followed the great grey head, and it thrust into that clump where, a moment before, I had been.

I knew I must strike. I leapt and smote with the Diskos. The blow was one—clean and final. The thing fell upon its side. Great grey legs came out of the hither bushes and twitched and drew upwards, while the head remained thrust into the moss where it had sought me. I stood away from it whilst it died; and in my hand the Diskos spun and sent forth fire, as if it lived and knew it had slain a great and horrid monster.

When it was dead, I went round to the far side of the fire-hole. I held the Diskos high, spinning and sending out fire, so that they within the Mighty Pyramid might know the Beast-Man was slain; for it might lie too much in shadow for them to see.

The Master spoke not again; for it was not meet, save to save me from sure danger. There are Powers in the Night Land that hearken to such doings, and it might be there had been already overmuch, even for my safety. Yet it could not be helped.

Now, being somewhat calmed, I knew the aether of the Night was disturbed by the gladness of the millions within the Great Redoubt. It was plain how great a multitude had given note unto the fight; their hearts had beat together in fear and sympathy, so that I felt companied and befriended, though yet shaken.

After a little I gathered my wits and looked to the hours, and found I had slept surely ten. I reproached myself; for I had slept oversound because I had not kept to my intended order. I resolved to obey reason thereafter, eating and resting in due season as I had planned.

With a self-reproachful heart I went round the small fire-hole and took up my cloak and matters. I turned and looked once up along the immeasurable slope of the Pyramid, climbing into the far blackness. I made no salutation, as I had resolved; and I desired not to stir the aether of the world by waking the emotions of the millions with any needless farewell.

Then I turned away and went off into the Night, swift and cautious, bearing the Diskos cunningly and with a kind of love for it; for it had befriended me and slain the foul Grey Man with one stroke. It seemed to know me, and to have comradeship with me—such as men of the old days may have felt for a sword that had saved their lives. Yet the Diskos was more than a sword; it seemed alive with the fire and flame of the Earth-Current that beat within it.

It was well acknowledged within the Great Redoubt that none might touch the Diskos of another; for the thing went crustily in a stranger’s hands. If any persisted in handling or trying to use it, it would act clumsy and bring hurt. This had been known, perhaps, a hundred thousand years, or more. Therefore men believed—and wisely—that an affinity grew between a person’s nature and the Diskos used in practice. And because of this, and because the place would else be lumbered with weapons of the dead, it was Law and Usage that the Diskos of the dead should be placed with them upon The Last Road in the Country of Silence, returning its power to the Earth-Current. There was wisdom in it; and yet I doubt not a human tenderness lay beneath also, for Love should ever mate with Wisdom to mother Comfort in sorrow.

So I went onward, looking ever to this shadow and to that. My heart stirred with swift fears; my body quivered, ready to leap aside, and then found nothing. I had imaginings continually of uncouth Brutes and Beings that might come out of the darkness. Yet there was also a proudness in me that I had come safe out of the Grey Man’s power and slain him. But praise belongs not overmuch to me; for I had died as I slept, but that those within the Great Redoubt watched me and waked me to my saving.

Presently, as I walked, faintness returned, and I knew I did foolishly; for I should have eaten after the fight. Yet a man may be forgiven some forgetting when his heart has been shaken. I sat down in a little clear place among the bushes and ate three tablets, and again shook forth the dust that turned in the air to water by that proper chemistry. The water had no taste of stream or stone; it was clean and cold, but without the remembered sweetness of a drawn well. Still, it eased my throat, which the sulphur airs had roughened, and it laid a small comfort in my belly beside the dry strength of the tablets.

After I had eaten, I sat a little and thought, and looked up at the great slope of the Pyramid in the Night. All the while I listened with ears and spirit, and kept the Diskos across my knees, turning my head frequent this way and that. But nothing came near.

Then I rose and went onward, and walked six hours toward the North and West. For a while I made much to the West, to come clear of the North-West Watcher. Yet after a space I did foolishly; for I changed my mind and kept something more toward the North, that I might have surer sight of that Monster.

This was rash and naughty; for if I were but seen, the grim Brute might signal the Evil Powers and I be met swift with destruction. Yet the heart is a strange thing, running from fear into rashness as a flame runs along spilled spirit. Perhaps some influence was upon me and drew me that way; but who shall say.

So I went forward, and now I began to keep my order better: at every sixth hour I halted to eat and drink, and to look a little toward the monstrous towering of the Great Redoubt, and to strengthen my heart again before I went on. Always I went warily, chiefly among the low moss-bush; but at times I must cross stony ground, and often places where sulphur puffed from the earth in low smoke, strong in the nostrils and ill to the inward parts. I stooped to crawl, and my hands bled somewhat upon the roughness; and after I was so troubled I put on the great gloves that belonged to the grey armour, and so was shod properly for that harsh going.

All the while I watched right, left, and rear, and kept a constant observation, as far as I might, of the Mighty Watcher toward which I drew nigh. And thus I went on into the Night Land.

Now I went downward, very quiet and slow, into that Darkness; and I went as a man goes in a blind pantry when the hearth is dead and the house asleep—one hand out, the heart listening, the breath held lest it make a sound that some other thing might answer. For this was no common night, but such a weight of blackness as seemed to press not only upon mine eyes, but upon my very soul; so that I had moments wherein I was near to believe I walked no longer upon earth at all, but drifted, untethered, into a void without floor or wall.

Yet ever was that folly corrected by the plain, rude speech of stone. I struck my shin on an upjutting rock and swore within; I went full upon a hidden boulder and sprawled, the armour taking the bruise that else had torn me; and so was I made to know, quick and sharp, that the world beneath me was hard and actual, and cared nothing for man’s dreams.

Only by the downwardness of the Mighty Slope had I a guide. For the dark was so utter that I could not set a true stride; and thus, though my will did urge me, I made scarce a mile in an hour, and sometimes in two. This fretted me bitterly, as hunger frets when it smells bread it cannot reach; and, indeed, I had an ache in me for the simple homely grace of a lamp—how in the Great Redoubt a light is not only for seeing, but for keeping a man’s thoughts in their proper shape.

Then I minded me of the Diskos. And I made it to spin, at whiles, not lavishly, but as a careful housewife opens a store-crock only when the need is true. The strange glistening would show a little tract of broken way down the Slope—one ragged shelf of rock, one scatter of loose stones, one slanting run of shale—no more than a few heartbeats’ measure; and I fixed that scant map within mine inward remembering, and then went onward again into blackness, feeling with foot and knee. Afterward, when the darkness and the stumbling shook my courage, I would fain have the blessedness of that light again, as a famished man longs for the smell of a pot that hath onions and salt and good fat set to sing.

And truly, the light of the Diskos did seem astonishing great, because the darkness about it was so monstrous and so without end. Yet I was sparing with it, knowing that the Diskos was my one true store against extremity, as grain is against winter.

So I went, hour upon hour, down and yet down, until six long and bitter hours were gone; and it was beyond the twentieth hour since I had last slumbered. Then I sat me upon the Mighty Slope in the everlasting Dark, and I did eat two of the tablets and made the water.

This small eating was a strange domestic act in that vast wildness; for there was no cheerful clink of cup upon board, no smell of broth rising, no kindly sound of a fire drawing breath. Only the faint rasp of my own fingers, the dry bite upon my tongue, and the water’s coldness in my throat. I could but feel and hearken, to know if I did all rightly, for mine eyes were useless as shut lids.

When I had done, I unfolded my cloak and wrapped it about me, and placed the scrip and pouch under my head, as a man makes shift with what he hath, turning gear into pillow. The Diskos I took to company me; and so I fell swift upon sleep, and as I went under, Naani came into my mind—not in clear image, but as a remembered flavour that strengthens a starving man: the thought of her was sustenance, though it had no words.

I slept near six hours, and waked sudden in that utter dark, as though some thing had touched me or come nigh. I got to mine elbow and listened so keen that I seemed to hear my own blood. I gripped the Diskos, ready; but there came not even a little sound out of all that Night. No scuffle of stone, no breath, no whisper of movement—only a vast hush that had weight, as though the earth itself held its tongue.

Presently I had more assuredness that naught did make harm about me. I sat up and reached for my scrip, and did eat and drink there in the dark, fumbling, for a man’s fingers are clumsy when sight is taken from him. Yet I was done in a while; and I set my gear upon me, and the Diskos into my hand, and so to my feet and forward.

All that day there was a strange unease upon my spirit. Oft I stopped to listen, because my soul told me something went near in the Dark, following quiet as a thief that knows the floorboards. Yet mine ears perceived nothing; and still I went downward.

In the early part of the seventh hour after I had eat and drunk, I had a sore tumble. My foot went sudden into a small hole, and I pitched forward, striking upon a sharp rock. For a time I lay very quiet, shaken; for though the armour saved my flesh from being ripped, the blow went through me like a hammer through a chopping-block. When strength returned, I judged I should go no more upon my feet, but upon hands and knees, so that I might feel the way and have less need to spend the Diskos.

Thus I crept all that day—bitter travel, yet not new to me, for I had done many a sore mile upon the Night Land in such manner. After eighteen hours of downward going, and three times eating and drinking, I sought a level place for rest. I felt about in the dark until I found a spot not so ill; and I pushed away such small boulders as would have irked me, making a little bed as best I could, as a farmer clears stones from a patch of ground before sowing.

Then I eat and drank; and afterwards composed me to sleep. Many thoughts of Naani went with me, yet also the memory of that half-fear which had haunted the day, as though something kept ever near. Twice I rose to mine elbow and listened; but heard no sound, and told myself it was fancy, though even in sleep I listened, as a man sleeps in a strange house and wakes at each settling of the timbers.

And when I had been asleep scarce six hours, I waked again very sudden, with that same belief of a Presence. I gripped the Diskos and hearkened. Still there was no sound that mine ears did wot of, nor aught that had power to be surely known of the spirit; yet the unease remained like a taste of metal upon the tongue.

The next day was as the day before; save that about the eighth hour I came near to fall into a monstrous pit upon the Great Slope. I fell with my breast upon the edge and clung, feeling the emptiness beneath as a cold breath; and I drew back and crawled around it in the dark, very slow, until I came safe to the lower side. This shook me more than before; for I knew not whether I had come into a country of such pits, or whether it was but a lone evil place to trouble me.

So for a great while I went over-cautious. Then I thought upon a plan to go with more surety and speed; but it needed a cord, and I had none. I searched myself and found no rope, and I thought, half grimly, upon the sayings of our time—how a boy is no proper boy if he hath no cord about him; and surely a man should be ashamed of the same lack.

Yet in the end I compassed it. I buckled the scrip and pouch together, and took one strap from the pouch—long and thin, yet strong. I fixed a stone into the end and buckled it fast; and thereafter, as I went upon hands and knees, I cast the stone before me and held to the hither end. Thus I had some knowledge if there lay a great deepness just ahead, for the stone would drop and draw the strap in a fashion I could read with my fingers.

So I went casting that stone continually, down the Slope. This was a cumbersome travel; yet I was in better case than at any time since first I began that descent into the everlasting Dark.

At the eighteenth hour I slept; and again I was waked strangely before the sixth hour, as I had been before. This continual waking did set a wonder and unease upon me; for it was as though some presence moved close at certain times, and my body knew it though my ears did not. Yet no harm came; and I forced myself to keep a quiet mind. That something was nigh in the dark I do truly believe; but I have no knowing that it was evil, for it harmed me not.

Three days more I journeyed thus, never ceasing to creep downward, weariful upon hands and knees. The Diskos I had to my hip, so that it should not weary my hand. Thus had I been on the Great Slope six days of utter Dark; and I had no wotting but that I went to some dire and dreadful place, for I had gone ever downward a monstrous way, and there seemed no end.

Here I must set down that the cold was much gone from out of the air upon the Slope; and the air was grown heavy unto my chest. This I perceived not only by breath, but by sound and touch; for the Night has its own hearing, and a man learns to read it as a cook reads the hiss of fat when it is ready for the onions. In the higher parts, the hush was thin, sharp, like a knife-edge silence wherein even one’s own swallow seems too loud. But as I went deeper, there came a thickness—as though the Dark had more body, and the very quiet had a different grain.

And it comes to me that I have said little of the air of the Night Land and the Mighty Pyramid; yet the matter is of great moment. For the air without was thin and keen, and lay not, as in this our age, in a great height above the land, but only nigh the earth. And there was a wondrous difference between the air within the Pyramid and that without around the base; for upward beyond a certain height there was no outward air that any should breathe, and so were the upper Cities sealed for ever. Whether the base itself was sealed utterly from the outer air I cannot surely remember, nor do I know how much newness of air came to us from without; for we drew air, as I mind, from the Underground Fields, and how those were renewed I have no knowledge. Truly I could write an hundred books upon that wonder of the Future and still be lacking.

It was common knowledge that the Peoples of the Upper Cities were greater to the chest than those of the Lower. Any could know an Upper man by that breadth and depth of breathing, as we in this age mark other peoples by colour or stature. There had been, in the Histories, a plan once to move peoples up and down through the Pyramid, city to city; but it met with disfavour and was put out of force, which is easy to understand, for the heart clings to its own level and custom.

Mayhap the great travel that each youth and maid made through all the Cities—three years and two hundred and twenty-five days—was for health as well as training of the mind, that they might breathe every height and discover what air best suited their need. Yet I cannot be sure; for Reason in this age may blind within me some Knowledge of that far time.

Also, there was in all the Night Land no flying thing, because the air was grown very thin. The Records told of monstrous flying-brutes in long-gone ages, that went over the Land in mighty bounds; but we could but suppose the Records true. And when the Monstruwacans learned I would journey out seeking Naani, there was foolish, well-intended talk that I take a small flying-ship from the Great Museum—sound yet, being of the grey metal that seems to have no power to cease. But none had the skill to master it, for the art was lost, and the Book of Flying told how mastery came only by constant practice and many a wreck; moreover, the air was too weak to uphold such a thing. And even had air and skill been sufficient, it were madness to hang aloft in the night, making a great noise for all the Evil to mark.

All this I set down, though it wearies me, because it touches upon what I did perceive next. For as I came deeper still, I perceived by mine ears and by my fingers that the air was changed again—grown thicker and more abundant, like unto the air of this our Age, or near it. And because of this, each time I made water it did fizz upward sudden, very loud and plentiful, and boiled over from the cup to the earth, wetting my hand. This small, rude bubbling in the dark made a strange companion to my thoughts; for it was as though the world spoke in a different tongue down there, and my body was forced to learn it without any teacher.

Thus I went on, down for ever, until I was near ready to believe I should come presently to the middle of the world.

Then, far off in the deep of the night, I perceived a little shining—weak and unsure. I cannot set down the ache of it. It was hope and pain together, like the first scent of bread to a man who hath long gnawed dry stuff in the dark. I grew sick in all my being to behold once again the blessedness of light, and to have proof that I went not downward to an utter desolation.

I stood up from my knees and stared very earnest. At whiles it seemed plain; at whiles it seemed naught but fancy—my desire making a deceit in mine eyes. Then again I saw it clear, not to be mistaken; and a shaking came upon me. I gat me to a run, and made a mad speed down the dark Slope; and lo, I had gone scarce any way when I went headlong and near brake myself. I held my teeth together fierce and quiet until the pain was somewhat gone; for noise is a kind of advertisement in that Land, and I had no wish to publish myself.

Afterward I went again upon hands and knees, as before, and so for a great hour or more, looking oft. Ever the light became more plain, yet it came and went oddly. I went six hours before I was anywise near it, by which you shall know how far off it had been. And when I thought myself anigh, it was still far away. Not until three hours more was I come indeed near; and all that while I went yet downward, though the Slope was no longer so utterly dark.

Presently I paused and stood upon my feet, to perceive the light the better. And as I looked, I heard far off a sound in the dark—as that something set up a strange and monstrous piping in the Night. It was not like wind in trees (for there were no trees), nor like water over stone, nor like any honest creature’s call. It had a long, hollow keening, as if the very rock were made into a flute by some breath that was not of man.

Immediately I went to my hands and knees among the stones and kept myself low, that I should be less plain to be seen should any Monster approach. Yet there came nothing to trouble me; and I went downward yet another hour, and all the time the piping grew more in the great eternity of the Night upon the Slope, until it seemed at moments as though the earth sent forth sound and revelry of wild roarings, a whole choir of hollow throats.

By this time I was truly near unto the light; yet I did not behold it plainly, for it burned beyond monstrous rocks that stood as barriers. I went to the left, maybe the half of a great mile; and all the while the piping made a mightier whistling in the Night. I went the more silent, and at last knelt among three rocks and peered forth upon the place before me.

Now I perceived that I crouched within the mouth of a mighty gorge. The left side was a great way off, seen at whiles when the light rose; but the light was to the right, and it was so wondrous great that it made clear to me a mountain on that side of the gorge, going upward into the everlasting night as though for ever.

Afar down the gorge I saw the shinings of strange fires, faint and distant. Thus was I come at last to the bottom of the Mighty Slope; though the gorge also went downward, yet not so vastly.

Then I went forward again and opened the point of the rocks, as sailors do say. And I saw that there gushed forth a great blue flame from the earth; and the mighty rocks stood about it as olden giants grouped to some strange service.

In Reason I was not overmuch astonished, for as I drew anigh I had guessed the fire and sound were made by burning gas issuing among the rocks. Yet though it were a natural matter, it was a wondrous sight and set amazement on all my senses. The flame danced and swayed monstrously; sometimes it dropt so low as an hundred feet, and then went upward with a vast roaring to the utter height, standing mighty and blazing, maybe a full thousand feet. So great was that light that the far side of the gorge was lit plain and wondrous, though surely it was seven great miles off or more; and by it I saw the flank of the mountain that made the right hand side of the gorge, going up measureless into the Night.

In two hours more I was come clear down out of the Gorge, and stood at last upon that strange Country; and though I did feel new-troubled and all in a maze, yet was I glad beyond telling, because of the sudden splendour and sharpness of the light. For it was as if the world had been kindled again, and not merely shown.

A little ere I came down wholly, I had stood high within the mouth of the Gorge, to take heed of the land that lay spread abroad under me. And I numbered seven and twenty great volcanoes that lifted their red crowns into the dark; and this did not include two monstrous ranges of fire-hills burning afar off somewhat upon my right, nor the hundred thousand lesser places of flame and glow that prickt and fretted the face of the earth, as embers in a hearth.

Truly it seemed a land made half of fire and half of water; and the two did strive together in continual labour, like cooks at a furnace, never resting. For I saw a small fire-hill standing within a sea—no more, it seemed, than a mile from where I stood—its sides red and black as a pot that hath stood too long in the coals; and behind it, by scores, other fire-hills spread, so that the horizon was as a line of ovens.

And I must tell of the seas. For I counted three that were small, and one mighty sea that went onward, seemingly for ever, into the red light of the far fire-hills, and so was lost out of my sight, showing no end. Islands rose out of the waters; and upon the islands were volcanoes, like hearths set upon serving-boards; but in other parts the fire-hills came up sheer from the sea itself. Over the near sea lay a plentitude of steam, as though it boiled at whiles and in diverse places, and the very air had the wet tang and mineral bite of hot stone newly dashed with water.

And there was ever a muttering thunder within that red atmosphere—a low, constant shaking of the air, now from this distance and now from that. I judged it to be the voices of the fire-hills speaking with the fire that lived in them. It was no single sound, but a woven cloth of noises: far rumbles like wagons over stones, near hisses like kettles, and at whiles a sudden crack as of a great crust breaking. The sound did not sit outside of me; it entered, as smoke enters garments, and made the mind restless. Yet within it I did perceive also something homely, as if the world were busy at its own hard bread-making—kneading, boiling, rising, and falling.

You shall understand how utter new this was to me; for in that Country there was a constant Voice of the Energy of Life, so that the World-Noise of our Age did seem to be there again—keen, undoubted, and more apparent in some wise than now. Yet it was not the thin, cruel clatter of machines without soul, such as in latter days men praise for progress; rather it was the proper noise of Earth at work, a soundscape that had rules—pressure, steam, flame, wind—each answering the other. And I did feel how any creature might be made sick if it were forced to dwell among harsh, unnatural sound, such as a city makes when it sells art for coin and calls it music; but here the noise, though mighty, had meaning, and meaning doth steady the heart.

Now I will set down more closely the things that were ready to my gaze.

First, that which drew mine eye and held it, there was a huge blackened mountain upon the left of the Gorge mouth; and it went up into the night maybe fifteen or twenty miles. Upon the far side of it grew out a mighty peaked volcano, so high up as five miles, as I guessed; and above that again, nine or ten miles up in the blackness, there was a second. Yet this was not all; for upon the left crest of that black mountain there burned and glowed two other vast fire-hills at an utter height, making strange, smouldering suns within the night. Below those perched volcanoes were heaped vast mountains of ash and burned stuff, poured down through Eternity, building grey and sombre monuments to the dreadful glory of Time—like endless siftings of a bitter flour, cast out by the world’s own grinding.

To my right was ever sea and sea and the red blazing of the fire-hills; but to my left were mighty forests, and beyond the great woods rose monstrous fire-hills here and there. Thus you may take somewhat of that first impressing upon my brain and sense: a land that breathed by steam, spoke by thunder, and smelt of hot iron, wet rock, and green sap torn by heat.

After I came down out of the mouth of the great Gorge, I stood in pause; for which way was proper unto my search? I looked about long, and then climbed back into the Gorge, calling myself foolish that I had not mapped my way ere I came down. But when I was come up again, lo, there was but one way I could go; for to my right were only the seas, but to my left, where the shores met the seas, there seemed, so far as my eyes did tell, a clear going for a space. Mayhap, when I had gone so far, I should find a further way onward. And so I descended again into that red-shining Country of the Seas, as I did ever name it.

By then it was four and twenty hours since last I slept; so I was fain to find some nook and come to slumber. I found a neat place where three great trees grew about a little basin of rock, very dry and warm. There I ate three of the tablets and drank of the water—yet my belly yearned as ever for proper eating-stuff: for bread that yields under the tooth, for broth that sends up savour, for fat that coats the mouth and makes strength. The tablets did keep a man from death, but could not give him the comfort of a meal, nor bring back the memory of a mother’s kitchen-speech, nor any old words of thanks said in the tongue of one’s own people; and I felt in that moment how hunger steals not only the flesh but the very language a man speaks to himself.

I made my bed within the basin, and lay down, and began to think awhile upon Naani; but sleep took me ere I was aware.

Lo, I was sudden awake and found myself swimming in hot water; and a mercy it was that I was not drowned. I got to my feet. The basin was full, hot and steaming, and the taste of it pungent—salt and mineral, as if one had sipped from a pot wherein iron had been boiled. I perceived the water came in by a smoothed slit upon the far side, with strange gurgling and bubbling; and I conceived some deep well to boil and so drive up this water into the basin—glad was I that it did not boil as it came forth.

When I was upon dry land and considered, I supposed the water had poured forth at seasons into the basin through an eternity, and then gone back by fissures in the bottom. This I did soon find to happen a little beyond the length of every hour; indeed the basin emptied slow as I watched, as though the earth drank it away.

Being much wetted, I stripped off mine armour, having dipped out my effects from the pool, and came down to the naked flesh. I found a place where the rock was hot as a griddle, and there spread my garments. Whilst they dried, I got into the hot pool and had a very pleasant bathing. The heat did draw the ache out of my limbs as simmering draws the hardness from tough meat; the steam filled my nostrils and made my head light; and the continual bubbling made a small music—wet, lively, and not without comfort, like a pot set gentle over coals.

Yet I had the Diskos upon the pool edge to my hand, for I had no proper assurance. As it proved, there were monstrous beasts in that Country, though I did never feel the nearness and horrid power of any Evil Force. Those Powers, as I conceive, were congregate about the Mighty Pyramid, being drawn thereto by the great spiritual essence of so wondrous a multitude of humans gathered close, even as sharks come after a ship that carries bullocks. How the Evil Powers were given entrance into this state of our life I know not; I have thoughts on it, yet more is vanity.

Presently my clothing was dry. I came out from the bath—by then the water was nigh all gone backward into the earth—dressed again, and got my armour upon me. My mind was more lightsome; yet I was ready to sleep again. This I did, and had six hours more beside the pool; and once I was wakened somewhat by the gruntling and bubbling as the pool filled time and again. It was as if the land itself kept household hours: fill, empty; fill, empty—like some stern elder at a farm measuring the day by tasks and returning seasons.

When the six hours were gone I woke well fitted to go onward through that red-lighted Country; and after I ate and drank, I set forth.

All that day I went at a great pace, the nameless woods upon my left and the sea-shore upon my right. Oft the trees grew even into the water, so that I went among them, hearing the sea’s thick slosh between roots. A wonderful thing it was to me—who had lived so long under earth—that a glad and wild mystery doth live among forest trees. The groves of the Underground Fields had a solemn beauty, but not this untamed life; here the scent of the woods—resin, bruised leaf, damp bark, and at whiles the faint sweetness of some flowering hidden thing—came rich to my spirit.

All the while was the shore upon my right; and to my left and around me, the great forests. There was life in those darksome woods; living eyes peered out odd whiles and went back into dark. I knew not whether to fear or to have no heed; yet nothing came anigh to do me hurt.

Thrice that day I came to little fire-hills that burned redly and sent out fire and noise, and I heard their trouble each time through the forest ere I reached them. About each was a deadness where fire had slain big trees; yet the quick life of little plants grew nearer, born and living between the times of the bursts. It minded me of how a people keeps a handful of old words alive in secret, though the dominant speech presses upon them like flame; the small green things cling, and the great trunks fall.

In that one day I passed seven and thirty boiling springs—whether they boiled truly I know not, only that they sent out great steam oft-times, and some made a strong roaring, so that to hear them afar off in the forest was to think a wild beast roared strangely. The sound did trouble my thoughts; for in each roar there was a note that seemed to call and answer itself, so that my mind began to listen for patterns, as if the land had dialects of thunder and hiss that one might learn if one had years.

When the eighteenth hour was come, I sat as I had done at the sixth and twelfth hours, and ate two tablets and drank of the water, which there did fizz rich and quick upon my tongue. Yet it gave no savour, no warmth of fat, no comfort of grain; it was as if I swallowed the idea of sustenance, not the thing itself.

Afterward I lay down to slumber, greatly wearied. I chose a place against a great rock, so that no creature might come upon me from behind. I came easy upon sleep; yet I set it within me that I slumbered only with the body. For by the shining of eyes in the woods I knew strange creatures abode there.

Ere sleep took me I thought upon Naani, as I had done all that day, as though her spirit hovered near and strove pitiful to speak with me. So it seemed in my fancy. I blessed her and set determination into my heart that I make more desperate speed, if I might, to come the sooner to that unknown place in the dead world where stood the Lesser Refuge. And I slept in a moment.

Lo, sudden I was awake. Lovely was the brightness of that Country, for it showed my danger at once, and did not keep me suspended in fearful doubt as the grey darkness of the Night Land. I saw, as I got to mine elbow, certain things crouching within the borders of the trees, no more than a score paces off. I knew in a moment my spirit had been given knowledge and waked me.

I stared, gripping the Diskos. There were six squat men, humpt at neck and shoulder, crouched in a row, half hid by shadow. They watched me; their eyes shone like the eyes of beasts. Thus you may know somewhat of the terror that came upon me. Yet I had the Diskos and mine armour; and though my heart shook a little, my spirit was assured to conquer.

I got to my feet with the Diskos ready, and behold, I could not see the Humped Men; they were gone. Never did I see them go, though my gaze had been steadfast upon that place. I was near ready to believe there had been nothing; yet I knew they had been there.

Presently I looked and found I had slept five hours. I ate two tablets standing watchful, and drank, and made ready to go onward; for I was grown anxious to be gone. I had no knowledge but that those humpt men were but a little way off among the trees, and might come upon me in a moment; or else they went to call an army to my destruction.

When my gear was secure I set off at a great stride, holding the Diskos handy, looking this way and that. Truly I was grown so lean and hard that it seemed I had power to outpace those men or aught of their kind.

Through thirty great hours I went thus, ever watchful; and at every sixth hour I ate two tablets and drank a little, and went on. I hoped to be lost from the Humped Men; yet my faith did not company my hope. For twice and thrice it came to me that things went through the woods to my left, keeping level with my speed, yet always hid. This shook my heart and made my hope of little account.

Because I had no faith, I could not be easy to slumber until I came to a place proper and safe. So I went onward through thirty hours; and in all that while I found nowhere that fitted my need.

Then, about the end of the thirtieth hour, I perceived water ahead besides the sea that was ever on my right. I thought the sea went inward there; but it was otherwise. When I came nigh I found a river entered the sea, coming out of all the country that lay to my left.

In the mouth of the river was a small island. I looked across and thought it a refuge from the Humped Men that played dog upon my going; yet this was but an idle thought. My need was to cross the river, that I might go forward beside the great sea, which stretched onward, seeming for ever, upon the far side.

I knew not how to cross; I had no power to swim, and had I swum, surely there were monsters in that great warm-flowing river. I went upward of the bank to find a narrow place, and would have walked a mighty distance, but soon I came to a second river entering the first not a mile above the sea-shore. So there was the sea to one side, this second river to the other, and the first river before me; and I was sore perplexed.

Yet need and danger put wit into me. I looked about for fallen trees. There were many and great; and I was much wearied and strained of spirit ere I got two little trees unto the water. Then I made a rough pole of a young tree-plant, and lashed the two trees together with my belts and straps, so that I had somewhat of a raft.

All the while I did this labour I was heedful lest the Humped Men come upon me ere I was free upon the water. That constant listening—forest hush broken by far hiss, leaf-shiver, and at whiles the deep belly-sound of the earth—did double my labour; for the ear was always casting out feelers, as a cook’s nose tests a pot before the boil. Yet in the end it was done, and I was ready to adventure over.

I pushed off with the pole, and pushed and paddled maybe half an hour; for it was clumsy contriving, and mayhap I clumsier for weariness. Presently I was so far over that I drew nigh to the island; and it seemed wise I should have my slumber there, and afterward go onward to the farther shore. This I did. After I ate and drank, I lay down; and by then it was three and thirty hours since I last slumbered, so that I was bitterly awearied.

I had a great restful sleep; for the island seemed safe. In verity I came to no harm, though I was as a dead man for nine great hours.

When I woke proper I ate two tablets and drank, and made an end of my voyage. Then I took back my belts and straps from the binding of the trees, and went forward again upon my way, no more fearful of the Humped Men; for I thought I had left them upon the far shore of the river—though afterward I minded they might grow on both sides, and only those of one side had discovered me.

All that day I went very swift, passing strange matters, coasting upon wonders. At the sixth and twelfth hours I ate and drank, as ever. Between the eighth and fourteenth hours I came past two mighty fire-hills that made all the country tremble with their noise, as if a giant beat upon a drum made of stone. Four times monstrous creatures passed by me; but I hid swift and came to no harm.

Oft as I went my thoughts were upon the dear Maid that I journeyed to save; yet ever my thinking was brought sharp to my going, so that scarce did I set myself to ponder her but some danger or wonder thrust itself upon my heed. Because of this, I was more put to plan free of instant peril than to have quiet chance for thoughts of love; yet truly was not my journey one whole thought of love unto Naani? Peril made oft dumb my brain; and that dumbness was but the truer song to my Maid—like a tongue that cannot find words in any lexicon fit enough, and so speaks by deed.

At times I was among trees; oft I went past unnumbered boiling springs and small fire-hills. Oft the air was full of their noise—the hiss and roar, the crackle and gruntle—yet no harm came. A thousand times I perceived things that had life; I made a cautious way, yet with a great speed and cleverness that made my heart glad.

Oft I came to parts where great life held the trees, and green stuff flourished exceedingly, and the air was rich and wondrous sweet. Then I thought how in some far-off time our children’s children might come down into this Country when the Upper Valley was grown to utter chill and lacking of air, and here build a new Refuge—if any could come clear of the Evil Forces and monsters about the Mighty Pyramid. Yet how should they come clear? So it was but a vague thought. And yet again, who shall say what may be? For a people may lose their tongue and yet keep a seed of it in song; and a seed kept may one day be planted where the air is kinder.

I went onward, and I mind me I saw the lower fires burn very fierce; I set it to the richness of the air, yet with no surety, and tell it only as one of the oddments that crossed my brain.

A little before the eighteenth hour I came out from among the trees, and the sea was downward of a great cliff on my right, for I had gone upward for a long weary hour. There I saw a thing that made me cautious, and yet held my heart to go swiftly and perceive it, for it was very strange.

I went forward quickly, yet with care, and was presently nigh. I perceived the thing was in part a high rock, tall and pointed, maybe an hundred feet; but afterward I found it to be more. Upon the top of the rock was a monstrous great thing, strange to see. I stopped and looked, then went on again, and so for a time until I was but a little way off. Then I saw it seemed as though a mighty long rock lay across the topmost part of the upstanding rock, yet had a strange shapely appearance. Upon the underpart it was as I had thought; upon the upper part grew trees and green things, even as upon odd ledges of the standing rock. Yet for the most it was stark, as if a blast had blown upon it and made it bare.

When I had looked awhile, I bethought me this should be a safe proper place for slumber, if I had power to come safe to the top. Upon that thought I began at once to climb. The rock was very high; in a while I was a great way from the earth, and yet not at the top. Because I was awearied I sought a safe place for mine hand, and lo, there was a shelf very nigh, going inward a little to the side.

I got me to this ledge, ate and drank, and presently slept—scarce had I thought of Naani in the moment of slumbering, for a great weariness was upon me, such as comes when a man hath spent himself beyond his store and must pay the debt with sleep.

I woke very sudden, maybe seven hours after, with knowledge within me that my spirit wotted of nigh danger. I rose very quiet, the Diskos ready in my hand. I looked swift about in the instant of waking, yet saw nothing; there was naught upon the ledge with me.

I crept to the edge and looked downward, and lo, I saw two Humped Men coming up the rock. They climbed very swift and silent. I perceived they smelled me and came to destroy me. I made ready the Diskos and ceased not to look down. I saw how they were humpt by reason of being monstrous thick and mighty of neck and shoulder, as if they were human bulls. They were very strong; and by their speed I knew they were swift. Then I made steady my attention and my heart to the saving of my life; for truly I knew that in a little I should be dead, or else they. I stepped back a space from the edge, holding the Diskos very ready; for it was needful I kill one of those brute men speedy, that one might not take me in the back whilst I fought with the other.

In that instant when my eyes found the shape of a vast Pyramid rising up into the Night, all the despair that had sat like ash upon my heart turned, sudden, to a fierce, hot gladness—so swift that it seemed I had but to run a little way and I should be with mine Own.

Yet it was no warm, windowed beacon such as a refuge should be. It stood black upon black, a wedge of deeper dark against the far, low burnings of the Land. And even while my feet began to fly down from the shoulder of the great volcano, a cold thought slid in beside the joy: if this were truly the Lesser Refuge, why did it show no answering glow, no living pulse of the Earth-Current, no homely ring of light where folk would move and work?

But longing is a hunger, and I was starved of her. I ran.

For a few minutes I went blind with haste, and then the ground betrayed me. My foot took a hidden drop; the world turned over; and I fell headlong with such hardness that pain burst white through me, as though my neck were snapped clean. There I lay, moaning small, helpless as a thing set out for slaughter. In that time any Brute might have come out of the dark and ended me with a careless blow, and I should not have lifted an hand.

After a great while—how long I know not—I got to sitting, both hands clapped about my neck. The ache eased by degrees, as if the Night itself grew tired of pressing. At last I stood again, shaking, and went forward with more wit than before. My hope had not died, but it had learned fear.

I thought to send the Master-Word forthwith—aye, to call Naani and tell her I was come—yet the blackness of the Pyramid troubled me. It might be no refuge. It might be a shell taken by Evil, or some foul Power laying a pretence upon my sight to draw me in. Still, the thing showed plain now, a great bulk shouldering the distant fires. Whether it were refuge or trap, it was the only sure landmark in all that waste; and my feet would not be denied.

The climb up the volcano had taken me, mayhap, thirteen hours. I went down in ten, though sore from the fall and wary of every treacherous slope. At the end of that tenth hour the ground leveled; I was come again upon the wide Plain of the Night Land. The Pyramid was hidden now in the upper dark, yet the hill on which it stood made a heavier shadow between me and the far glowings, and I knew my course.

Four hours I went across the Plain, passing here and there fire-holes that bled a dull red into the black, like coals buried under ash. Those fire-holes made a kind of false hearth-light, enough that the darkness was never whole; yet there was no comfort in it, for the air about them carried a sour tang—sulphur and hot stone—and the Land was full of small, listening noises that were not wind.

When I had gone those four hours, the bulk of the hill-bottom hid the distant shinings altogether, and the world ahead became a wall of black. I judged I was near, yet another full hour passed ere my hand could have touched the rise of the ground. In those five hours since leaving the volcano, sound came to me often: three times, and again, the quick patter and rush of running things in the darkness; once a great roar far off, as if a giant beast proclaimed itself; and once a screaming, strange and horrid, that set my teeth to grinding and made the skin tighten upon my arms beneath the armour.

I began to go up the hill.

At first an excitement took me so strongly that I could scarce keep from shouting her name aloud into the Night, as a child shouts for a lost mother in a wood. But the higher I went, the more my joy chilled. A caution returned—sharp, inward—as though my spirit, wiser than my heart, smelled corruption where my eyes saw only stone.

Near three hours the ascent took me. And then, when I came so high that the Pyramid showed its full grimness—an enormous slope going up, desolate and silent into the starless dark—an utter shaking fear seized me. It was not guessed; it was known. There was no human life within that bulk. No dear, small motions of folk at labour; no guarded voices; no domestic clatter of a place held by the living. Only waiting—monstrous, patient, and evil.

I went down the hill again, very quiet, keeping to darkness, and so away from that place.

Four hours I walked before I felt I had put the hill safely behind me, and even then there was no safety in all that Land. I went a little blindly, my mind loose within me, as though my heart had died and I were only a body that continued because it had not yet fallen.

So it was that I came upon the shore of the olden sea, and had no knowing how I had reached it, for I thought it lay far aside. Yet afterward I considered that the dry bed might curve in that Country, or that there be more than one dead sea there in the Night.

I sat down, weak and bewildered, on the cold, hard earth. In me was a desolation so deep that it was like a silence in the blood. For my spirit told me there was Evil in that dark Pyramid, and I doubted not that destruction had come upon the Peoples of the Lesser Refuge, and that wicked creatures and Powers now kept house there. And if that were so, then I was come too late to save the Maid; and in that thought I found a terrible readiness: let any Brute come now. Let me fight and be done. Life had no savour left.

Yet even in that bleakness there was a thread of sense: I had no sure proof the black Pyramid was the Lesser Refuge. It might be some other structure, some older bulk. But the sureness in my spirit was like iron; it allowed no comfort.

After a while, a new thought rose: I would send the Master-Word through the Night. If Naani yet lived anywhere, she would answer; and if no answer came, or if some Evil Power replied to destroy me, then at least my heart-ache would end.

I stood and looked about into the blackness. The land lay open, with scattered bushes like low, thorny shadows and the dull red of a fire-hole not far off, breathing heat and stink into the cold. I sent the Master-Word with my brain-elements, and then I called Naani thrice, sending the call with all the strength that was left in me.

And lo! it seemed but a moment, and the Master-Word broke round me, low and solemn, beating in the Night like a great, slow drum heard through stone. With it came a voice—far, small, lonesome—so faint it was as if it sounded from the end of the world. Yet it was her voice: Naani’s, and Mirdath’s, calling me by mine olden love-name.

I near choked with the affright of joy. The despair vanished as if it had never been. She lived. She called. For an age of grim labour and dread I had heard nothing of mine Own, and now the sound of her—even so faint—made the world new.

But in the same beat of gladness there was fear: the voice was very far. She was not safe under my hand. Peril could reach her before I could.

And then—before I made answer—my spirit pricked me with another knowing: someone was near, a little way off, among the bushes by the fire-hole. I did not speak again into the aether. I went swift, without sound, into a great bush on the near side, and peered through into the open about the fire.

There, in the red, wavering light, knelt a small figure upon the ground, sobbing. It was a slim maid. She shook as she wept, yet even in her weeping she listened—harking, desperate—as though she waited for a sound that would not come.

Mine own soul knew her in one white moment. This was she, here in the very flesh, and not a far voice only. And I understood, sudden, a bitter domestic truth hidden inside all that mighty travel: she had been crying to me for a month of loneliness, as a child cries into a storm; and because her strength was spent, her call could not carry plain through the great spaces. The Night had swallowed it. Her hunger and cold had stolen the force from her brain-elements, so that what should have been a clear Word became only a faint ache in the aether—if it reached me at all.

I drew in my breath, set my teeth to steady my lips, and spoke with the human tongue, softly but clear, out of the bush:

“MIRDATH.”

She stopped as though struck. Her head lifted; her wet face turned this way and that; fear and hope shone together in her tears in the fire-light. I parted the bush and stepped through, standing before her in my grey armour.

My heart cried to seize her and gather her close, for it seemed I had come back to Mirdath after an utter lost Eternity. Yet I was held a moment, adrift within myself; for she was Naani and she was Mirdath, and yet in my eyes she was a stranger too—dainty, pretty, torn and shaken with woe, as one who had walked long among stones.

In the same moment that I came out, she screamed and fell back, striving weakly toward the nearer bushes as if to flee from a monster. Then she saw my shape was human. I spoke the Master-Word aloud, that she might know peace and help, and I told my name, saying I am That One.

She knew even as the sounds left my lips. She cried out in a broken voice and ran to me, thrusting both her small hands into my keeping, and then fell to sobbing and shaking so that I was troubled to ease her. I held her hands fast—bare, for I had no armoured gloves—saying nothing, for speech was too small for that moment.

She leaned against me, weak as a child. Her breath came in catches. When her sobbing lessened, I saw her trembling did not wholly cease, and I bethought me at once of hunger. A body may be brave a long while, but it cannot live upon terror and hope. Her hands in mine were so thin that bone seemed close under skin.

I set her gently down beside a hump of smooth rock, so her back might rest. I stripped off my cloak and wrapped it about her quick, for her own clothes were scarce any covering—torn among bushes until they hung in rags—and the bitter chill took her. Part of the shaking was cold; part was weakness, for she was nigh starved unto death, and broken with grief and loneliness.

From my back I took the scrip and pouch. I drew out a food-tablet and broke it into my cup. The water—flat and cold—took the crumbled meal, and I made a little broth swift upon a hot rock at the very edge of the fire-hole, where heat seeped constant from the earth. The rock hissed faint under the cup; a thin steam rose with the smell of warmed grain and salt, a comfort so small and yet so mighty in that place that it near made me weep.

Her hands shook too much to manage it, so I fed her the broth myself, holding the cup steady to her mouth. She drank as one half-asleep. When the warmth reached her belly, her face crumpled and she fell again to sobbing, quiet as a child trying not to wake a house.

I slid my hands under the cloak and took her hands again, holding them firm. That simple pressure—skin upon skin, human steadiness against the tremor—seemed to lend her peace. The trembling eased. The weeping lessened. The broth did its work, like a spark catching in damp tinder.

After a little, her fingers stirred. I loosened my grip, and she answered by clasping my hands with a weak, gentle grasp. She did not yet look up; she lay quiet, gathering strength, as though she must first make a hearth within herself before she could speak.

All that while, a new fearfulness kept me listening. Danger had always been near me in the Night Land, yet it had been my own life only. Now mine Own sat wrapped in my cloak, and the thought of any hurt to her was like a knife held at my heart. I harked about me, again and again, to every shift of sound—the low bubbling of the fire-hole, the far, thin running noises, the soft scrape of something small upon stone.

Suddenly the Maid made as though to rise. I moved to help, but she caught my hand and slipped down upon her knees and kissed it, and began again to weep.

I was so abashed I stood stupid and let it be for a moment. Then I drew my hand free, for it might not be. I knelt likewise before her, took her hands, and kissed them once—humbled anew—so she might know what honour and love were in me, and that I understood her. She sobbed the more, for she was weak, and moved beyond bearing because I had come to her through the Night of the world.

I let her hands go, thinking she would need them for her tears, but she laid them back into my palms, bowing her head over her weeping. In that small act she gave herself to me, utterly, to the very essence of her dear spirit.

I took her into mine arms, very gently, without any rough caress, and then, unable to help myself, I stroked her hair. It was soft, though dusted with the Land’s grit; it held the faint, sour smell of sulphur and smoke, and beneath that the human scent of skin—proof, intimate and untranslatable, that she was alive. I called her Naani and Mirdath, and spoke many things I scarce remember now, yet which she knew in the after time. She lay quiet in my arms, wondrous content, and sobbed on a long while, not asking comfort save only shelter—only the fact of being held where no monster could reach her.

Presently she grew calmer. I would have set her again against the rock, wrapped close, that I might make more broth; yet she nestled toward me with a sweet wistfulness that warmed my heart in a strange fashion, for surely she was mine Own. Still, she obeyed gentle when I asked, and rested quiet, watching me with her gaze as I worked.

I made more broth upon the hot rock, breaking another tablet, stirring it until it thickened, careful not to scorch it where the rock was fiercest. The thin steam rose again, carrying that small domestic scent of food—an odour that belongs to kitchens and guarded rooms, not to the killing dark. I gave it her, and this time she drank using her own two hands, steadier now.

Then I ate myself—three tablets—and drank some water; for it was a foolish great while since last I had taken any proper sustenance. The food lay heavy and plain in the mouth, yet I blessed it, because in the Night Land even the simplest meal is an act of holding on to being human.

After a while the warmth and broth brightened her eyes, and she began to talk. She spoke with pauses, for strength came and went in her like a tide, and there was more to tell than any human heart can carry easy. Twice she fell again to weeping, for her father was dead, and the Peoples of the Lesser Redoubt were all slain or scattered through that dreadful Land.

An Evil Force—so she told—had made action upon the Peoples within the Lesser Refuge. Some, grown utter weak because the Earth-Current failed, had opened the Great Door and gone out into the Night. Immediately great and horrid monsters poured into the Lesser Pyramid. There had been a brutish chase in the passages and chambers; many were slain; some escaped into the darkness.

Naani fled with three other maids after her father, the Master Monstruwacan, was slain by a shaggy man—brutal and monstrous. They hid among bushes to sleep, but certain creatures came upon them in the dark and stole two away. The remaining maid ran off one way and Naani another, and they never met again.

How long a time had passed since then she could not tell, for there is no counting in such a life—no marked days, no bells, no steady change of labour to measure hours. It had been dread long to her. And by my questions concerning my callings, I learned she had been lost through all the time of my journey; she had heard none of my calls since she escaped the Lost Refuge into that Land.

Yet oft had she cried to me with her brain-elements, until her heart grew sick of her own unanswered longing. Worse, those callings had drawn Evil things, for the Land listens. More than once beasts and searching horrors had come toward her, and because she had the gift of hearing she had known their approach and slipped away; but with piteous runnings and hidings among rocks and bushes, until she dared make no calling save odd whiles, lest she bring monsters upon her. Her weakness had been such that she could not throw the Word strongly afar, nor shape her spiritual cry plain through the thick aether.

Her garments had been torn from her little by little by thorn and stone; she had no thread, no tool, no quiet place to mend. For food she had eaten moss from rocks, and strange berries and growths whose taste she described with a grim face—bitter as old metal, sharp as smoke, sweet at first and then making the belly turn. She drank from hot springs because the cold drove her, and oft that water made her sick with sulphur. Yet, she thought, it might have saved her from worse poison in the plants; in that, she and I could only guess.

In that dreadful time she had heard a score of drear things: once, the slaying of a young maid near by, by some Brute out in the darkness; thrice and more, the feet of people running, and the heavier tread of giants pursuing. By that I understood the sounds mine ears had taken in during my own goings across the Land, and a new pity and horror rose in me.

She told how once she came upon some of the Peoples of the Lesser Redoubt hiding among bushes. She called to them that she was human as they, but they ran from her without heed, so sore was the panic upon them.

The bitter chill kept her always striving to be near fire-holes, which were plentiful; yet the heat and dull red glow drew the monstrous Brutes, as I had known elsewhere in the Night Land and in the Upward Gorge. So she was oft forced to go without warmth, crouching in utter cold, teeth chattering, listening to the Land’s hateful small sounds. And odd whiles she grew so desperate she would venture near the fire for a time of heat; and because of this she had been nigh slain in her sleep, twice and thrice. There were snakes about the fires, though not everywhere; and spider-crabs; and monstrous scorpions.

And even this very time—when she had lain by the fire-hole, weak and thinking death close—there had been creatures like crabs squatting all about, a silent circle of waiting, impudent and horrible, content to let her die and then feed. She had been afeard to sleep lest they destroy her in slumber.

So, when my Master-Word beat through the Night, strong as a low spiritual thunder, it had stirred her heart—but she had thought it came from far away, from the Mighty Pyramid itself, and it brought her not hope but a sharper despair, as if the world mocked her with a sound of me that she could not reach. Then she heard her name spoken with the human tongue—different from the name my spirit had used after the Word—and in a minute I came out of the bush. She had fallen back in fear of a monster; then seen a young man in grey armour; and known at once that I was the olden one of her memory-dreams, the one that had spoken to her across half the dead world.

So she spoke, with that wonder still upon her, as if she could not fit the thing into words. And indeed no words can fully carry such meeting; there is always something left—some taste of the moment, some sound in the air—known only to the two who lived it.

When she spoke of the spider-crabs, I looked about at once, and saw that they were not gone. Beyond the red-lit circle about the fire-hole, in the border where light fell off into black, they sat—low, many-legged shapes, motionless, watching with a steady assurance that was like hunger given patience.

Disgust and anger took me. I rose, went to the edge of the light, and spurned them one by one, kicking perhaps a dozen until they scuttled away into the darkness. Their calm was foolish, and their courage small; they made no attack. Yet I thought, grimly, that a true crab of my own day would have been eager enough to pinch my toe had I offered it.

Then I returned to the Maid, and sat close, listening outward into the Night and inward to her breathing—thankful for that soft human sound, the gentlest music left in all the world.

After the long, drowned dimness of the old sea’s bottom, the open Land struck my eyes like a loosening of a band about the brow. The night was still night, yet it held a kind of pale allowance—as though the darkness itself had thinned—so that I could see I had come up upon ground lying somewhat to the right hand of where I had gone down on my outward way. Across that waste there were many fire-holes, each one a ruddy wound in the earth, breathing up heat and smoke and that steady glow that makes a man’s heart feel less alone.

Yet the sight warmed me and warned me in one same instant. Fire in this Land is never only comfort; it is summons. Where it burns, life gathers—creeping, crawling, or walking upright with intent that is hunger.

I looked down to Naani. She looked up, drawing nearer, as if the very little light asked her to cling to it. She was wondrous pretty—soothed in the face by her love of me, and yet pale with weariness, her lips faintly cracked from the dry cold. The long hours had overwalked her. I, made as though of iron by my armour and my strength, had driven her tenderness too far. I blamed myself aloud; but she would not have it, only standing close, her beautiful eyes steady upon me, as if she would feed me courage by looking.

So I put my arms about her, and kissed her, tasting the salt of her lips and the faint bitterness of the Land’s air upon her breath; and then I lifted my sight again, to shape our homeward going.

Far before us there spread, like a low sea of ghost-fire upon the horizon, the blue shining I had seen from the mouth-part of the Upward Gorge. It lay a great way off; but it had been my beacon, a cold star lowered into the world. By its glimmering in the night-sky’s murk I had steered across the olden sea-bed, keeping my course and not wandering in blind circles among the black swells of dead sand.

Now, studying the lay of the Land, I judged where the mouth of the Gorge should be, and knew I must bear somewhat left—yet not overmuch. For to the left, at a vast distance, there stained the night with red the great fire-pit of the Giants. And I must go so that I missed that place as much as might be, and likewise not come overnear to the blue-shining itself; for my spirit mistrusted the very ground where that light did burn, as though it were a lantern held over a pit.

When I had gained some knowing of our direction, I slipped my arm about Naani’s shoulders, where she stood husht beside me while I watched. I pointed outward into the dark, toward the leftward reach of the Land, and told her that somewhere that way—though far beyond my sight—was the Upward Gorge’s mouth, to be known only by memory of my first going out.

She had been looking about all that while, quiet as a field-creature. The place spoke to her in ways it would not speak to me; for she had wandered it, and the Lesser Redoubt had read it with their instruments. She asked how I meant to go.

“As straight as may be,” I told her, “but keeping well away from the Blue Shine and from the great Red Fire-Hole.”

Then she bade me look again along the line I had chosen. At first I saw nothing but scattered fire-holes, yet with an odd greenish halo about some, as though their smoke were sick. Naani set out to me, careful and sure, that there ran a tract of bad gas in that part of the Land, poisonous beyond any other peril of breath. This had been known in the Lesser Redoubt, for their devices could taste the air with needles and measure the hidden death in it. Wherever that gas drifted, the fire-holes wore that green shining, like a mould of light.

She showed me how the place of the gas ran far toward the North-West, and by her pointing I learned better the set of the ground. And it was in all the North-West that the great blue-shining burned.

I asked Naani what name they gave that blue. She said, in her own manner, that it had no name with them, save only The Shine.

At the saying of it, something in her voice tightened. She warned me earnestly of The Shine, urging that we go no more that-wards than our lives did require. It was not my wish to go near it, and yet her fear was another kind than my mere mistrust. She told me the reason: the Fixed Giants were within the borders of The Shine, hid in the light of it, save when the burning mist rolled this way or that, uncovering them like stones at a tide’s turning.

At that, I thought at once upon the Great Watchers about the Mighty Pyramid; and then, sharper still, upon that monstrous face I had once seen amid the bright smoke of The Shine when first I came into this Land. Surely that had been one of these Fixed Giants—Forces of great and horrid Evil.

I asked how far the Place of the Gas went across our way. She pointed, explained further, turning the Land into lines and hazards as if she were laying out a kitchen-yard by its sour patches and sweet. And in the end I saw I could not reach the mouth of the Gorge by that surface way if I held off from The Shine, for the gas-trail would bar me.

Then Naani asked how I had crossed when I searched for her. I told her; and as I spoke it became plain to me that, by chance or guiding, I had walked then always on the far side of the gas, and so down to the old sea-bed, and thus had come free of it without knowing.

Upon that she answered quickly: we should go down again into the bed of the Olden Sea, and walk some great hours under the shore, bearing to the South-West until we were beyond the Place of the Gas; then climb up into the Land again, and use utter caution to escape the watching of the Giants about the Great Red Fire-Hole. By this, we should come speedily enough to the entering-part of the Upward Gorge.

It was a good plan—sound and swift. I might have made it, yet she made it first, eager and quick; and it was very sweet to me to hear her set her mind to our saving. I loved the sound of her voice in that dead world, as a man might love the remembered clink of pots in a bright house, or the old tongue of his mother spoken over bread. For in such speech a self is shown, and in this Land selves were always at risk of being ground down to mere breathing. To hear Naani think aloud was to feel her human niceness resist the vast silence.

But it was then, as I reckoned, some seventeen hours since last we had slept, and I saw the weariness on her: the slackness at the corners of her mouth, the way she held her shoulders as if cold had weight. I told her we must rest soon, and then go on with new strength. She agreed at once; for she was very weary.

We chose to venture toward a fire-hole that lay not over-far, a little upon our right, toward the Northward-way. We walked to it, and it proved further than our eyes had guessed; it was a good hour before we came near, and then we saw it for a very great red-glowing shine, rising into the night from a hollow among rocks.

At the edge of that hollow I signed to Naani to be husht. I took the Diskos from my hip, and went forward before her, and then went down upon my knees and hands. I beckoned her to do likewise.

So we crept to the lip and looked down. The fire burned deep in the earth, a broad throat of heat, the rocks around it lit like old iron in a forge. At first glance I saw no monstrous bulk moving about it, and that put some peace into me—yet not overmuch. For I knew how living things congregated about such fires, and how the comfort they promised drew a man’s guard down, like a cook stepping too close to a spit because the smell is good.

We listened keen into the night. There was no disturbance of the air, no tremor in the aether. I whispered to Naani that we did well to stay off from fires. But she was utter cold, shaking with chill, and begged that we go down even if only to put warmth through our bodies. And truly I too was bitter cold, all weak to go beside the fire, and her shiverings worked upon my heart until it overcame the teachings of my head.

So we went down into the hollow and very swift to the fire.

Strange it is: I had searched so long for a fire-pit, yet when I stood beside one, my spirit warned me in a way I could not name. It is easy to forget the sharp teachings of experience when the cold is gnawing; danger seems far and thin, while chill is double-real, biting the joints and making the teeth chatter in their roots. Yet once close to the fire, the remembered truth of its perils gathered again about my heart, as smoke gathers under a roof.

I went among the rocks at the hollow’s bottom, searching for any hidden life that might come out upon us. I found no great creature, yet I saw three snakes, and two scorpion-things lodged in rock-holes, neither fleeing nor attacking, but holding to their heat as if the fire were their larder and their church.

Because of these, I knew we should not sleep near the fire. Creeping things love warmth, and in slumber a man becomes meat. Yet I said nothing of these poisonous folk to Naani at first; I wished her to have a little rest and happiness beside the glow. Afterwards I would tell her, and she would be the readier to go elsewhere. And if she would not, then I must command; for she was Mine Own, and my love meant safety more than ease.

Presently she was something warmed. She slipped the scrip from my shoulder and brought out food and drink with quick skill. Even there, in that hell of night, she handled our poor provision as though it were a household rite: the tablets broken clean, the water measured so none spilled, the stoppered vessel held close lest the Land steal its scent.

We sat together and ate and drank. The tablet’s taste was as ever—flat and stored, with a faint mineral tang like flour ground on stone too long. Yet the fire lent it an illusion of mealhood; the air carried a thin smell of hot rock, and the glow made our hands look living. Naani was sweet and quiet as she began her second tablet; and I had knowledge, in a hard tenderness, that she remembered in her body my whipping of her, and took it as a bond—utter mine.

All the while I watched about for creeping movement. After we had ended our food, she saw my looking and caught my unease. She stared over her shoulder, and in a little while she saw a snake slide among the rocks, a dark ribbon crossing the red light. Then she was eager we find a secure place.

We stayed within the hollow, for we found a little cave in an upstanding rock. The rock stood mayhap a hundred feet from the fire; the hollow was very great. The cave-mouth was a hole thrice my height above the bottom rocks, dry and sweet, with no crannies for creeping things to hide. I climbed and helped Naani up.

Within that hole the fire’s shine reached us, dim but friendly, laying a copper colour on the stone. It might have been cozy as a hearth-nook in some lost farm-house, save for the ever-fear of the Land. Upon me the fear lay heavier; for Naani trusted me utter, as if no evil could pass my arm’s length. Her trust was sweet, and bitter too, for it put weight upon every beat of my heart.

We slept as before, sharing the cloak. The fire made no great warmth at our height, yet it was less bitter than the open dark. We had gone twenty hours without slumber, and weariness took us like a tide; but even in sleep our spirits were set to hearken.

We slept seven hours.

Then, sudden, some need wakened us, and lo! I woke in one instant and Naani in the same instant, as if the same hand had plucked us both. There was screaming and crying in the night—utter human cries, torn raw by terror. The sound struck deeper than any beast-call, because it was our own kind. My heart ached to go, to help, to do something; but my duty was to Mine Own, and rashness had no leave in me.

Immediately there came great roaring in one part of the night, and again in another; and the roarings answered one another. They were big husky voices, as if men so large as houses ran and shouted across the Land.

Naani began to shake. I put my arm about her and drew her back into the cave’s shadow. She trembled like one broken in courage; for she had heard those sounds often in that long dreadful month of her wandering. And truly my own courage shook; it was the shouting of giants.

Then there came a dreadful screaming that cut the night like a blade: the scream of a young maid being slain very brutal. My heart sickened for Naani; and yet my spirit swelled with such anger it seemed it would burst my body. Beside me Naani broke into sobbing, dry and hopeless.

That screaming ended sudden, but others rose in diverse places, and with them the hoarse shoutings of the great men and the thudding of mighty feet running this way and that, a-chase. The cryings came nearer; the footfalls grew like blows upon the earth.

It seemed in a little minute the tumult was upon our hollow. I crept to the mouth and peered out. The night felt full of bodies. Then past the hollow’s lip there streamed a clustering of humans, running, screaming, gasping, weeping—men and women in rags or naked, torn by rock and bush, wild as hunted things. The fire’s shine made them plain, their skin smeared and raw, their eyes white with the lack of hope.

Pain and longing troubled me so that I would have gone after them, but for Naani. And even as I burned with that restraint, there came the thudding of monstrous feet. Four great men ran out of the night and past the hollow, very quick. Three were dull-coloured, much haired, brutish; the other was horrid white and livid-blotched, a man-shape filled with unwholesome life. In one moment they were within the fire-glow; in the next they were gone back into the dark, chasing.

When their feet had gone far, I heard them bellowing, then a far-away scream with a death-note in it. Silence followed, thick and heavy.

A fierce impatience of sorrow took me: if those poor humans had any courage, might they not have turned upon the giants and slain them with their hands, even if all died in the doing? For they would die anyway. Yet I knew the People of the Lesser Redoubt had been bred from parents starved of the Earth-Current through uncounted ages, and so lacked in many ways. Naani was otherwise; but that proved only the rule.

As I stooped husht at the cave-mouth, I heard Naani sobbing in the back. I would have gone to comfort her, but in that same moment I saw a naked maid run swift along the hollow’s edge, looking over her shoulder as she ran. She slid down into the bottom and crept under a rock-ledge, trying to vanish into shadow. She was worn through, desperate, her body moving on the last of its strength.

At once I understood her stealth; for a squat haired man, broad as a bullock, came silent down into the hollow, peering this way and that like a beast. He had instant knowing where she hid and rushed in upon her without a sound.

I did not pause. I leapt the great way down to the bottom—mayhap twenty feet and more—anger lifting me as if it were a spring. I landed hard but safe, and ran; yet before I could reach them, the Squat Man ript the maid. She gave one dreadful scream and died sudden in his hands.

My blood burned with wrath until my sight seemed red at the edges. The roar of the Diskos filled the hollow as I set it to spin—so that it seemed to rage with me, eager to be glut of the Brute-Man.

The monster turned on me, thinking to deal with me as with the maid. I swung; the Diskos sang, a keen metal cry that cut through the fire’s roar. My stroke went wide, for the brute dropped sudden upon its hands and my blow passed over. It seized my legs to rip me. I cut quick, and the Diskos took away one monstrous talon so that it had but one left. With the other arm it flung me half across the hollow. I fell, my armour clanging mightily; the Diskos rang like a bell in a dead chapel.

By graciousness I was not harmed beyond bruising. I was to my feet in one instant, the Diskos still in my hand. The Beast-Man bounded upon me twice, soundless, froth at its mouth, its teeth like knives set at the sides. I leapt and smote, lending my whole weight and will to the stroke. The Diskos took away the head and shoulder of the Squat Man.

The dead bulk knocked me backward with the force of its spring, but did not greatly harm me. I came at it again out of habit and fury, but it was truly dead, a horrid thing in the fire-glow, spilling its darkness onto the lit stones.

Then the anger drained and left sorrow in its place. I went, heart-shaken, to the maid’s torn body. There was no help to give, no name to call in any tongue that would matter now. I took her up with tenderness that came too late, and cast her into the fire-hole, that the heat might take her quick, and no creeping thing have her.

I turned then toward the cave, fearing lest Naani had seen too much, or swooned. But lo! she ran toward me, my belt-knife in her hand—the weapon I had given her. She had come to be my aid, if I had need. She was utter pale, yet steadfast, not trembling.

I would have drawn her away at once, but she went beyond me and looked upon the monstrous bulk of the Squat Man. She was very silent. She came back and stood before me, still saying no word. Yet I knew what she thought; her eyes gave me an honour she could not speak, as though some untranslatable part of her—made of her own dialect of love and fear—had risen and filled her gaze. I took that honour with gladness and a strange humbleness; for a man may be fed by such looking more than by any meat.

She spoke nothing then nor after; but all my life I was warmed when I remembered how she looked up at me in those moments, as if I were not only her shelter but her world’s last true word.

Afterwards, when the need for courage passed, trembling came upon her. She allowed herself into my arms, and I held her until her shaking eased; for we had seen a dreadful thing, and horror lay upon us both like frost.

I climbed back to the little cave and helped Naani up. There we rested awhile. Presently we ate again—two tablets each—and drank water; thirst had come upon us like fire, and our mouths were dry as old parchment.

For about an hour we listened keen, measuring every faint sound, the Land’s silence pricked through with far-away cracks and sighs. At last, when no new thudding came, we climbed down, gathered our gear, and came up out of the hollow.

Then we set forward with great caution toward the old sea-bed, moving slow, often pausing to harken. The fear of the monstrous men was upon us, and the night seemed to carry their breath in every draft. Yet no harm came anigh, and we perceived no disturbance. In two long hours we reached the dead sands of the olden sea again, and turned our faces to the homeward way.

Downward of the Gorge I bore the Maid for the space of an hour, as I had said; and, in that time, my strength did ebb until it seemed to leak from me at every step. My feet, that had served me so faithfully, went strange beneath me, as though they belonged to another man; and I stumbled often, and my sight would swim in the red glimmer, and I had a fierce fear that I should fall headlong and crush Mine Own against the stones.

Then did I know, with a sudden clearness, that I must win to a place for slumber—swiftly—or else sleep would take me walking, and death would take us with it.

So I forced my mind to the walls of the Gorge, and it was as if I waked out of a dream. For truly I had gone that last while like a sleep-walker; and the Gorge, that should have been familiar by my coming upward, seemed a fresh-strange place, narrow and towering, with a bitter echo to each footfall. Yet I had a good craft of remembering ways; and, drawing upon that, I minded a great ruddy fire-hole not far hence, and certain small caves I had marked near it upon my outward journey. In that earlier hour I had wished—oh, with such hunger of the heart—that if ever I should bring Mine Own safe back through peril, I might set her to rest in one of those very hollows, snug and dry and warmed by the earth’s secret fires.

And now, as though the world did answer a prayer without words, I came onward a little and saw that my memory had not deceived me. There, a little beyond the great fire-hole, were the caves—seven of them—opened like dark mouths in the left cliff. One, highest and most tucked-away, had the look of coziness and surety, a den made for two souls that must sleep with danger all about.

Yet, though my heart did lift, I was not made foolish by gladness. I had it in mind that no man hath surety in that Land. Though we had come, as I thought, safe from the Evil Forces behind, yet might some Monster come downward of the Gorge out of the Dark and find us in our helplessness. Therefore I chose the uppermost cave, for fewer beasts could come at us there unawares; and if any should climb, it must needs come up against the Diskos, and against such warning as a falling stone might give.

It was needful to wake the Maid. I kissed her where she lay in mine arms; and, sweet wonder, she kissed back, being still asleep. I kissed her again and shook her softly, and so brought her to wakefulness, telling her we were come to a place fit for slumber.

She looked about with drowsy eyes, and when I set her upon her feet she would have blamed herself that she had slept whilst I labored with her weight. But I kissed her—because the sight of her, with sleep still lying like a veil upon her gaze, was more dear than any speech—and she kissed me, scarce fully waked, and said with a little abandon that went straight to my blood, that she loved me utterly and forever.

Then I climbed to the topmost cave alone, bidding her walk to and fro a little to bring her body to full wakefulness; for she would need all her steadiness in the climbing. When I gained the mouth of it and drew myself within, a gladness took me. It was sweet and dry, and held a gentle warmth, as though some hidden vein of fire ran near in the stone. The red light from the fire-hole below did creep inward by reflection, making the place not black but softly dim, a womb of safety.

I went down again to Naani, calling that the cave was proper, and gave her my aid. So, with care and effort, we won up and in at last; and the feeling of shelter about us was like a hand laid upon the heart.

Yet before we slept, I loosed the scrip and pouch from my shoulders, took the straps, and went down into the Gorge again. There I found a boulder as heavy as I might manage, strapt it to my back, and came up. The Maid watched me with a grave anxiousness, lest I slip. Within the cave-mouth I set the stone so that it balanced light upon the edge; a touch would send it rolling down with a roar.

By this device I judged that any Beast or monstrous thing climbing up whilst we slept would start the boulder and be like to be harmed by it; but chiefly it should wake me by the noise, quick as a shout.

When I turned to slumber, I found the Maid had spread the cloak upon the rock for a bed. We had no need to wrap it over us, for the cave was warm as I have told; and, in truth, the cloak could scarce give softness through the sternness of mine armor. Yet she had made a couch for the two of us, sweet and natural, the way a household is made without thinking, when love hath already chosen its customs. She longed to be nigh me; and to keep her modesty, she did it as though it were but the plain right of resting—yet her heart, mayhap, guided her more than she knew.

I lay as she showed me, upon my side. She knelt and kissed me upon the lips, sober and loving, and laid herself beside me. In a little minute, as I do think, we were both gone into sleep.

Twelve great hours after, I was waked by the fizzing and hissing of water—one of those thin sounds that belong to the Gorge, like breath escaping from the earth’s own lungs. I looked and saw the Maid not beside me, but busy with our simple eating and drinking. The smell of the water—clean, with a faint sharpness of mineral—came to me; and the tablets were laid ready. She laughed softly when she saw me wake, sweet and tender for gladness; and she came, and kissed me bright upon the lips.

Afterward she kneeled and looked at me so dear that I knew, without knowing how, that she had been awake awhile and had watched me sleep—motherlike, as if her love must keep guard even when I did. Whether my spirit read hers, or her thoughts had tongues unto mine, I cannot tell; but the knowledge was plain.

I put up my arms to her as I lay, and she came into them gladly, snuggling there with such true delight that it was clear she loved in all her body and spirit to be anigh me, as I to her. Yet I was careful; the armor is a stern thing, and I feared to clasp her over-strong lest I hurt her. Still, she rested against it as though the hardness were part of my surety; and perhaps some part of her womanheart did take comfort in the very unyielding.

Presently she would go from me, and I loosed her readily, for I had alway a heed that she should have full sweet liberty of her Maidenhood. This care I held honest and wholesome, not over-pondering; for a man may keep reverence without turning it into a coldness.

She went to where the cup and tablets were, and I made to rise to look to the boulder; but she called me to lie back. She would spoil me, she said, that once; I need not trouble for the boulder nor for any Monster, for she had looked oft since waking, and there was nowheres any Creature in sight, and the stone lay safe as I had set it.

So I obeyed, and lay back, and liked the being spoilt. She brought the cup and tablets—she had taken them from the scrip whilst I went down for the boulder—and she would not have me rise. Instead, she took my head upon her knees. The warmth of her through her garments, the faint scent of her hair—clean like linen kept in a chest of herbs back in the Mighty Pyramid—did go through me with a comfort deeper than sleep. She kissed me once, dainty on the lips; then she took a tablet, kissed it, and gave it to me; and touched another to her own lips. So we ate, glad and easy of heart, as children at a hearth.

We ate a second tablet each in the same wise; and truly I was kissed more than once. Then we drank the water, cool and a little biting with the earth’s salts.

When we had ended, she bade me stand. I did so, wondering; and near cried out with the pains of my bruisings. For I had gone stiff in my sleep, and was more sore than ever; and now I knew that the fighting—aye, and the handling of the Four-Armed Man, and the violence of the Yellow Beast-Man—had hurt me more than I had understood in the heat of doing.

The Maid had thought of it. She had the pot of ointment ready, and her eyes were tender and grave as she looked upon my bruisings. She helped me with the armor, eased me of my garments, and then had me lie, making me comfortable with the cloak as though I were a child. For a great hour she rubbed the ointment in, skilful and gentle, until the soreness did ease and my limbs came back to me.

As she ministered, I lay very restful and harked to the low muttering of the fire-hole far below, and to the small, constant whisperings where steam did spirt in hidden cracks. The Gorge had its own sound-world—no clamor of engines, no harsh beating of iron—only the ancient, breathing noises of stone and water and fire; and in that acoustic, my mind did mend as my flesh did. It came to me that a man’s spirit is shaped by what he must listen to, day after day; and I was thankful, even in that dreadful Land, for any place where the sounds were not a torment contrived by malice.

When she had ended her care, she put by the ointment and gave me her hands to aid me up. I rose, and found I might move without great pain. This did please me mightily; for I had been troubled to think I should be helpless, being the Protector of Mine Own.

I looked about for my garments. She brought me my spare body-vest from the pouch, but held it back a moment, standing before me with an honest admiring. She wondered—sweetly, without any shame—at the hardness and shaping of my arms and breast. In the Mighty Pyramid we are all taught Exercises from our upbringing, and I had ever kept myself to them; moreover I owed the straightness of my body to the Mother that bore me. I had taken pride in health and strength, and hold it still a fit pride, when it is honest.

Her admiration was dear to me; I should be a liar to deny it. In a moment she dropped the vest and put out her hands, asking without speech that I take her. I took her into mine arms, glad and humbled that I was not worthy to hold so dear a treasure. She lay quiet and happy a little; and I felt—felt more than saw—that she kissed the great muscling of my breast where her face pressed. Then, as quick as a shy bird, she slipped free and helped me with my garments, and after with my armor.

When all was done, she stood off and looked at me half shy, half sweet, with a little naughtiness in her eyes. She came close, hands on my shoulders, eyelids lowered, stealing small looks up at me. Then, sudden, she was on her knees before me and weeping; and I was down at once, kneeling with her.

I asked not why. I knew it was joy, glad happiness, and that sweet trouble a true woman hath in her man. In her were worship and love and calm wisdom, all trembling together. She did see me as an hero, though I was but usual to others; and my heart was proud and humble at once, upraised and yet dreadly unworthy. I made no foolish denial to lessen myself in her sight; I resolved only to deserve her dear respect. For it would have been pitiful if I should seem less than an hero unto her, she being so utterly Mine Own.

In a little she steadied, wiped her eyes, and nestled to me husht, needing closeness. She rested against mine armor, and I held her and felt as though I were both father and lover in one man; and I was silent and joyful that I lived.

Then, as she lay within my left arm, I slipped her shoes from her little feet with my right hand. I condemned myself that I had not thought of it sooner; yet I had meant it, and forgot in the press of love and weariness. She would not have me blame myself; still my own heart reproached me until I had looked well to her feet. I bound the shoes again when I had done, and we gathered our gear.

With great care—because the cave was high in the cliff—we came down into the Gorge. Then we went onward downward, with as much speed as we might, to come away from the Dark Land of the Lesser Redoubt.

In six hours we had done very well, and halted to eat and drink. Afterward I looked to the Maid’s feet again. By good fortune there was a rock basin near, holding warm water—warmed, no doubt, by some fire-vein in the stone. I bathed her feet and set the ointment about them thoroughly and gentle for a good while, until she had ease.

Then we went onward; but now we went more easy. For I had considered the notings of my outward journey, and perceived we should be but six or seven hours from that part of the Gorge where the Slugs did be.

I was minded that we must rest and sleep ere we dared that dire place; for the passing through would take twelve great hours and more, and we could have no rest nor pause until we were safe outward again. We needed strength, and the Maid needed it doubly for her feet.

The Maid, for her part, was in delight of the fires in that portion of the Gorge where we now went, and her soul had some rest because she felt no Evil Force pressing near. I did not yet trouble her with speech of the horrid place ahead; there is a season for each truth, and I would not sour her small peace too soon.

So we went with caution. Fire-holes glowed before and behind, and in the air were little whistlings of steam spirted here and there. There was warmth in many parts by reason of the fires; at times a smell of sulphur, not over-strong, yet enough to sting the nose and set a tang upon the tongue. Alway there was the low muttering of pits, and the red lights, and the dancing of shadows when we passed a fire-pit where the flame frisked lively. On either side the grim walls of the Gorge went up measureless into the night, swallowing sound and giving it back thin and altered.

So, after six good hours since last we ate, we halted and ate and drank. I told the Maid we should make but a short journey that day and sleep, so as to be ready for the long dread passage where the Slugs were.

We searched for a safe place. Then I knew where we were; for we came upon that same cave where I had slept after I was come free of the Slugs on mine outward way. That we were come no farther showed how easy our present going had been, as you will understand. There was the spring where I had washed, and a fire-hole near; and truly these were not like to be gone away, as you shall say. Yet we cried out in a natural wonder, for the Maid was deep in interest of my coming and all the story that had brought me to her; and now it seemed that no other place could serve for our rest.

The place was as good as any, save that I would rather have had a high cave again; but we might block the mouth with boulders and so make a defence, or at least be waked by the falling.

Mine Own went inward, for the little cave was lit well by the fire-pit in front; but first I made a swift search, that there should be no creeping thing. It was sweet and free. The Maid was strangely taken that she should come to the very place where once I had lain; and I understood, for memory hath a power stronger than the body’s tiredness, and will not be dismissed.

Afterward we went to the hot spring that lay in a hollow of the rock near the fire-pit. I looked carefully for snakes and for the rat-things; seeing none, I had the Maid sit comfortable on the pool’s edge. I freed her shoes and bathed her feet in the warm water. The steam rose with a clean, stone-wet smell, and the warmth made her sigh as though a knot within her had loosened. Then I rubbed the ointment in steady, taking time, yet keeping alway a watch upon the Gorge.

When I had done with loving care, I tied her shoes again; and we went back to the cave. Together we carried boulders according to our strength. When we had enough, we went into the cave and builded a rough wall across the mouth, clumsy yet stout, rising nigh to fill the opening. It made us somewise in darkness, but safer. I had heed to make the chinks close in the bottom, so no small creeping thing could come through upon our sleep.

Then the Maid set the scrip and pouch for my pillow, and kept her bundle of torn garments for her own. I perceived she had the water-powder and tablets to hand for our waking, meaning to rise a little before me and make ready our food—so she might greet me from sleep with her gentle service. I said naught; for it gave her pleasure, and it was sweet to see her find homely duties even in that savage world, as though she were saving our culture one small tending at a time, against the great forgetting of the Night.

She bade me lie. She set the Diskos on the outward side of me, to my hand, with a dainty fearful touch—for the weapon was dreadful to her thoughts, though comforting for defence. She covered me with the cloak, kissed me sedate upon the lips, and went to her own side. She came under the cloak very nice and happy, and soon to her rest.

Presently she slept; I knew by her breathing. It seemed to my spirit she had an utter content in the ordering of our slumber, because she was nigh me, her Own Love.

Yet I could not sleep for a time. In a while she nestled to me in her sleep, sweet as a child and as though it were her right to be so near. I longed to put mine arms about her, to kiss her; but I moved not. A man should be manly with his maid that is so trustful in sleep.

At last slumber took me also. I stirred not for eight good hours, and woke then to the hissing of water. So I knew Mine Own was awake before me, as she had planned, making ready that we eat.

When she saw me move in the half-light, she gave a little word of joy, came over, put her arms about my neck, and kissed me thrice upon the lips. And in that moment it came to me that I had been kissed a while gone in my dreams, scarce knowing; so I perceived she had taken a naughty advantaging of my sleep, kissing me to her own secret delight. She said no word of it, and neither did I; but I resolved that, next time, I would wake and catch her in the act.

Then I rose and took down half the wall of boulders from the cave-mouth and looked out. There was no sight of any horrid thing in the Gorge—only one of the rat-things, very gorged-seeming, slumbering upon the side of the little fire-hole.

We came, in good time, out from that sorrowful inward place of the great Mountains—the throat and gullet of stone that I had named the Upward Gorge—and halted, soon after, between the feet of the vast hills, beyond the mouth of it. The air here lay wider, and there was a lightness upon the land, though ever under the great roof of the Night; yet it was as though some hidden hand had swept away a closeness from the lungs.

Naani looked every way at once, as one that hath been long shut within a narrow house and is brought sudden into a broad field. She drew breath quick, and then deeper, and her eyes shone with a bright wonder; and the freedom itself seemed to make her tremble, as if the very wind were a thing to be learned.

She turned, and looked upward into the dark gap whence we had come, spying upon the great mouth of the Gorge. Fear touched her then, and she ran a little way downward toward the open country of the Seas, and stopped, and looked backward again, with awe and a soul relieved; and then she looked outward, and outward, upon the spread land and the far Sea, and she near laughed and wept in the same breath, amazed and glad. She did never cease from looking, nor from those deep breathings of the wide air; for never in all her life had she been in so broad a place of lightness, as you shall perceive.

And we, both, felt that we had no more need to speak husht, as we had done alway in the gloom and narrowness of the Gorge. Surely she cried out, like a child that trieth an echo; and her voice went very prettily into the distance and seemed to be lost.

Yet lo, in a moment, there came back an answering from the dark Mountains behind us; and we turned very sudden. Whether it was true echo, or some strangeness making answer, or some unnatural call creeping downward from the Gorge, we could not be sure. Therefore we went down, a while more, until our breaths were steadier and our minds felt free of that mouth of horror that lay upward in the stone.

Then we sought a flat rock for our use, and found one that stood a little above the land; and we climbed upon it and sat there to eat and drink.

We ate close and happy; yet I kept a wise looking about, for fear of the Humpt Men, or other dangers. And alway the Maid questioned, and stared far over the Country, and her shining joy at the Sea stirred her so that sudden memories came upon her—vague, sharp as a spice upon the tongue, yet strange as dreams—mingled all with pleasure and pain. Once she broke to weeping and must be in mine arms until she knew herself again; and then she came back to her dear, natural joy.

Oft she spoke upon the clear wonder of the air, that seemed to her as a new drink. And it did seem so to me likewise, that had lived also in a Dark Land. But even as she was in her rapture, she would break sudden, and set back her speech an Eternity with olden words and half-remembered turns of phrase, englamoured and unsteady—like moonlight that once hath shone upon a table where folk did eat and talk. Then, in a moment, she would be forward again into her far-future speech, and her being close unto me; and oft we fell into a solemn silence of the heart.

The greatness of the Sea called to her with some old voice, half waking her; and I, by her stirring, was half-wakened also. Not so as on my outward way, when I had only hardness and haste; but now her awakenings pulled mine old thoughts and memory-dreams afresh upon my spirit. So we sat there shaken with dreamings of the old World, that lay, as it were, above us in that dreadful Night that roofed the Country. I have no speech to make known all the troublings and stirrings of our spirits at that time; for there are stirrings that die when you put them into words, and there are words that cannot bear them.

Far off, by miles, beyond the feet of the Mountain, the shore of the Sea went upon our left; and there lay a great mist and steam, rising and rolling. This was that same steam I had come through on mine outward journey. Naani asked of it, and I told her what I knew; and how we must pass through it.

She wondered also at the volcanoes that burned in the Sea and here and there upon the wide Country; the height and grandness exalted her and yet made her strangely humble. So, presently, I took her into mine arms and kissed her, because she was so utter a sweet Maid, and lovely with her naturalness and quick interest. Truly she kissed me in turn; and between her kissings she questioned me still—yearning for more knowing of the Country, and, as I think, being sweetly impudent in her joy.

Presently she kissed me thrice, very warm upon the mouth; and then she set her small hands upon my shoulders—very pretty, laid upon my broadness and the metal of mine armor—and strove to shake me to speedier answering, full of dear naughtiness, and needing that I should kiss her hard. I answered her, but after my fashion: a word between each kiss. She, quick and naughty, put her hand between our lips; and I kissed her palm, small and soft. Then, when I had no thought, she opened her fingers quick and kissed me through between them, and shut them again, stopping me, and laughed with her eyes.

Afterward I made her stand upon the rock. I loosed her hair down over her shoulders, and took the boots from her, so that her little feet showed bare and pretty. At first she half refused, and then stood dear and obedient that I should have my way, a little shy, the more pretty for her blushings. When I had her to my liking I stepped back a pace and looked at her. She looked again at me, quaint and naughty; then turned about very grave, pretending to be a dummy figure. And when she was come round and faced me with a sedate look, she stretched out her pretty foot all sudden and put her pink toes upon my lips. I was so surprised that I had no wit to do aught ere she snatched them back. Then she sprang into mine arms and would be hugged, and loved very great. I laughed, all tender; for I loved her utterly. I told her, as a man in love will tell his Maid, that I needed a pocket sufficient to carry her always nigh to my heart. She laughed mischievous, and said she would tickle me if I carried her thatwise—aye, and pinch me too. I had no answer but to shake her gently; yet in the midst she kissed me, very naughty upon the mouth. What shall a man do with such an one?

Then she would be more sedate and be set down upon the rock, and made me turn so she might come at the pouch upon my back. She took thence the comb and combed her hair, and I sat and talked with her and jested, my heart light as it had not been for long. Though I dreaded the Humpt Men and the monstrous beasts of that Country, yet I had no abiding horror, for there was a naturalness in those things, and no sense of an Evil Force, such as the dark places carry. Presently she bound up her hair; but I begged her to leave it upon her shoulders because it was so pretty, and she smiled and did it, happy to my pleasuring.

We grew, after that, truly sedate, and set our gear together. I put the boots again upon the Maid, and we began once more to journey downward into the Country of the Seas.

We went at a good pace, yet not to weariness; for my intent was that we should rest for our slumber upon this side of the place of steam and boilings near the shore, as you shall mind. We passed along the feet of the Mountains toward that steam, and went so for six good hours; yet still were a great hour from it, because we went not so fierce as on my outward way, when necessity drove me hard.

So, when we had walked six hours, we were somewhat beyond the eighteenth hour of that day’s journeying, and ready for sleep. We found a tall rock, hard to climb, with a flat top twice my length every way. It served well. When we were safe upon it we ate and drank, and presently slept, with the cloak under us, as was the Maid’s will; for the Country was warm and kind, and we had no need of covering.

We waked, both, after seven good hours. We sat up and looked newly each at the other, as though in that good lightness we saw each other anew; and there was fresh joy in it. She came into mine arms and kissed and needed to be kissed; and we had our need, both, and were the more hungry of one another for the having.

Then Mine Own made our breakfast. The water fizzed very strong and surprising, as it did ever in that place—like a pot beginning to sing upon a hearth—though there was no hearth save the hidden hotness of the Earth. We ate and drank and were utter happy, talking upon this and that. The Maid looked about as she ate, her eyes forever travelling to the wonders and newness; but I looked near, lest any danger be anigh.

In a while she drew my gaze to the Mountains through which the Gorge came. Now, in ease, I perceived with her how monstrous they were—like an endless wall, going upward until it was lost out of the lightness of that Country into the dark Night of the Upper World, gone beyond thought. On my outward way I had had but a vague knowing; now, being rested and having the Maid to speak with, I noted odd matters more. I tell you this little thing that you may see how restfulness lay upon me, by compare with my outward going.

We were slow with talk and eating, conscious of leaning to slackness; therefore we hurried somewhat, came down from the rock, and went forward again at a good pace.

After we had gone a while, we began to hear, far off, the hissing of the steam and the noise of upward burstings of boiling water. The sound was strange—at times like kettles upon kettles, at times like some great breath escaping—yet it troubled me less than the Maid, for I had heard it before. I assured her; and she came nigh to me; and so we entered presently into the steam.

We went more than three hours within that thickness. I had the Maid upon my back that I might go first, lest she step into a boiling pool in the mazingness of the steam that lay everywhere. I guided myself somewhat by the shore of the Sea upon our left; though we could see neither sea nor aught else, save when we went so close that the wetness was at our feet.

Truly the Sea seemed to boil in parts, and there were hot pools in all places, so that it was constant puzzle whether we went by a great pool or by the true sea. Therefore we went utter wary.

About us, from every part, came strange burstings and shriekings and whistlings where the waters broke upward from the deep world. Odd whiles the sounds were as of great monsters; and the earth shook under us. Other whiles there was a hush, and only the steam about us, and somewhere uncertain a low piping from some steam-cranny—thin, lonely, and mournful to hear.

When it was somewhere nigh upon the fourth hour, we came out of the thick steam. The pipings and roarings fell to our rearward; the steam thinned to a mist; the noises seemed far off. Presently we were clear into the air of that Country.

Now the Maid perceived the trees, in great forests upon our right hand, while the shore of the Sea ran upon our left. She was utter in wonder of the trees, needing to pluck branches, smell them, and look at each leaf. The scent of their green life—sharp, resinous, and clean—stirred her; for never in all her life had she seen such a matter as those great trees. Yet even that stirred her with vague memories, no more than dreams; and if you think but a moment, you will perceive how it was with her, and how a small thing can wake a corner of the heart.

When the sixth hour was full come, we halted in a wise place and took our tablets and water. After, the Maid bade me take her to a warm pool near by. She asked that I turn from her, yet be anigh as her Protector, as I was ever. So she washed and made herself happy with that sweet cleanness proper to her; and the warm air carried up a faint mineral smell from the pool, like wet stone heated. After she was done, she watched while I did mine, and helped me in all matters she was able; and truly I was glad that she had delight to attend upon me, treating me mother-wise, yet with her maid-heart not hid, as you have perceived.

So we went onward again, talking upon this thing and that; I watchful, bidding her keep her eyes wary, yet not be in unease.

When we had gone thus for seven good hours, we were come nigh opposed to the bright-burning fire-hill that lay offward in the Sea, which had given me a warm light when I slept in the tree on my outward way. We had been past seven hours from the place where the steam was, though I had gone that space faster alone; but I could not set so great a pace to the Maid, save odd whiles. Keep this always in mind, that you understand why parts of the homeward journey were long, by compare with mine outward going.

I set our hour for food because I saw we came nigh the place where the tree stood; and I knew the Maid would like to eat there, where I had slept. I took her to the tree, and when I told her, she begged that I indulge her and that we go upward to the branch where I had lain, and there eat our tablets.

I was willing. There was no danger in the climb; and I went alway below her, to be surety for her safeness. We came up to the great branch. She chose how we should sit, and I showed just where I had lain. She looked very close and saw how the hardness of mine armor had marked the bark under my weight. Then she sat upon that branch and ate and drank, looking outward at the light from the fire-hill, husht and thinking; and I did not disturb her.

When she was done she climbed down a little and kissed the place where I had lain. Then a thought came to her; she drew her knife and cut out a small piece of bark, and put it into her breast for a keepsake; and so seemed contented.

After we were down upon the earth again, I told her of the great beast. She cried out, and showed me the mark yet upon the ground where its belly had brushed as it ran, and the broken places of the foot-marks. By this she saw how great it had been—though it was a little thing beside the Slug—being horned and hard-skinned.

Her keen observings brought it home to me that but seventeen days had passed since I went onward from this place; and it seemed strange and scarce credible, for it felt as a great time—because it had been marked by stress of mind and great happenings, as you will agree. Yet also those times I call days did hold oft the hours of two days, and mayhap three, as you remember.

We went onward, and after the Maid had walked twelve hours I made to carry her, as ever—though she had walked thirteen that time. She said she would go upon her own feet through the next six hours, to ease me; but I knew she would be spent in a day or two if she did so, and we would make better speed if I kept to my way: that she should walk twelve hours of every journey, and afterward come into mine arms. For she was bred less hardy than I, and was yet weakened, I thought, by that dreadful month of lonesomeness and escaping before I came to succor her.

As I carried her, she spoke of her wonder at me—that I was so hard of body and set in determination. Truly I was exceeding strong, and my will likewise; else I think I had never borne to come to Mine Own through so much desolation. I smiled very happy, loving that I was strong, and in delight that Mine Own took gladness in it; for you will mind how you were in the love-days, and understand my human pride.

The Maid nestled unto me as she talked—somewise as a child to the mother, yet also as a Maid that loveth to be nigh her Man. I lifted her nearer to my lips; but she refused to kiss me, being a sweet impertinence in mine arms. Yet when I made to lower her to the easier carry, she slipped her pretty face snug under my chin and kissed me there, after her own fashion; and then was content to be as usual.

As I set her comfortable, it seemed to me she was tender. Sudden it came that mayhap my armor was hard and painful to her. I asked; she saw I would not be put off, and told me true. I was utter angered with myself—and somewise with her also, that she had not waked my unthinkingness to it.

I set her instant to the earth and made her bare her shoulders. Truly they were much bruised where she had lain so oft in mine arms against the hardness of the metal. Anger rose so that I near shook her; and she, seeing it, and being nigh to be shaken, put up her lips sudden, with a strange naughtiness, to tempt me from mine anger—an anger that she half liked. In verity I near slapped her upon her pretty shoulders, but that she ceased from tempting; instead she turned her shoulders to me, even as a child, that I might button her garment.

When I had buttoned her, she came round and closed her hand into a little fist—so small beside mine—and slipped it into my palm. I let it bide there, quiet, and made not to close upon it, as I was wont. She moved her hand within mine, to make me take notice, to grasp her; but I stayed stern, neither putting it away nor holding it—only letting it bide. Yet even then I was stirred in a secret place by her pretty ways.

After a little she drew her hand away, and had daring to be cold to me. My anger renewed quaintly, and I thought she well needed to be whipped. She made a naughty, foolish impudence upon what I said; and I told her she needed such as would teach her manners. Yet, as you will conceive, inwardly all that while her naughtiness did not stir me to proper anger, but made me masterful, wanting that she should know I was her Master, and in the same moment touched me deep, where love dwelleth and troubleth the heart with strange actings.

She asked me, in a saucy fashion meant to anger, what I meant she needed. I said she went the way to earn that she be flogged like any boy; and I meant it actual then, which amazeth me now. Yet even as I said it I was stirred by her beloved quaintness, so dainty even when she would anger me.

Lo, when I told her she went to earn a sharp reproof, she turned quick and came close, all tender and small, needing to be nigh. She slipped both her closed hands—two little fists—into my one hand. Then, because I could be no more stern with Mine Own, I put mine arm about her; and she nestled so that all my being wanted to be a shield about her.

Sense came back in broken mouthfuls, like broth taken too hot when the lips are split: first the sting, then the taste of blood, then the world returning with a slow, stubborn clarity. I lay flat upon my back, my body all one ache, my mind a bowl emptied and left in the rain. Near by was a small, harsh breathing—panting, as of some creature driven past endurance.

I turned my head, each inch a labour, and saw her.

Mine Own Maid was close at hand, bare to the air, her skin wet with river mist and sweat, her hair clinging and then lifting with each strained breath. She had set her hands upon a long pole—the same I had hewn in haste when the Humpt Men came—and she drove it down and shoved, again and again, with the desperate rhythm of a cook beating stiff dough though her wrists burn, because the bread must rise or none will eat. The raft trembled beneath me with every thrust. At that sight, memory rushed back, bitter and sharp: the flat rock, the struggle, the red spilling of my life, the river’s pull.

I tried to speak her name. Only a thin sound came, like steam escaping a cracked lid. She did not hear. Her gaze was cast backward over the water, her face set hard with fear and calculation. From the far shore came the thin, hungry howling of the Humpt Men, carried across the water and thinned by distance; yet it tore at the mind, for the Night Land holds sound in odd fashion, and cries may go wandering like lost folk.

So I understood: she had wakened while I lay senseless, had found me bleeding, and by love alone had moved what ought not be movable. Later she told me, in halting phrases, how she had come to herself on the earth with some weight upon her breast, and had seen that it was my head. She believed me dead, for I lay so still. She wriggled free, and when she drew me away and saw the blood, her heart near broke. Yet she found a breath in me. Hope sprang in her like a seed that refuses drought.

She looked about, quick as any housewife who hears a pot boil over and at the same time listens for danger at the door. There was the dead Humpt Man near, and the others slain upon and about the rock. No living thing in sight—only that thick waiting of the woods.

She ran to the raft, dipped my headpiece in the river and brought water, flinging it upon me. Still I would not wake. And then, by some inward warning—whether from the soul, or that keen animal sense the body holds when words are no use—she knew peril drew near. There are times in that dark world when the ear catches a wrongness before the eye can name it: a hush where insects should rasp, a shift in the river’s talk, a breath taken by the trees as though they listen.

She chose then, without any grand speech, only with the ferocity of love: either to save me, or to die with me.

She hauled me—dragged and lifted by turns—down to the raft. She got me upon it. She snatched up the pole. In that moment she saw her torn garments still clutched in the hand of the dead brute, as she had slipped them to escape him. She tore them free and ran back, pushed the raft from shore, and leaped aboard.

Then came a sound from the wood, and the two Humpt Men that yet lived ran out, silent and intent, following her track. They reached the bank as she drove the pole and forced the raft outward. They stood staring, stupid with thwarted hunger, and then began their howling. They did not swim—either they could not, or they feared the water’s Dread. The river had its own voice, deep and old, and no living thing crossed it lightly.

As I lay there and heard those cries falling farther behind, I tried to rise to help her. My will was a knife; my body was wet cloth. I was gone again into darkness, and she—naked, shaking, unthinking of herself—piloted us to the little island that had seemed no more than a lump of refuge.

After that there is only a drift of pain and half-waking: a life lived between breaths. Sometimes I opened my eyes and nothing held weight or meaning, as though the world were a thin gruel without salt. Other whiles I knew only that Love moved about me, watchful and gentle, like a hand laid upon a fevered brow. In those black mists of weakness, despair crept close, but hope was kept alive by her constant tending.

At length there came a waking that was clean.

I lay upon something soft, sheltered from wind. The air was quiet in a way that soothed the mind; not dead silence, but the right small sounds: water speaking around the island, a faint stirring in leaves, the distant groan of the Night. In such an acoustic peace a spirit can mend, as a broth mends a man more than dry tablets ever could. There was no clanging, no harsh machine-cry, none of that brutal, mind-flaying noise that in the old days men called progress and sold as art. Here, every sound belonged.

I perceived her kneeling beside me. When she saw my eyes open, her face filled with such glad love that it was as though I drank warmth and grew stronger merely by looking. She bent and kissed my mouth, her tears sweet upon my cheek. I kissed her again, and content went through me like good food after starvation.

She took my head into the crook of her arm and gave me to drink. The water had a faint taste of leaf and stone, and in it was the labour of her bringing. When I had swallowed, she kissed me once more, light as a cool breath on burnt skin, and eased my head down. Sleep took me again while she still watched.

This happened thrice, as I mind. Each time I surfaced, she was there, measuring my need with the care of one who tends a simmering pot, never letting it boil over, never letting it go cold. At the third waking I could move my hand. She understood at once and took it, holding it as though it were some precious thing that must not be spilled.

At the fourth time I whispered that I loved her. She broke into sudden weeping and pressed my hand against her breast, as though to keep the words from blowing away.

At the fifth waking the world was steadier. I knew then that I lay naked under the cloak, my body bound about with bandages. Those bandages, I learned after, were torn strips from her own garments. She had cut and wrapped and tied, again and again, with the plain skill of necessity—like a woman who, lacking proper twine, plaits grass and makes it do.

I saw also that she was clothed again in the garments I had given her—outer and inner—those same that the Humpt Man had torn away and she had slipped to escape him. She had mended them cunningly. She found thread in the torn cloth, drew it out strand by strand, and made needles from island thorns. The thorns broke often; she fetched another, and another, persisting through a hundred little failures until the seams held. Thus she sat by me in long hours, sewing as if repairing not only cloth but the very shape of our lives.

When she saw the interest in my gaze, she remembered how last I had seen her, and a gentle blush came. She kissed me to hide her face from my eyes. I wished then—fiercely—that I were strong enough to kneel to her in glad reverence. Such was my love, and is the love of any who truly have loved: not the taking, but the honouring.

Day by day my strength returned, slow as the thickening of stew left to work over a low flame. She kept time by my timepiece and gave me broth made of the tablets softened in water. It was a thin fare, yet she watched the measure of it as a careful cook watches ration and need. At set times she gave me drink, washed me, changed the bandages, and washed and dried the strips to use again, for we were sore lacking in all such matters.

By the fifth day I was at ease from the worst of pain, and happiness came upon us like sun remembered. She talked prettily, but kept me quiet, for weakness still lay in me.

On the sixth day she let me answer her more fully, and I told her in words what my eyes had been saying all those silent hours. She assured me she was well and recovered; yet I saw she had grown thin. Her eyes were weary beneath their gladness, the way a hearth-fire can still burn bright though the wood is low.

I made her bring her own tablets to my side as she always did. I kissed them first—our small ritual of tenderness—and she ate and drank. Then I bade her make me broth, and when it was ready I asked that the Diskos be set near. Afterward I drew her down beside me and had her lie by me. I took her head upon my arm and told her to sleep, to have no fear of wearying me; for I would be the more rested with her so near.

At first she fretted that she was too heavy. But I showed her some strength had come back, and pressed her gently to me. She nestled content and fell into utter sleep at once; she had been in sore need of it, having watched me as a mother watches a sick child, forgetting her own body’s demands.

She slept twelve great hours. Once she made a small moaning and then, still asleep, set her face closer against me. I lay without weariness or loneliness, full of content, looking down upon her where she slept in the hollow of my arm. Her face, even in rest, bore a goodness that made holiness in my heart.

At the third hour I drank part of the broth; at the sixth, more; at the ninth, I finished it. My right arm was free for this and for the Diskos. More than once I set my hand upon that great weapon, as upon a true comrade. In my uplifted state it seemed the Diskos knew me and loved me; perhaps that was but the strange tenderness of the hour. Yet the thing had ever an oneness with the man who bore it.

In the twelfth hour she woke with a sweet haste, rising from my arm, anxious to know I was well. When she saw me laugh softly at her dear concern, relief came over her. Then she looked about, counted the hours, and reproached herself for sleeping so long.

I put on a mocking sternness and forbade her to say one more word against herself. She was to be glad that I was happy, and glad likewise for her own rest.

At that, the impudent Maid set her little fist to my nose and threatened me. I laughed so heartily she feared I would set my wounds bleeding anew, and scolded herself again; yet no harm came.

When I could speak, I asked—without thinking—whether she had had brothers, because she played so naturally with me. The moment the words left, I knew my thoughtlessness. I took her hand so she might know I meant no hurt. She nodded, quiet, and kissed my hand, then slipped away a little distance, lest she weep where I should see.

Trouble came upon me at my own lack of care. I could do nothing but call her gently. She returned soon, smiling loving and cheerful; yet I saw the shine of tears she tried to hide while she made new broth.

Before I would take it, I drew her into my arms. She submitted glad and happy, yet held her weight away from me lest she pain my woundings. Then we ate, and our talk was glad again, and I slept, keeping her near though she watched more than she rested.

The seventh day—if days may be counted in that dark world—was wondrous happy. When I woke she slept like a child beside me, her face nestled against me. She wakened almost at once, for she had only drowsed in brief snatches, watching through the hours.

She washed me with gentle care, as one cleans a cherished vessel that must not be cracked. Then we ate and drank together. Now she allowed me my tablets whole, and water after, as when I had been well. This pleased me, for I ached to regain strength quickly, that I might guard her again and go forward with our journey toward the Mighty Pyramid. With the tablets whole I felt my blood begin to build again, and my hunger was better satisfied than by broth.

She gave me tablets oft. I made her count, and saw there were enough, if I grew strong with speed. I did not refuse, for I knew I must make blood within me; else, at the next need, I should be lacking.

We kissed each the tablets of the other, and drank from the same cup, and were utterly happy—like children in play, yet man and maid in truth.

Afterward she shifted my bandages, washed me, and set me in comfort. She kept me low-lying, and I did not mind, for strength was not yet sufficient for impatience. Besides, she was with me always, making sweet quips, talking, laughing, and sometimes singing softly—little tunes that seemed older than words, set to the island’s hush. The sound of her voice sat in the air as a scent sits in a room where good bread has been baked: it made the place a home, though the world outside was death.

Later she went a little way off for her toilet. I begged her to be swift, and she promised merrily. She returned with her hair in a lovely cloud about her shoulders, her pretty feet bare from her bathing in a pool beyond bushes. She said I was so impatient a man she must do half her dressing with me. Truly she came so because she knew my delight in watching her gather and set her abundance of hair, and because she hungered to be near me; yet there was still in her a quaint stirring of shyness that made her dearer.

I had her sit by my hand. Then I scolded her for not having proper care of her feet, and bade her set them toward me that I might look close. She, being a rogue, thought I meant to kiss them—and indeed she was not always wrong—but I had another plan. I plucked, sly as might be, a single hair from her head. She made a small “Oh!” and thought no more.

When she set her feet to me, I held them as strongly as I could and bound her pretty toes together with that hair. So she was captive to me, and we laughed as children. Afterward she stole her feet back; yet I knew she took wondrous heed not to break the hair, and sat beside me bound in that pretty way while pretending she was not.

She did up her hair upon her head very lovely; then I lifted my hands and took it down again. She kissed me and asked how she should ever set it proper if I teased so. Then she took the abundance and laid it on both sides of my face and kissed me while I looked up through so much beauty.

Afterward she cut a lock of my hair and a lock of her own, and plaited them together so that our hair blended. She hid it in her bosom. I was out of content at that, and wished to do likewise, but it wearied me to hold up my hands. So she cut a second lock from my head and a second tress from her own. She made me kiss the hair from her, and she kissed the hair from me, and plaited them and gave them to me. I set that plaiting under the great bandage that went over my heart.

Then she complained, with mock grievance, that the second plaiting had been kissed, and the first lacked. I refused to exchange. So we quarrelled in jest and had presently to be kissed into forgiveness. Such glad foolishness lives in the love-days, and is as necessary as salt.

She made me keep my hands low, for the lifting proved too much. She took my great hands and threatened dreadful things if I were not humble. I said I was an humble man. She held my hands with one small one—only by the thumbs, for her hand was so little—and with her free hand covered my eyes. Thus she held me helpless and kissed me, dainty and impudent, upon the mouth. Then she loosed me and became demure.

We were silent a space. I stretched out my hand—very great, yet white and trembling from lack of blood. She knew what I meant. She clenched both hands into fists and set them into my one hand. They were little fists. I was happy at the feel of them, as always, and she sat with her lids a little down, quietly glad.

I plagued her gently, saying she must be all a-lack if she tried to use those small hands for aught. In a moment she put both arms about my neck and kissed me with utter tenderness, then drew away lest she overset me.

Then I made her sit by me, and I began to tell her a tale—half in play, half as something that had been waiting in me through centuries like a recipe remembered only in the hands.

I spoke of a young man in the olden days who met the One Maid Upon All The Earth, and how they loved and were married; and how she died; and of the man’s desperate madness of grief that near destroyed him. And how he woke sudden into the far future, into a New Time, and learned that His Own lived in that Time also, and he set out to find her and truly came unto her. I told how she was different in beauty, yet utterly lovely, and how the man held an utter reverence for her, a love that lived like a constant sweet pain, breeding holy thoughts from her companionship and from memory.

I could not go further. Mine Own broke into weeping and fell to her knees, holding my hand against her breast. She put her hand gentle upon my lips to stop me. Through her tears she whispered something—words that were near no words, as though the tongue had no shape for the feeling.

There came into her face a dear Motherhood, and in her eyes a sudden shining of Memory—so sharp it was near dreadful, only it seemed to her like a dream she was half inside. In me, too, a strange solemn pain came through the part-open gateways of my own Memory. In that moment I remembered very clear, with anguish.

I told her how the babe had gone onward, after that the Beloved had died.

An utter quiet fell upon us, as when all the sounds of a house pause because a name has been spoken that no one speaks lightly. There are experiences that will not be translated, no matter how the mouth strives; they sit beyond any dominant lexicon, beyond any language that has survived. In that hush I felt, with a sudden sorrow, how much of mankind’s old speech had died, taking with it whole ways of knowing—flavours of thought, textures of tenderness—leaving us with words that fit like borrowed garments.

Suddenly she bent to me, and I took her into my arms, drawing her out of that vague dream. Before she came fully back, she tried to set into words what she had seen of that memory-vision of the babe; but she was strangely dumb, as though the thing had no true name in the tongue we used. I, too, was silent, for all that lay between us forever.

Presently she kissed me and was herself again, and went to attend our food.

That day was lovely. I had strength enough to take interest and to talk. She was rested now, and ease had come into her heart concerning me. We laughed often, making jests glad and foolish. Once I asked her an old puzzle that rose out of my Memory-dreams, and she listened as one hearing something both strange and familiar. Then, as though knowledge came from beyond Eternity, she answered that it was when he was a little hoarse.

You know the jest, troubled over in school in the olden days; yet it was strange beyond telling that such a thing should rise in us. In our Age we had never seen an Horse, nor knew that such a creature had ever been. The very word sat oddly in the mouth, like a spice from a lost country. When our laughter ended we looked at one another and wondered what an horse might be, and yet within us was a vague inward knowing—an inheritance with no language to carry it whole.

So our thoughts looked backward through dim Ages, and from jests we came toward solemnness. The Maid was near tears again. I drew our talk forward from the Abyss of Years and ceased from such Memory-dreaming for that time, for I would not have her troubled.

Then I told her—at length, for we had time and oneness—of the Mighty Pyramid: of its safety, its customs, its ordered living in the midst of the Night, and the strange marvels within. I had spoken of it before in brief, but never with such leisure.

At once she was eager and hushed, asking constant of every matter, as one who has lived on scant fare asks of a feast, not for gluttony but for longing to know. And I, speaking, saw her wonder and excitement, for it was to her as if a man came down from a far star to tell of a place where the human heart yet kept its stores—its histories, its songs, its remembered tastes—against the long erosion of time and silence.

In the tenth hour of that day we pushed the raft off, and the sea took us as a pan takes heat—steadily, if you set your hands right. The paddles lay easy in the rests I had fashioned; they did not fight the wrists, but answered as though they had known our palms aforetime. The Maid stood to the fore paddle and I to the hinder, and together we fell into a rhythm that was like kneading—press, draw, breathe—until the raft went forward with a speed a little less than walking upon harsh ground, yet with none of the jarring of stones beneath the boot.

There was a sound that went with our labour: the long, soft lick of water upon the timbers; the faint creak where the lashings took strain; the paddles’ hush as they entered and the darker gulp as they drew out. In that Deep Country, sound had a weight of comfort. There was no iron scream of engines, no thin chatter of dead mechanisms; only the sea’s mouth and our own breathing. We spoke, too, in a gentle, unhurried way, as folk speak while shelling beans at a table, the work simple enough that love may ride upon it.

About the twelfth hour we stopped. I set the raft’s nose a little toward the shore current, and we ate and drank. Our victual was plain, yet to us it carried a feast’s meaning, for in such a world to chew without fear is itself a rare spice. The Maid broke the food with careful fingers, as though portioning out memory; and when she drank, the little sound of her swallow seemed loud in the wide quiet. Then we went on again with the easy labour.

And truly when once the movement had us, it was as though we did no more than rock a little fore and back upon our feet, and the hours slipped by like grains through a hand. Oft she would look back to me with that bright daring that is half laughter, half hunger; she would set her lips in a way to tempt me, and yet when I would have left my paddle to go forward unto her, she shook her head—most dear—commanding patience as one commands a simmering pot: not yet, not yet, lest all be spoiled.

When the eighteenth hour came we drew in the paddles. The Maid spread the cloak very nice for our bed, smoothing it as though she made a place at a hearth; and afterward we ate and drank again, and presently to our slumber. We slept sudden and deep, as if the sea itself had folded over our minds.

Eight good hours after we woke together. For a little moment we scarce knew where we were—only the taste of sleep and the nearness of each other—then understanding came, and with it joy. We laughed each in the face of the other, like children waking safe after a storm. We kissed, and washed a little in the sea-water, cold and clean upon the skin, and so to our food again, with gratitude. Then once more to paddling.

So went we along the coast, peaceful and content, four good days of four-and-twenty hours each. We made no great haste, nor any boast of strength, but went easy that I might gather mine. In all that voyaging naught of dread befell us, save that once we saw a great beast come lumbersome out of the sea on to the shore, and there browse upon the herbage—so it seemed—though we were over far to have surety. Yet it carried no monstrousness in its bearing; it did not sour the air with any feel of unhuman evil. It was but life, ancient and blunt, feeding as it had fed in the first world.

And that thought took me—that in this Deep Country, so near to our ending, there were creatures that might have been in the beginning; and I remembered that saying, that extremes do meet. But I made no sermon of it; for while the mind may wander, the hands must still do the work, and love must be kept warm. Yet in me there lived an inward leaning to believe there is in every living thing a force peculiar to itself—whether cause or result, I cannot say—and that the spirit of man, untainted, is man, however the body be modified by need or foul breeding. I think on this not as a philosopher in a chair, but as one who has smelt the rankness of monsters half man, half beast, and known in the nose and in the heart that something is wrong there, beyond mere shape.

And also I do not speak here of that which may be afterward, when our life is done. Only this: I held in me a hope of beauteous things and of a sweet furthering, like the smell of bread in a house when you stand outside in the cold and know you are welcome within.

But I return to the telling.

There was one other thing of note whilst we were upon the seas: a great Fire-Hill standing out in the water, and we passed very nigh. The sea boiled about it, though not everywhere, and from it sprang a score great jets that went upward monstrous high. Their roaring came plain across the water, and beneath that roar were strange gruntings about the base, as though the sea itself had a throat and laboured to speak. I conceived the sounds to be of gases bursting up in this place and in that; yet whatever the cause, it made us to know the deep energy wakeful in that land. Long after we had passed, we stared backward upon that hill of fire and force, as folk stare after a bell’s last note, feeling it still in the bones.

Beyond this there was only our care in the place where the Great Sea was broken into smaller seas, knit with passages of water that let us through. And as we came near to that shore where the First Gorge began, I showed Mine Own Maid the places where I had slept when first I came into that Country. She was sweet in interest, ever hungry to learn, as one that treasures old receipts and insists upon knowing not only what was eaten, but how it was cooked, and with what hands.

So in the tenth hour of the fourth day upon the water—having begun likewise in the tenth hour—we came to the land, a flat place of shore where the country sloped upward toward the mouth of the First Gorge. It had been a sweet and peaceful water-journey, and I was happy; yet I knew much danger yet to come, and I set my heart in courage. For if we conquered and came safe into our Mighty Home, then should we have all our lives together in loveliness; and that is a prize worth any hunger and any weariness.

We stranded the raft as well as we might and stood a little, wondering if any should ever again behold it through all eternity. We looked each upon the other. Then the Maid cut free a small piece of the wood for remembrance, a token like a saved crust from a hard day’s meal, to be held afterward and tasted in the mind.

We gathered our gear. The Maid aided me to get once more into mine armour, with the carefulness of one wrapping and storing precious tools. Soon I had the scrip and pouch again to my back, the Diskos in my hand, all in readiness. The Maid carried her bundle—grown small now—and had her belt about her body with her knife ready to her hand.

And then she knelt and kissed the raft. Memories came upon her as a tide. In that kiss was a breaking from the first part of her life—quiet island days, the nursing of me back to strength, the homely brave little doings that had been their own kind of saga. I understood, and had a quiet sympathy, and perceived how sorrow and love may be one knot in the throat.

I stooped very gentle and put Mine Own to her feet, and led her from the raft. She needed to be near me, and I to have her near. So we went forward, making upward toward the dark mouth of the Great Gorge.

To our right, some miles off, rose that grim and huge Mountain where far up in the monstrousness of the night perched the four fire-hills. Below them climbed the ash-hills cast down throughout eternity. The Maid looked long upon it, never done with wonder, nor I; for no human, I think, could ever grow used to such a sight.

Presently we came into the high mouth of the Gorge and went onward into the gloom until the place where it bent sharp unto the left into darkness. There we paused and turned again to the Country of Seas for a last outward look over that Deep and living Land hid far downward in the everlasting night.

It was solemn to know we might be the last olden humans to look upon that country. In that moment I wondered whether the Humpt Men might ever in some far eternity develop unto the full sweetness of the spirit of Humanity that I believed lay inward of them. It came natural, as certain thoughts do when you stand at a threshold.

We looked a while and were hushed, and we harked to the far mutter of the Great Fire-Mountains and the Fire-Hills and the noise of life over that land. Even then we were but a few pacings from the silence of the Great Gorge, that should lead us toward the Eternal Strangeness of the Night Land. The Maid held mine arm very close, and together we took in that red light as though we might carry it like a hearth-coal through the coming dark.

Then I turned. She slipped her hand into mine, and tears went silent down her face. They were not all sorrow. There was happiness in them, and a vague ache for the island where she had nursed me, and for the places her memory should roam in after-life. I could see her telling, mayhap, to children—if such blessing were granted—of that country they should never see, only hold as wonder. And in that, I felt the hard truth: some experiences cannot be carried into another mouth’s language, no matter how faithful the teller. The sound of those seas, the smell of that air, the particular loneliness of that light—these would die, undelivered, if she had not me who had been there and could answer her words with mine.

We went past the Mighty Corner of the Gorge and stumbled into the gloom.

We travelled sixteen hours very steady, with naught but darkness to trouble us; and by this we had been six-and-twenty hours since last we slept. It was foolishness, for I needed strength before we reached the Night Land, and it was folly to over-tire; and the Maid told me so, practical as an elder that will not waste seed on a stony patch.

So we found a safe place for slumber. While we ate and drank, we counted from my notings of the outward way, and decided to go no more than sixteen hours’ journey each day through the Gorge, and sleep always eight good hours. This we did, then and after, until we came out of the Great Gorge, which took us five days in all.

When we came into the light places of the Gorge we were more cheered; yet oft we were half smothered with horrid gases that came up in this part and that, as you do mind. The smell of them was a vile seasoning in the air, like a kitchen tainted by spoiled fat—clinging, sickening, making the throat to close. And there was in that place an acoustic poverty: a deadness that pressed upon the mind. In the world of our old time men had made a commerce of noise and called it progress, but here was another cruelty—the emptiness, broken only by the wrong sounds of burning gas. I learned then how the ear, as much as the belly, craves a right nourishment.

My strength grew constant as we journeyed, yet the Maid would never suffer that I carry her. She went light and clever, grown set unto this constant wander.

At this place and that I paused to show Mine Own those spots where I had slumbered when I went outward alone, searching. She must stand in the very place, as though the stone might speak. And always then she was tender with me and lacking speech, for her heart called too loud for words.

Ever now she asked when we should come unto the Night Land, and how far it might be. Excitement grew in her, dear and natural, and in me likewise. I wondered what she should think of the Mighty Redoubt and of that strange land. And above all I was shaken with haste to have her safe, lest after coming so far some woe should yet happen. It was hard not to race and exceed the hours we had set; but we had wisdom and slept always after the sixteenth hour.

In all that great Gorge we saw no life. Only gas-burnings, boulders, stark rocks, and the rank smells. Always an utter quiet, save when some lonesome gas-fire moaned low or whistled thin. The whistling went dree across the waste, and the moaning was a thing to make loneliness felt in the heart. The Maid felt it with me; she would draw a little closer at each mournful note, as though to lend her own breath against the place’s hunger.

And always I knew she thought how I had come through there alone to seek her in unknown lands. I was but a natural man, and it made me glad in my spirit that she remembered. For love is fed not only by what is done, but by the keeping of it in the mind; and in that keeping there is power—who names the deed, owns it; who speaks it, shapes it. Yet between us there was no need to bargain with words. We had our private tongue of looks and touches, unmarketed and untranslatable, and it held us steady.

Upon the fifth day, about the seventh hour, I heard oddwhiles a sound in this place and that of the Gorge, as though the rocks made hushed and strange noises at us. I drew the Maid very nigh, the Diskos to hand, and we went onward with great caution.

Thrice we passed places where gas-fires burned and danced, moaning low and sometimes whistling; yet the other sounds came from the rocks, here and there, very strange and yet somehow familiar. Sudden it came to me that there was a far-away noise in these sounds; though they seemed to come from almost to my elbow. Then I knew I heard little echoings caught by near rocks, born of some far mighty sound.

It was the monstrous piping of the Great Gas Fountain.

I told Mine Own at once. She grew eager with me, for it was a wondrous thing and a landmark; it showed we were nigh to come out of the Gorge, our journey nearer an ending. We looked ahead earnest; yet there were so many leaping fires before us that we could not be sure which was the far monstrous dance of the Great Fountain, for at such distance the lesser fires made more upon the eye than the great flame did.

Presently, as we went onward, we saw a lightening and darkening far along the Gorge, as though the background of night lost somewhat of its intensity with constant shudders of light. That was surely the far dance of the Great Fountain. We watched as we journeyed, and the shudderings grew and merged into a strange uplifting and falling of a far-off blue flame.

The sound came steadier, growing by degrees into a monstrous piping, very great and wonderful, with constant changes of note—as though the earth played upon a vast hollow reed. It filled the air so that the mind could not escape it. There are sounds that comfort by their order—the cluck of hens at dusk, the steady hiss of a pot simmering, the wind through grain—and there are sounds that command by their inhuman scale. This piping was of that latter kind: it did not invite, it insisted. Yet even so, it was not the ugly rattle of a machine built for profit; it was wild, terrible, and honest, the world’s own throat.

We came past the last of the lesser fires and entered that part of the Gorge which was fireless save for the great dance of the Fountain, now grown huge and plain, making a quaking light over all the place. At last we were very nigh to it and half stunned by the noise, now an utter furious roaring. We stood as two lonesome strangers in the mouth-part of that deep desolate Gorge and stared voiceless at the great flaw. Mine arm was about the Maid. She stood very close. Neither spoke; how should we, with such sound battering the air?

After we had stared a great while, we turned and looked each on the other and kissed soberly there in the light from the monster flame. Then again we stared, and looked all ways, and marvelled how the blue light threw itself outward, spreading strange and far, revealing for moments the opposite walls like a lost world of desolate mountains.

We sought to see the way our journey must go. Naught was clear save when the flame rose to monstrous height, because huge rocks stood about it. Yet I could show the Maid something of the bottom part of the Mighty and Utter Monster Slope, the last way ere we came to the Night Land.

We went onward about a good mile so as not to be so deafened. It was beyond the seventeenth hour. We ate and drank and made our rest secure among great boulders. Even as we chewed, the roar was present behind the teeth, changing the very taste of silence, so that in our mouths the bread—plain though it was—seemed a gift wrested from a tyrant sound.

When we woke we ate and drank again, and were something silent, gazing at the flame dancing monstrous and lonesome, set about with stark rocks like giants of silence watching forever. Presently we took our gear and went forward toward the utter dark of the Mighty Slope.

We began that huge climb that should last through days in an eternity of night.

Oft in the first hours we turned from our blind stumblings and gazed downward from the long height unto the loom of the flame, shuddering far below and making quaking light in that deep dark. So we left it to dance forever in that lost place of the world, and bent all will and strength unto the climb.

Thus we stumbled sixteen great hours. By then we had found a pace proper to that task and were numbed, and the darkness made all seem unreal, as though the world had been reduced to touch alone: rock under palm, strap against waist, the Maid’s breathing behind me, and the steady ache of muscle.

For eight days then we went upward forever through that dreadful night. After the first day we crept always upon hands and knees. I went in front with the Diskos ready upon my hip. I took two straps from the pouch and scrip and made a certain length, fastening them from the Maid’s waist-belt unto mine, so that I might know always she came close after me. In such dark, a small assurance is as precious as a remembered song; and the faint tug of that strap was a language between us, saying: I am here. I follow. Do not leave me to the silence.

We kept to our measure: sixteen hours’ journey, and at the sixth and twelfth hours we ate and drank; likewise before sleep, and again upon waking. Our slumber held about eight good hours. Thus did I husband our strength for the dreadness yet before us across the terror of the Night Land. Food was not only fuel; it was discipline and culture and an anchor. The act of sharing it—breaking, handing, swallowing in time—kept us human when the world did all it could to grind speech out of the mouth and make the mind a mere animal of endurance.

Oft I was sickened and wearied of reaching forward and upward forever, of blind fumblings to find way about boulders and rocks and holes in the dark. It seemed we went lost from all life and knowing, in a blackness that should never slacken. At such times I paused and called soft unto Mine Own that she creep up nigh. Then I took her into mine arms out of the utter night, and we held, and gave and had comfort—warmth against the cold of that soundless slope, and the small, fierce proof that love could still make a home where no hearth might be built.

So I came at last into the nearer waste beneath the gaze of the Mighty Pyramid, and my great Home rose up against the endless Night like a mountain built of hearth-warmth and stored breath—an impossible bulk of ordered light in a country that hungered for all living. Yet even that vastness could not astonish me as it had once done; despair and weariness had their claws too deep. I cared for nothing save to bring Mine Own Maid into the Circle, into the clean safety where bread is baked and water is sweet and no thing may steal the mind.

The Pyramid was still a long way off.

I went forward with a strong going; and as I passed a hollow where a fire-hole burned—its heat like an open oven-mouth in the cold—there crawled something out. It rose, slowly, from four limbs to two, and became a great haired Man, huge as a farm-ox, his shoulders rolling as if he had lived too long in burrows. He stared at me, then came eager, hands stretched out.

The light from the fire-hole showed those hands plain: monstrous, armed brutish with hooked claws, the hands of a thing that could rip flesh as a cook splits a carcass.

I set Mine Own swift to the earth. I had no care for my life; only that he delayed me. Cold anger took me, the kind that steadies the wrist, and I leaped in furious and smote at him. He sprang aside as quick as a cat, and with one swing of his arm out of the shifting shadow cast by the fire-hole’s dancing, he caught my head-piece and plucked it away with such brutish strength that he flung me near a dozen feet and I went hard upon my back.

I was bruised and shaken, but the life in me held. I was up in a moment. I came in upon him, and the Diskos roared and blazed in my hands—its fierce light like a hot iron at the forge, its hum a harsh note in the Night’s foul music. I struck him above the middle. The Diskos glutted itself and went through him as though he were no more than a hanging of smoke. His upper part turned as he died and went horrid to the earth; his legs and trunk stood a heartbeat in the fire-hole’s glare, and the blood went upward like a fountain, black in the night air.

I made no pause. I leaped to the Maid, gathered her to my arms as one lifts a sleeping child from beside the hearth, and went onward past that dead thing—which only then fell wholly with an horrid sound that made the ground-answer ring in my bones.

And the Night was full of an astonishment and upliftedness of the Millions. Their spirit-crying went about me, not as noise but as a pressure like wind through a great hall: they perceived, they cast love and delight toward me, and a vast excitement rose among them because the thing had fallen and I still went forward with Mine Own.

Scarce a mile more and two vague things slid out of a dark place where rocks upstood. I smote them with the Diskos and ran on. What they were, I never knew.

After that it seemed I went smiting forever. Strange things came time and oft from bushes and from stones, as though the Land itself crawled with foul life. I struck, I passed, I struck again, as in a fever-dream—yet always my arms held the Maid close, the weight of her dear body growing more slack, her breathing more thin. My despair sharpened into a fierce, straight purpose: to reach the Circle, or die upon its very edge.

All the Land gave out grim roarings and odd-wise lower sounds, deadly and thick. Once I heard the heavy drumming of giants running, a rhythm like distant threshing—great footfalls beating the ground as men beat grain from the ear. The sound was a kind of pollution of the world’s proper silence, as if some monstrous engine had been set loose where only wind and the long small rustling of night-growth should have been. In that Country of Silence the noises of the beasts were not merely sound; they were dominion.

How I was not slain by some dread Force I cannot tell, unless that I was burned free of all weakness by my bitter training, so that no Evil Power could find purchase through fear.

Then came again, to the South-East, the deep dreadful baying of the Night-Hounds—nearer, nearer—and I knew at last that no strength of mine could protect Mine Own if they came full upon us.

And lo, from the upwardness of the night where the Last Light hung, there came a strange blue flash that smote down into the Land to the South-East. Again it came, and again—mayhap a score times—each flash a sharp tearing in the dark like the quick flare when a fat is cast into a very hot pan. With them came a peculiar crackling sound, less than the old thunder of our vanished world, yet louder than any other sound you shall hear; it rasped the ear and set the teeth to ache.

I knew the Humans had begun to fight for me, that I might bring Mine Own safe unto Home.

The wakefulness of the Land that had been seemed as sleep beside the wakefulness that now arose. The Night rocked with monster-roarings and the stirring of Great Forces. Over all, from the lost East, came that dreadful Laughter—yowling, obscene, unceasing—like a mockery flung across the world’s empty kitchens where no songs were left, only the clatter of hunger.

The baying of the Hounds grew constant and hoarse. A mighty pack was out, no more maybe than a good mile to the South-East. I was alone, save for the dying Maid in my arms. I looked vainly for the Hundred Thousand that were Prepared to aid me, but saw nothing—only strange lights and shadows, and movements in this place and that place where monstrous life shifted.

The Hounds came nearer with every moment. I ceased not from my stride, but went forward and began to run; for the Pyramid was not a huge way off, and the shine of the Circle about it could be seen save where it was hid oddly by rock and swell of ground. A despairing hope took hold of me: that I might yet come within that guarding line.

The baying drew in, a bitter thing—so near to Home, the great mountain of refuge towering before me, seeming at times almost within reach, yet still two great miles off. I called out in vain, asking why none came to aid in this extremity. The Hounds bayed now within half a great mile upon my left, and their voice told me they had scent.

The Millions had anguish for me. Their sympathy pressed upon my spirit, and in a moment there came about me a companioning, a sweet spiritual force bred of their love and swift understanding. It steadied me as a warm broth steadies a faint belly: not saving, not changing the facts, but giving a moment’s strength to endure them. They perceived I was at the end of hope. The Hounds were almost upon me.

In that instant I heard again the shaking beat of the Earth-Current—deep, like the pulse under a floor where great machinery turns. I knew the Humans took desperate means.

I saw the pack then, vast, upon my left: Hounds running at a great pace, heads low, huge as horses, appearing and vanishing in the same moment as they ran through shifting shadow. There were hundreds of them.

I knew we two were dead ere a minute be gone if the Humans did not haste. I stood where I was; there was no use to run. I looked from the Hounds to the Mighty Pyramid and back again, my hope gone. The Hounds were scarce two hundred fathoms off.

As I looked that last time to the Pyramid, there broke out a monstrous bursting flame that rushed downward from the sealed lower part. It smote upon the Land where the Hounds ran, and all the Night was lost from my sight in the brightness and strangeness of that flame. I saw no Pyramid, no ground, no running beasts—only a shining dreadful glory, as though the very air had become a furnace.

The blast hit the Night, a hotness that seemed to wither me even at distance, drying the mouth and tightening the skin as if I stood too near an open kiln. I perceived the Humans had turned loose the Earth-Current upon the Hounds. A constant great thundering went over the Land as the Earth-Force rent and split the air and tore up the earth. The roarings of the Monsters were hushed and lost in that mightier sound. Where the Hounds had been was only flame and broken ground; rocks were hurled all whithers with a vast noise. It was mercy I was not slain an hundred times by the bursting and failing of boulders flung blind through the dark.

Then in a moment the Humans cut off the Earth-Force and brought it again to control. A great silence seemed to settle, and an utter dark, save for flames and noise where the Current had struck. I shook free of dazedness and ran again; for now it seemed I might yet win to safety.

My eyes grew accustomed. I looked all ways lest something come upon me even then to end us. For a good time I saw nothing, and the Land’s wakeful sounds were hushed—save always that grim horrid Laughter from afar in the dead East, as though some power made sport of every human hope.

Often as I ran I stared upward, hungry-hearted, at the Pyramid. It seemed less bright than before. At first I thought my sight still dazed from the flame, but soon I knew it was truly dimmer. The great using of the Earth-Current to save us had drawn down the strength that fed the lights. The thought went cold upon my heart: if the Earth-Force were made too low, all the Millions within the Refuge might be in danger. The Masters would know this and could not again loose the Current to aid me until it flowed full, lest they destroy all the Peoples.

So I ran the more desperate, bearing the Maid.

Yet still the Hundred Thousand came not to meet me. The Land began again to give out monster-noises, and new, peculiar sounds as if more things were waked than ever I had heard. I saw now and then living shapes creeping between me and the Circle’s light. I knew I must yet fight bitterly. I swung the Diskos free and ran on.

Sudden my spirit knew warning of a new peril. I looked upward into the night, expecting the Master Monstruwacan to speak by the Set Speech. But there came no quick flashing of those dear, measured signs—only an upward stillness, and the dimness of the Pyramid’s lights. Later I learned that the Master did make to warn me, but all the instruments in the Tower of Observation failed, and likewise the machinery of the Pyramid ceased—lifts stilled, air pumps moaned no more—for nigh a great hour until the Earth-Current grew again more full. It shows how near Death had come to all the Millions because of the trial they dared for my sake.

Yet my spirit was warned by the trouble of the Millions, and because the Master called vaguely with his brain-elements. I went more warily and looked all ways.

Then I stared above me, and there was a pale circle, quiet and steadfast, that went always over the twain of us as we moved—no glare, no blaze, but a gentle whitening like moonlight remembered from old tales. I knew it for one of those sweet Powers of Holiness, standing between our souls and some dread Power come near to work our destruction. I had no over-fear. I put trust in that Holiness and ran on, wary.

So I came within, mayhap, four hundred paces of the Circle. I thought I should yet win Mine Own safe and undelayed into its guard. But the Circle’s light burned dim, and fear bit me whether it could hold while the Earth-Current was weak. I ran swift and anxious, tasting in my mouth the dry dust of the Land and the bitter tang of fear like scorched grain.

In a dim place three beast-men rose from the earth as if the ground itself had kneaded them up, and they came at me growling. The first was so close I had no room to swing the Diskos; I beat his head in with the haft. I leaped aside, swung then, and fury made me chill and exact. The Maid was no more than a babe in the crook of my arm, her face turned in against my breast. I met the other two and cut quick and light with the great Weapon, with that cold judgement which comes when all tenderness is gathered into one point and made into a blade. I slew them as though they had been mice. They did not so much as touch me.

In that moment there came a great shout of wonder and welcome from within the Circle. I looked and began again to run. Men in grey armour stood all within the line—scores, hundreds, ranks upon ranks—and yet none came out to aid.

Then I knew why the Hundred Thousand had held off. Along the outer part of the Circle, in the dark beyond, were monstrous Black Mounds, rocking and swaying with a force of strange life. They were like great heaps of breathing tar, visible signs of monstrous Forces of Evil. Had any Human ventured beyond the Circle, he would have been destroyed in the spirit and lost utterly; so none dared, nor would sacrifice have helped, for dead men are no helpers.

From within the Circle came constant shouting: that I haste, and indeed to haste. I did haste with all strength left. I looked to the pale Holy Circle above us, and it went steadfast; and I knew we should surely be saved if only my feet held.

Now I was no more than an hundred paces from the glow. And in that instant brutal things came to destroy us. An herd of squat brutish men burst from the shadows where they had hid, swarming as rats swarm toward spilled grain. They caught at me and at the Maid, clawing to tear her from my arm.

For a moment it seemed they must succeed. I could not free myself and guard her and use the Diskos all at once. I kicked with my metal boots, struck out, turned all ways, wrenched free, and leaped back. The herd rushed after me, snorting and grunting.

Now I had space. Grimness filled me. I came round sudden and ran in among them, smiting. I hit swift right and left, to and fro, in a constant circling. The Diskos spun and roared, and its light made a strange sheen on their faces. They had tusks like pigs’ tusks, and their breath stank of sourness and old blood, as if they had fed on carrion and never known clean salt.

They struck me a thousand times with great stones. My armour rang; it burst anew in places; I near sickened under the blows and fresh hurts. Yet they could not reach the Maid, for I carried her above their squat reach, her head against my shoulder, her dear hair brushing my cheek like a memory of gentler days—of kitchens, of stored grains, of warm steams rising from pots while elders spoke in the old dialects that hold the taste of a people. Out here those dialects died in the throat, swallowed by the Night’s vast mockery; and still I bore her, as one bears a loaf from an oven through a storm.

The brute-men seemed without end. But I made always forward toward the Circle. The night in that place was full of fierce shouting from the Hundred Thousand. Many, as I learned after, tried to come to me, but comrades held them from so useless a dying.

I was scarce fifty paces from the Circle now and nigh to fall. I was dazed and wounded and ill with vast weariness. I had not slept but carried the Maid forever through days and nights and fought often. My legs moved as if another commanded them, and my arms burned as if full of hot stones.

The Hundred Thousand stood just within the Circle. Those to the front swung each man his Diskos and hurled them in among the herd that strove to slay me. The herd thinned before me. I gathered what strength remained, charged with despair, smote and smote without ceasing. Dead creatures lay about. I broke through with Mine Own and came upon the Circle.

I stepped over. The line gave scarce any resistance now, as though the guard were tired. A thousand hands came forward to help, yet none touched me; they drew back again with a little awe, as though something about me forbade their grasp. I stood there, rocking mayhap, the Diskos in my hand running blood to the haft.

I looked at them and they at me. My breath came in gasps. I tried to tell them I had need of the Doctors for Mine Own Maid—dying in my arms. In that moment, out in the night, came again the sound of giants running. Voices cried different matters at once: to aid me, to beware of the giants, to bring the Doctors instantly.

Others called that the Holy light was gone from above, and likewise the Black Mounds from without the Circle. There was monstrous roaring out in the Land. All came bewildered into my failing brain.

Also there was a constant noise from near and from upward, and I knew as in a dream it was the shouting of the great Millions—an eternal vague roaring-sound that came down from the upper heights, no more loud than a continual murmuring from lofty miles. In that sound I heard what no one tongue can hold: fear, hope, command, prayer—each untranslatable into any other, and each shaped by the old words the People still kept, as cooks keep seed-grain in jars for the spring. Yet here, at the threshold, even that vast human voice seemed thin against the Night’s appetite.

In a little minute I found my voice and asked a near man whether there were Doctors among them. Then came forward a Master of the Diskos, as a commander. He made the salute of honour and would have eased the Maid from me; but I asked again, slow, whether a Doctor was near. He gave an order at once, and the great thousands shaped themselves, making a mighty lane unto the Great Gateway of the Mighty Pyramid.

The Master signed to certain near, and they stood about me lest I fall, yet did not touch. I near choked with despair, fearing I had come too late, and the men seemed to regard me as strange.

Orders went swift this way and that. In a little while two big men of the Upward Cities came running, and between them they bore a little man upon a sling—a Master of the Doctors. He aided me gently to lay Mine Own Maid upon the earth. The Master of the Diskos signed, and the men near turned their backs. The Doctor examined her for life.

About that time there came a seeming of silence in the Land. The Hundred Thousand were utterly quiet. There was a great quiet within the Pyramid. All knew there was fear the Maid I had brought out of the night was slain by the Evil Forces.

Then the little Master Doctor looked up, quiet and piteous, and I knew in a moment that Mine Own Maid was dead.

He saw that I knew. He covered her face and stood up quickly. He called softly to the men behind me, signing some to support me and some to lift the Maid and bear her to the Great Gateway. He looked keen at me. I fought a little to breathe. Then I made with my hands that none should come near me, neither touch Mine Own. The Doctor understood that I would be strong until I died, and he beckoned the men away from me, and away from her.

After a little I stooped and lifted Mine Own Maid into my arms for that last journeying.

I went down the mighty lane of the Hundred Thousand in their grey armour. Each made silent salute with Diskos reversed as I passed. They were utterly silent. I scarce wotted of aught save that all the world was emptied and quiet; my task had failed; Mine Own lay dead in my arms.

Yet had it failed utterly? I had saved her from the terror of the Second Night Land. She had not come alone, driven mad to die. She died in my arms; and within her spirit she was comforted by my love about her. I thought vaguely, terribly, on an hundred sweet love actions she had shown me—little kindnesses as homely as setting a bowl of warm broth within reach, as holy as sharing the last of a precious store. And sudden I remembered with a dreadful pain how I had meant to wake and find Mine Own Maid kissing me in my sleep, and never had.

A madness of anguish flashed through the numbness; my sight dimmed, my steps went crooked. I felt, dimly, the Master Doctor steady my elbow for a moment; then he left me be when I had again some control.

As I drew nigh unto the Great Gateway, the lights of the Pyramid began to glow more strong, and the machinery of the lifts and the air pumps worked again, because the Earth-Current grew once more to natural strength. Now they had power to open the Great Gate by the great machines.

There came forth to meet me a number of the Masters of the Mighty Pyramid, and the dear Master Monstruwacan came before them all, eager as though he were my own father. He had heard vaguely there was fear for the Maid’s life.

Someone near the Gateway told him she was dead in my arms. He and all the Masters paused and stood silent for me to go by, reversing each his Diskos—an honour than which there is scarce any greater.

When I came back to life, it was as if I rose through a remembered shaft of darkness and sweetness, borne upward in the Lift, and laid again upon the same bed where I had thought—most surely—that no man ever needs to lie twice. For I had gone down into the Country of Silence, and in my heart had made my farewells to all common human rooms and linens and the small, homely sounds of being tended.

Yet I was there again, and I knew it in a way that was neither waking nor dreaming.

Out of the immense deeps under the world there rolled a thunder that was not thunder, but the Underground Organs—those vast throats of metal and air that the Great Redoubt keeps for its high rejoicings and its old remembrances. The sound came up through the bones and the blood, and it was as if a whole buried people were breathing music, slow and tidal. At whiles it was a chanting, as of multitudes beyond far mountains, and again it thinned to a far-blowing wind low in the Deep; and then, suddenly clear as a bell in a night kitchen, it shaped itself into that ancient melody which all knew—the Song of Honour.

Even with my eyes shut, and with my strength so scattered that I could not have lifted a finger by my will, I felt how the Millions made festival in the depths of their living. The world’s great body was singing. It was not mere noise, nor the harsh clamor of machines uncared-for, but ordered sound—sound husbanded and set to purpose, as grain is stored, as fire is banked. In that music there was a tenderness that kept the mind from breaking, and a majesty that made my small life seem part of a long chain of lives, joined link to link by love and memory. Yet all was faint and half hid from me, and my lids had no power to open; I drifted as upon strange waters of unrealness, rising and falling in a slow, indifferent sea.

But there were odours—true, domestic, persuading—that were altogether of reality. They came up to me with the Lift’s ascent through those great miles: the sweet, warm breath of the Fields where flowers grew for ever about the Lift-passages. It was not the thin scent that an empty word might carry, but thick and various: honey and green sap, bruised leaf, pollen like fine meal, and sometimes the sharp clean tang of stems newly cut. It set in my mind, without my willing, the old human knowledge that there are things which live, and can be gathered, and can be brought near to another to comfort them.

I think I moved a little; for I heard, close and careful, the voice of the Master Doctor, low and gentle, as one speaks in a sick-room where the least sharpness of sound might drive a nail through the head.

“Rest,” he said. “All is well with the Maid.”

Then I slipped again into haze. There were spaces that might have been days, in which I half lived and half slept, and wondered without trouble whether I were dead indeed. And after that there came days when I lay very quiet, empty of thought, as though the mind had been washed clean and left to dry. The Master Doctor would bend over me at this hour and that, looking keenly into my face, watching the slight signals of breath and pulse, measuring me as carefully as a food-historian measures the fading of an old receipt, knowing that a little lost detail can mean the difference between life and ruin.

At last, after strange spaces, another bent above me; and I knew, before I could truly see, by the change in the air—by the nearness of a familiar warmth, by a scent that was hers alone, clean as warm cloth and faintly flowered—that Mine Own was there.

Her dear and lovely face came down into my world, and the eyes spoke love into my soul; yet she was calm and hushed, as one who has learned the hard discipline of quiet in the presence of frailty. I began again to live in my body. My hands fumbled without skill, like a child’s; and she took them and held them, and life came from her into me, not as a thing said, but as a thing done. She was wordless and gentle; and contentment grew in me, thick and slow as good broth. Presently I fell into a natural slumber.

There came a day when I was allowed to rise. They that tended me carried me—very carefully, like men carrying a precious jar that has once been cracked—into one of the Quiet Gardens of the Pyramid. There they set me, and left me, so that the air could do its work upon my lungs and my blood, and so that my mind could begin to take hold of the world again by means of light, leaf, and the smallest honest sounds: water’s hush, the stir of insects, the soft friction of plant against plant.

I sat, weak and astonishingly grateful for the simplest things. Even the absence of harshness was a kind of music. In the Great Redoubt there is always power, always humming and ordered labour; yet here the sound was as it should be, not an industrial shriek, not a needless clatter to show the world that work is being done, but a living quiet—an acoustic ecology that let the heart steady itself.

Then One came round a bush and looked at me a moment, with a half shyness—only the love that shone in her eyes made the shyness a small thing indeed. Truly I knew it was Mine Own Maid; yet I had never seen Naani dressed pretty as a maid. She was more dainty than ever I had known—clothed in a simplicity that had been chosen with care, as one chooses a clean cloth for bread, or a bright bowl for fruit, not to boast, but to honour the eating.

Suddenly, with the foolish courage of a recovering man, I made as if to rise and go to her. She ran quick to me and stopped me with her hands, laughing softly without noise, and sat beside me. She took my head against her breast; and she did not deny me her lips. In that moment she was both maid and mother to me—lover and keeper—so that my weakness was not shame, but only a season like winter, endured together.

Afterward she had me be very still, and we sat in an utter dumb happiness. Words were not needed. In truth, there are things no lexicon can hold without bruising them; and even in our own tongue, some sweetness cannot be translated out of the body into speech. We were content to let the experience remain whole, unbroken by naming.

When the attendants came again, the Master of the Doctors was with them, and I saw there was satisfaction in his face, as of a man whose long, exact labour has at last found its right ending.

After that day I saw Mine Own every day, and I got better with a wondrous quickness; for Love mended me as food mends a starving man. Soon I was let go down into the Fields; yet we went by private ways, because the Multitudes would have followed me always, out of kindness and a natural curiosity. I needed quiet, and the Master Monstruwacan and the Master Doctor agreed upon it. They brought an Officer of Marriage, and we were wed very quietly and simply, for I was yet over-weak for any public thing.

Later—much later—there was a public marriage, and the Millions made us a Guard of Honour eight miles high, from the top to the bottom of the Mighty Pyramid, because they would not be denied the giving of honour. But that was after; and in the meanwhile the Maid was with me always, now my wife. Strength came upon me again day by day; and Mine Own, too, grew again into perfect health.

Thus we came into the Love Days, which are the most beauteous, if love be true.

We wandered the mighty Fields at our will, walking in the Love Paths that ran near to those places where villages were set, scattered through the green immensities as islands in a sea. I hid our name, lest we be beset by folk’s kindliness; for we needed to be utterly together and quiet, and there is a way that crowds, even in affection, can drown the mind as surely as any harsh engine can drown the ear.

We chose places for our slumber where the flowers were most wondrous, and where the night-sounds were mild and sane: the low breathing of the Earth, the far pulse of the Redoubt’s life, the small conversations of living things. We carried some food with us, but also we ate when we came to the villages; for the Fields were so huge as Countries, and the villages each had their own manner of making do, their own little hoards and traditions of taste. In those meals I felt, as in a history written not in ink but in scent and salt, how each community kept memory alive—how a certain herb, a certain way of drying or preserving, could hold a fragment of the old world as safely as any record in a vault.

Mine Own made good her promise a hundred times over. She would prepare me a great and hearty meal with her own hands, and do it with a craft that was both loving and exact. She knew how to make the simplest provision into comfort: warming bread so the crust spoke softly under the knife; stirring a thick stew until it shone; adding a pinch of dried leaf at the last moment so the scent rose up bright and clean, as though a whole summer had been stored in a jar. She would tease me utterly that I was a glutton as I ate, and kiss me so I had no chance to speak in my own defence. The taste of her cooking, the nearness of her laughter held low so it did not jar the quiet, the way she watched to see if I tired—these were the true riches of that time.

Truly, she was all my heart and spirit desired. She companioned me with love, and entered my spirit into joy.

Once we went down into the Country of Silence; but we did not stay long then, because my Memory returned upon me—sharp and heavy. Yet afterward, in later times, we wandered there oft with memory, and with a holiness of great thinkings, and with that love which holds all. For the place was dreadful, yet it had a solemnity like an old ruin where the tongue that once named everything has died, leaving only shapes and echoes. In such a place a man feels how much of experience is untranslatable, how words—any words—are only a thin net cast over deep water.

As we left that Country I told Mine Own how, when she had been suspended of her life by the horrid Force of the House, I had minded me with dreadful pain that I had never waked to discover her kissing me when I slept. She blushed most lovely, for she had never known I was aware of her sweet naughtiness; and then her thought turned wholly to my agony, when she was dead—so I had thought—until the vapour of life of the Earth-Force set her spirit free of the Silence. She came to me in dear understanding, and spoke as simply as one tells of weather endured.

She told me the Doctors said she had been, as it were, stunned and frozen of the spirit—her being and life suspended. Then the great life-force of the Earth-Current had waked her spirit, and her body began to live, and her blood to flow proper again. The Doctors had talked and searched much of late in the old records of their work, and found note of one such happening in the olden time; but truly nothing of the sort had been through a mighty age of years. Thus her living was both miracle and rarity, like a seed found in an ancient store, still able to sprout—astonishing, and yet made of common matter.

While we wandered and rested in the Fields, I often told Mine Own this matter and that—how things had been, how they had seemed, what I remembered of the House and the journey and the darkness. I knew she had learned somewhat of odd things while I lay between worlds; but not overmuch, for she also had been utterly alack. She had come from her own bed to sit by mine even when she had scarce power to her feet; for the Master Doctor ordained it, fearing I would truly die if he did not awaken my spirit by the presence of my love.

Think upon the deepness of my love, when I knew she had held my hands so brave and gentle, with her strength near spent. I said a little holy praise of Mine Own then and at other times, though I knew praise is a poor vessel for such a thing.

So I come near my ending, and have but one more matter to tell, which happened a while later, after Mine Own and I had gone through the second marriage, which was the public marriage.

One day my wife—Mine Own—took me with a sweet cunning unto the Hall of Honour. When I came there I saw many of the Peoples in that great hall, standing about in silence, as if they meant to do nothing, and yet waited upon something. Their quiet was not empty; it was the kind of gathered hush that respects feeling, the opposite of the loud, thoughtless noise by which lesser folk in other ages might have tried to buy importance for themselves.

My wife went forward with me to the centre place of the Hall; and suddenly I saw why she had brought me so cunning-sweet. There, in the midst, in the Place of Honour, stood a statue of a man in broken armour, carrying a maid for ever.

I was dumb.

In this age you may not know the weight of that honour; for it was given only to the Great Dead. Yet I was but a young man, and utterly far from greatness, save that I loved with all my heart and all my spirit; and before such love, death is but a small thing, and fear a smaller. I stood there, humbled and wondering, with a natural pride that any should think to honour me so. Mine Own wept beside me with joy and an honest pride of her man.

There was an utter silence of dear sympathy through all the great Hall of Honour. They that were there let me go in quietness with Mine Own, which was a lovely thing of understanding; and the sound we made as we went—footfalls soft, cloth whispering—seemed part of the hall’s reverence, not an interruption.

I went loving and thoughtful with my wife, and she was very nigh to me. I had gained honour; yet I learned that honour is but as the ash of life, if one has not love. And I had love.

To have love is to have all; for that which is truly LOVE mothers honour and faithfulness, and those three build the House of Joy.
