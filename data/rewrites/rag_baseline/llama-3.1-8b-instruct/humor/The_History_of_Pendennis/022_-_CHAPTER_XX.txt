As Major Pendennis arrived at Oxbridge, a convergence of intellectual and social forces became apparent. It was here that he encountered Horace Bloundell, a gentleman of discerning taste and refined sensibilities, who had previously attended the University of Camford, but found himself at odds with the academic establishment. A scion of the esteemed Bloundell-Bloundell family, Horace's decision to leave Camford was precipitated by his disagreements with the tutors, which ultimately led him to seek refuge at Boniface, where Dr. Donne, the Master, was a distant relative and an ardent supporter of the Suffolk family.

Upon learning of Horace's ancestral ties to the Bloundell-Bloundell estate, Dr. Donne extended an offer of hospitality, which Horace accepted, and soon found himself ensconced among the collegiate ranks of Boniface. However, the circumstances surrounding his arrival were not without controversy, as several colleges had initially declined to admit him due to concerns about his reputation. Undeterred, Horace resolved to secure a living within the family, a goal that became all the more pressing following the untimely death of his brother, who had been the original intended recipient of the benefice.

Horace's decision to forsake the military life of a dragoon for the clerical garb of an English divine was motivated by a desire to establish a stable and respectable career, free from the vicissitudes of war. Though the setbacks he experienced at Camford momentarily derailed his plans, Horace remained resolute and set his sights on acquiring the living, which would provide him with a secure foothold in the ecclesiastical hierarchy.

During his second year at university, Major Pendennis paid a brief visit to his nephew, Pen, introducing him to a circle of acquaintances who would come to play significant roles in their lives. Among these individuals were Lord Plinlimmon, a paragon of courtesy and refinement; Magnus Charters, a gallant and open-hearted companion; Harland, a witty and perceptive observer of human nature; Ringwood, known affectionately as Rupert in the Union Debating Club, for his bold opinions and unorthodox approach to argumentation; and Broadbent, a Bristolian of dissenting background, renowned for his republican convictions and his formidable debating skills.
