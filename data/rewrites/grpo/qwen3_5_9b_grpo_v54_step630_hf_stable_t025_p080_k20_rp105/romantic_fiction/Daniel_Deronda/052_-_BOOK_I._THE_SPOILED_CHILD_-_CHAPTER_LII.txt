The next morning, among the letters awaiting Daniel Deronda, one stood out: a missive from Hans Meyrick, spanning four quarto pages in the family's distinctive, elegant script.

"My Dear Deronda," Hans began, "in return for your insightful sketch of Italian movements and your broader view of world affairs, I must report that here at home, the most prudent opinion is simply that 'time will show.' Regarding the causes of past effects, we now see that the recent swindling telegrams explain last year's cattle plague—a refutation of false philosophy and a justification for farmer compensation. My own theory, that a murrain will soon strike the commercial class and reveal itself through the sudden sale of rejected paintings, has been dismissed as unsound analogy. Yet, there are minds willing to rob even the neglected painter of his solace. To my feeling, there is great beauty in the idea that a bad judge might prize my *Berenice* series highly, punishing the city men for my ill-merited luck.

Meanwhile, I console myself for your absence by finding advantage in it—shining like Hesperus when Hyperion has departed. I sit with our Hebrew prophet, studying his head during the hours he used to spend with you, gaining credit as a learned young Gentile who would have been a Jew if he could. I agree with him in the general principle that whatever is best is therefore Jewish. Though I am not a severe reasoner, I see the logic: if whatever is best is A, and B happens to be best, B must be A. On this principle, I could see the force of a pamphlet proving all good art was Protestant. However, our prophet is an uncommonly interesting sitter—a better model than Rembrandt had for his Rabbi. I never leave him without a new discovery. It is a constant wonder that, with all his fiery feeling for his race, he is no straight-laced Jew, spitting after the word 'Christian' and enjoying the prospect of the Gentile mouth watering in vain for roasted Leviathan while Israel sends up plates ad libitum. (You perceive my studies taught me what to expect from the orthodox Jew.) I confess I have always held lightly to your account of Mordecai as merely apologetic, part of your disposition to make an antediluvian point of view lest you do injustice to the megatherium. But now, hearing him in his proper person, I find him a philosophical-allegorical-mystical believer with a sharp dialectic point, capable of pricking any argumentative rattler of peas in a bladder. This mixture may be a Jewish prerogative. His mind is so broad that my own correct opinions lie in it comfortably; how they agree with the vast remainder is his affair, not mine. I leave it to him to settle our basis, never yet seeing one that isn't a world-supporting elephant, expensive to keep. My means won't allow a private elephant, so I go into mystery instead—a cheaper, lasting gas supplied by the decomposition of elephants. If I like the look of an opinion, I treat it civilly. I have a friendly feeling toward Mordecai's notion that a whole Christian is three-fourths a Jew, and that since Alexandrian times, the most comprehensive minds have been Jewish. I intend to point out to Mirah that, aside from Arabic incidents, there is little difference between me and Maimonides.

But lately, I've found that your shallow lover can't help making a declaration. If Mirah's ways were less distracting, and it were less of a heaven to be in her presence, I must long ago have flung myself at her feet, requesting her to tell me, with less indirectness, whether she wished me to blow my brains out. I have a knack of hoping, as good as an estate in reversion, provided one keeps from the temptation of turning it into certainty, which may spoil all. My Hope wanders among orchard blossoms, feels warm snow falling on it through sunshine, and doubts nothing. But catching sight of Certainty in the distance, it sees an ugly Janus-faced deity with a dubious wink and turns quickly away. You, with your supreme reasonableness and self-nullification, know nothing about Hope, that immortal, delicious maiden forever courted, whom fools call deceitful, as if Hope carried the cup of disappointment. It is her deadly enemy, Certainty, whom she only escapes by transformation. (You observe my new vein of allegory?) Seriously, however, truth will prevail, prejudice will melt before it, diversity accompanied by merit will make itself felt as fascination, and no virtuous aspiration will be frustrated—all doctrines of the schools implying that the Jewess I prefer will prefer me. Any blockhead cites generalities, but the mind-master discerns the particular cases they represent.

I am less convinced that my society makes amends to Mordecai for your absence, but another substitute occasionally arrives: Jacob Cohen. It is worth catching our prophet's expression when he has that remarkable young Israel on his knee, pouring forth Semitic inspiration with a sublime look of melancholy patience. Sometimes Jacob thinks Hebrew would be more edifying if he stopped his ears with his palms, imitating the muffled sounds. When Mordecai gently draws down the little fists and holds them fast, Jacob's features take on extraordinary activity, as if walking through a menagerie, succeeding best with the owl and the peccary. He treats me with familiar ease, looking at me as a second-hand Christian commodity likely to come down in price, remarking on my disadvantages with frankness implying thoughts of future purchase. It is pretty to see the change in him if Mirah enters. He turns child suddenly—his age usually strikes one as near forty, yet with an air of recent production. With Mirah, he reminds me of dogs brought up by women, manageable only by them. Still, the dog is fond of Mordecai, bringing sugar-plums to share, filling his own mouth embarrassingly while watching how Mordecai deals with a smaller supply. Judging from this modern Jacob at six, I am astonished that his race hasn't bought us all up long ago, pocketing our feebler generations as slave property. There is one Jewess I should not mind being slave to. But I wish I didn't imagine Mirah gets a little sadder, trying all the while to hide it. It is natural enough while she watches the slow death of this brother, whom she worships with looks of loving devoutness that make me wish myself in his place.

For the rest, we are a little merrier than usual. Rex Gascoigne—remember the head you admired among my sketches, a fellow with a good upper lip reading law—now has rooms in town, with a neat sister (also with a good upper lip) staying with him. I introduced them to my mother and the girls, who found out from Miss Gascoigne that she is cousin to your Vandyke Duchess!!! I put the notes of exclamation to mark the surprise this produced on my feeble understanding. On reflection, I discovered there was no ground for surprise unless I had believed nobody could be anybody's cousin without my knowing it. This sort of surprise depends on a liveliness of the spine with a constant nullity of brain. There was a fellow I used to meet in Rome, in an effervescence of surprise at the simplest information. Tell him you were fond of easy boots; he would always say, "No! are you?" with the same energy of wonder. The very fellow of whom pastoral Browne wrote prophetically: "A wretch so empty that if e'er there be / In nature found the least vacuity / 'Twill be in him." I have accounted for it all—he had a lively spine.

However, this cousinship with the Duchess came out by chance one day Mirah was with them, talking about the Mallingers. Apropos: I am getting so important that I have rival invitations. Gascoigne wants me to go to his father's rectory in August, but I think self-interest will take me to Topping Abbey, for Sir Hugo has invited me and proposes—God bless him for his rashness!—that I paint his three daughters sitting on a bank in the Gainsborough style. He came to my studio recently and recommended I apply myself to portraiture. Of course I know what that means. "My good fellow, your attempts at the historic and poetic are simply pitiable. Your brush is just that of a successful portrait-painter—it has a little truth and a great facility in falsehood—your idealism will never do for gods and goddesses, but it may fetch a high price as flattery. Fate has made you the hinder wheel—run behind, because you can't help it." What great effort it costs our friends to give us these candid opinions! I have even known a man call to tell me I had irretrievably exposed my want of judgment, and if I had asked him, we would have lent him his own judgment. Such was my ingratitude that even while he spoke, I inwardly sketched a Last Judgment with that candid friend's physiognomy on the left. But all this is away from Sir Hugo, whose manner of implying one's gifts aren't of the highest order is so good-natured and comfortable that I begin to feel it an advantage not to be among those poor fellows at the tip-top. His kindness tastes better because it comes out of his love for you, old boy. His chat is amusing. By the way, he told me your Vandyke Duchess is gone yachting to the Mediterranean. I bethink me it is possible to land from a yacht or be taken on board. Shall you have an opportunity of continuing your theological discussion with the fair Supralapsarian? Is Duke Alphonso also theological?—perhaps an Arian who objects to triplicity? (Stage direction: While D. is reading, a profound scorn gathers in his face till at the last word he flings down the letter, grasps his coat-collar in a statuesque attitude and remains with a look generally tremendous throughout the following soliloquy, "O night, O blackness, etc., etc.")

Excuse the brevity. You are not used to more from me than a bare statement of facts. One fact I omitted: the Klesmers on the eve of departure behaved magnificently, shining forth as planets of genius and fortune in conjunction. Mirah is rich with their oriental gifts.

What luck it will be if you come back and present yourself at the Abbey while I am there! I shall behave with consummate discretion and win golden opinions. But I shall run up to town now and then for a peep into Gad Eden. You see how far I have got in Hebrew lore—up with my Lord Bolingbroke, who knew no Hebrew but "understood that sort of learning." If Mirah commanded, I would go to a depth below the tri-literal roots. Already it makes no difference to me whether the points are there or not. But while her brother's life lasts, I suspect she wouldn't listen to a lover, even one whose "hair is like a flock of goats on Mount Gilead"—and I flatter myself few heads would bear that comparison better than mine. So I stay with my hope among the orchard-blossoms.—Your devoted,

Some months prior, this letter from Hans would have divided Deronda's thoughts irritably; its romancing about Mirah would have had an unpleasant edge, scarcely anointed with commiseration for his friend's probable disappointment. But things had altered since March. Mirah was no longer so critically placed with regard to the Meyricks, and Deronda's own position had undergone a change crowned by the revelation of his birth. The new opening toward the future, though he would not trust in definite visions, inevitably shed new lights, influencing his mood toward past and present. Hence, what Hans called his hope now seemed to Deronda not a mischievous unreasonableness, but an unusually persistent bird-dance of an extravagant fancy. He would have felt quite able to pity any consequent suffering of his friend's, if he had believed it probable. But some of the busy thoughts filling that long day, which passed without a new summons from his mother, were given to the argument that Hans Meyrick's nature was not one in which love could strike deep roots that turn disappointment into sorrow. It was too restless, too readily excitable by novelty, too ready to turn itself into imaginative material, wearing its grief as a fantastic costume. "Already he is beginning to play at love: he is taking the whole affair as a comedy," Deronda said to himself. "He knows very well there is no chance for him. Just like him—never opening his eyes on any possible objection I could have to receive his outpourings about Mirah. Poor old Hans! If we were under a fiery hail together he would howl like a Greek, and if I did not howl too, it would never occur to him that I was as badly off as he. And yet he is tender-hearted and affectionate in intention, and I can't say he is not active in imagining what goes on in other people—but then he always imagines it to fit his own inclination."

With this touch of causticity, Deronda got rid of the slight heat currently raised by Hans's naive expansiveness. The nonsense about Gwendolen, conveying the fact that she was gone yachting with her husband, only suggested a disturbing sequel to his own strange parting with her. But there was one sentence in the letter which raised a more immediate, active anxiety. Hans's suspicion of a hidden sadness in Mirah was not in the direction of Deronda's wishes, and hence, instead of distrusting his observation, Deronda began to conceive a cause for the sadness. Was it some event that had occurred during his absence, or only the growing fear of some event? Was it something, perhaps alterable, in the new position made for her? Or—had Mordecai, against his habitual resolve, communicated to her those peculiar cherished hopes about him, Deronda, and had her quickly sensitive nature been hurt by the discovery that her brother's will or tenacity of visionary conviction had acted coercively on their friendship?
