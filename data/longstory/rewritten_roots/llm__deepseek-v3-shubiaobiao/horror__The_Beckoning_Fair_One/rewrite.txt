The "To Let" signs had leaned against the low fence for so long that the residents of the cramped triangular square couldn’t recall a time they hadn’t been there. They sagged now at jagged angles, like a row of rusted axes waiting to drop—yet never quite claiming a victim. Not that there were many victims to claim. The square was a forgotten pocket of the city, choked off by a tangle of alleys and tenements that had crept in over the years, leaving the old house stranded, a relic awaiting demolition.

It was a house of crumbling red brick, its façade adorned with the faded emblems of long-dead insurance firms—crowns, clasped hands, symbols of security that now seemed like grim jokes. The children of the square had swung on the broken gate until only the top bar remained, and the narrow alley leading to the entrance was scarred by the scrawls of vagrants on the boarded-up basement windows. Rain had warped the path, and the musky scent of animals clung to the damp stone.

For six months, Oleron had passed the place without a second glance, always taking the opposite side of the square. But one morning, by chance or some quiet pull of fate, he found himself pausing before one of the tilting signs. The agent’s name was still legible, along with a note—yellowed with age—that the key could be collected at Number Six.

Oleron hesitated. His current lodgings were already a strain on his meager earnings, and the constant division between his bedroom and workroom was a persistent irritation. The thought of a single, undisturbed space was tempting. He studied the house again—the blind, grime-coated windows, the sagging roof—then crossed the square to Number Six.

The door was ajar. He knocked. No answer. He knocked again, louder.

A gaunt man with a long nose finally appeared, chewing as if interrupted mid-meal. "I was saying grace," he muttered, as though Oleron had committed some sacrilege.

Oleron asked for the key.

"Lost," the man said flatly. "But you won’t need it. The door’s unlocked. A good shove’ll open any of the others." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "I’m the agent, if you’re thinking of taking it."

Oleron returned to the house. The entrance yawned wide, the steps down to the cellar gaping like a throat. The staircase ahead was broad, its carved banister thick with dust. He climbed, avoiding contact with the walls, and stopped at the first landing. One door was boarded shut; the other gave way with a groan.

The rooms were empty, thick with silence. He walked through them slowly, running a hand along the walls, testing the floorboards. Then, without venturing higher, he left.

The agent named a rent so low it was almost suspicious. Oleron inquired about taking just a single floor.

The man shrugged. "You’d have to ask the solicitors. Lincoln’s Inn. Mention my name—Barrett."

The lawyers, when Oleron visited, seemed baffled by his interest in only part of the house. They offered him the entire property for a paltry sum, as if eager to be rid of it. It took time to convince them he wanted no such burden. Eventually, they agreed—provided the "To Let" signs remained, and provided he understood that if the whole house was taken, his tenancy would end without warning.

Oleron barely considered it a risk.

The next day, he returned, pacing every room, imagining the space as his own. Stripped of grime, repainted, furnished with the pieces he had stored away—it could be perfect. He visited the warehouse where his grandmother’s things had been kept, measuring, remembering. Then he arranged for the work to begin.

Two weeks later, the first floor gleamed in pale, ghostly white. Oleron moved in, polishing wood, arranging chairs, positioning the china cupboard with its delicate, lattice-paned doors. The ceilings arched high above him, faint stars etched into the plaster. The fireplace’s ironwork curled like blackened lace.

"Charming," he murmured to himself, wandering from room to room. "Absolutely charming."

He installed a lock on his door, sealing off his sanctuary from the rest of the house. Now, if he wanted a book in the night, it was only steps away.

Late passersby in the square might have glimpsed the glow behind his red blinds—or seen the sudden shift of light as he moved through the rooms, candle in hand, settling into his new home.

And perhaps, if they had watched long enough, they might have wondered why the shadows in the upper windows seemed to shift as well, though no one lived there.

Yet.

Paul Oleron measured his life by the slow, creeping progress of his pen. At forty-four, he had long abandoned the notion of balancing scales—his art demanded too much to leave room for such trifles. The world had not been kind, but he repaid it in prose, weaving tales that shimmered with a nobility he still, stubbornly, believed in.

His new home—a relic of another century, its bricks scarred by old insurance marks—was meant to be a sanctuary. Yet even as he unpacked his few belongings, arranging them with the precision of a man accustomed to transience, he felt the weight of something unseen. The house breathed around him, its silence thick with withheld secrets.

The closet in the kitchen was the first to betray itself. Its door bore a curious hatch, once used for powdering wigs, now a grotesque little mouth. Oleron hesitated before prying it open, his fingers brushing against the warped wood. Inside, he found nothing but dust and two forgotten wig stands, their mushroom shapes looming like severed heads in the dim light. He laughed, but the sound withered in his throat.

Work should have been his refuge. *Romilly Bishop*, his half-finished novel, lay waiting, her voice murmuring just beyond reach. But the house would not let him listen. The noises of the square outside—hawkers, schoolchildren, the tinny strum of a mandolin—pressed in, relentless. And then there were the whispers of the rooms themselves. He caught himself staring at blank walls, wondering what had once hung there, what eyes had watched from those now-empty corners.

The window seats, he discovered, were false. Beneath layers of paint, his chisel exposed hinges, rusted shut. The first two were empty, gaping like toothless mouths. The third yielded something soft, folded, heavy with age. Shaking it free of dust, Oleron found himself holding a triangular bag of frieze, its crimson fabric faded to the color of old blood. Straps dangled from it, limp and useless.

He did not know what it was. But as he stuffed it into a corner of the kitchen, he felt the house exhale around him, as if it had been waiting for him to find it.

That night, he dreamed of *Romilly*. She stood in the doorway of his bedroom, her face veiled, her hands clutching a bundle wrapped in stained cloth. When he woke, the first light of dawn was creeping through the curtains, and the air smelled faintly of powder and decay.

The house was awake. And so, he realized with a slow, creeping dread, was something else.

It nettled Oleron that Miss Bengough barely spared a glance for the flat he had found so strangely enchanting. But then, Elsie had always been like that—indifferent to charm, careless of aesthetics, more herself gnawing biscuits from a crumpled paper bag than dining with any pretense of decorum. At thirty-four, she was a journalist of some success, brash and florid, pink as a peony, with a habit of expansive gestures and sudden, moist exclamations. She earned more than Oleron did, and through cunningly disguised endorsements, she acquired most of her flamboyant wardrobe. She moved in a whirl of veils and scarves, leaving behind gusts of perfumed air.

When she arrived at his door a month into his tenancy, her skirts rustled like dry leaves on the staircase. A single sharp rap announced her. She barged in, trailing the scent of the city, and tossed a bundle of ladies’ journals onto a chair.

“Don’t stop on my account,” she said around a mouthful of hatpins as she stripped off her hat and veil. “Wasn’t sure if you’d settled in yet, so I brought sandwiches. You’ve got coffee, I assume? No, stay put—I’ll find the kitchen—”

“No need, I’ll clear this away. Truthfully, I’m glad for the interruption,” Oleron admitted, gathering his scattered pages.

She was already filling the kettle. Ten minutes later, they sat with coffee and sandwiches between them.

“Well, what do you think of the place?” Oleron asked as she poured.

She gave a short hum. “Feels like you’re preparing for a wife, Paul.”

He chuckled. “Hardly. But it’s an improvement, don’t you think?”

“Is it? Suppose so. I liked the last one, black ceiling and all. How’s *Romilly*?”

Oleron rubbed his jaw. “Ah. Not proud to admit it, but I’ve hit a snag. Still, it’ll come right in the end, as you always say.”

“Stuck?”

“Thoroughly.”

“Anything you’d care to read to me?”

It was their old habit—Oleron sharing fragments of his work, Elsie offering blunt, practical feedback. She, in turn, never spoke of her own writing. His was *real* work, she insisted; hers merely filled columns.

“Afraid not,” he said, still stroking his chin. Then, with rare candor: “Truth is, Elsie, I haven’t written—actually written—much more. Doesn’t mean I haven’t progressed. In some ways, I’ve gone too far. I’m thinking of tearing it all apart.”

Elsie gasped. “*Tearing it apart?*”

“Rebuilding Romilly entirely. She’s not working anymore. I’ve lost interest in her as she is.”

“But—” Elsie sputtered, “she was *alive*, Paul! You made her real!”

He smiled faintly. Of course Elsie liked Romilly as she was—there was too much of *her* in the character.

“Are you serious?” she demanded, eyes wide.

“Quite.”

“You’d scrap fifteen chapters?”

“Not lightly. Only if it served something better.”

“Even the love scene? The shore description?”

“They wouldn’t be wasted,” he said, uneasy now.

Elsie flung up her hands. “You’re impossible! Must you always hover above mere mortals? It wouldn’t *lower* you to write something humans can grasp! To destroy those chapters would be criminal—*wasted* talent! Think practically. You’ve worked twenty years; you’re nearly there. You’re running out of money, and now you want to chuck the one thing that might make your name for some indulgent experiment? You infuriate me!”

Oleron shook his head. This was an old argument. Elsie was a splendid friend—until she hit the limits of her understanding. She saw genius as a measurable ingredient, something to be added or subtracted at will. She didn’t grasp that it was the *essence*, the *core*. They diverged there. He knew it. She didn’t.

“Yes, yes,” he sighed. “Practically, you’re right. If I handed *Romilly* to you, you’d make a success of her. But I can’t, and I’m beginning to doubt she’s worth my time.”

“What does *that* mean?”

“When something isn’t worth doing,” he said with a thin smile, “you don’t do it.”

Elsie’s gaze shot to the ceiling, as if begging patience. “Rubbish! Last I saw you, *Romilly* was pouring out—four chapters a week! If you hadn’t moved, she’d be three-quarters done. Why uproot in the middle of your most important work?”

He offered excuses—inconveniences, mostly—but she dismissed them. Perhaps she guessed the truth: he was sick of his shabby existence. Twenty years of garrets and dingy rooms had worn him down. The reward, if it existed, felt hollow now.

“At any rate,” he said, “I’m happier here than I’ve been in years. That counts for something.”

“And doing no work,” Elsie retorted.

A spark of irritation flared in him. “Why must I do nothing *but* work? What joy has it brought me? I love it when it’s *done*, but the doing is torture. Once in a blue moon, there’s a flicker of the old thrill. Now I’m forty-four, and it’s drudgery. No one wants it. *I* don’t want it. If any sane man asked if I was a fool to continue, I’d agree.”

Elsie’s face grew solemn. “You knew that long ago, Paul—and you chose it anyway.”

“Did I? Or was I told so? Youth *thinks* it knows. Then one day you wake at forty-four—”

“Forty-four,” she corrected.

“—and realize the glamour was behind you all along. A costly choice, youth.”

Her eyes dropped. “You don’t regret it?”

“Lately? I *do*. What do I have to show for it?”

“You know what you have.”

Her tone betrayed what else he might have had—her. Had he ever asked, she would have said, simply, *When?* He never had.

“Yours is the real work,” she said softly. “Without you, the rest of us couldn’t exist. You carry it all.”

For a moment, silence hung between them. Then Oleron laughed abruptly, chiding himself for the self-pity.

“Sorry you caught me like this, Elsie,” he said, stacking the tray. “Let’s take a walk.”

As they toured the flat, she paused at the kitchen, eyeing the faded red fabric.

“What’s this?”

“Ah. I was hoping *you* could tell *me*.” He recounted its discovery in the window seat.

“It’s harp wrapping,” she said. “For packing into its case.”

“Of course,” he murmured. “I couldn’t place it.”

Back in the sitting room, she asked, “Who lives in the rest of the house?”

“Tramps in the cellar, perhaps. No one else.”

She studied him. “I’ll tell you what I think.”

“Go on.”

“You’ll never work here.”

His brows rose. “Why not?”

“You won’t finish *Romilly* here. I don’t know why, but you won’t. You’ll leave before it’s done.”

He mused, then smirked. “Isn’t that a little… superstitious?”

“Completely. No logic to it. But there it is.”

He fetched his coat, chuckling. “Then I hope you’re wrong. If *Romilly* isn’t out by autumn, I’ll be in trouble.”

Oleron sat by the fire, the embers casting long, trembling shadows across the walls. Miss Bengough’s words lingered in the air like an unwelcome specter—*You’ll never finish Romilly here.* Absurd, of course. Superstitious nonsense. And yet, the seed had been planted, and now it festered in his mind, an infection of doubt.

He scoffed aloud, as if the room itself might argue with him. The firelight softened every edge, painting the space in warm, forgiving hues. The chintz curtains, with their faded floral patterns, hung motionless. The books lining the shelves gleamed like old bones in the dim glow. No, this place was not his enemy. If anything, it was *she* who had been out of place—Elsie, with her overwhelming presence, her too-vivid flesh, her suffocating certainty.

A grim realization settled over him. He had never noticed before how *wrong* she was for this room—for *him*. The thought slithered into his mind before he could stop it: she was too much. Too loud, too insistent, too *alive* in all the worst ways. And if Elsie was too much, then what of *Romilly*, her fictional echo?

His stomach turned.

He reached for the manuscript, the fifteen wretched chapters that had poured from him like pus from a wound. *Romilly Bishop*—his grand vision, his Beatrice—was nothing more than a garish, lumbering thing, coarse in thought and heavy in form. The realization struck him like a blow. How had he not seen it before? She was unbearable. Unfit. A grotesque.

The fire crackled, mocking him.

He should burn it. Every page. Let the whole wretched creation turn to ash.

But his fingers tightened on the stack instead. Somewhere in the house, water dripped—a faint, irregular plinking, like a child’s fingernails tapping glass. It lulled him, that sound. A strange little melody hidden in the drops. He found himself listening, waiting for the next note, the next whisper of movement.

His eyelids grew heavy. The fire dimmed.

When he woke, the room was cold, the candles guttering in their sticks. The sound was still there—*plink, plunk*—distant but insistent. He rose, numb, and stumbled to bed.

Morning came, and with it, Mrs. Barrett’s soft rap at the wall. He called her in, barely awake, barely human.

“Dear me,” she murmured, hesitating at the threshold. “That’s an old tune you were humming, Mr. Oleron. Haven’t heard it in forty years, I shouldn’t think.”

He froze.

“What tune?”

“‘The Beckoning Fair One,’” she said, and the name slithered between them like a secret.

Oleron’s blood ran cold.

He had not been humming.

At least, he didn’t *remember* humming.

But the dripping tap had been.

Oleron understood. The postcard Elsie had promised would never come now—because she knew. She knew, at last, that he did not want her.

Yet she came anyway.

A week had not passed before her unannounced knock rattled the door. Her voice, strained and thin, carried from the landing. She would not stay, she insisted. It took pleading to draw her inside.

Her excuse was flimsy—some market for short stories he might consider. He thanked her, hollowly. Then, duty done, she was already retreating, eager to escape. Oleron did not stop her. Even he could see through the lie. He followed her down the stairs.

But the house had other plans for Elsie Bengough.

A sharp crack—splintering wood—a choked cry. Her foot had punched through a stair. Oleron moved swiftly, alarm tightening his throat.

Her laughter was frayed at the edges. *"Too heavy, that’s all. Getting fat—"*

He dropped to his knees, fingers scraping at broken edges. The wood was rotten, though he’d passed over it a hundred times without incident. Her boot was torn; beneath, the skin would be raw.

*"I’ll be too much for anything soon,"* she muttered, half-sobbing, half-laughing.

She refused to climb back up. Refused to let him see the damage.

*"No—just let me go. Let me leave. I’m not—"* Her voice fractured. *"I’m not wanted here."*

The words struck like a slap. His head jerked back.

Her hand cut the air between them, sharp as a blade.

*"Don’t. Not like that. Not some half-hearted kindness. If it can’t be real, then let me keep what little pride I have left."*

What could he say? He didn’t love her.

*"Let me go,"* she whispered. *"Let me take what’s left of me out of this place."*

He offered his coat, a doctor, his arm—all refused. She limped away, past the jagged *"To Let"* signs, as if the very boards might lash out again if she lingered.

---

Night fell. Oleron did not eat. He paced, haunted by her voice—

If only he could force himself to love her. But it was impossible. She would have to stay away now. For her sake, he would make sure of it.

Yet as darkness thickened, his thoughts twisted. Where was she now? Staring at black water? Testing the weight of a rope? His mind conjured inquests, Barrett’s testimony, the wound on her hand, the ruined ankle—

No. Morbid fantasy. He forced his thoughts elsewhere.

The house creaked around him, alive with whispers.

Then—a sound.

Soft. Persistent. A slow, silken drag, faint as a breath.

At first, he dismissed it. But it returned, again and again, growing louder, more deliberate.

His body went rigid.

He knew that sound.

He was on his feet before he realized it, chair crashing behind him.

The rustling came once more—unmistakable now.

A woman’s hair being brushed.

His breath locked in his throat. He stumbled back, hands groping for the wall.

Silence.

Then—movement. A skittering? A mouse, surely. But the other sound—that other *thing*—was gone.

He shouted into the dark.

No answer. Only his own voice, trembling back at him.

Matches. He needed light. His hands shook as he fumbled along the mantel, sending a box clattering to the floor. The candle flared.

The room was empty.

But it wasn’t.

Something had been here. Something *was* here.

Oleron had once laughed at the idea of the house shaping its own ghost, weaving itself into a haunting. But now he knew—the past had already done the work.

The tenant was not just him.

And tonight, it had announced itself.

With the sound of a woman, unseen, brushing her hair in the dark.

Oleron did not remember choosing to leave, yet suddenly he was there—striding over the makeshift plank on the broken stair, descending into the gloom below. His head was bare, his movements stiff, automatic. Only later would fragments return to him: the candle still burning on the table, the door opened just wide enough to let him slip through, then closed again with unnatural quiet.

At the foot of the stairs, another shock. Something darted from the cellar shadows—a streak of movement, gone before he could blink. Just a cat, but the sight wrung a choked sob from him, thin and childish. He staggered out into the night, pausing beneath the *To Let* sign, fingers plucking absently at his lip as he glanced back at the dim red glow behind his window. Then, with another uneasy look over his shoulder, he stumbled up the square.

There was a pub around the corner—a place Oleron had never entered before. Tonight, he did. His hand shook as he slapped a shilling onto the counter; it missed, clattering to the floor.

"B-brandy," he muttered, bending to retrieve the coin.

The bar was nearly empty. A few laborers and tradesmen murmured in the far room, but here, Oleron sat alone on a hard settee, the sawdust grit beneath his shoes. He drank the first glass in two swallows, then ordered another. The liquor burned, but he welcomed it. He would not go back. Not tonight. Not to that room with its waiting candle, its hollow silence, its unseen things that moved when he turned his back.

No, he would return to his old lodgings, just for tonight. His fingers tightened around the glass. He had reason enough to leave. Nails that worked themselves back into the wood, stairs that crumbled beneath his feet, *her*—that figure in the dark, brushing out her long, unseen hair. The thought made his skin prickle.

A flicker of movement caught his eye. Faces peered through the gap above the glass partition, watching. When he turned, they vanished. He shifted, putting his back to them, but now the white-haired landlady stood before him, wiping a glass.

"Cold night," she remarked.

Oleron mumbled something—he hardly knew what—and she nodded, prattling about the weather, the late winter, the coming spring. The mundane words steadied him, if only for a moment. His mind flitted, absurdly, to whether her hair crackled when she brushed it at night—then he crushed the thought.

Another drink. Another resolve. He *would* go back. He wasn’t some superstitious fool to be chased from his own home by shadows. He’d walk first, clear his head, then return. He had work to do—*Romilly* wouldn’t write itself.

But as he stepped into the cold air, the bravado faded. The brandy’s warmth dulled, and in its place crept a slow, creeping dread. Not for himself—for *her*. For Elsie.

She had sensed it before he had. And she was right. Whatever lurked in that house, it hated her. That was reason enough to keep her away—not his own shame, not his fear, but *hers*.

He turned back toward the square, jaw set. If fear rose again, he would drink once more. But by the time he reached the pub, it was shuttered, the last stragglers lingering outside. They fell silent as he passed. Barrett, the long-nosed caretaker, did not return his greeting.

Oleron stepped through the broken gate, hesitated only a breath in the dark alley, then climbed the stairs.

The candle had burned low. He forced himself to walk through every room, though his pulse hammered in his throat. As he crossed the hall, a letter on the floor caught his eye—no stamp, slipped under the door by hand. The writing was crude, sprawling without punctuation.

Barrett’s work.

The message was plain: he would no longer serve Oleron’s meals or clean his rooms. And then—the postscript. A scrap of Scripture, twisted, insinuating. About Elsie.

Oleron’s hands clenched. The paper crumpled, then blackened in the candle’s flame. He ground the ashes beneath his heel.

Good. Let Barrett go. Let them all go.

But as he undressed, another disquieting sight: the bedclothes were rumpled, as if someone had lain there. Had he? He couldn’t recall.

No matter. The brandy and his fury blotted out the unease. He blew out the candle, sank into the bed, and slept—deep, dreamless, and far longer than he meant to.

To the man who listens to that inner voice—the one that murmurs warnings as twilight bleeds into his soul—terror ceases to be an abstraction. It becomes a living thing, coiled in the corners of his mind, demanding fortifications lest it unravel him entirely. And so he clings to the mundane: rituals, routines, the brittle armor of ordinary life. These are his wards against the dark.

Paul Oleron had begun to dismantle them.

What once would have chilled him—the suggestion of a haunting, the notion of an unseen presence sharing his home—now only stirred a gnawing curiosity. Horror, when examined, loses its edge. And Oleron *wanted* to examine it. He needed to *know*. The question was no longer *if* something shared the house with him, but *what*, and why it had chosen him.

The answer, he realized with a thrill of dreadful certainty, lay in his work. His abandoned novel, *Romilly*, had been flawed, lifeless—until he moved into this house. Then, almost as if in retaliation for his dissatisfaction, a new *Romilly* had emerged, vivid and demanding. He remembered now, with a jolt, how the first draft had repelled him—how he’d nearly burned it. And how, the morning after, Mrs. Barrett had overheard him humming a tune he didn’t recognize.

The name slithered through his thoughts. Was it coincidence? Or had something *listened* to his frustration and answered?

A darker thought followed. If his fiction could draw such attention, what of its inspiration? Elsie Bengough—his friend, his confidante—had been *Romilly’s* model. Was she now a target as well?

The idea sickened him. He shoved it aside, only for another memory to rise: his confrontation with Barrett. The landlord’s insinuations, the muttered threats of scandal. Oleron had dismissed it as petty malice, but now he wondered. Had Barrett sensed something? Seen something creeping at the edges of his tenant’s sanity?

The thought was cut short by a glimpse through the window—Elsie herself, battling the wind as she crossed the square toward his door. Panic seized him. He couldn’t let her enter. Not now. Not when the house felt *hungry*.

He intercepted her at the gate, spinning a hasty lie about an urgent trip to town. She listened, her face unreadable, and when he faltered, she spoke the words that unraveled him:

No pretense remained. She knew. And worse—she accepted it.

When she finally released him—her hand cold, her goodbye final—Oleron stood shaking on the curb, watching the bus carry her away. For the first time, she left without a smile.

And the house behind him waited, silent, and pleased.

He stood at the curb, swallowed by misery, watching her until she vanished from sight. Yet the moment she was gone, the weight upon his spirit lightened—almost imperceptibly at first, then with a swift, dizzying rush. She had released him. Or perhaps, in truth, he had never truly been bound. No matter. The path ahead was clear now, and he could move freely.

By the time he was halfway home, his steps had quickened, his mind sharpened by a giddy exhilaration. He knew exactly what came next.

The vicar’s house lay a short walk from the square—a man who might know something of the old house with its faded insurance marks and sagging *To Let* signs. If anyone could offer answers, it would be him. And once that final thread was pulled… Oleron’s lips curled. Then things would *happen*.

But the vicar proved disappointingly cautious.

*Yes, the house was old*—hardly a revelation. *Rumored to be haunted*—as if every crumbling structure didn’t whisper such tales to the gullible. The vicar’s voice was measured, careful, as though weighing each word before letting it slip free.

Oleron smiled thinly. “Spare my nerves. How long has it stood empty?”

“A dozen years, perhaps.”

“And the last tenant?” Oleron’s pulse stuttered as he added, “*Him or her?*”

“Him,” the vicar said. “A man named Madley. An artist. Kept to himself, rarely seen outside the place. And—” Here, the vicar faltered, then exhaled. “Since you’ve come asking, and since truth is preferable to whispers… Madley died there. Under unusual circumstances.”

Oleron stilled. “Oh?”

“The post-mortem found no food in his stomach, though he wasn’t destitute. His body was… withered. Suicide was suggested, but starvation?” The vicar’s mouth twisted. “An open verdict.”

Oleron absorbed this silently. “Is there a history of the parish?”

“Fragments. I’ve notes on its ecclesiastical records, if you’d care to see them. But the parish is large, my time limited…”

The interview dissolved into polite nothings. Oleron thanked him, left, and walked home slowly—deliberately. Twice he turned away mere steps from his gate, prolonging the journey.

What came next required precision. He needed his mind *empty*, receptive, as it had been that first morning when he’d sat down to write *Romilly*. Back then, he’d been cluttered, sluggish. The house’s influence had crept in slowly, saturating him drop by drop.

Now? Now he was unburdened. Free of *Romilly*, free of her ghost.

Now the Beckoning Fair One had his full attention.

———

At half-past two, he turned the key in the lock, stepped inside, and shut the door behind him.

The effect was instantaneous. Triumph surged through him. It was like stepping into another world—one where the air was lighter, the very walls hummed with welcome. The flowers in their bowls, the pale panels, the polished floor—all seemed to *laugh*.

Oleron laughed back. “Oh, you’re lovely,” he murmured.

He stretched on the couch, languid as a convalescent awaiting a beloved visitor. The hours slipped by in a haze of contentment, his gaze drifting lazily over the room until darkness fell.

And with darkness came the noises of the house.

———

Nothing *happened*. Not that night, nor the next. He maintained his watchful stillness, a plate waiting for an impression.

On the third day, understanding dawned. A sly amusement curled in his chest.

“Ah,” he chuckled. “Playing coy, are we?”

He wouldn’t summon Elsie. No need for blunt force.

Instead, he went out. Spent the day in the country. Let the Fair One stew in neglect.

He returned late, well past eleven.

“*Now*,” he murmured, fingers closing around his keys.

Inside, he was methodical. Deliberate. A single candle lit his path as he made his rounds—kitchen, bathroom, study—moving with calculated weariness.

Then, the bedroom.

He set the candle on the chest of drawers, feigned a yawn. His reflection flickered in the mirror above the mantel, alongside the gleam of the candle’s flame.

And then—movement.

A faint shimmer in the glass, a gliding spark. The reflection of his comb, suspended in air, moving in slow, deliberate strokes.

*Something* was being combed.

Oleron’s pulse hammered, but his face remained slack. He wound his watch. Counted coins. All the while, the comb kept its rhythm, sweeping through empty space.

Finally, he turned.

The comb hung midair, untouched.

A laugh caught in his throat. He reached out, plucked it from the air. Just an ordinary comb, cold in his palm.

Then his gaze fell on the manuscript in his chair—the original *Romilly*.

“Hm,” he muttered, tossing it aside.

———

Nights bled into days. Oleron coaxed, taunted, pleaded. His life narrowed to a single hunger: to meet the unseen thing that shared his home.

And still, it eluded him.

Time folded in upon itself in Oleron’s dim sanctuary. The postman’s footsteps, once a weekly intrusion, faded into oblivion. Letters from his publishers—unanswered, then forgotten—ceased altogether. The world beyond his door might as well have crumbled to dust.

Spring clawed its way into the city, painting the streets with blossoms. Oleron bought them by the armful, compelled by some unspoken demand from the very walls of his rooms. Yet each excursion into the open air left him trembling, his skin prickling with the dread of vast, unguarded spaces. *Agoraphobia*, the physicians would have called it. Oleron knew it by a different name: *her* will.

Soon, he arranged for deliveries—food, flowers—all brought to his threshold. The relief was exquisite. Now, he need never leave.

But solitude was not peace. A restless melancholy gnawed at him, a weeping weariness that he would not have traded for all the world’s joy. Noise became an abomination. The clatter of Barrett’s Sunday sermons in the square below sent him scrambling to slam the blinds, his name twisting like a knife in the preacher’s fervent curses.

And then there were the silences—precious, sacred—where he would stand breathless in the wedge of his bedroom doorway, surveying his domain. Five rooms, all visible from this one vantage, all his. Had Madley, the previous tenant, known this secret? Oleron smirked at the thought.

Light, too, became an enemy. The sun’s glare was a brand against his eyes. One by one, he drew the crimson blinds, plunging his rooms into a bloody twilight. The darkness soothed him, a womb where his great work could gestate. Oh, they thought he had ceased to write? Fools. He was crafting a masterpiece, a *Mistress*, more radiant and terrible than any mortal creation.

Yet she was coy. She demanded proof of his devotion.

The realization struck him like a blow: the manuscript of *Romilly*, abandoned in the window seat. How could she come to him while that rival still breathed? He snatched the pages, heart pounding. The fire, always burning now, awaited its offering.

As the first sheet blackened and curled, a sound pierced the stillness—a knock, then Elsie’s voice, trembling through the door.

He clenched his teeth, refusing to answer. Let her sob. Let her beg. She knew nothing of true danger. The flames consumed *Romilly* page by page, her pleas fading with the crackling paper. At last, her footsteps retreated.

Alone again, Oleron exhaled. The air thickened. A presence stirred—not in form, but in the sigh of the house itself. A draft nudged the bedroom door, setting it adrift on its hinges. His breath hitched.

He sank into a chair, pulse hammering, awaiting the summons only he would recognize. The Audience was at hand.

There is no compassion for the wasting of the soul. When life’s vigor fades, when the spirit twists inward until sweetness rots into horror, then humanity withdraws. The sane recoil, not out of cruelty, but self-preservation—for we are no exorcists. Devils must not be invited in, even by love’s trembling hand. Pleas of honor, duty, mercy—all fall on deaf ears. A letter slips beneath a darkened door, ignored. A telegram goes unanswered, its messenger unaware of the pale finger that parts the blinds for only a second before retreating.

No. Let the damned wrestle their phantoms alone. Let them, in their madness, embrace the succubus in the fetid dark where neither sun nor heaven’s breath may reach. The lost belong to the shadows. The living have their own burdens.

Oleron had long ceased to care for the world outside. The letters that lay just inside his door might as well have been written in a forgotten tongue. The telegraph boy’s knocking sent him into a frenzy, fingers clawing at his ears. He imagined the boy standing there, amid the withered flowers and spoiled provisions that littered his threshold—offerings he no longer had the strength to retrieve. The stench of decay thickened the air.

His strength bled away, siphoned by the thing that had fastened onto him. Hours slipped by in a red-tinged haze, his mind adrift. Flickers of memory taunted him: half-formed novels, grand visions of stories that would never be written. Sometimes he recalled past lodgings, garrets he had known—trivial fragments of a life now severed. None of it mattered. He had found his final dwelling, a place that suited him perfectly, a place that *wanted* him.

A novel? Oh, someone should write of such a place! There were depths here, if one dared plumb them. A man named Madley had tried—Oleron despised him on instinct. (A joke, that. A bitter laugh in the dark.)

But one thought refused to be banished: Elsie Bengough. She forced her way into his mind, unwelcome, disruptive. Her presence shattered his fragile ecstasies, tore down the glittering palaces of his imagination. She was a fog over a star, a pit yawning beneath his feet. He fought her, strangled her memory before it could take root—but then, without warning, she returned.

Not as memory. *As presence.*

In the black heart of night, she was *there*. No warning. No moment of transition. She simply *existed* in his mind, unshakable, undeniable. And worse—she *watched*.

The realization ignited fury in him. How *dare* she? To intrude now, of all times—to witness *this*—

"Damn you!" he snarled—

And then, terror.

The rage was not his own.

Some other wrath, vast and infernal, surged through him, white-hot, annihilating. His petty anger was a child’s slap compared to the force that now coiled to strike. Had *he* summoned this? Had his weak spark ignited something beyond reckoning?

Before he could scream a warning, the vision shattered.

He woke gasping, drenched in sweat, the nightmare receding. He was Paul Oleron again—just a man, sick and hollow, a failed writer past his prime. The truth settled over him like a shroud: he had grasped for greatness and missed. Twice over. The comforts of ordinary life, and the glory of art—both lost.

And now, only exhaustion remained.

He lay there, piecing together the strangeness around him. The stench of rot. The beard on his face. How long had he been ill? Why was he alone?

Then—a sound. A whisper.

"*Paul.*"

From the kitchen.

And another memory, sharp as a blade: the click of a key in the lock. Minutes ago? Hours?

"Who’s there?" he called. Silence. Then movement—soft, shuffling.

He should rise. Investigate.

But the dark stretched between him and the kitchen like an abyss. What waited there? Better not to know. Better to stay, to let the whispers fade.

Besides, she was near again—*his* muse, his torment, his obsession. He felt her in the air, the electric promise of a story yet unwritten. A tale of a woman wicked and wondrous, a creature to make men tremble.

She was beckoning.

And Oleron, weak as he was, could not resist.

The last thing he felt, before surrender, was the ghost of lips upon his—not Elsie’s, never hers—but something colder, hungrier, stealing his breath away.

The June sun blazed over the square, casting sharp shadows as a murmuring crowd gathered before the old brick house. Its red walls bore the ghostly imprints of antique insurance marks, and the "To Let" signs swayed like grim pendulums above the broken gate. Two constables barred the narrow alley, holding back the tide of onlookers. Women lingered at the edges, their whispers fluttering like moth wings as they angled for a better view of the house’s shuttered windows. The children had been hidden away, doors locked tight against the unseen horror.

A gaunt man with a hooked nose held court near the front, his voice low but urgent.

"I saw her face, clear as day," he insisted to the knot of listeners. "White as death itself. No mistaking it. I told my wife—'Jane, this ends now. We’ll have no part in it.' And from that moment, she never set foot past that gate again."

A murmur rippled through the crowd. Someone dared to ask, "Did she ever... *see* anything?"

The gaunt man’s eyes darkened. "What was seen stays sealed. 'Keep thy tongue from speaking evil,' as the Good Book says."

Nearby, a pudgy, wide-eyed man strained to be heard over the murmurs. "She took the key from me—three ones, three threes—111333!" His words dissolved into the din as others traded their own fragments of rumor.

"Shut your gobs," a burly laborer growled. "She ain’t been found yet. Here come the inspectors—we’ll know soon enough."

The officers pushed through, their presence hushing the crowd. The frantic little man rushed forward, babbling about the key, but they brushed him aside.

"Anyone been inside?" one inspector asked the constable.

"No, sir."

"Right. Brackley, with us. Smith, hold the gate. Squad’s on the way."

The three men disappeared into the alley, their footsteps swallowed by the oppressive silence of the house. The staircase groaned under their weight, dead leaves crackling underfoot.

"No need to knock," one muttered. "Break the glass."

A gloved fist shattered the door panel, the latch clicked, and the door swung inward.

"*Christ*—" one inspector choked, recoiling. "Open the windows. Now."

Outside, the crowd watched as the red blinds were torn up, the windows thrown wide. The stench that rolled out was thick, cloying—a miasma of rot and something worse.

Inside, the bedroom blind rose, flooding the room with cruel light. On the bed, a skeletal figure flinched, shielding his eyes. The man was barely recognizable—waxen, hollow, his mind adrift.

"Where is she?" one officer demanded, looming over him. "Elsie Bengough. Where?"

The man on the bed only shook his head, his lips moving soundlessly.

"Get him up. Carefully."

In the kitchen, the other inspector waded through ankle-deep decay—crushed petals, withered stems. Then he saw it: a trail swept clean, leading to the corner cupboard. The hatch slid open with a whisper.

And then—

A shape. Swaddled in faded cloth, slumped forward. Too large. Too *wrong*.

The inspector stepped back.

He didn’t touch it.

Instead, he forced the cupboard door shut, but not before a scrap of checkered fabric caught, jutting out like a final plea.

Outside, the murmurs had become a roar. A four-wheeler pulled up, a stretcher waiting. The emaciated man was half-carried, half-dragged toward it, his mind too broken to comprehend the shouts, the hatred, the rock that struck the cab.

The last thing he saw, before the door closed, was the "To Let" sign among the privet hedges.

Then darkness.

The cab rolled away.

The other vehicle turned toward the mortuary.

And the house stood silent, waiting for the next tenant.
