The three enemy columns came on as if time itself belonged to them—no running, no shouting yet, only that steady, measured creep of feet and shields which, heard from above, is like rain beginning far off and deciding to become a storm.

We had our men posted in a bent line on the hillside, the hollow of it facing Loo, the two horns reaching down toward the plain. Below, the centre column halted at the root of a long green tongue of grass that licked up into the hill’s curve, waiting while the other two divisions slid wide to right and left to fold us in. Even before spear met shield you could taste the day: sun-warmed stone, the metallic tang of sweat, and, drifting up from thousands of bodies, that faint animal smell of hide and hair rubbed hard with fat. The air carried sound with a cruel fidelity. Every clink of a spear-butt on rock, every low-voiced order, arrived at our ears as clean as a bellstroke. It was a world without engines; no iron wheels, no pounding pistons—only men, breath, and the old instruments of killing. There was a strange health in that honesty of noise, even as it promised slaughter.

“Oh, for a gatling!” Good groaned, staring down at the packed ranks. “I would clear that plain in twenty minutes.”

“We haven’t got one,” Sir Henry said, without taking his eyes off the manoeuvre. Then, with that cold cheerfulness he could put on like a coat, he turned on me. “Try a shot, Quatermain. See if you can drop that tall fellow who seems to be commanding. Two to one you miss him—and an even sovereign, honestly paid if ever we get out, that you don’t put your bullet within five yards.”

The wager pricked me like a thorn. I loaded the express with solid ball and lay down behind a rock. The officer—tall, confident, stepping ten yards forward with only an orderly—stood plain enough, his head and shoulders sharp against the grass. The rifle was sighted only to three hundred and fifty yards; I took him halfway down the neck for the drop, breathed once, and pressed.

Smoke jumped, cleared—and there he was, still upright, untouched. His orderly, three paces to the left, had folded as though a rope had been cut. The commander spun and began to run back.

“Bravo, Quatermain!” Good sang out. “You’ve frightened him.”

To miss is one thing; to miss with witnesses is another. My pride, that poor hungry creature, reared up and demanded feeding. I swung the second barrel after the running man and fired.

He flung up his arms and went down on his face.

Our men, watching from behind the spear-hedge, broke into wild cheering at the white man’s “magic,” and the regiment that had followed the fallen leader wavered and fell back in disorder. Sir Henry and Good began shooting in earnest; Good worked his Winchester like a cook turning a spit—quick, methodical, never pausing to admire the crackle—while I took what shots I could. Six or eight men went down before they were out of range, and then the fight ceased to be something a rifle could manage.

From far right came a deep roar; from far left another answered it. The outer divisions had struck our horns.

The mass below opened, advanced, and began to trot up the green tongue, singing as they ran—a thick, chest-born song that rolled like wind in a cave. We fired steadily, Ignosi firing now and then too, but our bullets were pebbles thrown at a breaking sea. The pickets among the lower rocks were swallowed. Then the climb began to tell; the attackers slowed to save breath, and the sound of their breathing rose with the sound of their feet.

We had three lines: one halfway down, one fifty yards behind it, and the third on the plateau’s edge. The enemy came on, shields forward, spears high, shouting their cry—“Twala! Twala! Chiele! Chiele!”—and our men answered, “Ignosi! Ignosi! Chiele! Chiele!”

Now the tollas flashed, those cruel throwing-knives slipping through sunlight like fish in a stream. Then the awful yell, and the closing shock.

What sound is a battle at spear-range? Not one sound, but a weave: the hollow drum of shield on shield, the wet crack of wood meeting bone, the hiss of breath drawn through teeth, the brief high cry when iron finds the soft place. The hillside became a living thing, heaving and swaying. Men fell as leaves do when the first hard wind of autumn arrives—not one here and there, but in gusts.

Our first line buckled under sheer weight and pressed back into the second. The second line fought like a wall fighting a flood. For a time it held; then it too was driven back, and within twenty minutes our third line was engaged along the plateau edge.

By then the assailants were blown and bleeding; their rush had spent itself on rock and spear. They had lost heavily, and that last hedge of points was too dense to break. The two bodies of men rocked back and forth in fierce, uncertain ebb, and the issue hung like a knife balanced on its tip.

Sir Henry’s eyes were alight. Without a word he vaulted down into the thick of it, battle-axe in hand, and Good went after him.

I stayed where I was, where I could see and hear and be useless in the way I am best at. The soldiers caught sight of Sir Henry’s great form plunging among them, and a roar went up—“Nanzia Incubu! Nanzia Unkungunklovo!”—Here is the Elephant! Here is the great Elephant! And then again, “Chiele! Chiele!”

From that moment the end of that thrust was certain. Inch by inch our men, heartened by the sight, drove the attackers back down the slope until they reeled into their reserves in something like confusion. A messenger came—left horn repulsed the assault. I found myself breathing again, foolishly thinking the worst was over.

Then, across the plain to our right, we saw our own men streaming back toward us—broken, pursued—enemy swarms behind them. The right defence had failed.

Ignosi took in the whole picture in one glance and snapped out an order. The Greys around us extended, shields interlocking in a long grey rib. Another command, caught up and flung along by captains’ voices—and to my intense disgust, I was swept forward with the rest into a full onslaught.

I have never pretended to love close fighting. I got as much of Ignosi’s great frame between myself and the world as I could and went on, small legs carrying me to be killed as if I had asked for it.

We plunged through our own fugitives; as we passed they began to re-form behind us, shame and fury stiffening their spines. Then the lines met.

After that, memory becomes sound and fragments: the dreadful rolling thunder of shields colliding; the smell of trampled grass crushed into green bitterness; the hot, copper reek of blood; the grit of dust between my teeth. A huge man burst out of the press at me, eyes starting, spear red already, and for one clear second I knew with perfect simplicity that if I stood upright I would die.

So I did the only clever thing I can remember doing in that charge. I flung myself flat in front of him at the last instant. He could not stop; momentum took him, and he pitched headlong over me. Before he could scramble up, I was on my feet and put a revolver shot into him from behind.

Not long after, someone struck me down. The world blinked out.

When I came to, I was back at the koppie. Good was bending over me, holding a gourd to my mouth. The water tasted of smoke and skin and heaven.

“How do you feel, old fellow?” he asked, and I heard in his voice the thin edge of fear he tried to hide.

I sat up, shook my head once as if to settle the brains. “Pretty well, thank you.”

“Thank Heaven! When I saw them carry you in, I felt quite sick; I thought you were done for.”

“Not this time. A rap on the head, I fancy. How has it ended?”

“Repulsed at every point—for a while. The loss is dreadful. We have two thousand killed and wounded, and they must have lost three. Look—there’s a sight.”

He pointed, and I saw long lines of men advancing by fours. In the centre of each group, borne shoulder-high, was a hide tray—one of the kind the Kukuanas always carried in quantity, with loops at the corners for hands. On them lay the wounded, endless as if the hill itself were producing them.

As the trays arrived, the medicine men—ten to a regiment—fell upon them with swift hands. They examined, pressed, peered, spoke in quick, flat syllables that seemed made for decisions. If a man might live, he was carried away and tended with all the care possible amid such ruin: binding with strips of hide, packing wounds with chewed herbs, splashing water, murmuring old phrases that sounded like lullabies from a language older than any of us.

But if the wound was hopeless, what happened turned my stomach colder than fear ever had. Under pretence of one more examination, a doctor’s knife would flash; an artery opened; and within a minute or two the man’s life ran quietly out. Often the sufferers were already beyond feeling; often the nick was so fast they scarcely seemed to notice. Mercy, perhaps—the cleanest mercy war allows—but it was ghastly all the same. The broad Kukuanas’ spears left such gaping tears that the body had no chance to knit itself back together.

We hurried away from that strip of red work, glad to escape the sight, and found Sir Henry on the far side of the koppie, still gripping his battle-axe. Ignosi stood with him, Infadoos and a few chiefs gathered close, their heads bent like stalks in a wind of urgent talk.

“Thank Heaven, here you are, Quatermain!” Sir Henry said when he saw me. “I can’t quite make out what Ignosi wants. Though we’ve beaten them off, Twala is receiving reinforcements and seems ready to invest us—to starve us out.”

“That’s awkward,” I said.

“Yes—especially as Infadoos says the water’s given out.”

“My lord, that is so,” Infadoos said. His voice was calm, but his eyes went, almost unwillingly, toward the spring. “The water cannot supply so great a multitude. It fails rapidly. Before night we shall all be thirsty. Listen, Macumazahn. Thou art wise. Thou hast seen many wars in the lands from whence thou camest—if indeed they make wars in the Stars. Tell us what we shall do.

“Twala has brought fresh men to fill the places of those who have fallen. Yet Twala has learnt his lesson; the hawk did not think to find the heron ready; but our beak has pierced his breast. He fears to strike again. We too are wounded, and he will wait for us to die; he will wind himself round us like a snake round a buck, and fight the fight of ‘sit down.’”

“I hear thee,” I answered.

“So,” he went on, rising and pointing toward the dense, watchful mass of the enemy, “we have no water here, and but little food. We must choose: to languish like a starving lion in his den, or to strive to break away towards the north, or”—his hand cut down like an axe—“to launch ourselves straight at Twala’s throat.

“Incubu, the great warrior—for to-day he fought like a buffalo in a net, and Twala’s soldiers went down before his axe like young corn before the hail; with these eyes I saw it—Incubu says, ‘Charge.’ But the Elephant is ever prone to charge. Now what says Macumazahn, the wily old fox, who has seen much, and loves to bite his enemy from behind? The last word is in Ignosi the king. But let us hear thy voice, and the voice too of him of the transparent eye.”

“What sayest thou, Ignosi?” I asked.

“Nay, my father,” Ignosi replied. It still struck me oddly to hear that title from a man who now stood in full war-gear, every inch a king, the old servant’s humility burned away by destiny. “Do thou speak, and let me, who am but a child in wisdom beside thee, hearken.”

I took a moment, hasty counsel with Good and Sir Henry—though it was plain enough. A garrison without water is a pot left on the coals: it will boil dry whether you watch it or not. And the longer men sit listening to thirst, the smaller their courage grows; the ears begin to magnify every enemy-shout, every spear-rattle, until even silence becomes a threat.

So I spoke briefly. Trapped as we were, with the spring failing, the best chance was to attack. Not later, but at once—before wounds stiffened, before the sight of Twala’s numbers melted the heart like fat near a fire. Delay, I warned, would invite treachery: captains might make peace, desert, or sell us outright.

Among the Kukuanas my words were received with a respect I have never earned elsewhere. But the decision was Ignosi’s; and as he stood thinking, the soundscape of war pressed in—the moan of the wounded beyond the koppie, the distant murmur of Twala’s host like a sea that never sleeps, the sharp, ritual calls of captains counting men. Even the language of commands mattered: a king’s syllables carried power because they were the king’s, and the wrong mouth speaking them could turn a regiment like a rudder.

At last Ignosi spoke.

“Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, brave white men and my friends; Infadoos, my uncle, and chiefs—my heart is fixed. I will strike at Twala this day and set my fortunes on the blow, ay, and my life—my life and your lives also.

“Listen. Ye see how the hill curves round like the half-moon, and how the plain runs like a green tongue towards us within the curve?”

“We see,” I said.

“It is now mid-day,” he went on. “The men eat and rest after toil. When the sun has turned and travelled a little way towards darkness, let thy regiment, my uncle, advance with one other down to the green tongue. When Twala sees it he will hurl his force at it to crush it. But the place is narrow; the regiments can come against thee one at a time only—so may they be destroyed one by one. The eyes of all Twala’s army shall be fixed upon a struggle the like of which has not been seen by living man.

“With thee shall go Incubu my friend, that when Twala sees his battle-axe flashing in the first rank of the Greys his heart may grow faint. I will come with the second regiment, that which follows, so that if ye are destroyed—as it might happen—there may yet be a king left to fight for. With me shall come Macumazahn.”

“It is well, O king,” Infadoos said, as calmly as if he were agreeing to take seed to a distant field, not to have his regiment ground to nothing.

“And whilst the eyes of Twala’s soldiers are thus fixed,” Ignosi continued, “one-third of the men who are left alive to us—about six thousand—shall creep along the right horn of the hill and fall upon Twala’s left flank. One-third shall creep along the left horn and fall upon Twala’s right flank. When I see the horns are ready to toss Twala, then will I, with the men who remain to me, charge home in his face. If fortune goes with us, the day is ours, and before Night drives her black oxen from the mountains to the mountains we shall sit in peace at Loo.

“Now let us eat and make ready. Infadoos, prepare that the plan be carried out without fail. And—stay—let my white father Bougwan go with the right horn, that his shining eye may give courage to the captains.”

What followed was done with astonishing speed. In little more than an hour rations were served out and devoured. There was no ceremony about it—no feasting, no fire-smoke perfumes, no slow comfort of a hearth—only the harsh, necessary business of putting strength back into bodies. I saw men squat in the dust and eat as if each mouthful were a command: strips of dried meat softened with saliva, handfuls of meal, a sip of precious water measured like gold. The noises of eating—teeth tearing, throats working, the short sigh a man gives when food hits an empty place—mingled with the clatter of shields being checked and the murmured repetitions of Ignosi’s plan as captains carried it down the line in their own dialects, each word a thread in a net that would either catch a king or strangle us.

Divisions formed. Leaders were instructed. Eighteen thousand men stood ready to move, leaving a guard with the wounded.

Good came up to Sir Henry and me. He looked oddly neat for a man going into that work, moustache stiff, face pale under the grime.

“Good-bye, you fellows,” he said. “I’m off with the right wing, according to orders. Came to shake hands—in case we shouldn’t meet again.”

We shook hands in silence, with as much feeling as Englishmen permit themselves to show without shame.

“It is a queer business,” Sir Henry said, and his deep voice trembled a little. “I confess I never expect to see to-morrow’s sun. So far as I can make out, the Greys, with whom I’m to go, are to fight till they’re wiped out to let the wings slip round and outflank Twala. Well—so be it. At any rate, it will be a man’s death. Good-bye, old fellow. God bless you! I hope you’ll pull through and live to collar the diamonds; but if you do, take my advice and don’t have anything more to do with Pretenders!”

In another second Good had wrung us both by the hand and gone. Infadoos came and led Sir Henry to the front of the Greys, where the narrow green tongue waited like a throat. With many misgivings, and the taste of dust and half-chewed ration still in my mouth, I went with Ignosi to my place in the second attacking regiment, and listened to the hill’s two horns filling with men who moved as quietly as wind through grass, readying themselves to speak—at the proper moment—in steel and in blood.
