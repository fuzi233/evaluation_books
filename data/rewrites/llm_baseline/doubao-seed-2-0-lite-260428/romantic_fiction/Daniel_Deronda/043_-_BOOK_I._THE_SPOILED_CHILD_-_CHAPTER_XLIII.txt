“My spirit is too weak; mortality
Weighs heavily on me like unwilling sleep,
And each imagined pinnacle and steep
Of godlike hardship tells me I must die
Like a sick eagle looking at the sky.”

The hush of the small room wrapped around them slowly, soft as wool, until the unfamiliar stillness seeped through Mordecai’s awareness. He lifted his eyes to Deronda not with bewilderment or surprise, but with a gaze brimming with restful, unshakable satisfaction, as if he had known this exact quiet moment was coming for months. Deronda rose without a word and dragged his chair nearer, close enough that there would never be any need to raise his voice, no matter how softly either spoke. Mordecai leaned into the small, careful gesture the way a fevered patient leans into a hand adjusting their pillow to ease the ache, and began to speak in a low, warm tone, as if he was not addressing another person at all, only thinking his thoughts out loud for the one soul that would not need them explained.

“In the Kabbalist tradition, souls are born again and again in new bodies until they are perfected and purified. A soul that slips free from its worn, tired flesh may seek out the fellow soul that waits for it, so that the two may finish the work they were meant to do together, grow whole side by side, before they leave the mortal realm to make space for new souls to be born from the eternal store. It is the lingering imperfection of the souls walking the earth right now that delays the coming of the Messianic age. That is no mere fable, you see: it is the shadow of a deeper truth we can almost touch. When my long-wandering soul is finally free of this weary, failing body, it will join yours. Then our work will be made perfect.”

His pause felt like a gentle question, and Deronda could not bring himself to leave it unanswered. He spoke as truthfully as he could, his voice thick with unspoken affection and reverence, “Every single thing I can do, in all good conscience, to make your life’s work count, I will do.”

“I know it,” Mordecai said, quiet and certain, no further proof needed. “I heard it in your voice the first day we spoke. You see the vision clearly—you stand with me on the mount of sight, and you can see the paths to the fulfillment that everyone else turns away from.” He fell silent for a beat, then drifted back into memory, soft and meditative. “You will pick my life up exactly where it broke. I can feel that day so clearly now. The sun was bright on the quayside at Trieste, the garments of sailors and merchants from every nation glinting like scattered jewels in the light. The boats were shoving off from the docks, and the Greek ship that was to carry us to Beyrout was set to sail in an hour. I was to go as clerk and companion to a merchant, I was going to see the holy lands of the East, and see the world with a full, unclouded vision. I breathed easy then, the way you do now, no ache in my chest. I had the light step and endurance of youth, I could fast for days, sleep on hard packed dirt, I had wedded poverty and loved it, for poverty was freedom to me. My heart swelled so big it felt like it held the strength of Moses ben Maimon, whole and ready to fill the sixty years of work I had dreamed of. It was my first time traveling south, and the soul inside me woke to the sun it had ached for for lifetimes. I stood on that quay and the ground beneath my feet seemed to glow, the shadows all tinted with the soft blue glow of made visible spirits, and I was so swept up in that flood of glorious life that my own small, numbered years melted away until I could not feel them at all, and a great sob rose in my throat from the sheer weight of bliss too big to hold. I stood there waiting for my travel companion, and did not see him until he spoke my name: ‘Ezra, I have been to the post, there is a letter for you.’”

“Ezra!” Deronda exclaimed, unable to hold the name back, a strange, thrumming shock running down his spine.

“Ezra,” Mordecai repeated, soft and sure, fully lost to the memory. “I had been expecting a letter, I wrote to my mother constantly. That sound of my name was like a wand tapping me, yanking me back from the ocean of universal existence I had melted into, back into the constraints of my own individual flesh. I opened the letter, and the name came again, a cry that would have woken me even if I had been curled in the bosom of heaven: *Ezra, my son*.”

He paused, frozen in that long-ago moment of anguish, and Deronda held his breath, his mind spinning with a sudden, wild, bright possibility. Mordecai stared at his lap for a few quiet moments before he went on. “My mother was the kind of woman of whom it might well have been said ‘her children rise up and call her blessed.’ When I was small I understood the old story of the master who heard his mother’s footsteps approaching and stood to greet her, saying ‘The Majesty of the Eternal draws near.’ That letter was her cry from the very pits of desolation and grief: the cry of a mother robbed of her last little ones. I was her eldest. Four babes had died one after the other, until my little sister was born late, the light of all her eyes. The letter tore into me: ‘Ezra, my son, they have stolen her away. The man took her, and left nothing but disgrace behind. They will never come back.’”

Mordecai looked up suddenly, his hand settling light and shaking on Deronda’s arm. “Mine was the lot of Israel. For the sin of our father, my soul was sent into exile, my work was broken, the day of fulfillment delayed. The woman who bore me was desolate, shamed, penniless. I turned back that very instant. Her spirit, and the spirit of her worthy Jewish forebears, pulled me home. The God who holds the whole universe inside himself was my strength, my reason to obey. I traveled home with nothing, saving every last penny for her need, leaving the warm southern sun behind to trudge north into freezing cold. On the last leg of the journey, I spent a full night out in the snow and ice. That was the start of this slow, lingering death.”

He pulled his hand away, his gaze drifting off, and Deronda bit back all the urgent questions burning on his tongue. Not now, not when Mordecai was so raw with emotion, no revelations but the ones he chose to give freely. The young man felt a kindred ache in his own chest, so big he feared his own speech would shatter the fragile, sacred air between them.

“But I worked, to keep us fed when all our belongings had been seized,” Mordecai went on quietly. “My mother was ill, her grief had wrapped itself around a hidden sickness in her bones. Some days she could not stand for the pounding of her heart, and her dreams turned into chambers of terror where she saw my little sister being raised in cruelty and sin. In the dead of night I would wake to her crying out for her child, and we would rise together, stretch our hands to the dark, and pour out our whole souls in prayer, begging that Mirah would be saved from harm.”

“Mirah?” Deronda repeated, soft, half afraid his own ears had tricked him. “Did you say Mirah?”

“That was my little sister’s name.” Mordecai smiled, faint and tender. “After we prayed for her, my mother would rest a little. Those last years lasted barely four, and the minute before she died, we were praying that exact same prayer: me aloud, her silent, her last breath drifting out on that name. She found peace at last. But I have never heard a single word of Mirah, not since. I do not know if our prayer was answered. Who can map the paths the wicked walk? Their poisoned will is strong. It poisoned my life, and it is slowly smothering this breath I draw. But there is no grief left in the withering of my body now.” His hand returned to Deronda’s arm, his face lit with the bright, hectic joy of a dying man who has just found his whole world. “The work will be done all the better now. I once thought that work was mine to do, and mine alone. But I shall do it. I shall live in you.”

His grip grew tight, almost convulsive, and Deronda’s heart hammered hard in his chest, his lips paling. He had never been so moved in all his life. The certainty that this man before him was Mirah’s long-lost brother wrapped all their strange, fated connection in a new layer of solemn, tender warmth. He dared not speak, dared not shatter that glowing high point of Mordecai’s exaltation by revealing he had already found Mirah, safe and well. He only lifted his own firm, gentle hand to cover the one clamped around his arm. Mordecai’s hand relaxed all at once, turning upward of its own accord to press palm to palm with Deronda’s, and the dying man seemed to come back to himself, quiet and sated. “Let us go now,” he said. “I cannot talk any longer.”

They parted at the door of the Cohen house without another word, only one last firm, lingering press of their joined hands. Deronda walked away with a strange weight on his chest, half bright joy, half soft, tender anxiety. The joy of finding that Mirah’s brother was every bit as noble, good, and worthy of her as he could have ever hoped was tangled in a quiet solemn sadness: the coming reunion between the two siblings would be the first gentle step toward the final parting that would tear Mirah away from her brother soon after, the kind of farewell that feels like a greeting, the last loving glance that stings sharper than any sorrow. He ached to make their first meeting perfect, no jarring, messy interruptions from the rest of the Cohen household, no awkwardness, no poverty or chaos to taint the sacred moment when the last two remaining pieces of that shattered family found each other again. He was determined to find a sunlit, clean lodging near Chelsea, perfect for Mordecai’s frail lungs, to fill it with his favorite old vellum books, his softest armchair, the plaster bas-reliefs of Milton and Dante he loved so well, to make it a soft, gentle home for the two of them. He did not for a moment doubt Mirah would understand her brother’s greatness, that she would pour all her gentle love into tending him through the last precious months of his life. Deronda thought of all the obscure, lonely souls who burn themselves out for a great dream, never finding a single soul to recognize their work before they reach the invisible shore, and he knew he would not let that fate befall Mordecai. He would go first to Mrs. Meyrick, to confide the whole discovery to her, and plan every last detail of the reunion. No tapestry, no fancy carpet, no fine teacup could ever make a home as perfect as Mirah’s soft face, or her sweet, lulling voice drifting through the rooms, he thought. This, after all, was the great reward for Mordecai’s lifelong devotion: he had not only found the man to carry his life’s work forward, he had found his sister, whole, unharmed, and waiting with all her love to meet him at last.
