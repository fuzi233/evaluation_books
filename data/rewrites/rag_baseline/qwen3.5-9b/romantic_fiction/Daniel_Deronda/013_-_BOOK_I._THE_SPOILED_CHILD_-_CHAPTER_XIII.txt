Having resolved to wed Miss Glanmore, Grandcourt displayed a formidable capacity for aligning means with ends. For the ensuing fortnight, scarcely a day passed without his arranging a meeting or demonstrating, through emphatic attentions, that she occupied his singular thoughts. With his cousin, Mrs. Torrington, now presiding over the household, Mrs. Davilow and Gwendolen were invited to a grand assembly at Diplow, where numerous witnesses observed how the host lavished distinction upon the dowerless beauty while displaying a conspicuous lack of concern for the heiress. The world—Mr. Gascoigne and all families of consequence within visiting distance of Pennicote—felt a settled assurance regarding the matter, a conviction that transformed in the rector's mind into a solemn resolve to fulfill his duty to his niece and ensure the settlements were adequate. Indeed, the wonder that persisted among them, and which Grandcourt himself shared, was that an offer, for which so many suitable occasions presented themselves, had not already been made. When he had declared his resolution to Lush, he had anticipated a swift conclusion; to his own surprise, he had repeatedly promised himself in the mornings that he would grant Gwendolen the opportunity to accept him that very day, only to find in the evenings that the necessary formality remained unaccomplished. This remarkable stagnation served only to heighten his determination for another day. He had never admitted to himself that Gwendolen might refuse him, yet—help us all!—we are often powerless to act upon our certainties. Our aversion to a contrary outcome, were it possible, rises like a spectral illusion between us and our truth; we are rationally assured that the blind worm cannot bite us mortally, yet the thought of being bitten is so intolerable, and the creature possesses such a menacing aspect, that we decline to handle it.

He had requested permission to bring a magnificent horse for Gwendolen to ride. Mrs. Davilow was to accompany her in the carriage, and they were to proceed to Diplow for lunch, conducted by Grandcourt. It was a fine mid-harvest season, neither too warm nor too cool for a noonday ride of five miles to be truly delightful. Poppies glowed along the borders of the fields; a gentle breeze moved softly like a social spirit among the ears of uncut corn, winging the shadow of a cloud across the soft gray downs. Here, the sheaves stood tall; there, horses strained their muscles beneath the last loads from wide expanses of stubble, while everywhere the green pasture provided a broader setting for the cornfields, and cattle rested beneath wide-spreading branches. The road wound through a corner of the countryside where dairy farms appeared much as they had in the days of our forefathers—a realm where peace and permanence seemed to find a sanctuary away from the busy changes that sent railway trains flying in the distance.

Yet the spirit of peace and permanence failed to penetrate the mind of poor Mrs. Davilow sufficiently to overcome her habitual unease. Watching Gwendolen and Grandcourt canter ahead of her, then slackening their pace to a conversational walk until the carriage caught up again, presented a gratifying sight; yet it served chiefly to fuel the conflict of hopes and fears concerning her daughter's future. Here lay an irresistible opportunity for a lover to speak and dissolve all uncertainties, and Mrs. Davilow could only hope, with trembling heart, that Gwendolen's decision would be favorable. Certainly, if Rex's love had been repugnant to her, Mr. Grandcourt possessed the distinct advantage of being a complete antithesis to him; and that he had produced some quite novel impression upon her seemed evident in her marked abstinence from satirical observations, nay, her total silence regarding his characteristics—a silence which Mrs. Davilow dared not break. "Is he a man she would be happy with?" was a question that inevitably arose in the mother's mind. "Well, perhaps as happy as she would be with anyone else—or as most other women are"—was the answer with which she tried to quiet herself; for she could not imagine Gwendolen under the influence of any feeling that would make her satisfied in what we traditionally term "mean circumstances."

Grandcourt's own thoughts were looking in the same direction: he sought to be done with the uncertainty belonging to his failure to speak. As to any further uncertainty—well, it was something without reasonable basis, a quality in the air acting as an irritant to his desires.

Gwendolen enjoyed the riding, but her pleasure did not burst forth in the girlish, unpremeditated chatter and laughter it had shown that morning with Rex. She spoke a little, and even laughed, but with a lightness akin to a far-off echo; for there was, for her too, some peculiar quality in the air. She was sure it was not any subjugation of her will by Mr. Grandcourt, nor the splendid prospects he intended to offer; for Gwendolen desired everyone, including that dignified gentleman himself, to understand that she was going to do exactly as she liked, and that they had better not calculate on her pleasing them. If she chose to take this husband, she would have him know that she was not going to renounce her freedom, or, according to her favorite formula, "not going to do as other women did."

Grandcourt's speeches that morning were, as usual, of that brief sort which never fails to make a conversational figure when the speaker is held important in his circle. By stopping so soon, they gave signs of a suppressed and formidable ability to say more, and possessed the meritorious quality of allowing lengthiness to others.

"How do you like Criterion's paces?" he said after they had entered the park and slowed from a canter to a walk.

"He is delightful to ride. I should like to have a leap with him, if it would not frighten mamma. There was a good wide channel we passed five minutes ago. I should like to have a gallop back and take it."

"Pray do. We can take it together."

"No, thanks. Mamma is so timid—if she saw me it might make her ill."

"Let me go and explain. Criterion would take it without fail."

"No—indeed—you are very kind—but it would alarm her too much. I dare take any leap when she is not by; but I do it and don't tell her about it."

"We can let the carriage pass and then set off."

"No, no, pray don't think of it any more: I spoke quite randomly," said Gwendolen; she began to feel a new objection to carrying out her own proposition.

"But Mrs. Davilow knows I shall take care of you."

"Yes, but she would think of you as having to take care of my broken neck."

A considerable pause ensued before Grandcourt said, looking toward her, "I should like to have the right always to take care of you."

Gwendolen did not turn her eyes on him; it seemed to her a long while that she was first blushing, and then turning pale, but to Grandcourt's rate of judgment she answered soon enough, with the lightest flute-tone and a careless movement of the head, "Oh, I am not sure that I want to be taken care of: if I chose to risk breaking my neck, I should like to be at liberty to do it."

She checked her horse as she spoke, and turned in her saddle, looking toward the advancing carriage. Her eyes swept across Grandcourt as she made this movement, but there was no language in them to correct the carelessness of her reply. At that very moment she was aware that she was risking something—not her neck, but the possibility of finally checking Grandcourt's advances, and she did not feel contented with the possibility.

"Damn her!" thought Grandcourt, as he too checked his horse. He was not a wordy thinker, and this explosive phrase stood for mixed impressions which eloquent interpreters might have expanded into sentences full of an irritated sense that he was being mystified, and a determination that this girl should not make a fool of him. Did she want him to throw himself at her feet and declare that he was dying for her? It was not by that gate that she could enter on the privileges he could give her. Or did she expect him to write his proposals? Equally a delusion. He would not make his offer in any way that could place him definitely in the position of being rejected. But as to her accepting him, she had done it already in accepting his marked attentions: and anything which happened to break them off would be understood to her disadvantage. She was merely coquetting, then?

However, the carriage came up, and no further tête-à-tête could well occur before their arrival at the house, where there was abundant company. To whom Gwendolen, clad in riding-dress with her hat laid aside, and clad also in the repute of being chosen by Mr. Grandcourt, was naturally a center of observation; and since the objectionable Mr. Lush was not there to look at her, this stimulus of admiring attention heightened her spirits and dispersed, for the time, the uneasy consciousness of divided impulses which threatened her with repentance of her own acts. Whether Grandcourt had been offended or not there was no judging: his manners were unchanged, but Gwendolen's acuteness had not gone deeper than to discern that his manners were no clue for her, and because these were unchanged she was not the less afraid of him.
