Anyone who’s ever passed through the doors of the Wheel of Fortune public-house—famed for hosting Mr. James Morgan’s Club and serving as the scene of Sir Francis Clavering’s dramatic tête-à-tête with Major Pendennis—will know that the place has three rooms on the ground floor, plus the bar where the landlady sits like a queen of minor domestic drama. One room is open to all comers; another is reserved for gentlemen in livery (a class whose dignity is rarely matched by their actual pay); and the third, marked plainly with “Private,” is the exclusive haunt of the “Confidentials,” a select society composed entirely of Messrs. Morgan and Lightfoot.

Now, Mr. Morgan, that quiet, observant soul who could listen to a conversation without so much as twitching an eyelash, had overheard a rather juicy exchange between Strong and Major Pendennis at the latter’s lodgings. He’d filed it away like a rare stamp or a secret recipe for success. And when Pendennis later visited the Wheel of Fortune, Morgan followed like a shadow, slipping into the Confidential room just as the two gentlemen began their argument in the parlour next door. From a certain corner—a spot that must have been designed by fate itself—Morgan could hear nearly every word, and what he heard confirmed his darkest suspicions: the whole affair was bigger than he’d imagined.

“Ah, so he recognized Altamont right off in Sydney?” Morgan muttered to himself, sipping brandy-and-water like a man with secrets. “Clavering ain’t married to Lady C. any more than I am! Altamont’s the real father—Altamont’s a convict! Harthur gets into Parliament, and the Governor promises not to split. By Jove, what a crafty old fox that governor is!” A grin spread across Morgan’s face, as if he’d just won the lottery while still wearing his master’s boots. “If only I weren’t so old and burdened with society’s ridiculous prejudices,” he said aloud, staring into the mirror, “I might marry her myself. Dammy, James Morgan, you could be Lord Blanche.”

But since marriage wasn’t in the cards, Morgan decided to play the next best hand: blackmail. With information this explosive, he could make money from almost everyone involved. Sir Francis Clavering? He’d keep quiet about the scandal. Colonel Altamont? He’d be terrified of exposure. Young Harthur? The lad would pay handsomely to avoid being revealed as a convict’s son—and worse, as someone who got his seat in Parliament through shady deals. As for Lady C., well, if she’s tired of Clavering, she’ll pay to get rid of him. If she’s worried about her son, she’ll pay to protect him. And Miss Blanche? She’ll pay to reclaim her rightful inheritance. “Dammy,” Morgan concluded, “with cards like these, James Morgan, you’re practically a millionaire. It’s practically a miracle. Everyone will subscribe. And with what I’ve already made, I can quit this job, give my old boss notice, become a gentleman myself, and even hire a servant—begad!”

All this while, Morgan remained calm, courteous, and utterly professional. He polished boots, curled hair, brought tea, and bore the Major’s grumblings and senile sarcasm with the patience of a saint—or perhaps a man who knew he was about to become richer than his employer.

One key player in this tangled web, Colonel Altamont, was conveniently absent from London when Morgan learned the truth. The valet, knowing Clavering’s favorite haunt—the Shepherd’s Inn—hurried there after the conversation ended. But the bird had flown. Altamont had collected his Derby winnings and fled to the Continent. “He’ll blow it all at the gambling dens along the Rhine,” Morgan sighed. “And I could’ve had a nice chunk of it. How annoying that he couldn’t wait a few days!” Yet Morgan bore disappointment with the same equable face he wore when polishing boots. Until the moment came, he served faithfully: boots shined, hair curled, tea delivered, insults swallowed with silent dignity. Who would guess, watching him pack trunks or assist at dinner in country houses, that Morgan was wealthier than his master, knew more secrets than the Prime Minister, and had turned the landlady into his personal slave?

In fact, Mrs. Brixham was under Morgan’s thumb. He owned the lease of the house she rented, held her name and her son’s on various bills, and even had a bill of sale that made her furniture his property. Her son worked in an insurance office—where Morgan could send him to “quod” (prison) with a single note. She was a clergyman’s widow, and Morgan took pleasure in making her fetch his bootjack and slippers. Her entire life—her son’s black profile, her daughter’s picture, the very image of Tiddlecot Church where she’d been married—was now part of Morgan’s collection, hanging in his back-parlour like trophies.

On this particular evening, Morgan sat in the ex-curate’s horse-hair chair, drinking brandy-and-water bought with the landlady’s own money, which only made him drink more freely. He’d eaten supper and was on his third tumbler when the Major returned from the Club, weary and foul-tempered.

Morgan swore violently at the bell and finished his drink before ascending. The Major, already annoyed, greeted him with a torrent of abuse. Morgan endured it silently, kneeling to remove the boots with perfect obedience—though below, beneath the surface, he growled like a caged bear.

When it came time to take off the coat, Morgan had to stand close enough for the Major to smell the brandy on his breath. “You reek of gin and bad decisions,” the Major snapped.

That was the final straw. Morgan exploded. “Drunk, am I? A beast? D——d? You infernal old miscreant! Shall I wring your neck? Drown you in that pail of water? Do you think I’m some dog who’ll take your insolence? I’ve waited on you for twelve years, and you’ve cursed me every day! You call me names, but I’ve seen plenty of gentlemen drunker than I am. And if I choose to drink, why shouldn’t I?”

The Major grabbed a knife from the table. “If you take one step forward, I’ll stab you. Go downstairs, you drunken brute. Send for your book and wages tomorrow. Never show your face here again. Your impudence has gone too far. You’ve grown rich, and rich men don’t serve. Get out.”

“And where should I go, pray?” Morgan retorted, sitting down calmly. “Isn’t tomorrow morning convenient enough? Tootyfay mame shose, sivvaplay, munseer?”

“Silence, you beast, and leave!”

Morgan laughed—a dry, chilling laugh. “Look here, Pendennis. Since I’ve been in this room, you’ve called me beast, brute, dog, and d——d. How do you think a man likes that? Twelve years I’ve served you. Twelve years of curses and insults. Do you think I’m a dog? That I can’t talk back? I’m not going to take it anymore. I’m quitting. I’ve combed your wig, buckled your girths, and tended your sick body long enough. Don’t look at me like that—I’m sitting in my own chair, in my own room, telling the truth. I won’t be your beast anymore. I’m James Morgan, and I’m done.”

The Major, stunned by the sudden rebellion, felt his anger cool like a fever breaking. He found himself oddly impressed—like a fencer admiring a bold opponent who lands a clean blow. “You are no longer my servant,” he said, regaining composure. “This house may be yours, but these lodgings are mine. I shall move out tomorrow. For now, I wish to sleep. Leave me.”

“We’ll settle things,” Morgan said, rising. “I’m not done with you. Or your family. Or the Claverings. You’ll see.”

“Leave the room, sir. I’m tired.”

“Hah!” Morgan sneered. “You’ll be more tired of me before it’s over.” And with that, he walked out, leaving the Major alone with his thoughts.

The old gentleman sat by the fire, reflecting on servants, ingratitude, and the difficulty of replacing a man who knew how to make mutton broth and polish boots better than anyone else. He missed Morgan’s boot-varnish—it was *incomparable*. He remembered how Morgan had cared for him when he was ill. “Gad, it’s hard to lose such a fellow,” he muttered. “But he must go. He’s grown rich. And rich men don’t stay loyal.”

He thought of the shower-bath, which had helped soothe his nerves. The umbrella incident, the paint-smell at the Club—they all faded under the weight of this new drama. “Confound the insolent villain,” he whispered. “He was the best servant in England.”

Just then, a timid knock. The door opened. Mrs. Brixham stood there, pale and trembling.

“God bless my soul, Mrs. Brixham!” the Major gasped. “It’s late. What is it?”

“I wish to speak to you, sir,” she said, voice quivering. “About Morgan.”

“Ah. Of course. He’s cooled off at the pump. Can’t take him back. I’d decided earlier—he’s involved in discount schemes. I suppose you’ve heard?”

“Oh, sir,” she cried, “I know. I borrowed from him five years ago. I’ve paid him back many times over. But he owns everything. My house, my furniture, my son’s future. He’s ruined me. He’s a dreadful man.”

The Major blinked. “Eh? Tout pis. Dev’lish sorry for you. And I must leave your house after lodging here so long. There’s nothing to be done. I must go.”

And so, with a sigh, the Major prepared for bed, leaving behind not just a servant—but a world turned upside down.
