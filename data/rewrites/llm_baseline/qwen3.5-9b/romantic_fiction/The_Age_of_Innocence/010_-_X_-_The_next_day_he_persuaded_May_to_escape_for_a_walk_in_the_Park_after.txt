The following day, Archer managed to persuade May to slip away for a stroll in the Park immediately after luncheon. In the rigid, old-fashioned Episcopal circles of New York, it was customary for daughters to accompany their parents to church on Sunday afternoons; however, Mrs. Welland had granted permission for this specific act of truancy. That very morning, she had successfully convinced her daughter of the absolute necessity of a prolonged engagement—a duration required to allow ample time for the meticulous preparation of a hand-embroidered trousseau containing the proper number of dozen linens.

The day itself was delectable. Along the Mall, the bare vaulting of the trees was draped in a canopy of lapis lazuli, arched overhead by snow that glittered like splintered crystals. It was precisely the sort of weather designed to ignite May's radiance, and she burned with the vibrant intensity of a young maple leaf in the frost. Archer watched her with pride, observing the glances cast her way; the simple joy of possession seemed to clear away the underlying perplexities that had lately clouded his mind.

"It is so delicious—waking every morning to smell lilies-of-the-valley in one's room!" May exclaimed breathlessly. "They arrived late yesterday. I simply hadn't the time in the morning—"

"But your remembering to send them each day makes me love them infinitely more than if you had placed a standing order for them to arrive punctually every single morning, like one's music teacher," Archer replied gently. "As I recall, Gertrude Lefferts's did exactly that when she and Lawrence were engaged."

"Oh, they certainly would!" Archer laughed, amused by her keen observation. He glanced sideways at her fruit-like cheek, feeling suddenly rich and secure enough to venture further. "When I sent your lilies yesterday afternoon, I noticed some rather gorgeous yellow roses nearby and packed them off to Madame Olenska. Was that correct?"

"How dear of you! Anything of that nature delights her. It is strange she hasn't mentioned it; she lunched with us today and spoke of Mr. Beaufort sending her wonderful orchids, and Cousin Henry van der Luyden delivering a whole hamper of carnations from Skuytercliff. She seems genuinely surprised to receive flowers. Don't people send them in Europe? She considers it such a charming custom."

"Oh, well, no wonder mine were overshadowed by Beaufort's," Archer said irritably. Then, remembering he had forgotten to include a card with the roses, he grew vexed at bringing them up. He wanted to confess, "I called on your cousin yesterday," but hesitated. If Madame Olenska had not mentioned his visit, acknowledging it now might seem awkward. Yet, failing to mention it cast an air of mystery over the affair that he deeply disliked.

To deflect the conversation, he steered toward their own plans, their future, and Mrs. Welland's insistence on a lengthy courtship.

"If you call *this* long!" May protested. "Isabel Chivers and Reggie were engaged for two years; Grace and Thorley for nearly a year and a half. Why aren't we considered very well off as we are?"

It was the traditional maidenly interrogation, and Archer felt a sudden shame at finding it so singularly childish. No doubt she merely echoed what others said for her benefit; yet she was approaching her twenty-second birthday, and he wondered at what precise age "nice" women began to speak entirely for themselves.

"Never, if we won't let them, I suppose," he mused aloud, recalling his earlier, mad outburst to Mr. Sillerton Jackson: *"Women ought to be as free as we are..."*

Soon, it would be his task to remove the blindfold from this young woman's eyes and bid her gaze upon the wider world. But how many generations of women, shaped by the same mold, had descended blindly into the family vaults? He shivered slightly, thinking of the radical new ideas in his scientific texts and the famous case of the Kentucky cave-fish, which had ceased to develop eyes because they served no purpose in its dark existence. What if, once he urged May Welland to open hers, they could only stare blankly into an abyss of emptiness?

"We might be much better off," he whispered. "We might be altogether together—we might travel."

Her face lit up instantly. "That would be lovely," she admitted. "I would love to travel."

"But your mother would not understand our desire to do things so differently," he noted.

"As if the mere word 'differently' doesn't account for everything!" the suitor insisted.

"Newland! You are so original!" she exclaimed, exultant.

His heart sank. He realized he was uttering the very sentiments young men in similar situations were expected to voice, and that she was responding with the automatic answers dictated by instinct and tradition—even to the point of labeling him "original."

"Original! We are all as identical as those dolls cut from the same folded paper. We are like patterns stenciled onto a wall. Can't you and I strike out for ourselves, May?"

He had stopped and faced her, seized by the excitement of their discussion. Her eyes rested upon him with a bright, unclouded admiration.

"Mercy—shall we elope?" she laughed.

"If you would—"

"You *do* love me, Newland! I am so happy."

"But then—why not be happier?"

"We cannot behave like characters in novels, can we?"

"Why not? Why not? Why not?"

She appeared slightly bored by his persistence. She knew perfectly well they could not, yet it was troubling to be forced to fabricate a reason.

"I am not clever enough to argue with you," she conceded, relieved to have discovered a word that would undoubtedly extinguish the entire subject. "But that sort of thing is rather... vulgar, isn't it?"

"Are you so terrified, then, of being vulgar?"

She was clearly staggered by the question.

"Of course I should hate it—and so would you," she rejoined, a trifle irritably.

Archer stood silent, tapping his walking stick nervously against his boot-top. Feeling that she had indeed found the perfect way to close the discussion, she resumed her light-hearted tone.

"Oh, did I tell you I showed Ellen my ring? She thinks it the most beautiful setting she has ever seen. There is nothing like it in the Rue de la Paix, she said. I do love you, Newland, for being so artistic!"

The next afternoon, before dinner, Archer sat smoking sullenly in his study. Janey wandered in upon him. He had skipped his usual stop at the club on his way home from the office, exercising the legal profession in the leisurely fashion common to wealthy New Yorkers of his class. He was out of spirits, slightly irritable, and haunted by a terrifying dread of performing the exact same actions at the exact same hour forever.

"Ssameness—sameness!" he muttered, the word echoing through his mind like a persecuting melody as he gazed at the familiar, tall-hatted figures lounging behind the plate-glass windows. Since he typically visited the club at that hour, he had returned home instead. He knew not only what they were discussing but the specific role each would play in the debate. The Duke, naturally, would be the principal topic; though the recent appearance in Fifth Avenue of a golden-haired lady in a small canary-colored brougham drawn by a pair of black cobs (generally attributed to Beaufort) would undoubtedly be thoroughly dissected as well.

Such "women"—as they were termed—were rare in New York; those driving their own carriages even rarer. The sight of Miss Fanny Ring in Fifth Avenue during the fashionable hour had profoundly agitated society. Only the previous day, her carriage had passed Mrs. Lovell Mingott's, prompting the latter to instantly ring the little bell at her elbow and order the coachman to drive her home.

"What if it had happened to Mrs. van der Luyden?" people asked one another with a shudder.

Archer could almost hear Lawrence Lefferts, at that very moment, lecturing on the disintegration of society. He raised his head irritably when his sister Janey entered, then quickly bent over his book (*Swinburne's "Chastelard"*—just published) as if he had not seen her. She glanced at the writing desk piled high with volumes, opened a copy of the *Contes Drolatiques*, made a wry face at the archaic French, and sighed.

"What learned things you read!"

"Well—?" he asked, hovering Cassandra-like before him.

"Mother is very angry."

"Angry? With whom? About what?"

"Miss Sophy Jackson has just been here. She brought word that her brother would come in after dinner; she couldn't say much because he forbade it. He wishes to give all the details himself. He is currently with Cousin Louisa van der Luyden."

"For heaven's sake, my dear girl, try a fresh start. It would take an omniscient Deity to know what you are talking about."

"It is not a time to be profane, Newland.... Mother feels badly enough about your absence from church..."

With a groan, he plunged back into his book.

"_Newland_! Do listen. Your friend Madame Olenska was at Mrs. Lemuel Struthers's party last night. She went there with the Duke and Mr. Beaufort."
