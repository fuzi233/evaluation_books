The heat in Cloisterham was a physical weight, pressing down on the cobblestones and the whitewashed walls of the Nuns' House. The cathedral and the ruined monastery stood like translucent ghosts against the blinding summer sun, their stone glowing from within rather than reflecting the light. The gardens were flushed with the color of ripening fruit, a violent, organic stain against the grey masonry.

Time seemed to dissolve into the dust. Wayfarers, stripped of their names and reduced to the texture of the earth itself, lounged on doorsteps. They were men of mud and straw, attempting to repair shoes that could not be mended, or offering them to the kennels as a hopeless trade. Their bare feet cooled at the public pumps, joining the gurgle of water that bubbled up to meet thirsty hands. The local police watched these intruders with a mixture of suspicion and irritation, eager for the nomads to vanish back onto the baking highways.

Inside the house, the atmosphere was thin and brittle. Helena Landless had departed to chase her brother's fortune, and Mrs. Tisher was away on leave. Miss Twinkleton, in her temporary absence, had traded her duties for a picnic and a veal pie. Rosa was alone.

The servant entered, her face pale. "Mr. Jasper desires to see you, miss."

The news struck Rosa with the force of a physical blow. She had hoped for privacy, for the sanctuary of the empty rooms. Instead, she found herself cornered. "Why," she whispered, the words tearing from her throat, "why did you say I was at home?"

The maid offered no comfort. "He said he knew you were here. He asked to see you."

Panic seized her. She clutched her hands together, the knuckles white. She imagined the locked doors, the barred windows, the suffocating intimacy of a room shared with a man she despised. Yet, a desperate logic took root in her mind. The house was filled with windows overlooking the garden. If she refused entry, she would be trapped inside. If she went outside, she could scream, she could run, she could expose him to the gaze of the street.

She hung her hat on her arm and stepped out.

From the porch, she saw him. Mr. Jasper leaned against the sundial, a silhouette of black cloth against the brilliant afternoon. The old, primal fear coiled in her stomach, tightening her chest. She wanted to turn away, to flee into the shadows of the house, but his presence acted as a magnet, pulling her feet forward until she sat heavily on the garden bench beside him. She kept her head bowed, unable to bear the sight of him, yet she registered the detail of his attire: deep mourning. So was she. The loss of Edwin had settled over them both like a shroud.

"I have been waiting," he began, his voice smooth and low, "for some time, to be summoned back to my duty near you."

Rosa formed words in her mind, shaping them into refusals, then dissolving them into silence. "Duty, sir?"

"The duty of teaching you," he continued, "of serving you as your faithful music-master."

"I have left off that study."

"Not left off," he corrected gently. "Discontinued. Your guardian told me you ceased under the shock we have all felt. When will you resume?"

"Never, sir."

"Never? You could have done no more if you had loved my dear boy."

"I did love him!" Rosa cried out, the anger flashing across her face, a brief, dangerous spark.

"Yes; but not quite—not quite in the right way, shall I say? Not in the intended and expected way. Much as my dear boy was, unhappily, too self-conscious and self-satisfied... to love as he should have loved, or as any one in his place would have loved—must have loved!"

Rosa shrank further into the bench. "Then, to be told that you discontinued your study with me, was to be politely told that you abandoned it altogether?"

"Yes," she answered, her voice gaining a sudden, sharp edge. "The politeness was my guardian's, not mine. I told him that I was resolved to leave off, and that I was determined to stand by my resolution."

"And you still are?"

"I still am, sir. And I beg not to be questioned any more about it. At all events, I will not answer any more; I have that in my power."

She felt the weight of his gaze, a heavy, possessive scrutiny that followed her movements. She sensed his admiration for the flush of anger on her cheeks, the fire it ignited in her eyes. As her spirit rose in defiance, it faltered, crushed by a wave of shame and terror reminiscent of that night at the piano.

"I will not question you any more, since you object to it so much; I will confess—"

"I do not wish to hear you, sir," she interrupted, rising abruptly.

This time, he touched her. His hand reached out, and as she recoiled, she sank back into the seat, defeated.

"We must sometimes act in opposition to our wishes," he murmured, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "You must do so now, or do more harm to others than you can ever set right."

"What harm?"

"Presently, presently. You question me, you see, and surely that's not fair when you forbid me to question you. Nevertheless, I will answer the question presently. Dearest Rosa! Charming Rosa!"

She started up again, fleeing the proximity of his voice. He did not touch her this time. Instead, he leaned against the sundial, his posture relaxed yet menacing, a dark blotch against the brightness of the day. The threat in his stillness was palpable.

"I do not forget how many windows command a view of us," he said, glancing toward the house. "I will not touch you again; I will come no nearer to you than I am. Sit down, and there will be no mighty wonder in your music-master's leaning idly against a pedestal and speaking with you, remembering all that has happened, and our shares in it. Sit down, my beloved."

She hovered on the brink of flight, her foot already lifting, but his face froze her movement. It was a mask of dark warning, promising consequences if she dared to move. She sat.

"Rosa, even when my dear boy was affianced to you, I loved you madly; even when I thought his happiness in having you for his wife was certain, I loved you madly; even when I strove to make him more ardently devoted to you, I loved you madly; even when he gave me the picture of your lovely face so carelessly traduced by him, which I feigned to hang always in my sight for his sake, but worshipped in torment for years, I loved you madly; in the distasteful work of the day, in the wakeful misery of the night, girded by sordid realities, or wandering through Paradises and Hells of visions into which I rushed, carrying your image in my arms, I loved you madly."

The violence of his delivery clashed grotesquely with the calm, theatrical composition of his pose. The words were hideous, amplified by the contrast.

"I endured it all in silence. So long as you were his, or so long as I supposed you to be his, I hid my secret loyally. Did I not?"

This lie, wrapped in such convincing phrasing, broke her resolve. "You were as false throughout, sir, as you are now. You were false to him, daily and hourly. You know that you made my life unhappy by your pursuit of me. You know that you made me afraid to open his generous eyes, and that you forced me, for his own trusting, good, good sake, to keep the truth from him, that you were a bad, bad man!"

His hands twitched, convulsing beneath the sleeves of his mourning coat, rendering his serene expression diabolical. "How beautiful you are! You are more beautiful in anger than in repose. I don't ask you for your love; give me yourself and your hatred; give me yourself and that pretty rage; give me yourself and that enchanting scorn; it will be enough for me."

Tears welled in her eyes, hot and impatient. As she rose to flee, seeking the safety of the porch, he stretched a hand toward the entrance, inviting her in.

"I told you, you rare charmer, you sweet witch, that you must stay and hear me, or do more harm than can ever be undone. You asked me what harm. Stay, and I will tell you. Go, and I will do it!"

Fear, instinctual and paralyzing, rooted her to the spot. Her breathing came in ragged gasps. Placing a hand over her heart to stifle the panic, she remained.

"I have made my confession that my love is mad. It is so mad, that had the ties between me and my dear lost boy been one silken thread less strong, I might have swept even him from your side, when you favoured him."

A film clouded her vision for a fleeting second, as if he had struck her. "Even him," he repeated softly. "Yes, even him! Rosa, you see me and you hear me. Judge for yourself whether any other admirer shall love you and live, whose life is in my hand."
