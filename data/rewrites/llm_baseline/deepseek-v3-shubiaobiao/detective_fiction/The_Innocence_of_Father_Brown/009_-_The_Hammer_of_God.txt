The village of Bohun Beacon clung to its steep hill like a climber to a cliff, its church spire piercing the sky like a stone needle. At the base of the hill stood the smithy, its furnace glowing red, hammers and scraps of iron scattered about like the bones of some great metal beast. Across the cobbled crossroads lurked *The Blue Boar*, the only inn for miles, where men drank away their sorrows—or their souls.

It was here, in the pale silver light of dawn, that two brothers met—one ending his night, the other beginning his day. The Reverend Wilfred Bohun, gaunt and devout, moved toward the church for his morning prayers. His elder brother, Colonel Norman Bohun, lounged outside the inn in rumpled evening wear, a glass in hand, his yellow hair wild, his deep-set eyes dark as a wolf’s. The Bohuns were an old family, ancient as the hills, but nobility had long since rotted into decadence. The colonel wore his dissipation like a second skin, his grin sharp, his words sharper.

Wilfred frowned as he caught his brother’s gaze fixed on the smithy. *Not the church, then. The blacksmith’s wife.*

“Good morning, Wilfred,” the colonel drawled. “Like a dutiful landlord, I keep watch over my people. I think I’ll pay the smith a visit.”

“He’s not there,” Wilfred said flatly. “He’s gone to Greenford.”

The colonel’s smile widened. “I know.”

Wilfred’s fingers tightened around his prayer book. “Norman… do you ever fear lightning?”

The colonel arched a brow. “Do I look like a man who fears the weather?”

“I mean,” Wilfred said softly, “do you ever think God might strike you dead where you stand?”

The colonel laughed. “Ah, I see. Not meteorology—*morality*.”

“Blasphemy is your pastime,” Wilfred snapped. “But if you don’t fear God, you should fear Barnes the blacksmith. He could break you over his knee.”

For a moment, something flickered in the colonel’s eyes—something almost like fear. Then he laughed again, lifting his absurd green hat to reveal a steel lining beneath. “One must dress for danger, dear brother.”

Wilfred turned away, crossing himself as he entered the church, seeking refuge in the cool shadows. But peace eluded him. Inside, he found *Mad Joe*, the blacksmith’s nephew—a hulking, slack-jawed youth who had never prayed in his life. Yet there he knelt, mumbling to himself.

Before Wilfred could question him, the village cobbler—an avowed atheist—burst in, his face ashen. “Reverend—you must come. It’s your brother.”

***

The body lay sprawled in the smithy’s yard, the skull crushed like an egg under a boot. The force of the blow had driven bone fragments into the earth like shrapnel.

“Only one man in forty miles could strike like that,” muttered Gibbs the cobbler.

“Where’s the blacksmith?” someone asked.

As if summoned, Simeon Barnes appeared, hammer slung over his shoulder, his face calm. When told of the colonel’s death, he merely said, “Then he’s damned.”

The inspector arrested him—then promptly released him when two Greenford men swore the smith had been with them all night.

Father Brown, the unassuming little priest, studied the murder weapon—a *small* hammer, its edge smeared with blood and hair.

“A woman would choose a light hammer,” the doctor mused. “But no woman could strike such a blow.”

Wilfred’s hands trembled. “Unless the killer was mad. A madman might pick up any tool—and in his frenzy, have the strength of ten men.”

His eyes darted to where *Mad Joe* had vanished into the morning light.

Father Brown shook his head. “A fine theory, Mr. Bohun. But wrong.”

And with that, the priest turned back to the hammer, leaving the truth hanging in the air—sharp as the edge of a blade.
