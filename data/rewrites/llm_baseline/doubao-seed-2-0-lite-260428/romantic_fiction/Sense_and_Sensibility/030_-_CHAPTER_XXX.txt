Mrs. Jennings came trundling back to their rooms the second she stepped through the front door, not even waiting for the maid to answer her knock before shoving it open, bonnet askew, face soft with unfeigned concern. “Oh my dears, how do you do?” she crooned, voice thick with pity, leaning over Marianne where the girl lay face-down on the bed. Marianne only turned her cheek further into the pillow, too raw to even speak.
“Poor little thing, she looks half dead, and no wonder!” Mrs. Jennings went on, turning to Elinor. “It’s all true, that horrid good-for-nothing scoundrel’s set to be married any day now. I swear I almost sank right through the floor when Mrs. Taylor told me half an hour ago—she heard it straight from Miss Grey’s own closest friend, else I’d never have believed such wickedness. I told Mrs. Taylor plain, if that’s true he’s treated a young lady I care for abominably, and I hope with all my soul his wife torments his heart out every day of their marriage. You can count on me to say that to anyone who’ll listen! I won’t stand for men leading young girls on like that, and if I ever run into him again I’ll give him a dressing-down he won’t forget for years. Still, there’s a comfort, my sweet Marianne—he’s not the only man worth having in the whole world, and with that pretty face of yours, you’ll never lack for admirers. I won’t fuss over you any longer, let you cry it all out, that’s the best way. Lucky the Parrys and Sandersons are coming over tonight, they’ll cheer you right up a bit.”
She tiptoed out of the room after that, as if even the soft thud of her slippers might make Marianne’s grief sharper.
To Elinor’s great surprise, Marianne insisted on coming down to dinner. Elinor tried to talk her out of it, but Marianne shook her head, eyes red-rimmed and hollow. “No, I’ll go down. I can bear it. If I stay locked up here, you’ll all fuss over me twice as much. It’s quieter among a crowd where no one’s paying me any mind.” Elinor, relieved to see her acting on even that small practical impulse, didn’t argue further. She helped Marianne smooth her crumpled gown while the girl still sat on the edge of the bed, and walked her down to the dining room the second the dinner bell rang.
Marianne looked wretched, her face drained of all color, but she ate a few more bites than Elinor dared hope, and stayed far calmer than expected. She never spoke a single word, though, too lost in her own swirling, hurting thoughts to even notice the world around her. If she had caught half of Mrs. Jennings’ well-meaning, unsubtle attentions—piling the richest meat on her plate, pressing every sweet delicacy within reach, chattering a mile a minute about trivial neighborhood news to distract her—she would never have held that calm.
Elinor thanked their kind hostess warmly on her sister’s behalf, when Marianne could not rouse herself to do so. Mrs. Jennings doted on Marianne that whole meal, the way a parent indulges their favorite child on the last day of their holiday. She staked claim to the warmest spot by the fire for her, nudged every treat within arm’s reach, and kept up a steady stream of light, silly stories meant to pull Marianne out of her dark mood. If Elinor’s chest hadn’t been tight with her sister’s grief, she might have laughed at the sight of Mrs. Jennings trying to cure a broken heart with plates of olives, heaped sweetmeats, and a roaring hearth fire.
It didn’t last long, though. When Mrs. Jennings repeated her little distractions three times over, Marianne finally jolted back to awareness. She gasped out a quiet cry of “Misery!” and waved Elinor off sharply so her sister wouldn’t follow, then fled the room as fast as she could.
“Poor soul!” Mrs. Jennings exclaimed the second the door clicked shut behind her. “It breaks my heart to see her so. She didn’t even finish her glass of wine, or the dried cherries I set out just for her! Lord, nothing seems to cheer her at all. I’d send all over town for anything I thought she might fancy, I swear I would. It’s the strangest thing, that a man could treat such a pretty, sweet girl so ill! But when one side has piles of money and the other has next to nothing, they don’t care a fig for love, do they?”
“The lady—Miss Grey, you said her name was—she’s very wealthy then?” Elinor asked.
“Fifty thousand pounds a year, my dear! Have you ever seen her? They say she’s a sharp, stylish girl, but no great beauty. I knew her aunt Biddy Henshawe well, she married an enormously rich man, the whole family’s rolling in money. Fifty thousand! And he needs it badly too, everyone says he’s deep in debt, what with his fancy curricle and his expensive hunters! No wonder he ran off. But that’s no excuse. A young man goes and courts a pretty girl, promises her the whole future, he has no right to break his word just because he’s short on cash and a richer girl offers to take him. Why couldn’t he sell his horses, let his big house, send his servants away, and cut back on his silly spending? I’d bet Marianne would have waited for him happily till he got back on his feet. But young men these days won’t give up a single bit of their pleasure, not for anything.”
“Do you know what Miss Grey is like? Is she said to be amiable?”
“I never heard a bad word about her, hardly ever heard her mentioned at all. Except Mrs. Taylor saying this morning that Miss Walker told her that Mr. and Mrs. Ellison—her guardians, you know—are dead glad she’s marrying, because she and Mrs. Ellison could never get along for five minutes.”
After a pause, Mrs. Jennings clucked her tongue. “Your poor sister’s gone up to her room to wail all alone now, isn’t she? There’s nothing we can do to cheer her up? Well, soon all our friends will arrive, that’ll take her mind off things a little. What game shall we play? I know she hates whist, is there any round game she might like?”
“Dear ma’am, your kindness is more than enough, but it’s quite unnecessary,” Elinor said softly. “Marianne won’t be coming back down this evening, I’m sure. I’ll persuade her to go to bed early, she’s terribly run down and needs rest.”
“Aye, that’s the best thing for her. Let her order whatever little supper she fancies and go straight to bed. No wonder she’s looked so pale and downcast these past two weeks! I suppose this awful news has been hanging over her all that time, and that letter that came this morning finished breaking her heart. I’m so sorry I teased her about letters the other day, I’d never have done that if I’d guessed what was going on! But how was I to know? I thought it was just a silly love note, and young people love to be teased about their sweethearts. Sir John and my daughters will be so upset when they hear! I ought to have stopped by Conduit Street on my way home to tell them, but I’ll see them tomorrow.”
“I would be so grateful if you’d ask Sir John and Mrs. Palmer never to mention Mr. Willoughby, not even to make the smallest allusion to what happened, in front of Marianne,” Elinor said earnestly. “Their own good nature will tell them how cruel it would be to let on they know anything about it when she’s near. The less anyone says to me on the subject, the easier it will be for me too, I’m sure you understand.”
“Lord yes, of course I do! It must be terrible for you to hear it talked of. I wouldn’t breathe a word of it to your sister for the whole world, you saw I didn’t say a thing all through dinner. Sir John and my girls are all thoughtful, I’ll give them a quiet hint, they’ll be as careful as can be. The less you talk about these heartbreaks, the faster they blow over and get forgotten. What good does gossiping do, anyway?”
“None at all in this case,” Elinor said. “It can only do harm, worse than most other such stories, because there are circumstances here that no one else needs to know, for everyone’s sake. I must do Mr. Willoughby that much justice—he never made any formal, binding promise to Marianne.”
“Law, my dear, don’t go defending him! No formal promise? He dragged her all over Allenham House, pointed out exactly which bedchambers they would live in after they were married! That’s as good as a promise to anyone with half a sense.”
Elinor didn’t push the matter further, for her sister’s sake. She knew no good would come of bringing the full truth out, not for Marianne, not for Willoughby. After a short quiet pause, Mrs. Jennings brightened up again, ever her usual cheerful self.
“Well, it’s an ill wind that blows no good, isn’t it? This will all work out for Colonel Brandon! He’ll have her in the end, mark my words. They’ll be married by midsummer, you’ll see! He’ll be over the moon when he hears this news. I hope he comes tonight. It’s a far better match for your sister, two thousand a year, no debts at all—except that little orphan girl he takes care of, but you can apprentice her out for almost nothing, it’s no trouble at all. Delaford is the loveliest place, all old-fashioned and cozy, with high garden walls draped with the best fruit trees in the whole county, and a mulberry tree in the back corner! Lord, your daughter Charlotte and I stuffed ourselves sick on mulberries the last time we visited. There’s a dove cote, lovely little stew ponds, a pretty little canal, everything you could ever want. It’s right next to the church, only a quarter mile from the turnpike road, so you never get bored—sit in the old yew arbour behind the house and you can see every carriage that goes past. A butcher lives right in the village, the parsonage is a stone’s throw away. It’s a thousand times nicer than Barton Park, where you have to send three miles for a cut of meat and the nearest neighbour is your own mother! As soon as I can, I’ll go cheer the Colonel up. One sorrow drives out another, right? We just have to get Willoughby out of her head.”
“If we can do that, ma’am,” Elinor said with a faint, tired smile, “we’ll manage perfectly well, with or without Colonel Brandon.”
She stood up then to go find Marianne, and found her exactly where she expected, huddled over the last smoldering embers of the fire, no other light in the room.
“Please, leave me alone,” was all Marianne said to her when she came in.
“I will leave you,” Elinor promised, “if you’ll get into bed.”
Marianne fought her at first, too raw and restless with pain to lie still, but Elinor’s soft, steady persuasion soon won her over. She laid her aching head on the pillow, and Elinor left her hoping she might get a few hours of quiet rest.
When she went back down to the drawing room, Mrs. Jennings came bustling in holding a crystal wine glass brimmed with dark amber liquid. “My dear, I just remembered I’ve got a bottle of the finest old Constantia wine in the whole house, it was my poor late husband’s favorite. Whenever he had that old colicky gout of his, he swore it did him more good than any medicine the doctor ever gave him. I brought a glass up for your sister!”
“Dear ma’am, you are too kind,” Elinor laughed softly, thinking of how different a broken heart was from a gouty stomach. “But I just left Marianne in bed, almost asleep. Rest is the only thing that will help her right now, if you don’t mind I’ll drink it myself.”
Mrs. Jennings was a little disappointed she hadn’t made it to Marianne five minutes earlier, but agreed to the compromise. Elinor drank most of the wine, and thought that if it could soothe a colicky gout, it might very well soothe a heavy, worried heart just as well.
Colonel Brandon walked in just as they were sitting down to tea. Elinor saw his eyes drift immediately around the room, searching for Marianne, and she knew at once he had not expected to find her there. He already knew she was gone.
Mrs. Jennings didn’t notice, though, leaning over to whisper at Elinor behind her teacup, “The Colonel looks as grave as a judge, he hasn’t heard a thing. Go tell him, dear.”
A few minutes later he pulled a chair up next to Elinor, and the quiet look in his eyes told her he already knew all of it, before he even opened his mouth to ask after her sister.
“Marianne is not well,” Elinor said. “She’s been unwell all day, we persuaded her to go to bed early.”
“Then,” he said hesitantly, “what I heard this morning might—there might be more truth to it than I could force myself to believe at first.”
“What did you hear?”
“I was in a stationer’s shop on Pall Mall, running an errand. Two ladies were waiting for their carriage, and one was talking about a coming match, not even bothering to keep her voice down, so I couldn’t help overhearing. The name John Willoughby came up over and over, and she said the marriage to Miss Grey was all settled, not a secret any longer, it would happen in just a few weeks, and as soon as the ceremony was over they would go straight to Combe Magna, his estate in Somersetshire. I can’t tell you how shocked I was. I waited in the shop till they left, asked who the talkative lady was, and it was Mrs. Ellison—Miss Grey’s guardian, as I later found out.”
“That’s correct,” Elinor nodded. “And you may have heard Miss Grey has fifty thousand pounds. That, if anything, is the reason for all this.”
“It may be,” he said, stopping for a moment, his voice soft and unsure. “And your sister—how did she take it?”
“Her pain is very sharp. I can only hope it fades quickly. It was a cruel shock. Up till yesterday she never doubted he loved her, and even now—she will still make excuses for him, if she can. He was very deceitful, and there’s a cold hardness in him I never expected.”
“Ah, there is,” Colonel Brandon said. He didn’t say anything more, and soon the tea things were cleared away and everyone settled down to their card games, so the conversation was dropped. Mrs. Jennings watched them chattering happily a little while, fully expecting that once Elinor told him the news the Colonel would light up, giddy with joy now that his rival was gone. But to her complete surprise, he stayed far more quiet, thoughtful, and grave all evening than he had ever been before.
