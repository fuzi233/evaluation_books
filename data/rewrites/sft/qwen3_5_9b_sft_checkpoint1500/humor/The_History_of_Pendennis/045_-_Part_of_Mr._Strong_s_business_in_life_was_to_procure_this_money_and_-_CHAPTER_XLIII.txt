Alsatia

Nestled somewhere behind the soot-stained gables and grimy chimneys of Wych Street and Holywell Street, hidden away like a secret from the bright outer world, lay the courtyard of Shepherd's Inn. It was a place where the sun rarely ventured, accessible only through winding, smoke-laden alleys that smelled of damp pavement and endless mud. Here, amidst the clutter of slop-sellers, brandy-ball hawkers, and peddlers of dingy furniture, life moved in a chaotic, noisy rhythm. Ballad singers chanted bitter satires against Whigs, bishops, and German royals in guttural tones, while Punch set up his tiny theatre, hoping to extract a halfpenny from the swarming tenants. Inside the courtyard, however, lay a tranquil, old-fashioned quadrangle. In its center stood a mangy grass plat guarded by iron railings, protecting the statue of Shepherd from the assault of local boys.

Though the Inn had once housed serious lawyers, the legal giants had long since vacated its precincts for grander chambers. Now, it was home to the whimsical and the weary. One ground-floor suite belonged to the Polwheedle and Tredyddlum Copper Mines, another to the "Union of Genius and Capital Company." Yet, among these fantastical enterprises, the only gentleman listed in the Law List was Mr. Campion. He wore distinctive mustachios and arrived in his cab twice or thrice a week. While known as Mr. Somerset Campion in the West End, where his wife entertained the gentry, within these walls he operated as Campion and Co. His cab-horse's harness blazed with heraldic bearings, and the groom's white breeches gleamed in the dim light, casting a temporary sunshine over the shadowy square.

Captain Costigan, our old friend, was a regular fixture here. Dressed in carpet slippers, a dressing-gown, and an old hat cocked over his eye, he trailed about the court, examining Campion's cab and horse with practiced interest. After breakfast, he would visit the porter's lodge, patting the heads of the children and chatting with Mrs. Bolton about theatres and her daughter, Leedy Mirabel. Mrs. Bolton, a former dancer at the Wells, remembered the glory days well.

Costigan lived in the third floor of No. 4, formerly the chambers of Charley Podmore, the pleasing tenor singer. Now shared with Mr. Bows, the room echoed with the sounds of a piano whenever the windows were open. Fanny Bolton, the porteress's daughter, was one of Mr. Bows's pupils. With a good voice and a pretty figure, she dreamed of emulating her mother's theatrical glories, preparing meals for the gentlemen in exchange for musical lessons. Though she suspected the Captain indulged in his "unfortunate propensity to liquor," she considered him the finest gentleman in the world, believing every tale he spun. Her fatherly fondness for the shy, kind-hearted Captain was mutual; indeed, his heart was so full of love that he felt uneasy without someone to care for.

Captain Costigan had once hosted carriages of distinction, boasting of the select nobility visiting the "old man, the honest old half-pay Captain." In truth, Lady Mirabel had left her husband's card on the mantelpiece months prior, coming personally to see her father only on rare occasions. Upon marrying Sir Charles, she had settled a small pension on him, admitting him to her table. Initially, Costigan's behavior, though absurd, was harmless. Clad in his best attire, he employed the longest, richest words in his vocabulary, adopting a solemnity that baffled everyone present. "Was your Leedyship in the Pork to dee?" he would ask, demanding to know if his daughter's chariot had appeared in town. He thanked the servants and toasted the company with champagne, treating the domestics with the same magnificence as the masters.

However, familiarity bred disaster. After a few visits, Costigan's caution vanished. During his evening sessions at the Back Kitchen, he would brag of his son-in-law's wine until his speech failed him following his sixth tumbler of whisky-punch. Eventually, he lamentably disgraced himself at Sir Charles's table by premature inebriation. A carriage was summoned, and the hospitable door shut upon him. Heartbroken, Cos spoke to his friends at the Kitchen of his resemblance to King Lear, complaining of his thankless child and describing himself as a poor, worn-out old man driven to drinking by ingratitude.

When his funds ran dry, Costigan would frequently beg money from his daughter, sometimes making statements inconsistent with the truth. Once, when a bailiff threatened imprisonment, he wrote a desperate plea for three pounds and five shillines to liberate his "grey hairs" from gaol. Lady Mirabel, naturally good-natured, sent the sum with a stern warning to be more economical. Later, after breaking a plate-glass window in the Strand, she again advanced the necessary funds. If one had followed the remittance, they would have seen her servant trailing behind the Captain's "aide-de-camp"—an equally impoverished Irishman—to the Roscius Head in Drury Lane, where the Captain was currently in pawn for rum-and-water.

On a third occasion, writing of an illness requiring a physician, Lady Mirabel, alarmed and perhaps guilt-ridden, drove to Shepherd's Inn. The porteress directed her to "No. 4, third floor, name of Podmore." She found the door opened by poor Cos in his shirt-sleeves, ready to receive mutton-chops on a gridiron purchased by Mrs. Bolton. Meanwhile, Sir Charles Mirabel suffered greatly. Letters addressed to him at Brookes's announced that Captain Costigan was in the hall waiting for an answer. Playing whist at the Travellers', he feared his father-in-law would seize him, stalking the streets with a cocked hat and a steady gaze fixed on the club windows. Weak, old, and infirm, Sir Charles cried to his wife, vowing to move abroad or to the country, certain that seeing Costigan again would kill him.

It required a stern second visit from Lady Mirabel to restore peace. Representing to Costigan that further harassment or loan requests would result in the withdrawal of his allowance, she managed to keep her papa in order. She also severely rebuked Mr. Bows, accusing him of abetting her father's conduct and failing to watch him properly. "Papa's conduct is bringing me to the grave," she declared, though she looked perfectly healthy. These were the thanks honest Bows received for his lifelong devotion, a burden he likely bore with little complaint.

Two floors above, at No. 3, lived two other acquaintances: Colonel Altamont, agent to the Nawaub of Lucknow, and Captain Chevalier Edward Strong. Their door bore no name. Strong preferred to conceal his address, using a Jermyn Street hotel card instead, while Altamont, the "Ambassador Plenipotentiary," conducted a confidential mission independent of the East India Company. They sought to sell the principal diamonds and rubies of the Lucknow crown to private capitalists, avoiding official scrutiny.

Strong's chambers, comfortably furnished since Sir Francis Clavering's arrival, boasted three rooms below—an office retaining the desks of departed officials, Strong's bedroom, and a sitting-room. A private stair led to two upper apartments: one for Altamont, the other a kitchen and bedroom for Mr. Grady, the attendant. From Grady's casement floated the most fragrant odors imaginable. The three old soldiers forming the garrison of No. 3 were culinary masters. Grady excelled at Irish stew; the Colonel was famous for pilau and curry; and Strong, when his purse was full, could cook anything from French dishes to omelettes to perfection. He was the most hospitable man in England when credit was good, offering friends a good dinner, a good glass of wine, and a good song. Poor Cos often heard with envy the roar of Strong's choruses and the musical clinking of glasses, wishing he were closer to those festivities, though he wisely avoided inviting Costigan due to his drunken tendencies.

The company surrounding the Chevalier was a strange, motley assembly, each member carrying a history of tide-like fortunes and misfortunes. Jack Holt, formerly in Don Carlos's army, organized a tobacco-smuggling scheme promising thirty thousand a year. Tom Diver, ex-Mexican navy, claimed knowledge of a sunken specie-ship holding hundreds of thousands in gold. Keightley, manager of the underwater copper mines, eyed a Smyrna Sponge Company and quicksilver operations. Then there was Filby, a former field-preacher and actor, who inherited a mysterious property from his deceased father, yielding no rents. And finally, Sir Francis Clavering, Bart., who, despite lacking convivial powers, was cherished for his wealth and position, having weathered his own share of prisons and stamped papers.

When Altamont first returned from Paris, stripped of his wealth but entrusted with the jewels, Strong was sent to accompany him by Sir Francis. Strong grumbled to Clavering about housing Altamont, complaining that the fellow dressed like a "nigger on a holiday" and behaved wildly at the theater. Altamont had once screamed and wept over a fictional death in *The Brigand*, causing a scene that nearly turned the audience out. Strong pressed Clavering for the truth of their connection, suspecting a robbery or some dark secret, urging the Baronet to confess before it was too late.
