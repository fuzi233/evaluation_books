Old Whateley bought cattle, yet the herd did not swell. He cut timber and mended the house—a sprawling, peaked structure swallowed by rock at its rear, where three ground-floor rooms had sufficed for generations. There existed a prodigious reserve of strength in the old man, allowing him to undertake such labor despite the intermittent babble of dementia. His carpentry, however, betrayed a cold, calculating logic. The restoration began with Wilbur's birth: a toolshed clad in clapboard, secured with a fresh lock. Now, the upper story was reclaimed with similar thoroughness. His madness manifested only in the tight boarding of every window in the restored section, a futile barrier against a silence he seemed determined to enforce.

He fitted a new room downstairs for his grandson, accessible to callers, while the upper chamber remained a sealed tomb. Inside, tall shelving rose like skeletal fingers. Upon them, Whateley arranged, with obsessive care, the rotting fragments of ancient books scattered through the house for decades. "I made some use of 'em," he muttered, applying paste heated on a rusted stove to mend a torn black-letter page. "But the boy is fitten to make better use of 'em. He'd orter hev 'em as well sot as he kin for they're goin' to be all of his larnin'."

By September 1914, when Wilbur was a year and seven months old, his growth was alarming. He possessed the stature of a four-year-old but spoke with the fluency of a scholar. He roamed the fields, accompanying his mother, yet at home, he pored over the strange charts in his grandfather's library. Long, hushed afternoons were spent in instruction, the air thick with the weight of forbidden knowledge. As the house restoration concluded, observers noted the peculiar transformation of a rear window into a solid plank door. A cleated wooden runway ascended the hill to meet it, a path to nowhere. Shortly thereafter, the toolhouse, locked and blind since Wilbur's birth, fell silent again.

Earl Sawyer, returning from a cattle sale, opened the toolhouse door and recoiled. The odor was singular, a stench unlike any he had known save for the Indian circles on the hills—an aroma that suggested something unsanctioned by nature, something unearthly. Yet Dunwich folk rarely prided themselves on olfactory refinement.

The following months offered no visible drama, save for a slow, steady intensification of the hill noises. On May Eve, 1915, tremors shook the Aylesbury valley. By Halloween, the ground rumbled beneath bursts of flame erupting from Sentinel Hill—"them witch Whateleys' doin's," the locals whispered.

Wilbur changed. Entering his fourth year, he resembled a ten-year-old boy. Reading became his solitary pursuit; conversation ceased. A settled taciturnity absorbed him, replacing his former chatter. For the first time, neighbors spoke openly of the dawning evil in his goatish face. He muttered unfamiliar jargons, chanting in rhythms that chilled listeners with a primal terror. Dogs, once indifferent, now fled the property and refused to approach the boy. To walk safely across the countryside, Wilbur carried a pistol, though its occasional discharge failed to earn him respect among the village's canine guardians.

Visitors found Lavinia alone on the ground floor, while odd cries echoed from the sealed second story. She never explained the activities upstairs, though she turned pale with an abnormal dread when a jocose fish-peddler attempted the locked stairway door. The peddler claimed to hear a horse stamping above. The villagers, recalling the cattle that vanished and the sealed runways, shuddered at the memory of Old Whateley's youth and the sacrifices demanded by heathen gods. Soon, the entire neighborhood repulsed by the presence of the boy, mirroring the specific hatred the dogs held for him.

Then came 1917. War descended upon the region. As chairman of the local draft board, Squire Sawyer Whateley struggled to find a single young Dunwich man fit enough for a development camp. Alarmed by this wholesale regional decadence, the government dispatched officers and medical experts. Their survey, later read by New England newspapers, drew reporters to the Whateley farm. The *Boston Globe* and *Arkham Advertiser* published flamboyant accounts of Wilbur's precocity, Old Whateley's black magic, the shelves of strange books, and the sealed chambers of the ancient farmhouse.

At four and a half years old, Wilbur looked fifteen. Coarse dark down covered his lips and cheeks; his voice broke. Earl Sawyer escorted two teams of reporters and photographers to the property, drawing their attention to the foul trickle emanating from the sealed upper spaces. It smelled, he insisted, exactly like the abandoned toolshed and the faint odors near the mountain stones.

The Dunwich folk read the papers and grinned at the obvious errors. They wondered why the writers fixated on the fact that Old Whateley always paid for his cattle in gold coins of extreme antiquity. The Whateleys received the visitors with ill-concealed distaste, refusing to court further scandal with violence, though they dared not refuse entry outright.
