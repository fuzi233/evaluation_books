Now that this volume is finally in print and ready to meet the public eye, a profound sense of inadequacy regarding both its style and substance weighs heavily upon me. Concerning the content, let it be clear that this is not intended to be a comprehensive chronicle of every event and sight encountered during our expedition into the heart of Kukuanaland. There are countless details—particularly the fascinating legends surrounding the chain armor that spared us in the Battle of Loo, and the enigmatic "Silent Ones" or Colossi guarding the entrance to the stalactite cavern—that I would have loved to explore at length. Had I followed my initial impulses, I might also have delved deeper into the intriguing linguistic divergences between the Zulu and Kukuana dialects, or dedicated space to describing the unique flora and fauna of this distant land.

Yet, the most compelling topic of all—the magnificent military organization of the Kukuanas, which I believe surpasses even the systems established by Chaka in Zululand due to its speed of mobilization and rejection of forced celibacy—has barely been touched upon. Nor have I fully addressed their domestic customs, which are often remarkably peculiar, nor their exceptional mastery of metallurgy. Their ability to smelt and weld metal is nothing short of remarkable, perfectly exemplified by their *tollas*, those formidable throwing knives featuring backs of hammered iron fused with expertly crafted steel edges.

In consultation with Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good, we concluded that the wisest course was to recount our tale plainly and directly, leaving room for future elaboration on these specialized subjects. For now, I pledge to share any further information within my reach with anyone who seeks it. Finally, I must apologize for the bluntness of my prose. To explain: I am far more accustomed to handling a rifle than a pen, and I cannot claim the ability to produce the ornate literary flourishes found in fiction. While I enjoy reading novels, I maintain that simple truths strike with greater impact and clarity when expressed without decoration. As the Kukuana proverb states, "A sharp spear needs no polish." By the same token, I trust that a true story, however extraordinary, requires no embellishment of fine words to hold its value.

*[1] During my travels, I identified eight varieties of hitherto unknown antelope and numerous new plant species, mostly belonging to the bulbous family.—A.Q.]*
