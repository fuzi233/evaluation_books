To begin knowing someone, or something, is first to carve a shape out of one's ignorance. For Gwendolen, the desire to meet Mr. Grandcourt had ripened slowly within her, like a seed waiting for spring. Yet, when Lord Brackenshaw stepped back to reveal the stranger, a sudden jolt struck her, flushing her cheeks with a vexatious heat. The shock lay not in his presence, but in his absence of expectation; he was the antithesis of every portrait she had painted in her mind.

He stood slightly taller than she, their eyes meeting on a level plane. There was no smile upon his lips, no flicker of self-consciousness or anxiety in his posture. As he raised his hat, it revealed an extensive baldness framed by a sparse, reddish-blonde fringe, yet his hands were perfect instruments, and his features, from brow to chin, possessed a handsome, unadorned symmetry. There was absolutely no grimace, no solicitous wiggle of the body. If a man could exist entirely free of animation, he did so now. While the "correct" Englishman draws himself up from a bow into a rigid stiffness, suggesting a suppressed vitality that might explode later, Grandcourt's bearing leaned toward the flaccid. His complexion held a faded fairness, like an actress stripped of artifice, and his long, narrow gray eyes expressed a profound, unyielding indifference.

Conversations are built on pauses, and here the silence was heavy. Grandcourt looked at Gwendolen with a persistent, slightly exploring gaze, his expression unmoving, while she offered him occasional flashes of observation, softened only by a touch of coquetry. After her answers, he allowed a longer silence before speaking again.

"I used to think archery was a great bore," Grandcourt began. His voice had a fine accent, though delivered with a broken drawl, as if he suffered from a distinguished cold.

"Are you converted today?" Gwendolen asked.

During the pause, Gwendolen imagined various opinions he might hold of her.

"Yes, since I saw you shooting. In things of this sort, one generally sees people missing and simpering."

"I suppose you are a first-rate shot with a rifle."

Another pause, during which Gwendolen mentally sketched a description of him for an indefinite listener.

"I have left off shooting."

"Oh, then you are a formidable person," Gwendolen replied, interpreting his words. "People who have done things once and left them off make one feel very contemptible, as if one were wearing cast-off fashions. I hope you have not left off all follies, because I practice a great many."

"What do you call follies?"

"Well, in general, I think, whatever is agreeable is called a folly. But you have not left off hunting, I hear."

Gwendolen recalled what she had heard of Grandcourt's station and decided he was the most aristocratic-looking man she had ever seen.

"One must do something."

"And do you care about the turf? Or is that among the things you have left off?"

"I run a horse now and then, but I don't go in for the thing as some men do. Are you fond of horses?"

"Yes, indeed! I never like my life so well as when I am on horseback, having a great gallop. I think of nothing. I only feel myself strong and happy."

"Do you like danger?"

"I don't know. When I am on horseback, I never think of danger. It seems to me that if I broke my bones, I should not feel it. I should go at anything that came in my way."

"You would perhaps like tiger-hunting or pig-sticking. I saw some of that for a season or two in the East. Everything here is poor stuff after that."

"You are fond of danger, then?"

Gwendolen wondered if a man of such cold manners could truly be dangerous, yet she felt the strength of her own insight regarding the nature of adventure.

"One must have something or other. But one gets used to it."

"I begin to think I am very fortunate, because everything is new to me: it is only that I can't get enough of it. I am not used to anything except being dull, which I should like to leave off as you have left off shooting."

"Why are you dull?"

"This is a dreadful neighborhood. There is nothing to be done in it. That is why I practiced my archery."

Gwendolen reflected that the life of an unmarried woman, with no command over her surroundings, must inevitably become dull.

"You have made yourself queen of it. I imagine you will carry the first prize."

"I don't know that. I have great rivals. Did you not observe how well Miss Arrowpoint shot?"

"Miss Arrowpoint. No—that is, yes."

"Shall we go now and hear what the scoring says? Every one is going to the other end now—shall we join them? I think my uncle is looking toward me. He perhaps wants me."

Gwendolen found relief in shifting the situation. The tête-à-tête was not entirely disagreeable, but she could not shake the unwonted flush in her cheeks or the sense of surprise that made her feel less mistress of herself than usual. This Mr. Grandcourt, who seemed to feel his own importance more than hers, must not assume that he was of great moment to her. How Grandcourt had filled the pauses would become clearer later.

"You have just missed the gold arrow, Gwendolen," said Mr. Gascoigne. "Miss Juliet Fenn scores eight above you."

"I am very glad to hear it. I should have felt that I was making myself too disagreeable—taking the best of everything," Gwendolen said easily.

It was impossible to be jealous of Juliet Fenn, a girl as middling as midday market in everything save her archery and plainness, in which latter trait she resembled her father: underhung jaw and a receding brow, like the more intelligent fishes. Surely, considering the weight placed on such accidents in female offspring, intending bridegrooms should look at themselves dispassionately, lest their natural selection of a prettier mate prove futile due to their own ugliness.

A lively movement swept through the mingling groups, carrying the talk with it. Everyone spoke to everyone else by turns. Gwendolen, choosing to observe the scene, noted that Grandcourt was having Klesmer presented to him by a middle-aged man with a dark, full face and fat hands, who seemed on easy terms with both. The stranger led them to join the Arrowpoints, whose acquaintance Grandcourt and Klesmer had already made. Gwendolen cared little for the stranger's identity but wished to watch Grandcourt's manner toward others. It was precisely the same as before, except that he did not look at Miss Arrowpoint; rather, he watched Klesmer, who spoke with animation—stretching long fingers, pointing downward, tossing his mane—while addressing himself to both men. Grandcourt listened with an impassive face and narrow eyes, his left forefinger in his waistcoat pocket and his right lightly touching his thin whisker.

"I wonder which style Miss Arrowpoint admires most," Gwendolen thought, a mocking expression gathering at her lips. But she restrained herself from indulging curiosity, giving all her animation to those immediately around her, determined not to care whether Mr. Grandcourt approached her again or not.

He did approach, however, at a moment when he could propose conducting Mrs. Davilow to her carriage. "Shall we meet again in the ball-room?" she said as he raised his hat. The reply was a slight drawl of "Yes" with perfect gravity.

"You were wrong for once, Gwendolen," said Mrs. Davilow during their short drive to the castle.

"In what, Mamma?"

"About Mr. Grandcourt's appearance and manners. You can't find anything ridiculous in him."

"I suppose I could if I tried, but I don't want to do it," Gwendolen said pettishly, and her mother dared say no more.

By custom, ladies and gentlemen dined apart, allowing for comparative ease. Indeed, the gentlemen had spun stories of the ladies' epicureanism, reporting revolting masculine judgments in venison, even requests for the fat—a proof of the frightful rate at which corruption might spread in women, were it not for severe social restraint. Every year, the amiable Lord Brackenshaw, somewhat of a gourmet, cited Byron's opinion that a woman should never be seen eating, introducing it with a confidential, "The fact is," as if admitting a secret concurrence.

In the ladies' dining-room, Gwendolen was not a general favorite. There were no intimacies forming with the other girls; in conversation, they noticed what she said rather than speaking freely to her. Perhaps it was that she was not much interested in them, and when left alone, she felt a sense of empty benches. Mrs. Vulcany once remarked that Miss Harleth was too fond of the gentlemen, but Gwendolen knew she was not fond of them; she was only fond of their homage, and women did not give her homage. The exception was Miss Arrowpoint, who often managed unostentatiously to be by her side, talking with quiet friendliness.

"She knows, as I do, that our friends are ready to quarrel over a husband for us," Gwendolen thought, "and she is determined not to enter into the quarrel."
