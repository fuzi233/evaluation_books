When the curtain fell on *Macbeth*, our party departed in high spirits for Mr. Warrington’s lodgings, where the young Virginians had arranged a lavish supper. Eager to play the gracious host, George spared no expense, and General and Mrs. Lambert accepted the invitation with pleasure, delighted to indulge their young friends. The gathering included the General, his lady, their son Jack—fresh from college—their two fair daughters, and Mr. Spencer of the Temple, a new acquaintance George had met at the coffee-house.

The seating was a matter of some mystery, though it was clear Miss Theo found herself beside the roasted chickens and Mr. George Warrington, while Miss Hetty divided her attention between Mr. Harry and a fine ham. Mrs. Lambert, no doubt, presided at George’s right hand, leaving the General, his son, and the Templar to arrange themselves as they saw fit.

The conversation turned to the evening’s performance, though opinions were divided.

“You mustn’t believe a word these gentlemen say, Mr. Spencer,” protested Mrs. Lambert. “It was a perfectly delightful play, and my husband and Mr. George behaved disgracefully!”

“We laughed where we ought to have wept,” admitted the General.

“You caused such a commotion, the whole box turned to hush you!” cried his wife. “I declare, I’ve never been so mortified!”

“Mamma thought it a tragedy; we thought it a farce,” said the General. “George and I were perfectly composed—weren’t we, Theo?”

“Not when I was watching you, Papa!” Theo replied with a sly smile.

“Insolent chit!” the General growled, though his eyes twinkled.

“I was moved, I confess,” Theo continued softly. “Mrs. Woffington was sublime—so tragic when the mother lost her child. But I suppose I ought not to have been affected?”

“Women are not as clever as men, you know,” Hetty teased from her end of the table, casting a mischievous glance at Harry.

“Next time, Jack must nudge us when to laugh and when to cry,” Theo agreed.

The General lamented the missing duel between Norval and the giant Norwegian. “Now *that* would have been worth seeing! Jack, you must write to Mr. Rich and suggest it.”

Harry, ever earnest, remarked, “I’ve not seen that, but I *have* seen Slack and Broughton at Marybone Gardens!” The company burst into laughter, though Harry looked puzzled at his own success.

Mr. Spencer, the evening’s wit, praised Garrick’s performance. “His Macbeth was masterful! And Lady Macbeth—how she chilled the blood! You should have seen Mr. Warrington in the greenroom, ladies. Mrs. Pritchard herself took a pinch from his snuffbox!”

“Did the Thane’s lady sneeze?” the General asked gravely.

“She thanked him in such ghastly tones, poor Macbeth started back and sent a cloud of rappee into the air!”

“Macbeth, Macbeth, Macbeth!” the General intoned, while the table dissolved into laughter.

Theo sighed. “Oh, to see behind the scenes! The ropes, the candles, the painted faces!”

George smirked. “You’d weep less if you saw the artifice. The hero’s grandeur is but rant, the mother’s grief a handkerchief.”

“You think me foolish for being moved?” Theo challenged.

“I think you kind-hearted,” George replied, meeting her gaze until Hetty’s bright eyes made them both glance away.

The talk turned to plays, old and new. Mr. Spencer dismissed the ancients. “Shakespeare knew nothing of true pathos compared to the moderns. Where is his Belvidera, his Monimia?”

“Have *you* written, Mr. Spencer?” someone asked.

“Garrick himself rejected a piece of mine,” the Templar admitted.

George confessed to four acts of a tragedy lurking in his trunk.

“Fetch it at once!” Hetty demanded.

“Alas, it lies buried beneath linen and boots at Bristol,” George lamented.

With mock despair, the ladies groaned, and the General proposed a toast: “To Madam Esmond Warrington! Who knows? The boys may return to find a stepfather awaiting them!”

Mrs. Lambert raised her glass. “I shall write and tell her what fine hosts her sons are—though I’ll omit their conduct at the play!”

The party departed in good humor, the General’s coach waiting to convey them home.

***

The next morning found George at the Lamberts’ door in Dean Street, feigning casual inquiry after their well-being. Inside, he found Theo alone, her cheeks as fresh as the roses beside her.

“Your brothers are gone to visit the youngest at Charterhouse,” she said, needle busy in her hand. “Harry comes often—now that the fine folk no longer court him.”

“And you, Theo—do you still think me heartless for laughing at the play?”

She met his eyes. “Perhaps I was wrong.”

Their talk was interrupted by the return of Mrs. Lambert and Hetty, full of news: Harry was restless, longing for purpose. He haunted the Military Coffee-House, poring over campaigns.

“He said to Jack, ‘Why can’t I be a soldier? I eat my brother’s bread while he shares his last crust with me,’” Mrs. Lambert confided.

George revealed he had written home, offering half—or all—his patrimony for Harry’s commission.

“He has a good brother,” Mrs. Lambert said softly.

“The Danes come to those who wait,” George murmured.

“Ah! You heard that? My Theo is—” She broke off, eyes shining.

Later, George recounted his disastrous audience with the Duke. Offered a place in the Prince’s campaign, he had dared to propose Harry in his stead.

The Prince’s face darkened. “You refuse *me*?”

“I study law, sir, but my brother—”

“Enough!” The Duke turned his back.

“George, George,” groaned General Lambert. “What a fine mess you’ve made!”

And so the chapter closed—on laughter, hopes, and royal displeasure.
