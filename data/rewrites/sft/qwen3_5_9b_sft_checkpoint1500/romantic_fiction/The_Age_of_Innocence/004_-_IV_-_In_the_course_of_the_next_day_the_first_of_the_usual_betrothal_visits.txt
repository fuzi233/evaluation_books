The following morning, the formalities of courtship unfolded with the precision of a well-oiled machine. New York society operated on a schedule as rigid as its calendar, and Newland Archer moved through the rituals with practiced ease. First, accompanied by his mother and sister, he presented himself to Mrs. Welland. Then, the trio—Archer, his mother, and May—departed for the mansion of Old Mrs. Manson Mingott to secure the blessing of the clan's matriarch.

For a young man of Newland's standing, a visit to Mrs. Mingott was less a social obligation than a theatrical diversion. Her residence on Fifth Avenue stood as a monument to a specific, flamboyant era, though it lacked the stolid, Gothic grandeur of the older homes nearby. Where those neighbors clung to the somber dignity of 1830s design—with heavy rosewood consoles, cabbage-rose garlands, and massive mahogany bookcases—Mrs. Mingott had swept the past aside. She had traded her ancestral heaviness for the frivolous opulence of the Second Empire, filling her space with upholstery that spoke of Parisian cafes rather than provincial parlor rooms.

Her daily routine was equally performative. She preferred to sit in a window of her ground-floor salon, observing the world pass by with the calm detachment of a queen watching ants march across a chessboard. There was no urgency in her posture, nor any anxiety about the changing times. To Mrs. Mingott, the chaotic sprawl of tenements, brick factories, and ragged gardens surrounding her estate was merely a temporary phase. She was convinced that soon, the asphalt would swallow the cobblestones, the sleek new mansions would eclipse the crumbling facades, and the world would finally look like the elegant capital of France she imagined it to be. For now, however, she remained the undisputed center of gravity, a sovereign who required no subjects to validate her reign.

Time and nature had transformed her physical form into something majestic and overwhelming. Once a plump, active woman with a neat ankle, she had succumbed to a deluge of flesh that now draped her like a living landscape. Yet, she wore this transformation with a philosopher's grace. In the mirror, she revealed an expanse of firm, unlined skin, pale as snow, where the faint contours of her youthful features lingered like ruins waiting to be excavated. Double chins cascaded smoothly toward a bosom swathed in snowy muslins, pinned by a miniature portrait of her late husband. Around her, waves of black silk spilled from a chair so vast it seemed to dwarf the occupant, leaving two tiny, porcelain hands resting atop the fabric like gulls perched on a storm-tossed sea.

Her mobility was limited by this accumulation of weight, rendering stairs impossible. With a characteristic disregard for convention, she had relocated her receiving rooms to the ground floor, defying the strictures of New York propriety that dictated a lady's reception areas be elevated above the domestic sphere. Consequently, guests seated in her salon often found themselves with a startling view into her private chambers through an open door and a looping curtain. They beheld a bedroom furnished with the audacity of a European novel: a low bed upholstered like a sofa, a vanity table fringed with lace, and a gilt mirror reflecting a space designed for intimacy rather than modesty.

Visitors were invariably struck by this arrangement, which felt dangerously akin to the illicit apartments of French literature. It suggested a freedom from restraint that the average American mind struggled to comprehend. To Newland Archer, who had once privately placed the romantic scenes of *Monsieur de Camors* within these walls, it was a fascinating paradox. He marveled at how this blameless woman conducted a life that mimicked the stage-set of adultery, yet he silently conceded that if a lover truly desired her, the intrepid Mrs. Mingott would surely have claimed him.

Fortunately, Countess Olenska was absent from the drawing room during the betrothed couple's visit. Mrs. Mingott noted casually that the Countess had gone out—a statement that, given the blazing sun and the shopping hour, carried a subtle sting of judgment regarding a "compromised" woman's conduct. However, her absence served a practical purpose: it spared the newly engaged pair the awkward shadow of Ellen's unhappy past, allowing the focus to remain squarely on their radiant future.

The reception proceeded smoothly. Mrs. Mingott, whose foresight had long predicted this union, expressed her delight. Her attention then turned to the engagement ring, a substantial sapphire set in invisible claws.

"The setting is quite modern," Mrs. Welland offered gently, glancing sideways at her future son-in-law. "Perhaps it appears a bit stark to traditional tastes."

"Stark? My dear, I embrace every novelty," Mrs. Mingott declared, lifting the gemstone to inspect it with bright, unaided eyes. "It is a masterpiece. Very liberal indeed. In my day, a cameo in pearls sufficed. But look at the hand holding it, Mr. Archer." She waved a slender finger, the wrist encircled by rolls of aged flesh that resembled ivory bracelets. "That hand was modeled in Rome by Ferrigiani yourself. You should commission May's hand similarly. No doubt he intends to. It is larger, of course—the modern sport spreads the joints—but the complexion is exquisite." She paused, her gaze sharpening as it fixed on Archer. "And when does the wedding take place?"

"Oh—" Mrs. Welland murmured softly. Archer, offering a warm smile to his fiancée, replied, "As soon as possible, if you will support me, Mrs. Mingott."

"Nonsense about taking time to know one another," the old lady interjected, dismissing the notion with a wave of her hand. "Everyone in New York knows everyone. Do not wait until the wine loses its bubbles. Marry them before Lent; I might catch pneumonia in this winter, and I intend to host the breakfast."

These declarations were met with the customary mixture of feigned surprise, amusement, and gratitude. As the visit began to wind down in a light-hearted tone, the front door swung open.

Countess Olenska entered, wrapped in a bonnet and mantle, followed closely by Julius Beaufort. A ripple of pleasurable murmurs ran through the female guests. Mrs. Mingott extended a hand toward the banker. "Ha! Beaufort, what a delightful surprise!" She addressed him with the familiar, slightly foreign cadence she reserved for intimates.

"Thank you," the banker replied with his customary air of arrogant ease. "I am rarely free, but I encountered Countess Ellen in Madison Square, and she kindly allowed me to escort her home."

"Ah! I trust the household will feel livelier now that Ellen returns!" Mrs. Mingott exclaimed, her voice ringing with genuine enthusiasm. "Do sit down, Beaufort. Push up that yellow armchair. Now that you are here, I require a lengthy chat. I heard your ball was a triumph, and I understand you extended an invitation to Mrs. Lemuel Struthers? Pray, tell me everything about this formidable widow."

Having forgotten the relatives who were quietly exiting under Ellen's direction, Mrs. Mingott launched into a monologue about the necessity of "new blood and new money." She expressed a voracious curiosity about Mrs. Struthers, a woman who had recently returned from Europe after years abroad, intent on besieging the tight little fortress of New York society. To the carnivorous old lady, the prospect of fresh influence—and presumably, fresh beauty—was a delicious treat.

While the others drifted into the hallway, Newland Archer watched from the entrance as Countess Olenska regarded him. Her expression held a faint, searching smile.

"You must already know," he said, answering her look with a shy laugh. "About May and me."

"She scolded me for not informing you last night at the opera," he continued. "I had her instructions to break the news immediately, but the crowd was too dense."

The smile softened, transforming her features into the likeness of the bold, brown-haired girl he remembered from childhood. "Of course I know," she said. "And I am so pleased. Though, naturally, one does not announce matters of such magnitude amidst a throng."

The ladies were moving toward the threshold, and she stepped forward to offer her hand. "Farewell. Come and see me sometime." Her eyes remained locked on his as she spoke.

Back in the carriage, rolling down Fifth Avenue, the conversation focused pointedly on Mrs. Mingott—her age, her indomitable spirit, and her extraordinary flair. Not a word was spoken of Ellen Olenska, yet the silence was palpable. Archer sensed clearly that Mrs. Welland was thinking: *It was a grave error for Ellen to appear today, parading up Fifth Avenue at the height of the shopping hour alongside Julius Beaufort.*

Newland echoed the thought internally, though perhaps with a touch more justification. *And she ought to understand that a man who has just become engaged does not waste his time calling upon married women.* Yet, he acknowledged the truth in the counter-argument: *But in the circle she inhabits, they do nothing else.* Despite his pride in his cosmopolitan outlook, he found himself thanking heaven that he was a New Yorker, about to unite himself with someone of his own kind, bound by the same unwritten laws that governed their world.
