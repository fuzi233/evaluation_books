Durance Begins to See

Ethne stood rigid by the drawing-room window of the Hill Street house, her gaze fixed on the pavement below. Beside her, seated before her tea table, was Mrs. Adair. Both women were silent, their ears strained to catch a specific rhythm rising from the street—a signal to penetrate the quiet room. The sash window hung wide open, eager to admit whatever sound broke the stillness. It was half-past five. Summer had returned, flooding London with a brilliance that turned the city into a jewel box of green avenues and sparkling pleasure. Across the way, windows overflowed with blooming flowers, and beneath them, carriages glided smoothly toward Hyde Park. Suddenly, the sharp *jingle* of horse bells cut through the air, swelling into a clamor. Mrs. Adair lifted her head sharply.

"That's a cab," she murmured.

"Yes."

Ethne leaned forward, peering down. "But it's not stopping here," she whispered, watching the vehicle pass with a fading ring of bells.

Mrs. Adair glanced at the clock, then turned her curious, slightly worried eyes toward Ethne. To her friend, Ethne's "yes" had sounded heavy with suspense rather than joy. Her posture at the window suggested not anticipation, but dread. And though Mrs. Adair wasn't certain, she sensed a flicker of relief when the carriage rolled past without halting. "I wonder why you didn't go to the station to meet Colonel Durrance?" she asked slowly.

The reply came instantly.

"He might have thought I came because I pitied him, looking so helpless. I cannot allow him to think that." Ethne gazed out the window again, pausing as if to speak, then deciding against it. Finally, she resolved.

"Do you recall the telegram I showed you?"

"The one from Lieutenant Calder stating Colonel Durrance had gone blind?"

"Yes. I require a promise from you: never to mention it again. I do not wish him to know I ever received it."

Mrs. Adair was flustered by the request, disliking the puzzle it presented. She had seen the telegram, but Ethne had never revealed she had written back to Durrance immediately, pledging herself to him. All Ethne had said was, "I am engaged to him." Mrs. Adair had assumed this engagement carried weight and had maintained that belief.

"You will promise?" Ethne pressed.

"Certainly, my dear, if you insist," Mrs. Adair returned, shrugging with a lack of warmth. "But there must be a reason. I don't grasp why you demand this oath."

"Because two lives must not be ruined because of me."

Mrs. Adair suspected Ethne was terrified of the blind man she intended to marry. It was true that Ethne felt a tremor of fear. Twelve months prior, in this very room, on a sunlit afternoon like today, Ethne had urged Durrance to forget her. Yet every letter since had proven otherwise—whether he tried or not, he had not forgotten. That final note, scribbled three weeks ago from Wadi Halfa, the childish scrawl where letters ran together across the page, detailing his calamity and renouncing his suit—it proved, perhaps more than anything hopeful before it, that he remembered everything. As she waited by the window, Ethne realized clearly that the burden of forgetting fell entirely on her shoulders. If she could not forget, she must ensure that no careless word slipped out in a vulnerable moment to reveal she still remembered.

"No," she declared, turning to Mrs. Adair. "Two lives shall not be spoiled because of me."

"Are you quite sure, Ethne?" Mrs. Adair ventured, her tone cautious. "That this method—the path of marriage—won't ultimately ruin those two lives? Don't you fear you'll eventually find Colonel Durrance, against your will, a hindrance and a drag? Might he feel the same?"

"No," Ethne said, her voice decisive. "I shall not feel it, and he must not."

According to Mrs. Adair, the "two lives" referred to Durrance and Harry Feversham. She was mistaken; the stakes were truly Ethne and Durrance. Ethne did not correct her friend, secretly glad that her companion shared this delusion, allowing it to persist.

Ethne returned to the window, projecting a future where she would never be caught off guard. The task loomed difficult, and fear began to coil within her chest as the minutes ticked by. Yet the goal justified the effort; she fixed her eyes upon it. Durrance had lost everything that gave life meaning the moment blindness struck—all save one thing. "What should I do if I were crippled?" he had asked Harry Feversham years ago, the day they last rode together. "A clever man might endure it. But what if I sat in a chair for the rest of my days?" Ethne hadn't heard those words, but she understood the man perfectly without them. By birth, he was heir to vast estates, yet he had lost his heritage. The journeys that thrilled him, the exotic faces of foreign lands, the crackle of a campfire against the desert's black silence, the sleep under starry skies, the cool night winds, and the work of governing—he had forfeited them all. Only one thing remained: herself, and only as long as he believed she desired him.

While Ethne wrestled with her resolve, the cab she awaited pulled up unseen at the door. It was only when Durrance's servant rang the bell that Ethne's attention snapped back to the street.

"He has arrived!" she breathed, starting.

True, Durrance was not naturally astute; he rarely scrutinized friends deeply. It would have been simple, Ethne mused, to soothe any doubts he harbored. But *now*, paradoxically, it would be easier than ever. There was no cause for alarm. She reasoned this, yet despite her logic, she steel-edged herself for the confrontation rather than rushing to embrace her betrothed.

Mrs. Adair slipped quietly out, leaving Ethne alone as Durrance entered. Ethne did not move immediately, holding her pose, bracing for the shock the changes in him might cause. Instead, she was surprised. The alterations she feared were invisible only by their absence. His face was gaunt, his hair streaked with gray, but there was no aura of helplessness or uncertainty—that was what she dreaded most for his sake. He stepped forward into the room as if guided by vision; his memory seemed to map the furniture's placement perfectly. At most, he reached out a hand twice, searching for a chair.

Ethne retreated silently toward the window, unsure of words to greet him. Instantly, he smiled and approached her directly.

"Ethne," he said.

"It isn't true, then," she gasped. "You've recovered." The words burst forth due to the ease of his movement.

"It is quite true, and I have not recovered," he answered calmly. "But you shifted at the window, and the street noise spiked suddenly. I knew someone stood before the glass. I guessed it was you."

Their exchange lacked the usual formalities of a reunited couple, yet it staved off awkwardness. Ethne avoided perfunctory expressions of regret, sensing they were unnecessary, and Durrance showed no desire to hear them. Many things passed between them unspoken. After shaking hands, Ethne moved back into the room.

"I will brew you some tea," she offered. "Then we can speak."

"Yes, we must talk, mustn't we?" Durrance replied seriously. Then his gravity melted, and he chatted cheerfully about his journey home, even finding humor in his initial days of blindness. Ethne's anxieties dissolved rapidly, nearly vanishing from her mind. Then, a shift in his demeanor brought them crashing back.

"I wrote to you from Wadi Halfa," he said. "Do you think you could read that letter?"

"Quite well," Ethne lied smoothly.

"My friend, a Sapper named Calder, held the paper for me and told me when I was writing on it versus the blotting pad," he continued, laughing lightly. "Calder... of the Sappers—but you don't know him."

He blurted the name quickly, striking Ethne like a blow. He had set a trap. The stillness of his face signaled intense listening, waiting for a start, a gasp, a slip-up that would reveal knowledge of Calder of the Sappers. *Did he suspect?* *Did he know of the telegram?* *Did he guess my letter was sent out of pity?* She studied his face, which offered no clues save for a piercing alertness. In former times, a year ago, the expression in his eyes would have given a definitive answer.

"I could read the letter without difficulty," she answered softly. "It was the letter you would have written. I had already written to you, and of course, your misfortune could alter nothing. I retract no word of what I penned."

Durrance sat with his hands on his knees, leaning forward slightly. Ethne was at a loss. She couldn't discern from his countenance whether he accepted or challenged her response. Again, she realized that a year ago, with his sight, she would have known his thoughts without a shadow of doubt.

"Yes, I know you," he said at last. "You would take nothing back." He paused, his deliberateness returning. "But consider my perspective."
