There’s a passage in *Robinson Crusoe*—page one hundred and twenty-nine, if you care to look—that reads: *“Now I saw, though too late, the Folly of beginning a Work before we count the Cost, and before we judge rightly of our own Strength to go through with it.”*

I’d just stumbled upon those very words yesterday when, this morning—May 21st, 1850—Mr. Franklin Blake, my lady’s nephew, paid me a visit. He wasted no time in getting to the point.

“Betteredge,” he said, “I’ve been to the lawyer’s on family business, and the matter of that cursed Indian Diamond came up—the one that vanished from my aunt’s house in Yorkshire two years ago. Mr. Bruff agrees with me: the whole affair ought to be set down in writing. For the sake of truth, and before time muddles the facts.”

Not yet grasping where I fit into this, but always keen to side with the lawyers (peacekeeping instincts, you understand), I nodded along.

Mr. Franklin continued. “Innocent people have already been shadowed by suspicion in this business,” he said. “And if we don’t put the truth to paper now, their reputations may suffer later for it. This story needs telling—properly. And Bruff and I think we’ve hit upon the right method.”

All very well for them, but I still didn’t see my role in it.

“Here’s the plan,” Mr. Franklin explained. “We’ll each write our own part of the Moonstone’s tale—only what we’ve witnessed ourselves. We begin with how the Diamond fell into my uncle Herncastle’s hands in India fifty years ago. That’s already documented in an old family paper. Next, we must account for how it came to be in my aunt’s house… and how it disappeared within a matter of hours. And that, Betteredge, is where you come in. No one knows the household’s affairs during that time better than you. So you’ll take up the pen and start the story.”

Ah. Now I understood.

If you’re wondering how I responded, I’ll confess: I did precisely what you might’ve done. I protested—modestly, of course—that I wasn’t equal to the task. Privately, though, I fancied myself more than capable, given half a chance.

Mr. Franklin, sharp as ever, saw right through me. He dismissed my false humility and insisted I give it a try.

That was two hours ago. The moment he left, I sat at my desk, ready to begin. And there I’ve remained since, utterly stalled—staring at the page, much like Crusoe staring at his own folly. Prophetic, wasn’t it? That I’d open to that very passage just before rashly accepting this undertaking? If that’s not fate nudging a man, I don’t know what is.

Now, don’t mistake me for some credulous fool. I’ve read my share of books. I’ve a sound mind, a strong memory, and legs that still carry me briskly at seventy. And I’ll say this plainly: there’s no book like *Robinson Crusoe*, nor will there ever be. It’s been my companion through every trial life has thrown at me. Low spirits? *Crusoe*. Need counsel? *Crusoe*. Wife vexing me? A drop too much drink? *Crusoe*, every time. I’ve worn out six copies in my service. My lady gave me a seventh on her last birthday—bound in blue, with an illustration, no less. I celebrated with a tad too much brandy, and who set me right again? *Robinson Crusoe*.

But this isn’t getting the Diamond’s story told, is it? I’m meandering like a drunkard lost in a fog. Let’s begin anew—fresh paper, fresh ink, and my respects to you.
