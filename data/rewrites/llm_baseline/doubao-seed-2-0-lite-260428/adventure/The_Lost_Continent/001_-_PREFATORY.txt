We were both stiff as fence posts after a full night sleeping rough out in the Gran Canaria highlands: even here, the thick mountain dew and the sharp post-sunset chill bite right through unlined hiking clothes, no matter how many layers you pile on. Normally I’d shake that ache out with a quick jog first thing, but the ground here is all jagged lava scree, not three straight yards of level ground for miles around. So when Coppinger started doing calisthenics he’d dreamed up, lifting two chunks of bristly, craggy lava like they were gym dumbbells, I followed suit.

Coppinger’s got more degrees than a university prospectus, for one thing — he picks up a new obscure qualification on average every other year — and he’s fanatical about his fitness routines, treats them like a religious observance. Two days of prior rain had left a thin trickle of water running down the bottom of the deep barranca gully nearby, so we trundled down there to wash the grime off and brush our teeth. You have no idea how heavenly a fresh toothbrush feels on a weeks-long backcountry expedition.

“Look at this,” Coppinger said, tipping out his pockets onto a flat rock. “We’ve got barely a crumb of grub left, and it’s all been wrapped in that scratchy local Spanish newspaper till it’s half ink.”
“Yours is mostly pipe tobacco ash.”
“Worse if we leave it. We’ve got hours of nasty scrambling ahead of us before we get a hot meal.”

That much was obvious, so we sat right there by the gully stream and ate every last crumb. We still had a ten-mile trudge ahead to the little roadside fonda at Santa Brigida where we’d left our heavy packs, and Coppinger was dead set on taking a hundred more photos and measurements of the cliffside caves before we left the site. We’d be ravenous long before we got to eat, and long before we got to taste the fonda patron’s legendary aged country wine — the kind no tourist holed up in the fancy Las Palmas hotels even knows exists, the stuff that’d vanish off the shelves in a week if word ever leaked out.

To be perfectly honest, the two crumbly Guanche mummies we’d already dug up were more than enough adventure for me. Their goatskin wrappings were brittle as old tissue paper, and the poor ancient things puffed out dust like a ripe puffball the second you so much as tapped them. But Coppinger was convinced he’d stumbled on the ruins of some lost sacred Guanche college hidden in the cliff face, and he wouldn’t rest until he’d ransacked every last cave pocket in that whole rock wall. He had 28 rolls of film left in his kodak and a full stock of flashlight powder, and insisted we clear the whole site out now instead of wasting weeks of time on a return trip. So he hefted the crowbar, I slung the knotted hemp rope over my shoulder, and we trundled up to the cliff ridge that had baked us to a crisp under the midday sun the day before.

It was obvious the caves had been left untouched for centuries: the old Guanche had used ladders woven from goathide rope back in the day, Coppinger claimed, yanking them up behind them to lock out trespassers, and the cave mouths sat in an even row 30 feet below the ridge top, 50 feet above the bouldered cliff base. No casual local hiker was going to climb vertical rock to poke around, that much was certain. Instead of hauling clunky ladders up from below, our plan was to rappel straight down the cliff face from the ridge top to each cave mouth, then scramble down to the base and hike back around to the next gap in the rock.

Coppinger was brave, and never got vertigo, but he was portly, pushing fifty, and the fact that he insisted on climbing down every single cave himself told me just how obsessed he was with the site. I went first every time, climbing into the cave mouth to reach down for him, but when you’re hanging off a 90-degree cliff face on a frayed thin rope, there’s not much real help you can offer the climber behind you besides yelling encouragement. The first three caves were nothing but empty old store chambers, and I told him he could save himself the climb, but he waved me off, insisting my untrained eye couldn’t tell a prison cell from a young priest’s classroom from a grain silo. Classic thorough, over-imaginative archaeologist, that.

By mid-afternoon the sun was blistering hot, and Coppinger was clearly at the end of his strength, but he was stubborn as a mule, refusing to quit even with an 80-foot drop below the ridge. Finally he had to pause, but there were three more unopened cave openings left, and he sweet-talked me into checking them, promising full legal ownership of whatever find I pulled out of them. I was sun-struck, my palms raw and oozing from the rough hemp rope, and sick to death of dusty mummies — but he was so dead set on it I caved.

I scrambled over loose scree, wedged the crowbar into a solid fissure at the cliff top, and rappelled down the rock face, swinging hard through an overhang to land with a thump on my back in the cave mouth, never letting go of the rope. This cave was nothing like the rough, hacked-out dens we’d explored before: it had been cut clean with sharp tools, walls smooth and flat, angling inwards towards the roof to make a whole connected warren of chambers. The three unmarked gaps Coppinger had spotted? They were just doorways and windows leading into this single massive complex. No wall inscriptions, just dust and bats, so I smoked a full cigarette (Coppinger hated rushing “official archaeological surveys”) before yelling down my report.

He panicked, yelled for me to hang on, and tied his kodak and flashlight gear to the end of the rope for me to haul up. I snapped photos of four of the chambers, then set the camera on a wide, high ledge by a fifth doorway to steady it — and the second I put my weight on that ledge to recharge the flash, there was a sharp crackle, and the whole slab dropped half a foot. It was a hidden compartment, hermetically sealed with cement. I dug out the rubble, pried at the contents with my pocket knife and the heel of my boot, and pulled out a solid 20x15x15 inch block of thin stacked sheets: each one made of shiny talc, coated in a dark toffee-coloured waxy substance, scored with tiny patterns I wrote off as decorative carvings — Coppinger had drilled it into my head a hundred times that the ancient Guanche people had no written language, so I never guessed those marks were letters in a lost, unknown script. I lowered the whole block down to Coppinger on the rope.

By the time I climbed back down to the base of the cliff, he was on all fours in the dirt, nearly mad with excitement. It wasn’t Guanche, he hissed. The talc wasn’t even native to the Canary Islands, the script was a variant no modern scholar had ever documented, the waxy coating was some unknown mineral compound, likely asphalt. This wasn’t a Guanche relic — this was Atlantean. The lost continent of Atlantis, the very land Plato wrote of that sank beneath the waves just north of the Canary Islands, the cataclysm that spawned every ancient flood myth around the world, including the old Greek story of Deucalion. He fumed that I’d smashed the top and bottom layers prying the block loose, claiming those lost pages held a full, unique account of ancient Yucatan, a far-flung Atlantean colony.

By then it was already four o’clock, and I was ravenous, so we trundled the ten miles back to the Santa Brigida fonda. Coppinger wrapped the precious manuscript tenderly in his coat, refusing to let it be tied to the pack for fear of chipping the edges, and carried it the whole way himself until he was nearly dropping from exhaustion. Back at the fonda, I ate the biggest dinner I could find, cracked open that legendary aged Canary wine, smoked a final pipe, and crashed straight into bed. Coppinger ate a meal too, but he never slept that night: he pored over the manuscript by candlelight, bleary-eyed but bouncing with excitement the next morning.

The author, he told me, was none other than Deucalion himself: an Atlantean priest-general, one of the very few survivors of the continent’s sinking, who’d carried this full history of his people to this hidden cave to save it from the waves. The pages were crammed with illustrations of long-extinct beasts: giant cave tigers, massive predatory bats, mammoths, creatures no living human had laid eyes on in ten thousand years. He claimed it would take him a full year of hard work to translate the full, damaged text.

That same afternoon I got an urgent cable ordering me back to England for critical business. I raced to Las Palmas, caught the Liverpool steamer just as it was pulling out of the harbour, and buried myself under work for a full year, barely thinking of Coppinger or the manuscript at all. Then I spotted a small paragraph in a national newspaper: he’d donated the talc manuscript to the British Museum, valued at no less than ten thousand pounds.

I wrote him a pointed note reminding him he’d explicitly promised the full find belonged to me. He sent back a stiff formal reply, enclosed the full English translation of the manuscript, and claimed the profits of publication would make full reimbursement. When I sent an angry follow-up letter, his solicitors wrote back to state all future correspondence had to go through them.

I’m not here to drag out old arguments. This is the full, unredacted history of Deucalion, the last survivor of Atlantis, the story of a lost world swallowed whole by the sea. Dr. Coppinger takes full credit — and full responsibility — for the translation work. All proceeds from this publication go to me, the man who pried those talc sheets out of that hidden cliffside compartment by pure luck. Any changes to this arrangement will be announced publicly if they ever come to pass. I don’t expect they ever will.
