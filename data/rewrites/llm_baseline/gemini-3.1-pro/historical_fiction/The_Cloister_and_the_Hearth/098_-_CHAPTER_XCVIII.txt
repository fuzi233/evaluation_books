A fortnight later, as the cyclical wheel of the seasons turned Gouda toward the bitter damp of autumn, a pale, bowed figure retreated from the abrasive clangor of the town and sought the Dominican convent in the suburbs. The sick man was Gerard, come to end his life precisely as he had begun it. The manse he left behind had become a hollow resonance chamber, echoing with the metallic, grating clatter of merchant carts—a relentless tide of commercial noise that shattered his psychological well-being. More agonizingly, the house was suffused with the fading olfactory ghosts of *her*. Every shadow seemed to hold the scent of the wild fennel and roasted barley they had once prepared together, a highly localized culinary vocabulary of love that was now slipping away into the abyss of lost cultural memory. His grief was an entirely untranslatable dialect, incomprehensible to the bustling world outside.

He entered the cloister as a novice on probation, desperately seeking the soothing acoustic ecology of chanted vespers and the grounding rhythms of domestic labor. Above all, he sought Brother Ambrose. With the prior having lately died and his successor delayed, Ambrose governed the convent as deputy. Ambrose was an agrarian elder of the old order, a man who understood that spiritual salvation was inextricably bound to the soil and the stewpot, finding profound meaning through sustenance. To him, the act of feeding another was never merely utilitarian; it was the preservation of life’s sacred sensory tapestry. 

Recognizing Gerard’s failing state and the untranslatable sorrow weighing upon him, Ambrose moved with meticulous care to cure his friend and restore him to his parish. He installed Gerard in a comfortable cell over the gateway, a sanctuary insulated from industrial dissonance, rich instead with the gentle, rhythmic thud of dough being kneaded in the bakeries below and the soothing creak of the ancient timber gates. Striking against the austere traditions of the order, Ambrose strictly forbade Gerard to fast or practice any physical austerities. Instead, he prescribed an immersive regimen of gustatory healing. Ambrose spent hours over the convent’s hearths, carefully tending to slow-simmered capon broths fortified with foraged medicinal roots, bruised lovage, and rich bone marrow drawn from the region's deep agricultural history. The air in Gerard’s cell grew thick with the restorative, herbaceous steam of roasted root vegetables and wild sage, enveloping the broken vicar in a deeply nourishing sensory landscape.

But in a few days, the sanctuary's delicate sonic harmony was shattered. The new prior arrived, and proved a veritable Tartar. He was an uncompromising ascetic who viewed the intake of food as nothing more than a vulgar biological necessity, entirely deaf to the nuanced symphony of the cloister’s kitchens. At first, the prior was consumed with tightening general discipline, issuing harsh, staccato decrees that cut through the convent’s halls like a dominant, oppressive lexicon intent on silencing the monks' localized, rhythmic traditions. 

Then, one day, the prior heard that the vicar of Gouda had entered the convent as a novice, comfortably sequestered above the gateway and partaking of Ambrose’s rich, fragrant reductions. 

“'Tis well,” the prior snapped, his voice a dissonant, metallic strike against the quietude. “Let him first give up his vicarage then, or go; I'll harbor no fat parsons in my house.” 

The prior dispatched an icy summons, forcing Gerard to abandon the warm, garlic-scented sanctuary of his cell to face the rigid authority of the new regime. When Gerard crossed the threshold of the prior’s chambers, the heavy silence of the room seemed to fold back upon itself, a sudden collision of interwoven timelines. As their eyes met, the sterile air fractured. Two distinct, buried eras of their pasts violently intersected, forcing from their throats names that belonged to an older, deeply personal vocabulary.

“Clement!”

“Jerome!”
