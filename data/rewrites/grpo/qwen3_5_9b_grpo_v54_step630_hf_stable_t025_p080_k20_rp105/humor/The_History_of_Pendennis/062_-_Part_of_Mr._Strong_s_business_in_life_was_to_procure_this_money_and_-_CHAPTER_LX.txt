It has been nearly a year since the last time we saw Arthur Pendennis in his black coat. Now, that somber garment is being swapped for a blue one, signaling a transformation far more pleasing than mere clothing. His wig has been retired to the attic, allowing his hair—though slightly thinned by the rigors of life—to reclaim its public stage. Most significantly, Arthur has earned the honor of appearing at Court in the uniform of a Cornet of the Clavering troop of the Yeomanry Cavalry, presented to the Sovereign by the flamboyant Marquis of Steyne.

This promotion was a measure vigorously, almost tearfully, advocated by Arthur's uncle, the Major. The old gentleman refused to let a single year pass without this ceremonial rite of passage into gentlemanhood. He believed his nephew deserved a club far more exclusive than the Megatherium and loudly proclaimed his disappointment that Arthur's income had settled at a mere fifteen hundred a year.

Such is the official valuation of Pendennis in the world—a figure that has surprisingly elevated his standing among publishers and even softened the edges of maternal disapproval. While the dazzling Myra might snag an Earl, leaving the slightly asymmetrical Beatrice to dangle after a boor, logic dictates that Mr. Pendennis should be her support. Indeed, in the first winter following his mother's inheritance, Mrs. Hawxby arranged for Beatrice to learn billiards from Arthur and insisted he drive her pony carriage, citing their shared "literary" nature. She publicly declared that Arthur, under the instigation of his "horrid old uncle," had behaved infamously by trifling with Beatrice's feelings. The truth, however, was that the Major, knowing Mrs. Hawxby's predatory nature, had spirited Arthur away from her claws at a country house, saving him from her immediate grasp if not her tongue. Arthur had declined the Major's invitation to spend Christmas at Clavering, finding the proximity to the haunted memories of Fairoaks too painful to bear.

Meanwhile, the Clavering family had vanished from our view until their reappearance at the Epsom racecourse. Their past year had been a trial by fire for Lady Clavering, a woman of immense good nature whose appetite and temper had been sorely tested by domestic chaos. "I'd rather eat a turnip for dessert than that pineapple," she lamented to her faithful friend, Strong, regarding the fruit laid before her. "If only I could eat it in peace! How much happier I was when I was a widow!"

The Claverings had indeed made a disastrous start. Their London debut was shaky, followed by a notorious failure. The town whispered that human patience could not withstand Sir Francis Clavering, describing him as hopelessly low, dull, and disreputable, casting a taint over the entire household. Questions abounded: Where did the Begum, with her money but missing 'h's, come from? Was the daughter's Gallicized affectation fit for English breeding? Sir Francis was a gambler surrounded by blacklegs; his son, Harry Foker, was accused of cheating at cards and showing the white feather. Consequently, Lady Rockminster dumped Lady Clavering, the great ladies barred their daughters from her parties, and the young men attending behaved with odious familiarity. Poor Lady Clavering was forced to admit she had to open her parlour to "the canal"—the lower strata of society—because the "tip-tops" refused to come.

Yet, Lady Clavering bore this rejection without pride or ill-will. She had simply followed the social godmother's rule: know whom they know, ask whom they ask. It is easier to leave a mistress than to be left by her; society can be abandoned with relief, but when society abandons you, the mortification is severe.

Among the faithless, one faithful figure emerged: Harry Foker. However, his lack of prudence turned his secret passion for Pen into a town-wide scandal, reaching the ears of his weak mother and ultimately the bald, inflexible Foker Senior. The result was a violent confrontation that banished young Henry Foker from England for a year. Ordered to return and marry his cousin or retire on a paltry three hundred a year, the exiled gentleman found even the Parisian Boulevard and the Italian sky bleak under his crape-covered eyes.

For Sir Francis Clavering, that same year was a catastrophe. As our sporting readers recall, Lord Harrowhill's horse, Podasokus, won the Derby, baffling the experts who had backed the non-existent Borax. Sir Francis, relying on "valuable information" from rascals on the turf, had heavily backed the favorite. The result, as his son noted to Lady Clavering, was a staggering loss of seven thousand pounds.

This blow crushed the widow, who had already paid her husband's debts countless times, funded his houses, and silenced his creditors. Now, she faced the instant demand for this enormous sum.

The Major Pendennis, acting as the family's adviser, had previously inspected every room of the Clavering estate, including the closet where the family skeleton was locked. While the Baronet hid his financial entanglement in a web of lies, the Major knew Lady Clavering's situation intimately. He understood the vast sacrifices the widow Amory had made for her second marriage and openly suggested that Miss Blanche deserved a better provision.

The Major revealed his knowledge of Lady Clavering's past, including her separation from her first husband in India, pointing out the Calcutta newspaper that detailed Amory's trial. He admitted his interest was motivated by a desire to marry his nephew to the daughter.

"My dear lady," the Major explained, "my boy has only five hundred a year. A wife with ten thousand pounds would barely improve his lot. But Arthur is a shrewd, ambitious fellow who has sown his wild oats. With a good start and a partner who can help him advance, he could make a name his sons would be proud of. Why keep a useless seat in Parliament for Sir Francis when Arthur could be a distinguished son-in-law? It is a bargain for both."

Lady Clavering, the good-natured Begum, agreed readily. She spoke warmly of Arthur, noting how much she missed his late mother, and confessed that while she wished Laura Pen (favored by Lady Rockminster) were to marry, her own daughter Blanche had not been a comfort. "Laura ought to marry," she said.

"Marry on five hundred a year?" the Major exclaimed. "You are mad! Consult me on all affairs with your unhappy husband. Old Pendennis is always your friend."

He had also preached these practical truths to Miss Amory, arguing that mutual convenience was the only foundation for a lasting marriage. Love matches, he claimed, inevitably led to quarrels and desertion, whereas a sensible union allowed the lady to bring the means and the gentleman to win the plate. "A couple like that have something to talk about," he insisted. "Even Cupid and Psyche would yawn after a few evenings if they had nothing but sentiment to discuss."

Miss Amory, content with Pen as long as no better option existed, accepted his presence in the house without engagement. They moved with extreme familiarity but little sentiment, meeting and parting in good humor. Arthur reflected that he was the same man who had once harbored a grand passion and raged over Briseis, yet time had brought its ordinary consolations.

We alter very little. When we lament changes in our friends, we fail to realize that circumstance merely reveals latent qualities. The selfish languor of today is the consequence of yesterday's selfish ardor; the scorn of the successful parvenu is the continuation of the needy struggler's career. Our mental changes are like grey hairs or wrinkles—the fulfillment of mortal growth. That which is now calm weariness was once fierce ambition; that which is sluggish obesity was once boisterous health.

Lucky is the one who can bear failure generously, giving up his broken sword to Fate with a humble heart. Consider, reader, how you stand in the crowd today. Perhaps you are the lucky captain riding into port, bowing from the deck with a care hidden under a star on your breast. Or perhaps you are the wrecked sailor lashed to a solitary spar, drowning out of sight. Yet, both the sinking man and the successful captain are likely thinking of home, remembering the time before the voyage began. You are the same person you were in childhood, having passed through struggles of defeat, success, crime, and remorse, loving and growing cold, weeping and laughing again. Alone on the spar, or alone in the applause, you remain the same.
