The map failed at the junction of the Aylesbury Pike, just past Dean's Corners. To take the left was to choose a geography that refused to conform to the laws of the mapped world. The road narrowed, the earth rising in a slow, suffocating ascent until the brier-laced stone walls pressed inward, closing the gap between the vehicle and the abyss of the rutted track. The forests here were not collections of timber but dense, suffocating masses; the trees grew with a grotesque largeness, their shadows swallowing the light before it could touch the ground. Weeds and brambles achieved a luxuriance that bordered on the pathological, choking the sparse, barren fields that managed to exist.

The houses were few, scattered like discarded bones, wearing a uniform expression of age and decay. They stood as monuments to a retreat from life itself. One hesitated to approach the gnarled figures perched on crumbling doorsteps or crouching in the rock-strewn meadows. Their silence was not peaceful; it was furtive, a held breath that suggested the presence of something forbidden nearby. To speak to them felt like an intrusion into a private nightmare.

As the road climbed, the mountains emerged from the deep woods, but they offered no comfort. Their summits were too perfectly rounded, too symmetrical, lacking the chaotic irregularity of nature. Against the sky, they displayed the queer, concentric circles of tall stone pillars that crowned their peaks. Gorges of impossible depth sliced the landscape, crossed by crude wooden bridges that seemed to vibrate with a latent instability. Below, the marshlands stretched out, a wet, sucking expanse that invited fear rather than exploration. At twilight, the atmosphere thickened. Unseen whippoorwills began their chatter, a rhythmic, insectoid drone that competed with the raucous, insistent piping of bullfrogs. Fireflies erupted in abnormal profusion, dancing to a rhythm that felt less like nature and more like a signal.

The Miskatonic River wound its way upward, a thin, shining line that possessed an undeniably serpentine quality as it hugged the feet of the domed hills. As proximity increased, the focus shifted from the stone crowns to the wooded slopes. These sides loomed dark and precipitous, pressing against the observer with a gravitational weight that demanded flight. Yet, there was no road leading away. Only the covered bridge remained, a tenebrous tunnel connecting the outside world to the village huddled between the stream and the vertical face of Round Mountain.

Beyond the bridge lay Dunwich. The cluster of rotting gambrel roofs spoke of an architectural era long abandoned by the rest of the region. Closer inspection revealed that most structures were deserted, collapsing into ruin. The broken steeple of the church housed the hamlet's sole mercantile establishment, a testament to a community that had ceased to function normally. A faint, malign odor clung to the village street—the scent of massed mold and the slow decomposition of centuries. Crossing the bridge felt like passing through a membrane separating sanity from madness. Relief came only when the road curved around the base of the hills, allowing the traveler to leave the valley behind and rejoin the Aylesbury Pike.

Those who returned often learned, with a jolt of recognition, that they had traversed Dunwich. Outsiders avoided it with a superstitious diligence. Since the season of horror in 1928, all signposts pointing toward the town had been removed. By any aesthetic standard, the scenery was breathtakingly beautiful; yet no artists or tourists ever visited. Two centuries prior, when superstition held sway, people gave specific reasons for their avoidance. Today, in our sensible age, the reasons are buried under the hush-up of the 1928 incident, deemed necessary for the welfare of the town and the world.

Perhaps the primary deterrent is the population. The natives are repellently decadent, having traveled far down the path of retrogression common to New England backwaters. They have evolved into a distinct race, marked by the physical and mental stigmata of degeneracy and inbreeding. Their collective intelligence is woefully low, while their history reeks of overt viciousness, half-hidden murders, incest, and acts of violence so perverse they defy naming.

Among them, the old gentry of the two or three armigerous families who arrived from Salem in 1692 maintain a precarious position above the general decay. Branches of the Whateley and Bishop lines still send their eldest sons to Harvard and Miskatonic University. However, these sons rarely return to the moldering gambrel roofs beneath which they and their ancestors were born.

No one, even those privy to the facts of the recent horror, can articulate precisely what is wrong with Dunwich. Old legends speak of unhallowed rites and Indian conclaves where forbidden shapes of shadow were summoned from the great rounded hills, accompanied by wild orgiastic prayers answered by loud crackings and rumblings from the earth. In 1747, Reverend Abijah Hoadley, newly installed at the Congregational Church, delivered a memorable sermon on the close presence of Satan and his imps. He declared that the blasphemies of an infernal train of demons were matters of too common knowledge to deny, citing the cursed voices of Azazel, Buzrael, Beelzebub, and Belial heard from underground by over a score of credible witnesses. Hoadley claimed he had caught a plain discourse of evil powers in the hill behind his house—a rattling, rolling, groaning, screeching, and hissing that no earthly thing could produce, emanating only from caves accessible solely through black magic and the devil's key.

Hoadley vanished shortly after preaching this sermon, but the text, printed in Springfield, remains extant. Noises in the hills continue to be reported annually, baffling geologists and physiographers alike. Other traditions describe foul odors near the hill-crowning stone circles and rushing airy presences heard faintly from the bottoms of great ravines. There is also the Devil's Hop Yard, a bleak, blasted hillside where no tree, shrub, or blade of grass dares to grow. Furthermore, the natives harbor a mortal terror of the numerous whippoorwills, which become vocal on warm nights. It is vowed that these birds are psychopomps, lying in wait for the souls of the dying. They time their eerie cries in unison with the sufferer's struggling breath; if they catch the fleeing soul, they flutter away in demoniac laughter, but if they fail, they sink into a disappointed silence.

These tales are obsolete, ridiculous relics of a distant past. Yet Dunwich is ridiculously old—far older than any community within thirty miles. South of the village, one may still spy the cellar walls and chimney of the ancient Bishop house, constructed before 1700. The ruins of the mill at the falls, built in 1806, stand as the most modern architecture visible. Industry never flourished here; the nineteenth-century factory movement proved short-lived. Oldest of all are the great rings of rough-hewn stone columns on the hilltops, generally attributed to the Indians rather than the settlers. Deposits of skulls and bones found within these circles and around the sizable table-like rock on Sentinel Hill sustain the popular belief that these were once burial places of the Pocumtucks, even though many ethnologists, dismissing the absurd improbability of such a theory, insist the remains are Caucasian.

Wilbur Whateley entered the world at five in the morning on Sunday, February second, nineteen thirteen. The clock struck the hour in the farmhouse perched against the hillside, four miles from Dunwich and a mile and a half from the nearest neighbor. It was Candlemas, though the villagers called it something else, a date marked by the persistent barking of the countryside dogs and the low, grinding noises rising from the hills. These sounds had begun the night before, a rhythmic, unsettling percussion that preceded the birth.

Less significant, yet noted in the local record, was the lineage. The mother was Lavinia Whateley, a thirty-five-year-old albino of the decaying branch of the family, her skin pale and her features distorted by a deformity that rendered her unattractive to the eye. She lived with her father, Old Whateley, a man of advanced age and fractured sanity, surrounded by whispers of wizardry that had haunted his youth. Lavinia had no husband, yet she made no move to deny the child, accepting him with a strange, possessive pride. To the townsfolk, who speculated wildly about the father's identity, she offered no defense. Instead, she murmured prophecies regarding the infant's unusual powers and a terrible future, her voice carrying the weight of a woman who understood the gravity of what she held.

Lavinia was a solitary figure, accustomed to wandering the hills amidst thunderstorms and poring over the great, odorous books inherited by her father—a collection of rotting volumes, eaten by worms and time, containing the fragmented lore of centuries. Uneducated and devoid of conventional order, she kept a home that mirrored her own decay, free from the standards of cleanliness that governed the rest of the county. Yet, on the night Wilbur was born, a hideous scream tore through the silence, echoing above the wind and the dogs. No doctor attended; no midwife was present. Only the raw, primal act of creation remained.

Neighbors learned of the child a week later, when Old Whateley drove his sleigh through the snow into Dunwich Village. He stopped at Osborn's general store, addressing a crowd of loungers with an incoherent fervor. There was a shift in him, a new layer of furtiveness in his clouded mind that transformed him from a passive observer into a source of dread. He spoke of the child's paternity with a mixture of defiance and pride, dismissing the locals' expectations. "I dun't keer what folks think," he declared, his voice rough. "Ef Lavinny's boy looked like his pa, he wouldn't look like nothin' ye expeck. Ye needn't think the only folks is the folks hereabouts. Lavinny's read some, an' has seed some things the most o' ye only tell abaout. I calc'late her man is as good a husban' as ye kin find this side of Aylesbury... Let me tell ye suthin'—some day yew folks'll hear a child o' Lavinny's a-callin' its father's name on the top o' Sentinel Hill!"

For the first month, Wilbur was seen only by Old Whateley and Mamie Bishop, the common-law wife of Earl Sawyer. Mamie visited out of curiosity, her stories confirming the oddities she observed. Zechariah Whateley, of the undecayed line, came to lead a pair of Alderney cows purchased by Old Whateley from his son Curtis. This purchase initiated a pattern of cattle acquisition that would continue until the horror of 1928, yet the ramshackle barn never seemed overcrowded. People grew curious enough to climb the steep hillside and count the herd grazing precariously near the farmhouse, but they could never find more than ten or twelve specimens. They were anemic, bloodless, afflicted by a mysterious blight. Some bore wounds resembling surgical incisions, and occasionally, observers swore they saw similar sores around the throats of the old man and his daughter.

In the spring following his birth, Lavinia resumed her walks in the hills, carrying the swarthy child in her misproportioned arms. Once the townsfolk had seen the baby, public interest waned, and no one commented on the rapid development he exhibited. His growth was phenomenal; within three months, he possessed the size and muscular power of an infant nearing a full year. His movements were deliberate, restrained, lacking the chaotic energy typical of babies. By seven months, he walked unassisted, stumbling briefly before mastering the motion completely.

It was shortly after this, on Hallowe'en, that a great blaze erupted at midnight atop Sentinel Hill, illuminating the ancient table-stone amidst its mound of bones. Silas Bishop, of the undecayed Bishops, claimed to have seen the boy running sturdily up the hill an hour prior, leading his mother. Silas was rounding up a stray heifer when he spotted the two figures in the dim light of his lantern. They moved almost noiselessly through the underbrush, appearing entirely unclothed to the startled watcher. Later accounts were vague regarding the boy's attire, suggesting perhaps a fringed belt and dark blue trousers, but thereafter, Wilbur was never seen alive and conscious without complete, tightly buttoned clothing. The disarray of his garments seemed to trigger immediate anger and alarm in him.

His contrast with his squalid mother and grandfather was striking, particularly in his dress, a distinction that would later acquire a grim significance. The following January, gossip turned to the fact that "Lavinny's black brat" had begun to speak at the tender age of eleven months. His speech was remarkable, differing from the regional accent and free of the infantile lisping common to children of three or four. Though not talkative, when he spoke, he projected an elusive quality absent from Dunwich and its inhabitants. The strangeness lay not in his words, but in his intonation and the internal mechanics of his voice. His face also betrayed a premature maturity; sharing his mother's and grandfather's lack of a chin, his firm, precociously shaped nose combined with large, dark, almost Latin eyes to create an aura of quasi-adulthood and preternatural intelligence.

Yet, beneath this brilliance lay an extreme ugliness. Something animalistic clung to his thick lips, his large-pored, yellowish skin, his coarse, crinkly hair, and his oddly elongated ears. He was soon despised more fiercely than his parents, and all speculation about him became seasoned with references to the bygone magic of Old Whateley. Tales circulated of the hills shaking when the child shrieked the dreadful name of Yog-Sothoth in the midst of a circle of stones, holding a great book open in his arms. Dogs abhorred him, forcing him to adopt constant defensive measures against their relentless barking.

Old Whateley bought cattle, yet the herd did not swell. He cut timber and mended the house—a sprawling, peaked structure swallowed by rock at its rear, where three ground-floor rooms had sufficed for generations. There existed a prodigious reserve of strength in the old man, allowing him to undertake such labor despite the intermittent babble of dementia. His carpentry, however, betrayed a cold, calculating logic. The restoration began with Wilbur's birth: a toolshed clad in clapboard, secured with a fresh lock. Now, the upper story was reclaimed with similar thoroughness. His madness manifested only in the tight boarding of every window in the restored section, a futile barrier against a silence he seemed determined to enforce.

He fitted a new room downstairs for his grandson, accessible to callers, while the upper chamber remained a sealed tomb. Inside, tall shelving rose like skeletal fingers. Upon them, Whateley arranged, with obsessive care, the rotting fragments of ancient books scattered through the house for decades. "I made some use of 'em," he muttered, applying paste heated on a rusted stove to mend a torn black-letter page. "But the boy is fitten to make better use of 'em. He'd orter hev 'em as well sot as he kin for they're goin' to be all of his larnin'."

By September 1914, when Wilbur was a year and seven months old, his growth was alarming. He possessed the stature of a four-year-old but spoke with the fluency of a scholar. He roamed the fields, accompanying his mother, yet at home, he pored over the strange charts in his grandfather's library. Long, hushed afternoons were spent in instruction, the air thick with the weight of forbidden knowledge. As the house restoration concluded, observers noted the peculiar transformation of a rear window into a solid plank door. A cleated wooden runway ascended the hill to meet it, a path to nowhere. Shortly thereafter, the toolhouse, locked and blind since Wilbur's birth, fell silent again.

Earl Sawyer, returning from a cattle sale, opened the toolhouse door and recoiled. The odor was singular, a stench unlike any he had known save for the Indian circles on the hills—an aroma that suggested something unsanctioned by nature, something unearthly. Yet Dunwich folk rarely prided themselves on olfactory refinement.

The following months offered no visible drama, save for a slow, steady intensification of the hill noises. On May Eve, 1915, tremors shook the Aylesbury valley. By Halloween, the ground rumbled beneath bursts of flame erupting from Sentinel Hill—"them witch Whateleys' doin's," the locals whispered.

Wilbur changed. Entering his fourth year, he resembled a ten-year-old boy. Reading became his solitary pursuit; conversation ceased. A settled taciturnity absorbed him, replacing his former chatter. For the first time, neighbors spoke openly of the dawning evil in his goatish face. He muttered unfamiliar jargons, chanting in rhythms that chilled listeners with a primal terror. Dogs, once indifferent, now fled the property and refused to approach the boy. To walk safely across the countryside, Wilbur carried a pistol, though its occasional discharge failed to earn him respect among the village's canine guardians.

Visitors found Lavinia alone on the ground floor, while odd cries echoed from the sealed second story. She never explained the activities upstairs, though she turned pale with an abnormal dread when a jocose fish-peddler attempted the locked stairway door. The peddler claimed to hear a horse stamping above. The villagers, recalling the cattle that vanished and the sealed runways, shuddered at the memory of Old Whateley's youth and the sacrifices demanded by heathen gods. Soon, the entire neighborhood repulsed by the presence of the boy, mirroring the specific hatred the dogs held for him.

Then came 1917. War descended upon the region. As chairman of the local draft board, Squire Sawyer Whateley struggled to find a single young Dunwich man fit enough for a development camp. Alarmed by this wholesale regional decadence, the government dispatched officers and medical experts. Their survey, later read by New England newspapers, drew reporters to the Whateley farm. The *Boston Globe* and *Arkham Advertiser* published flamboyant accounts of Wilbur's precocity, Old Whateley's black magic, the shelves of strange books, and the sealed chambers of the ancient farmhouse.

At four and a half years old, Wilbur looked fifteen. Coarse dark down covered his lips and cheeks; his voice broke. Earl Sawyer escorted two teams of reporters and photographers to the property, drawing their attention to the foul trickle emanating from the sealed upper spaces. It smelled, he insisted, exactly like the abandoned toolshed and the faint odors near the mountain stones.

The Dunwich folk read the papers and grinned at the obvious errors. They wondered why the writers fixated on the fact that Old Whateley always paid for his cattle in gold coins of extreme antiquity. The Whateleys received the visitors with ill-concealed distaste, refusing to court further scandal with violence, though they dared not refuse entry outright.

For a decade, the annals of the Whateleys sank indistinguishably into the morbid sediment of Dunwich, a community hardened by their own peculiarities and accustomed to the annual orgies of May Eve and All-Hallows. Twice yearly, fires blazed atop Sentinel Hill, triggering mountain rumblings that grew in violent crescendo; meanwhile, strange portents haunted the lonely farmhouse at all seasons. Callers claimed to hear sounds emanating from the sealed upper story even when the entire family remained below, speculating on the duration of sacrifices involving cows or oxen. Talk arose of reporting to the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, yet nothing came of it, for Dunwich folk possess no desire to invite the scrutiny of the outside world.

Around 1923, when Wilbur was a boy of ten whose mind, voice, stature, and prematurely bearded face projected an aura of maturity, a second great siege of carpentry commenced within the old house. Confined to the sealed upper section, discarded lumber suggested that the youth and his grandfather had demolished all internal partitions and stripped away the attic floor, leaving a single, vast open void spanning the distance between the ground story and the peaked roof. They had also razed the great central chimney, fitting the rusty range with a flimsy exterior tin stove-pipe. In the spring following this renovation, Old Whateley noted an increasing influx of whippoorwills emerging from Cold Spring Glen to chirp beneath his window at night. He regarded this phenomenon with grave significance, telling the loungers at Osborn's that his time had nearly arrived. "They whistle jest in tune with my breathin' naow," he rasped. "An' I guess they're gittin' ready to ketch my soul. They know it's a-goin' aout, an' dun't calc'late to miss it. Yew'll know, boys, arter I'm gone, whether they git me er not. Ef they dew, they'll keep up a-singin' an' laffin' till break o' day. Ef they dun't, they'll kinder quiet daown like. I expeck them an' the souls they hunts fer hev some pretty tough tussles sometimes."

On Lammas Night, 1924, Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was hastily summoned by Wilbur Whateley, who had lashed his solitary horse through the darkness and telephoned from Osborn's in the village. He found Old Whateley in a critical state, his cardiac action and stertorous breathing signaling an imminent end. By the bedside stood the shapeless albino daughter and the oddly bearded grandson, while from the vacant abyss overhead came a disquieting suggestion of rhythmical surging, akin to waves lapping upon a level beach. Yet, Dr. Houghton was chiefly disturbed by the chattering night birds outside—a seemingly limitless legion of whippoorwills crying an endless message in repetitions timed diabolically to the dying man's wheezing gasps. It was uncanny, unnatural, excessive, thought the doctor, mirroring the reluctant region he had entered.

Toward one o'clock, Old Whateley regained consciousness, interrupting his wheeze to choke out instructions for his grandson. "More space, Willy, more space soon. Yew grows--an' _that_ grows faster. It'll be ready to sarve ye soon, boy. Open up the gates to Yog-Sothoth with the long chant that ye'll find on page 751 _of the complete edition_, an' _then_ put a match to the prison. Fire from airth can't burn it nohaow!" He spoke with obvious madness. After a pause, during which the flock of whippoorwills outside adjusted their cries to the altered tempo while distant hill noises stirred, he added another fragment. "Feed it reg'lar, Willy, an' mind the quantity; but dun't let it grow too fast fer the place, fer ef it busts quarters or gits aout afore ye opens to Yog-Sothoth, it's all over an' no use. Only them from beyont kin make it multiply an' work.... Only them, the old uns as wants to come back...." Speech gave way once more to gasps, prompting Lavinia to scream as the whippoorwills seemed to follow the shift in rhythm. This cycle persisted for over an hour until the final throaty rattle ceased. Dr. Houghton drew shrunken lids over the glazing gray eyes as the bird tumult faded imperceptibly into silence. Lavinia sobbed, but Wilbur only chuckled, the hill noises rumbling faintly behind him. "They didn't git him," he muttered in his heavy bass voice.

By this time, Wilbur was a scholar of tremendous, one-sided erudition, quietly known by correspondence to librarians guarding rare and forbidden texts in distant repositories. He was increasingly hated and dreaded around Dunwich due to vague suspicions regarding certain youthful disappearances, yet he silenced all inquiry through fear or by deploying the fund of old-time gold that flowed regularly—and increasingly—for cattle-buying, just as in his grandfather's era. Now tremendously mature in aspect, his height, having reached the normal adult limit, seemed inclined to wax beyond it. In 1925, when a scholarly correspondent from Miskatonic University visited and departed pale and puzzled, Wilbur stood fully six and three-quarters feet tall. Throughout the years, he had treated his half-deformed albino mother with growing contempt, eventually forbidding her to accompany him to the hills on May Eve and Hallowmass. By 1926, the poor creature complained to Mamie Bishop of her terror of him. "They's more abaout him as I knows than I kin tell ye, Mamie," she whispered. "An' naowadays they's more nor what I know myself. I vaow afur Gawd, I dun't know what he wants nor what he's a-tryin' to dew."

That Hallowe'en, the hill noises roared louder than ever, and fire burned on Sentinel Hill as custom dictated, but villagers paid more attention to the rhythmical screaming of vast flocks of unnaturally belated whippoorwills assembled near the unlighted Whateley farmhouse. After midnight, their shrill notes erupted into a pandemoniac cachinnation filling the countryside, quieting only at dawn. Then they vanished, hurrying southward, a month overdue. No one could be certain of the meaning until later; none of the countryfolk seemed to have perished—but poor Lavinia Whateley, the twisted albino, was never seen again.

In the summer of 1927, Wilbur repaired two sheds in the farmyard and began moving his books and effects outward. Shortly thereafter, Earl Sawyer reported to the loungers at Osborn's that further carpentry was underway in the Whateley farmhouse. Wilbur was sealing all doors and windows on the ground floor, appearing to remove partitions as he and his grandfather had done upstairs four years prior. Living in one of the sheds, he seemed unusually worried and tremulous. People generally suspected him of knowledge concerning his mother's disappearance, and very few approached his neighborhood now. His height had increased to more than seven feet, showing no signs of ceasing its relentless development.

The winter following the initial excursion brought a repetition of strangeness, though the scale shifted from travel to obsession. Every correspondence sent to the Widener Library, the Bibliothèque Nationale, the British Museum, the University of Buenos Aires, and the Library of Miskatonic University ended in silence or refusal. The desired volume remained inaccessible through bureaucratic channels. Consequently, Wilbur traveled personally. He arrived in Arkham shabby, dirty, bearded, and speaking with a dialect that marked him as uncouth. Carrying a cheap new valise from Osborn's general store, this creature—almost eight feet tall, dark, and possessing the angular, goatish profile of a gargoyle—presented himself at the college library. His sole purpose was the acquisition of the forbidden text: the *Necronomicon* of the mad Arab Alhazred, specifically the Latin edition printed in Spain during the seventeenth century, held under lock and key.

Wilbur had never witnessed a city before, yet he navigated the streets with singular intent, passing heedlessly by the great white-fanged watchdog that barked with unnatural fury and tugged frantically at its chain. In his possession was the imperfect English copy of Dr. Dee's translation, bequeathed by his grandfather. Upon gaining access to the Latin original, he immediately began collating the two texts, searching for a specific passage located on the 751st page of his defective volume. He could not help but mention this discrepancy to the librarian, Henry Armitage—a man of considerable erudition holding degrees from Miskatonic, Princeton, and Johns Hopkins—who had previously visited the farm and now questioned him politely.

Armitage watched as Wilbur copied the chosen formula. The left-hand page of the Latin text contained monstrous threats regarding the peace and sanity of the world. The words, translated mentally by the librarian, spoke of a reality where humanity was neither the oldest nor the last of Earth's masters. "Not in the spaces we know, but between them," the text declared. "They walk serene and primal, undimensioned and to us unseen. Yog-Sothoth knows the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, future, all are one in Yog-Sothoth." It detailed how These entities knew where the Old Ones had broken through and where they would break through again, noting that while their scent could sometimes reveal their proximity, their true semblance was invisible except in the distorted features of those they had begotten upon mankind. The text described a realm where the wind gibbered with their voices and the earth muttered with their consciousness, bending forests and crushing cities without being beheld. It mentioned Kadath in the cold waste, the ice deserts of the South, and the sunken isles of the ocean, all bearing seals of these powers. Even Great Cthulhu, their cousin, could perceive them only dimly. The prophecy concluded with a chilling assertion: "Man rules now where They ruled once; They shall soon rule where man rules now."

Dr. Armitage, associating these lines with the brooding presences of Dunwich and the dim, hideous aura radiating from Wilbur Whateley—from a dubious birth to a cloud of probable matricide—felt a wave of fright as tangible as a draft from a tomb. The bent, goatish giant before him seemed like the spawn of another planet or dimension, linked to black gulfs of essence stretching beyond all spheres of force, matter, space, and time.

Presently, Wilbur raised his head and began speaking in a strange, resonant fashion, hinting at vocal organs unlike any run-of-the-mill human anatomy. "Mr. Armitage," he said, "I calc'late I've got to take that book home. They's things in it I've got to try under sarten conditions that I can't git here, an' it 'ud be a mortal sin to let a red-tape rule hold me up. Let me take it along, sir, an' I'll swar they wun't nobody know the difference. I dun't need to tell ye I'll take good keer of it. It wa'n't me that put this Dee copy in the shape it is...."

He paused, sensing the firm denial on the librarian's face, his own goatish features shifting into a crafty expression. Armitage, half-ready to suggest copying necessary passages, suddenly recoiled. The responsibility of handing such a being the key to blasphemous outer spheres was too great. Whateley perceived the rejection and attempted to deflect it lightly. "Wal, all right, ef ye feel that way abaout it. Maybe Harvard wun't be so fussy as yew be." Without further comment, he rose and strode out of the building, stooping awkwardly at each doorway.

From the window, Armitage heard the savage yelping of the watchdog and studied Whateley's gorilla-like lope across the campus. He recalled the wild tales and old Sunday stories from the *Advertiser*, the lore gathered from Dunwich rustics, and the certainty that unseen, non-earthly horrors rushed fetid through New England's glens. Now, he sensed the close presence of a terrible fragment of the intruding horror, glimpsing a hellish advance in the black dominion of the ancient nightmare. With a shudder of disgust, Armitage locked away the *Necronomicon*, though the room still reeked with an unholy, unidentifiable stench—the same odor that had sickened him at the Whateley farmhouse less than three years prior.

"Inbreeding?" Armitage muttered half-aloud, laughing mockingly at the village rumors. "Great God, what simpletons! Show them Arthur Machen's *Great God Pan* and they'll think it a common Dunwich scandal!" Yet the question persisted: what cursed, shapeless influence had fastened itself on the world in half-human flesh nine months after May Eve of 1912? What walked on the mountains that night?

During the ensuing weeks, Dr. Armitage collected all possible data on Wilbur Whateley and the formless presences surrounding Dunwich. Communication with Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury revealed much to ponder in the grandfather's final words. A visit to Dunwich Village yielded little new information, but a close survey of the *Necronomicon* in the sections Wilbur had sought provided terrible clues to the nature and desires of the strange evil threatening the planet. Talks with students of archaic lore in Boston and letters to others abroad fueled a growing amazement that escalated from alarm to acute spiritual fear. As summer approached, Armitage felt dimly that something must be done about the lurking terrors of the upper Miskatonic valley and the monstrous being known to the human world as Wilbur Whateley.

The year was 1928, situated somewhere between Lammas and the autumnal equinox. Dr. Armitage had already witnessed the monstrous prologue of the Dunwich horror. He knew of Whateley's grotesque excursion to Cambridge, of the frantic, desperate attempts to borrow or copy from the *Necronomicon* at the Widener Library. Those efforts had failed; Armitage had issued warnings of the keenest intensity to every librarian holding charge of the dreaded volume. Wilbur had been shockingly nervous at Cambridge, anxious for the book yet almost equally terrified to remain away, as if he feared the results of prolonged absence.

Early in August, the half-expected outcome developed. In the small hours of the third day, Dr. Armitage was awakened suddenly by the wild, fierce cries of the savage watchdog on the college campus. Deep and terrible, the snarling, half-mad growls and barks continued; always in mounting volume, but with hideously significant pauses. Then there rang out a scream from a wholly different throat—a scream that roused half the sleepers of Arkham and haunted their dreams ever afterward, a sound that could come from no being born of earth, or wholly of earth.

Armitage hastened into some clothing and rushed across the street and lawn to the college buildings. He saw that others were ahead of him; he heard the echoes of a burglar-alarm still shrilling from the library. An open window showed black and gaping in the moonlight. What had come had indeed completed its entrance; for the barking and the screaming, now fast fading into a mixed low growling and moaning, proceeded unmistakably from within. Some instinct warned Armitage that what was taking place was not a thing for unfortified eyes to see, so he brushed back the crowd with authority as he unlocked the vestibule door.

Among the others he saw Professor Warren Rice and Dr. Francis Morgan, men to whom he had told some of his conjectures and misgivings; and these two he motioned to accompany him inside. The inward sounds, except for a watchful, droning whine from the dog, had by this time quite subsided; but Armitage now perceived with a sudden start that a loud chorus of whippoorwills among the shrubbery had commenced a damnably rhythmical piping, as if in unison with the last breath of a dying man.

The building was full of a frightful stench which Dr. Armitage knew too well, and the three men rushed across the hall to the small genealogical reading-room whence the low whining came. For a second nobody dared to turn on the light; then Armitage summoned up his courage and snapped the switch. One of the three—it is not certain which—shrieked aloud at what sprawled before them among disordered tables and overturned chairs. Professor Rice declares that he wholly lost consciousness for an instant, though he did not stumble or fall.

The thing that lay half-bent on its side in a fetid pool of greenish-yellow ichor and tarry stickiness was almost nine feet tall, and the dog had torn off all the clothing and some of the skin. It was not quite dead, but twitched silently and spasmodically while its chest heaved in monstrous unison with the mad piping of the expectant whippoorwills outside. Bits of shoe-leather and fragments of apparel were scattered about the room, and just inside the window an empty canvas sack lay where it had evidently been thrown. Near the central desk a revolver had fallen, a dented but undischarged cartridge later explaining why it had not been fired.

The thing itself, however, crowded out all other images at the time. It would be trite and not wholly accurate to say that no human pen could describe it, but one may properly say that it could not be vividly visualized by anyone whose ideas of aspect and contour are too closely bound up with the common life-forms of this planet and of the three known dimensions. It was partly human, beyond a doubt, with very manlike hands and head, and the goatish, chinless face had the stamp of the Whateleys upon it. But the torso and lower parts of the body were teratologically fabulous, so that only generous clothing could ever have enabled it to walk on earth unchallenged or uneradicated.

Above the waist it was semi-anthropomorphic; though its chest, where the dog's rending paws still rested watchfully, had the leathery, reticulated hide of a crocodile or alligator. The back was piebald with yellow and black, and dimly suggested the squamous covering of certain snakes. Below the waist, though, it was the worst; for here all human resemblance left off and sheer fantasy began. The skin was thickly covered with coarse black fur, and from the abdomen a score of long greenish-gray tentacles with red sucking mouths protruded limply. Their arrangement was odd, and seemed to follow the symmetries of some cosmic geometry unknown to earth or the solar system. On each of the hips, deep set in a kind of pinkish, ciliated orbit, was what seemed to be a rudimentary eye; whilst in lieu of a tail there depended a kind of trunk or feeler with purple annular markings, and with many evidences of being an undeveloped mouth or throat.

The limbs, save for their black fur, roughly resembled the hind legs of prehistoric earth's giant saurians; and terminated in ridgy-veined pads that were neither hooves nor claws. When the thing breathed, its tail and tentacles rhythmically changed color, as if from some circulatory cause normal to the non-human side of its ancestry. In the tentacles this was observable as a deepening of the greenish tinge, whilst in the tail it was manifest as a yellowish appearance which alternated with a sickly grayish-white in the spaces between the purple rings. Of genuine blood there was none; only the fetid greenish-yellow ichor which trickled along the painted floor beyond the radius of the stickiness, and left a curious discoloration behind it.

As the presence of the three men seemed to rouse the dying thing, it began to mumble without turning or raising its head. Dr. Armitage made no written record of its mouthings, but asserts confidently that nothing in English was uttered. At first the syllables defied all correlation with any speech of earth, but toward the last there came some disjointed fragments evidently taken from the *Necronomicon*, that monstrous blasphemy in quest of which the thing had perished. Those fragments, as Armitage recalls them, ran something like "*N'gai, n'gha'ghaa, bugg-shoggog, y'hah; Yog-Sothoth, Yog-Sothoth....*" They trailed off into nothingness as the whippoorwills shrieked in rhythmical crescendoes of unholy anticipation.

Then came a halt in the gasping, and the dog raised his head in a long, lugubrious howl. A change came over the yellow, goatish face of the prostrate thing, and the great black eyes fell in appallingly. Outside the window the shrilling of the whippoorwills had suddenly ceased, and above the murmurs of the gathering crowd there came the sound of a panic-struck whirring and fluttering. Against the moon vast clouds of feathery watchers rose and raced from sight, frantic at that which they had sought for prey.

All at once the dog started up abruptly, gave a frightened bark, and leaped nervously out the window by which it had entered. A cry rose from the crowd, and Dr. Armitage shouted to the men outside that no one must be admitted till the police or medical examiner came. He was thankful that the windows were just too high to permit of peering in, and drew the dark curtains carefully down over each one. By this time two policemen had arrived; and Dr. Morgan, meeting them in the vestibule, was urging them for their own sakes to postpone entrance to the stench-filled reading-room till the examiner came and the prostrate thing could be covered up.

Meanwhile frightful changes were taking place on the floor. One need not describe the *kind* and *rate* of shrinkage and disintegration that occurred before the eyes of Dr. Armitage and Professor Rice; but it is permissible to say that, aside from the external appearance of face and hands, the really human elements in Wilbur Whateley must have been very small. When the medical examiner came, there was only a sticky whitish mass on the painted boards, and the monstrous odor had nearly disappeared. Apparently Whateley had had no skull or bony skeleton; at least, in any true or stable sense. He had taken somewhat after his unknown father.

Formalities were performed by bewildered officials; abnormal details were duly excised from the press and public consciousness. Men were dispatched to Dunwich and Aylesbury to locate property and notify heirs of the late Wilbur Whateley. They found the countryside in a state of high agitation, driven by subterranean rumblings beneath the domed hills and the unwonted stench rising from the great empty shell of Whateley's boarded-up farmhouse. Earl Sawyer, who had tended the livestock during Wilbur's absence, had developed a woefully acute case of nerves. The officials devised polite excuses not to enter the noisome, boarded place, content to confine their survey of the deceased's living quarters and newly mended sheds to a single, hurried visit. They filed a ponderous report at the court-house in Aylesbury, while litigations concerning heirship continued amongst the innumerable Whateleys, decayed and undecayed, of the upper Miskatonic valley. On the old bureau, which served as the owner's desk, lay an almost interminable manuscript in strange characters—a huge ledger adjudged a diary due to its erratic spacing and variations in ink. It presented a baffling puzzle to those who examined it. After a week of debate, it was sent to Miskatonic University, together with the deceased's collection of strange books, for study and possible translation; but even the best linguists soon saw that it was unlikely to be unriddled with ease. No trace of the ancient gold with which Wilbur and Old Whateley always paid their debts has yet been discovered.

It was in the dark of September ninth that the horror broke loose. The hill noises had been pronounced during the evening, and dogs barked frantically all night. Early risers on the tenth noticed a peculiar stench in the air. About seven o'clock, Luther Brown, the hired boy at George Corey's, rushed frenziedly back from his morning trip to Ten-Acre Meadow with the cows. He was almost convulsed with fright as he stumbled into the kitchen; in the yard outside, the herd pawed and lowed pitifully, having followed the boy back in the panic they shared. Between gasps, Luther tried to stammer his tale to Mrs. Corey. "Up thar in the rud beyond the glen, Mis' Corey—they's suthin' ben thar! They's _prints_ in the rud, Mis' Corey—great raound prints as big as barrel-heads, all sunk daown deep like a elephant had ben along, _only they's a sight more nor four feet could make_. Mrs. Corey, unable to extract more information, began telephoning the neighbors, thus starting the overture of panic that heralded the major terrors.

When she reached Sally Sawyer, housekeeper at Seth Bishop's, it became her turn to listen instead of transmit. Sally's boy Chauncey, who slept poorly, had been up on the hill toward Whateley's and had dashed back in terror after one look at the place and the pasturage where Mr. Bishop's cows had been left out all night. By that noon, fully three-quarters of the men and boys of Dunwich were trooping over the roads and meadows between the new-made Whateley ruins and Cold Spring Glen. They examined in horror the vast, monstrous prints, the maimed Bishop cattle, the strange, noisome wreck of the farmhouse, and the bruised, matted vegetation of the fields and road-sides. Whatever had burst loose upon the world had assuredly gone down into the great sinister ravine; for all the trees on the banks were bent and broken, and a great avenue had been gouged in the precipice-hanging underbrush. It was as though a house, launched by an avalanche, had slid down through the tangled growths of the almost vertical slope. From below, no sound came, but only a distant, undefinable fetor. It is not to be wondered at that the men preferred to stay on the edge and argue, rather than descend and beard the unknown Cyclopean horror in its lair. Three dogs that were with the party had barked furiously at first, but seemed cowed and reluctant when near the glen. Someone telephoned the news to the _Aylesbury Transcript_; but the editor, accustomed to wild tales from Dunwich, did no more than concoct a humorous paragraph about it; an item soon afterward reproduced by the Associated Press.

The silence in the room was not empty; it was heavy, a physical weight pressing against the spine of the shelf-lined chamber where Wilbur Whateley's manuscript lay. To the experts of Miskatonic University, the document was a puzzle of impossible proportions. Its alphabet, vaguely reminiscent of the shaded scripts of Mesopotamia yet utterly alien to any known tongue, defied classification. Linguists offered only a consensus of bafflement: an artificial cipher, resistant to every method of decryption. The ancillary texts seized from Whateley's quarters added to the dread—a heavy tome bound in iron, inscribed in a script resembling Sanskrit, offering no illumination.

Dr. Armitage accepted the burden. His age, seventy-three, carried a specific gravity, a focus honed by decades of studying the mystical formulæ of antiquity. He viewed the symbols not necessarily as a sacred language of forbidden cults, but pragmatically as a cipher masking a modern vernacular. If the writer possessed the patience to compose such a vast text, he reasoned, he would not laboriously adopt a foreign syntax unless compelled by specific incantations. Thus, Armitage attacked the manuscript with the assumption that beneath the garb lay English.

He fortified himself with the accumulated lore of cryptography, immersing himself in the dense, dusty volumes of Trithemius, Porta, Vigenère, and the later treatises of Davys and Thicknesse. Night after night, amidst the arcana of *Poligraphia*, he worked. The process was slow, a grinding of stone against stone. He realized he faced one of the most intricate cryptograms ever devised, a grid of shifting keys known only to initiates. Progress was elusive; moments of clarity were shattered by unforeseen obstacles.

Then, as September approached, the fog lifted. Letters emerged from the chaos, distinct and undeniable. The text was English. On the evening of September second, the final barrier crumbled. Armitage read the first continuous passage of Wilbur Whateley's annals. It was a diary, written in a style that betrayed a dangerous mixture of occult erudition and profound illiteracy.

The entry, dated November 26, 1916, struck with the force of a physical blow. Written by a child of three and a half who resembled a boy of twelve, the voice was chillingly detached. *"Today learned the Aklo for the Sabaoth,"* it read. *"Which did not like, it being answerable from the hill and not from the air. That upstairs more ahead of me than I had thought it would be, and is not like to have much earth brain."*

The narrative descended further into the uncanny. A dog shot for biting the narrator. A grandfather reciting formulas. Visions of the inner city at the magnetic poles. Plans to clear the earth, to wait for the "outside" helpers who could not take bodies without human blood. The diarist wondered how he would appear when the earth was stripped clean, when there were no earth-beings left. *"They from the air told me at Sabbat that it will be years before I can clear off the earth... I wonder how I shall look when the earth is cleared."*

Morning found Dr. Armitage in a cold sweat, his mind trapped in a frenzy of wakeful concentration. He had not left the manuscript all night. The electric light hummed above him as he turned page after page, his hands shaking, deciphering the cryptic text with a nervous intensity. He had told his wife he would not be home; when she brought breakfast, he could barely swallow a mouthful. Lunch and dinner followed, eaten only in fractions.

By the middle of the next night, he drowsed in his chair, only to wake instantly from nightmares as hideous as the truths he had uncovered. On the morning of September fourth, Professor Rice and Dr. Morgan visited him. They departed trembling, their faces ashen. That evening, Armitage slept fitfully. By Wednesday, he was back at the manuscript, taking copious notes. Before dawn, he slept briefly in an easy-chair; by noon, his physician, Dr. Hartwell, demanded he cease work. Armitage refused, insisting the completion of the reading was vital, promising an explanation later.

That evening, as twilight fell, he finished his terrible perusal and sank back, exhausted. His wife found him in a half-comatose state. When her eyes wandered toward his notes, he cried out sharply, driving her away. Rising weakly, he gathered the scribbled papers, sealing them in a great envelope tucked into his inside coat pocket. He managed to reach home, but Dr. Hartwell was summoned immediately. As the doctor laid him to bed, Armitage muttered endlessly, *"But what, in God's name, can we do?"*

The following day, Armitage slept but remained partly delirious. He spoke of the imperative need to confer with Rice and Morgan, his wilder wanderings becoming increasingly alarming. He shouted for the destruction of a boarded-up farmhouse, referencing plans for the extirpation of all life by an elder race from another dimension. He claimed the world was in danger, that the Elder Things wished to strip the earth and drag it into some other plane of entity. At other times, he screamed for the *Necronomicon* and the *Dæmonolatreia* of Remigius, hoping to find a formula to check the peril. *"Stop them, stop them!"* he would roar. *"Those Whateleys meant to let them in, and the worst of all is left! Tell Rice and Morgan we must do something..."*

Despite his frailty, Armitage's physique held firm enough to sleep off the disorder without fever. He woke late Friday, clear-headed but sobered by a gnawing fear and a tremendous sense of responsibility. Saturday afternoon, he summoned Rice and Morgan to the library. For the rest of the day and evening, the three men tortured their brains in wild speculation and desperate debate. Books were pulled from stacks and secure storerooms; diagrams and formulas were copied with feverish haste. Skepticism vanished. All three had seen the body of Wilbur Whateley lying on the floor of that very building; none dared treat the diary as the ravings of a madman.

Opinions divided on whether to notify the Massachusetts State Police, but the negative prevailed. There were forces involved that could not be believed by those who had not witnessed the evidence firsthand. Late at night, the conference disbanded without a definite plan. Yet, all day Sunday, Armitage compared formulas and mixed chemicals from the college laboratory. The more he reflected on the hellish diary, the more he doubted the efficacy of any material agent against the entity Whateley had left behind—the earth-threatening thing destined to burst forth in a few hours.

Monday mirrored Sunday, a repetition of research and experiment. Tuesday brought a definite line of action, a plan to travel to Dunwich within a week. Then, on Wednesday, the great shock came. Tucked obscurely in a corner of the *Arkham Advertiser* was a facetious item from the Associated Press reporting a record-breaking monster spawned by Dunwich's bootleg whisky.

Armitage, half-stunned, telephoned for Rice and Morgan. Far into the night they discussed; the next day became a whirlwind of preparation. Armitage knew he was meddling with terrible powers, yet saw no other way to annul the deeper, malign meddling of others.

Friday morning, Armitage, Rice, and Morgan departed by automobile for Dunwich, reaching the village around one in the afternoon. The daylight was bright, yet a specific, quiet dread hung over the landscape, settling upon the strangely domed hills and the deep, shadowed ravines of the stricken region. Occasionally, atop a mountain peak, a gaunt circle of stones stood silhouetted against the sky. From the atmosphere of hushed terror permeating Osborn's store, they deduced that something terrible had transpired; shortly thereafter, they confirmed the total annihilation of the Elmer Frye household. Throughout the afternoon, the trio circled Dunwich, interrogating locals regarding the events and witnessing with escalating horror the dreary ruins of the Frye property—the lingering residue of tar-like stickiness, the blasphemous tracks in the yard, the injured livestock belonging to Seth Bishop, and the vast swathes of displaced vegetation scattered across the terrain. To Armitage, the trail winding up and down Sentinel Hill possessed a cataclysmic significance; he lingered long before the sinister, altar-like stone upon the summit. Eventually, learning that a contingent of State Police had arrived from Aylesbury earlier that morning in response to initial reports of the Frye tragedy, the visitors resolved to locate the officers and exchange information. However, this objective proved easier to conceive than execute; no trace of the party could be found anywhere. Five officers had arrived in a single vehicle, yet now the car stood empty near the ruins in the Frye yard. The locals, having spoken with the police, initially appeared as bewildered as Armitage and his companions. Armitage, now confronted directly with the horror and its monstrous work, trembled under the weight of the responsibility he felt to bear. Through the glass, he saw Rice positioned perfectly above and behind the entity, poised to unleash the powder with devastating effect. To the naked eye, there was only an instant's flash of gray cloud, roughly the size of a large house, blooming near the summit.

Curtis dropped the instrument. It fell with a sharp crack into the ankle-deep mud. He reeled, collapsing where he stood, saved only by the grasping hands of bystanders. His voice emerged as a broken moan, barely audible: "Oh, oh, great Gawd... that... that..."

Pandemonium erupted, a cacophony of confused shouting. Only Henry Wheeler, amidst the chaos, managed to retrieve the fallen telescope and wipe the mud from its lenses. Curtis was beyond coherence; even fragmented answers dissolved instantly. "Bigger 'n a barn," he gasped, his speech slurring. "All made o' squirmin' ropes. Hull thing sort o' shaped like a hen's egg bigger'n anything, with dozens o' legs like hogsheads that haff shut up when they step... nothin' solid abaout it—all like jelly, an' made o' sep'rit wrigglin' ropes pushed clost together... great bulgin' eyes all over it... ten or twenty maouths or trunks a-stickin' aout all along the sides, big as stovepipes, an' all a-tossin' an' openin' an' shuttin'... all gray, with kinder blue or purple rings... an' Gawd in Heaven—that haff face on top!"

That final image shattered him. Before he could speak further, Curtis collapsed completely. Fred Farr and Will Hutchins lifted him onto the damp grass. Trembling, Henry Wheeler turned the rescued telescope toward the peak. Through the lenses, three tiny figures ran toward the summit, moving as fast as the steep incline permitted. Nothing else was visible.

Then, a strangely unseasonable noise filled the deep valley behind them, drifting up from the underbrush of Sentinel Hill itself. It was the piping of countless whippoorwills, but in their shrill chorus lurked a note of tense, malignant expectancy. Earl Sawyer took the telescope and reported the three figures standing on the highest ridge, nearly level with the altar-stone yet distant from it. One figure appeared to raise its hands above its head at rhythmic intervals. As Sawyer spoke, the crowd seemed to hear a faint, half-musical sound from the distance, as if a loud chant accompanied the gestures.

The silhouette on the remote peak must have been a spectacle of infinite grotesquerie, yet no observer possessed the capacity for aesthetic appreciation. "I guess he's sayin' the spell," Wheeler whispered, snatching the telescope back. The whippoorwills piped wildly, their rhythm curiously irregular, discordant with the visible ritual. Suddenly, the sunshine seemed to diminish without the intervention of clouds—a peculiar phenomenon noted by all. A rumbling brewed beneath the hills, mixed with a concordant vibration coming from the sky. Lightning flashed aloft, and the crowd searched in vain for the portents of a storm.

The chanting of the Arkham men became unmistakable. Wheeler saw through the glass that they were raising their arms in rhythmic incantation. From a farmhouse far away came the frantic barking of dogs. The quality of daylight shifted, and the crowd gazed at the horizon in wonder. A purplish darkness, born of a spectral deepening of the sky's blue, pressed down upon the rumbling hills. Lightning flashed again, brighter this time, revealing a certain mistiness around the altar-stone on the distant height. No one used the telescope at that exact moment.

The whippoorwills continued their irregular pulsation. The men of Dunwich braced themselves against an imponderable menace, the atmosphere surcharged with static. Without warning, deep, cracked, raucous vocal sounds tore through the air. These were not born of human throats; the organs of man cannot yield such acoustic perversions. If one had to assign a source, it would be the pit itself, yet the origin was unmistakably the altar-stone on the peak. To call them sounds is almost erroneous, for their ghastly, infra-bass timbre spoke to dim seats of consciousness and terror far subtler than the ear. Yet their form was indisputably that of half-articulate words.

They were loud—louder than the rumblings and thunder above which they echoed—and yet they emanated from no visible being. Imagination conjured a source in the realm of non-visible entities, causing the huddled crowd at the mountain's base to press closer together, wincing as if expecting a physical blow.

"_Ygnaiih... ygnaiih... thflthkh'ngha... Yog-Sothoth..."_ rang the hideous croaking out of space. "_Y'bthnk... h'ehye... n'grkdl'lh..."_

The speaking impulse faltered here, as if a frightful psychic struggle were underway. Henry Wheeler strained his eye at the telescope, seeing only the three grotesquely silhouetted human figures on the peak, moving their arms furiously in strange gestures as the incantation neared its culmination. From what black wells of Acherontic fear, from what unplumbed gulfs of extra-cosmic consciousness or obscure, long-latent heredity, were those half-articulate thunder-croakings drawn?

Presently, they gathered renewed force and coherence, growing in stark, utter, ultimate frenzy. "_Eh-ya-ya-ya-yahaah... e'yaya-yayaaaa... ngh'aaaa... ngh'aaaa... h'yuh... h'yuh... HELP! HELP!... ff--ff--ff--FATHER! FATHER! YOG-SOTHOTH!..."_

And then, silence. The pallid group in the road, still reeling from the indisputably English syllables that had poured thickly and thunderously down from the frantic vacancy beside the altar-stone, were never to hear such sounds again. Instead, they jumped violently at the terrific report that seemed to rend the hills—the deafening, cataclysmic peal whose source, whether inner earth or sky, no hearer could ever place.

A single lightning bolt shot from the purple zenith to the altar-stone. A great tidal wave of viewless force and indescribable stench swept down from the hill to all the countryside. Trees, grass, and underbrush were whipped into a fury. The frightened crowd at the mountain's base, weakened by the lethal fetor that seemed about to asphyxiate them, were almost hurled off their feet. Dogs howled from the distance; green grass and foliage wilted to a curious, sickly yellow-gray. Over field and forest were scattered the bodies of dead whippoorwills. The stench left quickly, but the vegetation never recovered. To this day, there remains something queer and unholy about the growths on and around that fearsome hill.

Curtis Whateley was only just regaining consciousness when the Arkham men descended the mountain, walking slowly in the beams of a sunlight once more brilliant and untainted. They were grave and quiet, seemingly shaken by memories and reflections even more terrible than those which had reduced the native group to a state of cowed quivering. In reply to a jumble of questions, they only shook their heads, reaffirming one vital fact.

"The thing has gone forever," Armitage said. "It has been split up into what it was originally made of, and can never exist again. It was an impossibility in a normal world. Only the least fraction was really matter in any sense we know. It was like its father—and most of it has gone back to him in some vague realm or dimension outside our material universe; some vague abyss out of which only the most accursed rites of human blasphemy could ever have called him for a moment on the hills."
