A wrong turn on the Aylesbury Pike, just past Dean’s Corners, leads travelers into a stretch of land that doesn’t belong—not to this time, not to this world. The road narrows, hemmed in by brambles and crumbling stone walls, while the trees loom unnaturally tall, their gnarled branches twisting like skeletal fingers. The fields are sparse, choked with weeds, and the few scattered houses sag with age and rot, their windows staring like hollow eyes.

The figures you glimpse—slouched on rotting porches or lurking in the overgrown meadows—never speak. They watch in silence, their faces gaunt, their eyes too knowing. Something in their stillness warns you away, as if they guard secrets best left buried.

Then the hills rise in the distance, their rounded crowns too perfect, too *wrong*. At their peaks stand rings of ancient stone, jagged teeth against the sky. The ravines that split the land yawn wide, their wooden bridges groaning underfoot. When dusk falls, the marshes come alive with the shrill cries of whippoorwills and the ceaseless drone of frogs—too loud, too eager. Fireflies swarm in unnatural numbers, their flickering light a mockery of life.

The Miskatonic River slithers through the valley, its waters glinting like scales. And beyond it, Dunwich clings to the shadow of Round Mountain, a cluster of sagging gambrel roofs and broken dreams. Most of its houses stand empty, their doors hanging open like slack jaws. The church, its steeple shattered, has been defiled into a general store, its shelves lined with dust and decay.

Crossing the covered bridge feels like stepping into a grave. The air inside is thick with the stench of mold and something older, something *hungry*. The village beyond is worse—a place where time has curdled, where the past refuses to stay dead.

No one comes to Dunwich anymore. The signposts were torn down long ago, after *what happened* in 1928. The few who remember whisper about inbred bloodlines, about curses carved into the hills. The locals are a withered breed, their faces twisted by generations of something darker than mere decay.

Legends linger—of Indian rites in the stone circles, of voices howling from beneath the earth. The Reverend Hoadley once preached of devils in the hills before vanishing without a trace. Even now, travelers swear they hear things in the ravines when the wind is right: whispers, laughter, the sound of wings where no birds fly.

And when the whippoorwills call at night, the people of Dunwich lock their doors. They say the birds come for the dying, that their cries sync with a man’s last breaths. If they catch a fleeing soul, they shriek in triumph. If not, they fall silent, waiting.

But those are just stories.

Aren’t they?

The farmhouse stood alone, clinging to the hillside like a rotten tooth in a withered jaw. Four miles from Dunwich village, a mile and a half from any other soul, it was here, in the dead hours before dawn on February 2nd, 1913, that Wilbur Whateley first drew breath. The date was Candlemas—though the folk of Dunwich called it by another name—and the night had been anything but silent. The hills groaned. The dogs howled as if the devil himself were loose in the valleys.

Lavinia Whateley, the child’s mother, was a gaunt, pallid thing—an albino with pink eyes that never quite met yours. At thirty-five, she was already bent by some unseen weight, her spine crooked as the old oaks that clawed at the sky beyond Sentinel Hill. She lived with her father, Old Whateley, a man whose name was whispered in the same breath as curses and black rites. No husband claimed Lavinia, and she made no effort to name one. Instead, she cradled the swarthy, squalling thing in her arms with something like pride, murmuring secrets to it in words that made even the hardiest farmers edge away.

The child was wrong from the start.

His skin was too dark, his limbs too long, his cries too knowing. Lavinia, who had spent her life knee-deep in her father’s crumbling, worm-eaten books, saw prophecy in his goatish face. She spoke of power, of futures that would split the earth. The neighbors, when they dared to speak of it at all, said only that the Whateleys had always been strange—and now they were stranger still.

The night Wilbur was born, there had been screaming. Not the cries of a laboring woman, but something worse—something that didn’t sound human at all. No midwife had been called. No doctor braved the snow-choked road. It was a week before Old Whateley rumbled into the village on his sleigh, his beard crusted with ice, his eyes alight with something that made the men at Osborn’s store clutch their whiskey tighter.

*“Ye needn’t think the only folks is the folks hereabouts,”* he’d hissed, grinning with too many teeth. *“Lavinny’s boy ain’t like you. Ain’t like any of ye. And some day—mark me—some day yew folks’ll hear him call his father’s name from the top o’ Sentinel Hill.”*

The words hung in the air like a curse.

In the months that followed, Wilbur grew too fast. At three months, he had the strength of a year-old child. At seven, he walked—first in stumbling steps, then with a terrible, deliberate certainty. By Hallowe’en, he was running up Sentinel Hill in the dead of night, bare as the day he was born, while a fire burned atop the ancient stones. Silas Bishop swore he’d seen it—the boy, darting through the underbrush like a shadow, his mother loping behind him. After that, Wilbur was never seen without clothes again. He buttoned himself up tight, as though hiding something beneath the fabric.

By January, he was speaking. Not in the babbling tongue of a child, but in words too clear, too smooth, as if something older lurked behind his lips. His voice carried an echo, a resonance that didn’t belong in Dunwich. His eyes, dark and knowing, watched the world with an intelligence that made even hardened men uneasy.

And the dogs—the dogs *hated* him. They snarled and snapped, their hackles rising whenever he passed, as if they sensed what lay beneath that buttoned-up shirt, that too-precise speech.

As for the cattle Old Whateley bought? They sickened. Their hides split with odd, incised wounds. Their throats festered with sores that looked too much like the ones festering on Old Whateley’s own neck.

The hills whispered. The books in the Whateley house moldered. And Wilbur grew.

Too fast.

Too wrong.

Old Whateley’s madness took shape in wood and nails. Though his herd never grew, cattle still vanished into his hands—paid for in tarnished gold coins that whispered of older, darker treasuries. And though his words often unraveled into senseless babble, his hands worked with grim purpose.

The house, a sagging skeleton half-swallowed by the hillside, began to change. The lower rooms had always sufficed for him and his slack-jawed daughter, Lavinia. But now the upper floor—long abandoned to dust and shadows—was being reclaimed. Boards groaned under his hammer, sealing every window with a craftsman’s precision. No one understood why he bothered. Fewer still understood why, when the work was done, one window in the east gable had been replaced with a heavy door, bolted from within. A rough wooden runway led up to it from the ground, as if something needed to come and go unseen.

Inside, another room took shape—this one for the child. Shelves rose like the ribs of some long-dead beast, carefully fitted to cradle the moldering books that had once littered the house. Old Whateley would crouch by the stove, stirring paste in a rusted pot, painstakingly repairing pages whose black-lettered secrets had crumbled with age.

*“The boy’ll use ’em better,”* he muttered to no one. *“He’s got the mind for it.”*

And the boy did.

By the time Wilbur was a year and seven months old, he spoke like a scholar and moved like something older. His limbs stretched too fast, his voice too deep for his years. He roamed the fields with his mother, but his true schooling came in the dim-lit room below the sealed upper floor, where the old man drilled him in words that twisted the air.

The tool-shed, locked tight since Wilbur’s birth, stood empty again. When Earl Sawyer stumbled inside after a fruitless haggle over cattle, he reeled back, gagging. The stench was unholy—thick as rotting meat, sharp as spoiled milk, but underneath it all, something worse. Something that made his skin prickle as if the earth itself had exhaled.

The hills, too, grew restless. On May Eve, tremors shivered through Dunwich, felt even in Aylesbury. By Halloween, the ground groaned in time with flickering lights atop Sentinel Hill—lights that danced where no fire should burn.

Wilbur, at four, looked ten. At four and a half, his face darkened with coarse stubble, his voice cracking between boy and man. He carried a pistol now. Dogs howled at his approach, their teeth bared in mindless terror. Farmers learned to chain them when the Whateley boy passed.

Visitors to the house found Lavinia alone, her fingers knotting in her apron as muffled thuds shook the ceiling above. Once, when a peddler jangled the locked stairway door, her face went gray.

*“What’s up there?”* he teased.

She didn’t answer. But later, in the village, the peddler swore he’d heard the sound of hooves.

The war came in 1917, and Dunwich’s sons were too sickly, too inbred, to fight. Government men arrived, sniffing out degeneracy. Reporters followed, their pencils scratching tales of a four-year-old who read Latin, of gold that stank of burial vaults, of a second floor no one could enter.

And through it all, the hills kept their secrets.

But the locked door creaked.

And something in the dark listened.

For ten years, the Whateleys faded into the shadowed rhythm of Dunwich—a town too accustomed to strangeness to question their ways. Twice a year, fires blazed atop Sentinel Hill, and each time, the earth groaned louder in response. At the old farmhouse, things stirred behind locked doors. Visitors swore they heard movement upstairs when the family stood plainly below, and whispers spread about what happened to the livestock that vanished into that sealed darkness. Once, there was talk of alerting the authorities, but Dunwich kept its secrets. Outsiders were not welcome.

By 1923, Wilbur had grown into something unnatural. Though only ten, his voice rumbled like a man’s, his beard bristled, and his eyes gleamed with a knowing too sharp for childhood. That year, the house shuddered under another frenzy of construction—all within the boarded-up upper floor. Discarded beams and splintered planks told of walls torn down, the attic floor ripped away, leaving only a gaping void from ground to rafters. The great chimney was dismantled, replaced by a trembling tin pipe that jutted like a skeletal finger from the roof.

Then the whippoorwills came.

They flocked from Cold Spring Glen in unnatural numbers, their cries threading through the night, synchronized with Old Whateley’s labored breaths. The old man grinned, yellowed teeth glinting in the lamplight as he told the men at Osborn’s: *"They’re tuning themselves to me. When my soul goes, they’ll be ready to catch it. You’ll know if they succeed—they’ll sing till dawn. If not… well, they’ll go quiet."*

On Lammas Night, 1924, Dr. Houghton answered a frantic summons to the farmhouse. He found Old Whateley drowning in his own breath, chest heaving like a bellows, while above them, the hollowed-out upper floor pulsed with something like the slow, wet drag of tides. The doctor barely noticed the albino daughter or the monstrous grandson. It was the birds that unsettled him—a ceaseless, mocking chorus of whippoorwills, their cries timed perfectly to the old man’s gasps.

Then, in a moment of clarity, Old Whateley seized Wilbur’s wrist. *"More space, boy. You grow… and* **it** *grows faster. When the time comes, open the gates. Use the chant—page 751. Then burn the prison. Fire from earth won’t touch it."* His voice frayed into a wet rattle. *"Feed it… but not too much. If it breaks out before the gates open, it’s over. Only the old ones… the ones who want back in…"*

The whippoorwills screamed with him as he died. When his breath stilled, so did they—all at once, as if cut by a blade. Wilbur chuckled. *"They didn’t get him."*

By then, Wilbur’s learning had stretched into places best left unspoken. Letters passed between him and keepers of forbidden texts, though few in Dunwich dared ask what he studied. The disappearances of local children were blamed on him in hushed tones, but gold and fear kept tongues still. He loomed taller each year, his frame straining past human limits. By 1925, a visiting scholar from Miskatonic fled white-faced after meeting him—Wilbur stood nearly seven feet then, and showed no sign of stopping.

His mother, the twisted albino, shrunk from him. In 1926, she confessed to Mamie Bishop, voice trembling: *"There’s things about him I know… and worse, things I don’t. I don’t know what he wants anymore."*

That Hallowe’en, the hills screamed. Fire burned on Sentinel Hill, but the town’s eyes turned toward the Whateley farm, where whippoorwills—far too late in the season—gathered in shrieking multitudes. At midnight, their cries became laughter, a cacophony that shook the valley until dawn. Then they vanished southward, a month out of time.

Lavinia was never seen again.

The next summer, Wilbur emptied the farmhouse. Books and oddities migrated to sheds in the yard. Earl Sawyer reported new carpentry—doors sealed, walls torn down. Wilbur, now over seven feet tall, moved like a man waiting for something to break. Few ventured near.

And in the hollow heart of the house, something waited with him.

The winter that followed was no less unsettling than Wilbur Whateley’s first venture beyond Dunwich. Letters to libraries—Harvard’s Widener, the Bibliothèque Nationale in Paris, the British Museum, the University of Buenos Aires—had all been met with refusal. The book he sought was too dangerous, too forbidden. Desperate, he turned to the last option: Miskatonic University in Arkham, where a single copy of the accursed *Necronomicon* lay chained in the depths of its library.

Wilbur arrived in Arkham like a nightmare given flesh. Towering nearly eight feet tall, his frame was gaunt yet powerful, his face a grotesque fusion of man and something far older. His beard was unkempt, his clothes reeked of damp earth and something fouler. In one hand, he clutched a cheap valise from Osborn’s store; inside, wrapped in stained cloth, was his grandfather’s incomplete English translation of the same dread text.

The city did not stir him. His eyes, dark and gleaming with alien intelligence, fixed only on the university gates. The library’s watchdog, a massive brute with teeth like splintered bone, howled at his approach, straining its chain as if it sensed the wrongness in him. Wilbur ignored it.

Inside, he was met by Dr. Henry Armitage—librarian, scholar, and one of the few men alive who knew the true horror lurking in Dunwich’s shadowed hills. Wilbur wasted no time. He needed the Latin edition, the *Necronomicon* in its most complete form, to cross-reference a passage missing from his own.

The name slithered through his mind like a serpent. The key, the gate, the guardian. The thing that was all things.

Armitage, though wary, allowed him access. As Wilbur pored over the ancient pages, the librarian’s gaze flickered to the open text. The words there were not mere script but a warning, a prophecy carved in the language of the damned:

*"Nor is it to be thought that man is either the oldest or the last of earth’s masters… The Old Ones were, the Old Ones are, and the Old Ones shall be… Yog-Sothoth knows the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the key and guardian of the gate…"*

A chill crept into Armitage’s bones. The air thickened, tainted with a stench like opened graves. He knew then, with dreadful certainty, that the thing before him was no man.

Wilbur’s voice, when he spoke, was wrong—too deep, too resonant, as if something else shaped the sounds. *"I need this book,"* he rasped. *"There are rites I must perform—conditions that can’t be met here."*

Armitage refused.

Wilbur’s face twisted, something inhuman flickering behind his eyes. For an instant, the librarian glimpsed the abyss that yawned behind the creature’s guise. Then, with a forced chuckle, Wilbur relented. *"Fine. Maybe Harvard won’t be so stingy."*

He left, his loping stride unnatural, the watchdog’s frenzied barks chasing him into the night.

Armitage locked the *Necronomicon* away, but the stench lingered. The same stench from the Whateley farm. The same stench that clung to Wilbur.

The words haunted him. What had sired Wilbur? What walked the hills of Dunwich, waiting in the dark?

That summer, Armitage began his own research, digging into old records, consulting scholars of the occult. The more he uncovered, the deeper his dread grew.

Something was coming.

And Wilbur Whateley was only the beginning.

The horror that would come to be known as the Dunwich terror began its final descent in the waning days of summer, 1928. Dr. Armitage, who had long feared such an event, found himself among the first to witness its grotesque overture.

Word had reached him of Wilbur Whateley’s desperate pilgrimage to Cambridge—his frenzied attempts to obtain the forbidden *Necronomicon* from the depths of Widener Library. Armitage had taken precautions, issuing stern warnings to all custodians of that accursed tome. Wilbur, it was said, had been a pitiful sight—his agitation palpable, torn between his hunger for the book and a frantic need to return home, as if some unseen clock were ticking toward catastrophe.

Then, in the dead of night on August 3rd, the inevitable came to pass.

Armitage was roused from sleep by the savage, unearthly howls of the college watchdog—a deep, guttural snarling that rose and fell in unnatural waves. And then—another sound. A scream. Not human, not animal, but something *other*, something that clawed at the mind and sent the sleeping citizens of Arkham bolting upright in their beds, their hearts hammering with a dread they could not name.

Armitage threw on his clothes and ran.

The campus was already stirring. The shrill wail of a burglar alarm pierced the night, and above it, the fading echoes of that terrible scream. A black, gaping maw of a window stood open in the moonlight. Whatever had entered was now inside. The dog’s barks had dissolved into a low, shuddering whine, thick with something like fear.

Armitage knew—*knew*—that whatever lay within was not meant for mortal eyes. He pushed back the gathering crowd with a sharp command, unlocked the vestibule door, and motioned for two colleagues—Professor Rice and Dr. Morgan—to follow.

The stench hit them like a physical blow—a reek of corruption and something worse, something *alien*. The dog’s whine had steadied into a watchful drone, but outside, the whippoorwills had begun their infernal chorus, their piping synchronized like a dirge for the dying.

The three men moved toward the genealogical reading room, the source of the sound. No one dared touch the light at first. Then Armitage, steeling himself, flipped the switch.

One of them screamed.

Professor Rice would later claim he had blacked out for an instant, though his legs never buckled. The thing sprawled before them defied reason. Nearly nine feet in length, it lay twisted in a pool of viscous, greenish-yellow slime, its clothing—and patches of its skin—torn away by the dog’s jaws. It was not yet dead. Its chest heaved in time with the whippoorwills’ chant, its limbs twitching in silent spasms.

Fragments of clothing and leather littered the floor. A revolver lay near the desk, unfired. An empty canvas sack had been tossed by the window.

But none of that mattered.

The *thing* commanded all attention.

To say it was indescribable would be a lie—but to describe it was to invite madness. Its upper half bore a ghastly mockery of humanity: manlike hands, a head with the unmistakable Whateley features—goatish and chinless. But below the waist, it was an abomination.

Its torso was a grotesque patchwork—leathery, scaled hide like some primordial reptile, furred in coarse black strands. From its abdomen hung a writhing mass of tentacles, their mouths glistening red and wet. Its hips bore pinkish, ciliated sockets—rudimentary eyes. And where a tail should have been, a pulsating appendage, ringed in purple, twitched obscenely.

Its breath came in ragged gasps, its flesh shifting colors unnaturally—greens deepening, sickly yellows pulsing beneath the surface. No blood flowed—only that foul ichor, seeping across the floor.

Then, as if sensing their presence, it began to mumble.

No words of this earth. At first, just sounds—guttural, impossible. Then, near the end, broken fragments of the *Necronomicon*’s black liturgy.

The words dissolved into silence as the whippoorwills’ shrieks reached a fever pitch.

Then—a final, shuddering breath.

The dog howled.

The thing’s face twisted, its great black eyes sinking inward. Outside, the whippoorwills fell abruptly silent. A great rustling filled the air as unseen things took flight, fleeing in terror from what they had sought to feast upon.

The dog, suddenly panicked, bolted through the window.

Armitage shouted for the doors to be barred. He drew the curtains tight, sealing away the horror within.

By the time the medical examiner arrived, there was little left to see.

Only a whitish, viscous smear on the floorboards.

And the lingering certainty that Wilbur Whateley had never been human.

Not truly.

Not in the ways that mattered.

The quiet unease that had settled over Dunwich was but a prelude to the true horror yet to come. Officials, bewildered and hesitant, went through the motions of their duties—inspecting the property of the late Wilbur Whateley, filing reports in Aylesbury, and avoiding the reeking, boarded-up ruin of his farmhouse at all costs. The air itself seemed thick with something foul, and from the hollow shell of the house came wet, slithering sounds, as if something vast stirred within.

Earl Sawyer, who had tended the livestock in Wilbur’s absence, was a wreck of frayed nerves. The men sent to assess the estate dared not linger long. They left quickly, grateful to escape the oppressive dread that clung to the place. Among the relics left behind was a massive ledger filled with cryptic symbols—a madman’s journal, perhaps, or something far worse. Sent to Miskatonic University for study, it defied all translation.

Then, on the night of September ninth, the earth itself seemed to revolt. The hills groaned, dogs howled in frenzy, and by dawn, a sickening stench tainted the air. Luther Brown, George Corey’s hired boy, stumbled back from the meadow wild-eyed and gasping.

“Up there—past the glen—” he choked out to Mrs. Corey. “Somethin’s been there! The bushes—torn up like a house was dragged through. And the *prints*—” His voice dropped to a whisper. “Big as barrels, sunk deep… but no four-footed thing made ’em. They had *marks*—like great big fans pressed into the dirt. Word spread like a brushfire. Sally Sawyer, over at Seth Bishop’s, had her own tale: Chauncey, her boy, had seen the Whateley house blown apart, timbers scattered like kindling, the lower floor smeared with a black, tarry slime. And in the yard—tracks. Enormous, sticky, leading off into the fields where the grass was flattened in a swath wider than a barn.

“Somethin’ ain’t right,” Sally whispered into the phone. “It ain’t human. Never was.”

By noon, the men of Dunwich gathered, grim-faced, to follow the trail. The prints gouged into the earth, the shattered trees, the stench—all led down into Cold Spring Glen. None dared descend. Whatever had emerged from the Whateley ruin had returned there, and the glen waited, silent and reeking, like the maw of some unseen beast.

That night, doors were barred, guns loaded. But no barricade could keep out the terror. At Elmer Frye’s farm, just east of the glen, the family awoke to a stench so thick it choked them. Then came the sounds—splintering wood, the cattle screaming, a *crushing* weight moving in the dark. By dawn, the Frye barn was in ruins, half the livestock gone, the rest torn apart.

The next day, fresh tracks scarred the road near Sentinel Hill. The thing had climbed straight up the cliffs, leaving a trail of crushed stone and that same foul stickiness upon the ancient ritual slab where the Whateleys once chanted to things best left unnamed.

Then, on the third night, the phones rang—a single, shattered plea: *“Help, oh, my Gawd!—”* before silence. By morning, the Frye farm was gone. Not ruined. *Erased.* The house collapsed inward, nothing left but stench and slime.

The Elmer Fryes had vanished from Dunwich.

And the horror was only beginning.

In the dim, book-choked chambers of Miskatonic University, a horror of another kind festered—silent, insidious, gnawing at the edges of reason. The manuscript left behind by Wilbur Whateley had become an obsession, a puzzle of maddening complexity that defied every expert who dared examine it. Its script was an abomination, a bastard offspring of Arabic and something far older, something never meant for human eyes.

The linguists had surrendered first. The cryptographers followed, their usual methods crumbling like ash in their hands. The books retrieved from Whateley’s lair only deepened the mystery—tomes bound in unknown tongues, one in particular locked with iron clasps, its pages whispering secrets in a script that mocked Sanskrit.

Only Dr. Armitage, steeped in forbidden lore, refused to yield. He worked in solitude, surrounded by the arcane texts of forgotten cryptographers—Trithemius, Porta, De Vigenère—their wisdom as brittle as the bones of the men who had penned them. Nights bled into days, his hands trembling over the manuscript, his mind fraying at the edges as he chased the ghost of a cipher older than civilization itself.

Then, in the small hours of September, the veil tore.

The words slithered into coherence, and what he read turned his blood to ice.

*November 26, 1916*—scrawled by a child who was no child, who spoke of things no human should know.

The words were a litany of blasphemy. Whateley wrote of formulas, of planes beyond human geometry, of an *"inner city"* glimpsed at the poles of the earth. He spoke of beings *"from the air,"* of blood rites, of a time when the earth would be *"cleared off."*

Armitage’s hands shook as he turned the pages. He did not sleep. He did not eat. His colleagues came, took one look at his work, and left as pale as corpses. The diary spoke of the *Necronomicon*, of elder races waiting to reclaim the earth, of a plan set in motion by the Whateleys to open the way for something unspeakable.

When the newspaper clipping arrived—a mocking, trivial report of a *"record-breaking monster"* in the backwoods—Armitage understood. The horror was no longer confined to ink and parchment.

The motorcar carrying Armitage, Rice, and Morgan rumbled into Dunwich just past noon under a sky that should have been cheerful. Yet the sunlight seemed to falter as it touched the warped hills, the twisted ravines—places where the earth itself felt wrong. Sentinel Hill loomed in the distance, its crown of ancient stones stark against the clouds like broken teeth.

At Osborn’s store, the air was thick with unspoken terror. The men didn’t need to ask what had happened—the ruin of the Frye house was answer enough.

Then the voice came.

Not human. Not earthly. A sound torn from the gulfs between stars, a croaking, shuddering blasphemy that vibrated in the bones before it reached the ears:

The words faltered, then surged again, rising to a shrieking crescendo:

And then—silence.

A deafening crack split the air. A bolt of lightning seared down from the purple-black sky, striking the altar-stone. An invisible force roared across the hillside, flattening grass, twisting trees, choking the crowd with a stench like opened graves. Dogs howled in the distance. The whippoorwills fell dead, their bodies littering the withered earth.

When the Arkham men descended, their faces were ashen.

“It’s gone,” Armitage said, hollow-voiced. “Shattered back into what it was. A thing that should never have been.”

Curtis stirred, clutching his head, the horror still fresh in his mind.

Old Zebulon Whateley, his voice a dry whisper, spoke at last:

“Fifteen years gone… Old Whateley said we’d hear Lavinny’s child call its father’s name on Sentinel Hill…”

Armitage’s gaze was grim.

“Burn the diary,” he said. “Destroy the stones. What we saw today was only a fraction of what waits outside. And it hungers.”
