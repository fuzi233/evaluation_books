"What! remain to be denounced—dragged, it may be, in chains?" Werner's words echoed in the silence of the Hale household, a grim prophecy hanging over the days that followed.

For the entire next day, the three sat together in a heavy, suspended quiet. Mr. Hale spoke only when his children demanded answers, forcing himself into the present tense as if the past had ceased to exist. Frederick's grief had receded into a stunned silence; the initial paroxysm of emotion had passed, leaving behind a man ashamed of his own collapse. Yet, beneath his composure lay a sorrow for his lost mother so profound it would haunt him for the rest of his life, a pain he deemed too sacred to utter aloud. Margaret, initially less volatile, now bore a deeper, more consuming suffering. Tears came easily to her, and even in conversation, her manner carried a mournful tenderness that intensified whenever her gaze met Frederick's, reminding her of his imminent departure. She welcomed his escape for her father's sake, despite her own heart breaking at the thought of losing him.

The terror gripping Mr. Hale had grown since Mrs. Hale's death, fed by his exclusive dwelling upon the danger. Every unusual sound made him jump; he found comfort only when Frederick remained unseen by anyone entering the room. As evening approached, his anxiety crystallized into a specific command.

"You will go with Frederick to the station, Margaret? I must know he is safely off. Promise me you will bring word that he is clear of Milton?"

"Certainly," Margaret replied softly. "I shall like it, if you won't be lonely without me, papa."

"No, no!" Mr. Hale insisted, his voice tight. "Unless you tell me you've seen him off, I shall constantly imagine someone has recognized him, that he has been stopped. Go to the Outwood station. It is nearly as close, yet far fewer people congregate there. Take a cab. The risk of him being seen is diminished. What time is your train, Fred?"

"Ten minutes past six. Very nearly dark."

"So, what will you do, Margaret?"

"Oh, I can manage," she assured him, trying to summon a strength she did not fully feel. "I am becoming braver, harder. The road home is well-lit, even if it grows dark. I was out last week much later."

Margaret felt a profound relief when the parting finally arrived—a separation from both the dead mother and the living father. She hurried Frederick into the cab to abbreviate a scene that would wound her father further. However, fate, in its capricious way, intervened. Due to a combination of her father's haste and a common error in the Railway Guide regarding arrival times at minor stations, they discovered upon reaching Outwood that they possessed nearly twenty minutes to spare. The booking office was closed; tickets could not be purchased. Instead, they descended the steps to the ground level beside the tracks. A broad path of cinders cut diagonally across a field adjacent to the carriage road, and there they paced back and forth, filling the void of time with silence.

Margaret's hand rested in Frederick's arm; he held it with an affectionate grip.

"Margaret," he began, his voice low and urgent, "I intend to consult Mr. Lennox regarding the possibility of exculpating myself. This is for your sake, more than for mine. I cannot bear the thought of your lonely position should anything happen to my father. He is terribly shaken, changed. I wish you could persuade him to consider the Cadiz plan. What could you do if he were taken away? We are curiously bereft of relations."

Margaret struggled to suppress tears at the tender anxiety with which he outlined a future she feared was already inevitable. The cares of the last few months had ravaged Mr. Hale. Yet, she rallied, attempting to steer the conversation toward the present.

"There have been such strange, unexpected changes in my life during these last two years," she said, her voice trembling slightly, "that I feel it is not worth while to calculate too closely what I should do if a future event takes place. I try to think only upon the present."

She paused. They stood still on the field side of the stile leading to the road, bathed in the golden light of the setting sun. Frederick held her hand, his eyes searching hers with wistful anxiety, reading a depth of care and trouble that her words failed to convey.

"We shall write often," she continued, her resolve hardening. "And I promise—for I see it will set your mind at ease—to tell you every worry I endure. Papa is—" She started, a barely perceptible jerk, but Frederick felt the sudden tension in her hand. He turned his full face toward the road, where a horseman was slowly riding, passing precisely where they stood. Margaret bowed stiffly; the rider returned the gesture with a cold, formal nod.

"Who is that?" Frederick asked, almost before the figure was out of earshot.

"Mr. Thornton," Margaret replied, her face flushing slightly, her spirit drooping. "You saw him before, you know."

"Only his back. He is an unprepossessing-looking fellow. What a scowl he has!"

"Something has happened to vex him," Margaret apologized gently. "You would not have thought him unprepossessing if you had seen him with mamma."

"I fancy it is time to go and take my ticket. If I had known how dark it would be, we wouldn't have sent back the cab, Margaret."

"Oh, don't fidget about that," she urged, trying to mask her own panic. "I can take a cab here, or return by the railway when I should have shops and people and lamps all the way from Milton. Don't think of me; take care of yourself. I am sick with the thought that Leonards may be in the same train with you. Look well into the carriage before you get in."

They returned to the station. Margaret insisted on entering the bright glare of the gas lamps to purchase her ticket. Several idle-looking young men lounged near the station-master. Margaret thought she recognized the face of one of them and returned a proud look of offended dignity for his undisguised, impertinent stare. She moved quickly to her brother, who stood outside, seizing his arm. "Have you got your bag? Let us walk about here on the platform," she said, her voice flurried, her bravery leaking away faster than she cared to admit.

A step followed them along the flagstones, halting as they halted, gazing down the line and listening to the whistle of the approaching train. They did not speak; their hearts were too full. Another moment, and the train would be here; a minute more, and he would be gone. Margaret almost repented the urgency with which she had urged him toward London; it seemed to throw more chances of detection in his path. If he had sailed for Spain via Liverpool, he might have been safe within hours.

Frederick turned, facing the lamp where the gas flared in vivid anticipation of the train's arrival. A man dressed as a railway porter stepped forward—a bad-looking fellow who appeared drunk into a state of brutality, though his senses seemed perfectly sharp.

"By your leave, miss!" he barked, shoving Margaret roughly aside and seizing Frederick by the collar. "Your name is Hale, I believe?"

In an instant, how Margaret did not see, for the world danced before her eyes, Frederick executed a sudden, desperate maneuver. He tripped the man, sending him crashing from the height of the platform onto the soft ground beside the railroad. He lay there, stunned.

"Run, run!" Margaret gasped. "The train is here. Was it Leonards? Oh, run! I will carry your bag."

She grabbed his arm, pushing him forward with all her feeble strength. A door swung open in a carriage; he leaped inside. As he leaned out to cry, "God bless you, Margaret!" the train surged past her, leaving her standing alone on the platform.

She was terribly sick and faint, grateful to retreat into the ladies' waiting-room and sit down for a moment. At first, she could only gasp for breath. The hurry, the sickening alarm, the nearness of the disaster—it all washed over her. Had the train not arrived at that precise moment, the man would have risen and called for assistance to arrest him. She wondered if he had gotten up, if she had seen him move, if he could have been seriously hurt. Venturing out, she found the platform brightly lit yet utterly deserted. She walked to the end and looked over the edge, somewhat fearfully. No one was there. Relief flooded her; otherwise, terrible thoughts would haunt her dreams. Even so, she trembled so violently that walking home along the lonely, dark road seemed impossible. She decided to wait for the down train to pass before taking her seat. But what if Leonards recognized her as Frederick's companion?

She peered around before venturing into the booking office to buy her ticket. Only railway officials remained, talking loudly among themselves.

"So Leonards has been drinking again!" said one, seemingly in authority. "He'll need all his boasted influence to keep his place this time."

"Where is he?" asked another. Margaret, her back turned, counted her change with trembling fingers, daring not to turn until she heard the answer.

"I don't know. He came in not five minutes ago with some long story about a fall he'd had, swearing awfully. He wanted to borrow money to go to London by the next up-train. He made all sorts of tipsy promises, but I had something else to do. I told him to go about his business, and he went off at the front door."

"He's at the nearest vaults, I'll be bound," the first speaker declared. "Your money would have gone there too, if you'd been such a fool as to lend it."

"Catch me! I knew better what his London meant. Why, he has never paid me off that five shillings"—and so they went on.

Now, all Margaret's anxiety focused solely on the train's arrival. She hid once more in the ladies' waiting-room, imagining every noise was Leonards' step, every boisterous voice his. But no one approached her until the train drew up. She was civilly helped into a carriage by a porter, into whose face she dared not look until they were in motion. Then she saw that it was not Leonards'.
