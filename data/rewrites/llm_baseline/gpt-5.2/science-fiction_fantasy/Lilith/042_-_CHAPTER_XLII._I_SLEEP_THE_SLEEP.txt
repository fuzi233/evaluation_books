When I came up out of the dark, the earth about my shoulders was wet as a cellar floor after rain, and the path I had clawed to the grave was already loosening—crumbing, sucking at my feet, turning to a living mire. The river in its ancient bed had swelled in the night like dough that will not be denied. It had begun to shoulder the stones and dead leaves and all the season’s refuse it carried, worrying its load as if the mountain itself had set a hand to its back. Soon it would be roaring over the precipice; and in its fall, split in two, one branch would hurry to resubmerge the orchard valley, the other to drown, perhaps, the horde of monsters; and between them it would cut the Evil Wood into an island.

I set out at once on my way back to those who had sent me.

At the precipice I took the narrow course between the two branches, for I would pass again by Mara’s cottage, lest she had returned. I hungered—not for bread alone, though my body had been long unkindly used—but for the plain comfort of her presence: the domestic surety of her open door, the homely order of her table, the clean taste of water in a world where even air could be bitter.

I reached the cottage. The door stood open still. The bread was still on the table, its crust dulled by the damp, as if the very silence had breathed upon it; the water still waited in its cup, holding a pale sky in its surface. No hearth snapped. No kettle muttered. No foot stirred the floor. The hush lay within and around the place like flour on a board after the hands have gone.

I stopped and called aloud at the door, but no voice answered. I went on.

A little farther, where the sand widened, I saw a grayheaded man sitting with his face in his hands, weeping, the tears dropping steadily as if he had been drawing water from an old well for too many years.

“What ails you, sir?” I asked. “Are you forsaken?”

He lifted his head. His eyes were red and bright. “I weep,” he said, “because they will not let me die. I have been to the house of death, and its mistress—though I am bent with years—refuses me. Intercede for me, sir, if you know her, I pray you.”

“Nay,” I answered, “that I cannot; for she refuses none whom it is lawful for her to receive.”

He stared as if I had spoken in a dialect he half remembered and resented. “How know you this of her? You have never sought death! You are much too young to desire it!”

“I fear your words may mean that, were you young again, neither would you desire it.”

“Indeed,” he said quickly, almost angrily, “young sir, I would not! And certain I am that you cannot.”

“I may not be old enough to desire to die,” I said, “but I am young enough to desire to live indeed. Therefore I go now to learn if she will at length take me in. You wish to die because you do not care to live: she will not open her door to you, for no one can die who does not long to live.”

“It ill becomes your youth to mock a friendless old man,” he returned. “Pray, cease your riddles!”

“Did not the Mother tell you something of the same sort?”

“In truth I believe she did,” he said, and his mouth twisted, “but I gave little heed to her excuses.”

“Ah then,” I replied, grieved for him as for a man starving beside a full table because he mistrusted the name of bread, “it is too plain you have not yet learned to die. Such had I too been, but for the Lady of Sorrow. I am indeed young, but I have wept many tears; pardon me if I presume to offer counsel: go to the Lady of Sorrow, and take with both hands what she will give you. Yonder lies her cottage. She is not in it now, but her door stands open, and there is bread and water on her table. Go in; sit down; eat of the bread; drink of the water; and wait there until she appear. Then ask counsel of her, for she is true, and her wisdom is great.”

He fell to weeping afresh, as if my words had only touched the rawness without reaching the root. I left him there, and the soft beat of his sobbing went on behind me like a small, steady drip in a cavern. What I said, I feared, he did not heed. But Mara would find him.

The sun was down, and the moon not risen, when I came to the abode of the monsters. It lay still as stone until I passed over its border, and then the world behind me changed. I heard a noise of many waters and a great cry, as if the swollen river had found a voice and the earth itself had answered it. The sound rose in a single rough chord—no bird-song, no leaf-whisper; it was the wild, untempered music of force. I did not turn my head. I would not let that roaring write its harsh words upon my heart.

Before I reached the house of death, the cold had sharpened into bitterness and the darkness had thickened until it seemed to have substance. Cold and darkness were one thing, and they entered my bones together, like a draught that burns and numbs in the same swallow. But from the window a small candle shone—the candle of Eve. Its light did not so much push the dark away as make a hearth-place in it, a little circle where the spirit could breathe. It guided me and kept frost and murk from my heart.

The door stood open, and the cottage lay empty.

I sat down, disconsolate. The clay floor held the chill like a stone platter holds cold meat, and the air had the faint, clean sterility of linen laid away too long. As I sat, a loneliness grew in me such as I had never yet known in all my wanderings. Thousands were near me, not one was with me. True, it was I who was dead, not they; yet whether by their life or by my death, we were divided. They were alive, but I was not dead enough even to know them alive: doubt would come, and doubt in that place was like a thin grit between tooth and tongue—small, but unendurable.

I took myself to task. The solitude was but seeming. I was awake, and they slept—that was all. It was only that they lay so still and did not speak. They were with me now, and soon, soon I should be with them. Yet the silence had its own sound, a pressure upon the ear as if the world were holding its breath.

I dropped Adam’s old spade. The dull thud on the clay floor went heavily through the house, and it seemed to reverberate from the chamber beyond. A childish terror seized me. I sat and stared at the coffin-door.

But father Adam, mother Eve, sister Mara would soon come to me, and then—welcome the cold world and the white neighbours! I forgot my fears, lived a little, and loved my dead.

Something moved in the chamber of the dead.

From it came what was like a dim, far-off sound—and yet it was not sound as I had known sound. There was no scrape of foot, no hinge, no breath; but my soul sprang into my ears. I knew without hearing, without feeling it. It was a tremor that belonged to no air: a message without a language I could name, the way one recognizes one’s own name called in a crowd before the syllables are caught.

It was coming. It drew nearer.

In the bosom of my desertion, an infant hope woke.

The noiseless thrill reached the coffin-door—became sound—and smote my ear. The door began to move with a low, soft creaking of hinges, like an old cupboard opened for bread in the dark. It was opening. I ceased to listen and stared, expectant.

It opened a little way, and a face came into the opening.

It was Lona’s.

Her eyes were closed, but her face was turned upon me and seemed to see me. It was white as Eve’s, white as Mara’s, but it did not shine as theirs did, as if the light in her had been drawn inward like a flame banked for sleep.

She spoke, and her voice was like a sleepy night-wind in the grass, the kind that barely bends the stalks yet changes the whole field’s whisper.

“Are you coming, king?” it said. “I cannot rest until you are with me, gliding down the river to the great sea, and the beautiful dream-land. The sleepiness is full of lovely things: come and see them.”

“Ah, my darling!” I cried. “Had I but known! I thought you were dead!”

She leaned upon my bosom—cold as ice frozen to marble. The cold struck through my clothes as if her very stillness had weight. She threw her arms, so white, feebly about me, and sighed as one who has tasted something sweet and is too tired to speak of it.

“Carry me back to my bed, king. I want to sleep.”

I bore her into the death-chamber, holding her close lest she should dissolve out of my arms like mist when the sun finds it. Unaware that I saw, I carried her straight to her couch, as if my body remembered the place for her better than my mind.

“Lay me down,” she said, “and cover me from the warm air; it hurts—a little. Your bed is there, next to mine. I shall see you when I wake.”

Already she was asleep. Her words faded the way the last scent of a cut herb fades when the blade is set down.

I threw myself upon my couch—blessed as never was man on the eve of his wedding.

“Come, sweet cold,” I said, “and still my heart speedily.”

But instead there came a glimmer of light in the chamber, and I saw the face of Adam approaching. He had not the candle, yet I saw him as one sees a familiar hand moving in dusk by knowing it. At the side of Lona’s couch he looked down on her with a questioning smile, then greeted me across it.

“We have been to the top of the hill to hear the waters on their way,” he said. “They will be in the den of the monsters to-night. But why did you not await our return?”

“My child could not sleep,” I answered.

“She is fast asleep,” he rejoined.

“Yes—now,” I said; “but she was awake when I laid her down.”

“She was asleep all the time,” he insisted, gently, as one correcting a man who misnames a thing because his tongue belongs to another country.

“She was perhaps dreaming about you—and came to you?”

“She did.”

“And did you not see that her eyes were closed?”

“Now I think of it, I did.”

“If you had looked ere you laid her down, you would have seen her asleep on the couch.”

“That would have been terrible!”

“You would only have found that she was no longer in your arms.”

“That would have been worse!”

“It is, perhaps, to think of,” he said, “but to see it would not have troubled you.”

“Dear father,” I said, and my voice sounded small in that wide hush, “how is it that I am not sleepy? I thought I should go to sleep like the Little Ones the moment I laid my head down.”

“Your hour is not quite come,” he answered. “You must have food ere you sleep.”

Food. The word, in that house, was not an abstract mercy; it called up texture and warmth, the act of taking into oneself what another has prepared—an exchange older than speech. Yet even that thought made me ache with my helplessness.

“Ah,” I said, “I ought not to have lain down without your leave, for I cannot sleep without your help! I will get up at once!”

But when I tried, I found my own weight more than I could move, as if the cold had turned my limbs to packed earth.

“There is no need,” he said. “We will serve you here. You do not feel cold, do you?”

“Not too cold to lie still,” I answered, “but perhaps too cold to eat.”

He came to the side of my couch, bent over me, and breathed upon my heart. At once warmth ran through me—not the feverish heat of struggle, but the steady comfort of a kitchen where the oven has been tended all day. My fingers remembered what it was to belong to life.

As he left me, I heard a voice and knew it was the Mother’s. She was singing. Her song was sweet and soft and low at first, as if she sat by my bed in the dark and would not wake the sleepers; it moved like a ladle in a pot, circling without clatter. But ere it ceased, it soared aloft, and seemed to come from the throat of a woman-angel, high above the region of larks, higher than man had ever yet lifted up his heart. The sound did not bruise the air like the river’s roar; it healed it. It made the silence hospitable. I heard every word she sang, but could keep only this, as if the rest belonged to a tongue older than mine, impossible to carry back into waking:

“Many a wrong, and its curing song;
Many a road, and many an inn;
Room to roam, but only one home
For all the world to win!”

And I thought I had heard the song before, not with my ears only but with something ancestral in me, as if it had survived in blood when the words of it had been forgotten.

Then the three came to my couch together, bringing me bread and wine, and I sat up to partake of it. Adam stood on one side of me, Eve and Mara on the other. The bread was plain, such as a careful hand bakes when flour is precious: a crust that yielded with a quiet crack, a crumb close and honest, smelling of grain and patient fire. The wine was dark, its scent rising like bruised fruit and sun stored in a jar. In that place, where even death had a domestic order, the act of eating felt like consent.

“You are good indeed, father Adam, mother Eve, sister Mara,” I said, and the words tasted of salt in my mouth. “To receive me! In my soul I am ashamed and sorry!”

“We knew you would come again,” answered Eve.

“How could you know it?” I asked.

“Because here was I,” said Mara with a smile—quick as the glint of a knife set right—“born to look after my brothers and sisters.”

“Every creature must one night yield himself and lie down,” said Adam. “He was made for liberty, and must not be left a slave.”

“It will be late, I fear, ere all have lain down,” I said.

“There is no early or late here,” he returned. “For him the true time then first begins who lays himself down. Men are not coming home fast; women are coming faster. A desert, wide and dreary, parts him who lies down to die from him who lies down to live. The former may well make haste, but here is no haste.”

“To our eyes,” said Eve, “you were coming all the time. We knew Mara would find you, and you must come.”

The bread strengthened me with a quiet insistence, as if each mouthful set a stone in place in a wall that had been falling. The wine made a small warmth behind my ribs, a reminder that even in cold there can be sweetness.

“How long is it since my father lay down?” I asked.

“I have told you that years are of no consequence in this house,” answered Adam. “We do not heed them. Your father will wake when his morning comes. Your mother, next to whom you are lying—”

“Ah, then it is my mother!” I cried.

“Yes,” he assented, “she with the wounded hand. She will be up and away long ere your morning is ripe.”

“I am sorry.”

“Rather be glad.”

“It must be a sight for God Himself to see such a woman come awake!”

“It is indeed a sight for God,” said Adam, “a sight that makes her Maker glad. He sees of the travail of His soul, and is satisfied. Look at her once more, and sleep.”

He let the rays of his candle fall upon her beautiful face. In the light she seemed not merely still, but gathered—as if all her life had been folded neatly within her, like linen pressed and laid by.

“She looks much younger,” I said.

“She is much younger,” he replied. “Even Lilith already begins to look younger.”

A drowsiness, blissful and heavy, began to settle upon me. It did not come like a blow, but like a clean cloth drawn gently over a loaf to let it rest.

“But when you see your mother again,” he continued, “you will not at first know her. She will go on steadily growing younger until she reaches the perfection of her womanhood—a splendour beyond foresight. Then she will open her eyes, behold on one side her husband, on the other her son—and rise and leave them to go to a father and a brother more to her than they.”

His words reached me as one in a dream, each phrase distinct, and yet belonging to a language I could not quite bring into my own. I was very cold, but already the cold caused no suffering. It was as if I had learned a new meaning for it, one my old vocabulary could not carry.

I felt them put upon me the white garment of the dead, the cloth cool and clean, like fresh-washed sheets shaken out in night air. Hands moved about me with the quiet competence of those who have set many tables and made many beds, who know that the body has its rites as surely as the soul.

Then I forgot everything.

The night about me was pale with sleeping faces, row on row, like a field under a thin moon; but I was asleep also, and did not know that I slept.
