The next morning, Archer stepped off the Fall River train and stepped straight into the thick, steaming breath of a mid-summer Boston. Streets near the station reeked of spilled beer, burnt coffee, and overripe fruit rotting in vendor crates, and crowds of men in rolled-up shirt sleeves wandered past with the loose, unselfconscious ease of boarders trundling down a boarding house hall to the shared bathroom. No European city could ever look this unpolished, this soaked through with unruly domesticity, no matter how high the heat rose: maids in calico gowns lolled on the front steps of wealthy brownstones, and the Common still bore the litter and sticky sweet traces of a Masonic picnic that had wrapped the night before.

If he had ever tried to imagine Ellen Olenska in an impossible, out-of-place scene, he could never have dreamed up a setting more ill-suited to her quiet, otherworldly grace than this heat-drunk, unkempt, half-abandoned city. He hailed a cab and drove straight to the Somerset Club for breakfast, eating with the sharp, unhurried appetite of a man who has spent weeks half-starved, working his way through a slice of cold melon first, then flipping idly through the morning paper while he waited for toast and scrambled eggs. A bright, thrumming new energy had hummed under his skin ever since he’d told May the night before that urgent last-minute business required him to catch the Fall River boat that night, and head back to New York two days early. The pretense had been easy, almost alarmingly so: the forged urgent office note he’d left conspicuously on the hall table before he left Portsmouth had justified every part of his changed plan, and for one fleeting, uncomfortable moment he’d thought he was pulling the same kind of slippery, clever trick Lawrence Lefferts used to sneak off on his little assignations. But the guilt slid right off him; he was in no mood for overthinking.

After breakfast he lit a cigarette and skimmed the *Commercial Advertiser*, and nodded and exchanged small talk with two or three acquaintances who wandered in. It was the same old world, he thought, but he had the giddy, disorienting sense that he had slipped through a crack in time and space, that he was moving through it half outside the rules he had followed his whole life. He checked his watch, saw it was half past nine, and stood up to head for the writing room. There he scribbled a short note, paid a messenger to take a cab straight to the Parker House, and wait for a reply. He settled back behind another newspaper, counting out every minute it would take a cab to drive across the city.

“The lady was out, sir,” a waiter’s voice murmured suddenly at his elbow.

Archer stammered, “Out?” as if the word was in a foreign tongue he could not parse. He stumbled to the hall. That could not be. She could not be out that early. Hot, sharp anger pricked at him at his own stupidity: why had he not sent that note the second he stepped off the train? He grabbed his hat and stick and stepped back out onto the street, and the whole city suddenly felt vast, empty, as unrecognizable as a foreign land he had never visited before. He hesitated on the doorstep for a long second, then set off for the Parker House anyway. What if the messenger had gotten bad information? What if she was still there?

He cut across the Common, and there on the first bench under an oak tree, he saw her. She held a grey silk sunshade tilted over her head—he had somehow always stupidly imagined her with a pink one, he realized, shocked at his own foolishness. She sat listless, like she had nowhere in the world to go, her profile soft and drooping, her hair knotted low at the nape of her neck under her dark hat, her long, slightly wrinkled gloved hand curled loosely around the parasol handle. She turned, and saw him.

“Oh,” she said, and for the first time he saw a flash of pure startled surprise on her face, which softened a second later into a slow, wondering, contented smile. “Oh,” she murmured again, the note softer this time, and without standing up she shifted to make space for him on the bench.

“I’m here on business—just got here,” Archer babbled, and for no reason he could name he faked his own shock at seeing her. “But what on earth are you doing in this wilderness?” He could barely hear his own voice over the thud of his heart; it felt like he was shouting across miles of empty space, terrified she would vanish before he could reach her.

“I? Oh, I’m here on business too,” she answered, turning her head so they were face to face. Her voice barely registered, except as a shock: he had forgotten entirely how it sounded, low and warm, with a faint, rough softness catching on every consonant.

“You do your hair differently,” he blurted, his heart hammering so hard he thought it might burst through his ribs, as if he had said something irrevocable and foolish.

“Differently? No—only that I do the best I can with no Nastasia around to help.”

“Nastasia? But she’s not with you?”

“No. I’m alone. Two days wasn’t long enough to drag her all the way out here.”

“You’re alone—at the Parker House?”

She flashed him that old familiar glint of playful malice. “Does that strike you as dangerous?”

“No, not dangerous—”

“But unconventional? I see. I suppose it is.” She paused for a beat, the faint edge of irony lingering in her eyes. “I hadn’t thought of it, actually. Because I just did something far more unconventional. I refused to take back a very large sum of money that belonged to me.”

Archer sprang to his feet and paced a few steps away. She furled her parasol, sat absently drawing little patterns in the gravel at her feet, and he walked back to stand in front of her a minute later.

“Someone came here to meet you? With that offer?”

She nodded.

“And you refused—because of the conditions?”

“I refused,” she said, after a quiet pause.

He sank back down beside her. “What were the conditions?”

“Oh, they were not harsh. Just that I would come back, and sit at the head of his dinner table now and then.”

Silence settled between them. Archer’s chest felt like it had slammed shut, all the words he wanted to say tangled up somewhere he could not reach them. “He wants you back—at any price?”

“Well. A very high price, at least. Far more money than I have ever known what to do with.” He hesitated, beating around the question he knew he had to ask. “You came here to meet him, didn’t you?”

She stared, then burst out laughing. “Meet him? My husband? Here? At this time of year he’s always at Cowes or Baden.”

“He sent someone else?”

“Yes.”

“With a letter?”

She shook her head. “No, just a message. He never writes. I don’t think I’ve gotten more than one letter from him in all our years of marriage.” A faint flush rose to her cheeks, and the heat of her embarrassment burned in Archer’s own face, bright and hot.

“Why does he never write?”

“Why should he? That’s what secretaries are for.”

Archer’s blush deepened. She said the word so simply, as if it had no hidden, shameful weight at all. For a second he almost asked if he had sent his secretary, but the memory of the single brutal letter Count Olenski had ever sent his wife rose sharp in his mind, and he bit the question back. He took a breath, and plunged on. “And this person—this emissary?”

“The emissary,” Madame Olenska smiled, “could leave any time for all I care. But he insisted on waiting till this evening… just in case… on the chance I might change my mind.”

“And you came out here to think that chance over?”

“I came out for a breath of air. The hotel is far too stifling. I’m taking the afternoon train back to Portsmouth.”

They sat silent for a long while, not looking at each other, but straight ahead at the people strolling past on the path. Finally she turned her eyes back to his face. “You haven’t changed at all.”

He nearly answered *I was, till I saw you again*, but instead he stood abruptly, glancing around the sweltering, untidy park. “This is horrible. Why shouldn’t we go out on the bay for a little while? There’s a breeze, it will be cooler out on the water. We could take the steamboat down to Point Arley.” She glanced up at him, hesitating, and he pushed on. “No one will be on that boat on a Monday morning. My train back to New York doesn’t leave till this evening. Why shouldn’t we go?” He looked down at her, and suddenly the words broke out unbidden. “Haven’t we done absolutely everything we possibly can to stay apart, already?”

“Oh,” she murmured. She stood up, unfurled her sunshade, glancing around at the park like she was asking permission of the trees to leave, to step into something they had both been craving for months. Then her eyes returned to his face. “You mustn’t say things like that to me.”

“I’ll say anything you want. Or nothing at all. I won’t open my mouth unless you tell me to. What harm could this possibly do to anyone? All I want is to listen to you, to hear what you’ve been doing all these months.”

She pulled out a tiny gold-faced watch on an enamel chain.

“Don’t calculate the time,” he burst out. “Give me the day. I want to get you away from that man waiting for you at the hotel. What time was he supposed to arrive?”

Her cheeks flushed again. “Eleven.”

“Then we have to go right now.”

“You don’t have to be afraid—if I don’t come back with you.”

“Nor you, if you do. I swear I only want to hear about you. It feels like a hundred years since we last spoke. It might be another hundred before we ever get this chance again.”

She still wavered, her anxious eyes fixed on his face. “Why didn’t you come down to the beach to fetch me, the day I was at Granny’s house?”

“Because you never looked around. Because I swore I wouldn’t bother you unless you looked for me.” He laughed, struck by how childish the confession sounded.

“But I didn’t look around on purpose.”

“On purpose?”

“I knew you were there. I recognized the ponies pulling your carriage the second you drove up. So I went down to the beach.”

“To get as far away from me as you possibly could?”

She repeated the words, soft and quiet. “To get as far away from you as I possibly could.”

Archer laughed, bright and boyish and giddy with satisfaction. “Well, you see it didn’t work. I might as well tell you now—all the business I came here for was just to find you. Now hurry, we have to go or we’ll miss our boat.”

“Our boat?” She frowned, confused for half a second, then smiled. “Oh, but I have to go back to the hotel first. I have to leave a note for him.”

“Write as many notes as you want. You can write them right here.” He pulled out his notecase and one of the new stylographic pens he had bought just a week before. “I even have an envelope. You see? Everything is predestined. There, rest the paper on your knee, I’ll get this pen working for you in a second. They’re fussy, you have to jostle the ink flow—wait—” He banged his hand holding the pen against the back of the bench, hard, “It’s like shaking down the mercury in a thermometer. Just a little trick. Now try.”

She laughed, bending over the sheet of paper he’d laid on his notecase, scribbling the note. Archer walked a few paces away, staring through unfocused, glowing eyes at the passersby, who paused to gawk at the unheard-of sight of a fashionable lady writing a note on her knee, on a public park bench. Madame Olenska slipped the sheet into the envelope, wrote a name on the front, and tucked it into her pocket. Then she stood up, too.

They walked back toward Beacon Street, and right outside the Somerset Club Archer spotted the same plush-lined herdic cab that had carried his note to the Parker House, its driver taking a break to splash cold water on his forehead at the corner hydrant. “I told you it was all predestined! Here’s our cab right now!” They both laughed, stunned at the miracle of hailing a public cab at ten o’clock in the morning in a city where cab stands were still considered a weird, foreign novelty. Archer checked his watch: they had plenty of time to stop by the Parker House before heading for the steamboat wharf. They rattled over hot, rutted cobblestones and pulled up in front of the hotel doors.

Archer held out his hand for her letter. “Shall I take it in for you?” But she shook her head, hopped out, and vanished through the glass doors. It was barely half past ten, but Archer’s chest twisted with anxiety—what if the emissary was already there, impatient, waiting, and had cornered her before she could leave? He paced up and down the sidewalk in front of the cab. A Sicilian boy with eyes exactly like Nastasia’s tried to shine his boots, an Irish matron tried to sell him peaches, and every few seconds the swinging hotel doors opened to let out another hot man in a tipped-back straw hat, all looking identical to every other hot man in the whole country, he thought.

Then suddenly a face emerged that did not look like all the others. He only caught a flash of it, as he turned at the end of his pacing path: among all the lank, weary, round-faced, mild hotel guests, this young man was pale, sharp, alive in a way no one else around him was, unmistakably foreign. Archer reached for a thread of memory, but it snapped, and the stranger vanished into the crowd. He figured that was the emissary. Panic clawed at his throat. If she did not come out in the next minute, he was going in after her.

The doors swung open again, and she was at his side. They climbed back into the herdic, and as it rattled off toward the wharf he pulled out his watch: she had been gone exactly three minutes. The cab’s loose windows clattered too loud for talking, and they bumped over disjointed cobblestones all the way to the dock.

Sitting side by side on a bench on the half-empty steamboat, they found they barely needed to speak at all. All the things they had to say hummed unspoken between them, in the blessed, quiet relief of finally being released from the world that had kept them apart, finally entirely alone. As the paddle wheels began to turn, and the wharves and warehouses receded through the haze of summer heat, it felt to Archer like all the old familiar world of habit, of obligation, of the rules of New York society that had hemmed them both in for so long, was receding with it. He wanted to ask Ellen if she felt it too: that they were sailing off on some long voyage from which they might never return. But he was afraid to speak it, afraid to shatter the delicate, fragile trust she had in him. For months the memory of their single kiss had burned on his lips, but now that she was here beside him, drifting out into this unknown new world, they had reached a deeper, softer kind of nearness that the wrong word, the wrong touch, could shatter entirely.

The boat cleared the harbour and turned toward open water, and a fresh cool breeze curled around them, the bay stretching out ahead in long oily swells, then ripples tipped with fine spray. The sultry fog still hung low over Boston behind them, but ahead lay a whole new world of ruffled blue water, and distant green headlands with lighthouses glinting in the sun. Madame Olenska leaned back against the boat rail, drinking in the cool air with her lips parted. She had wrapped a long sheer veil around her hat, leaving her face uncovered, and Archer could see the soft, tranquil, unguarded gaiety on her face. She took their little adventure completely in stride: no fear of unexpected meetings, no foolish giddy elation that would make this feel like a sordid assignation.

When they reached the inn, Archer had hoped they would have the bare little dining room entirely to themselves, but they found a loud, cheerful group of schoolteachers on holiday, and his heart sank at the thought of having to shout their conversation over all their noise. “This is hopeless. I’ll ask for a private room,” he said, and Madame Olenska made no protest, waiting while he spoke to the landlord. The small room opened onto a long wooden verandah, the sea rolling in right beneath the windows. It was bare and cool, a table covered in coarse checkered cloth holding a jar of pickles and a blueberry pie tucked under a wire food cage. No more innocent little private room had ever sheltered a pair of people who the whole world thought had no business being alone together. Archer saw the quiet, amused reassurance in the smile she gave him as she sat down across the table from him. This woman who had run away from her cruel husband, who society had gossiped about relentlessly, had mastered the art of taking things in stride—but the quiet, simple, unapologetic calm of her composure brushed away all the stuffy old conventions, and made it feel like the most natural thing in the whole world for two old friends who had spent months aching to speak, finally to be entirely alone together, with no one watching.
