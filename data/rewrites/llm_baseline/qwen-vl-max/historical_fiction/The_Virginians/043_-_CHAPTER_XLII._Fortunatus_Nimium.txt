Though Harry Warrington clung stubbornly to the promise he had made to his cousin, we trust no kind-hearted reader will imagine that this obligation was one he cherished—or that he did not long for a way to be free of it. Indeed, the beating he gave poor Will Esmond may well have been calculated precisely for such an end. Perhaps Harry reasoned thus: “A fight between us will surely provoke a quarrel with the family; in that quarrel, Maria might side with her brother. I shall, of course, refuse any apology or retraction. Then Will may challenge me—whereupon I shall know which of us is the stronger man. Amidst the uproar, perhaps the engagement will fall apart, and I shall regain my liberty.” So honest Harry laid his plan and set it in motion—but when the dust settled, there was no real harm done, save that William Esmond’s nose swelled and his eye turned black for a week.

Will did not send a challenge to Harry Warrington. He did not kill him, nor was he killed by him. He was knocked down—and rose again. How many sensible men would do the same if they could settle their score quietly, without witnesses to recount every blow?

Maria did not take her family’s side in the dispute. On the contrary, she declared openly for her cousin. Lord Castlewood, too, when informed of the altercation, sided with Harry. According to the chaplain’s account, Will had struck first. It was not the first—nor even the tenth—time he had been found quarreling under the influence of drink. Mr. Warrington, said the lord, had acted with proper spirit in defending himself. The fault lay with Will, not Harry. And it was Will who ought to ask forgiveness.

Harry, however, insisted he would accept no apology unless his horse was returned—or his bet paid. The chronicler has found no record in any surviving papers how this matter was ultimately resolved. But it is certain that soon afterward, the cousins met again at various social gatherings—without raising fists or drawing swords.

At first, Maria’s elder brother had been quite content with the idea of her marriage. After so many years of single life, she had accumulated more than her share of thorns, stains, and weariness. A husband from Virginia seemed a fitting conclusion to her journey. She could retire to his wigwam in the wilderness, and there find peace. Naturally, Harry would outlive her—after which he could choose whether to mourn or rejoice as he pleased.

But after a visit with Aunt Bernstein upon his arrival in London, Lord Castlewood changed his mind. He went so far as to try to dissuade Maria from the match—and even expressed pity for the young man who, through a foolish vow made at twenty-one, was doomed to a lifetime of misery.

“Misery?” Maria had asked, astonished. “Why should he suffer?”

“Pity!” echoed her brother. “I say, it’s no pity at all.”

She understood now. Her brother had spoken with Aunt Bernstein. That lady had offered money to break off the engagement. Maria saw clearly what her brother meant—but she trusted Mr. Warrington. He was a man of honour, and she believed him capable of keeping his word.

So Lord Castlewood walked away—to White’s Club, or wherever else he spent his time. It was likely that Maria had already guessed the nature of his conversation with Madame Bernstein. “And so,” he thought, “the end of my virtue is to see this fine young fellow devoured by others. There’s no point in sparing him. *Quem deus vult*—what was that old schoolmaster’s saying? If I don’t claim him, someone else will. My brother has had a taste; my dear sister wants to swallow him whole. Here I’ve been at home, pretending to protect his youth and innocence, refusing to play beyond a sixpence, acting as guardian and mentor. Why am I such a fool, fattening a goose only for others to eat? I’ve done few good deeds in my life—and this one benefits whom? Not me. Others. Talk of remorse! By all the fires and furies, the remorse I feel is for things I didn’t do—things I might have done. Why did I spare Lucretia? She hated me ever after, and her husband met his fate. Why have I let this boy go? Now March and the rest, who don’t want him, can pick him clean! And I have a bad reputation. People point at me and call me the wicked lord. Women warn their sons against me. Pardi! I’m not worse than anyone else—only far unluckier. And it’s my cursed weakness that has been my greatest enemy.”

Clearly, in recording thoughts never spoken aloud, the chronicler risks overstepping the bounds of belief. Yet must we not recall that Livy, Thucydides, and countless historians have invented speeches for their heroes—speeches we know were never uttered? If we know Lord Castlewood’s character as intimately as we do, may we not dare to transcribe what passed within his mind?

Look: a pack of wolves are hunting this lamb, and soon they’ll tear him apart. And one hungry old hunter stands aside, refusing even a single bite? Who has not admired Lord Clive’s noble words when reproached for taking too freely from India’s wealth? “Upon my life,” he said, “when I think of my opportunities, I’m surprised I took so little!”

To reveal unpleasant truths about a gentleman is always painful—especially when one hesitates to speak them until forced. Thus, though I knew from the very beginning of this history what sort of man Lord Castlewood was, and how poorly he was regarded by his contemporaries, I have held back much that was unflattering. Only gradually have I allowed the reader to see that he was not a man we should admire.

It is true that Lord March and others like him would have seen nothing wrong in betting Harry Warrington’s last shilling—and taking it. They’d use every trick, every advantage, every inside knowledge. In that, they followed the customs of their class. But when they played, they played fair. When they lost, they paid.

Madame Bernstein, however, was reluctant to tell her Virginian nephew everything she knew about his family’s failings. She was touched by Lord Castlewood’s restraint toward Harry on his arrival in Europe—and pleased that he had respected her wishes in this matter. But during their conversation about Maria’s intentions, Castlewood had spoken bluntly, as usual. He called himself a fool for having spared a young man whose ruin was inevitable. He pointed out Harry’s extravagances, his reckless friends, his nights at faro and hazard, his trips to Newmarket. “Why should I alone keep my hands off him?” he demanded.

Madame Bernstein pleaded that Harry was poor.

“Bah!” Castlewood snapped. “He’s heir to a principality that should have been mine. Our family could have been restored. (Indeed, Madame Bernstein thought Harry’s land in Virginia worth far more than it actually was.) Are there not money-lenders in town who’d lend him thousands on postobits? I know. I’ve tried them myself—during my father’s lifetime. Those cursed men swallowed two-thirds of my income.”

He spoke with such bitter honesty that Madame Bernstein began to fear for her favourite nephew. She decided to warn him at once.

That evening, she began writing a letter—but throughout her life, she disliked writing. “I never knew any good come of writing anything beyond *bon jour* or business,” she used to say. “What’s the use of writing ill when so many clever people can do it well? Even then, it’s better left unsaid.”

So instead, she sent one of her servants to Harry’s lodgings, bidding him come for tea the next day—when she intended to give him her warning.

But the next morning, she was unwell and unable to receive him. She remained in bed for several days. Then came a busy engagement. Then Harry left on a journey of his own.

In the whirl of London life, how often does one see a neighbour? A brother his sister? An old friend?

Days passed before Harry and his aunt finally met. She began gently, scolding him for his extravagance and wild ways—though in truth, she found them charming. He replied that young men must be young men, and that it was only while waiting dutifully on her that he had met the companionship he now valued most.

Then, after some preamble, she warned him about Lord Castlewood.

He burst into a bitter laugh. “The world has already told me enough about Lord Castlewood.”

“To say of a man of his rank, ‘Don’t play with him,’ is more than I care to do,” the lady continued. “But…”

“Oh, go on, aunt!” Harry said, almost with an oath on his lips.

“And have you played with your cousin already?” she asked.

“Lost and won, madam,” he answered gallantly. “I won’t say which. If we have a game in Virginia—a bottle, cards, or a quarrel—we don’t run home to tell our mothers. I mean no offence, aunt!”

Blushing, the handsome young man leaned forward and kissed her hand.

He looked splendid—rich lace, fair hair, a new velvet suit embroidered with gold. As he left, he handed his usual generous gift to her servants, who gathered around him.

It was a cold, rainy winter day. To preserve his silk stockings, he must travel in a chair.

“To White’s!” he called to the chairmen—and away they carried him to the place where he spent much of his time.

His friends might have wished he visited White’s less often. But this much can be said for Mr. Warrington: once he entered the game, he fought like a hero. He was calm in victory, undisturbed by loss. Fortune, they say, is fickle—but how many men are fickle to Fortune? How many flee from her advances, deserting her just when she might have stayed, had they not shown cowardice?

“By George, Mr. Warrington,” said Mr. Selwyn, roused by sudden enthusiasm, “you deserve to win! You treat luck as a gentleman should. While she stays with you, you behave with perfect politeness. *Si celeres quatit pennas*—you know the rest? Well, you’re not much worse off—you’ll call her coach and bow at the step. Look at Lord Castlewood over there, passing the box. Have you ever heard a man curse so fiercely over losing five or six pieces? She must be a jade indeed to favour such a niggardly rogue!”

“We don’t consider our family rogues, sir,” said Harry. “Lord Castlewood is one of them.”

“I forgot. I beg your pardon! And I offer my compliments to both him and Mr. Will Esmond.”

“The box is with me. Five’s the main! Deuce Ace! My usual luck. *Virtute mea me involvo!*” he exclaimed, sinking back into his chair.

Whether this was the occasion when Harry threw the fifteen mains mentioned in those other letters of Mr. Walpole—now lost to the editor—I cannot say. But certainly, on his first appearance at White’s, Harry enjoyed several evenings of extraordinary luck. He seemed more than ever the Fortunate Youth.

Five hundred pounds drawn from his inheritance multiplied into thousands. He bought fine clothes, horses, hosted grand dinners, gave lavish gifts, lived as if he were as wealthy as Sir James Lowther or the Duke of Bedford—yet his fortune never seemed to dwindle.

No wonder he gave freely—when giving was so easy. No wonder he was generous with Fortunatus’s purse. For such was his nature.

Other Fortunati lock up their endless purses, drink small beer, and go to bed by tallow candle. But Harry lived large.

During this golden streak, he naturally inquired about Lady Maria’s debts—and paid them all, to the last shilling. Her stepmother and half-sister, who disliked her, received magnificent presents.

“Had you not better get yourself arrested, Will?” Lord Castlewood said sardonically to his brother. “You bit him in that horse affair—the Mohock will surely pull you out of pawn.”

At that, William felt a pang of regret—not the humble kind that brought the Prodigal Son to his knees.

“Confound it,” he groaned, “to think I let this fellow slip away for forty pounds! He was worth a thousand at least.”

As for Maria, that generous soul accepted her good fortune with gratitude—and was ready to accept more. Having cleared her debts to milliners, tradesmen, and purveyors, she immediately began contracting new ones.

Mrs. Betty, her maid, went round telling the shops that her mistress was marrying a young gentleman of immense wealth—so they might extend credit freely.

Though the tradespeople had heard similar stories before, they were paid anyway—even Mrs. Pincott of Kensington, who received fresh orders without resentment.

When Maria drove from mercer to toy-shop, from toy-shop to jeweller, in a coach with her maid and Harry beside her, they thought her truly fortunate—to have captured the Fortunate Youth—even if they wondered at his choice of such an elderly beauty.

Mr. Sparks of Tavistock Street, Covent Garden, ventured to call on Harry at his Bond Street lodgings, bearing the pearl necklace and gold etwee he had purchased the day before with Lady Maria. Should he leave them at Harry’s lodging—or send them to her ladyship with his compliments?

Harry added a ring from the stock Sparks happened to carry, and bade him send the bill sumptuously. Then he took a majestic leave, and Sparks bowed low—even to Gumbo, the servant, as he departed.

His generosity did not stop there. Soon after, Harry drove up in his phaeton to Sparks’ shop and selected two trinkets—for two young ladies whose parents had been kind to him, and whom he deeply admired.

“Ah,” he thought, “how I wish I had George’s wit and talent for poetry! I’d send these gifts with sweet verses to Hetty and Theo. I’m sure, if goodwill and affection could make me a poet, I’d find rhymes easily.”

So he summoned Parson Sampson—and together they composed a note.
