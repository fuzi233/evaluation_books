“As is the race of leaves so is that of men.”

A great man, like a slow-risen loaf, was swelling unremarked this year in a tailor’s narrow house at Rotterdam; and a large man, loudly trumpeted, was dropping to earth at Bruges.

Philip, Duke of Burgundy, Earl of Holland, and a pageant of other titles besides, lay sick there; and in that age sickness was a question not of Nature’s sober kitchen, but of what meddling hands chose to do to her broth. A poor man fell ill and, if his constitution were stout, might be up again as quietly as a hen returns to her scratching after a shower. But woe to the rich: for where one Mr. Malady carried off his victim, three more were hustled out of life by Dr. Remedy.

The Duke’s ailment had no name then—names are a kind of memory, and memory comes late to pain. We call it now diphtheria: a smothering, weakening thing, like a cloth laid over the mouth of breath. The Duke was old besides; so, to “strengthen” him, the learned remedy was to drain him. They opened him as you would tap a cask, and let out his warmth by the bowlful. Presently he turned cold—marvellous!—as if men had never noticed that you cannot ladle away a man’s life and still expect the pot to simmer.

Then science, impatient as a cook who has burnt the first dish, cried for a stranger experiment.

“Ho! this is grave. Flay me an ape incontinent, and clap him to the Duke’s breast!”

Straightway officers of state scurried, their feet clattering on Bruges stone—an ill-tempered percussion that set dogs barking and made the very rafters of the palace seem to listen. They ran seven ways at once, hunting an ape as if salvation lay in fur and heat. But perdition! the Duke was out of apes. There were buffaloes, lizards, Turks, leopards—any unreasonable beast that men cage and name for sport—anything except the right creature for this bloodthirsty piece of folly.

“Why, there used to be an ape about,” said one. “If I stand here I saw him.”

So there had; but the mastiff had mangled the sprightly thief for stealing his supper. The crunch of bone, the yelp, the sudden silence—those are sounds every courtyard remembers. Thus the dog had obeyed, with honest teeth, the human maxim, “Soyez de votre siecle!”—be of your age; eat what you can, and call it wisdom.

In this need the seneschal cast his desperate eyes around the antechamber, where silk whispered, chain-mail chimed, and every soft shoe tiptoed as if quiet itself could hold back death. This time his gaze did not return empty. A hopeful light leapt into it.

“Here is this,” said he, sotto voce. “Surely this will serve: ’tis altogether apelike, doublet and hose apart.”

“Nay,” snapped the chancellor, sour with fear. “The Princess Marie would hang us. She doteth on this.”

And “this” was Giles—our friend Giles—strutting there, all unconscious, in cloth of gold, making the very air smell of new-dyed wool and rich people’s perfumes, while men weighed him like meat. For in a court, even affection has its price, and language itself becomes a knife: one man says “this,” and another man hears “thing.”

Dr. Remedy, thwarted and growing peevish, flung away the ape and reached for the next rung down the ladder of creatures.

“Then flay a dog. A dog is next best to an ape; only it must be a dog all of one colour.”

So they fetched a liver-coloured dog—poor, whole-coloured wretch that had perhaps known the honest music of fields and kitchens, the scrape of a trencher, the fall of scraps, the familiar calls in some homely dialect of whistle and name. They flayed it; and while its heart still battered its last rhythm against the world, they clapped the palpitating hide upon their sovereign’s breast, as if warmth were a garment you could borrow from the powerless and dress upon the mighty.

He died.

Thus Philip the Good was “scientifically” disposed of; and left behind him thirty-one children, of whom one—somehow or another—was legitimate, and reigned in his stead. The good duke had provided for nineteen out of the other thirty; the rest must shift, like unclaimed loaves in a market, to whatever hands would take them.

According to a Flemish chronicle, the deceased prince was descended from the kings of Troy through Thierry of Aquitaine and Chilperic, Pharamond, and the old kings of Franconia. But in truth this lineage was no distinction: in that day, I have not been able to discover a prince who did not come down from Troy. “Priam” was but the mediaeval way of saying “Adam”—a grand old word that made every lord feel native to every legend, and so helped them forget the plain, local tongue of truth.

The Duke’s body was carried into Burgundy and laid in a noble mausoleum of black marble at Dijon. Holland rang with his death—bells tolling, hoofbeats drumming, sermons swelling in the vaulted echoes—sound after sound thrown upon the air as if noise could prove importance. Yet Holland little dreamed that anything more famous was being born within her territory that year, not with tolling and proclamation, but with the small domestic hush of a working house: the snip of shears, the prick of needle, the soft fall of cloth, a woman’s breath counted between pains.

That judgment has long since been reversed. Men gaze now at the tailor’s house: here, they say, the great birth of the fifteenth century took place. In what house the good duke died “no one knows and no one cares,” as the song says.

And why?

Because dukes like Philip the Good come and go, and leave mankind not a halfpenny wiser, nor better, nor other than they found it. But when, once in three hundred years, such a child is born to the world as Margaret’s son—lo, a human torch, lighted by fire from heaven; and “FIAT LUX” thunders from pole to pole, while the cradle rocks to its own quiet rhythm, and the common sounds of home—spoon against bowl, hearth crackle, a mother’s low speech in her own dear dialect—keep time with a light the powerful never knew how to name.
