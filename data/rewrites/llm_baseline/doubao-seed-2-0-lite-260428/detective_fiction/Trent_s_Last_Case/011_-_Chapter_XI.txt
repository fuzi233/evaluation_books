This note is for if I miss you at the office. I’ve cracked who killed Manderson, and the dispatch attached lays out the full proof. It names a man no one on the force, no reporter, no member of the household even suspects, and the case against him is damning. I don’t need to tell you you can’t run this before his arrest, and it’s illegal to publish a full accusation before a verdict is handed down — but once that comes down, you’ve got the exclusive of a lifetime. Do what you can with the snippets to move papers between now and then, that’s your call. In the meantime, get this whole document over to Scotland Yard immediately. As for me? I’m done with the Manderson mystery. God help me, I wish I’d never laid eyes on it.

Yours,

Marlstone, June 16th

This is my third, and almost certainly my final, dispatch to the *Record* on the Manderson murder, and I write it with two warring feelings in my chest. The first is immense relief: for my first two dispatches, I hid critical facts that would have tipped off a man of unusual nerve and cleverness, and let him slip the noose before we even got close. Today, I can lay all those cards on the table. The second feeling? Nothing but distaste. This is a story of cold treachery, of twisted cleverness that turned every small, ordinary quirk of a quiet household into a tool for murder. It leaves a foul taste in the mouth, and I believe I’ve untangled the last dark thread of motive beneath the crime itself.

You will recall from my first dispatch that I arrived at the Marlstone house at dawn on the Tuesday after the murder, found the body laid out by the garden shed, described the baffling lack of any obvious culprit, walked through Manderson’s quiet, estranged household, and laid out his movements the night he died. I also mentioned a small, seemingly trivial detail: more than twice the amount of whisky Manderson ever drank after dinner was missing from his private decanter by the time he was found. At the inquest the next day, I only wired over a transcript of the coroner’s proceedings, which our stenographers captured in full at my request. Now, before this same day is out, I have finished the investigation that leads straight to the man who will now have to prove he is innocent of Manderson’s death.

Before I got to the key clue, two tiny, jarring contradictions niggled at me — points that must have stuck in the mind of every reader who followed the case. First: the body was found less than thirty yards from the back door. Manderson was not gagged. There were clear struggle marks on his wrists. A shot had been fired, at the very least one, more likely two if the killer fumbled his aim mid-fight. Yet not a single soul in the house heard a scream, a scuffle, or a gunshot. Worse, the butler Martin was a notoriously light sleeper, his bedroom window propped open every night, his line of sight and hearing pointed directly at that very shed. It should have been impossible for him to miss it.

The second oddity: Manderson’s upper dental plate, the one he wore every single waking hour, the one that held all his visible top teeth, sat forgotten in a bowl by his bedside. He’d dressed fully, necktie knotted, watch and chain fastened, and left the house without it. Anyone who wears a denture like that knows putting it in is second nature the second you wake up. He’d never leave the house without it, not even in a panic. He couldn’t speak clearly, couldn’t eat, would look like a stranger to everyone who knew him. That, too, made no sense.

Neither of those two details gave up an answer right away. They just hung there, like shadows in the corner of every room I walked through, hinting at some secret the killer had gone to extraordinary lengths to hide.

That secret cracked open in Manderson’s dressing room, on the long upper shelf where he kept his hundreds of pairs of custom shoes. I was glancing over them, not looking for clues — I’ve always been a judge of fine leather, and every single pair there was masterfully made — when my eye caught his evening patent lace-ups, the pair he’d worn to dinner the night before. They were well-worn, polished to a shine, fitted to their shoe trees, but two tiny splits gaped on the vamp of both shoes, right where the laces meet the toe. The reinforcing stitching there had snapped, and the leather tore no more than an eighth of an inch — a mark so small no one but a man who handles fine shoes would ever notice it. Even harder to spot, a faint strain in the stitching that joins the upper to the sole, tugged loose along the toe and outer edge of both shoes.

That could only mean one thing. Someone had worn those shoes who was at least a full size bigger in the foot than Manderson.

A quick check of every other pair in the house confirmed it. Manderson, vain of his small, narrow feet, would never squeeze himself into a shoe too tight, never risk those splits. Someone else had worn them, and recently: the torn edges of the leather were still fresh. No one had slipped them on after the body was found, the whole household had been in a panic for 26 hours straight, no one had time to borrow a dead man’s evening shoes for fun. That left one, unthinkable possibility.

I still can’t explain the exact jump of logic that followed, the way every scattered little oddity I’d noted that day crashed together in my head all at once. It was not Manderson’s heavy bedtime whisky. It was not Manderson, the immaculate, meticulous millionaire, who’d been found dead with his cuffs dragged up inside his sleeves, his shoes unevenly laced, wearing last night’s unwashed shirt, who’d forgotten to wash his face before leaving the house, who’d stuffed his watch in the waistcoat pocket not lined for it. It was not Manderson, who never spoke more than two words to his wife after dinner, who had roused her half-asleep to chat about his evening plans. It was not Manderson who left the house without his denture.

For one wild second, the thought hit me like a punch: the man who everyone swore had come home at 10 o’clock, who’d talked to Mrs. Manderson, who’d sat at the library telephone, who’d “gone to bed” in Manderson’s room — that man was not Manderson.

It sounded like utter nonsense. People had seen Manderson leave the house after dinner, climb into the car with Marlowe. But who came back?

I didn’t take long to puzzle out the imposter’s game. He didn’t just not want to leave his own footprints. He wanted to leave Manderson’s. He wanted every scrap of evidence to scream that John Manderson was alive, well, and sleeping in that bedroom until the early hours of the morning. That explained the denture, too. The imposter had brought it with him, taken from the dead man’s mouth, to drop by the bedside. That meant the real Manderson was already dead before the fake Manderson ever stepped foot in the house.

All the rest snapped into place. The man in Manderson’s broad-brimmed hat, his unmistakable tweed shooting jacket, sat hunched over the telephone with his back to the door. Martin never saw his face. Mrs. Manderson, half-asleep behind the half-open connecting door, never saw him at all. She’d only heard a voice she thought she recognized, picking up the thread of a conversation she’d had with her real husband an hour earlier. The imposter never needed a full face disguise. All he needed was a good ear for mimicry, the right build, and a strong enough nerve.

He’d slipped in through the library garden window, not the front door, so the light-sleeping Martin in his pantry across the hall would never hear him. He’d helped himself to Manderson’s decanter of whisky to steady his hands, still shaking from whatever he’d already done to kill the real Manderson, miles away where no one would hear a shot. Then he’d crept up to Manderson’s bedroom, and froze. Mrs. Manderson had spoken to him.

And the imposter, playing the part of Manderson, didn’t just mumble a reply. He told her, casually, that he’d just sent Marlowe off in the car to Southampton, on urgent business to catch a man sailing for Paris on the morning steamer.

Why? Why would a man who never spoke to his wife tell her that specific lie, all about Marlowe?

By then I had my full theory laid out. Here is what happened, as best I can piece it together:

Sometime after Manderson climbed into that car with Marlowe, before 11pm, he was shot dead somewhere far enough from the house that no one heard the shot. The killer drove the body back, left it by the garden shed, stripped it of its outer clothes. Then he donned Manderson’s too-tight shoes, his jacket, his hat, stuffed the stolen dental plate and the murder weapon in his pockets, slipped through the library window, rang for Martin, and stayed hunched over the telephone so he never had to turn his face. He snuck up the stairs, slipped into Marlowe’s bedroom, put the very revolver he’d used to kill Manderson back in its case on the mantelpiece, where it was supposed to belong. Then he went back to Manderson’s room, left the shoes outside the door for the maid to polish, dumped the dead man’s dinner clothes over a chair, set the denture by the bed, and dressed himself in a new set of Manderson’s clothes to slip out of the house unseen before dawn.

Now the only question left: who was he?

Five facts were certain about the imposter:
1. He knew Manderson extremely well, well enough to copy his voice, his mannerisms, his habits down to the smallest detail without slipping up in front of the people who knew him best.
2. He was roughly Manderson’s height and build, broad-shouldered, so that anyone seeing his back would never spot the difference — but his feet were noticeably, if not drastically, larger than Manderson’s.
3. He had real talent for mimicry, and stage experience.
4. He knew every tiny quirk, every unspoken routine of the Manderson household, inside and out.
5. He absolutely, desperately needed every single person to believe Manderson was alive and in that house, until long after midnight that Sunday.

Now cross-reference that list against John Marlowe, Manderson’s private secretary, who’d worked for him for four years:
1. He’d lived in the house as part of the family for nearly four years, closer to Manderson than any other living soul.
2. They stood almost identical heights, five foot eleven, both broad and powerfully built. Marlowe was 20 years younger, a little slimmer in the torso — and his shoes, which I checked later, were a full size longer and wider than Manderson’s.
3. I’d spotted a cast photo on his mantelpiece, of him and two friends dressed as Falstaff’s cronies from *The Merry Wives of Windsor*, marked with an Oxford photography studio stamp. I sent an urgent, confidential wire to a friend at Oxford who ran the dramatic society, asking for Marlowe’s acting record. His reply came back the morning of the inquest: Marlowe was in the OUDS for three years, served as president, played Bardolph, Cleon, Mercutio, was famous for dead-on character impersonations, and was the talk of the university for legendary pranks and hoaxes.
4. No one outside the servants knew the ins and outs of the household half as well as he did.
5. We have unshakable witness proof that Marlowe checked into a Southampton hotel at 6:30am that Monday, exactly as he claimed he’d been told to do by Manderson on that late night mission, before he drove back to Marlstone to put on a perfect show of shock and horror at the news his employer was dead.

We can now hold that fifth fact, and line it up against the fifth, unshakable conclusion about the imposter the whole world thought was Manderson that night.
