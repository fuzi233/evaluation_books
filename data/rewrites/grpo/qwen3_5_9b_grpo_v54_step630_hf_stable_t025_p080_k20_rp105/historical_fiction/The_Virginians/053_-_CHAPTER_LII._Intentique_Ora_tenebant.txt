Months bled into one another, a weary gray stretch of time spent within the stone walls of the fort. Life there was a cycle of friction and truce between the commandant and myself, punctuated by greasy card games and dismal musical duets—his wheezing flute battling my cracked guitar. The poor Fawn endured the dual burdens of her master's whims, accepting beatings and cans of liquor as they came, while simultaneously nursing her papoose, her gout-stricken lord, or a prisoner shivering with ague. This rhythm held until the onset of last autumn, when a hunter arrived bearing news that ignited the garrison with a feverish excitement.

The Marquis de Montcalm had dispatched a substantial detachment to reinforce French-held forts and seize new positions within British territory. Troops had departed Quebec and Montreal, descending the St. Lawrence and navigating the lakes in bateaux, laden with artillery and vast stores of war supplies. Rumors swirled that Museau would soon be replaced by an officer of superior rank—a replacement who might exchange me for a prisoner or surrender me to the Indians in retaliation for cruelties inflicted upon enemy officers. The men of the fort hungered for these reinforcements; they dreamed of marching into Pennsylvania and New York, seizing Albany and Philadelphia, driving the "Rosbifs" into the sea, and claiming all America from the Mississippi to Newfoundland.

Yet, amidst this triumphant rhetoric, a shadow lingered in Commandant Museau's satisfaction. The prospect of French conquest did not bring him joy.

"Eh, commandant!" I said, breaking the silence. "'Tis fort bien, but meanwhile, where is your farm in Normandy? Where is the pot of cider and the trippes à la mode de Caen?"

"Yes; 'tis all very well, my garcon," he replied, his voice dropping. "But consider: when poor old Museau is superseded, where will you be? Other officers are not companions like me. Few men possess my humanity. If a great garrison arrives, will my successors grant you the indulgences honest Museau has bestowed? You will be kept in a sty like a pig ready for slaughter. As surely as one of our officers falls into the hands of your frontier brigands and suffers, so surely must you pay with your skin. You will be handed over to our red allies—to the brethren of La Biche yonder. Did you see, last year, what they did to your countrymen captured at Braddock? Roasting was merely the smallest punishment, ma foi—was it not, La Biche?"

He launched into a vivid, jocular catalog of tortures: eyes burned out, teeth and nails wrenched loose, limbs and bodies gashed. You turn pale, dear Miss Theo! Well, I shall have pity and spare you the graphic details Museau recounted with such cheerful relish.

La Biche, however, was not so easily shaken. She had witnessed such horrors in her own time. Hailing from the Senecas, whose villages lay near the great cataract between Ontario and Erie, her people had fought both for and against the English. In the battles between our races, it was difficult to declare which skin—white or red—proved most savage.

"They may chop me into cutlets and broil me," I said coolly. "But again, I say, you will never have the farm in Normandy."

"Go get the whisky-bottle, La Biche," Museau commanded.

"And it is not too late, even now. I will reward the guide who takes me home generously. I promise, as a man of honor, ten thousand livres to anyone who brings me a token—say, my watch and seal with my grandfather's arms, which I believe are hidden in a chest somewhere in this fort."

"Ah, scelerat!" roared the commandant, his laughter turning hoarse. "Thou hast eyes, thou! All is good prize in war."

"Think of a house in your village, of a fine field with a half-dozen cows, of an orchard heavy with fruit."

"And Javotte at the door with her wheel, and a rascal of a child or two with cheeks as red as the apples! O my country! O my mother!" the commandant whimpered. "Quick, La Biche, the whisky!"

That night, the commandant sat deep in thought, and so did La Biche. She remained silent and melancholy, sitting apart from us while nursing her child. Whenever my gaze met hers, I saw a fixed intensity. The infant began to cry, and Museau, with his usual foul tongue, ordered the child away to the building La Biche occupied. Once she was gone, we spoke freely. I laid out reasons before him that his cupidity could not resist.

"How do you know," he asked, "that this hunter will serve you?"

"That is my secret," I replied. Yet, since we were not bound by honor, I might reveal it. When hunters came to the settlements for trade, they often stayed a day or two for rest, drink, and company. Our new friend loved these things. He gambled with the men, traded his furs for liquor, and enjoyed the fort's atmosphere, singing and dancing. I believe the men liked my songs; for lack of better entertainment, I often sang and scraped my guitar. We held many concerts, the men joining in chorus or dancing to my rough music, until interrupted by the drums and the retreat.

Our guest, the hunter, attended one or two of these gatherings. I decided to test if he understood English. After exhausting our stock of French songs, I declared, "My lads, I will give you an English song." To the tune of "Over the hills and far away"—a favorite air my grandfather once hummed in Marlborough's camp—I improvised some doggerel: "This long, long year, a prisoner drear; Ah, me! I'm tired of lingering here: I'll give a hundred guineas gay, To be over the hills and far away."

"What is it?" the hunter asked. "I don't understand."

"'Tis a girl to her lover," I answered. But I saw the twinkle in his eye; he understood.

The next day, when no men were within hearing, the trapper confirmed my suspicion. As he passed me, he hummed in a low, perfect English tone the burden of my previous verse: "Over the hills and far away."

"If you are ready," he said, "I am ready. I know your people and the way to them. Talk to the Fawn; she will tell you what to do. What! You will not play with me?" He pulled out some cards just as two soldiers approached. "Milor est trop grand seigneur? Bonjour, my lord!"

The man offered a mock bow and walked away, shrugging his shoulders, ready to seek other players.

I knew then that La Biche was to be the agent, and that Museau had accepted my offer. The poor Fawn performed her part faithfully and dexterously. She had long been allowed free communication with me, tending to my wounds and illnesses, helping with my chamber duties and cooking. She was permitted to leave the fort, wandering to the river and fields where the garrison's corn and garden produce were gathered.

Having gambled away most of the money earned from his peltries, the trapper gathered his store of flints, powder, and blankets, and took his leave. Three days after his departure, the Fawn signaled that the time for my trial of freedom had arrived.

When first wounded, I had been taken by my kind Florac and placed on his bed in the officers' room. When the fort was emptied of all officers except the old lieutenant left in command, I was allowed to remain in my quarters, sometimes left free, sometimes locked up and fed prisoners' rations, and sometimes invited to share his mess by my tipsy gaoler.

This officers' house, or room, was constructed of logs like the half-dozen others within the fort, mounting only four small-caliber guns, one of which stood on the bastion behind my cabin. Looking westward over this gun, one could see a small island at the confluence of the Ohio and Monongahela rivers, where Duquesne was situated. On the shore opposite this island stood some trees.

"You see those trees?" my poor La Biche asked me the day before, speaking in her French jargon. "He waits for you behind those trees."

During the day, the door to my quarters remained open, and La Biche was free to come and go. On the eve of my escape, she returned from the fields carrying a pick and a basket of vegetables and potherbs for soup. She sat on a bench at my door, the pick resting against it, the basket beside her. I stood talking to her for a while; I was so distracted by my condition that I never would have thought to use the pick had she actually pushed it into my open door, causing it to fall into my room. "Hide it," she said. "Want it soon." That afternoon, she pointed out the trees to me.

The following day, she appeared pretending to be furious, calling out, "My lord! My lord! Why do you not come to the commandant's dinner? He is very bad! Entendez-vous?" Peering into the room as she spoke, she threw a coil of rope at me.

"I am coming, La Biche," I said, hobbling after her on my crutch. As I entered the commandant's quarters, she whispered, "Pour ce soir." Then I knew the time had come.

As for Museau, he knew nothing of the matter. Not he! He growled at me, claiming the soup was cold. He looked me steadily in the face, talking of this and that, not only while his servant was present but afterwards as we smoked our pipes and played piquet, while the poor La Biche sat cowering in a corner.

My friend's whisky bottle was empty. With a knowing look, he said he needed another glass—that we both needed a glass that night. Rising from the table, he stumped to the inner room where he kept his fire-water under lock and key, leaving the poor La Biche behind, who could not resist that temptation.

As he turned his back, La Biche rose. He was no sooner gone than she was at my feet, kissing my hand, pressing it to her heart, and bursting into tears over my knees. I confess I was so troubled by this silent testimony of her attachment and fondness—an extent I had scarcely suspected before—that when Museau returned, I had not fully recovered my equanimity, though the poor Fawn was back in her corner, shrouded in her blanket.

He seemed to notice nothing strange in either of our behaviors. We sat down to our game, though my thoughts were so preoccupied that I scarcely knew what cards faced me.

"I gain everything from you tonight, milor," he said grimly. "We play upon parole."

"And you may count upon mine," I replied.

"Eh! 'Tis all that you have!" he countered.

"Monsieur," I said, "my word is good for ten thousand livres;" and we continued the game.

Finally, he claimed a headache and retired to bed, signaling that I should also retire. "I wish you a good night, mon petit milor," he said. "Stay, you will fall without your crutch," his eyes twinkling, his face wearing a sarcastic grin. In the agitation of the moment, I had forgotten I was lame and walked away at a pace as good as a grenadier's.

"What a vilain night!" he muttered, looking out. Indeed, a tempest raged abroad, a great roaring and wind. "Bring a lantern, La Tulipe, and lock my lord comfortably into his quarters!" He stood a moment looking at me from his own door, and I caught a glimpse of the poor La Biche behind him.

The night was so rainy that the sentries preferred their boxes and did not disturb me in my work. The log-house was built with upright posts deeply fixed in the ground and horizontal logs laid upon them. I had to dig beneath these, working a hole sufficient for my body to pass. I began in the dark, shortly after tattoo. It was some time after midnight before my work was done; lifting my hand under the log, I felt the rain falling from without. I had to work very cautiously for two hours after that, then creep through to the parapet and silently fling my rope over the gun. My heart trembled lest a sentry should see me and send a charge of lead into my body.

The wall was but twelve feet high, and my fall into the ditch was easy enough. I waited there a while, looking steadily under the gun, trying to discern the river and the island. I heard the sentry pacing above, humming a tune. The darkness cleared somewhat as the moon rose, revealing the shining river and the dark rocks and trees of the island rising from the waters.

I made for this mark as swiftly as I could, heading for the clump of trees to which I had been directed. Oh, what a relief I felt when I heard a low voice humming there, "Over the hills and far away!"

When Mr. George reached this part of his narrative, Miss Theo, seated by a harpsichord, turned and dashed off the tune on the instrument, while the entire little company broke out into the merry chorus.

"Our way," the speaker continued, "lay through a level tract of forest familiar to my guide, along the right bank of the Monongahela. By daylight, we reached a clearer country, and my trapper—Silverheels was the name by which he went—asked if I had ever seen the spot before. It was the fatal field where Braddock had fallen, and whence I had been wonderfully rescued the summer of the previous year. Now, the leaves were beginning to tint themselves with the magnificent hues of autumn."
