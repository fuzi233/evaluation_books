The smoke-laden haze of the Thames, usually a confused blend of grey and glimmer, was sharpening into a brilliant, glittering edge as the sun climbed high over Westminster. Against this backdrop, two men crossed Westminster Bridge. One was towering in height, the other diminutive, a contrast as stark as the arrogant clock-face of Parliament versus the humble, humped shoulders of the Abbey. The taller man, M. Hercule Flambeau, a private detective, was heading toward his new headquarters in a cluster of flats facing the Abbey. Beside him walked the Reverend J. Brown, a clergyman from St. Francis Xavier's in Camberwell, traveling from a local deathbed to visit his friend.

Their destination was a striking edifice, American in both its soaring altitude and the slick, mechanical complexity of its elevators and telephone systems. It was still under construction, sparsely populated, save for the offices immediately above and below Flambeau's, which were occupied. The upper floors and several levels beneath were empty shells. Yet, before one could admire the architecture, attention was seized by a single, glaring anomaly mounted outside the office above Flambeau: a colossal, gilt effigy of a human eye surrounded by radiating rays of gold, occupying the space of two or three windows.

"What on earth is that?" Father Brown asked, halting in his stride.

"Oh, a new religion," Flambeau laughed, the sound echoing off the damp stone. "One of those trendy sects that forgives your sins simply by declaring you never committed any. It reminds me of Christian Science. A fellow calling himself Kalon—though I can't vouch for the spelling—has rented the flat directly above mine. Beneath him are two lady typists, and above sits this enthusiastic old charlatan. He styles himself the New Priest of Apollo and worships the sun."

"Let him keep his distance," Father Brown murmured, staring upward. "The sun was the cruelest of all the gods. But what does that monstrous eye signify?"

"It's their central theory," Flambeau explained casually. "They believe a man can endure anything if his mind remains perfectly steady. Their symbols are the sun and the open eye; the idea being that a truly healthy man could gaze directly into the sun without flinching."

"If a man were truly healthy," Father Brown countered, "he wouldn't need to stare at it."

"That is all I know," Flambeau shrugged. "They claim, naturally, to cure every physical ailment."

"And what of the one spiritual malady?" Father Brown asked, his tone shifting to grave curiosity.

"And what might that be?"

"Thinking oneself perfectly well when one is not," Father Brown replied.

Flambeau, a lucid Southerner who viewed the world through the lens of either Catholicism or atheism, showed little interest in these bright, pallid cults. However, humanity itself, particularly when adorned with beauty, always captured his attention. Moreover, the ladies residing on the floor below held a peculiar fascination for him. Their office was run by two sisters, both slight and dark, dressed in severe, business-like black with sharp, masculine collars. The elder, Pauline Stacey, possessed a dark, eager, and aquiline profile—a face that seemed carved from the clean edge of a weapon. She cut a path through life with relentless determination. Her eyes burned with a brilliance akin to polished steel rather than diamond; her posture was stiff, a rigidity that betrayed a grace strained to breaking point. Her younger sister, Joan, trailed behind her like a faded shadow, paler, quieter, and far less imposing.

Pauline Stacey was, in reality, theheiress to a vast fortune and half a county. Raised amidst castles and gardens, she had succumbed to a frigid, modern ambition, driving herself toward what she deemed a harder, superior existence. She had not squandered her inheritance; quite the opposite. She retained her wealth, deploying it with a masterful utilitarianism toward practical social causes, specifically a model typewriting empire she founded alongside her sister. While Joan followed Pauline's lead with a dogged, almost tragic devotion, Pauline herself seemed to deny the existence of tragedy entirely, viewing it as a concept incompatible with her iron will.

On Flambeau's first visit, Pauline's rigid rapidity and cold impatience had amused him greatly. He had once waited in the lobby for the elevator boy to guide him, but Pauline had sharply dismissed this courtesy, asserting her knowledge of the machine and refusing dependence on boys—or men. In the brief ascent to her floor, she delivered a torrent of fundamental views: she was a modern working woman who adored modern machinery, blazing with an abstract fury against those who sought to reject mechanistic science in favor of lost romances. To her, operating a lift was proof of mastery; relying on glasses or prosthetics, she argued, was a badge of cowardice. She spoke of being "free-born," rejecting the idea that doctors' props marked one as a slave to illness. "The sun is not my master," she declared, her eyes sparkling with terrible crystal intensity. "I will open my eyes and stare at him whenever I choose."

Flambeau, charmed by this strange, stiff beauty, had once complimented her resolve. Yet, as he ascended to his own floor, he had drawn a deep breath and whistled to himself, noting with a chill realization that she had likely fallen under the spell of the conjurer above—the man of the golden eye. Indeed, the spiritual bond between the floors above and below was growing tight.

Kalon, the self-styled prophet, was a magnificent creature, physically worthy to be Apollo's pontiff. Nearly as tall as Flambeau, he possessed a golden beard, strong blue eyes, and a mane of hair flung back like a lion's. He embodied Nietzsche's "blonde beast," yet his raw animal beauty was tempered by genuine intellect and spirituality. Despite the vulgar incongruity of his setting—an office halfway up a Victorian street, a brass-plate name, and a gilt emblem advertising his creed like an oculist—he commanded an oppressive, inspiring presence. Even in a loose linen jacket, he was formidable; in white vestments and a golden circlet, saluting the sun on his balcony, he was a vision so splendid that passersby often fell silent. Three times daily—at dawn, dusk, and the shock of noon—he emerged to chant his litany to his shining lord.

It was during the noon prayer that Father Brown first saw him clearly. Kalon stood erect, silver garments flowing, hands uplifted, his voice a strangely penetrating melody cutting through the bustle of Westminster. His eyes were fixed upon the flaming disc of the sun. Doubtfully, one might ask if he saw anything at all on earth; it was certain he did not notice the stunted, round-faced priest below, who watched him with blinking, weary eyes. Father Brown, unlike the prophet, could not look at anything without blinking.

Suddenly, a rush and crash, like the reverse roar of a rocket, shattered the scene. Five people surged into the mansion gates as three fled outward, creating a deafening cacophony that filled the street with the sensation of abrupt horror. Two figures remained frozen in the aftermath: the fair prophet on the balcony above, and the ugly priest of Christ below.

Moments later, Flambeau burst into the foyer, his tall, titanic energy dominating the panicking mob. He bellowed for a surgeon. As he turned back into the dark, thronged entrance, Father Brown followed, ducking and diving through the crush of bodies, still hearing the magnificent, monotonous chant of the solar priest calling upon his god.

Inside the enclosed space where the elevator shaft opened, Flambeau and six others gathered around the void. The lift had not descended. Instead, something else had plummeted. For the last four minutes, Flambeau had gazed down into the abyss, seeing the brain-battered, bleeding figure of Pauline Stacey. He had no doubt she was dead. He recalled her priggish speeches and defiant beauty, and now, like a thunderbolt from nowhere, that image was shattered. Had she jumped? Suicide seemed impossible for such an insolent optimist. Murder? Who lived in these sparsely inhabited flats capable of killing?

Raucous questions erupted until a heavy, quiet voice interceded. "Kalon has been up on his balcony worshipping his god for the last fifteen minutes."

Flambeau turned, his swarthy face etched with sudden panic. "Then, if he has been up there all the time, who could have done it?"

"Perhaps we should go upstairs," suggested the voice, assuring him they had half an hour before the police arrived.

Flambeau dashed to the typewriting office, finding it empty, then raced to his own flat. Returning moments later with a pale, shocked expression, he announced, "Her sister... she seems to have gone out for a walk."

Father Brown nodded. "Or she may have gone up to the office of that sun-man." He guided Flambeau back downstairs to the Stacey flat. Brown took a large red-leather chair in the entrance, positioning himself to watch the stairs and landings. He did not wait long. Within four minutes, three figures descended, united by a somber gravity. First came Joan Stacey, the dead woman's sister, looking drawn and premature-aged in grey. Second was Kalon, the priest of Apollo, descending in utter magnificence, his white robes and parted hair resembling a painting of Christ leaving the Praetorium. Third was Flambeau, brow furrowed in bewilderment.
