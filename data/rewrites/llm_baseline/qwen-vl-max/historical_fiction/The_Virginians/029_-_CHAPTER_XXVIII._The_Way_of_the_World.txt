After only a few days at Tunbridge Wells, Harry Warrington found himself the most talked-about figure in the fashionable little resort. No nobleman, not even the Bishop of Salisbury himself, drew such attention. People turned to watch him pass through the streets; country folk gaped as he walked by on market day. At the assembly rooms, matrons smiled and invited him to join their circles, often contriving to leave him alone with their daughters—most of whom returned his polite glances with warm smiles. Everyone knew, down to the last acre and shilling, the size of his Virginia estate and the income it brought. At every tea-table, his winnings at cards were recounted, calculated, and admired. How curiously our neighbours know our affairs!

The fascination with Harry was so great that even his servant, Gumbo, became an object of interest. Strangers sought him out, bought him ale and cold meat, and pestered him for news of the young Virginian. Gumbo, well fed and flattered, grew plump and proud, becoming a central figure among the local valets—and never more inventive in his tales. No gathering was complete without Mr. Warrington’s presence.

Harry was both amused and astonished by this sudden fame. He bore it with good grace, though not without pride. Brought up in a household where he had always been held in high regard, he now found confirmation in the world’s admiration. Yet he did not let it go to his head. He did not boast of his victories or carry himself with arrogance. When challenged to games or races, he felt bound by a strange sense of honour to accept. To refuse would have seemed ungracious—or worse, un-Virginian. And so, when men invited him to horse-races, drinking bouts, or card matches, he responded with the same quiet dignity he’d shown back home.

He proved remarkably capable in these contests. With a steady head, a sure hand, and nerves of steel, he stood his ground against England’s finest. He shot better than Jack Morris, rode faster than Mr. Scamper, and soon held his own at tennis against Captain Batts—though he’d never played before. He faced the famed gamblers Lord Chesterfield and Lord March, who both praised his composure, gallantry, and gentlemanly conduct. Though not brilliant in books, he wrote clearly and fluently—better than many of the ton. His honest ignorance charmed Madame de Bernstein, who had once read widely and enjoyed Molière and Fielding, but whose true passion lay in life, not literature. She delighted in his simplicity and took pleasure in dressing him up: finding fine lace for his ruffles, a diamond brooch for his cravat. Soon, Harry became her favourite nephew, eclipsing all others.

Lady Maria, perhaps, was secretly pleased by his success. But her brothers—well, they must have felt a pang of envy when they heard how quickly he’d won their aunt’s favour. After two weeks at the Wells, Harry was no longer just a visitor—he was a celebrity. He knew all the best company. And if he also came to know some of the worst, could he be blamed? Was it wrong to take the world as he found it, to sip from the sparkling cup of pleasure offered so freely?

Madame de Bernstein revelled in his triumphs and encouraged him to enjoy them fully. She was no stern moralist, nor a particularly wise guide for youth. If Cattarina, the French dancer, sent him love notes, she might have advised him to reply. But Harry still carried with him the modesty of his colonial upbringing—the kind of plain honesty that went with homespun linen. In the wide-open spaces of America, libertinism was rare. The vices of London or Paris were unknown in the rough frontier towns. So when Harry heard the bold talk of his new companions, he blushed like a girl. Even Aunt Bernstein’s jokes startled him, and she would tease him, calling him “Joseph” or “simpleton.”

Yet though he remained innocent, the world assumed he was as corrupt as any other young man. How was he to know he shouldn’t walk arm-in-arm with Cattarina? He had seen Lord March driving her about in his phaeton. Surely there was no harm in offering her his arm, strolling with her in public? When she fancied a trinket at the toy shop, and his pockets were full of gold, he happily bought her the locket she desired. The next day, it was a piece of lace—again, he gave it to her. The day after, another whim—but then he stopped. With a laugh, he gently refused her request. He was generous, yes, but not reckless. He had no intention of buying diamonds for her ears. But who would believe his modesty?

Aunt Bernstein insisted he was playing Don Juan—taking Lord March’s place. She said this especially when Lady Maria was present, delighting in the pain it caused her niece. “Why, my dear,” she would say, “boys will be boys. I don’t want Harry to be the first milksop in his family!” And poor Maria, eating bread paid for by her aunt, sometimes choked on its bitterness. Oh, how cruel some women can make kindness taste!

Mr. Wolfe, meanwhile, kept coming from Westerham to court the lady he loved. Knowing the Colonel was devoted to her, Harry didn’t expect to see much of him—though he missed his conversation. It was different from the idle chatter around him. Mr. Wolfe never spoke of horses, cards, or hunting exploits. He didn’t brag about women or spread scandals. That was not a good time. The old world was far more dissolute than ours. There was a king with mistresses in open view, nobles mad for pleasure, and a looseness of speech and deed that we must record honestly, without going into details that might shock decent readers. Harry had fallen among some of the wildest people—and found an old relative living right at the heart of the merriment.

At first, Harry didn’t notice how Colonel Wolfe avoided him. When they met by chance, the Colonel’s manner was distant, cold. Harry didn’t know the stories being told. Who does? Who makes them? Who fathers such lies? Poor Harry had no idea of the reputation he was gaining. While riding, playing, and laughing, he was already spoken of as the most debauched, godless, and reckless young man in England.

These tales reached Colonel Lambert at Oakhurst—first one, then another, until they overwhelmed him. His kind heart filled with sorrow. His family noticed something was wrong. At first, he refused to speak. But Mrs. Lambert, anxious and persistent, wore him down. He told her what he knew of Harry Warrington. She was shocked and grieved. The sorrow passed from the bedroom to the drawing-room, then to Theo and Hester. O tender hearts! At first, Hester flushed red, clenched her fists, and swore she wouldn’t believe a word. But in the end, she did. Scandal always wins, especially over the good and innocent.

“Oh, the serpent we’ve nursed by our fire!” they lamented. “Oh, the wretched boy!” To think he walked with that painted Frenchwoman, gave her jewels, paraded his shame before all society! They wept together, then took the parson into their confidence. He preached a sermon on scandal the next Sunday, warning against judging others. But words fall short. When the next story comes, we believe it. So did the Oakhursts believe the worst of Harry Warrington.

Meanwhile, Harry was too happy to know how deeply his friends mourned him. He lived idly, pleasantly, though uselessly—unaware of the storm he stirred or the terrible name he bore.

One afternoon, after a tennis match with Captain Batts, Harry stepped out, pleased with himself and the world. He met Colonel Wolfe, returning from a visit to the woman he loved. Harry extended his hand. The Colonel took it—but his greeting was icy. Harry noticed, especially when the Colonel barely touched his hat in return, while Batts bowed deeply.

Batts walked away, looking unsettled. Harry stayed behind, speaking to his friend.

Mr. Wolfe walked beside him—upright, silent, cold.

“I haven’t seen you in days,” said Harry.

“You’ve had other companions,” replied Wolfe curtly.

“But I’d rather be with you than any of them,” Harry insisted.

“Indeed, you might find better company,” the Colonel said grimly.

“Do you mean Captain Batts?” asked Harry.

“He’s no favourite of mine,” said Wolfe. “He had a bad name in the army, and hasn’t improved since he left. You could do better than him. Pardon my frankness.”

“Friend? He’s not my friend—he teaches me tennis. He’s in league with my lord and all the fashionables here.”

“I’m not one of them,” said Wolfe.

“What’s wrong, Colonel? Have I offended you?”

“I’ll be frank,” said the Colonel. “I don’t like some of your friends.”

“But they’re the highest in England!” protested Harry.

“Exactly—too high for a hard-working soldier like me. If you keep their company, believe me, many of us humble folk can’t afford to follow you.”

“I’m here paying my respects to an honourable lady,” said Wolfe. “And yesterday, I saw you walking openly with a French ballet-dancer. You took off your hat.”

“I must frankly tell you,” he added, “I’d prefer you didn’t do that.”

“Sir,” said Harry, turning red, “do you mean I should give up your acquaintance altogether?”

“I shall ask you to, when you’re with that woman,” said Wolfe sharply—using a word too strong to repeat, though Shakespeare put it in Othello’s mouth.

“Great heavens!” cried Harry. “What shame is this? How dare anyone say such a thing about a woman?”

“You ought to know best,” said Wolfe, looking surprised. “Or the world has lied about you terribly.”

“What should I know?” asked Harry. “I see a poor French dancer, come with her mother, ordered by doctors to drink the waters. I know people of my rank don’t usually mix with hers—but why should I not offer her my arm? There are scarcely half a dozen here who speak her language. I can speak a little French. Why shouldn’t I help her? If Colonel Wolfe refuses to tip his hat when I walk with her, by George, he may leave it alone!”

“You don’t mean to say,” said Wolfe, watching him closely, “you don’t know her character?”

“Of course she’s a dancer,” said Harry. “But no better or worse than others. And if she were a duchess or your grandmother, I couldn’t respect her more.”

“You don’t mean to say you didn’t win her from Lord March at dice?”

“At what?”

“Dice. Everyone knows it. Not a soul at the Wells is unaware. I heard it myself, just now, from Mr. Richardson. The ladies said you’d become a colonial Lovelace.”

“What else have they said?” asked Harry.

Wolfe told him—stories of corruption, drunkenness, gambling, blasphemy, freethinking, and association with the worst company. Harry listened, stunned.

“I tell you these things,” said Wolfe, “so you know what people say. And because I believe, from your reaction, you’re innocent of most. I feel I’ve wronged you, and I ask your pardon.”

Harry eagerly accepted. They shook hands—this time with real warmth.

As for the charges, Harry denied most easily. As for gambling, he admitted it. A gentleman, he said, could not refuse a fair challenge—especially when he had the means. And he never played beyond what he could afford. At first, he won enough to play higher stakes. He thought play was fair—it was pleasant. Didn’t all England play, except the Methodists? Hadn’t he seen the best company at the Wells—his aunt included?

Wolfe made no comment on that. But he warned Harry plainly: the life he led might be pleasant, but it was not profitable.

“It can’t be,” said the Colonel, “that a man spends his days racing horses and playing tennis, and his nights carousing or at cards. Every man was made to work. A gentleman, if he has no duty, must create one. Do you know your country’s laws, Mr. Warrington? As a great landowner, you’ll likely become a magistrate. Have you studied the trades and industries of your land? These are worthy pursuits—better than cockfights or cricket. Do you know anything of our profession? It’s noble. There’s plenty to learn—perhaps suited to you. I mention it, not books, because I judge your genius lies elsewhere. But honour is the aim of life. Every man can serve his country one way or another. Be sure, sir, that idle bread is the most dangerous of all. Cards and pleasure may be pastimes after work—but not instead of it. And do you know, Mr. Warrington, instead of being the Fortunate Youth, as all call you, I think you are rather Warrington the Unlucky—for you are followed by daily idleness, daily flattery, daily temptation. May the Lord send you deliverance from your fortune.”

That evening, Harry did not wish to tell his aunt why he looked so grave. He meant not to drink—but the fellows at the ordinary passed the bottle round. He meant not to play—but a fourth was needed at his aunt’s table. How could he resist? He partnered her several times. He had luck—someone’s own luck, surely. And once again, he watched the dawn break, feasting on chickens and champagne as the sun rose.
