As Lady Castlewood, her son, and her daughter filed out through one door of the salon, having concluded their sitting, my Lord Castlewood exited by another. This departure allowed the demure eyes of the sisters to rise from the tambour-frame, where they had been diligently stitching innocent violets and jonquils. Their gazes lifted toward Harry Warrington, who stood beneath a grand ancestral portrait near the great fireplace. He appeared somewhat disheveled, having gathered a formidable heap of blushing flowers—those blooms that rarely appear after the gentler seasons—and using them to decorate his honest countenance, his cheeks, his forehead, and even his youthful ears.

"Why did you refuse to accompany our aunt, cousin?" asked the lady holding the tambour-frame.

"Because your ladyship commanded me to stay," replied the lad.

"I bid you stay! La, child! What one says in jest, you take as gospel! Are all you Virginian gentlemen so excessively obedient that you mistake every idle remark of a lady for a direct order? Virginia must be a delightful land for our sex if this is true!"

"You said—" Harry stammered, his voice trembling with sudden emotion, "—when we walked along the terrace two nights ago—Oh, heaven!"

"Oh, that sweet night, cousin!" cried the Tambour-frame lady.

"When you gave me this rose from your own neck," roared out Harry, suddenly producing a crumpled, decaying vegetable from his waistcoat pocket—"which I shall never part with! By heavens, not while this heart beats!"

"You said, 'Harry, if your aunt asks you to leave, you will go, and if you go, you will forget me.' Didn't you say that?"

"All men forget!" sighed the Virginian.

"In this cold, selfish country, perhaps," Harry continued, still elevated in spirit, "cousins may forget, but not in ours. I would sooner lose an arm than disobey the old lady. It went straight to my heart to deny her, and she has been so kind to me, and who introduced me to—Oh, heaven!" (Here, he kicked an intervening spaniel, which fled yelping from before the fire, followed immediately by a rapid advance toward the tambour-frame.)

"Look here, cousin! If you were to bid me leap from that window, I would do it; or commit murder, I would do it."

"La! But you need not squeeze my hand so tightly, you silly child!" remarked Maria.

"I can't help it—we are southerners. Where my heart is, I cannot help speaking my mind, cousin—and you know exactly where that heart lies! Since that evening—that—Oh, heaven! I tell you I have scarcely slept since. I feel compelled to do something—to distinguish myself—to become someone great. I wish there were giants, Maria, as I've read in books, that I might fight them. I wish you were in distress so I could help you somehow. I wish you needed my blood so I could spend every drop for you. And when you told me not to go with Madame Bernstein..."

"I tell thee, child, never."

"I thought you told me. You said you knew I preferred my aunt to my cousin. Then I said what I say now: 'Incomparable Maria! I prefer thee to all the women in the world and all the angels in Paradise—I would go anywhere, even to dungeons, if you ordered me!' And do you think I wouldn't stay anywhere, simply because you desire my presence?" he added after a brief pause.

"Men always talk in that manner—that is, that is what I have heard," corrected the spinster, adjusting her tone; "for what should a country-bred woman know about you creatures? When you are near us, they say you are all rapture and flame and promise and I-know-not-what; but when you depart, you forget us entirely."

"But I think I shall never wish to leave as long as I live," groaned the young man.

"I have grown weary of many things; not books, for I never cared much for study, but games and sports which I enjoyed as a boy. Before I saw you, I most desired to be a soldier; I tore my hair with rage when my poor dear brother went away instead of me on that expedition in which we lost him. But now, I care for only one thing in the entire world, and you know what that is."

"You silly child! Don't you know I am almost old enough to be..."

"I know—I know! But what is that to me? Haven't your brothers—or well, never mind who—told me stories against you? Didn't they show me the Family Bible, listing all your names and birthdates?"

"The cowards! Who did that?" cried Lady Maria.

"Dear Harry, tell me who did it? Was it my mother-in-law, the grasping, odious, abandoned, brazen harpy? Do you know everything about her? How she married my father while he was drunk—the horrid hussey!—and..."

"It certainly wasn't Lady Castlewood," interjected the bewildered Harry.

"Then it was my aunt," continued the infuriated lady. "A pretty moralist, indeed! A bishop's widow, forsooth, and I should like to know whose widow before and after. Why, Harry, she intrigues with the Pretender, and with the Court of Hanover, and I dare say she would with the Court of Rome and the Sultan of Turkey if she possessed the means. Do you know who her second husband was? A creature who..."

"But our aunt never spoke a word against you," broke in Harry, increasingly amazed by the nymph's vehemence.

She checked her anger. Reading a certain alarm in the inquisitive countenance opposite hers, she realized she was losing control.

"Well, well! I am a fool," she said softly. "I want you to think well of me, Harry!"

A hand reached out, was seized, and undoubtedly kissed by the rapturous youth.

"Angel!" he cried, gazing into her face with eager, honest eyes.

Two fish pools irradiated by a pair of stars would not glow with greater warmth than those elderly orbs into which Harry poured his gaze. Yet, he plunged into their blue depths and fancied he saw heaven in their calm brightness.

So that silly dog (of whom Aesop or the Spelling-book used to tell us in our youth) beheld a beef-bone in the pond, snapped at it, and lost the very bone he was carrying. Oh, absurd cur! He saw the beef-bone in his own mouth reflected in the treacherous pool, which dimpled, I dare say, with countless smiles, coolly sucked up the meat, and returned to its usual placidity. Ah! What a heap of wreckage lies beneath some of those quiet surfaces! What treasures we have dropped into them! What chased golden dishes, what precious jewels of love, what bone after bone, and sweetest heart's flesh! Do not some very faithful and unlucky dogs jump in bodily, getting swallowed up head and tail entirely? When some women come to be dragged, it is a marvel what will be found in the depths of them. *Cavete, canes!* Have a care how you lap that water. What do they want with us, the mischievous siren sluts? A green-eyed Naiad never rests until she has inveigled a fellow underwater; she sings after him, she dances after him; she winds around him, glittering tortuously; she warbles and whispers dainty secrets to his cheek, she kisses his feet, she leers at him from out of her rushes: all her beds sigh out, "Come, sweet youth! Hither, hither, rosy Hylas!" Pop goes Hylas. (Is the fable renewed forever and ever?) Does his captivator derive any pleasure? Does she take any account of him? No more than a fisherman landing at Brighton does of one out of a hundred thousand herrings....

The last time. Ulysses rowed past the Sirens' bank; he and his men did not care though a whole shoal of them were singing and combing their longest locks. Young Telemachus was for jumping overboard: but the tough old crew held the silly, bawling lad. They were deaf, and could not hear his bawling nor the sea-nymphs' singing. They were dim of sight, and did not see how lovely the witches were. The stale, old, leering witches! Away with ye! I dare say you have painted your cheeks by this time; your wretched old songs are as out of fashion as Mozart, and it is all false hair you are combing! In the last sentence, you see *Lector Benevolus* and *Scriptor Doctissimus* figure as tough old Ulysses and his tough old Boatswain, who do not care a quid of tobacco for any Siren at Sirens' Point; but Harry Warrington is green Telemachus, who, be sure, was very unlike the soft youth in the good Bishop of Cambray's twaddling story. He does not see that the siren paints the lashes from under which she ogles him; she puts by into a box when she has finished the ringlets into which she would inveigle him; and if she eats him, as she proposes to do, she will crunch his bones with a new set of grinders just from the dentist's, warranted for mastication.
