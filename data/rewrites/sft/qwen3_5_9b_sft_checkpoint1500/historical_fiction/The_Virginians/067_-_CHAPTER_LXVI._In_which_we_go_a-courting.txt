To those of my gentle readers who habitually stroll through the celebrated gardens of Regent's Park, where nature's creatures—fish, birds, beasts, and beasts of burden alike—are housed in exchange for a glimpse of their antics, I confess a peculiar distraction. While gazing into the glass tanks, observing the swimming patterns of various fins, I found my thoughts drifting inevitably back to the Virginians.

Among the aquatic beauties, there was one particular flounder, a creature of remarkable grace, gliding through his emerald bath with a fluidity that belied his humble reputation. To the uninitiated eye, who had devoured thousands of such specimens in Greenwich waters, this specimen appeared merely ordinary. Yet, as with mankind, so it is with flatfish; viewed in their natural element, they reveal an agility and health that transforms them into objects of genuine beauty. Remove them from the water, however, and watch how quickly that splendor vanishes, replaced by clumsy flapping and a desperate gasping for air. But if taken gently and returned to the Thames, they revive.

Such, I observed, was the case with Harry Warrington. For a long time, he had been "floundering" in a sea of difficulties, far removed from his proper element. Yet, once he found his footing, a transformation occurred. With the crisp tap of an epaulette on his shoulder, he rose from the mud of his former life into a soldier of striking vigor. He embraced his new profession with a zest that was infectious, mastering the intricacies of camp life with eager speed. Though I lack the literary flair of my friend Lorrequer to paint the full canvas of a military campaign—the mess nights, the parades, the camaraderie under canvas—I trust the reader can, with a bit of imagination, visualize Harry Warrington in his scarlet coat and buff facings, radiant with the joy of bearing the King's colors and eager to master every duty of his trade.

It was a mutual admiration society between the brothers. Each delighted in the virtues of the other, and George, seeing Harry's ascent, felt a surge of pride that bordered on delirium. In a heartfelt letter to his mother in Virginia, George recounted Harry's triumphs, carefully omitting any boastfulness on Harry's part, knowing his brother's modesty would never permit it. He detailed how Harry had secured his commission and then ventured further, offering to subsidize the cost of buying a higher rank. To George, helping his brother was not merely charity; it was a restitution. Harry, believing himself entitled to the entire fortune as an elder brother, had squandered resources he did not truly possess. Now, freed from this heavy misapprehension, George felt it was his solemn duty to restore what Harry had lost, viewing his own financial contribution as a fitting thank-offering for his brother's renewed prosperity.

Having settled matters regarding Harry, George turned his pen to his own affairs, addressing his mother on a subject of profound personal import. He spoke of the Lamerts, those true friends who, unlike his own blood relatives, had shown him nothing but kindness. Their benevolence was matched by the General's high standing in the army, and by Mrs. Lambert's tender, almost maternal, affection for the Esmond children. It was within this circle that George had found a love so deep it anchored his future hopes. He had formed an attachment for the elder Miss Lambert, a bond he believed essential to his happiness. Though he had not yet made formal proposals, he was candid with his mother: his feelings were plain, and the young lady and her parents appeared favorably disposed. Describing Miss Lambert as a model of perfection, George declared his conviction that his life's happiness hung in the balance, dependent entirely on securing her hand.

*Why do you not produce this letter?* one might ask, given the Editor's possession of the entire Warrington correspondence. *Why not?* Because it is cruel to expose the raw, incoherent vows of a young man's heart. To listen to the wild raptures of passion, to record the secrets whispered in twilight embrasures, or to bottle the hot tears of devotion, is a violation of the sacred. Love, wherever it blooms—in the glance between lovers, in the doting look of a mother, in the joy of a father watching his daughter whirl around the room with her captain—is holy. Let us not play the role of eavesdroppers, dissecting the throbs of delicate hearts or printing private confessions. Down with those who vulgarize the mysteries of love! To betray a lover's trust is to invite disaster; beware the man who prints your secrets, for he will surely cast you adrift before long.

While Harry sought glory in the smoke of battle, rushing toward reluctant dragons and dragging wounded comrades from the火线, George pursued a quieter, sweeter path. He penned sonnets to his mistress's brow, paced the darkness beneath her window watching her lamp, and crafted endless pretexts to send notes that required no answers yet somehow compelled them. He plucked verses from his favorite poets and flowers from Covent Garden for her adornment. He walked to St. James's Church, his mind elsewhere, singing along with the prayers he barely heard. He showered affection upon every relation of Miss Theo—from her siblings at school and college to Miss Hetty, his sparring partner in wit, and finally, to Mamma, who, according to Martin Lambert, was already half in love with him. Fathers may grow sulky at the prospect of a son-in-law, but mothers often become sentimental, falling in love with their own loves anew.

Meanwhile, Gumbo and Sady were perpetually on the move between Southampton Row and Dean Street, orchestrating summer festivities and proposing outings to Ranelagh, Hampstead, Vauxhall, and Marylebone Gardens. George desired the famous tragedy copied out for the stage, and who better to execute such beautiful Italian handwriting than Miss Theo? As the sheets passed back and forth, they were accompanied by a constant stream of notes expressing gratitude, inquiry, and admiration. *See,* marked a packet in Warrington's neat hand, *"T's letters, 1758–9."* Would we dare open them? No. Those virgin words were whispered for ears alone. Years later, the husband might read them with sweet pangs of memory, but to display them publicly would be sacrilege. Kind readers need only accept our assurance: the young lady's letters were modest and pure, the gentleman's respectful and tender. In truth, we have said little, yet in these few months, Mr. George Warrington had decided he had found the woman of women. She may not be the fairest, nor the wittiest—why, there were cousins like Flora, Coelia, and Ardelia, far more handsome—but her sweet face pleased him above all others. Her voice was the dearest to his ear, and in her company, he felt his intellect sharpen, his thoughts become eloquent. She possessed a generosity, nobility, and wit that made him delight in her presence. And regarding the young lady, may Heaven grant that we do not judge women too harshly: she endowed her young hero with every ounce of wit, wisdom, beauty, valour, and virtue imaginable.

When George's letter arrived home, it stirred no small excitement around Madam Esmond's fireside. He was in love and wished to marry! It was natural, and a prudent step to keep him out of harm's way. If he united himself with a well-bred Christian young woman, Madam saw no objection.

"I knew they would be setting their caps at him," remarked Mountain. "They fancy his wealth equals his estate. He hasn't mentioned if the young lady has money. I fear otherwise."

"People would set their caps at him here, I dare say," replied Madam Esmond, casting a grim look at her dependent. "And try to catch Mr. Esmond Warrington for their own daughters, who are no richer than Miss Lambert may be."

"I suppose your ladyship means me!" said Mountain. "My Fanny is poor, as you say; and 'tis kind of you to remind me of her poverty!"

"I said people would set their caps at him. If the cap fits you, *tant pis!* as my papa used to say."

"You think, madam, I am scheming to keep George for my daughter?" cried Mrs. Mountain. "I thank you, on my word! A good opinion you seem to have of us after the years we have lived together!"

"My dear Mountain, I know you much better than to suppose you could ever fancy your daughter would be a suitable match for a gentleman of Mr. Esmond's rank and station," said Madam, with much dignity.

"Fanny Parker was as good as Molly Benson at school, and Mr. Mountain's daughter is as good as Mr. Lambert's!" shrieked Mrs. Mountain.

"Then you did think of marrying her to my son!" exclaimed the mistress of Castlewood. "I shall write to Mr. Esmond Warrington and say how sorry I am that you should be disappointed!" And so it came to pass that Mrs. Mountain was indeed disappointed, harboring some ambitious views for her daughter; otherwise, why should she have been so agitated at the notion of Mr. Warrington's marriage?
