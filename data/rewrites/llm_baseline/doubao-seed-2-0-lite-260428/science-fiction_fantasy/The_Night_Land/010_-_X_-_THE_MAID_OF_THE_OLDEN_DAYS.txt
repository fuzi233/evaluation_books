All at once, the utter black weight of despair I had carried for so many leagues shattered into searing, unlooked-for gladness and sharp, bright hope: it felt I must stand beside my dearest love within the hour. This was an over-hasty expectation, of course, not destined for swift fulfillment. All I could plainly make out was the looming shape of a great pyramid, rising endless upward into the unblinking night.

I knew it must stand atop a central hill in that cursed dark country—only such elevation could make it loom so vast against the distant faint volcanic glows. I set off running headlong downhill toward it, my wits scattered by the shock of what I thought I had found.

I ran barely minutes before I pitched headlong over a hidden jagged rock, pain lancing so sharp through my neck I was certain I had broken my spine. I could not move for a long, stretched minute, left half-moaning and helpless on the cold earth, easy prey for any slavering thing that might stumble across me. At last I managed to sit upright, clutching my stinging neck in both hands, and the ache faded slow enough that I could stagger back to my feet. Now I moved with great caution, a cold prickle of unease coiling in my chest: why was the pyramid so utterly dark, no glimmer of the Lesser Refuge’s sentinel lamps, no faint hum of the ancient Earth Current I knew? A fear bloomed that this was no haven, but a House of Evil, some dark power weaving a false facade to lure me to ruin. Even so, the shape was plain against the far fires of the land, and stubborn hope refused to let me turn away.

In the first wild instant I spotted the pyramid I had been too unhinged by months of grueling, lonely search to do anything but run blind toward it. I had even meant to call out to Naani with my augmented brain-parts, send the Master-Word first, then tell her I had come. But now caution held me: I would find out what that utter dark truly hid, before I gave away my position.

I pressed onward down the slope, careful at first, then seized again by fierce eagerness, the soreness of my fall fading fast under the pull of my longing. I had taken thirteen hours to climb the upper plain of the great volcano behind me; I descended its full length in ten, slowed only by the lingering unsteadiness of my fall, until I stepped out onto the vast plain of the Night Land. The distant dark hulk of the pyramid on its hill blocked all view of the refuge’s lights, but I could see a great dark bulk rising between me and the far glows of the volcanic vents, and knew that was the hill I sought.

I tramped four more hours across the plain, past small fire-holes that spilled dim red light across the rock and sparse gnarled brush. The land was never fully black, lit by that faint, hellish luminescence, until the base of the hill rose up before me, cutting off all distant glows and swallowing the world in pitch dark that way. It was another full hour before I reached its foot, and over those five hours since I had left the volcano, I heard the pad of unseen running things in the dark over and over, once a great giant’s roar echoing from leagues away, followed by a high, unholy screaming that curdled my blood.

I began to climb the hill. For the first stretch, wild excitement seized me, so that I nearly shouted Naani’s name aloud into the dark, fool enough to hope she might hear me. But the euphoria faded fast, cold caution wrapping around me again, as if my attuned spirit sensed a horror my conscious mind could not yet name. Three hours of climbing brought me almost to the summit, where the grim, desolate silhouette of the great dark pyramid loomed upward into the night. A sudden, overwhelming shaking terror took me, unshakable and primal: my soul knew no human lived in that vast, lightless bulk. Monstrous, horrid things nested there, waiting to tear my soul apart. I slipped back down the hill as quiet as a shadow, put as much distance between me and that accursed place as I could.

It took four long hours to put the hill behind me, and I wandered half-blind, no heed to my path, until I found myself standing on the shore of a long-dead ancient sea. I had no memory of veering that far off my path; I can only guess the dry seabed curved far around, or there were multiple such dead inland seas scattered across the Night Land.

I sank down weak and bewildered, my heart feeling as if it had turned to ash inside my chest. I knew from the certain knowledge of my spirit that the Lesser Refuge had fallen. Its people were all slaughtered or scattered, and the pyramid was now a roost for evil things. If that was true, I had come too late to save the maid I had crossed the world to find. In my hollow despair, I found myself almost hoping some monster would stumble across me, that I might fight it and die fast, for there was nothing left in all the dark world that made life worth living.

I sat there for a long stretch, until a thought struck me sharp: I would send the Master-Word out into the dark. How else would I ever know if Naani still lived? Even if no answer came from her, and an Evil Power found me instead, it would only end the heartache I carried.

I stood, scanning the dark around me, and sent the Master-Word rippling out through the aether with every fiber of my augmented mind, then called Naani’s name three times after it.

Instantly, it seemed, the Master-Word thrummed back around me, low and solemn, beating through every shadow of the night. A faint, lone voice sounded directly in my brain, so distant it felt like it had drifted from the very end of the dead world. It was Naani’s voice, and it was the voice of Mirdath, the lost love of my countless olden memories, calling me by the name no one but she should have known.

I nearly choked on a sob of overwhelming, searing joy, all my despair vanishing like smoke. My dearest Naani lived. I had not heard that voice in what felt like an eternity of grim labor and creeping horror. Even as I stood dazed with joy, a cold prickle ran up my spine: someone was only a little way off, hiding in the brush beside the nearest fire-hole, my spirit had sensed them before my eyes did. I did not answer Naani across the dark world. I slipped fast into a thick bush near the fire-hole, pressed aside the branches to peer through.

There knelt a slim, small figure, sobbing softly on the ground beside the red glow of the vent, harkening with such desperate focus she barely breathed. It was Naani. My soul knew her in that single bright, perfect moment. She was listening for my spiritual call, sure it had come all the way from the distant Mighty Pyramid she had left behind—for weeks of lonely wandering she had cried out to me, over and over, and had never gotten a reply. She was too weak now to cast her voice far through the aether; all her desperate calls had only brushed the edge of my senses, faint flickers I had taken for hallucinations on the road.

I drew in a sharp breath, set my teeth to steady my trembling lips, and spoke her olden name, soft out of the bush: “Mirdath.”

The maid stopped sobbing mid-gasp. She looked this way and that, tears glinting in the firelight, her face torn between fresh terror and a wild, impossible hope she dared not name. I pushed through the branches, stepping out into the open in my dented grey armor, and stood frozen for a long moment, my heart screaming to pull her into my arms, as if I had found Mirdath again after a lifetime of lost eons. She was Naani, and she was Mirdath, and she was a stranger to my eyes, small and dainty, shattered by grief and terror and loneliness.

For the first split second she screamed and scrambled backward, trying to flee into the brush, thinking I was some monster that had snuck up on her. Then she saw I was human. I spoke the Master-Word aloud, and told her my name, that I was the one she had called for across the dark months. She knew it before my words even finished sounding. She cried out in a broken, shaking voice, and ran to me, shoving her small hands into my grip, then collapsed into great heaving sobs, so weak I could barely steady her. I had no armored gloves on, so I held her hands soft, saying nothing, letting her cry.

She leaned against me, light as a child, trembling so hard I could feel it through my armor. I realized then she was starving. She had wandered alone for weeks, near to death when I stumbled upon her. I laid her gentle against a smooth warm rock at the edge of the fire-hole, stripped off my thick wool cloak, and wrapped it tight around her—her own clothes were tattered almost to rags, torn apart by thorns and sharp rock, half the shivers wracking her from cold, half from hunger and exhaustion.

I pulled my scrip from my back, broke a nutrient tablet into my drinking cup, mixed it with water, and set it to warm on a hot rock at the edge of the fire-hole to make a thin, warm broth. Her hands shook too badly to hold the cup, so I fed it to her slow, drop by drop. The warm food calmed her, the sobs fading to quiet hitching breaths. She still would not look up at me, until suddenly she pulled free, slipped to her knees, and kissed my hand, starting to cry again. I was so abashed I stood stupidly for a moment, before I knelt in front of her, freed my hand, and kissed both of hers soft, to show her I was no hero to be revered—only a man who had crossed the Night Land to find the other half of his soul. She sobbed harder, overwhelmed that I had traveled so far through every horror to reach her.

I lifted her gentle into my arms, no careless caress, just holding her safe, stroking her tangled hair, murmuring both her names, Naani and Mirdath, whispering half-remembered soft things to her that she would recall later when she was strong. She went quiet, resting against my chest, content to be held for the first time in weeks, her sobs slowing to nothing. She nestled closer, looking up at me with soft, wet eyes, and began to speak, pausing often for lack of strength, the story pouring out of her in broken, trembling bursts.

The Lesser Redoubt’s Earth Current had failed long before, she told me, weakening everyone who lived there until a handful of terrified, half-mad citizens had pried open the Great Outer Door and fled out into the dark. The horrors of the Night Land had poured in right behind them, slaughtering hundreds, and chasing the rest out into the wilderness. Her father, the Master Monstruwacan, had been torn apart by a brutish shaggy man-monster right before her eyes. She had escaped with three other young maids, but night creatures had stolen two of them while they slept in the brush, the third had run screaming in the dark and vanished, and she had been entirely alone ever since.

She had survived on moss growing on the rocks, bitter strange berries, and the lukewarm sulphurous water from the hot springs, which made her sick often, but saved her from far deadlier poisons in some of the plants. She had hidden from the monsters that hunted her, listening for their approach with her sharpened spiritual sense, fleeing in the dark until her clothes had been torn to rags. That very night, she had been so weak she could barely move, a circle of squat, silent spider-crabs huddled at the edge of the firelight, waiting for her to die. She had thought death was moments away, until the Master-Word thundered through her mind. She had believed it was me calling from the distant Mighty Pyramid, never daring to hope I had crossed all that leagues of horror to find her.

Glancing around while she spoke, I saw the spider-crabs were still there, huddled in a patient ring, watching us. Hot anger surged up in me. I strode to the edge of the firelight, kicking the stupid, skittish creatures left and right, stamping my foot until a dozen of them scuttled away, and the entire rest of the pack fled into the dark, too cowardly to attack even though they outnumbered me a hundred to one.

I went back to my maid, sat beside her, and wrapped my arms around her again, as the soft red glow of the fire-hole wrapped around us, and for the first time since I left the Mighty Pyramid, I felt peace.
