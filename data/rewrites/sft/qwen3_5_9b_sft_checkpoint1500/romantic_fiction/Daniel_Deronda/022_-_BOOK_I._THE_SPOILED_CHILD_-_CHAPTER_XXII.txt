Gwendolen's note, arriving "just in time, yet too soon," slipped into Klesmer's hands precisely as he was departing Quetcham. Driven by a desire to honor her plea for kindness, despite the inconvenience to himself, he spent the night in Wanchester. There were compelling reasons why he could not remain at Quetcham.

That magnificent mansion, built with extravagant expense, had become stiflingly hot for him, largely due to its owners' reaction to an uprising against the established order. Like certain politicians who are astounded by rebellions they failed to foresee, the Arrowpoints had been blindsided by a shift in the local dynamic that ordinary observers could plainly see brewing beneath their noses.

As usual, the house was filled with guests. Among them stood a political man of good family, Mr. Bult, whom Miss Arrowpoint anticipated as a new contender for her hand. A man who confidently expected a peerage and believed, on sound public principles, that a title required a substantial fortune to support properly. Heiresses vary greatly in temperament and appearance—too pale or too flushed, tall and ungainly or short and square, volatile or dull—but it is generally assumed that they will view themselves merely as appendages to their wealth and marry where suitors dictate. However, Nature, accommodating herself poorly to our favored practices by creating "only children," occasionally endows the misplaced daughter with a clear intellect and a formidable will. The Arrowpoints had grown increasingly anxious regarding Catherine's refusal to accept the standard view of her social duty, specifically the requirement to marry a needy nobleman or a commoner climbing the ladder toward nobility. They were uneasy about her persistence in declining suitable offers. Yet, regarding the possibility that Catherine might be in love with Klesmer, they were remarkably unconcerned—a very common form of parental blindness. Generally, mortals are astonishingly surprised by effects toward which they have labored diligently, while remaining oblivious to the absence of effects toward which they have done nothing but hope. Parents marvel at their sons' ignorance despite investing vast sums in education; spouses are shocked by the loss of affection they neglected to nurture; and everyone is prone to wondering why neighbors do not admire them. Through this mechanism, truth appears highly improbable. Truth is distinct from the lazy, wish-born combinations we habitually construct. Finally, the Arrowpoints' moment of astonishment had arrived.

When a passion exists between an heiress and a proud, independent spirit, reaching an understanding proves difficult. However, these obstacles are often surmounted unless the proud man secures himself through a constant alibi. Brief meetings following studied absences are powerful agents of revelation; yet far more potent is frequent companionship marked by full sympathy in taste and admirable qualities on both sides. This dynamic is legendary in history, possessing a charm today no less profound than it held in the days of Abelard.

Nevertheless, such comparisons had not crossed the minds of the Arrowpoints when they first invited Klesmer to Quetcham. Having a first-rate musician in one's household is a privilege of wealth; Catherine's musical talents demanded every advantage, and she particularly sought to utilize her quiet country hours for rigorous study. Klesmer was not yet considered a Liszt, a figure universally adored by ladies across Europe (save for Lapland). Even with that reputation, it was not assumed he would propose to an heiress. Furthermore, no musician of honor would do so. Still less could it be conceived that Catherine would provide him the slightest pretext for such audacity. The large check Mr. Arrowpoint intended to draw in Klesmer's name appeared to render him as safe an inmate as a footman. Where marriage is deemed inconceivable, a girl's sentiments are considered secure.

Klesmer was a man of eminent honor, yet marriages seldom commence with formal proposals. Moreover, Catherine's limits of the conceivable did not perfectly align with her mother's. Outsiders might have perceived Klesmer's position as perilous if Miss Arrowpoint had been an acknowledged beauty, failing to recognize that the most powerful beauty is that which reveals itself only after sympathy has been established. There is a charm of eye and lip accompanying every phrase that certifies delicate perception or fine judgment, every unostentatious word or smile indicating a heart awake to others. No sweep of garment or turn of figure is more satisfying than that which restores confidence, signaling the presence of someone on whom no intention will be lost. What dignity of meaning accumulates in frowns and laughter never observed in the wrong place? What suffused adorableness resides in a human frame housing a mind capable of flashing comprehension and hands skilled in fine execution? The more obvious beauty, though sometimes adorable, begins as an apology for folly and eventually becomes tiresome through repetition. Therefore, Klesmer's passionate attachment to Miss Arrowpoint, despite his susceptibility to conventional beauty, was no paradox compared to any other triumph of manifold sympathy over monotonous attraction. We object less to being accused of enslaving excess in our passions than to our deficiencies in wider compassion; yet if the truth were known, our reputed intensity is often the dullness of not knowing what else to do with ourselves. Tannhäuser, one suspects, was a knight of ill-furnished imagination, scarcely possessing broader discourse than a heavy Guardsman; Merlin had surely seen his best days, merely repeating himself, when he fell into that hopeless captivity; and we know that Ulysses felt such manifest ennui under similar circumstances that Calypso herself furthered his departure. Though there is a report that he later left Penelope, given her habitual absorption in woolen work and the likelihood that Telemachus derived his mean, pettifogging disposition from her, no inference can be drawn from this dubious scandal regarding the relationship between companionship and constancy.

Klesmer proved as versatile and fascinating as a young Ulysses upon sufficient acquaintance—a soul whom Nature seemed to create generously first, then endowed with music as a dominant power utilizing all abundant rest. Finding expression not only in the highest finish of execution but in a fervor of creative work and theoretic belief that pierced the future of a life with the delight of congruous devotion, he mirrored Mendelssohn. His foibles of arrogance and vanity did not exceed those found in the best English families, and Catherine possessed no corresponding restlessness to clash with his. Despite her native kindliness, she was perhaps too coolly firm and self-sustained. Yet she belonged to those satisfactory creatures whose interaction holds the charm of discovery; whose integrity of faculty and expression generates a desire to know what they will say on all subjects or how they will perform whatever they undertake. Thus, they raise not only a continual expectation but a continual sense of fulfillment—the systole and diastole of blissful companionship. In such cases, the outward presentation easily becomes what the image is to the worshipper. It was not long before the two became aware that each was interesting to the other, though the extent of their mutual interest remained a matter of doubt. Klesmer did not conceive that Miss Arrowpoint viewed him as a potential lover, nor was she accustomed to thinking of herself as likely to stir more than a friendly regard, nor to fear the expression of more from any man who was not enamored of her fortune. Each was content to endure some unshared sense of denial for the sake of loving the other's society a little too well. Under these conditions, no need had arisen to restrict Klesmer's visits for the past year, whether in country or town. He knew very well that if Miss Arrowpoint had been poor, he would have made ardent love to her instead of sending a storm through a piano or folding his arms to pour out a hyperbolic tirade about something as impersonal as the North Pole. Nor was she less aware that if it had been possible for Klesmer to wish for her hand, she would have found overmastering reasons for granting it. Here lay the safety of full cups, which are as secure from overflow as the half-empty ones, provided no disturbance occurs. Naturally, silent feelings had not remained static, any more than the stealthy dial-hand. During this current visit to Quetcham, Klesmer had begun to think he would not return, while Catherine grew more sensitive to his frequent brusqueness, which she resented as a needless effort to assert his footing of superiority in every sense save the conventional.

Meanwhile entered the expectant peer, Mr. Bult, an esteemed party man who, though neutral in private life, held strong opinions concerning the districts of the Niger, the Brazils, and affairs in the South Seas. He spoke decisively of Parliamentary and itinerant speeches, possessing the general solidity and suffusive pinkness of a healthy Briton residing on the central table-land of life. Catherine, aware of the tacit understanding that he was an undeniable husband for an heiress, had nothing to say against him save that he was thoroughly tiresome. Mr. Bult was amiably confident, harboring no idea that his insensibility to counterpoint could ever be reckoned against him. He hardly regarded Klesmer in the light of a serious human being entitled to a vote, and he did not mind Miss Arrowpoint's addiction to music any more than her probable expenses in antique lace. Consequently, he was somewhat amazed by an after-dinner outburst from Klesmer regarding the lack of idealism in English politics.
