A shadow fell, not on Rome, but on a single man moving through Rome as if the streets had become sea and he a boat whose star had been blown out.

Margaret had been the fixed point by which Gerard steered—north in his blood, warmth in his mouth, bread in his hand. When that point went dark, he did not merely grieve; he raged. What stung him was the cruel arithmetic of it: all his watchings and wanderings, his temperance and his prayers, his nights slept hard and his days spent brave—wasted, and worse than wasted. If he had stayed at home, if he had been lazy and selfish like other men, then—so the thought bit and bit again—he might have saved her life.

Two poisons, despair and exasperation, worked together in him like sour wine in a young stomach. His mind, once so orderly, took to breaking its own rules. He rushed at pleasure as men rush at arrows when they are tired of dodging them. In those days, pleasure was not a neat pastime sold in bright wrappers; it was vice with its sleeves rolled up.

He learned the taverns by their sounds before he learned them by their names: the slap of dice on boards, the clack of cups, the hoarse chorus of men trying to drown their own thoughts in other men’s voices. Wine came first—thick, hot, and rough, with a smell like crushed skins and stale barrels; it warmed his throat as if it could warm the place behind his ribs where all had gone cold. Then women, whose laughter rang like coins in a purse, and gaming, whose little rattle of bone and wood could make a night feel busy enough to pass. Whatever could buy him an hour’s excitement, a moment’s oblivion—he paid for it lavishly.

He had money for this, money he had saved with a lover’s thrift, coin meant for Margaret’s comfort. Now he spent it as if scattering it might cover the grave. And because he never did anything by halves, he did not become vicious by halves. He was soon the foremost of companions he had once shunned, loud among the loud, reckless among the reckless. His heart spoiled with his habits; he grew short with gentle words. When his old landlady—homely, sharp-eyed, motherly—dared to press counsel on him, he sulked at her as if advice were an insult. At last he moved away to another quarter, to put distance between himself and both remonstrance and memory.

He could still draw; the old discipline of the hand remained a while after the discipline of the soul had begun to loosen. But even that art betrayed him. His hand shook at times; the fine patience needed for writing and delicate work would not come when his blood was full of fumes and late nights. So he left the pen more and more, and took to likenesses in colours—broad, indulgent strokes, easier to excuse than a line too thin. Yet he neglected even that whenever the idle rakes came to knock and call and laugh him into the street.

He dived in foul waters seeking that sorry oyster-shell, Oblivion.

His humble women friends gossiped of him in low kitchens and over shared loaves; the old landlady, grieving, told Teresa he was “going to the bad,” and begged her to find where he had gone. Teresa, who knew the taste of misfortune and the smell of courage in poor rooms, carried the tale home to her husband, Lodovico, and made him promise to look about.

“Shouldst remember his face, Lodovico mio?” she asked, her voice quiet, as if quietness could keep trouble from hearing.

“Teresa,” said he, “a man in my way of life never forgets a face, least of all a benefactor’s. But thou knowest I seldom go abroad by daylight.”

Teresa sighed, and the sigh seemed to carry all the city’s night-air with it. “And how long is it to be so, Lodovico?”

“Till some cavalier passes his sword through me. They will not let a poor fellow like me take to any honest trade.”

While these poor honest folk fretted in their narrow rooms, chance brought into Gerard’s new lodging one who bore prosperity worse than adversity: Pietro Vanucci. He had been thrust out, late and noisy, by his own landlady; and meeting Gerard in the street, greeted him with a warmth that belonged less to affection than to appetite for company. Soon after, Pietro installed himself in the same house.

He brought with him a lad named Andrea, who ground his colours, ran his errands, learned his tricks, and served him as model besides—being a youth of rare beauty and sharp as a needle. Pietro remembered old times enough to profess friendship; Gerard, in whom warmth seemed burned away, received the protestations coldly, as one receives rain when already drenched.

A fine acquaintance it was. Pietro was not merely a libertine; he had the sourness of one who despises what he pursues. Half misanthrope, open infidel, he made mock of piety as lightly as he made mock of fidelity, and yet clung to companions as if he could not bear to hear his own thoughts. So they ran together like mismatched dogs, coupled by accident and habit rather than kinship.

One day—when Gerard’s health had begun to show the underground labour he was doing to himself, and the bloom of his face had faded as fruit fades left too long in heat, and much of his money had found its way into other men’s pockets—Vanucci devised a revel that pleased the town’s idle mouths.

They were to mount the Tiber in a galley drawn from the bank by buffaloes—great blunt-brained brutes newly brought to Rome, a novelty Lorenzo de’ Medici had set in fashion elsewhere. Vanucci, beggar on horseback, must needs be drawn by monsters to prove himself a conqueror of novelty. Each libertine was to bring a lady, and she must be handsome or he should pay a fine; and he whose companion was adjudged the loveliest should be crowned with laurel and proclaimed a public benefactor. So did they read virtue: “Vir bonus est quis?”—a good man is he that brings the prettiest sin.

A splendid galley was hired; twelve buffaloes were yoked; the riverbank filled by degrees with silks, laughter, and the clink of flasks. The air smelled of wet rope, animal breath, and perfume fighting sweat. Men called to one another, women’s voices rose and fell like birds in a marketplace, and the Tiber made its old thick sound, patient as always beneath their froth.

But Gerard did not come.

They waited; impatience began to nip the merriment. Vanucci excused him with a sneer made smooth.

“I heard him say he had forgotten to provide himself with a fardingale. Comrades, the good lad is hunting for a beauty fit to take rank among these peerless dames. Consider the difficulty, ladies, and be patient!”

At last Gerard was seen in the distance, leading a female by the hand.

“She is long enough,” said one woman, measuring from afar with a jealous eye.

“Gemini, what steps she takes,” said another.

“Oh, it is wise to hurry into good company,” Vanucci answered, laughing, and the party made ready with their little stings.

But when the pair came up, satire choked in their throats.

Gerard’s companion was a peerless beauty—a figure that made the rest of the boat-load seem, in one glance, like candles beside a sun. Tall without being too tall; straight as a thrown spear yet supple as a young panther. Her face was a clean oval; her forehead white; her cheeks a rich olive with living blood mantling below. Her eyes—glorious, dark, and large—were fringed with long thick lashes that looked made to sweep hearts into captivity by the half-dozen. Her lips were red and saucy; her teeth ivory. She carried herself with a bold ease, as if all the world were a stage and she had paid for it.

The women drooped visibly, their mouths tightening as if they had bitten something bitter. The men broke into shouts, waving caps; one enthusiast even went down on his knees on the gunwale and hailed her as divine. His own chere amie answered by pulling his hair, and the goddess by giving him a small kick; the man sprawled, and she stepped lightly over his body as if he were part of the decking, taking her place at the prow without deigning him a glance.

Pietro Vanucci sat in collapse, staring at her, gaping like a fish on a board. Then his look narrowed, as if some private delight had begun to coil.

The drover spoke to the buffaloes; ropes tightened; the beasts leaned, and the galley began to move up-stream with a slow, stubborn drag. The animals’ breathing came in deep bellows; their hooves on the damp bank made a dull, sodden percussion. The river murmured thickly under them, and the city’s noises—vendors’ cries, distant bells, the slap of oars from other boats—wove themselves into a coarse tapestry.

“What think ye of this new beef, mesdames?” one gallant cried.

“We ne’er saw monsters so viley ill-favoured,” answered a lady, wrinkling her nose. “With their nasty horns that make one afeard, and their foul nostrils cast up into the air. Holes be they; not nostrils.”

“Signorina,” returned another with mock gravity, “the beeves are a present from Florence the beautiful. Would ye look a gift beef i’ the nose?”

“They are so dull,” objected a lively one. “I went up Tiber twice as fast last time with but five mules and an ass.”

“Nay, that is soon mended,” cried a gallant. He sprang ashore, drew his sword, and, despite the drovers’ protests, went down the line goading the dozen buffaloes. Steel flashed; the beasts snorted and whisked their tails—but they went no faster. The boat-load laughed loud and long, the laughter bouncing off the water and coming back oddly hollow.

At last he prodded a patriarch bull. The bull turned instantly on the blade, took the spark on its long horns, and with a furious jerk of its prodigious neck tossed him into the air. The man described a brave parabola and fell sitting into the yellow Tiber, still unconsciously waving his glittering sword.

The laughing ladies screamed and wrung their hands—all but Gerard’s fair companion. She uttered something very like an oath, seized the helm with quick competence, and steered the boat out so the swimmer would not be sucked under or struck. The gallant came up sputtering, gripped the gunwale, and was hauled in dripping, his doublet pierced, his skin spared by fortune’s mockery.

He glared round, confused and peevish. “I understand not that,” said he. “How ’tis that one moment a gentleman is out yonder a-pricking of African beef, and the next moment—”

“Disporting in his native stream,” said Gerard’s companion coolly, as if she had been born with a river in her mouth.

“Tell him not, a soul of ye,” cried Vanucci. “Let him find out ’s own riddle.”

“Confound ye all,” growled the spark. “I might puzzle my brains till doomsday, I should ne’er find it out. Also, where is my sword?”

“Ask Tiber!” returned the beauty. “Your best way, signor, will be to do it over again; and, in a word, keep pricking of Afric’s beef, till your mind receives light. So shall you comprehend the matter by degrees, as lawyers mount heaven, and buffaloes Tiber.”

A chevalier declared that the speaker transcended the sons of Adam in wit as much as she did the daughters of Eve in beauty. Compliments rained; she wore them as one wears sunlight—without gratitude, without shame.

Pietro’s conduct was singular. His staring had ceased; he sat coiled, snake-like, mouth muffled, bright eyes fixed on her, twinkling and scintillating with a secret glee. Yet she returned him no special notice. Her eyes swept him now and then, calm as a broom sweeping a floor, scarcely distinguishing him from benches and rope.

Soon the party’s harmony cracked. The women, mortified by the men’s attention to this newcomer, began to pick her to pieces, first under breath, then with small audible barbs. The lovely creature showed that if she was rich in beauty she was poor in feminine tact. Instead of revenging herself through the men as women of the world do, she let herself overhear and answered openly.

“There is not one of you that wears Nature’s colours,” said she, loud enough to sting. “Look here.” And she pointed rudely in one lady’s face. “This is the beauty that is to be bought in every shop. Here is cerussa; here is stibium; and here purpurissum. Oh, I know the articles—bless you—I use them every day; but not on my face, no thank you.”

Pietro’s eyes twinkled nearly out of sight.

“Why, your lips are coloured, and the very veins in your forehead—not a charm but would come off with a wet towel. And look at your great coarse black hair like a horse’s tail, drugged and stained to look like tow. And then your bodies are as false as your heads and your cheeks, and your hearts I trow. Look at your padded bosoms, and your wooden-heeled chopines to raise your little stunted limbs up and deceive the world. Skinny dwarfs ye are, cushioned and stultified into great fat giants. Aha, mesdames—well is it said of you, grande—di legni: grosse—di straci: rosse—di bettito: bianche—di calcina.”

The retort flew back at once.

“Avaunt, vulgar toad, telling the men everything. Your coarse ruddy cheeks are your own, and your little handful of African hair. But who is padded more? Why, you are shaped like a fire-shovel.”

“Ye lie, malapert!”

“Oh, the well-educated young person! Where didst pick her up, Ser Gerard?”

Gerard, roused by the shrill rise of voices from a black reverie, lifted his head as if waking from fever. “Hold thy peace,” said he. “Be not so insolent. The grave shall close over thy beauty as it hath over fairer than thee.”

“They began,” she said petulantly, but her eyes softened toward him.

“Then be thou the first to leave off.”

“At thy request, my friend.” She leaned and whispered him, and for a moment her tone lost its bravado and became almost tender. “It was only to make you laugh; you are distraught, you are sad. Judge whether I care for the quips of these little fools, or the admiration of these big fools. Dear Signor Gerard—would I were what they take me for! You should not be so sad.”

Gerard sighed, deep as if it came from under ruins, and shook his head. Yet the young voice, earnest beneath its impudence, moved him; he lifted his hand and caressed her jet-black locks, not like a lover but as one strokes an affectionate creature that follows him faithfully.

At that moment another galley, drifting slowly down-stream, got entangled for an instant in their ropes. The Tiber turned there; their boat had shot out; the stranger slid between them and the bank. Under a canopy sat a lady of great beauty, with gallants and dependents behind her like a carved retinue.

Gerard looked up. It was the Princess Claelia.

Colour rose to his face—shame, memory, the ghost of a nobler self. He withdrew his hand from the black hair as if it had burned him.

His companion, however, broke into admiration, careless as a bird. “Aha! ladies,” she cried, “here is a rival an ye will. Those cheeks were coloured by Nature—like mine.”

“Peace, child! peace!” Gerard said quickly, low and urgent. “Make not too free with the great.”

“Why, she heard me not,” the girl returned, delighted. “Oh, Ser Gerard, what a lovely creature!”

Two of the women had been whispering together, casting glances at Gerard’s companion. Now one addressed her with a false sweetness.

“Signorina, do you love almonds?” she asked, showing a lapful.

“Yes, I love them—when I can get them,” the other answered, eyeing the nuts with desire she did not care to hide. “But yours is not the hand to give me any, I trow.”

“You are much mistook,” said the second. “Here, catch!” And she flung a double handful into the beauty’s lap.

In the instant, the receiver snapped her knees together by an instinct too swift to counterfeit.

“Aha! you are caught, my lad,” cried the almond-thrower, triumphant. “’Tis a man; or a boy. A woman still parteth her knees to catch the nuts the surer in her apron; but a man closeth his for fear they should fall between his hose. Confess now—didst never wear fardingale ere to-day?”

“Give me another handful, sweetheart, and I’ll tell thee,” the beauty replied, calmly cracking an almond as if the world’s discovery were a small thing.

“There!” cried one. “I said he was too handsome for a woman.”

“Ser Gerard, they have found me out,” observed the epicaene, with perfect composure.

The libertines protested it was impossible, and stared as if their eyes could force the truth back into womanhood again. Pietro Vanucci, however, burst in, and reminded them of an old dispute in which they had laughed him and Gerard to scorn for saying that men were as beautiful as women to a true artist’s eye.

“Where are ye now?” he cried, delighting in their discomfiture. “This is my boy Andrea. And you have all been down on your knees to him. Ha! ha! But oh, my little ladies, when he lectured you and flung your stibium, your cerussa, and your purpurissum back in your faces—’tis then I was like to burst; he that grinds my colours. Ha! ha! he! he! he! ho!”

“The little impostor! Duck him!” cried a man, stung at being fooled.

“What for, signors?” Andrea cried in dismay, and his rich carnation faded beneath the sudden fear.

But the women, swift in their changes, flocked around him, shielding him with hands and laughter, as if tenderness were a cloak they could throw on and off at will.

“The dear child! How well his pretty little saucy ways become him.”

“Oh, what eyes and teeth!”

“And what eyebrows and hair!”

“And what lashes!”

“And what a nose!”

“The sweetest little ear in the world!”

“And what health! Touch but his cheek with a pin the blood should squirt.”

“Who would be so cruel?”

“He is a rosebud washed in dew.”

So they revenged themselves for their beaux’ earlier admiration by lavishing all their fondness on Andrea now.

But one man sat among these butterflies and was no longer of them. The passing glimpse of Princess Claelia had torn open Gerard’s wound, as a snag tears a garment already thin. Three months ago scarce, he had refused her—a love illicit and mad, yet refined in its very danger. He had been able then to stand in loyalty, as a man stands in clean air and thinks he can breathe forever. Now—what was he?

He had blushed to have Claelia see him with such company. Yet it was his daily company. He leaned over the gunwale, silent, watching the river’s thick glide and hearing in it an invitation. The buffaloes’ hooves thudded on the bank; the rope creaked; the galley’s boards groaned; above it all rose the shrill human laughter—noise meant to be music, music meant to be forgetfulness. None of it soothed. It only made the stillness inside him more absolute.

From that hour another phase of misery began and grew upon him. Some fools try to drown care in drink; the fumes vanish, and care remains, heavier, because the body has been poisoned and the spirit lowered. Gerard’s whole conduct had been of a piece with those maniacs. To survive his blow he needed all his forces—his virtue, his health, his labour, the calm sleep that is labour’s satellite; above all, his piety. Yet he flung these balms away and trusted to moral intoxication.

Its brief fumes fled. The bereaved heart lay still heavy as lead within him, and now the dark vulture Remorse sat upon it and tore.

Broken health; means wasted; innocence fled; Margaret parted from him by another gulf wider than the grave—by his own defilement. The hot fit of despair passed. The cold fit came, and it numbed instead of burning.

Then he spurned his gay companions and all the world. He wandered alone. He drank wine alone, not for company now, but as a drug—trying to stupefy himself, to paralyze for a moment the dark foes that preyed upon his soul. He walked among the temples of old Rome, where echoes live longer than men; he lay stony-eyed among the ruins, worse wrecked than they, and the city’s sounds—cart-wheels, distant singing, the hammer on an anvil, the far-off chant from a church—came to him as if from another life, not his. Even the river’s voice seemed to speak a language he could not translate into comfort.

Last came the climax to which solitude—gloomy, fascinating foe of diseased minds—pushes the hopeless. He wandered at night by dark streams and eyed them, and eyed them, with repugnance slowly lessening. There glided peace—perhaps annihilation. What else was left him?

Such spells have been broken by kind words, by loving and cheerful voices. The humblest friend the afflicted possesses may speak, or look, or smile, and stand like a sunbeam between him and that worst madness. Where was Teresa? Where the hearty old landlady? Their homely swiftness would have seen; their plain affection would have saved. But they knew not where he lodged now, nor where he walked, nor how near he slid to the edge.

Was there no mortal eye upon the poor wretch and the dark road he went?

Yes—one eye watched him; marked his steps abroad; tracked him home.

And that eye was the eye of an enemy. An enemy to the death.
