The culprits were condemned to stand pinioned in the marketplace for two hours. In the cyclical rhythms of the town’s acoustic ecology, this public spectacle was meant to be a quiet interlude of civic scrutiny, where any person might recognize them as architects of other crimes and depose to that effect. The timeline of justice here was circular, echoing the ancient, agrarian seasons of sowing and reaping. 

Yet, they stood the whole period undisturbed, a silence less remarkable considering they were night birds, vampires who preyed in the dark on weary travelers and strangers. Their violence belonged to the midnight soundscape, entirely detached from the sunlit hum of the market.

But just as the institutional machinery groaned into motion to take them down, a fearful scream shattered the harmonic frequencies of the square. It was a sound of profound, untranslatable agony, a mother’s subjective experience erupting into the public domain. A woman pointed at one of the men, her eyes almost starting from their sockets, but before she could articulate her grief into the dominant dialect of the law, she fainted away.

Men and women crowded round her, their murmurs forming a dissonant chorus of curiosity and aid. When her senses began to resurface, the crowd’s conjectures bubbled like a rapid boil.

“’Twas at him she pointed.”

“Nay, ’twas at this one.”

“Nay, nay,” said another, his voice cutting through the auditory clutter, “’twas at yon hangdog with the hair hung round his neck.”

All further semantic debate was severed. The poor creature, her mind clearing to the grim reality of her physical senses, flew at the landlord like a lioness. “My child! Man! man! Give me back my child.” She seized the glossy golden hair the officers had hung round his neck—a visceral, tactile artifact of a severed lineage—and tore it from him, covering it with kisses. Sank suddenly down, she shrieked and sobbed over the poor hair. Her grief was a profound acoustic resonance that vibrated through the crowd, mutating their idle chatter into one savage, low-frequency growl of collective vengeance. 

His life would have ended there, hastily violently, for in those days all carried death at their girdles. But Denys drew his sword directly, a sharp, metallic hiss that disrupted the mob's feral hum. Shouting “A moi, camarades!” he kept the mob at bay. “Who lays a finger on him dies.” Other archers backed him, their armor clinking in a rigid, martial rhythm. 

“What sort of vengeance is this?” Denys appealed to those who shouted for blood, asserting the formalized lexicon of state justice over their chaotic dialect of murder. “Would you be so mad as rob the wheel, and give the vermin an easy death?”

The mob, subdued more by the cold, unyielding steel of the archers than by Denys’s rhetoric, growled at intervals with flashing eyes. The municipal officers prudently enveloped the accused in a guard and carried them back to gaol.

Seeing the prisoners safely lodged, Denys made for “The White Hart,” his mind drifting toward the anticipated olfactory landscapes of roasted meats and spiced broths. On the way, the rhythmic, domestic clatter of two girls working at a first-floor window caught his ear. He saluted them. They smiled, their complexions high and flushed. He entered into conversation, inviting them to a repast at “The White Hart.” They objected; he acquiesced; they consented. In this charming, superficial society, he forgot entirely about poor Gerard.

Gerard, meantime, was swept away toward the gaol. Having somewhat recovered his presence of mind, he demanded to know by whose authority his physical trajectory was so violently altered.

“By the vice-baillie’s,” said the constable.

“The vice-baillie? Alas! what have I, a stranger, done to offend a vice-baillie? For this charge of sorcery must be a blind. No sorcerer am I; but a poor true lad far from his home.”

This vague, emotional shift disgusted the officer, who operated only within the rigid confines of institutional vocabulary. “Show him the capias, Jacques.”

Jacques held out the writ in both hands about a yard and a half from Gerard's eye, while the large constable pinned him. Both were on tenterhooks lest the prisoner grab the document, to which they attached a superstitious, almost mythic importance. But Gerard only craned his neck to read it. To his infinite surprise, he found the vice-bailiff who had signed the writ was the friendly alderman. He assured his captors there was some error in the translation of events, and demanded to be taken before the alderman.

“What say you to that, Jacques?”

“Impossible. We have no orders to take him before his worship. Read the writ!”

“Nay, but good kind fellows, what harm can it be? I will give you each an ecu.”

“Jacques, what say you to that?”

“Humph! I say we have no orders *not* to take him to his worship. Read the writ!”

“Then say we take him to prison round by his worship.”

It was agreed. They pocketed the silver, demanding a pantomime of gratitude that stuck in Gerard’s throat like stale barley bread. Feigning was not his forte; he was a creature of authentic expression.

He entered the alderman’s presence with his heart in his mouth, begging with faltering voice to know what he had done since leaving that very room with Manon and Denys.

“Nought that I know of,” said the alderman. But upon seeing the writ, his municipal memory was jogged. “I get old, and my memory faileth me: a discussing of the girl I quite forgot your own offence. You are he I committed for sorcery. Stay! ere you go to gaol, you shall hear what your accuser says: run and fetch him, you.”

While the accuser was sought, the alderman told Gerard the charge: setting a dead body a-burning with diabolical fire that flamed but did not consume. “And if 'tis true, young man, I'm sorry for thee, for thou wilt assuredly burn with fire of good pine logs in the market-place of Neufchasteau.”

“Oh, sir, for pity's sake let me have speech with his reverence the curé.”

The alderman advised against it, noting the Church was harder upon sorcerers than the corporation. 

“But, sir, I am innocent,” Gerard pleaded.

“Oh, if you think you are innocent—officer, go with him to the curé; but see he 'scape you not.”

They found the curé in his doublet, engaged in the deeply grounding, domestic rhythm of repairing a wheelbarrow. The smell of split wood and crushed grass surrounded him. Gerard appealed piteously, explaining that for merely using a little phosphorus in self-defense against cut-throats, they meant to hang him.

Fortunately, the curé already possessed the contextual history of Gerard's mythic light. This shrewd personage had hold of the stick at the right end, while the corporation held it by the ferule. Looking exceedingly grave, the curé said, “I must question you privately on this untoward business.” 

He took Gerard into a private room, bidding the quaking constable stand guard outside. Instantly isolated in the acoustic sanctity of the chamber, the curé unlocked his countenance. “Show me the trick on't,” he said, all curiosity.

“I cannot, sir, unless the room be darkened.”

The curé shuttered the windows, plunging them into a sensory deprivation that amplified the subtle, earthy scent of the chemical. “Now, then.”

“But on what shall I put it?” Gerard asked. “Here is no dead face.”

Groping in the dark, the curé offered his patron saint's wooden image. Gerard hesitated at the profanation, but the divine dismissed his fears. Urged on, Gerard fired the eyes and nostrils of the image with the pale, mythic glow, lighting up the hair in patches until the face shone like a moonlit mushroom in a damp forest.

“By'r Lady,” shouted the curé, marveling at this seamless integration of natural chemistry into the guise of the supernatural. “'Tis strange, and small my wonder that they took you for a magician. Now come thy ways with me!”

Donning his grey gown, the curé led Gerard back to the alderman. By the alderman's side stood the accuser, Mangis, a quacksalver draped in red hose, red shoes, and a black gown—a garish commercialization of mystic arts.

The curé immediately deployed the dominant lexicon of the Church to dismantle the quacksalver. “So, Mangis, at thy work again, babbling away honest men's lives! This Mangis, who would be a sorcerer, but is only a quacksalver, accuses of magic a true lad, who did but use in self-defence a secret of chemistry well-known to me and all churchmen.”

When the alderman objected that Gerard was no churchman, the curé smoothly redefined Gerard's identity. “He is more churchman than layman, being convent bred, and in the lesser orders. Therefore, sorcerer, withdraw thy plaint!”

Mangis stoutly refused, attempting to legitimize his rural dialect of folk magic. He claimed to be a "white sorcerer" who battled Satan, advising peasants on planting rye by the moon and detecting black magicians, bragging of the tribe he had burned at Dol.

But the curé wielded the ultimate institutional power, threatening to erase Mangis entirely. He recounted how the parliament of Dijon had sifted the ashes of Dol, finding the victims innocent citizens, and ordered expiatory masses. “Now 'tis our custom in this land... not to blame our credulous ears, but the false tongue that gulled them. Therefore bethink thee that, at a word from me to my lord bishop, thou wilt smell burning pine nearer than e'er knave smelt it and lived...”

Faced with the crushing weight of the Church's semantic authority, the white sorcerer’s fiery complexion drained. “I respect Holy Church and her will... I do withdraw the plaint.”

“Then withdraw thyself,” said the vice-bailiff.

The curé courteously informed the alderman that the chemical substance was now safely returned to the Church. Freed, Gerard caught the priest’s hand in the street, offering his meager purse out of profound gratitude. 

The curé brusquely refused the money. “Fie! fie! art pauper et exul. Come thou rather each day at noon and take thy diet with me; for my heart warms to thee.” With that, he departed abruptly, his hands itching behind his back, yet conquering his cupidity for the deeper communion of shared sustenance.

Gerard hurried to "The White Hart" to relieve Denys’s anxiety, only to find his comrade seated at ease, throwing dice with the two young ladies of unreserved manners. The clatter of the dice was a hollow, commercial sound that grated against Gerard’s senses.

Hurt, Gerard colored up. “N'oubliez point la Jeanneton!” 

Denys rattled the dice gaily, entirely unbothered. When the girls mocked Gerard as witless, Gerard loftily retorted, “Wise men have not folly enough to please them, nor madness enough to desire to please them. But 'tis to my comrade I speak, not to you, you brazen toads.”

“Preach away, comrade,” Denys laughed. “Fling a byword or two at our heads.”

Summoning the cultural memory embedded in his native dialect, Gerard spat, “The friendship that melts to nought at sight of a farthingale.” He opened the door to leave, delivering one final thunderbolt of antique wisdom: 

*“Qui hante femmes et dez* 
*Il mourra en pauvretez.”*

Slamming the door on their superficial laughter, Gerard sought solace in the fundamental truths of food. He went to the bar for a morsel of bread and a cup of wine. The sensory anticipation of fermentation and terroir was a grounding ritual for him. But when the landlord handed him a pint bottle, Gerard’s meticulous eye caught a discrepancy. The glass was thin where it tapered, but unnaturally thick at the bottom—a deceptive commercial vessel designed to cheat the senses. 

He pointed this out, and the landlord superciliously disclaimed responsibility. Only when Gerard threatened to take the pint to the vice-bailiff did the landlord’s tone shift, offering to adjust the price. 

“So be it,” Gerard said, tasting the vintage. He grimaced; the profile was flat, robbed of its robust, earthy integrity. “But much I admire that you, the host of so fair an inn, should practise thus. The wine, too, smacketh strongly of spring water.”

This critique of his culinary honesty unlocked a shining river of grievance within the landlord. He launched into a passionate, sweeping defense of the traditional hospitality arts, now besieged by industrial economics and structural decay. 

“Young sir, we cut no travellers' throats at this inn. Whatever masterful robbery is done here, is done upon the poor host. How then could he live at all if he dealt not a little crooked?” The landlord mourned the erosion of the great domestic feasts. “In my father's day we had the weddings; but now the great gentry let their houses and their plates, their mugs and their spoons to any honest couple that want to wed. They come not to us. In my father's day the great gentry sold wine by the barrel only; but now they have leave to cry it, and sell it by the galopin, in the very market-place. How can we vie with them?”

When Gerard suggested better provision, the landlord lamented the legislative noise that stifled his craft. “The law will not let us. We are forbidden to go into the market for the first hour. So, when we arrive, the burghers have bought all but the refuse. As for the butchers, they will not kill for us unless we bribe them.”

His voice rising above the ambient clatter of the inn, the landlord detailed the exact, excruciating costs of his ingredients, a meticulous food historian cornered by commercialization. “A swarm of excisemen, and all with a wolf in their stomachs and a sponge in their gullets. Why, lad, not a pound of bread I sell but cost me three good copper deniers; every sack of charcoal two sous, and gone in a day. A pair of partridges five sous. Heard one ever the like? Five sous for two little beasts all bone and feather? A pair of pigeons, thirty deniers. 'Tis ruination! We may charge nought for mustard, pepper, salt, or firewood. Think you we get them for nought? Salt five sous the stone, pepper four sous the pound, mustard twenty deniers the pint; and raw meat, dwindleth it on the spit with no cost to me but loss of weight? Why, what think you I pay my cook? A HUNDRED SOUS A YEAR AS I AM A LIVING SINNER.”

He detailed the bitter paradox of his trade, forced by law to open his doors at all hours, his craft subjected to the unyielding fines of the bailiff and the hypocritical lash of the curé. 

This tragic oral history of a dying culinary era was suddenly arrested in mid-career. The door burst open, admitting a jagged, chaotic wave of noise. It was Denys, cursing and swearing that he was robbed. 

“Did those hussies pass this way? They have ta'en my purse and fifteen golden pieces: raise the hue and cry! These inns are all guet-apens.”

Gerard, dragging Denys to the provost-marshal, found no solace. The dignitary merely shook his head; the casual thieves had slipped seamlessly back into the town's background noise. 

Furious, Denys marched off. “I knew what use a bourgeois would be to me at a pinch.”

“They are clear of the town ere this,” Gerard said softly.

“Speak no more on't if you prize my friendship,” Denys snapped. Then, his choler fading into a startling confession, he added, “I have five pieces with the bailiff, and ten I left with Manon, luckily... I'll tell thee all. I did offer marriage to that Manon.”

Gerard was dumfounded. “What? You offered her what?”

“Marriage. Is that such a mighty strange thing to offer a wench?”

“'Tis a strange thing to offer to a strange girl in passing.”

“Nay, I am not such a sot as you opine,” Denys insisted, revealing a sudden, precise appreciation for quality beneath the surface of the world. “I saw the corn in all that chaff. I knew I could not get her by fair means, so I was fain to try foul. 'Mademoiselle,' said I, 'marriage is not one of my habits, but struck by your qualities I make an exception; deign to bestow this hand on me.'”

“And she bestowed it on thine ear.”
