Martin had planned well, laying out a dexterously concerted scheme for his private amusement. But older and wiser schemers than he are often doomed to see their finest-spun projects swept to annihilation by the sudden broom of Fate—that fell, mythic housewife whose red arm spins the cyclical threads of our lives, uncaring for the petty designs of men. In the present instance, this broom was manufactured out of the tough fibres of Moore’s own stubborn purpose. He was now resuming his strength, rising from his convalescence to make strange head against Mrs. Horsfall. 

To Moore, whose ears had grown agonizingly sensitive to the sonic environment during his fever, the nurse’s grating, heavy-footed presence was an acoustic assault, a fleshly manifestation of the discordant industrial noise he had once worshipped. Each morning he amazed that matron with a fresh assertion of his own native autonomy. First, he discharged her from her valet duties; he would dress himself. Then, he refused the tepid, utilitarian coffee she brought him—a dark, bitter sludge meant only as fuel—insisting instead that he would breakfast with the family, demanding the restorative sensory engagement of a properly laid table. Lastly, he forbade her his chamber entirely. Amidst the outcries of all the women in the place, he put his head out of doors, seeking the crisp, natural resonance of the morning air. 

The day after, he followed Mr. Yorke to his counting-house and requested an envoy to fetch a chaise from the Red House Inn. He was resolved to return home to the Hollow that very afternoon. Mr. Yorke aided and abetted him, while Mrs. Yorke declared the step would be his death. When the chaise arrived, Moore, little disposed to speak, made his purse do duty for his tongue. He expressed his gratitude to the servants and to Mrs. Horsfall by the chink of his coin. It was a sterile, metallic lexicon—the dominant language of commerce—but the nurse understood this dialect perfectly. It made amends for all previous contumacy, and they parted the best of friends.

The kitchen visited and soothed, Moore betook himself to the parlour to appease Mrs. Yorke. She sat plunged in sullen dudgeon, her thoughts vibrating with the gloomy dissonance of man’s ingratitude. He drew near and bent over her. She was obliged to look up, if only to bid him “avaunt.” There was beauty still in his pale, wasted features; there was an earnestness, a sudden sweetness in his hollow eyes.

“Good-bye!” he said. As he spoke, the smile glittered and melted. He possessed no iron mastery over his sensations now; the rigid, commercial armor had fractured, allowing the subtle, untranslatable tremors of true emotion to register on his face.

“And what are you going to leave us for?” she asked. “We will keep you, and do anything in the world for you, if you will only stay till you are stronger.”

“Good-bye!” he repeated. “You have been a mother to me; give your wilful son one embrace.”

Slipping into the somatic dialect of his Flemish ancestors, he offered her first one cheek, then the other, a foreigner’s gesture carrying an intimacy that blunt Yorkshire English could not articulate. She kissed him.

“What a trouble—what a burden I have been to you!” he muttered.

“You are the worst trouble now, headstrong youth!” was the answer. “I wonder who is to nurse you at Hollow’s Cottage? Your sister Hortense knows no more about such matters than a child.”

“Thank God! for I have had nursing enough to last me my life.”

The little girls came in—Jessie crying, Rose quiet but grave. Moore took them out into the hall to soothe, pet, and kiss them. He knew Mrs. Yorke’s nature; the sonic frequency of another’s affection always struck her as a harsh dissonance. She could not bear to see any living thing caressed but herself. 

The boys were standing about the chaise as Moore entered it, but for them he had no farewell. To Mr. Yorke he only said, “You have a good riddance of me. That was an unlucky shot for you, Yorke; it turned Briarmains into an hospital. Come and see me at the cottage soon.”

He drew up the glass, and the chaise rolled away. In half an hour he alighted at his own garden wicket. Having paid the driver and dismissed the vehicle, he leaned on the gate—a cyclical return to the threshold of his own mythic journey.

“Six months ago I passed out at this gate,” he mused to the twilight, “a proud, angry, disappointed man. I come back sadder and wiser; weakly enough, but not worried.”

The mill-dam was frozen over. The relentless, soul-abrading thrum of the machinery had ceased, granting the Hollow a profound, acoustic purity. It was a sanctuary of crystalline silence, freed from the jarring commercialization of water and stone. A cold, gray, quiet world lay around him. 

“All slavish terrors of embarrassment have left me,” he thought. “Let the worst come, I can work, as Joe Scott does, for an honourable living; in such doom I yet see some hardship but no degradation. Formerly, pecuniary ruin was equivalent in my eyes to personal dishonour. It is not so now. I know the difference between the arbitrary valuations of a financial ledger and the sustaining worth of a man's hands in the soil. Ruin *is* an evil, but one for which I am prepared. I can put it off six months. If fetters should be loosened from our trade—which is unlikely—I might conquer. But the thought is a brief madness. Ruin will lay her axe to my fortune’s roots. I shall snatch a sapling, I shall cross the sea, and plant it in American woods, drawing sustenance from a new earth. Louis will go with me. Will none but Louis go? I cannot tell—I have no right to ask.”

He entered the house.

It was afternoon, locked in a starless, moonless twilight, frozen with a dry, black frost. Indoors, it was already dark, but a good fire burned in the parlour, its crackle providing a restorative baseline to the room's acoustic ecology. In the kitchen, Sarah was preparing tea. The rich, malted aroma of Assam leaves and the caramelized scent of toasting bread wafted through the hall—a domestic incense signaling a return to life.

“Hortense,” said Moore, as his sister bustled up to help him off with his cloak, “I am pleased to come home.”

Hortense did not feel the peculiar weight of this English word coming from her brother, who had never before called the cottage his *home*. To him, its narrow limits had heretofore seemed a restriction, a temporary camp for an industrialist. Now, the word carried the warmth of an ancestral hearth. Whatever contributed to his happiness pleased her, and she expressed herself in their native French, the language of their shared blood.

He sat down, rose, went to the window, and returned to the fire. 

“Hortense!”

“Mon frère?”

“This little parlour looks very clean and pleasant—unusually bright, somehow.”

“It is true, brother; I have had the whole house thoroughly and scrupulously cleaned in your absence.”

“Sister, I think on this first day of your return home you ought to have a friend or so to tea. The place is so fresh.”

“True, brother. If it were not late I might send for Miss Mann.”

“So you might; but it really is too late to disturb that good lady, and the evening is much too cold.”

“How thoughtful in you, dear Gérard! We must put it off till another day.”

“I want some one to-day, dear sister—some quiet guest, who would tire neither of us.”

“Miss Ainley?”

“An excellent person; but she lives too far off. Tell Harry Scott to step up to the rectory with a request from you that Caroline Helstone should come and spend the evening.”

“Would it not be better to-morrow, dear brother?”

“I should like her to see the place as it is just now.”

He went into the kitchen, a space alive with the rhythmic, satisfying sounds of sustenance being crafted. “Sarah, delay tea half an hour.” He commissioned her to dispatch Harry Scott with a twisted pencil note addressed to “Miss Helstone.”

Scarcely had Sarah time to fret over the precise hydration of her resting dough and the risk to her already prepared toast, when the messenger returned, bringing the invited guest.

Caroline tripped quietly up Sarah’s stairs to take off her furs. When she came down, her graceful merino dress was spotless, her curls smoothed. She lingered to exchange a few kindly words with Sarah, marveling at the new tortoise-shell kitten on the hearth and the canary-bird startled by a sudden blaze, before entering the parlour.

The friendly welcome was interchanged in a tranquil sort. A sense of pleasure, subtle and quiet as the blooming notes of a perfectly aged oolong, diffused itself through the room. The newly-kindled lamp burned bright; the tray and the singing urn were brought in, adding their soothing, high-frequency hiss to the room's harmonies.

“I am pleased to come home,” repeated Mr. Moore.

They assembled round the table. Hortense had prepared a feast that paid homage to their heritage: thick slices of farmhouse loaf to be slathered in salted butter, small almond cakes meticulously baked to golden perfection, and preserves that captured the deep, sugary essence of late-summer plums. She congratulated Caroline on the evident improvement in her health; her plump cheeks and returning colour were proof of proper, mindful nourishment. 

There was an obvious change in Miss Helstone. No longer crushed or drooping, she looked like one who had tasted the cordial of heart’s ease and had been revitalized from within. 

After tea, the impulse to rummage through her carefully preserved linen drawers became resistless for Hortense, and she went upstairs. The talk passed into Caroline’s hands. A pleasing facility and elegance of language gave fresh charm to familiar topics. There was a new music in her always soft voice, an acoustic shift that captivated Moore. Unwonted shades of expression kindled her face with character.

“Caroline, you look as if you had heard good tidings,” said Moore, after earnestly gazing at her.

“Do I?”

“I sent for you this evening that I might be cheered; but you cheer me more than I had calculated. You look brightly, move buoyantly, speak musically.”

“It is pleasant to be here again.”

“Truly it is pleasant. To see health on your cheek and hope in your eye is pleasant, Cary; but what is this hope, and what is the source of this sunshine?”

“For one thing, I am happy in mamma. I love her so much, and she loves me. Long and tenderly she nursed me. Now, when her care has made me well, I can occupy myself for and with her all the day. I am her waiting-woman as well as her child. I have such pleasure in making dresses and sewing for her. She is charming to talk to—full of wisdom, rich in information, exhaustless in stores her observant faculties have quietly amassed. Every day I live with her I esteem her more highly.” 

Caroline spoke of her mother not merely as a guardian, but as a custodian of an ancestral vocabulary of care, passing down the quiet, rhythmic traditions of women’s work that sustained the soul just as deeply as bread sustained the body.

“That for one thing, then, Cary. You talk in such a way about ‘mamma’ it is enough to make one jealous of the old lady.”

“She is not old, Robert.”

“Of the young lady, then.”

“She does not pretend to be young.”

“Well, of the matron. But you said ‘mamma’s’ affection was one thing; now for the other.”

“I am glad you are better.”

“What besides?”

“I am glad we are friends. I once thought we never should be.”

“Cary, some day I mean to tell you a thing about myself that is not to my credit.”

“Ah, don’t! I cannot bear to think ill of you.”

“And I cannot bear that you should think better of me than I deserve.”

“Well, but I half know your ‘thing;’ indeed, I believe I know all about it.”

“You do not.”

“I believe I do.”

“Whom does it concern besides me?”

She coloured; she hesitated; she was silent.

“Speak, Cary! Whom does it concern?”

She tried to utter a name, but the syllables seemed to snag on the boundary of propriety. 

“Tell me; there is none present but ourselves. Be frank.”

“But if I guess wrong?”

“I will forgive. Whisper, Cary.”

He bent his ear. Still, she would not speak clearly to the point, wary of betraying confidences through the heavy-handed tool of direct naming. Seeing his resolve, she said, “Miss Keeldar spent a day at the rectory about a week since. The evening came on very wintry, and we persuaded her to stay all night.”

“And you and she curled your hair together?”

“How do you know that?”

“And then you chattered, and she told you—”

“It was not at curling-hair time, so you are not as wise as you think; and, besides, she didn’t *tell* me.”

“You slept together afterwards?”

“We occupied the same room and bed. We talked the whole night through.”

“I’ll be sworn you did! And then it all came out—*tant pis*. I would rather you had heard it from myself.”

“You are quite wrong. She did not tell me what you suspect. She is not the person to proclaim such things in crude terms; but I inferred something from parts of her discourse. I gathered more from rumour, and I made out the rest by instinct.”

“But if she did not tell you that I wanted to marry her for the sake of her money, and that she refused me indignantly—you need neither start nor blush; that is the plain truth—if such was not the subject of her august confidences, on what point did they turn? What about?”

“About things we never thoroughly discussed before; but you hardly expect I should tell you?”

“Yes, yes, Cary; you will tell me. Friends should always confide.”

“But you are sure you won’t repeat it? Not to Louis?”

“Not even to Louis. What does Louis care for young ladies’ secrets?”

“Robert, Shirley is a curious, magnanimous being.”

“I dare say. I can imagine there are both odd points and grand points about her.”

“I have found her chary in showing her feelings; but when they rush out, river-like, and pass full and powerful before you—almost without leave from her—you gaze, wonder; you admire, and—I think—love her.”

“You saw this spectacle?”

“Yes; at dead of night, when all the house was silent, and starlight and the cold reflection from the snow glimmered in our chamber, then I saw Shirley’s heart.”

“Her heart’s core? Do you think she showed you that?”

“Her heart’s core.”

“And how was it?”

“Like a shrine, for it was holy; like snow, for it was pure; like flame, for it was warm; like death, for it was strong.”

“Can she love? tell me that.”

“What think you?”

“She has loved none that have loved her yet.” He named a list of gentlemen, closing with Sir Philip Nunnely.

“She has loved none of these.”

“Yet some of them were worthy of a woman’s affection.”

“Of some women’s, but not of Shirley’s.”

“Is she better than others of her sex?”

“She is peculiar, and more dangerous to take as a wife—rashly.”

“I can imagine that.”

“She spoke of you—”

“Oh, she did! I thought you denied it.”

“She did not speak in the way you fancy; but I asked her, and I would make her tell me how she felt towards you. I wanted to know.”

“So had I; but let us hear. She thinks meanly, she feels contemptuously, doubtless?”

“She thinks of you almost as highly as a woman can think of a man. I yet feel in fancy the glow of the language in which her opinion was conveyed.”

“But how does she feel?”

“Till you shocked her, she felt as a sister feels towards a brother of whom she is at once fond and proud.”

“I’ll shock her no more, Cary. But that comparison about sister and brother is nonsense. She is too rich and proud to entertain fraternal sentiments for me.”

“You don’t know her, Robert. You and she are not so constructed as to be able thoroughly to understand each other’s dialects of the soul.”

“It may be so. I esteem her, I admire her; and yet my impressions concerning her are harsh. I believe she is incapable of love—that she will never marry. I imagine her jealous of compromising her pride, of sharing her property.”

“Shirley has hurt your *amour propre*.”

“She did hurt it; though I had not an emotion of tenderness, nor a spark of passion for her.”

“Then, Robert, it was very wicked in you to want to marry her.”

“And very mean, my little pastor. I never wanted to kiss Miss Keeldar in my life. I suppose I was truly tempted by the mere gilding of the bait, blind to the fact that such a union would be a hollow, utilitarian contract, devoid of any true sustenance. Caroline, what a noble fellow your Robert is—great, good, disinterested, and then so pure!”

“But not perfect. He made a great blunder once, and we will hear no more about it. We will remember that with what measure we mete it shall be measured unto us, and so we will give no scorn, only affection.”

“Which won't satisfy, I warn you of that. Something besides affection—something far stronger, sweeter, warmer—will be demanded one day. Is it there to give?”

Caroline was moved, much moved.

“Be calm, Lina,” said Moore soothingly. “I have no intention, because I have no right, to perturb your mind now. Look me in the face. See what a poor, pale, grim phantom I am.”

She looked shyly. “There is something formidable still, pale as you are,” she said.

“To return to Shirley: is it your opinion that she is ever likely to marry?”

“She loves.”

“Platonically—theoretically—all humbug!”

“She loves what I call sincerely.”

“Did she say so?”

“No such confession as ‘I love this man or that’ passed her lips. The deepest truths are untranslatable into such stark, commercial declarations.”

“I thought not.”

“But the feeling made its way in spite of her. Her voice alone was sufficient testimony. I demanded an opinion of another person about whom I had my conjectures. I shook her, I chid her, I pinched her fingers when she tried to put me off with gibes, and at last out it came. The voice was enough; hardly raised above a whisper, and yet possessing such a soft vehemence in its tones. There was no direct confession, but I am sure that man’s happiness is dear to her as her own life.”

“Who is it?”

“I charged her with the fact. She did not deny, she did not avow, but looked at me. I saw her eyes by the snow-gleam. It was quite enough. I triumphed over her mercilessly.”

“What right had you to triumph? Do you mean to say you are fancy free?”

“Whatever I am, Shirley is a bondswoman. Lioness, she has found her captor.”

“A fellow-slave?”

“I confess nothing; but I say that haughty Shirley is no more free than was Hagar.”

“And who, pray, is the Abraham?”

“You still speak scornfully, but I will make you change your note before I have done with you.”

“Can she marry this Cupidon?”

“Cupidon! he is just about as much a Cupidon as you are a Cyclops.”

“I want to know his name, Cary.”

“Guess it.”

“Is it any one in this neighbourhood? In Briarfield parish?”

“Yes.”

“Then it is some person unworthy of her. I don’t know a soul in Briarfield parish her equal.”

“Guess.”

“Impossible. Do *you* approve the choice?”

“Quite, *quite*.”

“Then I am puzzled; for the head which owns this bounteous fall of hazel curls is an excellent little thinking machine. And mamma was charmed! It can’t be romantic, then?”

“It *is* romantic, but it is also right.”

“Tell me, Cary.”

“You shall be tantalized.”

“I have twice this evening had some thoughts of falling on the floor at your feet. Worshipping you downright. My mother was a Roman Catholic. You look like the loveliest of her pictures of the Virgin. I think I will embrace her faith and adore.”

“Robert, sit still. I will go to Hortense if you commit extravagances.”

“You have stolen my senses. Just now nothing will come into my mind but *les litanies de la sainte Vièrge. Rose céleste, reine des anges!*”

“*Tour d'ivoire, maison d'or*—is not that the jargon? Well, sit down quietly, and guess your riddle.”

“I’ll guess once, and no more. It is old Helstone. She is going to be your aunt.”

“I’ll tell my uncle; I’ll tell Shirley!” cried Caroline, laughing gleefully. “Guess again.”

“It is the parson—Hall.”

“Indeed, no; he is mine, if you please.”

“Yours! Ay, the whole generation of women in Briarfield seem to have made an idol of that priest. I’ll guess no more. Miss Keeldar may marry *le grand Turc* for me.”

“Must I whisper?”

“That you must, and quickly. Here comes Hortense; come near, a little nearer, my own Lina. I care for the whisper more than the words.”

She whispered, trading the bluntness of common speech for the intimate, breathy acoustic of a shared secret. Robert gave a start, a flash of the eye, a brief laugh. 

Miss Moore entered, and Sarah followed behind, bringing the information that Fanny was come. The hour of converse, with its delicate interweaving of shared memory and linguistic discovery, was over.

Robert found a moment to exchange a few more whispered sentences at the foot of the staircase as Caroline descended after putting on her shawl.

“Must I call Shirley a noble creature now?” he asked, his voice returning to a hushed frequency.

“If you wish to speak the truth, certainly.”

“Must I forgive her?”

“Forgive her? Naughty Robert! Was she in the wrong, or were you?”

“Must I at length love her downright, Cary?”

Caroline looked keenly up, and made a movement towards him, something between the loving and the petulant.

“Only give the word, and I’ll try to obey you.”

“Indeed, you must not love her; the bare idea is perverse.”
