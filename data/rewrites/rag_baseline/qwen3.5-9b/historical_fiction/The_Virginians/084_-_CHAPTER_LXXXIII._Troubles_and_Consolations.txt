The silence of the nursery was broken only by the rhythmic ticking of the clock and the distant, muffled roar of London traffic, a sound that seemed to press against the glass, demanding entry. I sat in the shadows, watching the fire die down to embers, contemplating the absurdity of my own trajectory. How could a boy who once skipped lessons with Harry, a boy whose tutor had resigned in despair, possibly ascend to the dizzying height of becoming a Governor? The irony tasted bitter, like the moldy jelly my aunt had once sent, yet it was the only currency I possessed.

My mother avoided the subject entirely. When we reunited, she spoke of "that terrible time," of "dreadful years," her voice tightening around the memory like a noose. She referred to Mr. Esmond Warrington only as "your friend in England" or "your wealthy Lambeth friend," never by name, and she refused to touch a single drop of his beer. Yet, at Warrington Manor, we brewed our own, and Mr. Foker, a man of shrewd business and surprising kindness, shipped barrels of his ale to Ipswich annually. His son, a fashionable sprig married to an earl's daughter, was a contrast to his father, who remained a brewer, a man of the earth. It was this connection, this accidental intersection of worlds, that delivered the guineas I needed.

But the money was secondary. It was the office itself, the Governorship I had seized, that offered a tangible anchor in a drifting sea. Mr. Foker's brewery stood near Pedlar's Acre, a site later altered by the relentless march of development. The surgeon who attended my wife, a Bavarian named Voelker, had introduced me to the brewer. Through shared pipes of Virginia tobacco and my ability to sing "Prinz Eugen the noble Ritter"—a tune inherited from my grandfather's campaigns—the barrier of class dissolved. Voelker's family embraced us; his daughter favored our infant, a child who, though now described by observers as ugly, had once been a beautiful thing. Even his son, Master Foker, maligned at Westminster for his father's trade, became my ward. I accepted the responsibility, the payment sufficient to sustain the illusion of dignity.

Mr. Foker listened to my accounts with a mixture of amusement and genuine affection. He mocked the trivial aid of my relatives—a coverlid, a jar of jelly, Lady Warrington's disdain—but his eyes filled with tears when I recounted the loss of little Miles's moidore. "I vow," he declared, "they shall have as much beer as they can drink." He extended his network to Lady Maria, treating her with obsequious deference, and funded Hagan's journey to Dublin. When Hagan departed, Lady Maria stayed with Mr. Foker, her needs met from the brewer's overflowing purse.

Foker viewed my restraint regarding my brother Harry's funds as evidence of exalted virtue. Harry, spending money he believed to be his own, sent a remittance for his promotion, urging me to draw on his agents if necessary. He was unaware of the depth of my depletion, how my capital had evaporated into thin air. To touch his money would delay his ascent; to ask General Lambert, a man of modest means, for support would crush him further. These acts of forbearance, silent and unseen, were celebrated by Foker in private letters to Harry in America, where he lauded us as paragons of humanity. He even sought an audience with Madame de Bernstein, a woman I considered selfish and worldly, only to discover, through her subsequent respectful treatment of us, that he knew the Baron from his apprenticeship days in Munich. Perhaps our family should avoid such inquiries into our uncle's past.

Life, in these moments, transformed from a melancholy exile into something resembling comfort. The bear I led was docile, dancing to my piping without resistance. It was preferable to loitering in booksellers' anterooms, waiting for the whims of managers. We spent evenings with these benefactors, sharing music and readings of my play, *Pocahontas*, performed with a conviction that Mr. Foker claimed surpassed Hagan's own delivery.

Then came the silence surrounding Miles Warrington, Jr., and the rare mentions of my paternal relatives. Sir Miles thrived at court, a figure of obedience to whatever Minister held power, much like a servant at Nero's table. I recalled the day Mr. Foker mentioned hearing on the Exchange that they intended to make my uncle a "Beer." "A Beer?" I asked, bewildered. "Can't you understand the word, ven I say it?" the testy old gentleman retorted. "Vell, veil, a Lort!" Indeed, Sir Miles was the humble servant of the state, surviving the First Duke's reign with a strange resilience. I remembered seeing him and his wife emerging from court, Mrs. Claypool presented to the Queen. On my shoulder sat my little boy. From the gilt coach window, my uncle and aunt stared resolutely at me, blind to the connection, while the footmen looked blankly over their flowers. Had I worn the Fairy's cap and rendered myself invisible, my father's brother could not have passed me with less notice.

We seldom availed ourselves of Lady Castlewood's invitations to tea, unless accompanied by the formidable Van den Bosch. Though shrewd, he lacked conversational charm, save for his daughter, who matched his intellect in discussing markets, stocks, and agriculture. Lord Castlewood was rarely present, or missed by his wife, existing in a perpetual state of unease among the old father. The Countess explained the dynamic simply: her husband desired money, her father guarded the keys. Without the father's vigilance, the Lord would have melted the plate. We paid for everything—cooks, horses, tailors—while ensuring the Lord retained a guinea in his pocket to maintain the facade of nobility. It was a delicate dance, a performance of respectability that cost us dearly.

Lord Castlewood, in exchange for clearing his estates and debts, had become a slave to the little wife and her father. No wonder he fled his own fine house, seeking refuge in clubs or any society that did not demand the presence of his captors. Leading a bear was unpleasant enough; waiting in an anteroom for an audience was harder for a man of my name and pride. Would I have traded my poverty for Castlewood's ignominy? Could I prefer his miserable dependence to my own? I earned my wage, however meager, and I never flattered my patrons. In fact, I dealt with them as sulky, overbearing, and intolerable as possible.

Yet, there was another partner in fate, a woman who bore her poverty with a smiling sweetness that softened even the hardest hearts. Fortune, usually a savage ogre, melted before her uncomplaining innocence. Despite her lack of wealth, she commanded the respect of tradesmen and neighbors alike. Mrs. Foker remarked that she looked like the mistress of the carriage, reducing the driver to a mere maid. Landladies adored her; shopkeepers executed her commands with the eagerness reserved for a duchess. I often thought of the lady in *Comus*, moving serenely through the rout and rabble, untouched by the chaos.

The Fokers lent us their carriage frequently, allowing us to drive abroad or visit the few friends we retained. Once, we visited the Protestant Hero, where the hostess insisted on addressing my wife as "her ladyship" throughout the afternoon. We also called on Mr. Johnson, taking tea in the company of the ingenious Mr. Goldsmith, and the Doctor waited upon my wife at her coach. But our most frequent destination was Aunt Bernstein. I confess, I was not jealous when she expressed a profound fondness for Theo. This affection grew into a dependency; she wished to spend her days with us, viewing Theo's child and husband as plagues that kept her favorite from her side. She hated us, in the most amusing way, for withholding her beloved.

Mrs. Bernstein flooded us with notes, pouring out heartrending appeals that resembled love letters. Her servants patrolled our kitchen. If my wife failed to visit, the Baroness wrote severe scoldings. When the child fell ill—a trial of temperaments that Fate seemed determined to inflict—Madame Bernstein arrived three days in succession, declaring the baby perfectly healthy and accusing us of feigning sickness solely to vex her.

Even the reigning Countess of Castlewood maintained an affable demeanor with her old aunt, dismissing the Baroness's status with characteristic bluntness. "Scraping and bowing to that old woman!" she exclaimed. "She is no finer than any other lady... Take away her wig, her rouge, her teeth, and what remains but a shriveled old woman?" And she was right. Beauty inevitably decays, whether underground or on the tree. Here was age without reverence, grey hairs hidden beneath paint, a woman tottering on the world with a crutch. For eighty years she had walked this path, eating from the forbidden and permitted trees, accumulating beauty, pleasure, and flattery alongside secret rages and humiliations.

"You are not a beauty, my dear," she told my wife, "and may thank your stars that you are not." If she contradicted herself, perhaps we all did. "Every woman would rather be beautiful than be anything else," she continued, gesturing to a portrait by Kneller, a painting that failed to capture her eyes, hair, or complexion.
