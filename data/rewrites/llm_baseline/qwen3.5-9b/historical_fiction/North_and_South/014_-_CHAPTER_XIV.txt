"I was used to sleep at nights as sweetly as a child,—Now if the wind blew rough, it made me start, And think of my poor boy tossing about Upon the roaring seas. And then I seemed To feel that it was hard to take him from me For such a little fault." —SOUTHEY.

It was a profound relief to Margaret, in these latter days, to discover that her mother drew nearer to her with a tenderness and intimacy unknown since the days of her girlhood. Her mother embraced her as a confidante, filling the role Margaret had always secretly coveted and envied in Dixon. Margaret responded eagerly to every appeal for sympathy, however trivial the cause might seem to her own judgment—an elephant lifting a tiny pin at its foot only because commanded by its keeper. Unconsciously, Margaret was drawing close to a revelation she had long sought.

One evening, with Mr. Hale away, her mother began to speak of her brother Frederick, the very topic Margaret had craved to discuss but whose mention usually silenced her natural boldness. The stronger her desire to know, the more reticent she became.

"Oh, Margaret, the wind last night was dreadful! It howled down the chimney into our room. I could not sleep; I never can when the wind is so fierce. I developed a waking habit when poor Frederick was at sea, and now, even if I do not wake suddenly, I dream of him amidst some tempestuous sea, surrounded by towering walls of glass-green water, rising higher than his ship's masts, curling over her with that cruel, terrible white foam, like a gigantic crested serpent. It is an ancient dream, yet it returns on every windy night until I am grateful to wake, sitting upright and rigid in bed, seized by terror. Poor Frederick! He is on land now, so the wind can harm him no longer. Though I feared it might topple some of those tall chimneys."

"Where is Frederick now, Mamma?" Margaret asked gently. "Our letters are addressed to the care of Messrs. Barbour in Cadiz. I know that, but where is he personally?"

"I cannot recall the specific town, but he does not answer to the name Hale; you must remember that, Margaret. Observe the initials 'F.D.' stamped in every corner of the envelopes. He has assumed the surname Dickinson. I wished him to retain the name Beresford, to which he had a certain claim, but your father deemed it wiser otherwise. He might be recognized if he bore my name."

"Mamma," Margaret ventured, "I was at Aunt Shaw's when everything occurred, and I suppose I was too young to be told plainly about it. Yet, I should like to know now, if I may—if speaking of it causes you no excessive pain."

"Pain!" Mrs. Hale replied, her cheek flushing warmly. "Not pain, surely. Yet, it hurts to think I may never see my darling boy again. Or, perhaps, he acted rightly. People may say what they please, but I possess his own letters as proof, and I shall believe him, though he is my son, sooner than any earthly court-martial."

She motioned toward a small Japanese cabinet. "Go to my little cabinet, dear, and in the second drawer on the left, you will find a packet of letters."

Margaret obeyed. There lay the yellow, sea-stained missives, carrying the distinct, briny fragrance of ocean mail. Returning to her mother, she watched as the older woman untied the silken cord with trembling fingers. Examining the dates, Mrs. Hale handed the bundle to Margaret, offering hurried, anxious commentary on their contents before her daughter could fully grasp them.

"You see, Margaret, from the very beginning, he detested Captain Reid. He was a second lieutenant aboard the *Orion* when Frederick sailed for the first time. How well he looked in his midshipman's uniform, holding his dirk as if it were a penknife, slicing open newspapers! But this Mr. Reid, then merely a lieutenant, seemed to harbor a dislike for Frederick from the outset. Then—wait! These are letters written aboard the *Russell*. When he was assigned to her and discovered his old adversary, Captain Reid, in command, he intended to endure his tyranny with patience. Read this, Margaret. Where does he say—"

She paused abruptly. "'My father may rely upon me that I will bear with all proper patience everything that one officer and gentleman can take from another. But from my former knowledge of my present captain, I confess I look forward with apprehension to a long course of tyranny on board the *Russell*.'"

"He promises patience, and I am certain he kept it," Mrs. Hale murmured, her voice softening. "He was the sweetest-tempered boy, when not provoked, that could possibly exist. Is this the letter regarding Captain Reid's impatience with the men for failing to execute ship maneuvers as swiftly as the *Avenger*? He notes that the *Russell* had many new hands, whereas the *Avenger* had spent nearly three years on the station, keeping slavers at bay and working her crew until they scrambled the rigging like rats or monkeys."

Margaret read the faded, partially illegible letter slowly. It described Captain Reid's imperiousness over trifles, likely exaggerated by the writer while the memory was fresh and hot. A sailor aloft in the main-topsail rigging had been ordered to descend rapidly, threatened with the cat-o'-nine-tails if he lagged behind. The man furthest out on the spar, realizing he could not pass his companions yet terrified of the disgrace of flogging, threw himself desperately downward to grab a lower rope. He missed, fell senseless onto the deck, and died within hours. The crew's indignation was at a boiling point when young Hale penned this account.

"But we received this letter long after hearing of the mutiny," Mrs. Hale whispered, her voice thick with emotion. "Poor Fred! Writing it must have comforted him, even if he knew not how to send it. Soon after, the newspapers reported an atrocious mutiny on the *Russell*, claiming the rebels retained possession and fled to become pirates, leaving Captain Reid adrift in a boat with a handful of officers and men whose names were listed—they were rescued by a West Indian steamer. Oh, Margaret! How your father and I grew ill reading that list when Frederick Hale's name was missing. We suspected a mistake; poor Fred was such a fine fellow, perhaps only slightly too passionate. We hoped 'Carr,' appearing on the list, was a printer's error for 'Hale'; newspapers are so careless."

She recounted how, the following evening, Mr. Hale walked to Southampton to fetch the papers. Unable to stay at home, Mrs. Hale met him under the hedge near the Oldham beech tree. He arrived late, his limbs limp, his head bowed, walking with the heaviness of a man bearing a crushing burden.

"Margaret, I see him now," she said, her eyes glistening.

"Don't go on, Mamma. I understand it all," Margaret interjected, leaning affectionately against her mother's side and kissing her hand.

"No, you cannot, Margaret. No one can unless they witnessed it. I could scarcely raise myself to greet him; the world seemed to reel around me. When I reached him, he neither spoke nor showed surprise at finding me three miles from home. Instead, he slipped my arm into his, stroking my hand as if soothing me under a great blow. When I trembled so violently I could not utter a word, he gathered me in his arms, bent his head to mine, and began to shake and sob in a strange, muffled, groaning voice. So frightened was I that I stood utterly still, begging him to tell me what he had heard. Then, with a jerky movement—as if another hand guided it—he thrust a vile newspaper into my hands, labeling our Frederick a 'traitor of the blackest dye,' 'a base, ungrateful disgrace to his profession.' Oh! I cannot recount the foul epithets they employed."

Mrs. Hale's voice broke. "I took the paper immediately. I tore it into tiny fragments—I tore it—oh, I believe, Margaret, I tore it with my teeth. I did not cry. I could not. My cheeks burned like fire, and my eyes felt as though they were burning inside my skull. I saw your father looking grimly at me. I declared it a lie, and indeed it was. Months later, this letter arrived, revealing the provocation Frederick endured. He did not rebel for himself or his own grievances; he refused to remain silent against Captain Reid's cruelty, and thus things deteriorated from bad to worse. Most of the sailors stood by Frederick."

After a long silence, her voice weak and trembling, she added, "I think, Margaret, I am glad of it. I am prouder of Frederick standing up against injustice than if he had simply been a compliant officer."

"I am sure I am," Margaret affirmed firmly. "Loyalty and obedience to wisdom and justice are noble, but it is even nobler to defy arbitrary power, unjustly and cruelly wielded—not for oneself, but for others who are more helpless."

"For all that," Mrs. Hale sighed wistfully, "I wish I could see Frederick once more—just once. He was my first baby, Margaret."
