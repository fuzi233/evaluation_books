April was nearing its close when Romola returned home with Tessa and the children, carrying the weight of a city teetering on the brink of civil war. Her mind, already frayed by domestic anxieties, was now seized by the publication of Fra Girolamo's trial—a document drafted by sixteen Florentine citizens commissioned by the Signoria. Issued officially, it had ignited a firestorm of public suspicion and discontent so severe that the government rushed to recall the printing press. Yet, as often happens when truth is suppressed, copies leaked into the hands of eager readers, and a second, unauthorized edition circulated rapidly.

Romola, having abandoned hope of ever speaking directly with the friar, devoured the text repeatedly, desperate to pierce through the fog of partisan distortion. Among the devout followers of Savonarola, the friar's collapse under torture and his retraction of prophetic claims caused a consternation that eventually hardened into a specific theory: that every contradictory word attributed to the prophet was actually the fabrication of Ser Ceccone, the notorious notary tasked with compiling the interrogation. This narrative painted the event not as a judicial proceeding, but as a slow-formality murder orchestrated by the Old Palace. The Signoria, Romola reasoned, had predetermined the friar's doom to secure a political bargain with the Pope and the Duke of Milan, extinguishing a threat to both foreign tyrants and a corrupt clergy. The choice presented to the Republic was stark: surrender the friar alive to the Papacy in exchange for taxing rights, or assert their sovereignty by executing him themselves. Under such circumstances, how could anyone accept the authenticity of the confession? If the friar had denied his gift, it was only due to the agony of the rope and pulley, a torment that would inevitably drive a sensitive man to ravings. Furthermore, the report claimed he had confessed only once, under no coercion, while the reality suggested far more brutal treatment designed to break the hearts of those who loved him.

Those less ardent in their devotion acknowledged the probable falsification by the notary but insisted the core substance remained genuine. They argued indignantly that no crime warranted death, let alone burning. To impartial observers, the case rested solely on political inconvenience: the friar had become a liability to the Pope, a nuisance to rapacious Italian states eyeing Tuscany, and a thorn to ambitious citizens. No shadow of political crime existed; no stain marred his private conduct. Even his former secretary, a man of superior intellect among his peers, testified to an unimpeachable purity of life that had commanded veneration until the shock of the retraction. The Pope himself could levy charges only of disobedience and ignoring excommunication—a breach of discipline that, while indefensible logically, sparked a moral insurgency in the minds of serious men who blurred the line between the institution of the Church and its flawed agents. To Soderini and Piero Guicciardini, the severity of the crackdown revealed a grotesque hypocrisy: a system willing to bypass all law to hang a man whose public career had merely warped his strict veracity. They noted bitterly that if the friar had mixed his fraud with less inconvenient zeal, it would have served as excellent lubricant for the ecclesiastical and political machines. Yet, even these shrewd men admitted the necessity of the Pope's defensive measures; leaving a rebel capable of stirring a General Council to depose him would have been sheer ineptitude added to iniquity.

Florence had heard Savonarola declare he would not obey the devil, making their conflict a life-and-death struggle. However, it was less inevitable that Florence should serve as the Pope's executioner. Romola found herself trapped between two conflicting visions: the ardent faith of her youth and the cold discernment of worldly politics. Neither satisfied her. Her meditations on the printed document were haunted by painful observations gathered during years of discipleship, whispering that the retraction was not merely a spasmodic escape from pain, but something deeper. Conversely, her soul screamed for an explanation that preserved the purity of his life's striving. The recent memory of her own disillusionment, feeling betrayed by a man who had incarnated her highest motives, had triggered a reaction akin to faith born of despair. She told herself that the negative thoughts drying her soul could not reflect the truth of things; the spirit that had once breathed through the friar's words was real, not a hollow pretense. Any falsehood in him was a tragic fall, a gradual entanglement he struggled against, not a calculated scheme nurtured by success.

Scanning the text, Romola identified sentences stamped with the clumsiness of fabrication—the repetitive, emphatic self-accusations typical of low-level hypocrites. Yet, these glaring lies stood in sharp opposition to the overall tone of the document, reinforcing the belief that the remainder represented the actual words of the friar. Hardly anything was dishonorable except his claims to special inspiration. Regarding his actions for Florence, the Church, and the world, he remained consistent. Apart from the false claim to divine inspiration used to capture minds, he admitted no use of unworthy means. Even within this compromised text, Fra Girolamo appeared as a man seeking glory, but a glory derived from laboring for the moral welfare of humanity, striving to transform beliefs into tangible energies for daily life.

One memorable passage, though possibly altered, resonated with her: *"Everything that I have done,"* he stated, *"I have done with the design of being for ever famous in the present and in future ages; and that I might win credit in Florence; and that nothing of great import should be done without my sanction."* He envisioned establishing his position locally before expanding to Italy and beyond, rousing Christian princes to subdue the infidels. He did not seek the papacy primarily, believing that leading such a work required excellence of virtue, whereas a man without virtue could still occupy the throne. This blend of ambition and belief in supreme goodness was familiar to Romola, echoing the voice that had rung through the Duomo. Savonarola conceived great things and believed he was the vessel to execute them. Iniquity must fall; justice, purity, and love must triumph through his voice, his work, and potentially his blood. While moments of ecstasy might have dissolved his ego into the unspeakable divine, in the presence of men, pre-eminence seemed essential. Perhaps this confession, describing a conscious doubleness, reflected only the natural wavering of human judgment under extreme duress. A life so tumultuous as his offered endless possibilities for shifting self-assessment. Instead of kneeling crowds and a flourishing project, he faced hoots, spittle, curses, and the hard faces of judges. Then came the torture, triggering the irrepressible cry: *"It is true, what you would have me say: let me go: do not torture me again: yes, yes, I am guilty. O God! Thy stroke has reached me!"*

As Romola contemplated the anguish following this confession—whether conscience retracted or confirmed the words in solitary prison—it pressed upon her own heart, drawing slow, bitter tears. Vulgar Florentines glibly pronounced his demerits, unaware of the depth of sorrow known only to a soul that has loved perfection and beheld its own fall. She had not yet witnessed the evidence of his mental state post-interrogation. Days passed; reports of new examinations ceased, yielding no changes. Savonarola was left alone in his prison, granted pen and ink to write with his bruised right arm. What emerged was no legal defense or protest against the proceedings, but a continuous colloquy with divine purity, an outpouring of self-abasement, a single long cry for inward renovation. The old, vehement assertion—"Look at my work, for it is good, and those who set their faces against it are the children of the devil!"—had vanished.

In its place, the Voice of Sadness spoke: *"God placed thee in the midst of the people even as if thou hadst been one of the excellent. In this way thou hast taught others, and hast failed to learn thyself. Thou hast cured others: and thou thyself hast been still diseased. Thy heart was lifted up at the beauty of thy own deeds, and through this thou hast lost thy wisdom..."* Hope, arguing that divine love remained, offered no promise of future works, only the comfort that his bowed heart was itself a gift. There was no evidence that from imprisonment to his final hour, Savonarola viewed himself as a martyr. Martyrdom had been a passion dividing his dreams of future triumph from the joy of completed work. Now, replaced by both, came a resignation he named with no glorifying title. *But therefore he may the more fitly be called a martyr by his fellow-men to all time.* Power rose against him not because of his sins, but because of his greatness—not because he sought to deceive, but because he sought to elevate the world. Through that greatness, he endured a double agony: the reviling, the torture, the death-throes, and the crushing pain of sinking from a vision of glorious achievement into a deep shadow where he could only whisper, *"I count as nothing: darkness encompasses me: yet the light I saw was the true light."*
