The afternoon sun had begun to wane, casting long, grey shadows across the floor when Tito returned to his home. In the narrow chamber opposite the cabinet, Romola sat over her documents, her hand pausing mid-copy as the light failed. Her husband entered not with the casual ease of a spouse, but with a singular, heavy purpose. He did not remove his cap or mantle; instead, he leaned an elbow upon the cabinet and stood before her, a dark silhouette against the dimming room.

Romola felt the sudden, sharp recoil of her own conscience. Throughout the day, the news of the Friar's safety had settled in her mind, washing away the immediate sting of her public confrontation. She realized now how foolish her impetuousness had been. The duplicity inherent in Florentine politics, a truth even her godfather had whispered, could easily explain Tito's connection to Spini without implying any treason on his part. She had acted in anger, driven by a distrust that she now saw as a mistake born of isolation. For hours, she had rehearsed a confession, hoping to break the two-year silence that had locked them together like a banquet hall where death had once shattered the feast. She had fled, and she had returned, yet neither had ever truly acknowledged the past that lay between them.

She looked up at him, her gaze softened by self-reproach, but the subtle shift in his demeanor halted her words. A crisis had arrived, invisible until now, altering the landscape of their marriage. Beneath his usual blandness and beseeching manner, Tito's determination to master his wife had surfaced, hardening his features as if a hidden muscle were strangling a dangerous, feeble thing within him.

"Romola," he began, his voice a cool, liquid tone that sent a shiver through her, "it is time that we should understand each other."

"That is what I most desire, Tito," she replied faintly. Her pale face, stripped of its anger and filled only with the timidity of doubt, seemed to highlight the predominance of his dark strength.

"You took a step this morning," Tito continued, "which you must now perceive to be useless—a step that exposed you to remark and may involve me in serious practical difficulties."

"I acknowledge that I was too hasty," Romola said, her voice finding a firmer register. She hoped this admission of regret would soften his gaze, preparing the ground for the words she truly needed to speak.

"I wish you to understand once and for all," Tito said, his voice unchanging, "that such collisions are incompatible with our position as husband and wife. Reflect on the manner in which you were led to that step, lest it be repeated."

"That depends chiefly on you, Tito," Romola retorted, a spark of resentment igniting her cheeks. It was not what she had intended to say, for the path of mutual speech was narrow and obscured.

"You would say, I suppose," Tito answered, "that nothing in the future should excite your unreasonable suspicions. You were frank enough last night to declare you hold no belief in me. I am not surprised by the exaggerated conclusions you draw from slight premises, but I wish to point out the fruit of making such conclusions a basis for interfering in affairs of which you are ignorant. Are you paying attention?"

He waited for a response.

"Yes," Romola flushed, unable to suppress her resentment at his superior coldness.

"Well, then," Tito pressed, "it may not be long before another chance word or incident sets your imagination to devising crimes against me. You might rush to the Palazzo Vecchio to alarm the Signoria and set the city ablaze. Shall I tell you the result? Not merely the disgrace of your husband, to which you look forward with such courage, but the arrest and ruin of many chief men in Florence, including Messer Bernardo del Nero."

Tito had made his move, decisive and absolute. The flush drained from Romola's face, leaving her lips pale—a rare sight, for she was little given to fear. Tito perceived his success.

"You might flatter yourself," he went on, "that you are performing a heroic deed of deliverance. You might as well try to turn locks with fine words as apply such notions to the politics of Florence. The question is no longer whether you can believe in me, but whether, now that you have been warned, you will dare to rush, like a blind man with a torch, into intricate affairs you know nothing of."

Romola felt her mind gripped in a vice by Tito's intellect. The possibilities he outlined rose before her with terrible clarity.

"I am too rash," she admitted. "I will try not to be rash."

"Remember," Tito insisted, his tone unsparing, "that your act of distrust this morning might have had far more fatal effects than the sacrifice of your husband, a fate you have learned to contemplate without flinching."

"Tito, it is not so!" Romola burst forth, rising and moving closer to him with a desperate resolve to speak the truth. "It is false that I would willingly sacrifice you. It has been the greatest effort of my life to cling to you. I went away in my anger two years ago, and I returned because I was bound to you more than to anything on earth. But it is useless. You shut me out from your mind. You pretend to think of me as too unreasonable to share in your affairs. You will be open with me about nothing."

She looked like a good angel pleading with a sinner, bending her face toward him with dilated eyes and laying her hand upon his arm. Yet, Romola's touch and glance stirred no fiber of tenderness in her husband. The good-humoured, tolerant Tito, capable of little hatred or impatience, felt himself growing strangely hard toward this wife who had once been his strongest influence. With all his softness of disposition, he possessed a masculine effectiveness of intellect and purpose, an energy sharp as an edge, working its way without great momentum. Romola possessed her own energy, one that thwarted his, and no man, unless exceptionally weak, endures being thwarted by his wife. Marriage must be either a relation of sympathy or of conquest.

No emotion crossed Tito's face as he heard Romola speak of having left him. His lips merely hardened slightly as he smiled faintly.

"My Romola, when certain conditions are ascertained, we must make up our minds to them. No amount of wishing will fill the Arno, as your people say, nor turn a plum into an orange. I have not observed that prayers have much efficacy in that regard. You are constituted to have strong impressions inaccessible to reason; I cannot share those impressions, and you have withdrawn all trust from me in consequence. You have changed toward me; therefore, I have changed toward you. It is useless to look backward. We must simply adapt ourselves to altered conditions."

"Tito, it would not be useless for us to speak openly," Romola said, her voice trembling with the exasperation of striking against an insurmountable, lifeless resistance. "It was the sense of deception in you that changed me, and that has kept us apart. And it is not true that I changed first. You changed toward me the night you first wore that chain-armour. You had some secret from me—it was about that old man—and I saw him again yesterday. Tito," she continued in a tone of agonized entreaty, "if you would once tell me everything, let it be what it may—I would not mind pain—that there might be no wall between us! Is it not possible that we could begin a new life?"

For the first time, a flash of emotion crossed Tito's face. He stood perfectly still, the flash seeming to whiten him. He ignored Romola's appeal. After a moment's pause, he said quietly, "Your impetuosity about trifles, Romola, has a freezing influence that would cool the baths of Nero."

At these cutting words, Romola shrank back, drawing herself up into her usual self-sustained posture. Tito continued, "If by 'that old man' you mean the mad Jacopo di Nola who attempted my life and made a strange accusation against me, of which I told you nothing because it would have alarmed you to no purpose, he, poor wretch, has died in prison. I saw his name in the list of the dead."

"I know nothing about his accusation," Romola said. "But I know he is the man I saw with a rope around his neck in the Duomo—the man whose portrait Piero di Cosimo painted, grasping your arm as he saw him grasp it the day the French entered, the day you first wore the armour."

"And where is he now, pray?" Tito asked, still pale but governing himself.

"He was lying lifeless in the street from starvation," Romola replied. "I revived him with bread and wine. I brought him to our door, but he refused to come in. Then I gave him some money, and he went away without telling me anything. But he had found out that I was your wife. Who is he?"

"A man, half mad, half imbecile, who was once my father's servant in Greece, and who bears a rancorous hatred toward me because I dismissed him for theft. Now you have the whole mystery, and the further satisfaction of knowing that I am again in danger of assassination. The fact of my wearing the armour, about which you seem to have thought so much, must have led you to infer that I was in danger from this man. Was that the reason you chose to cultivate his acquaintance and invite him into the house?"

Romola fell silent. To speak now would be like rushing with a bare breast against a shield.

Tito moved from his leaning posture, slowly removing his cap and mantle, and pushing back his hair. He was collecting himself for final words. Romola stood upright, looking at him as one might regard an oncoming deadly force, to be met only by silent endurance.

"We need not refer to these matters again, Romola," he said, in precisely the same tone as at the beginning. "It is enough if you will remember that the next time your generous ardour leads you to interfere in political affairs, you are likely, not to save anyone from danger, but to be raising scaffolds and setting houses on fire. You are not yet a sufficiently ardent Piagnone to believe that Messer Bernardo del Nero is the prince of darkness, and Messer Francesco Valori the archangel Michael. I think I need demand no promise from you?"

"I have understood you too well, Tito."

"It is enough," he said, and left the room.

Romola turned with despair written across her face and sank into her seat. "O God, I have tried—I cannot help it. We shall always be divided." These words passed silently through her mind. "Unless," she said aloud, as if a sudden vision had startled her into speech—"unless misery should come and join us!"

Tito, too, carried a new thought in his mind after closing the door behind him. Alongside his project of leaving Florence as soon as his life there became a sufficient stepping-stone to a new existence, perhaps in Rome or Milan, there arose for the first time a desire to be free from Romola, to leave her behind. She had ceased to belong to the desirable furniture of his life; there was no possibility of an easy relation between them without genuineness on his part. Genuineness implied a confession of the past, and confession involved a change of purpose. But Tito had as little bent that way as a leopard has to lap milk when its teeth are grown. From all relations that were not easy and agreeable, Tito shrank; why should he cling to them?

Moreover, Romola had made his relations difficult with others besides herself. He had had a troublesome interview with Dolfo Spini, who had returned in a rage after an ineffectual soak in the rain and a long wait in ambush. That scene between Romola and himself at Nello's door, once reported to Spini's ear, might be a seed of something more unmanageable than mere suspicion. But now, at least, he believed he had mastered Romola through a terror that appealed to the strongest forces of her nature. He had alarmed her affection and conscience with the shadowy image of consequences; he had arrested her intellect by hanging before it the idea of a hopeless complexity in affairs that defied moral judgment.

Yet Tito was not at ease. The world was not yet quite cushioned with velvet, and even if it had been, he could not have abandoned himself to that softness with thorough enjoyment. For before he went out again that evening, he put on his coat of chain-armour.
