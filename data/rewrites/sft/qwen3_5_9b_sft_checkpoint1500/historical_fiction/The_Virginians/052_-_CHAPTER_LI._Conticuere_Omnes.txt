Across the threshold, if you care to follow, you shall find our young gentlemen at Lord Wrotham's estate—a residence graciously lent to the General and his household. There sits that familiar circle we met at Oakhurst and Tunbridge Wells. James Wolfe has pledged his attendance, yet currently attends to Miss Lowther with such devotion that a glimpse of her eye holds far more allure for him than the most exquisite dishes prepared by Lord Wrotham's chef or the promised desserts awaiting your arrival. You shall occupy the sixth seat; take Mr. Wolfe's place, for you may be certain he will not come. As for me, I shall stand near the sideboard, chronicling the conversation.

First, observe the radiant countenance of the ladies. Recall when Harry Warrington was seized by bailiffs; I intend to recount how Mrs. Lambert, upon hearing of her son's plight, flew to her husband. She implored him with desperate fervor: "Let Martin help!" She dismissed his recent rebelliousness, his anger over returned gifts, and their poverty, insisting that somehow Harry must be freed. "Did you not rescue Charles Watkins two years ago?" she asked, tears glistening. "He repaid every penny, and his wife prays for you. Has everything not prospered since? Could it not do the same for you? You are a Major-General, surely!" Her faith was absolute.

Martin Lambert, requiring little persuasion for kindness, yielded readily. Having resolved to seek James Wolfe and secure bail for Harry, he donned his hat, squeezed Theo's hand—though whether Theo divined his errand or if Mother Lambert had inadvertently revealed it remains unclear—and kissed little Hetty's flushed cheek before departing. Yet, the devoted mother followed him from the room.

Alone with him, the enthusiastic matron could no longer contain her emotions. She threw her arms around his neck, kissed him repeatedly, beseeching God's blessing, and sobbed openly upon his shoulder. This display of conjugal tenderness was interrupted by Old Mrs. Quiggett, the lord's housekeeper, who bustled in, presumably stunned by the scene.

"We've had a squabble and are making it up!" the gentleman declared, walking away. "Do not spread tales, Mrs. Quiggett!"

"Well, I never!" shrieked Mrs. Quiggett, a laugh like a venerable, hook-nosed cockatoo echoing through the halls as she shook her sides, causing keys and ancient ribs to jingle. "Well, I never!"

"Oh, Quiggett!" sobbed Mrs. Lambert. "What a man he is!"

"You've been quarrelling and reconciling? That is right."

"Quarrel with him? He tells the greatest stories! My General is an angel, Quiggett. I would worship him, fall at his boots, and kiss them! There has never been a man so good as my husband. What have I done to deserve such a soul? How dare I possess such a wonderful husband?"

"My dear," the old lady murmured, "there appears to be a pair of you. And what shall we have for supper?"

When Lambert returned late, announcing Harry's freedom and his brother's miraculous rescue, the household erupted in commotion. If Mrs. Lambert's General was an angel before, what transformation awaited him now? If she desired to embrace his boots in the morning, what further acts of humble devotion might she crave in the evening? Little Hetty nestled silently against her father, stealing a sip from his glass. Theo and his mother's faces shone with happiness, like twin moons. After supper, the four knelt at a signal, paying glad homage in joyful mirth, rejoicing with a purity akin to angels repenting sins. Then, a heavy knocking shattered the quiet. My lord was miles away in the country; it was past midnight. Mrs. Lambert, sensing the visitor, nodded.

"This is George," announced a young gentleman, ushering in another. "We visited Aunt Bernstein but could not sleep without thanking you, Aunt Lambert. You dear, dear, good—" Their words dissolved into silence. Aunt Lambert embraced Harry; Theo snatched up the pallid Hetty, hugging her back to life. George stood with his hat removed. Once Harry's reunion was complete, George approached Mrs. Lambert to kiss her hand, while the General brushed his lips gently across hers. All were bathed in a tender, happy glow—the warmth of forgiveness, the restoration of peace, the return of lost love.

"Came from Aunt Bernstein's," Harry explained, "seeing lights here. We couldn't sleep without bidding goodnight." "Indeed, a famous nightcap you've brought us, boys," the General remarked. "Shall you dine with us tomorrow?" They declined, needing to visit Madame Bernstein instead. And so, the following day arrived—the very day we now chronicle, hosting precisely this dinner.

In lieu of Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe, absent on private matters, my gracious reader has been invited to sit. Why sit? Not for empty fare, but to hear the statement of **Mr. George Esmond Warrington**. They sat not in the grand dining hall, but in a cozy study; the cloth was withdrawn, the King's health drunk, servants departed, and the guests settled into contemplative silence. With some hesitation and blushes, Mr. George began:

"At the table of our General, I recall the little Philadelphia agent, whose wit and shrewdness we noted at home, voicing objections to the campaign's conduct, which disaster later vindicated. 'Of course,' he argued, 'your Excellency's troops, once before Fort Duquesne, will never withstand such a general, army, and artillery. But do you calculate on reaching the place? Your march cuts through untrodden woods, over roads you must forge, stretching four miles. This slender line, threading the forest, invites endless attacks from unseen enemies practiced in ambuscades.' 'Psha, sir!' scoffed the General. 'The savages may frighten your raw American militia, but they will never impress His Majesty's regulars.' 'I heartily hope not, sir,' sighed Mr. Franklin, drawing a sigh from the Postmaster, who was naturally sneered at by the General's family as a pert civilian lacking comprehension.

"We despised the Indians on our side, and our commander mocked them. Our officers outraged the accompanying chiefs by mistreating their women; only seven or eight chiefs remained loyal. Had a couple of hundred faced us on that fatal ninth of July, the outcome would differ vastly. They would have repelled the French-Indian assault, preventing the ensuing panic. It is known now that the French nearly surrendered, never imagining a defense, and their own Indians protested the audacity of attacking such overwhelming force.

"I stood with the General during the initial firing. Aide-de-camps were sent forward one by one. Initially, the enemy's attack was met briskly, with cheers rising from our lines. Soon, however, our fire slackened as single shots rang from behind every tree and bush, felling man after man. We marched in orderly formation—skirmishers ahead, colors and two small guns in center, baggage trailing—with open ground for a mile or two, flanked by thick, tangled brushwoods. After brief frontal fire, the ambush opened from both sides of the surrounding woods. Men dropped rapidly, officers falling faster than the ranks. At first, cheering continued, guns opened on the wood, seemingly silencing the hidden foe. But the rifle fire persisted. Men halted, huddled together, ignoring orders to advance, firing wildly into the brushwood without effect. Those in front ran back, terrified and wounded, claiming five thousand Frenchmen and a legion of screaming Indian demons awaited them, scalping the fallen. We heard their cries as comrades fell. Nowhere could induce men to move forward. One aide after another was dispatched and never returned. Finally, it was my turn. Sent with a message to Captain Fraser of Halkett's, I was never to receive nor deliver it.

"Had not gone thirty yards when a rifle-ball struck my leg, sending me crashing to the ground. I recall a frenzied rush of Indians and Frenchmen, the former wailing their fiendish war-cries, the latter as fierce as their savage allies. I was horrified to see so few whitecoats remaining—perhaps a score, certainly fewer than fifty in that accursed action where two of Britain's bravest regiments were routed.

"One figure, half-Indian, half-French, wearing moccasins and a white uniform coat with a cockade, spotted me prostrate. Turning back, he raised his musket, clubbed over his head, intending to dash my brains out and strip me. Luckily, my little fusil, gifted by Harry before the campaign, lay within reach. Seizing it, I felled the Frenchman dead at six yards. Saved for the moment, I bled profusely and grew faint, swooning as I tried to reload; the weapon slipped from my lifeless hand.

"Scarcely conscious, yells and shots dim in my ears, I saw an Indian standing over the slain Frenchman, glancing toward me. He rifled the corpse, tearing open the coat, searching pockets, then scalped him. Clutching a bleeding knife in his mouth, he advanced. Through a film, like a dream, I saw him coming, powerless to resist. He knelt upon my chest, seizing my long hair with one bloody hand to lift my head. In doing so, he revealed a French officer advancing rapidly behind him.
