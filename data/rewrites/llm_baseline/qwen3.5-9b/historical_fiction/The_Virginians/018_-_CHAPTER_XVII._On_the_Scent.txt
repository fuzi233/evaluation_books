Young Harry Warrington's act of rebellion struck Madame de Bernstein with such sudden force that her only recourse was the immediate eruption of anger with which we left her in the previous chapter. As she stormed from the room, she cast two piercing glares at Lady Fanny and her mother. Meanwhile, Lady Maria, hidden behind her tambour frame, managed to escape detection entirely. She barely lifted her head from her embroidery to watch her aunt retreat or to observe the exchange of looks between her "mamma-in-law" and her sister.

"So, despite all our efforts, you have won?" the maternal gaze seemed to inquire.

"Win what?" Lady Fanny's eyes asked in return. Yet, innocence offered no defense here. She appeared merely puzzled, lacking the calculated composure of a guilty party. Had she truly been culpable, she would have mastered the art of feigning ignorance with greater dexterity, composing a face ready to meet any accusation. Whatever expression graced Fanny's features, her mother stared at her with such intensity that it felt as though she might tear the eyes from her sockets. However, Lady Castlewood lacked the theatrical cruelty of the monsters described in *King Lear*. When her ladyship intended to harm her daughter, she would publicly embrace her with a smile, only to administer the punishment privately.

"So, you do not wish to accompany the old lady to Tunbridge Wells?" was all she finally said to Cousin Warrington, masking her displeasure with a perfectly bred, albeit thin-lipped, smile.

"And small blame to our cousin!" interjected my lord. (The figure behind the tambour frame glanced up for a fleeting second.) "A young man ought not to spend his entire existence idling and taking holidays. He should blend utility with pleasure; perhaps visit the fountains and pump-rooms at Tunbridge or Bath later. Mr. Warrington is destined to manage a vast estate in America; let him learn how estates are conducted here in England. Will has already shown him the kennels and stables, and the fashionable games, which I believe, cousin, you play as well as your tutors. After the harvest, we shall introduce him to English fowling and shooting; in winter, we will take him hunting. Although there has been a cooling of relations between us and our kinswoman in Virginia, we share the same blood. Before sending our cousin back to his mother, let us demonstrate to him what the life of an English gentleman entails at home. I should delight in reading with him as well as sporting alongside him, which is precisely why I have pressed him recently to remain and keep me company."

My lord spoke with such absolute candor that his mother-in-law, half-brother, and sister could not help but wonder at his intent. These three frequently engaged in quiet conspiracies, plotting or grumbling against the head of the household. When he adopted this open tone, his meaning remained unfathomable, often remaining so for months. He did not state facts; rather, he dictated the narrative his family was expected to accept. It was a known convention that Lord Castlewood possessed a noble desire to cultivate domestic affection and to educate, amuse, and improve his young relative, having developed a genuine fondness for the lad and wishing for Harry to stay near him for some time.

"What is Castlewood playing at now?" William whispered to his mother and sister as they slipped away down the corridor.

"Wait! By George, I have it!"

"What, William?"

"He intends to get him involved in gambling, hoping to win the Virginia estate back from him. That is his game!"

"But the lad holds no claim to the Virginia estate if he loses," remarked his mother.

"If my brother has no scheme in mind, may I be..."

"Hush! Of course he has a scheme. But what is it?"

"Could he mean Maria? Maria is as old as Harry's mother," Mr. William mused.

"Pooh! With her aged face, sandy hair, and freckled complexion! Impossible!" cried Lady Fanny, letting out a slight sigh.

"Of course, your ladyship fancied the Iroquois, too!" exclaimed his mother.

"I trust I know my station and duty better, madam! Even if I had liked him, that is no reason to marry him. Your ladyship has taught me that much."

"My Lady Fanny!"

"I am certain you married our father without loving him. You have told me so a thousand times!"

"And if you did not love our father before the wedding, you certainly did not fall in love with him afterward," interrupted Mr. William with a laugh. "Fan and I remember how our honored parents used to quarrel. Did we not, Fan? And our brother Esmond kept the peace."

"Do not recall those dreadful, low scenes, William!" cried his mother. "When your father drank too much, he became a madman; his behavior should serve as a warning to you, sir, who are fond of the same detestable habit."

"I am sure, madam, you were not much happier marrying the man you did not love, and your ladyship's title has brought very little fortune with it," whined Lady Fanny.

"What is the use of a coronet with the jointure of a tradesman's wife? How many of them are richer than we are? Recently, a grocer's widow from London Bridge moved into our square in Kensington. Her daughters possess three gowns where I have one, and although they are attended by only a man and two maids, I know they eat and drink a thousand times better than we do, subsisting on scraps of cold meat while our great, flaunting, impudent, lazy lackeys loiter!"

"He! He! Glad I dine at the palace and not at home!" said Mr. Will. (Through his aunt's influence with Count Puffendorff, Groom of the Royal Powder-Closet, Mr. Will held one of the minor court positions of Deputy Powder.)

"Why should I not be happy without any title save my own?" continued Lady Frances. "Many people are. I dare say they are even happy in America."

"Yes—provided they have a mother-in-law who is a perfect Turk and Tartar, as I hear, with Indian war-whoops echoing around them, and a constant danger of losing your scalp or being eaten by a wild beast every time you attend church."

"I would not go to church," said Lady Fanny.

"You would go with anyone who asked you, Fan!" roared Mr. Will. "And so would old Maria, and so would any woman, that is the fact." He laughed at his own wit.

"Pray, good folks, what is all this merriment about?" Madame Bernstein asked, peeking in from the tapestried door leading to the gallery where the conversation was held.

Will explained that his mother and sister had been fighting—a non-event for Madame Bernstein, who knew them well—because Fanny wished to marry their cousin, the wild Indian, and my lady Countess forbade it. Fanny immediately protested this fabrication. Since the day her mother ordered her not to speak to the young gentleman, she had scarcely exchanged two words with him. She knew her place. She did not wish to be scalped by wild Indians or devoured by bears.

Madame de Bernstein looked perplexed. "If he is not staying for you, for whom is he staying?" she asked. "In the households to which he has been sent, you have ensured he sees no woman who is not either a fright or confined to the nursery; and I believe the boy is too proud to fall in love with a dairymaid, Will."

"Hmph! That is a matter of taste, ma'am," said Mr. William, shrugging. "It is Mr. William Esmond's taste, as you say; but not that of the boy yonder. The Esmonds raised by his grandfather would not go courting in the kitchen."

"Well, ma'am, every man to his taste, I say again. A fellow might fare worse than my brother's servants' hall, and besides Fan, there is only the maids or old Maria to choose from."

"Maria! Impossible!" Yet, as she spoke, a sudden thought crossed Madame Bernstein's mind: this elderly Calypso might have captivated her young Telemachus. She recalled numerous instances in her own experience of young men infatuated by older women. She remembered Harry Warrington's recent frequent absences, which she had attributed to his love for field sports. She also noted how often, when Harry was away, Maria Esmond was likewise absent. Walks in cool avenues, whispers in garden temples, behind clipped hedges, casual hand-squeezes in twilight corridors, and sweet glances exchanged on the stairs—all these possibilities, suggesting a lively fancy and intimate worldly knowledge, occurred to Madame de Bernstein just as she dismissed them as impossible.

"Impossible, ma'am! I don't know," Will continued. "My mother warned Fan off him."

"Oh, your mother did warn Fanny off?"

"Certainly, my dear Baroness!"

"Didn't she? Didn't she pinch Fanny's arm black and blue? Didn't they fight about it?"

"Nonsense, William! For shame, William!" cried both implicated ladies in unison.

"And now, since we have heard how rich he is, perhaps it is sour grapes, that is all. And now, since he is barred from the young bird, perhaps he is hunting the old one, that's all. Why impossible? Do you know old Lady Suffolk, ma'am?"
