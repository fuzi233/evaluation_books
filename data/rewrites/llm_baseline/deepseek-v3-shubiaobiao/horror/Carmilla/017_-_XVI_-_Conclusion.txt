You might imagine I recount these horrors with steady hands and a calm heart. You would be wrong. Even now, the mere act of setting these words to paper sends a tremor through me, as if the past has sunk its teeth into my nerves and refuses to let go. Only your insistence—your relentless, pleading curiosity—could have compelled me to relive this nightmare. Years have passed since my deliverance, yet the shadow of those days clings to me, turning solitude into a waking dread and the dark into something unspeakable.

But before I lay down my pen, allow me to speak of Baron Vordenburg, that odd and learned man whose knowledge unearthed the truth of Countess Mircalla’s cursed grave. He lived in Gratz then, a pauper by necessity, the last remnant of his family’s once-great fortune. What little he had, he poured into his obsession: the study of vampirism. His shelves groaned under the weight of ancient tomes—*Magia Posthuma*, *Phlegon de Mirabilibus*, *Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis*—each a thread in the grotesque tapestry of the undead. From these, he distilled laws that govern their kind: some absolute, others flickering into force under certain conditions.

Do not believe the tales of pallid, bloodless specters. The vampire does not skulk like a corpse. No—it walks among the living, flushed with stolen vitality, its lips red, its gaze bright. Only in the grave does the truth reveal itself: the body pliant, the eyes wide and glistening, the mouth stained as if fresh from a feast. And yet, how they come and go without disturbing earth or coffin remains a mystery even Vordenburg could not unravel.

Their existence is a perverse duality—daylight hours spent in the false death of the tomb, nights animated by an insatiable hunger. They fixate upon their prey with a lover’s intensity, stalking with inhuman patience. Some they court, drawing out their torment like connoisseurs of suffering. Others they take swiftly, brutally, draining them in a single, ravenous night.

Carmilla—Mircalla—Millarca—she was bound by a singular rule: her name, an unbroken cipher. Alter a single letter, and the spell unravels. My father shared with Vordenburg the tale of the Moravian nobleman and the vampire at Karnstein, and in return, the Baron revealed his own grim discovery. His ancestor, another Vordenburg, had loved Mircalla in life. Her death, and the horror that followed, drove him to hide her remains, to spare her the final desecration of a stake through the heart. But guilt followed him into old age, and he left behind clues—notes, tracings—that led us, too late for so many, back to her resting place.

One last thing he told us: the vampire’s touch lingers. Its grip is iron, yes, but worse is the numbness it leaves behind, a cold that seeps into the bone and never fully fades.

The spring after Carmilla’s destruction, my father took me to Italy. For over a year, we wandered, as if distance could scour the terror from my mind. It did not. Even now, she visits me—sometimes the languid, laughing girl who leaned too close, sometimes the thing I saw writhing in the ruined church. And sometimes, in the quiet of evening, I startle at nothing, certain I hear her light step at the door.

She is gone.

But not gone enough.
