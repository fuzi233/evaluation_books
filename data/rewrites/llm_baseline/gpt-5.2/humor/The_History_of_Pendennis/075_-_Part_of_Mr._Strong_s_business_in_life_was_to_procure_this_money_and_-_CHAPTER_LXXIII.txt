Arthur wrote to Blanche as a man writes who has just heard the tureen-lid crack in the very moment of triumph, and sees all his promised gravy running away into the table-cloth.

“My dear Blanche,” he said, “you have been reading and dreaming your pretty dramas, where the heroine sweeps into her father’s palace as if into a well-warmed dining-room, and says to the astonished company, ‘All this is mine and thine,’ and the servants bring in the great silver covers with a flourish, and everybody’s portion is large and certain. Will you be prepared—only for once—to act the other part? Not the princess with the bridal cake and the trumpets; but the unlucky lady who discovers that the grand entertainment was all a bill of fare, and the dinner itself a crust eaten by the kitchen-fire.

“You remember Alnaschar with his tray of porcelain? He kicked it down, and with it went all his castles of tea-cups and saucers, all his hopes, all his glittering future. I am not sure he had even so much as a wife to help him pick up the pieces; but the moral is the same. Something has happened within the last few hours—something I cannot tell you now, and perhaps never need tell, if God is merciful—which has overset my tray as neatly as if a malicious foot had been waiting for it.

“You have known my little property, and you have known your own little fortune. It will be no Belgravia feast; it will be plain fare. There will be days when we shall dine off the steady, honest joint of our own industry, and be proud of it; and days when the joint will be small, and the carving a little anxious. There will be no stars and epaulettes for the hero; I shall not be a duke with a garter at his knee, nor a bishop on the bench, nor a member of Parliament for Clavering—at least not now. I have written to Sir Francis Clavering that I cannot accept the seat he offered me until after my marriage; and in like manner I cannot and will not accept any larger fortune with you than that which has always been yours since your grandfather’s death and the birth of your half-brother.

“Do you understand what it is to live decently on what we can honestly earn? We may take a cab sometimes, and not be too proud to take an omnibus when we are tired; we shall have friends, books, laughter, and the humble comforts which come like the smell of bread from a neighbour’s oven—common, and yet cheering. I may buy back the Pall Mall Gazette, since the publishers are weary of it after poor Shandon’s death, and will sell it for a small sum. Warrington will be my right hand. You shall have your share of the paper’s little vanities—opera-boxes if we can afford them, fashionable intelligence if you like it, and the poet’s corner if it must be. We can live over the office: four good rooms, a kitchen, and a garret for Laura, in Catherine Street in the Strand. Or a house in the Waterloo Road—pleasant enough, except for that halfpenny toll at the Bridge, which is the sort of tax Fate delights to lay on romance. The boys may go to King’s College, may they not?

“Does this read like a joke? It is none. Our fine day-dreams are gone—whisked away like Cinderella’s coach at midnight, only with less punctuality and more malice. If you can say, ‘Yes, Arthur, I will follow your fate, and be a loyal and loving wife to aid and cheer you,’ then come to me, dear Blanche, and may God help me to do my duty by you. If not—if you look to a higher station—I must not bar your fortune. I will stand in the crowd and see your ladyship go to Court when you are presented, and you shall give me a smile from your chariot window. Lady Mirabel went to the drawing-room last season, all flowers and glitter: her happy husband beside her blazing with stars and cordons, the coachman’s bosom a garden. Will you have the chariot, or walk on foot and mend your husband’s stockings?

“My uncle is very unwell, and takes my refusal of the seat in Parliament in sad dudgeon; the scheme was his, and he naturally bemoans its failure. But Warrington, Laura, and I have held a council of war: they know this dreadful secret, and back me in my decision. You must love George as you love what is generous and upright and noble; and Laura—she must be our sister, Blanche, our saint, our good angel. With two such friends at home, what need we care for the world without?”

To this came back Blanche’s letter—addressed to Laura—and another to Arthur, written with all the clearness of her neat hand, and perfumed so that the paper seemed to have been laid in a drawer of rose-leaves and sighs.

“You are spoiled by the world,” she wrote, “you do not love your poor Blanche as she would be loved, or you would not offer thus lightly to take her or to leave her. No, Arthur, you love me not. A man of the world, you have given me your plighted troth, and are ready to redeem it; but that entire affection, that love whole and abiding—where is it? I am but a pastime of your life, and I would be its all. I would have our two hearts one; but ah, my Arthur, how lonely yours is! how little you give me of it! You speak of our parting with a smile on your lip; of our meeting, and you care not to hasten it! I have wept—I have prayed—I have passed sleepless hours—bitter, bitter tears over your letter! I bring you the gushing poesy of my being—the yearnings of the soul that longs to be loved—love, love, love, beyond all!—and you are cold. You accept my soul’s treasure as though ’twere dross. You treat me like a slave, and bid me bow to my master! Is this the guerdon of a free maiden—is this the price of a life’s passion? Foolish girl that I was! One by one the flowers of my young life have faded away; and this, the last, the sweetest—where is it? But no more. Bless you, bless you always, Arthur!

“I will write more when I am more collected. My racking brain renders thought almost impossible. I long to see Laura! She will come to us directly we return from the country, will she not? And you, cold one!

“B.”

The words were plain enough, each letter set as carefully as if she had been decorating a cake; yet the meaning puzzled Arthur. Did she refuse him because he was cold? or accept him because she was tragic? Her phrases turned like a spit before two opposite fires. He sat twiddling the gilt-edged, musky riddle between finger and thumb, and laughed—sardonically, because laughter was easier than tasting the bitterness that rose in his mouth. Fortune had jilted him; and, like many a jilted gentleman, he found a kind of humour in examining the very shoe-print on his heart.

He was so employed, grimly amused, when his servant entered with a card.

“A gentleman wishes to speak to you very particularly, sir.”

“Show him in,” said Arthur; and if he had stepped into the passage he would have seen, sucking his stick, rolling his eyes, and wearing anxiety as ill as a borrowed coat, his old acquaintance Mr. Samuel Huxter.

Huxter came in, a young man with the air of one who has swallowed a pill without water.

“Pray take a chair, Mr. Huxter,” Arthur said, in his most superb manner. “In what way can I be of service to you?”

“I’d rather not speak before the flunk—before the man, Mr. Pendennis,” said Huxter; and Arthur’s attendant withdrew.

“I’m in a fix,” said the young surgeon, gloomily.

“Indeed.”

“She sent me to you.”

“Fanny? Is she well? I was coming to see her; but I have had a great deal of business since my return to London.”

“I heard of you through my governor and Jack Hobnell,” Huxter blurted. “I wish you joy, Mr. Pendennis—both of the borough and the lady, sir. Fanny wishes you joy, too,” he added, colouring a little in spite of himself.

“There’s many a slip between the cup and the lip,” Arthur said. “Who knows who will sit for Clavering next session?”

“You can do anything with my governor,” Huxter went on. “You got him Clavering Park. The old boy was mighty pleased at your calling him in. Hobnell wrote me. Do you think you could speak to him for me, Mr. Pendennis?”

“And tell him what?”

Huxter gave Arthur a look which tried to be daring and succeeded only in being miserable.

“I’ve gone and done it, sir.”

Arthur started up as if he had heard a plate crash. “You—you don’t mean to say you have—done any wrong to that dear little creature?”

“I hope not,” said Huxter, with a hangdog face. “But I’ve married her. And there’ll be an awful shindy at home. It was agreed I was to be taken into partnership when I’d passed the College—Huxter and Son. But I would have it—confound it. It’s all over now; and the old boy’s wrote that he’s coming up to town for drugs. He’ll be here to-morrow, and then it must all come out.”

“And when did this event happen?” Arthur asked, not over pleased—perhaps because a lady who had once taken a place in his own little court had now crowned another man.

“Last Thursday was five weeks,” Huxter answered. “Two days after Miss Amory came to Shepherd’s Inn.”

Arthur remembered Blanche mentioning that visit; and a cold little pain ran under his ribs.

“I was called in,” Huxter continued. “I was in the Inn, looking after old Cos’s leg—and about something else too, likely enough—and I met Strong, who told me there was a woman taken ill in chambers. I went up to give my professional services. It was the old lady that attends Miss Amory—housekeeper, or something. Taken with strong hysterics. I found her kicking and screaming like a good one—in Strong’s chambers—Strong and Colonel Altamont there, Miss Amory crying and pale as a sheet, and Altamont fuming about—regular kick-up. They were two hours in chambers; and the old woman went whooping off in a cab. She was much worse than the young one.

“I called next day at Grosvenor Place to see if I could be of use; but they were gone without so much as thanking me. And the day after—I had business of my own. Bad business too,” Huxter said, and looked down as though the floor reproached him. “But it’s done, and can’t be undone; and we must make the best of it.”

She has known the story for a month, Arthur thought—this accounts for her letter. She will not implicate her father. She wishes to let me off, and finds a pretext—the generous girl! The thought brought a pang of grief, and a gloomy kind of gratitude.

“Do you know who Altamont is, sir?” Huxter asked after a pause. “Fanny and I have talked him over; and we can’t help fancying it’s Mrs. Lightfoot’s first husband come to life again—and she that’s just married a second. Perhaps Lightfoot won’t be very sorry for it,” he added, with a savage sigh; for jealousy still gnawed him, and now, married as he was, he fancied Fanny’s heart had been packed away for Arthur Pendennis like contraband.

“Let us talk about your affairs,” Arthur said. “Show me how I can serve you. Let me congratulate you on your marriage. I am thankful that Fanny—so good, so fascinating, so kind—has found an honest man, and a gentleman who will make her happy. Tell me what I can do.”

“She thinks you can,” Huxter said, taking Arthur’s offered hand. “And I’m very much obliged. If you’d talk over my father and break it to him—and my mother, that always has her back up about being a clergyman’s daughter. Fanny ain’t of a good family, I know, and not up to us in breeding—but she’s a Huxter now.”

“The wife takes the husband’s rank,” Arthur said.

“And with a little practice in society,” Huxter went on, biting his stick, “she’ll be as good as any girl in Clavering. You should hear her sing and play. Old Bows taught her. And she’ll do on the stage if the governor throws me over—though I’d rather not have her there. She can’t help being a coquette, Mr. Pendennis—can’t help it. Dammy, sir, I’ll be bound two or three of the Bartholomew chaps I’ve brought into my place are sitting with her now. Even Jack Linton—took him down as my best man—is as bad as the rest; and she’ll go on singing and making eyes at him. Bows says if there were twenty men in a room and one not taking notice of her, she wouldn’t be satisfied till the twentieth was at her elbow.”

“You should have her mother with her,” Arthur said, laughing.

“She must keep the lodge,” Huxter said, drawing himself up with the dignity of a man in a dirty collar. “She can’t see so much of her family as she used. I can’t, you know, go on with that lot. Consider my rank in life.”

“Au fait,” said Arthur, who was infinitely amused—though, as happens with amused men, the joke bit himself somewhere.

In the midst of this, there came another knock; and the servant announced Mr. Bows.

The old man entered slowly, pale, blushing as if ashamed of being seen, his hand trembling a little as he took Arthur’s. He coughed, wiped his face with a checked cotton handkerchief, sat down with hands on knees, the sunlight shining frankly on his bald head. Arthur looked at him with a sudden kindness. Here, too, was a man who had brought his heart like a dish to a woman’s table, and had had it pushed aside.

“So we have lost, Mr. Bows,” Arthur said, “and here is the lucky winner.”

“Here is the lucky winner,” Bows answered, dryly.

“I suppose you have come from my place?” Huxter said; and having winked at Bows with one eye, he now favoured Arthur with a wink of the other—a wink which tried to say, “Infatuated old boy—head and ears—poor old fool,” and succeeded only in making him look like a man with grit in his eyelash.

“Yes,” Bows said. “I have been there ever since you went away. It was Mrs. Sam sent me after you—said she thought you might be doing something stupid—something like yourself, Huxter.”

“There’s as big fools as I am,” growled the young surgeon.

“A few, p’raps,” Bows returned. “Not many, let us trust. She sent me after you for fear you should offend Mr. Pendennis; and because she thought you wouldn’t give her message, and beg him to go and see her; and she knew I would take her errand. Did he tell you that, sir?”

Huxter blushed scarlet, and tried to cover it with an oath. Arthur laughed, because the scene suited his bitter humour.

“I have no doubt Mr. Huxter was going to tell me,” Arthur said. “And very much flattered I shall be to pay my respects to his wife.”

“It’s in Charterhouse Lane, over the baker’s, on the right-hand side as you go from St. John’s Street,” Bows continued, with pitiless exactness. “You know Smithfield, Mr. Pendennis? Doctor Johnson’s been down that street many a time with ragged shoes and a bundle of penny-a-lining for the Gent’s Magazine. You literary gents are better off now—ride in cabs, wear yellow kid gloves.”

“I have known so many brave men fail, and so many quacks succeed,” Arthur said, more sadly than before, “that you mistake me if you think I am puffed up by my own good luck. Do you think the prizes of life are carried by the most deserving? You must feel you are as good as I. I have never questioned it. It is you that are peevish against the freaks of fortune, and grudge what befalls others. It is not the first time you have unjustly accused me, Bows.”

“Perhaps you’re not far wrong,” the old fellow said, wiping his forehead. “A man grumbles when he thinks of himself. Here’s the fellow got the prize; here’s the fortunate youth.”

“I don’t know what you are driving at,” Huxter said, bewildered by talk which seemed to him to have nothing to do with drugs, marriage, or rank.

“Perhaps not,” Bows said. “Mrs. H. sent me here to look after you, and see you gave that message you didn’t give. Women always are right; they have always a reason. Why, sir,” he added, turning to Arthur with a sour twist, “she had a reason even for sending me. I was sitting with her after you left us—quiet and comfortable—talking away, and she mending your shirts—when your two young friends Jack Linton and Bob Blades looked in from Bartholomew’s; and then she found out she had this message. You needn’t hurry; she don’t want you back again. They’ll stay two hours, I daresay.”

At this, Huxter sprang up as if the chair had pricked him. He plunged his stick into his paletot pocket, seized his hat.

“You’ll come and see us, sir, won’t you?” he said to Arthur. “You’ll talk over the governor, won’t you, if I can get out of this place and down to Clavering?”

“You will promise to attend me gratis if ever I fall ill at Fairoaks,” Arthur said, good-humouredly. “I will do anything I can. I will come and see Mrs. Huxter immediately, and we will conspire together.”

“I thought that would send him out,” Bows said, settling back into his chair as the young surgeon bolted. “And it’s all true—every word. She wants you back again, and sends her husband after you. She cajoles everybody, the little devil. She tries it on you, on me, on poor Costigan, on the young chaps from Bartholomew’s. She’s got a little court already. And if there’s nobody, she practises on the old German baker downstairs, or coaxes the black sweeper at the crossing.”

“Is she fond of that fellow?” Arthur asked.

“There’s no accounting for likes and dislikes,” Bows said.

“Yes—she’s fond of him; and having took it into her head, she wouldn’t rest till she married him. Banns at St. Clement’s; nobody heard, nobody objected. One day she slips out of the porter’s lodge, gets the business done, goes off to Gravesend with Lothario, and leaves a note for me to go and explain to her Ma. Bless you—the old woman knew as well as I did, though she pretended not. And so she goes, and I’m alone again. I miss her, sir—tripping along that court, coming for her singing lesson. I’ve no heart to look into the lodge now; it looks empty without her. And I go and sit and dangle about her lodgings like an old fool. She makes ’em very trim, though; gets up all Huxter’s shirts and clothes; cooks his little dinner; sings at her work like a lark. What’s the use of being angry? I lent ’em three pound to go on with; for they haven’t got a shilling till the reconciliation, and Pa comes down.”

When Bows had gone, Arthur carried Blanche’s letter, and the news which had just come to him, to his usual adviser, Laura. It was curious how this gentleman—who commonly took his own way, and called it fate—now wanted another person’s counsel upon everything; as if his mind, having had one great dish snatched away, must needs ask leave before it tasted even bread and butter.

He produced Blanche’s letter and asked Laura to interpret it. Laura read, grew agitated, puzzled, and very grave.

“It seems to me,” she said, “as if Blanche is acting very artfully.”

“And wishes so to place matters that she may take me or leave me?” Arthur asked. “Is it not so?”

“It is, I am afraid, a kind of duplicity which does not augur well for your happiness,” Laura said; “and it is a bad reply to your candour and honesty. Do you know—I think—I scarcely like to say what I think.” She blushed deeply; but under Arthur’s persuasion the shy confession came. “It looks to me, Arthur, as if there might be—there might be somebody else.”

“And if there is,” Arthur broke out, “and if I am free once again, will the best and dearest of all women—”

“You are not free, dear brother,” Laura said, calmly. “You belong to another; of whom I own it grieves me to think ill. But I can’t do otherwise. It is very odd that in this letter she does not urge you to tell her the reason why you have broken arrangements so advantageous to you; and avoids speaking of it. She somehow writes as if she knows her father’s secret.”
