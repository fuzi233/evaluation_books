The night descended with a biting, unnatural cold that seeped through my bones, freezing the very marrow beneath me. Somewhere nearby, the wind carried the soft, rhythmic padding of wolf paws on rock, punctuated by quick, greedy pants. Through the encroaching dark, a circle of glowing eyes tightened its grip around me. My time had arrived. I scrambled to my feet, panic seizing my throat—only to realize with a jolt of horror that I possessed not even a stick to defend myself.

They surged forward in a blur of fury, black throats gaping to consume me. I stood paralyzed, waiting for the end. Just as they lunged, a cloud of emerald eyes erupted from the shadows, diving onto the wolves' flanks. Heads bearing those eyes shot forward, crying out with a sound fiercer yet weaker than the wolves' own snarls. I recognized the cry instantly: it was the Little Ones, led by a massive gray figure whose presence I felt more than saw. A terrible battle erupted in the darkness, a chaotic clash of teeth and claws whose details were lost to me save for the sheer violence of it.

Desire to flee warred with the knowledge that any movement would only mark me for death; both predators could see and smell me in the gloom. Suddenly, the howling ceased, replaced by a wilder, more frantic caterwauling. The soft padding returned, signaling flight—the cats had won. But victory brought no relief. In an instant, sharp teeth sank into my legs, and I was overwhelmed by a living cataract of attackers. They bit and scratched with furious intensity, a multitude clinging to my skin like limpets. I thrashed wildly, tearing at them, throttling them in vain; when I tried to fling them off, they clung to my hands with impossible tenacity. I trampled them, thrust fingers into their eyes, and bit harder than they could, yet I could not dislodge a single one. They constantly discovered new spaces on my body to feast, their curved claws hauling at my skin while hissing spittle coated my face. Desperate, I collapsed to the ground, and they abandoned my body to dart at my face. Rising again, they immediately returned to my legs. In agony, I broke free and ran blindly into the solid dark. They pursued me in a surrounding torrent, rubbing against me and leaping up, tormenting me without ceasing. Every time I stumbled, they allowed me a moment to rise; every time I slowed from fear, they attacked my legs anew.

That miserable night they drove me along a surprisingly smooth path, sparing me from gullies as I passed the Evil Wood unseen. By morning, I found myself beyond the channels, standing on the verge of the orchard valley. Overcome with joy, I wished to make peace with my persecutors, but not a cat was to be seen. Exhausted, I threw myself onto the moss and fell into a deep sleep.

I was awakened by a sharp kick, finding myself bound hand and foot once more, a thrall of the giants. "What fitter fate?" I muttered to myself, laughing in the triumph of self-disgust. "To whom else should I belong?" A second kick silenced my false merriment. Thus aided by my captors, I eventually rose to my feet. Six of them surrounded me. They untied the rope binding my legs, attached separate ropes to each limb, and dragged me away. I walked as best I could, though frequent double-pulls sent me crashing to the ground, requiring them to kick me upright again. They marched me straight to my old labor site, tied my leg-ropes to a tree, freed my arms, and placed the hateful flint in my left hand. Then they lay down and pelted me with fallen fruit and stones, rarely hitting me. If only I could free my legs and grab the stick I'd spotted a couple of yards away, I might have taken down all six of them! "But the Little Ones will come at night!" I told myself, finding comfort in the thought.

All day I labored hard. When darkness fell, they tied my hands and left me fastened to the tree. I slept fitfully, waking often from dreams of lying amidst heaps of children. With dawn, my enemies returned with their kicks and their bestial company.

Around noon, nearly succumbing to fatigue and hunger, I heard a sudden commotion in the brushwood, followed by a burst of bell-like laughter so dear to my heart. I cried out in delight. Immediately, trumpeting like baby elephants, neighing like foals, and bellowing like calves filled the air. Through the bushes came a crowd of Little Ones riding diminutive horses, small elephants, and little bears—the noises emanating from the riders, not the beasts. Walking among them were older boys and girls, including a woman cradling a baby crowing in her arms. The giants sprang to their lumbering feet but were instantly greeted by a storm of sharp stones; horses charged their legs, bears hugged them at the waist, and elephants wrapped their trunks around their necks, pulling them down for a trampling they had never received before. In moments, my ropes were undone, and I was embraced by what seemed like an innumerable sea of Little Ones. For a while, I saw no more of the giants.

They seated me, and Lona appeared, silently beginning to feed me the loveliest red and yellow fruits. The entire colony mounted guard until I finished. Then, two of their largest elephants were brought forward, placed side by side, and hooked by trunk and tail. The docile creatures could have untied themselves with a shake or forgotten the hooks entirely, but they held fast, clearly understanding they must remain parallel. I rose and lay in the hollow between their backs. As the wise animals leaned against one another to counteract my weight, they formed a comfortable litter. My feet projected beyond their tails, but my head rested pillowed on an ear of each. Smaller children mounted as a bodyguard, lining the backs of my bearers, forming a procession that began to move.

I did not try to guess my destination, yielding myself to their pleasure, almost as happy as they. At first, they chattered, laughed, and played endless tricks, but the moment they sensed my drifting toward sleep, they fell silent as judges.

I woke to a sudden musical uproar. We were traveling through the forest where they found the babies, a stretch of woods reaching from the valley to the hot stream. A tiny girl sat with her feet close to my face, looking down coaxingly before speaking, the rest of the group hanging on her words.

"We make a petisson to king," she said.

"What is it, my darling?" I asked.

"Shut eyes one minute," she answered.

"Certainly I will! Here goes!" I replied, closing my eyes tight.

"No, no! Not before I tell you!" she cried.

I opened them again, and we talked and laughed for another hour.

"Close eyes!" she commanded suddenly.

I closed them and kept them shut. The elephants stood still. I heard a soft scurry, a little rustle, and then a profound silence—for in that world, some silences are heard.

"Open eyes!" twenty voices shouted from a distance. But when I obeyed, not a creature was visible except the elephants bearing me. I knew the children were marvelously quick at clearing the way—the giants had taught them that—but when I raised myself and scanned the open, shrubless forest, seeing neither hand nor heel, I stared in blank astonishment.

The sun had set, and darkness was fast approaching, yet soon a multitude of birds began to sing. I lay listening, certain that if I left them alone, the hiders would soon emerge. The singing grew into a storm of bird-voices. "Surely the children must have something to do with this! And yet how could they set the birds singing?" I wondered aloud. Soon, however, looking up into the tree beneath my elephants, I spied a slight motion among the leaves. I looked keenly. Sudden white spots appeared in the dark foliage, the music died down, and a gale of childish laughter rippled the air. White spots emerged in every direction; the trees were full of children! In wild merriment, they began to descend, some dropping from bough to bough so rapidly I could scarcely believe they hadn't fallen. I left my litter and was instantly surrounded—a target for all their jubilant artillery. With stately composure, the elephants walked away to bed.

"But," I said after their uproarious gladness had run its course, "how is it that I never before heard you sing like the birds? Even when I thought it must be you, I could hardly believe it!"

"Ah," said one of the wildest, "but we were not birds then! We were run-creatures, not fly-creatures! We had our hide-places in the bushes then; but when we came to no-bushes, only trees, we had to build nests! When we built nests, we grew birds, and when we were birds, we had to do birds! We asked them to teach us their noises, and they taught us, and now we are real birds! Come and see my nest. It's not big enough for king, but it's big enough for king to see me in it!"

I told him I could not climb a tree without the sun to guide me, promising to try when he returned.

"Kings seldom have wings!" I added.

"King! King!" cried one, "oo knows none of us hasn't no wings--foolis feddery tings! Arms and legs is better."

"That is true. I can get up without wings—and carry straws in my mouth too, to build my nest with!"

"Oo knows!" he answered, and wandered off sucking his thumb.

A moment later, I heard him calling from his nest, high up in an enormous walnut tree:

"Up adain, king! Dood night! I seepy!"

And I heard no more of him until he woke me in the morning.
