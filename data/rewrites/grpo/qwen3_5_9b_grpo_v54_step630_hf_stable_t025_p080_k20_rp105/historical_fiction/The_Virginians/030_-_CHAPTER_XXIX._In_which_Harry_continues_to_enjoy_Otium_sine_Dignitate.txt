Madame de Bernstein found her contentment at the Wells, remaining there to scold her niece and play her rubber, while the rest of the ton scattered to the assembly rooms and card tables. For a man of Harry's age, almost any setting becomes a paradise if it offers lively company, fresh air, and a share of sport. Even pleasure itself seems delightful at twenty; we rush to meet it with alacrity, speculate on its arrival, and count the days until our reunion. Yet how gently and coolly we view it in the twilight of life! Do you not recall your first ball, Madam? Does your memory not wander to that golden past as you sit adorning the walls while your daughters dance? I, too, remember when I found joy in walking three miles to dine with old Captain Jones. Can you imagine liking such a trek now, merely to drink his half-pay port? Perhaps it was purchased from a small-town merchant for a modest sum, but it was served with a warm welcome, seasoned by youth in a way that no vintage or matured man can replicate today. *Viximus nuper.* I find no fault in young Harry's conduct or his idleness, unlike his stern friend, the Colonel of the Twentieth Regiment. Oh, blessed idleness! Divine lazy nymph! Fetch me a novel as I lie in my dressing gown at three in the afternoon; mix a sherry-cobbler, and bring a cigar! Dear, slatternly, smiling enchantress! They may assail you with vile names, swear away your character, and brand you the Mother of Evil; yet, for all that, you remain the finest company in the world.

Lord March departed for the North, and Lord Chesterfield, finding the Tunbridge waters ineffective for his deafness, returned to his solitude at Blackheath. However, other gentlemen remained to sport and revel, and Mr. Warrington soon found himself surrounded by companions at the White Horse. He quickly mastered the art of ordering a French dinner with the flair of a St. James's dandy, conversed with Monsieur Barbeau in his native tongue with greater fluency than most, and developed an exquisite palate for wine, distinguishing between Clos Vougeot and Romande with remarkable precision. He became the young king of the Wells, where the frequenters—easygoing men of the world—were far from shocked by the reputation for gallantry and extravagance that Harry had acquired, a reputation that had previously terrified Mr. Wolfe.

Though our Virginian lived amidst the revellers, swimming in the same waters as the loose fish, the boy possessed a natural shrewdness and honesty that kept him clear of the snares commonly set for the unwary. He made very few foolish bets with the jolly idle fellows around him, and the oldest hands found it difficult to take him in. He engaged in games, both indoors and out, due to a natural skill and aptitude, holding his own against almost any fair competitor. He was scrupulous to play only with gentlemen he knew and always settled his debts on the spot. Though he might have fared poorly at a college examination, he possessed prudence, fidelity, keen perception, great generosity, and dauntless personal courage.

He soon had occasion to demonstrate his mettle. When the unfortunate little Cattarina, who had brought him so much trouble, pushed her importunities beyond the limit Harry deemed generous, he withdrew from the advances of the Opera-House Siren with perfect coolness and skill, leaving her to exercise her blandishments upon a more susceptible victim. In vain did the mermaid's hysterical mother wait upon Harry, vowing that a cruel bailiff had seized all her daughter's goods for debt and that her venerable father was languishing in a London gaol. Harry declared that between himself and the bailiff there could be no dealings, stating that because he had the good fortune to be known to Mademoiselle Cattarina and had gratified her caprices with various trinkets, he was not bound to pay the past debts of her family. He refused to act as bail for her father in London or settle her outstanding accounts at Tunbridge. The Cattarina's mother first called him a monster and an ingrate, then asked, with a veteran smirk, why he did not accept payment for the services he had rendered to the young person. Initially, Mr. Warrington could not comprehend the nature of such payment; but when the old woman explained it, the honest lad rose in horror at the thought of a woman trafficking in her child's dishonor. He told her he came from a country where even the savages would recoil from such a bargain. Having bowed the old lady ceremoniously to the door, he ordered Gumbo to mark her well and never admit her to his lodgings again. No doubt she retired breathing vengeance against the Iroquois; she declared that no Turk or Persian would treat a lady so, and she and her daughter retreated to London as soon as their anxious landlord permitted.

Then came Harry's perils of gaming, alongside his perils of gallantry. A man who plays at bowls must expect to meet with rubbers. After dinner at the ordinary, having declined to play piquet further with Captain Batts, and being roughly asked his reason, Harry frankly told the Captain that he only played with gentlemen who paid, like himself. Yet he expressed such readiness to settle Mr. Batts's outstanding account that the Captain declared himself satisfied *d'avance* and straightway left the Wells without paying Harry or any other creditor. On another occasion, he displayed his spirit by beating a chairman who had been rude to old Miss Whiffler as she headed to the assembly. Furthermore, when the calumny regarding himself and the unlucky opera-dancer was repeated by Mr. Hector Buckler, one of the fiercest frequenters of the Wells, Mr. Warrington stepped up to Buckler in the pump-room, where the latter was regaling water-drinkers with the very slander. He publicly informed Buckler that the story was false and warned him to hold anyone accountable who uttered it again. Thus, though our friend, being at Rome, certainly did as Rome did, he proved himself a valorous and worthy Roman; hurling himself among the wolves, he was acknowledged by Mr. Wolfe himself to be as brave as the best of them.

Had that officer told Colonel Lambert the stories that had caused the latter so much pain, we may be sure that when Mr. Wolfe found his young friend innocent, he took the first opportunity to withdraw the odious charges. Joy rippled through the Lamberts at the lad's acquittal—a pleasure felt by higher natures at the recovery of sinners. Never had the little family been happier, not even when they received news of Brother Tom winning his scholarship, as when Colonel Wolfe rode over with the account of his conversation with Harry Warrington. "Hadst thou brought me a regiment, James, I think I should not have been better pleased," said Mr. Lambert. Mrs. Lambert called to her daughters in the garden, kissed them both upon their return, and cried out the good news. Hetty jumped for joy, and Theo performed some uncommonly brilliant operations upon the harpsichord that night. When Dr. Boyle came in for his backgammon, he could not initially account for the illumination in all their faces until the three ladies, in a happy chorus, told him how right he had been in his sermon and how dreadfully they had wronged that poor, dear, good young Mr. Warrington.

"What shall we do, my dear?" the Colonel asked his wife. "The hay is in, the corn won't be cut for a fortnight—the horses have nothing to do. Suppose we..." Here he leaned over the table and whispered in her ear.

"My dearest Martin! The very thing!" cried Mrs. Lambert, taking her husband's hand and pressing it.

"What's the very thing, mother?" cried young Charley, who was home for his Bartholomewtide holidays.

"The very thing is to go to supper. Come, Doctor! We will have a bottle of wine tonight and drink repentance to all who think evil."

"Amen," said the Doctor, "with all my heart!" And with this, the worthy family went to their supper.
