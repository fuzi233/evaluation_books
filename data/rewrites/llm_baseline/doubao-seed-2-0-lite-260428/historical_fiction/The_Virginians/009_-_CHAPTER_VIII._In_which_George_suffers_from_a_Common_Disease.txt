On the day appointed for Madam Esmond’s grand entertainment honoring General Braddock, every corner of Castlewood House sparkled with unaccustomed splendor. Fine linen draped the sideboards, silver candelabra gleamed along the dining table, and the whole air hummed with the scent of spiced cider, roasted venison, and freshly baked pastry. Madam Esmond herself had arrayed herself in a heavy silk brocade gown she had only worn twice in her life, determined to do her guest every possible honor, even though the feast weighed on her spirits like lead. The first arrival was the General’s new aide-de-camp, Mr. Washington. The widow met him in the covered gallery that ran along the front of the house, as he dismounted at the steps—no young gentleman in all the colony was a finer horseman, or kept a better string of mounts, than the tall young militia colonel.

Before he retired to change out of his dust-caked riding boots, the pair paced the gallery for some time, deep in talk. She heard from him full confirmation of his appointment to Braddock’s staff, and they spoke long of her elder son George’s upcoming departure for the campaign. Enslaved servants bearing trays piled high with dishes for the feast passed them in a steady stream as they talked, until they descended the steps to the rough lawn below, and strolled in the shade of the gnarled old oaks. Washington told her the General would be along shortly, riding in his coach with Benjamin Franklin of Pennsylvania. Madam Esmond huffed at the news. She had heard Franklin had started life as a common printer’s apprentice—what was the world coming to, when a tradesman rode in the Commander-in-Chief’s own coach? But Washington protested that no shrewder, more honorable, useful man ever walked on foot or rode in a carriage. The widow thought the Major far too generous to the low-born inventor, but he stood his ground firmly.
“I am glad at the very least,” Madam Esmond said, drawing herself up in her stateliest pose, “that when my boy marches out to campaign, he will not be in the company of tradesmen, but of gentlemen—gentlemen of honor and fashion, bred at court, friends of his Royal Highness the Duke of Cumberland.”

Washington, who had seen too many of those fine court gentlemen roaring drunk in the regimental mess, to say nothing of their careless cruelty to new young recruits, wisely held his tongue. He would not speak ill of his superior officers behind their backs. “Harry and George are coming into the world now,” he said instead. “They must take the good with the bad, and learn to sort the wise counsel from the foolish for themselves.”
Then the widow’s tone softened entirely. “And with you, dear Colonel Washington—my own father trusted you more than any man alive, you know. You do not know how high he held you in his regard. You are only five years older than my George, yet I trust you far more than I do men twice his age. My father told the boys, and I have told them a hundred times since, to look up to Mr. Washington in all things.”
“Madam,” Washington bowed, his manner warm and sincere, “there is nothing I would not have done to win your father’s good opinion. There is no risk I would not run now to merit the favor of his daughter.”
The widow blushed so deep the rouge on her cheeks could scarcely hide it, and dropped a curtsey so low it was famous across every tidewater county in Virginia. “Mr. Washington will always have a mother’s affection in this house, so long as he shows such care for her sons,” she said, and gave him her hand, which he kissed with profound, perfect politeness.

The little lady leaned on his arm as they stepped back into the house, and found George waiting for them there, his hair perfectly powdered, his rich silk suit spotless, bowing low to both his mother and Washington. But there was no good humor in him, not at all. A polite fixed smile played around his mouth, while his dark eyes glowered with wrath and suspicion. What had he seen from the window? The innocent hand kiss, the blush on his mother’s cheek, the soft tone between the two, and his mind was already leaping to wild, jealous conclusions.
Washington tried to smooth things over with light small talk, describing the ridiculous hunt he and Braddock had joined that morning, the General floundering through mud and undergrowth in his massive boots, chasing turkeys with a fowling piece, surrounded by hounds and a crowd of shouting enslaved attendants. Braddock had huffed and puffed the whole time, complaining that Sir Robert Walpole back in Norfolk, England, would have had a fit to see such unsporting behavior, hunting turkey with a shotgun instead of riding proper to hounds. Madam Esmond gave a neat little laugh, but George only grew more glum, making snide, sneering remarks about how he was nothing but a milksop tied to his mother’s apron strings, no hunter, no fighter, no proper man of action like the rest of them.

The widow was already close to tears when Harry burst into the room, dressed in a matching fine suit, gold-corded frock coat, silver-hilted sword and all, ready to greet the General. She flew to him at once, sobbing, and Harry wrapped his arms around her, utterly confused. George kept up his mocking jabs, until Harry finally spoke up, red-faced: “If you hate hunting and fighting so much, George, why not let me go on the campaign in your place? I was born for it! I’d march straight at the French and thrash them all.”
George laughed off the suggestion. “Honor obliges the eldest heir of Esmond to go first,” he said. “We cannot both leave, anyway—someone has to stay here and watch over mother, so we don’t end up both scalped by Indians or fricasseed by French soldiers.” Harry sighed, disappointed he would miss all the glory.
George wandered over to the harpsichord, plopped down, and began trilling through the mocking French war ballad *Malbrouk s’en va-t-en guerre*, over and over, the silly little tune ringing through the parlor. Washington pulled out his watch then, saying the General’s coach would be at the gates any minute, and begged leave to go change into his full dress uniform before the rest of the guests arrived. Madam Esmond, furious at George’s insolent behavior, sailed after him to show him to his chamber, leaving the two brothers alone.

George immediately pulled a crumpled scrap of paper out of his coat pocket, the fragment of letter he had found dropped on the gallery steps that morning, and thrust it at Harry. “Look at that,” he snarled. “Common kindness? Do you call that common kindness?”
Harry read the half-finished lines, written in Washington’s neat hand: *... is older than myself, but I, again, am older than my years; and you know, dear brother, have ever been considered a sober person. All children are better for a father's superintendence, and her two, I trust, will find in me a tender friend and guardian. The flattering offer which General Braddock hath made me, will, of course, oblige me to postpone this matter until after the campaign. When we have given the French a sufficient drubbing, I shall return to repose under my own vine and fig-tree. Under my own vine and fig-tree; where I hope soon to present my dear brother to his new sister-in-law. She has a pretty Scripture name, which is...*
“Rachel!” George shrieked, clenching his fist and shaking it at the ivy climbing the outside of the parlor wall. “Rachel Esmond now, I suppose! This whole house, our lands, our father’s legacy, he thinks he’ll slip right into it, with our mother as his wife, and us two saps calling him Papa! Come on, Hal—let’s go upstairs right now, kneel proper, and welcome our new stepfather home to Castlewood.”
