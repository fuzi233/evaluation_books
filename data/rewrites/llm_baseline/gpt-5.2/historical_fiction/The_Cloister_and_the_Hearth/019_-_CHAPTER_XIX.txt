Before the cocks had found their throats, Gerard started up as if the straw beneath him had turned to nettles. He shook Martin’s shoulder, and the old soldier, wrenched out of sleep, stared as though he looked on an apparition.

“By the rood,” muttered Martin, blinking, “I deemed you slipped out by the window yesternight.”

Gerard told him in broken haste what peril had brushed him so close that the warmth of it still clung to his skin. When he asked, low and quick, how a man might get out of Holland unseen, Martin’s answer came like a map he had carried folded in his head through twenty campaigns.

“There is but one safe way. I must take you through the great forest to a bridle-road I know—no cart-track, no village-lane where every dog knows a stranger’s step. It will carry you swift to a hostelry. There they’ll lend you a horse that can eat up miles. One day’s hard gallop and you are out of Holland. But we go ere the folk here so much as turn in their beds.”

Peter’s house lay scarce a furlong and a half from the black edge of the forest. They made ready with the economy of those who have fled before: no clatter, no shouted farewells, only hands that moved by habit. Martin took his bow and three arrows—it being Thursday, and the old man’s mind clung to the day’s rule as to a rosary. Gerard carried nothing but the stout oak staff Peter thrust into his palm, the wood smooth where a hand had worried it, the grain close as if it had grown stubborn on poor ground.

Margaret pinned up her kirtle and farthingale, for the road was wet, and the hem of a woman’s gown is a sponge that drinks mud and sorrow alike. Peter walked with them as far as his garden hedge. He was not a man prodigal of outward feeling; yet now his face worked, and his breath caught as if the cold had seized him.

He raised his hand over Gerard’s head. “God keep thee,” he said—only that, and it was a whole benediction.

The sun was just prying at the rim of the world when they crossed the stony field, dew clinging to every pebble and blade. The forest ahead stood like a dark loaf on a board, crusted with branches, smelling of damp needles and last year’s leaves. Halfway over the field Margaret kept turning her head, quick, like a hare that cannot help looking for the hawk. Then a sound, or a glimpse, or the tightening of some inward cord made her cry out.

She ran.

Not the measured running of one who knows she can outrun, but the wild flight of terror, skirts lifted, breath tearing her throat, screaming as she went as if the voice itself could push her feet faster. Martin and Gerard needed no second warning. Behind them, Ghysbrecht and his men came on hot, the clink and slap of pursuit crossing the open ground.

Resistance would have been madness there. They ran too.

“Only win the wood!” Martin shouted between breaths. “Only win the wood!”

The great trees seemed to stretch their boughs toward them like arms that promised shelter; the leaves—still and patient—hung as a screen. Hope leapt in Gerard’s chest with each stride, and in hope there was a strange lightness, as if his feet barely kissed the earth.

Then danger changed its shape.

Ghysbrecht, fiery even in age, flung himself upon his mule. The beast, goaded to a gallop, cut a wide semicircle; and while his men labored straight after the fugitives, their leader rode to head them off. He cared not though the others slipped into safety; he had set his malice on Gerard as a cook sets a spit: fixed, and turning it till it be done.

He gained the edge of the wood a heartbeat before Gerard. The mule’s breath steamed, sour with sweat. Ghysbrecht’s eyes blazed under his cap.

Margaret shrieked, threw herself against Gerard, clinging as if her body could be a shield. Gerard shook her off—no gentleness in the motion, only the hard necessity of a man whose life is measured in moments.

Ghysbrecht, in his ardor, forgot what hunters forget until it costs them: a beast at bay has teeth, and a man has more than teeth—he has hate, and he has the sudden fierce joy of striking back.

Gerard did not dodge. He sprang straight at the burgomaster with a savage, exulting cry, all the fear in him transmuted into one bright, brutal purpose. The oak staff whistled and came down on Ghysbrecht’s face with a crash like a cleaver on bone. The old man pitched under his mule’s tail, heels kicking a devil’s tattoo in the air, blood streaming and spattering his collar.

Before the mule’s startled snort had finished, the three were in the wood.

Behind them rose a yell—dismay first, then vengeance—like a pot boiling over. It told them what they already knew in the marrow: this was now a race for life.

“Why run?” Gerard panted, half turning. He held up the staff, darkened at the end. “You have your bow, and I have this.”

“Boy!” Martin roared, and his voice cracked like a whip. “The gallows! Follow me.”

They fled deeper, and soon the open field’s honest sounds were swallowed. The forest closed around them with its own breath: the hush of needles, the soft suck of wet earth underfoot, the far whisper of branches rubbing like old garments. Yet human noise followed—feet breaking twigs, men calling—and then, sharp and sickening, a cry like a pack of hounds opening on the scent.

Margaret moaned as she ran; her breath came in sobs that tasted of iron. Gerard’s jaw locked till his teeth ached, and his fingers clenched the staff as if it were rooted to his bones.

They came upon a stiff hazel coppice. Martin plunged into it as if it were standing corn, shouldering the young wood aside. Hazel switches slapped and sprang back, wet leaves smearing their faces. Fifty yards in, the ground forked into four blind paths—thin as scratches. Martin chose one without pause.

“Bend low,” he said.

Half crouching, they slid along, the hazel closing behind them as if it meant to erase their passage. Again little tortuous paths crossed theirs, weaving like threads in a net. They took one that seemed to turn them back upon themselves, and for a moment Gerard’s heart faltered; then it swung, and after a while led them into a thick pine grove.

Here the walking was firm and dry, the floor carpeted with needles that muted every footstep. Young firs stood so close you could not see three yards, their resinous scent thick in the air, clean and sharp as if the forest had been newly washed. No paths showed; it was a green room with a thousand narrow pillars.

When they had gone some way, Martin sat down as calmly as if he had reached a market bench. War had taught him this trick: when danger drops away for an instant, so does the trembling it leaves behind. He opened his wallet and drew out bread and a slice of ham.

The bread was coarse, dark with rye, its crust hard and honest. The ham was lean, salt-bitten, with a rim of fat that went translucent where his fingers warmed it. He chewed with the relish of a man who had lived on worse than hunger.

Gerard and Margaret stared at him, aghast, the chase still in their ears.

Martin jerked his chin at Gerard. “All Sevenbergen could not find you now. But you will lose your purse, Gerard, long before you get to Italy. Is that the way to carry a purse?”

Gerard looked down. A large triangular purse hung absurdly at his side, its chains entangled with the buckle and strap of his wallet as if it had grown there.

“This is none of mine,” he said. He tugged at it, but the coppice had snarled it past sense.

“It seems loath to leave me,” he said, and had to cut it loose with his knife.

They opened it. Silver coins of all sizes gleamed in the dim green light—some worn thin as wafers, some newer and sharp-edged. Yet the purse’s swollen look came chiefly from brown paper, folded and doubled, making a great show of wealth with no weight behind it.

A light broke on Gerard. “Why—it must be that old thief’s; see, stuffed with paper to deceive the world!”

How had it come to him? For a moment the thing seemed like a conjuration. Then the plain truth rose: the purse had hung at Ghysbrecht’s saddle-bow; and when Gerard flew at him, the staff and hand and fury had torn the chains away without his knowing it. With one gesture he had felled his enemy—and robbed him.

Gerard’s eyes shone, not with greed alone, but with that fierce, foolish delight a man feels when fortune appears to take his side in the midst of ruin. Margaret, however, went pale.

“Throw it away, Gerard,” she whispered, as if the coins could cry out their owner’s name. “Or let Martin take it back. Already they call you a thief. I cannot bear it.”

“Throw it away? Give it him back?” Gerard’s voice was low but hard. “Not a stiver. This is spoil lawfully won in battle from an enemy. Is it not, Martin?”

“Why, of course,” Martin said, mouth full, indifferent as a butcher weighing meat. “Send him back the brown paper, and you will. But the purse or the coin—that were a sin.”

“Oh, Gerard,” Margaret pleaded, and her hands trembled as if she were kneading dough that would not come together. “You are going to a distant land. We need the goodwill of Heaven. How can we hope for that if we take what is not ours?”

But to Gerard the happening wore a different color, like light through stained glass. “It is Heaven that gives it me by a miracle,” he said, and pressed the purse to him as if it were a relic. “Thus the favoured people spoiled the Egyptians, and were blessed.”

Margaret’s gaze fell. The damp of the forest lay on her lashes, making them heavy. “Take your own way,” she said humbly. “You are wiser than I am. You are my husband,” she added in a low murmur, as if the words were new on her tongue and tasted strange—like speaking in a dialect learned for love. “Is it for me to gainsay you?”

Those words—so yielding from Margaret, who till that day had held the whip-hand—made Martin glance at her, surprised, though he said nothing. Later they would return to him, and then he would understand them more.

Gerard, moved by her sudden obedience, kissed her. Then they went on hand in hand, Martin leading them deeper into the huge forest. The farther they pressed, the more the world of men seemed to thin behind them. Even the townspeople, bold enough on roads and in fields, did not venture so far into this trackless heart where sound itself changed—where a snapped twig could seem far as a horn-call, and a man could be lost a stone’s throw away.

And as danger fell behind like a cloak dropped in haste, Gerard’s nature—impetuous in both passion and repentance—turned upon itself. His conscience began to prick, each step giving it room.

“Martin,” he said at last, “would I had not struck quite so hard.”

“Whom?” Martin scoffed. “Oh, let that pass. He is cheap served.”

“I saw his grey hairs,” Gerard said, and his voice wavered. “As my stick fell on him. I doubt they will not from my sight this while.”

Martin grunted, contemptuous as ever of scruples that did not keep a man alive. “Who spares a badger for his grey hairs? The greyer your enemy is, the older; and the older, the craftier; and the craftier, the better for a little killing.”

“Killing?” Gerard stopped short, and shook all over, as if a cold wind had found him among the pines. “Killing, Martin? Speak not of killing!”

“I am much mistook if you have not,” Martin returned cheerfully, like a man pronouncing tomorrow’s weather.

“Now Heaven forbid!”

“The old vagabond’s skull cracked like a walnut. Aha!”

“Heaven and the saints forbid it!”

“He rolled off his mule like a stone shot out of a cart. Said I to myself, ‘There is one wiped out.’”

The old soldier grinned, ruthless as a blade that has been sharpened too long to pity the flesh it enters.

Gerard fell on his knees on the pine-needles, and began to pray for his enemy’s life. His lips moved fast, the words tumbling out in the learned tongue that had shaped his conscience—Latin, heavy with authority, yet in that instant feeling too grand and too rigid to carry the raw taste of fear and regret in his mouth.

At the sight of him, Martin’s patience broke like a snapped bowstring.

“Here’s mummery!” he cried. “What! you that set up for learning, know you not that a wise man never strikes his enemy but to kill him? And what is all this coil about killing old men? If it had been a young one now, with the joys of life waiting for him—wine, women, and pillage! But an old fellow at the edge of the grave, why not shove him in? Go he must, to-day or to-morrow; and what better place for greybeards? Now, if ever I should be so mischancy as to last so long as Ghysbrecht did, and have to go on a mule’s legs instead of Martin Wittenhaagen’s, and a back like this”—he struck the wood of his bow—“instead of this”—he struck the string—“I’ll thank and bless any young fellow who will knock me on the head, as you have done that old shopkeeper; malison on his memory.”

“Oh, culpa mea! culpa mea!” Gerard cried, smiting his breast, his prayer and shame spilling out in that same priestly tongue—words that meant much, yet could never quite translate the feel of a man’s staff striking a human face.

“Look there!” Martin sneered at Margaret. “He is a priest at heart still—and when he is not in ire, St. Paul, what a milksop!”

“Tush, Martin!” Margaret cried, sharply reproachful; then she went to Gerard, wound her arms about him, and drew him close. Her voice changed, soft and sure, as if she were laying a warm cloth over a burn.

“Sweetheart,” she murmured, “you forget. You went not a step out of the way to harm him who hunted you to your death. You fled from him. He it was who spurred on you. Then did you strike—but in self-defence, and a single blow, and with that which was in your hand. Malice had drawn knife, or struck again and again. How often have men been smitten with staves, not one but many blows, yet no lives lost! If then your enemy has fallen, it is through his own malice, not yours, and by the will of God.”

“Bless you, Margaret,” Gerard whispered, grasping at her words as a starving man grasps bread. “Bless you for thinking so!”

“Yes,” she said, and her tone hardened with practical dread, “but, beloved one, if you have had the misfortune to kill that wicked man, the more need is there that you fly with haste from Holland. Oh, let us on.”

“Nay, Margaret,” Gerard answered, rising slowly. “I fear not man’s vengeance, thanks to Martin here and this thick wood. Only Him I fear whose eye pierces the forest and reads the heart of man. If I but struck in self-defence, ’tis well; but if in hate—He may bid the avenger of blood follow me to Italy—to Italy? ay, to earth’s remotest bounds.”

“Hush!” Martin snapped, suddenly peevish, his head cocked as if his very skull were an ear. “I can’t hear for your chat.”

“What is it?” Gerard asked, the forest closing its damp silence around them.

“Do you hear nothing, Margaret? My ears are getting old.”

Margaret held her breath and listened. At first there was only the small music of the grove—the faint drip from needles, the soft stir where a branch settled. Then, threading through it, came a tuneful sound: one single stroke, deep and ringing, like a bell sounded once in some far church and carried here by miracle and air.

She described it so, in a whisper.

“Nay, I heard it,” Martin said.

“And so did I,” Gerard breathed. “It was beautiful. Ah—there it is again. How sweetly it blends with the air. It is a long way off. It is before us, is it not?”

“No, no,” Martin answered quickly. “The echoes of this wood confound the ear of a stranger. It comes from the pine grove.”

“What! the one we passed?”

“Why, Martin,” Margaret said, and now she saw his face in the dimness—drawn, almost ashen—“is this anything? You look pale.”

“Wonderful!” Martin said, with a sickly sneer that did not hide his dread. “He asks me is it anything? Come on, on! At any rate, let us reach a better place than this.”

“A better place—for what?” Gerard asked, though his stomach had already begun to sink, as if the sound had entered his body and weighed it down.

“To stand at bay, Gerard,” Martin said gravely; “and die like soldiers, killing three for one.”

“What’s that sound?” Gerard whispered, and the pines seemed to press in to listen too.

“It is the avenger of blood.”

“Oh, Martin, save him! Oh, Heaven be merciful—what new mysterious peril is this?”

“Girl,” Martin said, and the words came out like iron. “It’s a bloodhound.”
