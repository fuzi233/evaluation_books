Shirley emerged from her bedroom the following morning in a disposition that seemed entirely at odds with the gloom of the season. The air outside was thick and gray, the woods damp with a pale, waning autumn chill, yet Shirley's countenance burned with a light that suggested the warmth of an Italian sun.

"Who will take a walk with me?" she asked, her voice buoyant after breakfast. "Isabella and Gertrude?"

Such an invitation from Miss Keeldar was a rarity. Usually, the cousins found her distant, offering polite civilities that lacked depth. Yet today, the trio set out, drawn by her infectious energy. Shirley managed to animate even the mundane topics of conversation, infusing them with a spark that made the ordinary seem extraordinary.

However, the source of this sudden radiance was internal, not environmental. As they returned, a delay caused by instructions to her foreman, John, separated Shirley from her cousins for twenty minutes. When she finally rejoined them, she had lingered by the gate, and a summons to lunch interrupted her departure. She excused herself, retreating to her chamber.

An hour later, finding her absent from the dining hall, Isabella discovered Shirley sitting at the foot of her bed, her head resting heavily in her hand. She looked pale, profoundly thoughtful, and strangely quiet.

"You are not ill?" Isabella asked.

"A little sick," Shirley murmured.

By evening, the transformation had vanished. She ate, laughed, and conversed with her usual vigor, dismissing any mention of her earlier malaise as a fleeting faintness. Yet, a subtle shadow lingered. By the next day, a strange, unnerving quietude had settled over her. Her movements were measured, her voice subdued, and her expression carried a weight that refused to dissipate.

When the cousins pressed her regarding her health, she reacted with sharp indignation. "I am not ill!" she declared, her tone cutting. "If it suits me to grow plain, why should others fret? I have a right to alter at my ease."

Though she maintained a lively facade in company, alone she was harsh with herself. "Fool! Coward!" she cried out silently, blaming her trembling hands and racing heart. "Quail where no eye sees you!" She struggled to mask her anxiety, forcing herself into the role of the spirited hostess, while seeking solace only in the wild solitude of the moors. Mounted on her mare, Zoë, she rode long hours over Stilbro' Moor, indifferent to the rain or wind, until her face grew thin and her large eyes hollow.

Rumors began to circulate among the villagers. Some whispered that Shirley was involved in disastrous financial speculations regarding Hollow's Mill; others guessed she was secretly arranging a marriage. Gossip followed her everywhere, noting how she had ridden past Rushedge to visit Mr. Pearson Hall, a local solicitor.

Inside the schoolroom, the atmosphere was equally charged, though for different reasons. Henry Sympson, the tutor's pupil, sat before an open book, unable to focus.

"Henry, make haste. The afternoon is getting on," Mr. Moore urged gently.

"No, sir," Henry replied, his voice trembling. "I have not construed a line."

His tutor observed the boy's unusual demeanor. "My boy, you are ill."

"Sir, I am not worse in bodily health than usual, but my heart is full."

Moved by the boy's distress, Moore invited him to the fireside. Henry wept, confessing that his grief centered entirely on Shirley.

"She has just made her will," Henry sobbed, recounting the details Shirley had shared with him. She spoke of leaving him her estates, excluding his sisters, and bequeathing the manor to him. She had designated a specific beneficiary for the remainder—a "good man, who would make the best use of it"—describing him as gentle, brave, merciful, and possessing the "secret of religion pure and undefiled."

"She told me," Henry continued, his voice breaking, "that if I inherited, I was to take the name of Keeldar. And she said... she said she hoped I would never marry Malice, and if she died before me, I must lay my coffin by her side."

As Henry recounted these words, tears flowed freely. Moore listened with a mixture of compassion and professional composure, attempting to soothe the boy's agitated state.

Later, Mr. Moore summoned Shirley to the schoolroom. He found her working quietly, wearing a simple indoor dress. Her air was not the haughty defiance he expected, but a soft, youthful shyness.

"I ventured, Miss Keeldar, to send for you," Moore said, trying to maintain his stoic professorial demeanor, though his heart beat rapidly. "All your friends are unhappy about your health."

"I am quite well," she replied briefly, though her eyes betrayed a nervous alarm.

Moore pressed her, asking if she slept and ate as before. When she admitted she did not, he produced a small gold ring from his pocket—the one she had stolen from his watch-chain years prior.

"Try now," he said.

Shirley slipped her finger onto the band. It slid off easily, falling into his palm. The ring was far too large; her finger had shrunk.

"Nervous alarms should always be communicated," Moore said firmly, sensing the gravity beneath her evasions. "I believe confession would be half equivalent to cure."

"No," Shirley said abruptly, suspending her work. She revealed a fresh, jagged scar on her forearm, hidden beneath her sleeve. "On account of that little mark, I am obliged to look forward to a possibility that has its terrors."

She explained that three weeks prior, a dog named Phœbe had attacked her, biting her arm fiercely. Terrified, she had walked into the laundry and cauterized the wound herself with a hot iron from the stove, refusing to seek medical aid or tell anyone. She feared the bite was poisonous and dreaded the prospect of dying alone, surrounded by the panic and hysteria of her relatives.

"You need not fear," Moore assured her, his voice softening. "I have suspected the dog was not mad. Even if the virus entered your blood, you are too young and robust for it to take hold. I will investigate the dog's condition personally."

Relieved, Shirley asked for a promise. "In case the worst happens... they will smother me. My uncle will be horrified. Promise to befriend me—to keep Mr. Sympson away, to lock the chamber door against the surgeons, and to administer a narcotic if I give trouble."

"I promise all you ask," Moore said, bending over her. "Without comment, without reservation."

With the burden lifted, Shirley's spirits returned instantly. As they chatted, reflecting on their respective lives and families, the shadows of fear receded. However, as dinner approached, a flicker of anxiety crossed Shirley's face when she mentioned her brother Robert, who had been away in London for months.

"Write to him and urge him to come," she instructed Moore. "He must not abandon the county."

Moore nodded, penning a letter. As he finished, the dinner bell rang, signaling the end of their private conference. Shirley rose, gathering her silks, her face flushed with a mix of relief and lingering unease.
