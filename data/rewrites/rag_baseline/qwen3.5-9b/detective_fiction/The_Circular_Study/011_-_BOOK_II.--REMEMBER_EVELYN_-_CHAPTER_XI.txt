The afternoon sun beat down upon the Belleville hotel piazza, casting a glare that seemed designed to test the resolve of anyone attempting to read. In a remote corner, a well-dressed lady of indeterminate age sat hunched over a volume she turned with industrious diligence, though her eyes betrayed a far greater fascination with the world beyond the printed page. They were fine eyes, sharp yet kindly, drawn irresistibly to the spectacle unfolding before her.

The scene was a tableau of idle luxury: the azure sea dotted with white sails, a beach thronged with youths dressed in garish, multi-colored finery, and a village street bustling with activity. Even the lawn, scattered with couples wielding tennis rackets and golf clubs, commanded her attention. Children darted about, yet this amiable spinster, whose unmarried status was as plain as the dust motes dancing in the light, barely spared them a second glance. However, the mere sight of a young married pair, or an imposing elderly gentleman, was sufficient to break her concentration. She would lower her book, which she held perilously close to her face, her gaze wandering until the distraction faded.

Such a critique, if voiced, would likely fall upon the sprightly but frail octogenarian seated at the opposite end of the piazza. He was utterly absorbed in a novel held conspicuously open, his benevolent features occasionally breaking into smiles that suggested the text was delightfully amusing. Yet, midway through a chapter, he abruptly closed the book, extracted a newspaper, and spread it wide before him. Simultaneously, the lady at the far end lifted her spectacles to scrutinize two gentlemen emerging from the grand entrance between her and the old reader. Was it recognition, or merely curiosity? The manner in which she slammed her book shut and rose suggested she had spotted old acquaintances among the distinguished pair approaching her. Yet, joy was not on her face; instead, an air of embarrassed distress settled over her. Turning away, she retreated onto the lawn, standing with her back to them while mechanically stroking a small dog. The gesture revealed a profound disconnect; the animal, clearly terrified by her touch, trembled, betraying that she possessed no natural sympathy for creatures that frightened her when her composure finally cracked.

The two gentlemen, however, displayed total indifference to her presence. They walked mechanically to the end of the piazza and began retracing their steps. Their faces, now visible to the elderly man engrossed in his paper, prompted him to shift the sheet slightly—perhaps due to the sun's glare, or perhaps because he wished to catch a glimpse of the two remarkable men pausing nearby. One was elderly, matching the age of the man watching him, yet possessing the robust vitality of hardy Scottish stock. His figure remained upright and graceful, his expression cool and unflappable. Beside him stood a younger man, equally stalwart, whose eyes shrank with acute sensitivity from every soul he encountered, except for the mild old reader near whom they halted for a brief exchange.

"I don't think it does me any good," the young man muttered, his voice heavy with gloom. "I am wretched when with her, and doubly wretched when I try to forget myself for a moment out of her sight. We had better go back. I'd rather sit where she can see me than have her wonder... Oh, I'll be careful. But you must remember how unnerving is the very silence I am obliged to keep about what is destroying us all. I am nearly as ill as she."

They drifted away, leaving the old man to turn the page of his paper. Five minutes elapsed before they returned. This time, it was the elder gentleman who spoke, his voice lowered and his words obscured by discretion.

"Escape? South coast—she will forget to watch you for a clinging nature—impetuous, but foolishly affectionate—you know that. No danger. Found out. Time. A cheerful home. Courage. Happiness—all forgotten."

A gesture from the young man indicated he shared none of these hopes. Meanwhile, Miss Butterworth—surely a familiar figure to you—had her own opportunities for observation. Still stooping over the wriggling dog, which did not flee despite her hesitant touch, her attention was captured by two girls chatting on a nearby bench. They spoke freely, as if alone, gossiping about a fellow inmate of the hotel. Miss Butterworth leaned in, catching fragments of their conversation as they mentioned the name Adams.

"She is sick enough to need a nurse and a doctor," one girl exclaimed. "I glimpsed her yesterday; I've never seen eyes so big or cheeks so pale. Look at him! He must adore her; he won't speak to another woman, pacing that small, stifling room all day. Are they bride and groom? They're young enough, and if you've noticed her clothes..."

"Oh, don't talk of clothes," the other interjected. "I saw her the first day and felt like a dowdy compared to those wonderful waists and jackets. She was the victim of despair until she suddenly fell ill. I felt like a fright in that pale blue..."

"Blue suits blondes," the first replied dismissively. "You'd look dreadful in it. I didn't care for her attire, but I feared for us if she chose to smile upon the few men staying out this week. Yet, she isn't a beauty; no good nose, no handsome eye, no flawless complexion. It must be her mouth—or her walk. Did you notice? She floated, until she collapsed in that faint. I wonder why. Nobody was doing anything, not even her husband. Perhaps that troubled her. He looked very serious, and when she tried to attract his attention several times without success, she simply fell from her chair. That roused him. He hasn't left her since."

"Do they look happy, for such a rich and handsome couple?"

"Perhaps he is dissipated. I've noticed the old gentleman never leaves them."

"Well, handsome men are prone to dissipation. But I wouldn't care if—"

Just then, the dog yelped and bolted. Miss Butterworth, indignant at the turn of the girls' conversation, had unconsciously pinched it. The commotion distracted the young ladies, who moved off, leaving Miss Butterworth momentarily vacant. Then, a young man crossing her path delivered a whisper that filled her with dread.

"You recognize them? There is no mistake?"

"None," she replied, her voice tight. "The one this way is the young man who left Mr. Adams's house, and the other is the old gentleman who entered afterward."

She strolled to a seat, feeling a sudden weakness. In her mind's eye, she saw the young wife—sick, overwhelmed, battling a paralyzing fear—sink under this crushing blow, isolated from any woman capable of offering comfort. The father did not inspire confidence; beneath his polished exterior lay an icy demeanor that repelled the keen-eyed spinster. Remembering his coldness, she felt an irresistible urge to be near the suffering young creature, driven by a desperate need to ease the tension in her own aching heartstrings.

Rising to seek Mr. Gryce, she found him gone. Searching the piazza for the other two gentlemen, she saw them vanish around the corner toward a small smoking room. Unable to follow, she ascended the stairs. Meeting a maid in the upper hall, she requested Mrs. Adams. Told the young wife was ill, she was directed to a room at the end of a long corridor. Since all halls terminated in a window beneath a sofa, Miss Butterworth felt circumstances favored her. Taking her seat on the sofa, she waited in a state of complacent anticipation.

Instantly, a sweet, distressed voice drifted through the open transom behind her.

"Where is Tom? Oh, where is Tom? Why does he leave me? I'm afraid of what he may be tempted to do or say down on those great piazzas alone."

"Mr. Poindexter is with him," a measured, kind voice answered. "Mr. Adams was growing weary, and your father persuaded him to go down for a smoke."

"I must get up; indeed I must. Oh! the camphor—the—"

A sudden bustle followed. The poor young wife had fainted again.

Miss Butterworth cast miserable glances at the door, her heart heavy.

Inside the secluded smoking room, a terrible drama unfolded. The two gentlemen sat in forced attitudes, their cigars unlit or ignored, displaying a clear distaste for the act performed solely for the other's sake. An old man, strangely serious and yet oddly at home, entered and shut the door.

"Gentlemen," he announced immediately, "I am Detective Gryce of the New York police, and I am here—but I see that one of you at least knows why I am here."

Recognition flashed instantly. Denial was impossible. At the first note of the detective's authoritative tone, honed by years of experience, the young man collapsed in sudden shock. His companion, though less overtly overcome, showed unmistakable signs of devastation, realizing in a single moment that all their hopes were extinguished.
