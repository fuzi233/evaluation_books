The low, honeyed glow of the late Tuscan afternoon sun gilded Florence’s terracotta rooftops far below as Romola paused on the quiet side street, the ragged, heavy-shouldered old man she had half-dreaded and half-expected to confront her stepping out from a doorway shadow.
“I would speak with you,” Baldassarre said, his voice rough and hoarse. It was plain he had followed her, waited for her there. The secret that had lurked behind Tito’s polished, smiling lies for months was finally unspooling, and Romola met his gaze with the same quiet submission she might have brought to a required penance.
“Yes,” she said. “You wish to go somewhere no one can overhear us?”
“Somewhere *he* cannot stumble on us unawares,” Baldassarre muttered, glancing over his shoulder with a skittish, hunted wariness. “Out in the open air, far from the crowded streets.”
“I often walk up to San Miniato at this hour,” Romola offered. “If you would like, I will set out ahead, and you may follow. It is a steep climb, but we shall find total solitude there.”
He nodded assent, and Romola turned to start the long upward path. For many women of Florence, venturing to a remote, half-wild hilltop with a ragged stranger Tito had publicly labeled a madman would have seemed unthinkably reckless. But Romola was no stranger to hardship, and she welcomed the long stretch of climbing as a reprieve: a small delay before the next inevitable blow of truth landed on her.
By the time they neared the line of gnarled old cypresses cresting the hill, the sun hung low enough to cast long, deep shadows across the stony ground. Romola paused and turned, waiting for Baldassarre to catch up, and watched him sink heavily onto a weathered stone ledge the moment he reached her. The thick, sturdy frame that had shouldered its way through the Duomo crowds months earlier, rope slung over his shoulder, was gaunt now, sapped by years of slavery, prison, and gnawing hunger. The exertion of the climb left him trembling, his eyes distant and vague, as if his thoughts kept slipping their moorings.
“The hill is unforgiving,” Romola said gently, sitting beside him. “I fear long hardship has robbed you of your strength?”
He turned his head and fixed his eyes on her, silent for long moments, the words he had rehearsed so many times slipping just out of his reach now that he stood face to face with the man’s wife who had every reason to hate Tito. She stayed perfectly still, not wishing to seem impatient, watching the distant, unkempt old man who looked like nothing more than a common street beggar, even as the faint, familiar pang of pity she had learned to carry for Florence’s forgotten poor welled in her chest.
Slowly, his gaze sharpened, and he spoke with abrupt, burning emphasis:
“Ah! You would have been my daughter.”
A hot flush surged up Romola’s cheeks, then drained away as swiftly as it had come, leaving her lips pale and parted, like a marble statue frozen in horror. In that single instant, every scattered, inconsistent detail of Tito’s origin story slotted into place, and she understood the full truth without needing to hear another word.
Baldassarre felt the shock of her recognition, and his voice gathered new, eager urgency, his rough, calloused hand closing around her arm.
“You are a woman of proud, noble blood, are you not? You go to hear Fra Girolamo preach, you hate all falseness that smiles and pretends to goodness. You hate your husband, do you not?”
“O God—were you truly his father?” Romola breathed, too caught up in the rush of old memories to register his question. “Or was it as Tito told us? Did you take him in when he was small, alone and unclaimed?”
“Ah! You believe me—you know what he truly is!” Baldassarre exulted, his grip on her arm tightening as if the contact with her gave him new strength. “You will help me, will you not?”
“Yes,” Romola said, not understanding the vengeful weight he put to the question. She laid her soft palm over his rough hand, tears stinging her eyes. “It is so piteous. Tell me—you were a great scholar, once, you taught him all you knew. How did it come to this?”
Her words trailed off, a flicker of Tito’s story of this man’s madness darting across her mind as she scanned his ragged appearance for any trace of the refinement of a learned man. But she did not pull her hand away, holding perfectly still, listening with new, quiet caution.
“It is all gone,” Baldassarre said, his voice cracking. “All of it. They would not listen to me, because he lied and called me mad, and they dragged me off to a dark prison cell. I am old now, and my wits will never return. The whole world has turned against me.” He paused, his eyes dropping as a wave of hopeless despondency washed over him, before he looked back up at her, fierce and urgent again. “But *you* are not against me. He tricked you into loving him, and he has betrayed you. You hate him, do you not? He tricked me too. I was a lonely old man, and he was beautiful and soft-spoken, a little boy being beaten in the streets, and I took him in. He slept curled against my chest when he was small, I taught him every line of the classics I knew, every secret of old languages, I meant for all my books, my gold, my rare gems, to be his one day. He stole my gems and sold them, and left me rotting in slavery in a Turkish camp. He never came looking for me. When I stumbled back to Florence, ragged and destitute, he denied he knew me. He said I was a raving madman.”
“Tito told us his father was dead—drowned at sea,” Romola whispered, faint with shock. “Surely… surely he must have believed that once, in those early days, when we first knew each other. He could not have been so base *then*.” A vivid memory rose up of the soft, laughing young man who had sat beside her in her father’s library, turning the pages of ancient manuscripts with her, and the tears burst forth in a great, shaking sob.
“Tears are no use for us,” Baldassarre said, with a flicker of impatience. “They only douse the fire that burns in my chest, the fire that does the work. They will only slow us down. Listen to me.”
Romola started a little, a flash of fear darting through her that this might be a madman’s raving after all, but her deep, gut belief in the truth of his story far outweighed that fear, and she leaned in, gentle so as not to wound him with any sign of doubt.
“Tell me,” she said softly, “how did you lose all your memories, all your scholarship?”
“I was very ill, for so long I cannot count the days. It was all a blank. I remember nothing of that time, until I woke sitting in the sun on a pile of stones, and everything else was dark around me. Slowly, little by little, I began to feel a hunger for something I could not name, a hunger that would not leave me. When I was on the ship sailing back to Italy, I finally knew what I was longing for: for the Boy to come back, for all my old thoughts to come home to me, for I was locked outside my own mind, as if behind a wall of stone. I am still outside that wall. All I can feel is dark.”
Baldassarre’s voice went soft and dreamy, and he sank his head into his hands, falling quiet. All of Romola’s caution melted away, replaced by a sharp, familiar pity: had she not watched her own father grieve for years over the lost son, Dino, who had thrown away his birthright?
“Once, it all came back to me,” Baldassarre said after a long pause. “I had all my wits back, I could see all my old books, my gems, I knew I held him in my power. I went to denounce him where the lanterns burned bright and the trees grew in the square, but he lied again, called me mad, and they dragged me back to prison. Wickedness wears armor, it is strong, no one will see past his fine smile.”
Fierceness flamed back in his eyes, and he grasped her arm again.
“But *you* will help me. He has betrayed you too. He has another wife, and small children with her. He lets her think he is lawfully married to her, and she is a foolish, helpless little thing, no wits to see through his lies. I will show you where she lives.”
A hot, sharp flush of outraged indignity burned through Romola at those words, the woman’s natural rage at this double betrayal of the vows he had sworn to her surging to the surface. Baldassarre felt her sympathy, and pressed on, eager.
“You hate him, do you not? There is no love left between you. I know women can hate, when they have proud blood. You hate falseness more than anything, and you crave revenge.”
Romola sat frozen, overwhelmed by the clashing storm of feelings warring in her chest, not even noticing that his rough grip was bruising the soft skin of her arm.
“You will plan it for us,” Baldassarre whispered, leaning in, his voice tight with eagerness. “I have learned it by heart, you are his rightful, noble wife. You go to hear the preacher who speaks of justice and vengeance, you will help us make things right. You can think clearly when my wits slip away, when everything but the fire vanishes. That fire is justice, it is the hand of God, it will never die. You believe that, do you not? If no one will hang him for robbing me, for leaving me to rot in chains, you will strip off that armor of his, that mask of fine lies, so he is left exposed. Then I will stab him. I have a knife, and my arm is still strong enough to drive it home.”
He slid his hand under his tunic, pulling out a sharp, worn blade, running his thumb along the edge absently, as if the cold metal was the only thing keeping his ragged train of thought from slipping away.
Romola felt her breath catch, every new truth he spoke making the path ahead look steeper and more impossible. She knew better than to spout useless pleas for mercy to a man in this fevered state, so she chose to say nothing of his plan for vengeance, buying time for his excitement to fade by asking a question of her own, her voice trembling a little.
“You say this other wife is foolish and helpless, and truly believes he is her lawful husband. Perhaps… perhaps he married her first, before he married me.”
“I cannot tell,” Baldassarre said, pausing mid-stroke along the knife’s edge, his face going blank and bewildered. “I cannot remember that part. I only know where she lives. I will take you to see her, but not now,” he added quickly, glancing down at the darkening valleys. “He might be there. Night is falling fast.”
“It is true,” Romola said, starting to her feet, suddenly aware the sun had vanished entirely below the horizon, the distant hills turning deep purple in the dusk. “But you will come to take me? When?”
“At midday tomorrow,” Baldassarre said, already dreaming that she burned with the same hunger for vengeance as he did. “Meet me where you first saw me, in the Duomo. I will be there at ten, if you do not come I will wait until noon. Can you remember that?”
“Midday,” he repeated, gripping her arm again as he staggered to his feet, the knife still held in his other hand. “Same spot, midday. And after that, we will have our revenge. He shall feel the sharp edge of justice. The whole world is against me, but you will help me.”
“I would help you in other ways,” Romola said, making a soft, careful first attempt to break his illusion. “I fear you go hungry, you work hard for no pay. I would bring you food, warm clothes, a place to rest, so you know there is someone who cares for you.”
“Speak no more of that,” Baldassarre snarled, fierce and angry. “I will take nothing less. Help me wring one drop of vengeance out of this life before I die. All I have left is my knife. It is sharp, and when the blade sinks in, a man sees the face of the one who kills him before he dies. His last sight will be my face.”
He loosed his hold on her arm, sinking back down onto the stone ledge, exhaustion washing over him. Romola knew she could not reason with him tonight, not when his mind was still burning with that hot, unquenchable fire.
“Midday, then,” she said, clear and steady.
“Go,” he murmured, the energy draining out of him entirely. “I will rest here a while.”
She hurried down the hill, her heart heavy. When she reached the last bend in the path where she could still see the stone ledge, she turned back, and saw he was still seated there, alone in the gathering dark, long after the last of the sunset light had faded.
