I clamped down on my private misgivings, and respectfully asked Mr. Franklin to go on with his story. “Don’t fidget, Betteredge,” he chided, and did exactly that.

Our young gentleman’s first bits of news told me his investigation into the wicked Colonel and the Moonstone had kicked off with a visit he’d paid to our family lawyer, Mr. Bruff, out in Hampstead, before he ever turned up at our door. A chance offhand remark Franklin made over dinner one day, alone with Bruff, let slip that his father had tasked him with delivering a birthday present to Miss Rachel. One detail led to another, until the lawyer spilled the full truth of what that present actually was, and how the late Colonel and old Mr. Blake had struck their strange, unholy bargain. The facts of it are so outlandish I’d never trust my own clumsy words to do them justice, so I’ll stick as close as I can to repeating Mr. Franklin’s own account.

“You remember the spell, Betteredge, when my father was tearing himself apart trying to prove his claim to that blasted Dukedom? Well, that was the same time my uncle Herncastle sailed home from India. My father figured out his brother-in-law was sitting on a stack of papers that would win him that lawsuit, easy. He called on the Colonel, pretending he’d just popped round to welcome him back to England. But that old scoundrel wasn’t born yesterday. ‘You want something,’ he said, ‘or you’d never stoop low enough to be seen calling on a man like me.’ My father saw he had no choice but to show his whole hand, and admitted straight out he needed those documents. The Colonel asked for a single day to think his answer over. What he sent back was one of the most bizarre letters a man ever wrote, the same letter Bruff showed me myself. He opened by saying he had a favour to ask of my father, and proposed they trade friendly services. The fortune of war, that’s how he phrased it, had put him in possession of one of the largest diamonds on the face of the earth. And he was convinced neither he nor that jewel would ever be safe, under any roof anywhere on the globe, so long as they stayed together. Under those dire circumstances, he’d decided to park the diamond with someone else. That someone wouldn’t have to run a scrap of risk. All they needed do was lock it away in some properly guarded spot — a banker’s vault, a jeweller’s strongroom, anywhere purpose-built to keep high-value valuables safe. Their only actual responsibility was a passive one: either they, or a trusted representative, had to receive a note from the Colonel at a pre-arranged address, on a set day every single year. That note would only say one thing: that John Herncastle was still breathing. If that day rolled around and no note arrived? The Colonel said that was sure proof he’d been murdered. Only then, and never before, were the sealed instructions stored alongside the diamond to be opened, and followed to the letter. If my father agreed to this ridiculous charge? The Colonel’s coveted lawsuit papers were his, no strings attached. That was the whole of the letter.”

“What did your father do, sir?” I asked.

“Do?” Mr. Franklin huffed. “I’ll tell you what he did. He dragged that precious gift of human sense we call common sense out to stare the Colonel’s letter down. The whole thing, he declared, was absolute nonsense. Somewhere tramping around the Indian wilderness, the Colonel had picked up some cheap lumpy crystal and convinced himself it was a diamond. As for the threats of murder, all those silly precautions to keep himself and his chunk of crystal safe? This was the nineteenth century, he said — any sane man with a problem like that just called the police. The Colonel had been a notorious opium addict for decades, and my father decided that if the only way to get the papers he needed was to humour a drug-crazed old man’s fantasy? He’d take on that silly, no-effort ‘responsibility’ in a heartbeat. Off the diamond and its sealed instructions went to his banker’s strongroom, and Bruff got the job of opening those yearly notes from the Colonel, as my father’s representative. No sensible man in his position would have seen it any other way. Nothing in this world seems real to us, Betteredge, unless it lines up with our own tiny, trivial little lives. We only ever believe a real romance is true if we read it printed in a newspaper.”

It was plain as day to me Franklin thought his father’s dismissal of the whole business was hasty, and dead wrong. “What’s your own private opinion on it, sir?” I asked.

“Let’s finish the story of the Colonel first,” Franklin waved me off. “There’s a ridiculous lack of system to the English way of thinking, Betteredge — and that question of yours is a perfect example. When we’re not building new machines, we’re, mentally speaking, the sloppiest lot of people on the entire planet.”

“Ah,” I thought to myself, “there’s that foreign education showing through. He picked up that snide little jab at us while he was living in France, no doubt.”

Franklin picked the thread back up and kept going.

“My father got the papers he wanted, and never laid eyes on his brother-in-law again after that. Year after year, on the exact prearranged day, that pre-written note turned up, and Bruff opened it. I’ve seen the whole stack of them, all short, business as can be: ‘Sir — This is to certify that I am still a living man. Let the Diamond be. John Herncastle.’ That was all he ever wrote, regular as clockwork. Until six or eight months back, when the note changed for the very first time. It said: ‘Sir — They tell me I am dying. Come to me, and help me make my will.’ Bruff went straight out, and found him holed up in a little standalone suburban villa, surrounded by its own grounds, where he’d lived completely alone ever since he came home from India. He had dogs, cats, birds to keep him company, but not a single other human around except a housekeeper who popped in once a day to clean, and the doctor who sat at his bedside. The will was dead simple. The Colonel had burned through almost all his fortune funding his mad chemical experiments. It only had three clauses, all dictated from his bed while he was still sharp as a tack. The first one set aside money to keep his animals fed and cared for for the rest of their lives. The second founded a whole professorship of experimental chemistry at a university up north. The third left the Moonstone, as a birthday present, to his niece Miss Rachel — on the strict condition that my father agreed to be his executor. My father said no, at first. Then he changed his mind, partly because they promised him the executorship wouldn’t be any work at all, and partly because Bruff pointed out, for Rachel’s sake, that the diamond might actually be worth real money after all.”

“Did the Colonel ever give a reason, sir,” I asked, “why he left that diamond to Miss Rachel specifically?”

“He not only gave a reason — he wrote the whole thing into the will,” Franklin said. “I’ve got an extract right here, I’ll show you in a minute. Don’t be slovenly-minded, Betteredge! One thing at a time. You’ve heard about the will. Now you get to hear what happened after the Colonel died. By law, we had to get the diamond valued before we could even prove the will was legitimate. Every jeweller we brought in confirmed the Colonel’s claim right away: that was one of the biggest diamonds in the whole world. The exact valuation was tricky as hell, though. Its size made it a total anomaly on the diamond market. Its strange yellow colour put it in a category all its own. And to top all that uncertainty off, there was a flaw, dead centre in the heart of the stone. Even with that big downside, though, the lowest any of them would value it was twenty thousand pounds. Imagine my father’s shock! He was a hair’s breadth away from refusing to act as executor, and letting that magnificent jewel slip right out of the family’s grasp. He was so curious by that point he dug out the old sealed instructions that had been stored with the diamond all those years. Bruff showed me that document, along with all the rest of the papers — and in my mind, it gives us the full clue to what that conspiracy threatening the Colonel’s life was really about.”

“So you do believe, sir,” I said, “that there actually was a conspiracy to kill him?”

“I don’t have my father’s perfect brand of convenient common sense,” Franklin laughed. “So I believe the Colonel’s life was under threat, exactly the way he said it was. Those sealed instructions make it obvious how he still died peacefully in his bed, after all. If he was ever murdered — that is, if his regular yearly note never turned up on the right day — my father was ordered to smuggle the Moonstone over to Amsterdam, hand it off to a famous master diamond-cutter there, and have him split it up into four to six separate perfect stones. Those stones would then be sold for all they could fetch, and every penny of the proceeds would go straight to fund that experimental chemistry professorship the Colonel later endowed in his will. Now, Betteredge, use that sharp old wit of yours, and work out what that conclusion tells us.”

I strained my wits, but they’re the messy, un-systematic English kind, and I muddled it all up, until Franklin walked me through it.

“Look,” he said. “The Colonel made the very existence of the whole, unbroken Moonstone entirely dependant on no one murdering him. He didn’t just tell the people he was scared of: ‘Kill me, and you’ll never get the diamond, it’s locked away in a bank vault you can’t break into.’ He told them: ‘Kill me, and the Moonstone stops being the Moonstone. Its whole identity gets destroyed.’ What does that mean?”

I thought I had a flash of that clever foreign insight he was always going on about. “I know!” I said. “It means making the stone worth less, so the rogues wouldn’t bother stealing it!”

“Nothing of the sort,” he said. “I looked that up. That flawed big diamond, cut up into four to six perfect brilliants? It’d be worth far more than the whole, imperfect single stone. If the conspiracy was just ordinary thieves out for cash, the Colonel’s instructions would have made the Moonstone even more valuable to steal. They’d get more money for it, and it’d be a hundred times easier to sell on the open market, once it was cut up small in Amsterdam.”

“Lord save us, sir!” I burst out. “What kind of plot was it, then?”

“A plot put together by the Indians who owned that gem originally,” Franklin said. “A plot rooted in some ancient Hindu religious superstition. That’s my theory, backed up by the family paper I’ve got right here in my pocket.”

I saw it immediately, then — why those three Indian jugglers turning up at our house had seemed so important to Franklin.

“I don’t want to force my idea down your throat,” Franklin went on. “The thought of a small group of devoted servants to an old Hindu god spending decades fighting every possible obstacle and danger just to get their sacred gem back makes perfect sense to me, when you think about how patient Oriental people are, and how deep their religious beliefs run. But I’m an imaginative man, you see — I don’t only think the butcher, the baker, and the tax man are the only real things in the world. Let’s put my guess aside for now, and get to the only practical question that matters to us. Did that conspiracy to get the Moonstone outlive the Colonel? And did the Colonel know it was still going on, when he wrote that legacy to his niece?”

I started seeing Lady Verinder and Miss Rachel at the very end of all this mess, now. Not a single word Franklin said slipped past me.

“I wasn’t happy at all, once I learned the full story of the Moonstone, to be the one bringing it down here to this house,” Franklin said. “But Bruff reminded me someone had to put that legacy directly in Rachel’s hands, and it might as well be me as anyone else. Right after I took the diamond out of the bank, I swore I was followed down the street by a shabby, dark-skinned man. I went back to my father’s house to pick up my luggage, found a letter there that unexpectedly delayed me in town. I took the diamond back to the bank, and I spotted that same shabby man a second time. I pulled the diamond out of the vault again this morning, saw him a third time, lost him in the crowd, and caught the early morning train instead of the afternoon one I’d planned for. Here I am, the diamond safe and sound in my pocket — and what’s the first news I get? Three wandering Indian jugglers were already at this house, asking questions, pumping the local boy for information about a man coming down from London, and something that man was supposed to be carrying. The trick where they poured ink in the boy’s hand, and told him to look into it and see that distant man, and the thing in his pocket? That’s just hocus-pocus, I’ve seen that done a hundred times in the East. The only real question we have to answer is: am I making a big deal out of nothing, or are we actually looking at proof that those Indians are hot on the Moonstone’s trail the second it’s pulled out of that bank vault?”

Neither of us felt much like digging into that question right that second. We stared at each other, then stared out at the tide, creeping slow and smooth higher and higher over the sucking Shivering Sand.

“What’re you thinking about?” Franklin asked me, suddenly.

“I was thinking, sir,” I said, “that I’d like to heave that diamond straight into the quicksand, and settle the whole question right there.”

“If you’ve got twenty thousand pounds in cash in your pocket, Betteredge,” he grinned, “say the word, and in it goes.”

It’s funny how even the silliest little joke can take a load off your chest when you’re wound tight. We both got a whole lot of silly laughs out of the idea of vanishing Miss Rachel’s rightful property, and landing Mr. Blake the executor in a whole heap of legal trouble — though where that merriment came from, I can’t for the life of me work out now.

Franklin was the first to yank our conversation back to the matter at hand. He pulled an envelope out of his pocket, opened it, and handed me the paper inside.

“Betteredge,” he said, “for my aunt’s sake, we have to face up to what the Colonel’s real motive was, leaving this legacy to his niece. Think about how Lady Verinder treated her brother, from the day he came back to England, to the day he told you he’d remember Rachel’s birthday. Read that.”

It was the extract from the Colonel’s will. I’ve still got it by me as I write these words, so I’ll copy it out word for word, exactly:
> Thirdly, and lastly, I give and bequeath to my niece, Rachel Verinder, daughter and only child of my sister, Julia Verinder, widow — if her mother, the said Julia Verinder, shall be living on the said Rachel Verinder’s next Birthday after my death — the yellow Diamond belonging to me, and known in the East by the name of The Moonstone: subject to this condition, that her mother, the said Julia Verinder, shall be living at the time. And I hereby desire my executor to give my Diamond, either by his own hands or by the hands of some trustworthy representative whom he shall appoint, into the personal possession of my said niece Rachel, on her next birthday after my death, and in the presence, if possible, of my sister, the said Julia Verinder. And I desire that my said sister may be informed, by means of a true copy of this, the third and last clause of my Will, that I give the Diamond to her daughter Rachel, in token of my free forgiveness of the injury which her conduct towards me has been the means of inflicting on my reputation in my lifetime; and especially in proof that I pardon, as becomes a dying man, the insult offered to me as an officer and a gentleman, when her servant, by her orders, closed the door of her house against me, on the occasion of her daughter’s birthday.

A few more lines followed that, laying out that if Lady Verinder or Miss Rachel were dead by the time the Colonel died, the diamond was to be sent off to Holland in line with those old sealed instructions, and all the proceeds from selling it would go straight to that chemistry professorship up north.

I handed the paper back to Franklin, too troubled to find the right words to say. Up until that moment, I’d been fully convinced the Colonel died as wicked as he’d lived. I won’t say that will extract completely changed my mind: but it left me staggered, all the same.

“Well?” Franklin said. “You’ve read his own words. What do you make of it? Am I here dragging that cursed Moonstone into my aunt’s house, acting as the blind tool of some horrible last revenge of his? Or am I just carrying out the last wish of a penitent dying Christian man?”

“It seems awful hard to believe, sir,” I said, “that he died with a terrible vengeance in his heart, and a terrible lie on his lips. God alone knows the truth. Don’t ask me.”

Franklin sat twisting and crumpling that will extract between his fingers, like he thought he could squeeze the truth right out of the paper. At the same time, his whole mood shifted. He’d been bright and sharp and brisk a minute before, now he suddenly turned slow, solemn, stuck deep in thought.

“This question has two sides,” he said. “An Objective side, and a Subjective side. Which one do we take?”

Oh right, he’d had a German education as well as a French one. I’d guessed the French side of him had been running the show up until now, and the German side was just shoving it out of the way. I live by a simple rule: never draw attention to anything you don’t understand. I stared at him blankly, and said nothing at all.

“Let’s dig out the real hidden meaning of this,” he said. “Why did the Colonel leave the diamond to Rachel? Why not just leave it to my aunt directly?”

“That’s not hard to guess, sir,” I said. “Colonel Herncastle knew Lady Verinder well enough to know she’d never accept a single penny of any legacy coming from him.”

“But how did he know Rachel wouldn’t refuse it, too?”

“Is there a young lady alive, sir, who could turn down a birthday present that was the Moonstone?”
