“The soup is hot,” Gerard said.

“But how shall we bring it to our lips?” the elder man asked, his voice heavy with weariness.

“Father,” Margaret replied softly, a glint in her eye, “the young man has brought us straws.”

“Ay, ay!” the old man murmured. “But my bones are stiff, and this fire burns too fiercely for one such as I to kneel over with these short reeds. St. John the Baptist, but the lad is clever!”

Even as he spoke, Gerard acted. In an instant, he untied the knot at his chest, removed his hat, placed a stone into each corner of it, then wrapped his hand in the tail of his jerkin and swiftly lifted the flask from the flames. He wedged it securely between the stones and set the hat before the old man with a cheerful grin. The man trembled as he slipped the rye-straw pipe into his mouth and drew in a long breath.

Lo and behold—his pale, drawn face began to glow. A warmth spread across his features until he seemed reborn. As he exhaled:

“Hippocrates and Galen!” he exclaimed. “This is *soupe au vin*—the restorative of all restoratives! Blessed be the nation that devised it, the woman who made it, and the youth who brought it to those on the brink of fainting! Have a sip, my dear, while I tell our young host the history and virtues of this divine concoction. This elixir was unknown to the ancients—we find no trace of it in their medical treatises or in the popular tales that recount their remedies. Even Hector, in the *Iliad*, if memory serves—”

(Margaret: “Alas, he’s off again.”)

—was offered wine by one of the ladies in the poem, yet refused, saying he was about to enter battle and could not afford to weaken himself. But had the *soupe au vin* existed in Troy, he surely would have added in hexameter verse, *‘But a soupe au vin, madam, I will taste, and gratefully.’* Not only would that have been proper courtesy—a trait no true commander lacks—but failing to do so would have marked him as shallow and unprepared, unfit to lead men into war. Soldiers going to battle need sustenance and strength, as history proves: foolish generals who sent hungry troops against well-fed enemies were defeated time and again, even by smaller forces. The Romans lost a great battle in northern Italy to Hannibal, the Carthaginian, solely because they neglected this truth. Now, this celestial brew invigorates the limbs and lifts the spirit in a single draught; had Hector drunk it just before combat, aided by Phoebus, Venus, and the blessed saints, the Greeks might well have suffered defeat. Observe how weak and weary I was but moments ago—now, after this heavenly cordial, I am bold as Achilles and strong as an eagle.”

“Oh, father, now? An eagle, indeed?”

“Girl, I defy you and all the world. Ready as a steed in full foam to gallop from here to Rotterdam, strong enough to face life’s trials—even poverty and age, which philosophers call the *summum malum*. Nonsense! Unless a man’s life has been wasted—though, by the way, most lives are. Now, let us speak of modern times!”

“Father! Dear father!”

“Fear me not, child; I’ll be brief—unreasonably, even unseasonably brief. The *soupe au vin* does not appear in modern medicine, but this only confirms what we already know: for centuries, physicians have been fools, peddling chicken broth and gold decoctions, praising the weakest meat and the least reactive metal. Mountebanks! Fools! Murderers! Since no wisdom comes from them, let us turn to chronicles. First, we find Duguesclin, a French knight, preparing to fight the English—who ruled half France at the time and struck hard by sea and land—drank not one, but three *soupes au vin* in honor of the Holy Trinity. Then he charged the enemy and, as expected, slew many and drove the rest into the sea. Yet he was merely the first in a long line of noble warriors who, through this sacred tonic, were sustained, strengthened, and comforted.”

“Dear father,” Margaret pleaded, holding the hat gently in both hands, “do add yourself to that honored company before the soup cools.”

The old man smiled, and once more inserted the straw.

This spared them the modern examples—and gave Gerard a chance to tell Margaret how proud her mother would be that her soup had helped a learned man.

“Ay,” she said, “but she’d be displeased to see her son give all and take nothing. Why did you bring only two straws?”

“Fair mistress,” he answered, “I hoped you would allow me to share your straw. There were only two.”

Margaret smiled and blushed. “Never beg when you may command,” she said. “The straw is not mine—it belongs to you. You cut it in that field.”

“I cut it, and thus it was mine. But then your lips touched it—and so it became yours.”

“Did it? Then I lend it to you. There—now it is yours again. Your lips have touched it.”

“No,” he said, “it belongs to us both now. Let us divide it.”

“By all means—you have a knife.”

“No,” he said, “I won’t cut it—that would be ill luck. I’ll bite it. There—I keep my half. Yours, I doubt, you’ll burn once home.”

“You don’t know me,” she replied. “I waste nothing. I’ll likely make it into a hairpin—or something.”

Her words surprised Gerard, not with anger, but with renewed resolve. He fell silent.

With bread and soup finished, the old scholar prepared to continue his journey. But Gerard, though clever, could not retie his ribbon as Catherine had done. Margaret watched him for a moment, then offered help—girls at her age delight in being coy and kind, playful and tender by turns, and she sensed she had put him off balance. She bowed her head, its crown of auburn hair gleaming like molten copper beneath silver strands, and with delicate fingers, she worked the stubborn ribbon into place. Soft touches shaped it, gentle strokes smoothed it. A sudden thrill passed through the innocent youth, and visions of a new world—of feeling, of tenderness—opened before him.

And Margaret, unaware, prolonged the moment. It is not natural for women to rush through matters of the toilet. When at last her fingers subdued the ends, she still felt uneasy—so, with a motion unique to her sex, she pressed her palm gently against the center of the knot, making it convex, as if whispering, *Now be good and stay.* The kiss was given to the ribbon—but the heart of the wearer leapt to meet it.

“There,” she said, stepping back to admire her work. Then, lifting her eyes to seek simple approval, she met a gaze so intense, so full of devotion, that she lowered her lashes and flushed deeply. A trembling seized her, and she retreated, cheeks burning, taking her father’s arm on the other side. Gerard, ashamed of having startled her, took the remaining arm. And so the two walked downcast and aware, supporting the old man in silence.

They entered Rotterdam through the Schiedamze Poort. Since Gerard knew nothing of the city, Peter directed him to the Hooch Straet, where the Stadthouse stood. He himself was going with Margaret to his cousin’s house in the Ooster-Waagen Straet. Almost at the gate, their paths diverged. They exchanged a friendly farewell, and Gerard disappeared into the bustling town.

A profound loneliness settled upon him, despite the crowded streets. He regretted, too late, that out of shyness he had not asked who they were or where they lived.

“Beshrew my modesty!” he muttered. “Their speech and bearing were above their station, and something whispered they wished to remain unknown. I shall never see her again. Oh, weary world, I hate you and your ways. To think I must meet beauty, goodness, and learning—three pearls beyond price—and never see them again!”

Lost in sorrow, he wandered aimlessly, letting his feet carry him. Soon, he found himself among a crowd moving in one direction. He joined them, reasoning they must be heading toward the Stadthouse. Before long, the noisy throng emerged not onto the Stadthouse, but onto a wide meadow beside the Maas River. There, the source of the gathering became clear: games of every sort were underway—wrestling, the game of palm, the quintain, legerdemain, archery, tumbling—women as well as men performing with great skill, much to the delight of the audience. A trained bear stood on his head, marched upright, and bowed gravely to his master. A hare beat a drum, and a rooster strutted on tiny stilts, disdainful and proud.

Gerard laughed now and then, but the festive scene could not truly cheer him—his heart was elsewhere. Hearing a young man say the Duke had been there but had gone to the Stadthouse to entertain burgomasters, aldermen, prize competitors, and their guests, Gerard suddenly remembered he was hungry—and would love to dine with a prince. He left the riverside and, this time, found the Hooch Straet. It led quickly to the Stadthouse.

But when he arrived, he was turned away—at one door, then another—until he reached the grand courtyard gate. It was guarded by soldiers and overseen by a pompous major-domo, resplendent in an embroidered collar and a golden chain, clutching a white staff tipped with gold. A crowd gathered before him, pleading in vain. They came forward like waves, only to retreat as quickly. Gerard struggled to approach, and when he stood four heads from the gate, he saw something that made his heart leap: Peter, with Margaret on his arm, begging humbly for entry.

“My cousin the alderman is not at home—they say he’s here.”

“What does that matter to me, old man?”

“If you won’t let us in to him, at least take this leaf from my tablet. See, I’ve written his name—he will come out to us.”

“For what do you take me? I deliver no messages. I guard the gate.”

Then, in a thunderous voice, he roared:

“No strangers enter—only competitors and their parties!”

“Come, old man,” someone shouted, “you’ve had your answer. Make way.”

Margaret turned halfway, her voice pleading:

“Good people, we’ve come from far, and my father is old. My cousin has a new servant who does not know us—and refuses to let us sit in his own house.”

The crowd laughed harshly. Margaret flinched as if struck. At that moment, a hand closed around hers—strong, warm, magnetic. It felt like soul meeting soul. She turned sharply—and it was Gerard. A small cry escaped her, half joy, half appeal. She began to whimper softly.

They had jostled her, frightened her. Her cousin’s carelessness—failing even to inform his servant of their arrival—was cruel. And the servant’s caution, though wise, was deeply humiliating to her father and herself. And now, in that moment of distress, this kind hand and face appeared. *Hinc illae lacrimae.*

“All is well now,” a coarse humorist remarked. “She’s found her sweetheart.”

“Haw! haw! haw!” the crowd jeered.

She dropped Gerard’s hand instantly and turned, eyes flashing through her tears:

“I have no sweetheart, you rude men. But I am friendless in your crude town, and this is a friend—one who knows, what you do not, how to treat the aged and the weak.”

Silence fell. They had been thoughtless, and now felt the sting of her rebuke, though sharp, it was just. The quiet allowed Gerard to speak to the porter.

“I am a competitor, sir.”

“What is your name?” the man asked, suspicious.

“Gerard, son of Elias.”

The janitor checked a parchment slip:

“Gerard Eliassoen may enter.”

“With my companions—these two?”

“No. They came before you. They are not your party.”

“What matters? They are my friends. Without them, I go not in.”

“Stay without, then.”

“That I will not.”

“That we shall see.”

“We will—and swiftly.” With that, Gerard raised his voice—deep, powerful, commanding—and echoed through the street:

“Are you mad?” the porter cried.

“Hush, hush!”

“Hush! Murder! The Dukes are there! I’m dead!” the janitor shrieked, trembling.

Then, trying to drown Gerard’s voice, he bellowed with all his might:

“OPEN THE GATE, YE KNAVES! WAY THERE FOR GERARD ELIASSOEN AND HIS COMPANY! (The fiends go with him!)”

The gate swung open as if by magic. Eight soldiers lowered their pikes, forming an arch. Under it, the trio marched in triumph. The moment they passed, the pikes clashed shut—nearly pinning a citizen who tried to squeeze through.

Beyond the gate, a few steps revealed a scene of Eastern splendor. The courtyard was filled with tables piled high with rich meats and adorned with glittering plate. Guests in colorful garments sat beneath a canopy of fresh branches tied with golden, silver, and blue silken cords. Fruits of every hue—some real, others crafted from gold, silver, and wax—hung like jewels among the green leaves of plane and lime trees. The Duke’s minstrels played lutes at intervals, and a fountain sprayed red Burgundy in six jets that danced and collided in midair. The evening sun pierced the wine streams, turning them into molten rubies, casting crimson light on fair faces, snowy beards, velvet, satin, jeweled hilts, glowing gold, gleaming silver, and sparkling glass. Gerard and his friends stood transfixed, spellbound.

Then a whisper passed: “Salute the Duke! Salute the Duke!”

They looked up—and there, under the dais, was their sovereign, waving kindly. The men bowed low. Margaret curtsied with grace and depth. The Duke waved again, pointing them out to a group of valets. Seven of them rushed forward, seated the trio at a table, and placed fifteen colorful soups in little silver bowls, and as many wines in crystal vases.

“Nay, father,” Margaret said, recovering from the whirlwind, “let us not eat until we thank our good friend.”

“Girl, he is our guardian angel.”

Gerard buried his face in his hands.

“Tell me when you’ve finished,” he said, “and I’ll return to supper. I am hungry. But I know which of us three is happiest to meet again.”

“Me?” she asked.

“No. Guess again.”

“Father?”

“No.”

“Then I have no guess,” she said, laughing lightly. “Ah!”

The soup vanished in a flurry of fourteen hands. Fish arrived in a dozen forms—lobster patties mixed with almonds, almond-and-cream cakes, and countless *brouets*, known today as rissoles. Next came a wild boar—its scent divine. Yet why did Margaret recoil with two cries of horror and pinch Gerard?

Because the Duke’s chef had been too clever. He had restored the animal’s hair and bristles with burnt sugar and edible pigments. The tusks remained intact, giving the beast a winning smile. Two eyes of colored sugar glowed in its head—bright, bloodshot, menacing—watching every move of knife and spoon.

St. Argus! What eyes!

And I need Granville or Tenniel to paint the scene: two gilded valets placing the monster before the guests with smiling, self-satisfied reverence; old Peter clasping his hands in holy awe; Margaret wheeling round, eyes wide with terror, hand on Gerard’s shoulder, squealing and pinching; Gerard’s face lit with foolish delight at being pinched; the grizzly brute glaring sulkily; guests grinning from ear to ear.

“What’s the matter?” the Duke called, hearing the outcry. Seven servants rushed to report. He laughed.

“Give her the beef-stuffing, then. Bring me Sir Boar.”

Generous monarch! The beef-stuffing was his private dish. On such occasions, a whole ox was roasted and reserved for the poor. But the wise and charitable king discovered that skewering venison, lamb, poultry, etc., into the ox’s cavity cooked them to perfection—retaining their juices and absorbing the ox’s rich flavor. These he called his beef-stuffing—and now, at his word, seven servants plunged silver tridents into the steaming flesh, spearing a kid, a cygnet, and a flock of wildfowl. They soon smoked before Gerard and his companions. Peter’s face, sad at losing the savage hog, brightened and shone.

Afterward came twenty different tarts of fruit and herbs, and finally, confectionery on a colossal scale—sugar cathedrals with gilt bas-reliefs, castles with moats and ditches, elephants, camels, toads, knights jousting, kings and princesses watching trumpeters blow—all eating, veins filled with sweet nectar. Works of art meant to be destroyed. The guests shattered a bastion, crushed a crusader and his horse, cracked a bishop in cope and crosier, as casually as one breaks a caraway candy. They sipped hippocras, spiced drinks, Greek and Corsican wines, while turbaned, jeweled, gilt Turkish boys knelt, offering golden troughs of rose and orange water to cool and perfume their hands.

But long before this feast reached its end, appetite had fled. Gerard suddenly remembered he carried a letter for Princess Marie. In a low tone, he asked a servant if he could deliver it. The man bowed deeply: “I cannot deliver it myself, but I will give it at once to one of the Princess’s attendants—several are here.”

It may be recalled that Peter and Margaret came not to dine, but to find their cousin. Well, the old man ate heartily, and—exhausted—fell asleep, forgetting all about him. Margaret did not remind him. We shall learn why.

Meanwhile, their cousin sat just behind them, unnoticed—until Margaret turned and screamed at the boar. But he said nothing. Municipal pride held him back. Margaret wore plain clothes; Peter looked threadbare. So the alderman decided:

“It will be time to welcome them when the sun sets and the guests disperse. Then I will take my poor relations home—no one need know.”

Half the courses passed Gerard and Margaret unnoticed. They were not great eaters, and now their minds feasted on sweeter thoughts—thoughts that always dull the appetite.

Margaret leaned back, eyes half-closed, murmuring to Gerard: “What a lovely scene—the warm sun, the green shade, the rich dresses, the music of the lutes, the cool song of the fountain, all faces joyful and bright. And it is all thanks to you.”

Gerard said nothing—only his eyes moved.

“Now, speak not,” she sighed. “Let me listen to the fountain. What are you competing for?”

He told her.

“Very well. You will win at least one prize.”

“Which? Which? Have you seen my work?”

“I? No. But you will win.”

“How can you be sure?”

“Because you were so kind to my father.”

Gerard smiled at her logic, hung his head at her praise, and remained silent.

“Speak not,” she murmured. “They say this is a world of sin and misery. Can that be? What is your opinion?”

“No,” Gerard said. “That’s just an old song. A byword our elders repeat by habit. It’s not true.”

“How can you know? You’re but a child,” she said, with thoughtful dignity.

“Look around! And then—thought I had lost you forever. And now you are here. And the minstrels are about to play again. Sin and misery? Nonsense!”

The lutes burst forth. The courtyard rang with delicate harmony.

“What do you admire most of all these wonders, Gerard?”

“You know my name? How?”

“White magic. I am a—witch.”

“Angels are never witches. But I can’t understand—”

“Foolish boy! Was it not shouted loud enough at the gate to deafen one?”

“So it was. Where is my head? What do I admire most? If you will lean a little more this way, I’ll tell you.”

“This way?”

“Yes—so the light falls upon you. There. I see many fair things here, fairer than I ever dreamed. But the fairest of all, to my eye, is your lovely hair, framed in silver, kissed by the setting sun. It reminds me of what the Vulgate calls beautiful—*an apple of gold in a network of silver*. And oh, how I regret I did not know you before I sent in my efforts at illuminating! I could illuminate so much better now. I could do everything better. Look—now the sun is full upon it. It shines like an aureole. So did Our Lady look—and none since her—until today.”

“Oh, fie! It is wicked to speak so. Compare a poor, plain girl like me with the Queen of Heaven? Oh, Gerard! I thought you were a good young man.”

Margaret seemed genuinely shocked.

Gerard tried to explain. “I am no worse than any other. But how can I help having eyes—and a heart, Margaret?”

“Gerard!”

“Be not angry now!”

“Now? Is it likely?”

“I love you.”

“Oh, for shame! You must not say that to me,” she said, flushing violently.

“I cannot help it. I love you. I love you.”
