The two hours of suspended animation had altered my physical state more profoundly than I could have anticipated. I found myself physically incapable of remaining stationary in any single location, and morally unable to engage in conversation with any human being until I had first heard the entirety of Ezra Jennings' account. In this agitated frame of mind, I abandoned my intended visit to Mrs. Ablewhite and even shrank from the prospect of encountering Gabriel Betteredge.

Returning to Frizinghall, I left a note for Betteredge explaining that an unexpected summons had detained me for a few hours, assuring him that I expected to return around three o'clock in the afternoon. I instructed him to order dinner at the usual hour and to amuse himself as he saw fit, knowing well that his numerous friends in the village would ensure he had no shortage of company until my return.

Having dispatched this message, I hurried out of the town once more, wandering the desolate moorlands surrounding Frizinghall until my watch indicated it was time to return to Mr. Candy's residence.

I found Ezra Jennings waiting for me.

He sat alone in a sparsely furnished room connected by a glazed door to a surgery. The barren buff-colored walls were adorned with hideous colored diagrams depicting the ravages of terrible diseases. A bookcase filled with dusty medical texts, topped by a skull rather than a customary bust, stood nearby. A large deal table was splattered with ink, flanked by wooden kitchen chairs, a threadbare rug, and a rough sink integrated into the wall, evoking the grim reality of surgical operations. Outside the window, bees hummed among flowers, birds sang in the garden, and the faint, intermittent jingle of a tuneless piano drifted from a neighboring house. In any other setting, these sounds would have signaled the comfort of the everyday world; here, they intruded upon a silence broken only by human suffering. I glanced at the mahogany instrument case and the large roll of lint on the shelves, shuddering inwardly at the familiar sounds associated with the room's daily use.

"I make no apology, Mr. Blake, for the place in which I am receiving you," he said. "It is the only room in the house, at this hour, where we can be certain of undisturbed privacy. Here are my papers ready for you, along with two books we may need to consult. Bring your chair to the table, and we shall begin."

I drew up to the table, and Ezra Jennings handed me his manuscript notes. They consisted of two large folio leaves. One contained writing scattered across the surface at intervals; the other was densely filled from top to bottom with writing in both red and black ink. In my irritated state of curiosity, I set aside the second sheet in despair.

"Have some mercy on me!" I exclaimed. "Tell me what I am to expect before I attempt to read this."

"Willingly, Mr. Blake! Do you mind if I ask you one or two more questions?"

"Ask me anything you like!"

He looked at me with a sad smile and kindly interest in his soft brown eyes.

"You have already told me," he said, "that you have never, to your knowledge, tasted opium in your life."

"To my knowledge," I repeated.

"You will understand directly why I speak with that reservation. Let us proceed. You are not aware of ever having taken opium. At this time last year, you were suffering from nervous irritation and slept wretchedly at night. However, on the night of the birthday, there was an exception to the rule—you slept soundly. Am I right so far?"

"Quite right!"

"Can you assign any cause for your nervous suffering and lack of sleep?"

"I can assign no cause. Old Betteredge made a guess, I remember, but it was hardly worth mentioning."

"Pardon me. Anything is worth mentioning in a case like this. Betteredge attributed your sleeplessness to something. To what?"

"To my leaving off smoking."

"Had you been an habitual smoker?"

"Yes."

"Did you leave off the habit suddenly?"

"Yes."

"Betteredge was perfectly right, Mr. Blake. When smoking is a habit, a man must have no common constitution who can cease it suddenly without temporary damage to his nervous system. Your sleepless nights are accounted for. My next question concerns Mr. Candy. Do you remember entering into any dispute with him at the birthday dinner or afterwards regarding his profession?"

The question instantly awakened a dormant memory. The foolish wrangle between Mr. Candy and myself, described at length in Betteredge's narrative, had faded from my mind. All I could recall was that I had attacked the art of medicine at the dinner table with sufficient rashness to put Mr. Candy out of temper temporarily. I remembered that Lady Verinder had intervened to stop the dispute, and that the little doctor and I had "made it up again" before shaking hands that night.

"There is one more thing," said Ezra Jennings, "which is very important I should know. Did you have any reason for feeling special anxiety about the Diamond at this time last year?"

"I had the strongest reasons. I knew it was the object of a conspiracy and was warned to take measures for Miss Verinder's protection."

"Was the safety of the Diamond the subject of conversation between you and anyone immediately before you retired to rest on the birthday night?"

"It was the subject of a conversation between Lady Verinder and her daughter..."

"Which took place in your hearing?"

"Yes."

Ezra Jennings took up his notes and placed them in my hands.

"Mr. Blake," he said, "if you read those notes now, illuminated by the light of my questions and your answers, you will make two astounding discoveries concerning yourself. First, that you entered Miss Verinder's sitting-room and took the Diamond in a state of trance produced by opium. Second, that the opium was given to you by Mr. Candy without your knowledge, as a practical refutation of the opinions you had expressed to him at the dinner table."

I sat with the papers in my hand, completely stupefied.

"Try and forgive poor Mr. Candy," said the assistant gently. "He has done dreadful mischief, I own; but he has done it innocently. If you look at the notes, you will see that—but for his illness—he would have returned to Lady Verinder's the morning after the party and acknowledged the trick. Miss Verinder would have questioned him, and the truth hidden for a year would have been discovered in a day."

I began to regain my self-possession. "Mr. Candy is beyond the reach of my resentment," I said angrily. "But the trick he played me is not the less an act of treachery. I may forgive, but I shall not forget it."

"Every medical man commits that act of treachery in the course of his practice, Mr. Blake. The ignorant distrust of opium in England is not confined to the lower classes. Every doctor in large practice finds himself, every now and then, obliged to deceive his patients, as Mr. Candy deceived you. I do not defend the folly of playing you a trick under the circumstances. I only plead with you for a more accurate and merciful construction of motives."

"How was it done?" I asked. "Who gave me the laudanum without my knowing it?"

"I am not able to tell you. Nothing relating to that part of the matter dropped from Mr. Candy's lips during his illness. Perhaps your own memory may point to the person to be suspected."

"No."

"It is useless, in that case, to pursue the inquiry. The laudanum was secretly given to you in some way. Let us leave it there and go on to matters of more immediate importance. Read my notes if you can. Familiarize your mind with what has happened in the past. I have something very bold and startling to propose to you regarding the future."

Those last words roused me.

I looked at the papers. The sheet with the smaller quantity of writing was on top. On it, the disconnected words and fragments of sentences dropped from Mr. Candy in his delirium appeared as follows:

"... Mr. Franklin Blake ... and agreeable ... down a peg ... medicine ... confesses ... sleep at night ... tell him ... out of order ... medicine ... he tells me ... and groping in the dark mean one and the same thing ... all the company at the dinner-table ... I say ... groping after sleep ... nothing but medicine ... he says ... leading the blind ... know what it means ... witty ... a night's rest in spite of his teeth ... wants sleep ... Lady Verinder's medicine chest ... five-and-twenty minims ... without his knowing it ... tomorrow morning ... Well, Mr. Blake ... medicine today ... never ... without it ... out, Mr. Candy ... excellent ... without it ... down on him ... truth ... something besides ... excellent ... dose of laudanum, sir ... bed ... what ... medicine now."

"That is what you heard at his bedside?" I said.

"Literally and exactly what I heard," he answered—"except that the repetitions are not transferred here from my shorthand notes. He reiterated certain words and phrases a dozen times over, fifty times over, just as he attached more or less importance to the idea they represented. These repetitions assisted me in putting the fragments together. Don't suppose," he added, pointing to the second sheet, "that I claim to have reproduced the expressions Mr. Candy would have used if he had been capable of speaking coherently. I only say that I have penetrated through the obstacle of disconnected expression to the thought underlying it. Judge for yourself."

I turned to the second sheet, which I now knew to be the key to the first.

Once more, Mr. Candy's wanderings appeared, copied in black ink; the intervals between the phrases filled in by Ezra Jennings in red ink. I reproduce the result here in one plain form:

"... Mr. Franklin Blake is clever and agreeable, but he wants taking down a peg when he talks of medicine. He confesses that he has been suffering from want of sleep at night. I tell him that his nerves are out of order, and that he ought to take medicine. He tells me that taking medicine and groping in the dark mean one and the same thing. This before all the company at the dinner-table. I say to him, you are groping after sleep, and nothing but medicine can help you to find it. He says to me, I have heard of the blind leading the blind, and now I know what it means. Witty—but I can give him a night's rest in spite of his teeth. He really wants sleep; and Lady Verinder's medicine chest is at my disposal. Give him five-and-twenty minims of laudanum tonight, without his knowing it; and then call tomorrow morning. 'Well, Mr. Blake, will you try a little medicine today? You will never sleep without it.'—'There you are out, Mr. Candy: I have had an excellent night's rest without it.' Then, come down on him with the truth! 'You have had something besides an excellent night's rest; you had a dose of laudanum, sir, before you went to bed. What do you say to the art of medicine, now?'"

Admiration for the ingenuity that had woven this smooth texture out of the raveled skein was my first impression. He modestly interrupted my surprise by asking if the conclusion he had drawn was also the conclusion at which my own mind had arrived.

"Do you believe as I believe," he said, "that you were acting under the influence of the laudanum in doing all that you did on the night of Miss Verinder's birthday?"

"I am too ignorant of the influence of laudanum to have an opinion of my own," I answered. "I can only follow your opinion and feel convinced that you are right."

"Very well. The next question is this. You are convinced; and I am convinced—how are we to carry our conviction to the minds of other people?"

I pointed to the two manuscripts lying on the table. Ezra Jennings shook his head.

"Useless, Mr. Blake! Quite useless, as they stand now, for three unanswerable reasons. In the first place, those notes have been taken under circumstances entirely out of the experience of the mass of mankind. In the second place, those notes represent a medical and metaphysical theory. In the third place, those notes are of my making; there is nothing but my assertion to guarantee that they are not fabrications. Remember what I told you on the moor—and ask yourself what my assertion is worth. No! my notes have but one value, looking to the verdict of the world outside. Your innocence is to be vindicated; and they show how it can be done. We must put our conviction to the proof—and you are the man to prove it!"

"How?" I asked.

He leaned eagerly nearer to me across the table.

"Are you willing to try a bold experiment?"

"I will do anything to clear myself of the suspicion that rests on me now."

"Will you submit to some personal inconvenience for a time?"

"To any inconvenience, no matter what it may be."

"Will you be guided implicitly by my advice? It may expose you to the ridicule of fools; it may subject you to the remonstrances of friends whose opinions you are bound to respect."

"Tell me what to do!" I broke out impatiently. "And, come what may, I'll do it."

"You shall do this, Mr. Blake," he answered. "You shall steal the Diamond, unconsciously, for the second time, in the presence of witnesses whose testimony is beyond dispute."

I started to my feet. I tried to speak. I could only look at him.

"I believe it _can_ be done," he went on. "And it _shall_ be done—if you will only help me. Try to compose yourself—sit down, and hear what I have to say to you. You have resumed the habit of smoking; I have seen that for myself. How long have you resumed it."

"For nearly a year."

"Do you smoke more or less than you did?"

"More."

"Will you give up the habit again? Suddenly, mind!—as you gave it up before."

I began dimly to see his drift. "I will give it up, from this moment," I answered.
