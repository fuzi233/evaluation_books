Archer reeled like he’d taken a physical blow the second old Catherine’s news landed. Of course Ellen Olenska would have rushed back from Washington the moment her grandmother summoned her—but choosing to move back into Mrs. Mingott’s home, especially now that the old matriarch had all but recovered her health? That made no sense, not at first glance.

Archer didn’t doubt for a second her choice had nothing to do with money. He knew exactly how small the allowance Count Olenski had grudgingly granted her at their separation was; without the top-up from her grandmother, it barely covered the cost of a modest life by Mingott family standards, and now that Medora Manson, her long-time companion, had lost every last penny of her own fortune, that pittance would scarcely feed and clothe the two women. Ellen was reckless with generosity, wildly impulsive with money the way people who’d once lived in the lap of impossible luxury often were, but she had never cared a fig for the trivial comforts her relations considered non-negotiable. Lovell Mingott and Mrs. Welland had spent years bemoaning that a woman who’d known the cosmopolitan splendors of Olenski’s European estates could be so cavalier about “proper” New York standards. Besides, Archer knew perfectly well her husband had cut off that tiny allowance months prior, and she hadn’t lifted a finger to curry favor with her grandmother in all that time. There was only one possible reason she’d changed her mind.

He didn’t have to dig far to find it. On the ferry ride back from New Jersey, she’d told him they had to stay apart, had said it with her face pressed tight to his breast, no coquetry, no games—fighting their fate as hard as he was, clinging to her fragile resolve not to betray all the people who trusted them. But in the ten days since she’d slipped back into New York, she must have noticed his silence, the fact that he’d made no move to track her down. She must have guessed he was gearing up to cross a line there was no coming back from, and the fear of her own weakness, of the moment she’d finally cave and run away with him, had pushed her straight back to the one place she thought could keep them both safe.

An hour earlier, when he’d rung Mrs. Mingott’s doorbell, he’d thought his path was laid out clear as day. He’d meant to corner Ellen alone, and if he couldn’t, badger her grandmother for the exact date and train number of her return to Washington. He’d planned to meet her on that train, ride with her as far as she’d let him go—his own secret daydream was Japan, a world away from every stuffy New York rule. He’d leave a note for May that burned every last escape route, make his choice irrevocable. He’d thought he was braced for that plunge, even giddy to take it. But the second he heard Ellen was staying in New York, his first stupid, unbidden reaction was relief.

By the time he tramped home down Fifth Avenue, that relief curdled into a slow, creeping loathing for the life that now stretched out in front of him. It wasn’t unknown, that path. Every man of his class knew the code: the little lies, the carefully arranged alibis, the elaborate game of pretense they called “protecting a woman’s honor,” taught to them in novels and over after-dinner cigars by their elders. But when he’d played at that game before, he’d been a free, unmarried man, amused and detached. Now the role turned his stomach. It was exactly the life he’d always secretly mocked Thorley Rushworth for living: a never-ending string of small, smiling lies, by day and by night, in every caress and every quarrel, every word and every silence.

Society might have given women a pass for that kind of performance, wrote their truthfulness off as secondary to the needs of their husbands, let them hide behind moods and “nerves” if they got caught. But New York never laughed at a deceived wife. Men who chased other women after they married were written off as weak, pathetic, the kind of man no one invited to dinner twice. Archer had always thought men like Lawrence Lefferts, the city’s most notorious philanderer, were despicable. But Ellen was nothing like any other woman, he was nothing like any other man—their situation was unique, wasn’t it? They answered to no one’s judgment but their own?

His feet hesitated on the corner of his own street, and he kept walking, further down the frost-edged avenue. Ahead of him loomed the dark, shuttered bulk of the Beaufort house, once the most dazzling spot on Fifth, its steps carpeted and awned every night, carriages lined two deep waiting to drop off guests. He’d stolen his first kiss from May in that dead, dark conservatory at the side of the house, had seen her step into the glow of the ballroom chandeliers on their engagement night, tall and silver as a young Diana. Now the whole place was dark as a grave, save for a flicker of gas in the basement and one lit upstairs window, the blind left pulled crookedly up. Parked out front was Mrs. Manson Mingott’s carriage.

Archer’s chest tightened. He’d been moved to his core by old Catherine’s story of Ellen choosing to stand by the disgraced Mrs. Beaufort when every other proper New York lady had cut her dead. But he knew exactly what the gossips at the Union Club would say the second word got out Ellen was calling on the Beauforts. He paused, staring up at that one lit window, and the front door creaked open.

Ellen stepped out, her sealskin coat pulled tight, a faint glow of hallway light behind her. She turned to murmur a goodbye to whoever was holding the lamp for her, the door clicked shut, and she came down the steps.

“Ellen,” he whispered.

She jumped, and just then two young men in smart overcoats and silk mufflers rounded the corner: Lawrence Lefferts and young Reggie Chivers, clearly on their way to the group trip to see *Romeo and Juliet* at the opera house everyone was gossiping about. They spotted the two of them on the curb, and discreetly veered across the avenue, the exact unspoken gentlemanly solidarity Archer had always gone along with to spare other men embarrassment. Now it made him sick to his stomach, this quiet conspiracy to hide secrets that would ruin lives. He reached for Ellen’s hand, and the warm weight of her gloved fingers in his chased away every mean, petty thought of the gossip they’d just avoided.

“I’ll see you now—we can be together,” he blurted, hardly knowing what the words meant.

“Ah,” she said softly. “Granny told you I’m staying?”

“Tomorrow I have to see you,” he said, his voice sharp with a hunger he couldn’t hide. “Somewhere completely alone.”

She wavered, stepping toward her waiting carriage. “But I’ll be at Granny’s, for the time being, anyway.”

“Somewhere no one we know will find us.”

She gave a faint, wobbly laugh that grated on his raw nerves. “In New York? There are no hidden churches, no quiet monuments where no one knows us.”

“The Art Museum, up in the Park,” he explained quickly. “Half past two. I’ll be waiting by the front door.”

She didn’t answer, just slipped up into the carriage. As it pulled away, he saw her lean forward, and thought he caught the flutter of her hand waving in the dark. He stared after the carriage, his head spinning with tangled, contradictory feelings, half elated, half horrified at the cheap, secretive turn their romance had already taken. But he knew, deep in his bones, that she would come.

They’d dodged the crowded main galleries full of kitschy historical canvases no one could stop staring at, and wandered all the way back to the dusty, almost entirely abandoned wing holding the Cesnola collection: broken, weathered fragments pulled from the ruins of ancient Troy, no visitors to disturb them. They sank onto a divan curled around the central steam radiator, staring at the ebonized wood display cases full of shards of clay, discolored bronze, and half-melted glass.

“It’s so strange,” Ellen said, her voice soft in the quiet. “I’ve lived in New York for months and never came here before.”

“Someday it’ll be a grand, famous museum,” Archer said.

“Maybe,” she said absently, wandering across the room to stare at one of the cases. Archer stayed seated, watching her, the girlish lightness of her step under her heavy furs, the heron wing tucked into the brim of her fur cap, the two dark curls coiled like flattened vines just above her ears. Every little detail of her made his chest ache. He stood up and joined her at the case, full of tiny unidentifiable objects: little pots, hair pins, trinkets no living person could name the use for anymore.

“It feels so cruel,” she said. “After a while nothing matters. Not any of the things that were once so precious and necessary to people who are long forgotten. Now we just squint at them through magnifying glasses and mark their labels ‘Use unknown.’”

“Nothing matters to those old people, maybe,” he said, reaching to brush a stray strand of hair back from her face. “But everything that matters to you, matters. Right now. Meanwhile.”

She turned and walked back to the divan, and he sat down beside her, his heart hammering. Far down the corridor, he heard the faint echo of the guard’s boots, and he felt every second of their stolen time slipping through his fingers.

“You knew what I was going to do, didn’t you?” he said quietly. “You knew I was planning to show up in Washington, and run off with you. That’s why you stayed here. You were afraid.”

She stared down at the little fur muff in her lap, her hands shifting restlessly inside it. “Yes. I was afraid.”

“This is better, isn’t it?” she sighed, after a long, quiet moment. “We hurt fewer people this way. Isn’t this what you’ve always wanted, in the quiet part of your head?”

“To have you here, close enough to touch and too far to ever reach? To sneak around like we’re doing something shameful? It’s the exact opposite of what I want,” he said, his voice sharp with frustration. “I told you exactly what I want the other day.”

“Then you think this is worse?”

“A thousand times worse,” he said. “I could lie to you, but it’s detestable. I hate every second of it.”

“Oh, I do too!” she burst out, and the relief flooded out of her on a deep, shaking breath.

“Then what on earth do you think is better?” he said, jumping to his feet. The guard rounded the corner then, ambling past them like a ghost through a necropolis of mummies and sarcophagi, and they both stared pointedly at the nearest display case until he vanished back down the corridor.

“I promised Granny I’d stay with her,” Ellen said, not meeting his eyes. “Because I thought I’d be safer here.”

“Safer from me?”

“Safer from hurting people irreparably,” she whispered, a tear spilling over her lashes and catching in the mesh of her veil. “Don’t let us end up like everyone else, lying and hurting the people who’ve been good to us.”

“What people want us to do, that isn’t us,” Archer said, reaching for her hands through her muff.

“Shall I come to you, just once?” she said, her voice small and clear, like she was daring to say something she’d been too afraid to admit even to herself. “And then go home.”

The blood rushed so fast to his head he thought he might faint. “Home? To your husband?”

“What else is there?” she said, lifting her wet, troubled eyes to his. “I can’t stay here and lie to everyone who’s helped me build a new life.”

Archer stood frozen, staring down at her, every argument dying on his lips. He knew if he said yes, if he convinced her to spend that one night with him, he could easily talk her out of ever going back to Olenski, easily twist that moment of softness to get everything he wanted. But that raw, unflinching honesty in her face made the lie impossible. He couldn’t trap her that way. If he let her come to him, he had to let her go again. And that was a fate worse than any other.

“Ellen,” he said, his voice breaking. “Wait. Don’t go.”

“I have to go,” she said, yanking her little watch out from under her coat, standing up fast. He caught her wrist, and for a second they stared at each other, breathless, almost like enemies. “I’ll see you. Two days from now.”

“Dearest,” he whispered, and all the fight went out of him. Their eyes held, and the pale, sad glow of her face flooded with a soft, shining light, a love so bright it made his chest ache with awe. He had never seen love made visible before, not like that.

“Oh, I’m late, goodbye,” she gasped, pulling her wrist away and hurrying down the long gallery before he could say another word. At the door, she turned for half a second to wave, and then she was gone.

Archer walked home in a daze. Darkness had fallen by the time he let himself into his own house, staring at the familiar coat hooks and umbrella stands in the hall like he was looking at them from the other side of a grave. The maid told him May had gone out in the carriage after luncheon and still wasn’t back, and he sagged into his library armchair, staring at the red glow of the dying fire, no thoughts in his head, just a heavy, quiet amazement that felt like he was suspended in the grip of fate. This was what had to be, he kept repeating to himself. This was what had to be.

The door opened, and May walked in.

“I’m dreadfully late—you weren’t worried, were you?” she said, pressing her hand lightly to his shoulder in one of her rare soft caresses. She was paler than usual, bright with a strange, unaccustomed energy, her cheeks flushed deep pink. “I went to see Granny, and Ellen walked in just as I was leaving. We sat and talked for what felt like hours. I haven’t had a real, proper talk with her in forever.”

Archer leaned back against the mantelpiece, out of the light of the student lamp, watching her. She was trying so hard, he realized, trying to push down the quiet, unspoken resentment she’d always held for Ellen, trying to be fair, trying to be the wife he wanted her to be. He almost told her everything, right then, almost threw himself on her mercy.

“The family has been so hard on her, I know,” May rambled on, breathless from the long speech. “She’s different, from all of us. She’s seen so much more of the world. I don’t want to judge her unfairly.”

Archer laughed a sharp, impatient little laugh, and the open space for honesty between them clicked shut again.

“Time to dress for dinner,” he said, moving toward the stairs.

May stepped forward fast, impulsively, wrapping her arms around his neck and pressing her warm cheek to his. “You haven’t kissed me today,” she whispered, and he felt her tremble in his arms.
