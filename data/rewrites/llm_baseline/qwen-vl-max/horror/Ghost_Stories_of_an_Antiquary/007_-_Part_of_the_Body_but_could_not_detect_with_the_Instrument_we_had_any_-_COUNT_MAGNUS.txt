The papers from which I have constructed this tale came into my possession under circumstances I shall reveal only at the very end. But before I present the excerpts, I must explain how they have reached me.

They are a curious mixture: part travel journal, part personal record, and ultimately—though not at first intended—a chronicle of a man’s descent into something far darker than mere curiosity. The documents began as notes for a book of travels, much like those popular in the 1840s and 50s—books such as Horace Marryat’s *Journal of a Residence in Jutland and the Danish Isles*. These were often light-hearted, chatty accounts of foreign lands, filled with descriptions of inns, customs, and conversations with peasants and nobles alike. They were meant to entertain, not to unsettle.

But these papers evolved. What started as a traveler’s sketchbook became, over time, a confession—not of sin, but of dread. The author was Mr. Wraxall. I know little of him beyond what he wrote. He appears to have been a man past middle age, comfortably off, yet rootless—drifting between hotels and boarding houses across Europe, never settling. He had no fixed home in England, and though he once published a book about a holiday in Brittany, it seems to have appeared under a pseudonym or anonymously, for no trace of it remains in any catalog.

From his writings, I infer that he was intelligent, well-read, and possibly once near becoming a Fellow of Brasenose College at Oxford. His greatest flaw—and perhaps his fatal one—was an insatiable curiosity. It was not malice that drove him, nor greed, but a hunger to know, to see, to understand things others would leave buried.

In 1863, he set out for Sweden, drawn by the quiet grandeur of Scandinavia, then still largely unknown to English travelers. He had read old Swedish histories and thought there was room for a book combining travelogue with historical narrative—especially one that wove together the lives of noble families with the landscapes they shaped.

He secured letters of introduction and arrived in Stockholm, where a scholar directed him to a collection of family papers at Råbäck, an ancient manor in Vestergothland. The estate belonged to the De la Gardie family, descendants of one of Sweden’s most powerful noble lines. The house itself was impressive—a red-brick mansion with stone trim, built shortly after 1600, resembling an English country seat of the same era. It stood in a park, surrounded by mature trees, with a walled garden and a close wood bordering a small lake. Beyond the demesne wall rose a steep knoll crowned by a church, its dark pines silhouetted against the sky.

The church was odd to English eyes. Low ceilings, wooden galleries, and a painted organ with silver pipes. The ceiling bore a lurid depiction of the Last Judgment—cities collapsing, ships burning, souls shrieking, demons grinning. Brass coronae hung from the rafters; the pulpit looked like a dollhouse adorned with tiny saints and cherubs. And at the eastern end of the north aisle stood the mausoleum—a domed, eight-sided structure with oval windows and a copper roof painted black. It was separate from the church, accessible only through its own portal on the northern side.

Mr. Wraxall stayed at the village inn rather than at the manor, preferring independence and wary of his limited Swedish. Each day, he walked the mile to Råbäck, returning in the evening. On his first visit, he found the church door open and took notes of its interior. But the mausoleum remained closed. Peering through the keyhole, he glimpsed marble effigies, copper sarcophagi, and coats of arms carved in relief. He longed to enter.

The papers he studied were exactly what he’d hoped for: journals, letters, account books from the earliest De la Gardie owners. They revealed a man named Magnus de la Gardie—strong, capable, ruthless. During a peasant uprising, he had crushed the rebellion with brutal efficiency. Men were flogged, branded, executed. One portrait of him hung in the hall: a face of immense power, yet deeply ugly. Mr. Wraxall described it as “almost phenomenally” so.

That night, dining with the family, he noticed the sexton standing on the steps of the mausoleum, seemingly locking or unlocking the door. “I must ask him tomorrow,” he wrote, “if he can let me in.”

The next morning, he spoke with the innkeeper, Herr Nielsen. He wanted to know if any local legends survived about Count Magnus. The answer was swift and chilling: the Count was not liked. He punished tardy tenants with the wooden horse or branding. Some landowners who encroached on his domain had their homes burned down—entire families perishing in winter fires.

But the landlord hesitated when asked about the *Black Pilgrimage*.

“What is that?” Mr. Wraxall pressed.

Herr Nielsen looked away. Then, abruptly, he said he had to go to Skara and would return later.

Frustrated, Mr. Wraxall turned back to the papers. He read letters between Sophia Albertina and her cousin Ulrica Leonora, spanning 1705–1710—letters rich with cultural detail, now preserved in official Swedish archives. In the afternoon, while replacing the boxes, he pulled down a volume from a nearby shelf. It was not an account book, but a sixteenth-century manuscript filled with alchemical tracts: *The Book of the Phoenix*, *The Book of the Thirty Words*, *The Book of the Toad*, *Turba philosophorum*.

Near the center, on a blank leaf, was a note in Count Magnus’s hand:

> *"If any man desires to obtain a long life, if he would obtain a faithful messenger and see the blood of his enemies, it is necessary that he should first go into the city of Chorazin, and there salute the prince…."*

A word was erased—only partially—so Mr. Wraxall could make out *aëris* (“of the air”). Below it, in Latin: *Quære reliqua hujus materiei inter secretiora.* (Seek the rest of this matter among the more private things.)

It was unsettling. Alchemy, yes—but magic? Something darker?

Later, walking home, he passed the churchyard and stopped short. He found himself standing before the mausoleum.

“Ah,” he murmured, “Count Magnus, there you are. I should dearly like to see you.”

He did not expect a reply. But the woman cleaning the church dropped a metal object—clanging on stone. He jumped.

“Count Magnus, I think, sleeps sound enough.”

That evening, the landlord introduced him to the parish deacon. A visit to the mausoleum was arranged for the next day.

Over dinner, Mr. Wraxall asked about Chorazin.

The deacon stiffened. “You mean the place Jesus cursed?”

“Yes. Is it still standing?”

“I suppose it is a ruin,” the deacon said. “Some say Antichrist will be born there. And there are tales…”

“What tales?”

“The ones I’ve forgotten,” the deacon replied, and left.

Now alone, Mr. Wraxall turned to Herr Nielsen.

“Tell me,” he said, “what did the Count bring back from the Black Pilgrimage?”

The landlord stared at him for a full minute. Then stepped close.

“In my grandfather’s time—ninety-two years ago—two men said: ‘The Count is dead. We do not care for him. Tonight we will hunt freely in his woods.’ Others warned them: ‘Do not go. You will meet those who should not walk. They should rest.’ They laughed.

“They went that night. My grandfather sat here, window open, watching the woods.

“At first, silence. Then—scream. Not human. As if a soul was being torn from the body. All in the room seized each other. For three-quarters of an hour, no sound.

“Then—laughter. Loud. From someone not among them. Not even human. Then—great doors shut.

“At dawn, they went to the priest. Said: ‘Father, come. Bury Anders Bjornsen and Hans Thorbjorn.’

“They went to the wood. Found Hans Thorbjorn standing against a tree, pushing at nothing—his hands moving as if repelling a thing unseen. He lived until winter, but always pushed.

“Anders Bjornsen was dead. Once a handsome man. Now—his face was gone. Flesh sucked away from bone. They laid him on the bier. Covered his head. The priest walked ahead. They sang the psalm.

“As they finished the first verse—someone fell. Carrying the head of the bier. They looked back.

“The cloth had fallen off.

“Anders Bjornsen’s eyes were open. Looking up. No flesh to cover them.

“They could not bear it.

“The priest covered him again. Sent for a spade. Buried him where he lay.”

The next day, the deacon led Mr. Wraxall to the mausoleum. The key hung near the pulpit. He noted that the church door was often left unlocked—meaning a second visit might be possible.

Inside, the space was somber. Three copper sarcophagi stood beneath the dome. Two bore crucifixes. The third—Count Magnus’s—had a full-length effigy engraved upon it. Around the edge, scenes were depicted: a battle, an execution, a man running through the woods, hair flying, hands outstretched. Behind him came a figure—short, hooded, wrapped in a cloak that touched the ground. Only one limb protruded: not a hand, not an arm, but a thick, sinuous appendage—like a devil-fish’s tentacle.

Mr. Wraxall smiled grimly. “This,” he wrote, “must be the origin of the story—the Count and his companion.”

But the hunter was not a demon. Only a cloaked man on a hill, leaning on a staff, watching the chase.

Three steel padlocks secured the sarcophagus. One was loose, lying on the floor. He picked it up, placed it on the window ledge. Tried to relock it. Failed.

He left.

That night, returning from the manor-house, he found himself at the churchyard gate without realizing how he’d gotten there. He was humming—or chanting—something:
“Are you awake, Count Magnus? Are you asleep, Count Magnus?”

He couldn’t recall the rest.

The key was still there. He entered, copied the epitaphs, and stayed until dusk.

“I must have been wrong,” he wrote, “to say one padlock was loose. Two are now unfastened. I picked both up and laid them carefully on the windowsill. I tried to close them—failed. The third remains fast. I believe it is a spring lock. I cannot guess how it opens. If I had managed it… I am almost afraid I would have opened the sarcophagus.”

He felt a strange pull toward the Count—a fascination with a man who had lived so fiercely, so cruelly, so mysteriously.

The next day, he received letters calling him back to England. His work was done. He packed his notes, bid farewell, and prepared to leave.

He would never return to Råbäck.

And the papers he left behind—those pages—would remain unread for decades, until I found them.

And now, I warn you: do not seek the secrets of Chorazin.

Do not speak the name of Count Magnus.

For some doors, once opened, cannot be closed.
