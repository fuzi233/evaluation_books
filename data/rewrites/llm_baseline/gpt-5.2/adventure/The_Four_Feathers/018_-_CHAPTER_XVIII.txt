Ethne did not turn her head. She kept the violin laid along her knees as though it were a child she had lulled to sleep, her fingers resting on the varnished belly where the wood still held the warmth of her hands. Beyond the window the garden lay washed in moonlight: roses shut tight as clenched fists, borders dark with leaf-shadow, and, between two elms, a pale ribbon of water or path—silver and quiet as a blade.

She held herself so deliberately still because stillness helped her commit a small, necessary deceit. If she did not look at Durrance—if she stared instead into the moonlit gap—then it was easier to pretend that Harry Feversham himself spoke to her, and not Durrance speaking for him. She wanted the voice to go slowly, syllable by syllable, like broth coaxed to clarity over a low flame, so she could gather every word and hoard it.

“It was on the night before I started eastward into the desert—for the last time,” Durrance said.

The weight he gave to last time—its longing, its regret—did not touch her. She had no room left for it.

“Yes,” she answered. “That was in February. The middle of the month, wasn’t it? Do you remember the day? I should like the exact day.”

“The fifteenth.”

“The fifteenth,” she repeated, tasting the date as if it were a spice whose name she once knew in a dead dialect. “I was at Glenalla all February. What was I doing on the fifteenth? It does not matter.”

Yet it did matter, in the strangest way. All morning, while Willoughby told his story, she had been haunted by surprise—surprise that she had not somehow known, in her blood or bones, at the very hour things happened. That astonishment returned now, sharpened. It was odd that she had been made to wait for this August night, for this garden where even the flowers kept their mouths shut, before she learned of a meeting on February 15 and heard a message that ought, by all rights, to have reached her like a familiar tune carried on wind.

Remorse slid in under the door of her heart.

It was my own fault, she thought. If I had kept faith, I should have known. I am well punished.

Still she assumed the message would be good. It would complete what she had already been told; it would round the day to perfection, like a circle closed without a seam.

“Well?” she urged softly. “Go on.”

“I had been busy all that day in my office finishing up my work,” Durrance said. “I turned the key in the door at ten o’clock, glad I shouldn’t open it for six weeks, and I strolled northward out of Wadi Halfa along the Nile bank into the little town of Tewfikieh. As I entered the main street I saw a small crowd—Arabs, negroes, a Greek or two, and some Egyptian soldiers—standing outside a café, lit by a hard glare from within. And as I came nearer I heard the sound of a violin and a zither—both most vilely played—jingling out a waltz.”

Ethne’s fingers tightened on the neck of her own violin. Her garden was full of night-sounds she had always loved: the small click of insects, the hush of leaves, the distant wash of water in the dark. Even Durrance’s voice, measured and low, belonged to that gentler ecology. But what he described—an unshaded paraffin lamp, a room made loud and flat by whitewashed walls, a crowd that jeered—came to her as a kind of acoustic pollution, the ugliness of noise where there should have been resonance.

“I stood at the back of the crowd,” he went on, “and looked over their shoulders into the room. Four bare whitewashed walls. A bar in a corner. A bench or two. A single paraffin lamp swinging and glaring from the ceiling. And a troupe of itinerant musicians playing to that crowd for a night’s lodging and the price of a meal.”

Ethne saw it as he spoke: the lamp’s crude circle of light, the shadows it made too black, the faces turned inwards like a ring around a pot. She imagined the air—hot paraffin, sour sweat, dust carried in from the street and ground into grit. The waltz, not flowing but stumbling, like dough beaten without care.

“There were four of them,” said Durrance, “and so far as I could see they were Greeks. Two were man and wife—both old, slatternly, almost in rags: he thin and sallow, grey hair and black moustache; she fat and coarse of face, heavy-bodied. Of the other two, one seemed their daughter—a girl of seventeen. Not really good-looking, but dressed with scrupulous care, which in those sordid surroundings lent her good looks. A clean ribbon to bind back her hair, an untorn white frock, shoes neat. I was rather touched by it—the father and mother in rags so she might be trim. The fourth was a young man, seated in the window with his back to me, bending over his zither. I could see he wore a beard.”

Ethne’s mind caught, fast as a hook. A young man, beard, back turned, a zither in the window. She thought of hands she knew—hands that had once been so useless at anything delicate, hands that had treated music as if it were a foreign tongue spoken too quickly, hands that had only ever listened with any attention when she herself had played and he could pretend it was meant for him alone.

“What was he doing with that troupe?” she thought. Harry had money. Willoughby had said so. He had no need to barter a meal for a tune.

When Durrance spoke of the old man’s violin, Ethne almost winced.

“When I came up,” he said, “the old man was playing the violin, though playing is not the word. The noise was like a pencil squeaking on slate—it set one’s teeth on edge; the violin itself seemed to squeal with pain. And while he fiddled and the young man hammered at the zither, the old woman and the girl slowly revolved in a waltz.”

Ethne could hear it: the bow scraping too close to the bridge, the strings crying, the zither notes dropping like pebbles into a tin basin. In her own quiet room, that imagined racket was an intrusion—like a factory whistle blown into a garden.

“It may sound comic to hear about,” Durrance said, “but if you could have seen—It fairly plucked at one’s heart. The crowd outside—negroes, mind you—laughing, passing low jests at their expense. And inside the four white people: the old woman clumsy and heavy-footed, shining with heat, panting; the girl young and lissom; the two men with discordant torturing music. And just above you the great planets and stars of an African sky, and just about you the silent dignity of the moonlit desert. Imagine it! The very ineptness hurt one.”

He paused, and Ethne let the picture form in her: the desert’s vast hush pressing against a room that could not hear itself properly; the lamp’s glare and the jeering crowd; the taste of dust at the back of the throat.

But her sympathy did not go to the troupe. It ran ahead, impatient, to the figure with the beard in the window.

“You spoke to him?” she asked suddenly.

“To whom? Oh—to Harry?” Durrance returned.

“Yes. How did you find out?”

“The waltz ended. The old woman sank exhausted on the bench; the young man lifted his head from the zither; the old man scraped a new chord; and the girl stood forward to sing. Her voice had youth and freshness, but no other music. Yet the old man smiled, the mother beat time with her heavy foot, nodding with pride at their daughter. And again in the throng outside the ill-conditioned talk—the untranslatable jests—went round. Horrible, don’t you think?”

“Yes,” Ethne said, but her voice wandered. Untranslatable. The word pricked her. In those jeers there would be meanings that could not be carried across tongues—cruelty seasoned with local knowledge, contempt preserved in idiom. Sound as a weapon; language as a net.

She sat gazing into the moon’s broad panel on the floor by the window, as if it were itself a doorway into that café in Tewfikieh. If she stared hard enough, perhaps she would see it—tiny, far off, yet blazing.

“Well?” she whispered. “After the song?”

“The young man with his back towards me began to fumble out a solo,” Durrance continued. “He struck so many false notes that at first there was no tune. The laughter grew. I was just turning away, sick at heart, when by chance he played a succession of notes correctly. They arrested me. I listened again, and a sort of haunting melody began to emerge—a weak thin thing with no soul in it, a ghost of a melody, and yet familiar. I stood there in the sandy street between hovels and stunted trees, and I was carried away out of the East to Ramelton, to a summer night in Donegal, when you sat by the open window as you sit now and played the Musoline Overture—which you played again tonight.”

Ethne’s breath caught, as if she had bitten into something too sharp.

“It was a melody from the overture?”

“Yes. And it was Harry Feversham who played it.”

For an instant she could not keep the exultation from her voice. “You are sure?”

“I did not guess it at once,” said Durrance. “I was not very quick in those days.”

“But you are now,” she said.

“Quicker, at all events. I should have guessed it now. Then I was only curious—how an itinerant Greek had picked it up. I determined to reward him for his diligence. I thought you would like me to.”

“Yes,” Ethne breathed, the word escaping like steam from a covered pot.

“When he came out from the café,” Durrance said, “and with his hat in his hand passed through the jeering crowd, I threw a sovereign into the hat. He turned to me with a start. In spite of his beard I knew him. Besides—before he could check himself—he cried out, ‘Jack!’”

“You can have made no mistake then,” Ethne said, wonder thickening her voice.

“No. The man who strummed the zither was—” Durrance stopped, and she felt how near he was to speaking Harry’s name aloud into the room. “—Feversham.”

“But he knew no music,” she said quickly, half laughing at the remembered certainty of it. “He had no ear. You couldn’t invent a discord harsh enough to attract his attention. He could never have remembered any melody from the Musoline Overture.”

“Yet it was Harry Feversham,” Durrance answered. “Somehow he had remembered. I can understand it. He would have so little he cared to remember, and that little he would strive with all his might to bring back clearly. Imagine him working the scrap of music out in his brain—humming, whistling it uncounted times, making perpetual errors—until one day he got it safe and fixed. And then picking it out laboriously on the strings. I can. Indeed, I can.”

So the answer came to her: that he had not forgotten. Not her—she would not name that. But the sound she had once made at an open window, the tune that belonged to her hands and his watching, had lodged in him like a seed. He had tended it in foreign soil until it sprouted thinly, pale and stubborn, in a café under a paraffin lamp.

She sat silent, moved in spite of every vow she had made herself. It seemed fitting—almost mythic in its plainness—that the message should travel not by letter but by melody, and that it should arrive broken, altered by distance and bad instruments, yet still itself. A tune could carry what language could not; no dominant lexicon could quite colonise it. It did not translate, it endured.

She pictured him in that bare room, the zither on his knee, his beard hiding his face, his fingers searching for her overture while the crowd laughed. Had he dreamed, as she had dreamed tonight, that even thin and feeble an echo might reach across the world?

She knew now, with an ache that made her throat taste of iron, that she would never truly forget him. At best she would pretend. And because she had once been unjustly harsh, she grew more resolved that Durrance must not suffer for her fault. Whatever grief she carried, she would carry it quietly.

“I told you last year, Ethne, in Hill Street,” Durrance resumed, “that I never wished to see Feversham again. I was wrong. The reluctance was all on his side, not mine. The moment he realised he had called my name he tried to edge backward into the crowd. He began to gabble Greek. But I caught his arm and wouldn’t let him go. He had done you some great wrong—that I know; that I knew. But I couldn’t remember it then. I only remembered that years before Harry Feversham had been my friend—my one great friend. We rowed in the same college boat at Oxford, he at stroke, I at seven—those stripes on his jersey made my eyes dizzy in the last hundred yards past the barges. We bathed at Sandford Lasher on summer afternoons. We supped on Kennington Island. We cut lectures and paddled up the Cher to Islip. And there he was at Wadi Halfa, herding with that troupe—an outcast, sunk to such ill-fortune that he must come to that squalid town and play vilely for a night’s lodging and the price of a meal.”

“No,” Ethne broke in, sharp as a snapped string. “It was not for that reason that he went to Wadi Halfa.”

“Why then?”

“I cannot think. But he was not in need of money. His father had continued his allowance, and he had accepted it.”

“You are sure?”

“Quite sure. I heard it only today,” she said—and did not even notice the slip. Her mind was too intent on the story’s missing piece.

Durrance, lost in his own recollection, let it pass.

“So you never knew what brought him to Halfa?” she pressed. “Did you not ask him? Why didn’t you? Why?”

The disappointment tasted bitter, like something burned at the bottom of a pan. Here was the last news of Harry Feversham and it came incomplete, like a letter torn in half. The missing part might never be recovered.

“I was a fool,” Durrance said. Regret thickened his voice until it almost matched hers. “I shall not easily forgive myself. He was my friend, you see. I had him by the arm—and I let him go. I was a fool.” He knocked his forehead with his fist.

“He tried Arabic,” Durrance continued. “He pleaded that he and his companions were poor peaceable people. If I had given too much money, I should take it back. All the while he dragged away. But I held him fast. I said, ‘Harry Feversham, that won’t do.’ And then he gave in and spoke English—whispering. ‘Let me go, Jack, let me go.’ The crowd was about us. He had some reason for secrecy—shame, perhaps, at his downfall. I said, ‘Come up to my quarters in Halfa as soon as you are free,’ and I let him go.”

Ethne leaned forward unconsciously, as if she could pull more out of the air by force.

“All that night I waited for him on the verandah,” Durrance said, “but he did not come. In the morning I had to start across the desert. I almost spoke of him to a friend who came to see me start—to Calder, in fact—you know of him—the man who sent you the telegram.”

“Yes, I remember,” Ethne answered—another slip, falling from her without sound.

“At the very last moment,” Durrance went on, “when my camel had risen, I stooped to speak to Calder, to tell him to see to Feversham. But I did not. I knew nothing about his allowance then. I only thought he had fallen low. It didn’t seem fair that another should know. So I rode on and kept silence.”

She nodded. She could not help approving the instinct, even while it stung her.

“So you never saw him again?”

“I was away nine weeks. I came back blind,” he said simply.

The simplicity pierced her. In the hush of her room, with the garden’s soft sounds beyond, the word blind fell heavy as a stone into water.

“I went at once to Cairo,” Durrance continued, “and Calder came with me. There I told him of Feversham—how I had seen him at Tewfikieh. I asked Calder, when he got back to Halfa, to make inquiries, to find and help him if he could; and to let me know the result. I received a letter from Calder a week ago, and I am troubled by it—very much troubled.”

“What did he say?” Ethne asked, and this time fear slid coldly beneath her ribs.

She turned away from the moonlight into the room’s shadow toward Durrance, bending forward as though sight could be forced from darkness. But the dark hid his face; only his voice came, and it seemed to her suddenly an unbearable human noise in a night that ought to have been quiet.

“That the two women and the old Greek had gone back northward on a steamer to Assouan.”

“Feversham remained at Halfa then?” she asked quickly. “That is so—isn’t it?”

“No,” Durrance replied. “Feversham did not remain. He slipped past Halfa the day after I started east. He went out in the morning—to the south.”

“Into the desert?”

“Yes. The desert to the south—the enemy’s country. He went just as I saw him, carrying his zither. He was seen. There can be no doubt.”

For a little while Ethne could not make a sound. The garden noises seemed to retreat, as if the world held its breath.

Then she forced herself to speak. “You have that letter with you?”

“Yes.”

“I should like to read it.”

She rose and crossed to Durrance. He took the letter from his pocket and gave it to her. She carried it to the window where the moonlight lay thick and clear on the sill, and stood there with a hand pressed to her heart as though to keep it from breaking its bounds.

She read it once, and then again. It was explicit: the Greek who owned the café admitted that Joseppi—the name under which he knew Feversham—had wandered south with a water-skin and a store of dates, though why he either did not know or would not tell.

Dates, she thought wildly, dates and water—provisions measured like rations in a recipe too small for the hunger it must face.

She had a question, but it took time to shape it without faltering.

“What will happen to him?”

“At the best, capture; at the worst, death,” Durrance said. “Starvation, thirst, or the Dervishes. But there is just a hope it might be only capture and imprisonment. He was white. If caught, they might think him a spy—sure he knew our plans and strength. They would most likely send him to Omdurman. I have written to Calder. Spies go out and in from Halfa; we often hear things that happen in Omdurman. If Feversham is taken there, sooner or later I shall know. But he must have gone mad. It is the only explanation.”

Ethne had another explanation. It rose in her like a remembered word from a language she had once spoken at home—something no outsider could translate cleanly, because it belonged to the private country of loyalty and shame.

Off her guard, she spoke it aloud.

“Colonel Trench,” she said, “is a prisoner at Omdurman.”

“Oh, yes,” Durrance answered. “Feversham will not be quite alone then. There is comfort in that, and perhaps something may be done. When I hear from Calder I will tell you. Perhaps something may be done.”

He had misconstrued her, and she knew he must remain in the dark. She would not betray Harry’s motive with any careless syllable. She would still pretend—pretend to have forgotten, pretend that this news did not tear her. She must not cry out. She must not swoon. She must stand very still, like a cook who has burnt her hand and cannot drop the pan because the meal must still be served.

She stood at the window, the letter clenched, as Durrance began to speak again.

“Perhaps you will wonder,” he said, “why I have told you tonight what I have up till now kept to myself. I did not dare to tell you before. I want to explain why.”

Ethne did not hear the exultation in his voice; she did not even weigh what his explanation might be. The mere fact of speech—any speech—had become unendurable. The night outside called to her with its cool quiet: the dark of the trees, the honest rustle of leaves, the soft, living sounds that did not demand translation and did not wound with meaning.

She was close by the window. One step, silent, and she would be across the sill and free.

Durrance continued, engrossed in his own words, and she did not listen. She gathered her skirts with careful fingers so they would not rustle, and stepped out through the window into the garden.

This was the third slip she made upon that eventful night.
