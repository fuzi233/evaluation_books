As the sun began its descent into the horizon, casting a warm orange glow across the cobblestone streets of Viborg, my cousin found himself standing before the Golden Lion Inn, its imposing façade a testament to the town's rich history. The once-majestic building, spared from the great fire of 1726 that ravaged the town, now stood as a relic of a bygone era, its red-brick exterior softened by the gentle curves of corbie steps and a weathered wooden sign creaking in the breeze. The courtyard, a stark contrast to the rustic charm of the façade, was a simple yet elegant arrangement of black and white wood and plaster.

My cousin, a scholar driven by a passion for the Church history of Denmark, had arrived in Viborg seeking refuge and solitude. His researches had led him to the Rigsarkiv, a treasure trove of documents saved from the flames, holding secrets of the last days of Roman Catholicism in the country. He hoped to spend several weeks poring over the dusty tomes and manuscripts, and the Golden Lion's promise of a quiet and comfortable stay had drawn him in. After explaining his requirements to the landlord, a kindly man with a bushy beard and a twinkle in his eye, they set off to explore the available rooms.

The top floor, with its steep stairs and narrow corridors, was quickly dismissed as impractical for a scholar such as my cousin, who preferred to devote his energy to his research rather than his physical exertions. The second floor offered little in the way of suitable accommodations, but the first floor presented a trio of rooms that caught his eye. The landlord, a stout man with a hearty laugh, was adamant that Number 17 was the perfect choice, but my cousin demurred, pointing out that the room's windows overlooked a blank wall, casting the space in a dismal gloom. Either Number 12 or Number 14 would be a better fit, he argued, their windows offering a bright and cheerful view of the street, the evening light dancing across the cobblestones like a thousand tiny lanterns.

In the end, Number 12 was chosen, its three windows a blessing for a scholar who valued natural light and a connection to the world outside. The room itself was a simple but pleasant space, its long walls adorned with a beautiful old stove, its cast-iron body a testament to the craftsmanship of a bygone era.
