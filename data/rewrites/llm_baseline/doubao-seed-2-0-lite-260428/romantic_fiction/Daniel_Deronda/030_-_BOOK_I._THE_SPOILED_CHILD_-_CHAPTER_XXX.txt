No penitence and no confessional, No priest ordains it, yet they’re forced to sit Amid deep ashes of their vanished years.

Picture a rambling, patched old house, its finest walls hewn from soft gray stone and capped with weathered red tile, a round turret jabbing out from one corner, its dark conical roof crowned with a whirling weathercock that glows warm in summer sunlight or stands sharp against the heavy, snow-heavy winter clouds. Broad spreading trees cast its grounds in dappled shade: one great gnarled oak towers at the side, a stand of bare-branched Scotch firs clinging to a broken bank behind it with roots exposed like knuckled fingers, and beyond them, a rookery chattering at dusk. The other edge of the house overlooks a dark pool fringed with brambles, where wild fowl flutter and shriek, and all around stretches a wide meadow so large it passes for a park, ringed with old planted woodland and guarded by squat stone lodges that look like tiny, barred prisons.

Outside the iron gates, the once soft, green rural countryside has turned black, blighted by coal mines, the roads caked in coal dust, the spoil heaps rising like dark funeral mounds draped in perpetual mourning. The district’s streets are filled with colliers, each with a candle stuck in the brim of his hat, their faces smudged dark as devils’—a sight that filled the four beautiful Glasher children, who had lived secluded at Gadsmere for three years, with quiet, childish suspicion. It was mid-November now, the flowerbeds bare, the trees stripped of every leaf, the pool shivering black under gray skies, and the whole place would have felt unbearably somber if not for the four small children chasing their dogs across the gravel drive, their high laughter cutting through the hush.

But Lydia Glasher liked Gadsmere as well as she might like any home these days. The total seclusion, guaranteed by the ugly, uninviting mining country surrounding them, was exactly what she craved. When she drove her two ponies harnessed to a waggonet full of children, she never ran into fine ladies in carriages, only sharp-faced businessmen in gigs. At church, there were no eyes she feared: the curate and his wife either knew nothing of her past or chose to ignore it, seeing her simply as a quiet widowed lady, the tenant of Gadsmere. The grand name of Grandcourt meant nothing here, far less than the names of Fletcher and Gawcome, the men who leased the local collieries.

Ten years had passed since the scandal of the Irish officer’s beautiful young wife eloping with the heir Grandcourt, followed by a farcical duel where the two men’s bullets never came close to grazing anyone. Most folks who remembered the affair long ago assumed Grandcourt had finally freed himself from the disreputable connection, that the foolish woman who’d run off with him had sunk even lower in the world, after abandoning her little son to leave with her husband. No one spoke of Lydia Glasher now any more than they spoke of the forgotten victim of a ten-year-old manslaughter trial. She was a lost ship no one bothered to launch a rescue mission for, while Grandcourt was still seen sailing proudly into harbor, all flags flying, pronounced perfectly seaworthy.

But Grandcourt had never untangled himself from Lydia. His passion for her had been the strongest, longest-lasting he’d ever known, and even now, when that passion was as dead as the music from a cracked, discarded flute, it left a slow, dull lingering fondness. When her husband died three years before, he’d half-heartedly toyed with the idea of marrying her, just as they’d often promised each other back in the wild early days of their affair. Back then, he’d have happily paid to arrange a divorce, but Lydia’s husband had refused to cooperate: he had no interest in remarrying, and even less interest in parading his own terrible domestic habits in open court.

Lydia’s feelings, meanwhile, had shifted entirely in the opposite direction. At the start of their elopement, she’d barely cared about the possibility of marriage. She’d escaped five years of brutal, rude treatment at the hands of her first husband, and the sheer bliss of being adored by a young, handsome, wealthy lover who’d seen every corner of the world was enough for her. She was passionate, vivacious, starved for affection, and the thrill of freedom had quieted every other anxiety. An ambiguous, unmarried position hadn’t bothered her back then; she’d never envied the hollow honors of a ignored, dull wife. The only crack in her perfect new world was the memory of the three-year-old little boy she’d left behind, who died two years later—his first soft “mamma” sounding nothing like the voices of the children she’d had after running away.

But years had changed everything, eroding the soft curves of her cheeks and throat, and now marrying Grandcourt was her entire, all-consuming desire. The once irrelevant ambiguous position was now harming her children, whom she loved with a devotion stoked hot by the burning need to atone for her past. She had no other penitence. If Grandcourt married her, her children would suffer no stigma. They would see their mother stand in a dignified, respected position, and no one in the world could look down on them. Her son could be named his father’s rightful heir. That yearning was the only thing that mattered to her now; her love for Grandcourt had long since softened into sharp, constant anxiety that he would give her the permanent, unimpeachable status of his wife. She expected no other happiness from marriage than the satisfaction of her maternal love and pride, the pride of holding her head high in front of her own children. She had schooled herself for years to never badger him with passionate pleas, never make a scene, because she’d learned early that Grandcourt turned hard, unyielding, the more anyone begged him. All those withheld sharp words, all that smothered rage, had been slowly curdling into venom. She was completely dependent on him; though he’d always been generous with their expenses, every penny he spent was a voluntary favor, never a right. Even if he never married her, she’d told herself, he might never have a legitimate son, and their boy could still inherit the bulk of his vast estates. Even Grandcourt’s quiet right-hand man Lush had once said he’d bet on Lydia marrying his master as soon as any other possible outcome. But when Gwendolen Harleth had appeared out of nowhere, derailing his plans to court Miss Arrowpoint, Lydia had leaped eagerly into Lush’s scheme to warn the new girl away from Grandcourt, letting a little of that stored-up venom finally loose.

After that, Lush had written to say Gwendolen had left town, and the threat was gone for good. No one had written to tell her the threat had come back, and was now a certainty. She’d written to Grandcourt as usual, but he’d taken far longer than normal to reply. She’d dared to hope, as only desperate, strong wishing can make you, that the sting of that failed earlier courtship would push him easily back into the old promise of marrying her.

Grandcourt had two unshakable purposes driving him on his journey to Gadsmere that day. First, he would break the news of his impending marriage in person, to make this first, messy hurdle final. Second, he would take back the diamonds he’d long ago given Lydia to wear, diamonds he’d decided belonged on the neck of his new wife. They weren’t some legendary mountain of light, just a set of modest earrings, necklace, and hair jewels worth several thousand pounds, but Grandcourt was determined to have them back. The last time he’d asked her to return them, claiming they’d be safer locked in a bank vault, Lydia had refused calmly, absolutely, and finally told him, “If you ever marry another woman, I will give them up to her. Are you going to marry another woman?” Back then, he’d had no reason to push the issue. He’d always held back from throwing his weight around to hurt or cow Lydia, the last lingering trace of the power she’d once held over him, the last faint ghost of that old passion that had once made life feel bright. But new novelty, the thrill of chasing Gwendolen, had woken him up now, and it pushed him to cast that old softness aside.

Lydia was seated in her favorite morning room, the four children clustered around her. The square bay window looked out over broad gravel and grass sloping down to a tiny brook that fed the black pool. The top of a low black oak cabinet, the old dining table, the scuffed tawny leather chairs were all littered with toys, storybooks, and the children’s messy garden coats, while a pastel portrait of a smiling, indulgent maternal lady looked down on the chaos from the wall. The three little girls, barely older than toddlers to nine years old, were exact miniature copies of Lydia: dark-eyed, delicate-featured brunettes with rich rose bloom on their cheeks, their small noses and eyebrows so perfectly formed they looked like tiny grown women. Their little boy Henleigh sat cross-legged on the carpet some distance away, his blonde head bent over a set of Noah’s ark animals, lecturing each one in a stern, commanding voice, occasionally licking the spotted toy beasts to check if their paint would smudge. The eldest girl Josephine was having her French lesson, while the other two sat demurely with their dolls in their laps, as still as Madonna statues.

Lydia had dressed with careful, deliberate effort that morning, as she did every day now, telling herself Grandcourt might walk through the door at any moment. Her fine profile, the crisp curves of her dark hair, her sharp, distinct eyebrows still made her head look striking, rising above her bronze silk and velvet gown and the old gold necklace Grandcourt had clasped around her neck all those years ago. It wasn’t vanity that made her fuss over her appearance; when she looked in the mirror, all she saw was how much she had changed, how worn she’d grown. But she would hold onto every scrap of the beauty she had left. If her greatest wish came true, she knew she would bloom again, growing into the dignified, handsome matron fit to stand at his side. Her children’s faces, exact copies of her own younger self, reminded her of the soft, blooming curves she’d lost, but the children kissed her pale sunken cheeks all the same, and never thought them anything but perfect. Their love was the whole point of her life now.

Suddenly, Lydia pulled her gaze away from Josephine’s lesson book and tilted her head, listening. “Hush, dear! I think someone’s coming.”

Henleigh jumped up immediately. “Mamma! Is it the miller bringing my new donkey?”

He got no answer, and tugged insistently at her knee to repeat his question, just as the door swung open, and the servant announced Mr. Grandcourt. Lydia rose to her feet, trembling with agitation. Henleigh scowled, furious that it was not the miller, and the three little girls lifted their dark eyes to Grandcourt shyly, none of them caring much for this strange man who visited their mother. When Grandcourt took Lydia’s hand, then leaned down to rest his other hand on Henleigh’s head, the little boy began beating his arm away with small, furious fists. The girls submitted bashfully to a light pat under the chin and a quick kiss, but it was clear the best course was to send them all out to the garden, where they were soon dancing and chattering with the dogs on the gravel.

“How far have you ridden?” Lydia asked, as Grandcourt set down his hat and overcoat.

“From Diplow,” he drawled slowly, seating himself across from her and looking at her with that detached, unnoticing gaze that made her skin prickle.

“Then you must be tired.”

“Not at all. I rested at the junction—hideous little hole. Railway journeys are always a confounded bore, but I had coffee and smoked.” He pulled out his handkerchief, rubbed his face, then stared down at his crossed knee and spotless polished boot, as if a total stranger sat across from him, not the woman quivering with suspense, every word and look of his tipping the scale between her greatest hope and worst dread. The difference between them was stark: Lydia, worn thin by years of sharp, desperate dependence, and Grandcourt, dulled into a thick, unyielding stubbornness.

“I expected you,” she said, her voice quick and sharp, the tone matching her fine features like the tight strings of a violin match its carved wood. “It’s been so long since I heard from you. The weeks drag so much slower at Gadsmere than they do at Diplow.”

“Quite,” he said, plucking idly at his whisker, not looking at her at first. “But you got the money I paid into your bank account, didn’t you?”

“Oh, yes,” she said shortly, tingling with impatience. He had never been this distant with her before, or at least she had never noticed it so sharply.

“Things have moved at quite a rattling pace for me lately, when usually they drag by slow as molasses. A lot has happened, as you know—” he finally lifted his eyes to meet hers.

“What do I know?” she snapped.

He paused a beat, his tone not shifting an inch. “That I was thinking of marrying. You saw Miss Harleth, didn’t you?”

“Did she tell you I spoke to her?” The pale skin of her cheeks went even whiter, lit from within by the fierce bright fire in her eyes.

“No. Lush told me,” he answered slowly. It felt as if the screws of a torture device were being set down, creeping, within sight of the waiting victim.

“Good God—just say it out, then. You’re going to marry her,” she burst out passionately, her knees shaking, her hands clenching tight until her knuckles went white.

“Of course something like this had to happen sooner or later, Lydia,” he said, now that the metaphorical screw was fully in place, not wanting to make the pain any sharper than he had to.

“You didn’t always think it was necessary.”

“Maybe not. I see it now.”

Those few low, calm words hit her like a slamming iron door, her thin fingers pushing uselessly against its solid surface. She knew exactly how helpless she was, and she shrank from making a fool of herself, begging to a man whose ears were already dead to her pleas, clinging to his dead knees only to stare at his unmoving, cold face. She didn’t weep, she didn’t speak, stunned by the sudden certainty that carried all the sick chill of a physical blow. For a long, breathless moment, the defeated, useless clutch of her dying hope made her feel like she was suffocating. Finally, she made a spasmodic effort to stand, and unconscious of everything but her own wretchedness, pressed her forehead hard against the cold glass of the window. The children playing on the gravel saw her, thought she was calling them, and ran up to press their sweet faces to the glass, staring up at her expectantly. It jolted her back to her senses. She shook her head at them, waved them away, and the effort was so painful she sank back into the nearest chair, gasping.

Grandcourt had stood up too. He was doubly annoyed: annoyed by the messy, emotional scene playing out in front of him, and annoyed that even all his imperious willpower couldn’t make it go away. But the task had to be finished, and he had to arrange everything so there would be as little future annoyance as possible. He leaned against the corner of the fireplace. Lydia looked up at him, her voice sharp with bitterness.

“All of this means nothing to you. Me and the children are just burdensome, importunate creatures. You can’t wait to get back to Miss Harleth.”

“Don’t make this more disagreeable than it has to be, Lydia. There’s no use harping on things that can’t be changed. It’s deucedly unpleasant for me to see you making yourself miserable. I came all this way to tell you what you have to make up your mind to. You and the children will be provided for exactly as before, and that’s an end to it.”

Silence hung heavy in the room. She dared not speak. This woman with the bright, hungry eyes had the iron of a mother’s anguish driven deep into her soul, and it gave her a harder, stonier repression than any screaming or struggle could manage. Underneath that still surface, though, rage and vindictiveness lashed out. She wished this new marriage would make two people as wretched as she was.

After a minute, Grandcourt spoke again. “It will be better for you in the long run. You can stay right here at Gadsmere. Later on, I’ll settle a very large sum on you and the children, so you’ll be able to live wherever you like. You’ll never have anything to complain of. You’ll be completely secure, no matter what happens. Nothing could be arranged beforehand, everything has happened very quickly.”

He finished his slow, flat speech. He didn’t expect her to thank him, but he thought it was reasonable for her to be at least somewhat contented, as much as Lydia could ever be contented. She showed no sign of shifting, and after another minute he added, “You never had any reason to fear I’d be illiberal. I don’t care a curse about the money.”

“If you did care about it, I suppose you wouldn’t give any of it to us,” Lydia said. The sarcasm slipped out before she could stop it.

“That’s a devilishly unfair thing to say,” Grandcourt replied, his voice dropping low. “I’d advise you not to say that sort of thing ever again.”

“Are you going to punish me by letting the children starve in beggary?” The little crack in her self-control had let another spurt of venom out.

“There’s no question of that,” Grandcourt said, still in that low, dangerous tone. “I advise you not to say things you’ll regret later.”

“I’m used to repenting,” she said, bitterly. “Maybe *you* will repent. You already repented of loving me.”

“All this is only going to make it uncommonly hard for us to ever meet again. What other friend do you have besides me?”

“Quite true.” The words came out as a soft, ragged moan. At that exact moment, a thought flashed through her head: after Grandcourt had promised himself a happiness better than anything he’d ever had with her, he would be struck by such terrible loneliness and misery that he would come crawling back to her, chasing the memory of the days when he had been young, glad, and hopeful. But no. He would walk away scatheless. She was the one who had to suffer.
