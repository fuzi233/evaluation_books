Lieutenant Sutch, though he had turned in late, was on his feet with the first thin light, and he filled the house as wind fills a chimney. Doors clicked, drawers slammed, boots were dragged from one room to another, and a dozen times the same battered valise was packed, unpacked, and packed again, as if the very act of folding a collar and laying it atop a shirt might compel the day to hurry and the long years behind him to loosen their grip.

For thirty years he had lived on what he called the world’s half-pay list, with nothing demanded of him except patience; and now, like yeast suddenly wakened in warm water, he rose—restless, expanding, foaming over into every corner. The servants, who had grown used to a quieter household than any farm at harvest, scurried along the passages, their footsteps and whispers chased by Sutch’s sharper summonses. The house had not held so much commotion in all his decades there. Even the clocks seemed offended, their ticks drowned by the flap of linen and the clatter of buckles.

At breakfast, the table was steady enough—white cloth, the faint scent of tea leaves opening in the pot, toast browned to the edge of bitterness—but the air around Sutch trembled as if with kettledrums. He ate in snatches, as though food were merely an inconvenience between himself and the platform lights of a station.

“I shall be ready this afternoon,” he said briskly to Durrance. “Night mail to the Continent. We might go up to London together; for London lies on your road to Wiesbaden.”

“No,” Durrance answered. “There is one more visit I must pay in England. I did not think of it until I was in bed last night. You put it into my head.”

Sutch set down his knife and fork with a small, decisive tap, like the strike of a spoon against a saucepan to test its temper. “Oh,” he observed. “And whom do you propose to visit?”

“General Feversham.”

Surprise took Sutch full in the face. “Why in the world do you wish to see him?”

Durrance’s hand, moving with the careful economy he had learned, found the rim of his cup. The china was warm; the tea smelled of Assam and faint smoke, and beyond the window a rook called once, as if to mark the point.

“I want to tell him how Harry has redeemed his honour—how he is still redeeming it. You said last night you were bound by a promise not to tell him anything of his son’s intention, or even of his son’s success, until the son returned himself. But I am bound by no promise.” His voice did not rise; it did not need to. “Such a promise bears hardly on the General. Nothing could pain him so much as the proof that his son was a coward. Harry might have robbed and murdered; the old man would have preferred both those crimes. I shall cross into Surrey this morning and tell him that Harry never was a coward.”

Sutch shook his head, stubborn as a man salting meat against a winter he cannot prevent. “He will not understand. He will be grateful, of course. He will be glad that Harry has atoned his disgrace, but he will never understand why he incurred it. And after all, he will only be glad because the family honour is restored.”

“I don’t agree,” Durrance said. “I think the old man is rather fond of his son—though he would never admit it. I rather like General Feversham.”

Sutch had seen little of the General during these last years, and what he had seen left an old sourness on his tongue. He could not forgive the man for his share in the ruin of Harry Feversham. Had there been sympathy enough at Broad Place—had there been any understanding of a boy’s hidden terrors—the white feathers would never have flown into Ramelton like carrion birds. In Sutch’s mind’s eye the General sat stern on his terrace, certain he was blameless, half pleased to play the martyr before the dark, uniformed Fevershams staring down from the hall walls. Sutch felt he could not endure a patient conversation with so fixed a conscience, nor did he believe argument could shift it an inch.

“You are very thoughtful for others,” he said instead.

“It’s not to my credit,” Durrance replied. “I practise thoughtfulness out of self-preservation. Selfishness is the natural, encroaching fault of the blind. I know that; so I am careful to guard against it.”

So Durrance travelled that morning—branch lines that stitched Hampshire to Surrey, carriages that smelt of coal-dust and damp wool, stations where the announcements bounced and died beneath iron roofs. The soundscape changed with every stop: hedge-birds giving way to the hard, metallic echo of towns, then back again to the softer hush of fields. In each place, noise pressed upon him as a thing with weight; he measured space by it, as a cook measures a broth by the way its simmer speaks.

By afternoon he came to Broad Place, carried into its long-established quiet where even the gravel seemed to crunch underfoot with deliberate restraint. The pines held the wind; their needles made a thin, dry susurration like flour sifted slowly from a sieve.

General Feversham was within a few months of eighty. His back was still stiff, his bearing still the old drill-ground straightness, but he had shrunk, and his face, once carved and commanding, seemed now to have grown smaller upon his skull, as if time had tightened the skin. Durrance had walked with him on the terrace two years before; blind though he was, he heard and felt the difference—heavier steps, a breath that came more grudgingly, and a childish note in the voice, not of softness but of fretful impatience.

“You have joined the veterans before your time, Durrance,” the General said. “I read of it in a newspaper. I would have written had I known where to write.”

If he suspected why Durrance had come, he hid it well. He rang the bell. Tea was brought into the great hall where the portraits hung—dark canvases of dead men in uniform, their painted medals catching what little light lay in the room. The tray arrived with the faint aroma of bergamot and warm milk, and the small domestic sounds—the spoon against the pot, the cup set into its saucer—fell oddly among those rigid, watchful faces.

The General asked after officers in the Soudan, discussed the iniquities of the War Office, and declared, with a rising irritability, that the country was going to the deuce.

“Everything through ill-luck or bad management is going to the devil, sir,” he exclaimed. “Even you, Durrance—you are not the same man who walked with me on my terrace two years ago.”

The words were blunt, and the solitude he kept had not improved his tact. Durrance might have answered him in kind, but he did not.

“I come upon the same business,” he said instead.

Feversham sat up as though a string had been drawn tight within him. “And I give you the same answer. I have nothing to say about Harry Feversham. I will not discuss him.”

He spoke the name without tremor or tenderness, as though it belonged to some stranger in the county paper. Durrance, listening, wondered whether affection had dried up entirely in that stern heart.

“It would not please you, then, to know where Harry Feversham has been,” Durrance asked, “and how he has lived during the last five years?”

There was a pause—not long, but real; a pause in which the hall seemed to hold its breath, in which even the kettledrum beat of modern life could not intrude upon the old house’s thick silence. Then:

“Not in the least, Colonel Durrance.”

“Nor on what business he has been engaged?”

“I am not interested in the smallest degree. I do not wish him to starve, and my solicitor tells me that he draws his allowance. I am content with that knowledge.”

Durrance set his cup down carefully. “I will risk your anger, General. There are times when it is wise to disobey one’s superior officer. This is one of the times. Of course you can turn me out of the house. Otherwise I shall relate to you the history of your son and my friend since he disappeared from England.”

General Feversham laughed once, short and dry. “Of course I can’t turn you out,” he said, and added severely, “But I warn you that you are taking an improper advantage of your position as my guest.”

“Yes. There is no doubt of that,” Durrance returned, as calmly as a man adjusting the flame under a pan.

And then he told him—plainly, without ornament and without mercy. He spoke of Berber and the Gordon letters recovered from danger; of Wadi Halfa, and the meeting there; of Omdurman and the imprisonment. He brought the tale down to this very day, ending with Lieutenant Sutch’s departure for Suakin. His voice paced the events with the steadiness of a knife’s stroke through a loaf: each slice clean, each fact laid down where it could not be denied.

Through it all the General did not interrupt. He did not shift in his chair. He did not even make the small sounds men make when they doubt. He listened, and his silence was as rigid as the painted men above them.

When Durrance finished, Feversham sat for some time without speaking. He raised his hand to his forehead and shaded his eyes as though the other man’s blindness might be reversed and sight restored by that old habitual gesture. Pride would not let him show those portraits any open joy, any weakness so common as relief.

“What I don’t understand,” he said at last, slowly, “is why Harry ever resigned his commission. I could not understand it before; I understand it even less now, since you have told me of his great bravery. It is one of the queer inexplicable things. They happen, and there’s all that can be said.” He drew breath, and something in the breath was nearer to gratitude than he had ever allowed himself. “But I am very glad you compelled me to listen to you, Durrance.”

“I did it with a definite object. It is for you to say, of course; but for my part I do not see why Harry should not come home and enter in again to all that he lost.”

“He cannot regain everything,” Feversham said. “It is not right that he should. He committed the sin and must pay. He cannot regain his career, for one thing.”

“No,” Durrance agreed. “But he can find another. He is not yet so old but that he can find another. And that is all he will have lost.”

Now Feversham took his hand away and moved in his chair. He looked quickly at Durrance; his mouth opened as though to ask a question, then closed again. The question hovered in the air like an unspoken dialect word—full of private meaning, with no safe translation into the harsh public language he lived by.

“Well,” he said briskly, as though the matter were trivial, “if Sutch can manage Harry’s escape from Omdurman, I see no reason why he should not come home.”

Durrance rose. “Thank you, General. If you can have me driven to the station, I can catch a train to town. There’s one at six.”

“But you will stay the night, surely,” Feversham cried, and the sudden eagerness startled even him.

“It is impossible. I start for Wiesbaden early to-morrow.”

The General rang. Orders were given; somewhere deeper in the house wheels were readied, leather creaked, a groom’s voice answered. In those practical sounds—soothing, orderly—there was a kind of domestic comfort, like hearing bread crackle as it cools after the oven.

“I should have been very glad if you could have stayed,” Feversham said, turning back. “I see very few people nowadays. To tell the truth, I have no great desire to see many. One grows old and a creature of customs.”

“But you have your Crimean nights,” Durrance said, attempting cheerfulness.

Feversham shook his head. “There have been none since Harry went away. I had no heart for them.”

For a second the mask lifted. His stern features softened, and behind them Durrance saw—by tone, by pause, by the way the words fell—the depth of the man’s grief. He had suffered alone through five years of old age, and none had been allowed to hear it. He had held a stubborn front to the world; made pride of the fact that no one could point and say, There is a man struck down. Yet in that one sentence he admitted the emptiness of his evenings, the way courage and cowardice alike, spoken of by guests as if they were stories for a fireside, would have cut him.

Outside, the carriage wheels rattled on the gravel, a brisk, reassuring rhythm against the great stillness of the estate.

“Good-bye,” Durrance said, and held out his hand.

Feversham took it; then, as though remembering a practical matter at the last moment, he asked, “By the way—to organise this escape from Omdurman will cost a great deal of money. Sutch is a poor man. Who is paying?”

“I am.”

Feversham’s grasp tightened, firm and sudden. “It is my right, of course,” he said.

“Certainly. I will let you know what it costs.”

“Thank you.”

He accompanied Durrance to the door. On the steps he lingered, uneasy, as if the pines had turned to listeners and the gravel itself might carry tales. The delicate question—so difficult to phrase in the General’s hard vocabulary—pressed at him again.

“Didn’t I hear, Durrance,” he began, with an air of careless inquiry, “that you were engaged to Miss Eustace?”

Durrance paused with one foot on the carriage step. “I think I said that Harry would regain all that he had lost—except his career,” he replied.

He got in. The carriage rolled away between the pines, the sound shrinking down the drive until it was swallowed by the estate’s hush. Durrance’s work was done. There was nothing more now but to wait at Wiesbaden and pray that Sutch might succeed. He had devised the plan; it remained for those with eyes to execute it.

General Feversham stood on the steps looking after the carriage until it disappeared. Then he turned and walked slowly back into the hall, where the portraits watched from their shadows. The air held the faint ghost of tea and old polish, of stone that had absorbed generations of footsteps.

“There is no reason why he should not come back,” he said, lifting his gaze to the painted Fevershams in their uniforms. The dead men would not be disgraced. “No reason in the world,” he repeated, and the words, spoken in that resonant chamber, seemed less like a declaration than a charm.

“And, please God, he will come back soon.”

As night came, the dangers of an escape from the Dervish city—remote among sands that swallowed tracks and men alike—grew in his mind until they crowded the room. He admitted to himself, in the privacy he had always guarded, that he felt tired and old. Many times he repeated his prayer, “Please God, Harry will come back soon,” as he sat erect upon the bench that had once been his wife’s favourite seat and listened to the countryside.

The house was quiet, but the world outside was not mute: a distant owl, the soft push of wind through pines, the small, honest noises of a living land—sounds older than any war-office grievance and truer than any polished talk. Across the moonlit country, toward the Sussex Downs, he gazed as if sight could pull a son home along the dark, and the name he had refused to speak with tenderness moved at last, unspoken, in the spaces between each measured breath.
