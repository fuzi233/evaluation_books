Kind friends, generous neighbours, a name respected through generations, a vast estate, and enough wealth to live comfortably—these are the marks of a fortunate life. Add to them a well-kept home filled with all that is needed and much that is desired, servants both white and black eager to serve, good health, loving children, and—let us be honest—a fine cook, a well-stocked cellar, and a library full of books: should not such a person be considered blessed? Madam Esmond Warrington had all these gifts. She counted them each day in her morning and evening prayers. She was devout, charitable to the poor, and never knowingly wronged another soul.

Imagine her seated in her domain, Castlewood, in Westmorland County, Virginia—a true queen in her own right. The local gentry came to pay their respects; her sons were dutiful; her servants vied to obey her every word; the poor whites were grateful for her kindness and trusted her remedies when sick; even the lesser landowners gave way to her opinions, especially at backgammon, where she always won. With all this power and comfort, one might think the little Princess Pocahontas, as she was fondly called, lived in perfect contentment. But perhaps her husband’s early death was a mercy. Had he lived longer, they would have quarrelled bitterly—or he would have become a henpecked husband, a fate not uncommon in those days. For truth be told, Madam Esmond ruled over everyone she met. If people submitted, she was kind and generous; if they resisted, she fought until either they or she gave in.

We all carry our sins, as we confess every Sunday—and no one declared this more plainly than she. As a mortal woman, she may have been mistaken, but she rarely admitted it to herself, and never to others. Her father, in his later years, watched her with amusement as she displayed her stubbornness and pride. He saw her flaws, and his quiet humour unsettled her. But after the Colonel passed away, no one remained who could tame her will. And so I must confess, I am glad I did not live at Castlewood a hundred years ago. I suspect life there would not have been very pleasant.

Who among us is truly happy? Even in Eden, there was a serpent. Would Eve have listened to him if she had been perfectly content? Perhaps not.

The management of Castlewood had long rested in the capable hands of the energetic Madam Esmond, even before her husband died. Now she oversaw everything with strict care: she dismissed the Colonel’s English factor and appointed a new one; built and improved buildings; planted crops; grew tobacco; hired a new overseer; and brought in a fresh tutor. Though she loved her father deeply, she did not follow all his ways. Had she not obeyed her parents throughout their lives, as any dutiful daughter should? So, too, ought all children to obey their parents, that their days might be long in the land.

This little queen ruled her little kingdom with an iron hand, and her sons were merely her first subjects. Soon after her husband’s death, she dropped the name Warrington and began calling herself Madam Esmond in public. Her family lineage was known throughout the colony. She took pride in speaking of the Marquis title granted by King James to her father and grandfather. Though her father had shown great generosity by giving up his titles to the younger branch—the half-brother, Lord Castlewood, and his heirs—she and her sons belonged to the elder line of Esmonds. They deserved proper recognition.

Lord Fairfax was the only man in Virginia whom she allowed precedence over herself. She insisted on being addressed first at gatherings, ahead of Lieutenant-Governors’ wives and judges’ ladies. Only before the Governor’s wife—representative of the Crown—would she yield. Records from family letters reveal fierce disputes between her and other colonial ladies over such matters of etiquette.

As for her husband’s family, the Warringtons of England, she regarded them as insignificant. She had married the younger son of an English baronet from Norfolk—not out of love, but to please her parents, whom she always obeyed. At such a young age—fresh from boarding school—she would have jumped into the sea if her father had commanded it. “And that,” she once said, “is always the way with the Esmonds.”

The English Warringtons were not pleased with how she treated them or spoke of them. Once a year, she sent formal letters to her relatives in England, including the noble Esmonds of Hampshire. But when a Judge’s wife returning to England met Lady Warrington in London, she repeated some of the things Madam Esmond had said about her English kin. Lady Warrington likely shared the story with Lady Castlewood, and from then on, the letters from Virginia went unanswered. This angered Madam Esmond deeply, and she soon stopped writing altogether.

Thus, the good lady fell out with her neighbours, her relatives—and, as it must be admitted, with her sons as well.

An early conflict arose when she dismissed Mr. Dempster, the boys’ tutor and her late husband’s secretary. During her father’s lifetime, she had barely tolerated him—and he had found her hard to bear. She distrusted scholars, believing bookworms dangerous, prone to spread subversive ideas. She heard whispers that Dempster was a Jesuit in disguise, and so he was forced to leave Castlewood, build a cabin in the woods, and teach school while practising medicine among the scattered settlers.

Master George vowed never to abandon his old tutor—and kept his promise. Harry, however, preferred fishing and hunting to books, and had never grown close to the poor scholar.

Another dispute followed. Upon the death of an aunt and then her husband, George Warrington became heir to six thousand pounds, with Madam Esmond named as one of the trustees. Yet she refused to accept that she was only a trustee, not the owner. When the London lawyer—the other trustee—refused to send the money at her command, she flew into a rage.

“Is not all I possess my sons’?” she demanded. “Would I not cut myself into pieces to help them? With that money, I could have bought Mr. Boulter’s estate and slaves—adding a thousand pounds a year to our income, and securing a proper future for my Harry!”

Her neighbour, Mr. Washington of Mount Vernon, tried to explain that the agent had a duty to act only for the rightful beneficiaries. But Madam Esmond would not listen. She accused the lawyer of insolence, calling him a pettifogger who doubted the honour of a mother and an Esmond.

It must be acknowledged—this Virginian princess had a fiery spirit.

When George, her eldest son, was asked to decide, he sided with Mr. Washington and the lawyer. He said he could not take the money—it wasn’t his to claim. He wished he could give it to his mother, but he had no authority. Yet Madam Esmond would hear none of it. Her feelings were her law.

Here was a chance to secure Harry’s future—dear Harry, left with only a meagre inheritance as the younger brother. And the London men refused to help! Her own son, who inherited her father’s estate, refused to assist. To think a child of hers could be so ungenerous at fourteen!

She wept, scorned, hinted, and raged—then withdrew, only to erupt again in passionate appeals to Heaven. It was a familiar scene, still seen today: women whose logic is drenched in tears, and justice in their hearts forever aflame.

This clash hardened her resolve. She began saving strictly for Harry, determined to provide him with a proper fortune. Work on the grand buildings Colonel Esmond had begun at Castlewood ceased. Ships from New York had brought Dutch bricks; mantelpieces, carved woodwork, glass, carpets, and fine furnishings had been shipped from England—all now abandoned. No more books were purchased. Wine shipments were halted.

Madam Esmond regretted deeply the cost of a fine carriage from England. On Sundays, she rode only to church, groaning inwardly as she looked at her sons beside her.

“Harry, Harry!” she whispered. “I wish I had saved that money for you, my poor, portionless child—three hundred and eighty guineas to Messieurs Hatchett!”

“You’ll give me plenty while you live,” Harry replied cheerfully. “And George will give me plenty when you’re gone.”

“Not unless he changes his heart,” she replied grimly, casting a glance at her elder son. “Not unless Heaven softens him and teaches him charity—of which I pray night and day. As Mountain knows, do you not, Mountain?”

Mrs. Mountain, widow of Ensign Mountain and Madam Esmond’s companion and housekeeper, sat beside her. She took the fourth seat in the family coach on Sundays.

“Humph,” Mrs. Mountain muttered. “I’ve seen you fretting over this legacy long enough. I don’t see why you need to.”

“Oh no! No need!” the widow cried, rustling in her silks. “Of course I have no need to worry—because my eldest son is disobedient and unkind—because he has an estate, and poor Harry, bless him, has nothing but a mess of pottage!”

George stared at her, eyes filling with tears. “I wish you’d bless me too, O my mother!” he sobbed, breaking down completely.

In an instant, Harry had his arms around his brother’s neck, kissing him again and again.

“Never mind, George,” he said softly. “I know whether you’re a good brother or not. Don’t listen to what she says. She doesn’t mean it.”

“I do mean it, child,” the mother snapped. “Would to Heaven—”

“HOLD YOUR TONGUE, I SAY!” Harry roared.

“It’s a shame to speak like that to him, ma'am,” said Mrs. Mountain, shaking his hand.

“And so it is, Harry,” she agreed. “You’ve never spoken truer words.”

“Mrs. Mountain, do you dare set my children against me?” the widow demanded.

“From this very day, madam—” Mrs. Mountain began.

“Turn me and my child into the street? Do,” she replied. “That will be a fine revenge for the English lawyer refusing your boy’s money. Find another companion who’ll tell you black is white and flatter you. That’s not my way, madam. When shall I go? I shan’t be long packing. I brought little into Castlewood, and I’ll take little out.”

“Hush! The bells are ringing for church, Mountain. Let us try, if you please, to compose ourselves,” said the widow. And she looked at her children with eyes full of affection—perhaps at one, perhaps at both.

George kept his head bowed. Harry, sitting near, slipped his arm around his brother’s shoulders during the sermon, holding him close.

Harry had been telling his story in his own lively way, punctuated by youthful exclamations, answering questions as they came. The elderly lady seemed never tired of listening. Her hostess and daughters came several times, inviting her to ride, walk, drink tea, or play cards—but she declined each time, saying she found endless pleasure in Harry’s talk.

Especially when members of the Castlewood family were present, she grew more attentive, insisting Harry speak close to her ear, and calling out, “Hush, my dears! I can’t hear our cousin speak.” The others would leave, trying to appear cheerful.

“Are you my cousin, too?” Harry asked honestly. “You seem kinder than my other cousins.”

They were in the wainscoted parlour, where the family had eaten meals for two centuries. Portraits of ancestors lined the walls. Above the old lady’s chair hung a Kneller painting—one of the finest in the gallery—depicting a young woman of twenty-four or twenty-five, dressed in the flowing garments of Queen Anne’s reign, her auburn hair parted across a fair forehead, falling over pearly shoulders and a graceful neck.

When Harry asked if she was his cousin, she replied, “That portrait is by Sir Godfrey, who thought himself the greatest painter in the world. But he was not as good as Lely, who painted your grandmother—my Lady Castlewood, Colonel Esmond’s wife. Nor as good as Van Dyck, who painted your great-grandfather—yonder. And he looks, Harry, like a much finer gentleman than he really was. Some of us are painted darker than we are. Did you recognise your grandmother in that picture? She had the most beautiful fair hair and figure of any woman of her time.”

“I thought I knew it instinctively,” Harry said. “A certain likeness to my mother.”

“Did Mrs. Warrington—excuse me, I believe she calls herself Madam or my Lady Esmond now—ever tell you about another daughter her mother had in England before marrying your grandfather?”

“She never mentioned one.”

“Nor your grandfather?”

“Never. But in his picture-books—he made them for us children—he often drew a face very like the one above you. That, and Viscount Francis, and King James III., he drew dozens of times.”

“And the picture above me—does it remind you of anyone, Harry?”

“No, indeed.”

“Ah! Here is a sermon!” the lady sighed. “Harry, that was my face once—yes, it was. And I was called Beatrix Esmond. And your mother is my half-sister, child. And she has never even mentioned my name!”
