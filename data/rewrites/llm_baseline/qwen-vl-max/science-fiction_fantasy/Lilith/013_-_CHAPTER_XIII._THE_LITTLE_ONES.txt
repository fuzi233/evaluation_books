I had barely begun my task when I heard faint voices nearby—soft, quick, like the rustle of leaves in a hidden breeze. Then they emerged: tiny figures slipping through the thickets of stunted trees that filled the spaces between the towering giants’ grove. In moments, dozens surrounded me, their eyes wide and bright, their movements swift and light as shadows.

I gestured urgently, signaling that the giants had just left and were still near. But they only laughed—a sound like wind chimes in spring—and assured me the air was clean. “They’re too blind to see us,” one chimed, and the whole group giggled in unison, a chorus of bells ringing across the forest floor.

“Do you like that rope around your ankles?” asked a small boy with curious eyes.

“I wear it so they think I can’t remove it,” I replied.

“They can’t even see their own feet!” he shot back, dancing on his toes with delight. “Just walk slowly and they’ll believe it’s still tied.”

One of the larger girls knelt beside me, her fingers delicate as spider silk, and began untangling the knot. I smiled, thinking no such hands could manage such a clumsy tie—but in seconds, it was undone.

They led me to sit, then offered me fruit—tiny, jewel-like things bursting with flavor. Afterward, the smallest among them swarmed around me, playing games that twisted and turned with wild abandon. I couldn’t work. I couldn’t think. They kept me laughing, spinning, chasing shadows until the sun dipped low behind the trees.

Then came the heavy tread of approaching footsteps. The Little Ones vanished instantly—like smoke drawn into the wind. Only one girl remained, pressing her hand to my arm. “We must be careful,” she whispered. “A single step from one of those monstrous feet could crush a little one.”

“Can’t they see you at all?” I asked.

“They might notice movement—if we were piled atop you like we were just now. But they hate anything alive but themselves… though honestly, they aren’t much alive either.” She whistled once—sharp and clear—and in an instant, not a trace of them remained.

It was my master—the giant who claimed ownership of me—coming to drag me home. He freed my ankles, pulled me to his hut, threw me to the ground, bound my feet again, kicked me hard, and walked away.

I could have fled. I should have. But I had found something precious—something rare in this strange world. Friends. Not just friends, but companions who sang, who played, who watched over me with silent devotion. Their presence made the loneliness bearable, the labor lighter. I couldn’t leave them—not yet.

That night, long after the huts fell quiet, I heard whispers—faint, persistent, circling close. I knew they were there, watching, guarding. From then on, they never let me sleep alone. Often, I woke to find myself cradled in a nest of children—one tucked against my chest, others curled around me like warm feathers. I didn’t shiver in the cold. I didn’t dream of escape. I dreamed of them.

Each morning, before dawn, they brought food to the giant’s door—fruit, nuts, honey-sweet berries—always in silence, always in haste. One morning, I awoke to find myself utterly alone. But then I heard soft steps, muffled laughter, and from the edge of the wood stepped Lona—the tallest, calmest, most solemn of them all. Behind her came the rest, joyous yet hushed, afraid to wake the sleeping giant.

In her arms, she carried a baby boy—his skin pale, his eyes closed, his breath soft as mist. Until now, the youngest had been a girl, perhaps a year old. Now there was a new life.

She placed the infant gently in my arms. His eyes fluttered open, looked at me, then shut again. He slept.

“He loves you already,” Lona said softly.

“Where did you find him?” I asked.

“In the wood,” she answered, smiling. “Where we always find them. Isn’t he beautiful? We’ve been searching all night. Sometimes it’s hard to locate them.”

“How do you know when one is there?”

“I don’t know,” she admitted. “Everyone tells each other at once—but we never know who started it. Sometimes I think someone said it in their sleep, and another heard it half-awake. When there’s a baby in the wood, no one stops to ask questions. And by the time we find it, it’s too late to wonder.”

“Are there more boys or girls among you?”

They didn’t come to the wood—they came *from* it. “We go to the wood and find them,” she corrected. “And yes—we have both. But we don’t count. We wouldn’t like to be counted.”

“Why not?”

“It wouldn’t be smooth. We’d rather not know.”

“And where do they come from first?”

“From the wood—always. There’s nowhere else.”

She knew nothing beyond that. The mystery ended there.

“How often do you find one?”

“Such a joy takes all our gladness,” she said. “We forget the last time. You’re glad to have this one, aren’t you, good giant?”

“Yes,” I said. “But how do you feed him?”

She went to fetch plums—small, round, glowing with color. She held one to the baby’s lips. “He would open his mouth if he were awake,” she said, then lifted him and squeezed the juice onto the surface. The baby sucked instinctively, even in sleep. She continued, slowly extracting every drop until only skin and stone remained.

“There!” she said, voice full of pride. “A world of big apples would be terrible for babies! We wouldn’t stay in it—would we, darling? We’d leave it to the bad giants!”

“But what if the stone gets in his mouth?”

“No mother would do that,” she said firmly. “I wouldn’t be fit to hold a baby.”

I thought then of what she might become—how lovely she would be as a woman. But what happened when they grew? Where did they go?

“Will you tell me where you lived before?” I asked.

“Here,” she said simply.

“Have you never lived anywhere else?”

“Never. We all came from the wood. Some say we dropped from the trees.”

“But why are so many of you still very small?”

“I don’t understand. Some are smaller, some bigger. I am very big.”

“The baby will grow bigger?”

“Of course.”

“And you?”

“I don’t think so. I hope not. I am the biggest. It frightens me sometimes.”

“Why?”

She didn’t answer.

“How old are you?”

“I don’t know what you mean. We are all just that.”

“How big will the baby grow?”

“I cannot tell. But some…” Her voice wavered. “…begin to grow after we think they’ve stopped. That is a frightful thing. We don’t talk about it.”

“What makes it frightful?”

She was silent. Then: “We fear they may be becoming giants.”

“Why would you fear that?”

“Because it is so terrible. I don’t want to talk about it.”

She pressed the baby to her chest, her face tight with worry. I didn’t press further.

Soon, I noticed changes in some of the younger ones—traces of greed, selfishness. The older girls watched them closely, their eyes shadowed with concern. None of them ever touched my tools, never helped the giants. But they cared for me endlessly.

They sang to me—each taking turns, one after another—for hours. They climbed trees to reach my mouth, popping fruit into it with fingers like rosebuds. They guarded me from the giants, always alert, always ready.

Sometimes, they gathered in circles and told stories—simple tales, often without meaning, yet full of rhythm and wonder. Once, a moody child sang a lullaby so sorrowful that tears streamed down my face—though I didn’t understand a word.

The others stared at me, puzzled. Then I realized: I had never seen water in this world—no rivers, no rain, no streams. It had existed long ago, perhaps, but the Little Ones had never known it—until now.

Yet they seemed to sense its origin. A tiny child rushed to the singer, shook his fist in his face, and cried: “Ou skeeze ze juice out of ze good giant’s seeberries! Bad giant!”

One day, I asked Lona: “Why do I never see any children among the giants?”

She blinked, as if trying to grasp the question. “They are giants. There are no little ones.”

“Have they never had children?”

“No. There are never any in the wood for them. They do not love children. If they saw ours, they would crush them.”

“So the number of giants stays the same?”

She burst into laughter—bright, sudden, then fading fast. “No, good giant. WE are THEIR firsters.”

Her joy died. She looked frightened.

“How can that be?” I asked.

“I don’t say. I don’t understand. But we were here. They were not. They go from us. I am sorry—but we cannot help it. THEY could have helped it.”

“How long have you been here?”

“Always, I think. I think somebody made us always.”

I returned to scraping wood. She saw I didn’t grasp it.

“The giants were not made always,” she continued. “If a Little One doesn’t care, he grows greedy, then lazy, then big, then stupid, then bad. The dull creatures don’t know they come from us. Very few believe we exist. They say nonsense!”

She pointed to a boy named Blunty, eating an apple nearly as large as his head.

“Look at him,” she whispered. “He will be the next! Oh, oh—he will soon be big and bad and ugly, and not know it!”

He stood apart, chewing with grim satisfaction. I had always thought him odd. Now he seemed repulsive.

“I’ll take that horrid thing from him!” I shouted.

“It’s no use,” she sighed. “We’ve done all we can. It’s too late. We feared he was growing—he wouldn’t listen. When he refused to share his berries, saying he gathered them himself—that was the sign. He’s a glutton. No hope for him. It sickens me to watch him eat.”

“Couldn’t someone stop him?”

“He may have them if he wants. It’s all the same—to eat the apples, or to be a boy who would eat them. No. He must go to the giants. He belongs to them. Look how much bigger he is than when you first came. Bigger even than yesterday.”

“He looks like that green lump in his hand,” I said.

“It suits what he’s making himself.”

“His head and that thing could swap places!”

“Perhaps they do.”

“Does he want to be a giant?”

“He hates the giants—but he’s becoming one anyway. He likes their apples.”

“Oh, baby,” she murmured. “He was just as sweet as you when we found him.”

“He’ll be miserable when he realizes he’s a giant.”

“Oh, no. He’ll like it well enough. That’s the worst part.”

“Will he hate the Little Ones?”

“He’ll be like the rest. He won unlikely to remember us—or believe we exist. He’ll eat his apples.”

“Tell me what happens next. I understand so little of your world. I come from a place where everything is different.”

“I don’t know about ‘world.’ What is it? Just a word in your big mouth? That makes it something.”

“Don’t mind the word. Tell me what will happen to Blunty.”

“He will wake one morning and find himself a giant—not like you, good giant, but like any other bad one. You won’t recognize him. But I will. He’ll think he’s always been a giant. He won’t know you, or any of us. Peony says the giants have lost themselves—that’s why they never smile. I wonder—are they glad because they’re bad, or bad because they’re not glad?”

“They can’t be glad,” I said, “without babies.”

“No,” she agreed. “And they can’t be happy when they have none. I wonder what ‘bad’ really means, good giant.”

“I wish I knew no more about it than you,” I said.

“But I try to be good,” I added. “I mean to keep trying.”

“So do I,” she said. “And that’s how I know you are good.”

We sat in silence.

Then I asked, one last time: “So you don’t know where the babies come from in the wood?”

“There’s nothing to know,” she said. “They’re in the wood. They grow there.”

“But why do you never find one before it’s fully grown?”

She frowned, thoughtful. “They’re not there until they’re finished.”

“A pity the little sillies can’t speak before they forget everything they need to tell,” I mused.

“Little Tolma, the last before this baby, looked like she had something to say when I found her under a beech tree, sucking her thumb. But she didn’t. She just looked up at me—so sweetly. SHE will never go bad and grow big.”

“When they begin to grow big,” she said, “they care for nothing but bigness. When they can’t grow taller, they try to grow fatter. The bad giants are proud of being fat.”

“So are the ones in my world,” I said. “Only they don’t call it fat. They call it rich.”

“In one of their houses,” she continued, “sits the biggest and fattest of all—so proud no one can see him. The giants go to his house at certain times, shout to him, praise how fat he is, beg him to make them strong so they can eat more and grow fat like him.”

Later, I heard news: Blunty was gone.

I saw a few somber faces among the older ones. But no mourning. No grief.

The next morning, Lona crept to me and whispered: “Look—by that quince-tree. That is the giant who was Blunty.”

“Never,” I said. “But now you tell me—I could almost think it was Blunty staring through fog. He does look stupid.”

“He is forever eating those apples now,” she said. “That’s what comes of Little Ones who WON’T stay little.”

“They call it growing up in my world,” I muttered.

“If only she would teach me to grow the other way—to become a Little One. Shall I ever laugh like them?”

I had had the chance. I had thrown it away.

Blunty and I were alike. He didn’t know he was losing himself. And I—had to be taught mine.
