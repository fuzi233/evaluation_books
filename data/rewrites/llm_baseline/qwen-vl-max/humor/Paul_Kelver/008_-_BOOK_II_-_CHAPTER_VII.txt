Better is little, than treasure and trouble therewith. Better a dinner of herbs where love is, than a stalled ox and hatred therewith. Only a truly great man—or perhaps a very tired one—would have the gall to say such an obvious thing out loud. But then, life has a way of making us all philosophers when we’re too broke to care about sounding profound.

Now, Sundays weren’t the only days we got a hot joint. Every day had one. And pudding? Pudding was practically a religion. We had it every single day until you started seeing the same flan in your dreams and waking up with a craving for something *different*—like despair. So much for joy-giving luxuries. What began as a treat became just another item on the list of things that didn’t matter anymore. No more worrying about tomorrow’s hash. No more measuring bread like it was gold dust so we could save some for Saturday’s pudding. We could eat now. Drink now. Live like kings. Except… we didn’t.

Because somewhere between the roast and the apple pie sat a silent guest—no one invited, no one noticed, but always there. A ghost made of silence, whose presence turned our laughter into awkward pauses and our teasing into polite questions about the weather. The old days, when we’d joke over half a pound of sausages or two fragrant herrings, were gone. Now we talked about nothing—except what we couldn’t talk about.

Moving to Guilford Street had been discussed—briefly, hopelessly. My parents would mention it in those flat, lifeless voices they’d adopted since everything stopped being fun. But sooner or later, someone would slip—say something careless—and the conversation would collapse like a poorly built house. Then the months would drag on, heavier than before, and the cloud above us grew thicker.

Then came the news: Old Teidelmann was dead. Not from old age. Not from illness. From suddenness. In his counting house. Like a bad check bounced without warning.

“You’re going to her?” my mother asked.

“I’ve been sent for,” said my father. “It might mean business.”

My mother laughed—a short, sharp sound that made me want to hide under the table. My father stormed out of the house like he’d just been told the world was ending. I stayed behind, listening. Later, I heard her crying in her room. I didn’t understand why she’d be upset over *him*. He was a grumpy old man who smelled of ink and regret.

But after that, Mrs. Teidelmann came around more often. She wore black, yes—but it suited her. Made her look even more mysterious, like a character in a book I hadn’t read yet. Her beauty wasn’t flashy. It was quiet, deep, like a well you don’t know how far down it goes. And she was kind—to everyone, especially my mother, whom she treated like a lost sister. To me? She was magic. Always showing up with books I actually wanted to read (not the ones my mother picked, which were full of moral lessons and zero adventure), or plans for treats I’d never dreamed of.

She knew what I liked better than I did. Maybe because she understood what I *didn’t* say.

One morning, she looked at me with a mischievous twist of her mouth. “Has he ever seen a pantomime?” she asked my father.

My heart nearly jumped out of my chest.

“What would your mother say?” my father replied, raising an eyebrow.

“She thinks theatres are wicked places,” I answered. And suddenly, for the first time in my life, I wondered if maybe… just maybe… she was wrong.

Mrs. Teidelmann smiled. “Dear me,” she said. “I suppose that makes me quite wicked. I’ve always thought pantomimes were perfectly moral. All the villains get their comeuppance—right away. And we can promise to leave before the Clown steals the sausages, can’t we, Paul?”

My mother came in then. She looked small beside Mrs. Teidelmann—pale, plain, stiff in her dark dress. Like a shadow next to sunlight.

“You’ll let him go, Mrs. Kelver,” Mrs. Teidelmann pleaded, soft as silk. “It’s Dick Whittington. Such a fine moral tale.”

My mother stood silently, twisting her hands. I’d seen her do this before—when she was scared, or sad, or trying not to cry. Her lips trembled.

“I’m very sorry,” she said. “It’s very kind of you. But I’d rather he didn’t go.”

“Just this once,” Mrs. Teidelmann insisted. “It’s holiday time.”

A beam of sunlight hit her face, turning her into gold. My mother seemed to shrink into the corner.

“I’d rather he didn’t go,” she repeated. Her voice cracked. “When he’s older, others may judge for him. But for now, I must guide him—alone.”

“I really don’t think there’s any harm,” my father said, trying to lighten the mood. “Things have changed since we were young.”

“That may be,” my mother said, cold as ice. “It’s been a long time.”

“I didn’t mean it that way,” my father said, laughing shortly.

“I know,” she said. “I just feel so old. So out of place. I’ve tried to change. But I can’t.”

“We won’t say anything more about it,” Mrs. Teidelmann said, sweetly. “I just thought it might bring him joy. And he’s worked hard this term, hasn’t he?”

She touched my shoulder—gently, like a secret. And left it there.

“It was very kind of you,” my mother said. “I’d do anything to give him pleasure. Anything. He knows that. He understands.”

Her hand reached for mine. But I was angry. I pulled away. Without another word, she left the room.

She never mentioned it again. But the next afternoon, she took me to a hall nearby. There was a magic lantern show—then a conjurer. She wore a nicer dress than usual, and she was brighter, happier. Laughing. Talking. But I was still mad. I didn’t care about the sleeping man who swallowed rats or the rabbit found inside a gentleman’s hat (which, honestly, was the most ridiculous thing I’d ever seen). All I could think about was the theatre—the real one—the one with the lights, the music, the *magic* I’d heard other boys talk about.

We walked home through muddy streets, saying little. That night, I woke—half-awake—and saw a figure in white crouching at the foot of my bed. I must have fallen back asleep. But later, I opened my eyes again—and it was still there.

I screamed.

My mother rose from her knees.

She laughed—a strange, broken laugh. “It was just a dream,” she said. “I must have been thinking about the conjurer. I dreamed a wicked magician stole you from me. I couldn’t find you. I was all alone.”

She hugged me so tightly I could barely breathe. And we stayed like that until I fell asleep again.

Later, during the holidays, my mother and I visited Mrs. Teidelmann at her house in Clapton. She’d written a note saying she was suffering terrible headaches and couldn’t come out. She was leaving England soon. Would my mother come say goodbye?

My father read the letter. “Of course you’ll go,” he said. “Poor girl. I wonder what’s wrong. She used to be so healthy.”

“Do you think it’s wise for me to go?” my mother asked. “What could she possibly want to say?”

“Oh, just to say farewell,” my father said. “It would seem odd not to.”

The house outside was dull and gloomy. But inside? It was like stepping into a museum that forgot to charge admission. Old Teidelmann had collected everything—pictures stacked like firewood, statues glowing in corners, carvings, embroideries, china, gems, illuminated manuscripts, books bound in leather and gold. The man himself must have been the only thing in the place that wasn’t interesting. And now he lay shriveled in a coffin, beneath a monument that made an otherwise forgotten cemetery famous.

We were shown upstairs to Mrs. Teidelmann’s boudoir. She was asleep on a sofa near the fire, dressed in a lace robe that slipped open slightly, revealing her thin arms and dark hair falling loose. In sleep, she looked less beautiful—harder, almost coarse. My mother said she’d wait. Maybe she’d wake.

The servant closed the door quietly. Left us alone.

An old French clock ticked softly—Cupid holding a heart, ticking like a lullaby. My mother sat by the window, staring at the sleeping woman. I watched them both. And then—I swear I saw it—her lips moved. A faint smile. For just a second. Then nothing.

Neither of us spoke. Just the ticking.

Then she began to murmur in her sleep.

At first, it was nonsense. Then—clear as glass—she whispered my father’s name.

“Ah no, we must not. Luke, my darling.”

My mother stood up quickly. But her voice was calm.

“Go, Paul,” she said. “Wait downstairs.” She pushed me gently out, shut the door behind me.

Half an hour passed. Then she came down. We left. On the walk home, she said nothing. Walked like a ghost. At the gate, she finally snapped back to life.

“You must say nothing, Paul,” she said. “Understand? When people are delirious, they say things that mean nothing. You must never speak a word. Never.”

I promised.

And from that day, everything changed.

My mother never raised her voice again. No anger. No flashes in her eyes. She wrote letters to Mrs. Teidelmann—long ones—always in the twilight, by the window. My father didn’t know. He’d sometimes catch her writing and ask why she chose such dim light.

“You’ll strain your eyes,” he’d say.

“I think better this way,” she’d reply.

When Mrs. Teidelmann returned, my mother was the first to visit her—even before my father knew she was back. They became friends. Almost like sisters. My mother praised her to everyone. Peace returned. My father was tender again. My mother was gentle, smiling, soft-spoken—just like before. Almost.

But every day, she grew weaker. Little things—habits, quirks—faded from her. Like she was slowly dissolving.

Summer came and went. Hot, stifling days. Nights heavy with city smells. The air crawled through the windows like a dirty breath. At night, you could hear the whole city breathing—deep, uneven, like a beast too tired to rest.

We took a holiday. Went to the coast. Climbed the steep path to old Jacob’s tower—the one he built in a fit of madness. Sat on the top platform, sheltered from the wind. Saw the distant mountains, ghostly in the haze. Ships appearing and vanishing. Farm fields below. The river, calm and sweet.

We stayed in a small village—smaller than I remembered. Sunnier. Calmer. Mrs. Fursey was still there—cheerful now, eager to show off me to the neighbors. “The material was unpromising,” she’d say. “But with patience—and a natural gift—you see what can be done!”

Anna was married now—buxom, affectionate, forever lunging to kiss me at the worst moments. I had to watch my back. Old Chumbley still cobbled shoes in his tiny cave. His bench was full of boots talking:

“Be quick, be quick—we want to toddle!” piped the little ones.

“Oh, please make haste—we’re waiting to dance!” sighed the dapper ones.

“As soon as you can,” said the stout ones. “We’ve work to do.”

“And just a patch,” muttered the old, flat-footed ones. “We haven’t much further to go.”

Old Joe still peddled his pack, telling the same jokes. Tom Pinfold still scratched his head, holding up the rejected fish. One might have thought it was the same fish.

Grown-ups hadn’t changed much. But children—oh, the children! The ones I’d known as babies now swaggered in knickerbockers. The ones I’d teased were now mincing like adults. It annoyed me. Why grow up so fast?

One afternoon—near the end of our stay—my mother and I climbed the tower again. She leaned on the parapet, staring at the mountains. A smile touched her lips.

“What are you thinking of?” I asked.

“Oh, just things that happened over there,” she said, nodding toward the hills. “When I was young.”

“You lived there, didn’t you?” I asked. (Boys don’t always think about tone.)

“You’re very rude,” she said. “It was only a little while ago.”

Then she lifted her head. Listened.

For twenty seconds, she stood like that—lips parted. Then, from the woods, came a long-drawn “Coo-ee!”

We ran to the edge. And there he came—my father—emerging from the pines, waving his stick, shouting.

His eyes sparkled. His step was springy. We knew instantly—he’d faced trouble. And he’d met it with courage. That old Norse blood, I suppose. Like a pirate rising when the storm hits.

He climbed the stairs. Breathed hard. Laughed.

He stood beside my mother. Neither spoke. Both stared at the distant hills—where, my mother had said, things had happened.

“Do you remember,” my father said, “the first time we came here? We walked up together. Talked about our future. How we’d fix things.”

“And the future,” my mother replied, “has a way of fixing its own plans.”

“It would seem so,” he said, chuckling. “I’m an unlucky beggar, Maggie. I dropped all your money—along with mine—down that cursed mine.”

“It was fate,” she said. “You couldn’t help it, Luke.”

“If only that damn pump hadn’t jammed,” he muttered.

“Do you remember Mrs. Tharand?” she asked.

“Yes. What of her?”

“A worldly woman. Called on me the morning we left. Said she’d been through more worries than you’d think. ‘And of all the troubles in this world,’ she said, ‘money troubles are the easiest to bear.’”

“I wish I could believe that,” my father said.

“She irritated me at the time,” my mother continued. “I thought it was just one of those comforting lies. But now I know she was right.”

They were silent.

Then my father grinned. “I’ve broken with Hasluck.”

“I knew it would happen sooner or later,” she said.

“Hasluck,” he said, suddenly fierce, “is no better than a thief. I told him so.”

“What did he say?”

“Laughed. Said that was better than some people.”

My father laughed too.

I don’t mean to dishonor Noel Hasluck. He was kind to me—then and later. And for Barbara’s sake, I’d prefer to write only good things about him. But if his thick-skinned, good-natured ghost were reading this now, I think it would nod. Because even though he was a friend, he was also—let’s be honest—one of the most unprincipled men in the City.

To him, business meant doing to others what you’d expect them to do to you—if given half a chance. And usually, he won.

“I play a game,” he’d say, “where cheating is the rule. Nine out of ten players are sharpers like me. The tenth is a fool who shouldn’t be there. We prey on each other. The cleverest wins.”

“But the widows and orphans?” I once asked.

“My dear lad,” he said, patting my shoulder, “I once got a letter from a widow. She’d invested in my Silver Company. Her savings were gone. Her son was her only support. You know the sort of thing. So I offered to buy her out at par. A week later, she wrote back—thanking me, but saying it didn’t matter. The shares had risen. She sold them—at a profit. To her brother-in-law. I found out later. You can find widows and orphans at Monte Carlo card tables, if you look. They’re no more deserving than anyone else. Besides,” he added, laughing, “I’m an orphan myself.”

And he laughed again—that deep, honest, hearty laugh that sounded like truth itself.

No one ever had a laugh like that.

So there we were—on the hill, under the sky, with the past and the future whispering between us. And though the shadow had passed, it left behind a silence that would never truly go away.
