Ten days after our harrowing ordeal brought us back to the familiar quarters at Loo. Miraculously, we emerged scarcely worse for the wear, save for the undeniable graying of my stubble—a testament to the cavern's atmosphere—and the profound, lingering shadow cast by Foulata's passing. Her loss struck Good with a ferocity that altered his spirit forever, yet I, viewing the matter through the lens of seasoned pragmatism, deemed her removal a necessary mercy. An alliance between a man of his nature and a girl of such stately, ethereal beauty and refined intellect would have been a disaster from the outset. As she herself poignantly asked, "Can the sun mate with the darkness?"

Our return to the subterranean labyrinth was swift and decisive. We abandoned any thought of penetrating Solomon's Treasure Chamber a second time. Our recovery required forty-eight hours, during which we attempted to locate the exit shaft in the great pit, only to find it obscured by heavy rains and a chaotic network of ant-bears' burrows that made navigation a fool's errand. Before departing, we revisited the Stalactite Cave and ventured once more beneath the spear of the White Death into the Chamber of the Dead. Standing before the immovable blockage—the massive rock sealing our fate alongside Gagool's remains—we felt a chilling mix of awe and despair. Despite our efforts, the secret mechanism remained elusive, buried under layers of mystery and time. It was a marvel of engineering, simple yet inscrutable, a relic of an age that had vanished centuries prior. Yet, faced with five feet of solid granite, we knew that nothing short of dynamite could breach it. With a heavy heart, we turned away from the unimaginable wealth trapped behind that wall, convinced that those diamonds and pearls would remain in eternal, silent company with Foulata's bones, never to grace the necks of living beauties.

Disappointment, however, was a fleeting emotion. By a stroke of providential foresight, I had stuffed the wide pockets of my shooting coat and trousers with gems before our imprisonment ended, along with Foulata's basket, which brimmed with an additional hoard. Though our tumble down the pit scattered several of the larger stones, an immense fortune remained. We were, if not millionaires by American standards, undeniably wealthy, possessing enough stones to outfit the finest jewelry collections in Europe for three families.

Upon reaching Loo, King Ignosi awaited us with open arms, his health restored and his authority firmly consolidated following his struggle with Twala. He listened with rapt attention to our tale, his expression growing somber when we recounted Gagool's gruesome demise. Summoning an ancient counselor, he inquired about the witch-doctoress's youth and appearance, receiving confirmation that she had always been a figure of ugliness and malice. "An ancient curse is lifted from the land," the elder declared, and Ignosi nodded, relieved that a woman capable of killing his father and usurping his throne was finally silenced.

As I prepared to depart, addressing Ignosi as a sovereign who had risen from rebellion to rule, I implored him to honor his promises: to govern justly, uphold the law, and refrain from arbitrary executions. "To-morrow, at break of day," I urged, "you will provide an escort to lead us across the mountains." Ignosi covered his face with his hands, his grief palpable. "My heart is sore," he confessed, his voice breaking. "What have I done to you... that ye should leave me desolate?" He offered gifts beyond measure—maidens, cattle, land, weapons—but I refused them all. "We seek our own place," I insisted.

His bitterness flared briefly. "Now I learn that ye love the bright stones more than me!" he cried, his eyes flashing. "Cursed be the white stones! Death shall it be to him who seeks them..." Yet, as I reminded him of his longing for his mother's native land, where he had played as a boy, his resolve softened. "In like manner, Ignosi, do our hearts turn to our land," I said. His tone shifted to one of wisdom and sorrow. He decreed that no other white man should ever attempt to cross the mountains or trade with his people; no guns, no gin, no "praying-men" to sow fear. But for Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, the path remained open. "Ye are dearer to me than aught that breathes," he proclaimed. In a gesture of supreme honor, he declared their names *hlonipa*—names forbidden to be spoken, reserved for the memory of dead kings, ensuring their legacy would endure forever.

"We must go," he whispered, "ere my eyes rain tears like a woman's."

Leaving Loo at dawn the next day, we were accompanied by Infadoos, Ignosi's uncle, and a regiment of Buffalos. The streets were lined with multitudes, showering us with flowers and blessings, celebrating our role in defeating Twala. Just outside the town, a whimsical scene unfolded: a young girl presented flowers to Good and requested a glimpse of his "beautiful white legs." After some initial hesitation, citing indecency, Good relented, drawing up his trousers to the knee amidst the adoration of the crowd. He walked thus until we cleared the town, a spectacle that would likely haunt his memory, though perhaps not diminish his reputation.

Infadoos guided us via a secondary pass north of Solomon's Great Road, leading us down the sheer cliff separating Kukuanaland from the desert, near the legendary "Sheba's Breasts." Here, Infadoos shared a poignant story of Kukuana hunters who, seeking ostriches, had stumbled upon a vast, fertile oasis miles away, saved from thirst by trees on the horizon. It was through this route that we intended to traverse the desert.

At the precipice, we bid a tearful farewell to Infadoos. "Never, my lords," he sobbed, "shall mine old eyes see the like of you again." As a token of our gratitude, Good presented him with an eyeglass—a spare pair—which Infadoos, delighted by the prospect of enhanced prestige, managed to screw onto his own eye, creating an absurd and comical image against his leopard-skin cloak and black ostrich plumes.

Descending the steep slope, we reached the plains below without injury. That night, by the firelight, Sir Henry remarked on the peculiarities of our recent adventures, noting that while Kukuanaland might hold horrors, the past month had been uniquely strange. Good sighed, expressing a wish to return, but I, reflecting on a lifetime of shaves, felt a quiet contentment. All was well that ended well, despite the cold chill the memory of battle still sent through me.

The following morning, we embarked on a toilsome trek across the desert, aided by five guides carrying ample water supplies. By the afternoon of the third day, the green canopy of trees belonging to the promised oasis came into view. Within an hour of sunset, we stepped once more onto soft grass, hearing the gentle murmur of running water, safe and refreshed after our perilous journey.
