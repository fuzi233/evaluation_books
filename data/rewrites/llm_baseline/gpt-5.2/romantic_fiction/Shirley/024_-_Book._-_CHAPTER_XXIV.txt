The future does not always announce itself with a crash. Sometimes it steals in as weather does: a pressure in the air, a wrongness in the light, a thread of sound on the wind that makes the heart listen before the mind understands. And sometimes it breaks through without warning, as if the earth had parted and shown, all at once, what it had been quietly preparing to hide.

Caroline Helstone came home from Hollow’s Cottage believing herself well. The walk had been mild, the lanes sweet, the hedges breathing hawthorn and early leaf; nothing in her limbs foretold disaster. Yet on waking the next morning, she felt a weight upon her, not pain—something duller, more ominous: a languor that sat on her chest and made her arms feel as if they belonged to another. At breakfast she tried to take what was offered; the bread, warm and brown, might have been ash. Butter had no richness; tea, usually her small comfort, ran tasteless over a tongue already parched.

“Am I ill?” she whispered, and went to the glass as if it could answer. Her eyes were bright to strangeness, pupils wide; her cheeks, too, carried a bloom that looked like health and was not. She stared at that false colour and wondered why her mouth refused even the thought of food.

In her temples the pulse beat quick, an impatient hammer. Her mind, instead of sinking, raced: splinters of thought, brilliant and broken, flew like sparks from a fire that would not settle. That unnatural liveliness fed the glow in her face, and hid, for the moment, what was kindling underneath.

Then came night—hot, dry, restless. Her thirst was not quenched by water; her skin burned, and yet she shivered if the sheet shifted. Towards morning a dream took her with the sudden cruelty of a beast; she woke as if from an assault, and knew—quietly, irrevocably—that she was ill.

How the fever had entered her she could not tell. Perhaps a soft, honeyed breath from some hollow meadow had been tainted, and she had drawn it in with the heedlessness of one whose lungs are pure and unsuspecting; perhaps the very sweetness of the season, the heavy perfume of dusk and dew, had carried its own poison. Or perhaps the body, long strained by inward conflict, offers a spark to anything that will burn. The mind’s excitement, the heart’s habitual ache—these might have been dry tinder; the air merely the match.

At first it seemed a gentle flame. Two hot days, two worried nights, but no violence. Mr. Helstone frowned at the fuss, Fanny watched dutifully, the doctor spoke of a chill and prescribed with easy confidence, and when Miss Keeldar came, she laughed away the notion of danger with a brightness that had never made room for death.

“A few days,” each said, “and she will be herself.”

The few days went, and the revival did not begin. A fortnight passed. Caroline did not grow fierce with illness; she simply diminished. She wasted as snow wastes in a thaw—quietly, without struggle; she faded as a flower fades when the root can no longer drink, though the sun still shines.

Mrs. Pryor, who had been coming daily, sat one morning at the bedside and watched Caroline longer than she had ever watched her before. She took the thin hand, placed a finger on the wrist, and sat as if listening not only to the beat beneath her touch, but to something else—some inward clock that was running down.

She left the room without remark and went to Mr. Helstone’s study. They were closeted together for half the morning. When she came back, she did not keep her bonnet on. She laid shawl and bonnet aside as if she had come home. She stood by the bed with her hands folded one over the other, and rocked gently—an habitual motion, like a lullaby she gave herself when she could not give it to another.

“I have sent Fanny to Fieldhead,” she said at last, “to fetch a few things for me—such as I shall want during a short stay here. It is my wish to remain with you till you are better. Your uncle kindly permits my attendance. Will it be acceptable to you, Caroline?”

Caroline tried to smile; her smile had become an effort, but she still offered it as a courtesy.

“I am sorry you should take such needless trouble. I do not feel very ill,” she said—still clinging to that delusion as if it were strength. “But I cannot refuse. It will be such comfort to know you are in the house—to see you sometimes. Only do not confine yourself on my account, dear Mrs. Pryor. Fanny nurses me very well.”

Mrs. Pryor bent and smoothed the hair under Caroline’s cap, lifted the pillow, set the sheet straight with hands that made no sound upon linen. Caroline, with a sudden tenderness, lifted her face to kiss her.

“Are you free from pain? Are you tolerably at ease?” Mrs. Pryor asked softly.

“I think I am almost happy.”

“You wish to drink? Your lips are parched.”

She held to Caroline’s mouth a glass of something cool—water faintly flavoured, as if with a slice of lemon or a bruised leaf of balm; it seemed to take the heat down one degree as it passed.

“Have you eaten anything to-day, Caroline?”

“I cannot eat.”

“But soon your appetite will return,” Mrs. Pryor said, and her voice trembled with a will that sounded like prayer. “It must return—that is, I pray God it may.”

As she laid Caroline back, she gathered her for an instant—an embrace that seemed to escape her before she could govern it: the impulse of a heart that could not bear distance.

“I shall hardly wish to get well, that I may keep you always,” Caroline murmured.

Mrs. Pryor did not smile. A tremor ran over her face, and she fought it down as one fights down sobs in a room where they would do harm.

“You are more used to Fanny than to me,” she said after a pause. “My attendance must seem strange—officious?”

“No. Quite natural. So soothing. You must have been accustomed to wait on sick people. You move so softly; you speak so quietly; you touch me so gently.”

“I am dexterous in nothing, my dear,” Mrs. Pryor returned. “You will often find me awkward, but never negligent.”

Negligent she was not. From that hour the sick-room became her kingdom. Fanny and Eliza were present, but as shadows. Mrs. Pryor lived beside the bed; she made the small services of nursing into a continual devotion. She learned, as if by instinct, what coolness the fevered skin could bear, what draught soothed without chilling, what morsel might be tempted to the lips—toast delicately browned and dipped in weak tea, a spoonful of broth cleared and strained, a little jelly that trembled like a promise. Yet Caroline could scarcely swallow. The body refused nourishment; the very smells that should have coaxed appetite—fresh bread, warm milk, the faint, clean scent of mint—seemed to pass her by as if she were already half removed from common life.

With all this care, it was strange she did not get well. Miss Keeldar, who had never been haunted by the idea of death, began at last to feel its shape in the room. Each visit showed a further sinking; alarm seized her at last, and she went with her imperious candour to Mr. Helstone.

“She is not merely poorly,” Shirley said. “She is going away.”

Mr. Helstone resisted, as men resist what they cannot command. But energy like Shirley’s, added to Mrs. Pryor’s quiet insistence, forced him to yield. When Mrs. Pryor demanded another physician, he answered stiffly that she might send for two if she pleased.

One came—an oracle. He looked, felt, listened, spoke in dark sentences which seemed to leave the burden of meaning upon time. He wrote prescriptions and directions, with an air of authority that would have crushed hope if hope had been in his power to crush. He took his fee and went. It was likely he knew there was little to be done, and did not choose to say so.

Still the neighbourhood did not yet breathe the rumour of danger. At Hollow’s Cottage it was believed to be only a severe cold; Caroline had written to Hortense to that effect, keeping the worst from every eye but the eyes that watched her closest. Hortense answered with practical kindness: two pots of currant jam—sharp, dark, homely preserve made to tempt a stubborn palate—also a recipe for a tisane, and a note of advice in her clear, brisk hand.

Mrs. Yorke, hearing that a physician had been called, sneered as if she tasted something sour.

“Hypochondria,” she said. “Rich and idle folk send for doctors when their little finger aches—having nothing else to think of.”

But the “rich and idle,” in the person of Caroline Helstone, were sinking into a prostration that would have sobered any scoffer who had seen her. All were puzzled but one; for one alone understood how quickly a structure long undermined can fall, not by storm but by its own silent decay.

Sick people have fancies which seem to nurses mere whim, yet come from some deeper law of the heart. Caroline had such a fancy. On a certain day in the week, at a certain hour, whether she was a shade better or worse, she would beg to be raised, dressed, and placed in her chair near the window. There she would sit, wrapped in her white dressing-gown, her hands thin as winter twigs, her gaze fixed on the world outside with a patience that was itself a kind of pain. No persuasion could make her lie down again until noon had passed. When the church clock tolled twelve, she would yield at once, meek as a child.

Once put back on the couch, she would bury her face deep in the pillow and draw the coverlets round her as if to shut out the day, the sun, the sound of life. More than once, after noon, a slight convulsion shook the bed, and a faint sob broke the hush. Mrs. Pryor noticed all.

One Tuesday morning Caroline sat as usual at the lattice. The room was quiet—quiet in the way a sick-room is quiet, where even footsteps are trained to whisper. Mrs. Pryor sat a little behind, knitting, or seeming to knit. The clicking of the needles was a soft, steady sound, like a small metronome set against the fever’s irregular beat—a domestic music meant to soothe. Outside, summer made its own acoustics: a low murmur of bees in the garden, a far-off cart-wheel on the road, the intermittent call of a bird that repeated its phrase as if it had forgotten what came next.

Caroline’s face changed. A light shot into her eyes; she half rose, leaning forward with sudden eagerness.

Mrs. Pryor moved noiselessly to look over her shoulder. From that window one could see the churchyard, and beyond it the road. A horseman went sharply by, not so far off that long sight could not know him. Mrs. Pryor knew Mr. Moore. Before the figure was lost behind an intercepting rise of ground, the church clock began to strike.

Twelve.

“May I lie down again?” Caroline asked, as if the question had been waiting only for the sound.

Mrs. Pryor helped her back to bed. She drew the curtain, and then stood a moment listening. The couch trembled; the suppressed sob stirred the air.

A contraction as of anguish altered Mrs. Pryor’s face. She wrung her hands once, quickly, as if she could twist away pain. And then she understood what she had not understood before. Tuesday was Whinbury market-day. Mr. Moore must pass the rectory on his way thither, just before noon—always, habitually, as fixed as the clock.

Caroline wore continually round her neck a slender braid of silk with some trinket attached. Mrs. Pryor had seen the bit of gold glimmer now and then, but Caroline never let it out of her keeping. When dressed it lay hidden in her bosom; when in bed she held it in her hand as if it were a charm, or a last thread tying her to earth.

That Tuesday afternoon the day’s fever brought her a doze—less sleep than stupor. The air was hot; even the shaded room held the scent of summer in its fabric, honeysuckle from the garden mingled with the faint, clean smell of linen and the sharper edge of some cooling draught. In restless turning Caroline pushed the coverlets aside. Mrs. Pryor bent to replace them, and saw the small hand lying nerveless on Caroline’s breast, clasping the jealously guarded treasure. The attenuated fingers had relaxed in sleep.

With a gentleness that was nearly reverence, Mrs. Pryor disengaged the braid and drew out a tiny locket, slight and cheap enough to suit Caroline’s small purse. Beneath its crystal face lay a curl of black hair—too short, too crisp to have been cut from a woman’s head.

The silken chain twitched. The sleeper started awake. Her mind, when she woke thus, often came back in fragments, like a bird returning to a familiar bough and not at once knowing it. Half rising, terror in her eyes, she cried out:

“Don’t take it from me, Robert! Don’t! It is my last comfort; let me keep it. I never tell any one whose hair it is; I never show it.”

Mrs. Pryor had already withdrawn behind the curtain. She sat far back in a deep chair by the bedside, hidden. Caroline looked round the chamber; it seemed empty. Her scattered thoughts returned slowly, one by one, exhausted as if each had flown a long distance to reach her. Finding silence, believing herself alone, she did what in health she had never done: she spoke aloud what had been kept locked in the breast.

“Oh, I should see him once more before all is over! Heaven might favour me thus far!” she cried. “God grant me a little comfort before I die!”

Then, the helpless bitterness of it:

“But he will not know I am ill till I am gone, and he will come when they have laid me out, and I am senseless, cold, and stiff.”

And because her mind—keen even in weakness—clutched at every hint the world gave, she turned from longing to that listening which illness sharpens: the ear seeking meaning in the air.

“What can my departed soul feel then?” she went on, breath quick and thin. “Can it see or know what happens to the clay? Can spirits, through any medium, communicate with living flesh? Can the dead at all revisit those they leave? Can they come in the elements? Will wind, water, fire, lend me a path to Moore?

“Is it for nothing the wind sounds almost articulately sometimes—sings as I have lately heard it sing at night—or passes the casement sobbing, as if for sorrow to come? Does nothing, then, haunt it, nothing inspire it?

“Why, it suggested to me words one night; it poured a strain which I could have written down, only I was appalled, and dared not rise to seek pencil and paper by the dim watch-light.”

Her voice fell; then rose again with that feverish curiosity that reaches for explanations like a hand in the dark.

“What is that electricity they speak of, whose changes make us well or ill, whose lack or excess blasts, whose even balance revives? What are all those influences that are about us in the atmosphere, that keep playing over our nerves like fingers on stringed instruments, and call forth now a sweet note, and now a wail—now an exultant swell, and anon the saddest cadence?

“Where is the other world? In what will another life consist? Why do I ask? Have I not cause to think that the hour is hasting but too fast when the veil must be rent for me? Do I not know the Grand Mystery is likely to burst prematurely on me?”

Her tone changed—grew childlike, imploring, as if she spoke to the earliest God she had known.

“Great Spirit, in whose goodness I confide—whom, as my Father, I have petitioned night and morning from early infancy—help the weak creation of Thy hands! Sustain me through the ordeal I dread and must undergo! Give me strength! Give me patience! Give me—oh, give me faith!”

She fell back on the pillow.

Mrs. Pryor found means to steal from the room without being seen. When she returned, she brought with her the composed face of a woman who will not let her own heart hinder the tending of another.

The next day, callers came; news had crept out that Miss Helstone was worse. Mr. Hall and his sister Margaret arrived; both left the sick-room in tears, having found Caroline more altered than they had been prepared to see. Hortense Moore came, brisk and warm in her foreign kindness. Caroline, stirred by her presence, put on a faint show of cheerfulness. She smiled, insisted she was not dangerously ill, spoke in a low voice that tried to carry its old tone. For the length of Hortense’s visit, excitement kept up the colour in her cheeks; she almost looked better.

When Hortense rose to go, Mrs. Pryor asked, keeping her voice steady with effort, “How is Mr. Robert?”

“He was very well when he left.”

“Left?” Mrs. Pryor’s control tightened like a cord. “Is he gone from home?”

Hortense explained: some police intelligence about the rioters whom he pursued had called him away that very morning to Birmingham; perhaps a fortnight might pass before he returned.

“He is not aware that Miss Helstone is very ill?”

“Oh no,” Hortense said. “He thought, like me, she had only a bad cold.”

After that, Mrs. Pryor did not approach Caroline’s couch for more than an hour. She heard her weep, and could not bear to look upon her tears.

As evening came on, she brought tea. Mr. Helstone had made it himself, with a stiff pride in his own skill—strong, clear, and hot, as if correctness could stand in for comfort. Caroline opened her eyes from a moment’s slumber and looked at Mrs. Pryor without recognition.

“I smelt the honeysuckles in the glen this summer morning,” she said, dreamily, “as I stood at the counting-house window.”

Such words, spoken from pallid lips, pierce deeper than any dramatic cry. They carry the brightness of remembered life into the present ruin.

“My darling, do you know me?” Mrs. Pryor asked.

“I went in to call Robert to breakfast. I have been with him in the garden. He asked me to go. A heavy dew has refreshed the flowers. The peaches are ripening.”

“My darling—my darling!” Mrs. Pryor said again, as if the repetition could hold Caroline to herself.

“I thought it was daylight—long after sunrise. It looks dark. Is the moon now set?”

The moon, newly risen, was gazing full and mild into the room—calm, remote, silvering what it touched with a light that made everything seem at once more peaceful and more sorrowful.

“Then it is not morning? I am not at the cottage? Who is this? I see a shape at my bedside.”

“It is myself—your friend—your nurse—your—” Mrs. Pryor checked the word, swallowed it back as if it were a flame. “Lean your head on my shoulder. Collect yourself.” Then, in a lower tone, as if speaking not to Caroline but to Heaven: “O God, take pity! Give her life, and me strength! Send me courage! Teach me words!”

Caroline lay mute a little while in the trembling arms and on the throbbing bosom that held her. The room listened with them: the hush broken only by a faint stir of leaves outside, and the far-off, ceaseless undertone of night insects—a small chorus that went on regardless of human agony, yet seemed, to an overwrought mind, like the world’s own whisper.

“I am better now,” Caroline said at last. “Much better. I feel where I am. This is Mrs. Pryor near me. I was dreaming. I talk when I wake up from dreams; people often do in illness. How fast your heart beats, ma’am! Do not be afraid.”

“It is not fear, child—only a little anxiety. I have brought you some tea, Cary. Your uncle made it himself. You know he says he can make a better cup of tea than any housewife can. Taste it. He is concerned to hear that you eat so little; he would be glad if you had a better appetite.”

“I am thirsty. Let me drink.”

She drank eagerly, as if thirst were the one appetite left to her.

“What o’clock is it, ma’am?”

“Past nine.”

“Not later? Oh! I have yet a long night before me. But the tea has made me strong. I will sit up.”

Mrs. Pryor raised her and arranged the pillows with practised care, making a nest of softness where bones threatened to bruise.

“Thank Heaven,” Caroline said, with a faint return of her old reflective manner, “I am not always equally miserable, and ill, and hopeless. The afternoon has been bad since Hortense went; perhaps the evening may be better. It is a fine night, I think? The moon shines clear.”

“Very fine—a perfect summer night. The old church-tower gleams white almost as silver.”

“And does the churchyard look peaceful?”

“Yes, and the garden also. Dew glistens on the foliage.”

“Can you see many long weeds and nettles amongst the graves? or do they look turfy and flowery?”

“I see closed daisy-heads gleaming like pearls on some mounds. Thomas has mown down the dock-leaves and rank grass, and cleared all away.”

“I always like that to be done,” Caroline murmured. “It soothes one’s mind to see the place in order. And, I dare say, within the church just now that moonlight shines as softly as in my room. It will fall through the east window full on the Helstone monument. When I close my eyes I seem to see poor papa’s epitaph in black letters on the white marble. There is plenty of room for other inscriptions underneath.”

Mrs. Pryor’s throat tightened, but she answered steadily, offering what comfort could be offered—news of small care, small continuity.

“William Farren came to look after your flowers this morning. He was afraid, now you cannot tend them yourself, they would be neglected. He has taken two of your favourite plants home to nurse for you.”

“If I were to make a will,” Caroline said, as if speaking of dishes and linen rather than of her own leaving, “I would leave William all my plants; Shirley my trinkets—except one, which must not be taken off my neck; and you, ma’am, my books.”

After a pause she added, with a sudden longing that was like hunger:

“Mrs. Pryor, I feel a wish for something.”

“For what, Caroline?”

“You know I always delight to hear you sing. Sing me a hymn just now. Sing that hymn which begins—

‘Our God, our help in ages past,
Our hope for years to come,
Our shelter from the stormy blast,
Our refuge, haven, home!’”

Mrs. Pryor sang at once.

Her voice, even in speech, was sweet and clear; in song it became something rarer—pure, unwavering, with a tenderness that seemed to have been distilled from long restraint. The melody filled the room and changed its very air. In the kitchen the servants, hearing it, stole to the stair-foot to listen; the sound, rising through the house, softened boards, walls, and beams, as if it passed through the very grain of the wood.

Even old Helstone, walking in the garden among his borders, stood still. The hymn floated out to him over the dew-wet leaves. He could not have said why it stirred him; why it brought, like a scent carried on the night, the image of a dead wife long buried under his pride. He did not like low spirits. When they came upon him he marched them off as he would have marched off a vagrant. He remembered he had promised to pay Wynne, the magistrate, a visit that evening, and he was glad of the pretext. He quickened his pace, trying to outrun the melody.

Mrs. Pryor sang on, verse after verse, and the words, ancient and English, bore with them a whole weight of generations—language held fast against time’s erasure:

“Thy word commands our flesh to dust,—
‘Return, ye sons of men;’
All nations rose from earth at first,
And turn to earth again…”

When the hymn was ended, Caroline asked for more—like one who has been given a mouthful of warm broth after days of starvation and wants, desperately, another.

“Now sing a song—a Scottish song,” she said. “‘Ye banks and braes o’ bonnie Doon.’”

Mrs. Pryor obeyed, or tried. At the end of the first stanza she stopped. The pathos of the air—its untranslatable sadness, the way its very vowels seemed to carry a country’s mourning—broke her. Her full heart overflowed.

“You are weeping at the pathos of the air,” Caroline said, with a pitying gentleness. “Come here, and I will comfort you.”

Mrs. Pryor came and sat on the edge of the bed. Caroline’s wasted arms encircled her, and she kissed her cheek as if she had become, suddenly, the elder, the protector.

“You often soothe me; let me soothe you,” Caroline murmured. “I hope it is not for me you weep?”

Mrs. Pryor did not answer.

“Do you think I shall not get better?” Caroline asked, and her voice was small. “I do not feel very ill—only weak.”

“But your mind, Caroline—your mind is crushed,” Mrs. Pryor broke out at last. “Your heart is almost broken; you have been so neglected, so repulsed—left so desolate.”

“I believe grief is, and always has been, my worst ailment,” Caroline said simply. “I sometimes think if an abundant gush of happiness came on me I could revive yet.”

“Do you wish to live?”

“I have no object in life.”

The words fell without complaint—only with the tired truth of one who has stopped bargaining.

“You love me, Caroline?” Mrs. Pryor asked, and now there was something in her tone that shook—something urgent, desperate.

“Very much—very truly—inexpressibly sometimes. Just now I feel as if I could almost grow to your heart.”

Mrs. Pryor laid Caroline down again with hands that trembled.

“I will return directly, dear,” she said.

She glided to the door, softly turned the key, and assured herself the lock was fast. Then she came back. She drew the curtain aside to let the moonlight fall more fully over the bed. It lay on Caroline’s face like a pale blessing—showing how sharp the cheekbones had become, how the lashes lay dark against skin too white, how the lips had lost their natural colour.

Mrs. Pryor gazed as if she would fix every feature in memory against the threat of loss. Then she spoke quickly, the words pushing through the barriers of habit and fear with a force that altered her voice.

“Then, if you love me,” she said, “if you feel as if, to use your own words, you could grow to my heart—it will be neither shock nor pain for you to know that that heart is the source whence yours was filled; that from my veins issued the tide which flows in yours; that you are mine—my daughter—my own child.”

Caroline lifted her head a little, bewildered, the world wavering.

“Mrs. Pryor—”

“My own child!”

“That is—that means—you have adopted me?”

“It means that, if I have given you nothing else, I at least gave you life; that I bore you, nursed you; that I am your true mother. No other woman can claim the title; it is mine.”

“But Mrs. James Helstone—my father’s wife, whom I do not remember ever to have seen—she is my mother?”

“She is your mother. James Helstone was my husband. I say you are mine. I have proved it.” Her breath came fast now, as if long-held words were escaping at last. “I thought perhaps you were all his, which would have been a cruel dispensation for me. I find it is not so. God permitted me to be the parent of my child’s mind. It belongs to me; it is my property—my right. These features are James’s own. He had a fine face when he was young, and not altered by error. Papa, my darling, gave you your blue eyes and soft brown hair; he gave you the oval of your face and the regularity of your lineaments—the outside he conferred; but the heart and the brain are mine. The germs are from me, and they are improved—they are developed to excellence. I esteem and approve my child as highly as I do most fondly love her.”

Caroline stared, as if the moonlight had turned to water and she were drowning in it.

“Is what I hear true?” she whispered. “Is it no dream?”
