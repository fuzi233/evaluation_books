More than three weeks passed before the final contents of the library were packed and removed. Instead of closing herself off to the world, Romola observed the entire process with a vigilance born of grief. Exhaustion following violent emotion often breeds a dreamlike denial of reality's causes; yet, as the workmen departed each evening, Romola lit her handlamp and wandered slowly through the chaos of straw and wooden crates. She paused at every empty pedestal, every familiar object laid low, driven by a bitter, desperate need to confirm that there was a sufficient reason for her love having vanished and the world becoming barren. For several nights in succession, she returned to this ritual. It was no longer merely to assuage herself, but because this vivification of pain and despair surrounding her father's memory remained the sole vital thread in her affections. By the twenty-third of December, she knew the last packages were departing. She rushed to the loggia at the summit of the house, unwilling to miss the final, crushing pang of watching the slow wheels traverse the bridge.

The sky was overcast, approaching dusk. The Arno flowed dark and trembling; the hills wore a mournful expression. Florence, encircled by its stone towers, possessed a silent, sepulchral aspect, cast by unbroken shadows upon the city viewed from above. Santa Croce, where her father lay interred, was shrouded in that same gloom. Slowly creeping across the bridge and ascending the narrow street, the white cargo moved like a cruel, deliberate fate, carrying her father's lifelong hope to be buried in an unmarked grave. Romola felt less as though she were witnessing this event herself than as if her father, helpless beneath the imprisoning stones, were conscious of it, unable to reach out her hand to tell him that he was not alone.

She stood motionless even after the load had vanished, indifferent to the biting cold, soothed by the gloom that seemed to cloak her like a mourning vestment, silencing the discord of joy. Suddenly, the great bell in the palace tower rang out a mighty peal—not the hammer-clash of alarm, but an agitated clamor of triumph. One by one, every other bell in every other tower caught the vibration and joined the chorus. As the swelling crescendo filled the air until it seemed composed of sound alone, little flames, vibrating as if ignited by the noise, burst forth between the turrets of the palace and along the girdling towers.

That sudden clanging, those leaping lights struck Romola like sharp wounds. They were the triumph of demons celebrating the success of her husband's treachery and the desolation of her life. Scarcely three weeks prior, she had been intoxicated by the ringing of those very bells; in the gladness of Florence, she had heard a prophecy of her own joy. Now, however, the general jubilation appeared cruel to her. She stood aloof from common life—from that Florence shouting its loud exultation to deafen the ears of sorrow and loneliness. She could never again join hands with gladness, save with those whom the hard nature of joy inevitably forgot. In her bitterness, she felt that all rejoicing was mockery. Men chanted paeans with souls heavy with melancholy, then scanned their neighbors' faces to verify whether joy truly existed. Romola had lost her faith in the happiness she once thirsted for; it was a hateful, smiling, soft-handed entity, possessing a narrow, selfish heart.

She fled down from the loggia, pressing her hands against her ears, and hurried across the antechamber when she was startled by an unexpected encounter with her husband, who came seeking her. His step was elastic, and a radiance of satisfaction, unusual in him, illuminated his countenance. "What! The noise was a trifle too much for you?" he asked, noticing how closely she held her hands to her ears. He took her gently by the wrist, drew her arm within his, and led her into the salon adorned with dancing nymphs and fauns. Continuing to speak, he declared, "Florence has gone quite mad with acquiring its Great Council, which promises to terminate all evils under the sun, particularly the vice of merriment. You may well appear stunned, my Romola, and you are chilled. You must not remain so late under that windy loggia without wrappings. I was coming to inform you that I am suddenly summoned to Rome regarding some learned business for Bernardo Rucellai. I depart immediately, joining my party at San Gaggio tonight, so we may start early in the morning. I need give you no trouble; my packages are already prepared. It will not be long before I return."

He knew he could expect nothing from her but quiet endurance. He dared not venture to kiss her brow that evening, contenting himself with pressing her hand to his lips before leaving. Tito sensed that Romola was a more unforgiving woman than he had imagined; her love was not that sweet, clinging instinct, stronger than all judgment, which constituted the great charm of a wife. Yet, this petrified coldness seemed preferable to a passionate, futile opposition. Her pride and her capacity to recognize where resistance was useless offered a certain convenience.

But when the door closed behind Tito, Romola shed the look of cold immobility that descended upon her like an inevitable frost whenever he approached. Internally, she was far from a state of quiet endurance. The days passing since the scene that divided her from Tito had been days of active planning and preparation for the fulfillment of a purpose.

The first task she undertook was summoning old Maso. "Maso," she said, her tone resolute, "we embark on our journey tomorrow morning. We shall now be able to overtake that first convoy of cloth while they await at San Piero. Attend to the two mules tonight, be ready to set off with them at dawn, and wait for me at Trespiano."

She intended to travel with Maso as far as Bologna, then send him back bearing letters to her godfather and Tito, informing them that she had departed and never intended to return. She had devised her flight so thoroughly that its secrecy would be absolute, her broken love and shattered life concealed from vulgar scrutiny. Bernardo del Nero had been absent at his villa, eager to evade political suspicions by returning to his favorite occupation of tending his lands, and she had settled his debt without a personal audience. He remained ignorant that the library had been sold, left to conjecture that some sudden stroke of fortune had enabled Tito to raise such a sum. Maso had been included in her confidence only to the extent that he knew her journey was a secret; doing precisely what she commanded was the paramount desire of his withered, wintry age.

Romola did not intend to sleep that night. Once the door was fastened, she took her taper to the carved and painted chest containing her wedding garments. The white silk and gold lay there, along with the long white veil and the pearl circlet. A great sob rose within her as she beheld them; they seemed the shroud of her dead happiness. In a tiny gold loop of the circlet, a sugar-plum had lodged—a pink hailstone from a shower of sweets. Tito had detected it first and remarked that it should forever remain there. At certain moments—and this was one of them—Romola was swept, by a sudden wave of memory, back into the era of perfect trust. She felt again the presence of the husband whose love rendered the world as fresh and wonderful to her as to a child sitting in stillness among sunny flowers; she heard the gentle tones, saw the soft eyes devoid of deceit, and breathed again that vast freedom of the soul that arises from faith in the being nearest to oneself, greater than oneself. In those fleeting instants, tears invariably rose: the woman's lovingness felt something akin to what a bereaved mother experiences when tiny fingers seem to lie warm upon her bosom, yet feel like marble to her lips as she leans over the silent bed.

However, something else lay in the chest beside the wedding clothes: it was something dark and coarse, rolled tightly in a bundle. Turning her eyes away from the white and gold to the dark bundle, her tears began to subside as her hands touched the serge. That coarse roughness anchored her fully in the present, from which love and delight had fled. She unfastened the thick white cord and spread the bundle upon the table. It was the grey serge dress of a sister belonging to the Third Order of Saint Francis, living in the world but devoted chiefly to deeds of piety—a figure the Florentines customarily termed a *Pinzochera*. Romola intended to don this dress as a disguise and resolved to wear it immediately, ensuring that if she required sleep before morning, she would awaken in perfect readiness to depart. She removed her black garment, and as she thrust her soft white arms into the harsh sleeves of the serge mantle and felt the hard girdle of rope cut into her fingers as she tied it, she welcomed those rude sensations. They accorded with her newfound scorn for that thing called pleasure, which rendered men base—that dexterous contrivance for selfish ease, that shrinking from endurance and strain when others bowed beneath burdens too heavy for them, a burden which now united her with her husband.
