To reconstruct the architecture of this confrontation, one must first understand the absolute tension of the air in that room at the precise moment I revealed myself. Rachel rose from the piano, a singular exertion of will that seemed to drain every other faculty from her body, merging her entire existence into the act of looking at me. A profound silence stretched between us, filled only by the full length of the room and the weight of unspoken history.

Fear seized me—a sudden dread that I had intruded too abruptly. Yet, compelled by a gravity I could not defy, I advanced. "Rachel!" I called softly.

The sound was a key turning in a rusted lock. Life rushed back into her limbs; color flooded her pale cheeks. She moved toward me, silent and fluid, as if driven by currents beyond her own volition. Warmth bloomed on her skin, and a light of reviving intelligence ignited in her eyes, brighter with every step. In that luminous proximity, the object of my journey vanished; the vile suspicion staining my reputation dissolved; every obligation of honor, past and future, evaporated. I saw nothing but the woman I loved drawing closer, trembling and resolute. Overwhelmed, I embraced her, burying my face in hers.

For a heartbeat, I believed the kiss was returned—that she, too, had forgotten the world outside this embrace. But before such an illusion could solidify, her first voluntary motion shattered it. With a cry that bordered on horror, fueled by a strength I doubted I could withstand, she shoved me away. Her eyes burned with merciless anger; her lips curled in contempt. She scanned me from head to toe, assessing me not as a lover, but as a stranger who had committed a grave insult.

"You coward!" she spat. "You mean, miserable, heartless coward!"

Those were her opening words. The most unbearable reproach a woman can hurl at a man was the one she chose to direct at me.

"I recall the time, Rachel," I replied, steadying my voice despite the storm within, "when you might have told me I had offended you in a far wiser manner. I beg your pardon."

Some of the bitterness I felt must have seeped into my tone. Immediately, her eyes, previously averted, turned back to me with reluctant intensity. She answered in a low register, adopting a sullen submission entirely foreign to my experience of her.

"Perhaps there is some excuse for me," she murmured. "After what you have done, is it a manly action to seek me out as you did today? It seems a cowardly experiment to test my weakness for you. A cowardly surprise to force me into letting you kiss me. But that is merely a woman's view. I should have known it wasn't yours. I would have done better controlling myself and saying nothing."

That apology was more crushing than the insult itself; even the most degraded soul would feel humiliated by it.

"If my honor was not in your hands," I declared, "I would leave you this instant and never see you again. You have spoken of my actions. What have I done?"

"What have you done! *You* ask *me* that question?"

"I ask it."

"I have kept your infamy a secret," she answered, her voice trembling. "And I have suffered the consequences of concealing it. Do I have no claim to be spared the indignity of you asking me what you have done? Is all sense of gratitude dead in you? You were once a gentleman. You were once dear to my mother, and dearest still to me..."

Her voice failed. She collapsed into a chair, turning her back on me, covering her face with her hands.

I waited, unsure whether I felt more acutely the sting of her contempt or the proud resolve that barred me from sharing in her distress.

"If you will not speak first," I said, "I must. I have come here with something serious to say. Will you grant me the common justice of listening?"

She neither moved nor answered. Making no further appeal, I held my ground, matching her obstinacy with my own. I recounted my discovery at the Shivering Sand and the trail that led to it. The narrative required time, yet from start to finish, she refused to turn her head or utter a syllable.

I maintained my composure. My entire future likely hinged on retaining possession of myself in this critical moment. It was time to test Mr. Bruff's theory. Driven by the breathless urgency of this experiment, I circled until I stood directly before her.

"I have a question to ask," I said. "It forces me to revisit a painful subject. Did Rosanna Spearman show you the nightgown? Yes, or No?"

She sprang to her feet, walking close to me of her own accord. Her eyes searched my face, scanning for truths they had never encountered before.

"Are you mad?" she demanded.

I restrained myself. "Rachel, will you answer my question?"

She ignored me, continuing her tirade. "Do you have some objective I cannot fathom? Some base fear regarding the future? They say your father's death has made you wealthy. Have you come here to compensate me for the loss of my Diamond? Do you possess the heart to feel ashamed of your errand? Is that the secret of your feigned innocence, your story about Rosanna Spearman? Is there a motive of shame beneath all this falsehood this time?"

I halted her. Control slipped from my grasp.

"You have done me an infamous wrong!" I exploded, heat rising in my throat. "You suspect me of stealing your Diamond. I have a right to know, and I *will* know, the reason why!"

"Suspect you!" she cried, her fury mirroring mine. "_You villain, I saw you take the Diamond with my own eyes!_"

The revelation that crashed over me in those words—the total collapse of the case Mr. Bruff had built—left me paralyzed. Though innocent, I stood before her in silence, appearing to her, and indeed to any observer, as a man crushed by the discovery of his own guilt.

She recoiled from the spectacle of my humiliation and her own triumph. The sudden silence that enveloped me seemed to terrify her. "I spared you then," she said. "I would spare you now, if you had not forced me to speak." She began to move toward the door, hesitating at the threshold. "Why did you come here to humiliate yourself? Why to humiliate me?" She stepped forward again, pausing desperately. "For God's sake, say something!" she exclaimed, passionately. "If you have any mercy left, do not let me degrade myself in this way! Speak—and drive me out of the room!"

I advanced toward her, barely conscious of my movements. Perhaps I harbored a confused desire to detain her until she confessed more. Since learning that the evidence condemning me in Rachel's mind was the testimony of her own eyes, nothing—not even my certainty of my innocence—felt clear. I took her hand; I attempted to speak with firmness and purpose. All that emerged was, "Rachel, you once loved me."

She shuddered, averting her gaze. Her hand lay powerless and trembling in mine. "Let go of it," she whispered faintly.

Yet, my touch produced the same effect on her as my voice had when I first entered: a paralysis of resistance. After she had branded me a coward, after she had accused me of theft, while her hand lay in mine, I remained her master.

I drew her gently back to the center of the room and seated her beside me. "Rachel," I said, "I cannot explain the contradiction in what I am about to tell you. I can only speak the truth as you have spoken it. You saw me—with your own eyes, you saw me take the Diamond. Before God, who hears us, I declare that I now know I took it for the first time! Do you doubt me still?"

She paid me no heed. "Let go of my hand," she repeated weakly. That was her sole response. Her head sank onto my shoulder; her hand unconsciously closed around mine at the very moment she begged me to release it.

I refrained from pressing further. But my forbearance ended there. My chance of ever raising my head among honest men depended on inducing her to complete her disclosure. My only remaining hope was that she had overlooked a detail in the chain of evidence—a triviality, perhaps, that could become the instrument of my vindication under careful scrutiny. I held fast to her hand, speaking with all the sympathy and confidence of our bygone times I could summon.

"I wish to ask you something," I said. "I want you to recount everything that occurred, from the moment we wished each other goodnight until the time you saw me take the Diamond."

She lifted her head from my shoulder, making an effort to withdraw her hand. "Oh, why go back to it!" she cried. "Why go back to it!"

"I will tell you why, Rachel. We are both victims of some monstrous delusion wearing the mask of truth. If we examine what happened on the night of your birthday together, we may yet reach mutual understanding."

Her head dropped back onto my shoulder. Tears gathered in her eyes, spilling slowly down her cheeks. "Oh!" she sobbed. "Have *I* never had that hope? Have *I* not tried to see it, as you are trying now?"

"You have tried alone," I answered. "You have not tried with me to help you."
