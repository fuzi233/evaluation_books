The bells of Cloisterham tolled for morning service, marking the passage of time with a rhythm that seemed to ignore the distance Neville Landless had covered. He was eight miles away, his stomach a hollow drumbeat demanding sustenance. He had begun his journey on a crust of bread, a meager fuel for a body moving with urgent purpose. He halted at the roadside tavern, the sign of The Tilted Wagon, seeking the alchemy of tea, toast, and bacon.

Inside, the atmosphere was thick with the humidity of neglect. The wagon itself, perched atop a hill, offered a view of a landscape marred by damp hoofprints and trodden straw. A scolding landlady managed a baby in the bar, one sock red, the other missing—a visual testament to the establishment's chaotic economy. On shelves, cheese lay abandoned beside a mouldy cloth and a green-handled knife resting in a cast-iron canoe. Bread, pale and weeping crumbs, suffered a shipwreck in another vessel nearby. Linen hung in a limbo of half-washed, half-dried states, leading a public life of exposure. Everything here was designed to rhyme with 'mug,' yet it failed to provide the promised entertainment for man or beast. Neville, however, was not critical. He accepted the banality, drank from the available vessels, and prepared to continue his march.

Leaving the tavern, he paused at a quarter-mile mark, weighing the direct road against a cart track cutting through a breezy heath. The latter path wound between high hedgerows, promising a shortcut across a slope, though the ascent was steep and rutted. He chose the toil.

As he labored upward, the silence of the heath was breached. Pedestrians emerged behind him, moving with a pace that betrayed intent. Four passed him quickly; four slowed, trailing like shadows; and six turned back, retreating at a frantic rate. Neville watched the four approaching, then the four ahead. They locked gazes. He resumed walking, but the formation held firm. When they spilled onto the open slope, maintaining their ranks regardless of his lateral movements, the illusion of solitude shattered. He stopped, testing the perimeter. They stopped with him.

"Why do you attend upon me?" Neville asked the assembly. "Are you a pack of thieves?"

"Don't answer him," one voice commanded, though the speaker remained unseen. "Better be quiet."

"Better be quiet?" Neville repeated, anger rising like bile. "Who said so?"

Silence answered.

"It is good advice, whoever this skulking voice belongs to," Neville snapped. "I will not submit to being penned between four men yonder and four men here. I intend to pass those four in front."

No movement. No retreat.

"If eight men, or four, or two, set upon one," Neville declared, his voice cracking with rage, "the victim has no choice but to strike. By the Lord, I will do it if I am interrupted further!"

Shouldering his heavy stick, he surged forward to bypass the lead group. The largest figure shifted instantly, intercepting him with dexterity. The stick fell smartly, meeting resistance.

"Let him be!" the man whispered, suppressing the violence in his tone. "Fair play! His frame is slight compared to mine, and he carries a weight strapped to his back. Leave him. I will handle him."

They rolled together on the grass, a close combat that smeared blood across their faces. Eventually, the larger man disengaged, kneeling from Neville's chest. "There! Now take him arm-in-arm, any two of you!"

Compliance was immediate.

"As to our reputation as thieves, Mr. Landless," the speaker said, wiping blood from his lip, "you know better than that at midday. We would not have touched you had you not forced us. We are escorting you to the high road, where help awaits if you seek it. Someone, wipe his face; look how it trickles down him!"

Once cleansed, Neville recognized the speaker: Joe, the driver of the Cloisterham omnibus, a man he had glimpsed only once upon his arrival.

"My recommendation for the present is silence, Mr. Landless," Joe instructed. "You will find a friend awaiting you at the high road—he went ahead via the alternate route when we divided. Say nothing until you reach him. Bring the stick along, someone else, and let us move!"

Bewildered, Neville offered no words. Flanked by his conductors, whose arms gripped his own, he walked on in a dreamlike trance until they rejoined the high road and a gathering crowd. Among them were the men who had retreated earlier, and at the center stood Mr. Jasper and Mr. Crisparkle. The escorts delivered Neville to the Minor Canon, releasing him as a gesture of deference.

"What is this? What is happening? I feel as though I have lost my senses!" Neville cried, surrounded.

"Where is my nephew?" Jasper demanded, his voice wild.

"Where is your nephew?" Neville echoed, confusion clouding his features. "Why do you ask me?"

"I ask you," Jasper retorted sharply, "because you were the last person in his company, and he is nowhere to be found."

"Not to be found!" Neville gasped, aghast.

"Stay, stay," Crisparkle interjected gently. "Permit me, Jasper. Mr. Neville, you are confounded. Collect your thoughts. It is vital you do so. Attend to me."

"I will try, sir, but I seem mad."

"You left Mr. Jasper last night with Edwin Drood?"

"Yes."

"At what hour?"

"Was it twelve o'clock?" Neville asked, hand pressed to his throbbing temple, turning to Jasper.

"Quite right," Crisparkle confirmed. "The hour Mr. Jasper has already stated. You went down to the river together?"

"Undoubtedly. To observe the wind's action there."

"What followed? How long did you remain?"

"About ten minutes, perhaps less. We then walked to your house, and he took his leave at the door."

"Did he mention returning to the river?"

"No. He said he was going straight back."

The bystanders exchanged glances, their eyes settling on Crisparkle. Jasper, who had been watching Neville with intense scrutiny, lowered his voice. "What are those stains upon his dress?"

All eyes turned to the blood soaking Neville's clothes.

"And here are the same stains upon this stick!" Jasper exclaimed, seizing the weapon from the man holding it. "I know this stick to be his; he carried it last night. What does this mean?"

"In the name of God, explain!" Crisparkle urged.

"That man and I," Neville pointed to his former adversary, "struggled for the stick moments ago. You can see the matching marks on him. What was I to suppose, when molested by eight people, when they offered no explanation?"

They admitted their discretion in silence and confirmed the struggle. Yet, the very witnesses regarded the drying smears on Neville's coat with dark, unreadable expressions.

"We must return, Neville," Crisparkle said. "You will be glad to come back to clear yourself?"

"Of course, sir."

"Mr. Landless will walk at my side," the Minor Canon announced, surveying the group. "Come, Neville!"

They set off, the others straggling behind at varying distances. Jasper walked on Neville's opposite side, never breaking contact. He remained silent while Crisparkle repeated his inquiries and Neville reiterated his answers, despite the Minor Canon's attempts to provoke explanatory conjecture. Jasper's fixed demeanor refused engagement. Near the city, when Crisparkle suggested visiting the Mayor immediately, Neville nodded sternly but spoke not a word until they entered Mr. Sapsea's parlour.

Upon learning the circumstances necessitated a voluntary statement before the Mayor, Jasper broke his silence. He placed his entire reliance on Mr. Sapsea's penetration. Unless the Mayor could suggest a reason for his nephew's sudden flight, Jasper deferred judgment. Unless drowning in the dark appeared likely, he deferred. He washed his hands of all suspicion unless the Mayor deemed it inseparable from Neville, his last companion and current antagonist. His own mind, distracted by dismal apprehension, was unsafe; Mr. Sapsea's was not.

Mr. Sapsea opined the case possessed a dark, distinctly Un-English complexion. After wandering into a dense haze of nonsense unsuitable for a mayor, he arrived at the brilliant conclusion that killing a fellow creature meant destroying something that did not belong to you. He hovered on the brink of issuing a warrant for Neville's committal under grave suspicion but was restrained by Crisparkle's indignant protest, who guaranteed Neville's presence in his own home and availability for production. Jasper interpreted this as a directive to scour the river, examine its banks rigorously, dispatch particulars to outlying regions and London, and circulate placards imploring Edwin Drood—if he had withdrawn from his uncle's society—to reveal himself to alleviate his grieving kinsman's sorrow. These measures were enacted immediately.

It was difficult to discern who bore greater horror: Neville Landless or John Jasper. Yet, Jasper's position compelled activity, while Neville's enforced passivity; the difference was negligible. Both were bowed down, broken by the weight of the mystery.
