The thought of the Thornton dinner party had seized Mrs. Hale’s fancy with the wide-eyed curiosity of a small child, begging to hear every detail of the joy it has already begun to anticipate. For invalids confined to a narrow, unchanging routine soon come to resemble children in this: they lose all sense of proportion between events, and the curtains and four walls that make up their whole world feel far larger and more important than anything that lies hidden outside. Mrs. Hale had carried quiet girlhood vanities, too, long smothered and set aside after she married a poor country clergyman and endured the mortification of shrinking from the social standing she had once known—but they had never died, and now she delighted to dwell on the thought of Margaret dressed for a grand occasion, fretting over what she should wear with a tender, flustered anxiety that made Margaret smile. She had spent a full year in London’s Harley Street immersed in social life, far more practiced in such trivialities than her mother had been in five and twenty quiet years at Helstone.

“So you are set on your white silk? Are you certain it still fits? It’s nearly a year since Edith’s wedding, after all.”
“Oh yes, mamma! Mrs. Murray stitched it, and her work never fails. It might sit a hair’s breadth shorter or longer at the waist, depending on if I’ve gained or lost a little weight, but I’ve barely changed at all.”
“Shouldn’t you let Dixon check it over? It might have turned yellow tucked away in the press all this time.”
“I will, if you’d like. But even if the worst comes to pass, I’ve that lovely pink gauze Aunt Shaw gave me just months before Edith married—it can’t have yellowed.”
“No, but it might have faded.”
“Then there’s my green silk, too. It’s the very opposite of not having anything to wear, I’ve more than enough options.”
“I do wish I knew what would suit you best,” Mrs. Hale fretted, twisting her fingers in the edge of her shawl.
Margaret’s face softened at once. “Shall I go and put them on one after another, and you can pick the one you like most?”
Her mother nodded, and off Margaret ran. She was half tempted to play the silly pranks she’d loved as a girl: puff the full white silk skirt out like a balloon, retreat backwards across the room as if she were a queen making a royal procession, but she quickly saw these light tricks were interrupting her mother’s earnest pleasure, and grew grave and still, posing patiently for her inspection. She could not for the life of her understand why everyone around her fussed so over a simple gown—but that very afternoon, when she mentioned the dinner engagement to Bessy Higgins (speaking of the new housemaid Mrs. Thornton had promised to help them hire), Bessy sat bolt upright, her pale cheeks flushing with surprise.
“Dear heart, you’re going to dine with the Thorntons over at Marlborough Mills?”
“Yes, Bessy—why are you so startled?”
“Oh, I don’t rightly know. But they keep company with all the very first folk in Milton, don’t they?”
“And you don’t think we quite rank among Milton’s first folk, eh?” Margaret laughed gently.
Bessy’s face burned pink at being caught out. “Well, you see, this town values money above all else, and I reckon you haven’t got much of that.”
“That’s very true,” Margaret agreed. “But we are educated, and have always lived among educated people. Is it so strange that a man who came to my father for tutoring should invite us to dinner? I don’t blame Mr. Thornton, mind—very few former draper’s assistants could ever build themselves a life as fine as his.”
“But can you give them a dinner back, in that little house of yours? The Thorntons’ home is three times the size of yours.”
“I think we could manage to return the favour, never fear. It wouldn’t be in so grand a room, or with so many guests, but we’ve never measured company by the size of our dinner table here.”
“I never thought I’d live to see you dine with the Thorntons,” Bessy repeated, wonder in her voice. “Why, the mayor himself dines there, and Members of Parliament, and all the most important people in town.”
“I think I can rise to the honour of meeting Milton’s mayor,” Margaret said, still smiling.
“But those ladies there dress so grand!” Bessy fretted, glancing down at Margaret’s simple printed cotton gown, which her Milton eyes priced at no more than sevenpence a yard.
Margaret laughed, her dimples deepening. “Thank you, Bessy, for caring so much that I look nice among all the fine folks. But I have more than enough pretty gowns—only a week ago I would have said they were far too fancy for anything I’d ever need in this dull smoky town, but since I’m dining at Mr. Thornton’s and meeting the mayor, you can be sure I’ll wear my very best.”
“What will you wear?” Bessy asked, already a little relieved.
“White silk,” Margaret told her. “The gown I wore for a cousin’s wedding a year ago.”
“That’ll do,” Bessy said, sinking back against her cushions. “I’d hate to see you looked down on.”
“Oh, I’ll be fine enough to avoid that, never fear.”
“I wish I could see you all dressed up,” Bessy said softly. “I don’t reckon anyone would call you pretty, exactly—you haven’t the bright pink and white cheeks folks favour here. But do you know, I dreamt of you long, long before I ever laid eyes on you.”
“Nonsense, Bessy!”
“It’s true! Your very face, your clear steady eyes looking out of the dark, your hair blown back from your smooth straight forehead like rays of light—and you always came to give me strength, strength I drank right out of your kind, comforting eyes. And you were dressed all in shining white, just the gown you’re going to wear to dinner. So you see, it was you, all along.”
“Nay, Bessy,” Margaret said gently, “it was only a dream.”
“And why shouldn’t I dream a dream in my sickness, same as anyone else? Didn’t the good people in the Bible dream dreams and see visions? Even my father sets great store by dreams. I tell you I saw you as plain as day, coming swift towards me, your hair blown back by how fast you moved, just like the way it sticks up a little from your forehead now, all in that shining white dress. Let me come and see you in it. I want to see you, and touch you, so I know for certain the girl from my dream is really here.”
“Of course you can come, you are more than welcome. But don’t speak so—you make me feel quite unworthy.”
“Then I’ll hold my tongue, even if I bite it in two. It’s all true, anyway.”
Margaret fell silent. “We can talk of it whenever you like,” she said at last. “But not now. Tell me, has your father come home yet?”
“Aye,” Bessy said, her tone dropping suddenly heavy, far removed from the soft wonder of a minute before. “Him and hundreds of others—all of Hamper’s men, and many more besides. The women are as wild and bitter as the men this time. Food has got so dear, and they need to feed their bairns. If the Thorntons spent just the money they lay out on that fancy dinner on potatoes and meal, they could quiet a dozen crying babies, and ease a dozen mothers’ broken hearts for a little while.”
“Don’t speak so,” Margaret said, flushing. “You’ll make me feel wicked and guilty for going at all.”
“No,” Bessy said. “Some are pre-elected to sumptuous feasts, and purple and fine linen—maybe you’re one of them. Others toil and moil all their lives long, and not even the dogs pity them these days, like they pitied Lazarus in the old story. But if you ever needed me to cross that great gulf between us to cool your tongue with the tip of my finger, I’d come, for all the kindness you’ve shown me here.”
“Bessy, you’re feverish— I can feel it in your hand, and hear it in your words. On that final day, we won’t be judged by the accident of who was rich and who was poor. We’ll be judged by how faithfully we followed Christ.”
Margaret fetched cool water, soaked her handkerchief in it, and laid it on Bessy’s forehead, then chafed her cold, stiff feet until Bessy’s eyes fluttered shut, soothed.
“You’d be worn quite daft, same as me, if all hours of the day folks tramped in asking for father, and sat down to pour out all their troubles to me,” Bessy murmured. “Some spoke of deadly hatred, and told me such terrible things about the masters they made my blood run cold—but most of them, the women, just went on complaining, tears streaming down their cheeks unheeded, about the price of bread, and how their bairns wouldn’t sleep at night for hunger.”
“Do they truly think the strike will fix all this?” Margaret asked.
“They say so,” Bessy said. “Trade’s been good for years, and the masters have made heaps of money. Father doesn’t know how much, but the Union does. It’s only natural they want their fair share of the profits now that food’s so dear, and the Union says it’s doing its duty pushing the masters to give it. But the masters have got the upper hand somehow, and I’m afraid they’ll keep it forever. It’s like the great battle of Armageddon, the way they grin and fight each other, and even while they’re swinging their fists, they’re falling one by one into the pit.”
Just then Nicholas Higgins tramped in, and caught his daughter’s last words.
“Aye! And I’ll fight on too, and we’ll win this time. It won’t take long for them to cave. They’ve got a pile of orders all under contract, and they’ll soon learn it’s cheaper to give us our five percent raise than to lose all their profits, never mind the fines they’ll pay for breaking their contracts. Aha, my fine masters! I know who’s going to win.”
Margaret thought he had been drinking, not so much from his words as from the wild, over-excited edge to his voice, and Bessy’s obvious hurry to see her out the door confirmed the suspicion.
“The twenty-first—that’s Thursday week. I’ll come see you dressed for the Thorntons’ dinner, right? What time does it start?”
Before Margaret could answer, Higgins cut in.
“Thornton’s! You’re going to dine at Thornton’s? Ask him to drink a toast to the success of his orders. By the twenty-first, he’ll be scratching his head trying to figure out how he’s going to get them all filled on time. Tell him seven hundred of us will march into Marlborough Mills the morning after he gives us the five percent, and get all that work done for him in no time. You’ll meet all the top mill-owners at his dinner, no doubt. I’d love to corner them after the meal, when they’re full of food and slow to move, and tell them exactly what I think of the hard way they’re grinding us down.”
“Goodbye, Bessy!” Margaret said, hurrying for the door. “I’ll look for you on the twenty-first, if you’re well enough.”
Dr. Donaldson’s new treatment worked wonders on Mrs. Hale at first, so much so that Margaret, and even Mrs. Hale herself, began to dare hope the doctor had been wrong, and she might make a full permanent recovery. Mr. Hale, who had never fully grasped how grave her condition was, openly rejoiced at the change, his relief a clear sign of how deeply he had been frightened by the threat of losing her. Only Dixon grumbled endless dark warnings into Margaret’s ear, but Margaret defied the old raven, and clung tight to hope.
They badly needed that small glimmer of joy inside their home, for outside, even their untrained, country eyes could see the dark, brooding cloud of discontent hanging over all of Milton. Mr. Hale had made friends among the working men of the town, and came home heavy-hearted with their earnest, terrible tales of suffering and endless endurance. He brought all their stories and grievances to Mr. Thornton, to ask the master mill-owner to explain their root causes, and Thornton always did so clearly, laying out his cold, unflinching economic principles: that trade naturally rises and falls, and in the lean times, a certain number of masters and men alike will be crushed into ruin, wiped out of the ranks of the prosperous. He spoke as if this consequence was so perfectly logical no one had any right to complain if it befell them: the ruined master forced to drop out of the race, bitter at his own failure, trampled down by his rivals, begging for work he once handed out to others with a lordly air. Of course, if he could speak so coldly of the ruin that might one day be his own, he had no sympathy at all for the fate of the workmen, cast aside by the swift, merciless march of industrial progress—men who would gladly lie down and die if not for the clinging cries of the helpless families they would leave behind, who envied the wild birds that could feed their own young with their very heart’s blood.
Margaret’s whole soul rose up in rebellion against him as he spoke, as if commerce was everything and humanity nothing. She could barely thank him that very evening when he arrived at their house privately, with a quiet, delicate kindness that made him avoid drawing attention to the favour, to offer every medical comfort and supply his own well-staffed household held that Dr. Donaldson had warned him Mrs. Hale might soon need. The contrast was jarring: his generosity, his obvious pity written in his soft eyes, his tremulous gentle voice, all tangled up with that cold, hard, merciless logic of trade he had spoken of earlier, the very logic that threatened the future she was trying so desperately to pretend would not take her mother away. She knew he had guessed the dark, sacred secret she locked away in the deepest corner of her heart, that one day soon she would call for her mother and get no answer but silence from the empty room. She could not reconcile that hard, unfeeling reasoning man with this kind, pitying visitor, and the discord grated on her more than words could say.
Nicholas Higgins was appointed a strike committee man that same week, and he boasted he knew secrets no ordinary worker did. He was arguing the point with his neighbour John Boucher, whom Margaret had heard of often: a clumsy unskilled worker with a huge family to support, who alternately stirred Higgins’ pity, and roused his rage for lacking what Higgins called “spirit”. When Margaret walked in, it was clear Higgins was in a towering passion. Boucher stood leaning his weight on the high mantelpiece, swaying a little as he stared wild-eyed into the fire, a despair so raw it roused Higgins’ irritation even as it tore at his heart. Bessy rocked violently back and forth in her chair, as she always did when agitated, while her sister Mary tied on her bonnet with her clumsy, work-calloused fingers to go to her fustian cutting job, snivelling aloud, desperate to escape the tense, sad scene.
Margaret paused in the doorway, put a finger to her lips, and stole over to sit on the small sofa beside Bessy. Higgins saw her, and gave her a gruff, not unfriendly nod. Mary bolted for the door the second she could, crying loud the moment she was out of the house. Only Boucher noticed no one, his gaze fixed firmly on the flames.
“It’s no use, Higgins. My wife won’t live much longer like this. She’s wasting away, not for want of food herself, but because she can’t bear to see our bairns starving. Aye, starving! Five shillings a week might be enough for you, with only two mouths to feed and one of them a lass who earns her own keep. But for us, it’s starvation. I tell you plain—if she dies, before we ever get that five percent raise, I’ll fling your money back in the masters’ faces, and tell them to go to hell, the whole cruel lot of them, for robbing me of the best wife a man ever had. And look you, lad: I’ll hate you, and the whole cursed Union. Aye, and I’ll chase you through heaven with my hatred, I will, if you’ve led me astray in this. You said a fortnight ago that before two weeks were up, the masters would be begging us to come back to work on our own wages. Time’s almost up, and our little Jack lies in bed too weak to cry, only sobbing every now and then his little heart out for want of food. Our little Jack, that used to wake me every morning by pressing his soft little lips to my rough dirty face, hunting for a smooth spot to kiss—now he’s starving.” Deep, ragged sobs choked off his words, and Higgins lifted his own eyes full of tears to Margaret before he could find the courage to speak.
“Hold up, man. Your little Jack won’t starve. I’ve got money tucked away, and we’ll go buy the lad a sup of milk and a fresh four-pound loaf this very minute. What’s mine is yours, if you’re in need. Only don’t lose heart,” he said, fumbling in an old teapot for the savings he had stashed there. “I swear on my soul we’re going to win. It’s only one more week, and you’ll see the masters come crawling to us, begging us to come back to the mills. The Union—that’s to say, me—will make sure you’ve got enough to feed the bairns and your wife. Don’t you go running faint-hearted to the tyrants to beg for work.”
Boucher turned around, his face so white, gaunt, and streaked with tears that his very calmness made Margaret weep.
“You know well that a worse tyrant than any master ever was says starve to death, and let all of us starve, before you dare cross the Union. You know that, Nicholas, even if you are one of their leaders. You might all be kind men on your own, but once you’ve banded together, you’ve no more pity for a man than a wild, hunger-maddened wolf.”
