Three distinct columns advanced with a terrifying, deliberate slowness, devoid of any visible panic or frantic haste. As they drew within roughly five hundred yards, the central division halted at the base of a ridge, forming a tongue of open plain that jutted upward toward the hill. This pause allowed the flanking wings to maneuver into position, completing a horseshoe-shaped encirclement of our defences. Their objective was clear: a simultaneous, triple assault.

"Oh, for a Gatling gun!" groaned Good, staring at the dense phalanx of enemies below. "I'd clear that field in twenty minutes."

"We lack such a weapon, so wishing serves no purpose," Sir Henry replied, adjusting his grip on his rifle. "However, suppose you attempt a shot, Quatermain?" He gestured toward a tall figure commanding the center, who stood slightly apart from the main body. "Two to one you'll miss him. An honest sovereign awaits you if you can drop a bullet within five yards of his feet, and I promise fair payment if we survive this."

The wager stung my pride. A man who masters a single craft strives to maintain his reputation in it. I accepted the challenge. Loading my express rifle with solid ball, I waited until the officer stepped ten paces forward, flanked only by a single orderly, offering me a clearer view. I dropped low, bracing the heavy weapon against a rocky outcrop. My express, sighted only to three hundred and fifty yards, required careful calculation for the bullet's drop; aiming for the space halfway down the man's neck, I estimated the impact would be squarely in his chest.

He stood immovable, presenting a perfect target. Whether it was the adrenaline, the altitude, or simply the distance, I missed. The smoke cleared to reveal the officer standing unscathed, while his orderly, positioned merely three paces to the left, lay motionless on the earth, seemingly dead. The commander instantly spun, shouting orders and rushing back toward his ranks in a state of alarm.

"Bravo, Quatermain!" shouted Good, grinning. "You've spooked him."

Anger surged through me. To miss publicly was intolerable. Driven by a blind fury to salvage my honor, I acted rashly. As the fleeing officer rounded a bend, I raised the second barrel of my double-rifle and fired. Instantly, the man threw up his hands and collapsed onto his face. I had struck true. A cold, brutal satisfaction washed over me—not for the kill itself, but for the vindication of my skill. It is a dark truth of human nature: when survival, pride, or reputation hangs in the balance, we become capable of terrible acts.

The regiments watching us erupted into wild cheers, interpreting this display of white-man's magic as an omen of victory. Meanwhile, the unit the fallen officer had commanded—a force we later learned he actually led—broke formation and fled in disarray. Seizing the moment, Sir Henry and Good unleashed a storm of lead. Good worked his Winchester repeater tirelessly, mowing down the thickening mass of attackers, while I added my share. Within moments, perhaps six or eight men lay *hors de combat* before they could recover their footing.

Just as we ceased firing, a guttural roar erupted from our far right, answered almost immediately by a similar thunderclap from our left. The remaining two divisions were engaging.

Sound alone altered the battlefield. The dense mass before us spread thin, trotting steadily up the spine of the hill and across the strip of bare grass. They chanted a deep, resonant war-song as they advanced. We returned fire with steady, disciplined volleys; Ignosi joined in, his aim precise, accounting for several stragglers. Yet, like stones thrown against a crashing wave, our bullets failed to halt the mighty surge of armed humanity.

They closed the distance, screaming and clashing spears, driving into our picket lines anchored among the rocks. The pace slowed as gravity claimed them; climbing uphill cost breath, and they moved cautiously to conserve energy. Our defenses were tiered: the first line sat halfway down the slope, the second fifty yards back, and the third guarded the edge of the plateau.

"They come!" they shouted, their voices rising in a crescendo. *"Twala! Twala! Chiele! Chiele!"* ("Smite! Smite!") *"Ignosi! Ignosi! Chiele! Chiele!"* answered our troops.

Now they were close. The *tollas*, or throwing knives, flashed like silver lightning in the air. With a bloodcurdling yell, the battle closed.

The mass of warriors swayed back and forth, a chaotic ocean of flesh and steel. Men fell faster than leaves in an autumn storm. Soon, however, the sheer weight of numbers became undeniable. Our first line crumbled, pressing backward into the second. The struggle there was ferocious, a desperate melee, but eventually, our defenders were forced back once more, retreating step-by-step until the third line, impenetrable as a hedge of spears, finally engaged.

By then, the attackers were exhausted and bleeding heavily. Breaking through that final barrier proved impossible. For a brief, agonizing interval, the lines swayed in a fierce ebb and flow, the outcome hanging in the balance. Sir Henry watched the carnage with a burning gaze, then, without a word, charged into the thickest part of the fray, followed closely by Good.

As the soldiers spotted his towering form diving into the chaos, a cry rang out: *"Nanzia Incubu! Nanzia Unkungunklovo!"* ("Here comes the Elephant!") *"Chiele! Chiele!"*

From that moment, the tide turned. Inch by inch, backed by incredible courage, the attacking force was pushed back down the hillside until they retreated in confusion to their reserves. Just as I began to breathe easier, convinced the ordeal was over, a messenger burst in with grim news: the left flank had been repulsed, but disaster struck on the right. Our men engaged there were being driven across the plain, pursued by swarms of the enemy who had succeeded in breaching the line.

Ignosi read the situation instantly and issued a rapid command. The reserve regiment surrounding us—the Greys—sprang into action, extending their formation. Another word from Ignosi, relayed by the captains, and suddenly I found myself swept into a furious onslaught. Behind the massive shield-wall of Ignosi, I scrambled to keep up, moving as if I relished the danger. Within a minute, we were plunging through flying groups of our own men, who were reforming behind us. Then, memory fades into a nightmare of rolling noise—the clash of shields, the crash of steel.

A vision flashes before me: a hulking giant, eyes bulging from his skull, charging straight at me with a spear dripping with blood. Most men would have frozen, paralyzed by terror. But I rose—or rather, sank—to the occasion. Realizing that standing still meant death, I dove flat on the ground just as the monster loomed overhead. Unable to brake in time, he crashed directly over my prostrate form. Before he could rise, I sprang up and dispatched him with a quick shot from my revolver.

Then, darkness. Someone knocked me unconscious.

When I regained awareness, I was back at the kopje, Good crouched over me, pouring water from a gourd.

"How do you feel, old chap?" he asked, his face etched with worry.

I sat up, shaking off the daze. "Pretty well, thanks."

"Thank heavens!" he breathed, relief washing over him. "When I saw them carry you in, I thought you were finished. I felt sick with fear."

"Not this time, my boy. Seems I only took a glancing blow to the head that stunned me. What's the status?"

"For now, they've been repulsed everywhere," Good reported, pointing toward the horizon. "But the toll is dreadful. We've lost nearly two thousand men, and they must have lost three times that. Look!"

Long lines of men marched forward in files of four. Carried in the center of each file was a hide-tray, a standard of the Kukuana forces, with loops for handles at each corner. Upon these trays lay the wounded. As they approached, medicine men examined them quickly. If the wound was survivable, the patient was tended to with whatever care circumstances allowed. But if the injury proved fatal, a grim ritual ensued. Under the guise of examination, a doctor swiftly sliced an artery with a sharp knife. Within moments, the sufferer died painlessly. Many such cases occurred that day; the enormous breadth of the Kukuanan spears often severed vital arteries, rendering recovery impossible. In many instances, victims were already unconscious, while in others, the 'nick' was so swift and silent that they seemed unaware of their fate. It was a ghastly spectacle, one from which we were fortunate to escape, surpassing even the horror of Swazi warriors burying their hopelessly wounded alive.

Hurrying past this macabre procession, we found Sir Henry, Ignosi, Infadoos, and several chiefs gathered in urgent council. Sir Henry still gripped a battle-axe.

"Praise God you're safe, Quatermain!" Sir Henry exclaimed. "I can't fathom Ignosi's next move. Though we've beaten off the immediate attack, Twala is receiving massive reinforcements and intends to invest us, hoping to starve us out."

"That sounds dire."
