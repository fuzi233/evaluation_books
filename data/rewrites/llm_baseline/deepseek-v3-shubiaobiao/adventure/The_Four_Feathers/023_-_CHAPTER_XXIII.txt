The drawing room at The Pool was quiet, save for the ticking of the clock on the mantel. Durrance stood by the window, his hat in hand, ready to depart. He had timed it so there would be little room for lingering farewells—his carriage already waited at the steps of Guessens, luggage secured, his servant impatient by the door.

Ethne followed him onto the terrace, where Mrs. Adair stood at the top of the stone steps, her hands clasped tightly before her.

"I’d like a word with Colonel Durrance before he goes," Mrs. Adair said abruptly.

Ethne nodded. "Then we’ll say goodbye here." She turned to Durrance, her voice softening. "You’ll write from Wiesbaden?"

"The moment I arrive," he promised.

With that, Ethne remained on the terrace as Durrance descended the steps beside Mrs. Adair. The weight of pretense had lifted—the months of silent strain, of careful words and guarded looks, were over. Relief sharpened Durrance’s stride as they crossed the lawn, but Mrs. Adair lagged, her steps heavy.

"You’re leaving," she said, her voice low. "In two days, you’ll be in Wiesbaden, Ethne in Glenalla. The house will be empty. It’s already empty."

She glanced around. The garden, once vibrant, was dulled beneath a gray sky. The flowers had faded, the stream ran dark between the trees, and fallen leaves scattered like discarded memories.

"How long will you stay?" she pressed.

"As long as necessary."

"That tells me nothing."

He offered no elaboration, and she bristled at his silence. She knew nothing of his plans—whether he meant to release Ethne or hold her to her promise. The uncertainty gnawed at her.

"You don’t trust me," she accused, her voice edged with bitterness.

"Have I reason to?" His reply was quiet, but it struck like a lash. "You interfered. You told me of Willoughby’s visit when Ethne wished it kept secret."

"I thought you deserved the truth."

"Yet you were her friend."

"And yours," she countered, then laughed harshly. "But you never really saw me, did you? Not then, not now."

He didn’t answer, and the silence spurred her forward, words tumbling out in a reckless tide.

"I told you brutally because I wanted to hurt you. I *wanted* you to see—what you refused to see. There you were, always with Ethne, always in the dark together, while I stood alone on the terrace. Always outside." Her voice dropped to a whisper. "She never loved you, not truly. Only pity. Pity and fear. Fear of your blindness, of the burden it would be. But *I*—I was glad when I heard. Glad because it meant she would shrink from you, and I… I wouldn’t."

Durrance recoiled as if struck. The cruelty of it, the raw confession, was unlike anything he had known. Yet beneath the venom, he sensed a deeper desperation.

"You’re unjust to Ethne," he said quietly.

The words seemed to drain the fury from her. She faltered, then exhaled sharply. "Perhaps. But I’ve had little kindness from life to teach me fairness."

They reached the stile at the garden’s edge. He turned, and for the first time, truly looked at her—not past her, not through her, but *at* her.

"Tell me," he said.

And she did.

She spoke of a girl thrust into marriage by an imperious mother, of years stifled in gilded monotony. Of a husband kind but distant, of loneliness that gnawed like hunger. And then—of Durrance himself, a fleeting brightness in her gray world, a hope she had clung to until Ethne eclipsed it.

"I tried to keep you," she admitted, the confession bitter on her tongue. "I schemed to tie Ethne to another so you’d turn elsewhere. And I succeeded—only for you to leave for the Soudan. Three years gone. And when you returned…" She laughed mirthlessly. "She refused you, then claimed you out of guilt. And here we are."

Durrance absorbed it in silence. The smallness of it all struck him—how vast consequences sprang from quiet, desperate acts.

"I’m sorry," he said at last. It was all he could offer.

She studied him, then nodded once, sharply. "Goodbye, Colonel."

He climbed the stile and crossed the field without looking back.

Mrs. Adair remained, gripping the wooden rail. She had laid herself bare, and for what? No wound dealt, no victory won—only the certainty that when he returned (if he returned alone), there would be no easy friendship waiting.

The wind stirred the leaves at her feet. The garden was emptier than ever.
