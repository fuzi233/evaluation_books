The difference between the two young ladies—so loud overnight, so fluttering and tragic in the morning—lasted about as long as a dish of hot cakes at a children’s tea. Laura was always too eager to forgive and be forgiven; and as for Miss Blanche, her hostilities were never of that durable sort which keeps well—like smoked bacon. Nobody is seriously injured by being accused of wickedness: it is not a charge that pricks vanity; it only tickles it. Blanche was, in truth, rather pleased than provoked by her friend’s indignation, which never would have risen into flame but for a cause which both perfectly knew, and neither chose to name.

So Laura, with one of those sighs which seem made to be served with a lace handkerchief, was forced to own that the romantic part of her first friendship was ended; and that the object of it—once a heroine in Laura’s private epic—was only fit henceforth for a very ordinary sort of regard.

Blanche, on her side, did not sit down to plain bread like other mortals. She instantly composed touching verses about desertion and disenchantment—love met with coldness, fidelity repaid by neglect; the old recipe, whisked up with fresh rhymes, and sent to table as if it were a new dish. And, as it happened, some new neighbours arrived from London about this time with daughters; and Miss Amory, who could always find a sister as readily as she could find a ribbon, had the advantage of selecting an eternal friend on the spot, to whom she poured out her sorrows in a tone meant to be overheard by Providence.

The tall footmen came but seldom now with notes for sweet Laura; the pony-carriage was rarely despatched to Fairoaks to be at the ladies’ orders. Blanche, when Laura visited, assumed a look of suffering martyrdom, as though she had been roasted—slowly—upon the spit of sensibility. Laura laughed at her, and treated the whole performance with a good humour that was not in the least respectful.

But if Miss Blanche found new female friends to console her, it must also be said—by any faithful historian with a palate for truth—that she discovered acquaintances of the other sex who seemed to administer comfort quite as successfully. If ever this artless young creature met a young man and had ten minutes’ talk with him—whether in a garden walk, a drawing-room window, or between the first and second acts of a waltz—she confided in him, so to speak. She played with her beautiful eyes as a clever cook plays with a spice-box: a little, and then a little more, until the whole air was perfumed. She spoke in a tone of tender interest and simple appeal, and then left him—warm, flattered, and seasoned—for the same pretty drama to be served to his successor.

When the Claverings first came down to the Park there were scarcely any audiences before whom Blanche could perform; hence Pen had all the benefits of her glances and confidences, and the drawing-room window or garden walk entirely to himself. In the town of Clavering there were, it has been said, no young men at all; in the surrounding country only a curate or two, or a rustic young squire with large feet and clothes that looked as if they had been cut with a hedging-bill. To the dragoons quartered at Chatteris the Baronet made no overtures: it was unhappily his own regiment; he had left it on bad terms with certain officers—an ugly business about a horse bargain, a disputed play account, blind-Hookey, a white feather—who need ask? We will not go rummaging in the cold larder of bygones when the present course is still on the table.

But autumn, and the end of Parliament and the London season, brought one or two county families down to their houses; it filled the little watering-place of Baymouth; it opened Mr. Bingley’s Theatre Royal at Chatteris; it collected the usual company at Assizes and Race-balls. Up to this time the old county families had been shy of Clavering Park. The Fogeys of Drummington, the Squares of Tozely Park, the Welbores of The Barrow, and their like, had all sorts of stories in circulation; and anyone who says country people have no imagination should only hear them talk of new neighbours. About Sir Francis and his Lady—about her birth and parentage—about Miss Amory, about Captain Strong—there had been endless histories, which we will not reheat.

But at the end of the season the Earl of Trehawk, Lord Lieutenant of the county, coming to Eyrie Castle, and the Countess Dowager of Rockminster (whose son was also a magnate) taking a mansion on the Marine Parade at Baymouth—these great folks called publicly, immediately, and in state upon the family at Clavering Park; and the carriages of the county families rolled promptly after, as if the wheel-ruts of rank were as binding as Scripture.

Then Mirobolant had opportunity to exercise his skill and forget, in the honest occupations of his art, the pangs of love. One could almost hear the kitchen itself revive—knives with their quick, bright whisper on the boards; saucepans answering one another with their metallic chime; spits turning like clockwork; the whole domestic orchestra tuning up to welcome county grandeur. Then the large footmen were too much employed at the Park to linger over cups of small beer with the poor little maids at Fairoaks. Then Blanche found other dear friends than Laura, and other places to walk in besides the river-side where Pen was fishing. He came day after day and whipped the stream; but the “fish, fish!” would not do their duty, nor the Peri appear.

And here—strictly between ourselves, with a request that the matter go no further—we may mention a delicate business, of which hint has already been given. There was a certain hollow tree at which Pen used to take his station in his passion; and whose cavity he afterwards used for purposes beyond baits and fishing-cans. The truth is, he converted that tree into a post-office. Under a piece of moss and a stone he used to put little poems—or letters equally poetical—addressed to an Undine or Naiad of the stream; and once or twice these were replaced by a receipt in the shape of a flower, or by a modest word or two of acknowledgment, written in a delicate hand, in French or English, on pink scented paper. Miss Amory did walk by that stream; and it is a fact she used pink scented paper. But after the great folks had invaded Clavering Park, and the family coach passed out of the lodge-gates evening after evening to other great houses, nobody came for Pen’s letters at the post-office. The white paper lay undisturbed under its stone and moss, while the tree reflected itself in the water and the Brawl went rolling by—its old sound unbought, unadvertised, and all the sweeter for it; for rivers, unlike theatres, do not require a printed bill to make them admirable.

There was not much in the letters, certainly; and in the pink notes scarcely anything—only a word or two half jocular, half sympathetic, such as any young lady might write. But oh, you silly Pendennis—if you wanted this one, why did you not speak? Perhaps neither party was in earnest. You were only playing at being in love, and the sportive little Undine humouring you at the same game.

Yet when a man is baulked at play he often turns suddenly serious, as if the toys were sacred. When nobody came any more for Pen’s poems, he began to look upon those compositions as if they were a tragedy in five acts. He felt romantic again; at any rate, he was bent on an explanation.

One day he went to the Hall, and found a room full of visitors. Another day Miss Amory was not to be seen: she was going to a ball that night, and was lying down to take a little sleep. Pen cursed balls, and cursed also his narrow means and humble position which caused him to be passed over by the givers of these entertainments—cursed them with that bitterness which has, at the bottom, a great deal of wounded appetite. On a third occasion Miss Amory was in the garden, and he ran thither; but she was walking there in state with the Bishop and Bishopess of Chatteris and the episcopal family, who scowled at him and drew up in dignity when he was presented and they heard his name. The Right Reverend Prelate had heard it before—and also of the little transaction in the Dean’s garden.

“The Bishop says you’re a sad young man,” good-natured Lady Clavering whispered to him. “What have you been a doing of? Nothink, I hope, to vex such a dear Mar as yours? How is your dear Mar? Why don’t she come and me? We an’t seen her this ever such a time. We’re a goin about a gaddin, so that we don’t see no neighbours now. Give my love to her and Laurar, and come all to dinner to-morrow.”

Mrs. Pendennis was too unwell to come out; but Laura and Pen came, and there was a great party—voices and giggles, the scrape of chairs, the thin laugh of politeness, and, between it all, the orchestra of the house: doors opening with little sighs, fire crackling, glasses clinking like tiny bells. Pen only got opportunity for a hurried word with Miss Amory.

“You never come to the river now,” he said.

“I can’t,” said Blanche; “the house is full of people.”

“Undine has left the stream,” Mr. Pen went on, choosing to be poetical.

“She never ought to have gone there,” Miss Amory answered. “She won’t go again. It was very foolish: very wrong: it was only play. Besides, you have other consolations at home,” she added, looking him full in the face for an instant, and dropping her eyes.

If he wanted her, why did he not speak then? She might have said yes even then. But when she spoke of other consolations at home, he thought of Laura—so affectionate, so pure—and of his mother, who had bent her fond heart on uniting him with her adopted daughter. “Blanche!” he began in a vexed tone—“Miss Amory!”

“Laura is looking at us, Mr. Pendennis,” the young lady said. “I must go back to the company,” and she ran off, leaving Mr. Pendennis to bite his nails in perplexity and to stare out into the moonlight in the garden, where the night air smelt of damp earth and clipped box, and the distant talk within sounded like a sea of shallow laughter.

Laura indeed was looking at Pen. She was talking with—or appearing to listen to the talk of—Mr. Pynsent, Lord Rockminster’s son, and grandson of the Dowager Lady, who was seated in state in the place of honour, gravely receiving Lady Clavering’s bad grammar, and patronising the vacuous Sir Francis, whose interest in the county she desired to secure. Pynsent and Pen had been at Oxbridge together, where the latter, during his heyday of good fortune and fashion, had been the superior of the young patrician and perhaps rather supercilious. They had met for the first time since the University that day at table, and given each other that exceedingly impertinent demi-nod of recognition practised in England only, and to perfection by University men—the nod which says as plainly as speech: Confound you; what do you do here?

“I knew that man at Oxbridge,” Mr. Pynsent said to Miss Bell—“a Mr. Pendennis, I think.”

“Yes,” said Miss Bell.

“He seems rather sweet upon Miss Amory,” the gentleman went on. Laura looked at them, and perhaps thought so too, but said nothing.

“A man of large property in the county, ain’t he? He used to talk about representing it. He used to speak at the Union. Whereabouts do his estates lie?”

Laura smiled. “His estates lie on the other side of the river, near the lodge-gate. He is my cousin, and I live there.”

“Where?” asked Mr. Pynsent, with a laugh.

“Why, on the other side of the river, at Fairoaks,” answered Miss Bell.

“Many pheasants there? Cover looks rather good,” said the simple gentleman.

Laura smiled again. “We have nine hens and a cock, a pig, and an old pointer.”

“Pendennis don’t preserve, then?” continued Mr. Pynsent.

“You should come and see him,” the girl said, laughing, vastly amused at the notion that her Pen was a great county gentleman and had perhaps given himself out to be such.

“Indeed, I quite long to renew our acquaintance,” Mr. Pynsent said gallantly—and with a look which plainly added, It is you I would like to come and see. Laura vouchsafed a smile and made a little bow.

Here Blanche came stepping up with her most fascinating smile and ogle, and begged dear Laura to come and take the second in a song. Laura, ready for any kindness, went to the piano; and Mr. Pynsent listened as long as the duet lasted, and until Miss Amory began for herself, when he strode away.

“What a nice, frank, amiable, well-bred girl that is, Wagg,” said Mr. Pynsent to a gentleman who had come over with him from Baymouth—“the tall one, I mean, with the ringlets and red lips—monstrous red, ain’t they?”

“What do you think of the girl of the house?” asked Wagg.

“I think she’s a lean, scraggy humbug,” said Mr. Pynsent with great candour. “She drags her shoulders out of her dress, she never lets her eyes alone; and she goes simpering and ogling about like a French waiting-maid.”

“Pynsent, be civil,” cried the other. “Somebody can hear.”

“Oh, it’s Pendennis of Boniface,” Mr. Pynsent said. “Fine evening, Mr. Pendennis; we were just talking of your charming cousin.”

“Any relation to my old friend, Major Pendennis?” asked Mr. Wagg.

“His nephew. Had the pleasure of meeting you at Gaunt House,” Mr. Pen said with his very best air; and the acquaintance was made in an instant.

In the afternoon next day, the two gentlemen staying at Clavering Park were found by Mr. Pen, on his return from a fishing excursion in which he had no sport, seated in his mother’s drawing-room at Fairoaks in comfortable conversation with the widow and her ward. Mr. Pynsent, tall and gaunt, with large red whiskers and an imposing tuft to his chin, was striding over a chair in the intimate neighbourhood of Miss Laura. She was amused by his talk—simple, straightforward, rather humorous and keen—and interspersed with homely expressions sometimes called slang. It was the first specimen of a young London dandy that Laura had seen or heard; for she had been but a chit when Mr. Foker had been introduced at Fairoaks, and that ingenuous gentleman was little more than a boy.

Mr. Wagg, as he entered the premises with his companion, eyed and noted everything, as if his mind were a pantry and he must label every jar. “Old gardener,” he said, seeing Mr. John at the lodge—“old red livery waistcoat—clothes hanging out to dry on the gooseberry-bushes—blue aprons, white ducks—gad, they must be young Pendennis’s white ducks—nobody else wears ’em in the family. Rather a shy place for a sucking county member, ay, Pynsent?”

“Snug little crib,” said Mr. Pynsent; “pretty cosy little lawn.”

“Mr. Pendennis at home, old gentleman?” Mr. Wagg asked. John answered that Master Pendennis was agone out.

“Are the ladies at home?” asked the younger visitor. John said yes; and as the pair walked over the trim gravel and by the neat shrubberies, up the steps to the hall-door which old John opened, Wagg noted everything: the barometer and letter-bag, umbrellas and ladies’ clogs, Pen’s hats and tartan wrapper. Such minutiae attracted Wagg instinctively; he seized them in spite of himself, as a dog seizes a bone.

“Old fellow does all the work,” he whispered to Pynsent. “Caleb Balderstone. Shouldn’t wonder if he’s the housemaid.”

The next minute they were in the presence of the Fairoaks ladies; and Pynsent could not help recognising two perfectly well-bred women. Mr. Wagg made his obeisance with florid bows and extra courtesy, accompanied by an occasional knowing leer at his companion. Mr. Pynsent declined to acknowledge these signals, except by extreme haughtiness towards Wagg and particular deference to the ladies.

If there was one thing laughable in Mr. Wagg’s eyes, it was poverty. He had the soul of a butler brought from his pantry to make sport in the drawing-room. His jokes were plenty and his good-nature genuine; but he did not seem to understand that a gentleman could wear an old coat, or that a lady could be respectable without a carriage or a French milliner.

“Charming place, ma’am,” said he to the widow; “noble prospect—delightful to us Cocknies, who seldom see anything but Pall Mall.” The widow said simply she had never been in London but once in her life—before her son was born.

“Fine village, ma’am, fine village,” said Mr. Wagg, “and increasing every day. It’ll be quite a large town soon. It’s not a bad place to live in for those who can’t get the country, and will repay a visit when you honour it.”

“My brother, Major Pendennis, has often mentioned your name to us,” the widow said, “and we have been very much amused by some of your droll books, sir.” Helen continued, who never could be brought to like Mr. Wagg’s books, and detested their tone most thoroughly.

“He is my very good friend,” Mr. Wagg said with a low bow, “and one of the best known men about town—and where known, ma’am, appreciated; I assure you appreciated. He is with our friend Steyne at Aix-la-Chapelle. Steyne has a touch of the gout, and so—between ourselves—has your brother. I am going to Stillbrook for the pheasant-shooting, and afterwards to Bareacres, where Pendennis and I shall probably meet;” and he poured out a flood of fashionable talk, names of peers and places rattling like coins in a shallow dish, whilst the simple widow listened in silent wonder. What a man, she thought; are all the men of fashion in London like this? I am sure Pen will never like him.

Mr. Pynsent meanwhile was engaged with Miss Laura. He named some houses in the neighbourhood whither he was going, and hoped very much to see her at some of them. He hoped her aunt would give her a season in London. He said that in the next Parliament it was probable he should canvass the county, and he hoped to get Pendennis’s interest. He spoke of Pen’s triumph as an orator at Oxbridge, and asked was he coming into Parliament too? He talked pleasantly, and greatly to Laura’s satisfaction—until Pen appeared, as has been said, and found these gentlemen in his quarters.

Pen behaved courteously to the pair. Though he remembered with a twinge an Oxbridge conversation—after a great Union debate, with supper and champagne-cup inflaming everybody—in which he had announced his intention of coming in for his native county, and had absolutely returned thanks in a fine speech as the future member; yet Pynsent’s manner was so frank and cordial that Pen hoped he might have forgotten the fanfaronnade and any other youthful braggadocio. Pen suited himself to the tone of the visitors and talked about Plinlimmon and Magnus Charters and the old set at Oxbridge with careless familiarity and high-bred ease, as if he lived with marquises every day and a duke were no more to him than a village curate.

But at this juncture—being then six o’clock—Betsy the maid, who did not know of the advent of strangers, walked into the room without any preliminary but flinging the door wide open, bearing in her arms a tray containing three tea-cups, a tea-pot, and a plate of thick bread-and-butter.

It was as though, in the middle of a grand speech about empire, somebody should cough and say, “Soup’s on.” All Pen’s splendour vanished. He faltered, and became quite abashed. What will they think of us? he thought. And indeed Wagg thrust his tongue in his cheek, thought the tea infinitely contemptible, and leered and winked at Pynsent to that effect.

But to Mr. Pynsent the transaction appeared perfectly simple. There was no reason in his mind why people should not drink tea at six if they were minded, as well as at any other hour. When they went away he asked Wagg what the devil he was grinning and winking at, and what amused him.

“Didn’t you see how the cub was ashamed of the thick bread-and-butter?” Mr. Wagg chuckled. “I dare say they’re going to have treacle if they are good. I’ll take an opportunity of telling old Pendennis when we get back to town.”

“Don’t see the fun,” said Mr. Pynsent.

“Never thought you did,” growled Wagg; and they walked home rather sulkily—each offended by a different thing, like two men quarrelling at a dinner-table because one dislikes the salt and the other the silence.

Wagg told the story at dinner very smartly, with wonderful accuracy. He described old John, the clothes drying, the clogs in the hall, the drawing-room and its furniture and pictures: “Old man with a beak and bald head—feu Pendennis I bet two to one; sticking-plaster full-length of a youth in a cap and gown—the present Marquis of Fairoaks, of course; the widow when young in a miniature, Mrs. Mee; she had the gown on when we came, or a dress made the year after, and the tips cut off the fingers of her gloves which she stitches her son’s collars with; and then the sarving maid came in with their teas, so we left the Earl and the Countess to their bread-and-butter.”

Blanche, near whom he sat, and who adored les hommes d’esprit, burst out laughing and called him such an odd, droll creature. But Pynsent, who began to be utterly disgusted with Wagg, broke out loud: “I don’t know, Mr. Wagg, what sort of ladies you are accustomed to meet in your own family, but by gad, as far as a first acquaintance can show, I never met two better-bred women in my life; and I hope, ma’am, you’ll call upon ’em,” he added, addressing Lady Rockminster.

Sir Francis turned to his guest and whispered, “That’s what I call a sticker for Wagg.” And Lady Clavering, giving the young gentleman a delighted tap with her fan, winked her black eyes at him and said, “Mr. Pynsent, you’re a good feller.”

After the affair with Blanche, a difference ever so slight—a tone of melancholy, perhaps a little bitter—might be perceived in Laura’s converse with her cousin. She seemed to weigh him and find him wanting too. The widow saw the girl’s clear honest eyes watching the young man at times, and a look of almost scorn pass over her face as he lounged in the room with the women, or lazily sauntered smoking upon the lawn, or lolled under a tree over a book he was too listless to read. Even the place itself, quiet as it was, had its own soundscape—leaves shifting, a distant cart, the river’s murmur—and Pen, who might have listened and learned contentment from such honest music, seemed deaf to it, as though his ears had been trained only to the loud instruments of town.

“What has happened between you?” eager-sighted Helen asked the girl. “Something has happened. Has that wicked little Blanche been making mischief? Tell me, Laura.”

“Nothing has happened at all,” Laura said.

“Then why do you look at Pen so?” his mother asked quickly.

“Look at him, dear mother!” Laura cried. “We two women are no society for him. We don’t interest him; we are not clever enough for such a genius as Pen. He wastes his life and energies away among us, tied to our apron-strings. He interests himself in nothing: he scarcely cares to go beyond the garden-gate. Even Captain Glanders and Captain Strong pall upon him,” she added with a bitter laugh; “and they are men, you know, and our superiors. He will never be happy while he is here. Why, is he not facing the world, and without a profession?”

“We have got enough, with great economy,” said the widow, her heart beginning to beat violently. “Pen has spent nothing for months. I’m sure he is very good. I am sure he might be very happy with us.”

“Don’t agitate yourself so, dear mother,” the girl answered. “I don’t like to see you so. You should not be sad because Pen is unhappy here. All men are so. They must work. They must make themselves names and a place in the world. Look—the two captains have fought and seen battles; that Mr. Pynsent, who came here, and who will be very rich, is in a public office; he works very hard, he aspires to a name and a reputation. He says Pen was one of the best speakers at Oxbridge, and had as great a character for talent as any of the young gentlemen there. Pen himself laughs at Mr. Wagg’s celebrity (and indeed he is a horrid person), and says he is a dunce, and that anybody could write his books.”

“I am sure they are odious and vulgar,” interposed the widow.
