“My heart revolts within me, and two voices make themselves audible within my bosom.” — WALLenstein.

When Margaret returned home, she found two letters waiting on the table: one addressed to her mother, the other—bearing foreign postmarks, thin and silvery, rustling as she lifted it—clearly from Aunt Shaw. She picked up the latter, her eyes scanning the handwriting, when her father entered abruptly.

“So your mother is tired and has gone to bed early!” he said, his voice tight with concern. “I’m afraid such a stormy day was not ideal for the doctor’s visit. What did he say? Dixon told me he spoke with you.”

Margaret hesitated. Her father’s expression deepened into something more serious, more anxious.

“He doesn’t think her seriously ill?” he asked, pressing forward.

“No,” she replied quietly. “Not at present. He says she needs care. He was very kind, and promised to call again to see how the medicine works.”

“Only care?” Her father frowned. “He didn’t suggest a change of air? Didn’t he mention that this smoky town might be harming her?”

“No,” Margaret said firmly. “Not a word. I think he was genuinely concerned.”

“Doctors always look worried,” he said dismissively. “It’s part of their manner.”

But Margaret saw through the pretense. In the way he paced, in the tension of his hands, she sensed the fear he refused to name—the quiet dread that had taken root beneath his calm façade. He could not let go of the subject. Over and over, he returned to it, each time trying to dismiss any hint of danger, even as he clung to hope like a lifeline. It made Margaret’s heart ache.

“This letter is from Aunt Shaw, Papa,” she said gently. “She’s arrived in Naples, but finds it too hot. So she’s moved to Sorrento. But she doesn’t seem to enjoy Italy.”

He ignored her words. “Did the doctor say anything about diet?” he asked sharply.

“It was to be nourishing, digestible,” she answered. “Mother’s appetite seems fairly good.”

“Yes,” he murmured, “and that makes it all the stranger he should have mentioned food at all.”

“I asked him, Papa,” she said.

A silence followed. Then she added, with a slight smile, “Aunt Shaw says she sent me some coral ornaments. But she’s afraid the Milton dissenters won’t appreciate them. She still thinks of Dissenters as Quakers, doesn’t she?”

Her father gave a small nod. “If ever you notice your mother wanting anything—anything at all—be sure to tell me. I’m so afraid she hides what she truly desires. And please see after that girl Mrs. Thornton recommended. If we had a proper house-servant, Dixon could attend to her full-time. I’d guarantee it—we’d soon have her back on her feet. A little rest, a little peace—she’ll recover. Won’t she, Margaret?”

“I hope so,” Margaret said—but the sadness in her voice did not escape him.

He reached out and pinched her cheek. “Come now,” he said, forcing cheerfulness. “You’re looking pale. I may need to rouge you up. Take care of yourself, child, or you’ll be next needing the doctor.”

Yet he couldn’t settle. All evening, he crept back and forth on tiptoe, checking the bedroom door, listening for signs of movement. Margaret watched him, pained by his unease, by the way he tried to suppress the terror gnawing at his soul. Finally, he returned, somewhat relieved.

“She’s awake now,” he said softly. “She smiled when she saw me. Just her old smile. She says she feels refreshed, ready for tea. Where’s the note for her? She wants to read it. I’ll read it aloud while you prepare the tea.”

The note turned out to be an invitation from Mrs. Thornton—formal and precise—for Mr., Mrs., and Miss Hale to dine at the Thorntons’ on the twenty-first. Margaret was startled. After everything she’d learned that day, she expected refusal. Yet her mother had already decided.

Even before Margaret knew the contents, her mother had seized upon the idea with fervor. The dinner would break the monotony of her invalid life. When Margaret voiced hesitation, her mother insisted.

“Nay, Margaret,” she said firmly. “If she wishes it, then we must go. She wouldn’t ask unless she felt stronger—truly stronger than we believe, eh, Margaret?”

Mr. Hale looked at her anxiously. “Eh, Margaret?” he repeated, his hands trembling slightly.

It was cruel to deny him comfort. And yet, his fierce denial of fear somehow stirred a flicker of hope in her own heart.

“I do think she’s better since last night,” Margaret said. “Her eyes are brighter. Her color looks clearer.”

“God bless you,” her father whispered, almost reverently. “But is it true? Yesterday was so oppressive—everyone felt unwell. It was unfortunate Dr. Donaldson came then.”

And so he left, returning to his duties—now heavier, for he had pledged to deliver lectures at a nearby Lyceum. He chose Ecclesiastical Architecture, not because it suited the audience, but because it aligned with his own scholarly tastes. The institution, struggling financially, welcomed the free course from a man of learning like Mr. Hale, regardless of subject matter.

That evening, at the Thornton household, conversation turned to the guest list.

“Well, mother,” Mr. Thornton asked, “who has accepted your invitation for the twenty-first?”

Fanny fetched the notes. “The Slicksons accept. The Collingbrooks accept. The Stephenses accept. The Browns decline. The Hales—father and daughter come. Mother is too ill. Macphersons come. Mr. Horsfall. Mr. Young. I thought of asking the Porters, since the Browns can’t attend.”

“Very good,” said Mr. Thornton. “But I must say—I’m truly concerned about Mrs. Hale’s health, based on what Dr. Donaldson told me.”

“It’s odd they’d accept if she’s very ill,” Fanny remarked.

“I didn’t say *very* ill,” her brother corrected sharply. “Just far from well. They may not realize it themselves.”

Then, suddenly, he remembered—Dr. Donaldson had spoken plainly to Margaret. She likely knew the truth.

“Perhaps they’re fully aware,” he mused aloud. “Of the advantage it would bring Mr. Hale—to meet people like the Stephenses and Collingbrooks.”

“That motive wouldn’t influence them,” he insisted. “No. I think I understand why they’re coming.”

“John!” Fanny laughed lightly, nervously. “How you claim to understand these Hales, yet refuse to admit we might know anything about them. Are they really so different from anyone else?”

She meant no harm. But the question stung. He clenched his jaw, refusing to respond.

“They don’t seem unusual to me,” Mrs. Thornton observed. “Mr. Hale appears decent enough—perhaps a bit too simple for trade. That’s why it’s fortunate he was first a clergyman, then a teacher. His wife is a fine lady in her sickness, and the daughter—ah, the daughter is the one who puzzles me. She carries herself with airs, though I cannot fathom why. She seems to think herself above her company. And yet—they’re not wealthy. From what I’ve heard, they never were.”

“And she’s not accomplished,” Fanny added. “She can’t play the piano.”

“Go on, Fanny,” her mother said, amused. “What else does she lack to meet your standards?”

“Nay, John,” Mrs. Thornton said, turning to her son. “Fanny’s remark did no harm. I myself heard Miss Hale say she couldn’t play. If you’d leave us alone, we might learn to like her—and see her merits.”

“I’m certain I never could!” Fanny murmured, shielded by her mother’s presence.

Mr. Thornton heard, but chose silence. He walked the length of the dining room, wishing candles would be lit so he could retreat to reading or writing—anything to end the talk. But he never questioned his mother’s habits, born of lifelong thrift.

“Mother,” he stopped, speaking plainly, “I wish you would like Miss Hale.”

She looked surprised. “Why? You’re not thinking of marrying her? A girl without a penny?”

“She would never have me,” he said, laughing shortly.

“No, I suppose not,” his mother agreed. “She laughed in my face when I praised her for speaking plainly about Mr. Bell’s remarks in your favor. I liked her honesty—it showed she had no designs on you. But then, the next moment, she annoyed me terribly by acting as if—well, never mind. But you’re right—she holds herself too high to consider you. The impudent girl! I’d like to know where she’d find a better match!”

“Then you’ll believe me,” he said steadily, “when I say I speak of her not out of personal interest. I am convinced—just as you are—that she’s in trouble. Perhaps lacking proper care. I only ask you to be ready to help her, should she need it. Now, Fanny,” he continued, “I trust you have enough delicacy to understand that suggesting I have ulterior motives would wound her more than me. She would take it harder.”

“I cannot forgive her pride,” Mrs. Thornton declared. “I’ll befriend her—if you ask it, John. I’d befriend Jezebel herself if you asked. But this girl—who turns up her nose at us all—turns up her nose at *you*—”

“Nay, mother,” he said quietly, “I’ve never put myself within reach of her scorn. I mean never to.”

“Scorn, indeed!” she snorted—a sound full of meaning. “Don’t speak of her if I must be kind to her. When I’m near her, I don’t know whether I like or dislike her. But when I think of her—when I hear you talk—then I hate her. I can see she treats you with contempt, as if you’d confessed it.”

“And if she does,” he said slowly, “I am not a boy to be cowed by a proud glance. I laugh at it.”

“Of course! And at her too—with her lofty notions and haughty ways!”

“Then why do you speak of her so often?” Fanny asked, weary. “I’m sick of the subject.”

“Well,” her brother replied, a trace of bitterness in his tone, “let’s find something more pleasant. How about a strike? That might cheer us.”

“Have the workers actually walked out?” Mrs. Thornton asked, her interest sharpened.

“Hamper’s men are out,” he said. “Mine are finishing their week—afraid of prosecution. I’d have punished every one who left early. But the cost of legal fees would exceed their worth—ungrateful scoundrels!”

“To be sure,” she agreed. “But I’d show them who’s master. I’d hire whoever I pleased.”

“You can,” he said. “And you will—if this goes on. It’ll cost money, and there’s risk. But I’d rather do it than give in.”

“If there’s extra expense,” she sighed, “I regret we’re hosting a dinner now.”

“So do I,” he admitted—not for the cost, but because he’d have much on his mind. “But we must invite Mr. Horsfall—he won’t stay long. The others? We owe them dinners. It’s all one burden.”

He resumed pacing, breathing deeply, as if fighting off a troubling thought. Fanny peppered her mother with trivial questions—about the weather, the servants, the price of milk—each met with curt replies. She was relieved when, at ten o’clock, the servants filed in for prayers. Mrs. Thornton read a chapter from the Old Testament, then led the family in prayer. When it ended, she wished her son goodnight—with that steady gaze of hers, devoid of warmth, yet carrying a depth of affection he could not ignore.

He continued walking. His plans—carefully laid, painfully earned—had been thrown into chaos by the looming strike. Hours of foresight, months of preparation, undone by blind folly. These men, who believed themselves capable of directing the masters’ affairs, would destroy themselves even more than him. Hamper had said just that day: if ruined by the strike, he’d start anew, comforted by knowing those who caused it were worse off—because he had both head and hands, while they had only hands. But for Mr. Thornton, revenge brought no satisfaction. He valued the position he’d built with blood, sweat, and relentless effort. To see it threatened by ignorance—by arrogance—was agony.

He paced, grinding his teeth. At last, the clock struck two. Candles flickered. He lit his own, muttering under his breath:

“Once and for all, they shall know whom they have to deal with. I’ll give them two weeks—no more. If they don’t see their madness by then, I’ll bring in Irish labor. I suspect it’s Slickson’s doing—confound him and his schemes! He thought he was overstocked. So he pretended to yield when the delegation came—only to embolden them. That’s where it began.”
