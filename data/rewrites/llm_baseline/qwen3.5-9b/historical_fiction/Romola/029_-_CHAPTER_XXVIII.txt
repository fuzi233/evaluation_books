Four days later, Romola set out for the house of Piero di Cosimo on the Via Gualfonda. The streets she traversed were thick with a peculiar tension. On one side stood Frenchmen, gazing at Florence with an air of condescending superiority; on the other, Florentines stared back with growing irritation. The dynamic was far from harmonious. Having initially played the role of gracious hosts, presenting wondrous religious mysteries with floating clouds and angelic machinery, the city now found its patience worn thin. After the royal guest had indulged in "Most Christian" glances at balls and suppers, the veneer of diplomacy cracked. To the new "Charlemagne," Florence appeared less a sovereign ally and more a conquered prize; he spoke of leaving a viceroy behind and hinted at restoring the Medici. This logic seemed absurd to those who knew that the sole offense of Florence against the French crown was the unpopular policy of Piero de' Medici, whom the people had already cast out. Determination to resist hardened among the citizens within the Old Palace and manifested boldly on street banners designed to mock the insolent visitors. Consequently, the thoroughfares became unsafe promenades for ladies of quality. Yet, Romola, veiled in black and accompanied by the elderly Maso, felt secure from prying eyes, driven instead by an urgent need to visit the painter.

Piero's work on a copy of her father's portrait—as Oedipus—remained unfinished. Known for his erratic habits, the artist would sometimes abandon a commission for months or hide it away in a corner, only to resurface it weeks later. Though Romola was his favorite and he generally eager to please, his whimsical nature meant no promise could be fully trusted. Having heard the previous week that the piece might be ready, she grew nervous. She feared that without a physical record, the image of her blind father might fade from her mind. Driven by a profound sense of duty and love—a devotion that seeks its own completeness through perfect loyalty—she felt compelled to ensure the preservation of his likeness.

By special favor, Romola was permitted to enter the studio unannounced. She lifted the iron slide and called out in a soft, flute-like tone, mimicking the gesture of a little girl once offered eggs by the same master. Piero answered quickly, but when he opened the door, his explanation was surprisingly mundane.

"Ah, Madonna Romola, is it you?" he said. "I thought my eggs had arrived; I wanted them."

"I have brought you something better than hard eggs, Piero," Romola replied, smiling as she adjusted her veil. "Maso has secured a basket of cakes and *confetti* for you."

She handed the basket to Maso and stepped inside. "I know you enjoy these treats when they arrive without effort. Confess, do you?"

"Yes, whenever they come to me as effortlessly as the light," Piero admitted, crossing his arms and looking down at the sweets as Romola unveiled them. His gaze then rose to her face and hair, filled with a painter's appreciation as her heavy hood slipped back. "But I suspect these sweetmeats are intended to silence you while you scold me. Very well, proceed to the next room; you will find I have made progress on the picture, though it remains incomplete. Remember, I never promised—" quoting the proverb, *"He who promises and does not keep, shall never have his soul in order."*

The door leading to the wild garden was shut, and the painter was indeed working. However, he was not attending to Romola's commission. That canvas stood propped against the wall, and as Piero bent to lift it into the proper light, he inadvertently revealed another work beneath it: an oil sketch of Tito. It was significantly smaller than the other pieces, tucked deep within the arrangement, and Piero, distracted by a minor detail in the painting he held, failed to notice what he had exposed until he moved to place the Oedipus portrait on an easel.

Romola gasped, her face flushing with shock. "That is Tito!"

Piero glanced around, offering a silent shrug of vexation at his own lapse in memory. While Romola continued to stare at the sketch in bewildered alarm, she turned to the painter with a mixture of confusion and dread.

"What a strange picture!" she exclaimed. "When did you paint this? What does it signify?"

"A mere whim," Piero dismissed, removing his skullcap and scratching his head while making a characteristic grimace to deflect deeper inquiry. "I needed a handsome young face, and your husband's was perfectly suited."

He moved toward the sketch, holding it with his back to Romola, feigning a casual inspection before attempting to set it aside as unworthy of display. But Romola, recalling the distinctive chain-mail armor she had seen on Tito, felt a chill run through her. The coincidence of the armor linking Tito to the concept of fear struck her deeply. Stepping close to the painter's elbow, she insisted, "Do not put it away; let me look again. That man with the rope around his neck—I saw him. I saw you approach him in the Duomo. What drove you to include him in a picture with Tito?"

Seeing no better path than partial honesty, Piero hesitated. "It was an accident. The man was fleeing—running up the steps—and grabbed onto your husband. Perhaps he stumbled. I happened to be present, saw the scene, and thought the savage-looking old fellow made a suitable subject. But it is worthless; merely a freakish daub."

With an air of finality, he moved the sketch away and placed it high on a shelf. "Come and view the Oedipus."

His excessive eagerness to conceal the sketch had achieved the opposite of his intent: it confirmed Romola's suspicion that there was something troubling regarding the encounter. Pride and delicacy, however, prevented her from pressing further; to question him might imply she harbored doubts about her husband's character. Instead, she spoke quietly, "He was a strange, pitiful-looking prisoner. Do you know anything else of him?"

"No more," Piero replied smoothly. "I merely showed him the way to the hospital. Now, observe the face of Oedipus; it is nearly complete. Tell me your thoughts."

For a long moment, Romola studied the portrait in silence. Finally, she turned to him with bright, tear-filled eyes. "Ah, you have achieved exactly what I desired. You have captured the listening look. My dear Piero, I am deeply grateful."

"Now, that is precisely what I cannot endure in women," Piero snapped, kicking aside clutter on the floor. "You pour forth emotions where none are warranted. Why should you thank me for a painting you paid for, especially when I make you wait? If I paint, surely it is for my own pleasure and credit to execute it well. Should you thank a man for not being a rogue or a fool? It suffices if he thanks God for granting him such virtue. Women imagine walls are held together with honey."

"You crusty Piero! I forgot how irritable you can be," Romola smiled, wiping away tears as she offered him a crisp sweetmeat from the basket.

Piero accepted the treat with the grace of a bear dreaming of pears—he genuinely enjoyed the sweetness despite his rough exterior, accustomed to hiding both his joys and sorrows behind a shaggy coat. "It is excellent, Madonna Antigone," he remarked, reaching for another. He had subsisted on hard eggs for two weeks.

Standing opposite him, Romola felt her mounting anxiety momentarily suspended by this naive enjoyment. Soon, however, she prepared to leave. "Good-bye, Piero," she said, placing the empty basket down. "I promise not to thank you if you finish the portrait promptly and well. In that case, I shall remind you that you were bound to do it for your own reputation."

"Good," Piero replied curtly, assisting her neatly with her mantle and veil.

Once the door clicked shut behind her, Piero breathed a sigh of relief. "I am glad she asked no more questions about that sketch," he thought. "I would be distressed if she guessed I considered her fine husband a fitting model for a coward. But I treated it lightly; she will not dwell on it."

Yet, Piero proved too sanguine, falling prey to the common error of open-hearted men attempting subtle deception. As Romola walked home, the image of the sketch weighed heavily on her mind. She could not reconcile the facts: the chain-armour and the encounter described by Piero, both occurring on the morning the armor was acquired. Had the painter witnessed the look of terror on Tito's face? What did it all signify?

"It means nothing," she whispered to herself, trying to steady her resolve. "It was merely a coincidence. Shall I ask Tito about it?"

Her reason finally prevailed: "No. I will not question him about anything he has not voluntarily shared. To do so would be an offense against the trust I owe him."

But her heart countered, trembling with fear: "I dare not ask him."

There existed a terrible fracture in their bond. Like a person clutching something precious, terrified that it might be broken, she froze, unable to move hastily toward the truth.
