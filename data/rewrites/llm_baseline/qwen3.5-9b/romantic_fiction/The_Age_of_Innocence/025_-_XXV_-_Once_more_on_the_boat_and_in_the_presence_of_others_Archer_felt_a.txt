Once more aboard the boat, and once again surrounded by the indifferent crowd, Archer experienced a profound tranquility that both astonished and steadied him. By any conventional measure, the day had been a ludicrous failure; he had not so much as kissed Madame Olenska's hand nor drawn a single word from her promising future encounters. Yet, for a man consumed by unrequited love and facing an indefinite separation from his passion, he felt strangely, almost humbly, calm and comforted.

It was the exquisite equilibrium she had maintained between their mutual loyalties to others and their honesty with themselves that had so deeply moved—and now so pacified—him. This balance was not the product of cold calculation, as her tears and hesitant pauses had revealed, but flowed naturally from her unashamed sincerity. Now that the immediate peril had passed, it filled him with a tender awe, prompting him to thank fate that no vanity, no desire to perform before sophisticated observers, had ever tempted him to lead her astray.

Even after they had clasped hands in farewell at the Fall River station and he had turned away into solitude, the conviction remained that he had salvaged far more from their meeting than he had lost. He wandered back to the club and retreated to the empty library, replaying every fleeting second of their time together in his mind. Clarity sharpened with scrutiny: if she ultimately decided to return to Europe—to return to her husband—it would not be because her old life enticed her, even under the new conditions proposed. No, she would leave only if she sensed she was becoming a temptation to Archer, a lure pulling him away from the moral standard they had jointly erected. Her choice would be to remain close to him as long as he did not invite her closer; it fell entirely to him to keep her exactly there: safe, yet secluded.

These thoughts accompanied him into the train ride. They enveloped him in a golden haze, rendering the faces around him remote and indistinct. He felt that if he attempted to speak to his fellow travelers, they would fail to comprehend his meaning. Lost in this state of abstraction, he woke the following morning to the stifling reality of a September day in New York. The heat-withered faces streaming past in the long train car blurred through the same golden filter, until suddenly, as he stepped off the station platform, one face detached itself, pressing insistently into his consciousness.

It was the face of the young man he had noticed the previous day leaving the Parker House—a figure who did not fit the mold, lacking the typical "American hotel" visage. The same impression struck him anew, stirring a vague resonance of prior associations. The young man stood surveying his surroundings with the dazed air of a foreigner cast adrift amidst the harsh realities of American transit. Then, he approached Archer, tipped his hat, and spoke in English: "Surely, Monsieur, we met in London?"

"To be sure! In London!" Archer responded, seizing his hand with a mixture of curiosity and sympathy. "So you *did* arrive, after all?" he exclaimed, casting a searching glance at the sharp, haggard little countenance of the French tutor accompanying the young Count Carfry.

"Oh, I arrived—yes," M. Rivière smiled, though his lips were drawn tight. "But not for long; I depart the day after tomorrow." He clutched his light valise in one neatly gloved hand, gazing into Archer's face with an expression of anxiety, perplexity, and almost desperate appeal. "I wonder, Monsieur, since fate has allowed us to cross paths, if I might..."

"I was just about to suggest it," Archer interrupted warmly. "Come to luncheon. Downtown, I mean. If you care to visit my office, I will take you to a very respectable restaurant in that district."

M. Rivière was visibly touched and surprised. "You are too kind. But I was merely intending to ask if you could direct me to some form of transportation. There are no porters, and no one here seems willing to listen—"

"I understand; our American stations can be bewildering. When you ask for a porter, they often offer you chewing gum instead. But if you will follow, I will extricate you. And you truly must lunch with me, you know."

After a barely perceptible hesitation, the young man replied with profuse gratitude, though his tone lacked full conviction, stating he was already engaged. However, once they reached the relative safety of the street, he asked if he might call later that afternoon. Archer, relaxed in the mid-summer leisure of his office, fixed an appointment and scrawled his address. The Frenchman pocketed the paper with reiterated thanks and a sweeping flourish of his hat before boarding a horse-car, leaving Archer to walk away.

Punctual to the hour, M. Rivière appeared—shaved, pressed, yet undeniably drawn and grave. Archer was alone in his office, and before accepting the seat offered, the young man began abruptly: "I believe I saw you, sir, yesterday in Boston."

The statement seemed trivial, and Archer prepared to agree, but his response was halted by something mysterious yet illuminating in the visitor's intense gaze. "It is extraordinary, very extraordinary," M. Rivière continued, "that we should have met under the circumstances in which I currently find myself."

"What circumstances?" Archer asked, wondering somewhat crudely if the young man sought financial aid.

M. Rivière studied him with tentative eyes. "I have come not to seek employment, as I mentioned previously, but on a special mission—"

"Ah!" Archer exclaimed. In an instant, the two recent encounters clicked together in his mind. He paused to absorb the situation, suddenly illuminated, while M. Rivière remained silent, seemingly aware that his admission was sufficient.

"A special mission," Archer repeated slowly.

The young Frenchman opened his palms, raising them slightly, and the two men stared across the office desk until Archer rallied himself to say, "Do sit down." M. Rivière bowed, took a distant chair, and waited.

"It was regarding this mission that you wished to consult me?" Archer finally asked.

M. Rivière nodded. "Not in my own interest; on that score, I—I have fully reconciled myself. I should like—if I may—speak to you concerning the Countess Olenska."

Archer had suspected the topic for the last few minutes, but hearing it spoken aloud sent blood rushing to his temples as if he had been trapped by a bending branch in a thicket. "And on whose behalf," he demanded, "do you wish to act?"

M. Rivière met the question squarely. "Well—I might say *hers*, if that did not sound presumptuous. Should I say instead: on behalf of abstract justice?"

Archer regarded him with ironic detachment. "In other words, you are Count Olenski's messenger?" He observed the darker reflection of shame on M. Rivière's sallow face. "Not to *you*, Monsieur. If I approach you, it is on quite different grounds."

"What right do you possess, given the circumstances, to stand on any other ground?" Archer retorted sharply. "If you are an emissary, you are an emissary."

The young man considered. "My mission is concluded: as far as the Countess Olenska is concerned, it has failed."

"I cannot help that," Archer rejoined on the same note of irony.

"No—but you can help," M. Rivière interjected, pausing to turn his hat over in his still-carefully gloved hands, peering into its lining before fixing Archer's gaze again. "You can help, Monsieur, I am convinced, ensure it fails equally with her family."

Archer pushed back his chair and stood. "Well—and by God, I will!" he shouted. He stood with his hands in his pockets, glaring down wrathfully at the diminutive Frenchman, whose face, though he had risen, remained an inch or two below Archer's eye level.

M. Rivière paled to his natural hue; indeed, his complexion could scarcely grow paler. "Why the devil," Archer exploded, "should you have assumed—that I suppose you are appealing to me based on my connection to Madame Olenska—that I would hold a view contrary to the rest of her family?"

For a moment, the shift in M. Rivière's expression was his only reply. His look transformed from timidity to absolute distress; for a young man of his usual resourcefulness, it would have been difficult to appear more disarmed and defenseless.

"Oh, Monsieur—"

"I cannot imagine," Archer continued, "why you chose to come to me when there are others so much closer to the Countess; still less why you believed I would be more receptive to the arguments I assume you were sent to deliver."

M. Rivière absorbed this assault with a disarming humility. "The arguments I intend to present to you, Monsieur, are my own, not those I was sent to convey."

"Then I perceive even less reason to listen to them."

M. Rivière looked into his hat again, as if weighing whether these final words constituted a broad enough hint to don his hat and depart. Then, with sudden resolve, he spoke. "Monsieur—will you tell me one thing? Is it my right to be here that you question? Or do you perhaps believe the entire matter is already closed?"

His quiet persistence made Archer feel the clumsiness of his own bluster. M. Rivière had successfully imposed himself; Archer, flushing slightly, sank back into his chair and signaled for the young man to sit.

"I beg your pardon: but why is the matter not closed?"
