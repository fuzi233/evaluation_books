It followed a script written in stone every single time.

On the night of her annual ball, Mrs. Julius Beaufort never missed a performance at the Opera. In fact, she deliberately scheduled her grandest evening parties to coincide with an Opera night, sending a clear, unspoken message to her circle: she was above domestic drudgery, entirely liberated from household cares, and surrounded by a retinue of servants so capable they could orchestrate a feast of epic proportions while she remained unseen.

Their townhouse was a fortress of status, one of the few remaining in New York to boast a true ballroom—a space that predated the opulent halls of the Mansons and the Headlys. While the fashion was shifting toward "provincial" pragmatism, where carpets covered drawing-room floors and furniture was shoved upstairs, the Beauforts stood defiant. Their ballroom, shut tight for three hundred and sixty-five days a year, sat in a silence broken only by the dust motes dancing in the dark. Gilt chairs were stacked like soldiers in a corner, and the massive chandelier slept inside its protective sack. Yet, this sheer, undeniable excess compensated perfectly for any shadow in the Beaufort past.

Mrs. Archer, a woman who loved distilling social truths into sharp axioms, once mused, "We all have our pet common people." Though the phrase was bold, it was whispered in hushed tones within many exclusive hearts. And yet, the Beauforts were not merely common; some whispered they were worse. Mrs. Beaufort, however, hailed from one of America's most venerated lines. She was the former Regina Dallas, a Southern beauty of legendary charm, introduced to the city by her cousin, Medora Manson—a woman whose motives were noble, but whose actions were consistently disastrous. One born into the Mansons and the Rushworths held a *droit de cité*, a birthright to the social stage, as noted by Mr. Sillerton Jackson, who had known the Tuileries intimately. But did one truly forfeit that crown by marrying Julius Beaufort?

Who, exactly, was Mr. Beaufort? To the world, he was an English gentleman, agreeable, handsome, witty, and hospitable. He had arrived in America with glowing recommendations from Old Mrs. Manson Mingott's son-in-law, a banker, and swiftly carved out a formidable position in the world of affairs. But behind the veneer lay a man of dissipated habits, a bitter tongue, and a history shrouded in mystery. When Medora announced her cousin's engagement to him, it was dismissed as yet another reckless stumble in her tragic chronicle.

Yet, folly often births miracles as surely as wisdom does. Two years after their marriage, it became undeniable that Mrs. Beaufort presided over the most distinguished house in New York. The mechanics of this transformation remained a closely guarded secret. She was indolent, passive, and occasionally described as dull, even by those who called themselves caustic. Yet, draped in pearls, growing younger, blonder, and more radiant with each passing season, she sat enthroned in Mr. Beaufort's heavy brownstone palace. She commanded the entire galaxy of society without lifting a jeweled finger.

Those who read between the lines claimed it was Mr. Beaufort himself who ran the show. Perhaps he instructed the staff, taught the chef new recipes, directed the gardeners on which exotic blooms to cultivate for the dinner table, curated the guest list, mixed the punch, and penned the intimate notes his wife sent to her friends. If this were true, these tasks were performed with invisible hands. To the public eye, he remained the picture of a careless, wealthy bachelor, strolling into his drawing room with the detached amusement of a host who happens to be visiting his own home, exclaiming, "My wife's gloxinias are a marvel, aren't they? I believe she sourced them directly from Kew."

The consensus was absolute: Mr. Beaufort's genius lay in execution. It was easy to whisper rumors about his origins, suggesting he had been aided to escape England by a powerful international bank, but he swept those whispers aside as lightly as the others. Though New York's business conscience was as prickly as its moral code, Mr. Beaufort moved forward unimpeded. He dragged the entire city into his drawing rooms, and for over twenty years, visitors flocked to the Beauforts with the same unshakeable confidence they reserved for the Mansons, enjoying the promise of roasted canvas-back ducks and vintage wines rather than lukewarm champagne and reheated croquettes from the city.

Thus, as expected, Mrs. Beaufort took her seat in her box moments before the Jewel Song. And when, as tradition dictated, she rose at the close of the third act, wrapped her opera cloak around her shoulders, and vanished, New York knew the signal: the ball would commence within the hour.

The Beaufort mansion was a shrine that New Yorkers proudly displayed to foreigners, particularly on ball nights. They had been pioneers in owning their own scarlet velvet carpet, having it unrolled down the steps by their own lacquered-footed servants beneath their own awnings, rather than renting it as part of a package deal. They had also innovated the custom of allowing ladies to remove their wraps in the foyer, sparing them the indignity of scurrying to the hostess's private chambers to curl their hair under the gaslight; Mr. Beaufort was rumored to have remarked that he assumed all his wife's acquaintances had maids to ensure their hair was perfect before they left home.

Furthermore, the house itself was boldly designed around the concept of the ball. Instead of navigating a cramped hallway to reach the festivities, as at the Chiverses', guests marched solemnly down a vista of enfiladed drawing rooms—the sea-green salon, the crimson chamber, the *bouton d'or*—gazing from afar at the reflection of many-candle chandeliers in the polished parquet, and beyond that, the lush, shadowed depth of a conservatory where camellias and tree ferns arched their expensive greenery over seats of black and gold bamboo.

Newland Archer, fitting the role of a young man of his station, strolled in somewhat late. He had left his coat with the silk-stockinged footmen—a peculiar vanity of Mr. Beaufort's—and dawdled in the library, a cavern hung with Spanish leather and furnished with Buhl and malachite, where a few men were chatting and donning their dancing gloves. Finally, he joined the line of guests awaiting reception on the threshold of the crimson drawing room.

Archer was distinctly nervous. Unlike the carefree "young bloods" who typically retreated to their clubs after the Opera, he had walked several miles up Fifth Avenue on the cool night air before turning back toward the Beauforts'. His anxiety stemmed from a singular fear: that the Mingotts might be pushing too far. There was a chilling possibility that Granny Mingott had given orders to escort the Countess Olenska to the ball.

Recalling the gravity of the situation in the club box, he realized how terrible a mistake that would be. Although he was more determined than ever to "see the thing through," his chivalrous eagerness to champion his betrothed's cousin had dimmed since their brief exchange at the Opera.

Drifting into the *bouton d'or* drawing room, where Mr. Beaufort had the audacity to hang Bouguereau's controversial nude, *Love Victorious*, Archer found Mrs. Welland and her daughter standing near the entrance to the ballroom. Beyond the open doors, couples were already gliding across the floor. The light of wax candles illuminated swirling tulle skirts, girlish heads wreathed in modest blossoms, the dashing plumes (*aigrettes*) and ornaments of the young wives' coiffures, and the gleam of highly glazed shirt fronts and fresh *glacé* gloves.

Miss Welland, poised to enter the dance, lingered on the threshold, holding a sprig of lilies-of-the-valley—she carried no other bouquet. Her face was faintly pale, but her eyes burned with a candid excitement. A cluster of young men and girls swirled around her, exchanging handshakes, laughter, and pleasantries. Mrs. Welland, standing slightly apart, cast a beam of qualified approval upon the scene. It was evident that Miss Welland was announcing her engagement, while her mother maintained an air of the parental reluctance deemed appropriate for such occasions.

Archer paused. This announcement had been his express wish, yet it was not the manner in which he would have preferred to unveil his happiness. To declare it amidst the clamor and heat of a crowded ballroom robbed it of the delicate bloom of privacy that things nearest the heart deserved. His joy was so profound that this blurring of the surface left its essence untouched, but he had wanted the surface, too, to remain pure. He found a small comfort in realizing that May Welland shared this sentiment. Her eyes fled to his with a beseeching look, conveying a silent plea: *Remember, we are doing this because it is right.*

No appeal could have found a quicker resonance in Archer's breast. He wished, however, that the necessity of their action had been driven by some ideal principle, rather than simply by the plight of poor Ellen Olenska. The group surrounding Miss Welland parted for him with significant smiles. After accepting his share of congratulations, he drew his betrothed into the center of the ballroom and placed his arm around her waist.
