X. Bereaved

It had been nearly ten months since we last saw him, yet in that time he seemed to have aged a decade. The man who once carried himself with quiet dignity now moved with a strained stillness, his frame leaner, his face etched with shadows of sorrow and something darker—anger, perhaps, or grief so deep it had turned to rage. His dark blue eyes, once warm and steady, now burned with an unnerving intensity beneath thick, grey brows. There was no trace of the calm composure that had once defined him; instead, a haunted fury clung to every line of his face. This was not merely the toll of mourning—it was as though some deeper, more terrible force had cracked his soul open.

We had resumed our ride in silence for a while, but soon the General broke it, speaking with the blunt precision of a soldier long accustomed to facing truth without flinching. He spoke of his loss—the death of his beloved niece and ward—and the words came out like a wound reopened. Then, abruptly, his voice rose, thick with venom. He cursed the “hellish arts” that had taken her life, railing against the heavens for allowing such evil to flourish unchecked. He spoke of demons, of ancient pacts, of sins so monstrous they defied reason. His tone was less prayer than accusation.

My father, sensing the weight behind these words, gently asked if he might hear the full story—provided it did not cause further pain.

“I would tell you everything,” the General replied, “but you wouldn’t believe me.”

“Why not?” my father pressed.

“Because,” the General snapped, “you only accept what fits your narrow view of the world. I once thought as you do. But I’ve seen things. Things that shatter every notion I ever held.”

“Then try me,” my father said quietly. “I may not be as rigid as you think. And you’ve always demanded proof before believing—so I’ll listen, not just because I’m curious, but because I respect your judgment.”

The General exhaled sharply, as if relieved and burdened at once. “You’re right. I don’t leap into belief lightly. What I’ve come to know is beyond belief. It’s *unthinkable*. Yet I’ve seen it. Felt it. Lived through it. I’ve been deceived by a conspiracy older than memory—something supernatural, abominable. A horror wrapped in silence.”

At this, I caught my father’s glance—a flicker of doubt, a hesitation. Not disbelief, but wariness. He looked at the General, not with pity, but with a sharp, assessing gaze. The General didn’t notice. He stared ahead, fixed on the trees ahead, as if searching for signs of something hidden in the woods.

“You’re going to the Ruins of Karnstein?” he asked suddenly.

“Yes,” my father replied. “And it’s fortunate—I’d planned to ask you to accompany me there. I want to examine them closely. There’s a ruined chapel, isn’t there? With tombs—many of them belonging to the old Karnstein family?”

“Indeed there are,” the General said. “Fascinating ruins. You’re thinking of claiming the title and lands?”

My father smiled faintly, trying to lighten the mood. But the General didn’t smile back. He remained grim, almost fierce, lost in thoughts too dark for jest.

“No,” he said, low and hard. “Something far different. I intend to dig up those graves. To unearth the dead. By God’s mercy, I hope to perform a sacred desecration—one that will rid the world of monsters. Let honest people sleep again, unafraid. I have things to tell you, my friend—things I myself would have laughed at just months ago.”

My father looked at him again—not with suspicion now, but with sudden, piercing alertness. His eyes narrowed, not in doubt, but in dread.

“The house of Karnstein,” he said slowly, “has been extinct for over a century. My wife’s mother was descended from them, but the name died out long ago. The castle is nothing but stone and shadow. The village is abandoned—no smoke has risen from its chimneys in fifty years. Not a roof remains standing.”

“True,” the General said. “But I’ve learned much since last we met—things that will shock you. Better to tell it all in order.”

He paused, then added, “You saw my dear ward—my child, I could call her. No one more beautiful. Only three months ago, she was radiant with life.”

“Yes,” my father murmured, voice softening. “She was lovely. I grieved deeply when I heard. I knew how much she meant to you.”

They clasped hands briefly, a gesture of shared sorrow. The General’s eyes welled with tears, which he did not hide. “We’ve been friends long enough,” he said, voice breaking. “I knew you’d understand. She was everything to me—her love made my home warm, my life meaningful. Now it’s gone. The years left to me may be few—but I swear, by God’s grace, I’ll see justice done. I’ll make the fiends pay for stealing her from me—when she was still young, still full of promise!”

My father nodded, solemn. “You said you’d tell the story from the beginning. Please do. I assure you, I’m not asking out of idle curiosity.”

By then, we had reached the fork where the Drunstall road split from ours. The General glanced forward, tense.

“How far to the ruins?” he asked.

“About half a league,” my father answered.

“Then let us hear it,” the General said. “Now.”
