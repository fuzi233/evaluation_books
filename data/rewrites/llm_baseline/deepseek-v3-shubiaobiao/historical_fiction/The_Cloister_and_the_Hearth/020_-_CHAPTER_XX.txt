The courage of ordinary men, like their skill, moves within narrow bounds. Venture but a step beyond, and they falter. Martin Wittenhaagen was steadfast in the face of familiar perils—those he had known in his rough, soldier’s life. But this? A bloodhound on his trail? That was a terror beyond his reckoning. The beast’s relentless instinct, unshaken by human cunning, seemed almost supernatural. After a few strides, he leaned heavily on his bow, his spirit draining away like water from a cracked vessel.

Gerard, whose fear had not yet hardened into despair, urged him forward. "Why stop? We must run!"

"What use?" Martin muttered. "If we escape the wood, they’ll cut us down like dogs. Better to stand here—find a place where we can sell our lives dear."

Margaret, though pale, clutched Martin’s arm. "Then let us part ways! Gerard’s life is the one they seek—let them chase us, and he can slip free!"

Martin shook his head. "You don’t know the beast. It doesn’t track a man—it tracks blood. They’ve led it to where Ghysbrecht fell, and from his blood to the hand that spilled it, that devil will follow, even if Gerard swam the Meuse or hid in an army."

The hound’s deep bay echoed through the trees—a sound both beautiful and terrible, like the emerald-eyed serpent that lures its prey before the strike. Martin stood frozen, his courage undone by the unknown. Gerard, too, felt the dread seep into his bones. Had Heaven itself marked him for vengeance?

But Margaret, though trembling, thought faster than either man. "Give me your knife," she said. Martin handed it over. "Little use in your hands," he muttered.

Then, swift and silent, she stepped behind Gerard, drew the blade across her arm, and smeared her shoes and stockings with her own blood. Neither man saw.

Seizing Martin’s wrist, she hissed, "Be a man! Lead us to thick cover—where numbers won’t save them."

Martin led them sullenly to a hazel thicket. "Here. We’ll wait. When they come through, we’ll cut down as many as we can before they finish us."

"Is that your only plan?" Gerard snapped.

"It’s all that’s left."

"Then follow me." Gerard slashed at the hazel shoots, twisting them behind him as he moved. Martin did the same, but his hands were heavy with despair.

The baying grew louder, nearer. Then—hoofbeats? No. Mule’s steps. Ghysbrecht himself, broken-nosed but relentless, was hunting them down.

"They’re in the wood," Martin whispered.

Gerard’s plan was set. "Martin, take your bow. Hide here. I’ll draw the hounds to that oak—when they break cover, shoot. I’ll brain the rest."

Martin’s eyes kindled. They took their positions.

Then—Margaret’s hand on Gerard’s shoulder. "Martin says if we’re separated, make for the high ash-tree."

Before he could answer, a massive hound burst from the thicket, nostrils flaring. It never saw her—scent was its world. With a snarl, it lunged for Gerard’s tree—and fell dead, Martin’s arrow through its ribs.

A second hound came, sniffed its fallen kin—then a white streak (Martin’s last arrow) sent it howling into the dirt.

Now the wood rustled with men. Gerard slipped back into the brush just as three pursuers broke through. He crawled, silent as a fox, while Martin and Margaret did the same.

A shout—the dead hounds found. Voices clamored, wasting precious moments.

Margaret trembled. "They’ll kill him, and I’m not there!"

"Hush," Martin growled. "He’s cleverer than they are. To the ash-tree—but quietly."

They neared the edge of the wood—then froze. Ghysbrecht himself sat on his mule, bandaged but watchful, blocking their escape.

Martin’s bow creaked as he drew his last arrow. Margaret covered her face—but peered through her fingers.

Then—a figure dropped from above like a hawk. A kerchief whipped over Ghysbrecht’s head, and he crashed to the ground, groaning. Gerard stood over him.

"Quick! The mule!"

Martin mounted, Gerard lifted Margaret into the saddle—then Ghysbrecht writhed free, shrieking, "Treason! Murder!"

Gerard stomped his throat silent. "Ride!"

But too late—Dierich Brower and five men burst from the trees, roaring.

The chase was not over yet.
