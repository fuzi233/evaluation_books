"A spade! a rake! a hoe! A pickaxe or a bill! A hook to reap, or a scythe to mow, A flail or what ye will— And here's a ready hand To ply the needful tool, And skill'd enough, by lessons rough, In Labour's rugged school." — Hood.

Higgins's door remained locked the following day when Margaret and her father attempted to pay their call on the widow Boucher. However, an officious neighbor informed them that Higgins was actually away on business of some sort. Before departing, he had indeed visited Mrs. Boucher, though the encounter proved deeply unsatisfactory. The widow viewed herself as a grievously wronged woman due to her husband's suicide, a sentiment containing enough truth to make refutation nearly impossible. Nevertheless, it was distressing to witness how entirely her thoughts were consumed by her own plight and status. This selfishness even permeated her relationship with her children, whom she regarded merely as burdens despite possessing a somewhat animalistic affection for them.

Margaret endeavored to connect with one or two of the children, while Mr. Hale struggled to elevate the widow's thoughts above mere helpless querulousness. He discovered that the children were far truer and simpler mourners than their mother. "Daddy" had been kind to them; each could recount, in an eager, stammering voice, instances of tenderness or indulgence shown by the lost father. "Is yon thing upstairs really him?" one asked timidly. "It doesn't look like him. I'm feared on it, and I never was feared o' daddy." Margaret's heart bled to learn that the mother, in her desperate demand for sympathy, had dragged her children upstairs to gaze upon their dead, disfigured father. It was a cruel intermingling of coarse horror with the profundity of natural grief. She tried to redirect their minds toward practical matters: what they could do for their mother, or, phrased differently, what their father would have wished of them.

Margaret proved more successful than Mr. Hale in these efforts. Seeing their small duties lay within reach, the children began attempting to tidy the slatternly room under her guidance. Yet, Mr. Hale set a standard too high and offered a view too abstract for the indolent invalid. He could not rouse her torpid mind to imagine her husband's misery prior to his final, terrible step; she could only perceive the tragedy through the lens of her own suffering. Unable to comprehend the enduring mercy of God who had not intervened to save her prostrate husband from drowning, she simultaneously blamed her husband for falling into such drear despair, denied him any excuse for his rash act, yet remained inveterate in abusing anyone who might have driven him to such desperation.

She listed the masters—Mr. Thornton in particular, whose mill had been attacked by Boucher and who had subsequently withdrawn the warrant for his arrest on charges of rioting—the Union, of which Higgins was a representative, the numerous, hungry, and noisy children. To the widow, these constituted a single great army of personal enemies responsible for her current state as a helpless widow. Margaret heard enough of this unreasonableness to become thoroughly disheartened, finding it impossible to comfort her father upon their return.

"It is the town life," Margaret observed. "Their nerves are quickened by the haste and bustle and speed of everything around them, to say nothing of the confinement in these pent-up houses, which of itself induces depression and worry of spirits. In the country, people live so much more outdoors, even the children, even in winter."

"But people must live in towns," Mr. Hale replied. "And in the country, some develop such stagnant habits of mind that they become almost fatalists."

"Yes," Margaret acknowledged. "Each mode of life produces its own trials and temptations. The dweller in towns finds it as difficult to be patient as the country-bred man finds it to be active and equal to unwonted emergencies. Both struggle to realize a future: the urbanite because the present is so living, hurrying, and close around him; the rural inhabitant because his life tempts him to revel in mere animal existence, unaware of, and unconcerned with, any pungent pleasure attainable through planning and self-denial."

"And thus, both the necessity for engrossment and the stupid contentment in the present produce similar effects. But this poor Mrs. Boucher! How little we can do for her!"

"And yet we dare not leave her without effort, though it may seem futile. Oh, Papa! It's a hard world to live in!"

"So it is, my child. We feel it acutely just now, certainly; but we have known happiness even amidst our sorrow. What a pleasure Frederick's visit was!"

"Yes, it was," Margaret said brightly. "Such a charming, snatched, forbidden thing."

Suddenly, she stopped speaking. She had spoiled the memory of Frederick's visit through her own cowardice. Of all faults, she most despised in others the lack of bravery; the meanness of heart that leads to untruth. Here, she had been guilty of it! Then came the thought of Mr. Thornton's knowledge of her falsehood. She wondered if she would have minded detection half so much from anyone else. She mentally rehearsed confessing to her Aunt Shaw, Edith, her father, Captain and Mr. Lennox, and finally, Frederick. The thought of the last knowing what she had done, even in his own behalf, was the most painful, considering the fresh flush of mutual regard between brother and sister. Yet, even a fall in Frederick's opinion paled before the shame, the shrinking shame, she felt at the prospect of meeting Mr. Thornton again.

Still, she longed to see him, to get it over, to understand where she stood in his estimation. Her cheeks burned as she recalled how proudly she had previously implied an objection to trade, citing the deceit inherent in passing off inferior goods or assuming credit for nonexistent wealth. She remembered Mr. Thornton's look of calm disdain, how in few words he conveyed that in the great scheme of commerce, all dishonest methods inevitably proved injurious in the long run. Testing such actions by the poor standard of success revealed folly rather than wisdom; every form of deceit in trade, as in other things, was condemned. She remembered herself, then strong in her own untempted truth, asking if buying cheapest and selling dearest lacked the transparent justice intimately connected with truth. She had used the word *chivalric*, and her father had corrected her with the higher term *Christian*, drawing the argument onto himself while she sat silent beside him with a slight feeling of contempt.

*No more contempt for her! No more talk about the chivalric!* Henceforth, she must feel humiliated and disgraced in his sight. But when would she see him? Her heart leaped with apprehension at every ring of the doorbell, yet when silence returned, she felt strangely saddened and sick at heart at each disappointment.

It was evident her father expected to see him and was surprised by his absence. The truth was, there were points in their previous conversation they had no time to expand upon, but it had been understood that if possible, the following evening—or at least the first evening Mr. Thornton could spare—they should meet for further discussion. Mr. Hale had looked forward to this meeting eagerly since they parted. Having not yet resumed instruction to his pupils, which he had ceased at the onset of his wife's serious illness, he had fewer occupations than usual; the intense interest generated by Boucher's suicide had driven him back with renewed vigor to his speculations. He was restless all evening, muttering, "I quite expected to have seen Mr. Thornton. I think the messenger who brought the book last night must have had some note and forgot to deliver it. Do you think there has been any message left today?"

"I will go and inquire, Papa," Margaret said.

"Stay, there's a ring!" she exclaimed, sitting down instantly and bending her head attentively over her work. She heard a step on the stairs, but it was only one, and she knew it was Dixon's. Lifting her head, she sighed, believing she felt glad.

"It's that Higgins, sir. He wants to see you, or else Miss Hale. Or it might be Miss Hale first, and then you, sir; for he's in a strange kind of way."

"He had better come up here, Dixon; then he can see us both and choose which he likes for his listener."

"Oh! Very well, sir. I've no wish to hear what he's got to say, I'm sure; only, if you could see his shoes, I'm sure you'd say the kitchen was the fitter place."

"He can wipe them, I suppose," Mr. Hale said.

Dixon flung off to bid him walk upstairs. Margaret was slightly mollified when Higgins looked at his feet with a hesitating air. Sitting down on the bottom stair, he removed the offending shoes and, without a word, walked upstairs.

"Sarvant, sir!" he said, slicking his hair down upon entering the room. "If hoo'l excuse me (glancing at Margaret) for being i' my stockings; I've been tramping a' day, and streets is none o' th' cleanest."

Margaret thought fatigue might account for the change in his manner; he was unusually quiet and subdued, evidently struggling to articulate why he had come. Mr. Hale's ever-ready sympathy for shyness or hesitation prompted him to assist.
