The next morning dawned with all the solemnity of a court trial—except that the only judge present was a man who’d once commanded troops in India and now found himself locked in a battle of wits with his own valet.

Pendennis’s shutters were drawn back by Morgan, who arrived as usual—dressed like a man who’d just walked out of a portrait painted by someone who didn’t like him much. He carried clothes, water cans, and a silver dressing case so polished it could’ve been used to start a fire. The old major lay in bed, half-awake, half-irritated, like a lion who’d just realized he’d been served breakfast by a monkey.

“It’s you, is it?” he grumbled, squinting at Morgan through the haze of early morning and bad decisions. “I shan’t take you back again, you understand.”

“I ain’t the least bit interested in being taken back,” Morgan replied, straightening his cravat with the dignity of a man about to deliver a eulogy at his own funeral. “Nor serving you nor any man. But seeing as you’re still in my house, and I want you to be comfortable—until you leave—I thought I’d come up and do what’s necessary.”

And so, for the last time, Mr. James Morgan laid out the razor, adjusted the comb, and placed the toothbrush where only a gentleman would expect it—next to the soap, not under the sink. Then, with a gravity that might have made a bishop blush, he turned to the major.

“Thinking you might need a respectable fellow until you sorted yourself out,” Morgan said, “I spoke to a young man last night. He’s here.”

“Indeed,” said the major, from beneath his sheets like a king inspecting a new servant. “And who might this paragon be?”

“He’s been in the first families,” Morgan declared, puffing slightly. “I can vouch for his respectability. Lord De la Pole gave him to his nephew, young Lord Cubley. He went abroad with him, but refused to go to Scotland—his chest is delicate, you see—and so he’s free to serve you or not, as you please.”

“You’re monstrously polite,” grinned the major, clearly enjoying the absurdity of the situation.

And indeed, Morgan had gone to his club—the Wheel of Fortune—after the previous night’s drama, found Frosch (a courier and valet fresh off a foreign tour), and told him: “I’ve had a devil of a blow-up with my employer. I’m retiring from the business altogether. If you want a temporary job, apply at Bury Street.” And thus, Frosch was born into the Pendennis household.

“Very polite,” said the major, nodding. “Your recommendation will carry immense weight.”

Morgan blushed. He knew the major was teasing him—‘a-chaffin’ of him,’ as he put it in his head. But he stood tall. “He has served you before, sir,” he said proudly. “Lord De la Pole gave him to his nephew. He’s been on tour. He doesn’t wish to go to Fitzurse Castle—cold weather, delicate chest. So he’s available.”

“Ah,” said the major, “you’re quite the diplomat. Come in, Frosch—you’ll do very well. Mr. Morgan, would you kindly—”

“I shall show him what’s necessary,” Morgan interrupted, already stepping forward. “What’s customary for you to have done. Will you take breakfast here or at the Club, Major Pendennis?”

“Here,” said the major. “Then we’ll make our arrangements afterward.”

“If you please, sir.”

“And now,” said the major, “would you oblige me by leaving the room?”

Morgan withdrew, seething. The major’s politeness was worse than insults. It was like being insulted by a statue.

While the old gentleman performed his mysterious toilette—applying cologne like a general preparing for battle—we, too, shall retire with modesty and discretion.

After breakfast, the major and his new aide-de-camp began packing. The bachelor’s life was simple. No unnecessary wardrobe. Just a Bible (his mother’s), a road book, Pen’s novel (bound in calf, because elegance matters), the Duke of Wellington’s despatches, and portraits of generals, sovereigns, and various other important people—mostly men who’d never met him but looked stern enough to impress guests.

His belongings fit neatly into the same boxes he’d brought fifteen years earlier. A maid named Betty—known as “Slavey” to Morgan—brought them down and dusted them with the reverence of someone cleaning a sacred relic.

Morgan watched her every move. He hadn’t spoken to Mrs. Brixham since the previous night’s threats, but his silence was louder than any shout. She trembled in the corner, waiting for the axe to fall.

Meanwhile, the major, cane in hand, supervised the packing. Papers were burned. Closets emptied. Doors flung open like they were confessing sins. All boxes closed—except one: the desk.

That’s when Morgan returned, books in hand.

“Since I wish to speak to you privately,” he said, “perhaps you’d ask Frosch to step downstairs?”

“Bring two cabs, Frosch,” said the major. “Wait downstairs until I ring.”

Frosch obeyed. Morgan watched him walk away, then produced his accounts.

Simple. Clear. Settled in minutes.

“And now,” said Morgan, pocketing the cheque and signing his name with a flourish, “now that our accounts are closed, I propose to speak to you as one man to another.”

Morgan liked the sound of his own voice. He often practiced speeches at the club or in the kitchen.

“I possess valuable information,” he announced.

“Of what nature?” asked the major.

“A marriage that isn’t a marriage,” Morgan said. “A baronet who isn’t married at all. His wife? Married to someone else. You know it, sir. I know it.”

The major understood instantly.

“Oh,” he said, raising an eyebrow. “So this is why you were listening at the door last night. I forgot to check the keyhole. Should’ve known there was a spy behind it.”

“I may have my schemes,” Morgan replied. “Just like you may have yours. I get information. I act on it. I profit from it. A poor servant can have luck, too. Don’t you try to play the aristocrat with me. That’s all gammon. I’m English. I’m as good as you.”

“To what the devil does this tend?” the major asked, standing up. “How does this concern me?”

“How does it concern *me*?” Morgan shot back. “How does it concern your nephew? His seat in Parliament? Bigamy charges? How does it concern *that*? Why should you be the only one with secrets? Why shouldn’t I get a cut? I found it out too. Make it worth my while, and I’ll keep quiet. Let Arthur take his seat. Let him marry the rich widow. I don’t want her. But I want my share. Five hundred a year. Easy. Pay me the first quarter now, and I’m as silent as a mouse. There’s your chequebook.”

“And there’s this,” said the major, pointing to a small double-barrelled pistol on his desk.

It belonged to the Indian commander-in-chief, a man who’d led armies and survived battles. It had seen action. It had killed men. It had also, apparently, sat in a drawer for fifteen years.

“One more word,” said the major, “and I’ll shoot you like a mad dog. Stop—by Jove, I’ll do it now. You’ll assault me? Strike an old man? Kneel down and say your prayers, sir. By God, you shall die.”

Morgan stared. For a moment, he looked terrified. Then—he shrieked.

“Murder! Police!”

To everyone’s surprise, the major wheeled the table aside, walked to the other window, and beckoned the policeman passing by.

“Come up here, policeman,” he said calmly. Then he turned to Morgan.

“You miserable sneak,” he said. “That pistol hasn’t been loaded in fifteen years. You’d have known that if you weren’t such a coward.”

The policeman entered, followed by Mrs. Brixham and her maid, who had been trying to close the door against the curious crowd gathering outside.

“I’ve discharged this drunken scoundrel,” the major announced. “He insulted and assaulted me. I took up a pistol. As you can see, it’s unloaded. This coward screamed before he was hurt. I’m charging him with theft. I want his trunks searched.”

“No!” roared Morgan. “You gave me those things! You gave them to me!”

The major laughed.

“We’ll see,” he said.

And the guilty valet remembered the lawn-fronted shirts, the gold-headed cane, the opera-glass—things he’d assumed ownership of, assuming no one would notice.

The policeman examined the trunks. Inside were cigars of dubious origin, jewels of even more dubious quality, and French prints that would’ve made any decent man blush.

Morgan looked like a man who’d just discovered his soul was on sale.

“He’ll smash me,” thought the major. “But I’ll crush him now.”

But then he saw Mrs. Brixham’s face—fearful, pleading. And he hesitated.

A man in prison might talk. And some secrets were better left buried.

“Stop,” he said. “Policeman. I’ll speak with him alone.”

“Do you give Mr. Morgan in charge?” asked the officer.

“I haven’t brought a charge yet,” the major replied, giving Morgan a look that said, *You’re not getting out of this.*

“Thank you, sir,” whispered Morgan.

“Go outside and wait,” the major said. “Now, Morgan. You played one game with me. And you lost. Though you had the best hand. But you’ve got to pay.”

“Yes, sir,” Morgan muttered.

“I only found out last week,” the major continued, “what kind of game you’ve been running. Young De Boots, of the Blues, recognized you. The man who came to barracks, doing business—one-third money, one-third eau-de-Cologne, one-third French prints. You confounded demure old sinner! I didn’t care what you took. But I took my shot—and hit the bullseye.”

“What do you want?” Morgan asked.

“My bills,” said the major. “In your leather pocket-book. You’ll burn Mrs. Brixham’s bill?”

“No!” growled Morgan. “I won’t part with my property.”

“You lent her sixty pounds five years ago,” said the major. “She’s paid you fifty pounds a year ever since. You have a bill of sale on her furniture and a note for a hundred and fifty pounds. She told me last night. You’ve bled that poor woman dry.”

“I won’t give it up,” Morgan insisted.

“Policeman!” called the major.

“You shall have the bill,” Morgan said, defeated.

“You’re not going to take money from me, and you a gentleman?” the major said.

“I shall want you directly,” he told the policeman, who left and returned shortly.

“No, my good sir,” the major said, “I have no desire for further financial dealings. But let us draw up a little paper. You’ll sign it. No—wait. You’ll write it. You’ve improved your handwriting lately. Sit down. Write: ‘Bury Street, St. James’s, October 21, 18—.’”

Morgan wrote.

“‘I, James Morgan, having come into the service of Arthur Pendennis, Esquire, acknowledge receipt of liberal wages and board for fifteen years.’ You can’t object to that.”

“During fifteen years,” Morgan wrote.

“‘In which time, by my own care and prudence, I have amassed sufficient funds to purchase the house in which my master resides, and to effect other savings. Among others, I may mention my tenant, Mrs. Brixham, who, in consideration of sixty pounds advanced five years ago, has paid me two hundred and fifty pounds, and given me a note of hand for one hundred and twenty pounds, which I hereby restore to her at the request of my late master, Major Arthur Pendennis, and thereby release her furniture from the bill of sale.’ Have you written?”

“I’m d——d if I sign it,” Morgan said.

“Too bad,” said the major. “It will happen whether you sign or not.”

“Let us continue,” he said. “‘And having, in return for my master’s kindness, stolen his property—now upstairs in my trunks—and having uttered falsehoods regarding his and other honourable families, I hereby express regret for these actions, declare myself unworthy of belief, and hope to amend my ways in the future. Signed, James Morgan.’”

“I won’t sign it,” Morgan said.

“My good man,” the major chuckled, “it will happen anyway. I won’t use this unless forced. Mrs. Brixham and the policeman will witness it. I’ll return her note. And you’ll confirm she’s quit of you.”

Frosch returned with the cab. The boxes were packed. The major left.

Mrs. Brixham, once Morgan was gone, rushed to thank him, tears in her eyes, a speech ready on her lips.

The major shook her hand, cut her off mid-sentence, and walked out.

From the parlour window, Morgan watched him go, spitting curses like a man who’d just lost his empire.

But the major wasn’t afraid. He strutted away with his cane, looking like a man who’d just won a war he didn’t know he was fighting.

Hours later, as Morgan sat on the doorstep, smoking a cigar (a treasonous act in the presence of the major), Arthur Pendennis arrived.

“My uncle out, I suppose, Morgan?” he asked.

“Major Pendennis is out, sir,” Morgan replied, bowing without touching his cap. “He left today. I no longer have the honour of serving him.”

“Indeed. Where is he?”

“I believe he’s taken temporary lodgings at Cox’s Hotel, in Jermyn Street.”

Pause.

“Are you in town long, sir? Are you in chambers? I should like the honour of calling on you. Perhaps you’d grant me a quarter of an hour?”

Arthur raised an eyebrow. “Do you want my uncle to take you back?”

“I want no such thing,” Morgan snapped. “I’d see him—”

He stopped. Glared. Then softened.

“No, sir. Thank you. I only wish to speak with you. On business. Perhaps you’d walk into my house?”

“If it’s only a minute or two,” said Arthur, thinking, *He wants me to patronize him.* “I’ll listen.”

And so, he entered.

A card already hung in the window: **APARTMENTS TO LET.**

Morgan led him to the dining room, offered a chair, sat down, and began to speak.

Of course, the reader already knows what he said.
