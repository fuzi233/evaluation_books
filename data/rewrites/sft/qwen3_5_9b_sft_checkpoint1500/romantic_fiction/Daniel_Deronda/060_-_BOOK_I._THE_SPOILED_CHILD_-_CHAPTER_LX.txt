"But I shall say no more of this at this time; for this is to be felt and not to be talked of..." Jeremy Taylor once wrote words that echo through the quiet corridors of the human heart, suggesting that some truths are too fragile for the rough handling of conversation. There is a lineage of souls, ancient and unyielding, who mistake the sting of resentment for the warmth of justice. They punish the innocent because they believe they see the pattern of guilt, blind to the deeper, quieter logic of gratitude. Love, they argue, requires proof that exists only in the heart, not in the ledger. Yet, as the ancient masters of language knew, the root "love" must exist before the branch "lovable" can grow. One cannot demand affection where it has never taken root.

The journey to Mainz brought Elias Deronda to the Schuster Strasse, where the air held a sharp, clean chill. Inside the banking house, amidst the hum of commerce, he sought the man named Joseph Kalonymos. A servant guided him past the clutter of business to an inner chamber, where a figure sat at a table, surrounded by scattered letters. It was the same white-bearded man Deronda had glimpsed a year prior in Frankfurt, though now he appeared older, more weary, as if the weight of time pressed heavily upon his shoulders. Beside him lay a packed portmanteau, wrapped tightly, suggesting a departure imminent.

Upon Deronda's entrance, the old man did not rise immediately. Instead, he fixed his gaze upon the younger man with eyes that seemed to burn like black embers within a face of parchment and bone. When he finally spoke, his voice was low, tinged with a German accent that sounded both foreign and intimate.

"You seek me?" Kalonymos asked, a hint of irony softening his tone. "And now, it is you who stand before me."

"Yes," Deronda replied, stepping forward with a steady grace. "I seek you with gratitude, sir. You were a friend to my grandfather, and I owe you a debt for the trouble you have taken on my behalf." He spoke the language fluently, a dialect rich with cadences that bridged cultures.

Kalonymos smiled, then extended a hand. "Then you are no longer angry at being something more than merely an outsider? No longer bitter?"

"On the contrary," Deronda said, bowing slightly. "I thank you for saving me from a lifetime of ignorance regarding my parents, and for guarding the trust my grandfather placed in your hands."

"Sit," Kalonymos urged gently, pulling back his chair. "Sit." He removed his hat, revealing a crown of snow-white hair, and ran a hand through his beard, studying Deronda with an intensity that made the younger man feel exposed, yet strangely honored. In that moment, Deronda felt a strange, electric pull, as if he were standing on the threshold of a world his grandfather had dreamed of. To be seen by a man who had sworn loyalty to his ancestor, to hear a voice that echoed the hopes of a man who had died waiting for this reunion—it was as if the silence of the grave had been broken, and the spirit of Daniel Charisi whispered across the centuries.

"It pleases me," Kalonymos said, shifting into Hebrew, "that I was not called away to the ends of the earth before you arrived. I wished to see the likeness of my friend, alive and well, free from the shadow of shame." He paused, his eyes searching Deronda's. "You come not with pride, but with thanks. You acknowledge the blood that binds us. 'I am the grandson of Daniel Charisi.' Is this true?"

"Undoubtedly," Deronda affirmed. "Though I want you to know, sir, that I never intended to treat anyone with coldness simply because of their name. My hesitation was not malice, but confusion. How could I speak freely to a stranger about a mother I barely knew?"

"A sin!" Kalonymos exclaimed, clutching his chest. "A terrible theft from our people, akin to the ancient sorrows of Rome. But fortune has turned. I have saved what was lost. When Daniel Charisi and I were boys, we bound our fates together, swearing to be brothers in spirit. He said, 'Let us bind love with duty,' for duty is the law of the eternal heart. Though years separated us, the bond remained unbroken. When others tried to take his legacy, they failed. I protected the remnant he cherished. And now, I return it to its rightful heir."

Leaving the room briefly, Kalonymos returned with a clerk bearing a sturdy wooden box, adorned with intricate ironwork and etchings of Arabic script. The lid was lifted, revealing the key resting beneath a layer of leather.

"This is the key," Kalonymos said, handing it to Deronda with a mixture of trust and caution. "Keep it safe. I trust you to handle matters with wisdom."

"I will," Deronda promised, tucking the key into his breast pocket. "It holds more than gold; it holds the hope of a father and the promise of a future. May I ask further questions of my grandfather? Do you have time before you depart?"

"In an hour and eighteen minutes, I sail for Trieste," Kalonymos noted, glancing at his watch. "My sons await me. Would you allow me to introduce you to them? They live in comfort, unlike me, who choose the road."

"I would be honored," Deronda said warmly. "However, I fear I must return to England soon. Friends there wait for me, and I have been absent far too long."

"Then let them know you," Kalonymos suggested. "I am a wanderer, nearing the end of my days, carrying my own shroud. But my sons... they are united, wealthy, and strong. Since Karl the Great brought our ancestors from Italy, we have fought hard to secure our place. We have built a fortress of knowledge, feeding the minds of Germany, though many wear masks of gentleness."

"And what of your people, sir?" Deronda asked, leaning forward. "Have you seen little of their lives?"

"Not entirely," Kalonymos admitted. "But you, young man, have shown signs of preparation. You studied their history before the truth revealed itself to you. Did you not?"

"I did," Deronda confessed. "In seeking to understand my grandfather, I found myself drawn to the stories of our people. It seems fate was guiding me."

Kalonymos nodded slowly. "Perhaps you would have followed in his footsteps if the world had not forced another path. Look at you, Elias. You share his features, yet you bear his gentle strength rather than his iron will. Daniel Charisi had a line carved deep in his brow, a mark of determination. You have none of that hardness. He believed that a firm, if unjust, will was better than a wavering conscience. He hated indifference above all else."

"Was his knowledge limited, then?" Deronda ventured, thinking of the complexity of his grandfather's mind.

"Narrow? Never!" Kalonymos laughed softly, a sound like dry leaves rustling. "He drank in knowledge like a thirsty plant. He traveled the globe, spending his fortunes to see and learn. He understood that the strength of humanity lay in the balance between separation and connection. He feared that if our people lost themselves entirely among the nations, they would become indistinguishable grains in a vast field. He mingled sciences, arts, and laws, just as the great Arab scholars did in their golden age. Yet, despite our differences, we were soulmates. He looked always to the future of our people, pouring his soul into their destiny. I, however, found peace in the present, in the wandering, in the beauty of the world. We loved each other deeply, bound by a vow to defend one another until the end. I have kept my promise."

Kalonymos rose, and Deronda followed suit, holding the old man's gaze. "By honoring your oath, you have ensured justice for me. I would have lived a life of shadows without knowing the inheritance he prepared."

"Then prove yourself worthy," Kalonymos said, his voice dropping to a whisper. "What is your calling, young man?"

Elias hesitated. To admit he read law felt like a betrayal of the old man's expectation of a traditional path, yet to hide it felt like a lie.

"I cannot say," Deronda admitted quietly. "Not yet."

"You must choose," Kalonymos insisted, placing a hand on Deronda's shoulder. "A Jew must be diligent. Will you call yourself a Jew? Will you profess the faith of your fathers?"

"I will call myself a Jew," Deronda declared, his voice gaining strength. "But I do not intend to worship exactly as my fathers did. Their beliefs shifted with the times, and they learned from others. I wish to carry forward my grandfather's vision: to separate yet communicate. My first duty is to my people, and if there is work to be done to restore or elevate our common life, that shall be my vocation."

For a fleeting second, the air in the room seemed to shift. The necessity of speaking, the pressure to define oneself, crystallized Elias's resolve. He saw clearly, for the first time, what he truly wanted.

"Argue and look forward," Kalonymos murmured, a smile breaking through his stern features. "You are indeed the grandson of Daniel Charisi." He offered a blessing in Hebrew, and then, with a final nod, they parted ways.
