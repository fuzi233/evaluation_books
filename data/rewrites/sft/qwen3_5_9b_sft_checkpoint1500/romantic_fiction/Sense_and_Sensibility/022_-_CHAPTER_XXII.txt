Marianne, a spirit who had always harbored little patience for anything resembling impudence, vulgarity, mediocrity, or a divergence from her own refined tastes, found her temper particularly irritable. In this fragile state, she was wholly incapable of finding pleasure in the company of the Miss Steeles or encouraging their overtures. To their unvarying chill, which stifled every attempt at intimacy, Elinor chiefly owed the fact that both sisters eventually favored her, a preference that became strikingly obvious in their behavior, especially Lucy's. Miss Steele seized every chance to engage Elinor in conversation, striving to deepen their acquaintance with an air of casual friendliness.

Lucy possessed natural wit; her observations were often sharp and amusing, and for thirty minutes, she could prove a delightful companion. Yet, her intellect had received no nourishment from education. Ignorant and illiterate, her lack of mental cultivation and her poverty of information on common subjects could not be masked by Elinor's efforts to maintain a favorable appearance. Elinor viewed Lucy's squandered potential with pity, but she observed the lady's utter lack of delicacy, rectitude, and moral integrity with a sterner, colder gaze. The attentiveness, assiduousness, and flattery Lucy displayed at the Park revealed a nature devoid of genuine virtue. Elinor derived no lasting satisfaction from the company of a woman who coupled insincerity with ignorance; whose lack of instruction prevented equality in discourse, and whose conduct toward others rendered any outward show of deference to herself utterly hollow.

One afternoon, as they strolled from the park to the cottage, Lucy broke the silence. "You'll think this a peculiar question, I'm sure," she began, "but pray, are you personally acquainted with your sister-in-law's mother, Mrs. Ferrars?"

Elinor did find the query oddly specific, and her expression reflected her surprise as she admitted she had never met Mrs. Ferrars.

"Indeed!" Lucy exclaimed, feigning wonder. "I marvel at that, for I imagined you must have encountered her at Norland occasionally. Perhaps, then, you cannot describe her?"

"No," Elinor replied, carefully guarding her true opinion of Edward's mother and disinclined to satisfy what appeared to be prying curiosity. "I know nothing of her."

"I am certain you consider me strangely curious," Lucy pressed, watching Elinor intently. "Yet, perhaps there is cause. I trust you won't take offense; I truly hope you believe I have no intention of being rude."

Elinor offered a polite response, and they walked in silence for a brief interval until Lucy resumed the thread. "I cannot bear for you to deem me inquisitive. I'd sooner endure anything than offend someone whose regard is so precious to me. I wouldn't dream of troubling you with advice on managing such an awkward position, but..." She hesitated. "It is a shame you do not know Mrs. Ferrars."

"A pity," Elinor said, genuinely astonished, "if knowing my opinion could assist *you*. However, I never realized you were connected to that family, which makes this serious inquiry into her character somewhat surprising."

"You're right to be surprised," Lucy murmured, lowering her head in a display of modesty, glancing sideways to gauge Elinor's reaction. "If I could reveal everything, you wouldn't be so shocked. Mrs. Ferrars means nothing to me now—not that the future isn't possible. How swiftly it arrives depends on her, but eventually, we shall be intimately bound."

"Good heavens!" Elinor gasped, her composure cracking. "Do you mean you know Mr. Robert Ferrars? Could that be it?" The prospect of such a relationship filled her with dread.

"No," Lucy corrected softly, "not Mr. *Robert* Ferrars—I've never laid eyes on him. But..." She fixed her gaze on Elinor. "His eldest brother."

Astonishment, fierce and painful, seized Elinor. An immediate, instinctive disbelief followed, shielding her from the shock. She turned to Lucy, silent and bewildered, unable to fathom the motive behind such a declaration. Her cheeks flushed, yet she stood firm in her skepticism, far from fainting.

"You needn't be alarmed," Lucy continued, undeterred. "You couldn't possibly guess beforehand, could you? I daresay he never hinted at it to you or anyone else. It was always meant to remain a profound secret, and I assure you, I have guarded it faithfully to this hour. Not a single relation of mine knows, save for Anne—and I would never have disclosed it to you had I not placed absolute faith in your discretion. My numerous inquiries about Mrs. Ferrars must have seemed odd, hence the need for explanation. I am certain Mr. Ferrars won't be displeased that I have trusted you, for he holds your entire family in the highest esteem, regarding you and your sisters as kin."

She paused. Elinor remained silent for a moment, stunned. Forcing herself to speak, she managed a tone of calm composure that barely concealed her turmoil. "May I ask if your engagement has been of long standing?"

"We have been engaged for four years."

"Four years!"

"Yes."

Though deeply shaken, Elinor still struggled to accept the truth. "I was unaware," she stammered, "that we were even acquainted until recently."

"Our friendship dates back many years," Lucy explained. "He was under my Uncle's care for a considerable time."

"My Uncle?"

"Yes, Mr. Pratt. Didn't he ever speak of Mr. Pratt?"

"I believe he did," Elinor replied, summoning strength as her emotions rose.

"He stayed with my uncle at Longstaple, near Plymouth, for four years. That is where our acquaintance began. My sister and I spent much time there, and it was then our engagement was formed, though not officially until a year after he ceased his studies. Yet, he remained with us constantly thereafter. I entered into it, as you can imagine, without my mother's knowledge or approval. I was too young, and loved him too dearly, to exercise the prudence I should have."

Turning to Elinor, she continued, "Though you may not know him as well as I, you must perceive his capacity to inspire sincere attachment."

"Certainly," Elinor answered, though her voice trembled. After a pause, she recovered slightly, bolstered by her conviction of Edward's honor and love, contrasting it sharply with Lucy's apparent deceit. "Engaged to Mr. Edward Ferrars! I confess I am so astounded by your statement that I fear there is some confusion of identity. We cannot mean the same gentleman."

"We mean no other," Lucy smiled confidently. "Mr. Edward Ferrars, the elder son of Mrs. Ferrars of Park Street, and brother of your sister-in-law, Mrs. John Dashwood, is precisely whom I refer to. Pray, acknowledge that I am unlikely to err in naming the man upon whom my happiness rests."

"It is strange," Elinor said, her heart heavy, "that I should never have heard him mention your name."

"Not strange," Lucy agreed. "Given our situation, secrecy was paramount. You knew nothing of me or my family, so there was no occasion to introduce me. Furthermore, Mr. Edward feared his mother's suspicions above all things, which explains his silence."

Silence fell between them. Elinor's sense of security crumbled, yet her self-control held firm. "Four years," she stated firmly.

"Yes," Lucy sighed, dabbing at her eyes with a handkerchief. "And heaven knows how much longer we may wait. Poor Edward! It depresses him terribly." She produced a small miniature from her pocket. "To avoid any misunderstanding, please examine this portrait. It does not fully capture his features, but I trust you recognize the likeness. I have possessed it for over three years."

Handing the painting to Elinor, Lucy waited. When Elinor beheld the image, all lingering doubts vanished. Whatever fears she had harbored regarding haste or deception dissolved instantly. She accepted the portrait, confirming the resemblance.

"I have never been able to reciprocate with my own picture," Lucy lamented, "which vexes me, for he has always desired it so eagerly. I intend to send it at the first opportunity."

"You act wisely," Elinor replied calmly. They walked a few steps in quiet contemplation before Lucy spoke again. "I am convinced of your fidelity in keeping this secret, for you must understand its gravity. Its revelation to his mother would be disastrous; she would never approve. I possess no fortune, and I suspect she is excessively proud."

"I sought no confidence from you," Elinor said gently, "yet you render me the justice of assuming I am trustworthy. Your secret is safe with me. However, forgive me if I express surprise at this unnecessary disclosure. Surely, you must realize that my knowledge adds nothing to its security."

Speaking thus, Elinor scrutinized Lucy's face, hoping to detect a crack in the facade, perhaps evidence of the greatest lie told. But Lucy's countenance remained unaltered.

"I feared you might think I overstepped," Lucy admitted. "We haven't been formally acquainted, yet I have known you and your family by reputation for years.
