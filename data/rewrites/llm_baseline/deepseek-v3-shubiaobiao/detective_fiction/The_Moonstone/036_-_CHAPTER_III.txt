The most striking figure at the dinner party was, without question, Mr. Murthwaite. Having returned to England after years of perilous travels, he had become something of a celebrity—a man who had stared danger in the face and lived to recount his exploits. Now, he announced his intention to venture once more into uncharted territories, a decision that reignited public fascination. There was something thrilling in the idea that this might be the last time society would see him alive. After all, what were the odds of a man tempting fate twice and surviving?

When the gentlemen retired to the dining-room, I found myself seated beside the illustrious traveler. The conversation, as was customary among Englishmen once freed from the moderating presence of ladies, soon turned to politics—a subject I have always found tedious. Glancing at Mr. Murthwaite, I noticed he seemed equally disinterested, though he concealed it with admirable politeness. It occurred to me then that a different topic might rouse him: the Moonstone.

"If I recall correctly, Mr. Murthwaite," I began, "you were acquainted with the late Lady Verinder and took some interest in the curious affair of the Moonstone?"

The effect was immediate. He turned to me with sudden attention, asking who I was. I explained my professional connection to the Herncastle family and my involvement in the matter of the Colonel’s Diamond. At once, Mr. Murthwaite shifted in his chair, dismissing the political debate around us, and fixed his gaze on me.

"Have you heard anything recently of the Indians?" he asked.

"I have reason to believe," I replied, "that one of them visited my office just yesterday."

This visibly startled him—no small feat. I recounted the events concerning Mr. Luker and myself in detail.

"The Indian was clearly probing for something when he asked about repayment terms," I added. "Why such interest in when a debt might be settled?"

"You truly don’t see his motive, Mr. Bruff?"

"I confess my ignorance."

Mr. Murthwaite studied me with something like amusement. "Let me ask you this: where does the conspiracy to reclaim the Moonstone now stand?"

"I couldn’t say. The Indian plot remains a mystery to me."

"Only because you’ve never examined it closely. Shall we trace it step by step? It may prove vital to Miss Verinder’s interests that you understand this fully. Would you prefer to deduce the Indian’s motive yourself, or shall I spare you the effort?"

I chose the former.

"Good," he said. "First, consider the ages of the three Indians. They appear middle-aged—not yet forty, I’d say. Now, think back to Colonel Herncastle’s return to England. These men must be successors to the original Brahmins who pursued him here. But they’ve done more than inherit the quest—they’ve assumed an entire network established by their predecessors. A modest one, by our standards: access to funds, connections with certain disreputable Englishmen, and sympathizers among their countrymen in London. Not formidable, but worth noting."

He leaned forward. "Now, when did the Indians first get their chance at the Diamond?"

"Upon the Colonel’s death," I said. "They would have known of it, and the Will named Miss Verinder as heir."

"Precisely. They’d obtain a copy, learn the Diamond would be transferred to her, and gather intelligence on the family. The question was whether to strike during its removal from the bank or wait until it reached Yorkshire. The latter was safer—hence their appearance in Frizinghall, disguised as jugglers, biding their time."

I recalled how Franklin Blake had outmaneuvered them, arriving early and securing the Diamond in the bank before they could act. Yet why, then, had they not attempted to take it from Lady Verinder’s house in the intervening weeks?

"Because no Indian takes unnecessary risks," Murthwaite explained. "The Will specified Miss Verinder would possess the Diamond outright on her birthday. Why confront Blake, who had already thwarted them, when they could wait for a young woman who would wear it openly? Their patience was rewarded when they saw it on her person that very night."

He went on to describe how the Diamond’s disappearance that evening had shattered their plans, and their subsequent arrest had left them powerless.

"Now," he said, "when did their second chance arise?"

He produced a letter from his pocketbook—a cryptic message in Hindustani, urging the Indians to London upon their release. They had obeyed at once.

"And what followed?" he asked.

"They harassed Mr. Luker," I said.

"Indeed. His testimony revealed a dismissed foreign worker, likely their accomplice. The Indians had traced the Moonstone to Luker—but his swift actions thwarted them again. He sent the Diamond to his banker before they could strike."

I suddenly understood the Indian’s visit to my office. "They assume it’s pledged and want to know the earliest redemption date—when it can be moved from the bank!"

"Exactly. A year from the pledging date—likely late June ’forty-nine—marks their next opportunity. I advise you to be in London then."

"You expect violence?"

"I’d rather face Central Asian fanatics," he said dryly, "than carry the Moonstone past that bank’s doors. The Indians have lost twice. They won’t accept a third defeat."

With that, our discussion ended. I noted the date: *June, ’forty-nine. Watch for the Indians.*

And now, the narrative passes to another hand.
