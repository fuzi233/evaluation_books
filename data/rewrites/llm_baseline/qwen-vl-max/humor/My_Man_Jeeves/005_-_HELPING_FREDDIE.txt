I don’t want to bore you—no, really, I don’t—but I’ve got to tell you about dear old Freddie Meadowes. I’m not much of a writer, mind you—more the sort who’d rather have a good cigar and a decent game of croquet than wrestle with prose—but once I get this down, I’ll hand it over to some literary type to polish up. That way, it’ll sound like something out of a book instead of just a bloke rambling on after too many whiskies.

Freddie’s been my mate for years—solid, dependable, and all that sort of thing. So when I walked into the club one morning and found him slumped in a dark corner, staring blankly at the wall like a man who’d just seen the last of his fortune vanish into thin air, I knew something was wrong. Normally, Freddie is the life and soul of any party—full of jokes, charm, and an uncanny ability to sing “The Rosary” with one finger. But this? This was different. He looked like he’d lost the last sixpence in his pocket—and then swallowed it.

Jimmy Pinkerton was there with me. Jimmy’s a playwright—a clever fellow, though he spends half his time trying to convince people that he’s not actually a madman. Between us, we managed to coax the truth out of Freddie. As we suspected, it was a woman. Angela West—the girl he was engaged to—had broken things off. She wouldn’t speak to him, wouldn’t answer his letters, wouldn’t even let him near her phone. The whole business had left him looking like a man who’d just discovered that his favorite hat had gone missing.

I felt for him. I know what it’s like. Once, I was madly in love with a girl named Elizabeth Shoolbred. She couldn’t stand me. It’s all in my autobiography, if you ever read it. So I knew exactly what Freddie needed.

“Change of scene,” I said. “Come with me to Marvis Bay. I’ve rented a cottage there. Jimmy’s coming down on the twenty-fourth. We’ll be a cozy little group.”

“Absolutely right,” said Jimmy. “Change of scenery is the cure. I once knew a man whose fiancée refused him. He went abroad. Two months later, she wired: ‘Come back. Muriel.’ He started writing a reply—then suddenly realized he couldn’t remember her surname. Never sent it. And never saw her again.”

Freddie didn’t seem convinced. He just stared at the ceiling like a man who’d forgotten how to blink. But eventually, he agreed to come. “Might as well be there as anywhere,” he muttered.

Now, do you know Marvis Bay? It’s in Dorsetshire. Not exactly the kind of place where adventures happen—unless you count being eaten alive by gnats as an adventure. You spend your days swimming, sitting on the sand, and in the evenings, walking along the shore while the insects swarm around you like tiny, irate helicopters. At nine o’clock, you rub antiseptic on your bites and go to bed. Peaceful. Quiet. Boring.

But it seemed to agree with Freddie. Once the moon came out and the wind whispered through the trees, he became inseparable from the beach. The gnats adored him. They’d hang around waiting for him like loyal subjects, ignoring perfectly respectable passersby just so they could be ready when he emerged.

It was nice, really. But by the end of the first week, I began to wish Jimmy had arrived earlier. Because Freddie, bless him, wasn’t exactly the most lively companion. When he wasn’t chewing on a pipe and glaring at the carpet, he was at the piano, playing “The Rosary” with one finger. He couldn’t play anything else. And even “The Rosary” was a disaster—about halfway through, a fuse would blow, and he’d have to start over.

One morning, I came in from a swim to find him mid-performance.

“Reggie,” he said, voice hollow, “I’ve seen her.”

“Seen who?” I asked.

“Miss West. I was at the post office. We met in the doorway. She cut me.”

He started “The Rosary” again, but missed the second bar.

“You ought never to have brought me here,” he said. “I must go away.”

“Go away?” I said. “Don’t be absurd. This is exactly what you need. This is where you come out strong.”

“She cut me.”

“Never mind. Be a sportsman. Try again.”

“She looked clean through me!”

“Of course she did. But don’t worry. Put this in my hands. I’ll see you through. What you need,” I said, “is to make her owe you something. Get her thanking you timidly. That’s the key.”

“But why would she thank me?”

I paused. Then it hit me.

“Save her from drowning,” I said.

“I can’t swim,” he replied.

Ah, there it was. Freddie—dear, sweet Freddie—was the kind of man who could win a polo match blindfolded but couldn’t rescue a cat from a tree without getting himself wet.

He cranked the piano again. I dashed outside.

I strolled along the beach, thinking. Freddie wasn’t much help. He had his strengths—he was brilliant at polo, and his imitation of cats fighting in a backyard was uncanny—but when it came to initiative, he was about as useful as a rubber duck in a hurricane.

Then, rounding some rocks, I saw a blue dress. And by Jove—it was her. Angela West. I hadn’t met her, but Freddie had sixteen photos of her scattered around his room, so I knew it was her. She was helping a small, fat child build a sandcastle. Nearby, an elderly lady sat reading a novel. “Aunt,” the girl called her. So, using my best Sherlock Holmes logic, I deduced the kid was her cousin. I imagined Freddie would’ve tried to sentimentalize the situation—maybe say the kid reminded him of his own childhood. Me? I just thought he looked like a round, slightly grumpy potato.

After finishing the castle, the kid grew bored and started whimpering. The girl took him to a sweets stall. I walked on.

Now, if you ask most fellows, they’ll tell you I’m a chump. And honestly? I don’t argue. I *am* a chump. The Peppers have always been chumps. But every now and then—when you least expect it—I get a brainwave so hot it could fry an egg on a winter’s day. And that’s what happened now.

On my way back, I saw the kid smacking a jellyfish with a spade. The girl was nowhere in sight. I stopped. Then it struck me.

What if I kidnapped the kid? Just for a moment. If Angela starts panicking, and Freddie suddenly appears, leading the child by the hand, telling a dramatic story about how he found him wandering alone and practically saved his life—well, wouldn’t she feel obliged to forgive him?

I grabbed the kid and made off with him. On the way back, I pictured the scene: Freddie stepping forward, heroic, Angela melting into his arms. I could see it so clearly I nearly choked on emotion.

When I burst into the cottage with the kid, Freddie didn’t exactly jump for joy. The child had started screaming. Poor Freddie looked like he’d been handed a live eel.

“Stop it!” he yelled. “Do you think nobody has troubles except you? What the deuce is going on?”

The kid answered with a yell that rattled the windows. I ran to the kitchen, fetched a jar of honey. It worked. The kid stopped crying and began spreading the honey on his face like it was art.

“Well?” Freddie said, when silence returned.

I explained the plan. After a few moments, he finally grasped it.

“You’re not such a fool as you look, sometimes, Reggie,” he said, impressed. “This seems promising.”

He pulled the kid off the honey jar and headed out to find Angela.

I was thrilled. I loved Freddie. Knowing he’d soon be his old self again made me feel like I’d just won the lottery. I was lounging on the veranda, smoking peacefully, when I saw Freddie returning—still with the kid. And he looked like he’d just lost a fight with a badger.

“Hello!” I said. “Couldn’t find her?”

“Yes,” he said, with a hollow laugh. “I found her.”

“Well, then——?”

Freddie sank into a chair and groaned.

“This isn’t her cousin, you idiot!” he said. “He’s not related to her at all. She just met him on the beach. Never seen him before.”

“What? Who is he?”

“I don’t know. Oh, Lord, I’ve had a time! Thank goodness you’ll probably spend the next few years in Dartmoor for kidnapping. That’s my only comfort. I’ll come and jeer at you through the bars.”

“Tell me everything,” I said.

It took him ages. He kept interrupting to call me names, but I pieced together the story. She’d listened to his heroic tale like an iceberg. Then—without calling him a liar—she made it clear that if he and Dr. Cook ever met and compared stories, it would be the biggest duel in history. He’d crawled away, humiliated.

“And mind,” he concluded, “this is your mess. I’m not involved. If you want to avoid prison, go find the kid’s parents and return him before the police show up.”

By Jove, I didn’t realize how hard it would be to return a kidnapped child. I searched Marvis Bay like a bloodhound. No one claimed him. It was as if he belonged there. Then, by inspiration, I asked the sweet-stall man. He told me the kid’s name was Medwin, and his parents lived at Ocean Rest, Beach Road.

I raced there and knocked. No answer. I knocked again. I heard movement inside, but no one came. Just as I was about to take the knocker apart, a voice shouted from above:

“Hi!”

I looked up. A round, pink face with grey whiskers stared down from a window.

“Hi!” it shouted again.

“What do you mean by ‘Hi’?” I asked.

“You can’t come in,” the face said. “Hello, is that Tootles?”

“My name’s not Tootles,” I said. “And I don’t want to come in. Are you Mr. Medwin? I’ve brought back your son.”

“I see him. Peep-bo, Tootles! Dadda can see ’oo!”

The face vanished. Voices echoed. It reappeared.

“Hi!”

I kicked the gravel furiously.

“Do you live here?” I asked.

“I’m staying here for a few weeks.”

“What’s your name?”

“Pepper. But——”

“Pepper? Any relation to Edward Pepper, the colliery owner?”

“My uncle. But——”

“I used to know him well. Dear old Edward Pepper! I wish I was with him now.”

“I wish you were,” I said.

He beamed.

“This is most fortunate,” he said. “We were wondering what to do with Tootles. My daughter Bootles has mumps. Tootles mustn’t be exposed. We couldn’t think what to do. It was most fortunate you found him. He strayed from his nurse. I wouldn’t trust him to a stranger, but you’re different. Any nephew of Edward Pepper’s has my full confidence. You must take Tootles to your house. It will be ideal. I’ve written to my brother in London to fetch him. He may arrive in a few days.”

“May?”

“He’s busy, of course. But he should be here within a week. Until then, Tootles can stay with you. Very much obliged. Your wife will like Tootles.”

“I haven’t got a wife,” I yelled—but the window slammed shut, as if the man had just trapped a germ escaping.

I sighed. The window flew open again.

“Hi!”

A heavy package hit me on the head and exploded like a bomb.

“Did you catch it?” the face asked. “Dear me, you missed it! Never mind. You can get it at the grocer’s. Ask for Bailey’s Granulated Breakfast Chips. Tootles takes them with milk.”

My spirit was crushed. I accepted my fate. Taking Tootles by the hand, I walked away. Napoleon’s retreat from Moscow was a picnic by comparison.

As we turned the corner, we met Angela.

Tootles pointed at her and said, “Wah!”

She smiled. I let go of him. He ran to her.

“Well, baby?” she said, bending down. “So father found you again, did he? Your little son and I made friends on the beach this morning,” she said to me.

I froze. Coming on top of the whiskered lunatic, this was too much. She had nodded goodbye and was halfway down the road before I could gasp, “Wait! I’m not his father!”

I didn’t expect Freddie to be joyful. But I thought he might show a bit of grit. Instead, he leaped up, clutched his head, and didn’t speak for ages. When he did, it was a torrent of insults and emotional outbursts I didn’t know he was capable of.

“Well,” he said finally, “say something! Man, why don’t you say something?”

“You don’t give me a chance,” I said gently.

“What are you going to do about it?”

“What can we do?”

“We can’t spend our lives nursing this—this exhibit.”

He stood up.

“I’m going back to London,” he said.

“Freddie!” I cried. “Would you desert a pal like this?”

“I would. This is your problem. You’ve got to fix it.”

“Freddie,” I said, “you’ve got to stand by me. You must. Do you realize this child needs undressing, bathing, dressing again? You wouldn’t leave me to do it alone? We were at school together. Your mother likes me. You owe me ten bob.”

He sat down.

“Oh, well,” he said, resigned.

“Besides,” I said, “I did this all for you.”

He looked at me strangely.

“Reggie,” he said, voice strained, “one moment. I’ll stand a lot—but not being expected to be grateful.”

Looking back, I realize what saved me from madness was buying out the local sweet shop. By feeding Tootles constantly, we survived the day. At eight o’clock, he fell asleep in a chair. We undressed him—unbuttoning everything, pulling where buttons weren’t—and carried him upstairs.

Freddie stared at the pile of clothes. I knew what he was thinking: how on earth do we get him dressed again?

The next morning, we borrowed a nurse from the next bungalow. Women are amazing. She had the kid dressed and looking like a champion in eight minutes. I paid her handsomely. She promised to come daily.

Breakfast was almost pleasant. Then Tootles spilled milk on Freddie’s trousers.

When Freddie returned, he launched into a monologue about King Herod. “The more I see of this kid,” he said, “the less I wonder at his views on infanticide.”

Two days later, Jimmy Pinkerton arrived. He took one look at the screaming child and picked up his suitcase.

“For me,” he said, “the hotel. I can’t write dialogue with that noise. Whose idea was this? Which of you adopted this little treasure?”

I explained the mumps situation. Jimmy perked up.

“I might turn this into a farce,” he said. “Act two, perfect setup.”

“Farce!” snarled Freddie.

“Exactly. Curtain falls on hero—well-meaning, half-baked idiot—kidnapping the child. Second act: his misadventures. I’ll sketch it tonight. Come on, Reggie, show me the hotel.”

On the way, I told him about Angela.

He dropped his suitcase and stared at me like an owl.

“What?” he said. “Why, this is a play! Ready-made! Parted lovers. Lisping child. Reconciliation over the cradle. Classic. Child center stage. Girl L.C.; Freddie upstage, by the piano. Can Freddie play?”

“He can play a bit of ‘The Rosary’ with one finger.”

Jimmy shook his head. “We’ll cut the music. But the rest is gold. Look.” He squatted in the sand. “This stone is the girl. This seaweed is the child. This nutshell is Freddie. Dialogue builds to child’s line. ‘Boofer lady, does i’oo love dadda?’ Business of hands reaching. Freeze. Freddie crosses, takes girl’s hand. Swallows lump. Big speech. ‘Ah, Angela, has not this gone on too long? A little child rebukes us!’ And so on. Freddie writes his own part. We need a better line for the kid. ‘Kiss Freddie.’ Short, crisp, punchy.”

“But Jimmy,” I said, “there’s no way to get the girl here. She cuts Freddie.”

Jimmy frowned. “Awkward. But we’ll make it exterior. We’ll corner her on the beach. Meanwhile, we must rehearse the kid. First rehearsal—eleven sharp.”

We decided not to tell Freddie. He was already in a bad mood. So we focused on Tootles. And quickly realized: the only way to teach him lines was with sweets.

“The challenge,” said Jimmy after the first rehearsal, “is linking the line to the reward. Once he understands that saying those words brings acid-drops, we’re golden.”

It was thrilling. Some days, he nailed it. Other days, he said “Kick Freddie.” Jimmy vetoed that.

But fate had other plans.

Freddie was at the piano. I was taking Tootles out. Suddenly, Angela appeared on the path.

Tootles screamed. She stopped at the steps.

“Hello, baby!” she said. “Good morning. May I come up?”

She didn’t wait. She walked in, fussed over the kid. Six feet away, Freddie pounded the piano.

I tried to break it up.

“We were just going to the beach,” I said.

“Yes?” she replied. “So you’re tuning the piano? My aunt’s been looking for a tuner. Mind if I go in and tell him to come to us?”

“Er—not yet!” I said. “He can’t be disturbed. Artistic temperament. I’ll tell him later.”

“Very well,” she said. “Ask him to call Pine Bungalow. West is the name. Oh, he seems to have stopped. I suppose he’ll be out soon. I’ll wait.”

“Don’t you think—shouldn’t we go?”

She ignored me, rummaging in her pocket.

“The beach,” I babbled.

And that was it. The curtain rose. The performance began. And none of us had rehearsed Freddie’s part.
