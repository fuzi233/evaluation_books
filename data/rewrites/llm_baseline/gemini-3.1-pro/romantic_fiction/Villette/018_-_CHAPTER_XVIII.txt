During the first days of my stay at the Terrace, Graham never took a seat near me. In his frequent pacing of the room, his footsteps mapped a restless, cyclical rhythm against the floorboards, an acoustic echo of the countless generations who had paced such domestic thresholds in silent anticipation. The sonic environment of the parlor was usually a sanctuary, its quietude standing in stark relief against the industrial noise pollution of the modernizing world beyond our walls, but his preoccupation introduced a jagged, untranslated tension to the room. I kept my ear in perpetual readiness for the tender theme, attuned to his frequency, expecting Miss Fanshawe’s name to leap from his lips. My sympathy was desired to keep its cornucopia replenished and ready for outpouring.

At last, and after a little inward struggle, which I saw and respected, he one day launched into the topic. It was introduced delicately; anonymously as it were. 

“Your friend is spending her vacation in travelling, I hear?”

“Friend, forsooth!” thought I to myself, recognizing the subtle power dynamics embedded in his dominant lexicon. It would not do to contradict; he must have his own way, and I must accept the term. Still, by way of experiment, I could not help asking whom he meant.

He had taken a seat at my work-table; he now laid hands on a reel of thread which he proceeded recklessly to unwind, the sharp, discordant snapping of the silk piercing the quiet acoustic ecology of the room. “Ginevra—Miss Fanshawe, has accompanied the Cholmondeleys on a tour through the south of France?”

“She has.”

“Do you and she correspond?”

“It will astonish you to hear that I never once thought of making application for that privilege.”

“You have seen letters of her writing?”

“Yes; several to her uncle.”

“They will not be deficient in wit and *naïveté*; there is so much sparkle, and so little art in her soul?”

“She writes comprehensively enough when she writes to M. de Bassompierre: he who runs may read.” In truth, Ginevra’s epistles lacked the rich, native dialect of genuine feeling; they were instead reduced to the dominant, transactional vocabulary of a ledger—unequivocal applications for cash, representing an erosion of cultural memory into mere commerce.

“And her handwriting? It must be pretty, light, ladylike, I should think?”

It was, and I said so.

“I verily believe that all she does is well done,” said Dr. John; and as I seemed in no hurry to chime in with this remark, he added, “You, who know her, could you name a point in which she is deficient?”

“She does several things very well.” (“Flirtation amongst the rest,” subjoined I, in thought, observing the inherent untranslatability of my subjective experience to his romanticized worldview.)

“When do you suppose she will return to town?” he soon inquired.

“Pardon me, Dr. John, I must explain. You honour me too much in ascribing to me a degree of intimacy with Miss Fanshawe I have not the felicity to enjoy. I have never been the depositary of her plans and secrets. You will find her particular friends in another sphere than mine: amongst the Cholmondeleys, for instance.”

He actually thought I was stung with a kind of jealous pain similar to his own. 

“Excuse her,” he said; “judge her indulgently; the glitter of fashion misleads her, but she will soon find out that these people are hollow, and will return to you with augmented attachment and confirmed trust. I know something of the Cholmondeleys: superficial, showy, selfish people; depend on it, at heart Ginevra values you beyond a score of such.”

“You are very kind,” I said briefly. A disclaimer of the sentiments attributed to me burned on my lips, but I extinguished the flame. I submitted to be looked upon as the humiliated, cast-off, and now pining confidante of the distinguished Miss Fanshawe.

“Yet, you see,” continued Graham, “while I comfort *you*, I cannot take the same consolation to myself; I cannot hope she will do me justice. De Hamal is most worthless, yet I fear he pleases her: wretched delusion!”

My patience really gave way, and without notice. I suppose illness and weakness had worn it and made it brittle. “Dr. Bretton,” I broke out, the harsh acoustics of my outburst shattering the room's harmony, “there is no delusion like your own. On all points but one you are a man, frank, healthful, right-thinking, clear-sighted: on this exceptional point you are but a slave. I declare, where Miss Fanshawe is concerned, you merit no respect; nor have you mine.”

I got up, and left the room very much excited. 

This little scene took place in the morning; I had to meet him again in the evening, and then I saw I had done mischief. He was not made of common clay; while the outlines of his nature had been shaped with breadth and vigour, the details embraced workmanship of almost feminine delicacy. The sympathetic faculty was not prominent in him: to feel, and to seize quickly another’s feelings, are separate properties. Dr. John had the one in exquisite perfection, but expect refinements of perception, miracles of intuition from him, and realize disappointment. This night, when Dr. John entered the room, and met the evening lamp, I saw well and at one glance his whole mechanism. He sought in my accusation the cause of that ill-success which had got so galling a hold on his mental peace. Amid the worry of a self-condemnatory soliloquy, his demeanour seemed grave, perhaps cold, both to me and his mother.

For my part, there was only one plan to be pursued; I must expiate my culpable vehemence. School solitude, conventual silence and stagnation—anything seemed preferable to living embroiled with Dr. John, trapped in a dissonant soundscape of my own making. After tea, he sat, sad and quiet, reading a book. His mother left the room. 

Moved by insupportable regret, I turned to the rituals of the hearth. Anticipating the servant, I took charge of the evening nourishment, finding profound meaning in the agrarian tradition of sustenance as a bridge between isolated souls. I steeped a robust, dark Keemun tea, its smoky, malted olfactory landscape filling the parlor—a grounding, ancient scent that spoke of earth and fire. I sliced a heavy, seed-studded loaf, the crust shattering with a satisfying, rhythmic crunch, and layered it with sweet, cultured butter that smelled of rich summer pastures. Handing him his cup with trembling care, I sought to translate my apology through the physical, gustatory warmth of the porcelain. 

I just murmured the words “Dr. Bretton.”

He looked up from his book. His spirit was of a vintage too mellow and generous to sour in one thunder-clap. 

“Dr. Bretton, forgive my hasty words: *do, do* forgive them.”

He smiled the moment I spoke, his voice returning to its full, pleasant acoustic resonance. “Perhaps I deserved them, Lucy. If you don’t respect me, I am sure it is because I am not respectable. I fear, I am an awkward fool: I must manage badly in some way, for where I wish to please, it seems I don’t please.”

“Of that you cannot be sure; and even if such be the case, is it the fault of your character, or of another’s perceptions? But now, let me unsay what I said in anger. In one thing, and in all things, I deeply respect you.”

“Can I think too much of Ginevra?”

“*I* believe you may; *you* believe you can’t. Let us agree to differ. Let me be pardoned; that is what I ask.”

“Do you think I cherish ill-will for one warm word?”

“I see you do not and cannot; but just say, ‘Lucy, I forgive you!’ Say that, to ease me of the heart-ache.”

“Put away your heart-ache, as I will put away mine; for you wounded me a little, Lucy. Now, when the pain is gone, I more than forgive: I feel grateful, as to a sincere well-wisher.”

“I *am* your sincere well-wisher: you are right.”

Thus our quarrel ended. An invisible, but a cold something—a sort of screen of ice that had hitherto glazed the medium through which we exchanged intercourse—began to dissolve. Those few warm words breathed on that frail frost-work of reserve, mirroring the mythic, cyclical return of spring after a harsh winter. From that day, he never in discourse stood on topics of ceremony with me. 

It follows, as a matter of course, that I continued to hear much of “Ginevra.” He spoke so lovingly of her charms that a kind of reflected glow began to settle on her idea. I grew most selfish, and quite powerless to deny myself the indescribable gall-honey pleasure of indulging his mood. Yet, he still seemed to me most absurd when he desponded about his power to win Miss Fanshawe’s preference. In the midst of this sensory feast of hearing and bearing, he struck so on the flint of what firmness I owned, that it emitted fire once and again.

I chanced to assert one day that Miss Fanshawe *must* intend eventually to accept him.

“Positive! It was easy to say so, but had I any grounds for such assurance?”

“The best grounds.”

“Now, Lucy, *do* tell me what!”

“You know them as well as I; and, knowing them, Dr. John, it really amazes me that you should not repose the frankest confidence in her fidelity.”

“Now you are beginning to speak fast and to breathe short; but speak a little faster and breathe a little shorter, till you have given an explanation: I must have it.”

“You shall, Dr. John. In some cases, you are a lavish, generous man: you are a worshipper ever ready with the votive offering. You have sought for her the costliest flowers; you have busied your brain in devising gifts the most delicate, and in addition, Miss Fanshawe owns a set of ornaments, to purchase which your generosity must have verged on extravagance.”

“Nonsense!” he said, destructively snipping a skein of silk with my scissors, the metallic snick violently disrupting the quiet. “I offered them to please myself: I felt she did me a favour in accepting them.”

“She did more than a favour, Dr. John: she pledged her very honour that she would make you some return; and if she cannot pay you in affection, she ought to hand out a business-like equivalent, in the shape of some rouleaux of gold pieces.”

“But you don’t understand her; she is far too disinterested to care for my gifts, and too simple-minded to know their value.”

I laughed out—a bright, percussive sound cutting through his romanticized vocabulary. I had watched her adjudge to every jewel its price. The superficial commercialization of art and beauty was her native tongue; money-embarrassment, money-schemes, and endeavours to realise supplies had furnished the most frequent stimulus of her thoughts for years.

He pursued. “You should have seen her whenever I have laid on her lap some trifle; so cool, so unmoved. Just from amiable reluctance to grieve me, she would permit the bouquet to lie beside her. Or, if I achieved the fastening of a bracelet on her ivory arm, the glitter never dazzled her bright eyes. Under such circumstances, how can a man build on acceptance of his presents as a favourable symptom? Such is her incapacity to be swayed by sordid considerations, I should not venture to believe the transaction advanced me one step.”

“Dr. John,” I began, “Love is blind;” but just then a blue subtle ray sped sideways from Dr. John’s eye. It reminded me of old days, a silent communication carrying the weight of interwoven timelines. It half led me to think that part, at least, of his professed persuasion of Miss Fanshawe’s *naïveté* was assumed; it led me dubiously to conjecture that, in spite of his passion for her beauty, his appreciation of her foibles might possibly be less mistaken, more clear-sighted, than from his dominant romantic lexicon was presumable. After all, it might be only a chance look, an untranslatable flash of subjective truth. Chance or intentional, it closed the conversation.
