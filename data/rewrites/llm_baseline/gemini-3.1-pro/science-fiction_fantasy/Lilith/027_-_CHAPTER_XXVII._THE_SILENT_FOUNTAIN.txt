I turned and followed the spotted leopardess, catching but one glimpse of her muscular, rosette-patterned flank as she tore up the brow of the hill toward the palace gate. The night air was thick with the olfactory landscape of the hunt—crushed wild sage, the oxidized tang of fresh blood, and the loamy musk of disturbed earth. When I reached the entrance-hall, the princess was just casting a robe over her shoulders, which she had left on the floor. The blood from her lacerations had ceased to flow, dried into dark, iron-rich crusts by the wind of her flight. 

When she saw me, her face tightened into a flash of anger before she wrestled it aside. Then, with an attempted smile, she looked at me. "I have met with a small accident!" she said, her voice carrying the grating, superficial cadence of a rehearsed performance, jarring against the natural acoustic ecology of the quiet hall. "Happening to hear that the cat-woman was again in the city, I went down to send her away. But she had one of her horrid creatures with her: it sprang upon me, and had its claws in my neck before I could strike it!" 

She gave a shiver. I could not help pitying her, yet her words felt like industrial noise—a fabricated history designed to overwrite the organic truth. Her wounds were visceral, raw as butchered meat, and her face held the same desperate, untranslatable hunger I had witnessed in the cave. My heart reproached me for letting her fight unaided, my compassion evidently written on my features. 

"Child of folly!" she said, attempting another brittle smile. "Not crying, surely! Wait for me here; I am going into the black hall for a moment. I want you to get me something for my scratches." 

But I followed her close. Left alone with her shifting truths, I feared her. The instant she crossed the threshold, an oppressive buzzing assaulted my ears—a dense, polluted soundscape like the mechanical roar of a grinding mill, masking the subtle, natural resonances of the mind. It was a cacophony of low voices, the dying whispers of forgotten dialects struggling to breathe. Shifting rays of illumination began to travel across the vast, dark ellipsoid. Group after group emerged from the vagueness, flashing into view before sinking back into the cultural amnesia of the dark. 

These were interwoven timelines, cycles of mythic history I recognized from my own sensory experiences, now playing out in continuous loops. The skull-headed dancers pounded the forest grass, crushing wild mint beneath their bone-white feet—and there was the princess, observing from the door! The brutal, metallic clashing of the Evil Wood rang out—there she was, urging the slaughter! Yet in the physical space, she stood motionless right before me, her head sunk on her bosom. 

The auditory pollution murmured on, a confused commotion of bruised colors and shapes. Finally, the light settled on a hollow in the heath. There stood the princess, pacing, vainly trying to cloak herself in vapor. Then, the old librarian—that meticulous archivist of forgotten texts and the deep, agrarian sustenance of the soul—walked toward her. He stood regarding her with the quiet power of an elder who knows the true, untranslatable names of things. Before his gaze, her eidolon collapsed; her limbs forsook her and fled; her body vanished. 

A wild shriek shattered the room's polluted acoustics. With the fall of her phantom, the physical princess collapsed heavily onto the cold stone, lying still. I turned at once and walked out. Not again would I attempt to restore a vessel so violently emptied of its own truth. 

Standing beside the cage, trembling, I realized that within that black ellipsoid, I had tasted the bitter, chaotic vintage of the princess's own brain. I saw the leopardess's tail quiver once in the shadows. 

While still endeavoring to compose my breathing, the princess's voice materialized beside me. "Come now," she said. "I will show you what I want you to do for me." 

She led me into the court. I followed, caught in the circular pacing of our strange dynamic. The moon neared the zenith, its silver light pouring down like clarified butter, richer than the absent sun's gold. She brought me through the groves to the tallest tree in the center. Its branches drew together at the summit in a dense, cone-like cluster, radiating the ancient, yeasty aroma of primordial bark and deep-tap roots. 

"On the summit of that tree," she murmured, gazing upward, "grows a tiny blossom which would at once heal my scratches! I might be a dove for a moment and fetch it, but I see a little snake in the leaves whose bite would be worse to a dove than the bite of a tiger to me! How I hate that cat-woman!" 

She turned to me quickly. "Can you climb?" The sweet smile vanished as quickly as a dissipated scent, replaced by a look of stark suffering. I should have let her endure it, but the way she pressed her hand to her wounded neck—a gesture so fundamentally human, so steeped in the everyday domestic fragility of the body—went to my heart. I studied the trunk. All the way up were rough projections, layered like the shucked leaves of an ancient palm. 

"I can climb that tree," I answered. 

"Not with bare feet!" she insisted. In my haste following the leopardess, I had left my sandals in my room. 

"It is no matter," I said. "I have long gone barefoot, accustomed to the textures of the earth." 

I looked up again, my eyes tracing the rugged bole until it was lost in the canopy. The moonlight caught the bark like silvery sea-foam, and a breeze moved through the high branches, creating a pure acoustic illusion—a murmurous sound of water falling softly into water, an unpolluted sonic oasis. I approached the trunk. 

"I cannot let you attempt it with your feet bare!" she commanded. "A fall from the top would kill you!" 

"So would a bite from the snake!" I retorted, though I suspected the serpent was merely a fabrication of her dominant lexicon. 

"It would not hurt *you*!" she replied. "Wait a moment." 

Kneeling with the deliberate grace of a traditional elder preparing a harvest binding, she tore the two wide, embroidered borders from the front of her garment. She placed my left foot, then my right, upon her knee, and bound them tightly with the thick fabric. 

"You have left the ends hanging, princess," I noted. 

"I have nothing to cut them off with; but they are not long enough to get entangled," she replied. 

I turned to the tree and began to climb, pulling myself up the tactile, fibrous steps. In Bulika, the evening air usually held a mild chill, nothing like the deep, frost-bitten temperatures near the sexton's cottage where root cellars kept the winter's bounty. Yet, as I ascended, a profound cold seeped into my bones. It was a sudden, rapid chilling, like the shock of a cold-water plunge used to blanch and preserve the vividness of harvested greens. The higher I went, the colder it became, until I reached the branches and my hands and feet felt entirely numb, stripped of all sensory appreciation. There was scarcely any wind, the acoustic environment eerily silent, the branches entirely still. 

Yet, as I neared the summit, searching for that mythic, healing botanical, a strange unsteadiness took hold. Every branch I gripped or stood upon felt soft, on the verge of collapsing. As my head rose above the canopy into the open moonlight, seeking the olfactory trace of the blossom, I was instantaneously drenched from head to foot. 

The physical sensation was utterly untranslatable—one moment I was enveloped in freezing air, the next I was plunged into a storm-tossed fluid. I was sinking, flung wildly in a dark, aquatic churn. Tossed up and down, rolled over and over, checked, tossed the other way, and dragged down again, I swallowed mouthfuls of the icy, tasteless flood. Gasping, gurgling, my breath choked by the invasive deluge, I fell lower and lower until I slammed against a solid, unyielding bottom. 

Through the dense, liquid silence, a harsh, discordant dialect ruptured the acoustic ecology of my mind. 

"I told you so!" it croaked in my ear.
