The morning sun had barely warmed the room when Mrs. Bretton entered, her purpose clear and immediate. She commanded me to empty my drawers and display my wardrobe, a request I obeyed in silence. After a brief inspection, she declared my collection insufficient and departed, returning moments later with a dressmaker in tow. Measurements were taken with brisk efficiency, accompanied by her declaration that she intended to dictate the fashion, following her own taste without compromise.

Two days later, the result arrived: a dress of vibrant pink. My reaction was instantaneous and visceral. "That is not for me," I protested, feeling a sudden, sharp aversion to the garment, imagining myself paraded in the garb of some exotic Eastern dignitary. My godmother, however, met my hesitation with her characteristic, unyielding resolve. "We shall see whether it is for you or not," she retorted, sealing her decree with the assurance that I must wear it that very evening.

The command extended beyond the dress. I was to accompany her and Dr. John Graham to a grand concert hosted by the principal musical society. The event promised to be a spectacle of the highest order: performances by the Conservatoire's most advanced students, a charity lottery for the poor, and, most significantly, the presence of the King, Queen, and Prince of Labassecour. Graham, in his invitation, had emphasized the necessity of appropriate attire as a tribute to royalty and urged punctuality.

By six o'clock, I found myself upstairs, already dressed. Against my own will, I had been led into the pink gown, softened only by a mantle of black lace. I was pronounced *en grande tenue* and urged to admire my reflection. With trembling hands and a heart full of dread, I glanced at the mirror, then quickly turned away. Seven o'clock struck; Dr. Bretton had arrived, and we descended. My godmother wore a suit of brown velvet, a color of grave majesty that I secretly envied as I walked in her shadow. Graham awaited us in the drawing-room.

"I hope he does not think I am dressing to draw attention," I murmured to myself, plagued by insecurity.

"Here, Lucy, some flowers," he said, presenting a bouquet. He offered no comment on my attire save for a kind smile and a satisfied nod. This simple gesture instantly calmed my shame and fear of ridicule. Upon closer inspection, I realized the dress was remarkably simple, lacking excessive frills; it was the bright hue and light fabric that had unsettled me. Since Graham accepted it without censure, my own judgment gradually reconciled itself to the outfit.

The journey to the concert hall was a revelation. Having rarely attended public entertainments, I felt a fresh, exhilarating joy in the experience. The carriage ride was cozy despite the cold, fine night; the stars glinted through the trees, and the city lights burned brightly as we passed through the gates. The atmosphere was charged with a sense of novelty and friendship. Dr. John and his mother were in high spirits, their conversation animated and their kindness toward me evident, treating me as if I were their own kin.

As we drove through the illuminated streets of Villette, the vibrancy of the city contrasted sharply with my memories of the Rue Fossette—the walled garden, the dark classrooms, and the solitary nights spent gazing at stars through blind windows. The shadow of my routine future crossed the radiant present, sobering the moment. Soon, we arrived at a great, illuminated building. We alighted under a bustling portico and ascended a majestic staircase carpeted in crimson, leading to doors of the same deep red.

Inside, the hall was a masterpiece of grandeur. High domed ceilings, sweeping circular walls, and intricate moldings were painted in shades of gold and alabaster, accented by deep crimson drapery and carpets. A massive chandelier hung from the dome, a dazzling array of crystal and gemstones that seemed to hold the light of a thousand stars. It was a space of such splendor that I felt transported to an Eastern palace, guarded by the genie of the lamp.

Moving deeper into the hall, we encountered another party. For a fleeting second, I saw a group that seemed entirely unfamiliar: a handsome middle-aged lady in dark velvet, a gentleman of striking beauty, and a young woman in a pink dress and black lace. The illusion shattered instantly when I realized we were facing a great mirror; the party was our own. It was the first time in my life I truly saw myself as others saw me. The reflection brought a pang of regret and discord; it was not flattering, yet I was grateful it could not have been worse.

We took our seats in a location offering a clear view of the vast hall, which was already filled with a splendid assembly. The women were not necessarily the most beautiful, but their dresses were flawless. Foreigners, often awkward in private, possessed a unique grace in public, their movements refined for gala occasions. Among the crowd, I observed a specific type of beauty: solid, sculptural, and marble-like. These women possessed a classical, round-faced calm, requiring no ornamentation. One such figure, a Juno-like beauty in pale blue satin, sat prominently near our view.

Dr. John's attention was drawn to her. "For heaven's sake, shield your heart," I whispered urgently. "You might die at her feet, and she would never love you."

He shrugged, amused. "Perhaps her insensibility is the very stimulus I need. But you know nothing of these things; I shall address my mother."

"She would not care!" Mrs. Bretton dismissed the notion.

"My mother is unsentimental," John replied, lamenting his lot. "She never considers the calamity of a daughter-in-law."

"If I don't, it is not for lack of warning," Mrs. Bretton countered, recalling threats from a decade prior.

"But eventually, I shall take a wife," John insisted, pointing out a goddess in pale blue satin with "reflets satinés." "Would you not be proud, Mamma, if I brought her home?"

"You will bring no goddess to La Terrasse," Mrs. Bretton retorted, describing the rival beauty as a "mighty doll in wood and wax." "That château will not contain two mistresses."

"The foreign usurper!" John teased. "But hush, Mamma, hold your tongue and use your eyes."

While they sparred, the hall filled further. The stage, once empty, was now alive with activity. Two grand pianos were surrounded by a white flock of young girls, the Conservatoire pupils, arranged by two gentlemen. One was M. Josef Emanuel, a noted pianist and teacher, while the other was his brother, M. Paul. M. Paul was a force of nature, a small man with immense energy who commanded the hundred young ladies with absolute authority. He was there for display and control, fleeing the spotlight of famous singers whenever they appeared, preferring to dominate the amateurs.

Finally, the stage was ready. A crimson compartment, reserved for royalty, awaited. Doors rolled back, the orchestra swelled, and the King, Queen, and Court of Labassecour entered to a chorus of welcome.

I had never seen royalty before. Expecting figures seated on thrones, I was slightly disappointed to see a middle-aged, bowed soldier and a younger lady. The King, a man of fifty with grey hair and iron-hard lines on his brow, sat silently. He was a nervous sufferer, his eyes haunted by the specter of hypochondria, freezing the light in his gaze. Beside him, the Queen moved with a benignant grace. Though her features carried the weight of lineage, her eyes were filled with pity and goodness. She tried to rouse her husband from his melancholy by engaging their young son, but the King invariably relapsed into abstraction. The courtiers and bourgeoisie seemed oblivious to the tragedy unfolding before them.

The royal party was joined by foreign ambassadors and the elite of Villette's expatriate community. They occupied the crimson benches, while the rest of the hall stood in a dark foil of sable coats. The foreground benches were reserved for the young aristocracy of Villette—girls in white, pale rose, or blue, embodying purity and aerial grace. Among them were Mesdemoiselles Mathilde and Angélique, former pupils of Madame Beck who had struggled academically, and a spirited English girl seated beside Ginevra Fanshawe.

Ginevra, radiant and confident, chatted volubly. I noticed Dr. Bretton growing quiet, suppressing sighs as he watched her. From a distance, Ginevra's eyes swept the room, landing on Dr. John and his mother. She raised her glass to inspect them, then whispered to her neighbor. When the music began, her attention drifted to the stage.

The concert itself was a mix of timid performances and bold displays. The Conservatoire students played with trembling hands, lacking the heroic self-possession M. Paul might have forced upon them. A sulky lady in white sang with theatrical flair, and a gentleman lamented a "fausse Isabelle" with bitter emotion. The highlight was the rousing choruses performed by provincial societies, whose hearty exertions satisfied the ear.

Throughout the performance, my attention was split between the stage and Dr. John. He remained composed until he spoke to me. "How do you like it all, Lucy? You are very quiet."

"I am quiet because I am very interested," I replied.

"Miss Fanshawe is here," I whispered. "Have you noticed her?"

"Oh, yes," he said calmly. "I saw you notice her too. She is with a lady of rank, Lady Sara. She is proud but not insolent. I doubt Ginevra has gained much ground by making a butt of us."

"What neighbors?" I asked.

"Merely myself and my mother," he admitted, recounting the sensation of Ginevra's sarcastic glance and levelled glass. "It gave me a curious sensation."

"Think nothing of it," I advised. "She is heedless folly. To a feather-brained school-girl, nothing is sacred."

"But you forget," he insisted, his voice rising. "She was my divinity, the angel of my career. Three days ago, I was her slave. Through herself, she could not have hurt me in ten years; but through my mother, she has done it in a moment."

His blue eyes burned with a rare, fierce passion, devoid of sunshine. "Look at my mother," he commanded. "In what light does she appear to you now?"

"As she always does," I answered honestly. "An English gentlewoman, independent and cheerful."

"The merry laugh *with* mamma, but the weak laugh *at* her," he declared, his indignation swelling. "She shall not be ridiculed with my consent."

"Do I frighten you, Lucy?" he asked suddenly.

"I cannot tell why you are so angry."

"For this reason," he muttered. "Ginevra is neither a pure angel nor a pure-minded woman."

"Nonsense! She has no great harm in her."

"Too much for me. I can see where you are blind. Dismiss the subject."

He turned to tease his mother about her energy, deflecting the tension. The concert ended, followed by the lottery. The interval was a time of relaxed commotion. M. Paul reappeared, directing the removal of pianos with vigorous energy. His naïveté and physical vigor were undeniable, even as I disapproved of his fuss.

The hall became animated as people stood and mingled. The Queen glided among the young ladies, offering tokens of kindness to all, including the two English girls, Lady Sara and Ginevra Fanshawe. Ginevra glowed with gratification. A circle of gentlemen gathered around them, including Count de Hamal.

Dr. Bretton, impatient with the heat, suggested fresh air. Reluctantly, I accompanied him. Outside, the night was cold and starry.

"You look pensive, Lucy," Graham asked. "Is it on my account?"

"I feared you were grieved."

"Not at all," he said, his demeanor shifting instantly from fiery indignation to cool tranquility. "I am glad she laughed at my mother. I would not give the old lady for a dozen beauties. That sneer did me all the good in the world. Thank you, Miss Fanshawe!" He lifted his hat in mock reverence.

"Yes," he continued, "I thank her. She has made me feel that nine parts of my heart are sound as a bell, and the tenth bled from a mere puncture that will heal in a trice."

"You are angry just now," I observed.

"_I_ heated and indignant! You don't know me. The heat is gone. I am as cool as the night."

He led me back inside, where the lottery was underway. Amidst the crowd, I caught M. Paul's gaze. He looked at me gravely, his eye gleaming with a sardonic comment on my pink dress. Remembering his habit of criticizing the teachers' attire, I deliberately turned my face to Dr. John's black coat sleeve, seeking comfort in his presence. Dr. John noticed my avoidance and gently pulled me closer, shielding me from the crowd.

M. Paul, however, had penetrated my thought. His mocking gaze turned to a stern frown. When I bowed in conciliation, he returned only a stiff, cold nod.

"Whom have you made angry, Lucy?" Dr. Bretton whispered.

"One of the professors at Madame Beck's: a very cross little man."

"He looks mighty cross," Dr. John agreed. "What have you done to him?"

"No mystery," I lied. "He thinks I failed in respect because I looked at your sleeve instead of curtseying."

Before Dr. John could respond, M. Paul rudely pushed past us, elbowing his way through the crowd with utter disregard for others' safety. We were nearly knocked down.

"I think he is what he himself would call 'méchant,'" Dr. Bretton remarked.
