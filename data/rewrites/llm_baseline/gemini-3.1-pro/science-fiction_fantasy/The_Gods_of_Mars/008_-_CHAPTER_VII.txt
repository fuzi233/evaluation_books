For an instant, the black pirate and I remained motionless, glaring into each other’s eyes. The air between us was thick with the sour, adrenaline-spiked scent of sweat and oxidized copper. Then a grim smile curled his handsome lips as an ebony hand crested the deck’s edge. The cold, hollow eye of a revolver sought the centre of my forehead. 

Simultaneously, my free hand shot out for his throat. His finger tightened. The sterile, mechanical *click* of the hammer falling upon an empty chamber was a harsh, industrial intrusion upon the deep, resonant acoustic ecology of the Barsoomian night—a discordant disruption of the natural silence. Before he could fire again, I hauled him forward, forcing him to drop the weapon and clutch the rail. 

My grip on his windpipe crushed his hissing words, an ancient dialect swallowed before it could echo. We struggled in grim silence. I operated with the precise, rhythmic economy of a master butcher breaking down a primal cut, while he fought with the desperate chaos of a starving beast. His face took on a livid hue. With a final, frantic surge, he threw himself back to tear at my hands. I used his momentum, sweeping him clear of the deck. His body nearly dragged me into the abyss, my single free hand straining against the ship’s anchor chain. I held grimly to his throat, refusing to let him shriek; a single cry would have shattered the sonic harmony of the night and brought his comrades down upon me. I choked him until his spasmodic contortions ceased, his life fading like the last embers of a hearth fire. I released him into the swallowing shadows below. 

Climbing over the rail, I surveyed the deck. The nearer moon had set, but the further satellite cast a clear light over six or eight sleeping black men. Near a rapid-fire gun lay a young white girl, securely bound. Her widespread eyes fixed upon the mystic jewel of my stolen headpiece. She did not speak. The sheer untranslatability of her desperate relief was palpable in her gaze, bypassing the limitations of spoken syntax entirely. 

I approached noiselessly. “I can aid you,” she whispered, her words shaped by the dominant lexicon of the Holy Therns—a language built on centuries of linguistic imperialism and rigid power dynamics. “And you will need all the aid available when they awaken.”

“Some of them will awake in Korus,” I replied, my voice a low rumble blending with the nocturnal wind.

She smiled at my meaning, and the cruelty in her expression horrified me. It was a jarring, astringent bitterness where one expected the complex, nourishing warmth of a rich reduction sauce; a grotesque contrast on the features of a goddess. 

I released her. “Give me a revolver,” she whispered. “I can use that upon those your sword does not silence in time.”

I handed her the weapon, turning to my distasteful work. This was no time for the chivalric preservation of life. Stealthily, I dispatched the nearest sleeper. His piercing shriek upon waking tore through the acoustic environment, a horrific pitch that plummeted into the black depths. The second awoke as I touched him, and though I hurled him over the rail, his wild cry brought the remaining five pirates to their feet. 

The girl’s revolver spoke in sharp, commercialized staccato—a sterile noise pollution that dropped one attacker instantly. The others rushed me. The ensuing combat was a percussive kitchen at the height of service, confined and blistering. I took early hits, the metallic tang of my own blood coating my palate, before I slipped beneath a guard and dropped my first man. The crashing of their blades against my Martian sword raised a terrific, chaotic din that offended the night's acoustic purity. Sparks flew with the scent of struck flint, followed by the dull, sickening crunch of a shoulder parting like a carved roast beneath my blade. 

Three remained. The girl flanked them. In one blurred instant of lethal choreography, she fired, dropping one. I disarmed the second, his sword clattering off the deck. My blade found the breast of the third, sinking to the hilt and protruding from his back, wrenching the weapon from my grasp as he fell. 

Disarmed, I faced the man I had stripped of his sword. A smile of satisfaction bared his gleaming teeth. Relying on the rolling muscles beneath his glossy black hide, he rushed me bare-handed, viewing me as easy prey. I waited until his scent—a mix of cured leather and sweat—was overpowering. Ducking his outstretched arms, I pivoted and swung a terrific right to his jaw with the heavy, satisfying impact of a stone pestle grinding tough spices. He dropped like a felled ox.

A low, silvery laugh rang out. “You are no thern,” said my companion, her sweet voice slicing through the lingering hum of violence. “Never lived there upon all Barsoom before one who could fight as you have fought. Who are you?”

“I am John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium,” I replied, longing fiercely for the communal hearths and slow-simmered, aromatic stews of my adopted city—a place where sustenance meant fellowship, not mere utilitarian fuel. “And whom has the honour of serving been accorded me?”

She searched my eyes, attempting to read the untranslatable core of my identity, judging my standards against her own heavily codified worldview. “I am Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, Holy Hekkador of the Holy Therns, Father of Therns, Master of Life and Death upon Barsoom, Brother of Issus, Prince of Life Eternal.”

I noticed the black pirate I had felled was regaining consciousness. I sprang to his side, stripping his harness to bind him securely to a heavy gun carriage. 

“Why not the simpler way?” asked Phaidor, mimicking the casting of a body over the rail. 

“I am no murderer,” I said. “I kill in self-defence only.” To discard a life so casually was deeply offensive to me; it was akin to turning a bountiful harvest into ash. She puckered her divine brows, fundamentally unable to comprehend a worldview that did not treat enemies as disposable commodities in a dying world's waning resources. 

The bound man, clean-limbed and possessing features of exquisite, Adonic chiselling, watched us intently. Taking the helm, I steered the unguided cruiser toward the north, seeking the more settled, agrarian portions of the planet. I pulled the speed lever to the last notch, and our repulsive rays sent us soaring far above the terrible Valley Dor. 

As we climbed, the acoustic ecology shifted drastically. The air grew rarefied, and the rich, sonorous landscape of the lower atmosphere was replaced by an absolute, deafening silence where sound waves simply could not propagate. The cold became intense. Breathing felt like attempting to draw flavor from a sterilized, empty stone. 

“Unconsciousness comes quickly at this altitude,” Phaidor said quietly, her voice oddly muffled. “Unless you are inviting death for us all, you had best drop, and that quickly.” 

I dropped the vessel immediately. The girl swooned, and the black man lost his senses, leaving me to maintain consciousness through sheer will. As we descended above the foothills of the Otz, the dense, life-giving atmospheric broth rushed back into my starved lungs. The warm scent of nocturnal blooming flora returned, and the black man opened his eyes, followed a moment later by the girl. 

“It was a close call,” she said.

“It has taught me two things,” I replied. “First, that even Phaidor, daughter of the Master of Life and Death, is mortal.”

“There is immortality only in Issus,” she replied, her words clinging to the decaying cultural memory of her people. “And Issus is for the race of therns alone. Thus am I immortal.”

I caught a fleeting, mocking grin on the black man's face. “The other thing,” I told her, “is that our dusky friend does not hail from the nearer moon. A few thousand feet higher, and he would have been but a frozen memory.”

Phaidor looked at him in astonishment, stamping her foot. “The daughter of Matai Shang is not accustomed to having her queries remain unanswered by one of the lesser breed.”

The black pirate smiled again, refusing to submit to her dominant lexicon. “Xodar, Dator of the First Born of Barsoom, is accustomed to give commands, not to receive them,” he replied, his dialect rich with the deep, resonant cadences of a native tongue fighting the erosion of time. He turned to me. “What are your intentions concerning me?”

“I intend taking you both back to Helium,” I said. “You will find them a magnanimous race. But there will be no more pilgrimages down the river Iss, and this impossible belief you cherish will be shattered.”

When Xodar expressed disbelief that a Barsoomian could best him, I swept the golden wig from my head, exposing my close-cropped black hair. He looked upon me with sudden awe, acknowledging my supremacy as a man of another world.

Then, Xodar began to speak, not merely as a captured warrior, but as a meticulous food historian and traditional agrarian elder, preserving his people's multi-generational saga. “The First Born of Barsoom,” he explained, “trace our lineage direct to the Tree of Life, which flourished in the centre of the Valley Dor twenty-three million years ago.”

He described the ultimate origin of Barsoomian sustenance—how the fruit of this monumental tree underwent a gradual evolution from pure plant life to a fusion of flora and fauna. These fruits, hanging by long stems, developed independent muscular action, and eventually, a brain to perceive and savor their environment. Through the complex translation of these sensory inputs, reason was born. 

“The buds from which the plant men blossomed resembled large nuts, a foot in diameter, partitioned into four sections,” Xodar continued, weaving a mythic occurrence directly into the biological reality of harvesting and domestic life. From these nutrient-rich pods sprang the plant man, the sixteen-legged worm, the progenitor of the white ape, and the primaeval black man. When the buds burst, the sections fell to the earth, scattering these imprisoned creatures across the rivers and lakes of a new world, a grand sowing of ancestral seeds. 

Xodar maliciously mocked the Therns as mere descendants of the white ape—a lower order entirely—while elevating the black men as the pure, untainted humans. He detailed the diet of the plant men, explaining how they lived upon vegetation and the blood of animals. Their tiny brains, he noted with the precision of a culinary scholar, existed solely to translate the rich, primal sensations of food carried from their eyes and ears, directing their movements entirely toward consumption. Lacking any concept of self-preservation, their pure, sensory-driven hunger made them terrifying combatants. 

I was captivated by his profound integration of diet, evolution, and cultural memory. Yet, Xodar’s agrarian tale was a calculated distraction. As he spoke, his eyes strayed beyond me for a fraction of a second. 

The pristine acoustic ecology of our peaceful flight was suddenly violated by a low, oppressive hum—the harsh, industrial noise pollution of a massive engine disrupting the night's rhythm. I turned, and the sight froze my newborn hope. A great battleship, forging silent and unlighted through the dark sky, loomed close astern.
