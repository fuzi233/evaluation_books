"A dull rotation, never at a stay,
Yesterday's face twin image of to-day."

"Of what each one should be, he sees the form and rule,
And till he reach to that, his joy can ne'er be full."

For Margaret, the profound stillness of the Harley Street residence, maintained during Edith's convalescence, offered the necessary respite to process the seismic shift in her fortune over the past two months. She found herself suddenly ensconced in a palace of luxury, a place where the mere whisper of worry seemed alien to the air. The intricate gears of domestic life here ran with a frictionless, oily smoothness. Mrs. Shaw and Edith treated Margaret with an abundance of affection, insisting on the house being her home, yet a quiet pang of ingratitude gnawed at her. She could not shake the memory of Helstone vicarage, or even the modest dwelling at Milton, where her father's anxiety and her mother's frailty formed the true texture of home. Edith, eager to reclaim her health, rushed to fill Margaret's room with the soft comforts and ornamental trinkets that adorned her own. Mrs. Shaw and the maid busied themselves restoring Margaret's wardrobe to a state of elegant variety. Captain Lennox was a model of easy kindness, spending hours with his wife or playing with his son, while the remainder of his time was spent at his club or dining out.

Just as Margaret began to feel the suffocating weight of this eventless ease, Edith descended the stairs to resume her role in the household. Margaret, accustomed to service, gladly assumed the burden of managing Edith's social obligations—answering correspondence, reminding her of engagements, and caring for her when the gaiety of the season waned. Consequently, Margaret often felt inclined to feign illness herself. Yet, the rest of the family was immersed in the full business of the London season, leaving Margaret frequently alone. In these solitary moments, her thoughts drifted back to Milton, struck by a strange dissonance between that life and this. She was becoming surfeited by a peace that required no struggle, fearing she might slowly deaden herself into a forgetfulness of everything beyond the luxury lapping at her shores. Though toilers existed in London, she never saw them; the servants inhabited an underground world of their own, appearing only to serve the whims of their masters. A vacuum of unsatisfied purpose opened in Margaret's heart. Once, she had dimly hinted at this to Edith, who, weary from dancing, had languidly stroked Margaret's cheek.

"Poor child!" Edith had said. "It is a little sad to be left, night after night, when the world is so gay. But we shall have dinner-parties soon—as soon as Henry returns from circuit—and then there will be variety. No wonder you are moping, darling!"

Margaret did not believe dinner-parties were a panacea. Edith, however, took pride in them, boasting of how they differed from the dowager-style dinners of her mother's regime. Mrs. Shaw seemed to derive equal pleasure from the new, lighter entertainments suited to the Lennoxes' tastes as she had from her own formal gatherings. Captain Lennox remained consistently kind and brotherly, though Margaret's affection for him wavered whenever he became anxiously attentive to Edith's appearance, seeking to impress the world. In those moments, the latent Vashti in Margaret stirred, and she struggled to suppress her feelings.

Margaret's days followed a predictable rhythm: quiet hours before a late breakfast; a lazy, drawn-out meal where she was expected to offer sympathy on plans that concerned her nothing; an endless stream of notes to write, which Edith left to her with compliments on her "eloquence"; playing with Sholto; caring for the children during the servants' dinner; drives or calls; and finally, engagements for her aunt and cousins that left her free but exhausted by the day's inactivity.

She looked forward with silent longing to Dixon's return from Milton, where the old servant was winding up the Hale family's affairs. The sudden cessation of news from the people she had known for so long felt like a famine to her heart. True, Dixon's letters occasionally quoted Mr. Thornton's opinions on furniture or landlords, but the name rarely appeared, nor did any other Milton connection. One evening, sitting alone in the Lennox drawing-room, Margaret held Dixon's letters but refused to open them. Instead, she recalled the busy life from which she had been plucked, wondering if anyone in that whirlwind missed her—or Higgins, whom she was not thinking of. Suddenly, Mr. Bell was announced. Margaret hurried the letters into her work-basket, rising with a blush that suggested guilt.

"Oh, Mr. Bell! I never thought of seeing you!"

"You give me a welcome, I hope, as well as that very pretty start of surprise."

"Have you dined? How did you come? Let me order you some dinner."

"If you're going to have any. Otherwise, there is no one who cares less for eating than I do. But where are the others? Gone out?"

"Oh yes! and it is such a rest. I was just thinking—But will you risk dinner? I don't know if there is anything in the house."

"To tell you the truth, I dined at my club. They don't cook as well as they did, so I thought, if you were dining, I might try to make out a meal. But never mind! There aren't ten cooks in England to be trusted at impromptu dinners. Their tempers won't stand it. You shall make me some tea, Margaret. And now, what were you thinking of? Whose letters were those, god-daughter, that you hid away so speedily?"

"Only Dixon's," Margaret replied, turning very red.

"Whew! Is that all? Who do you think came up in the train with me?"

"I don't know," Margaret said, resolving against guessing.

"Your what d'ye call him? What's the right name for a cousin-in-law's brother?"

"Mr. Henry Lennox?"

"Yes," Mr. Bell replied. "You knew him formerly, didn't you? What sort of a person is he, Margaret?"

"I liked him long ago," Margaret said, glancing down, then looking up in her natural manner. "You know we have been corresponding about Frederick since; but I have not seen him for nearly three years, and he may be changed. What did you think of him?"

"I don't know. He was so busy trying to find out who I was, and what I was, that he never let out what he was; unless that veiled curiosity as to what manner of man he had to talk to was not a good piece, and a fair indication of his character. Do you call him good-looking, Margaret?"

"No! certainly not. Do you?"

"Not I. But I thought perhaps you might. Is he a great deal here?"

"I fancy he is when he is in town. He has been on circuit now since I came. But—Mr. Bell—have you come from Oxford or from Milton?"

"From Milton. Don't you see I'm smoke-dried?"

"Certainly. But I thought that might be the effect of the antiquities of Oxford."

"Come now, be a sensible woman! In Oxford, I could have managed all the landlords in the place with half the trouble your Milton landlord has given me. He won't take the house off our hands till next June. Luckily, Mr. Thornton found a tenant for it. Why don't you ask after Mr. Thornton, Margaret? He has proved himself a very active friend of yours. Taken more than half the trouble off my hands."

"And how is he? How is Mrs. Thornton?" Margaret asked hurriedly, below her breath.

"I suppose they're well. I've been staying at their house till I was driven out by the perpetual clack about that Thornton girl's marriage. It was too much for Thornton himself, though she was his sister. He used to sit in his own room perpetually. He's getting past the age for caring for such things. I was surprised to find the old lady falling into the current, carried away by her daughter's enthusiasm for orange-blossoms and lace. I thought Mrs. Thornton had been made of sterner stuff."

"She would put on any assumption of feeling to veil her daughter's weakness," Margaret said in a low voice.

"Perhaps so. You've studied her, have you? She doesn't seem over fond of you, Margaret."

"I know it," Margaret said. "Oh, here is tea, at last!" she exclaimed, as if relieved.

With the arrival of tea came Mr. Henry Lennox, who had walked up after a late dinner, evidently expecting to find his brother and sister-in-law at home. Margaret suspected he was as thankful as she was for the presence of a third party, on this their first meeting since the memorable day of his offer and her refusal at Helstone. She could hardly find words at first, grateful for the tea-table occupations that gave her an excuse for silence and him an opportunity to recover. To tell the truth, he had forced himself to Harley Street to get over an awkward meeting, doubly awkward now that he found her the only lady there. She was the first to recover her composure.

"Mr. Lennox, I have been so much obliged to you for all you have done about Frederick."

"I am only sorry it has been so unsuccessful," he replied, with a quick glance toward Mr. Bell, reconnoitering how much he might say. Margaret, reading his thought, addressed herself to Mr. Bell, including him in the conversation.

"That Horrocks—the very last witness of all—has proved as unavailing as all the others. Mr. Lennox has discovered that he sailed for Australia only last August; only two months before Frederick was in England, and gave us the names of—"

"Frederick in England! you never told me that!" Mr. Bell exclaimed in surprise.

"I thought you knew. I never doubted you had been told. Of course, it was a great secret, and perhaps I should not have named it now," Margaret said, a little dismayed.

"I have never named it to either my brother or your cousin," Mr. Lennox replied with professional dryness.

"Never mind, Margaret. I am not living in a talking, babbling world, nor among people trying to worm facts out of me; you needn't look so frightened because you have let the cat out of the bag to a faithful old hermit like me. I shall never name his having been in England; I shall be out of temptation, for no one will ask me. Stay!" He interrupted himself abruptly. "Was it at your mother's funeral?"

"He was with mamma when she died," Margaret said softly.

"To be sure! To be sure! Why, some one asked me if he had not been over then, and I denied it stoutly—not many weeks ago—who could it have been? Oh! I recollect!"

But he did not say the name. Although Margaret would have given much to know if her suspicions were right, and it had been Mr. Thornton who had made the inquiry, she could not ask Mr. Bell, much as she longed to.

There was a pause. Then Mr. Lennox said, "I suppose as Mr. Bell is now acquainted with all the circumstances attending your brother's unfortunate dilemma, I cannot do better than inform him exactly how the research into the evidence we once hoped to produce in his favour stands at present. So, if he will do me the honour to breakfast with me to-morrow, we will go over the names of these missing gentry."

"I should like to hear all the particulars, if I may. Cannot you come here? I dare not ask you both to breakfast, though I am sure you would be welcome. But let me know all you can about Frederick, even though there may be no hope at present."

"I have an engagement at half-past eleven. But I will certainly come if you wish it," Mr. Lennox replied with a willingness that made Margaret shrink into herself, wishing she had not proposed her natural request. Mr. Bell got up to look for his hat.

"Well!" he said, "I don't know what Mr. Lennox is inclined to do, but I'm disposed to be moving off homewards. I've been a journey to-day, and journeys begin to tell upon my sixty and odd years."

"I believe I shall stay and see my brother and sister," Mr. Lennox said, making no movement of departure. Margaret was seized with a shy dread of being left alone with him. The scene on the little terrace in the Helstone garden was so present to her that she could hardly believe it wasn't so with him.

"Don't go yet, please Mr. Bell," she said hastily. "I want you to see Edith; and I want Edith to know you. Please!" She laid a light but determined hand on his arm. He looked at her, seeing the confusion stirring in her countenance, and sat down again, as if her little touch possessed resistless strength.

"You see how she overpowers me, Mr. Lennox," he said. "And I hope you noticed the happy choice of her expressions; she wants me to 'see' this cousin Edith, who, I am told, is a great beauty; but she has the honesty to change her word when she comes to me—Mrs. Lennox is to 'know' me. I suppose I am not much to 'see,' eh, Margaret?"

He joked to give her time to recover from the slight flutter he had detected, and she caught the tone, throwing the ball back. Mr. Lennox wondered how his brother, the Captain, could have reported her as having lost all her good looks. In her quiet black dress, she was a contrast to Edith, dancing in white crape mourning with long floating golden hair. She dimpled and blushed becomingly when introduced to Mr. Bell, conscious that she had her reputation as a beauty to keep up. Mrs. Shaw and Captain Lennox, each in their separate way, gave Mr. Bell a kind welcome, winning him over almost in spite of himself, especially when he saw how naturally Margaret took her place as sister and daughter of the house
