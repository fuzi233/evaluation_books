For days after Tito’s quiet request to clear the library, he saw barely anything of Romola. The next morning, when he found her seated in her private room beside the little painted household tabernacle, he spoke to her soft and gentle, explaining that agents would soon arrive to pack up all the antiquities her father had spent his life collecting, and it would be far more peaceful for her to stay tucked away in this small, familiar chamber, far from the noise of strange footsteps tramping through the rest of the house. Romola said no word of protest, no sign of anger; the night before had been one long, unbroken stretch of wakefulness, and even with her robust, healthy frame, every sensation in her bones had curdled into a dull, constant ache, as if she had been stunned and bruised all over.

Tito guessed she was unwell, but dared not press her for more than that quiet assent. When he noticed her hand and brow were stone cold, he slipped away to fetch a thick furred mantle, and draped it lightly over her shoulders without another word. Every short interval he stole home between errands, he tried to win her back to him with small, unobtrusive acts of softness: a handful of the candied citron she had loved as a girl, a new small devotional print to prop beside the tabernacle, a quiet murmur that the packers were making good progress, that the noise would be over soon. But she seemed to have lost the ability to speak to him, or even to lift her eyes to his face. *Patience*, he told himself over and over. She would get over this hurt, forgive him at last. The bond between them was still the strongest thing in her life. When the person who strikes a blow suffers no bruise of their own, while the one struck lingers in their pain instead of acting as if nothing has happened, it is only too easy for the striker to slip into the role of the aggrieved party, smugly conscious of how amiable and gentle they have been ever since the harm was done. Tito was not naturally inclined to nurse that petty grievance, though: his whole instinct was pointed towards propitiation, and he would have endured a great deal just to feel Romola’s hand rest lightly on his head again, the way it had that morning he had first been too cowardly to meet her eyes.

It grew easier and easier for him to wait patiently for that return of domestic peace, because his life outside the house was growing more absorbing, more thrilling, by the hour. A course of action that seems the deliberate, premeditated work of a whole life’s character can almost always trace its apparent origin back to one single sharp, defining impression. Ever since that moment in the Piazza del Duomo, when Tito had perched atop a stack of trade bales and tasted the sharp, bright pleasure of realizing he could wrap any phrase in a shape that would make any crowd cheer him, his imagination had been turning over the thought of political activity, exactly the kind of activity Florence’s current unrest was certain to create openings for. The dread of Baldassarre, woken that same exact moment, had lain like an immovable boulder across that path, pushing him to sell the library as a safety net for the day he might be forced to flee Florence, just when he was starting to feel the city hold a new, irresistible attraction. Now that dread was nearly gone. He would still keep his guard up, still keep his wits sharp for any demand for quick thinking or coolness, but he no longer felt forced to take the inconvenient step of running from Florence to hunt for new fortune. The old man had rejected the atonement Tito had offered, forced him into open defiance; a lost old foreigner in a strange city, his memory frayed to nothing, was weak enough to defy with no real risk.

Tito’s half-hidden unspoken desires were hardening now into clear, definite plans. As the hot flush of young romantic passion faded, life was coming more and more to feel like a game to him, a perfectly pleasant mix of practiced skill and lucky chance. And the game unfolding in Florence promised to be fast, fierce, electrifying: a game of revolution and factional clash, full of all those unspoken, unacknowledged maneuvers where a sharp, brilliant ingenuity that casts aside every inconvenient scruple save the obvious truth that “ginger is hot in the mouth” sees its own path as the height of superior wisdom.

No sooner had the last of the French guests marched out of the city gates than Florence buzzed with the restlessness of an anthill after a terrifying shadow has lifted, every scurrying worker rushing to repair the damage done while the dark hung over them. *How are we to raise the ransom money we owe the French king? How are we to crush those stubborn Pisan rebels who have broken away from us? Most of all, how do we rewrite our whole system of government, to make sure our magistrates are chosen fairly and our laws are passed without half the city rioting in protest?* Until those questions were answered, all trade risked grinding to a halt, and the huge mass of working men who were not even counted as citizens, who had no vote to distract them from their empty bellies, were certain to grow impatient. Something had to be done, and fast.

First, the great bell of the Palazzo della Signoria was rung, summoning every citizen to a public parliament in the Piazza de’ Signori. When the crowd was packed so tight no one could move, and armed men were posted at every exit to seal the square off, the Signoria — the ruling Gonfaloniere and his eight sitting Priors — stepped out onto the stone platform beside the carved marble lion in front of the Old Palace, and made their proposal. Twenty of the city’s most respected leading men would be granted full dictatorial authority, for a term of one full year, during which they would appoint every magistrate and restructure the entire framework of government to set things right. The crowd roared their assent, cheering loud enough to rattle the windows of the palace, and felt as if they themselves had freely elected that Council of Twenty. It was the same old Florentine trick, the ancient custom that made the narrow will of the small ruling few look like the free, chosen desire of the many.

The shouting in the square died away quickly, but the debate inside the palace stretched on for days, far into the small hours of every night. Should Florence adopt the Great Council model used by Venice, where all qualified citizens of a certain age could vote to elect all government officers and pass all laws, no matter their rank or party loyalty, no favor granted to old noble lineages? Or should the city be governed under a narrower, more exclusive system, where the hereditary influence of long established wealthy families would not be watered down by the votes of ordinary shopkeepers? Learned doctors of law argued back and forth endlessly: Messer Pagolantonio Soderini laid out a mountain of excellent, airtight reasons in favor of the popular Great Council, while Messer Guidantonio Vespucci laid out an equally unassailable stack of reasons in favor of the more aristocratic form of rule. It was the old, trivial argument over whether to boil meat or roast it, already decided long before the debate began by the personal appetites of the men arguing, and all that brilliant rhetoric would have dragged on indefinitely if there had not been a force outside the palace walls pushing the vague discontent of the majority of disenfranchised Florentines into a concrete, determined will.

That force was the preaching of Savonarola. Driven half by the absolute spiritual conviction laid upon him to guide his people to good, half prompted by public leaders who could not get any popular measure passed without his backing, his daily sermons had rapidly shifted from general exhortations for all citizens to set aside private passion and personal interest for the public good, to explicit, concrete demands: he did not just tell his flock to choose whatever was best for the whole community, he told them exactly what government they must have to achieve that good. *Choose the Great Council*, he thundered from the pulpit. The Great Council is the will of God. For Fra Girolamo, those two statements were one and the same. The Great Council was the only workable plan to give the public will a voice large enough to drown out the corrupting influence of petty factional interests, the only system that could make honest, impartial public action even possible. The purer Florence’s government became, the more secure from the designs of men who profited from the moral degradation of their neighbors, the closer the Florentine people would come to being a pure, holy community, worthy to lead the whole Christian world in the renovation of the Church and the renewal of all human society. Fra Girolamo’s mind never stopped short of that most sublime of ends: every goal he worked towards held that same immense moral magnificence. He nursed no private grudges, sought no petty personal gratification. Even in those final terrible days to come, when ignominy, torture, and the threat of more torture had laid bare every hidden weakness of his soul, he would say to the judges who hounded him for endless lists of names and confessions: “Do not marvel that I have told so few things. The purposes I held were few, and great.”
