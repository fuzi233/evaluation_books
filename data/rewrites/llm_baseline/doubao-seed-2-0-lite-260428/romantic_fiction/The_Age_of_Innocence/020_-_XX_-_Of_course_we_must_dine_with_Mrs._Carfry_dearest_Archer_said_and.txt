"Of course we must dine with Mrs. Carfry, dearest,” Newland Archer murmured, tapping the edge of her teacup gently across the hulking, polished Britannia ware that dominated their lodging house breakfast table, as fat raindrops streaked the fog-frosted London panes behind them.
Across the table, his wife lifted her gaze with a small, anxious frown, all soft newlywed sweetness tangled in quiet uncertainty. The whole of autumnal London felt like a damp, empty desert, and the only two compatriots they knew in the sprawling city were the pair of English sisters his mother and Janey had befriended years prior at a Tyrolean inn. They had avoided reaching out to them entirely, after all: old New York’s unwritten code held it was never “dignified” to force one’s acquaintance on foreigners while traveling.
Mrs. Archer and Janey had upheld that principle with unflinching rigor across decades of European trips, their reserved, impenetrable guard so firmly in place they could boast of barely having exchanged a word with any stranger outside hotel and railway staff. Fellow Americans not pre-vetted by the Mingott, Dagonet or Chivers clans were met with even stiffer disdain, making their months abroad little more than endless, quiet tête-à-têtes—until a rainy night in Botzen, when a knock came at their door. The English lady across the hall, Mrs. Carfry, had been struck by a sudden, brutal bronchitis attack, and her sister Miss Harle had come begging for liniment. The Archer ladies, who never traveled without a full family pharmacy, not only gave them the remedy but nursed the sick woman back to health with endless little thoughtful comforts, their lady’s maid tending her through the worst of the fever.
The Archers had left Botzen never expecting to cross paths with the two sisters again. To Mrs. Archer’s mind, nothing could be more undignified than to intrude on a foreigner to whom one had accidentally done a small kindness. But Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle, who found that New York reserve utterly incomprehensible, clung to that chance bond like a precious gift, tracking the Archer ladies down at every continental stop, showing supernatural sharpness in learning when they would pass through London en route to or from New York. The intimacy grew unbreakable: they shared a love of ferns kept in sealed Wardian cases, the craft of macramé lace, the memoirs of Baroness Bunsen, and strong opinions about the preachers at London’s most famous pulpits. By the time Newland got engaged, the families were so close that the two English ladies received a wedding invitation, and sent in return a dainty glass-enclosed bouquet of pressed Alpine flowers. Mrs. Archer’s very last parting order on the New York docks, as Newland and May boarded their steamer for their wedding tour, had been: “You simply must take May to call on Mrs. Carfry.”
They had both fully intended to ignore that gentle injunction, but Mrs. Carfry, with her usual uncanny knack for tracking people down, had found their lodgings and sent a formal dinner invitation—and it was over that very slip of cream vellum that May was now wrinkling her soft, fair brow.
“It’s all very well for you, Newland, you know them,” she said, pouting a little over her muffin. “But I shall feel so shy among a house full of people I’ve never met. And whatever am I supposed to wear?”
Newland leaned back in his chair and smiled at her, marveling at how the soft, moisture-laden London air had deepened the rose in her cheeks, softened the sharp, virginal edge of her features until she looked more Diana-like than he had ever seen her, the glow of her wedded happiness shining through like a lantern lit under clear ice.
“Wear, dearest? I thought a whole trunk full of new gowns had arrived from Paris last week,” he teased.
“Yes, of course,” she huffed, nudging the edge of her plate. “I mean I shan’t know which one to pick. I’ve never dined out in London. I won’t have them thinking I look ridiculous.”
He leaned forward, pretending to ponder her perplexity. “But aren’t Englishwomen dressing just like everyone else for evening, these days?”
“Newland, how absurd!” she laughed. “You’ve seen them troop to the theatre in old, faded ball gowns, their heads completely bare of bonnets or even lace veils!”
“Ah, but Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle certainly won’t be wearing anything half so bold,” he reassured her. “They’ll turn up in soft silk shawls and little frilled lace caps, exactly like the ones my mother wears at home.”
“But what about the other guests?”
“None of them will be half as lovely as you, dear,” he told her, amused to see she had picked up Janey’s old, morbid obsession with dressing just right for every possible social situation.
She pushed back her chair with a sigh. “That’s sweet of you, but it doesn’t solve my problem.”
He had a sudden, bright idea. “Why not wear your wedding gown? That can’t possibly be wrong, can it?”
“Oh, dearest, if only it were here!” she groaned. “I sent it to Paris last week to have Worth alter it for the winter season, and he hasn’t shipped it back yet.”
“Then look,” he said, standing and drawing back the curtain to show the fog outside was thinning to pale silver. “We can dash for the National Gallery before the light fades, and catch a glimpse of the Turners before we have to worry about dinner at all.”
Their three-month wedding tour had been blissful, as May wrote to all her old girlhood friends, though they had skipped the Italian Lakes Newland had half dreamed of visiting—he had realized quickly he could not picture bright, athletic May among the cypress groves and sun-warmed marble. After a month of fittings in Paris with her dressmakers, they had followed her own itinerary: mountaineering in July around Interlaken and Grindelwald, then August at the quiet, tiny Norman fishing village of Etretat, where May spent whole days swimming, riding, and trying her hand at the new, wildly fashionable game of lawn tennis. Once or twice in the mountains Newland had pointed south over the peaks and said “That’s Italy over there,” and May, her feet tucked in a bed of bright blue gentians, had smiled cheerfully and said they could visit there next winter—if only he did not have to be back in New York for his law practice.
Truth be told, traveling interested her far less than he had expected, once all her new clothes were ordered. The only parts of London that captured her attention at all were the shops and the theatres, though even the theatres paled next to the Paris open-air cafes they had visited under blossoming horse chestnuts on the Champs-Élysées, where she had leaned against his side on the terrace to listen to the music while he carefully translated only the most innocent lines of the songs for her new bridal ears.
Newland had found himself slipping gently back into all the old inherited habits of thought he had dallied with in his bachelor days, chasing all those grand half-formed theories about emancipating his wife and building a marriage free of New York’s stale constraints. It was far simpler, far kinder, to treat May exactly as all his friends treated their own wives—for May had not the faintest idea that she was not free, and the only liberty she would ever choose to take would be to lay it reverently on the altar of her wifely adoration. Her innate dignity would never let that devotion feel abject, and he knew well there might come a day when she would find the strength to pull that gift entirely back, if she thought it was for his own good. But with her uncomplicated, gentle view of marriage, that crisis could only ever come if he did something visibly, unforgivably wrong—and he could not imagine ever hurting her that way. Whatever came, she would always be loyal, gallant, and unresentful, and that pledged him to the same virtues.
His artistic, intellectual life would go on outside the domestic sphere, he told himself. Coming home to her would never feel like stepping into a stuffy, airless room after a long walk in the open, and once they had children, all the empty little corners in both their lives would be filled.
These thoughts hummed softly in his head as their hansom cab trundled slow from Mayfair down to South Kensington, where the Carfrys lived. Even in London, Newland had kept to the old family tradition of traveling as a quiet, unobtrusive sightseer, affecting a haughty unawareness of the other people around him. He had run across the Duke of St. Austrey not long after arriving, and the duke had invited him to visit—but no self-respecting New Yorker would ever consider that a serious invitation, so they had avoided that acquaintance entirely, along with May’s English aunt, the banker’s wife who was still up in Yorkshire, purposefully delaying their arrival in London until autumn so they would not look pushing and snobbish to those distant unknown relatives.
“Probably no one else will even be there,” he said to May, glancing at where she sat beside him, spotless and glowing in her sky-blue cloak edged with soft swansdown, too perfect for the soot-streaked London streets. “The whole of London is empty this time of year, and you’ve made yourself far too beautiful for any ordinary dinner party.”
“I won’t have them thinking Americans dress like savages,” she replied, with a quiet, quiet conviction that struck him all at once—this was the secret armor of even the most unworldly American women, he realized, their defense against the unknown, their gentle defiance of it. That was why May had gone through the solemn, weeks-long ritual of picking and ordering her entire new Paris wardrobe, even though she would never have tied a single ribbon in her hair just to charm him.
He was right that the party was small, in the long, dim, chilly drawing room of the Carfrys’ South Kensington home: only their hostess, Miss Harle, another shawled lady and her genial vicar husband, a quiet pale young man Mrs. Carfry introduced as her nephew, and a small, dark, lively-eyed gentleman with a French name, who was the nephew’s tutor.
May floated into the dim group like a swan strayed into a twilight parlour, her skirts rustling voluminously, her cheeks pink from shyness so deep it felt almost infantile. Newland could read the helpless question in her eyes, What on earth will they expect me to talk about? and the vicar and the young tutor both immediately set to work, gallantly trying to put her at her ease.
Even so, the dinner was a languishing, quiet affair. Newland noticed May’s go-to trick for feeling comfortable around strangers: she leaned entirely into her most uncompromising New York references, talking of Fifth Avenue parties and Mingott family dinners so that, for all her loveliness, her conversation left little room for light repartee. The vicar gave up the effort fairly quickly, and the ladies soon excused themselves back to the drawing room, to the unspoken relief of everyone at the table.
The vicar left shortly after for a parish meeting, the sickly young nephew was packed off to bed, and soon Newland and the French tutor, Monsieur Rivière, were sitting alone over their port, talking more freely than Newland had talked since his last long late-night symposium with Ned Winsett back in New York.
He learned that the young tutor’s father had died young, a low-ranking diplomat, and Rivière had been meant to follow the same career, only his insatiable hunger for books had led him first to journalism, then to unpublished authorship, and finally to tutoring wealthy English boys in Switzerland. He had haunted the Goncourts’ garret in Paris, Maupassant had once told him plainly not to waste his time trying to write fiction, he had talked for hours with Mérimée in his mother’s small Paris drawing room. He had always been desperately poor, supporting his mother and unmarried sister, and his literary ambitions had all but failed—but he told Newland, with bright, passionate eyes, that he would never trade his intellectual liberty for any material comfort.
“The air of ideas is the only air worth breathing, don’t you see?” he said, lighting another cigarette. “To be able to look life in the face, no strings attached—worth living in a garret for, isn’t it? But sometimes I wonder… would there be any work for me, do you think, in New York?”
Newland stared at him, perplexed, wondering how to tell this man who had sat at the feet of all Paris’s greatest literary minds that New York, his own city, had no place for someone who lived for good conversation, not profit. He stammered out a noncommittal reply, and Rivière quickly dropped the subject, rising to lead him back to the drawing room without a trace of awkwardness.
On the drive home, Newland pondered the conversation deeply. That hour with Rivière had filled his lungs with fresh, sharp new air, and his first impulse had been to invite the man to dine with them the very next day. But when he tentatively mentioned that the tutor was a fascinating man, full of interesting talk about books and art, May roused herself from her soft, dreamy silence and looked at him with cold, quiet surprise.
“That little Frenchman? Wasn’t he dreadfully common?” she said. He could see the faint, secret disappointment in her: it was not snobbery, not quite, but the old unspoken New York sense that if you risked your dignity to host or be hosted by foreign acquaintances, you ought to be offered a better guest list than a country vicar and a private tutor. If her parents had invited the Carfrys to Fifth Avenue, they would have laid on a far grander dinner than that.
“Common? Common where?” he asked, a little sharper than he meant to.
“Why, anywhere but inside his schoolroom,” she answered. “Those sorts of people are always so awkward in society. But I suppose I wouldn’t know if he was very clever anyway,” she added, disarming him with a soft smile.
Newland hated the way she used the word “clever” almost as much as he hated the word “common” falling from her lips—but he had begun to fear his own habit of fixating on all the little things about her he did not like. After all, her perspective was exactly the same as every single person he had grown up around, a perspective he had long dismissed as negligible, unimportant, something he could ignore around the edges of his own life.
“Ah, then I won’t invite him to dine,” he laughed.
May blinked at him, bewildered. “Goodness—invite the Carfrys’ tutor? Why on earth would you do that?”
“He’s looking for work in New York,” he said.
“What sort of work? People don’t hire random French tutors for no reason,” she said, indifferent, and laughed when he told her Rivière mostly wanted to move there for the good conversation. “Newland, that’s so funny! How very French of him.”
He was, in the end, quietly relieved that she had dismissed the idea out of hand. He never would have been able to fit M. Rivière into the rigid, predictable world of old New York he was returning to. He felt a cold, sharp flash of insight then: so many of the small problems he would face in his marriage would be solved this way, passively, without any argument. He told himself, as he paid the hansom driver and followed her long train up the steps to their lodgings, that everyone said the first six months of marriage were the hardest, that they were still just rubbing off each other’s sharp little angles. But the worst of it, he thought, the quiet, unnameable ache under his happiness, was that May’s gentle, steady pressure was already wearing away at the very sharp, bright edges of his own curiosity, the very parts of himself he had most wanted to keep.
