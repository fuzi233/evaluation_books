We stepped out of the cavern, blinking in the sudden brightness, feeling absurdly foolish. The cold air bit at our faces, and the weight of exhaustion pressed down on us like a physical force.

“I’m going back,” Sir Henry declared abruptly.

“Why?” asked Good.

“Because it just occurred to me—what we saw… might be my brother.”

That stopped us all. A new possibility, sharp and chilling, had pierced through our weariness. We turned and re-entered the cave, stepping cautiously into the oppressive gloom. After the blinding glare outside, our eyes struggled to adjust, blurred by fatigue and the lingering afterimage of snow. But slowly, shapes began to emerge from the shadows.

Sir Henry knelt beside the body, peering closely at the face.

“Thank God,” he breathed, relief washing over him, “it’s not my brother.”

I moved forward, drawn by an almost magnetic pull. The corpse was that of a tall man, middle-aged, with strong aquiline features, grizzled hair, and a long black mustache. His skin was yellowed, stretched tight over his bones like parchment over a frame. His clothes had been stripped away—except for what remained of woolen hose—leaving only the skeletal form exposed. Around his neck, frozen stiff, hung a yellow ivory crucifix.

“Who on earth could this be?” I whispered.

“Can’t you guess?” Good asked, his voice low but edged with excitement.

I shook my head.

“José da Silvestra, the old Dom, of course—who else?”

“That’s impossible,” I gasped. “He died three hundred years ago!”

“And what stops him from lasting three thousand in this climate?” Good countered. “If the temperature is low enough, flesh can preserve itself as well as New Zealand mutton. And here? It’s colder than the heart of winter. No sun reaches this place. No animals come near. Nothing disturbs it. No doubt his servant—the one mentioned in the writing—took off his clothes before he died. He couldn’t have buried him alone.” He bent down, picking up a strangely shaped bone, sharpened at one end. “Look—this is the ‘cleft bone’ Silvestra used to draw his map.”

We stared, stunned. The realization settled over us like frost: this was the man whose words had led us across deserts and mountains. Here was the very tool he’d used to write them. And around his neck, the crucifix he’d kissed with his dying breath.

A shiver ran through me—not from cold, but from awe. I could almost see the final moments: the explorer, starving, freezing, yet driven to leave a message for the world. The loneliness of it—the silence, the emptiness—was written in every line of the dead man’s face. I even thought I saw a resemblance to Silvestre, my friend who had died twenty years prior, though perhaps that was merely imagination. Still, there he sat, a monument to the fate that awaits those who dare enter the unknown—a silent sentinel, frozen in time, waiting for the next wanderer to stumble upon his grave.

The sight overwhelmed us, already weakened by hunger and cold.

“Let’s go,” Sir Henry said quietly. Then, after a pause, “Wait—we’ll give him a companion.” He lifted the body of the Hottentot Ventvögel and placed it beside the old Dom. Then, fingers numb and clumsy, he tore the string holding the crucifix, unable to untie it. I believe he still has it. I took the bone pen—it rests before me now, sometimes used to sign my name.

Leaving the two men—one noble, one humble—to keep eternal watch among the snows, we crept out into the sunlight and resumed our journey, each of us wondering how many hours would pass before we too were as still as they.

After walking half a mile, we reached the edge of the plateau. The mountain peak rose slightly off-center, not directly from the middle, though from the desert side it had seemed otherwise. Below us, the landscape was veiled in morning fog. But gradually, the higher mist lifted, revealing a patch of green grass five hundred yards below, threaded by a running stream. And beyond that—ten to fifteen large antelopes, basking in the sun.

Joy surged through us, unreasoning and fierce. Food—plenty of it—if only we could reach it. But six hundred yards was a long shot, especially when our lives depended on it.

We debated stalking them, but dismissed the idea. The wind was wrong. And against the blinding white snow, we’d be seen no matter how carefully we moved.

“Well,” said Sir Henry, “we’ll try from here. Repeating rifles or expresses?”

Another dilemma. Our Winchesters were sighted for a thousand yards. The expresses, only three hundred fifty—beyond that, luck. But if they hit, the expanding bullets were more likely to bring the animal down. I decided we had to take the risk.

“Each of us takes the buck opposite,” I said. “Aim high, just behind the shoulder. Umbopa, give the signal so we fire together.”

We paused, each aiming with desperate care. Life hung on this shot.

“Fire!” Umbopa called in Zulu.

Three rifles cracked simultaneously. Smoke curled upward, echoes ricocheted across the snow. Then, through the haze—joy! A great buck lay on its back, kicking wildly in death throes.

We roared in triumph. We were saved. We wouldn’t starve.

Weak as we were, we rushed down the slope. Within ten minutes, the heart and liver were laid out before us. But then came a new problem: no fuel. No fire. No way to cook.

“Starving men shouldn’t be picky,” Good said grimly. “We eat raw meat.”

No other choice. Hunger dulled any disgust. We buried the organs in snow to cool them, washed them in icy water, then devoured them greedily. Horrible as it sounds, I’ve never tasted anything better. In less than fifteen minutes, strength returned. Our pulses quickened, blood flowed warm again. But we stopped before fullness—knowing the danger of overeating on empty stomachs.

“Thank Heaven,” Sir Henry said. “That beast saved us. What kind of animal was it, Quatermain?”

I went to examine it. About the size of a donkey, with curved horns, brown fur, faint red stripes, thick coat. Unfamiliar. Later I learned the natives call them *inco*—rare creatures found only at high altitudes where no other game survives. The bullet had struck high in the shoulder—whose shot brought it down, we couldn’t tell. But Good, remembering his perfect aim at the giraffe, probably believed it was his. We didn’t argue.

We’d been too busy eating to notice our surroundings. Now, with Umbopa cutting meat for carrying, we looked around. The mist had vanished—eight o’clock, the sun had burned it away. Before us unfolded a panorama beyond description.

Behind us, Sheba’s snowy peaks rose like ancient sentinels. Below, five thousand feet down, lay a vast, fertile land. Forests stood dense and dark; a silver river wound through the valley. To the left, rolling grasslands stretched endlessly, dotted with herds—perhaps cattle, perhaps game—too far to distinguish. Mountains ringed the horizon. To the right, hills rose from the plain, with cultivated fields and dome-shaped huts nestled between.

It was breathtaking. Rivers flashed like silver snakes. Snow-capped peaks twisted like frozen lace. Sunlight bathed everything in golden life.

Two things struck us: first, this land was at least three thousand feet higher than the desert we’d crossed. Second, all rivers flowed south to north. We knew the southern side of the range held no water. But here, on the northern face, streams poured down, feeding the great river we could now trace far into the distance.

We sat in silence, awed.

Then Sir Henry spoke.

“Isn’t there something about Solomon’s Great Road on the map?”

I nodded, still gazing.

“Look,” he said, pointing slightly to the right.

We turned—and there it was: a wide, paved road winding toward the plain. At first we hadn’t seen it because it disappeared behind broken terrain.

“It must be close,” Good said. “Shouldn’t we start?”

Good advice. After washing our faces in the stream, we set off. For miles we walked over boulders and patches of snow, until suddenly, at the top of a rise, the road lay beneath us.

It was magnificent—cut from solid rock, fifty feet wide, seemingly well-maintained. Yet it appeared to begin right there. Behind us, toward Sheba’s Breasts, it vanished entirely—only boulders and snow remained.

“What do you make of this, Quatermain?” Sir Henry asked.

I shook my head. I had no answer.

“I’ve got it!” Good exclaimed. “The road once crossed the entire range and continued into the desert—but sand covered it. Above us, lava flows buried it.”

Plausible. We accepted it and continued downward.

Descending on the road was vastly different from climbing over snow—especially with full stomachs. If not for the memory of Ventvögel’s fate and the grim cave, we might have felt cheerful. Each mile, the air grew warmer, softer. The land shimmered with beauty. The road itself was an engineering marvel—comparable, Sir Henry said, to the St. Gothard Pass in Switzerland.

At one point, we came to a ravine three hundred feet wide, a hundred deep. It was filled with massive dressed stones, arches carved beneath for water flow. The road passed over it as if nothing had been there.

Elsewhere, it zigzagged down cliffs five hundred feet high. Elsewhere, it tunneled through ridges—thirty yards long.

Inside the tunnel, the walls bore strange carvings: armored figures in chariots. One scene showed a battle, captives marched away.

“Well,” Sir Henry said after studying it, “they may call it Solomon’s Road, but I suspect the Egyptians—or Phoenicians—were here first. This looks like their work.”

By midday, we reached woodlands. Scattered bushes gave way to a vast grove of silver trees—like those on Table Mountain at Cape Town. I’d never seen them outside the Cape. Their presence here amazed me.

“Ah!” Good said, delighted. “Wood! Let’s stop and cook. I’ve digested that raw heart.”

No objections. We left the road, found a nearby stream, built a fire. We cut chunks from the *inco*, skewered them on sticks, and roasted them Kafir-style. We ate heartily, then lit pipes and relaxed.

The brook sang beside us, banks thick with giant maidenhair ferns and wild asparagus. Soft air rustled through silver leaves. Doves cooed. Bright birds darted like jewels. It felt like paradise.

The magic of the place, combined with the sense of danger left behind, lulled us into quiet. Sir Henry and Umbopa talked softly in broken English and Zulu. I lay back on the fragrant ferns, watching.

Then I noticed Good was gone. I looked—there he was, by the stream, bathing. Only his flannel shirt remained. His natural fastidiousness had returned. He’d washed his gutta-percha collar, shaken out trousers, coat, waistcoat—folded them neatly, shaking his head at the tears. He scrubbed his boots with fern, rubbed them with fat from the *inco*, making them look presentable. Then, using a tiny mirror in a pocket comb, he examined himself.

Still unsatisfied. He began grooming his hair. Then paused—felt his chin, where a ten-day beard had grown.

“Surely,” I thought, “he’s not going to shave.”

But he was. He washed the fat from his boots, dipped it in water, pulled out a safety razor. Rubbed his face with the fat, then began shaving.

It was painful. He groaned. I watched, laughing inwardly—how odd, in such a place, to bother with grooming. He managed one side of his face when—suddenly—I saw a flash of light near his head.

Good leapt up, cursing (if it hadn’t been a safety razor, he’d have slit his throat). I jumped too.

And there—no more than twenty paces away—stood a group of men. Tall, copper-skinned. Some wore black feather plumes, leopard-skin cloaks. In front, a youth, seventeen, hand raised, body poised like a Greek statue—just after throwing a spear.

The flash had come from the weapon.

An older man stepped forward, grabbed the youth’s arm, said something. Then they advanced.

Sir Henry, Good, and Umbopa raised rifles, threateningly. The natives kept coming.

“They don’t know what rifles are,” I realized. “Or they’d hesitate.”

“Put down your guns!” I shouted. “Our only chance is peace.”

They obeyed. I stepped forward, addressed the elder.

“Greeting,” I said in Zulu.

To my surprise, he replied—in a dialect so close to Zulu that both Umbopa and I understood it easily. It was an old form, like Chaucerian English compared to modern speech.

“Whence come you?” he asked. “Who are you? Why are three of your faces white, and the fourth like our own?” He pointed to Umbopa.

I glanced at him. Indeed—he looked like these men. Same build, same features.

“We are strangers,” I answered slowly. “We come in peace. This man is our servant.”

“You lie,” the elder said. “No stranger crosses these mountains—none survive. But why speak lies? If you are strangers, you must die. No strangers live in the land of the Kukuanas. It is the king’s law. Prepare to die, O strangers.”

I was startled—especially as I saw hands slipping to knives at their sides.

“What does he say?” Good asked.

“He says we’re going to be killed,” I replied grimly.

“Oh, Lord!” Good groaned. Then, as always when confused, he touched his false teeth—dragging the upper plate down, letting it snap back.

It was a lucky move.

The Kukuanas shrieked in horror and bolted back.

“What’s happening?” I asked.

“It’s his teeth,” Sir Henry whispered urgently. “He moved them! Take them out, Good!”

Good slipped the dentures into his sleeve.

Curiosity overcame fear. The men crept forward.

“How is it,” the elder asked solemnly, “that this fat man—clothed yet bare-legged, with hair on one side of his face and not the other, with one shining eye—how is it that his teeth move, vanish, and return?”

“Open your mouth,” I told Good.

He grinned—revealing two thin lines of gum, utterly toothless.

The crowd gasped.

“Where are his teeth?” they cried. “We saw them!”

Good turned slowly, swept his hand across his mouth with contemptuous disdain. Then grinned again.

There—two rows of perfect teeth.

The young spear-thrower collapsed, wailing in terror. The elder trembled, knees knocking.

“I see,” he whispered. “Ye are spirits. Never has man born of woman had such a face, such an eye, such teeth that melt and grow again. Pardon us, O lords.”

Luck—pure luck.

I seized the moment.

“It is granted,” I said, smiling imperially. “Nay, ye shall know the truth. We come from another world—though we are men like you. From the biggest star that shines at night.”

“Oh! oh!” they moaned in unison, awestruck.

And so, by the power of illusion, we lived.
