Scarcely had the dust settled on the events of the previous days when news arrived that Margaret Van Eyck was dead and buried. By a will executed a year prior, she had bequeathed all her possessions—after settling funeral costs and granting specific gifts to Reicht Heynes—to her beloved daughter, Margaret Brandt. Her final instruction was clear: she requested that Reicht remain in her service as long as the daughter remained unmarried.

Through this inheritance, Margaret acquired a fully furnished house, alongside a collection of pictures and sketches that, in modern times, would constitute a vast fortune. Among these treasures was one painting she cherished above all others, valuing it more highly than an entire gallery. It depicted "A Betrothal." The solemn gravity of the occasion was etched into the grave expression of the groom, while the bride wore a look of demure complacency. Though largely painted by Margaret Van Eyck herself, the remainder of the masterpiece was the work of Jan. Its accessories were finished with exquisite precision, remaining a marvel of skill to this day.

Margaret Brandt sent word to Reicht to remain in the house until she felt strong enough to step inside, asking him to guard the painting carefully as it rested in a small cupboard—the diptych.

Soon after, Luke Peterson returned home and learned that Gerard had become a monk. He nearly went mad with joy. Rushing to Margaret, he declared, "Take heed, mistress. If he cannot marry you, I can."

"You?" Margaret asked, surprised. "Why, I have seen him."

"But he is a friar!" Luke insisted.

"He was my husband, and the father of my boy, long before he took vows," Margaret retorted firmly. "I have seen him; I have seen him."

Luke was thoroughly confused. "Tell you what," he suggested, "I have a cousin who is a lawyer. I will go ask him whether you are married or single."

"Nay," Margaret snapped. "I shall ask my own heart, not a lawyer. So that is your regard for me? To turn me into the subject of town gossip? Oh, fie!"

"That has already been done without a word from me," Luke muttered.

"But not by someone seeking my respect. And if you proceed, never come near me again."

"Ay," sighed Luke, "you are like a dove to everyone else, but a hard-hearted tyrant to me."

"'Tis your own fault, dear Luke, for wooing me," Margaret explained gently. "That prevents me from being as kind to you as I wish, Luke, my bonny lad, listen. I am rich now; I can make my friends happy, though perhaps not myself. Look around the street, look around the parish. There are many women fairer than I, twice told, and not ruined by weeping. Look higher; choose wisely. Speak to the maiden herself, and I will speak to her mother; they shall not refuse you; take my word for it."

"I see what you mean," Luke said, turning crimson. "But if I cannot have your favor, I want none of your money. I was your servant when you were as poor as I, and poorer. No; if you would rather be a friar's mistress than an honest man's wife, you are not the woman I took you for. Let us part without malice. Seek your comfort on that road where no woman has ever found it, and for me, I will live and die a bachelor. Good evening, mistress."

"Farewell, dear Luke," Margaret said sadly. "And may God forgive you for speaking thus to me."

For several days, Margaret dreaded, almost as much as she desired, the inevitable interview with Gerard. She reasoned to herself, "I wonder he stays away for a while; for so should I." Yet, he knew he was a father, and the longing to see their child would surely overcome all barriers. "And," the poor girl murmured, "if that meeting does not kill me, I feel I shall be better afterward than I am now."

However, as day succeeded day and Gerard remained unheard of, a freezing suspicion began to creep into her mind. Was his absence intentional? Had he gone to some cold-blooded fellow monks who forbade him from seeing her again? The convent had already proven itself as merciless to true lovers as the grave itself. At this thought, the very life seemed to drain from her.

Now, for the first time, deep indignation mingled with her grief and apprehension. "Could he have ever truly loved me?" she wondered. "To flee from me and his own son without a word! Why, this poor Luke thinks more of me than he does."

Just as her mind churned in this turmoil, Giles burst in, shouting, "I've hit the mark! Our Gerard is the Vicar of Gouda!"

A brief sketch of the dwarf's rise at court prepares the reader for his account of this achievement. Months prior, his intelligence had begun to bud. He himself dated the change to a certain eighth of June, when, swinging by one hand along with a week's laundry on a tightrope in the drying yard, something cracked inside his head. Lo! Intellectual powers were unleashed. At court, his shrewdness, blunt speech, gigantic voice, and diminutive stature made him a power; without the last attribute, they might have led him to the gallows. Both the young Duchess of Burgundy and Marie, the heiress apparent, doted on him, as great ladies have always favored dwarfs. The court poet melted butter by the six-foot rule and poured enough down his back to stew Goliath. Even he amplified, versified, and softened certain rough sentences dictated by Giles.

The resulting centipede-like verbosity was sent to Eli by letter, titled: *"The high and puissant Princess Marie of Bourgogne her lytel jantilman hys complaynt of y' Coort, and praise of a rusticall lyfe, versificated, and empapyred by me the lytel jantilman's right lovynge and obsequious servitor, etc."*

But Giles reached his zenith through a happy mixture of mind and muscle. The day before a grand tournament, he challenged the Duke's giant to a trial of strength. This provoked a grimace of amusement and raised expectations.

Giles had a lofty pole erected. At the appointed hour, he ascended it like a squirrel, using the strength of his arm to form a right angle with his body, holding the pose. Then, sliding down so swiftly that the High and Puissant Princess squealed and hid her face in her hands, he escaped before anyone could witness the demise of her pocket-Hercules.

The giant managed only about ten feet, then looked ruefully up and down before descending, bathed in sweat to explain the discrepancy. "It was not the dwarf's greater strength, but his smaller body," he argued.

The spectators received this excuse with loud derision. Here was the fact: the dwarf was great at climbing poles; the giant only great at making excuses. In short, Giles had gauged their intellects—with his own body, no doubt.

"Come," he said, "if you go to that, I'll wrestle you, my lad, if you will let me blindfold your eyes."

Smarting under defeat and hoping to recover honor this way, the giant readily agreed.

"Madam," Giles announced, "do you see yonder blind Samson? At a signal from me, he shall make me a low obeisance and remove his cap."

"How can that be, being blinded?" inquired a maid of honor.

"I'll wager on Giles for one," the princess declared.

"That is my affair."

Once several wagers were placed, Giles struck the giant squarely in the breadbasket. The giant doubled over (the obeisance), and his bonnet fell off.

The company yelled with delight at this delicate stroke of wit, and Giles took to his heels. The giant followed as soon as he could catch his breath and tear off his bandage. But it was too late; Giles had prepared a small door in the wall, wide enough for him but not for a giant, colored so artfully it resembled a section of the wall. "When the princess summoned me to her bedroom, as was her custom, to keep her spirits up, I did not arrive merry or full of country ditties, as is my wont, but dull as lead.

"'Why, what ails thee?' she asked. 'Are you sick?'

'"At heart,' I replied.

'"Alas, he is in love,' she said. Whereupon five brazen hussies, whom they called maids of honor, giggled loudly.

'"Not so mad as that,' I said, 'considering what I see at court regarding women folk.'

'"There, ladies,' the princess interjected, 'let him be. He is a liberal manikin and still gives more than he takes in saucy words.'
