The final social engagement of their time in town was a brief visit to Harley Street, where Elinor accepted her brother's congratulations on their successful journey toward Barton without financial strain, and learned that Colonel Brandon intended to accompany them to Cleveland within the next couple of days. This concluded the formal interactions between the siblings in London. What followed were only faint invitations: Fanny suggested they drop by Norland if their path ever crossed it—a prospect deemed highly improbable—and John offered Elinor a warmer, though private, promise of his own swift arrival at Delaford. These were the sole hints of future reunions in the countryside. It was amusing to Elinor how everyone seemed determined to send her to Delaford; yet, of all places, it was the one she least wished to visit or inhabit. Not only did her brother and Mrs. Jennings regard it as her destined home, but even Lucy, upon their parting, pressed an urgent invitation for her to stay there.

Very early in April, the two groups departed from their respective residences in Hanover Square and Berkeley Street, agreeing to meet en route. To accommodate Charlotte and her infant, the journey would take more than two days, while Mr. Palmer, traveling faster alongside Colonel Brandon, planned to join them shortly after their arrival at Cleveland. Marianne, having spent so few comfortable hours in London and longing so desperately to escape it, nonetheless felt profound pain bidding farewell to the house where she had last harbored her hopes and trust in Willoughby—hopes and trust now extinguished forever. She also struggled to leave the city where Willoughby remained, busy with new engagements and schemes from which she was excluded, without weeping. Conversely, Elinor's relief at leaving was absolute. She held no lingering attachments that caused regret, parted from no one she loved, and welcomed freedom from the suffocating pressure of Lucy's friendship. Grateful for the chance to depart unseen by Willoughby since his marriage, she looked forward with hope to the tranquility of Barton, confident it would restore Marianne's peace and solidify her own.

Their journey proceeded smoothly. On the second day, they entered Somerset, a county alternately cherished and dreaded in Marianne's imagination. By the morning of the third, they reached Cleveland. It was a spacious, modern estate set upon a sloping lawn, lacking a park but boasting extensive pleasure grounds. A smooth gravel road wound around a plantation, leading to the front door, while the lawn was scattered with timber. The house itself was shielded by a dense screen of firs, mountain-ashes, and acacias, interspersed with towering Lombardy poplars that hid the service buildings.

Marianne entered the house with a heart swelling with emotion, knowing she was barely eighty miles from Barton and less than thirty from Combe Magna. Yet, within five minutes of stepping inside, while the others assisted Charlotte in settling her child with the housekeeper, she slipped away. She wandered through the winding shrubberies, which were just beginning to display their spring beauty, seeking a distant hilltop. From a nearby Grecian temple, her gaze swept across the vast landscape to the southeast, resting fondly on the farthest ridges of the horizon, imagining that from those peaks, Combe Magna might be visible. In these moments of exquisite misery, she rejoiced in tears to be at Cleveland. Returning via a different path, savoring the luxury of solitary country freedom, she resolved to spend nearly every hour of her stay exploring the grounds alone.

She returned just in time to join the group for an excursion through the immediate grounds. The rest of the morning passed leisurely as they toured the kitchen garden, admiring the blooms on the walls, listening to the gardener's complaints about blight, dawdled through the greenhouse (where Charlotte laughed at the loss of favorite plants damaged by lingering frost), and visited the poultry yard. Here, the dairy-maid's disappointments—hens abandoning nests, thefts by foxes, or dwindling broods—provided fresh amusement.

The morning had been fine and dry, and Marianne had not anticipated bad weather. Consequently, she was surprised to find herself confined indoors after dinner due to a persistent downpour. She had hoped for a twilight stroll to the temple and throughout the grounds; a merely cold or damp evening might not have deterred her, but a heavy, settled rain was unbearable to her. With a small party and quiet hours passing, Mrs. Palmer attended to her child, and Mrs. Jennings worked on her carpet. They discussed their departed friends, arranged Lady Middleton's schedule, and speculated whether Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon would reach Reading that night. Elinor, though largely unconcerned, joined the conversation. Meanwhile, Marianne, possessing an uncanny ability to locate the library regardless of the family's efforts to avoid it, quickly secured a book.

Mrs. Palmer displayed constant, friendly goodwill, making her guests feel welcome. Her lack of refinement and occasional lapses in polite formality were more than compensated for by her open-heartedness and engaging kindness, highlighted by her lovely face. While her folly was evident, it was not repulsive because it lacked arrogance. Elinor could forgive everything about her save her laugh.

The following day, the two gentlemen arrived for a late dinner, delightfully expanding the party and enriching the conversation, which had grown dull from the prolonged morning rain. Elinor, having observed so little of Mr. Palmer previously, wondered what to expect in his domestic setting. She found him perfectly gentlemanly with visitors, though occasionally rough with his wife and mother-in-law. He was a pleasant companion, hindered only by a tendency to believe himself superior to everyone else, particularly to Mrs. Jennings and Charlotte. Otherwise, his character showed nothing unusual for his age and station: fastidious about food, irregular with his hours, affectionate toward his child despite pretending otherwise, and wasting mornings playing billiards instead of working. Overall, she liked him far better than expected, and inwardly she was glad she could not love him further. Observing his epicureanism, selfishness, and conceit only strengthened her contentment in remembering Edward's generosity, simple tastes, and modest nature.

Colonel Brandon, who had recently visited Dorsetshire, brought news regarding Edward. Treating Elinor both as Mr. Ferrars' impartial friend and his own trusted confidante, he spoke extensively about the rectory at Delaford, detailing its shortcomings and outlining his plans to remedy them. His behavior—his evident joy in seeing her after only ten days apart, his eagerness to converse, and his deference to her opinions—seemed to validate Mrs. Jennings's belief in his attachment. Indeed, it would have been sufficient proof had Elinor not already suspected Marianne was his true favorite. Nevertheless, such a notion rarely occurred to her unless prompted by Mrs. Jennings. Confident in her own keen observation, she scrutinized his eyes while Mrs. Jennings focused solely on his actions. When Mrs. Jennings failed to notice Colonel Brandon's look of anxious concern for Marianne's unspoken headache and sore throat—the onset of a severe cold—Elinor saw clearly the quick, alarmed feelings of a lover.

Two delightful twilight walks on the third and fourth evenings, traversing not just the dry gravel paths but the entire grounds, especially the wilder sections with ancient trees and long, wet grass, combined with the imprudence of sitting in wet shoes and stockings, inflicted a violent cold upon Marianne. Though initially dismissed or denied, the illness inevitably worsened, drawing the attention of everyone present and Marianne herself. Prescriptions flooded in from all sides, as is customary, and were uniformly refused. Despite being heavy, feverish, plagued by limb pains, a cough, and a sore throat, she insisted that a single good night's sleep would cure her completely. Only with difficulty did Elinor persuade her to try one or two of the simplest remedies before retiring.
