("Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night-- Treading, as moon-flakes step across a dark-- Kissing the very dew to holier light ... Thy Voice a song past mountains, which to hark Frightens my soul with an utter lost delight.")

It drew near the end of the sixteenth hour, when the millions of the Mighty Pyramid had already settled to their long rest, and the great girders hummed soft with the steady thrum of the Earth-Current that held all the dark of the outer world at bay. I had stripped off my heavy observation leathers to turn to my cot, when the aether all about my skin thrilled sharp and electric, a sensation I had come to know well in those strange, heady days. But this thrill carried a weight no signal had ever borne before, and Naani’s voice bloomed plain in my bones, no faint transmission, no distant hum: it was as if she spoke pressed to my ear.

I did not answer at once. Wariness ran deep in all who dwelt in the Redoubts, for the Night Land teemed with things that mimicked voices to lure the unwary to their doors. I sent the steady pulse of the Master-Word rippling out into the dark, and it beat back to me at once, solemn, unmistakeable. I sent my query then: why did she speak to me through the Observation Tower’s Instrument at this hour, when the watch of her father’s Monstruwacans had long since settled, and her people’s sleep-cycle had run five hours deep? She should have been abed, nowhere near the aether-transmitters, far from any spot she could speak to me across the hundreds of miles of outer dark.

She sent no direct reply. Instead, she laid a feeling on my spirit that wracked me to the marrow, and the words drifted soft through my skull:
“Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night--”

I trembled so hard my knees went weak. Those were my own words, the poem I had scrawled in charcoal on the stone slab above Mirdath the Beautiful’s grave, a hundred thousand eons prior, in the last days when the sun still hung in the sky, before the light died and the world turned to everlasting night. No living soul in this age could have known those lines. My heart reeled with the force of ancient, long-buried grief, and I called out across the dark to demand she explain how she had come by them.

Again she did not answer. She spoke the lines a second time, and the truth hit me like a blow to the chest: it was not Naani speaking, not at first. It was Mirdath, my lost love, reaching out across the gulf of all the dead ages. I cried her name out with every fibre of my brain, and the voice answered once more, and I knew then that it was both. Naani was Mirdath, reborn, the other half of my soul that had walked with mine through every turning of the world since the first light dimmed. I called out to her, screaming my need through the aether, for hours on end, until my vision blurred and my limbs gave out, and I fell into a deep, exhausted sleep.

I woke gasping, the memory of that transmission seared into the front of my mind. I called her name three times, sharp and clear, and the Master-Word throbbed back at once, steady in the hum of the aether. Her voice came to me then, faint, as it always was when she spoke without the aid of the Instrument, using only the power of her own mind. I pressed her at once for answers about what had passed while the rest of her people slept, and she replied, confused: she had no memory of speaking to me at all, she had slept the whole night through, save for one very strange, vivid dream.

She let the memory of it spill across the aether to me: in the dream, she had stood in a small, dim room where a tall, broad man in clothing no one had worn for a million years sat hunched over a slab, weeping so hard his shoulders shook. She could not read the script he wrote, it belonged to a dead language, but the name Mirdath was scrawled at the top of the page, and she could recall one single line of the verses he had set down. Then she spoke it, soft, across the miles of dark:
“Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night--”

I filled in the rest of the poem, my soul singing, and I felt the words strike her mind like a blow. She went still, dumbstruck, for a long stretch, as the gates of her long-buried past memories creaked open. When she spoke again, her voice was breaking, thick with the tears of her spirit, and she asked, half awestruck, half afraid, if it could be true, if she was really that Mirdath I had loved across all the dead eons. I was so shaken I could not speak for a long minute, the blood thudding loud in my ears, until the words finally came, and we both wept across the empty dark, our spirits meeting at last after a hundred thousand years apart.

But the wonder of that moment only stoked the fire in my chest. I had to reach her, to stand beside her in the flesh, no longer speaking only through the thrum of the aether. There was one fatal problem: no record, no map, no scholar in all the Mighty Pyramid could pinpoint the location of the Lesser Redoubt. The only vague guess the Monstruwacans and students held was that it lay somewhere between northwest and northeast of our walls. No compass worked, not out here: the immense power of the Earth-Current gushing from the Crack beneath the Pyramid spun every needle wild, no matter how carefully you balanced it, so no direction held any certainty. The Master Monstruwacan of the Lesser Redoubt ran tests on their own ancient compass, and it swung perpetually between north and south, never settling, as if our own Earth-Current’s pull dragged at it faint across the miles.

It was useless, all of it. But I noticed a strange thing: no matter how carefully I was blindfolded, no matter how many lead screens the Monstruwacans wrapped around me to block out all aetheric signals, I would always turn, unconsciously, to face that vague northward direction, my feet pointing the way to Naani even when I had no logical reason to know which way north was. The Master Monstruwacan of our Great Redoubt wrote a whole study of the quirk, published it across the Hour-Slips, and soon every household in the Pyramid was pestering me for demonstrations, calling through their home Instruments to hear more of my power to speak across the dark. I grew tired of the endless attention, but I did not let it distract me: I still itched to set out, to walk the Night Land, no matter what horrors lurked there, to find her.

Then the terror came. It was the seventeenth hour, the entire Pyramid wrapped in sleep, and I was on duty in the Observation Tower beside our own Master Monstruwacan, when Naani’s voice cut through the aether, thin, fraying, so faint my Night-Hearing could barely make out the words. The Earth-Current powering the Lesser Redoubt had failed, all at once, without warning. Their wards were flickering out. Their lights were dying. The hordes of unnamed Things that pressed against their walls were creeping closer, and dozens of their people had already broken, mad with the raw, unshielded terror of the dark outside, ripped open the great gate of the Lesser Pyramid, and run out into the black to be torn apart, their souls scattered no one knew where.

For a month, we talked, two lovers on the edge of eternal parting. Her voice grew fainter, every day, as the last dregs of their dying Earth-Current sputtered out. The millions of our Great Redoubt grieved for them, wrote sad poems, drafted hundreds of grand, impossible rescue plans that no one ever attempted, the talk of safe, overfed people who had never stepped foot outside the Pyramid walls. But I knew, deep in my bones, that I would have to make the journey, no matter how many miles of monster-haunted dark stretched between us, even if I died halfway, it would be better than to sit here and hear the last of her voice go silent forever.

Then the eighteenth hour struck, and the Master Monstruwacan shook me roughly awake. The monitoring Instruments had picked up the pulse of the Master-Word, faint but steady, and none of his other Monstruwacans had the soul-sensitivity to tell where it was coming from. I sat up, and the words hit me, loud, near, so near I thought Naani was standing right outside the Great Door: “We are coming! We are coming!”

I sent the Master-Word rippling out at once. No reply came from that close, nearby spot. Instead, the faint, fraying pulse of Naani’s own answering signal drifted back to me, from hundreds of miles out in the dark. She told me she had heard that loud, near transmission too, woken from a half-sleep, and thought we had sent an expedition out to her. I calmed her, told her the signal had come from close to the Great Pyramid, not from anyone who had yet set out north, bade her rest, then turned to tell the Master Monstruwacan what I had sensed.

We climbed to the highest platform of the Observation Tower, and trained the great spy-glass out over the Electric Circle that girded the Pyramid’s base. There, marching steadily past the glowing ward-lines, were five hundred strong, armed young men from the upper cities, all heading north into the dark, towards the direction of the Lesser Redoubt. They had bound the sleeping watch at the Great Gate, gagged them, tampered with the alarm systems, and slipped out through the narrow Eye-Gate cut into the top of the great door, before anyone could raise a hue and cry. The Master Watchman soon confirmed it, when he arrived at the gate to free his bound men.

New rules were written that very day, new checks added to the watch protocols, nightly tests of every aetheric alarm in the Pyramid. But it was far too late. Those five hundred brave, reckless men were already gone, vanished into the endless, hideous dark of the Night Land, chasing the faint, fading voice of a people they would never meet, risking every monster and trap the outer world held to reach the Lesser Redoubt before its last light died. Naani’s voice was still there, faint, when I checked on her that night, but every hour it grew a little quieter, a little further away, and the hushing of her voice had only just begun.
