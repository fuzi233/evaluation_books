Shirley proved her sincerity in welcoming Caroline's company by actively seeking it out. Indeed, without Shirley's initiative, such companionship would likely never have occurred, for Miss Helstone was naturally reticent about forming new acquaintances. Burdened by the belief that no one desired her company and that she possessed nothing amusing to offer, the reserved governess viewed the vibrant, wealthy heiress of Fieldhead as entirely alien to her own modest sphere.

Yet, Shirley was not merely brilliant; she was undeniably happy. No one remains immune to the warmth of genial society. Within a month, she had cultivated friendships with nearly every family in the vicinity, becoming confidantes with the Misses Sykes, the Misses Pearson, and the formidable Misses Wynne of Walden Hall. However, despite these connections, she found none among them truly congenial. As she put it, she fraternized with none of them. Even if she had possessed the title of Shirley Keeldar, Esquire, Lord of the Manor of Briarfield, she confessed to Mrs. Pryor that she would not have invited a single local maiden to become Lady of the Manor. Mrs. Pryor, accustomed to her pupil's eccentricities, received this declaration with quiet composure, advising her gently not to let the habit of referring to herself as a gentleman take root, lest outsiders mistake it for an affectation of masculinity.

Shirley never mocked her former governess; she regarded even the minor formalities of Mrs. Pryor's character as worthy of respect. To ridicule quiet virtue would be the act of a weak soul. Thus, she accepted her advice in silence. Standing by the window, she watched a bird perched on a lower branch of a magnificent cedar tree on her lawn. Soon, she began to mimic the bird's chirps, her imitation growing clearer until she was whistling a distinct, sweet melody.

"My dear!" Mrs. Pryor exclaimed.

"Was I whistling?" Shirley asked, startled. "Forgive me, ma'am. I had sworn to myself never to whistle in your presence."

"But, Miss Keeldar, where did you acquire this skill? You must have picked it up since arriving in Yorkshire. I never suspected you of it before."

"Oh, I learned it long ago."

"Who taught you?"

"No one. I adopted it by listening and later abandoned it. However, yesterday evening, as I walked up the lane, I heard a gentleman whistling that exact tune across the hedge in the field. It jogged my memory."

"Which gentleman was it?"

"There is but one gentleman in this region, ma'am, who isn't gray-haired—Mr. Moore. At least, he is the only one who hasn't turned silver. My two favorite elders, Mr. Helstone and Mr. Yorke, are indeed distinguished old beaux, infinitely superior to the dull youths around here."

Mrs. Pryor fell silent.

"You do not approve of Mr. Helstone, ma'am?"

"My dear, Mr. Helstone's official duties shield him from criticism."

"You usually excuse yourself when he arrives."

"Are you walking today, my dear?"

"Yes, I intend to visit the rectory, locate Caroline Helstone, and encourage her to exercise. She needs a brisk walk across Nunnely Common."

"If you head that way, please remind Miss Helstone to dress warmly. The wind has shifted, and she requires extra care."

"You shall be meticulously obeyed, Mrs. Pryor. Meanwhile, would you join us?"

"No, my love; I would hinder you. I am heavy-footed and cannot match your pace."

Shirley easily persuaded Caroline to accompany her. Once they were on the quiet road traversing the vast, solitary expanse of Nunnely Common, conversation flowed effortlessly between them. Overcoming initial reserve, Caroline soon delighted in Miss Keeldar's company. Their exchange of small remarks quickly established mutual understanding. Shirley expressed her admiration for the lush green turf of the common, preferring even more the heath-covered ridges, which reminded her of the Scottish moors she had visited during travels. She recalled a specific journey taken one sultry, sunless summer afternoon, traveling from noon until sunset across what seemed an endless wilderness of deep heath, witnessing only wild sheep and the cries of wild birds.

"I know exactly how the heath looks on such a day," Caroline observed. "Purple-black—a deeper shade of the sky's hue, appearing almost livid."

"Quite livid, with brass-colored edges to the clouds, punctuated here and there by a white gleam, more ghastly than the lurid tint, which one expects to ignite into blinding lightning at any moment."

"Did it thunder?"

"It muttered distant peals, but the storm broke only in the evening, after we reached our inn—an isolated house at the foot of a mountain range."

"Did you watch the clouds descend upon the mountains?"

"I did. I stood at the window for an hour observing them. The hills seemed wrapped in a sullen mist, and when the rain fell in whitening sheets, they were suddenly erased from view, washed clean from the world."

"I have witnessed such storms in the hilly districts of Yorkshire. At their chaotic peak, with the sky turning into a waterfall and the earth flooding, I have recalled the Biblical Deluge."

"It is strangely refreshing after such hurricanes to feel the calm return, receiving a consoling gleam through the breaking clouds, softly signaling that the sun has not been extinguished."

"Miss Keeldar, stop here for a moment and gaze down at Nunnely Dale and the woods below."

Halting on the verdant brow of the common, they looked down upon the deep valley dressed in May attire: varied meadows, some dotted with pearl-white daisies, others glowing gold with kingcups. Today, this young verdure shimmered brightly in the sunlight, playing with transparent emerald and amber hues. Upon Nunnwood—the sole remnant of ancient British forest in a landscape once covered in woodland, with highlands thick with heather—a cloud cast its shadow. Distant hills were dappled, the horizon shaded and tinted like mother-of-pearl; silvery blues, soft purples, fleeting greens, and rose tones melted into fleecy white clouds, pure as azure snow, drawing the eye toward a distant glimpse of heaven's foundations. The breeze brushing their brows was fresh, sweet, and invigorating.

"Our England is a beautiful island," Shirley declared, "and Yorkshire is undoubtedly one of its finest corners."

"Are you a Yorkshire girl as well?"

"I am—in blood and birth. Five generations of my ancestors rest beneath the aisles of Briarfield Church. I drew my first breath in the old black hall behind us."

Caroline extended her hand, which was promptly grasped and shaken. "We are compatriots," she said.

"Yes," Shirley nodded gravely.

"And that," Miss Keeldar pointed to the forest, "is Nunnwood?"

"It is."

"Have you ever been there?"

"Countless times."

"Into the heart of it?"

"Yes."

"How does it appear?"

"It resembles an encampment of forest giants. The trees are immense and ancient. Standing at their roots, the summits seem to belong to another realm. The trunks stand still and firm like pillars, while the branches sway with every breeze. Even in the deepest calm, their leaves are never perfectly hushed; in high winds, a torrent rushes overhead, a sea thundering above you."

"Was it not one of Robin Hood's haunts?"

"Yes, and remnants of his legend still exist. To enter Nunnwood, Miss Keeldar, is to step far back into the dim days of antiquity. Can you see a break in the forest near the center?"

"Yes, clearly."

"That opening leads to a dell—a deep, hollow cup lined with turf as green and short as the grass on this common. The very oldest trees, gnarled mighty oaks, crowd the brink of this dell. At the bottom lie the ruins of a nunnery."

"We shall go—you and I alone, Caroline—to that wood on a fine summer morning. We can spend the entire day there, taking pencils, sketchbooks, and books of interest, along with provisions. My housekeeper, Mrs. Gill, could pack our food into two small baskets, which we can carry ourselves. Would the walk tire you?"

"Oh, no; especially if we rest within the wood all day. I know the most delightful spots. I know where to gather nuts in season, where wild strawberries thrive, and certain lonely, untouched glades carpeted with strange mosses—some yellow as if gilded, others a sober gray, some gem-green. I know groups of trees that captivate the eye with their perfect, pictorial contrasts: rough oaks, delicate birches, glossy beeches clustered together; ash trees stately as King Saul standing alone; and superannuated wood-giants draped in bright ivy shrouds. Miss Keeldar, I could guide you."

"Would you be dull in my company?"

"I should not. I believe we would suit each other perfectly. Who else could be present without spoiling our pleasure?"

"Indeed, I know of no one of our age, certainly no lady; and regarding gentlemen—"

"An excursion changes entirely when gentlemen are included," Caroline interrupted.

"I agree—it becomes quite a different affair from what we proposed."
