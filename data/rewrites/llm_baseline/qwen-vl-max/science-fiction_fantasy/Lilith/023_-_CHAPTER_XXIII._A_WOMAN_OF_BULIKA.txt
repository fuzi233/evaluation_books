I stepped into a narrow alley, seeking refuge beneath the shadow of a low archway. The moonlight spilled across the cobbled stones, silvering the air like frost. I paused at the mouth, gazing out into the stillness—then she appeared, gliding silently behind me. She stopped, trembling, and turned to look out as well, her breath quick and shallow in the quiet night.

A few heartbeats passed. Then, with a sudden flash of movement, a great leopard darted across the archway. Its coat was pale, marked with dark splotches like ink on parchment, and its eyes gleamed with cold intelligence.

The woman pressed close to me, her body rigid with fear. My chest tightened with pity. Without thought, I reached out and drew her gently against me.

“If it comes here,” I said, voice steady, “I’ll hold it off. You run.”

“Thank you,” she whispered, her voice barely audible.

“Have you seen it before?” I asked.

“Many times,” she replied, still shaking. “She belongs to the princess. You’d know her if you were from Bulika.”

“I’m not,” I said. “But why is she allowed to roam free?”

“She’s kept in chains,” she answered, “her mouth muzzled, her paws wrapped in crocodile leather. But she escapes often—always at night. And when she does… she hunts children. Sucks their blood. There aren’t many mothers left in Bulika, thank the stars.”

She broke down then, tears streaming down her face. “I wish I were home,” she sobbed. “The princess returned only last night—and already the beast is loose! How will I get inside? It’s *me* she wants—I can feel it! She’ll be waiting at my door!”

She clamped her hands over her mouth, ashamed. “I shouldn’t speak to a stranger like this.”

“All strangers aren’t cruel,” I said softly. “And this creature won’t touch you while I’m here. By the time she finishes with me, you’ll be safe indoors. At least you have a home to go to. That wind is terrible.”

“You’ll be sheltered too,” she said, wiping her eyes. “If you take me safely home, I’ll give you a place to rest. But we must wait.”

We stood in silence for a moment, listening to the wind moan through the alleys. Then I began to ask questions.

She told me that the people of Bulika did nothing but dig for gems beneath their homes. They were rich beyond measure, yet they never made anything themselves—everything came from distant cities.

“Why don’t you work?” I asked.

“Because it is a disgrace,” she said flatly. “No one in Bulika works. It’s known by all.”

“But how do you stay rich?”

“Our ancestors saved for us,” she explained. “We never spend. When we need coin, we sell a few jewels. We’ve so many, buried deep beneath the city, that we’ll never run out.”

“And what about the poor?”

“There are some,” she admitted, “but we forget them. Once someone falls, they vanish from our minds. That’s how we keep wealth—by ignoring those who lose it. We intend to remain rich forever.”

“But what happens when your mines are empty? When your gems are gone?”

“We have more than enough,” she said confidently. “And new ones grow beneath the earth. The day of scarcity will never come.”

“What if outsiders attack? What if a foreign army takes everything?”

“No one dares,” she replied. “They fear our princess. She protects us. She holds power over land, sea, sky—and fire, perhaps. No force could ever defeat her. She rules without question, without equal.”

As she spoke, she glanced back again and again, her eyes wide with dread.

I asked why her people hated strangers so fiercely.

“The presence of a foreigner defiles the city,” she said. “We are older, nobler, purer than any other race. Therefore, no outsider may stay after nightfall. We cast them out.”

“But then how can you let me in?”

“I’ll make an exception,” she said. “For you.”

“Is there no place in Bulika for travelers?”

“There would be,” she said, “if such a house existed. But it would be torn down. The owner burned. Purity cannot be preserved unless the unworthy are kept far away. Dignity is fragile.”

She told me the princess had ruled for millennia—her reign unbroken, her power absolute. She commanded nature itself, shaped storms and rivers with a word. No law bound her. No court judged her.

At last, she seemed ready. We slipped through twisting lanes, avoiding the silent streets, until we reached her home—a tall building between two others, atop a steep flight of narrow steps. She climbed slowly, and I followed.

Halfway up, she froze. Her breath caught. Then she bolted forward, scrambling the rest of the way. I reached the top just as the door slammed shut behind her.

I stood alone on the landing, stunned. Between the two houses, the space was barely long enough to lie down. Exhausted, and no longer caring whether I profaned the city, I collapsed onto the stone floor, letting the wind and shadows claim me.
