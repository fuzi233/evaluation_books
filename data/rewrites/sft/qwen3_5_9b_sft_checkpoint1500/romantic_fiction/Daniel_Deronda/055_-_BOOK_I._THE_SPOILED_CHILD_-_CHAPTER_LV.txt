"The return to your knowledge,
Which seeks how much the thing is more perfect,
More feels the good, and thus the pain."
—Dante.

When Daniel Deronda encountered Gwendolen and Grandcourt upon the stairs, his heart was a storm-tossed sea, utterly preoccupied. Just minutes prior, he had been summoned for a second, harrowing interview with his mother. Now, standing amidst the bustle of the hotel, he felt a strange paralysis. Two hours after leaving her, the Princess Halm-Eberstein had departed, leaving the path clear for Deronda to journey to Mainz, to deliver Joseph Kalonymos's letter and claim the family chest. Yet, a heavy, indeterminate weight anchored him to Genoa. It was not logic, nor duty, but a retrospective grief that held him fast. In the solitude of his own mind, he relived the scene with a fresh, piercing acuity, weeping silently over a life so intimately connected to his own destiny, yet so tragically distant. He felt the world shift beneath his feet; the certitude of ties binding him to a lineage of wanderers altered the balance of his hopes and fears. Under the cover of night, he realized he had wandered in the wrong company, only to find, as dawn approached, that his kindred pitched their tents far away. A quivering imagination stirred within him, sensing a close bond with a grandfather whose strong impulses and deep loves were perhaps waking from a long slumber inside his own soul. Through this passionate meditation, Mordecai and Mirah stood ever present, clasping hands with him in a silent, sympathetic unity.

Such was the fiber of Deronda, quick and responsive, draped beneath a mantle of self-controlled reserve forged by early hardship.

A persistent bell signaled the hour, urging him to consult Bradshaw and prepare for the next train to Mainz. He thought of it, then thought of London, of the beings there who formed the strongest attachments of his life. But other wishes clung to Genoa, rooted in a force that makes us linger over interviews carrying the shadow of final farewells. He did not consciously decide to wait until Friday evening to attend the synagogue service, hoping to see the Grandcourts again. Instead, simply, he sat doing nothing, refusing to pack or ring for his bill. His mind drifted to the synagogue, imagining the faces there—perhaps unchanged from his grandfather's time—and hearing the Spanish-Hebrew liturgy. It sounded like a plant with wandering seeds, spreading across seasons and exiles, weaving a kinship between far-off lands and the homes of the displaced. Simultaneously, his thoughts turned to Gwendolen, haunted by anxious memories of what had passed. There lingered a half-admitted fear that it would be cruel, a form of hardness, to leave immediately without making some effort, despite Grandcourt's likely disapproval, to show his continued sympathy following their abrupt separation.

Thus, he deferred his departure. Dinner tasted of ash, and he rose quickly, asking the porter if Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt remained in the hotel and for their apartment number. The porter supplied the number but added, with a casual shrug, that they had gone out boating. This single piece of news possessed a power sufficient to split Deronda's focus, dividing it with the memories awakened among the sparse *talithim* and the keen, dark faces of worshippers. Their ease in handling awful prayers and invocations, with a familiarity that seemed like Hebrew dyed Italian, struck him deeply. He reflected that his grandfather, according to the Princess's hints, must have been nearly as exceptional a Jew as Mordecai himself. Were not men of ardent zeal and far-reaching hope everywhere exceptional? Those who hold visions, as Mordecai described, are the creators and feeders of the world, molding the passive lives that would otherwise wither into the narrow tenacity of insects, unshaken by thoughts beyond the reach of their antennae. A mournful impatience mingled with his growing solicitude for Gwendolen, urging him to hasten from the synagogue. He chose an evening walk toward the quay, a favorite haunt, drawn by the possibility that he might catch the Grandcourts returning from their boating trip. If so, he resolved to approach them deliberately, ignoring any grounds Grandcourt might have for wishing him elsewhere.

The sun had dipped behind a bank of clouds, casting only a faint, dying yellow light upon the waves, which churned under an active breeze. Deronda sauntered slowly within sight of the strand, observing the groups concentrating their attention on a sailing-boat approaching swiftly toward land, rowed by two men. Amidst the clamorous chatter in various tongues, Deronda suppressed questions, pushing his way to the foreground to become an unobstructed witness. Telescopes were raised, and loud speculations erupted. "It is the *milord* who has drowned!" one cried. Another insisted the prostrate figure seen below decks was *miladi*. A Frenchman, lacking a glass, ventured that it was *milord*, perhaps taking his wife out to drown her, a notion echoing a certain national practice. An English skipper retorted in his native idiom, dismissing the idea as nonsense that had "undergone a mining operation," finally deciding the reclining figure was indeed a woman. For Deronda, terribly excited by fluctuating terrors, the rhythm of the oars was shattered by swift visions of events, both possible and impossible, leading to this tragic conclusion—or a broken fragment thereof, with a worse half concealed. Could the woman snatched from the waters truly be Mrs. Grandcourt?

Soon, however, doubt vanished. The boat was pulled to shore. Deronda saw Gwendolen, half-raising herself on her hands by sheer effort, encased in a heavy covering of tarpaulins and pea-jackets. She was pale as one of the sheeted dead, shivering, with wet hair streaming down her face. Her eyes held a wild, amazed consciousness, as if she had woken in a world where judgment hung suspended, and the beings surrounding her were coming to seize her. The first rower who leaped onto the sand was soaked through and ran off. The sailors crowded around the boat, blocking Deronda's advance. He could only watch as Gwendolen cast scared glances, shrinking with terror as she was carefully, tenderly lifted out and led away by the strong, bronzed arms of the rough men. Her wet clothes clung to her limbs, adding to the impediment of her weakness. Suddenly, her wandering eyes fell upon Deronda, standing before her. Immediately, as if she had been expecting him, searching for him, she tried to stretch out her arms, though they were held back by her supporters. In a muffled voice, she whispered, "It is come, it is come! He is dead!"

"Hush, hush!" Deronda commanded, his tone authoritative yet gentle. "Quiet yourself." Turning to the men assisting her, he declared, "I am a connection of this lady's husband. If you will get her aboard the *Italia* as quickly as possible, I will undertake everything else."

He remained to learn from the remaining boatman that her husband had gone down irrecoverably, his boat left floating empty in the water. They, along with their companion, had heard a cry and arrived just in time to see the lady jump in after her husband, saving her from terrible damage.

Afterward, Deronda hastened to the hotel to ensure the best medical aid was provided. Once satisfied on this point, he telegraphed the event to Sir Hugo, begging him to arrive forthwith, and also to Mr. Gascoigne, whose residence at the rectory offered the nearest known route to inform Gwendolen's mother. Certain words Gwendolen had spoken in the past returned to him with the effectiveness of an inspiration: in moments of agitated confession, she had often mentioned her mother's presence as a possible help, if she could have secured it.
