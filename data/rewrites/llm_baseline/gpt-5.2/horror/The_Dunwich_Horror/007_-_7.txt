All that had happened before—Wilbur’s death in Arkham, the scholar’s astonishment, the frantic locking-away of books—was only a clearing of the throat before the true Dunwich horror began to speak.

The bewildered officials did what officials always do when the world turns inside out: they performed their formalities, and they kept the abnormal details from paper and pulpit as if secrecy were a lid strong enough to hold down any boiling pot. Men were sent out toward Dunwich and Aylesbury to search out property and notify any who might be heirs of Wilbur Whateley, and they came back with the same story in different voices—no voice steady. The whole upper Miskatonic valley seemed to be listening, not with ears but with floorboards and root-hairs, to the low rumblings that came and went under the domed hills like a great stomach working on an indigestible meal.

Dunwich itself was in agitation for reasons that had nothing to do with wills and courts. From the great boarded-up shell that had been Whateley’s farmhouse there rose, day by day, a stench that did not belong to any honest rot of pigpen or compost heap. It was not the sweet-sour breath of silage, nor the clean iron reek of slaughter. It was like thunder made tangible—like hot stone after lightning, mixed with something slimy and old, and a note of salt as if a sea had been poured where no sea should be. Along with it came sounds: a surging and lapping, as though some vast thing, sleepless and hungry, shifted its weight against the boards and the nailed seams. Earl Sawyer, who had tended the horses and cattle during Wilbur’s absence, took on a nervousness that sharpened with each night. The acoustic world of the farm—usually a comfort of known creaks, known lowings, the settled talk of animals—had been altered. Now every familiar sound was overlaid by another rhythm, one that did not match the timbers, the wind, or the season.

The men from Aylesbury and the county—men who would ordinarily stride through any farmhouse kitchen without asking, sniffing at pantry jars and counting chairs as though domestic life were a ledger—devised excuses not to enter that noisome boarded place. They were glad to confine their survey to the deceased’s living quarters and the newly mended sheds, and even that single visit they made as quickly as men can move when they tell themselves they are being sensible.

A ponderous report was filed at the court-house in Aylesbury, and litigations concerning heirship began their slow, dry chewing among the innumerable Whateleys—decayed and undecayed—scattered like old seeds up and down the valley. Among Wilbur’s effects they found an almost interminable manuscript in strange characters, written in a huge ledger and judged a sort of diary because of the spacing and the variations in ink and penmanship. It lay on the old bureau that had served as its owner’s desk, as if the act of writing had been as daily and domestic as peeling apples at the table. Yet the marks would not yield to any known tongue. After a week of debate it was sent to Miskatonic University with the deceased’s collection of strange books for study and possible translation; but even the best linguists soon saw it would not be unriddled with ease. The symbols seemed to mock the very idea of a shared lexicon, as if whatever Wilbur had written belonged to a language that did not wish to be carried into human mouths. No trace was found of the ancient gold with which Wilbur and old Whateley had always paid their debts—as though it, too, had gone back into whatever country minted such coin.

It was in the dark of September ninth that the horror broke loose.

That evening the hill noises were pronounced—long, deep mutterings that made the kettles faintly ring on their hooks and set the crockery to whisper against itself. Dogs barked frantically all night, not the quick yelp at fox or stray, but a frantic, worn-out chorus that rose and fell like a siren and then dropped into whines. Early risers on the tenth noticed a peculiar stench in the air, and noticed too how sound carried oddly, as though the valley had been scooped into a different shape. The distant calls of a rooster came thin and wrong, and the wind in the pines seemed to hold its breath.

About seven o’clock Luther Brown, hired boy at George Corey’s between Cold Spring Glen and the village, rushed frenziedly back from his morning trip to Ten-Acre Meadow with the cows. He stumbled into the kitchen so hard his boots tracked mud across the hearthside—mud with a strange oily sheen in it—while outside the herd pawed and lowed pitifully, pressing close to the yard as if the fence were a wall against a storm.

Mrs. Corey had been turning cornmeal into johnnycake batter, the bowl warm in her hands, the smell of sour milk and hot fat already rising from the skillet. When Luther burst in, the domestic sounds—the spoon against crock, the soft spit of lard—seemed suddenly brittle.

“Up thar in the rud beyont the glen, Mis’ Corey—they’s suthin’ ben thar!” Luther gulped, as if each word scraped his throat. “It smells like thunder, an’ all the bushes an’ little trees is pushed back from the rud like they’d a haouse ben moved along of it. An’ that ain’t the wust, nuther. They’s prints in the rud, Mis’ Corey—great raound prints as big as barrel-heads, all sunk daown deep like a elephant had ben along, only they’s a sight more nor four feet could make. I looked at one or two afore I run, an’ I see every one was covered with lines spreadin’ aout from one place, like as if big palm-leaf fans—twict or three times as big as any they is—hed of ben paounded daown into the rud. An’ the smell was awful, like what it is araound Wizard Whateley’s ol’ haouse....”

He faltered there and shivered afresh, as if the name itself carried a draught of cold. Mrs. Corey tried to coax more out of him, but the boy’s mouth was full of fear the way a pan can be too full of boiling syrup to take a spoon. So she did the only thing she could do with her hands still smelling of meal and grease: she went to the telephone and began to ring the neighbors, sending panic along the party line as easily as a recipe.

When she got Sally Sawyer—housekeeper at Seth Bishop’s, the nearest place to Whateley’s—Mrs. Corey found it her turn to listen instead of transmit. Sally’s boy Chauncey, who slept poorly, had been up on the hill toward Whateley’s and had dashed back after one look at the place and at the pasturage where Mr. Bishop’s cows had been left out all night.

“Yes, Mis’ Corey,” came Sally’s tremulous voice over the wire, thin and crackling, “Cha’ncey he just come back a-post-in’, and couldn’t haff talk fer bein’ scairt! He says Ol’ Whateley’s haouse is all blowed up, with the timbers scattered raound like they’d ben dynamite inside; only the bottom floor ain’t through, but is all covered with a kind o’ tarlike stuff that smells awful an’ drips daown offen the aidges onto the graoun’ whar the side timbers is blowed away. An’ they’s awful kinder marks in the yard, tew—great raound marks bigger raound than a hogshead, an’ all sticky with stuff like is on the blowed-up haouse. Cha’ncey he says they leads off into the medders, whar a great swath wider’n a barn is matted daown, an’ all the stun walls tumbled every which way wherever it goes.

“An’ he says, says he, Mis’ Corey, as haow he sot to look fer Seth’s caows, frighted ez he was; an’ faound ’em in the upper pasture nigh the Devil’s Hop Yard in an awful shape. Haff on ’em’s clean gone, an’ nigh haff o’ them that’s left is sucked most dry o’ blood, with sores on ’em like they’s ben on Whateley’s cattle ever senct Lavinny’s black brat was born. Seth he’s gone aout naow to look at ’em, though I’ll vaow he wun’t keer ter git very nigh Wizard Whateley’s! Cha’ncey didn’t look keerful ter see whar the big matted-daown swath led arter it leff the pasturage, but he says he thinks it p’inted towards the glen rud to the village.

“I tell ye, Mis’ Corey, they’s suthin’ abroad as hadn’t orter be abroad, an’ I fer one think that black Wilbur Whateley, as come to the bad eend he desarved, is at the bottom of the breedin’ of it. He wa’n’t all human hisself, I allus says to everybody; an’ I think he an’ Ol’ Whateley must a raised suthin’ in that there nailed-up haouse as ain’t even so human as he was. They’s allus ben unseen things araound Dunwich—livin’ things—as ain’t human an’ ain’t good fer human folks.

“The graoun’ was a’talkin’ lass night, an’ towards mornin’ Cha’ncey he heerd the whippoorwills so laoud in Col’ Spring Glen he couldn’t sleep none. Then he thought he heerd another faintlike saound over towards Wizard Whateley’s—a kinder rippin’ or tearin’ o’ wood, like some big box or crate was bein’ opened fur off. What with this an’ that, he didn’t git to sleep at all till sunup, an’ no sooner was he up this mornin’, but he’s got to go over to Whateley’s an’ see what’s the matter. He see enough, I tell ye, Mis’ Corey! This dun’t mean no good, an’ I think as all the men-folks ought to git up a party an’ do suthin’. I know suthin’ awful’s abaout, an’ feel my time is nigh, though only Gawd knows jest what it is.

“Did your Luther take accaount o’ whar them big tracks led tew? No? Wal, Mis’ Corey, ef they was on the glen rud this side o’ the glen, an’ ain’t got to your haouse yet, I calc’late they must go into the glen itself. They would do that. I allus says Col’ Spring Glen ain’t no healthy nor decent place. The whippoorwills an’ fireflies there never did act like they was creaters o’ Gawd, an’ they’s them as says ye kin hear strange things a-rushin’ an’ a-talkin’ in the air daown thar ef ye stand in the right place, atween the rock falls an’ Bear’s Den.”

By noon fully three-quarters of the men and boys of Dunwich were moving over roads and meadows between the new-made Whateley ruins and Cold Spring Glen. They went as they would go to a butchering or a barn-raising—out of habit, out of grim neighborliness—yet with faces set as if each step must be negotiated with an unseen will. They examined in horror the vast monstrous prints; the maimed Bishop cattle with their hideous dry wounds that looked less like biting than like being emptied; the strange wreck of the farmhouse with its boards flung wide; and the bruised, matted vegetation along fields and road-sides.

That tarry stickiness was everywhere near the ruin, hanging in ropes from splintered beams, glossing the earth as if someone had poured hot pitch and let it cool. It had an odor that clung to the nostrils, insinuating itself behind any other smell—behind manure, behind smoke, behind the faint sweetness of late-season apples. Even those who had come from kitchens still carrying the memory of bread crust and coffee found that this new scent chased all appetites away, leaving only a cold saliva and the sick, fainting knowledge of the body’s vulnerability.

Whatever had burst loose upon the world had assuredly gone down into the great sinister ravine. Along the banks, trees were bent and broken, and a great avenue had been gouged in the underbrush that clung to the precipice. It was as though a house, launched by an avalanche, had slid down through tangled growths on an almost vertical slope. From below came no sound—no splash, no growl, no bellow—only a distant, undefinable fetor that rose as if the glen exhaled. Men stood on the edge and argued, glad to keep their boots on firm ground rather than descend and beard the unknown Cyclopean thing in its lair. Three dogs with the party barked furiously at first, then fell cowed and reluctant, tails low, as they neared the lip; even their hearing, keen as it was, seemed to pick up something in the silence that frightened them.

Someone telephoned the news to the Aylesbury Transcript, but the editor—used to Dunwich and its tales, used to translating strange country fear into a city chuckle—made only a humorous paragraph. The Associated Press repeated the joke as if ridicule were a charm.

That night everyone went home and barricaded house and barn as stoutly as possible. No cattle were left in open pasturage. The ordinary nightly sounds of farm life—buckets set down, doors latched, the last sleepy cluck from a hen—were performed with exaggerated care, as though any stray clatter might draw attention. And in the gaps between those human-made noises, the valley seemed to listen back.

About two in the morning a frightful stench and the savage barking of dogs awakened the household at Elmer Frye’s, on the eastern edge of Cold Spring Glen. All agreed they could hear, somewhere outside, a muffled swishing or lapping sound—wet, heavy, patient—like some immense body shifting in mud, or a tide nosing at a shore that had never known tides. Mrs. Frye proposed telephoning the neighbors. Elmer was about to agree when the sound of splintering wood burst in upon their deliberations. It came, apparently, from the barn, and was followed by hideous screaming and stamping among the cattle.

The dogs did not charge. They slavered and crouched close to the feet of the family, whining, pressed to human ankles as if the old domestic hierarchy had been reversed and men were now the shelter. Frye lit a lantern through force of habit—because hands, when frightened, reach for ritual—but he knew it would be death to step into the black yard with that small, bobbing circle of light. The children and women whimpered, yet did not scream. Some buried instinct of defense—older than speech, older than prayer—told them their lives depended on silence.

At last the cattle-noise subsided to a pitiful moaning. Then came a great snapping, crashing, crackling—as if timbers were being wrenched and chewed, or as if some titanic weight shifted and the world’s joints complained. The Fryes, huddled in the sitting-room, did not dare move until the last echoes died away far down in Cold Spring Glen, swallowed by that ravine which seemed to drink sound and give back only whippoorwill cries.

When it was over, amidst the dismal moans from the stable and the demoniac piping of late whippoorwills in the glen, Selina Frye tottered to the telephone and spread what news she could of the second phase of the horror. Her words, crackling along the wire, entered other kitchens where doughs were rising and beans simmering, and turned those homely scents suddenly thin and unreal.

The next day the countryside was in panic, and cowed, uncommunicative groups came and went where the fiendish thing had occurred. Two titan swaths of destruction stretched from the glen to the Frye farmyard; monstrous prints covered bare patches of ground. One side of the old red barn had completely caved in. Of the cattle only about a quarter could be found and identified. Some were in curious fragments, as if they had been pulled apart without any care for joints or bone; and all that survived had to be shot, their familiar warmth and usefulness ended in a mercy that tasted of iron and smoke.

Earl Sawyer suggested help be asked from Aylesbury or Arkham, but others maintained it would be of no use. Old Zebulon Whateley—of a branch hovering between soundness and decadence—made darkly wild suggestions about rites that ought to be practised on hilltops. Tradition ran strong in his line, and his memories of chantings in the great stone circles were not altogether connected with Wilbur and his grandfather. He spoke in words that belonged to older mouths, and there were sounds in his speech—turned vowels and half-swallowed consonants—that felt like a dialect trying to hold together a crumbling cultural memory. Yet even his inherited talk could not quite name what had happened. The thing in the glen did not seem to fit inside any human vocabulary, however ancient.

Darkness fell on a stricken countryside too passive to organize for real defense. In a few cases closely related families banded together under one roof and watched in the gloom, muskets loaded, pitchforks handy, listening to the night’s acoustic ecology as if they could judge safety by the pattern of crickets and wind. But in general there was only barricading again, and the futile gesture of weapons meant for wolves and men.

Nothing occurred that night except hill noises; and when day came, many hoped the new horror had gone as swiftly as it had come. There were even bold souls who proposed an offensive expedition down in the glen—said it with mouths that tasted of morning coffee and bravado—but none ventured to set an example, and the majority remained reluctant.

When night came again, the barricading was repeated, though there was less huddling of families. In the morning both the Frye and Seth Bishop households reported excitement among the dogs and vague sounds and stenches from afar. Early explorers noted with horror a fresh set of monstrous tracks in the road skirting Sentinel Hill. As before, the road-sides showed bruising that suggested a bulk so stupendous it blasphemed against the scale of barns and boulders. The conformation of the tracks argued a passage in two directions, as if the moving mountain had come from Cold Spring Glen and returned to it along the same path.

At the base of the hill a thirty-foot swath of crushed shrubbery and saplings led steeply upward, and the seekers gasped when they saw that even perpendicular places did not deflect the trail. Whatever the horror was, it could scale a sheer stony cliff of almost complete verticality. By safer routes the men climbed around to the summit, and there they saw the trail ended—or rather, reversed. It was here the Whateleys used to build their hellish fires and chant their hellish rituals by the table-like stone on May Eve and Hallowmass. Now that very stone formed the center of a vast space thrashed around by the mountainous horror, and on its slightly concave surface lay a thick fetid deposit of the same tarry stickiness seen on the floor of the ruined farmhouse when the thing escaped.

Men looked at one another and muttered, then looked down the hill. Apparently the horror had descended by a route much like its ascent. To speculate was futile. Reason, logic, and normal ideas of motivation stood confounded, as though the human mind were a small kitchen knife set to carve a shape too hard and too slick to hold. Only old Zebulon—absent from the group—might have done justice to it or offered an explanation that fitted his half-remembered chants. Yet even he would have been naming shadows with the wrong names, translating the untranslatable into human syllables for comfort.

Thursday night began much like the others, but ended less happily.

The whippoorwills in the glen screamed with such unusual persistence that many could not sleep. Their calls did not sit in the night as they should; they became a continuous tearing, a drilling noise that made people press pillows to their ears and still hear it through cloth and bone. About three in the morning all the party telephones rang tremulously as if the line itself shook. Those who took down their receivers heard a fright-mad voice shriek, “Help, oh, my Gawd!…” and some thought a crashing sound followed the breaking off of the exclamation. Then there was nothing. The silence afterward was worse than the scream, because it made room for imagination and for that faint, far-off lapping.

No one dared do anything. No one knew till morning whence the call came. At daylight those who had heard it called everyone on the line and found only the Fryes did not reply.

The truth appeared an hour later, when a hastily assembled group of armed men trudged out to the Frye place at the head of the glen. It was horrible, yet hardly a surprise. There were more swaths and monstrous prints in the churned earth, but there was no longer any house. It had caved in like an egg-shell, and among the ruins nothing living or dead could be discovered—only that stench and that tarry stickiness, glistening on broken boards where a family’s table had stood, where bread had been sliced and milk poured and children fed in the old way.

The Elmer Fryes had been erased from Dunwich, as cleanly and wordlessly as if some vast, hungry thing had licked the place bare and left behind only a smell that no human language, however old, could ever rightly name.
