A Supper in the Rucellai Gardens.

The heavy doors of the pavilion swung open, admitting the cool night air and the scent of jasmine to the warm interior. Tito's eyes, sharp and assessing, scanned the assembly instantly. His initial suspicion was confirmed: this was not merely a feast, but a political gathering. Yet, the nature of the politics was subtle, woven into the very fabric of the celebration. In Florence, it was widely believed that good wine and sumptuous food sharpened the mind for political discourse, that the "inspired ease" of the post-prandial hour revealed truths inaccessible to the sober, uninvited mind. The Rucellai, a family of immense wealth, maintained a table of such splendor that it seemed a testament to their power. Here, amidst the bust of Plato watching from a corner and the frescoes of the cardinal virtues adorning the walls, high philosophy was left to the shadows. Tonight, the agenda was simpler: the strengthening of party lines through fellowship and the shared pleasure of the senses.

Plato's bust, brought from Lorenzo's villa after his death, had long served as a silent sentinel over these gatherings. On the thirteenth of November, the anniversary of the philosopher's passing, it looked down upon a select circle of scholars. Among them were the ghosts of an era: Pico della Mirandola, once a Quixotic genius who had astonished Rome with heresy before retreating into a humble quest for inner perfection; Marsilio Ficino, the diligent feeder of Platonism whose mind had grown soft from such exclusive nourishment; Angelo Poliziano, the vibrant poet whose phrases still seemed to pulse with life; and the towering Leon Battista Alberti, a universal mind of practical and theoretical grandeur. These were the valiant workers whose names are often forgotten, yet whose labors form the unseen foundation of our inheritance.

Bernardo Rucellai, the host, stood in the prime of life, barely forty, possessing a haughty and cautious dignity. He was a man of pure Latinity, though, as Erasmus noted, one could not extract a single word of Latin from him by force. He received Tito with marked favor, seating him between Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci, both accomplished young members of the Medicean faction.

As the brass bowl of scented water circulated, allowing the company to wash their hands, the atmosphere lightened. Rings flashed on white fingers under the wax candles, mingling with the fragrance of fresh French damask. The conversation drifted to the habits of posterity. Someone mused what Dante would think if he could see silver forks on the table; the consensus was that future generations would seem equally surprising to their ancestors. While silver forks dangled delicately over morsels of liver cooked to melt in the mouth, the guests admired the enamelled centers of the service and discussed the silver dish for confetti, a masterpiece by Antonio Pollajuolo, seduced by papal patronage from Florence to Rome.

"I recall," said Niccolò Ridolfi, his manner negligently commanding, "how our Antonio grew bitter about chiseling metal, declaring that 'the artist who puts his work into gold and silver puts his brains into the melting-pot.' He took to painting in a fury."

"And that was a true prophecy," Giannozzo Pucci observed. "If this war with Pisa continues, spreading to our other towns, it will not only be our silver dishes that vanish. I doubt Antonio's silver saints in San Giovanni will not soon be worshipped more devoutly as coin."

"The Frate prepares us for this already," Lorenzo Tornabuoni added dryly. "He tells the people God will not have silver crucifixes and starving stomachs; that the church is adorned best with the gems of holiness and the gold of brotherly love."

"A useful doctrine of war-finance," Bernardo Rucellai replied. "But politics come after the confetti, Lorenzo, when we have drunk enough wine to wash them down. They are too solid for roast and boiled meat."

"Our Luigi Pulci would say this boiled kid requires an impartial mind," Niccolò Ridolfi continued, recounting a story of Pulci's. "He claimed that opinion corrupts the saliva—that is why men took to pepper. Scepticism is the only philosophy that leaves the mouth clean. Yet, poor Lorenzo de' Medici disagreed, citing Matteo Franco, who wanted hotter sauce because he believed so strongly in his own verses. Pulci said his mind was like sharpest steel, cutting everything it touched, yet he was a gentle-hearted creature, temperate as a butterfly."

The light talk persisted through the roast and boiled meats, leading inevitably to the capon and game. The crowning glory was a peacock cooked according to Apicius, served with its feathers intact to resemble a living bird taking repose. It required the skill of a master carver to turn the classical bird on its back, exposing the plucked breast for the honorables, unless one possessed the independent mind to decline the expense and choose the vulgar digestibility of capon. Hardly anyone dared. Tito quoted Horace and scattered his slice in small particles; Bernardo Rucellai made a learned observation on the price of peacock eggs but did not eat; Niccolò Ridolfi held a mouthful while telling a tale of a man who boiled a wild goose in its feathers to pass it off as a pea-hen.

Very little peacock was consumed, but the satisfaction lay in the spectacle of such luxury, a caprice accessible only to the wealthiest. It would have been rash to criticize such venerable institutions when Fra Girolamo was preaching that the rich had no duty to be luxurious for the sake of the poor.

Outside, in the chill obscurity surrounding the warmth of the pavilion, a lonely figure walked in narrowing circles. Baldassarre paused among the trees, looking into the windows where the guests appeared as brilliant pictures against the gloom. He could hear the laughter, see Tito gesticulating with careless grace, and hear his voice mingling in the merry confusion. Baldassarre's mind was highly strung, preparing for the moment of his entrance. He derived a savage satisfaction from Tito's easy gaiety, viewing it as the unconscious victim preparing itself for more effective torture. But the men seated among the tapers and flashing cups knew nothing of the pale, fierce face watching them from without. Light can be a curtain as well as darkness.

As the conversation deepened, the sense of citizenship forced itself upon even the indifferent minds. The disturbing words of Fra Girolamo were uppermost in everyone's thoughts. Before the stewed fish was removed, the talk turned political, despite Rucellai's earlier prohibition. Gossip spread of the voting in the Palazzo, of Francesco Valori's domineering temper, and of Soderini's speeches, which seemed to share the same dough as the Frate's sermons.

"My opinion is," Niccolò Ridolfi stated, "that the Frate has a longer head for public matters than Soderini or any Piagnone. Soderini is his mouthpiece."

"No, Niccolò," Bernardo Rucellai corrected. "The Frate has an acute mind serving his own ends, but Pagolantonio Soderini, with his experience of the Venetian Council, owes little to a monk. Soderini loads the cannon; the Frate brings the powder and lights the match. He is master of the people, and the people are getting master of us."

Lorenzo Tornabuoni spoke up as the servants cleared the room. "Whether Soderini is indebted or not, *we* are indebted to the Frate for the general amnesty accompanying the Council scheme. We might have faced the fear of God and moral reform, but the proposition that our Medicean heads remain on our shoulders and our property is not confiscated has my warm approval. Only the Frate's predominance could have secured that."

A murmur of surprise rippled through the table, but Rucellai nodded, encouraging Tornabuoni to continue.

"I was favored with an interview in the Frate's own cell," Tornabuoni explained. "Even Francesco Valori rarely sees him in private. I see clearly that the only safe policy for us Mediceans is to throw our strength into the Frate's party. We are not strong enough to stand alone. If the Frate and the popular party were upset, the Arrabbiati—Nerli, Alberti, Pazzi—would be uppermost. Instead of amnesty, they would fly at our throats like mad dogs."

"There were strong interjections of assent," the narrative implies, as Tornabuoni paused. "A wise dissimulation is the only course for moderate men in times of violent party feeling. The Frate's theory of a popular government where every man strives for the general good is suitable for an island discovered by Columbus, but never for our quarrelsome Florence. A change must come, and with patience and caution, we can determine it in our favor. Meanwhile, we must keep the Frate's flag flying; any other flag raised now would be black for us."

"It is true," Niccolò Ridolfi declared curtly. "I am too old to change my feathers. There are men like our old Bernardo del Nero who would never borrow another's shield. But we can lie still like sleepy old dogs. Barking would be useless. As for the psalm-singing party, their day will not be long. After all the talk of scholars, there are but two sorts of government: one where men show their teeth, and one where men show their tongues and lick the feet of the strongest. Their Great Council will be voted tomorrow, and they will think they have found a new plan, but as sure as there is a human skin under every lucco in the Council, their new plan will end in snarling or licking. That is my view as a plain man. I shall be true to my old alliances; I have never seen a chalk-mark on political reasons to tell me which is true and which is false."

"If that is an appeal to me, Niccolò," Bernardo Rucellai replied with formal dignity, "I may say that while my wishes are partly determined by personal relations, I cannot enter into positive schemes with persons over whose actions I have no control. I might be content with a restoration of the old order, but with important modifications. The one point on which I concur with Lorenzo Tornabuoni is that the best policy is to throw the weight of our interest into the scale of the popular party. I condescend to no dissimulation; nor do I see the party or scheme that commands my full assent. In all alike there is crudity and confusion of ideas."

Niccolò Ridolfi shrugged and left the floor to others. As the wine circulated, the talk became more frank and lively, breaking into small knots. Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci, foreseeing this shift, turned aside from the general conversation to engage Tito, who sat between them, gradually pushing their seats back and turning their attention fully to him.

"In truth, Melema," Tornabuoni said, crossing one hose-clad leg over the other, "I know of no man in Florence who can serve our party better than you. Most of our friends cannot hide their prejudices, or their ties are so notorious they invite suspicion. Giannozzo and I possess the power of concealment and finesse necessary for a rational man, without which one is at a disadvantage compared to a wild bull. But except yourself, I know of no one else on whom we could rely for the necessary discretion."

"Yes," Giannozzo Pucci added, placing his hand on Tito's shoulder. "You can help us better than if you were Ulysses himself, for Ulysses often made himself disagreeable. To manage men, one needs a sharp mind in a velvet sheath. There is no soul in Florence who could undertake a journey to Rome with the same safety as you. Your scholarship provides a pretext, and your talent is harder to match. Niccolò Machiavelli might have done for us if he had been on our side, but he is too much bitten with notions and lacks your power of fascination. He has lost a great chance; you have got it."

"You have only to play your game well, Melema," Tornabuoni said, lowering his voice significantly. "The future belongs to you. The Medici will keep a foot in Rome as well as Florence, and the time may not be far off when they can make a finer career for their adherents. Why shouldn't you take orders some day? There is a cardinal's hat at the end of that road, and you would not be the first Greek to wear it."

Tito laughed gaily. He measured Tornabuoni's exaggerated flattery but found it pleasant.

"My joints are not so stiff yet," he said, "that I cannot be induced to run without such a high prize."

"I was not joking," Tornabuoni replied with grave suavity. "We will talk of that another time. One object to bear in mind is that you must win the confidence of the men who hang about San Marco. Giannozzo and I shall do the groundwork, but you can carry it farther because you are less observed. You can gain a thorough knowledge of their doings and make a broader screen for your agency. Nothing can be done before you start for Rome, as the business between Piero de' Medici and the French nobles must be effected immediately. When you return, however, you could make yourself the pet votary of San Marco, though you are wise enough to know that effective dissimulation is never immoderate."

"If it were not that an adhesion to the popular side is necessary to your safety as an agent of our party, Tito mio," Giannozzo Pucci said, his manner more fraternal, "I could have wished your skill employed in another way. But now we must find someone to manage our sworn enemies, the Arrabbiati. We need to know their movements more than those of the Frate's party, who are strong enough to play above-board. It would have been difficult for you, given your known relations with the Medici and your wife's kinship with Bernardo del Nero. We need a man with no distinguished connections who has not yet taken a side."

Tito pushed his hair backward automatically, looking straight at Pucci with a scarcely perceptible smile.
