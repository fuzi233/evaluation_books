The air in the drawing room was thick with the scent of stale powder and suppressed rage, a mixture that clung to the linen curtains like a second skin. The Baroness Bernstein sat with a posture of rigid composure, her face a mask of porcelain indifference, though her eyes betrayed a flicker of calculation. Across from her, Lady Maria, the niece, held herself with the brittle dignity of someone who knows she is being watched.

"Of course, my Lady," the Baroness said, her voice smooth as polished glass. "You cannot expect me, as Harry Warrington's relative, to rejoice at the prospect of his marrying a woman older than his mother, penniless and... well, unsuitable. Yet, if he insists on such folly, it is not my concern. I doubt I ever intended to take that offer of five thousand pounds seriously; it was said in the heat of the moment. So, it was already settled at Castlewood? If I had known the depth of your resolve, I might have spared my objections. Once a pitcher is shattered, no amount of railing can restore its shape."

"Madam!" Lady Maria interrupted, her tone sharp.

"Pardon me," the Baroness replied, unbothered. "I speak only of facts. Your honor is secure, as Harry attests, and as you claim. What further proof is needed?"

"You have spoken to Mr. Warrington?"

"And he confirmed the promise made at Castlewood. You possess his writing."

"Certainly," Lady Maria admitted, her chin lifting.

"Ah," the elder lady noted, showing no sign of distress. "I admit I misread your letters initially. They implicated other family members—"

"Who slandered me and sought to poison Mr. Warrington's view of me. Yes, I wrote against them to clear my name."

"But surely, you are pained that a wretch should seize tales damaging our lineage and turn them into public scandal. That explains your recent agitation."

"Exactly," Lady Maria conceded. "With Mr. Warrington, I need conceal nothing. But that differs vastly from exposing the private disputes of a noble house."

"My dear Maria, I admire you now, and I owe you an apology. For twenty years, perhaps."

"I am glad you finally grant me justice," the niece said, her relief palpable.

"When I saw you last night, opening the ball with my nephew, what did you imagine, my dear?"

"I have no idea what the Baroness de Bernstein imagined," Lady Maria retorted, her pride stiffening.

"I recalled you performing to that same tune with the dancing-master at Kensington."

"It was a lie," Lady Maria declared.

"For which the poor dancing-master received a beating for nothing."

"It is cruel to revive such falsehoods," Maria said, her spirit flaring. "I shall decline residing with anyone who speaks thus."

"You wish to depart? I suspect Tunbridge will feel the heat if those letters surface."

"There was not a word against you in them," Maria assured her.

"So Harry said, and you deserve credit. Well, we are weary of one another. Separation suits us."

"That is my sentiment," Lady Maria curtseyed.

"Mr. Sampson will escort you to Castlewood. You and your maid may take a postchaise."

"We shall take the chaise, and Mr. Sampson will guide me," the younger lady echoed, playing the dutiful niece perfectly. "You see, I act with propriety."

"Do you know," the Baroness mused, "I suspect Mr. Sampson holds the letters?"

"I confess the thought crossed my mind."

"And you intend to flee home, coaxing the documents from him? Delilah. Well, they serve no purpose for me. I trust you recover them. When do you leave? The sooner, the better. We are women of the world, Maria. We trade insults only in passion. We require no company. Let us part amicably. Shall we attend Lady Yarmouth's gathering? It is her night. A change of scenery suits you after your nervous episodes; cards dispel gloomy thoughts better than physicians."

Lady Maria agreed to the cards, dressing quickly to await her aunt in the drawing room. As Madame Bernstein descended, she noticed Maria's door ajar. *She carries the letters,* the old lady thought. Together, they departed in separate carriages, exchanging a display of cordiality and respect that masked the bitterness of their conflict.

That night, returning from the Countess's assembly, Mrs. Brett, the Baroness's maid, approached Lady Maria's chamber bell. But Betty, Mrs. Brett's subordinate, was unfit to appear. She had been reveling with Mr. Warrington's black servant, Lord Bamborough's valet, and various others of similar station. The liquor, Mrs. Brett confessed with shock, had overwhelmed Betty. Should Mrs. Brett assist her mistress? Lady Maria declined, preferring solitude, dismissing Mrs. Brett. *She has the letters in her stays,* Madame Bernstein surmised. They parted on the stairs with feigned warmth.

Betty faced a reprimand the following morning, emerging from the closet adjacent to Lady Maria's apartment. She admitted, with contrite humility, her fondness for rum-punch, brewed delicately by Mr. Gumbo. She accepted her chastisement meekly, attended to her duties, and withdrew.

Betty was one of the Castlewood maids, captivated by Gumbo, and possessed a striking blue-eyed beauty that caught the eye of Mr. Case, the Baroness's confidential man. Jealousy had festered between Gumbo and Case, leading to quarrels. Gumbo, naturally peaceable, feared the Baroness's gentlemen, who threatened to break his bones or end his life if he persisted in courting Betty.

Yet, on the night of the rum-punch, despite finding Gumbo and Betty whispering in the doorway, Mr. Case displayed remarkable grace. He proposed the bowl of punch, brewed and consumed in Betty's room, concocted by Gumbo with skill. He praised Gumbo's music. Though usually sober, he insisted on more drinking until Betty succumbed to the state that warranted Lady Maria's censure.

Mr. Case, however, suffered greatly. Lying outside the house, he remained bedridden the entire next day, lacking the strength to attend his ladies until supper. His mistress, good-naturedly, rebuked him for his indiscretion.

"Why, Case," Mr. Warrington observed later, dining with his relatives, "I could swear it was you riding the London road this morning."

"Me! Law bless you, sir! I was in bed, my head splitting with pain. I ate at six, drank small beer, and am nearly restored. But that Gumbo... I shall never taste his punch again." The honest major-domo resumed his duties among the bottles.

After the meal, Madame Bernstein offered fortifying drinks to Maria against her fainting spells, urging consultation with her London physician via Harry. Harry was departing for two days on an errand for his aunt. "Business beneficial to him," she explained. "I wish Mr. Draper to include him in my will. Since I travel soon, I request Mr. Warrington carry my trinket-box to London in his postchaise; robberies are frequent, and I dislike being halted by highwaymen."

Maria looked blank at the prospect of Harry's departure, hoping for his escort back to Castlewood, where her elder brother had returned. "Nay," his aunt countered. "The lad has been tied to our apron-strings long enough. A day in London will do him no harm. He can complete my errand and return by Saturday."

"I would accompany Mr. Warrington," Mr. Sampson interjected, noting he preached on Friday before Lady Yarmouth, a performance Madame Bernstein had influenced the King's favorite to witness.

Harry embraced the idea of a rattling journey to London, eager for sport. He promised his pistols were reliable and would deliver the diamonds safely to the banker's vault. Would he stay at his aunt's London house? No, it was dreary, staffed only by a maid and groom. He preferred the Star and Garter in Pall Mall or an inn in Covent Garden. "Ah! I have often discussed this journey," Harry said, his expression darkening.

"And with whom, sir?" Lady Maria asked.

"With one who promised to join me," the young man murmured, his mind drifting tenderly to his lost brother. Sudden gusts of grief often swept over him—the separation from his sibling, the memory of George's fall, plans abandoned. "I doubt," the chaplain whispered to Madame Bernstein after one such episode, "that some in England mourn their elder brother so profoundly."

Nevertheless, sorrow was not eternal. Mr. Warrington set forth for London with eagerness, cheerful and happy, perhaps relieved to escape his elderly attachment. At Castlewood, on an unfortunate evening, he had offered his heart to his mature cousin, who had accepted. Marriage was now impossible; he must consult his mother, the mistress of the Virginian estate. Her refusal was certain. This thought hung like a weight around his neck, breeding remorse.

His spirits lifted as he neared London, gazing delightedly from the postchaise window at the city approaching. No highwayman intercepted him on Blackheath. Yonder gleamed the domes of Greenwich, wooded and grand. The Thames stretched out, crowded with ships, and there stood the Tower of London. "Look, Gumbo! The Tower!"
