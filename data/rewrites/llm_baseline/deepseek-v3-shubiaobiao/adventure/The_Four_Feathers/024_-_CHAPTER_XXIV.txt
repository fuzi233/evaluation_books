The land was merciless, and so were its people. That was Calder’s thought as the steamer carried him down the Nile from Wadi Halfa to Aswan, his furlough granting him a brief escape from the harshness of the Sudan. Leaning against the upper deck’s railing, he stared down at the barge lashed alongside, where native passengers crowded beneath the sun’s relentless glare. Among them, on a simple *angareb*, lay a motionless figure shrouded in black—whether man or woman, he couldn’t tell.

The two Arabs who had carried the stretcher aboard at Korosko now lounged in the stern, laughing as though the silent form beside them were nothing more than cargo. The sick man—if he still lived—had been left to the sun’s cruelty, untouched, unfed, while the gangrene spread. Calder clenched his jaw. This was the Sudan’s way: life and death written in dust, accepted without struggle.

As sunset painted the dunes gold, a wind rose, churning the river into whitecaps. Calder found himself relieved—at least the breeze might bring some small comfort to the dying man.

At dinner, he struck up a conversation with a German passenger. "You’re not a doctor, by chance?"

"A medical student," the man replied, adjusting his eyeglasses. "From Bonn. Here to see the cataracts, though they wouldn’t let me beyond Wadi Halfa."

"Then I’ll impose on your holiday," Calder said. "There’s a man below who needs help."

The German hesitated only a moment before nodding. "All experience is valuable."

They descended into the night, where the single swaying lamp cast erratic shadows over the barge’s deck. The wind had grown fiercer, spraying foam across the bow—and there, left in the wash, was the *angareb*, its occupant still unmoving beneath the black veil.

Calder grabbed a lantern. "They haven’t moved him."

Together, they crossed to the barge. The Arabs protested when Calder ordered the covering removed, but he ignored them, peeling back the cloth himself. The wound beneath was festering, alive with infection. The German recoiled.

"This wasn’t from a fall," he muttered. "A spear did this."

Calder bent low. The Arab’s eyes tracked his words—he understood English.

"Where are you from?" Calder pressed.

No answer came, but the man’s gaze sharpened at certain names: *Omdurman. Berber.* And then—*Effendi Feversham.*

A spark of recognition.

Calder’s pulse quickened. This was news he had waited for—news of Harry Feversham. But the Arab could say no more. His lips trembled, yet no sound escaped.

By morning, they delivered him to the hospital in Aswan. The doctors offered little hope. Recovery, if it came at all, would be slow.

Calder sailed on to Cairo, frustration gnawing at him. The chance to aid Feversham had slipped through his fingers. The wounded Arab—had he been able to speak—was Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish. His presence here, broken and silent, meant one thing: Feversham’s plan to rescue Colonel Trench had failed.
