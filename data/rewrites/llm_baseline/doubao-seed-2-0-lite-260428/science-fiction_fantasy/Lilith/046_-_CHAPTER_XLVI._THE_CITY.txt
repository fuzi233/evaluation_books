Lona and I picked our way along sun-dappled ground beneath the world-spanning canopy when a great, ringing shout tore down through the leaves above us. Moments later, the Little Ones came tumbling out of the foliage one by one, cheeks flushed, gowns smudged with sap, all chattering over each other to share their news: the bravest of them had scrambled to the very crown of a tree taller than any that had stretched skyward before it, and there, far across the rolling plain, had spied something strange clinging to the flank of a lone, towering mountain. That peak, they swore, climbed so high it pierced the very heart of the sky, which had bunched thick and heavy to hold it down, snapping its summit clean off. “It might be a city,” they bubbled, “but it looks nothing at all like Bulika.”

I scaled the colossus of a tree myself to see, and there it was: a city climbing upward into banks of hazy blue cloud, until I could no longer tell where mountain crag ended, sky began, and the gilded spires of palaces dissolved into mist. Cloud and stone and sky, precipice and domed roof wove together into a glittering chaos of broken shadow and shifting light.
I climbed back down, the Little Ones clinging to my heels, and we all sped forward faster than we had ever walked before. Their merriment only swelled as they went, darting ahead to lead the way, never once glancing over their shoulders. The river at our side grew more lovely with every step, until I realized I had never once seen real, living water in all my days on Earth. No lake, no stream I had known back there had ever been more than a faint, pale copy of this.

Before long, we could spot the city from the flat of the plain, half-shrouded in its blue cloud wrappings. But other clouds were coiling now around a spire so tall I could not tell if it was masonry or raw bedrock, jabbing up above the city, almost to the mountain’s crest. Gray, deep charcoal, soft bruised purple, they writhed in conflicting, swirling motions, tossing foamy plumes of vapor up into the air, while pockets of them spun like living whirlpools.
At last, a blinding flash burst forth, and for a heartbeat it danced across the hair and faces of the Little Ones running before us. Blinding darkness closed in after it, but through the hush we heard their voices, low and thrumming with unnameable delight.
“Did you see?”
“I saw!”
“What did you see?”
“The most beautiful man that ever was!”
“I heard him speak!”
“I didn’t! What did he say?”
The smallest, softest voice piped up then: Luva, the tiniest of the Little Ones, lisping around a gasp of joy. “He said, ‘All of you are mine, little ones: come along!’”

I had seen the lightning, but heard no voice. Lona, though, had seen and heard exactly what the children had. A second flash flared, and my eyes, if not my ears, were opened. That great, quivering sheet of light was stitched together from angel faces, blazing bright for a heartbeat before they winked out of sight.
A third flash burst, and its radiance was human, warm and familiar. “I see my mother!” I cried out.
“I see hundreds of mothers!” Luva chirped.
Once more the cloud blazed, and every kind of living creature filled that glow: horses, elephants, lions, hounds, beasts far more gentle and splendid than any I had ever imagined. And the birds! Great wide-winged ones whose feathers held every colour of sunset and rainbow, each plume glowing on its own; tiny songbirds whose feathers sparkled as if dusted with every precious stone the Earth had ever hoarded deep in its crust. Silver cranes, crimson flamingoes, opal-pale pigeons, peacocks draped in gold and emerald and sapphire light, jewel-bright hummingbirds, great flocks of butterfly wings the size of sails, lithe graceful creeping things—all of it, every one, alive in that single heavenly burst of light.
“Serpents turn into birds here,” Lona said softly, wonder in her tone, “just the way caterpillars used to turn into butterflies back home.”
“I saw my white pony,” I said, suddenly breathless, “the one that died when I was seven. I never needed to grieve so hard. All I had to do was wait a little longer.”

There was no thunder, no crack of clap or roll to follow the flashes. Instead, a soft, sweet rain began to fall, wrapping the air in a cool, gentle caress. We breathed deep, and stepped forward with strides that felt stronger, lighter, than any we had taken before. Every falling droplet glowed with the light of every gem that had ever woken under the Earth, and a rainbow so vast it spanned the whole width of the city arched overhead.
The blue clouds thickened, the rain poured down in torrents, and the children shrieked with joy and ran, darting so fast we had to hustle to keep them in sight. Beside us, the river rolled on silent and radiant, brim-full to the edge of its soft, yielding banks. There was no rock, no shingle, no sand beneath its waters: it flowed over thick, lush grass dotted thick with primroses and daisies, crocuses and narcissi, pimpernels and anemones, a whole host of star-shaped blooms glowing bright and clear through the luminous water. The river did not turn murky from the rain, did not tinge a hint of yellow or brown. Its clear, luminous mass shone with the pale, sea-green gleam that rose up from its dainty, living bed.

As we drew closer to the mountain, we saw that the river sprung straight from its very peak, rushing in full, frothing volume straight through the city’s main street. It cascaded down toward the gate over a wide, deep stair hewn from porphyry and serpentine that ran all the way to the mountain’s foot. When we reached it, we found shallower steps cut into both banks, leading up to the gate and along the ascending street.
Without pausing for even a breath, the Little Ones sprinted straight up the stairs to the gate, which stood wide open, unbarred. On the landing just outside sat the portress, a dark-faced woman angel leaning her shadowed brow on an idle hand. The children swarmed her in a wave, showering her with hugs and kisses, and before she could even stand to greet them, they had taken heaven itself by surprise, vanishing into the city still running up the stairs right beside the roaring torrent.

A great, towering angel came down with a retinue of shining ones to meet and welcome them, but the Little Ones dodged around all of them gaily, still scrambling higher. A group of woman angels descended on them in a merry flurry, though, and in a moment the children were wrapped safe in heavenly arms. The radiant beings carried them away, and I saw them no more.
“Ah,” the mighty angel said, as he kept walking down to meet Lona and me, who were nearly at the gate now, within easy hearing, “that is perfect! These are soldiers who would storm heaven itself to claim their home. I have heard word that a horde of black bats lurks on the frontiers. These little ones will make short work of such vermin!” He spotted the horse and elephants we had seen earlier clambering up the stair behind us. “Take those creatures to the royal stables,” he bade two of his attendants, “tend them well, then turn them loose in the king’s forest.”
“Welcome home,” he said to us, bending low with the softest, sweetest smile I had ever seen. He turned at once to lead the way higher. The scales of his armour flashed like flecks of living lightning.

I had no words for the feeling that swelled in my chest, to be welcomed so by the very stewards of heaven. Every longing I had ever held, every unspoken want I had not even dared name, must surely be waiting for me just a little further up the path. We paused for a moment at the gate, where the radiant river roared past us out of the mountain. I do not know what far-off quarries yielded the stones that made this city, but I could spot the prototypes of every gem I had ever loved on Earth, and they were far more beautiful than any earthly copy: these were living stones. In each one I saw not just the idea that had shaped it, but the mind that had thought it, the maker who had breathed it into being, the hand that had sent it out into the world. Nothing in this kingdom was dead. Nothing was merely an object.

We climbed up through the city, and passed out the far side of it. There was no wall on the upper edge, only a massive jumble of broken rock sloping upward like the moraine of an eternal, never-melting glacier, and through the gaps between the boulders, the river of life billowed outward. At the very top of the slope I could just make out three or four massive steps of a grand stair, vanishing into a cloud as white as fresh-fallen snow. Above those steps, not with my physical eyes but with the sight of my soul, I glimpsed what looked like an ancient, grand throne: the seat of the Ancient of Days. Over, under, and between those steps, the river of living water bubbled forth, endless, forever new-born.

The great angel could go no further. We must climb those rocks alone. My heart thudding with equal parts hope and hungry desire, I squeezed Lona’s hand tight, and we began to climb. Soon we let go of each other, so we could use our hands as well as our feet to scramble up the huge, uneven stones.
At last we drew near the cloud, which hung down over the top steps like the hem of a king’s garment. We passed through its soft, fringing edges, and stepped into its deep, billowing folds. A hand, warm and strong, wrapped around mine, and drew me toward a little door with a golden lock. The door swung open, the hand let go of mine, and gave me a soft, gentle push through. I spun around fast, just in time to see the cover of a great, massive book click shut behind me.
I stood alone, back in my own library.
