Buck was changing—deeply, silently. The ancient instinct that slept in his blood stirred and grew stronger with every mile of trail, every gust of wind, every bitter night under the stars. But it was not a wild, reckless force; it was cunning, deliberate, patient. He moved through the harsh world of the northern wilderness with quiet control, never rushing into conflict, never showing weakness. Though he felt the hatred burning between him and Spitz, he held back. There was no foolishness in Buck now. He watched, waited, and learned.

Spitz, on the other hand, could not hide his disdain. He saw Buck as a threat—a strange dog from the South, too large, too strong, too silent. And so he tested him constantly, snarling, snapping, trying to provoke a fight. But Buck refused to rise. Not yet.

Then came the night at Lake Le Barge.

Snow fell like knives. The wind bit through fur and bone. They made camp on the ice, sheltered only by a sheer rock wall. No tent, no firewood enough for warmth. François thawed fish over a small blaze that melted through the ice and left them eating in darkness. Buck curled up close to the rock, digging a nest where the cold could not reach him. When François brought the fish, Buck ate quickly and returned to his spot—only to find Spitz already there.

A low growl rumbled in Spitz’s throat. Buck froze. Then something inside him broke.

He lunged.

It wasn’t rage—it was instinct. A roar rose from deep within, older than memory, older than fear. Spitz was stunned. He had always thought Buck timid, a brute who survived only because of size. But this was different. This was a challenge.

They circled, eyes locked, muscles coiled. Just as they were about to clash, chaos erupted.

An oath from Perrault. A crack of a club. A yelp of pain.

The camp was swarming with starving huskies—dozens of them, drawn by the smell of food. They had crept in while the two dogs fought. Now they surged forward, teeth bared, eyes wild with hunger. One had its head buried in the grub-box. Perrault struck it hard. The box tumbled. In an instant, the wolves tore into the food, fighting over scraps, ignoring the clubs that swung down upon them.

The team-dogs were caught off guard. They burst from their nests and found themselves surrounded. These huskies were nothing like the ones they knew—gaunt, half-dead, driven mad by hunger. Their bones showed through skin, their jaws dripped saliva, their eyes burned with frenzy. They attacked without mercy.

Buck was set upon by three. His head and shoulders were ripped open. Blood ran warm over his muzzle. He fought back, tearing one’s throat, feeling the spray of life in his mouth. It stoked his fury. Then, from the side, fangs sank into his neck.

Spitz.

He had turned traitor, striking while Buck was occupied.

Perrault and François rushed in, swinging clubs. The wave of beasts recoiled. Buck shook free—but only for a moment. The men had to return to save the food, and the attack resumed.

Billee, usually gentle, bolted. Pike and Dub followed. The rest fled after them. As Buck prepared to leap, he saw Spitz charge—intent on knocking him down into the pack. He braced himself, absorbed the impact, and then sprinted across the ice, joining the others.

Later, they gathered in the woods. Every dog was wounded. Dub limped badly. Dolly had a torn throat. Joe had lost an eye. Billee whimpered all night, her ear shredded. At dawn, they limped back to camp. The marauders were gone. But the damage was done: half the food was eaten, the sled lashings chewed, even François’s whip cord gnawed. The men were furious.

François looked over the dogs, voice soft.

“Ah, my friends,” he said. “Maybe these bites make you mad dogs. Maybe all of you are mad now. What do you think, eh, Perrault?”

Perrault frowned. With four hundred miles still ahead, madness among the dogs was dangerous. They worked fast—two hours of swearing and mending—and the team was back on the trail, struggling through the hardest stretch yet.

The Thirty Mile River was open. Water roared beneath thin ice. They crossed it in agony. Perrault broke through twelve times, saved only by his long pole. Each time, he had to build a fire to dry his clothes. The temperature dropped to fifty below zero. Once, the sled broke through, dragging Dave and Buck into the freezing current. They were half-drowned before being pulled out. They stood around the fire, covered in ice, forced to run until they sweated and thawed, so close to the flames they were singed.

Another time, Spitz went through, pulling the entire team with him. Buck strained backward, claws digging into the ice edge. Behind him, Dave pulled. Behind the sled, François hauled with cracked tendons.

Then the rim ice gave way. There was no escape but up the cliff. Perrault climbed, miracle-like. François prayed. They tied every rope, harness, and lashing together and hoisted the dogs one by one. The sled came last. Then they searched for a path down—using the rope again. Night found them back on the river, barely a quarter-mile ahead.

By the time they reached the Hootalinqua, Buck was exhausted. His feet hurt. They were softer than the huskies’, worn down by generations of domestication. All day he limped, collapsed at camp, refusing food unless François brought it to him. Every night, the dog-driver rubbed his paws for half an hour, then cut the tops off his own moccasins to make boots for Buck.

One morning, François forgot. Buck lay on his back, legs waving in the air, refusing to move. Even Perrault grinned.

Soon, Buck’s feet hardened. The moccasins were discarded.

At the Pelly, something terrible happened.

Dolly—always quiet, unremarkable—suddenly went mad. She let out a long, heart-wrenching howl that sent chills through every dog. Then she charged straight at Buck.

He had never seen such madness. He ran—not from fear of death, but from the horror of what she had become. He fled, terrified, with Dolly panting, frothing, just one leap behind. He raced across islands, through rivers choked with ice, desperate to escape. He didn’t look back—he could hear her snarling, closing in.

François called from a quarter-mile away. Buck turned, still one leap ahead, gasping, trusting the man would save him.

The axe came down—crash—on Dolly’s skull.

Buck staggered, breathless, sobbing, helpless.

And then Spitz struck.

He leapt on Buck, teeth sinking into flesh, ripping deep. But François’s lash snapped down. Buck watched as Spitz received the worst beating any dog had ever endured.

“One devil, that Spitz,” Perrault muttered.

“Dat Buck? Two devils,” François replied. “I watch him. I know. Some fine day, he’ll go mad as hell—and he’ll chew Spitz up and spit him out on the snow. Sure.”

From that moment, war began.

Spitz, the lead-dog, felt his rule threatened. Buck was unlike any Southland dog he’d known. Most failed—died under frost, work, starvation. But Buck endured. He matched the huskies in strength, cunning, and savagery. And he was smarter. The club had broken his old recklessness, leaving only cold, calculating will.

This was inevitable—the clash for leadership.

Buck wanted it. Not for power alone, but for pride. The pride of the trace—the same pride that drove Dave to pull until he dropped, Sol-leks to strain with every ounce, Spitz to punish the weak. It was the pride that made dogs live for the trail, die in the harness, break their hearts when removed.

And Buck had it.

He challenged Spitz openly. He stepped between him and the dogs he punished. Once, Pike hid under a foot of snow. Spitz raged, digging, snarling. When Pike was found, Spitz lunged to strike. But Buck flew in—just as fast, just as fierce. Spitz was thrown back. Pike, trembling, jumped on him. Buck joined in.

François laughed—then cracked the lash. Buck wouldn’t yield. The butt of the whip hit him. He fell, dazed, but stayed. Spitz beat Pike mercilessly.

After that, Buck kept interfering—only when François wasn’t watching. A quiet rebellion spread. The team unraveled. Dave and Sol-leks stayed loyal. But the others grew bold. Pike stole half a fish. Dub and Joe fought Spitz. Billee whined less. Buck never passed Spitz without snarling, swaggering past like a bully.

Discipline broke. The camp became a battlefield. François cursed, stamped, tore his hair. His lash sang, but it did no good. When his back turned, the dogs were at it again.

He backed Spitz. Buck backed the rest.

But Buck was too clever to be caught. He worked hard—because the trail had become joy. Yet greater joy was stirring trouble. He loved seeing fights start, traces tangled, chaos reign.

At the Tahkeena, one night, Dub flushed a snowshoe rabbit. It vanished into a creek. The whole team chased it—sixty dogs, including fifty police huskies from a nearby camp.

Buck led the pack. He ran low, muscles flashing, breath coming in eager whines. Moonlight painted the snow white. The rabbit danced ahead—light, swift, a ghost of frost.

This was more than hunting. This was primal ecstasy—the bloodlust, the joy of the chase, the thrill of killing with your own teeth. It was deeper than any man’s hunt. Buck was running with the pack, chasing living meat, driven by instincts older than language.

There is a peak of life—an ecstasy beyond words. It comes when one is most alive, yet forgets being alive. It strikes the artist in flame, the soldier in battle, the animal in flight.

And it came to Buck—leading the pack, howling the old wolf-song, straining after prey that fled through moonlit snow. He was touching the depths of his soul, diving back into time itself. He was ruled by the surge of life—the tidal wave of being—the pure joy of every muscle, every sinew, expressing itself in motion, flying under stars, over dead earth.

But Spitz, colder, sharper, cut across the bend. He leapt from the bank—into the rabbit’s path.

The rabbit couldn’t turn. Teeth snapped. A shriek echoed—loud as a man’s cry.

Life falling from life’s height into death.

The pack roared—hell’s chorus of triumph.

And Buck, at the front, felt the echo of the wild song rise in his chest. He had been born again—not as a pet, not as a servant—but as a beast of the wild, answering the call.
