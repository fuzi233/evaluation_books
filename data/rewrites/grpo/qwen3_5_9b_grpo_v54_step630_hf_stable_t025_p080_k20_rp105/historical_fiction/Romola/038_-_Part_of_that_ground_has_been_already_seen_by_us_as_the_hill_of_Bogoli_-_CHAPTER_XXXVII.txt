The first sound of the morning was a tap at the door, sharp and intrusive against the silence of the room. Romola woke to find Maso standing there, his face illuminated by the pale, hard light of dawn. He had come for the travelling-wallet. When she opened the door, the old man started, expecting the familiar, graceful silhouette crowned with the brightness of her hair. Instead, he saw only the heavy folds of a grey mantle and a pale face obscured by a dark cowl.

"It is well, Maso," Romola said, forcing her voice to remain steady and calm. "The wallet is ready. Go on quietly; I shall not be far behind. Once you pass the gates, you may walk slower, for I hope to join you before you reach Trespiano."

She closed the door behind him, locking the silence back into the house. Then, her hand went to the key she had taken from the casket the night before. It was the original key to the little painted tabernacle. Tito had failed to drown it in the Arno; instead, it had lodged in the corner of the embroidered *scarsella* he wore beneath his purple tunic. Years later, Romola had found it there, setting it aside without use, yet feeling a quiet satisfaction that it remained within reach.

The cabinet holding the tabernacle had been moved near a window, where the morning light now struck it, rendering the painted figures distinct enough for Romola to see them clearly—the triumphant Bacchus with his vine-clad spear embracing the crowned Ariadne; the Loves scattering roses; the wreathed vessels and the cunning-eyed dolphins amidst the rippling sea, all framed by a flowery border like a paradise bower. On this chill morning, however, the familiar images offered no comfort. They seemed a more pitiful mockery than ever, a lying screen rather than a tomb of sorrow. Foolish Ariadne, with her gaze of love, believing that bright face held the secret of her life!

"Ariadne is wonderfully transformed," Romola thought bitterly. "She would look strange among these vines and roses now."

She picked up the mirror and gazed at her reflection. The sight was startling in the harsh morning light, and she laid it down again, feeling a shrinking almost as intense as when she had turned away from the joyous painting. To recognize her own face, veiled in the cowl, brought back the dread of being drawn into the fellowship of wretched superstition, of joining the howling fanatics and weeping nuns whom she had despised since childhood. With trembling fingers, she thrust the key into the tabernacle, opened it hurriedly, and withdrew the crucifix without looking at it. She passed a cord through the small ring, hung the cross around her neck, and hid it deep within the bosom of her mantle. "For Dino's sake," she whispered to herself.

Still, there were matters to attend to. The letters for Maso to carry back to Bologna were brief. The first was addressed to Tito:

"Tito, my love for you is dead; and therefore, so far as I was yours, I too am dead. Do not attempt to enforce any laws to fetch me back; it would bring you no happiness. The Romola you married can never return. I need explain nothing further after the words I spoke to you during our last long conversation. If you believed them to be words of transient anger, you will now know they marked an irreversible change.

"I believe you will fulfill my wish that my bridal chest be sent to my godfather, who gave it to me. It contains my wedding clothes and the portraits and other relics of my father and mother."

She folded the ring inside this letter and wrote Tito's name on the outside. The second letter was to Bernardo del Nero:

"Dearest Godfather,—If I could have been of any good to your life by staying, I would not have gone so far away. But now I am gone. Do not ask the reason; and if you love my father, try to prevent anyone from seeking me. I could not bear my life in Florence. I cannot bear to tell anyone why. Help to cover my lot in silence. I have requested that my bridal chest be sent to you: when you open it, you will understand the reason. Please give all the things that belonged to my mother to my cousin Brigida, and ask her to forgive me for not saying words of parting to her.

"Farewell, my second father. The best thing I have in life remains the memory of your goodness and my gratitude.

"Romola."

Romola placed the letters and the crucifix within the bosom of her mantle. Everything was done. She was ready to depart.

The house was empty. She descended the stairs and stepped into the silent street almost as quietly as a grey phantom. Her heart hammered violently, yet she felt a strange enjoyment in the firm tread of her feet on the broad flags, in the swift movement that felt like a chained resolution finally set free. The anxiety to complete her act and the dread of any obstacle kept sorrow at bay. As she reached the Ponte Rubaconte, she felt less that Santa Croce was visible before her than that the yellow streak of morning light, parting the grey, was widening. Unless she hastened, she would soon encounter faces.

Her direct path led straight to the Borgo Pinti, then along the walls to the Porta San Gallo, the exit from the city, a route that took her past the Piazza di Santa Croce. Yet she walked steadily and rapidly through the square, refusing to look toward the church. The thought that eyes might turn upon her with curiosity or recognition, that indifferent minds might speculate on her private sorrows, made her shrink physically as from imagined torture. She felt degraded even by the act of her husband from which she suffered helplessly. Yet there was no sign that any eyes looked forth from the windows to notice this tall grey sister, with her firm step and the proud, hidden attitude of her cowl. Her road lay clear of the early traffic's stir. Reaching the Porta San Gallo, she slipped through easily while a dispute raged over the toll for panniers of eggs and market produce entering the gate.

Out! Once past the houses of the Borgo, she would be beyond Florence's last fringe. The sky would be broad above her, and she would enter a new life—one of loneliness and endurance, but of freedom. She had been strong enough to snap the bonds she had accepted in blind faith. Whatever befell her, she would no longer feel the breath of soft, hated lips warming her cheek, nor the stifling breath of an odious mind crushing her own. The bare wintry morning, the chill air, were welcome in their severity. The leafless trees and sombre hills were not haunted by the gods of beauty and joy, whose worship she had forever forsaken.

Presently, the light burst forth with sudden strength, casting long shadows across the road. It seemed the sun intended to chase away the greyness. Perhaps light is never felt more strongly as a divine presence stirring the inarticulate sensibilities of our deepest lives than in these moments when it instantly awakens the shadows. A certain awe, inevitable in this most momentous act of her life, became a conscious element in Romola's feeling as she found herself in the sudden presence of the impalpable golden glory and the long shadow of herself that could not be escaped.

Thus far, she had met only occasional countrymen with mules, and the many turns of the level road had prevented her from seeing that Maso was not far ahead. But when she passed Pietra and climbed onto rising ground, she lifted the hanging roof of her cowl and looked eagerly before her.

The cowl was dropped immediately. She had seen not Maso, but two monks approaching within a few yards. The edge of her cowl had acted as a penthouse over her brow, shutting out anything above eye level; for the last few moments, she had seen only the brightness of the path and her own shadow, tall and shrouded like a dread spectre.

She wished now that she had not looked up. Her disguise made her especially dislike encountering monks; they might expect pious passwords of which she knew nothing. She walked along with a careful appearance of unconsciousness until she saw the skirts of their black mantles pass her. The encounter made her heart beat unpleasantly. Romola harbored an uneasiness in her religious disguise, a shame at this studied concealment, made more distinct by her special effort to appear unaware under actual glances.

But the black skirts would vanish the faster, for they were going downhill. Seeing a great flat stone against a cypress that rose from a projecting green bank, she yielded to the desire the slight shock had given her to sit and rest.

She turned her back on Florence, intending not to look at it until the monks were quite out of sight. Raising the edge of her cowl again once seated, she discerned Maso and the mules at a distance where it was not hopeless for her to overtake them, for the old man would likely linger in expectation.

Meanwhile, she might pause a little. She was free and alone.
