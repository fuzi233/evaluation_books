There are moments in life when the subconscious, busy with its hidden agenda, leaks a fragment of destiny into our conscious awareness. Who has not felt that inexplicable surge of assurance that things are about to turn out right? It is not the manic confidence of a man facing disaster, nor the stubborn delusion of an optimist, but an unsolicited conviction rising like a bird from the heather: success is imminent in some grand endeavor. As Trent climbed the stairs outside the library, he felt this certainty settle over him. A swarm of guesses and inferences churned through his mind, unsorted and chaotic; a few private observations lingered, disconnected from any plausible theory of the crime. Yet, ascending the steps, he knew indubitably that the light was coming.

The bedrooms lined a broad, carpeted corridor illuminated by a tall end window that stretched the length of the house before angling into a narrower passage housing the servants' quarters. Martin's room was an outlier, opening onto a small landing midway up the stairs. Trent glanced inside as he passed; a modest, clean, and utterly ordinary space. He ascended the remainder of the staircase with exaggerated caution, hugging the wall and placing each foot deliberately, though a series of loud creaks betrayed his progress.

He knew Manderson's room was the first on the right upon reaching the bedroom floor and went straight to it. The latch and lock yielded normally; he inspected the key's wards before entering.

Inside, the apartment was small and strangely bare. The plutocrat's toiletries were minimal. Everything remained exactly as it had been on the morning of the ghastly discovery in the grounds. The sheets and blankets of the unmade bed lay in a tumbled heap over a narrow wooden frame, while bright sunlight streamed through the window, illuminating them. The light also caught the gold components of delicate dental work resting in water within a shallow glass bowl on a small, plain table beside the bed. A wrought-iron candlestick stood nearby, and some clothing lay untidily draped over one of two rush-bottomed chairs. Various objects atop a chest of drawers, repurposed as a dressing table, were scattered in the disorder of a hurried departure. Trent surveyed them with a searching gaze, noting that the occupant had neither washed nor shaved. He turned the dental plate over with his finger, frowning again at its incomprehensible presence.

The emptiness and disarray of the sun-drenched room produced a sense of gruesomeness in Trent. His imagination conjured a picture of a haggard man dressing in careful silence by the first light of dawn, glancing constantly at the inner door behind which his wife slept, his eyes filled with terror.

Trent shivered. To ground himself in reality, he opened two tall wall cupboards flanking the bed. They held clothing, a large selection of which had clearly been among the few comforts available to the man who had slept there.

Regarding footwear, Manderson had indulged his wealth. An extraordinary number of shoes, treed and meticulously maintained, lined two long, low shelves against the wall. No boots were present. Trent, an amateur of fine leather himself, turned his appreciative eye to the collection. It was evident Manderson had taken pride in a small, well-formed foot. The shoes were of a distinctive shape—narrow and round-toed, beautifully crafted—all seemingly from the same last.

Suddenly, Trent's eyes narrowed upon a pair of patent-leather shoes on the upper shelf.

These were the shoes the inspector had previously described; the ones Manderson had worn the night before his death. They were well-worn, yet Trent noticed they had been polished very recently. Something about the uppers seized his attention. He bent lower, frowning as he compared them to the neighboring shoes. Then, lifting them, he examined the junction of the uppers and soles.

As he did so, Trent began to whistle faintly and precisely, an air that Inspector Murch, had he been present, would have instantly recognized. Most men with a habit of self-control possess an involuntary tic that signals suppressed excitement to those who know them. The inspector had noted that when Trent encountered a strong scent, he would whistle a specific melodious passage—the opening movement of Mendelssohn's *Lied ohne Worte* in A Major, though the inspector could not have articulated this fact.

Trent turned the shoes over, measured them with a marked tape, and scrutinized the soles. On each, in the angle between the heel and the instep, he detected a faint trace of red gravel.

Placing the shoes on the floor, Trent walked with his hands behind him to the window. Still whistling faintly, he gazed out with eyes that saw nothing. Once, his lips parted to emit the Englishman's expletive of sudden enlightenment. Finally, he turned back to the shelves and swiftly, carefully examined every pair.

Having done so, he took the garments from the chair, inspected them closely, and replaced them. He returned to the wardrobes, hunting through them methodically. The litter on the dressing table engaged his attention for the second time. Then, sitting on the empty chair, he took his head in his hands and stared at the carpet for several minutes. Rising at last, he opened the inner door leading to Mrs. Manderson's room.

At a glance, it was clear the large room had been hastily stripped of its function as a lady's bower. The array of objects belonging to a woman's dressing table had vanished; beds, chairs, and smaller tables were devoid of garments, hats, bags, or boxes. No trace remained of the obstinate conspiracy of gloves, veils, handkerchiefs, and ribbons to break the captivity of the drawer. The room resembled an unoccupied guest chamber. Yet, in every detail of furniture and decoration, it spoke of an unconventional but exacting taste. As Trent's expert eye noted the various perfections of color and form amid which the ill-matched lady had dreamed her dreams and harbored her loneliest thoughts, he knew she possessed at least the resources of an artistic nature. His interest in this unknown personality grew stronger; his brows furrowed heavily as he considered the burdens laid upon her, and the deed whose history was now taking shape with increasing substance before his busy mind.

He approached the tall French window in the center of the wall facing the door and opened it, stepping out onto a small balcony with an iron railing. Below lay a broad stretch of lawn beginning immediately beneath him, separated from the house wall only by a narrow flower bed, stretching away with an abrupt dip at the far end toward the orchard. The other window opened with a sash above the garden entrance of the library. In the far inner corner stood a second door giving onto the passage—the door by which the maid usually entered and her mistress exited in the morning.

Seated on the bed, Trent quickly sketched a plan of the room and its neighbor in his notebook. The bed stood in the angle between the communicating door and the sash window, its head against the wall dividing the room from Manderson's. He stared at the pillows, then lay down deliberately on the bed and looked through the open door into the adjoining room.

Taking this observation, he rose and proceeded to note on his plan that small tables with covers stood on either side of the bed. Upon the one furthest from the door was a graceful electric lamp standard of copper, connected by a free wire to the wall. Trent looked at it thoughtfully, then at the switches controlling the other lights in the room. They were, as usual, on the wall just inside the door, some distance out of his reach while seated on the bed. He rose, satisfied himself that the lights were in order, then turned on his heel, walked quickly into Manderson's room, and rang the bell.

"I want your help again, Martin," he said as the butler presented himself, upright and impassive, in the doorway. "I want you to prevail upon Mrs. Manderson's maid to grant me an interview."

"Certainly, sir," Martin replied.

"What sort of a woman is she? Has she her wits about her?"

"She's French, sir," Martin replied succinctly, adding after a pause, "She has not been with us long, sir, but I have formed the impression that the young woman knows as much of the world as is good for her—since you ask me."

"You think butter might possibly melt in her mouth, do you?" Trent said. "Well, I am not afraid. I want to put some questions to her."

"I will send her up immediately, sir." The butler withdrew, and Trent wandered round the little room with his hands at his back. Sooner than expected, a small, neat figure in black appeared quietly before him.

The lady's maid, with her large brown eyes, had taken a favorable notice of Trent from a window when he crossed the lawn, hoping desperately that the resolver of mysteries—whose reputation was as great below-stairs as elsewhere—would summon her. For one thing, she felt the need to make a scene; her nerves were overwrought. But her scenes were discounted by the other domestics, and as for Mr. Murch, he had chilled her into self-control with his official manner. Trent, she had glimpsed, did not have the air of a policeman, and at a distance he had appeared *sympathique*.

As she entered the room, however, instinct decided for her that any approach to coquetry would be a mistake if she sought to make a good impression at the outset. It was with an air of amiable candor that she said, "Monsieur desire to speak with me." She added helpfully, "I am called Célestine."

"Naturally," said Trent with businesslike calm. "Now what I want you to tell me, Célestine, is this. When you took tea to your mistress yesterday morning at seven o'clock, was the door between the two bedrooms—this door here—open?"

Célestine became intensely animated in an instant. "Oh yes!" she said, using her favorite English idiom. "The door was open as always, monsieur, and I shut it as always. But it is necessary to explain. Listen! When I enter the room of madame from the other door in there—ah! but if monsieur will give himself the pain to enter the other room, all explains itself." She tripped across to the door and urged Trent before her into the larger bedroom with a hand on his arm. "See! I enter the room with the tea like this. I approach the bed. Before I come quite near the bed, here is the door to my right hand—open always—so! But monsieur can perceive that I see nothing in the room of Monsieur Manderson. The door opens to the bed, not to me who approach from down there. I shut it without seeing in. It is the order. Yesterday it was as ordinary. I see nothing of the next room. Madame sleep like an angel—she see nothing. I shut the door. I place the *plateau*—I open the curtains—I prepare the toilette—I retire—voilà!" Célestine paused for breath and spread her hands abroad.

Trent, who had followed her movements and gesticulations with deepening gravity, nodded his head. "I see exactly how it was now," he said. "Thank you, Célestine. So Mr. Manderson was supposed to be still in his room while your mistress was getting up, dressing, and having breakfast in her boudoir?"

"Oui, monsieur."

"Nobody missed him, in fact," remarked Trent. "Well, Célestine, I am very much obliged to you." He reopened the door to the outer bedroom.

"It is nothing, monsieur," said Célestine as she crossed the small room. "I hope that monsieur will catch the assassin of Monsieur Manderson. But I not regret him too much," she added with sudden and amazing violence, turning round with her hand on the knob of the outer door. She set her teeth with an audible sound, and the color rose in her small dark face. English departed from her. "Je ne le regrette pas du tout, du tout!" she cried with a flood of words. "Madame—ah! je me jetterais au feu pour madame—une femme si charmante, si adorable! Mais un homme comme monsieur—maussade, boudeur, impassible! Ah, non!—de ma vie! J'en avais par-dessus la tête, de monsieur! Ah! vrai! Est-ce insupportable, tout de même, qu'il existe des types comme ça? Je vous jure que—"

"Finissez ce chahut, Célestine!" Trent broke in sharply. Célestine's tirade had brought back the memory of his student days with a rush. "En voilà une scène! C'est rasant, vous savez. Faut rentret ça, mademoiselle. Du reste, c'est bien imprudent, croyez-moi. Hang it! Have some common sense! If the inspector downstairs heard you saying that kind of thing, you would get into trouble. And don't wave your fists about so much; you might hit something. You seem," he went on more pleasantly as Célestine grew calmer under his authoritative eye, "to be even more glad than other people that Mr. Manderson is out of the way. I could almost suspect, Célestine, that Mr. Manderson did not take as much notice of you as you thought necessary and right."

"A peine s'il m'avait regardé!" Célestine answered simply.

"Ça, c'est un comble!" observed Trent. "You are a nice young woman for a small tea-party, I don't think. A star upon your birthday burned, whose fierce, serene, red, pulseless planet never yearned in heaven, Célestine. Mademoiselle, I am busy. Bon jour. You certainly are a beauty!"

Célestine took this as a scarcely expected compliment. The surprise restored her balance. With a sudden flash of her eyes and teeth at Trent over her shoulder, the lady's maid opened the door and swiftly disappeared.
