Nature shudders at the thought of separation, and I shall therefore offer no commentary on the private turmoil within Harry Warrington's heart as he prepares to bid farewell to his kin. Let us not forget the thousands of others standing in identical desolation. Consider Mr. Wolfe, whose mother waits with lips ready to press his cheek, while his father lies cold in death—a victim of the glorious campaign at Louisbourg. Consider his beloved, awaiting a final embrace. Look further to the sea: stout Admiral Holmes, commanding *The Somerset*, *The Terrible*, *The Northumberland*, *The Royal William*, *The Trident*, *The Diana*, and *The Seahorse*—his flag flying proudly above *The Dublin*—stands on the dock to tear himself away from Lady Holmes and his daughters. And what of Admiral Saunders, who sails the following day? Is he immune to the human spirit?

Away drifts William and his crew of boisterous mariners, cutting through tumbling swells, while poor Black-eyed Susan remains on the shore, watching the vessel shrink until it vanishes into the gloom of the setting sun. It fades into the West. Night descends swiftly, a heavy curtain over the ocean. They are gone, yet their spirits linger at home for a fleeting moment. In a silence heavy with a tenderness that words cannot capture, every man turns inward, thinking of those left behind. What a vast, sorrowful hymn of prayer ascends to the Father—from the wife kneeling in tears beside a vacant bedside, from the mother and children gathered round the fire, pouring out their pleas, to the sailor on deck gazing upward at the stars as his ship carves a path through the midnight roar. Tomorrow, the sun will rise, and we shall resume our mundane tasks of labor and duty.

George accompanies his brother, remaining at Portsmouth while they wait for the winds to fill the sails. He shakes Mr. Wolfe's hand, gazes one last time at his brother's pale countenance, and watches the ships depart amidst the clamor of ringing bells and the thunder of cannon fired from the coast. The next day finds George back home, returning to that occupation which, for nearly every man past thirty, proves the most self-absorbing and consuming of worldly pursuits. A pang of deep sadness strikes him as he glances into the lodgings where Harry once slept, seeing his half-burned papers lingering in the grate. Soon, however, he is off to Dean Street again, whispering affectionately to his devoted sweetheart by the flickering light of a dying fire. She is ecstatic at his return—so incredibly happy! yet she feels a profound shame regarding this joy. "Is it not cruel to be so happy?" she asks, considering the melancholy state of poor Hetty. "Poor little Hetty!" George reflects, understanding the sting of her grief. "Indeed, it is selfish to rejoice when she is so sad. It makes one quite wretched to see her." "Do not, sir!" Theo exclaims, her voice trembling with guilt. "Well, I ought to be wretched, and it is terribly wicked of me if I am not." One understands her soft-hearted repentance perfectly. Perhaps her "Do not" was spoken in one of those moments when the darkness seemed to swallow the room whole. Servants arrive with supper and candles; the family gathers, and conversation flows freely. The destination of the fleet is now common knowledge throughout the realm. The force aboard is deemed sufficient to crush the French in Canada, and under an officer of Wolfe's caliber, to correct the blunders and disasters of previous campaigns. He appeared dreadingly ill, indeed, but he possesses a great soul housed within a frail body. The ministers and the nation hold their breath, hoping for everything from him. After supper, as per custom, Mr. Lambert convenes his modest household—including George Warrington, who has become something of a fixture—and as he offers prayers for travelers by land and sea, Theo and her sister kneel side by side. Thus, as the ship races farther and farther into the West, these fond thoughts chase it across the waves; the night slips away, and the sun rises anew.

A day or two more, and everyone returns to their books or customary labors. As for George Warrington, that celebrated dramatist is already engrossed in another composition. When the tragedy of *Carpezan* had played for some thirty or forty nights, other geniuses inevitably took possession of the theater. There may have been souls who marveled at how quickly a town could grow weary of such a masterpiece, unable to bear seeing actors in different costumes reciting other men's verses. Yet George, of a skeptical temperament, accepted the fate of his tragedy with philosophical equanimity, pocketing the proceeds with quiet satisfaction. From Mr. Dodsley, the bookseller, he received the standard hundred pounds; from the theater manager, two hundred or more. With such praise from critics and friends, he set to work preparing a new piece, confident it would surpass his first performance in success.

Over these studies, and the other charming enterprises that engage him, months slip by. This is happy business! The happiest season of youth and life, when love is first spoken and returned; when the dearest eyes shine with welcome daily, and the fondest lips never tire of whispering their sweet secrets; when the parting glance accompanying "Good night!" serves as a delightful promise of tomorrow; when the heart overflows with love and happiness, leaving so little to spare for the world; when days close with glad prayers and open with joyful hopes; when doubt seems like cowardice, misfortune impossible, and poverty merely a sweet trial of constancy! Theo's elders, gratefully recalling their own primes, sit softly by to witness this pretty comedy enacted by their young people. And in one of his later letters, dutifully penned to his wife during a temporary absence, George Warrington records how he had gazed up at the windows of the dear old house in Dean Street, wondering who sat in the chamber where he and Theo had shared such bliss.

Meanwhile, we may discern how time passes and how our friends are occupied through extracts from George's letters to his brother.

*Why have you returned to rugged rocks, bleak shores, burning summers, and nipping winters, when you might have been harvesting laurels aplenty in Germany? Kingsley's gardens are returning covered with them, as Jack-a-Green on May-day. Our six regiments did wonders; and our cavalry would have done likewise if my Lord George Sackville had allowed them. But when Prince Ferdinand cried 'Charge!' his lordship either could not hear the order or could not translate the German word for 'Forward'; and so we only beat the French, failing utterly to annihilate them as we might have, had Lord Granby or Mr. Warrington commanded. My lord has returned to town and is shouting for a Court-Martial. He held his head high enough in prosperity; in misfortune, he displays such a constancy of arrogance that one almost admires him. He looks as if he envied poor Mr. Byng, and the fact that he was not shot was a mere lack of proper regard.*

*The Duke has received notice to prepare for departure from this world of grandeur, victory, downfall, and disappointment. An attack of palsy has visited His Royal Highness; and pallida mors has just peeped in at his door, saying, 'I will call again.' Tyrant as he was, this prince has been noble in disgrace; and no king has ever possessed a truer servant than ours has found in his son. Why do I favor the losing side, and am I inclined to revolt against the winners? Your famous Mr. P----, your chief's patron and discoverer, I have heard speak in the House of Commons twice or thrice. I revolt against his magniloquence. I wish some little David would topple over that swelling giant. His thoughts and his language are perpetually posturing. I prefer Barry's manner, though the other is the more awe-inspiring actor.*

*Pocahontas progresses rapidly. Barry enjoys his part as Captain Smith; and although he insists on wearing a red coat with blue facings and an epaulet, I have a fancy to dress him exactly like one of the portraits of Queen Elizabeth's gentlemen at Hampton Court—with a ruff, a square beard, and square shoes. 'And Pocahontas—would you like her to be tattooed?' asks Uncle Lambert. Hagan's role as the warrior who loves her, and who, seeing her partiality for the captain, nobly rescues him from death, I trust will prove a hit. A strange fish is this Hagan: his mouth full of stage plays and rant, yet good, honest, and brave, if I do not err. He is angry at having been cast recently for Sir O'Brallaghan in Mr. Macklin's new farce, *Love à la mode*. He claims he does not care to disgorge his tongue with imitations of that rascal's brogue. As if there were any need for imitation, when he possesses such an admirable twang of his own!*
