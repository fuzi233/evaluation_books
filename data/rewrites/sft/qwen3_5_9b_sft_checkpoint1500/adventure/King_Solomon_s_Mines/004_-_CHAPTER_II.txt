"What news did you gather regarding my brother's expedition at Bamangwato?" Sir Henry asked, pausing as I leaned back to pack fresh tobacco into my pipe before answering Captain Good.

"I heard this," I replied, "though I have kept it silent from every soul until today. Word reached me that he has departed for Solomon's Mines."

"Solomon's Mines!" both listeners exclaimed simultaneously. "Pray, where do they lie?"

"I am ignorant of their exact location," I said. "I know only where they are *said* to be. Once, I caught sight of the mountain ranges bordering them, but a vast expanse of one-hundred-and-thirty-mile desert stood between those peaks and me. To my knowledge, not a single white man has ever crossed that wasteland, save for one lone individual. Yet, perhaps the wisest course for me is to recount the legend of Solomon's Mines as I understand it, provided you both pledge, under oath, to reveal nothing of what I say without my express permission. Do you agree? I have reasons for demanding this secrecy."

Sir Henry nodded solemnly, and Captain Good assented immediately, "By all means, certainly."

"Well," I began, "as you might surmise, elephant hunters are typically a rugged breed of men, preoccupied solely with the necessities of survival and the customs of the Kaffirs. Yet, occasionally, one encounters a man of different temperament—one who takes the trouble to collect traditions from the indigenous peoples and attempts to reconstruct the fragmentary history of this shadowed continent. Such a man was Evans, who first imparted the legend of Solomon's Mines to me nearly thirty years prior. This occurred during my maiden elephant hunt in the Matabele lands. Regrettably, Evans met his end the following year at the hands of a wounded buffalo; he lies buried near the Zambesi Falls.

"One night, I recall, I recounted to Evans some remarkable excavations I had stumbled upon while stalking koodoo and eland in what is now the Lydenburg district of the Transvaal. Though recent prospectors have rediscovered these works, I knew of them from afar. A magnificent wagon-road, hewn directly from solid rock, leads to the entrance of a vast gallery. Within that mouth, piles of gold-quartz stood waiting to be roasted, evidence that whoever operated them fled in haste. Furthermore, roughly twenty paces inward, the tunnel was abruptly blocked by a work of exquisite masonry.

"'Ay,' Evans remarked, 'but I shall weave you a yarn far stranger than that.' And so he did, describing a ruined city he had located in the deep interior, which he believed to be the biblical Ophir—a notion later echoed by more erudite scholars. Young and impressionable, I listened with rapt attention to tales of ancient civilizations and treasures looted by Jewish or Phoenician adventurers from a land long descended into savage darkness. Suddenly, however, he shifted the topic: 'Lad, have you ever heard of the Suliman Mountains, lying northwest of the Mushakulumbwe?' I admitted ignorance. 'Ah,' he said, 'there lies the true seat of Solomon's operations—his diamond mines.'"

"How do you know this?" I asked.

"Know it!" he retorted. "Why, 'Suliman' is merely a corruption of Solomon! Besides, an ancient Isanusi, a witch-doctoress of the Manica region, revealed everything to me. She spoke of tribes living beyond those mountains, a 'branch' of the Zulus speaking a refined dialect, composed of giants and mighty sorcerers who had mastered forbidden arts from white men in a time when the world was shrouded in darkness. They held the secret of a wondrous mine filled with 'bright stones.'"

At the time, I dismissed this as fanciful, especially as the Diamond Fields remained undiscovered, yet Evans's tragic fate silenced me. For twenty years, the matter lingered in the back of my mind until, just twenty years hence—a long interval for a hunter whose trade demands constant vitality—I encountered further specifics concerning Suliman's Mountains. I was stationed beyond the Manica country at a kraal known as Sitanda's, a bleak existence where sustenance was scarce and game elusive. Plagued by a severe fever, I lay in a wretched state when a Portuguese traveler arrived, accompanied by a single companion, a half-caste lad.

I am intimately acquainted with the low-born Delagoa Portuguese, notorious devils who prey upon human misery and traffic in slaves. Yet, this visitor was a distinct anomaly. He possessed the bearing of a gentleman, tall and slender with large, dark eyes and curling grey mustaches. Over tea, he introduced himself as José Silvestre, proprietor of a station near Delagoa Bay. As his half-caste companion prepared to depart the following day, Silvestre tipped his hat in the old fashion.

"'Farewell, señor,'" he murmured. "'If our paths cross again, I shall stand as the wealthiest man alive, and I shall remember you.'"

I chuckled weakly, watching him stride into the western desert, wondering if madness drove him or some grand delusion. A week later, my fever broke. One evening, seated before my tent, gnawing on the final leg of a bird purchased for twenty fowls, I stared at the setting sun burning the horizon when I spied a figure on the ridge opposite, three hundred yards distant. It appeared to be a European, clad in a coat. The figure crawled on hands and knees, then rose to stagger a few steps before collapsing, resuming the crawl. Recognizing distress, I signaled one of my hunters, who returned moments later with startling news.

"It was José Silvestre," Captain Good interjected.

"Precisely," I confirmed. "But not the man you know. It was his skeleton, draped in tattered skin. His face was a cadaverous yellow, ravaged by bilious fever; his eyes bulged from sockets stripped bare of flesh, revealing only parchment-white skin and gaunt ribs jutting like twigs."

"'Water!' he gasped, a plea born of Christ, 'water!'"

His lips were fissured cracks, his tongue swollen and black, protruding grotesquely. I offered him a mixture of water and milk, which he gulped down voraciously, refusing further drink. The fever returned swiftly, dragging him down until he began to ramble incoherently of Suliman's Mountains, diamonds, and the desert. Carrying him into my tent, I did little to aid him, sensing the inevitable. By eleven o'clock, he quieted. Exhausted, I slept, only to awaken at dawn to find Silvestre propped upright, gazing vacantly toward the west.

As the first rays of sunlight sliced across the plain, illuminating the distant crest of a towering Suliman peak over a hundred miles away, the dying man cried out in Portuguese, "'There it stands!' Pointing with his emaciated arm, 'yet I shall never reach it. None will ever reach it!'"

He paused, a sudden resolve hardening his features. "Comrade," he whispered, turning to me, "my vision fails. Are you there?"

"Yes," I assured him. "Lie down and rest."

"Ay," he agreed. "Rest soon I shall. Eternity awaits. Hear me, I perish! You have shown kindness. I shall leave you a writing. Perhaps you possess the strength to traverse the desert that claimed my poor servant and me."

Groping within his shirt, he produced what I mistook for a leather tobacco pouch of the Swart-vet-pens antelope. Secured by a strip of hide, a *rimpi*, he struggled to loosen it, finally handing it to me. "Untie it," he urged.

I complied, extracting a fragment of torn yellow linen inscribed with rust-colored characters. Beneath it lay a folded paper. His voice failed as weakness overcame him. "The paper holds the truth. It took me years to decipher. Listen: my ancestor, a political exile from Lisbon, one of the first Portuguese to tread these shores, penned this when dying amidst mountains no white foot has ever pressed. His name was José da Silvestra, living three centuries past. His slave, awaiting him beyond the range, found him dead and bore the writing home to Delagoa. It rested in our family, ignored until I, driven mad by greed, read it. I have lost my life for it, but another may succeed and claim the crown of the world—the richest man alive. Give it to no one, señor; venture yourself!"

With these words, he drifted away, and within an hour, the struggle ended. God rest his soul! He passed quietly, and I buried him deep beneath massive boulders, ensuring the jackals could not disturb his grave. Thus, I departed.

"The document?" Sir Henry asked, his voice thick with curiosity.

"Yes, the document. What did it contain?" Captain Good added.

"Gentlemen, if you wish, I will recount it. I have shown it to naught but a drunken old Portuguese trader, who translated it for me and forgot it by morning. The original rag rests in my home in Durban, alongside Dom José's translation. However, I carry the English rendering in my pocket-book, along with a facsimile of the map—if chaos can be termed a map. Behold."
