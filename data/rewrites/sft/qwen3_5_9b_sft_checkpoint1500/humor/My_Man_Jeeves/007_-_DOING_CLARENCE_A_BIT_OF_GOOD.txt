The sheer audacity with which the female sex occasionally operates is a subject I have, on several occasions, subjected to rigorous scrutiny. While most gentlemen merely nod politely at the concept, I have been personally assaulted by the reality of it, specifically through the lens of the infamous "Yeardsley Venus" scandal.

To grasp the gravity of the situation, one must understand the history between Mrs. Yeardsley and myself. When I first encountered her, she was Elizabeth Shoolbred—a girl of ancient Worcestershire stock, immense fortune, and breathtaking beauty. Her brother, Bill, was my contemporary at Oxford. I adored Elizabeth; we were, for a fleeting seven-week period, formally engaged. However, just as I was preparing to treat life with solemn seriousness, consulting furniture catalogs, and groaning softly under the strains of "The Wedding Glide" at the local orchestra, she abruptly called off the engagement. A month later, she was wed to Clarence Yeardsley, an artist.

Thanks to the soothing influence of golf, billiards, and a supportive circle of friends who gently nudged me back to normalcy, I eventually categorized this affair as a closed chapter. With Elizabeth and Clarence retired to the countryside, seemingly content to avoid London entirely, I believed the wound had fully healed. Indeed, by the time the letter arrived, I was practically recovering my appetite.

Yet, destiny intervened with a note that appeared out of the blue sky:

"My dear old Reggie,—What ages it seems since I saw anything of you. How are you? We have settled down here in the most perfect old house, with a lovely garden, in the middle of delightful country. Couldn't you run down here for a few days? Clarence and I would be so glad to see you. Bill is here, and is most anxious to meet you again. He was speaking of you only this morning. *Do* come. Wire your train, and I will send the car to meet you.

—Yours most sincerely,

"P.S.—We can give you new milk and fresh eggs. Think of that!"

"P.P.S.—Bill says our billiard-table is one of the best he has ever played on."

"P.P.S.S.—We are only half a mile from a golf course. Bill says it is better than St. Andrews."

"P.P.S.S.S.—You *must* come!"

A gentleman waking up to a summons from a former flame, especially one capable of blighting his life, usually feels a certain trepidation. However, the mention of golf proved decisive. Trusting Bill's judgment on sporting matters, I departed for the estate.

Upon arrival, Bill greeted me at the station. "Thank goodness you've come," he exclaimed as we drove off. "I was just about at my last grip."

"What's the trouble?" I asked.

"If I didn't suffer from 'artistic neuritis,'" Bill explained, "perhaps I wouldn't be so wretched. As it is, it's dreadful!"

"Artistic neuritis?"

"Yes. Clarence is an artist, as is his father. And you know what Elizabeth becomes when given free rein. Most of our time together involved touring galleries. Though paintings are toxic to me—as they are to Bill—I somehow failed to realize that Elizabeth would remain obsessed years after marrying an artist. Apparently, they discuss art at every meal. It drives a chap out of his mind."

He suggested I return to London immediately via telegram to play a match against the Scots. I attempted to highlight the silver lining regarding the superior local golf course.

"You don't seriously mean she said it was better than St. Andrews?" Bill asked, nearly running us into a bank.

"She said you said so."

"So I did. Did she forget I qualified it by saying, 'I don't think'?"

"No, she omitted that."

"It's the worst course in Great Britain."

Stunned, I pivoted to billiards. "At least the table is excellent," I offered.

"It is half-size," Bill corrected grimly, "and features a seven-inch gouge caused by Clarence's cue slipping. Elizabeth repaired it with pink silk. It looks dashing, but it ruins the playing surface."

As we approached the estate, the house loomed large and ominous in the twilight. I felt a distinct unease, reminiscent of the tales of travelers lured to isolated dwellings only to be ambushed. Elizabeth knew me well enough to know that the promise of a world-class golf course was a potent lure, yet I suspected a deeper trap. Had she arranged a romantic reunion? Or worse, a matchmaking scheme involving a local spinster?

"Bill," I whispered urgently, "are there any... undesirable young ladies staying here?"

"Wish there were," Bill replied cheerlessly. "No such luck."

Inside, Elizabeth awaited us. She appeared unchanged, radiant as ever. "Is that you, Reggie? I'm so glad you could come." She ushered me in for tea. Moments later, Clarence emerged. He was a slight, nervous man of thirty-five, wearing pince-nez and a drooping moustache. Compared to him, I felt like a robust giant.

"How do you do, Mr. Pepper? Hark! Can you hear a mewing cat?" Clarence announced breathlessly.

"A mewing cat?" I echoed.

Before I could respond, the door opened and an elderly gentleman, clearly Mr. Yeardsley senior, entered. He resembled Clarence but with the weathered visage of an earlier era.

"Did you hear a cat?" Clarence asked his father. "I am positive."

"No," the elder declared. "There was no cat."

"I cannot abide mewing cats!" Clarence protested. "They agitate me!"

"I dislike them too," agreed Elizabeth.

"And I," affirmed the old man.

Thus began the evening, dominated by an obsessive fixation on feline noises and, inevitably, art. When the topic shifted to picture robberies, specifically a recent theft of a Romney portrait from Sir Bellamy Palmer at Dryden Park, I seized the opportunity to intervene. Producing a newspaper clipping from my pocket, I read aloud details of the burglary.

"The 'Venus'!" the family cried in unison. "Could it be theirs?"

Excitement mounted until dinner, where the subject of the stolen artwork became central. Later, in the drawing-room, Elizabeth summoned me aside.

"Reggie," she said softly, "I wish to ask a great favor."

She stoked the fire and leaned closer. "Do you recall once promising to do anything in the world for me?"

"That was a long time ago," I murmured.

"There is a matter concerning Clarence," she continued. "Months ago, he adored cats. Now, they torment him. Everything torments him. He suffers from the Artist's Temperament."

She gestured toward a large oil painting hanging near the door. "This is the 'Venus.' It was painted by his father, given to Clarence as a wedding gift, and hangs above his dining table. Every day, Clarence must gaze upon it. It makes him ill. The Artist's Temperament prevents him from storing it away, lest he disappoint his father."

"But surely..." I hesitated.

"Look at this," she interrupted, switching on a second lamp. Beside the Venus hung another canvas. "Clarence painted this."

"It appears similar to the Venus," I observed cautiously.

"It is called 'Jocund Spring'," she snapped, extinguishing the light. "You have no taste for art. But consider Clarence's agony. Imagine a musician forced to endure a vulgar tune repeated endlessly. His nerves must snap. Clarence needs relief."

"And how do I assist?"

"I want you to steal the 'Venus.'"

"You want me to—"

"Steal it!" Her eyes gleamed. "Providence has aligned perfectly. A robbery occurred nearby tonight. The police will assume the same gang stole the Romney and the Venus. It will be the greatest honor the old man can receive. Cut the canvas from the frame, take it, and I'll ensure you have a roaring fire to burn it."

"But what if I am discovered?"

"You cannot be. Leave the window open, slip back to your room, and I will tend to the fire."

The plan seemed deceptively simple. Yet, as I prepared to execute the theft at one o'clock, a creeping sense of foreboding took hold. Sitting in my room with a lantern and a knife, the operation felt far more perilous than anticipated.

Just as I opened the window, a blow landed squarely on my chin. Sparks erupted behind my eyes, followed by a splash of liquid. I found myself on the floor, illuminated by a gas lamp, with Bill kneeling beside me holding a soda siphon.

"What is going on?" I sputtered.

"I was startled by a lantern and an open window!" Bill explained, wiping his brow. "I thought someone was burglarizing the house!"

"It was me," I admitted. "Elizabeth asked me."

Bill sighed. "If I hadn't acted on impulse, you wouldn't have needed this antidote. Let us finish the job quietly."

Together, we descended to the dining room. With surgical precision, we removed the canvas from its frame. It was a massive undertaking, requiring careful cutting and rolling. We retreated to my room, where we toasted our success with whiskey and soda, burning the offending canvas piece by piece in the fireplace. It was a cathartic experience, a secret act of charity.

Suddenly, Bill gripped my arm. "Hear that?"
