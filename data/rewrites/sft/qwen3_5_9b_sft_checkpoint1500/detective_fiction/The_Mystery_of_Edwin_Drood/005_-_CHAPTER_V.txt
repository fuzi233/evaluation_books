As John Jasper walked home through the shadowed archways of the Close, the path was suddenly blocked by a solitary figure leaning heavily against the iron railing of the burial ground. It was Stony Durdles, burdened with his dinner bundle, staring vacantly into the moonlit gloom. Beside him, a ragged, grotesque urchin hurled stones at the stonemason with the precision of a marksman. Some struck their mark; others sailed past harmlessly, yet Durdles appeared utterly unmoved by either success or failure. In return, the boy, missing several teeth in a jagged gap across his jaw, blew a triumphant, whistling shriek upon impact, or yelped "Mulled agin!" with a fresh, vicious aim whenever he missed.

"What business are you conducting with this man?" Jasper demanded, stepping from the shadows into the pale light.

"Playing cock-shy," the boy replied, his voice rasping like dry leaves.

"Hand over those stones."

"Yes, I'll shove 'em down your gullet if you try to catch me," the boy snarled, retreating and shaking his frame. "Watch out for my eye!"

"You little devil," Jasper observed, "what crime has this man committed against you?"

"He refuses to go home."

"And why does that concern you?"

"He pays me a halfpenny to chase him down if I catch him lingering outside his lodging hours," the boy explained, then broke into a wild, stumbling chant that echoed through the quiet street:

"Widdy widdy wen!
I—ket—ches—Im—out—ar—ter—ten,
Widdy widdy wy!
Then—E—don't—go—then—I—shy—
Widdy Widdy Wake-cock warning!"

With a sweeping flourish on the final line, he lobbed another stone at Durdles. This seemed to serve as a rhythmic prelude, a cautionary song urging the mason to retreat or hasten homeward.

Jasper gestured with his head, inviting the boy to follow him—a futile gesture, as dragging or cooing at the creature was impossible. He moved to the railing where Durdles stood, lost in profound meditation.

"Do you know this fellow, this child?" Jasper asked, struggling to find words to describe the being.

"Deputy," Durdles nodded solemnly.

"Is that his name?"

"Deputy," confirmed the mason.

"I'm a servant up at the Travellers' Twopenny in the Gas Works Yard," the boy explained, stepping back into the road and raising his aim once more. "Every servant at the Travellers' Lodgings bears the name Deputy. When the house is packed and the travelers are asleep, I venture out for health." He began his chant anew, cutting it short as Jasper raised a hand.

"Hold," Jasper cried sharply. "Cast no further while I stand so close; I could have you killed. Come, Durdles, let me escort you home tonight. May I carry your bundle?"

"Not for a penny," Durdles muttered, readjusting his load. "I was reflecting here when you arrived, sir, surrounded by your works, like a poplar author." He pointed to a sarcophagus within the railing, white and chilling in the moonlight. "That is Mrs. Sapsea." He indicated the monument of the devoted wife. "And the Late Incumbent," pointing to the reverend gentleman's broken column. "Departed Assessed Taxes"—a vase and towel resting atop what looked like a block of soap. "Former pastrycook and Muffin-maker, highly respected," he noted regarding a simple gravestone. "All safe and sound here, sir, all my handiwork. The common folk, merely wrapped in turf and thorns, deserve little speech. A sorry lot, quickly forgotten."

"This creature, Deputy, trails us," Jasper noted, glancing backward. "Shall he follow?"

The bond between Durdles and Deputy was fickle. When the mason turned with the heavy, beer-drunk gravity of a sudden thought, the boy executed a wide circle into the road, adopting a defensive stance.

"You haven't sung the Widdy Warning since you started tonight," Durdles remarked, seemingly recalling an insult.

"Lie!" Deputy snapped, offering his only form of polite contradiction.

"Brother, sir," Durdles observed, turning back just as swiftly as he had remembered the grievance. "He is kin to Peter the Wild Boy, yet I gave him a purpose in life."

"At which he aims?" Jasper suggested.

"That is precisely it, sir," Durdles returned, satisfied. "I took charge and assigned him an objective. Before me, what was he? A destroyer. What did he accomplish? Nothing but ruin. What profit did he earn? Brief stays in Cloisterham gaol. No person, no property, no window, no horse, no dog, no cat, no bird, no fowl, no pig escaped his stoning, simply because he lacked an enlightened goal. I placed that goal before him, and now he earns an honest halfpenny, three times a week."

"I wonder he faces no competition."

"There is plenty, Mr. Jasper, but he stones them all away." Durdles pondered the nature of his enterprise with a sodden seriousness. "I confess, I cannot precisely categorize this scheme. Is it a form of National Education?"

"I doubt it," Jasper replied.

"No," Durdles assented. "Then let us avoid naming it."

"He still lingers behind," Jasper repeated, checking over his shoulder. "Will he pursue us?"

"We cannot avoid rounding via the Travellers' Twopenny if we take the shortcut, the back way," Durdles answered. "We shall leave him there."

Thus they proceeded. Deputy, trailing in the rear ranks, invaded the silence of the hour and place by pelting every wall, post, pillar, and inert object along the desolate path.

"Is there anything new down in the crypt, Durdles?" Jasper asked.

"Something ancient, perhaps, you mean," Durdles grunted. "A place ill-suited for novelty."

"Did you make any new discoveries yourself?"

"There is an old one beneath the seventh pillar on the left, descending the broken steps of the former underground chapel. I believe it belongs to one of the old rogues with the crook. Judging by the dimensions of the passages, steps, and doors used for entry and exit, those crooks must have occupied significant space among the ancients! If two met promiscuously, they likely hooked elbows by the mitre often, I should say."

Jasper surveyed his companion, covered from head to toe in old mortar, lime, and stone grit, as if he were becoming imbued with a romantic fascination for this strange existence.

"It is a curious life you lead."

Without clarifying whether he viewed this as praise or reproach, Durdles gruffly responded, "Yours is another."

"Well! Since our fate is cast in the same old, earthy, chill, unchanging soil, yes. Yet there is far more mystery and intrigue in your connection with the Cathedral than in mine. Indeed, I begin to entertain the notion of asking you to accept me as a student, or a free apprentice, allowing me to accompany you occasionally and explore these odd recesses where you spend your days."

The Stony One replied vaguely, "All right. Everyone knows where to find Durdles when he is needed." If not strictly accurate, it was approximately true, implying that Durdles could always be located in some state of vagrancy.

"What occupies me most," Jasper pursued, dwelling on his romantic curiosity, "is the remarkable accuracy with which you seem to determine exactly where people are interred. What troubles you? That bundle impedes you; let me bear it."

Durdles halted and stepped back slightly (Deputy, keenly observing his movements, immediately skirmished into the road), searching for a ledge or corner to rest his burden, when relieved of it by Jasper.

"Just remove your hammer from there," Durdles commanded, and produced it.

"Now, listen closely. Do you tune your note, Mr. Jasper?"

"Yes."

"So I seek mine. I take my hammer and I strike." He tapped the pavement, and the attentive Deputy skirmished at a wider range, fearing his head might become necessary. *Tap, tap, tap.* Solid! Continuing to tap. Still solid! *Tap again.* *Halloa!* Hollow! *Tap again,* persistent. Solid within hollow! *Tap, tap, tap,* trying it further. Solid within hollow; and inside solid, hollow again! There you are! An old one crumbling away in a stone coffin, in a vault!"

"Astonishing!"

"I have even gone this far," Durdles continued, producing his two-foot ruler. (Meanwhile, Deputy skirmished closer, suspecting treasure might soon be unearthed, leading to his own enrichment and the delightful prospect of seeing the discoverers hung by the neck on his testimony until they died.) "Consider that hammer of mine a wall—my work. Two; four; and two is six." He measured on the pavement. "Six feet inside that wall lies Mrs. Sapsea."

"Not truly Mrs. Sapsea?"
