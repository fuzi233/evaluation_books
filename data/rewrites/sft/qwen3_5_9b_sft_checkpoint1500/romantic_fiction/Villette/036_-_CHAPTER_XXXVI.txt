Before the fragile bond of friendship between Lucy and Mr. Paul could truly take root, a third voice intervened, watchful and severe. While the world outside slept, a vigil was kept by a pair of eyes that refused to blink: Father Silas, the spiritual director of Mr. Paul, peered through the mystical lattice of the confessional, monitoring the boy's progress month by month. It was a gaze born of jealousy, protecting Rome's son from what the priest perceived as contamination.

Lucy reflected on the previous evening's conversation, wondering aloud if Mr. Paul had not already proven his affection through countless acts of kindness. "Why did you express such joy at becoming friends?" the internal voice asked, echoing the curiosity of any observer. "Was he not already a devoted friend? Did he not offer evidence of partiality?"

The answer lay in the warmth of his words. Lucy relished the earnestness with which he called her his "close, true friend," the modesty in his doubts, and the tender deference he showed. He had named her "sister," and it suited her perfectly, provided he maintained that specific, platonic distance. As long as he viewed her as a spiritual sister, she was content; she would not allow herself to become a rival for a husband he had vowed never to seek. Yet, beneath this surface agreement, a tension simmered. Lucy spent the entire night analyzing the interview, eager for dawn to break so she could prepare for the next encounter. She desired to test the limits of this alliance—to gauge how far Mr. Paul would lower himself as a brother and to determine if she possessed the fortitude of a sister.

However, the reality of the following day defied her expectations. When Mr. Paul entered the classroom, he did not approach her. His lesson was delivered with a quiet gravity, lacking the usual warmth. Where she anticipated a smile or a word of greeting, she received only a hurried, shy nod. The distance between them, which she initially dismissed as accidental, persisted and grew. For days, he avoided her, and Lucy suppressed her confusion, swallowing the surge of emotions that threatened to overwhelm her.

Her frustration peaked on a particular evening when Mr. Paul, as per his custom, dedicated an hour to reviewing their studies. Usually, they met in the bustling second division, amidst the noise of day students, but tonight the room was empty, bathed in the cool shadows of twilight. Through the open doors, the courtyard glowed with the dying light of sunset, painting the walls and the varied colors of the students' dresses in a single, warm flush. Mr. Emanuel stood in the center of the circle, his dark coat and jet-black hair catching the crimson hues of the sun. His Spanish features lit up with an animated smile as he greeted a teacher.

Nearby, orange trees and blooming plants basked in the sun's generosity, now thirsting for water. Mr. Emanuel, known for his love of gardening, attended to them with a gentle hand. He watered the roses, the cacti, and the camellias, his movements rhythmic and soothing. Beside him trotted Sylvie, his beloved spaniel, a creature of silky fur and adoring eyes. The dog moved with the grace of a miniature lion guarding its kingdom, leaping to catch Mr. Paul's discarded hat or handkerchief. For a long time, the scene was idyllic: the clock ticked on, the sun dipped below the horizon, and the courtyard emptied as daylight faded.

As the last of the plants were watered, Mr. Paul turned away, washing his hands in a stone basin. He stood contemplating the pale moon rising over the cathedral, his expression thoughtful. Sylvie, restless, whined and jumped, breaking the silence. Mr. Paul picked her up, carrying her across the courtyard, his voice soft and tender as he cooed to the dog. They paused at the front door, where Mr. Paul looked out at the moonlit streets before turning back. A keen beam from his eye swept over the classrooms, and though he bowed slightly, Lucy, caught off guard, could not return the gesture. Before she could speak, he stepped inside, leaving the threshold pale and shadowless.

Back in her quarters, Lucy gathered her books, her heart heavy with ennui. She sat alone, the silence of the house pressing in on her. From the depths of her desk drawer, she pulled out a pamphlet she had not seen before—a thin, lilac booklet that smelled of recent introduction. Curiosity compelled her to open it. It was a theological treatise, preaching the virtues of Roman Catholicism with a honeyed, unctuous voice. It promised comfort, indulgence, and tender guidance, appealing directly to the heart rather than the intellect. It spoke of the solace of praying for departed loved ones to escape purgatory, a concept Lucy found strangely consoling despite her Protestant convictions.

Amused by the book's sentimental simplicity, she laughed softly until her gaze fell upon the flyleaf. Written in clear pencil were the words: *"From P.C.D.E. to L-y."*

The initials shattered her amusement, replacing it with a jolt of bewildered clarity. P.C.D.E. was Paul Charles Emmanuel De Guille. L-y was Lucy. The pamphlet had not fallen there by chance; it was a deliberate offering, likely planted by Mr. Paul himself. But why? And what did Father Silas think? Lucy realized that the priest had likely interrogated Mr. Paul extensively regarding their relationship, extracting every detail of their "fraternal communion" and her status as an "adopted sister." In Father Silas's eyes, such intimacy with a heretic was a perilous aberration. He would surely be urging his penitent to sever ties, to avoid the "dangerous" influence of Lucy's "invulnerable creed."

Driven by this new knowledge, Lucy waited. The next evening, the weather turned damp and foggy, the marshes exhaling a gray mist. As she sat in her classroom, listening to the rain drum against the glass, a sharp bark echoed from the garden. It was Sylvie. The dog usually reserved her enthusiastic greetings for Mr. Paul alone, yet today she raced toward the window, her tail a blur of white feathers.

Peering through the glass, Lucy saw Mr. Paul in the garden, digging furiously in the wet earth among the shrubs. He worked with a frantic intensity, his brow knitted, his teeth set, oblivious to the rain soaking his clothes. Sylvie barked incessantly at the windowpane, urging Lucy to join him. Finally, worn out by her persistence, Mr. Paul tossed down his spade and pushed the door open. Sylvie burst in, springing onto Lucy's lap and showering her with kisses and licks, scattering papers across the desk.

Mr. Paul entered to restore order, capturing Sylvie and tucking her safely under his cloak. As he smoothed the pages back into place, his eyes landed on the religious tract. For a moment, his lips moved as if to speak, but he restrained himself. Then, with a gentle resolve, he asked, "Have you read the brochure? It is not sufficiently inviting?"

"I have read it," Lucy admitted, her voice steady.

"But?" Mr. Paul pressed, waiting for a response.

Lucy hesitated, then launched into a defense of her faith. She spoke of the Bible as her ultimate guide, superior to any sectarian dogma. She described the unity she saw in the Protestant churches, contrasting it with the rigid divisions of the Catholic world. She recounted her visits to the French, German, and English chapels, noting how their doctrines, despite minor formal differences, shared a common core of truth. She explained that her loyalty lay not in a denomination, but in the Scripture itself, which she believed offered a purity that the ornate rituals of Rome obscured.

As she spoke, Mr. Paul listened with an expression of profound concern. He confessed that Father Silas had warned him of the dangers of Lucy's "wild, careless daring," describing Protestants with terms that sounded suspicious to the devout priest. Yet, as Lucy articulated her beliefs, the accusations began to unravel in his mind. He saw the sincerity in her voice, the lack of malice, and the genuine reverence she held for the divine. He realized that Father Silas's fears were based on prejudice, not fact.

"I do not respect that tract," Lucy said firmly, holding up the pamphlet. "What then?"

"Not respect it? But it speaks of faith, love, charity!" Mr. Paul exclaimed, his voice rising with emotion. "I thought it would touch you. I laid it in your desk with a prayer. I must indeed be a sinner if Heaven will not hear the petitions that come warmest from my heart. You scorn my little offering."

"Monsieur, I do not scorn it," Lucy reassured him, her tone softening. "I do not reject your gift. Sit down, listen to me. I am not a heathen, nor am I hard-hearted. I believe in God and Christ, and so do you."

"But do you believe in the Bible?" he asked, his voice trembling. "Do you accept Revelation? What limits are there to the boldness of your sect?"

With patience, Lucy guided him through her reasoning, explaining that she had observed the similarities between the Protestant denominations and recognized the flaws in the Catholic practices Father Silas had praised.
