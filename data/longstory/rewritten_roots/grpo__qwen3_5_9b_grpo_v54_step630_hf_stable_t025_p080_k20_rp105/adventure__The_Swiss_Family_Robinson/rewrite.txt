The tempest had raged for seven days, a relentless fury that had stripped us of our course, our masts, and finally, our hope. The sea was a churning void of black water and white foam, swallowing the ship whole. Inside the cabin, the air was thick with the scent of salt and fear. My heart ached as I looked upon my family: my wife, trembling yet brave, and my four sons, clutching one another in terror.

"Dear children," I whispered, my voice steady despite the chaos outside, "if the Lord wills, He can save us from this peril. If not, let us calmly yield our lives into His hands, thinking of the joy of being united forever in His home above. Even death is not bitter when it does not tear apart those who love one another."

My wife looked up, her tears drying as she rallied her sons with calm, loving words. We knelt together, our voices rising above the thunder, praying with a desperate earnestness. Fritz, my eldest, prayed for us with such fervor he seemed to forget his own fear. In those moments of child-like trust, the crushing weight of the storm felt slightly lighter.

Suddenly, a cry pierced the roar: "Land! Land!"

The ship struck with a shock that threw us to the deck, followed by the groans of timber snapping. The captain's voice screamed orders to lower the boats. "Lost!" I cried out, the word stinging like a dagger, but I forced a smile for my children's sake. "Take courage! We are still above water! God helps those who help themselves!"

I rushed to the deck, only to be tossed by a massive wave. The ship was dying, shattered on all sides. The crew, abandoning us without a second thought, shoved their lifeboat clear into the surf, leaving us to our fate. Despair threatened to consume me until I saw the stern of our wreck jammed between two high rocks, holding our cabin aloft. Through the mist, I glimpsed a rocky coast—a sign of life.

"We must find food," my wife urged, her spirit unbroken. "We cannot faint from hunger."

As night fell, the storm raged on. My youngest asked if God would help soon. "Don't settle what God will do," Fritz corrected sharply, though I wished he had spoken with kindness. We ate a meager meal, and while the boys slept, Fritz kept watch. He suggested we make swimming belts from empty flasks and tin cans. It was a brilliant idea. We fashioned floats for my wife and the younger boys, and I gathered matches, knives, and cord, preparing for the worst.

Dawn broke with a moderating gale and a blue sky. We found the deck empty; the sailors were gone. "God has not forsaken us," I assured them, urging them to act. Fritz proposed swimming, but Ernest wisely suggested building a raft.

We scavenged the wreck. Fritz retrieved guns and ammunition; Ernest found tools; Jack discovered two magnificent dogs who greeted him with such enthusiasm he rode one like a pony. My wife saved the livestock—goats, sheep, a cow, and a sow—and even found a sailor's dress for herself, which she wore with modest grace.

Together, we constructed a vessel from eight large casks, lashing them together with planks to form a narrow boat with eight compartments. It was heavy and clumsy, requiring the leverage of a capstan bar to launch. Once afloat, we added outriggers for stability. We loaded the boat with our most essential treasures: tools, weapons, food, and even ten hens and two roosters, secured in a tub.

As we pushed off from the wreck, the tide carried us forward. The dogs, Turk and Juno, howled as we left them, then leaped into the sea, swimming tirelessly to keep pace with us. Jack wanted to leave them behind, but I reminded him that mercy is a virtue even for a man stranded on an island.

The journey was arduous. The coastline looked barren and threatening, but eventually, we spotted green grass and palm trees. Guided by the geese and ducks swimming toward a small inlet, we steered into a calm bay. The ground sloped gently to the shore, and we landed safely.

The dogs greeted us with joyful barks, and the birds created a cacophony of welcome. We quickly erected a tent using our sailcloth and a spar for a pole. As evening approached, we prepared our first meal. My wife cooked soup from portable cakes, explaining that they were made from the essence of meat, perfect for long voyages.

While the soup simmered, the boys explored. Jack found a lobster in a deep pool, only to be bitten by its powerful claw. I rescued him, teaching him to be merciful even to his enemies. Ernest discovered oysters and salt in the rocks, correcting Jack's assumption that they were mussels. Fritz, meanwhile, hunted successfully, bringing back a small agouti—an animal that looked like a pig but was actually a rodent, much to Ernest's amusement.

We gathered around the pot, lacking plates or spoons. Ernest cleverly used a large mussel shell to scoop the hot soup, saving it for the dogs first, a gesture of prudence I praised. We dipped our fingers into the broth, laughing at our makeshift utensils.

Suddenly, the dogs, attracted by the smell of the agouti, attacked Fritz's prize. In a fit of rage, Fritz struck one of the dogs with his gun, bending the barrel. I stopped him, reminding him of the consequences of his anger: he had hurt the dogs, frightened his mother, and ruined a vital tool. Fritz, ashamed, apologized immediately.

As the sun set, bringing sudden darkness, we fed the poultry and settled into our tent. The lack of twilight confirmed we were near the equator. Exhausted but safe, we thanked God for our deliverance and closed our eyes, ready for the challenges of the new day.

The night was as biting as the day had been scorching, yet we slept soundly, our exhaustion serving as the best pillow. The sentinel crow's cry, heralding the moon's ascent, was the final sound of the evening, but it was the dawn that truly woke me. I roused my wife while the children still slumbered, seeking a quiet moment to weigh our options. Our priorities were clear: first, to determine the fate of our shipwrecked companions, and second, to assess the resources of this strange land.

We agreed that after breakfast, Fritz and I would embark on an expedition, while my wife guarded the landing site with the three younger boys.

"Rouse up, my boys!" I called cheerfully, waking the children. "Help your mother prepare breakfast."

"As to that," she smiled, "we can only boil more soup!"

"Why, you've forgotten Jack's fine lobster!" I replied. "Where is it, Jack?"

"It's safe in this rock crevice, Father," Jack answered. "I thought, since the dogs like good things, they might fancy this as well as the agouti."

"A sensible precaution," I noted. "Even heedless Jack is learning wisdom. It is fortunate the lobster is so large; we shall need it for our journey today."

At the word 'excursion,' the four children erupted in delight, dancing around me. "Steady!" I cautioned. "Not all of you can go. Such a trek would be too perilous for the younger ones. Fritz and I shall go alone, accompanied by one dog, leaving the other to protect you."

At the mention of 'guns,' Fritz blushed deeply, struggling vainly to straighten his weapon. I let him be for a moment, then permitted him to take another, pleased to see the shame in his face transform into determination.

He then attempted to restrain Flora, but the dog, remembering recent blows, snarled and refused to approach. Turk behaved similarly, requiring my own voice to coax them near. Tears streamed down Fritz's cheeks as he begged for a biscuit, declaring he would forego the rest of his meal to make peace with the dogs. He carried the treats, stroking and caressing them, seeking forgiveness. Dogs, being quick to forgive and slow to hold grudges, instantly softened. Flora licked his hands, though Turk remained wary, lacking confidence in Fritz's advances.

"Give him a claw of my lobster," Jack cried. "I meant to give it to you anyway for your journey."

With that treat, Turk forgave Fritz. We armed ourselves—guns, game-bags, pistols for Fritz, a hatchet for me—and stowed the remaining lobster, biscuits, and water.

"Wait!" I exclaimed. "We have left something vital undone."

"Surely not," Fritz said.

"Yes," I replied. "Morning prayer. Amidst life's cares, we too easily forget the God to whom we owe all."

After commending ourselves to His care, I bid farewell to my wife and children, instructing them not to stray far from the boat and tent. Anxiety lingered on both sides as we parted, unsure of the dangers awaiting us in this unknown region.

The stream's banks were so rocky that only a narrow passage allowed access to the water, with no corresponding path on the other side. This was a relief; it meant my family was on a relatively inaccessible spot, shielded by steep cliffs.

Fritz and I ascended the stream until we reached a cascade where large rocks lay half-submerged. Using these, we crossed safely. On our left lay the sea; on our right, a long line of rocky heights dotted with trees stretched inland.

Scarcely fifty yards into the tall, tangled grass, a rustling alarmed us. The grass waved as if an animal passed through. Fritz instantly raised his gun, ready to fire. I admired his coolness, a sign of the reliability I could place in him. No savage beast emerged, however. It was Turk, whom we had forgotten, sent after us by my thoughtful wife.

I praised Fritz's discretion in waiting to identify the threat before acting. This incident highlighted the danger of our position; escape would be difficult should a fierce beast strike unawares. We hastened toward the open seashore.

The scene was delightful: hills in the background, green grass, and trees reaching the water's edge. We searched the smooth sand for traces of our companions but found no footprints.

"Shall I fire a shot or two?" Fritz asked. "To summon our companions, if they are within hearing."

"It would indeed," I replied, "or attract any savages. No, let us search diligently but quietly."

"But why trouble ourselves about them?" Fritz argued. "They abandoned us. I don't wish to see them again."

"You are wrong, my boy," I corrected. "First, we must not return evil for evil. Second, they might assist us in building. Third, they took nothing from the vessel and may be starving."

"But while we waste time searching, why not return to the wreck to save the animals?"

"When duties conflict, choose the one offering the most solid advantage," I explained. "Saving a human life is higher than aiding a few quadrupeds we have already fed. Besides, the sea is calm; the ship won't sink before we return."

Pushing on, we reached a pleasant grove by the water's edge and halted under a large tree beside a murmuring rivulet. Gaily plumaged birds twittered overhead. Suddenly, Fritz started. "A monkey!" he exclaimed.

He spun around the tree, stumbling over a small round object he handed to me. "A round bird's nest," he noted.

"You may have heard of them," I laughed, "but not every round hairy thing is a nest. This is a coconut. Remember the fibrous covering over a hard shell? Time has destroyed the outer hull, revealing the twisted fibers inside."

We split the nut, only to find the kernel dry and inedible.

"Hullo!" Fritz cried. "I always thought coconuts were full of sweet liquid like almond milk."

"So they are," I replied, "when young and fresh. As they ripen, the milk congeals and solidifies. Eventually, the germ swells, bursts the shell, and sprouts a new tree."

"How does the germ get through such a thick shell?" Fritz asked.

"Nature provides," I answered, pointing to three small holes near the stalk. "The germ exits through these."

Finding a fresh, non-dried coconut proved difficult, but we managed one that was slightly oily. Refreshed by the fruit, we deferred our dinner until later to conserve provisions.

Continuing through a dense thicket of lianas, we emerged onto the seashore, where the forest swept inland and single, remarkable trees stood at intervals. Fritz pointed to them. "Absurd-looking trees, Father! Strange bumps on the trunks."

We approached and identified them as calabash trees, bearing gourds on their stems. "The savages," I remarked, "use these ingeniously to hold liquids and even cook food in them."

"Oh, impossible!" Fritz insisted. "The rind would burn through instantly."

"I didn't say it was set on fire," I corrected. "Water is added, fish placed inside, and red-hot stones dropped in until boiling. The gourd remains uninjured."

"That is clever," Fritz admitted. "Suppose we prepare some for use when we return."

Fritz tried to split a gourd equally but failed, cutting it jaggedly. "What a nuisance!" he flung it down.

"Stay," I said. "Those pieces aren't useless. Try fashioning spoons while I provide a dish."

I took a piece of string, tied it tightly around a gourd near one end, tapped it with the back of my knife until it penetrated the shell, tightened the string further, and pulled with all my might. The gourd divided exactly as desired.

"That is clever!" Fritz cried. "What put that plan in your head?"

"A plan adopted by savages," I replied, "learned from books of travel."

"Well, it makes a capital soup-tureen," Fritz said. "But how to make a bottle?"

"Easier, if possible. Cut a round hole at one end, scoop out the interior, and drop in shot or stones to clean it."

"That wouldn't make a convenient bottle," Fritz noted. "More like a barrel."

"True. For a shapely vessel, intervene while it is young. Tie a bandage around the young gourd on the tree; it will swell everywhere except where checked."

As I spoke, I filled the gourds with sand to dry, marking the spot for our return.

"For three hours or more," we pressed forward, scanning for signs of our companions, until we reached a bold promontory. From its rocky summit, we surveyed the surrounding country. A wide, lovely bay fringed with yellow sands stretched before us, enclosed by shadowy promontories. The scene inland was equally beautiful, yet a sense of loneliness stole over us.

"Cheer up, Fritz," I said. "We chose a settler's life long ago. We didn't expect total isolation, but what matters a few people? With God's help, let us live contentedly. The heat is unbearable; let us find shade."

We descended toward a clump of palm trees, passing through a dense thicket of reeds. I cut a reed, fearing venomous snakes. Noticing thick juice exuding from one end, I tasted it and found it sweet. "Sugar-canes!" I realized.

I advised Fritz to cut one for defense. As he beat the ground, the reed split, covering his hand with juice. He tasted it, confirmed its sweetness, and springing back, exclaimed, "Oh, Father, sugar-cane! Taste it! How delicious! Let us take some home to Mother."

"Gently," I warned. "Cut some, but don't take more than you can carry."

Ignoring my caution, Fritz cut a dozen large canes, stripped them of leaves, and carried them under his arm. Pushing through the cane-brake, we reached the palms. A troop of monkeys, previously playing on the ground, sprang up the trees, chattering and grimacing.

Fritz, provoked by their gestures, raised his gun. "Stay!" I cried. "Never take life needlessly. A live monkey is more useful than a dozen dead ones."

Gathering small stones, I threw them upward. The stones missed, but the monkeys, driven by their instinct to imitate, seized coconuts within reach and rained them down upon us.

Fritz delighted in the stratagem, picking up the finest nuts. We drank the milk through the holes I pierced. While the milk wasn't pleasant, it quenched thirst. We preferred the solid cream adhering to the shells, scraping it off with our spoons.

After this meal, we despised the lobster we carried, throwing it to Turk, who ate it gratefully. The dog, however, gnawed at the sugar-cane ends and begged for coconuts. I slung two nuts over my shoulder, fastening them by their stalks. Fritz resumed his burden, and we began our homeward march.

Fritz soon found the canes heavier than expected, shifting them from shoulder to shoulder until he sighed. "I had no idea reeds could be so heavy. I pity the poor negroes carrying such loads! Yet I shall be glad when Mother tastes them."

"Patience and courage!" I encouraged. "Remember Aesop's breadbasket: heavy at the start, light at the end. Fasten the bundle crosswise with your gun."

We did so. Fritz noticed me sucking the end of my cane. "Oh, come," he said, "that's a capital plan. I'll do that too."

He began sucking vigorously but extracted nothing. "How is this?" he asked.

"Think," I replied. "You are capable of finding the way."

"Oh, of course," he realized. "Like trying to suck marrow from a bone without a hole at the other end."

"Exactly," I said. "You create a vacuum, expecting air to force liquid down an end it cannot enter."

Fritz quickly mastered the art, discovering the necessity of a fresh cut at each joint. He talked of teaching his brothers and sharing the cocoanut milk he had filled his flask with.

"My dear boy," I said, "you need not have added that to your load. It will likely be vinegar by the time we arrive."

"Vinegar! Horrid!" Fritz cried, swinging the flask and pulling out the cork. With a loud pop, the contents foamed forth like champagne.

"There now!" I laughed. "Moderation, friend Fritz! It may be delicious, but it could go to your head."

"My dear Father, you cannot think how good it is! Vinegar? This is excellent wine!"

Invigorated by the unexpected draught, we merrily covered the remaining distance. We found our gourd dishes dry and light. Just as we passed the grove where we breakfasted, Turk darted away, springing furiously among a troop of monkeys playing on the turf. Taken by surprise, the dog, ravenous from hunger, seized a monkey and tore it apart before we could intervene.

The victim was a mother monkey, whose flight had been hindered by her child on her back. The little creature hid in the grass, trembling, watching its mother's tragic fate. Seeing Turk's bloodthirsty intent, Fritz rushed to rescue, flinging away his load and losing his hat. To no avail for the mother, but a laughable scene ensued. The young monkey, seeing Fritz, leaped onto his shoulders, clinging tightly to his curly hair despite Fritz's frantic efforts to dislodge it.

I laughed so hard I could scarcely help my terrified boy. "The animal, having lost its mother, seems determined to adopt you," I said. "Perhaps he senses a father figure in you."

"Or rather," Fritz retorted, "the rogue found a chicken-hearted man shrinking from ill-treating an animal seeking protection. But Father, he's yanking my hair terribly!"

Finally, by coaxing the monkey and offering a biscuit, I disentangled its paws from Fritz

"I shall spare you a detailed account of our first day's labors," my wife said, her voice steady despite the weariness in her eyes. "Truth be told, I spent the morning in anxious contemplation, watching your progress and waiting for your signals. I rose before dawn, and upon seeing your sign that all was well, I hastened to reply. Then, while my sons still slept, I sat alone to ponder how we might improve our precarious situation.

'There is no way,' I murmured to myself, 'that we can endure much longer in this exposed spot. The sun beats down relentlessly, and our only shelter is this flimsy tent, where the heat is stifling even worse than on the open shore. Why should not I and my little boys contribute as much as Fritz and your father? Why should we not strive to accomplish something useful?

'If we could but trade this gloomy, unhealthy dwelling for a pleasant, shaded retreat, our health and spirits would surely improve. Among those delightful woods where Fritz and his father discovered so many wonders, I am certain there exists a small sanctuary where we could establish ourselves comfortably. There must be one, and I shall find it.'

By this time, the boys had awakened. I observed Jack quietly and industriously working with his knife near where Fritz's jackal had lain. Watching him, I saw him cut two long, narrow strips from the animal's skin, cleaning and scraping them with great care. He then took a handful of large nails from his pocket, driving them through the skin points outward. Next, he cut strips of canvas sailcloth twice as wide as the thongs, doubled them, and laid them over the raw side of the skin to cover the protruding nail heads.

At this stage, Master Jack approached me with a polite request that I kindly stitch the canvas and the moist skin together for him. I handed him needles and thread, though I could not bear to deprive him of the pleasure of performing the task himself. However, seeing how good-naturedly he persisted with his awkward, unskilled fingers, I felt pity for him. Conquering my own disgust, I finished lining the ingenious dog-collars he had devised. Soon after, I was called upon to complete a fine belt of skin he had fashioned for himself. I advised him to consider a method to prevent the skin from shrinking.

Ernest, though initially treating Jack's creations with ridicule, proposed a sensible plan which Jack immediately executed. He nailed the skin flat onto a board and placed it in the sun to dry.

My scheme for a journey was met with joyful agreement by my young companions. Preparations were set in motion instantly: weapons and provisions were gathered. The two elder boys carried guns, while they entrusted me with the water flask and a small hatchet.

Leaving the tent in as good order as possible, we proceeded toward the stream, accompanied by the dogs. Turk, who had joined you on your first expedition, seemed to understand immediately that we intended to follow the same route, proudly leading the way. As I looked at my two young sons, each armed with a gun, and reflected on how the safety of the party depended so heavily on these little fellows, I felt profound gratitude toward you, dear husband, for having taught them the use of firearms in their childhood.

Filling our water-jar, we crossed the stream and climbed to the height from which, as you described, a lovely prospect unfolds. At the sight of it, a pleasurable sensation of buoyant hope, long absent from my heart, awoke within me. A pretty little wood in the distance caught my eye, and thither we directed our course. Soon, however, we found it impossible to force our way through the tall, strong grass growing in dense luxuriance, towering higher than the children's heads. Turning toward the open beach on our left, we followed it until we reached a point much nearer the wood. Quitting the strand, we made for the trees.

We had not entirely escaped the tall grass, and with utmost fatigue and difficulty, we struggled through the reeds. Suddenly, a great rushing noise terrified us all. A very large and powerful bird sprang upward on the wing. Both boys attempted to take aim, but the bird was far away before they were ready to fire.

"Oh dear, what a pity!" exclaimed Ernest. "Now, if I had only had my light gun, and if the bird had not flown quite so fast, I should have brought him down directly!"

"Oh yes," I replied, "no doubt you would be a capital sportsman if only your game would always give you time to make ready comfortably."

"But I had no notion that anything was going to fly up just at our feet like that," he cried.

"A good shot," I answered, "must be prepared for surprises: neither wild birds nor wild beasts will send you notice that they are about to fly or run."

"What sort of bird can it have been?" inquired Jack.

"Oh, it certainly must have been an eagle," answered little Franz, "it was so very big!"

"Just as if every big bird must be an eagle!" replied Ernest, in a tone of derision.

"Let's see where he was sitting, at all events!" I said.

Jack sprang toward the place, and instantly a second bird, rather larger than the first, rushed upward into the air with a startling noise. The boys stood staring upwards, perfectly stupefied, while I laughed heartily, saying, "Well, you are first-rate sportsmen, to be sure! You certainly will keep my larder famously well supplied!"

At this, Ernest blushed and looked inclined to cry, while Jack put on a comical face, pulled off his cap, and with a low bow called after the fugitive, "Adieu for the present, sir! I live in hopes of another meeting!"

Searching the ground carefully, we discovered a rude nest made untidily of dry grass. It was empty, though we perceived broken egg-shells nearby and concluded that the young brood had escaped into the grass, which we could see waving at a distance as the little birds ran through it.

"Now look here, Franz," said Ernest presently. "Consider how this bird could possibly have been an eagle. Eagles never build on the ground, nor can their young leave the nest and run as soon as they hatch. That is a peculiarity of the gallinaceous tribe alone. The species, I think, is indicated by the white belly and dull red color of the wing coverts I observed, and I believe them to be bustards, especially since I noticed in the largest the fine moustache-like feathers over the beak, peculiar to the Great Bustard."

"My dear boy!" I said. "Your eyes were actively employed, I must confess, if your fingers were unready with the gun. And after all, it is just as well, perhaps, that we have not thrown the bustard's family into mourning."

Thus chatting, we approached my pretty wood. Numbers of birds fluttered and sang among the high branches, but I did not encourage the boys in their wish to shoot any of the happy little creatures. We were lost in admiration of the trees of this grove, and I cannot describe to you how wonderful they are, nor can you form the least idea of their enormous size without seeing them yourself. What we had called a wood proved to be a group of about a dozen trees only, and what was strange, the roots sustained the massive trunks exalted in the air, forming strong arches and props around each individual stem, which was firmly rooted in the center.

I gave Jack some twine, and scrambling up one of the curious open-air roots, he succeeded in measuring round the trunk itself, making it out to be about eighteen yards. I saw no fruit, but the foliage was thick and abundant, throwing delicious shade on the ground beneath, which was carpeted with soft green herbage, entirely free from thorns, briars, or bushes. It was the most charming resting-place ever seen, and I and the boys enjoyed our midday meal immensely in this glorious palace of the woods, so grateful to our senses after the glare and heat of our journey.

The dogs joined us after a while. They had lingered behind on the sea-shore, and I was surprised to see them lie down and go comfortably to sleep without begging for food, as they usually do when we eat.

The longer we remained in this enchanting place, the more it charmed my fancy. If we could but manage to live in some sort of dwelling up among the branches of those grand, noble trees, I should feel perfectly safe and happy. It seemed absurd to suppose we should ever find another place half so lovely, so I determined to search no further, but return to the beach to see if anything from the wreck had been cast up by the waves.

Before starting, Jack persuaded me to sit quietly a little longer to finish making his belt and the spike-collars for the dogs, for you must know that the child had actually been carrying the board on which these were stretched all this time to ensure they received the full benefit of the sun.

As they were now quite dry, I completed them easily, and Jack girded on the belt with great pride, placing his pistols in it, and marching about in a most self-important style, while Ernest fitted the collars on the two dogs.

On reaching the shore, we found it strewed with many articles, doubtless of value, but all too heavy for us to lift. We rolled some casks beyond high-water mark and dragged a chest or two higher on the beach. While doing so, we observed that our dogs were busy among the rocks, carefully watching the crevices and pools, and every now and then pouncing downwards to seize something they swallowed with apparent relish.

"They are eating crabs," said Jack. "No wonder they have not seemed hungry lately."

Sure enough, they were catching the little green crabs with which the water abounded. These, however, did not entirely satisfy them. Some time afterwards, just as we were about to turn inland toward the ford, we noticed that Juno was scraping in the sand, turning up some round substances which she hastily devoured.

Ernest went to see what these were and reported in his calm way that the dog had found turtles' eggs.

"Oh," cried I, "then let us by all means share in the booty!" Mrs. Juno, however, did not approve of this, and it was with some difficulty that we drove her aside while we gathered a couple of dozen eggs, stowing them in our provision bags. While thus employed, we caught sight of a sail appearing to approach the shore beyond the cliffs. Ernest declared it must be our raft. Little Franz, always fearing savages, began to look frightened, and for a moment I myself was doubtful what to think.

However, we hastened to the stream, crossed it by the stepping-stones, and came in sight of the landing-place, where we joyfully met you.

"Now I hope you approve of the proceedings of your exploring party, and that tomorrow you will do me the favor of packing everything up and taking us away to live amongst my splendid trees."

"Aye, little wife," said I, "so that is your idea of comfort and security? A tree, I do not know how many feet high, on which we are to perch and roost like birds? If we had but wings or a balloon, I own, it would be a capital plan."

"Laugh as much as you like," returned my wife, "my idea is not so absurd as you make it out. We should be safe up there from jackals' visits during the night. And I know I have seen at home in Switzerland a pretty arbour with a strong floor up among the branches of a lime tree, where we went up a staircase to reach it. Why could not we contrive a place like that, where we could sleep safely at night?"

"I will consider the idea seriously, my wife," said I. "Perhaps something may come of it, after all! Meantime, as we have finished supper and night is coming on, let us commend ourselves to Almighty protection and retire to rest."

Beneath the shelter of our tent, we all slept soundly as marmots until break of day. When my wife and I awoke, we took counsel together regarding future proceedings. Referring to the task she had proposed the previous evening, I remarked that undertaking it would involve so many difficulties that it was highly necessary to look closely into the subject.

"In the first place," said I, "I am unwilling hastily to quit a spot to which I am convinced we were providentially led as a landing-place. See how secure it is; guarded on all sides by these high cliffs, and accessible only by the narrow passage to the ford, while from this point it is so easy to reach the ship that the whole of its valuable cargo is at our disposal. Suppose we decide to stay patiently here for the present—at least, until we have brought on shore everything we possibly can?"

"I agree with you to a certain extent, dear husband," replied she, "but you do not know how dreadfully the heat among the rocks tries me. It is almost intolerable to us who remain here all day while you and Fritz are away out at sea or wandering among the shady woods, where cool fruits refresh and fair scenes delight you. As to the contents of the ship, an immense deal has been cast ashore, and I would much rather give up all the remainder and be spared the painful anxiety it gives me when you even talk of venturing again on the faithless deep."

"Well, I must admit that there is much right on your side," I continued. "Suppose we were to remove to your chosen abode and make this rocky fastness our magazine and place of retreat in case of danger. I could easily render it still more secure by blasting portions of the rock with gunpowder. But a bridge must be constructed in the first place to enable us to cross bag and baggage."

"Oh, I shall be parched to death before we can leave this place if a bridge has to be made," cried my wife impatiently. "Why not just take our things on our backs and wade across as we have done already? The cow and the donkey could carry a great deal."

"That they will have to do, in whatever fashion we make the move," said I. "But bags and baskets we must have to put things in, and if you will turn your attention to providing those, I will set about the bridge at once.

The morning sun broke over the island with a warmth that seemed to ignite the spirits of the children. Before the dew had even dried from the grass, they were scrambling up the great tree, chattering and bounding like young monkeys.

"What shall we begin to do, Father?" they cried in unison. "What do you want us to do today?"

"Rest, my boys," I replied gently. "Rest."

"Rest?" they repeated, bewildered. "Why should we rest?"

I quoted the scripture we had memorized: "Six days shalt thou labour and do all that thou hast to do, but on the seventh, thou shalt do no manner of work." This is the seventh day," I explained. "Therefore, let us rest."

"But, Father," Jack protested, "that isn't resting. That's just standing still. I'll take my bow and arrow and shoot birds, and we'll climb the tree and have fun all day!"

"That is not resting," I said firmly. "That is not how you are to spend the Lord's Day."

"No!" Franz argued. "But there is no church here, no clergyman, and no organ."

"The leafy shade of this great tree is far more beautiful than any church," I assured them. "Here we will worship our Creator. Come, boys, down with you. Turn our dining hall into a breakfast room."

One by one, the children slid down the ladder. My wife, Elizabeth, and I sat with them in the pleasant shade on the fresh, soft grass. After singing hymns and offering heartfelt prayers to the Almighty Giver of all good, I told the children I would relate a parable rather than preach a sermon.

"Oh, that would be delightful!" Franz exclaimed. "I like the parables in the Bible better than anything. When can we hear you read from the Bible again, Father?"

"Ah, my little boy," I returned, feeling a pang of guilt. "Your words reproach me. While I was eagerly striving to procure food from the wreck to feed our bodies, I blush to think I neglected the Bread of Life, the Word of God. I shall search for a Bible on my next return to the ship; although our own books were nearly all destroyed, I am sure to find one."

At these words, my wife arose. She fetched her magic bag and drew from it a copy of the Holy Scriptures, which I thankfully received. After reading aloud from its sacred pages, I spoke as follows:

"A Great King, ruling in power and splendor over a vast realm of light and love, possessed within its boundaries a desolate and unfruitful island. He made this spot the object of his special care; lavishing on it all the varied resources of his might and goodness, it bloomed in beauty and became the happy residence of a band of colonists. Each was bound not only to cultivate the soil but to cherish in his soul the spirit of love and true allegiance to his Sovereign.

"While this faithful union was maintained, the colony flourished, and the noblest virtues exalted the existence of every member. Yet, a discontented and rebellious spirit infected these fortunate subjects. Disobedience and pride brought misery and punishment; the fair prospects of the colony were blighted, and total separation from the parent kingdom seemed inevitable.

"Nevertheless, a message of pardon—of free forgiveness—was accorded to these rebels. To all who, humbly accepting it, molded their future lives to the will of the Great King, was held out the promise of removal from the ruins caused by the great rebellion to the glory and undimmed splendor of the realm of Light and Blessedness."

Having interested the children, I pressed home the truths I sought to teach, leaving allegory for a moment to speak simply and earnestly to each young heart. With a short prayer for a blessing on my words, the service closed.

After a thoughtful pause, we separated, each employing himself as he felt disposed. I took some arrows and endeavored to point them with porcupine quills. Franz begged me to make a little bow for him, while Fritz asked advice regarding the tiger-cat skin and the cases he was to contrive from it. Jack assisted with the arrow-making, inserting a sharp spine at one end of each reed and fastening it with pack-thread.

"Oh, Jack!" cried Franz. "Mamma's soup is as sticky as anything! Shall I run and ask for a cake of it?"

"No, no, little goose! Better look for some real glue in the tool-box."

"There he will find glue, to be sure," I said. "And the soup would scarcely have answered your purpose. But Jack, my boy, I do not like to hear you ridicule your little brother's idea. Some of the most valuable discoveries have been the result of thoughts which originally appeared no wiser than his."

While thus directing my sons, we were startled by a shot just overhead. At the same moment, two small birds fell dead at our feet. Looking up, we beheld Ernest among the branches, bending his face joyfully toward us.

"Well hit! Well hit! A good shot, wasn't it?" he cried.

Slipping down the ladder, he picked up the birds and brought them to me. One was a thrush, the other a small dove called the ortolan, esteemed a great delicacy for its exquisite flavor. Since the figs on which these birds fed were only just beginning to ripen, we reasoned that they would soon flock in numbers to our trees. By waiting until we could procure them in large quantities, we might preserve them for the rainy season, placing them in casks with melted lard or butter.

By this time, Jack had pointed a good supply of arrows and industriously practiced archery. I finished the bow and arrows for Franz, expecting to be left in peace; but the youngest demanded a quiver, and I had to invent one also, stripping a piece of bark from a small tree, fitting a flat side and a bottom to it, and attaching a string. Attaching it to his shoulders, the youthful hunter filled it with arrows and went off, looking, as my mother said, like an innocent little Cupid bent on conquest.

Not long after, we were summoned to dinner and all willingly obeyed. During the meal, I proposed to decide on suitable names for the different spots we had visited on this coast.

"For," I said, "it will become more and more troublesome to explain what we mean unless we do so. Besides, we shall feel much more at home if we can talk as people do in inhabited countries. Instead of saying, 'the little island at the mouth of our bay where we found the dead shark,' or 'that wood where we caught the monkey,' let us begin by naming the bay in which we landed. What shall we call it?"

"Oyster Bay," said Fritz.

"No, no—Lobster Bay!" cried Jack, "in memory of the old fellow who took a fancy to my leg!"

"I think," observed my mother, "that, in token of gratitude for our escape, we should call it Safety Bay."

This name met with general approbation and was forthwith fixed upon. Other names were quickly chosen: our first place of abode became Tentholm; the islet in the bay, Shark's Island; and the reedy swamp, Flamingo Marsh. It took some time to decide on a name for our leafy castle, but finally it was entitled Falconhurst. We then rapidly named the remaining points: Prospect Hill, the eminence we first ascended; Cape Disappointment, from whose rocky heights we had strained our eyes in vain search for our ship's company; and Jackal River, for the large stream at our landing place.

In the afternoon, the boys continued their various employments. Fritz finished his cases, and Jack asked my assistance in carrying out his plan of making a cuirass for Turk, the dog, out of the porcupine skin. After thoroughly cleansing the inside, we cut and fitted it round the body of the patient dog. When strings were sewn on and it became tolerably dry, he was armed with this ingenious coat of mail, cutting a most singular figure. Juno strongly objected to his friendly approaches and got out of his way as fast as she could; it was clear that he would easily put to flight the fiercest animal he might encounter, protected by armor at once defensive and offensive. I determined to make also a helmet for Jack out of the remainder of the skin, which to his infinite delight I speedily did.

Amid these interesting occupations, the evening drew on. After a pleasant walk among the sweet glades near our abode, we closed our Sabbath day with prayer and a glad hymn of praise, retiring to rest with peaceful hearts.

The next morning, I proposed an expedition to Tentholm, wishing to make my way thither by a different route. We left the tree well armed: I and my three elder sons each carrying a gun and game-bag, while little Franz was equipped with his bow and quiver full of arrows. A most curious party we formed: Fritz adorned with his belt of margay skin, and Jack, with his extraordinary headdress, looked like a couple of young savages. Their mother and I walked together; she, of the whole party, being the only one unarmed, carried a jar in which to get butter from Tentholm. We were preceded by the dogs: Turk, armed most effectively with his cuirass of porcupine skin, and Juno keeping at a respectful distance from so formidable a companion.

Master Knips fully intended to mount his charger as usual, but when he saw him arrayed apparently in a new skin, he approached him carefully. The flamingo saw us starting and, having been much petted during the last day or two, considered himself entitled to accompany us. We strolled on in the cool evening air, following the course of the stream. The boys roamed ahead of me, intent on exploration. Presently, I heard a joyful shout and saw Ernest running at full speed towards me, followed by his brothers. In his hand he held a plant, panting for breath and with sparkling eyes, he held it up to me.

"Potatoes! Potatoes, Father!" he gasped out.

"Yes," said Jack, "acres and acres of potatoes!"

"My dear Ernest," I said, for there was no mistaking the flower and leaf, and the light clear-green bulbous roots, "you have indeed made a discovery; with the potato we shall never starve."

"But come and look at them," said Jack. "Come and feast your eyes on thousands of potatoes."

We hurried to the spot: there, spread out before us, was a great tract of ground covered with the precious plant.

"It would have been rather difficult," remarked Jack, "not to have discovered such a great field."

"Very likely," replied Ernest, smiling. "But I doubt if you would have discovered that it was a potato field."

"Perhaps not," said Jack. "You are quite welcome, at all events, to the honor of the discovery; I'll have the honor of being the first to get a supply of them." So saying, he dug up, with hands and knife, a number of plants and filled his game-bag with the roots. The monkey followed his example, scratching away with his paws most cleverly, soon having a heap beside him. So delighted were we with the discovery, and so eager were we to possess a large supply of the roots, that we stopped not digging until every bag, pouch, and pocket was filled.

Some wished to return at once to Falconhurst to cook and taste our new acquisition, but I overruled them, and we continued our march, heavily laden but delighted.

"How," I said, "can we thank the Giver of all these blessings sufficiently?"

"Oh," said Franz, "we can say, 'We thank thee, O Lord, for all thy goodness and mercy; and bless us for Jesus Christ's sake. Amen.'"

"That would not be sufficient," said Fritz. "Do you think it would be enough just to say to father and mother, 'Thank you for all you do,' and not to show that we were really thankful by loving them and doing what we can to please them?"

"You are quite right, Fritz," I said. "Franz did not say all that was necessary; he should have added, 'Give me grace to do Thy will, and to obey Thee in all things.'"

Rich tropical vegetation flourished on every side: the tall stately palms, surrounded by luxuriant ferns; brilliant flowers and graceful creepers; the prickly cactus, shooting up amidst them; aloe, jasmine, and sweet-scented vanilla; the Indian pea, and, above all, the regal pineapple, loaded the breath of the evening breeze with their rich perfume. The boys were delighted with the pineapple, and so eagerly did they fall to that my wife had to caution them that there were no doctors on our territory, and that if they became ill, they would have to cure themselves as best they might.

While the breakfast preparations were underway, I turned my attention to the magnificent kangaroo skin, anxious to preserve it in its entirety as a testament to our survival. Our journey across the bridge at Jackal River was interrupted by a burst of laughter. Emerging from a concealed spot where they had been waiting, Jack and Ernest startled us with their mischievous prank. I tasked them with explaining that my work on the ship would consume a long time and that my wife must endure our absence for a night. I had intended to say this upon our initial departure, but fear of her objection had held me back; however, the boys' antics inadvertently gave me the opportunity to convey this news.

"Goodbye, boys! Take care of yourselves! We're off!" shouted Fritz as I joined him in the tub-boat, and we pushed off into the current. Clambering up the broken sides, we stood on the remnants of the deck and began to formulate our plans. My objective was clear: to construct a raft capable of transporting a vast array of heavy and bulky items that our small boat could not carry. We identified a series of empty water-casks as the ideal foundation. Sealing them tightly, we pushed them overboard and arranged twelve of them side by side in rows of three, binding them firmly together with spars. Upon this sturdy base, we laid a substantial floor of planks, reinforced by a low bulwark, creating a first-rate raft perfectly suited to our needs.

Fatigue prevented us from returning to land that evening. Having eaten only light refreshments from our wallets and worked without respite, we rejoiced in the knowledge that we were not expected home. We prepared an excellent supper from the ship's provisions and retired to sleep on spring mattresses—a genuine luxury after days spent in narrow hammocks.

The following morning, we vigorously began loading the raft and boat. We first cleared our own cabins, then moved to the captain's quarters, removing furniture, doors, window frames, and their associated hardware. We loaded the officers' chests and those of the carpenter and gunsmith, dividing the heavier contents into manageable portions. One large chest contained an assortment of fancy goods that resembled a jeweler's display: gold and silver watches, snuff-boxes, buckles, studs, chains, rings, and countless trinkets. Alongside a box of money, these items captivated our attention, though a case of common knives and forks proved far more practical than the silverware we had previously salvaged.

To my delight, we discovered a number of young fruit trees, meticulously packed and labeled with names familiar to European ears: apples, pears, chestnuts, oranges, almonds, peaches, apricots, plums, cherries, and vines. This cargo, originally destined for a distant colony, became our rich and nearly inexhaustible treasure trove. Among the supplies were ironmongery, plumber's tools, lead, paint, grindstones, cart wheels, and everything necessary for a smith's forge, including spades and plough-shares. We also found sacks of maize, peas, oats, and wheat, a hand-mill, and the numbered parts of a saw-mill, ready for assembly if we possessed the strength to erect it.

Overwhelmed by the sheer volume of wealth surrounding us, we struggled to decide what to take. Realizing that a single storm could destroy the ship and wipe out everything we left behind, we selected the most essential items, prioritizing the grain and fruit trees. Fishing lines, reels, cordage, harpoons, and a mariner's compass were added to the load. Fritz, remembering our encounter with the shark, positioned the harpoons with readiness, amusing himself by imagining he was a whaler flourishing his weapon. By early afternoon, both crafts were heavily laden, and we prepared to head for shore. Little did I suspect the chaos that would follow. With his back turned to me and the broad sail blocking my view, I missed his actions entirely. Suddenly, I felt a sharp shock, followed by the sensation of a line running through a reel. Before I could cry out, a second shock hit us, and the boat was rapidly dragged through the water.

"My dear boy, the turtle will be a terrible bargain if he upsets all our goods into the sea, even if he doesn't drown us first. For heaven's sake, be careful! I will wait a few minutes, but the instant there is danger, cut the line."

As the turtle began to head for the open sea, I hoisted the sail again. Finding the resistance too great, the creature turned landward, dragging us rapidly after it. The shoreline we approached was considerably to the left of our usual landing place, featuring a gradual shelf. At some distance from land, we grounded with a sharp shock but managed to avoid capsizing. The turtle was evidently exhausted from acting as a steam tug, hauling two heavily laden vessels at full speed. Its intention was clearly to reach land. I leaped into the water, waded up to it, and dispatched it with my axe. Despite its tenacity of life, it ceased struggling only after I severed its head from its body.

Not far from Falconhurst, Fritz signaled our approach by firing his gun and shouting in triumph. While we secured the boats and brought the turtle ashore, the entire family appeared in the distance, hastening eagerly toward us. Our new prize, along with the well-loaded boat and raft, sparked lively interest. My wife's primary relief, however, was seeing us return safely; our night's absence had disturbed her, and she was horrified by my description of our perilous chase in the wake of the fugitive turtle.

Anxious to unload some goods before nightfall, the boys ran to fetch the sledge. I, lacking an anchor, moored the boats using the heavy iron blocks we had brought. It required our combined strength to hoist the massive turtle onto the sledge; with the addition of the sapling fruit trees, it constituted a sufficient load. We hurried home, chatting merrily about our adventures. The first priority upon arrival was to prepare the turtle's flesh for supper. To my wife, this seemed a difficult task, but I turned the beast onto its back and quickly detached a portion of the meat from the breast with a hatchet by breaking the lower shell. I directed that it be cooked with a little salt, shell and all.

"But let me first cut away this disgusting green fat," my wife said, shuddering slightly. "See how it sticks all over the meat. No one could eat anything so nasty."

"Leave the fat, whatever you do!" I exclaimed. "Why, my dear, that is the very best part, the delight of the epicures. If there is too much, cut some off—it can be used as lard, and let the dogs make a supper of the refuse."

"And the handsome shell!" cried Fritz. "I should like to make a water-trough of that, to stand near the brook and stay full of clear water. How useful it would be!"

"That is a capital idea," I replied. "We can manage it easily if we find clay to create a firm foundation."

"Oh, as to clay," said Jack, "I have a grand lump of clay there under that root."

"Well done, my lad! When did you find it?"

"He found a bed of clay near the river this morning," his mother interjected, "and came home in such a mess I had to scrape his clothes and wash him thoroughly!"

"Well, mother, I can only tell you I should never in all my days have found the clay if I hadn't slipped and fallen amongst it."

"That I can well believe," she returned, "only, to hear your talk this morning, one would have thought your discovery was the result of very arduous search indeed."

"When you have finished discussing the clay and the turtle-shell," said Ernest, "I should like to show you some roots I found today. They are getting rather dry now. They look something like radishes, although the plant itself was almost a bush. I haven't dared to taste them, although our old sow was devouring them at a great rate."

"In that you did wisely, my boy. Swine eat many things injurious to men. Let me see your roots. How did you discover them?"

"I was rambling in the wood this morning and came upon the sow, very busy grubbing under a small bush and eating something ravenously. So I drove her away and found a number of these roots, which I brought for you to see."

"Indeed, Ernest," I exclaimed, examining the roots attentively, "I am inclined to believe you have made a brilliant discovery! If this proves to be the manioc root, as I expect, we might lose every other edible we possess and yet not starve. In the West Indies, cakes called cassava bread are made from it. Already having potatoes, we shall be very independent if we can succeed in preparing flour from these roots. Great care must be taken in the manufacture to express the juice, otherwise the flour may be injurious and even poisonous."

Seeing there was still time for another trip with the sledge, I went off with the elder boys, leaving Franz with his mother. We all looked forward with satisfaction to the princely supper awaiting us, for our day's work had been no light burden.

"I have been thinking about my turtle, father," said Fritz as we walked along, "is not the shell very valuable? Surely beautiful combs, boxes, and ornamental things are made of tortoise-shell, and if so, it seems a pity to use it for a water-trough."

"Your turtle, Fritz, is only fit for eating; its shell is worthless regarding ornament. Whereas the species whose shell is prized so much is unfit for food. The sledge quickly received its second load from the raft: chests, four cart-wheels, the hand-mill, and various smaller articles. We returned to Falconhurst without delay.

My wife welcomed us joyfully, remarking that we had been regularly overworked during the last two days. "However, now you are come home to rest," she said, "and you little think what refreshment awaits you here in the shade. Come and see my cellar!" She smilingly exhibited a small cask half sunk in the ground, well sheltered with leaves and branches.

Returning to shore, we found my wife and Franz waiting at Tentholm. She had moved her quarters there to be closer to our work, a gesture that filled me with gratitude. What awaited us was a scene of devastation. The side of the ship was torn open, debris floated in the water, and the compartment was exposed. We returned to Tentholm, claiming the explosion was merely a means to access the remaining cargo. Over the following days, we finished rigging the pinnace and mounting her two brass guns. The boys' imaginations ran wild with tales of naval battles and savage encounters, though I reminded them that our true victory would be living in peace.

We then ventured inland to show her the garden she had cultivated in our absence—rows of vegetables, fruit trees, and a neat layout that spoke of her diligence and love.

The following Sunday, we gathered for worship and instruction. In the afternoon, I demonstrated athletic exercises to the boys, emphasizing that true courage required both moral strength and physical prowess. The next day, I introduced them to the lasso, a versatile tool for hunting and survival. Fritz quickly mastered the technique, proving himself adept with the new weapon.

As a gale approached, we prepared for a family excursion to the Calabash Wood. Loaded with provisions and accompanied by our pets, we set out. The journey led us through lush landscapes where Fritz shot a magnificent bustard, a feat that initially caused alarm for the bird's offspring but ultimately resulted in the rescue of the wounded mother. We encountered a coconut crab descending a tree, which Jack captured with a clever trick using his jacket.

Our trek revealed the wonders of the island, including the precious liane rouge, a vine providing fresh water. We harvested gourds from the calabash trees, carving them into bowls and cups. To our amusement, the beast turned out to be our own runaway sow, feasting on potatoes we had inadvertently disturbed.

Continuing our journey, Jack mistook a large iguana for a crocodile. I patiently demonstrated how to capture the harmless lizard using a noose and a whistle, a method that fascinated the boys. Carrying the iguana on my back, we returned to Falconhurst, where we enjoyed a hearty supper of roasted iguana, acorns, and the fruit we had gathered. The day's adventures, filled with danger, discovery, and teamwork, reinforced our bond and our resolve to thrive in this new world.

The following day, our first priority was to retrieve the sledge laden with the crockery and baskets we had crafted, located deep within the Calabash Wood. I insisted on keeping the younger boys with their mother, deeming a large party unsuitable for the expedition I intended to lead. Fritz volunteered to accompany me, eager to explore beyond the chain of rocky hills that bordered our known territory.

As we traversed the grove of evergreen oaks, we spotted our sow feasting on acorns. The fright we had inflicted upon her the previous day seemed to have vanished; she appeared not only unharmed but unusually friendly, perhaps viewing us as deliverers from the savage dogs that had threatened her. The forest was teeming with life, the birds undisturbed until Fritz, in a burst of enthusiasm, fired his gun and brought down a blue jay and two parakeets—one scarlet, the other green and gold. Just as he began reloading, a strange, dull thumping sound, reminiscent of a muffled drum, struck our ears. It triggered an immediate alarm, reminding us of the ever-present danger of savages.

We approached the sound with extreme caution, hiding among the thick undergrowth until we reached an open glade. There, perched atop a fallen log, stood a magnificent bird, roughly the size of a rooster, with rich chestnut-brown plumage mottled with dark grey. Distinctive tufts of velvety black feathers, glossed with green, adorned his shoulders. He strutted and wheeled with a strange, stately grace, performing for a small audience of similar birds lacking these elaborate ruffs. After a time, he spread his tail like a fan, stiffened his wings, and began beating them in rapid, rhythmic strokes. The resulting rumble, amplified by the strikes against the hollow log, created a thunderous drumming effect that had initially startled us.

My interest was piqued by this rare spectacle until a shot rang out from behind me. The performance ended abruptly; my overzealous son had transformed a delightful comedy into a tragic slaughter. The enthusiastic drummer fell dead, and his admiring companions scattered in panic. I was deeply annoyed by the cruelty of the interruption and reproached Fritz for firing without permission, lamenting the loss of such a fine specimen of the ruffed grouse.

We placed the bird on the ass, which waited patiently for our return, and pressed on. The sledge remained secure where we had left it. Seizing the opportunity, I resolved to fulfill my original intent: to explore the coastal cliffs and rocky hills that extended along the shoreline, searching for any potential opening into the interior or confirming our isolation. Leaving the Calabash Wood, we advanced across fertile ground rich with manioc, potatoes, and unknown flora, watered by gentle streams.

My attention was drawn to bushes laden with small, white, wax-like berries that stuck to my fingers. Recognizing them as *Myrica cerifera*, I explained to Fritz that by melting and straining these berries, we could produce candles. This prospect brought great joy to my mind, imagining the relief it would offer my wife, who currently had to retire to rest the moment the sun set. Though the greenish wax would be more brittle than beeswax, it burned well and emitted a pleasant fragrance. We gathered enough to fill a canvas bag and continued our journey.

Soon, we encountered another natural wonder: a colony of birds, resembling yellowhammers but plain in brown plumage, sharing a single dwelling. Their nests were clustered around the trunk and branches of a solitary tree, covered by a roof of grass and fibers that shielded them from the elements. The structure resembled a massive bath-sponge, with numerous openings serving as apartments for the community. Twigs protruded from the walls, acting as perches for the young. Thousands of these birds swarmed in and out, including many beautiful parrots that seemed to vie for space with the rightful owners.

Fritz, an expert climber and eager to inspect the nests, ascended the tree. He reached into several empty holes until, finally, his hand was seized and sharply bitten by a strong beak deep within a nest. With a cry, he withdrew his hand, shaking it to alleviate the pain. Recovering, he seized the offending bird, drew it from its retreat despite its shrieks, and stuffed it into his pocket, buttoning his coat quickly. He slid down the tree pursued by a swarm of relatives, who screeched and pecked at him in rage. His prize was not one of the sociable grosbeaks, but a small, green parrot, which he delighted in, planning to tame and teach it to speak.

This colony sparked a lively discussion about social behavior in nature. I compared their communal living to beavers, bees, wasps, ants, and even the coral insect, marveling at their united efforts. Fritz recalled watching ants in his homeland, correcting me gently on the distinction between ant eggs and larvae. I expanded on this, describing the impressive mounds of foreign ants, their internal architecture, and their destructive nature. I detailed the ant-eater, or tamanoir, a formidable creature with a long, toothless snout and a sticky tongue designed to devour the ants, contrasting it with the smaller, arboreal variety. My explanation captivated Fritz, occupying us for a considerable time as we traveled.

Eventually, we reached a grove of tall trees with broad, thick leaves bearing round, sour fruits. Fritz noticed gummy resin exuding from the bark, reminiscent of cherry-tree gum he had collected as a child. Upon scraping some and attempting to soften it, he discovered it possessed elasticity, springing back to its original shape.

"Oh father, only look! This gum is quite elastic! Can it possibly be india-rubber?" he exclaimed.

"What!" I cried, examining it. "A valuable discovery indeed! You are perfectly right!"

"India-rubber," I explained, "or caoutchouc, is a milky resinous juice from trees indigenous to Brazil, Guiana, and Cayenne. The natives used it to coat earthen flasks to create waterproof bottles. It can be molded into various forms, and I am delighted to have it here; I intend to manufacture boots and shoes."

Shortly after, we reached the coconut wood and saw the bay and the promontory we named Cape Disappointment. Passing through, I noticed a smaller palm with a mealy pith, identifying it as the renowned sago-palm. Inside the pith, I found fat worms, recalling that they were eaten as a delicacy in the West Indies. Curiosity overcoming repugnance, I roasted a few over a fire with salt. They smelled temptingly rich and fat. I swallowed one boldly, and to my surprise, found it delicious. Fritz, summoning courage, joined me, and the worm became a savory addition to our baked potatoes.

Encountering a dense thicket blocking our direct path, we diverted toward the sugar-brake near Cape Disappointment, cutting a bundle of sugar-canes for the donkey to carry alongside the bag of wax berries. Returning to the Calabash Wood, we loaded the sledge. The ass, now relieved of his burden, readily dragged the load. We arrived at Falconhurst in the evening to a warm welcome. Our tale was listened to with interest, and the newly acquired green parakeet enchanted the boys. After a grateful supper, we ascended to our tree-castle, exhausted but content.

The idea of candle-making seized the boys' imaginations. The next morning, "candle" was the sole topic of conversation. I agreed to become "chandlers," though privately I harbored doubts regarding the lack of animal fat for a steady flame and the complexity of the process. Nevertheless, I hid my reservations. Under my direction, the boys picked the berries, melted the green wax in a shallow iron vessel, skimmed the pure wax, and dipped wicks prepared by my wife. Fritz hung them on a bush to dry. By repeating the dipping process, the wicks grew into sturdy candles. We hung them to harden, and that same night, three hours after sunset, Falconhurst was brilliantly illuminated for the first time.

My wife praised my ingenuity but requested a method to preserve the cream that constantly went bad. I proposed a plan attributed to the Hottentots. Using a hollowed gourd bottle, we filled it with cream and corked it tightly. The boys then rolled the gourd back and forth in a cradle of sailcloth for half an hour. When opened, a large lump of fresh butter emerged, delighting my wife. Meanwhile, I constructed a clumsy but sturdy cart to replace the sledge, which proved immensely useful for harvesting.

We then turned our attention to landscaping. We planted walnut, cherry, and chestnut trees in parallel rows to form a shady avenue from Falconhurst to Family-bridge, laying down a road to reach Tentholm. We also planted vines around the mangrove roots and other trees to adorn Tentholm. My primary goal for Tentholm was not beauty, but safety; I planted a thick, prickly hedge and mounted two guns on nearby hillocks to defend against wild beasts or savages.

Six weeks of hard labor passed. The boys developed remarkable strength, though their clothes became increasingly ragged. Determined to replenish our wardrobe, I led three boys and myself back to the wreck in the pinnace. We rummaged the hold thoroughly, loading the pinnace with chests of cloth, linen, guns, tables, shutters, and pitch. Before departing, I declared my intention to blow up the brave ship that had carried us from Switzerland. I salvaged a large copper cauldron, securing it to empty casks to ensure it floated. As the fuse burned, a pillar of fire rose from the black waters, and the ship exploded in a sullen roar. We felt a profound sadness, as though losing a dear friend, yet we knew the destruction was necessary.

My wife brought news that ducks and a goose had successfully reared families in the river reeds. The sight of these birds inspired Ernest to dream of eating duck and peas again, feeling like civilized mortals. This prompted me to visit Falconhurst, where we found several young trees in our avenue bent by the wind. Resolving to cut bamboos for support, I organized an expedition with my wife and the younger boys.

At the caoutchouc tree, we tapped the bark to collect sap for waterproof boots. Moving forward, we reached a delightful plain bounded by sugar-cane and bamboo, with the calm sea stretching before us. Jack suggested pitching our tent there permanently, but I wisely declined, citing the danger of wild beasts lurking in the nearby thickets. Instead, we made it our headquarters.

While the boys cut bamboo and collected sugar-cane, they decided to gather coconuts. Without the aid of monkeys or land-crabs, they struggled up the smooth, slippery trunks. I intervened, providing them with buskins made of shark's skin and binding ropes around their bodies and the trees. Equipped thus, they climbed successfully. My wife and I watched as they harvested fruit. Ernest, initially lying on the grass, sprang up at a sarcastic remark, climbed a tree, and severed the leafy crest of a cabbage-palm. He offered the prized palm-cabbage to his father, explaining it was worth more than all the nuts combined. He then descended with a nutshell filled with a clear, rosy liquor, which he presented to his mother as a precious wine. We toasted to his health and enjoyed the drink.

As evening fell, our donkey suddenly brayed, pricked up his ears, and galloped off into the bamboo thicket. We chased him briefly, sending the dogs after him, but he vanished into the darkness. Alarmed by the loss of such a valuable beast, I ordered a large fire and instructed the family to sleep with their weapons at hand.

At dawn, finding no sign of the ass, I organized a search party with Jack and the dogs, leaving the elder boys to guard their mother. We followed the hoof prints for an hour before they disappeared, mingled with those of a larger herd. Undeterred, Jack urged me to continue, suggesting that from a hilltop we might spot the herd. We pushed through bushes and swamps until we reached a wide plain where a herd of buffaloes grazed. I resolved to approach them stealthily through a bamboo marsh.

Emerging onto the plain, we faced the herd. They stared inquisitively but did not move. Jack raised his gun, but I checked him, ordering a retreat. However, our dogs, unrestrained, dashed forward and seized a buffalo calf. This triggered a stampede; the herd charged, bellowing and tearing the ground. The leader closed in on us, rendering my gun useless. I fired a pistol, killing the leader, which caused the rest to halt, snuff the air, and flee. The dogs held the calf, struggling to bring it down.

Jack devised a plan. He unwound his lasso, cast it around the calf's hind legs as it reared, and brought it to the ground. We fastened the rope to a bamboo and subdued the animal. I then pierced the cartilage of its nose with a knife and passed a cord through the hole to secure it. Subdued and overawed, the young bull followed us without resistance.

We attended to the dead buffalo, saving the tongue and steaks for later, while the dogs feasted on the rest. Birds of prey soon descended to scavenge, leading to a chaotic contest between the dogs and the intruders. Jack wanted to shoot, but I forbade it, knowing the birds would be useless and the shots ineffective. Satisfied, we decided to return to camp, leaving the search for the ass for another day.

The next morning, the boys and I set out with the cart, heavily laden with bundles of bamboo, to tend the avenue of fruit trees. We left the buffalo behind; his services were no longer needed, and I wished the wound in his nostrils to heal completely before subjecting him to labor again.

Others were bent double, while only a few remained standing. We lifted the fallen trees, dug deep at their roots, and drove stout bamboo props into the earth, lashing them firmly to the trunks with strong broad fibers.

"Papa," Franz said, handing me a fiber as I needed it, "are these wild or tame trees?"

"Oh, these are wild trees, the most ferocious of them," Jack laughed. "We are tying them up lest they run away. Soon we will untie them, and they will trot after us wherever we go, giving us fruit. Oh, we will tame them! They shall have a ring through their noses just like the buffalo!"

"That is not true," Franz replied gravely. "But there are wild and tame trees. The wild ones grow in the woods like crab-apples, and the tame ones in the garden like the pears and peaches at home. Which are these, Papa?"

"They are not wild," I answered. "They are grafted, or cultivated, or as you call them, tame trees. No European tree bears good fruit until it is grafted!" Seeing a puzzled look cross the little boy's face, I hastened to explain.

"Grafting," I continued, "is the process of inserting a slip or twig of a tree into what is called an eye—that is, a knot or hole in the branch of another tree. This twig then grows and produces not the fruit the original stock would have borne, but the fruit of the tree from which the slip was taken. Thus, if we have a sour crab tree and an apple tree bearing fine Ribston Pippins, we take a slip of the latter, insert it into an eye of the former, and in a year or two, that branch will be laden with excellent apples."

"But," asked Ernest, "where did the slips of good fruit come from, if none grow without grafting?"

"From foreign countries," I replied. "It is only in the cold climate of our part of the world that they require this grafting. In many parts of the world, in southern latitudes, the most luscious fruit trees are indigenous to the soil, flourishing and bearing sweet, wholesome fruit without the slightest care. In England, Germany, and even France, these same trees require the utmost exertion of horticultural skill to produce any fruit at all."

"Thus, when the Romans invaded England, they found nothing but crab-apples, nut bushes, and brambles. But by grafting upon these, fine apples, filberts, and raspberries were produced. It was the same in our dear Switzerland—all our fruit trees were imported."

"Were cherries, Father? May we not even call cherries Swiss? I always thought they grew nowhere else."

"I am afraid we cannot even claim cherries as our own, not even the name. They are called cherries from *Cerasus*, a state of Pontus in Asia, whence they were brought to Europe by Lucullus, a Roman general, about seventy years before Christ."

"Hazelnuts also come from Pontus; walnuts originally from Persia. As for grapes, they are of the greatest antiquity. You remember Noah cultivating vines; they have been transported from place to place until they are now found in most parts of the civilized world."

"Do you think all these trees will grow?" Fritz asked as we crossed Jackal River and entered our plantation at Tentholm. "Here are lemons, pomegranates, pistachio nuts, and mulberries."

"I have little doubt of it," I replied. "We are evidently within the tropics, where such trees are sure to flourish."

"These pines come from France, Spain, and Italy; the olives from Armenia and Palestine; the figs originally from the island of Chios; the peaches and apricots from Persia; plums from Damascus in Syria, and the pears of all sorts from Greece."

"However, if our countries have not been blessed in the same way with fruit, we have been given wisdom and skill, which has enabled us to import and cultivate the trees of other lands."

We talked and worked until every tree requiring treatment was secured with a stout bamboo prop. Then, with appetites a gourmand might envy, we returned to Falconhurst.

My good wife was almost alarmed at the way we fell upon the corned beef and palm-cabbage she had set before us, but eventually, these good things produced the desired effect, and one by one we declared ourselves satisfied. As we reclined after our labor, digesting our dinner, we discussed our various projects.

"I wish," said my wife, "that you would invent some other plan for climbing to the nest above us. I carefully considered the project, turning over every plan for its accomplishment.

"It would be impossible, I am afraid, to make stairs outside, but within the trunk it might be done. More than once I have thought that this trunk might be hollow or partly so. If such be the case, our task would be comparatively easy. Did you not tell me the other day that you noticed bees coming from a hole in the tree?"

"Oh, yes," said little Franz. "I went to look at them, and one flew right against my face and stung me. I almost cried, but I didn't."

"Brave little boy," I said. "Well, now, if the trunk is sufficiently hollow to contain a swarm of bees, it may be hollow for the greater part of its length. Like the willow in our own country, it might draw all its nourishment through the bark and, in spite of its real unsoundness, retain a flourishing appearance."

Master Jack, practical as usual, instantly sprang to his feet to put my conjecture to the proof. The rest followed his example, and they were soon climbing about like squirrels, peeping into the hole and tapping the wood to discover by sound how far down the cavity extended.

They forgot, in their eagerness, who were the tenants of this interesting trunk. They were soon reminded of it, however, for the bees, disturbed by this unusual noise, burst out with an angry buzz and instantly attacked the cause of the annoyance. They swarmed round them, stinging hands, faces, and necks, settling in their hair, and pursuing them as they ran to me for assistance.

It was with difficulty that we got rid of the angry insects and attended to the boys. Jack, who had been the first to reach the hole, fared the worst and was soon a most pitiable sight, his face swollen to an extraordinary degree. Only by the constant application of cold earth was the pain alleviated. They were all eager to commence an organized attack upon the bees at once, but for an hour or more, by reason of their pain, they were unable to render me much assistance.

In the meanwhile, I made my arrangements. I first took a large calabash gourd, intending to make a beehive so that, when we had driven the insects from their present abode, we might not lose them entirely. I flattened the lower half of the gourd, cut an arched opening in the front for a doorway, made a straw roof for protection from rain and heat, and the little house was complete.

Nothing more could then be done, for the irritated bees were still angrily buzzing round the tree. I waited till dark, and then, when all the bees had returned to their trunk, with Fritz's assistance, I carefully stopped up every hole in the tree with wet clay, ensuring the bees would not issue forth next morning before we could begin operations.

Very early we were up and at work. I first took a hollow cane and inserted one end through the clay into the tree. Down this tube, with pipe and tobacco, I smoked most furiously. The humming and buzzing within was tremendous; the bees evidently could not understand what was happening. I finished my first pipeful, put my thumb over the end of the cane, and gave the pipe to Fritz to refill. He did so, and I smoked again. The buzzing became less noisy, subsiding into a mere murmur. By the time I had finished the second pipe, all was still; the bees were stupefied.

"Now then, Fritz," I said, "quick with a hammer and chisel, and stand here beside me."

He was up in a moment. Together, we cut a small door by the side of the hole. We did not take this door out, but left it attached by one corner so it might be removed at a moment's notice. Giving the bees a final dose of tobacco smoke, we opened it.

Carefully but rapidly, we removed the insects as they clung in clusters to the sides of the tree and placed them in the hive prepared for their reception. As rapidly, I took every atom of wax and honey from their storehouse and put it in a cask I had made ready.

The bees were now safely removed from the trunk, but I could not tell whether, when they revived from their temporary stupor, they might refuse to occupy the house I had presented them and insist on returning to their old quarters. To prevent this possibility, I took a quantity of tobacco, placed it upon a board nailed horizontally within the trunk, lit it, and allowed it to burn slowly so the fumes might fill the cavity.

It was well I did so. As the bees returned to consciousness, they left their pretty hive and buzzed away to the trunk of the tree. They seemed astonished at finding this uninhabitable, and an immense amount of noisy humming ensued. Round and round they flew, backwards and forwards between the gourd and the tree, settling here and there, until, at length, after due consideration, they took possession of the hive and abandoned their former habitation to us, the invaders of their territory. By evening they were quite quiet, and we ventured to open the cask in which we had stored our plunder.

We first separated the honey from the honeycomb, pouring it off into jars and pots. The rest we threw into a vessel of water placed over a slow fire. It soon boiled, and the entire mass became fluid. We placed this in a clean canvas bag and subjected it to heavy pressure. The honey was thus soon forced out, and we stored it in a cask. Though not perhaps quite equal to the former batch in quality, it was yet capital. The wax remaining in the bag I also carefully stored, knowing it would be of great use in the manufacture of candles. Then, after a hard day's work, we turned in.

The internal architecture of the tree now had to be attended to. Early the following morning we prepared for the laborious task. A door had first to be made, so at the base of the trunk we cut away the bark and formed an opening just the size of the door we had brought from the captain's cabin, which, with hinges, was ready to be hung.

The clearing of the rotten wood from the center of the trunk occupied us some time, but at length we had the satisfaction of seeing it entirely accomplished. Standing below, we could look up the trunk, which was like a great smooth funnel, and see the sky above.

It was now ready for the staircase. First, we erected in the center a stout sapling to form an axis round which to build the spiral stairs. In this we cut notches to receive the steps, and corresponding notches in the tree itself to support the outer ends. The steps themselves we formed carefully and neatly of planks from the wreck, clenching them firmly in their places with stout nails.

Upwards and upwards we built, cutting windows in the trunk as required to admit light and air, until we were flush with the top of the center pole. On this pole we erected another to reach the top of the tree, securing it firmly, and built in the same way round it until we reached the level of the floor of the nest above.

To make the ascent perfectly easy, we ran a handrail on either side—one round the center pillar and the other following the curve of the trunk.

This task occupied us a whole month. By the end of that period, so accustomed had we become to having a definite piece of work before us that we began to consider what other great alteration we should undertake.

We were, of course, not neglecting the details of our colonial establishment. There were all the animals to be attended to. The goats and sheep had both presented us with additions to our flock, and these frisky youngsters had to be seen after. To prevent them straying to any great distance, for we had no wish to lose them, we tied round their necks little bells we had found on board the wreck, which would assist us in tracking them.

Juno, too, had a fine litter of puppies. The other pets were also flourishing and being usefully trained. The buffalo, after giving us much trouble, had now become perfectly domesticated and was a very useful beast of burden, besides being a capital steed for the boys. Spring had finally broken the winter's grip. The winds ceased their howling, the sun pierced the lingering clouds with brilliant rays, and the relentless rain gave way to a gentle silence. Returning to Falconhurst, our minds were full of wonder and plans for the new home. Nothing was now talked of but the new house. The safety of Falconhurst, once our pride, now dwindled to nothing in our estimation; it would serve merely as a summer residence, while the cave would become our impregnable winter castle.

We hewed a row of windows in the rock, fitting the cases from the officers' cabins. We brought the door from Falconhurst and fitted it into the aperture. I determined to conceal the opening in the tree trunk with bark to avoid attracting wild beasts or savages. Inside, we divided the cave into four parts: a large front compartment subdivided into sitting, eating, and sleeping areas; a right-hand division for the kitchen and workshop; a left-hand section for stables; and a dark recess behind for the storehouse and powder-magazine.

Knowing the discomforts of the rainy season, we planned useful arrangements. We did not intend to be smoke-dried again, so we built a proper fireplace and chimney. Our stable arrangements were improved, and ample space was left in the workshop for extensive operations.

Our frequent visits to Tentholm revealed new advantages. Splendid turtles often came ashore to deposit eggs, and their delicious flesh provided sumptuous meals. When more than one appeared, we cut off their retreat to the sea, turned them on their backs, and fastened them to stakes driven near the water's edge. Thus secured, they thrived and remained in excellent condition for weeks. Lobsters, crabs, and mussels also abounded.

One morning, approaching Tentholm, we witnessed a curious phenomenon. The waters out to sea appeared agitated, heaving and boiling, their surface illuminated by flashes of fire. Hundreds of birds hovered overhead, screaming and darting down to plunge beneath the water or skim the surface. The shining mass rolled onwards, approaching our bay directly.

I was convinced it was a shoal of herrings. Explaining the phenomenon to the boys, I described the immense size of the living bank, the ravenous fish that devoured them, and the birds that hunted stragglers. To escape these enemies, the shoal sought the shallow shores, only to meet a third enemy: man. "In one year millions of these fish are caught," I noted, "yet the roes of only a small number would suffice to supply as many fish again."

Soon our fishery was in operation. Jack and Fritz stood in the water with baskets, baling out the fish and throwing them to us on shore. We quickly cleaned them, placing them in casks with alternating layers of salt and herrings until we had many casks of pickled fish. As the barrels were filled, we closed them carefully and rolled them to the cool vaults at the back of our cave.

Our good fortune did not end here. A day after the herring fishery, a great number of seals appeared, attracted by the refuse we had thrown into the sea. Though I feared they were unsuitable for our table, we secured a score or two for their skins and fat. We drew the skins off for harness and clothing, and boiled the fat down for oil, which we stored for tanning, soap-making, and lamp fuel.

These occupations briefly interrupted our work at Rock House, but we soon returned with renewed vigor. I noticed that the salt crystals rested on a base of gypsum, which I knew could be made into plaster. Breaking off pieces and subjecting them to heat, I reduced them to powder. The resulting plaster was smooth and white. Lacking a specific use, I plastered over some herring casks to ensure all air was excluded. The remainder I intended to preserve by smoking.

To do this, the boys and I built a small hut of reeds and branches. We strung our herrings on lines across the roof and lit a great fire of brushwood and moss on the floor. Dense smoke curled in volumes around the fish, curing them perfectly in a few days.

About a month later, other shoals of fish appeared. Jack spotted them first, calling out that young whales were off the coast. We ran down to discover the bay swarming with great sturgeon, salmon, and trout making for the mouth of Jackal River to deposit their spawn.

Jack was delighted. "Here are proper fish!" he exclaimed. "None of your paltry fry. How do you preserve these?"

"Not so fast," I replied. "Tell me how they are to be caught, and I will tell you how they are to be cooked."

"Oh! I'll catch them fast enough," he replied, darting off to Rock House.

While I pondered the method, he returned with a bow, arrow, and ball of twine. At the arrow-head, he had fastened a barbed spike and secured the arrow to the end of the string. Armed with this weapon, he advanced to the river's edge. His arrow flew and struck one of the largest fish in the side.

"Help, father, help!" he cried as the great fish darted off, carrying the arrow and all with it. "Or he will pull me into the water!"

I ran to his assistance, and together we struggled with the finny monster. He pulled tremendously, lashing the water, but we held the cord fast. Weaker and weaker grew his struggles, and eventually, exhausted by exertion and loss of blood, he allowed us to draw him ashore.

He was a noble prize. Fritz and Ernest, arriving just as we completed the capture, were envious of Jack's success. Not to be outdone, they rushed for weapons. Soon we were all in the water: Fritz with a harpoon, Ernest with a rod and line, and I, armed like Neptune with an iron trident. The shore was soon strewn with a goodly number of the finest fish.

At length, Fritz, after harpooning a great sturgeon eight feet long, could not get the beast ashore. We all went to his assistance, but our united efforts were unavailing.

"The buffalo!" proposed my wife. Off went Jack for Storm. The buffalo was harnessed to the harpoon rope, and soon the monstrous fish lay panting on the sand. Once we had captured as many fish as we could utilize, we set about cleaning and preparing their flesh. Some we salted, some we dried like the herrings, and some we treated like Mediterranean tunny, preparing them in oil.

Of the sturgeon's roe, I decided to form caviare, the great Russian dish. Removing the membranes, washing it in vinegar, and salting it, I pressed out the moisture, packed it in small barrels, and stowed it away. Knowing that the sturgeon's bladder yields the best isinglass, I carefully collected the air-bladders, washed them, and hung them to stiffen. Peeling off the outer coat, I cut the remainder into strips, or staples. Placing these in an iron pot over the fire, I reduced them to a proper consistency, strained off the glue through a clean cloth, and spread it on a slab of stone to dry. The substance obtained was beautifully transparent and promised to serve as an excellent substitute for glass in our window-frames.

Fortunately, in this beautiful climate, the kitchen garden required little attention. Seeds sprang up and flourished regardless of the season. Peas, beans, wheat, barley, rye, and Indian corn seemed constantly ripe, while cucumbers, melons, and other vegetables grew luxuriantly. The success of our garden at Tentholm encouraged me to hope that my experiment at Falconhurst had not failed. One morning, we visited the spot. Passing the field where potatoes had been dug, we found it covered with barley, wheat, rye, and peas in profusion.

Turning to my wife in amazement, I asked, "Where has this fine crop sprung from?"

"From the earth," she replied, laughing. "Where Franz and I sowed the seed I brought from the wreck. The ground was ready tilled by you and the boys; all we had to do was scatter the seed."

The morning broke with a crisp clarity, and before the sun had fully claimed the sky, the family was already at work. Breakfast was a brief affair, consumed with the urgency of purpose, before we turned our attention to the bird-lime. The adhesive mixture, a potent blend of caoutchouc oil and turpentine, proved to be a formidable tool. The boys, eager and efficient, took the rods I had prepared, smearing them thoroughly before positioning them high among the branches where the fruit hung dense and the flocks congregated.

The sheer volume of pigeons that day was staggering, a sight that reminded me we had not witnessed such a gathering in years. Last year, we had only seen the stragglers of the season; now, the trees in the neighborhood bowed under the weight of masses of birds. Even the sweet acorns of the evergreen oaks were heavily patronized, with large flocks roosting overnight, evidenced by the disturbed earth beneath the canopy. Seeing this, I resolved to launch a night raid, employing torches in the manner of the Virginian colonists.

Meanwhile, the daytime snares yielded excellent results. Pigeons alighting on the sticky rods found themselves trapped; the more they flapped and struggled, the more they were coated in the tenacious mess, eventually collapsing to the ground with pitiful cries, dragging their ensnared sticks with them. Once removed, fresh lime was applied, and the cycle repeated. Soon, the boys mastered the technique, working with such independence that I was free to prepare for the nocturnal assault. I mixed pinewood and turpentine to create torches, while Jack brought me a particularly fine pigeon, distinct from the others. Reluctant to harm it, he showed me that it belonged to our European stock, a breed we intended to preserve until its numbers swelled. I gently cleaned the bird, removing the sticky residue with oil and ashes, and placed it in a wicker cage. Jack followed suit, bringing me several more of these European captives. It was a triumph; having released our original pair upon landing, they had gone wild, offering no benefit. These new birds, however, promised future meals of pigeon-pie and a steady supply of eggs.

As evening fell, we marched to the wood of sweet acorns, armed with long bamboo canes, torches, and canvas sacks. To the children, our makeshift weaponry seemed curious and inadequate, but the darkness that enveloped us before we reached the trees rendered our strategy clear. I lit the torches, and as anticipated, the branches were thick with ortolans and wild pigeons, roosting in astonishing density. The sudden glare of light sent chaos through the terrified flock. Dazzled and bewildered, the birds fluttered helplessly through the branches, and many fell before we even began to beat them. When we did strike the limbs, the harvest was immense; my wife and Franz struggled to keep pace with the quantities tumbling to the ground. Our sacks filled rapidly, and we returned to Falconhurst to secure our bounty before retiring to rest.

The following day was consumed entirely by the processing of the catch—plucking, boiling, roasting, and stewing—leaving no time for anything else. By the next morning, however, a new expedition was planned: a mission to Woodlands to prevent a recurrence of the monkey invasion. I hoped to catch the mischievous creatures in the act of destruction and mete out a punishment severe enough to drive them from our farm forever. My wife stocked us with provisions for a multi-day absence, remaining at home with Franz and Turk. I took with me a superior, stronger bird-lime, short posts, string, and a supply of coconut shells and gourds. The buffalo carried the bulk of the gear, accompanied by a few boys, while I rode the ass. We established a concealed camp near Woodlands, pitching a small tent and tethering the animals, ensuring the dogs were secured to avoid alerting our quarry.

The cottage at Woodlands was quiet and deserted. Working swiftly, we drove stakes into the ground to form an irregular paling around the house, weaving string in and out to create a labyrinth impossible to traverse without contact. Every surface was liberally smeared with bird-lime, and basins of the mixture were scattered with rice, maize, and other delicacies as bait. Night fell without incident, and once our preparations were complete, we retreated to our tent.

Very early the next morning, a confused noise signaled the approach of a large troop of apes. Armed with clubs and cudgels, we held the dogs on leashes and moved silently behind the thickets to gain a vantage point. The scene that unfolded was chaotic and deafening. Rustling, crackling, and creaking filled the air, mingled with horrid cries and shrieks. From the forest poured the disorderly rabble of monkeys, scrambling and leaping across the grass toward the house. Attracted by the novelties, they dashed fearlessly through the palings, rushing at the food or scrambling onto the roof to tug at wooden pegs.

Gradually, the apes became besmeared with our bird-lime. Some sat in ludicrous poses, trying to clean themselves; others were hopelessly entangled in stakes and cordage, trailing debris in bewildered despair. Many had gourds and shells clattering about them, caught while reaching for the bait. Most comical was an old ape who had drunk from a calabash of palm wine; the shell stuck to his forehead and whiskers, masking his eyes and causing him to dance wildly in his confusion. While some fled, others found themselves fixed to the surrounding trees, hanging in woeful durance. As panic spread, I released the three dogs, who rushed to the attack with zealous fury. The place resembled a ghastly battlefield, the din of howling and barking giving way to an awful silence as we surveyed the mangled remains of at least forty apes.

Though the boys grew sad at the slaughter, I quickly turned their thoughts to the grim task of burying the dead and destroying the evidence of our trap. We then restored order to the dismantled cottage and gathered the scattered livestock, hoping to settle them peacefully once more. During this work, we heard a heavy thud from a tree and discovered three splendid birds caught on loose limed sticks. Two were Blue Molucca pigeons, and the third, with its resplendent green, bronze, and steely-blue plumage, was undoubtedly a Nicobar pigeon. I was delighted by the prospect of domesticating them.

"First tenants, father!" Fritz exclaimed. "Do you expect to catch more like these?"

"Not exactly catch them," I replied, smiling at his puzzled look. "I intend to practice a secret art. Magic can achieve much, Fritz!"

In a few days, Woodlands was restored, and we returned to Falconhurst to a joyful welcome. The consensus was immediate: we must proceed to Tentholm to build the proposed pigeon-house in the rock. My plan involved hollowing out a space in the cliff facing the Jackal River, fitting it with partitions and perches, and installing a large wooden front with slides and shutters.

After weeks of labor, the structure was ready. I summoned Fritz, instructing him to prepare a mixture of soft clay, aniseed, and salt—a scent designed to attract wild pigeons. I also prepared oil of aniseed by pounding the seeds and straining the essence, which I applied to the pigeon-holes. The European birds, now friendly with the three beautiful strangers, were installed in their new residence. When the other boys peered through a small pane of glass, they saw the birds contentedly pecking at the "magic food," unaware of the secret arts employed to lure them.

On the third morning, I awakened Fritz to arrange the sliding door mechanism and pour fresh aniseed oil around the entrance. Awakening the family, I invited them to witness the release. With mock solemnity, I waved a wand and muttered an incantation before signaling Fritz to open the panel. The captives emerged, hesitated, and then, startled, took wing. Circling above us, they rose higher until they vanished from sight. Yet, moments later, they returned, settling quietly on the dove-cot. Just as we congratulated ourselves on their acceptance of the home, the three blue pigeons—the noble foreigners—rose in circles and flew directly toward Falconhurst. Their departure seemed decisive, and we feared they were lost forever.

We consoled ourselves with our four remaining birds, but the disappointment lingered until midday. Suddenly, Jack burst into the cavern, shouting, "He is there! He is come!"

"The blue pigeon?" I asked.

"Hurrah! He's brought a mate!" Jack cried.

Sure enough, the pretty fellow was billing and cooing to a stranger of his own breed, bowing and sidling until the shy lady yielded and entered. Before we could close the door, Fritz spotted another pair arriving, and shortly after, the third and handsomest Nicobar pigeon returned with a lovely bride. Encouraged by the success of the first arrivals, the new couples soon made themselves at home.

Nest-building commenced quickly. Among the materials collected, I noted a long grey moss or lichen, similar to that used in the West Indies for stuffing mattresses. My wife's active mind immediately devised plans to collect enough to stuff bolsters, pillows, saddles, and cushions. The pigeons also brought us nutmegs, which we carefully planted in the orchard.

Peace was not long maintained, however. Jack, in his usual spirit of adventure, ventured into the swamp behind the powder magazine to gather reeds for his wickerwork. He slipped into the thick mud, sinking deeper with every attempt to reach the finer reeds. His screams went unanswered until his faithful dog, Fangs, appeared. Unable to pull him out, the dog helped Jack construct a raft of reeds, allowing him to crawl to safety. Though covered in mud and slime, Jack returned with a bundle of Spanish canes that were exactly what I needed.

I had secretly intended to construct a loom for my wife, who understood weaving. Using two strong reeds bound together and smaller pieces sharpened for the comb, I assembled the frame. To the children's incessant questions, I mystically replied that it was an "outlandish musical instrument" that Mother would know how to play. When the time came to present it, her joy was matched by the children's amusement as she "played the loom."

Around this time, a beautiful foal, a son of the onager named Swift, was added to our stud, promising to be a strong and tractable mount for me. With the interior of the cavern progressing well, I turned my attention to an aqueduct to bring fresh water from the Jackal River closer to our home. Using hollow bamboo pipes and a large cask as a reservoir, the system proved highly effective, earning my wife's praise as if we had built a marble fountain.

Anticipating the rainy season, I pressed forward with winter stores, gathering roots, fruits, grains, and pine-cones. Casks and barrels were pressed into service until the raft was dismantled to provide tubs for the store-rooms. The weather soon turned unsettled, with heavy clouds, thunder, lightning, and torrential rains sweeping the landscape. The sea churned with groundswells, heralding the grandest work of the year.

We established a routine, living with the animals in the salt-cave while the boys rode to Falconhurst for supplies. To combat the darkness of the inner cavern, Jack and I erected a tall bamboo pole reaching the vaulted roof, securing a pulley to draw up a large ship's lantern. The rooms were divided and furnished: Ernest and Franz organized the library, Jack and his mother arranged the sitting-room and kitchen, and Fritz and I set up the workshops, complete with a carpenter's bench, lathe, and a fully equipped forge found in the ship.

Despite the endless work of building shelves, tables, and cupboards, I found constant employment to be the key to maintaining mental and physical health. We improved the cave with a wide bamboo porch and terrace. The library proved invaluable, containing voyages, natural history, histories, and scientific works, alongside maps, globes, and instruments. We decided to study various languages—German, French, English, Dutch, Latin, Spanish, and Malay—to ensure someone in the family could communicate with any nation we might encounter. The household buzzed with a "Babel in miniature," each member sporting newly acquired phrases.

Amidst the labor, we uncovered treasures from the ship: mirrors, wardrobes, marble-topped tables, elegant chairs, clocks, a musical box, and a chronometer. Our rocky home transformed into a palace, and the children unanimously named our dwelling "Rockburg."

As the rains subsided, the weather cleared, revealing the lush tropical vegetation. One day, Fritz spotted a strange shape on a small island near Flamingo Marsh. Examining it with a telescope, we realized it was a huge stranded whale. We repaired our boat and made the excursion. The island was steep and rocky, but we found a landing place. The boys hurried to the beach where the monster lay, while I climbed to the highest point to survey the mainland.

Rejoining my sons, they were already collecting shells and coral. I explained that the recent storms had stirred the ocean depths, washing ashore thousands of shellfish and the whale itself. Fritz noted the creature's immense size, while Ernest suggested harvesting train oil, though he preferred the beauty of the shells. I agreed to return later with implements to utilize the carcass.

Ernest requested permission to remain alone on the island to emulate Robinson Crusoe, but I denied it, reminding him that our fate as a solitary family provided sufficient experience of shipwreck. The boys found rowing back arduous, joking about inventing a rowing machine from the kitchen jacks. I teased them about their laziness but acknowledged Fritz's suggestion of using iron wheels from the jack's clockwork.

Jack asked about the utility of coral. I explained its historical use by chemists, its value as ornamentation, and its potential for trade with natives. We decided to arrange the coral in our library as the foundation for a Museum of Natural History. Fritz correctly identified coral as the calcareous cells of minute animals forming a tree-like structure, noting its presence in fisheries from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean.

Plans were set for a voyage to Cape Disappointment and Prospect Hill the following day. That evening, we prepared our gear—dresses, weapons, and food—and retired early. The next morning, armed with spades and mattocks, we departed to gather young coconut trees and shrubs for a future plantation on Whale Island.

Our journey across the smooth bay was leisurely. Leaving Safety Bay and Shark Island behind, we admired the coastal panorama until we landed near Prospect Hill. As we approached our farm, the crowing of roosters stirred a pang of nostalgia, recalling the familiar sounds of home. Yet here, save for wild beasts, solitude reigned. Our long absence had confused the original animals; to their offspring—lambs, kids, and chicks who had never seen a human—we appeared as a terrifying army.

The boys struggled to milk the goats until they lassoed them, tied their legs, and offered salt to calm them. Soon, milk flowed into coconut shells and calabash flasks. We coaxed the fowls with grain, and my wife gathered eggs. For dinner, we served cold provisions, the centerpiece being a piece of whale tongue cooked specifically for this feast. However, the taste was disastrous. Everyone recoiled, begging for pickled herring to mask the flavor of the train oil, and the dog, Fangs, was the only one to enjoy the delicacy. Fortunately, fresh coconuts and goat's milk restored our spirits.

After packing up, Fritz and I collected sugar-cane shoots for planting. On our return to Whale Island, I felt compelled to survey the coast beyond Cape Disappointment, but a long sandbank and hidden reefs blocked our path. Fritz spotted breakers ahead, and we turned back toward the island.

Upon landing, I planted the cane shoots. The boys soon grew bored, wandering off to collect shells and coral, leaving my wife and me to finish. Suddenly, Jack shouted, "Father! Mother! Look! An enormous skeleton! A mammoth!"

"Have you forgotten the whale?" I laughed.

"No, father! This has real beast bones, not fish bones. It's bigger than the whale. Come!"

The turtle was indeed scrambling toward the water. I grabbed an oar as a lever, and with Ernest's help, we flipped the eight-foot creature onto its back. Once helpless, we inspected Jack's "mammoth," confirming it was merely the whale's skeleton. I explained to Jack that whales are mammals, named for their mammary glands, and pointed out the hollow bones designed for buoyancy—a testament to the Creator's wisdom.

"What can we use these bones for?" Fritz asked.

"We'll leave them to bleach," I decided. "Perhaps later we can make chairs or a museum desk."

Turning our attention to the turtle, we devised a plan to tow it. Fastening an empty cask to a line looped around its neck and fore-paws, we lifted it slightly. The turtle, buoyed by the cask, swam vigorously, towing us behind. The boys compared me to Neptune in his chariot pulled by dolphins. We landed safely near Rockburg, where the turtle was soon dispatched. Its massive shell would become a water trough for the cave, and its meat was salted for future meals.

I had hoped to plow a field before the rains, but our animals were unaccustomed to the yoke. Instead, with Ernest's help, I completed a loom, using isinglass instead of flour to size the threads. As the annual herring shoals arrived, followed by seals and other fish, we harvested them in abundance. The boys, eager for hunting, paused for me to construct baskets to replace failing sacks. Later, sitting on the verandah, Fritz suddenly pointed to the distance. "Something strange approaches! It moves like a cable, then rises like a mast."

My wife retreated to the cave with the others, barricading the entrances and arming themselves. I watched through my spyglass. "It is an enormous serpent!" I cried. "It will cross the bridge."

"Can we attack it?" Fritz asked bravely.

"Only with extreme caution," I replied. "We must wait for the right moment."

The serpent, a gigantic boa constrictor over thirty feet long, advanced slowly, rearing its head high. As it crossed the bridge, we took our positions at the upper openings of the cave. The beast seemed puzzled by our presence, coiling and uncoiling. Suddenly, the boys and my wife fired. The shots startled the monster but failed to harm it. It glided into the reedy marsh and vanished.

Relieved, we discussed the creature. I explained that it was a boa constrictor, named for its method of killing by constriction. For three days, we remained in suspense, confined to the cave, fearing the beast's return. Our geese and ducks, agitated by its presence, fled to Shark Island.

Our anxiety ended when our old donkey, Grizzle, broke free and bolted toward the marsh. Before we could stop him, the serpent emerged. Grizzle froze, braying pitifully, as the snake struck. It wound itself around the donkey, compressing him until he was a shapeless mass. The snake then began to swallow him whole.

"Shoot him!" the children cried.

"It is impossible!" I said. "But now we have a chance to destroy the snake."

I explained that snakes lack teeth to chew and must swallow prey whole, crushing bones in their stomachs. My wife and little Franz fled in horror, but the rest of us watched fascinated as the monster consumed its meal.

"Now is the time!" I declared.

I led the boys forward, leveling my gun at the stiff, motionless serpent. Fritz and I fired simultaneously, the bullets entering the skull. The beast convulsed, its tail lashing wildly, before falling dead. Jack, seeking glory, fired into its side, causing a final twitch, but he was unharmed.

My wife approached cautiously. "Thank God we destroyed this enemy."

"What do we do with it?" Jack asked.

"Stuff it for the museum," Fritz suggested.

I recounted a tale of pigs on Lake Superior that had survived and thrived by eating rattlesnakes. Ernest questioned the story, suggesting secretary birds might have done the same, but I clarified that such birds lived in Africa, not North America.

The dog Bruno willingly carried the load, and we returned to Woodlands. There, we found Ernest surrounded by dead rats. Tired from the day's exertions, we retired to our mattresses.

The next morning, we journeyed to a makeshift arbor between the sugar-brake and the Gap, using a sailcloth for shelter. Searching the cane brake for the boa constrictor, we found no trace. Refreshing ourselves with fresh cane juice, we were suddenly alerted by barking dogs and rustling canes. A herd of grey peccaries, showing sharp tusks, burst forth. My double-barrel gun felled two, and the others fled in single file. I immediately ordered the removal of their odoriferous glands to prevent the meat from spoiling. While Jack fetched the cart, I cleaned the animals. Ernest reported that the procession had passed near the hut, where he and Juno had secured three more.

We loaded the cart, adorned it with flowers, and marched triumphantly back to the hut. After dinner, we began processing the peccaries, singeing the bristles and salting the meat. The boys, eager to experiment, dug a pit to cook the smallest pig in the Otaheitean fashion. Fritz stuffed it with potatoes, onions, and herbs, wrapping it in large leaves before burying it in hot ashes and earth. The result was a delicious roast, seasoned by the native "ravensara" leaves, which combined the scents of nutmeg, clove, and cinnamon.

During the days spent curing our hams, we roamed the neighborhood, finding no serpents but acquiring giant bamboos suitable for casks and pots. However, our visit to Prospect Hill was marred by the mischief of vagabond apes, who had scattered the poultry and torn the cottage. We left the disorder to be rectified later and continued our march to the Gap. Finding the barricades broken down, we realized the snake and the peccaries had already passed through. Resolving to fortify the defile, we emerged into a wide, varied landscape of hills and plains. Crossing the East River, we found the soil increasingly arid, resembling a desert. The boys groaned at the barrenness, but I encouraged them, pointing to the distant mountains where we hoped to find water and refuge.

As we rested, Knips sniffed the air and led the dogs up a glen to a pool of water. Meanwhile, Fritz spotted what he believed to be horsemen. Through the spy-glass, we confirmed they were actually large ostriches. I proposed capturing one for its feathers. As the birds approached, playing and varying their pace, I noted the male's striking white plumes against its black body. Realizing a direct chase was futile, I waited for the right moment. When the dogs, unable to restrain their impatience, rushed forward, the ostriches took flight. Just as they were escaping, I unhooded Fritz's eagle. The falcon made a fatal swoop, piercing the male's skull. Grieving the loss of a live mount, we admired the magnificent feathers.

Ernest inquired how ostriches survived in such deserts. I explained that wild melons scattered by Providence sustained them. Suddenly, Jack shouted about a nest of ostrich eggs. We found more than twenty, each weighing about three pounds. Unable to carry them all, we set a landmark and used a bent elastic stick to suspend two eggs on Jack's shoulder, allowing him to carry them like a dairy maid.

Continuing our journey, we rested by a marshy pool where we found tracks of buffaloes and antelopes, but no serpents. Here, Jack discovered a small mud-tortoise, a useful garden pest. Soon after, we entered a verdant valley we named Glen Verdant, where herds of antelopes grazed. Leaving the valley, we found ourselves near the Jackal Cave. Suddenly, a cry of terror erupted as Ernest ran back, screaming about a bear. Two enormous bears appeared, and the dogs charged. My sons and I fired, wounding both beasts, but the dogs' interference made precise shooting impossible. Seizing the opportunity, we advanced with pistols and fired simultaneously, killing both bears—one through the head, the other through the heart.

Relieved, we dragged the carcasses into their den to hide them from scavengers. By sunset, we rejoined my wife and Franz, who had discovered a bed of pure white clay suitable for porcelain and built a hearth. The next morning, I tested the clay, finding it baked hard and glazed, though slightly melted. We then visited the bear's den, where Fritz spotted a flock of wild turkeys attending the bears' "funeral." A large bird, the "Watcher," guarded the entrance. As the turkeys pressed in, a mighty rush of wings signaled an attack. Fritz fired, killing an enormous bird that fell head-first, breaking its neck. We rushed in to find the bears' tongues and eyes eaten, but the skins largely intact. The large bird was a condor, the mate of the Watcher.

We skinned the bears, cutting magnificent hams and preserving the paws for delicacies. The bones and offal were cleared by the birds and insects, leaving clean skulls for our museum. The skins required careful scraping and drying over two days. Lamenting the distance from the ravensara tree, I discovered a climbing pepper plant nearby. We gathered the berries, drying the red ones in salt water and the green ones in the sun, securing a supply of black and white pepper for the future. We also collected young plants to grow at Rockburg.

With the meat smoking and the peppers drying, the boys grew restless. I granted them permission to explore the savannah alone, assigning Fritz as their leader to foster independence. Armed and mounted on cattle, they departed with a hearty blessing. We remained behind, tending to the curing meat and investigating the cave's interior. I found the walls composed of talc mixed with asbestos and mica, successfully splitting a block into clear, transparent sheets perfect for window panes. My wife was delighted with this substitute for glass, declaring the lack thereof had been a constant trouble. Thus, our day of exploration concluded with practical discoveries and preparations for the challenges ahead.

As the sun dipped below the horizon, casting long shadows across the clearing, the campfire crackled with the promise of a hearty meal. The stewing bear paws, simmering in the pot, released a savory aroma that drifted through the evening air. We sat gathered around the flames, our conversation punctuated by anxious glances toward the treeline, waiting for the return of our young explorers.

Finally, the rhythmic tramp of hooves and the sharp beat of hooves against the earth broke the silence. A little troop of riders appeared, galloping across the open ground at a sharp trot. A shrill, ringing cheer erupted from our throats as we rose to meet them.

The boys sprang from their saddles, releasing the animals to graze and refresh themselves. They eagerly approached us, beaming with pride to display their acquisitions and recount their adventures. What funny figures they made! Franz and Jack each had a young kid slung across their backs, the four legs tied together and sticking out beneath their chins like strange appendages. Fritz's game-bag looked peculiarly lumpy, filled with round shapes and sharp points, occasionally shifting as if containing living creatures within.

"Hurrah for the chase, Father!" cried Jack, his face alight with excitement. "Nothing compares to real hunting after all. Just to see how Storm and Grumble handle a grassy plain! It is perfectly splendid! We soon tired out the little antelopes and caught them."

"Yes, Father," added Franz, "and Fritz has two Angora rabbits in his bag. We also wanted to bring you some honey. Only think! Such a clever bird—a cuckoo—showed us exactly where it was hidden!"

"My brothers forget the chief thing," said Fritz, correcting them gently. "We have driven a little herd of antelopes right through the Gap into our territory. There they are, all ready for us to hunt whenever we like—or to catch and tame!"

"We had a splendid ride," Fritz began, his voice animated. In fact, Levaillant mentions in his *Voyage au Cap de Bonne Esperance* that the Hottentots use this exact method to pen in antelopes they have caught."

"Well done," I said, pleased to see him recall his reading. "The antelopes are welcome to New Switzerland. But, my boy, I cannot say the same for the rabbits you have there. They increase so rapidly that if you establish a colony, your next difficulty will be to get rid of them."

I saw them running about, so I unhooded him, and in a few minutes he brought me three—one dead, with whose body I rewarded him, and these two here, unhurt."

"Certainly, Jack," I said, ready to listen. "First and foremost, how did you bring down those beautiful little animals?"

"My dear boy," I interjected, "according to your statement, Fritz must have been seven and a half miles off."

Franz declared it must be an enchanted princess, so I thought I would rid it of its spell. But Fritz stopped me from shooting it, saying it was a 'Honey Indicator' leading us to a bees' byke. I think I will get Mother to bathe it for me," and off rushed the noisy boy, leaving Fritz and me to attend to the fawns and examine the rabbits.

With the latter, I determined to follow Fritz's proposal and colonize Whale Island. I was all the more willing because I had been considering establishing a fortress on that island to which we might retreat in extreme danger, and where we would be thankful to possess a constant supply of animal food.

Having attended to the antelopes, I tried to interest the boys in my discovery of the block of talc, but just then their mother summoned us to dinner.

The principal dish consisted of the bear paws—savory-smelling delicacies so tempting that their close resemblance to human hands, and even Jack's roguish "Fee-fo-fum," did not prevent a single member of the family from enjoying them heartily.

After supper, we lit our watch-fire, retired to our tent, and slept soundly.

We had been working diligently; the bear meat was smoked, the fat melted down and stored, and a large supply of bamboos collected. Yet I wished to make another excursion. At early dawn, I aroused the boys. Fritz mounted the mule, I rode Lightfoot, and Jack and Franz took their usual steeds. With the two dogs, we galloped off—first to visit the euphorbia to collect the gum, and then to discover whether the ostrich had deserted her eggs in the sand.

Ernest watched us depart without a sigh of regret, returning to the tent to assist his mother and study his books.

Our steeds carried us down the Green Valley at a rapid rate, following the direction of our former expedition. We soon reached Turtle Marsh, filled our water-flasks, and arrived at the rising ground where Fritz had previously discovered the mounted Arabs.

As Jack and Franz wanted a gallop, I allowed them to press forward while Fritz and I visited the euphorbia trees. A quantity of red gum had exuded from the incisions I had made and coagulated in the sun. I rolled it into little balls and stored it in a bamboo jar I had brought for the purpose.

As we rode after the boys, who were some way ahead, Fritz remarked, "Did you not tell me that the juice of that tree was poisonous, Father? Why have you collected such a quantity?"

"I did indeed say so," I replied. "It is a most deadly poison. The inhabitants of the Cape of Good Hope use it to poison springs where wild animals assemble to quench their thirst, thus slaughtering immense numbers for their hides. I intend, however, to use it to destroy the apes should they again commit depredations, and also in preparing animal skins to protect them from insect attacks."

The two boys were still at some distance when suddenly four magnificent ostriches rose from the sand where they had been sitting. Jack and Franz perceived them and, with a great shout, drove them toward us. In front ran a splendid male bird, his feathers of shining black and his great tail plume waving behind. Three females of an ashen grey color followed him. They approached us with incredible swiftness, within gun-shot before they perceived us. Fritz had had the forethought to bind up the beak of his eagle so that, should he bring down an ostrich, it might be unable to injure him.

He now threw up the falcon, which towered upwards and swooped down upon the head of the foremost bird. Confused and alarmed, the ostrich could not defend himself or continue his flight. His speed was so greatly checked that Jack overtook him, hurling his lasso to enfold his wings and legs in deadly coils and bring him to the ground. The other ostriches were almost out of sight, so leaving them to their own devices, we leaped from our steeds and attempted to approach the captured bird. He struggled fearfully, kicking with such violence right and left that I almost despaired of getting him home alive.

However, it occurred to me that if we could cover his eyes, his fury might be subdued. I instantly acted upon this idea, flinging over his head my coat and hunting bag, which effectually shut out the light. No sooner had I done this than his struggles ceased, and we were able to approach. We first secured round his body a broad strip of sealskin, on each side of which I fastened a stout piece of cord to lead him easily. Then, fastening another cord in a loop round his legs to prevent him from breaking into a gallop, we released him from the coils of the lasso.

"Do you know," I said to the boys, "how the natives of India secure a newly captured elephant?"

"Oh, yes!" said Fritz. "They fasten him between two tame elephants. We'll do that to this fine fellow and tame him double quick."

"The only difficulty will be," remarked Jack, "that we have no tame ostriches. However, I daresay Storm and Grumble will have no objection to perform their part, and it will puzzle even this great monster to run away with them."

So we at once began operations. Storm and Grumble were led up on either side of the recumbent ostrich, and the cords secured to their girths. Jack and Franz, each armed with a stout whip, mounted their respective steeds. The wrappers were removed from the bird's eyes, and we stood by to watch what would next occur.

For some moments after the return of his sight, he lay perfectly still. Then he arose with a bound and, not aware of the cords which hampered him, attempted to dash forward. The thongs were stout, and he was brought to his knees. A fruitless struggle ensued, and then, seeming to accommodate himself to circumstances, he set off at a sharp trot, his guards making the air re-echo with their merry shouts. These cries stimulated the ostrich to yet further exertions, but he was at length brought to a stand by the determined refusal of his four-footed companions to continue such a race across loose sand.

The boys having enjoyed the long run, I told them to walk with the prisoner slowly home, while Fritz and I returned to examine the ostrich's nest. The eggs were quite warm, and I was certain the mother had quite recently left. Leaving about half, I packed the rest in a large bag I had brought for the purpose and slung it carefully on the saddle before me.

We soon caught up our advance guard and, without other notable incident, reached our tent. Astonishment and dismay were depicted on the face of my wife as we approached.

"My dear husband," she exclaimed, "do you think our provisions so abundant that you must scour the deserts to find some great beast to assist us to devour them? You must discover an iron mine next, for iron is what ostriches chiefly live on, is it not?"

The heavy clay of my pottery had barely cooled when the sky turned a bruised purple, signaling the arrival of another winter. The rains came with terrifying speed, forcing us to abandon our daily excursions and retreat into the safety of our spacious home. Yet, even within our walls, surrounded by our varied tasks, a profound heaviness settled over us. The spirits of the family were low, and even the brief pauses in the storm, offering moments for rapid rides, failed to lift our gloom.

Fritz, sensing the weight of our depression, turned to me with a bright idea. "Father," he asked, "why should we not build a canoe? Something swifter and more manageable than our current vessels? I long for a light skiff to skim across the water." The notion thrilled us all, except for my wife. She, who harbored a deep fear of the sea, argued vehemently that our chances of drowning with our existing pinnace and canoe were already perilous enough. She refused to add another fragile craft to tempt us onto the treacherous waves.

However, my reassurances quickly calmed her fears. I promised that the vessel I intended to build would not be a flimsy shell, but a sturdy masterpiece, modeled after the Greenlander's *cajack*. I resolved to engage the children in the work, ensuring we produced a boat of exceptional strength. The boys were eager, and construction began immediately. We fashioned a skeleton of whalebone, reinforcing the sides and creating a continuous deck of split bamboo that spanned the entire length, leaving only a central square for the occupant to sit.

The project consumed our every thought until the rain finally ceased, revealing a brilliant sun. We completed the boat outside, fitting it through our front door. We covered the sides and deck with seal-skin, sealing every seam meticulously with rubber. The resulting *cajack* was a curious sight—light enough to be lifted by one hand—and when launched, it bounced upon the water like a giant rubber ball. Fritz was unanimously named its captain. Yet, before he could venture into the skiff, my wife insisted on a precaution: a swimming-dress. She reasoned that if the boat were punctured by a sharp rock or a fish's fin, the dress would ensure his survival.

Though I did not share her view that the boat was as fragile as a soap bubble, I gladly assisted in crafting the garment. It was a peculiar invention, resembling a double waistcoat made of linen treated with a rubber solution. The seams were coated with caoutchouc, rendering it airtight. We designed a small valve to inflate the space between the outer layer and the lining, allowing the suit to expand like a bladder.

Meanwhile, the island teemed with life. The seeds we had scattered were sprouting into magnificent crops, and the verandah was draped in fragrant creepers reaching for the pillars. The air buzzed with birds, and the earth vibrated with activity.

Soon, the dress was finished. One afternoon, Fritz offered to demonstrate its efficacy. Gathering on the beach, he solemnly donned the inflated suit and entered the water. Amidst his brothers' laughter, he paddled effortlessly across the bay toward Shark Island, where we followed in our boat. The experiment was a resounding success. Despite their amusement at his strange attire, Ernest, Jack, and Franz begged their mother to create similar suits for them.

Our visit to the antelope colony we had established the previous autumn revealed that the animals had thrived under our shelter. We left them handfuls of maize and salt before crossing to the other side of the island. The shoreline was strewn with beautiful shells and delicate corals, which the boys collected for their museum. Nearby, vast quantities of colorful seaweed washed ashore. My wife, recognizing its utility, directed the boys to gather it.

As we returned, my wife selected a specific type of seaweed with saw-toothed edges. Back home, she washed and dried these leaves in the oven. Her actions hinted at a mystery that piqued my curiosity.

"Are these leaves a substitute for tobacco?" I asked, noting her well-known dislike for the weed.

She smiled playfully. "Do you not think a mattress stuffed with these leaves would be wonderfully cool in summer?" Her twinkling eyes suggested the answer was more complex than a simple cooling agent.

Later, after a tiring expedition, the family rested on the verandah. My wife appeared bearing a porcelain dish filled with a translucent, glittering jelly.

"Ambrosia!" Fritz exclaimed upon tasting it. It was exquisite.

"Behold the produce of my mysterious seaweed," she laughed. "It is a far better substitute for tobacco than the nasty weed itself."

"My dear wife," I pressed, "where did you learn this? How did you conceive it?"

"While visiting my Dutch friends at the Cape," she explained, "I saw this dish and recognized the leaves from Shark Island. Once known, the process is simple: soak the leaves in fresh water for a week, then boil them with orange juice, citron, and sugar."

Delighted by the delicacy, we thanked her profusely. The boys immediately rushed to the island to gather more leaves. I accompanied them, eager to inspect our plantations. The palms and mangroves had grown marvelously, and seeds scattered in rock crevices were beginning to clothe the barren boulders. We also discovered a sparkling spring among the rocks, frequented by the antelopes.

Turning our attention to Whale Island, we found the rabbit population had exploded. Their greedy teeth had stripped the tender shoots of the trees, sparing only the coconut palms. To protect the remaining growth, I erected prickly thorn hedges around the palm stems. Noticing that the rabbits had also gnawed on some of the collected seaweed, I gathered samples to investigate later.

The bleached skeleton of a whale, purified by sun and rain, also caught my eye. I separated several vertebrae, intending to use them for a new invention: a crushing machine. Since stone was beyond my reach, I believed the whale bones could serve as the core mechanism.

The following morning, I sought a suitable tree for timber. However, finding the stable empty, I learned the boys had departed early with their guns and traps, leaving me the choice between a bull and a buffalo. Content with my horse, Storm, I set out. Upon reaching the cassava field, I found it devastated by mischievous intruders. Examining the tracks, which resembled those of pigs, I followed the trail.

The path led me past our old potato patch until Floss and Bruno's barking announced the discovery of the culprits. The entire family of our old sow stood at bay, snarling savagely. Raising my gun, I fired twice, bringing down two pigs while the rest fled. I retrieved the fallen animals and continued my journey.

I soon located a tree perfect for my purpose, marked it, and returned home. Ernest helped me flay the young porkers for supper, hoping the other boys would return soon. Late in the evening, the sound of trampling hooves echoed through the night. Jack thundered in on his two-legged steed, followed by Fritz and Franz carrying game-bags. They unloaded their haul: four birds, a kangaroo, twenty musk-rats, a monkey, two hares, and half a dozen beaver rats. Fritz also tossed down a bundle of thistles without explanation.

The boys were wild with excitement. Jack recounted the thrill of riding an ostrich, describing the terrifying rush of wind that nearly blinded him.

"You must make me a mask with glass eyes," he pleaded.

"No," I replied firmly. "First, I do not feel compelled to grant every request. Second, I believe you are capable of making it yourself. However, I congratulate you on your abundant catch; you must have worked hard."

I reminded them of the anxiety their long expeditions caused at home, urging them to check on the animals before supper. Once the boys returned, we enjoyed a hearty meal of roast pig and fragrant mead. Fritz detailed the day's hunt, explaining how they trapped musk-rats with carrots and beaver rats with fish bait, and how Jack had chased down the kangaroo. They also contributed cuttings from cinnamon and apple trees to the garden. Finally, Fritz presented the bundle of thistles, believing them to be used in wool manufacturing.

I recognized them instantly as the fuller's teazle, a plant whose sharp thorns raise the nap of cloth. We resolved to prepare the bounty early the next morning. Seeing the boys growing drowsy, I concluded the day with evening prayers.

The sheer volume of game required significant labor to skin. Determined to reduce this burden, I discovered a large syringe in the surgeon's chest. After modifying it with valves, I created a powerful air pump. The boys stared in amazement as I hung the kangaroo by its hind legs and inserted the syringe.

"Skin a kangaroo with a squirt?" they laughed.

Ignoring their jests, I made a small incision and forced air between the skin and the carcass. The hide distended, changing the animal's shape entirely. As I pumped more air, the skin separated completely from the flesh. With a few cuts to sever the remaining ligaments, the animal was flayed.

"What a splendid plan!" cried the boys. "But why does it work?"

"For a simple reason," I explained. "Animal skin is attached to the flesh by delicate fibers and tiny air chambers. By forcing air into these bladders, the fibers stretch and eventually crack, releasing the skin with ease. The Greenlanders use this method constantly when handling seals and walruses."

The remaining animals were treated similarly, and within two days, all the skins were removed and prepared.

Next, I summoned the boys to help procure wood for the crushing machine. We set out with axes and saws, reaching the selected tree. Fritz and Jack climbed to cut the high branches to prevent damage to neighbors. Then, Fritz and I attacked the trunk with a pit-saw, cutting it halfway through. Guiding the fall with ropes, we pulled with united strength until the trunk crashed down.

Over the next few days, we felled the tree and sawed it into blocks. On the third day, we transported four large and two small blocks home. Combining these with the whale vertebrae, I quickly completed the machine.

My attention to the grain fields had been minimal, so I was surprised when the poultry returned with full crops, heading toward our cornfield. Rushing there, I found the grain perfectly ripe. The harvest was so abundant that it startled my wife, who worried about the additional work of reaping and threshing alongside our existing tasks of fishing and pickling.

"Don't be downhearted, wife," I assured her. "Digging potatoes in this light soil is easier than in Switzerland, and we can leave the younger plants to grow. We will harvest in the Italian fashion; while not economical, it saves time and allows for two crops a year."

I leveled a large area of clayey ground, creating a threshing floor by rolling, beating, and stamping it until it was hard and smooth. We armed ourselves with reaping hooks and baskets, gathering the corn efficiently. The boys grasped the stalks without stooping, winding them around their hands and tossing them into the basket. This method left the field covered in tall, headless stubble, which horrified my wife.

"This is dreadful!" she cried. "You have wasted the straw!"

"It is not a bad plan," I replied. "Italians use this high stubble for cattle fodder, feeding them the leftover grain and grass. Now, for threshing in the same fashion."

We laid the sheaves in a circle. The boys mounted our horses—Storm, Grumble, Lightfoot, and Hurry—and trotted briskly around the circle, trampling and stamping out the grain. Dust and chaff flew in clouds. My wife and I used hayforks to move the sheaves, ensuring the horses treaded evenly. Occasionally, the animals took mouthfuls of the tempting food, a reward we deemed just, recalling the biblical command not to muzzle the ox.

After threshing, we winnowed the grain by throwing it high into the air during a strong breeze, letting the wind carry away the chaff while the grain fell to the ground. Our poultry visited the floor frequently, gobbling up the spilled grain. I allowed them to feast, reasoning that the loss in grain would be gained in meat, anticipating delicious chicken pies and roasted geese.

When our harvest stores were housed, we found we had reaped sixty, eighty, even a hundred-fold what we had sown. Our garner was truly filled. Anticipating a second harvest, we began mowing the stubble. Flocks of quails and partridges came to glean among the scattered ears. Though we caught few, we resolved to spread nets next season to capture them in larger numbers.

My wife was satisfied seeing the straw stacked. Our maize crop, unthreshed, provided soft leaves for mattress stuffing, while burning the stalks yielded rich alkaline ashes. I replanted the ground with rye, barley, and oats, hoping they would ripen before the rains returned.

Just as our agricultural work concluded, shoals of herring appeared. We pickled only two barrels, but we were less merciful to the seals that arrived shortly after. We hunted them vigorously, needing their skins for various purposes, particularly to finish the *cajack*. I planned to cover the deck of this tiny vessel with seal-skin to make it watertight, storing pistols, ammunition, water, and provisions inside. Harpoons fitted with seal bladders would hang alongside, ready for the next adventure.

The day finally arrived for Fritz's trial voyage in the cajack. Dressed in his swimming costume—trousers, jacket, and cap—he cowered comically in the canoe, puffing and blowing until he resembled the frog of the fable. All traces of his original figure vanished, drawing shouts of laughter from the family. Even his mother, the inventor of the outfit, could not resist smiling. I retrieved the larger boat so my wife and I could rush to his aid if necessary.

Launched from a convenient shelf, the cajack floated lightly on the sea-green ocean. Fritz practiced evolutions—darting with arrow-like speed, wheeling left and right—until he tipped onto his side. His mother shrieked in terror, but the craft neither capsized nor sank. Recovering his balance, he sped onward. Encouraged by our cheers, he boldly entered the strong current of Jackal River and was rapidly carried out to sea.

Seeing this exceeded my expectations, I immediately gave chase with Ernest and Jack, urged on by my wife who declared the "horrid soap-bubble" would surely meet an accident. We soon reached the rocks where the wreck had once lain, scanning for signs of the runaway. Soon, a faint puff of smoke and the crack of a pistol signaled his position. We fired a signal shot, answered by another, and heard Fritz's cheery halloo. The cajack darted from behind a point of land, and we quickly joined him.

"Come to this rocky beach," cried Fritz. "I have something to show you."

With blank amazement, we beheld a fine, well-grown young walrus, harpooned and dead. "Did you kill this creature, my dear Fritz?" I exclaimed, anxious to ensure no naked savage claimed the prize. "To be sure, father! Don't you see my harpoon?" Fritz replied. I laughed, admitting that success so speedy and unexpected for an amateur Greenlander took me by surprise. I congratulated him but scolded him for alarming us with such a long trip, leaving his mother in grievous trouble.

"I had no idea of passing out of sight," Fritz explained. "Once in the current, I was carried along. Then I came on a herd of walruses and chased them until I could harpoon this fine fellow. He swam slowly, and I struck him twice before he sought refuge among these rocks. I landed, but I took care to give him the contents of my pistol first, remembering the big serpent's parting fling at you, Jack."

"You ran a great risk," I warned. "Though inoffensive, a wounded walrus can become furious and destroy a whaleboat with its tusks. Thank God for your safety! Now, what shall we do with him? He must be fourteen feet long."

"I am glad you followed," Fritz said. "Our united strength won't move this weight from the rocks, but let me carry away the head with these grand snow-white tusks! I should like to fasten it on the prow of the cajack and name it the Sea-horse."

"We must carry away the beautiful ivory tusks," I agreed, noting the air felt excessively close and sultry, suggesting a brewing storm. "But the head!" cried Jack. "Think how splendid it will look!"

"And how splendid it will smell too when it begins to putrefy," added Ernest. "What a treat for the steersman!"

"Oh, we will prepare for that," Fritz assured them. "It shall be soaked, cleaned, and dried till it is as hard as a wooden model; it shall not offend your delicate nose, Ernest!"

Ernest remarked that walruses were peculiar to the Arctic regions. I corrected him, explaining they could wander from the Antarctic seas, noting the smaller dugong found in Africa which resembled a seal. While discussing this, we actively engaged in decapitating the walrus and cutting strips of its skin. Lacking proper implements, Fritz noted the cajack needed a hunting-knife, hatchet, and a small compass in a glass-topped box. I promised these additions.

Our work done, I proposed taking Fritz and the canoe aboard our boat, but yielded to his wish to return alone as an *avant-courier* to announce our approach to his mother. He skimmed away over the water while we followed at a slower rate. Black clouds gathered thick and fast, and a tremendous storm descended. Fritz was soon out of sight.

We buckled on swimming belts and lashed ourselves to the boat to avoid being washed overboard by towering seas. The horizon shrouded in darkness, gusts of wind lashed the ocean into foam, rain poured in torrents, and livid lightning glared through the gloom. My boys faced the danger nobly, though my alarm mingled with hope as the boat behaved well. The tempest swept on, yet the sky cleared suddenly, though the stormy waves continued to threaten our frail bark.

I never lost hope for ourselves, but my agonized heart was entirely for Fritz, whom I gave up for lost, praying for strength to accept Thy will. At last, we rounded the point and entered Safety Bay. Our overwhelming delight knew no bounds when we saw my wife with Fritz and his little boy on their knees, praying earnestly for our deliverance. Their approach was unperceived until our cries of joy attracted their notice.

A happy meeting ensued, and we gave thanks together for the mercy that spared our lives. Returning joyfully to Rockburg, we changed our drenched garments for warm dry clothes and sat at a comfortable meal to recount the perils of the storm.

Later, the walrus head was conveyed to our workshop. Undergoing a skilful process of cleaning, embalming, and drying, it was eventually fixed on the prow of the cajack, presenting an imposing appearance. The tanned strips of hide made valuable leather.

The recent storm had caused significant damage: floods injured crops, and the bridge over Jackal River was partly broken. Our time was occupied restoring order. One day, near the cascade, we found dark-red berries, the fruit of the clove. After the boys rejected them due to their taste, I decided to plant them in the nursery garden, providing an excellent spice to flavor rice in lieu of pepper.

With a good supply of clay from Falconhurst, I proposed building aqueducts to irrigate the fields, encouraged by the recent rain promoting corn growth. The fishing season was successful, yielding salmon, sturgeon, and herring. I rejected Jack's naive proposal to tether a shoal of salmon to the bay bottom.

Quiet days passed, and the boys, weary of farm work, longed for a cruise. Fritz suggested visiting Woodlands for hurdles and clay. It was settled that Fritz, Jack, and Franz would start together, while Ernest remained with us to assist in constructing a sugar-mill. Before departing, Fritz begged for bear's meat to make pemmican—a portable, nourishing food consisting of pounded bear or deer flesh. His mother consented to humor him, and within two days, a stock sufficient for a Polar expedition was fabricated.

Just as they were ready to start, I noticed Jack slipping a basket containing several pigeons under the cart packages. I chuckled, thinking the little fellow doubted the pemmican and preferred a tough old pigeon. The weather was exquisite, and with exhortations to caution, the three lads departed in high spirits. Storm and Grumble drew the cart, ridden by Fritz and Franz, while Hurry carried Jack across the bridge ahead, followed by Floss and Bruno.

The sugar-mill occupied us for several days. On the evening of the first day, resting in the porch, we wondered about the absentees. Ernest hinted he might have news. Suddenly, a bird alighted on the dove-cot. Ernest returned with a scrap of paper: "News, father! The very latest news by pigeon-post!"

I read the note from Fritz at Woodlands: "A brute of a hyaena has killed a ram and two lambs. The dogs seized it. Franz shot it. It is dead and skinned. The pemmican isn't worth much, but we are all right. Love to all."

"A true hunter's letter!" I laughed. Thus, the winged letter-carriers kept us informed of their adventures.

Approaching Woodlands, the boys were startled by human laughter. Oxen grew uneasy, dogs growled, and the ostrich bolted into the rice swamp. Through an opening in the thicket, Franz saw an enormous hyaena in a state of excitement, dancing round a killed lamb and uttering ghastly hysterical laughter. The beast ran backwards and forwards, rising on hind legs and whirling frantically. Franz kept his presence of mind, waiting until the hyaena calmed down to tear its prey, then fired both barrels, breaking its foreleg and wounding its breast.

Meanwhile, Fritz unyoked the oxen and secured them to trees. The dogs and the dying hyaena engaged in mortal strife; the beast severely wounded both Floss and Bruno before succumbing. The boys raised a shout of triumph, guiding Jack to the scene. Their first care was for the dogs, whose wounds they dressed before examining the hyaena. It was as large as a wild boar, with stiff bristles forming a mane, grey color marked with black, and teeth of extraordinary strength. Without its wounds, it would have been a match for the dogs.

After unloading the cart, they returned for the carcase of the tiger-wolf (hyaena), skinning it during the remainder of the day. Dispatching the carrier-pigeon to Rockburg, they retired to rest on their bearskin rugs.

The following day, they surveyed the shores of Wood Lake, marking practicable crossings with tall bamboo poles. They captured three young black swans after considerable resistance from the old ones. A beautiful heron thrust his neck from the reeds, only to be captured by Fritz's eagle, whose legs and wings were gently but firmly bound.

Suddenly, a large powerful animal came puffing with a curious whistling sound through the reeds, discomposing them before vanishing. It must have been a tapir, dark brown, resembling a young rhinoceros but with a trunk-like upper lip. Gentle but fierce when attacked, it can wound dogs dangerously with its powerful teeth. Fritz followed its direction in the cajack but saw no more of it.

The other boys returned via the rice-fields, where they caught a flock of cranes, including two demoiselle cranes. Using arrows with loops of cord dipped in birdlime, they brought the birds down uninjured. The young hunters lived comfortably on peccary ham, cassava bread, fruit, baked potatoes, and milk. One trial of the pemmican was sufficient; it was handed over to the dogs. Fritz, however, determined to attempt its manufacture again, knowing its value when properly prepared.

Collecting rice and cotton, they traveled to Prospect Hill. As Fritz later described, they entered a pine wood possessed by troops of monkeys who howled, chattered, and pelted them with pine cones. Becoming unbearable, they fired shots, causing several to die and the rest to flee. They continued to Prospect Hill only to discover the havoc the apes had made, ruining the cottage just as they had last summer. Preferring the tent, Fritz devised a plan using poisonous gum of the euphorbia mixed with milk and millet in coconut shells, hung high in the trees.

That evening, as the sea murmured and the moon shed beauty over the landscape, the spell was broken by an outburst of hideous noises. Every beast of the forest arose, snorting, snarling, and shrieking. Jackals howled, answered by Fangs and his friends; distant sounds reminded one of hippos, while deep roars made hearts quail with the conviction of hearing lions and elephants. Overawed, they retired to rest, but the cries of suffering apes prevented sleep until morning. Rising late, they looked upon the awful spectacle of dead monkeys and baboons strewn under the trees. They buried some deep in the earth and carried the rest to the shore, pitching them over the rocks into the sea. That day, they traveled on to the Gap.

The same evening a messenger-pigeon arrived at Rockburg with a note concluding: "The barricade at the Gap is broken down. Everything laid waste as far as the sugar-brake... Come to us, father—we are safe, but feel we are no match for this unknown danger."

I lost no instant, saddling Swift late in the day to ride to their assistance, while Ernest prepared the small cart for my wife and me to follow at daybreak. The bright moonlight favored my journey, and my arrival surprised and delighted the boys. Early the next morning, I inspected the footprints and ravages. The cane-brake had undoubtedly been visited by an elephant, which alone could leave such traces. Thick posts were snapped like reeds, and trees stripped of leaves. The worst mischief was done among the young sugar-cane plants, which were devoured or trampled.

It seemed not one, but a troop of elephants had invaded our grounds. The tracks were numerous and varied in size, but they could be traced back to the Gap in equal numbers. We did not suppose the animals remained hidden; rather, after this freebooting incursion, they had withdrawn to their native wilds. We hoped to oblige them to stay by greatly increasing the strength of our ramparts.

During the night of my arrival, sitting by an enormous watch-fire, I chatted with my boys, hearing details of their adventures. My wife and Ernest arrived the next day, rejoicing to find all well, making light of the trampled fields since her sons were sound in life and limb.

A systematic scheme of defense was elaborated, and the erection of the barricade occupied us for at least a month, designed to be firm and durable. Since our tent was unsuited for long residence, I adopted Fritz's idea of a Kamschatkan dwelling. Ten years have passed, a decade chronicled in the pages of my journal, a tale told as the ancient proverb suggests. Time slipped away imperceptibly, year by year, until the shadows of the past were dispelled by a profound gratitude for the welfare of my beloved family. I rejoiced to see our sons mature into men, strengthened by early training for lives of usefulness wherever their lot might fall. My greatest wish is that young readers learn from this record how admirably suited a peaceful, industrious, and pious life of a united family is to forming strong, pure, and manly characters. None take a better place in the great national family than those who emerge from such homes to fulfill new duties.

Having detailed our residence in New Switzerland, I need not exhaust your patience with monotonous accounts of hunting expeditions or clumsy inventions. Yet, before concluding, I must share the state of our dominion. Rockburg and Falconhurst had become our winter and summer headquarters, transformed by fountains, trellised verandas, and plantations that turned a previously distasteful residence into a paradise. Flowering creepers draped the balconies, and native and European trees grew luxuriantly in groves. In the distance, Shark Island, now clothed in graceful palms, guarded the entrance to Safety Bay, its battery and flagstaff prominent against the sky.

The swamp, once a bog, was now a considerable lake, teeming with waterfowl. Stately black swans, snow-white geese, and colorful ducks sailed its blue waters, while glimpses of the brilliant Sultan, crimson flamingos, and crested herons appeared among the rushes. Beneath the trees, old Hurry the ostrich marched with dignified pace, and every variety of pigeon nested in the rocks, their soft cooing filling the air. The rabbit-warren on Shark Island supplied us with food and fur, while antelopes grazed in the shady groves alongside them. Our farm at Woodlands flourished, and my wife's dairy was a constant source of activity.

Our sons retained their old habit of naming animals. Fritz had a creamy-white cow named Blanche and a bull with a tremendous voice called Stentor. Jack possessed a descendant of his old favorite jackal, Fangs, whom he named Coco, insisting that no word could be distinguished at a distance without an 'o' in it—a theory he illustrated until our ears were deafened. All of us enjoyed excellent health, though my wife occasionally suffered from slight fevers and the boys met with minor accidents. They were fine, handsome fellows: Fritz, twenty-four, strong and high-spirited; Ernest, tall and studious, having overcome his early faults; Jack, agile and graceful; and Franz, witty and shrewd. Despite our seclusion, I maintained the hope that we would one day be restored to society, yet the approaching old age of my wife and myself sometimes filled me with gloomy thoughts of their future if we were left alone. On such occasions, I prayed to the Almighty, finding renewed strength and hope.

One day, Fritz was absent for a whole day. Only when evening fell did we notice his cajack was gone. Anxious, I joined Ernest and Jack on Shark Island to watch for him from the tower, hoisting our signal flag and loading a gun. Finally, we discerned a small black speck in the distance. As he drew near, we saw his skiff towing a large sack and heavily laden.

"Welcome, Fritz!" I cried. "You seem to have quite a cargo!"

"Yes, and my trip has led to discoveries as well as booty," he answered. "Come, let's carry up the things, and while I rest, I will relate my adventures."

Assembled around him, Fritz explained that he had long desired to explore the coast beyond the point where he had killed the walrus. He had prepared his skiff with provisions, weapons, and his eagle, Pounce. Leaving the harbor unperceived, he had rounded the point and passed over the wreckage of our old ship, seeing the guns and iron strewn on the rocky bottom. He navigated past rugged cliffs inhabited by sea-fowl, seals, and walruses, eventually reaching a high cape with a magnificent archway leading to a vast cavern.

Inside, he found thousands of nests belonging to a small species of swallow, the esculent swallow. The nests were unique, resembling sponges or fungi, and he had brought them home in his net. I informed him that these edible birds' nests were highly valued in China, worth their weight in silver, and used to make strengthening soups. My wife, though hesitant, agreed to try them for curiosity's sake.

Continuing his journey, Fritz entered a lonely bay, Pearl Bay, sheltered by reefs and sandbanks. Here, he discovered beds of giant oysters attached to the rocks. Upon examining them, he found they contained pearly balls of varying sizes—treasure indeed, valuable should we ever contact the civilized world. He also encountered a cloud of sea-fowl, including a mighty albatross. In a moment of frustration, he struck the bird, which fell stunned.

However, a remarkable sequel awaited him. Upon raising the albatross to his canoe, he discovered a piece of rag wound around its leg bearing English words: "Save an unfortunate Englishwoman from the smoking rock!" This message sent a thrill through his nerves. He wrote a reply, "Do not despair! Help is near!", bound it to the bird's leg, and watched as the creature revived and flew westward. Fritz confided this discovery to me alone, fearing to unsettle the others if the message were old or the "smoking rock" a volcano. We decided he should search for the writer, but first, he needed to alter his canoe to carry two persons and provisions for a long absence.

With preparations underway, an excursion to Pearl Bay became the focus of our attention. I formally announced to the family that Fritz, now of age, was to act according to his own judgment regarding voyages, trusting in his prudence and affection. His brothers assumed he intended to take one of them as a shipmate, a mistake Fritz allowed to continue.

We departed on the yacht, accompanied by the dogs, with Jack proudly piloting the canoe alongside Fritz. We passed through the rocks near Walrus Island into calm waters, where we spotted the fairy-like shells of the paper-nautilus, securing several for our museum and naming the location Nautilus Creek. Further on, we rounded a promontory we named Cape Pug-Nose and approached the majestic cliffs of a headland. Fritz guided us through a natural archway, resembling a Gothic cathedral, which we named Cape Minster. Inside, we harvested more swallow nests, leaving those containing eggs or young.

Emerging into the sunlight of Pearl Bay, we surveyed the fertile meadows and shady groves, seeking a landing place near the oyster-beds. We found a spot close to a sparkling streamlet, burned a watch-fire, and spent the night anchored within gunshot of the land. Our lively little ape, Mercury, occupied a berth on deck, ready to alert us to any danger.

The following morning, we worked diligently at the oyster-beds, building a massive pile of shells on the beach to be covered with seaweed for later processing into alkali. We also hunted in the neighborhood, keeping ourselves supplied with game. On the last day of our fishery, intending to make a longer excursion into the woods, Ernest set off first with Floss, followed by Jack and Coco. Fritz and I were loading tools when we heard a shot, a cry of pain, and another shot.

The dogs rushed toward the disturbance, and Fritz, calling Pounce from his perch, darted off. Before I could reach the scene, shouts of victory echoed, and then appeared the disconsolate figure of Jack, hobbling along, supported by his brothers. He moaned, "I'm pounded like a half-crushed pepper-corn!"

Upon examination, I found severe bruises. Ernest explained that a huge wild boar with fierce eyes and monstrous tusks had broken through the bushes. Floss had chased it, causing the boar to turn to bay. Jack and Coco had attacked from the rear, but the jackal was sent sprawling, provoking the boar to turn its fury on Jack. As Jack fled, Ernest attempted to check the boar but only slightly wounded it. Jack stumbled over a tree root just as the animal caught him. Had the other dogs not arrived to tackle the boar, it would have been a tragedy. Pounce had dashed upon the beast, and Fritz finished it with a pistol shot.

While helping Jack, I noticed curious knotty roots the boar had been grubbing. I identified them as truffles, excellent and tender, unlike the tough ones from Europe. Fritz tasted them and agreed they were delicious.

Later that evening, as we prepared to retire, a deep, fearful sound echoed through the woods. It made our blood curdle. We heard the dread voice roar again, nearer, and an answer peal from the distance. Fritz springing to his feet, ordered the fire blazed and the guns ready, declaring he would reconnoiter in the canoe.

We obeyed, arming ourselves with cutlasses and loading the guns. Suddenly, the whole pack of dogs, Coco, and Mercury galloped up to the fire. Mercury was discomposed, chattering in fear at the water. The dogs planted themselves by the fire, gazing landward. I concluded that leopards or panthers had been attracted by the scent of the boar's carcase.

Soon, a large powerful animal sprang from the underwood, muttering a roar, and approached the fire. It was unmistakably a lion, far surpassing any I had seen in Europe. The dogs slunk behind the fire as the lion sat almost like a cat, glaring alternately at them and the hanging hams. He walked up and down, occasionally uttering short, angry roars, and drank from the brook with such haste that I expected him to spring at any moment.

Gradually, his manner became more threatening. He crouched, stretched his body, waved his tail, and glared furiously. I was in doubt whether to fire or retreat when the sharp crack of a rifle rang through the darkness.

"That is Fritz!" everyone exclaimed. With a fearful roar, the lion sprang to his feet, stood stock still, tottered, sank on his knees, rolled over, and lay motionless.

"We are saved!" I cried. "That was a masterly shot. Stay on board, boys. I must join my brave Fritz."

I landed quickly; the dogs met me with pleasure but whined uneasily, looking toward the darkness. Seeing nothing of Fritz, I lingered by the boat when suddenly a lioness bounded from the shadow of the trees. She paused, startled by the fire, then circled the illuminated area, uttering roarings that were clearly calls to her mate. Finding him motionless, her manner betokened great concern. She touched him with her forepaws, smelled him, and licked his bleeding wounds. Then, raising her head, she gnashed her teeth and gave forth a lamentable sound, a mingled roar and howl of grief, rage, and a vow of revenge.

Crack! Another shot lamed her right forepaw. The dogs, seeing me raise my gun, gathered courage and ran forward just as I fired. My shot wounded the lioness, but not mortally, and a terrific combat ensued. It was impossible to fire again for fear of hitting the dogs. The scene was fearful beyond description: the fitful blaze of the fire shed a strange light on the prostrate body of the huge dead lion and the wounded lioness fighting desperately against the attack of the four gallant dogs. Their cries and roars were enough to try the stoutest nerves.

Old Juno, staunch to the last, was foremost in the fray. After a time, she changed her plan and springed at the throat of the lioness. In an instant, the cruel claws laid open the body of the dog, destroying the life of the true and faithful companion of so many years.

Just then, Fritz appeared. The lioness was much weakened, and we ventured near enough to fire with safety. Finally, I dispatched her by plunging a hunting-knife deep in her breast.

Ernest and Jack were summoned from the yacht to witness the completed victory. I regretted having left them on board, seeing how greatly the noise and tumult had alarmed them. They hastened toward us in great agitation, their joy on seeing us safe only equalled by their grief on learning of Juno's death.

The night was far advanced, and the fire burnt low. We piled on more wood and, by the renewed light, drew poor Juno from between the paws of the lioness. By the brookside, we washed and bound up the torn body, wrapping it carefully in canvas and carrying it aboard the yacht to be buried at Rockburg the following day.

Wearied and sorrowful, but full of thankfulness for our personal safety, we lay down to sleep with all the dogs aboard.

Next morning, before quitting Pearl Bay, we once more landed to possess ourselves of the magnificent skins of the lion and lioness, whose visit had caused such a commotion. We left the flayed carcasses to the tender mercies of the birds of prey.

"Homeward bound!" sang out the boys as they cheerily weighed anchor. I could see, though he did not complain, that poor Jack had not yet recovered from the boar's rough treatment and moved very stiffly.

"You must pilot us through the channel in the reef this time, Fritz," I said, adding in a lower tone, "and then is it to be 'farewell', my son!"

"Yes, dear father—Au revoir!" returned he, brightly with a glance full of meaning, while he threw into his canoe a cushion and fur cloak.

"Thanks, Fritz! But I'm going to honour them with the care of my battered bones in the yacht here. You are awfully considerate though, old fellow," remarked Jack, not for a moment doubting that his brother expected him to return beside him in the cajack.

Five days passed, yet Fritz remained absent. My anxiety grew until I resolved to follow him. Everyone cheered the plan, and even my wife, eager to sail in the pinnace, agreed to accompany us. On a bright morning with a favorable breeze, we five, accompanied by the dogs, boarded the boat and raced toward Cape Minster. Our little yacht bounded gaily over the water, the sunshine and sea breeze lifting our spirits to the highest pitch. As we approached the archway, I spotted a dark, shadowy mass just beneath the surface. "A sunken rock," I thought, "yet strange that I never noticed it before." I dropped the helm instantly, but a shock seemed inevitable; we surged forward, and then—danger passed! Glancing astern, I saw the rock had vanished, its green shadow replaced by clear water.

Before we could recover, Jack shouted, "There is another, father, to starboard!" Indeed, another submerged rock lay ahead. "The rock is moving!" cried Franz. Suddenly, a great black body erupted from the sea, spouting a column of water that rained down around us. The mystery solved itself: it was a cachalot whale. Enraged by our earlier scratchings, the monster retreated briefly before meditating a rush upon us. Fearful tales of this savage beast destroying ships filled my mind, and in desperation, I sprang to one gun while Jack leaped to the other. Simultaneously, we fired. Both shots took effect; the whale lashed the water violently before plunging beneath the surface.

We kept a sharp lookout, unwilling to lose such a valuable prize. Reloading, we stood toward the shore where he was heading. Soon, we sighted him again in shallow water, lashing his tail and dyeing the waves with blood. Daring to approach as closely as safety allowed, we fired again. The whale's struggles became frantic, then, with a final quiver from head to tail, he lay motionless—dead.

The boys were about to cry out in victory when a shout halted them. Behind a rock, they saw a canoe paddled by a tall, muscular savage standing up to examine us. Determined not to be easily taken, we loaded our guns and readied them.

After describing our whale hunt, I asked if he knew a suitable anchorage. "Certainly," he replied, casting a meaningful glance. Now confident of Fritz's success, I explained his project to my wife, preparing her for the surprise awaiting her. She was greatly startled, nearly overcome with emotion at the prospect of meeting a human being, especially one of her own sex. "Why did you not tell me sooner?" she asked. "I was unwilling," I replied, "to raise hopes that might never be realized. But now, thank Heaven, he has succeeded."

The boys could not fathom the suppressed excitement shared by my wife, Fritz, and me. Curious glances were cast toward the island. As soon as the sails were furled and the anchor dropped, they sprang ashore. In silence, we followed Fritz through the thicket until we emerged to see a hut of sheltering boughs with a cheerful fire burning at its entrance. Fritz dived inside, leaving his brothers outside, mute with astonishment. Moments later, he emerged leading by the hand a slight, handsome youth dressed as a young English naval officer. The pair advanced to meet us, and Fritz, radiant with joy, introduced his companion as Edward Montrose.

"And," he continued, looking at my wife and me, "will you not welcome him as a friend and brother to our family circle?" "That will we, indeed!" I exclaimed, extending my hands. "Our wild life may have roughened our looks, but it has not hardened our hearts." My wife embraced the young stranger heartily. The lads and even the dogs testified to their delight; the former thrilled at a new companion, the latter charmed by her sweet voice and appearance.

From Fritz's expressions, I perceived that the girl wished her sex to remain unrevealed until my wife could provide a more suitable costume. The young men ran down to the yacht to fetch supper supplies and prepare a camp for the night. Once done, my wife set before us a substantial meal. The boys, eager to make their new acquaintance feel at home, did their best to amuse her. Soon, the initial strangeness wore off, and she laughed and chatted as gaily as any of us. She admired our dishes, tasted our mead, and kept up a lively conversation without alluding to her past.

Meeting any human after years of isolation excited the boys greatly, but finding someone so handsome, so gay, and so charming completely fumed their heads. When I signaled the end of the feast, and our new friend was about to be led to the night-quarters prepared for her on the yacht, the health of Edward Montrose was proposed and drunk in fragrant mead amidst cheers and acclamations.

Once she was gone and silence restored, Jack exclaimed, "Now, Fritz, tell me where you found this jolly fellow. Did you seek him out, or meet him by chance? Out with your adventures while we sit by the fire." Casting more wood on the blaze, Jack settled down to listen.

Fritz hesitated briefly before beginning. "Perhaps you remember how, upon returning from my expedition in the cajack, I struck down an albatross? Few knew what became of the wounded bird, yet it was that albatross who brought me notice of the shipwrecked stranger. I determined he should carry back a message to cheer the sender."

He recounted how he prepared the cajack for two passengers, left us with Pounce seated before him, and paddled into the open sea. Hours later, keeping near shore for potential shelter, he found himself in a quiet cove just as the sea turned to foam and great surging waves arose. He spent the night in his cajack and next morning, after a frugal meal of pemmican, ventured forth again. The wind had subsided, and he paddled on, scanning every direction for smoke or signs of life.

As he neared the shore, the landscape changed: sandy shores gave way to dense forests where he heard the roars of beasts, the yells of apes, the hyena's laugh, and the death cries of deer. Alone in his frail canoe, he rested his paddle, contemplating the solitude. Near the shore, he observed strange-looking birds—toucans—with enormous, colorful beaks that seemed heavy enough to cause perpetual headaches. Amused by their antics, he forgot to capture a specimen.

Paddling on for hours, he passed mouths of streams and rivers but felt compelled to press onward to his destination. Night fell, and he moored his cajack in a sheltered cove, stepping ashore to stretch his legs. Too tired to sleep on land, he returned to his boat after supper. Next morning, Pounce and he landed for breakfast. As he roasted a young parrot, he heard a rustle in the grass and turned to see an immense tiger glaring at him, tail swaying menacingly.

In another moment, the spring would have been made. My gun lay nearby; had he stooped to pick it up, the monster would have seized him. Pounce saw the danger and darted upon the tiger, blinding it with flapping wings and fierce beak blows. This check gave Fritz time to recover his composure, seize his gun, and fire. The brute, pierced to the heart, gave one spring and rolled over dead. But beside him lay poor Pounce, crushed and lifeless. One blow from the great beast's paw had ended the faithful bird's life forever.

Fritz's voice shook as he described burying the bird and fleeing the scene. Gloomy thoughts overwhelmed him; without his companion, he feared he could not continue. Doubts plagued him: had the albatross flown far? Was the stranger on different shores? Was he drifting away from the signal? But soon, a sight banished all fears. A high point of land appeared, revealing a calm bay with a reef running from its shores. Rising from the reef was a column of smoke curling steadily upward.

Straining every nerve, he reached the rock where the beacon burned, seeing no human sign. About to shout, he saw a slight figure moving along the chain of rocks. Mastering his emotion, he called out in English, "Welcome, fair stranger! God, in His mercy, has heard your call, and has sent me to your aid!"

"Miss Montrose came quickly forward—" Fritz paused. "Who? What?" shouted the boys. "Who came forward?" Amid the hubbub, Ernest rose calmly. "I did not like to remark until you revealed the secret, Fritz, but we need no longer pretend not to see through Edward Montrose's disguise."

Though disconcerted, Fritz recovered his composure, bearing the jokes with equanimity before joining in three cheers for their new sister. Once the laughter subsided, he continued. "Miss Montrose grasped my hands warmly. Guessing my lack of daily English practice, she spoke in French: 'Long, long have I waited since the bird returned with your message. Thank God, you have come at last!'"

Tears of joy and gratitude filled her eyes as she led him to the shore, where she had built a hut and safe sleeping-place among tree branches—a miniature Falconhurst. Delighted by her ingenuity, Fritz noted the bows, arrows, lances, and bird-snares hanging round the walls, and the work-table displaying fish-hooks of mother-of-pearl, needles from fishbones, and bodkins from bird beaks. These, along with a chest of clothing, were almost the only things washed ashore after her wreck three years prior.

Marveling at her ability to overcome obstacles that would appall most women, he described her hut: stout posts driven into the ground, cross-pieces of bamboo forming a framework, walls woven with reeds, a thatched roof, and clay plastering with an open space for a chimney. As they entered, a cormorant flew from under the table, angry at his intrusion. Miss Montrose called it off, explaining she had tamed it soon after landing. Now a pleasant companion, it brought her food of every description—diving for fish, striking birds on the wing, or seizing rabbits on land.

Before darkness fell, she displayed all her curiosities: the kitchen-stove, cooking utensils, skin bottles, shell plates, spoons, the fishing raft, and countless other inventions. Sitting down to a most appetizing meal, she gave a short account of her life. Jenny Montrose, daughter of a British officer who served in India, lost her mother at age three. Under her father's care, she mastered accomplishments suited to her sex and developed an ardent love for field sports. By seventeen, she was as at home on horseback as in her drawing-room.

When Colonel Montrose received orders to return home, he arranged passage for Jenny on a separate vessel. The separation was painful, but unavoidable. She sailed in the Dorcas for England. A week after leaving Calcutta, a storm drove the vessel off course.

The long, grey evenings of our captivity gave way to a season of golden restoration. As the relentless rain ceased and the sun once more warmed the face of nature, the heavy stone walls of our prison seemed to dissolve. We stepped forth into the balmy breath of spring, disbelief mingling with joy at the return of life. The island, once a silent tomb, erupted into activity. The lads threw themselves into the fields and orchards, while their mother and sister found endless purpose tending the poultry yard and household.

Our scattered settlements—Falconhurst, Woodlands, Prospect Hill, and the distant islands of Shark and Whale—demanded our attention. Jack and Franz were assigned the defense of the island battery. They spent the day reinforcing the flagstaff, hoisting the colors, and polishing the cannons until they gleamed. By evening, as the rest of us strolled along the beach, the boys completed their labor. They loaded their guns, paddled their canoe out to the open sea to serve as a target, and fired. With a triumphant shout, they watched the barrel shatter into pieces, proving the weapons were ready for war, before running them back to the fort.

Scarcely had the echoes faded when a new sound cut through the air: three distinct booms rolling across the water from the west. We froze, speechless. Was it a hallucination? A trick of the wind? Or the signal of a stranger? Before we could debate, Jack and Franz leaped into their canoe and paddled frantically toward us. They had heard it too.

A tempest of emotions seized us—fear, hope, anxiety, and ecstatic anticipation. Was it a European vessel, a link to civilization? Or a Malay pirate bent on destruction? Who could foresee the outcome?

Before we could voice our fears, the canoe touched shore.

"Did you hear them?" Jack gasped, his face pale with urgency. "What shall we do?"

"It must be a European ship," my youngest son, Fritz, declared, seizing his brother's hands in an embrace of pure joy. "We shall find our Fatherland again!"

I saw then how deeply the longing for civilized life had taken root in their hearts, awakened by the presence of their European sister. All eyes turned to me for counsel.

"At present, we can do nothing," I advised calmly, though my heart raced. "Night is falling. We must prepare and pray."

Excitement kept us awake until late. We took turns watching from the verandah, guarding against further signals or hostile visits. But around midnight, the sky darkened, and a hurricane descended with terrifying fury. The sea rose in monstrous waves, rendering any boat useless. If a ship had fired upon us during the storm, the roar of the blast would have drowned the sound.

For two days and nights the gale raged. On the third, the sun broke through, and the sea began to recede. Driven by anxiety, I joined Jack and Franz at the fort to fire the warning guns once more. One, two—the report rang out, and moments later, an answering boom rolled from the distance. Doubt vanished; the strangers were still there, aware of our presence.

We waved the flag as a sign of peace and returned to the house, where the family awaited in a frenzy of questions. I tried to calm them, knowing that the arrival of a ship did not guarantee salvation.

Fritz and I immediately embarked on a reconnaissance. Armed with guns, pistols, cutlasses, and a spyglass, we paddled out of the bay, circling the high cliffs to the left. For an hour we scanned the horizon, seeing only frowning rocks and restless surf. Finally, we rounded Cape Pug-Nose.

In that instant, our doubts dissolved. Beyond the cape, nestled in a sheltered cove, lay a brig-of-war with English colors flying proudly. Her sails were furled, her anchor dropped. Through the glass, I saw figures on deck and tents pitched on the shore, smoke rising from fires.

A sudden misgiving struck me. "What business does an English vessel have so far from the usual shipping lanes?" I wondered, recalling tales of mutinous crews seeking refuge to launch raids.

"I see the captain!" Fritz exclaimed, pointing through the glass. "He is speaking to an officer. His face is unmistakably English, and the flag confirms it!"

I peered closer, hiding behind the cliff's shadow. The ship was immaculate; the decks were spotless, the brass burnished, the discipline evident in every movement. My fears evaporated. The camp on shore was orderly and peaceful. Satisfied that the ship would not depart for days, we decided to return unseen, unwilling to approach strangers until we could present ourselves with dignity.

Back at Rockburg, the family listened to our report with approval. Even Jenny, whose heart soared at the prospect of meeting countrymen, agreed to wait until the following day. That day, we transformed our small yacht. We scrubbed the decks, polished the guns, cleared away clutter, and hoisted the English flag to the peak. My wife oversaw the wardrobe, selecting the finest uniforms for us and the boys. Though we were accustomed to rough work, we knew that to impress a naval officer, we must dress as gentlemen.

At dawn, the eventful morning arrived. We ate breakfast hurriedly, in silence, our hearts too full for words. Fritz and Jack brought baskets of fresh, fragrant fruit as gifts. Then, with the anchor weighed and sails set, our little vessel bounded over the waters of Safety Bay, giving a wide berth to the foaming reef, and headed straight for the cove.

As we rounded Cape Pug-Nose, the strangers on the brig and the shore gazed in utter amazement. A pleasure yacht, manned by such an unusual party, cruising this inhospitable shore? It was an anomaly they had never imagined.

Fritz and I stepped into our boat and pulled alongside the brig. Within a minute, we were on deck. The captain, a man of simple frankness, welcomed us cordially and led us to his cabin.

"You owe this visit to good fortune," he said, asking how residents of a coast deemed uninhabited had come to his aid.

I recounted our shipwreck, our years of struggle, and the providential rescue of Miss Montrose.

"Then let me thank you in my own name and that of Colonel Montrose," the officer said, gripping Fritz's hand. "I sailed these shores specifically to find traces of that brave girl. The disappearance of the *Dorcas* has been a terrible blow to her father. Though no word has reached England in three years, he never lost hope. A few weeks ago, I found three survivors who claimed their boat was the only one to reach land safely. From them, I learned everything. I applied for permission to cruise these latitudes, and my efforts have been rewarded."

Fritz accepted the praise modestly. The captain then requested to meet my wife and Miss Montrose. He sent a boat for the rest of our party, and soon the mother, Jenny, and the three boys were aboard.

Our host treated us with warmth, and the officers vied to show us honor. At luncheon, the captain mentioned an invalid gentleman, Mr. Wolston, and his family, who had sailed with him from Sydney. The sea voyage had not improved Mr. Wolston's health, and the storm that drove the *Unicorn* into our bay for repairs had left him weak. He was eager to rest on land.

We were anxious to meet them. In the afternoon, we visited their camp under the shady trees. Mr. Wolston and his family were delighted to see us. The evening was so enjoyable that we stayed ashore, sleeping in tents provided by the captain.

That night, I had a serious discussion with my wife. Should we return to Europe simply because the opportunity arose? It would be childish. Gradually, we realized neither of us harbored a strong desire to leave. Our true wish was to adopt New Switzerland as our permanent home.

"What can be more delightful than harmony of opinion in those we love?" I reflected. My wife expressed her desire to spend her remaining days here, provided I and at least two of my sons remained. She was willing to let the others return to Europe, with the understanding that they would help establish a prosperous colony, and that the island should retain the name of our native Switzerland, even if populated by English colonists as well.

I approved of this vision and suggested we consult Captain Littlestone about placing the island under British protection. Next came the question of which sons should stay and which should go. We left it undecided, trusting their judgment.

The following morning, Captain Littlestone agreed to bring his ship to Safety Bay so we could receive them at Rockburg. We invited Mr. Wolston and his family to stay with us, hoping the change of air would benefit his health.

When the guests arrived, rounding the Rocky Cape to behold the beauty of Rockburg, their astonishment was profound. Their amazement grew as eleven guns boomed from the battery on Shark Island, saluting the royal standard of England floating majestically in the breeze.

A glow of pleasure beamed on every face. Mr. Wolston's spirits revived at the sight of such peace and happiness. He was carried ashore with great care and settled comfortably in my room, with a camp-bed arranged for his wife nearby.

Meanwhile, the harbor and Rockburg buzzed with excitement. Guests explored our domain, marveling at our achievements, until a summons to dinner barely registered. When we finally gathered for supper, Mr. Wolston was able to join us, his health seemingly renewed by the hope of staying among us.

He expressed a distinct wish to settle permanently on the island, proposing that he and his wife, along with his eldest daughter, make a long stay. The younger daughter would remain at the Cape of Good Hope, with the option for her brother to join us later.

"With sincere satisfaction," I replied, "I welcome this proposal. It is my wish and that of my wife to remain here for the rest of our days in New Switzerland."

"Hurrah for New Switzerland! New Switzerland forever!" the company shouted, raising their glasses in a musical ring of unanimous joy.

"Prosperity to New Switzerland; long may she flourish," echoed around the table.

"Long life and happiness to those who make New Switzerland their home!" Ernest added, leaning forward to clink his glass with mine, my wife's, and Mr. Wolston's.

"Will someone wish long life to those who go away?" Jenny asked with an arch smile. "Though I long to return to England and my father, my inclination wavers if all the cheers are for New Switzerland!"

"Three cheers for England and Colonel Montrose!" Fritz cried. "Success and happiness to us who return to Europe!" As the vaulted roofs rang with cheering, Jenny's glance showed him how much she appreciated his acknowledgment of her wish to reunite with her father.

"Well," I said when silence returned, "since Fritz resolves to go to England, he must undertake the duty of bringing happiness to a mourning father by restoring to him this dear daughter, whom I have been ready to regard as my own."

"Ernest chooses to remain with me," I continued. "His mother and I rejoice in this decision and promise him the highest scientific appointments in our power."

"And now, what is Jack's choice?" I asked. "The only talent I can say he possesses is that of a comic actor, and to shine on the stage, he must go to Europe."

"Jack is not going to Europe," he replied with a laugh. "He means to stay here. When Fritz is gone, he will be the best rider and the best shot in New Switzerland, which is the summit of his ambition."

"The fact is," Jack continued, "I rather stand in awe of their European schools. I should expect to find myself caught and clapped into one if I ventured too near them."

"A good school is exactly what I want," said Franz. "Among students, there is emulation and enthusiasm, and I shall have a chance of rising in the world."

"Fritz will probably return here someday," Franz added. "But it might be well for one member of the family to go home with the intention of remaining there altogether. As I am the youngest, I could more easily adapt myself to a different life. My father, however, will decide for me."

"You may go, my dear son," I replied. "God bless all our plans and resolutions. The whole earth is the Lord's, and where, in His sight, you lead good and useful lives, there is your home."

"Now that I know your wishes, the only question remains: will Captain Littlestone kindly enable you to carry them out?"

All eyes turned to the officer. After a brief pause, he spoke:

"My way in this matter is perfectly clear. I consider I have been providentially guided to place this family once more in communication with their friends and the civilized world. My orders were to search for a shipwrecked crew. Survivors from two wrecks have been discovered. Three passengers express a wish to leave my ship here, instead of at the Cape, while, at the same time, I am requested to give three persons a passage to England. Could anything suit better? I am most willing to undertake the charge of those committed to my care. Every circumstance has been wonderfully ordered by Divine Providence. If England gains a prosperous and happy colony, it will prove a fitting clasp to this fortunate chain of events. Three cheers for New Switzerland!"

Deep emotion stirred every heart as the party separated for the night. Many felt they stood on the threshold of a new life. For myself, a weight was lifted from my heart, and I thanked God that a difficulty which had oppressed me for years was solved.

Nothing was thought of but preparing for the departure of those bound for England. Captain Littlestone allowed as much time as possible, but it was short, necessitating incessant industry throughout the settlement. Everything was packed that could add to the children's comfort or benefit them upon arrival. A large share of my possessions—pearls, corals, furs, spices, and other valuables—would enable them to take a good position in the world of commerce.
