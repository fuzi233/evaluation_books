Young Harry Warrington’s sudden defiance took Madame de Bernstein entirely by surprise. There was no time for measured response—only a swift, stormy outburst of anger that left her standing in the doorway, eyes blazing, before she swept from the room. Her glance darted first to Lady Fanny, then to her mother, as if searching for complicity. But Lady Maria, absorbed in her embroidery at the tambour-frame, barely stirred. She did not lift her head, nor did she acknowledge the aunt’s departure or the subtle exchange of glances between mother and daughter.

“So,” the maternal gaze seemed to say, “in spite of everything, you have done it?”

“Have what?” asked Lady Fanny’s eyes, wide with feigned innocence. But the expression was too hesitant, too uncertain. If she had been guilty, she would have masked it better—her face carefully composed, her demeanor unflappable. As it was, she looked puzzled, perhaps even a little afraid. Her mother, however, stared at her with such intensity that one might have thought she wished to pluck out those very eyes.

But Lady Castlewood could not act like the monstrous figures in the stage directions of *King Lear*. When she wanted to punish her daughter, she would smile, place an arm around her waist, and later, in private, exact her retribution.

“So you don’t care to accompany the old lady to Tunbridge Wells?” she said, voice cool, face serene, with the practiced grace of someone who knows how to mask intent beneath politeness.

“And small blame to our cousin!” interjected my lord, stepping forward with a calmness that startled his family. (For just a moment, Lady Maria raised her eyes from her needlework.)

“A young man should not spend his days idly chasing pleasures,” he continued. “Let him mix useful pursuits with his amusements. He may visit the pump-rooms and fiddling halls of Tunbridge or Bath later. Mr. Warrington has responsibilities in America—a great estate to manage. Let him see how we conduct ours here in England. Will has already shown him the kennels and stables, the games we play—games, I believe, that he handles as well as any master. After harvest, we shall take him hunting; in winter, we’ll introduce him to English fowling. Though there has been some distance between us and our kinswoman in Virginia, we share blood. Before sending our cousin back to his mother, let us show him what life is like for an English gentleman at home. I would like to read with him as much as I would hunt with him—that is why I’ve urged him to stay, to be near me.”

He spoke with such open sincerity that his mother-in-law, half-brother, and sister could not help but wonder at his true intention. These three often conspired together, whispering grievances against the head of the house. When he adopted this tone, they never knew what lay beneath. He did not say, “This is true,” but rather, “This is what I wish my family to accept.” And so it became accepted: Lord Castlewood had a noble desire to foster domestic affection, to educate and guide his young relative. He had taken a genuine liking to the boy and wished him to remain nearby.

“What is Castlewood up to now?” William whispered to his mother and sister as they retreated into the corridor.

“Stop! By George, I’ve got it!” he exclaimed.

“What, William?” asked his sister.

“He wants to get him playing—and win back the Virginia estate from him. That’s it!”

“But the lad doesn’t own the estate yet,” pointed out their mother. “He can’t lose what he hasn’t got.”

“If my brother isn’t hatching some scheme, may I be—” she began, then stopped herself.

“Hush! Of course he has a plan. But what is it?”

“He can’t mean Maria,” mused William. “She’s older than Harry’s mother.”

“Pooh! With her faded hair, freckled skin, and ancient face? Impossible!” Lady Fanny declared, though her voice held a hint of sigh.

“Of course your ladyship fancied the Iroquois too!” snapped their mother.

“I trust I know my station and duty better than that, madam! If I had liked him, it wouldn’t mean I’d marry him. You taught me that much.”

“My Lady Fanny!”

“I’m certain you married our father without loving him. You’ve told me so a thousand times!”

“And if you didn’t love him before marriage, you certainly didn’t fall in love afterward,” Mr. William broke in, laughing. “Fan and I remember how our parents used to quarrel. Don’t we, Fan? And poor Esmond kept peace between them.”

“Don’t bring up those dreadful scenes, William!” cried their mother. “When your father drank too much, he became a raving madman. His behavior should serve as a warning to you, sir, who seem fond of the same disgusting habit.”

“I’m sure, madam, you weren’t much happier marrying a man you didn’t like. And your title has brought us very little,” Lady Fanny muttered.

“What use is a coronet when your jointure is no better than a tradesman’s wife? How many women in London are richer than we are? Just look at the grocer’s widow who moved into our square at Kensington—she has three gowns where I have one. And though she’s waited on by only a man and two maids, I know she eats and drinks far better than we do, with our scraps of cold meat and our grand, lazy, impudent footmen!”

“He! he! Glad I dine at the palace, not at home!” chuckled Mr. Will. (He owed his minor Court position—Deputy Powder—to his aunt’s influence with Count Puffendorff, Groom of the Royal {and Serene Electoral} Powder-Closet.)

“Why shouldn’t I be happy without any title but my own?” Lady Frances went on. “Many people are. Perhaps even happy in America.”

“Yes—surrounded by a mother-in-law who’s a perfect Turk and Tartar, Indian war-whoops echoing all around, and the constant danger of losing your scalp or being devoured by a bear every time you go to church.”

“I wouldn’t go to church,” said Lady Fanny.

“You’d go with anyone who asked you, Fan!” roared William. “And so would old Maria. And so would any woman, that’s the truth.” He laughed at his own wit.

“Pray, good folks, what is all this merriment about?” asked Madame de Bernstein, peering through the tapestried door into the gallery where the conversation had taken place.

William explained that his mother and sister had been arguing—nothing new, as his aunt well knew—because Fanny wanted to marry their cousin, the wild Virginian, and the Countess refused.

Fanny protested. Since the day her mother forbade her to speak to the young man, she had scarcely exchanged two words with him. She knew her place. She didn’t want to be scalped by Indians or eaten by bears.

Madame de Bernstein frowned. “If he’s not staying for you, then whom is he staying for?”

“At every house he’s visited, you’ve made sure to show him only women who are either frights or in the nursery. And I think the boy is too proud to fall in love with a dairymaid,” said William.

“Humph. Taste is taste, ma'am,” replied William, shrugging.

“Of Mr. William Esmond’s taste, perhaps—but not of that boy’s. The Esmonds of his grandfather’s upbringing would not court in the kitchen.”

“Well, every man to his taste,” William retorted. “A fellow might go farther and fare worse than my brother’s servants’ hall. Besides Fan, there’s only the maids—or old Maria.”

“Maria? Impossible!”

Yet as soon as the word left her lips, a thought struck Madame de Bernstein: perhaps this elderly Calypso had captured her young Telemachus after all. She recalled half a dozen cases from her own experience where young men had become infatuated with older women. She remembered Harry Warrington’s frequent absences—absences she had dismissed as mere sport. And how often, when he was gone, so was Maria Esmond.

Walks in shaded alleys, whispers behind clipped hedges, fleeting handclasps in dim corridors, secret glances on staircases—her imagination, seasoned by years of worldly observation, conjured these possibilities just as she declared them impossible.

“Impossible, ma'am! I don’t know,” William persisted. “My mother warned Fanny off him.”

“Oh, your mother warned Fanny?” the Baroness asked.

“Certainly, my dear Baroness!”

“Didn’t she? Didn’t she pinch Fanny’s arm black-and-blue? Didn’t they fight about it?”

“Nonsense, William! For shame!” both ladies cried at once.

“And now, since we’ve heard how rich he is, perhaps it’s sour grapes,” Madame de Bernstein mused. “Now that he’s been warned off the young bird, maybe he’s hunting the old one. Why impossible? You know old Lady Suffolk, ma'am?”

“William, how can you speak of Lady Suffolk to your aunt?”

A grin crossed William’s face. “Because Lady Suffolk was a favorite at Court? Well, others have succeeded her.”

“Sir!” Madame de Bernstein said sharply.

“So they have,” William insisted. “Who, pray, is my Lady Yarmouth now? And didn’t old Lady Suffolk fall in love with George Berkeley and marry him when she was quite old? Nay, ma'am, if I recall correctly—though we hear plenty of gossip at our table—Harry Estridge went mad for you when you were nearing twenty, and would have changed your name a third time if you’d let him.”

This mention of a past affair—well-known in society—did not provoke anger, as the earlier remark about her favor at King George II’s court had. Instead, it warmed her mood.

“Au fait,” she said, tapping lightly on the table, her mind drifting to the reckless young Harry Estridge. “It’s not impossible, William, that both young and old might play the fool.”

“But I still can’t understand a young man falling for Maria,” William said. “Though he might for you, ma'am. That’s another matter, as our French tutor used to say. You remember the Count, ma'am? He—he!—and so does Maria!”

“William!”

“And I dare say the Count remembers the bastinado Castlewood gave him. A confounded French dancing-master, calling himself a count, daring to fall in love in our family! Whenever I want to make myself especially agreeable to old Maria, I just speak a few words of *parly voo* to her. She knows exactly what I mean.”

“Have you spoken ill of her to your cousin, Harry Warrington?” Madame de Bernstein asked.

“Well—I know she’s always abusing me—and I’ve said my piece about her,” William admitted.

“Oh, you idiot!” the old lady exclaimed. “Who but a fool ever speaks ill of a woman to her sweetheart? He’ll tell her everything, and they’ll both hate you.”

“The very thing, ma'am!” William burst out, laughing. “I had a suspicion, you see. Two days ago, riding together, I told Harry Warrington my thoughts about Maria. Why shouldn’t I? She’s always criticizing me, isn’t she, Fan? And your favorite turned as red as my plush waistcoat—shocked that a gentleman could slander his own flesh and blood. He trembled with rage and called me no true Esmond.”

“Why didn’t you chastise him, sir, as my lord did the dancing-master?” Lady Castlewood demanded.

“Well, mother—you see, in quarter-staff, two sticks are used,” William replied. “And my opinion is that Harry Warrington can defend himself quite well. Perhaps that’s why I didn’t offer him a beating. And now you mention it, I know he told Maria. She’s been glaring at me like battle, murder, and sudden death ever since. Which proves—”

He turned to his aunt.

“Proves what?”

“That I think we’re on the right scent. We’ve found Maria—the old fox!” And with that, the ingenuous youth clapped his hand to his mouth and gave a loud halloo.

How far had the intrigue gone? That was the question.

Mr. Will believed that at her age, Maria would move swiftly—time was short. There was little affection between William and his half-sister.

“Who will investigate thoroughly?” Madame de Bernstein mused. “Scolding won’t help. Threats only inflame. I’ve only been in danger once in my life, young people,” she said, “and I believe it was because my poor mother contradicted me. If this boy is like others of his family, the more we oppose him, the deeper he’ll become entangled. We’ll never pull him out.”

“Faith, ma'am, suppose we leave him in it?” grumbled William.

“Old Maria and I don’t love each other much,” he admitted, “but an English earl’s daughter is good enough for an American tobacco-planter, all things considered.”

His mother and sister objected loudly. They would not hear of such a match.

“Then why grudge him to Maria?” William retorted. “I think Castlewood wants her to have him.”

“Why grudge him to Maria, sir?” Madame de Bernstein asked, her voice sharp with emotion. “Do you remember who the boy is? What your family owes to his? His grandfather was your father’s closest friend, the man who gave up this estate, this title, this very castle—so you and yours might benefit. And the reward? You shut the door on the child when he knocks, and talk of marrying him to an elderly woman who might be his mother! He shan’t marry her.”

“The very thing we were thinking, my dear Baroness!” Lady Castlewood interjected. “Our branch of the family isn’t eager for the match, though my lord and Maria may be.”

“You would like him for yourself now that you know he’s rich,” Madame Beatrix snapped, turning on the women. “Mr. Warrington may be wealthy, madam, but there’s no need to remind us constantly that we are poor.”

Lady Castlewood responded with spirit. “At least there’s little difference in Fanny’s age and Mr. Harry’s. And surely you won’t claim that a lady of our name and breeding isn’t good enough for any gentleman born in Virginia or elsewhere.”

“Let Fanny take an English gentleman, Countess,” Madame de Bernstein replied. “Not an American. With her looks, accomplishments, and your support, she cannot fail to find a worthy match. But from what I know of your daughters—and what I suspect of our young cousin—I’m certain no happy union could result.”

“What does my aunt know about me?” Lady Fanny asked, flushing.

“Only your temper, my dear. I don’t believe all the idle gossip one hears in town. But temperament and upbringing speak volumes. Imagine one of you banished from St. James’s and the Mall, forced to live in a plantation surrounded by savages! You’d die of boredom—or drive your husband mad with your ill-humor. You were born to adorn courts—not wigwams. Let this boy return to his wilderness with a wife suited to him.”

The other two ladies declared in unison that, for their part, they desired no better fate. After a few more words, they departed, while Madame de Bernstein lifted the tapestry and retired to her chamber.

Now she saw the full design. She admired the ways of women, recalling a dozen small signs she had overlooked. She wondered at her own blindness. How far had the affair progressed? Was Harry’s passion deep and serious, or merely a fleeting flame? How deeply was he involved?

She feared the strength of Harry’s feelings—and the desperation of Maria’s. A woman of her age, she thought, might do anything to secure love. Scandal? Bah. She could retire to Virginia and become a princess, letting England chatter as it pleased.

Was there always something women withheld from one another? Something they agreed to conceal—not from deceit, but from modesty?

Men, as soon as they feel an inclination, seek a friend to share the joy. Women, with greater skill, bury their secrets. For weeks, had not Maria made fools of everyone—Maria, the family joke?

I refrain from probing too deeply into Lady Maria’s past. But I have my own opinions about Madame de Bernstein’s.

A hundred years ago, the world was less rigid. People were not all pure, moral, modest. Skeletons existed in closets. Schemes were hatched. Old stories were buried. Girls sold themselves for wealth. Mothers helped them.

Suppose Lady Maria tries to play her game—what then? Where is her eccentricity?

On these matters, no doubt, the Baroness pondered long and hard in the quiet of her private room.
