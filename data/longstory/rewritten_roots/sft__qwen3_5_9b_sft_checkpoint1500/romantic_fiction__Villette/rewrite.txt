My godmother resided in a stately house within the pristine, ancient town of Bretton. Her husband's lineage had anchored itself there for generations, bearing the name of their soil—Bretton of Bretton. Whether this was a fortuitous accident or the legacy of a distant ancestor of considerable standing, I could never discern.

As a child, I visited Bretton roughly twice a year, finding profound delight in the locale. The estate and its inhabitants were tailor-made for my sensibilities. The vast, tranquil chambers, the meticulously arranged furnishings, the expansive windows admitting clear light, and the balcony overlooking a magnificent, antiquated street—all these elements appealed to me deeply. It was a place where Sundays and holidays seemed eternally suspended, bound by an atmosphere of such profound quietude and cleanliness that time itself seemed to slow.

In a household comprised entirely of adults, a single child invariably becomes the center of attention. In this quiet sphere, I was similarly cherished by Mrs. Bretton. She had become a widow, leaving behind one son, prior to my acquaintance with her; her husband, a physician, had passed away while she remained youthful and comely.

Though she was no longer young in appearance, she retained a striking beauty. Tall, well-proportioned, and despite possessing a dark complexion uncommon among Englishwomen, her cheeks wore a luminous vitality that spoke of robust health. This radiance animated a pair of fine, cheerful black eyes. Regrettably, nature had not bestowed this complex upon her son. His eyes were blue—though piercing even in boyhood—and his hair, when illuminated by the sun, appeared golden, a hue friends hesitated to define otherwise. Yet, he inherited his mother's facial lines, her perfect dentition, her stature (or the promise thereof, for he was still growing), and, above all, her flawless health and a spirit marked by a steady, equal temperament that proved more valuable than any fortune.

It was during the autumn of a certain year that I stayed at Bretton, having been summoned personally by my godmother to reclaim me from the relatives with whom I had permanently settled. I suspect she then clearly foresaw approaching events, shadows of which I barely perceived, yet whose faint suspicion instilled within me a restless melancholy, rendering me grateful for the prospect of changing scenes and company.

Time, under her roof, flowed with a gentle, untroubled current. Unlike the tumultuous swiftness of youth, it moved blandly, akin to a broad river gliding through a flatland. My stays there mirrored the pilgrimage of Christian and Hopeful beside a serene stream, flanked by "green trees on each bank, and meadows beautified with lilies all the year round." There was no charm of variety, no thrill of incident; indeed, when stimulation finally arrived, it felt like a disturbance, something I wished to avoid rather than embrace.

One day, a letter arrived that evidently startled and concerned Mrs. Bretton. At first, I assumed it originated from home and trembled, anticipating some catastrophic news. However, no mention was made of it, and the gathering clouds dissipated quickly.

The following day, returning from a lengthy walk, I discovered an unexpected transformation upon entering my bedroom. Beside my own French bed, nestled in its shady alcove, stood a small crib draped in white linen. Next to my mahogany chest of drawers lay a diminutive rosewood bureau. I paused, gazing in bewilderment. "What portents are these?" I wondered. The answer, however, was manifest: "A second guest is arriving; Mrs. Bretton anticipates another visitor."

Descending to dinner, explanations unfolded. A little girl would shortly join me as a companion; she was the daughter of a friend and distant relative of the late Dr. Bretton. It was noted that this little girl had recently lost her mother, though Mrs. Bretton soon qualified this tragedy, suggesting the loss was less severe than initially appeared. Mrs. Home—the mother's name—had been remarkably beautiful yet giddy and careless, neglecting her child and failing to satisfy her husband. Their union, far from congenial, had ended in separation by mutual consent, devoid of legal proceedings. Shortly thereafter, overwhelmed by exertion at a ball, the lady contracted a cold, developed a fever, and succumbed after a brief illness. Her husband, a man of sensitive feelings, was shattered by the suddenness of the news, convinced that his own lack of patience and indulgence had hastened her demise. He had brooded over this guilt until his spirits were severely affected. Medical advice suggested travel as a remedy, and in the interim, Mrs. Bretton had offered to care for his little daughter. "And I trust," concluded my godmother, "that the child will not resemble her mother—a silly, frivolous flirt that every sensible man foolishly marries. For," she confided, "Mr. Home *is* a sensible man in his own way, though not overly practical. He is devoted to science, spending half his life in a laboratory conducting experiments—a pursuit his butterfly-like wife could neither comprehend nor endure. Indeed," she admitted, "I should not have enjoyed it myself."

Addressing my curiosity, she revealed that her late husband had attributed Mr. Home's scientific inclination to a maternal uncle, a French savant. The gentleman was of mixed French and Scottish descent, maintaining connections in France, several of whom held the title *de*, claiming nobility.

That same evening, at nine o'clock, a servant was sent to intercept the coach carrying our anticipated visitor. Mrs. Bretton and I sat alone in the drawing-room awaiting her arrival; John Graham Bretton was absent, visiting a schoolfriend in the countryside. My godmother perused the evening papers while I stitched. Outside, the air was wet; rain lashed against the glass panes, and the wind sounded angry and restless.

"Poor child!" Mrs. Bretton murmured intermittently. "What terrible weather for her journey! I wish she were safely here."

Just before ten, the bell announced Warren's return. As soon as the door opened, I rushed down the hallway. There lay a trunk and several band-boxes, beside them stood a figure resembling a nursemaid, and at the foot of the stairs, Warren carried a shawled bundle in his arms.

"Is that the child?" I asked.

"Yes, miss."

I reached out to open the shawl, eager to glimpse the face, but it was swiftly turned away, pressed close to Warren's shoulder. "Put me down, please," a small voice commanded when Warren opened the drawing-room door. "And remove this shawl," the speaker continued, using a minute hand to extract the pin and removing the clumsy wrapping with a fastidious haste. The creature that now appeared attempted to fold the shawl deftly, but the drapery was far too heavy and cumbersome for those small hands and arms to manage. "Give it to Harriet, please," the direction was given, "and she can put it away." With that, the child turned and fixed her gaze upon Mrs. Bretton.

"Come here, little dear," the lady said warmly. "Come, let me see if you are cold and damp; let me warm you by the fire."

The child advanced promptly. Freed from her wrappings, she appeared exceedingly tiny, yet a neat, perfectly formed little figure, light, slight, and straight. Seated on my godmother's ample lap, she looked like a mere doll; her neck, delicate as wax, and her head of silky curls increased the resemblance.

Mrs. Bretton spoke in soft, affectionate phrases as she rubbed the child's hands, arms, and feet. Initially regarded with a wistful gaze, she soon returned a smile. Though rarely a caressing woman—even with her deeply beloved son, her manner was seldom sentimental, often the opposite—when the small stranger smiled at her, she kissed her, asking, "What is my little one's name?"

"Missy."

"But besides Missy?"

"Polly, papa calls her."

"Will Polly be content to live with me?"

"Not *always*; but until papa returns. Papa is gone away." She shook her head expressively.

"He will return to Polly, or send for her."

"Will he, ma'am? Do you know he will?"

"I think so."

"But Harriet does not think so—at least, not for a long while. He is ill."

Her eyes filled with tears. She drew her hand from Mrs. Bretton's and made a movement to leave her lap. It was first resisted, but she said, "Please, I wish to go: I can sit on a stool."

She was permitted to slide down from the knee, took a footstool, and carried it to a corner where the shade was deepest, seating herself there. Mrs. Bretton, though commanding and peremptory in grave matters, was often passive in trifles; she allowed the child her way. Turning to me, she said, "Take no notice at present." Yet I did take notice. I watched Polly rest her small elbow on her small knee, her head propped on her hand. I observed her draw a square inch or two of pocket-handkerchief from the doll-pocket of her skirt, and then I heard her weep. While other children in grief cry aloud without shame or restraint, this being wept silently. The tiniest occasional sniff testified to her emotion. Mrs. Bretton did not hear it—which was, perhaps, as well. Soon, a voice from the corner demanded, "May the bell be rung for Harriet!"

I rang; the nurse was summoned and arrived.

Days drifted by, and it became evident that Paulina possessed little capacity for affection within the confines of the house. She was not unruly, nor did she defy command; yet, to behold her was to witness something profoundly unsettling. She offered no comfort, no tranquility. Instead, she moped with a gravity that belied her years, bearing upon her infant face the deep, legible grooves of adult exile—the kindling sorrow of a soul longing for Europe while stranded at its antipodes. To me, Lucy Snowe, who felt guilty of possessing an overheated and wandering imagination, she appeared ancient and unearthly. Whenever I opened a door to find her seated alone in a corner, her chin resting on her fist, the room did not seem occupied by a child, but haunted by a spirit of desolation.

On moonlit nights, awakening from sleep, I would often catch sight of her figure, stark white in her nightdress, kneeling upright in bed. She prayed with the fervor of a zealot, a precocious fanatic, or an untimely saint. I confess I could not discern the thoughts swirling behind those eyes, but I suspected they were no healthier or more rational than the mind of the child herself. Her prayers were usually whispers, lost to the air, sometimes unspoken entirely save for the recurring, crushing burden: "Papa, my dear Papa!" It was a singular obsession, a monomania that betrayed the most unfortunate flaw in human nature—the inability to exist without a single, consuming idea.

If this fretting had continued unchecked, its conclusion would have been a tragedy of conjecture. However, a sudden turn arrived. One afternoon, Mrs. Bretton coaxed her from her usual corner of gloom and lifted her onto the window-seat. To occupy her, the governess asked her to watch the passersby and count the ladies descending the street. Paulina sat listlessly, eyes glazed, failing to count. Yet, as I watched her closely, I witnessed a startling transformation within her irises. These "sensitive" natures offer curious spectacles to the cool-hearted. Her fixed, heavy gaze swam, trembled, and suddenly glowed with fire. Her clouded brow cleared; the dejection vanished, replaced by a sudden, intense eagerness. "It *is*!" she cried.

Like a bird taking flight or a shaft leaving the bow, she vanished from the room. How she managed to unlock the door remains a mystery; perhaps it stood ajar, or perhaps Warren, who happened to be nearby, obeyed her impulsive summons. From the window, watching calmly, I saw her—clad in a black frock and a tiny braided apron, a garment she detested—dart halfway down the street. Just as I prepared to announce her madness to Mrs. Bretton, urging immediate pursuit, I saw her snatched away. A gentleman had intercepted her, draping his cloak over her small form, and advanced to return her to the house whence she had fled.

I assumed he would entrust her to a servant and depart, but instead, he ascended the stairs. His reception by Mrs. Bretton revealed his familiarity; she recognized him, yet she was fluttered, surprised, and taken unawares. Her manner was nearly expostulatory. In response to her silent questioning, he declared, "I could not help it, madam: I found it impossible to leave the country without seeing with my own eyes how she settles."

"But you will unsettle her," she warned.

"I hope not. And how is Papa's little Polly?" he asked, addressing the child as he sat and gently lowered her to the floor.

"How is Polly's Papa?" she replied, leaning heavily against his knee and gazing up into his face.

The scene was not loud, nor wordy, yet it was brimful with a feeling that oppressed the weary observer. There is a certain disdain or ridicule that comforts a spectator during scenes of unrestrained emotion, but I have always found the most burdensome that sensitivity which bends of its own volition, a giant slave to the sway of simple, unfeeling reason.

Mr. Home was a stern, hard-featured man. His forehead was knotty, his cheekbones marked and prominent, giving his countenance a distinctly Scotch quality. Yet, there was depth in his eye and genuine emotion in his now agitated features. His Northern accent harmonized with his physiognomy; he was simultaneously proud and homely. Laying his hand upon the child's uplifted head, he heard her whisper, "Kiss Polly." He kissed her. I wished, in my heart, for some hysterical cry, some display of drama to relieve the tension, but she made wonderfully little sound. She seemed to have obtained everything she desired, falling into a trance of contentment. In neither mien nor feature was this creature like her sire, yet she carried his strain within her, as a cup receives wine from the flagon.

Indisputably, Mr. Home possessed manly self-control, though secrets may lurk beneath the surface. "Polly," he said, looking down at his daughter, "go into the hall. You will see Papa's great-coat lying on a chair. Put your hand into the pockets; you will find a pocket-handkerchief there. Bring it to me."

She obeyed, moving with deftness and nimbleness. As she returned, Mr. Home was conversing with Mrs. Bretton, seemingly unaware of her presence until she stood at his knee. Seeing that he continued to talk, apparently oblivious, she took his hand, opened his unresisting fingers, slipped the handkerchief inside, and closed them around it. For a time, he remained unconscious of her return, but eventually, he lifted her to his knee. She nestled against him, and though neither spoke to the other for an hour following, I suppose both were deeply satisfied.

Later, during tea, the minute movements of this little creature demanded full attention. First, she directed Warren, as he arranged the chairs. "Put Papa's chair here, and mine near it, between Papa and Mrs. Bretton: *I* must hand his tea." Taking her seat, she beckoned to her father. "Be near me, as if we were at home, Papa." Intercepting his cup as he passed, she stirred the sugar and poured the cream herself. "I always did it for you at home, Papa," she insisted. "Nobody could do it as well, not even your own self."

Her attentions throughout the meal were somewhat absurd. The sugar tongs were too wide for her hands, requiring two to wield; the weight of the silver cream ewer, the bread-and-butter plates, and the cups taxed her insufficient strength and dexterity. Yet, she lifted and handed with determined effort, miraculously breaking nothing. Candidly, I thought her a busy-body, but her father, blind like other parents, seemed perfectly content to let her wait on him, soothed by her offices.

"She is my comfort!" he exclaimed to Mrs. Bretton. That lady, who possessed her own "comfort" and nonpareil on a much larger scale, was momentarily absent, so she sympathized with his foible.

A second "comfort" appeared later that evening. I knew the day had been fixed for his return, and that Mrs. Bretton had awaited him all day. After tea, we sat round the fire when Graham joined our circle—or rather, broke it up, for his arrival caused a bustle, necessitating refreshments for the fastening guest. Mr. Graham and Mr. Home met as old acquaintances, though Graham initially ignored the little girl.

Once his meal was finished and numerous questions from his mother answered, Graham turned from the table to the hearth. Seated opposite him was Mr. Home, and at his elbow, the child. If I used the term "child," it is an inappropriate descriptor; it suggests any picture rather than that of the demure little person in a mourning frock and white chemisette, resembling a large doll perched on a high chair beside a stand holding her toy work-box of white varnished wood. She held a shred of a handkerchief, professing to hem it, boring perseveringly with a needle that seemed almost a skewer in her fingers. She pricked herself repeatedly, marking the cambric with tracks of minute red dots, occasionally starting when the weapon swerved and inflicted a deeper stab, yet remaining silent, diligent, and absorbed, strangely womanly.

Graham was then a handsome, faithless-looking youth of sixteen. By "faithless-looking," I do not mean a perfidious disposition, but because the epithet strikes me as proper to describe the fair, Celtic character of his beauty: his waved light auburn hair, his supple symmetry, his frequent smiles, destitute neither of fascination nor subtlety. He was a spoiled, whimsical boy.

"Mother," he said, after eyeing the little figure in silence for some time, relieved from the half-laughing bashfulness of Mr. Home's presence, "I see a young lady in the present society to whom I have not been introduced."

"Mr. Home's little girl, I suppose you mean," said his mother.

"Indeed, ma'am," replied her son, "I consider your expression of the least ceremonious: Miss Home *I* should certainly have said, in venturing to speak of the gentlewoman to whom I allude."

Mr. Home remained for two days, a somber figure who refused to venture outdoors. He spent his hours by the fire, alternating between silence and engaging in Mrs. Bretton's conversation—a dialogue suited to his melancholy temperament, neither overly sympathetic nor unkind, grounded in sense and tempered with a gentle, maternal warmth appropriate for a woman of his seniority.

Paulina, meanwhile, existed in a state of joyful muteness, her energy focused entirely on observation. Her father frequently hoisted her onto his knee, where she sat until she imagined him growing restless, at which point she would declare, "Papa, put me down; I shall tire you with my weight." With a heavy, contented slide, she descended to the rug, positioning herself at his feet amidst her white work-box and scarlet-speckled handkerchief. This handkerchief, destined to be a keepsake for her departing father, demanded constant attention from the seamstress, whose industrious pace yielded about twenty stitches in thirty minutes.

The evening brought back Graham to the household, restoring a spark of animation that the nature of his interactions with Miss Paulina could not easily dim. A certain haughty distance marked their early encounters following the slight Paulina suffered on Graham's first arrival. Her standard response to his address was a curt, "I can't attend to you; I have other things to think about." When pressed to specify these matters, she simply replied, "Business."

Graham attempted to capture her attention by displaying the treasures of his desk: colorful seals, bright wax, penknives, and a collection of engravings, including a vivid etching of a child playing with a Blenheim spaniel. Though she initially eyed these objects with furtive curiosity, pride ultimately restrained her. An etching fluttered to the floor, drawing her gaze. "Pretty little dog!" she exclaimed, delighted.

Graham, seizing the moment, offered it to her. "Well—you may have it," he said. Yet Paulina hesitated. To accept would be to compromise her dignity. She placed the picture down and turned away. "You won't have it, then, Polly?" he persisted. "I would rather not, thank you."

"Shall I tell you what I will do with the picture if you refuse it?" he threatened lightly. She listened, half-turned. "Cut it into strips for lighting the taper." "No!" she protested. "But I shall." "Please—don't."

Graham, unmoved by her pleading, produced scissors from his mother's basket. "Here goes!" he announced, making a menacing flourish at the image of the dog and child. "Right through Fido's head, and splitting little Harry's nose."

"No! _No!_ NO!"

"Then come to me. Come quickly, or it is done."

Hesitating briefly, she complied. "Now, will you have it?" he asked. "Please." "But I shall want payment." "How much?" "A kiss." "Give the picture first into my hand."

As she handed it over, Paulina looked momentarily faithless. Graham accepted it, and she promptly fled to her father, seeking refuge on his knee. Graham rose in mock wrath, following her. Buried in Mr. Home's waistcoat, she cried, "Papa—papa—send him away!" "I'll not be sent away," Graham retorted.

Face averted, she extended her hand to ward him off. "Then, I shall kiss the hand," he teased. At that instant, it transformed into a miniature fist, delivering a sharp pinch. Graham, displaying a wit equal to his playmate's, feigned defeat. He collapsed onto a sofa, burying his face in his hands and groaning dramatically. Paulina, sensing his distress, peered at him anxiously.

"Papa, what is the matter?" she whispered. "You had better ask him, Polly." "Is he hurt?" she asked, prompting a fresh groan. "He makes a noise as if he were," Mr. Home observed. "Mother," Graham feebly suggested, "I think you had better send for the doctor. Oh my eye!"

"If I were to become blind——?" the thought slipped out. The chastiser could not endure the suggestion. Paulina rushed to his side. "Let me see your eye: I did not mean to touch it, only your mouth; and I did not think I hit so _very_ hard." Silence was her answer, followed by working features and soft sobs. "Have done trying that child, Graham," Mrs. Bretton interjected. "It is all nonsense, my pet," Mr. Home cried, lifting her again. While she tugged at his lion-like locks, she berated him as "the naughtiest, rudest, worst, untruest person that ever was."

On the morning of Mr. Home's departure, a private conversation ensued in a window recess. "Couldn't I pack my box and go with you, papa?" Paulina asked earnestly. He shook his head. "Yes, Polly... Because you are little and tender. It is only great, strong people that should travel." He reassured her, promising his return, urging her to remain cheerful despite the sorrow. "Farewell," he said, as time pressed. "_Now_?—just _now_?" she stammered. "Just now."

As he left, she dropped to her knees, crying out "Papa!" a low, prolonged lament akin to "Why hast thou forsaken me?" For several minutes, she endured an agony rare for her infancy. Mrs. Bretton shed a tear; Graham, writing nearby, watched her silently. Lucy Snowe remained calm. Alone, Paulina wrestled with her grief, eventually repressing it to a degree. Over the next few days, she grew increasingly passive.

By the third evening, Graham found her sitting quietly on the floor. Without a word, he lifted her. She nestled into his arms, weary, and fell asleep in his embrace. He carried her upstairs to bed. The next morning, her first demand was, "Where is Mr. Graham?"

Graham was absent, occupied with school exercises. Paulina volunteered to deliver tea to his study. Pausing on the threshold, she asked, "What are you doing?" "Writing," he replied. "Why don't you come to take breakfast with your mamma?" "Too busy." "Do you want any breakfast?" "Of course." She set the cup down on the carpet, like a jailor offering water to a prisoner, and retreated. Moments later, she returned, asking what food accompanied the tea. "Anything good," he instructed. "Bring me something particularly nice."

Returning to Mrs. Bretton, she pleaded for something special for Graham. The mistress, unable to refuse, allowed her to select the finest items from the table. Paulina fetched marmalade as well, which Graham, seated alone, praised to the skies. He promised that when he owned a house, she would be his housekeeper, and perhaps, if she displayed culinary genius, his cook. She stood by his elbow, sharing his fare, though she delicately declined the marmalade, unwilling to claim credit for procuring it.

Their bond, formed in this intimacy, deepened rather than dissolved. Though disparate in age and nature, they found endless common ground. With Paulina, Graham revealed a softer side; when alone with her, he could contemplate, read, and engage in quiet conversation. She learned the names and characters of his schoolfellows with astonishing speed, mimicking their quirks and voices. Their relationship was not without friction, however.

One afternoon, during Graham's birthday, friends arrived. Left alone in the dining room, the boys boisterously entertained themselves. Paulina, watching from the stair landing, knitted her brow. "I wish that door was clear glass," she confided. Could she go to them? She knocked timidly, then more firmly. Graham's head appeared, impatient. "What do you want, you little monkey?" "To come to you." "Do you indeed? As if I would be troubled with you! Away to mamma..." The door slammed. Stunned, Paulina realized he preferred them to her. She curled up on the mat, refusing Warren's or the cook's efforts to rouse her.

That evening, Graham forgot his impatience, but Paulina rejected his advances, her eye flashing with wounded pride. For days, she remained silent, a statue of marble. Eventually, tears broke the ice, and friendship resumed, though she never again solicited his attention with the former eagerness. Even when invited to ride on his favorite pony, she responded with icy coolness, dismissing the offer. "Lucy Snowe is a _tatter_-box," she muttered, walking away.

Later, discussing her preferences, Paulina revealed a secret affection: "I find him the most excellent on a Sunday; then we have him the whole day, and he is quiet, and, in the evening, _so_ kind." Indeed, Sundays brought a serenity to Graham. They would read together, reciting hymns and biblical stories. Paulina, a quick scholar, often moved to tears over passages like Joseph in the pit or Daniel in the lions' den. "Poor Jacob!" she would murmur, gathering Graham in her arms. "If you were to die... I should refuse to be comforted, and go down into the grave to you mourning."

These tender moments struck Lucy as strangely rash, bordering on dangerous, yet Graham bore them passively, sometimes smiling with a complacent wonder at her earnest devotion.

Leaving Bretton, a few weeks after Paulina's departure, I returned home, absent for six months. Little did I foresee then that I should never tread those calm, old streets again. While it is natural to assume I rejoiced in returning to the bosom of my kin, let me confess the contrary. For the ensuing eight years, I existed not as a traveler, but as a vessel anchored in a harbor of glassy silence. I was a spirit asleep, stretched upon a cushioned deck, bathed in an artificial, eternal sunshine, rocked by gentle, indolent breezes that knew no storm. A great many women and girls spend their lives in such a stasis, drifting gently toward an inevitable end. Why not I?

Yet, however idyllic this illusion appeared, it was a delusion. Beneath the surface of this placid existence, a terrible tempest raged. I remembered a time of profound cold, of danger, and of bitter contention. To this day, when nightmares seize me, I taste the salt of briny waves in my throat and feel the crushing weight of ice pressing against my lungs. I knew then that the storm was not of an hour or a day; it had lasted many days and nights, a darkness where neither sun nor star dared to shine. The sails were torn, the rigging thrown overboard, and hope, that fragile thing, shattered completely. In the end, the ship was lost, and the crew perished.

I had no one to whom I could complain of these troubles. Mrs. Bretton, who once stood as a pillar of support, had vanished from my life. Barriers erected by others had severed our path years prior, and time had wrought further changes. Her fortune, vast once, had dwindled to a fraction through speculative ventures, and both she and Graham, her son, had departed for London. Left alone in the wake of this dissolution, I realized with a jolt that I must rely solely on myself. Though I was not naturally self-reliant or vigorous, necessity forged these qualities in me, as it does in countless souls. When Miss Marchmont, a wealthy spinster of our neighborhood, summoned me, I obeyed, hoping she might offer me some task to occupy my weary hands.

Miss Marchmont resided in a stately home, yet she was a prisoner of her own body. A rheumatic cripple, her limbs stiff and useless for twenty years, she had never known freedom from pain. She lived upstairs, her drawing-room adjoining her bedroom, a fortress of solitude. I had heard rumors of her eccentricities, but seeing her for the first time revealed a woman furrowed by grief, gray-haired and grave, hardened by long suffering, and irritable. A former companion, nearing marriage, was to depart, leaving a void that Miss Marchmont hoped I could fill. She extended this invitation after tea, sitting alone by her fireside.

"It will not be an easy life," she said frankly, "for I demand considerable attention, and you will be heavily confined. Yet, compared to the desolation you have recently endured, it may seem tolerable."

I pondered her words. Logically, it ought to be tolerable, yet some strange fatality whispered otherwise. To dwell in this cramped chamber, watching suffering, perhaps enduring her temper, through the remainder of my youth, while all joy had fled, seemed a cruel bargain. My heart sank, then revived. Perhaps I was merely too pragmatic to romanticize the horrors ahead.

"My doubt lies in my strength," I observed. "Will I have the fortitude?"

"That is my own fear," she admitted, studying me with a sharp gaze. "You look like a worn-out creature."

And I did. In the glass, I saw a hollow-eyed specter in mourning, pale and faded. Yet, I suspected the blight was superficial; deep within, the vital spark of life still burned.

"What other prospects do you hold?" she pressed.

"None clear as yet," I confessed. "But possibilities may arise."

"So you imagine. Perhaps you are right. Follow your own path, and if it fails, try mine. Consider my offer open for three months."

Her kindness moved me. As I spoke, a sudden spasm of pain seized her. I attended to her, applying remedies as directed, and in the care of her distress, a bond of intimacy quickly formed. I learned from her stoic endurance that she possessed a firm, patient nature, though her mind sometimes festered with bitterness. She, in turn, perceived the goodwill in my service and found a warmth in me that softened her iron exterior. Over the next week, as I tended to her, her flaws and oddities became apparent, yet a deeper respect emerged. Even when she scolded me, it was not with the cruelty of a tyrant, but with the grumbling of an irascible mother correcting a beloved child; her logic, even in rage, was unassailable. Soon, a growing attachment took root, transforming the thought of remaining with her from a burden into a refuge. Within another week, I agreed to stay.

Thus, two stifling, overheated rooms became my entire world. A crippled, ancient woman was my mistress, my confidante, and my everything. Her service became my duty; her pain, my suffering; her relief, my hope; her anger, my punishment; and her favor, my reward. Outside the steam-fogged lattice of her sick-chamber, the world of fields, forests, and oceans ceased to exist. I grew content to forget them, narrowing my existence to the immediate circle of her needs. I required no fresh air, no substantial food, simply the tiny portions meant for an invalid. Beyond this, she offered me the unique opportunity to study the depth of her character: the constancy of her virtues, the terrifying power of her passions, and the raw truth of her emotions. Clinging to these qualities, I would have followed her for twenty more years, had fate permitted it.

But another decree was written. I was to be goaded into action, driven by a sting that woke me from my slumber. My small piece of human affection, treasured like a solid pearl, was destined to dissolve in my fingers like melting hail. My adopted duty, which I had accepted with a contented conscience, was to be snatched away. I had sought to negotiate with Fate, trading occasional agony for a lifetime of quiet privation, but Providence would not sanction such shrinking sloth.

It was one night in February, a season when the winter's calm was breaking into the turmoil of spring. Having laid Miss Marchmont to rest, I sat by the fireside, sewing. The wind wailed outside, a mournful, persistent sound, but as night deepened, it shifted into a keening, piercing cry, almost articulate in its despair. It sounded like a lament, piteous and disconsolate, vibrating through the bones.

"Oh, hush!" I murmured, dropping my needle in alarm, doing my best to block out the sound. I had heard this voice before, and a grim theory had taken hold in my mind. Three times in my life, strange storms had preceded disaster. These accents, this restless, hopeless crying, signaled an atmosphere hostile to life. I recalled the legends of the Banshee, the sobbing winds that heralded plague and death. I had also noted the strange disturbances of the earth during such times—volcanic eruptions, swollen rivers, and rising tides. "The globe seems torn and disordered," I had thought, "and the weak among us wither in its feverish breath."

I listened, trembling, while Miss Marchmont slept soundly. Then, around midnight, the storm collapsed instantly into a dead calm. The fire, previously sputtering, flared up with vivid intensity. The air turned crisp and keen. Raising the curtains, I peered out into the starlit sky, where a sharp frost glittered.

Turning back, I found Miss Marchmont awake, lifting her head from the pillow with an earnestness I had never seen.

"Is it a fine night?" she asked.

"Yes," I replied.

"I thought so," she said, her voice gaining strength. "I feel so strong, so well. Raise me. I feel young tonight—light-hearted and happy. What if my complaint is turning, and I am destined to enjoy health? It would be a miracle!"

"And these are not the days of miracles," I thought, surprised by her buoyancy. She turned her gaze toward the past, recalling scenes and figures with singular vividness.

"I love memory tonight," she declared. "It is my dearest friend, bringing back to my heart realities that I thought long since dissolved into dust. I possess the hours, the thoughts, the hopes of my youth. I renew the love of my life—its only love—for I am not an amiable woman, yet I have had feelings strong and concentrated. Through that winter, my heart beat in rhythm with Frank's heart. Oh, my noble Frank, my faithful Frank! So much better than myself, his standard so far higher! With my mistress gone, I found myself standing alone once more, forced to seek shelter elsewhere. My nerves, perhaps, were a trifle shaken—not enough to ruin me, but enough to make me feel pale, thin, and hollow-eyed, like someone who has stayed up all night or lost their footing in a storm. Yet, I was not truly in debt, nor entirely destitute. Though Miss Marchmont could not fulfill her promise of a gift on that final night, her second cousin, the heir, eventually paid me my wages. He was a man of sharp features and a pinched nose, an avaricious soul who, I learned later, lived a life of stingy miserliness—a stark, ironic contrast to the generosity of his kin, whose memory remains a blessing to those who need it.

Possessing fifteen pounds, a body weary but unbroken, and a spirit that mirrored my physical state, I realized that compared to many, I held an enviable position. Yet, it was an awkward spot. On a specific afternoon, I felt the weight of this ambiguity acutely; soon, I would leave my current home, knowing full well I had nowhere else prepared for me.

Desperate for guidance, I visited an old servant from my family, now the housekeeper of a grand estate nearby. Once my nurse, she offered comfort but lacked wisdom for my particular plight. As twilight fell, leaving me with a heavy heart and a two-mile walk ahead, I stepped out into a clear, frosty night. Alone, poor, and perplexed, I walked through silent fields under a moonless sky, guided only by stars. My heart, however, beat with a vigor born of youth, uncounted twenty-three winters yet. It did not falter. Indeed, the eerie, moving lights of the Aurora Borealis dancing in the northern sky did not frighten me; instead, they seemed to summon a new strength. Drawing breath from the keen, low wind, a bold thought took root in my mind, hardening it to accept whatever fate awaited.

*"Leave this wilderness,"* it whispered, *"and go forth."*

*"Whither?"* I asked silently.

I needed not look far. Gazing upon the flat, fertile lands of central England, I imagined something I had never seen with my own eyes: London.

The following day, I returned to the hall to speak with the housekeeper, Mrs. Barrett. A grave and sensible woman, though she knew little of the wider world, she did not dismiss my plan as madness. I carried myself with a staid dignity that often served as a shield, allowing me to pursue ambitious ideas that might have branded me a dreamer if expressed with excitement.

As Mrs. Barrett pondered the difficulties of my move, preparing orange rinds for marmalade, a small child bounded past the window and into the room. A lovely, laughing boy, he danced straight to me, and I lifted him onto my knees. His mother, young Mrs. Leigh, entered shortly after. Time had transformed her from a dull, lower-class schoolgirl into a beautiful, kind-hearted wife and mother, though intellect seemed to have yielded to domestic grace. She had forgotten me, and I made no effort to remind her; why should I? She wished to take her son for a walk, accompanied by a nurse carrying an infant.

It was the nurse who caught my attention. Addressing her, Mrs. Leigh spoke French—bad French, with an accent so thick it transported me instantly back to our school days. The little boy chattered back in the same tongue. When the visitors departed, Mrs. Barrett revealed that the nurse was a foreigner, brought home two years prior on a continental trip, treated almost like a governess, and tasked mostly with walking with the baby and speaking French. "She claims," Mrs. Barrett noted, "that many Englishwomen find themselves similarly situated in foreign households."

I tucked away this fragment of news, saving it for a future moment of need. Before I left, she kindly provided the address of a respectable, old-fashioned inn in the City, a haunt of my uncles in days past.

Venturing to London required less audacity than one might assume; it was merely fifty miles. My funds covered the journey, a few days' lodging, and the return ticket if necessary. I viewed this not as a perilous adventure, but as a brief respite for my wearied faculties, a chance to breathe. One must always estimate one's actions moderately; grandiose schemes tend to induce fever, whereas calm calculation preserves the mind.

What was once a day's journey became a reality by nine on a wet February night. Upon reaching London, I found myself in a Babylon and a wilderness, a vast, strange city that tested the limits of my clear thinking and self-possession. Leaving the coach, the dialect of the cabmen sounded like a foreign tongue to me, a jumbled chaos of sounds I had never heard before. Yet, I managed to navigate, understanding enough to secure transport to the inn I had been given.

How oppressive and confusing this flight felt! For the first time, I stood in London, at an inn, exhausted by travel, confused by the darkness, numbed by the chill, and utterly lacking in experience or counsel. And yet, I was compelled to act.

I entrusted the situation to common sense. Alas, it was as chilled and bewildered as the rest of me, acting only under the spur of an inexorable necessity. With spasmodic effort, it directed me to pay the porter, to inquire for a room, and timidly to summon the chambermaid. This young lady, a model of town smartness, greeted me with a mincing, glib accent that seemed to rebuke my own speech, her spruce attire flaunting an easy scorn against my plain country garb.

"Well, it can't be helped," I told myself, steeling my resolve. "The scene is new; I shall gain from it."

I maintained a quiet demeanor toward her arrogance and the black-coated, white-neckclothed waiter, and soon, civility replaced suspicion. They likely mistook me for a servant at first, but quickly shifted to a hovering uncertainty between patronage and politeness.

I held my ground until I had eaten, warmed myself by a fire, and locked myself safely into my room. Only then, as I sat by the bed resting my head and arms upon the pillow, did a terrible oppression seize me. Suddenly, my position loomed large, ghost-like and anomalous. Desolate, almost devoid of hope, it stood before me. What was I doing, alone in great London? What would I do tomorrow? What prospects lay in store? Who were my friends? Where did I come from, and whither should I go?

Tears rushed over me, wetting the pillow, my arms, and my hair. A dark interval of bitter thought followed this outburst, yet I felt no regret for the step I had taken, nor any desire to turn back. A strong, vague persuasion dominated my spirit: it was better to move forward than backward, and I *could* move forward. A way, however narrow and difficult, would surely open in time. Its influence soothed my turmoil, bringing me enough tranquility to pray and prepare for sleep.

I had just blown out my candle and lain down when a deep, low, mighty tone swelled through the night. At first, I knew not what it was, but it rang twelve times. On the twelfth colossal hum and trembling knell, I whispered to the silence, "I lie in the shadow of St. Paul's."

The dawn broke on the first of March, a pale and struggling light fighting through a thick, suffocating fog. As I raised my curtain, the heavy, solemn mass of THE DOME hovered above the rooftops, nearly touching the low-hanging clouds, a dark blue orb in the gloom. Yet, as I gazed upon it, a stirring within my soul seemed to loosen the chains that had held me captive. For the first time, I felt a terrifying, exhilarating impulse to live—to breathe the air of existence rather than merely survive its shadows. That morning, my spirit expanded with the rapidity of Jonah's gourd.

"I acted wisely in coming," I told myself, moving quickly to dress with a newfound efficiency. "There is a vitality in this vast London, a spirit that demands to be engaged. Is it not the cowardice of a timid soul to spend a lifetime in a hamlet, allowing one's faculties to rot in the silence of obscurity?"

Dressed and composed, I descended. I was not travel-worn, but refreshened. When the waiter entered with my breakfast, I greeted him with a cheerful civility. We engaged in ten minutes of conversation, a polite exchange that transformed a stranger into a familiar acquaintance. He was an elderly man with grey hair, a veteran of this specific post for twenty years. Learning this, I realized he must remember my uncles, Charles and Wilmot, who had frequented this establishment fifteen years prior. Mentioning their names, I invoked a bond of respect. His demeanor shifted instantly from wary uncertainty to warm, obliging courtesy; he assured me I resembled Uncle Charles, a compliment I accepted with grace. Suddenly, the path ahead was clear, paved with a sense of belonging.

Stepping out into the street, I found it narrow, quiet, and impossibly clean. The few pedestrians were ordinary, provincial types, nothing to fear. A surge of elation filled me; to wander alone through London felt like an adventure in itself. Soon, I found myself in Paternoster Row, the classic thoroughfare of booksellers. Entering a shop kept by a Mr. Jones, I purchased a small volume—a frivolous extravagance I could barely afford, yet one I hoped to gift to Mrs. Barrett someday. Mr. Jones, a dry man of business, stood behind his counter, appearing as contented as I felt.

Then, I ascended the steps to St. Paul's Cathedral and climbed to the dome. From that height, the city unfolded beneath me: the winding river, the bridges, the ancient spires of Westminster, the verdant Temple Gardens bathed in sunlight, and above it all, a sky of hopeful blue pierced by a thin veil of haze. Descending, I allowed chance to guide my footsteps, swept up in a still ecstasy of freedom. I plunged into the heart of the city, ventured onto the Strand, walked up Cornhill, and mingled with the throngs. I dared the chaos of the crossings, stepping into the flow of the crowd entirely alone. There was a raw, irrational pleasure in this immersion. The West End, with its parks and squares, offered amusement, but the City offered excitement. Here, life was earnest; the business, the rush, the roar—they were vital, serious things. The City was working; the West End was merely playing.

By two o'clock, faint and starving for the first time in years, I returned to my dark, silent inn. I dined on a plain joint and vegetables, which tasted infinitely superior to the dainty, insipid meals Miss Marchmont's cook used to send to my late mistress and me, meals that left us with no appetite whatsoever. Exhausted and delighted, I lay across three chairs, as the room lacked a sofa, and slept. Upon waking, I sat in contemplation for two hours.

My mind was clear, my resolve hardened. The circumstances favored a bold, perhaps desperate, new course. I had nothing left to lose. A loathing so profound for my desolate past that returning to it was impossible. If I failed, if I died far from home—who would weep? I was inured to suffering; the terror of death had lost its power over me. Prepared for any consequence, I devised a plan.

That evening, I sought the waiter's advice regarding voyages to Boulogne-sur-Mer. Time was short; I must depart that very night. Though I could wait until morning, I chose not to risk delay. "Take your berth at once, madam," he counseled. I agreed. After settling my bill at a rate that seemed princely to me but absurd to him, acknowledging his service with generous thanks, I summoned a coach. The driver received instructions to take me directly to the wharf, sparing me the watermen. However, the coachman departed immediately upon receipt of his fare, abandoning me to the mercy of the river.

A dark night enveloped the scene. Watermen swarmed around me, shouting oaths that cut through the chill air. They fought for my trunk, their voices shaking my composure. One seized the luggage; I watched calmly. But when another reached for me, I spoke firmly, shook off his hand, and stepped into a waiting boat. I commanded the rower sternly to place my trunk beside me—"Just there"—and he obeyed instantly. An alliance formed, and I was rowed away.

The river was a torrent of black ink, illuminated only by the glimmer of buildings and rocking ships. Lantern light revealed the names of passing vessels: *The Ocean*, *The Phoenix*, *The Consort*, *The Dolphin*. Finally, *The Vivid* appeared, lying further down. As we glided toward her, the black water evoked thoughts of the Styx and Charon ferrying souls to the realm of shades. Amidst the rude rowers, their insane oaths, and the biting wind, I questioned whether I felt wretched or terrified. Neither emotion held sway. Instead, I felt animated, alert, strangely free. "Methinks," I mused, "I am alive, not depressed."

"The Vivid" emerged from the darkness, a white glare against the night. "Here you are!" shouted the waterman, demanding six shillings. "You ask too much," I replied. He pulled away, swearing he would not embark me without payment. A young steward, grinning with anticipation at the coming dispute, leaned over the side. To avoid disappointing him, I paid the sum. Three times that afternoon, I had paid crowns where shillings would suffice, but I comforted myself with the thought, "It is the price of experience."

"They've cheated you!" the steward exclaimed triumphantly as I boarded. I answered phlegmatically, "I knew it," and went below.

In the ladies' cabin, a stout, handsome, and insolent woman awaited me. She scrutinized me with a mix of curiosity and disdain, muttering about the unusual hour of my arrival. Her face was comely, yet her manners were brazen and selfish. "Since I am on board, I shall certainly stay," I declared firmly, requesting my berth. She complied, though sullenly. Arranging my things, I lay down. Difficulties had been overcome; a victory was won, granting my homeless, anchorless mind a brief respite. Until the ship reached harbor, no further action was needed. Yet, beyond that point, the future remained a blank, terrifying void. Harassed and exhausted, I drifted into a half-trance.

Throughout the night, the stewardess chattered incessantly, not to me, but to her young steward, her son and mirror image. They argued, quarreled, and reconciled twenty times in an hour. She pretended to write a letter home to her father, reading passages aloud with feigned secrecy, referring constantly to a younger sister, Charlotte, seemingly poised to make a romantic, imprudent match. The elder lady protested loudly; the dutiful son scoffed. She was a strange creature, perhaps thirty-nine or forty, yet blooming with the vigor of a twenty-year-old, hard, loud, vain, and vulgar. Her mind and body seemed equally indomitable. Born to public life, she might well have started as a barmaid.

Towards dawn, her topic shifted to "the Watsons," a wealthy family party whose fees amounted to a small fortune for her. Boisterous was her welcome to them; great was the bustle in their honor. Four in number, two male and two female, they stood in stark contrast to the other passengers. Rich and confident, the two young women were dressed in garish silks, velvet cloaks, and bonnets adorned with bright flowers—attire suited for parks, not a damp packet deck. Their husbands, short, plain, fat, and vulgar, stood by them. Deep amazement gripped me at this union, especially when I saw the beautiful bride-to-be not despairing, but gay to the point of giddiness. "Her laughter," I reflected, "must be the frenzy of despair."

Before I could process this, she approached me, an utter stranger, holding a camp-stool and offering it to me with a smile of bewildering levity. Declining politely, she danced off, lightsome and heedless. She must have been naturally good-natured, yet one wondered how she could marry a man resembling an oil-barrel.

The other young lady, accompanied by a languid-looking gentleman, presented a different picture. Dressed simply in print and straw, she looked like a Quaker's child, yet appropriate for her station. As I completed my toilette, a knock echoed at the door. "Enter," I called, expecting the chambermaid. Instead, a burly man strode in.

"Gif me your keys, Meess," he grunted.

"Gif!" he snapped impatiently, snatching the keys from my hand as he spoke. "All right! haf your tronc soon."

Fortunately, the situation resolved smoothly; he was from the customs office. Though unsure where to procure breakfast, I hesitantly began my descent. It was then that I truly perceived the scale of the establishment—it was a grand hotel, not merely a modest lodging. As I climbed the sweeping marble staircase, pausing at intervals due to a lack of urgency, I marveled at the opulence surrounding me: the soaring ceilings, the frescoed walls, the vast windows flooding the space with light, and the intricate veining of the stone beneath my feet. This splendor stood in stark, jarring contrast to the tiny, sparsely furnished closet that had been allotted to me.

A philosophical melancholy settled over me. I wondered at the uncanny acumen displayed by the staff in sizing up guests. How did waiters and chambermaids instantly discern my lack of social standing and financial weight? They certainly did; in a split second, they appeared to categorize me as a figure of negligible value. The observation was disquieting, yet I forced myself to maintain a semblance of composure.

Finally arriving in the cavernous hall, bathed in the harsh glare of skylights, I navigated toward what I assumed was the coffee room. Entering, a chill tremor ran through me. I felt isolated, wretched, and plagued by uncertainty regarding the correctness of my actions. It seemed the final step, yet I could not retreat. Embracing a fatalistic calm, I seated myself at a small table, where a waiter promptly delivered breakfast. I ate with little appetite, my frame of mind ill-suited for digestion. The room teemed with other diners, yet I felt a profound sense of alienation unless I could spot a single woman among them; alas, there was not one—all were men. Nevertheless, no one seemed to view my presence as odd. Occasional glances from a gentleman or two suggested they attributed my solitary state to my status as an "Anglaise."

Once breakfast was consumed, the question arose: where next? An inner voice urged, "Go to Villette." This impulse likely stemmed from a careless remark made earlier by Miss Fanshawe, who had mentioned seeking an English governess at Madame Beck's, though she had offered no details on who Madame Beck was or where she resided. Assuming Villette was her destination, I resolved to travel there. Forty miles away, it was a desperate gamble, but in the vast, churning sea of uncertainty in which I found myself, I would grasp at straws—or cobwebs, if necessary. After inquiring about transport and securing a seat on the diligence, I departed, clinging to this shadow of a plan.

Before judging the rashness of this venture, consider the barren desert I had fled and how little I truly risked. It was a game where loss was impossible, and victory, however distant, was possible.

Though I deny being of an artistic temperament, I do possess the ability to savor the pleasure of the present moment, when it aligns with my tastes. I enjoyed that day, despite the slow journey, the biting cold, and the relentless rain. The landscape was stark and flat, devoid of trees, with sludge-covered canals winding like lethargic green serpents alongside the road. Formal pollard willows lined the leveled fields, which resembled kitchen gardens. The sky was a monotonous gray, and the air hung heavy and stagnant. Yet, amidst these suffocating influences, my imagination bloomed anew, and my heart warmed to a private sunshine. These feelings, however, were constantly restrained by a secret, ceaseless anxiety lurking in the shadows like a tiger coiled in the jungle. Its breath whispered in my ear, its heart beating in rhythm with mine, waiting patiently until sunset to spring from its ambush.

I had hoped to reach Villette before nightfall, thereby avoiding the compounded embarrassments of arriving at an unknown place in the dark. However, our sluggish progress, numerous delays, a thick fog, and a driving, dense rain conspired to bury the city in an impenetrable gloom by the time we reached its outskirts.

We passed through a gate guarded by soldiers, visible only by lamplight, then rattled over a pavement of strangely rough, flinty stones. The diligence halted at a bureau, and the passengers disembarked. My immediate concern was retrieving my trunk—a trivial matter to others, but vital to me. Knowing that impatience was counterproductive, I adopted a strategy of patient vigilance. Standing apart, I fixed my gaze on the section of the vehicle where my portmanteau had been stowed, watching as heaps of additional bags and boxes piled up and were unloaded. One by one, they were seized and carried away.

By rights, mine should have been visible by now. It was not. I remembered tying a strip of green ribbon to the handle to identify it at a glance; not a trace of green remained. Every package was cleared away; every tin case and paper-wrapped bundle vanished. The oilcloth cover was lifted, revealing an empty space. Where was my portmanteau, containing my few clothes and the remainder of my fifteen pounds?

I asked this question now, but at that moment, I could not have voiced it. I possessed no speaking French, and the entire world around me seemed to babble a language I could not comprehend. What was to be done? I approached the conductor, placing my hand on his arm and pointing first to the missing trunk, then to the roof, attempting to convey a query through my eyes. He misinterpreted my gesture, grabbed the indicated trunk, and prepared to hoist it back onto the vehicle.

"Let that alone!" a voice cried in perfect English. Then, correcting himself, "Qu'est-ce que vous faîtes donc? Cette malle est à moi."

Hearing the familiar accent of the Fatherland, my heart leaped. I turned to the speaker, ignoring his appearance in my distress. "Sir," I appealed, "I cannot speak French. May I entreat you to ask this man what he has done with my trunk?"

His expression held a mixture of surprise at my plea and doubt regarding the wisdom of intervening.

"Do ask him," I insisted. "I would do as much for you."

Whether he smiled, I could not tell, but he addressed the conductor in a gentlemanly, non-threatening tone. "What sort of trunk was yours?"

I described it, noting the missing green ribbon. Immediately, he took charge, questioning the conductor thoroughly. Through the storm of unintelligible French, I sensed him dismantling the driver's excuses. Soon, he returned to me.

"The fellow claims he was overloaded and admits removing your trunk after you witnessed it being loaded. He has abandoned it at Boue-Marine with other parcels, but promises to forward it tomorrow. Consequently, you will find it safe at this bureau the day after tomorrow."

"Thank you," I said, though my spirits plummeted.

Meanwhile, what was I to do? Perhaps the Englishman noticed the failure of courage in my countenance. Kindly, he inquired, "Have you any friends in this city?"

"No, and I do not know where to go."

In the brief pause that ensued, as he turned fully into the glow of a nearby lamp, I observed that he was a young, distinguished, and handsome man. He might have been a lord; nature had endowed him with qualities suitable for a prince. His face was pleasant, his demeanor high yet not arrogant, manly without being overbearing. I was turning away, acutely aware that I had no right to expect further aid from someone of such stature.

"Was all your money in your trunk?" he asked, stopping me.

Relief washed over me at the chance to speak the truth. "No. I have enough in my purse"—I held nearly twenty francs—"to keep me at a quiet inn until the day after tomorrow. However, I am a complete stranger in Villette and know neither the streets nor the inns."

"I can provide the address of an inn suited to your needs," he offered. "It is not far off; with my directions, you will find it easily."

Tearing a leaf from his pocket-book, he scribbled a few words and handed it to me. I considered him incredibly kind. To distrust him, his advice, or his directions would have been akin to doubting the Bible. There was genuine goodness in his countenance, and honor in his bright eyes.

"The shortest route will be to follow the Boulevard and cross the park," he continued. "However, it is too late and too dark for a woman to traverse the park alone. I will accompany you this far."

He stepped forward, and I followed, walking through the darkness and a fine, soaking rain. The Boulevard was deserted, its path muddy, water dripping from the trees. The park was black as midnight. In the dual gloom of trees and fog, I could barely see my guide; I could only follow the cadence of his footsteps. I felt not the slightest fear; I believed I would have followed that frank stride, through endless night,

Delivered into the care of the *maîtresse*, I was ushered through a long, narrow corridor into a kitchen that was strangely alien, pristine yet bewildering. It lacked any conventional hearth or oven, save for a massive black furnace in one corner that I later learned served as a miraculous substitute. Before I could succumb to the whispers of pride, a sense of relief washed over me: instead of remaining in the kitchen, as I had half-feared, I was guided forward into a small inner chamber designated a "cabinet." A cook, dressed in a jacket, a short petticoat, and wooden clogs (*sabots*), brought my supper. It consisted of meat of unknown origin, served in an oddly acidic yet agreeable sauce; chopped potatoes seasoned with vinegar and sugar; a tartine of buttered bread; and a baked pear. Hungry and grateful, I ate.

Following the evening prayer, Madame Beck herself descended to inspect me again, requesting that I accompany her upstairs. We traversed a series of peculiar, diminutive dormitories—rumored to have once been nuns' cells, given the building's antiquity—and passed through an oratory. This was a long, low, gloomy room where a pale crucifix hung against the wall, flanked by two tapers keeping a dim vigil. She conducted me to an apartment where three children slept soundly in tiny beds. The air, stifled by a heated stove, was thick with an overpowering scent, a perfume surprisingly discordant for the circumstances: a blend of smoke and some volatile spirit, akin to the smell of whisky.

Beside a table, where a candle sputtered its last flicker, sat a coarse woman, garbed heterogeneously in a broad-striped, ostentatious silk dress and a heavy apron, fast asleep. To complete the scene, a bottle and an empty glass rested at her elbow. Madame Beck surveyed this remarkable tableau with profound calm. Neither a smile nor a frown distorted her grave countenance; no trace of anger, disgust, or surprise marred her equanimity. She did not even disturb the sleeper. With serene deliberation, she pointed to a fourth bed, intimating it was to be mine. After extinguishing the candle and replacing it with a night-lamp, she glided through an inner door she left ajar—the entrance to her own chamber, a large, well-appointed suite discernible through the opening.

That night, my devotions were entirely ones of thanksgiving. My journey since morning had been fraught with strangeness, my provisions unexpectedly generous. Scarcely could I believe that less than forty-eight hours had passed since I left London, a passenger-bird with no guardian but the vague protections of transit, drifting upon the dubious tracery of hope.

A light sleeper, I awoke suddenly in the dead of night. The room was hushed, yet a white figure stood therein—Madame, in her night-dress. Moving with imperceptible sound, she visited the three children, then approached me. Feigning sleep, I watched her study me for a long time. A curious pantomime ensued; she likely sat for a quarter-hour on the edge of my bed, gazing at my face. Drawing nearer, she leaned close, slightly lifting my cap to turn back the border and expose my hair, examining it. She inspected my hand resting on the bedclothes. Having completed this scrutiny, she turned to the chair at the foot of the bed where my clothes lay. Hearing her touch and lift them, I cautiously opened my eyes, driven by curiosity to see where her investigative zeal would lead. It led her far: she examined every article. I surmised her motive—to judge the wearer, her station, resources, and neatness—from the garments. The intent was not evil, but the methods were hardly fair. She turned my pocket inside out, counted the coins in my purse, and opened a small memorandum book, coolly perusing its contents until she extracted a small, plaited lock of Miss Marchmont's grey hair. To a bunch of three keys—for my trunk, desk, and work-box—she paid special attention. Indeed, she withdrew a moment to her own room with them. Rising softly, I followed her gaze: these keys were not returned until they had left impressions of their wards in the wax of the toilet in the adjoining room. Once everything was done decently and in order, my property was restored, my clothes carefully refolded. What conclusions did she draw? Were they favorable? These questions were vain. Madame's face, in its nocturnal aspect, resembled stone; whereas it had been human and motherly in the salon, it now revealed no response whatsoever.

Having fulfilled her duty—a burden she viewed strictly as such—she rose, noiseless as a shadow, and moved toward her chamber. At the door, she turned, fixing her eye on the heroine of the bottle, who still snoored loudly. This woman, I presumed, was Mrs. Sweeny, perhaps Anglicized or Hibernicized. In Madame Beck's eye, Mrs. Sweeny's doom was sealed; an immutable purpose radiated from her gaze. Madame's visits for shortcomings might be slow, but they were inevitable. This was thoroughly un-English; I was clearly in a foreign land.

The following day deepened my acquaintance with Mrs. Sweeny. It appeared she had introduced herself to her employer as an English lady in reduced circumstances, a native of Middlesex speaking the tongue with a pure metropolitan accent. Madame, relying on her own infallible methods for uncovering truth, displayed a singular intrepidity in hiring service off-hand, as evidenced by my own case. She accepted Mrs. Sweeny as nursery-governess to her three children. Need I explain that this lady was, in effect, a native of Ireland, her actual station indeterminate? She boldly claimed to have "had the bringing-up of the son and daughter of a marquis." I suspect she might have been a hanger-on, nurse, foster-mother, or washer-woman in some Irish family. She spoke a smothered tongue, curiously overlaid with mincing Cockney inflections. Somehow, she had acquired and retained a wardrobe of suspicious splendor: gowns of stiff, costly silk that fitted her indifferently, seemingly made for other proportions; caps with real lace borders; and, the chief item in her inventory and the spell by which she struck awe through the household, quelling the otherwise scornful teachers and servants, and influencing Madame herself so long as her broad shoulders bore the folds—"a real Indian shawl," a "véritable cachemire"—she maintained her footing. Without this shawl, Madame noted with unmixed reverence and amazement, she would not have lasted two days; by virtue of it alone, she persisted for a month.

However, when Mrs. Sweeny discovered I had arrived to fill her shoes, she declared herself fully. Then she rose against Madame Beck in her full power, descending upon me with her concentrated weight. Madame bore this revelation and visitation with such stoicism that I, ashamed, could support it only with composure. For one brief moment, Madame Beck absent herself from the room. Ten minutes later, a police officer stood amidst us. Mrs. Sweeny and her effects were removed. Throughout the scene, Madame's brow remained unruffled, her lips dropping not a single sharply accented word.

This brisk affair of dismissal was settled before breakfast. Order to march was given, the policeman called, the mutineer expelled; the "chambre d'enfans" was fumigated, cleansed, windows thrown open, and every trace of the accomplished Mrs. Sweeny—even the fine essence and spiritual fragrance that gave token of her head and front—was annihilated from the Rue Fossette. All this transpired between the moment Madame Beck issued forth like Aurora from her chamber and the instant she coolly sat down to pour her first cup of coffee.

About noon, I was summoned to dress Madame. (It appeared my role was a hybrid between *gouvernante* and lady's-maid.) Until noon, she haunted the house in her wrapping-gown, shawl, and soundless slippers. How would the lady-chief of an English school approve of this custom?

The dressing of her hair puzzled me. She had plenty of it, auburn and unmixed with grey, though she was forty. Seeing my embarrassment, she said, "You have not been a *femme-de-chambre* in your own country?" Taking the brush from my hand and setting me aside, not ungently, she arranged it herself. In performing other offices of the toilet, she half-directed, half-aided me, displaying no temper or impatience. Note: That was the first and last time I was required to dress her. Henceforth, that duty devolved upon Rosine, the portress.

When attired, Madame Beck appeared a personage of a figure somewhat short and stout, yet still graceful in a peculiar way, possessing the grace resulting from proportion of parts. Her complexion was fresh and sanguine, not too rubicund; her eye, blue and serene; her dark silk dress fitted her as only a French seamstress can. She looked well, though a little bourgeois; indeed, she was bourgeois. I know not what harmony pervaded her whole person, yet her face offered a striking contrast: its features were not usually seen in conjunction with a complexion of such blended freshness and repose. Their outline was stern. Her forehead was high but narrow, expressing capacity and some benevolence, but no expanse. Nor did her peaceful yet watchful eye ever know the fire kindled in the heart or the softness flowing thence.

My days were now filled with purposeful industry. Between instructing others and sharpening my own intellect, I possessed scarcely a moment of idleness. It was a satisfying regimen; I felt my faculties growing, polished by constant use rather than decaying into the stagnant mold of inaction. Before me lay a vast field of experience. Villette is a cosmopolitan hub, and within Madame Beck's school gathered girls of nearly every European nation and rank, from the lowest to the highest. While the institution was not republican in form, equality reigned in substance. At the desks of this establishment, a young countess sat side by side with a young bourgeoise, indistinguishable to the casual observer save for subtle nuances in demeanor. Often, the lower-born displayed franker, warmer courtesy, whereas the nobility exuded a delicate, chilling blend of insolence and deceit. There was frequently a dash of quick French blood mixed with the marshy phlegm of the north in these aristocratic souls; alas, this vivacious mixture manifested chiefly in the oily, glib delivery of flattery and fabrication—light, lively, yet utterly heartless and insincere.

Justice must be done to all parties, however. Even the honest, native daughters of Labassecour possessed their own brand of hypocrisy, albeit of a coarse order. Whenever a lie served their ends, they produced it with a careless breadth, wholly untroubled by the sting of conscience. No soul in Madame Beck's house, from the scullion to the director herself, harbored shame for a fib. To invent a falsehood was not precisely a virtue, but it was the most venial of faults. "I have lied several times" formed a standard item in every girl's and woman's monthly confession; the priest heard it without shock and absolved them readily. Conversely, missing mass or reading a chapter of a novel constituted a true crime, warranting rebuke and penance.

Though I was only half-aware of this moral landscape, I progressed well in my new sphere. After the initial difficulties of lessons taught amidst a moral volcano that rumbled beneath my feet, sending sparks of anxiety into my eyes, the eruptive spirit seemed to subside regarding me personally. My mind was fixated on success; I could not tolerate the thought of being thwarted by undisciplined rebellion and willful indocility in my first venture into life. Many nights I lay awake, devising strategies to gain a reliable hold over these mutineers, to bend this stiff-necked tribe under permanent influence. First and foremost, I realized that aid of any kind was impossible from Madame. Her righteous plan was to maintain unbroken popularity with the pupils at any cost of justice or comfort to the teachers. For a teacher to seek her alliance during a crisis of insubordination was tantamount to securing one's own expulsion. Madame took only what was pleasant, amiable, and commendatory from her pupils, while rigorously requiring her lieutenants to suffice for every annoying disturbance, where acting with inadequate promptitude meant becoming unpopular. Thus, I must rely solely on myself.

It was clear as daylight that this swinish multitude could not be driven by force. They required humor, patient endurance. A courteous yet sedate manner impressed them; a rare flash of raillery did good. Severe or continuous mental application they could not, or would not, bear; heavy demands on memory, reason, or attention were rejected point-blank. Where an English girl of average capacity would quietly take a theme and bind herself to the task of comprehension, a Labassecourienne would laugh in your face and toss it back with the phrase, "God, how difficult! I do not want it. It annoys me too much."

A teacher who understood her craft would accept the rejection instantly, without hesitation or contest, proceeding with exaggerated care to smooth every difficulty, reducing it to the level of their understanding, returning it modified, and laying on the lash of sarcasm with an unsparing hand. They would feel the sting, perhaps wince slightly, but they bore no malice against this sort of attack, provided the sneer was not sour but hearty, and presented clearly, boldly, and brightly so that their incapacity, ignorance, and sloth might be read like print. They would riot over adding three extra lines to a lesson, but I never knew them to rebel against a wound to their self-respect: the little they had of that quality was trained to be crushed, and it preferred the pressure of a firm heel.

Gradually, as I acquired fluency and freedom in their language, applying its more nervous idioms to suit their case, the older and more intelligent girls began to like me in their own way. I noticed that whenever a pupil was roused to feel the stirring of worthy emulation or the quickening of honest shame within her soul, she was won. If I could but once make their large ears burn beneath their thick, glossy hair, all was comparatively well. Soon, bouquets began to be laid on my desk in the mornings. In acknowledgment of this foreign attention, I sometimes walked with a select few during recreation. It happened, in the course of conversation, that I made an unpremeditated attempt to rectify some of their singularly distorted notions of principle, specifically expressing my views on the evil and baseness of a lie. In an unguarded moment, I chanced to say that, of two errors, I considered falsehood worse than an occasional lapse in church attendance. The poor girls were tutored to report in Catholic ears whatever the Protestant teacher said. An edifying consequence ensued. Something—an unseen, indefinite, nameless entity—stole between myself and these my best pupils. The bouquets continued to be offered, but conversation thereafter became impracticable. As I paced the alleys or sat in the *berceau*, a girl never came to my right hand unless, by magic, a teacher appeared at my left. Furthermore, wonderful to relate, Madame's shoes of silence brought her continually to my back, as quick, as noiseless, and as unexpected as some wandering zephyr.

The opinion of my Catholic acquaintances concerning my spiritual prospects was somewhat naively expressed to me on one occasion. A pensionnaire, to whom I had rendered some little service, exclaimed one day as she sat beside me: "Mademoiselle, what a pity you are a Protestant!"

"Why, Isabelle?"

"Because, when you shall be dead—you will burn immediately in Hell."

"Do you believe that?"

"Certainly that I believe it; everyone knows it; moreover, the priest told me so."

Isabelle was an odd, blunt little creature. She added, *sotto voce*: "To ensure your salvation up there, it would be well to burn you alive here below."

I laughed, as indeed it was impossible to do otherwise.

Most of her other faculties seemed to be in the same flimsy condition: her likings and dislikings, her loves and hates, were mere cobwebs and gossamers; but she had one thing about her that seemed strong and durable enough, and that was her selfishness.

She was not proud; and, *bonne d'enfants* as I was, she would forthwith have made of me a sort of friend and confidant. She teased me with a thousand vapid complaints about school quarrels and household economy: the cookery was not to her taste; the people around her, teachers and pupils alike, she held to be despicable because they were foreigners. Much longer did I have to endure her demands on me in the way of work. She cried upon receiving this information and accused me of having ceased to be her friend; but I held by my decision and let the hysterics pass as they could.

Madame Beck was a creature of iron consistency, a fortress of forbearance that knew no deviation, let alone tenderness. Even her own children, the blood of her veins, failed to soften the hard lines of her stoic calm. She was, undoubtedly, a vigilant guardian: solicitous for their safety, sharp-eyed regarding their futures, and endlessly practical. She never sought to draw them close, to press their cheeks against hers, or to wrap them in the gentle warmth of a maternal embrace. To her, love was a matter of administration, not feeling.

I once watched her from the garden bench, gazing at a cluster of bees laboring in the distance alongside Trinette, their *bonne*. Her expression was one of pure calculation, a mask of prudence masking deep anxiety for "leur avenir"—their future. She allowed the child to hover for a fleeting second, offering no kiss, no word of comfort, before rising with mechanical efficiency to return the toddler to Trinette. She rarely corrected Désirée's behavior directly, nor explained the harm of such habits; instead, she relied on constant surveillance, a strategy that ultimately failed. While the child was kept at arm's length from the household staff, she turned her mischief inward, stealing trinkets from her mother's dressing table and hiding them. The dynamic shifted dramatically when Désirée, displaying her innate talent for theatricality, declared herself ill. Behind the house at the Rue Fossette stretched a garden, surprisingly spacious for a courtyard nestled in the throat of a bustling city. To me, it remains a sanctuary of memory, though time, like distance, possesses a softening alchemy that turns stone and mortar into something tender. In a world of blank walls and scorching pavements, such an enclosed, planted paradise seems a divine gift.

Legend spoke of Madame Beck's former life as a convent. Centuries ago, before the city sprawled over this quarter, transforming fields and avenues into urban density, a tragedy had occurred here. Some vague, chilling tale persisted of a black-and-white nun, glimpsed in the shadows on certain nights, haunting the vicinity. Though the surrounding area was now choked with houses, ancient relics survived: massive, gnarled fruit trees, consecrated by age, stood sentinel. Beneath the roots of a great pear tree, a "Methuselah" that still bloomed with perfumed snow in spring and honeyed pendants in autumn, one could scrape away the moss to reveal a slab of smooth, hard, black stone. Unconfirmed, unverified, yet fiercely propagated, the rumor ran that this was the portal to a vault, sealing beneath the flower-strewn earth the bones of a girl buried alive by a monkish conclave for some sin against her vows. Her shadow, it was said, terrified the timid, her black robe and white veil mocking the moonlit air as they swayed in the garden-thicket.

Yet, stripped of this romantic rubbish, the garden possessed genuine charms. On summer mornings, I rose early to claim it alone; on summer evenings, I lingered solitarily, keeping trysts with the rising moon or tasting the cool kiss of the evening breeze. The turf was emerald, the gravel paths bleached white, and sun-bright nasturtiums clustered beautifully around the legs of the doddered orchard giants. A large *berceau* offered shade beneath an acacia, while a smaller, more secluded bower nestled in the vines running along the high grey wall, gathering tendrils in knots of beauty and hanging clusters of jasmine and ivy in loving profusion.

Certainly, at high noon, when Madame Beck's school erupted into chaos, with externes and pensionnaires vying with the local boys' college in a brazen exercise of lungs and limbs, the garden was merely a trite, trodden-down thoroughfare. But at sunset, or during the hour of *salut*, when the externes had returned home and the boarders were silent at their studies, it was delightful to wander the peaceful alleys, listening to the sweet, soft, exalted tolling of St. Jean Baptiste's bells.

One evening, I walked thus, detained by the deepening calm, the mellow coolness, and the fragrant breathing of flowers answering the persuasion of the dew. A light glowed in the oratory window, signaling that the Catholic household was gathered for evening prayer—a ritual from which I, as a Protestant, occasionally exempted myself.

"One moment longer," whispered solitude and the summer moon. "Stay with us: all is truly quiet now. Your presence will not be missed for another quarter of an hour. Enjoy these precious minutes."

The windowless backs of the houses bordering the garden, particularly the entire length of one side, were skirted by the rear of a long line of premises belonging to the neighboring college. This rear facade was mostly blank stone, save for certain attic loopholes high up, serving the sleeping rooms of the women-servants, and one casement in a lower story said to belong to a master's chamber or study. Secure though it appeared, an alley ran parallel to this high wall, forbidden to the pupils. Known as "l'allée défendue" (the forbidden walk), any girl venturing there risked penalties as severe as the mild rules of Madame Beck's establishment allowed. Teachers might enter with impunity, but the walk was narrow, flanked by neglected shrubs grown thick and close, weaving a roof of branch and leaf that let in the sun only in rare, chequered patches. After dusk, it was carefully shunned.

From the start, I felt drawn to break this rule of avoidance. The seclusion, the profound gloom of the path, attracted me. For a long time, the fear of appearing singular held me back; but gradually, as people became accustomed to me and the peculiar shades of my nature—shades not striking enough to intrigue, perhaps not prominent enough to offend, yet born with me and inseparable from my identity—I became a frequent visitor. I took charge of some colorless flowers growing between the closely ranked shrubs, clearing away the relics of past autumns to free a rustic seat at the far end. Borrowing a pail of water and a scrubbing brush from Goton, the *cuisinière*, I cleaned the seat until it sparkled. Madame observed my work and smiled with apparent approval.

"You see," she exclaimed, "how clean and neat this young lady, Miss Lucie, is! You love this alley, *mes enfant*?"

"Yes," I replied, "it is quiet and shady."

"C'est juste," she cried with an air of *bonté*. She kindly recommended that I confine myself to it as much as I pleased, noting that since I was not charged with surveillance, I need not accompany the pupils. However, she added that I might allow her children to come there to speak English with me.

That night, I sat on the reclaimed seat, listening to what seemed the far-off sounds of the city. They were not far off; the school lay in the city's center, mere minutes from the park and ten from buildings of palatial splendor. Wide streets nearby were brightly lit and teeming with life; carriages rolled through them bound for balls or the opera. While curfew tolled for our convent, extinguishing lamps and drawing curtains around couches, it rang out for the gay city as a summons to festal enjoyment. I thought not of this contrast, for my nature lacked gay instincts. I had never seen a ball or the opera, and though I had heard them described and wished to see them, it was not the feverish yearning to attain, the hunger to taste, but the calm desire simply to look upon a new thing.

A moon hung in the sky—not full, but a young crescent. I saw her through a gap in the overhead boughs. She and the stars beside her were no strangers where everything else was alien; my childhood knew them well. I remembered that golden sign with the dark globe leaning back on azure, beside an old thorn atop an old field in Old England, just as it now leaned beside a stately spire in this continental capital.

Oh, my childhood! I had feelings: passive as I lived, little as I spoke, cold as I looked, when I thought of past days, I *could* feel. About the present, it was better to be stoical; about the future—such a future as mine—to be dead. And in a state of cataleptic trance, I studiously held the quick of my nature in suspension.

I well remember what excited me then: certain accidents of weather, nearly dreaded, for they woke the being I was always lulling, stirring up a craving cry I could not satisfy. One night, a thunderstorm broke; a sort of hurricane shook us in our beds. The Catholics rose in panic, praying to their saints. As for me, the tempest took hold with tyranny. I was roughly roused and forced to live. Creeping outside the casement by my bed, I sat on its ledge, my feet resting on the roof of a lower adjoining building. It was wet, wild, and pitch-black. Inside, the dormitory gathered round the night-lamp in consternation, praying aloud. I could not go in; resistless was the delight of staying with the wild hour, black and full of thunder, pealing out an ode that language had never delivered to man—too terribly glorious, the spectacle of clouds split and pierced by white, blinding bolts.

I did long, achingly, then and for twenty-four hours thereafter, for something to fetch me out of my present existence, leading me upwards and onwards. This longing, and all of a similar kind, it was necessary to knock on the head, which I did, figuratively, after the manner of Jael to Sisera, driving a nail through their temples. Unlike Sisera, they did not die; they were but transiently stunned, turning on the nail at intervals with a rebellious wrench, making the temples bleed and the brain thrill to its core.

Tonight, I was not so mutinous, nor so miserable. My Sisera lay quiet in the tent, slumbering. If his pain ached through his slumbers, something like an angel—the Ideal—knelt near, dropping balm on the soothed temples, holding before the sealed eyes a magic glass wherein sweet, solemn visions were repeated in dreams, shedding a reflex from her moonlight wings and robe over the transfixed sleeper, over the tent threshold, over all the landscape lying without. Jael, the stern woman, sat apart, relenting somewhat over her captive, but more prone to dwell on the faithful expectation of Heber coming home. By which words I mean that the cool peace and dewy sweetness of the night filled me with a mood of hope: not hope on any definite point, but a general sense of encouragement and heart-ease.

Villette possesses a climate as fickle, though perhaps less damp, as any English town. A night of fierce winds gave way to a subsequent day of dry tempest; the air was thick with darkness and cloud, yet devoid of rain. The streets were dim, choked with sand and dust swirling from the boulevards. Even if the weather had been fair, I doubted I would have relished spending my evening of study and leisure in the garden where I had resided yesterday. My alley, and indeed every walk and shrubbery within the grounds, had acquired a novel, albeit unwelcome, interest. Their seclusion had become precarious; their tranquility insecure. That casement, which had previously showered me with billets, had vulgarized the once cherished nook it overlooked. Elsewhere, the eyes of the flowers seemed to have gained sight, and the knots in the tree-trunks listened like secretive ears. Some plants, alas, had been trampled by Dr. John in his frantic search, his hurried and heedless progress leaving chaos in its wake. Though I desired to prop up, water, and revive what he had disturbed, I found some plants crushed. Furthermore, he had left footprints on the flower beds. Despite the strong wind, I managed to erase these traces with a brief moment's leisure early in the morning, before prying eyes could discover them. With a pensive sort of contentment, I seated myself at my desk to tackle my German as the pupils settled into their evening lessons, while the other teachers picked up their needlework.

The setting for the "evening study" was invariably the refectory, a significantly smaller apartment than any of the three schoolrooms. Here, access was restricted solely to the boarders, numbering merely a dozen. Two lamps hung from the ceiling above two tables; their lighting at dusk signaled the abandonment of schoolbooks, the adoption of a grave demeanor, the enforcement of general silence, and the commencement of "la lecture pieuse." As I soon discovered, this so-called "pious reading" was primarily designed as a wholesome mortification of the intellect and a useful humiliation of reason—a heavy dose for Common Sense to digest at her leisure.

The volume produced (which was never changed, but recommenced upon finishing) was a venerable tome, as old as the hills and grey as the Hôtel de Ville. I would have given two francs for the chance to seize that book, turn over its sacred yellow pages, ascertain the title, and read with my own eyes the monstrous fabrications that, as an unworthy heretic, I was only permitted to consume with bewildered ears. This book contained legends of the saints. Good heavens! (I speak these words reverently) what legends they were. What brazen rascals those saints must have been if they first boasted of these exploits or invented these miracles. These legends were nothing more than monkish extravaganzas, over which one laughed inwardly; alongside them were priestly matters, and the priestcraft of the book was far worse than its monkery. My ears burned on either side of my head as I listened, forced to endure tales of moral martyrdom inflicted by Rome; the dread boasts of confessors who had wickedly abused their office, trampling high-born ladies into deep degradation, making countesses and princesses the most tormented slaves under the sun. Stories like that of Conrad and Elizabeth of Hungary recurred again and again, laden with dreadful viciousness, sickening tyranny, and black impiety—nightmares of oppression, privation, and agony.

I sat out several evenings of this "pious reading" as quietly and patiently as possible, breaking off the points of my scissors only once by inadvertently stabbing them deeply into the worm-eaten wood of the table. But eventually, the heat became unbearable. My temples, heart, and wrist throbbed so violently, and my sleep afterwards was shattered by excitement, that I could no longer remain. Prudence dictated henceforth a swift clearance of my person from the premises the moment the guilty old book was brought out. Never did Mause Headrigg feel a stronger call to testify against Sergeant Bothwell than I did to voice my opinion regarding the popish "lecture pieuse." Nevertheless, I managed somehow to curb and rein in my impulses. Though I always shot from the room quickly as soon as Rosine lit the lamps, I did so quietly, seizing the vantage moment afforded by the slight bustle preceding the dead silence, vanishing while the boarders stowed their books away.

When I vanished, I entered darkness. Candles were not permitted to be carried about, and the teacher who abandoned the refectory possessed only the unlit hall, schoolroom, or bedroom as a refuge. In winter, I sought the long classrooms, pacing them rapidly to maintain warmth—fortunate if the moon shone and stars were present, soon reconciled to their dim gleam or even to their total absence. In summer, it was never truly dark, and I ascended to my quarter of the long dormitory, opened my casement (that chamber was illuminated by five casements as wide as great doors), and leaned out to gaze upon the city beyond the garden, listening to band music drifting from the park or palace square, meanwhile entertaining my own thoughts, living my own life, within my own still, shadow-world.

That evening, fleeing as usual before the Pope and his works, I mounted the staircase, approached the dormitory, and quietly opened the door, which was always kept carefully shut and revolved noiselessly on well-oiled hinges. Before I *saw*, I *felt* that life was present in the great room, usually void. Not that there was stir, breath, or rustle of sound, but vacuum lacked; solitude was not at home. All the white beds—the "lits d'ange," as they were poetically termed—lay visible at a glance; all were empty; no sleeper reposed therein. The sound of a drawer sliding out cautiously struck my ear. Stepping slightly to one side, my vision took a free range, unimpeded by falling curtains. I now commanded my own bed and my own toilet, with a locked workbox upon it and locked drawers underneath.

There stood a dumpy, motherly little figure, clad in a decent shawl and the cleanest possible nightcap, working diligently before this toilet, apparently performing me the kindness of "tidying out" the "meuble." The lid of the workbox stood open, the top drawer ajar; duly and impartially was each succeeding drawer opened in turn. Not a single article of their contents was lifted and unfolded, not a paper glanced over, not a little box unlidded; and beautiful was the adroitness, exemplary the care with which the search was conducted. Madame worked at it like a true star, "unhasting yet unresting." I will not deny that I watched her with a secret glee. Had I been a gentleman, I believe Madame would have found favor in my eyes; she was so handy, neat, and thorough in everything she did. Some people's movements provoke the soul by their loose awkwardness, but hers satisfied it by their trim compactness. I stood, in short, fascinated; but it was necessary to make an effort to break this spell, for a retreat must be beaten. The searcher might have turned and caught me; there would have been nothing for it then but a scene, and she and I would have had to confront each other suddenly, stripped bare of conventions and disguises. We would have looked into each other's eyes and known that we could work together no more, and parted for eternity.

Where was the use of tempting such a catastrophe? I was not angry, nor did I harbor any wish to leave her. I could hardly find another employer whose yoke would be so light and easy of carriage; truly, I liked Madame for her capital sense, whatever I might think of her principles. As to her system, it did me no harm; she might work me with it to her heart's content, and nothing would come of the operation. Loverless and inexpectant of love, I was as safe from spies in my heart-poverty as the beggar from thieves in his destitution of purse. Turning, then, I fled, descending the stairs with progress as swift and soundless as that of a spider running down the banister at the same instant.

How I laughed when I reached the schoolroom. I knew now she had certainly seen Dr. John in the garden; I knew what her thoughts were. The spectacle of a suspicious nature so far misled by its own inventions tickled me greatly. Yet, as the laugh died, a kind of wrath smote me, followed by bitterness. It was the rock struck, and Meribah's waters gushing out. I had never felt such a strange and contradictory inward tumult as I did that evening: soreness and laughter, fire, and grief shared my heart between them. Hot tears welled up—not because Madame mistrusted me (I did not care twopence for her mistrust)—but for other reasons. Complicated, disquieting thoughts disrupted the entire repose of my nature. However, that turmoil subsided; by the next day, I was again Lucy Snowe.

As soon as Georgette recovered, Madame sent her away to the countryside. I missed the child deeply; her departure left me poorer in spirit than before, though I must not complain. The Parisienne, conversely, was prodigal and profligate in disposition. Her venomous nature revealed itself to me but once, like a snake cautiously peering from the foliage of a bad novel. Had it emerged boldly, I might have faced it with philosophical calm, surveying it from forked tongue to scaly tail-tip; but it merely rustled in the shadows of a cheap romance, recoiling at a hasty demonstration of wrath before vanishing with a hiss. From that day, she hated me. She was perpetually in debt, her salary anticipated on dress, perfume, cosmetics, and confectionery. She was a cold, callous epicure, thin-faced and sallow, with a large, prominent chin and a frozen, light-filled eye that seemed both craving and ingrate. She mortally hated work, loving instead what she termed "pleasure"—an insipid, heartless dissipation of time.

Madame Beck knew this woman's character perfectly. Speaking of her, Madame mixed discrimination, indifference, and antipathy. When I asked why she retained her, Madame answered plainly, "because it suited her interest to do so." She pointed out that Mademoiselle St. Pierre possessed a unique power to maintain order among the undisciplined scholars. A petrifying influence surrounded her; without passion, noise, or violence, she held them in check like a breezeless frost-still air. While of little use in communicating knowledge, she was invaluable for strict surveillance. The school was a strange, frolicsome, noisy little world where chains were hidden with flowers. A subtle essence of Romanism pervaded every arrangement, allowing large sensual indulgence as a counterpoise to jealous spiritual restraint. Every mind was being reared in slavery, yet pretexts for physical recreation were seized upon to prevent reflection on this fact. "Eat, drink, and live!" the Church seemed to say. "Look after your bodies; leave your souls to me. I hold their cure—guide their course: I guarantee their final fate." It was a bargain wherein every true Catholic deemed himself a gainer, much like Lucifer's offer: "All this power will I give thee... If thou, therefore, wilt worship me, all shall be thine!"

In the ripest glow of summer, Madame Beck's house became as merry as a school could well be. Broad folding-doors and casements stood wide open; sunshine seemed naturalized in the atmosphere, while clouds sailed away beyond the sea, resting round islands such as England, withdrawn wholly from the drier continent. We lived far more in the garden than under a roof. Classes were held, and meals partaken of, in the "grand berceau." A note of holiday preparation turned freedom almost into licence. The autumnal long vacation was but two months distant, but before that, a great day awaited: the *fête* of Madame.

The conduct of this *fête* devolved chiefly on Mademoiselle St. Pierre, who supposedly stood aloof, disinterestedly unconscious of what was happening in her honor. She never suspected that a subscription was annually levied on the whole school to purchase a handsome present. In a brief, secret consultation in Madame's chamber, the plan was finalized. When asked what to buy, Madame looked benign and modest, suggesting silver spoons and forks. St. Pierre, protruding her chin and calling Madame's airs of "bonté" "grimaces," expedited the decision. The result was a handsome case containing 300 francs' worth of plate.

The program comprised the presentation of the plate, a collation in the garden, a dramatic performance, a dance, and a supper. Zélie St. Pierre, who understood these things, managed them ably. The play was the main point, requiring a month of drilling. The choice of actors and lessons in elocution and attitude necessitated further management, supplied by M. Paul Emanuel, the professor of literature. I often saw him crossing the *carré*, the square hall between the houses, and heard him lecturing in the warm evenings. Our former acquaintance, Miss Ginevra Fanshawe, who was selected for a prominent part, frequently larded her discourse with allusions to him. She esteemed him hideously plain and claimed to be frightened almost into hysterics by his step or voice. He was a dark little man, pungent and austere, with a close-shorn black head, a broad sallow brow, thin cheeks, and wide, quivering nostrils. Irritable and harsh, he apostrophized the "awkward squad" with vehemence. "Vous n'êtes donc que des poupées," he thundered. "Vous n'avez pas de passions—vous autres. Votre chair est de neige, votre sang de glace! Moi, je veux que tout cela s'allume, qu'il ait une vie, une âme!"

When he realized his resolve was vain, he suddenly abandoned the grand tragedy for a compact little comic trifle, which he promptly knocked into their smooth round heads. Mademoiselle St. Pierre always presided at M. Emanuel's lessons, and I was told her polished manner, seeming attention, and tact impressed him favorably, though her feelings dried like dew in an hour.

The day preceding the *fête* was as much a holiday as the day itself, devoted to clearing, cleaning, and decorating the schoolrooms. For my part, I took refuge in the garden, wandering alone and finding warmth in the sun and shelter among the trees. I exchanged but two sentences with any living being, content to be quiet. Looking on, it sufficed to observe the changes being wrought: green-rooms and dressing-rooms contrived, a little stage erected, and an eager band of pupils, including Ginevra Fanshawe, working gaily under the joint direction of M. Paul Emanuel and Mademoiselle St. Pierre.

The great day arrived. The sun rose hot and unclouded and burned until evening. All doors and windows were open, granting a pleasant sense of summer freedom. Teachers and pupils descended to breakfast in dressing-gowns and curl-papers, anticipating the evening's toilette with a luxury of slovenliness. About nine o'clock, the "coiffeur" arrived, fixing his headquarters in the oratory amidst benitiers and candles to solemnize the mysteries of his art. Each girl was summoned in turn, emerging with heads as smooth as shells, intersected by faultless white lines and wreathed with Grecian plaits. I took my turn, marveling at the garlandry of woven brown hair that seemed to defy the laws of physics, fearing it was not all my own.

The dormitory became the scene of elaborate ablutions and bedizenings. To me, it remained an enigma how they spent so much time doing so little. The result was simple: a clear white muslin dress, a blue sash (the Virgin's colors), and white or straw-colored kid gloves. Three mortal hours were devoted to this gala uniform, which, though simple, was perfect in fashion, fit, and freshness. Every head was dressed with exquisite nicety, suiting the full, firm comeliness of Labassecourien contours, though too stiff for any more flowing style.

Beholding this diaphanous and snowy mass, I felt myself a mere shadowy spot on a field of light. I lacked the courage to put on a transparent white dress; the weather and rooms were too hot for substantial fabrics. Instead, I had sought through a dozen shops for a crape-like material of purple-gray—the color of dun mist lying on a blooming moor. I had no flower, no jewel to relieve it, and no natural rose of complexion. Yet, in this gown of shadow, I felt at home and at ease. In the wake of Madame Beck's fête, with its three weeks of uneasy respite, its fleeting twelve-hour explosion of gaiety and indulgence, and the single, droning day of exhaustion that followed, a profound reaction set in. Two months of rigorous application, of intense and unyielding study, descended upon the school. These final months of the "school year" were the only authentic working season of the calendar. Into them, driven by professors, mistresses, and pupils alike, the entire burden of preparation for the upcoming examinations—and consequently the distribution of prizes—was funneled. Those seeking accolades were compelled to labor with genuine earnestness; masters and teachers had to brace themselves, pushing the lagging students forward and cultivating the promising ones. A grand display, a compelling exhibition, was demanded for public view, and every available means was deemed legitimate for this purpose.

I paid scant attention to how the other teachers embarked upon their tasks; I had my own business to attend to. My undertaking was not the least onerous: it was to imbue approximately ninety sets of minds with what they considered a highly complex and difficult science—the English language—and to drill ninety tongues in an almost impossible pronunciation, specifically the lisping and hissing dentals of the Islands.

The day of examination finally arrived. An awful day! It had been prepared for with anxious deliberation and dressed for with silent dispatch; there was nothing vaporous or fluttering now, no white gauze or azure streamers. The order of attire was grave, close, and compact. To me, it seemed I was especially doomed this day—the main burden and trial falling solely upon me among all the female teachers. The others were not expected to examine in the subjects they taught; the Professor of Literature, M. Paul, undertook this duty upon himself. He, the school autocrat, gathered all and sundry reins into the hollow of his single hand; he fiercely rejected any colleague; he would not have assistance. Madame herself, who evidently wished to conduct the examination in geography—her favorite subject, which she taught well—was forced to submit and remain subordinate to her despotic kinsman's direction. He set aside the entire staff of instructors, male and female, and stood alone upon the examiner's estrade. It irked him that he was compelled to make one exception to this rule. He could not manage English; he was obliged to leave that branch of education in the hands of the English teacher, which he did, not without a flash of naive jealousy.

A constant crusade against the "amour-propre" of every human being save himself was the peculiar obsession of this able, yet fiery and grasping little man. He possessed a strong relish for public representation in his own person, coupled with an extreme abhorrence of any similar display in anyone else. He quelled, suppressed, and stifled whenever possible; and when he could not, he fumed like a bottled storm.

On the eve of the examination-day, I was walking in the garden, as were the other teachers and all the boarders. M. Emanuel joined me in the "defended alley;" his cigar hovered at his lips; his paletôt—a garment of most characteristic, shapeless menace—hung dark around him; the tassel of his Greek bonnet sternly shadowed his left temple; his black whiskers curled like those of a wrathful cat; his blue eye held a cloud within its glitter.

"Ainsi," he began abruptly, confronting and arresting me, "you are going to reign like a queen; tomorrow—to reign beside me? No doubt you will savor beforehand the delights of authority. I perceive in you some radiant, petty ambition!"

And the truth was, he happened to be entirely mistaken. I did not—and could not—estimate the admiration or the high opinion of tomorrow's audience at the same rate he did. Had that audience numbered as many personal friends and acquaintances for me as for him, I know not how matters might stand; I speak of the case as it existed. Upon me, school triumphs cast but a cold lustre. I had wondered—and I wondered still—how it was that for him they seemed to shine with the warmth of a hearth-fire. *He* cared for them perhaps too much; *I*, probably, too little. Nevertheless, I had my own fancies as well as he. For instance, I liked to see M. Emanuel jealous; it illuminated his nature and woke his spirit. It threw all sorts of queer lights and shadows over his dun face and into his violet-azure eyes (he used to declare that his black hair and blue eyes were "une de ses beautés"). There was a relish in his anger; it was artless, earnest, quite unreasonable, yet never hypocritical. I offered no disclaimer regarding the complacency he attributed to me; I merely asked where the English examination fitted in—whether at the commencement or conclusion of the day?

"I hesitate," he said, "between placing it at the very beginning, before many persons have arrived, and when your aspiring nature will not be gratified by a large audience, or quite at the close, when everybody is tired, and only a jaded, worn-out attention will be at your service."

The examination passed over well; M. Paul was as good as his word, and did his best to make my part easy. The following day came the distribution of prizes; that, too, passed successfully. The school broke up; the pupils went home, and now began the long vacation.

That vacation! Shall I ever forget it? I think not. Madame Beck went, on the first day of the holidays, to join her children at the seaside; all the three teachers had parents or friends with whom they took refuge; every professor quit the city; some went to Paris, some to Boue-Marine; M. The house was left quite empty, save for me, a servant, and a poor, deformed, and imbecile pupil, a sort of cretin, whom her stepmother in a distant province would not allow to return home.

My heart almost died within me; miserable longings strained its chords. How long were the September days! How silent, how lifeless! How vast and void seemed the desolate premises! Throughout my stay, some little plan was proposed daily that resulted in beneficial enjoyment. Though fully occupied, Dr. John still managed to join us on each brief excursion. No immortal could have resembled him in his temporary oblivion of all but the present, nor in his passing passion for that immediate moment. The reader is requested to note a seeming contradiction in the two views presented of Graham Bretton—the public and private, the out-door and in-door view. In the first, the public, he appears oblivious of self, modest in the display of his energies, earnest in their exercise. In the second, the fireside picture, there is expressed consciousness of what he has and what he is; pleasure in homage, some recklessness in exciting, some vanity in receiving it. Both portraits are correct.

It was hardly possible to oblige Dr. John quietly and in secret. When you thought that the fabrication of some trifling dedication to his use had been achieved unnoticed, and that, like other men, he would use it when placed ready and never ask whence it came, he amazed you by a smilingly-uttered observation proving that his eye had been on the work from commencement to close. He noted the design, traced its progress, and marked its completion. It pleased him to be thus served, and he let his pleasure beam in his eye and play about his mouth.

This would have been all very well if he had not added to such kindly and unobtrusive evidence a certain wilfulness in discharging what he called debts. When his mother worked for him, he repaid her by showering about her his bright animal spirits with greater affluence than his gay, taunting, teasing, loving wont. If Lucy Snowe were discovered to have put her hand to such work, he planned, in recompense, some pleasant recreation.

I often marveled at his perfect knowledge of Villette—a knowledge not merely confined to its open streets, but penetrating to all its galleries, salles, and cabinets. Of every door which shut in an object worth seeing, of every museum, of every hall sacred to art or science, he seemed to possess the "Open! Sesame." I never had a head for science, but an ignorant, blind, fond instinct inclined me to art. I liked to visit the picture-galleries, and I dearly liked to be left there alone. In company, a wretched idiosyncrasy forbade me to see much or to feel anything. In unfamiliar company, where it was necessary to maintain a flow of talk on the subjects in presence, half an hour would knock me up with a combined pressure of physical lassitude and entire mental incapacity. I never yet saw the well-reared child, much less the educated adult, who could not put me to shame by the sustained intelligence of its demeanour under the ordeal of a conversable visitation of pictures, historical sights, or lions of public interest. Dr. Bretton was a cicerone after my own heart; he would take me betimes, ere the galleries were filled, leave me there for two or three hours, and call for me when his own engagements were discharged. Meantime, I was happy—not always in admiring, but in examining, questioning, and forming conclusions.

Two days later, the garment arrived—a resplendent pink dress.

"That is not suitable for me," I protested, the words escaping me with a sudden urgency. The hue, the cut, evoked to my imagination the exotic finery of a Chinese lady of high rank, a fantasy utterly incongruous with my reality.

"We shall determine its suitability," she countered, her decision absolute and unyielding. "Take my word for it: you will wear it tonight."

A sinking feeling settled in my stomach. I suspected I would not, imagining that no earthly force could compel me into such attire. Pink! I knew nothing of pink, nor did I suspect that pink knew me. I had yet to prove myself worthy of it.

I reflected that those who frequent public amusements nightly struggle to comprehend the fresh, gala spirit with which an opera or concert is received by those for whom it is a novelty. Finally, we were seated in places commanding a good general view of that vast, dazzling, yet warm and cheerful hall. It was already filled with a splendid assemblage. Among the guests were fine forms, models of a peculiar style of beauty, never seen in England. A solid, firm-set, sculptural style. To those who dwell in seclusion, within the walls of schools or guarded retreats, the sudden and prolonged erasure from the memories of friends living in the free world is a common hazard. Without apparent cause, and often following a cluster of lively circumstances that should have spurred further contact, communication halts abruptly. A stony silence descends; the frequent letters cease, the periodic visits stop, and the tokens of remembrance vanish into oblivion. An unbroken, unexplained blank remains.

For the hermit, this void stretches interminably, while outside, the lives of friends whirl in the vortex of activity. For the isolated, time drags so heavily that clocks seem frozen and hours limp like weary travelers resting at milestones. Yet, in that same interval, the world outside teems with events, panting with haste for those left behind. A sensible hermit learns to swallow his thoughts and lock his emotions away during these winters of inward solitude. He accepts his role as a dormouse, curling into a tight ball against the drift of life which soon buries him in ice, safe for the season.

Perhaps, eventually, the snow-sepulcher opens. Spring returns with softness, and the sun warms the frozen heart. The budding hedges and singing streams call for a gentle resurrection. Or perhaps not; perhaps the frost penetrates the core and never thaws, leaving only bones for a crow to peck from the wall. Even then, it is all right. One must know from the start that death awaits, and "as well soon as syne" goes everyone.

Following that eventful evening at the theatre, seven weeks passed for me as blank as seven sheets of unwritten paper. No word, no visit, no token marked the pages.

By the middle of this desolate time, I fancied some disaster had befallen my friends at La Terrasse. For the solitary, the midpoint of a blank is a clouded point where nerves ache with strained expectancy. Doubts, previously repelled, gather into a mass and roll back with vindictive force. Night becomes unkind; sleep refuses agreement with nature. Strange starts and struggles harass the couch, joined by a sinister band of bad dreams headed by horror of calamity and a sick dread of total desertion. Poor wretch! I did my best to endure, yet remained pale and wasting despite my efforts.

Towards the end of these seven weeks, I finally admitted what I had jealously excluded for six: the conviction that these silences were inevitable. The result of circumstances, the fiat of fate, a part of my lot. Above all, a matter whose origin must never be questioned, nor whose painful sequence murmured against. I did not blame myself for suffering; I thanked God for a truer sense of justice than to indulge in self-accusation. Regarding others, my reason knew them blameless, and my heart acknowledged it, but the road was rough and heavy, and I longed for better days.

I tried various expedients to sustain existence: elaborate lace-work, studying German, and devouring the driest, thickest books in the library. In all, I was as orthodox as I could be. Yet, was there error somewhere? Likely. The result felt as if I had gnawed a file to satisfy hunger or drunk brine to quench thirst.

My hour of torment was the post-hour. I knew it too well, trying vainly to cheat myself of that knowledge. I dreaded the rack of expectation and the sick collapse of disappointment that followed the well-recognized ring. Animals kept in cages, starving on the verge of famine, await their food as I awaited a letter. The beloved letter would not come; it was all sweetness in life I had to look for.

It transpired to be the birthday of one of the younger princes of Labassecour, the eldest, I believe, the Duc de Dindonneau. Consequently, a general holiday was declared throughout the schools, particularly at the principal institution, the "Athénée." The students of this college had concocted a loyal address intended for presentation; for this purpose, they were to assemble in the public hall where the annual examinations and prize distributions took place. Following the ceremony, an oration, or *discours*, was scheduled from one of the professors.

Several of M. de Bassompierre's acquaintances—the savants—who were connected with the Athénée, along with the respectable municipality of Villette, M. le Chevalier Staas, the burgomaster, and the parents and kin of the Athenians, were expected to attend. M. de Bassompierre was persuaded by his friends to accompany them; his fair daughter, naturally, would be part of the party, and she sent a brief note to Ginevra and me, inviting us to arrive early so we might join her.

As Miss Fanshawe and I dressed in the dormitory on Rue Fossette, she suddenly burst into laughter, suspending her grooming to gaze at me.

"What now?" I asked, surprised by the interruption.

"It seems so odd," she replied, with her customary mixture of honesty and insolence, "that you and I should now be on such equal footing, visiting in the same sphere, sharing the same connections."

"Why, yes," I answered. "I had not held much respect for the circles you chiefly frequented a while ago: Mrs. Cholmondeley and company would never have suited me at all."

"Who _are_ you, Miss Snowe?" she inquired, her tone a blend of undisguised and unsophisticated curiosity that made me laugh in return.

"You used to describe yourself as a nursery governess; when you first came here, you truly had charge of the children in this house. I have seen you carrying little Georgette in your arms like a *bonne*—few governesses would have condescended so far. Now, Madame Beck treats you with more courtesy than she treats the Parisienne, St. Pierre; and that proud chit, my cousin, makes you her bosom friend!"

"Wonderful!" I agreed, amused by her bewilderment. "Who am I indeed? Perhaps a personage in disguise. Pity I do not look the character."

"I wonder you are not more flattered by all this," she continued. "You take it with strange composure. If you really are the nobody I once thought you, you must be a cool hand."

"The nobody you once thought me!" I repeated, feeling a slight heat rise in my face, though I refused to be angry. A schoolgirl's crude use of terms like 'nobody' and 'somebody' held little weight. I confined myself to observing that I had merely received civility, asking what she saw in civility to throw the recipient into a fever of confusion.

"One can't help wondering at some things," she persisted.

"Wondering at marvels of your own manufacture. Are you ready at last?"

"Yes; let me take your arm."

"I would rather not: we will walk side by side."

When she finally took my arm, she leaned upon me with her whole weight. Since I was not a gentleman, nor her lover, I did not relish the burden.

"There, again!" she cried. "I thought, by offering to take your arm, to intimate approbation of your dress and general appearance: I meant it as a compliment."

"You did? You meant, in short, to express that you are not ashamed to be seen in the street with me? That if Mrs. Cholmondeley should be fondling her lapdog at some window, or Colonel de Hamal picking his teeth in a balcony, and should catch a glimpse of us, you would not quite blush for your companion?"

"Yes," she said, with a directness that was her finest trait—which gave an honest plainness to her very lies, which acted as the sole preservative in a character otherwise unfit to keep.

I left the trouble of commenting on this "yes" to my countenance; or rather, my lower lip voluntarily anticipated my tongue, conveying reverence and solemnity in a look that betrayed neither.

"Scornful, sneering creature!" she went on as we crossed a great square and entered the quiet, pleasant park leading to Rue Crécy. "Nobody in this world was ever such a Turk to me as you are!"

"You bring it on yourself: let me alone: have the sense to be quiet: I will let you alone."

"As if one _could_ let you alone, when you are so peculiar and so mysterious!"

"The mystery and peculiarity being entirely the conception of your own brain—maggots—neither more nor less, be so good as to keep them out of my sight."

"But _are_ you anybody?" she insisted, pushing her hand under my arm despite my protests, and pressing it with inhospitable closeness against my side to ward off the intrusion.

"Yes," I said. "I am a rising character: once an old lady's companion, then a nursery-governess, now a school-teacher."

"Do—_do_ tell me who you are? I'll not repeat it," she urged, adhering with ludicrous tenacity to the wise notion of an incognito she had grasped. She squeezed the arm she now fully possessed, coaxed and conjured until I was obliged to pause in the park to laugh. Throughout our walk, she rang the most fanciful changes on this theme, proving by her obstinate credulity, or incredulity, her incapacity to conceive how any person not bolstered up by birth or wealth, not supported by some consciousness of name or connection, could maintain an attitude of reasonable integrity. As for me, it sufficed for my mental tranquility that I was known where it mattered that I should be known; the rest sat lightly upon me. Pedigree, social position, and recondite intellectual acquisitions occupied about the same space and place in my thoughts; they were my third-class lodgers, to whom I assigned only the small sitting-room and the little back bedroom. Even if the dining and drawing-rooms stood empty, I never confessed it to them, considering minor accommodations better suited to their circumstances. The world, I soon learned, held a different estimate: and I make no doubt the world is very right in its view, yet believe also that I am not quite wrong in mine.

There are those whom a lowered position degrades morally, to whom loss of connection costs loss of self-respect. Are not these justified in placing the highest value on that station and association which serves as their safeguard from debasement? If a man feels that he would become contemptible in his own eyes were it generally known that his ancestry were simple and not gentle, poor and not rich, workers and not capitalists, would it be right severely to blame him for keeping these fatal facts out of sight—for starting, trembling, quailing at the chance which threatens exposure? The longer we live, the wider our experience grows; the less prone are we to judge our neighbor's conduct, to question the world's wisdom. Wherever an accumulation of small defenses is found, whether surrounding the prude's virtue or the man of the world's respectability, there, be sure, it is needed.

We reached the Hôtel Crécy. Paulina was ready; Mrs. Bretton was with her. Under her escort and that of M. de Bassompierre, we were soon conducted to the place of assembly and seated in good seats at a convenient distance from the Tribune. The youth of the Athénée were marshaled before us; the municipality and their burgomaster held places of honor; the young princes, with their tutors, occupied a conspicuous position; and the body of the building was crowded with the aristocracy and first burghers of the town.

Concerning the identity of the professor by whom the *discours* was to be delivered, I had entertained neither care nor question. Some vague expectation I had that a savant would stand up and deliver a formal speech, half dogmatism to the Athenians, half flattery to the princes.

The Tribune was empty when we entered, but in ten minutes it was filled. Suddenly, in a second of time, a head, chest, and arms grew above the crimson desk. This head I knew: its color, shape, port, and expression were familiar both to me and Miss Fanshawe. The blackness and closeness of the cranium, the amplitude and paleness of the brow, the blueness and fire of the glance, were details so domesticated in the memory, and so knit with many whimsical associations, as almost to tickle fancy to a laugh by their sudden apparition. Indeed, I confess, for my part, I did laugh till I was warm; but then I bowed my head, making my handkerchief and a lowered veil the sole confidants of my mirth.

M. Paul Emanuel possessed an exquisite sensitivity regarding interruptions. To disturb his lessons, regardless of the cause, was considered by the faculty and students alike a transgression tantamount to a mortal sin for a young woman. Madame Beck, if compelled to traverse the classroom, would move with frantic caution, hugging her skirts and skirting the high platform as if navigating treacherous shoals. One morning, seated in the *carré* and working on embroidery begun by a pupil, I listened to the crescendo of voices rising in the adjacent class. A sturdy wall separated me from the gathering storm, and a glass door offered a quick escape to the courtyard. Thus, I observed the commotion with amused detachment. Poor Rosine was not so fortunate; having already risked crossing the threshold four times, she faced her fifth perilous mission: retrieving a student from M. Paul's wrath.

"Mon Dieu!" she cried, "What shall I become? Monsieur will surely kill me; he is so angry!"

Driven by desperation, she opened the door. "Mademoiselle La Malle au piano!" she shouted.

Before she could retreat, a voice boomed: "From this moment, the class is closed! The first to enter or exit shall be hanged—even if it be Madame Beck herself!"

Within ten minutes, Rosine returned, her slippers shuffling softly. "Mademoiselle," she whispered, "I cannot possibly return there again today. His glasses are dreadful. A messenger from the Athénée has arrived with an urgent summons for Monsieur. Madame Beck insists I charge you with delivering it."

"Myself?" I replied, though my heart beat faster. Curiosity mingled with apprehension. "Come, Rosine, bear your cross."

"I cannot!" she pleaded. "Five times today I have crossed him. Madame must hire a policeman."

"Bah!" I dismissed her fears. "What is the message?"

"It is precisely what he despises: an official inspector has arrived, and Monsieur *must* meet him immediately. He hates obligations."

I knew this well. The restive little man detested any spur or curb. Yet, I accepted the task, not without fear, but with a blend of curiosity and resolve. I opened the door, entered swiftly, and closed it silently. To linger or bustle would only aggravate the situation. Inside, he sat, his humor visibly dark. He had been teaching arithmetic, a dry subject that invariably agitated him; not a pupil remained unmoved by his stern tone. Bent over his desk, he refused to look up at the intrusion, a testament to his rigid will.

Approaching the *estrade*, I paused. Too short to peer over his desk, I ventured to look around. Twice I saw him clearly, his face resembling a sullen tiger. On the third glance, his "lunettes"—spectacles—caught my eye. Rosine was right; they radiated a terrifying, immutable dread, far worse than the unglazed fury of his own eyes. Realizing their utility for spotting criminals was nullified by his proximity, he removed them. Suddenly, standing eye-to-eye, the dynamic shifted.

To my relief, I felt no genuine terror. When he demanded the cord and gibbet mentioned in his threat, I offered him a needleful of embroidery thread with accommodating civility, handing it discreetly around the corner of his desk and attaching it to his chair back.

"What do you want?" he growled, teeth clenched, the sound vibrating within his chest.

"Monsieur," I began firmly, "I seek the impossible, unheard-of things." I delivered the message from the Athénée, exaggerating its urgency. Naturally, he refused to budge. "He would not leave," I realized, knowing that duty and self-interest demanded compliance. I waited in silence until he asked for further demands.

"Only Monsieur's answer for the commissionaire."

He waved his hand impatiently. Then, I stretched toward the *bonnet-grec* resting on the windowsill. He watched, a mix of pity and astonishment in his gaze. "If Miss Lucy meddles with my hat," he muttered, "she might just wear it, turn boy, and go to the Athénée in my stead."

Respectfully, I placed the hat on his desk, where its tassel seemed to nod ominously. "I'll write a note of apology—that will suffice!" he insisted, trying to evade departure.

Knowing an apology would not do, I gently nudged the bonnet toward his hand. It slid across the polished, varnished surface, dragging his steel-framed spectacles behind it. In a tragic twist of fate, they tumbled onto the *estrade*. A dozen times before, they had fallen safely, but this time, the clear lenses shattered into countless fragments.

Panic seized me. These glasses were precious, uniquely fitted to his peculiar vision, his "treasures." Trembling, I picked up the shards. My fear was eclipsed by profound sorrow. I avoided meeting his gaze for several seconds. Finally, he spoke.

"Là!" he exclaimed. "I am widowed of my glasses! Now I suspect Miss Lucy will admit the cord and gallows were fully deserved. She trembles at the thought of her doom. Ah, traitress! You intend to render me blind and helpless!"

I raised my eyes. Instead of anger or gloom, his face bloomed with a smile, reminiscent of the warmth I had seen at the Hôtel Crécy. He was not angry, nor grieved. Showing clemency despite the provocation, he was as patient as a saint. This accident, which I feared ruined my chances, became my greatest asset. Having caused him harm, he became pliant and gracious.

Railing gently at me as "a fierce woman, a terrible Englishwoman, a little wreck-ball," he confessed he dared not disobey one who demonstrated such dangerous prowess. Comparing me to the Great Emperor who smashed a vase to inspire awe, he crowned himself with the bonnet, took the broken glasses from my hand with a gesture of kindness, bowed, and departed for the Athénée in high spirits.

Yet, by evening, I found myself quarreling with M. Paul once more, a contradiction I could not prevent.

His customary evening ritual involved bursting unannounced into the silent hours of study, imposing a sudden despotism. Books were stowed, workbags produced, and in lieu of sleepy, pious lectures, he substituted grand tragedies read with ardent fervor. He used literature as a vessel for his own passionate outpourings, pruning any passage deemed unsuitable for a congregation of young girls, often improvising superior dialogue where cuts would have left voids.

That evening, the room was silent as nuns in retreat. We heard the sharp bell, the familiar tread, and the words "Voilà Monsieur!" Before the double doors could fully part, he stood amidst us. Two study tables were arranged with lamps overhead. Teachers sat at the ends, girls flanking them. As per custom, he offered a chair to Zélie St. Pierre, the senior mistress, who smiled broadly, displaying every tooth in a sharp, thin curve that failed to reach her eyes. However, M. Before I could rise to clear the path, he whispered, "Do not move," and positioned himself between me and Miss Fanshawe, my usual neighbor. It was easy to comply, yet difficult to endure her proximity. I cleared my workspace, withdrawing to create space for him, leaving barely a yard between us. M. Paul, flint and tinder, ignited instantly.

"You do not wish me for a neighbor," he growled, "you assume airs of caste; you treat me as a pariah." Scowling, he declared, "Very well, I shall arrange matters!"

He ordered the girls to rise and file to the other table. Placing me at one extreme of the long bench, he meticulously brought me my basket, silk, and scissors, then seated himself at the opposite end. This absurd isolation drew no laughter; the atmosphere was too tense. I sat, mind on my work, undisturbed.

"Is this sufficient distance?" he demanded.

"The arbiter is Monsieur," I replied.

"You know it is not," he countered. "You created this vast void; I laid not a finger upon it."

With this, he began reading. He had selected a French translation of what he termed "a drama of William Shakespeare; the false god," dismissing the English as foolish pagans. The translation was inefficient, and I could not suppress the contempt some of its failures aroused. Though I could not speak, I could look. My glances were scrutinized by his vigilant "lunettes." Consequently, he abandoned his screen, his eyes blazing free, growing hotter at his northern exile than the sun under Cancer.

When the reading concluded, it was unclear if he would express his anger. He rose to depart, then returned briefly to retrieve his pencil-case. Breaking a lead against wood, recutting it, and pocketing it, he walked promptly to me. Standing behind me, while the others talked freely nearby, he inquired about my work.

"A watchguard," I answered. "For a gentleman—one of my friends."

I rose before dawn, the grey light of pre-sunrise filtering through the dormitory windows, and knelt by the centre stand to complete my final vigil. The night-lamp flickered weakly, casting long, dancing shadows across the floor as I worked. Every bead, every thread of silk was spent before the chain reached the desired length and opulence. By the rule of contraries, I knew that to truly suit the specific taste I sought to gratify, an overwhelming display was essential. Thus, I crafted it double. I detached it, re-attached it, coiled the completed *garde-corps* tightly, and nestled it inside a small box I had purchased for its brilliant hue. It was carved from a tropic shell of "nacarat" color, adorned with a tiny coronet of sparkling blue stones. Inside the lid, with the precise tip of my scissors, I carefully engraved the initials: P.C.D.E.

Recall, if you will, the grandeur of Madame Beck's annual fête. At these gatherings, the school subscribed to a handsome present for the matron, a tradition observed solely for her and, in modified form, for her kinsman and counselor, M. Emanuel. While it was an honor spontaneously awarded to him—an accolade reflecting the genuine esteem in which the Professor of Literature was held despite his frequent irritabilities and prejudices—no valuable articles were ever offered. He had made it clear that plate or jewelry were unwelcome. Yet, he craved a slight tribute, not for its monetary worth, but for the sincerity behind it. A diamond ring or a gold snuff-box presented with pomp would have palled upon him; a flower, or a simple sketch offered with heartfelt emotion, would have delighted him. Such was his nature; a man perhaps not wise by the standards of his generation, yet possessing a filial sympathy with the "dayspring on high."

M. Paul's fête fell on the first of March, a Thursday. It was a fine, sunny day, coinciding with the customary morning for Mass and the half-holiday that allowed for afternoon strolls, shopping, or visits. These factors combined to induce a general smartness and freshness of dress. Clean collars were in vogue, replacing the ordinary, dingy woolen classes dresses with lighter, clearer fabrics. Mademoiselle Zélie St. Pierre, on this particular Thursday, even ventured into a "robe de soie"—an item deemed in economical Labassecour an article of hazardous splendour. Nay, it was remarked that she had summoned a *coiffeur* to dress her hair that morning; the pupils were acute enough to detect that she had bedewed her handkerchief and fingers with a new, fashionable perfume. Poor Zélie! She was wont to declare around this time that she was tired to death of her secluded, laborious life, longing for the means and leisure for relaxation, for a husband who would pay her debts—which were woeful—and supply her wardrobe, leaving her at liberty to "goûter un peu les plaisirs." Rumors abounded that her eye was fixed upon M. Emanuel, and certainly, his eye was often fixed upon her. He would sit and watch her perseveringly for minutes at a time. I have seen him give her a quarter-hour's gaze while the class sat silently composing, he throned upon his estrade, unoccupied.

Aware of this basilisk attention, she would writhe beneath it, half-flattered, half-puzzled. And M. Paul would follow her sensations, sometimes looking appallingly acute. For in some instances, he possessed the terrible, unerring penetration of instinct, piercing the hiding-places of the heart's last lurking thoughts, discerning under florid veils the bare, barren places of the spirit. Yes, he could perceive its perverted tendencies and hidden false curves—all that men and women would not have known—the twisted spine, the malformed limb born with them, and far worse, the stains or disfigurements they might bring upon themselves. No calamity so accursed that M. Emanuel could not pity and forgive, if acknowledged candidly. But where his questioning eyes met dishonest denial, where his ruthless researches found deceitful concealment, oh, then he could be cruel. I thought him wicked! He would exultantly snatch the screen from poor, shrinking wretches, passionately hurrying them to the summit of the mount of exposure, showing them all naked, all false—poor living lies, the spawn of that horrid Truth which cannot be looked upon unveiled. He thought he did justice; yet, in my opinion, I doubt whether man has a right to do such justice on man. More than once in these visitations, I have felt compelled to give tears to his victims and spare no ire or keen reproach to himself. He deserved it, though it was difficult to shake him in his firm conviction that his work was righteous and needed.

Breakfast concluded, Mass attended, and the school-bell rang, filling the rooms. A very pretty spectacle was presented in *classe*. Pupils and teachers sat neatly arrayed, orderly and expectant, each bearing in hand a bouquet of felicitation—the prettiest spring-flowers, fresh and fragrant. Alas, I alone had no bouquet. I like to see flowers growing, but when they are gathered, they cease to please me. I regard them as things rootless and perishable; their likeness to fleeting life makes me sad. I never offer flowers to those I love, nor do I wish to receive them from hands dear to me. Mademoiselle St. Pierre marked my empty hands; she could not believe I had been so remiss. With avidity, her eye roved over and round me, surely expecting me to possess some solitary symbolic flower, some small knot of violets to win myself praise for taste and ingenuity. The unimaginative "Anglaise" proved superior to the Parisienne's fears: I sat literally unprovided, as bare of bloom or leaf as a winter tree. This ascertained, Zélie smiled, well pleased.

"How wisely you have acted to keep your money, Miss Lucie!" she exclaimed. "Silly I have gone and thrown away two francs on a bouquet of hot-house flowers!" And she displayed with pride her splendid nosegay.

But hush! A step—*the* step. It came promptly, as usual, but with a promptitude that inspired us to flatter ourselves, driven by feelings other than mere excitability of nerve or vehemence of intent. We believed our Professor's "foot-fall" (to speak romantically) carried a friendly promise this morning; and so it did.

He entered in a mood that made him as good as a new sunbeam to the already well-lit first *classe*. The morning light played amongst our plants and laughed upon our walls, catching an added lustre from M. Paul's all-benignant salute. Like a true Frenchman (though I know not why I should say so, for he was of strain neither French nor Labassecourien), he had dressed for the "situation" and the occasion. Not obscured by the vague folds, sinister and conspirator-like, of his soot-dark *paletôt*, were the outlines of his person. On the contrary, his figure (such as it was, I do not boast of it) was well set off by a civilized coat and a silken vest quite pretty to behold. The defiant and pagan *bonnet-grec* had vanished; bare-headed, he came upon us, carrying a Christian hat in his gloved hand. The little man looked well, very well. There was a clearness of amity in his blue eye, and a glow of good feeling on his dark complexion, which passed perfectly in the place of beauty. One did not care to observe that his nose, though far from small, was of no particular shape, his cheek thin, his brow marked and square, his mouth no rosebud. One accepted him as he was, and felt his presence the reverse of damping or insignificant.

He passed to his desk, placing his hat and gloves upon it. "Bon jour, mes amies," he said, in a tone that somehow made amends to some amongst us for many a sharp snap and savage snarl. Not a jocund, good-fellow tone, still less an unctuous priestly accent, but a voice belonging to himself—a voice used when his heart passed the words to his lips. That same heart did speak sometimes; though irritable, it was not an ossified organ. In its core was a place tender beyond a man's tenderness, a place that humbled him to little children, that bound him to girls and women to whom, rebel as he might, he could not disown his affinity, nor quite deny that, on the whole, he was better with them than with his own sex.

"We all wish Monsieur a good day, and present to him our congratulations on the anniversary of his fête," said Mademoiselle Zélie, constituting herself spokeswoman of the assembly. Advancing with no more twists of affectation than were with her indispensable to the achievement of motion, she laid her costly bouquet before him. He bowed over it.

I recall vividly a heated episode involving a certain Madame Panache, a lady temporarily hired by Madame Beck to teach history. She was intelligent, possessing a vast amount of knowledge, and, more importantly, mastered the art of leveraging what she knew. Words and confidence were at her command. Her physical appearance was far from lacking advantage; many would have deemed her "a fine woman." Yet, there were aspects of her robust and ample figure, along with her bustling, demonstrative demeanor, that clashed with the fastidious and capricious tastes of M. Paul. The echo of her voice through the *carré* sent him into a strange trance; her long, free stride along the corridor often caused him to snatch up his papers and flee instantly.

One day, with malicious intent, he decided to interrupt her class. As swift as lightning, he absorbed her teaching method, which differed from his own pet plan. Without ceremony or courtesy, he pointed out what he termed her errors. Whether he expected submission, I know not; instead, he met an acrid opposition accompanied by a sharp rebuke for his unjustifiable interference.

Rather than withdraw with dignity, he threw down the gauntlet of defiance. Madame Panache, bellicose as Penthesilea, snatched it up immediately. She snapped her fingers in the intermeddler's face and rushed upon him with a storm of words. M. Emanuel was eloquent, but Madame Panache was voluble. A fierce antagonism ensued. Instead of laughing secretly at his fair foe, with all her sore pride and loud self-assertion, M. Paul detested her with intense seriousness. He honored her with his earnest fury, pursuing her vindictively and implacably. He refused to rest peacefully in his bed, to enjoy his meals, or to savor his cigar until she was completely removed from the establishment. The Professor conquered, but I cannot say the laurels of this victory graced his temples. Once I ventured to hint as much. To my great surprise, he conceded I might be right, but averred that when confronted with men or women of coarse, self-complacent quality, like Madame Panache, he had no control over his passions; an unspeakable and active aversion compelled him to a war of extermination.

Three months later, hearing that his vanquished foe had met reverses and was likely distressed for want of employment, he forgot his hatred. Active in both good and evil, he moved heaven and earth to find her a position. When she came to thank him for his recent kindness and make up for former differences, the old voice—a little loud—the old manner—a little forward—acted upon him so strongly that within ten minutes he started up and bowled her, or rather himself, out of the room in a transport of nervous irritation.

To pursue a somewhat audacious parallel, in his love of power and eager grasp for supremacy, M. Emanuel was like Bonaparte. He was a man not always to be submitted to. Sometimes it was necessary to resist; it was right to stand still, to look up into his eyes, and tell him that his requirements exceeded reason—that his absolutism verged on tyranny.

The first developments of peculiar talent within his range, curiously excited and disturbed him. He watched their struggle into life with a scowl; he held back his hand, perhaps saying, "Come on if you have strength," but he would not aid the birth.

When the pang and peril of the first conflict were over, when the breath of life was drawn, when he saw the lungs expand and contract, when he felt the heart beat and discovered life in the eye, he did not yet offer to foster.

"Prove yourself true ere I cherish you," was his ordinance. How difficult he made that proof! What thorns, briers, and flints he strewn in the path of feet not inured to rough travel! He followed footprints that, as they approached the bourne, were sometimes marked in blood, following them grimly, holding the austerest police watch over the pain-pressed pilgrim. And when at last he allowed a rest, before slumber might close the eyelids, he opened those same lids wide, with pitiless finger and thumb, gazing deep through the pupil and iris into the brain and heart, searching if Vanity, Pride, or Falsehood, in any subtle form, was discoverable in the furthest recess of existence. If, at last, he let the neophyte sleep, it was but a moment; he woke him suddenly to apply new tests: he sent him on irksome errands when he was staggering with weariness; he tested the temper, the sense, and the health. It was only when every severest test had been applied and endured, when the most corrosive acid had been used and failed to tarnish the ore, that he admitted it genuine, and, still in clouded silence, stamped it with his deep brand of approval.

I speak not ignorant of these evils.

Until the date at which the last chapter closed, M. Paul had not been my professor; he had not given me lessons. Crossing this deserted square, where drops nearly the size of five-franc pieces darkened the pavement, I saw no sign of life save for an infirm old priest. He walked past, bent and propped on a staff—the embodiment of age and decay. He emerged from the very house to which I was directed. When I paused before the door, just closing after him, and rang the bell, he turned to look at me. He did not soon avert his gaze; perhaps he considered me, with my basket of summer fruit and my lack of the dignity age confers, an incongruous figure in such a scene. I thought, had a young, ruddy-faced maid opened the door, I would have found her equally out of harmony. But when I found myself confronted by a very old woman dressed in a very antique peasant costume—a cap hideous yet costly, with long flaps of native lace, a petticoat and jacket of rough cloth, and wooden clogs (*sabots*) resembling little boats—it felt right, soothingly in character.

Her facial expression, however, was less soothing than her attire. She was the most cantankerous creature I had ever seen, scarcely replying to my inquiries about Madame Walravens. I believed she would have snatched the basket from my hand had not the old priest, hobbling forward, checked her. He lent an ear to my message himself.

Escorting me upstairs, he ushered me into a sort of salon and left me there.

The room was large, possessing a fine old ceiling and windows of colored glass akin to those in a church. Yet, it was desolate, and under the shadow of the coming storm, it looked strangely lowering. Within opened a smaller room; here, the blind covering the single window was drawn tight. Through the deep gloom, few details of furniture were visible, though I amused myself trying to decipher them. In particular, I was drawn to the outline of a picture on the wall.

Gradually, the picture seemed to dissolve. To my bewilderment, it shook, sank, and rolled back into nothing. Its vanishing left an arched opening leading into a passage and a mystic, winding stair, both constructed of cold, uncarpeted stone. Down this dungeon-like stair descended a rhythmic *tap, tap*, like a stick striking the steps. Soon a shadow fell upon the stairs, and finally, I became aware of a substance.

Yet, was this actual substance? This obstruction, partially darkening the arch, approaching me?

It drew nearer, and I saw it clearly. I began to comprehend my location. Well might this old square be known as the Quarter of the Magi; well might the three towers overlooking it claim three mystic sages of a dead and dark art as godfathers. Here, hoar enchantment prevailed; a spell had opened an elf-land to me. That cell-like room, the vanishing picture, the arch, the passage, and the stone stair were all parts of a fairy tale. Even clearer than these scenic details stood the central figure: Cunegonde, the sorceress! Malevola, the evil fairy.

She might have been three feet tall, but she had no shape. Her skinny hands rested upon each other, pressing the gold knob of a wand-like ivory staff. Her face was large and set, not upon her shoulders, but before her breast; she seemed to possess no neck. Her features suggested a hundred years, perhaps more in her eyes—her malign, unfriendly eyes, framed by thick grey brows and livid lids. They viewed me with severe, dull displeasure.

She wore a gown of brocade dyed a bright blue, full-tinted as the gentianella flower, covered with a large pattern of satin foliage. He was roused, and I loved him in his wrath with a passion surpassing anything I had yet felt.

"What you do is wrong," pursued Madame; "it is an act characteristic of men of your unreliable, imaginative temperament; a step impulsive, injudicious, inconsistent—a proceeding vexatious, and not estimable in the view of persons of steadier and more resolute character."

"You know not what I have of steady and resolute in me," said he, "but you shall see; the event shall teach you. Modeste," he continued less fiercely, "be gentle, be pitying, be a woman; look at this poor face, and relent. You know I am your friend, and the friend of your friends; in spite of your taunts, you well and deeply know I may be trusted. Of sacrificing myself I made no difficulty, but my heart is pained by what I see; it *must* have and give solace. *Leave me!*"

This time, in the "_leave me_" there was an intonation so bitter and so imperative, I wondered that even Madame Beck herself could, for one moment, delay obedience; but she stood firm. She gazed upon him dauntlessly; she met his eye, forbidding and fixed as stone. She was opening her lips to retort; I saw over all M. Paul's face a quick rising light and fire; I can hardly tell how he managed the movement—it did not seem violent; it kept the form of courtesy; he gave his hand; it scarce touched her, I thought; she ran, she whirled from the room; she was gone, and the door shut, in one second.

The flash of passion was all over very soon. He smiled as he told me to wipe my eyes; he waited quietly until I was calm, dropping from time to time a stilling, solacing word. Soon I sat beside him once more—reassured, not desperate, nor yet desolate; not friendless, not hopeless, not sick of life, and seeking death.

"It made you very sad then to lose your friend?" said he.

"It kills me to be forgotten, Monsieur," I said. "All these weary days I have not heard from you one word, and I was crushed with the possibility, growing to certainty, that you would depart without saying farewell!"

Man cannot read the future; love offers no oracle. Sometimes, fear constructs a phantom of doom. Those three years of silence! How I had wasted myself upon the anticipation of them! The misery they held seemed as inevitable as death. I understood the trajectory of our path; I never doubted how it would scourge us. The Juggernaut stood there, a grim, towering figure laden with a terrible weight. As he approached, sinking those broad wheels into the crushed earth, I, the prostrate devotee, felt the crushing crunch of annihilation before it happened.

Yet, strangely—and truthfully, though life mirrors it often—that anticipated destruction accounted for nearly *all* the torture. The great Juggernaut, drawn by his massive chariot, advanced slowly, loudly, and sullenly. Then, passing silently like a shadow sweeping across a noon sky, he vanished. Only a chilling dimness remained. I looked upward. Chariot and demon driver were gone, yet the votary still breathed.

Monsieur Emanuel had been absent for three years. To you who might scoff at this paradox, listen: they were the three happiest years of my existence. I began teaching; I labored, and I labored hard. I considered myself the steward of his estate, resolved to render a faithful account unto God. Students came—first burghers, then a higher class. In the midst of the second year, an unexpected turn of fortune placed an additional hundred pounds in my hands. One day, a letter arrived from England bearing that sum. It came from Mr. Marchmont, the cousin and heir of my beloved, deceased mistress. He was then convalescing from a grave illness; the money was an offering to soothe his conscience, reproached by memories of certain papers or memoranda found after my kinswoman's passing, involving names or recommendations for Lucy Snowe. Madame Barrett had given him my address. How deeply his conscience had been troubled, I never asked. I posed no inquiries, accepted the gold, and set it to work.

With this hundred pounds, I dared to take the house adjacent to mine. I would not abandon the dwelling Monsieur Paul had chosen, the home where he had departed, and where he expected to find me returning. My boarding school expanded into a pensionnat, and it flourished.

The secret of my prosperity lay less within myself, in any innate gift or power, than in a transformed state of circumstance, a wonderfully altered life, and a liberated heart. The spring that animated my energies lay far beyond the seas, upon an island in India. Upon parting, I had been left a legacy; such a thought for the present, such a hope for the future, such a motive for perseverance, for labor, for enterprise, for patience, and for courage—I *could* not falter. Few things shook me now; few matters possessed the weight to vex, intimidate, or depress me: most things delighted me; mere trifles held a charm.

Do not suppose this gentle flame burned solely on a bequeathed hope or a departing promise. A generous provider supplied abundant fuel. I was spared all cold, all scarcity; I was not allowed to fear want, nor tested by suspense. Through every vessel, he wrote; he wrote as he gave and as he loved, with open-handed, whole-hearted plenty. He wrote because he enjoyed writing; he did not withhold, because he cared not to withhold. He sat down, took pen and paper, because he loved Lucy and had much to tell her; because he was faithful and considerate, because he was tender and true. There was no sham, no deceit, nothing hollow or unreal about him. No apology ever dripped its slippery oil onto his lips; he offered no cowardly feints or paltry excuses through his pen: he would give neither a stone, nor an excuse, nor a scorpion, nor a disappointment. His letters were genuine food that nourished, living water that refreshed.

Was I grateful? God knows! I am convinced that scarce a living soul, so remembered, so sustained, treated with such constancy, honor, and nobility, could fail to remain grateful until the end.

Loyal to his own religion (for he lacked the substance of a facile apostate), he freely allowed me to keep my pure faith. He did not tease, nor tempt. He declared:

"Remain a Protestant. My little English Puritan, I love Protestantism in you. I acknowledge its severe charm. There is something in its ritual I cannot accept myself, but it is the sole creed for 'Lucy.'"

All of Rome could not instill bigotry into him, nor could the Propaganda transform him into a true Jesuit. He was born honest, not false; artless, not cunning—a freeman, not a slave. His tenderness had rendered him flexible in a priest's hands; his affection, his devotion, his sincere piety had sometimes blinded his kind eyes, causing him to abandon justice to himself to perform the work of craft and serve the ends of selfishness. Yet, these are faults so rare, so costly to their possessor to indulge, that we scarcely know if they will not one day be reckoned among jewels.

Now, the three years have passed: Monsieur Emanuel's return is fixed. It is Autumn; he shall be with me before the mists of November descend. My school thrives, my house stands ready: I have created a small library for him, filling its shelves with the books he left in my keeping; I have cultivated, out of love for him (though I was by nature no gardener), the plants he preferred, and some of them still stand in bloom. I thought I loved him when he left; I love him now in a deeper measure: he is more my own.

The sun crosses the equinox; the days shorten, the leaves turn brown; but—he is coming.

Frost appears at night; November sends his fogs ahead; the wind takes its autumn sigh; but—he is coming.

The skies hang heavy and dark—a ragged sail drifts from the west; the clouds mold themselves into strange shapes—arches and broad rays; resplendent mornings arise—glorious, royal, purple as a monarch in his state; the heavens burn like fire; so wild are they, they rival battle at its fiercest—so bloody, they shame Victory in her pride. I know certain signs of the sky; I have noted them since childhood. God watch that sail! Oh! Guard it!

The wind shifts to the west. Peace, peace, Banshee—"keening" at every window! It will rise—it will swell—it shrieks out long: wherever I roam through this house tonight, I cannot quell the blast. The approaching hours strengthen it: by midnight, all sleepless watchers hear and fear a wild south-west storm. That storm roared furiously for seven days. It did not cease until the Atlantic was strewn with wreckage; it did not subside until the depths had gorge themselves full of sustenance. Not until the destroying angel of the tempest had accomplished his perfect work would he fold the wings whose breath was thunder—the tremor of whose feathers was storm.

Peace, be still! Oh! A thousand mourners, praying in agony on waiting shores, listened for that voice, but it was not spoken—not spoken until; when the hush fell, some could not feel it: until, when the sun returned, his light became night to some!

Here I pause: pause immediately. Enough has been said. Do not disturb a quiet, kind heart; let sunny imaginings preserve their hope. Let them conceive the delight of joy reborn fresh from great terror, the rapture of rescue from peril, the wonderful reprieve from dread, the fruition of return. Let them picture union and a happy, succeeding life.

Madame Beck prospered all the days of her life; so did Père Silas; Madame Walravens fulfilled her ninetieth year before she died. Farewell.
