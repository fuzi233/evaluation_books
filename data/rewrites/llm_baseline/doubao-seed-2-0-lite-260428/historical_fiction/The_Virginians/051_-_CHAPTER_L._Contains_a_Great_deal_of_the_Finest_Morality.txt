When first I had the honour of being presented to Sir Miles Warrington in St. James’s Palace drawing room, I will own that—gazing at his ruddy round face, his broad padded waistcoat, and the unforced hearty bluster of his country manner—I fully believed I had stumbled upon the most agreeable acquaintance imaginable: that much-celebrated ideal of humanity, the good old English country gentleman.

To be that very figure, the world assures us, is to stand next to the gods, perched at the very peak of earthly happiness. You hold a vast rent-roll free of encumbrances, your farmers blessing the stars for a landlord as generous as your honour, their rents paid promptly without a grumble—save for one dilatory soul, of course, whose sole purpose is to give you occasion to show off your sublime Christian charity and universal benevolence. You hunt three days a week, live for the sport, and enjoy the robust health and ravenous appetite that comes of such exercise. You sit in your parish church, the very first man of the congregation, the village’s unchallenged benefactor, surrounded by a murmur of unspoken blessings, and join the rest in intoning “have mercy upon us miserable sinners” only for form’s sake, because the words are printed in the prayer book and it sets a fine example for the poorer folk. A good old country gentleman a miserable sinner? Ridiculous! So hale, so wealthy, so jovial, so revered by the vicar, so adored by his tenants, so worshipped by his family that his old gunroom story of the grouse that nearly shot the gamekeeper gets laughed at for three generations. You cannot make a man in such a position honestly believe he is miserable. Give any man five thousand a year, perfect health, a parish that bows to him, and a family that dotes on him, and you will never convince him he is a wretch worthy of pity. Were you a royal highness, arriving at church to see the entire congregation waiting for you to take your seat before the service began, would you think yourself miserable? You might, of course, if you were wracked with gout, all alone, staring death in the face, the doctor having forbidden you claret and ordered you nothing but arrowroot and a single glass of weak sherry. Then you might be humbled, admit your shortcomings, and acknowledge the vanity of earthly things. But in full health, under bright sunshine, in high spirits? That word miserable is no more than a turn of phrase. You cannot truly think you are to be pitied, not when Colin the ploughman lives with the ague, seven hungry children, eight shillings a week, and two pounds a year to pay for his tiny cottage. No: a rich, jolly, healthy country gentleman’s misery is very easy to bear, and there are barely a soul alive who will dare tell him he is a sinner. He may grow a little selfish, but he is perfectly satisfied with himself. For miles around, there is no one half so good or half so great—save my lord in the castle, of course. His excellent wife tends to his every want, and doles out small acts of charity to the parish; his children bow to his every word; the vicar treats him with deference; he carries weight at quarter sessions, makes poachers tremble, and every man doffs his hat to him at market. His great painted coach rumbles through the market town, his spotless daughters and sublime lady sitting on the cushions inside, and tradesmen cringe bareheaded to greet them, while farmers’ wives drop curtsies thick as blackberries. The ladies inside look down with beneficent smiles on the poor folk, buy a yard of ribbon with great affability, condescend to haggle over an ounce of salts or a packet of flower seeds, and deign to beat down the price of a fat goose at the stall. Their weekly drive is a royal progress, after all, the happy populace presumed to press around them and heap blessings on their heads.

But what if the market woman hid her fat goose when their coach drew near, for fear my lady would insist on buying it for a third of its worth? What if not a single copper ever came flying out of that grand coach window for the ragged boys cheering? What if Sir Miles served his tenants notoriously thin small beer at harvest feasts, and the poor of the parish nothing but watery broth that barely had a ghost of a meat flavour? Such fine old English gentlemen and ladies have existed by the dozen, you know. People who patronised the poor without ever relieving them, who shouted “Amen!” in church as loud as the parish clerk, who went through every form of piety and all the etiquette of the country gentleman’s life, and bought virtue for a bargain price, fully convinced they were honouring virtue by the purchase.

Poor Harry Warrington, stuck in debtors’ prison, sent a desperate plea for help to his relations. His aunt sent him a pious tract and her blessing. His uncle conveniently happened to be out of town on business, and of course could not find time to answer the poor boy’s petition. How much of this sort of behaviour passes every day in respectable society, do you suppose? You can picture Lord and Lady Macbeth huddling awkwardly after they have committed a murder, of course. But Lord and Lady Skinflint, the pair who lock themselves in their bedroom at night and agree to turn their luckless nephew away penniless, then go down to family prayers, discourse virtuously before their children and servants, then go back to whispering nose to nose about the kinsman fallen among thieves, groaning unheeded in his distress? What must they think of each other? How dare they keep up their virtuous airs? How dare they look each other in the face? Dare? Do you suppose they think they have done wrong? Do you imagine Skinflint lies awake tortured by remorse, thinking of the hunger he turned away empty? Not a bit of it. He is indignant at the prodigal for being a fool, never ashamed of himself for being a miser. What? A young man with such fine prospects squandered them on gamblers and tavern feasts? Horrible! Shocking! Take warning, children, that is what comes of extravagance! Per the great established church of the Pharisees, this is the perfect chance for a moral lecture.

“To think he deceived us so!” cries Lady Warrington.
“Very sad, very sad, my dear,” nods Sir Miles, wagging his jowly head.
“To think of such wild extravagance at his age!”
“Cards, bets, lavish feasts in the most wicked taverns, carriages and fine riding horses, the company of all the rich profligates of his own sex, and I fear, the most iniquitous persons of ours too!”
“Hush, my lady!” Sir Miles cuts her off, glancing at his daughters Dora and Flora, who have hung their heads, blushing bright red at the mention of those last naughty figures. “The children are in the room, for goodness sake.”
“No wonder my poor dears hide their faces!” their mother goes on. “I wish the very existence of such wicked people could be kept from you, my loves!”
“They can’t go to the opera or the park without seeing them, to be sure,” Sir Miles chuckles.
“To think we let that young serpent make himself at home in this family, that we left him in the company of our little guileless darling Miles!” She points to her small son, playing on the rug at her feet.
“Who’s a serpent, mamma?” the boy pipes up. “First you said Cousin Harry was good, then you said he was bad, now he’s a serpent. Which is he, Sir Miles?”
“He has faults like all of us, Miley dear. Your cousin has been wild, and you must take warning from him.”
“Wasn’t my older brother Hugh, the one who died, wild too? He was never kind to me when I was small, never gave me toys, never rode me on his shoulders, never—why are you crying, mamma? I remember you and Hugh were always fighting—”
“Silence, sir!” his father and sisters cry out at the same time. “You know you are never to speak that name!”
“I don’t care!” the sturdy little rebel declares. “I love Harry, and I never loved Hugh. If Cousin Harry is in prison, I’ll give him the half-guinea my godfather gave me, and everything I have—except my little pony, and my silver waistcoat, and Snowball the rabbit and Sweetlips the spaniel, and—my custard after dinner. But I’ll give him half the custard, anyway!”
“Shut thy mouth with that custard, child, and run along,” Sir Miles laughs. He had a good store of easy, good-natured humour.
“Who would ever have thought Harry would turn out so wild?” Lady Warrington resumes.
“Nay, youth is the time for sowing wild oats, my dear.”
“That we were so mistaken in him!” the two girls sigh in unison.
“To think he kissed both of you at the New Year party!” Sir Miles grins.
“Sir Miles Warrington! That sort of vulgar talk is unforgivable!” his wife huffs, drawing herself up to her full majestic height.
“Which of you was his favourite, girls?”
“Favourite indeed! I told him over and over again of my engagement to dear Tom, I did, Dora—why are you sneering, may I ask?” Flora tosses her pretty head.
“To do her justice, Dora turned him down too,” her father says.
“Flora was forever forgetting that engagement to Tom, if you ask me,” Dora remarks.
“I never! Never, never, never wanted to break things off with Tom! It’s wicked of you to say so, Dora! It’s you who are always sniping at him, always jealous because gentlemen happen to think I’m not ill-looking, and prefer me to people who are all full of their own ‘learning’ and witticisms!” Flora snaps, glancing at her reflection in the glass over the mantel.
“Why are you always staring at that glass, sister?” little Miley puts in. “Surely you know what your own face looks like by now!”
“Some people stare at that glass just as often who have far less reason to,” Sir Miles says gallantly.
“If you mean me, Sir Miles, I thank you,” Dora retorts. “My face is as Heaven made it, given to me by my father and mother. It is no fault of mine if I favour your side of the family. If my face is plain, at least there are brains inside my head. Me jealous of Flora, for capturing poor Tom Claypool? I might as well be proud of charming a ploughboy!”
“Pray miss, wasn’t your Mr. Harry from Virginia far cleverer than Tom Claypool? You would have had him at the asking, and you know it!” Flora fires back.
“And so would you, miss! You would have dumped Tom Claypool straight in the Thames if Harry had asked you!”
The bickering goes on, the shuttlecock of wrath batted briskly back and forth between the two sisters. “Oh my children! Is this how you dwell together in unity?” their mother cries, laying down her embroidery with a dramatic flourish. “What an example you are setting for this innocent little boy!”
“Go on, fight! I want to see!” Miley cheers, rubbing his small hands together. “At her, Flora! Worry her, Dora!”
“Good sport, isn’t it, Miley?” his father cackles.
“Oh Sir Miles! Oh my children! These disputes are unseemly, they tear a fond mother’s heart apart!” Lady Warrington moans, though she looks remarkably calm for a woman whose heart is supposedly lacerated. “How thankful we ought to be that our watchful care prevented any foolish engagements between you and your misguided cousin. If we were mistaken in him, is it not a mercy we found out in time? If either of you ever felt a fondness for him, your good sense will teach you to stamp out that silly, vain feeling. That we were kind to him can never be a regret—it only proves our good nature. The only thing we have to grieve over is that your cousin proved unworthy of our kindness, coming fresh from the company of gamblers and play-actors, and bringing contamination—almost pollution, I dare say—into this pure family!”
Flora snorts under her breath, “Oh bother mamma’s sermon.” Sir Miles has already tiptoed out of the room whistling softly, and little Miley is winding up his top, pegging it under the trailing hems of his sisters’ gowns. The top hums, staggers in drunken little circles, wobbles, and collapses long before Lady Warrington has finished her long pious harangue.
“Were you listening to me, my child?” she asks, resting her hand on Miley’s head.
“Yes mother,” he says, the whipcord from his top still in his mouth. “You were saying Harry is very poor now, and we ought not to help him. That’s what you were saying, isn’t it madam?”
“My poor child, you will understand me better when you are older!” his mother sighs, rolling her eyes up towards the ceiling, her favourite position for pious speeches.
“Get out, you little wretch!” Dora yelps, when the top nicks her toe. Miley cackles with glee at her discomfiture.
But what is this? Sir Miles comes hurrying back into the drawing room, his face as red as a holly berry, and Tom Claypool, his neighbour, trundles after him, breathless.
“Here’s a fine business, my lady!” Sir Miles cries. “The most wonderful wonder of wonders, girls!”
“For goodness sake, gentlemen, what is your news?” the virtuous matron demands.
“The whole town is talking of it!” Tom puffs, still out of breath. “I saw him last night at Ranelagh, Sir Miles, walking with a fellow who looks exactly like him. Same face, same height, one in a blue suit, one in a brown—suits Harry was wearing here in this very house, not a month back!”
“What suits? What fellow?” the girls cry, all in a breath.
“Why your fine young Virginian of a cousin, to be sure!” Sir Miles says. “Our precious heir to a whole Virginian principality.”
“Is my nephew released from his prison then?” Lady Warrington asks. “Is he plunged back into the vortex of dissipa—”
“Confound him!” Sir Miles roars, swearing far more loudly than his lady would approve of. “What would you think if that precious nephew of mine turned out to be an impostor? No better than a common adventurer!”
“An inward monitor whispered me as much!” the lady gasps. “But I drove the unworthy suspicion from my mind. Speak Sir Miles, we are burning to hear your news!”
“I’ll speak when you’ve finished gasping, my love,” Sir Miles says. “What do you think of this fellow who waltzed into my house, ate my dinners, was treated like one of the family, kissed my wife’s hand, talked of his vast Virginian estates, and turns out to be nothing but a beggarly SECOND SON? A penniless nobody, my lady!”
“Sir Miles Warrington, no such violent language in front of the children! I sink to the earth, confounded by this unutterable hypocrisy. Did I leave you alone with that pretender, my innocent little boy?” the matron cries, hugging Miley tight.
“Who’s an impostor, mummy?” Miley asks.
“That confounded young scamp Harry Warrington!” his father bellows.
The boy stares for a second, puzzled, then bursts into full-throated howls, squirming out of his mother’s embrace. Just then the butler throws open the door, and announces, “Mr. George Warrington, Mr. Henry Warrington!”
Lady Warrington drops Miley straight onto the rug. Sir Miles’s face turns the colour of Tom Claypool’s scarlet waistcoat. The three ladies rise to their feet, dropping three of the frigidest curtsies ever seen, as the two young men walk into the room. Little Miley runs straight towards them, holding out one grubby hand.
“Oh Harry! No—wait, which is Harry? You’re my Harry!” He points straight at the right twin, throwing his arms around Harry’s legs. “Oh you dear Harry! I’m so glad you’re here! They’ve been saying all sorts of horrible things about you!”
“I came to pay my duty to my uncle,” the dark-haired twin, George, says, smiling. “And to thank him for all the kindness he showed my brother Henry while we were apart.”
“What, nephew George! The very spitting image of my brother! Both of you boys, I am delighted to see you!” Sir Miles cries, all his anger vanishing in a second, as he grabs a hand from each of them, his ruddy face glowing with unfeigned pleasure.
“This is truly a most mysterious and providential revelation,” Lady Warrington says, dropping another frosty curtsy. “Though I cannot help wondering why nephew Henry concealed the fact he was not the heir until now.”
“He did not know it himself, no more than your ladyship,” George says. The young ladies look at each other, their eyes cast down.
“Indeed! A most singular circumstance,” their aunt says. “We had heard the rumour, you see, and Mr. Claypool was just bringing us the news when you arrived. We were discussing it with my daughters only this very minute.”
“Yes!” Miley’s little voice pipes up. “And they said Harry was a imp—”
“Silence, child! Screwby, take Master Warrington up to his room at once!” his mother snaps. “These, nephew George, are your cousins Dora and Flora.”
The girls curtsy again, holding out their hands very coldly. George makes a deep, elegant bow that takes in all three ladies, hat pressed to his heart. “It is my duty, madam, to pay my respects to you and my uncle, and thank you for whatever hospitality you saw fit to extend to my brother.”
“It was not much, but it was our best!” Sir Miles roars, clapping George on the shoulder. “Ods bobs, it was our best!”
