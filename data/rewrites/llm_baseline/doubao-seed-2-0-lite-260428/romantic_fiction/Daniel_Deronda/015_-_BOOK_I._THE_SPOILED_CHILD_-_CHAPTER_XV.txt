*“Festina lente—let celerity be tempered by care.”* — Sir Thomas Browne

For weeks abroad, Gwendolen Harleth had drunk deep of the giddy new thrill of the gambling tables, half-convinced she was born an empress of luck. The sharp upheaval that had wrecked her family’s fortune at the hands of the shadowy, ruthless lenders known only as Grapnell & Co. had left her with a reckless, faint belief that nothing in the world mattered so long as you could amuse yourself: that all the old rules of prudence were made for people too dull to bend fate to their will. But try as she might to ignore it, a heavy little secret clung to her when she sailed back to England: the pearl necklace she’d pawned in her lowest moment, which some unknown stranger had redeemed and returned to her, against every wish of hers.

While she was making her way home, the man who’d come close to laying a marriage proposal at her feet was journeying to find her — but in his own endless, unhurried fashion. Mr. Grandcourt had not rushed post-haste from his estate at Diplow straight to Leubronn, where he’d been told she was staying. The presence of a few Russian acquaintances at Baden-Baden had made him linger for days, making and breaking plans with them on a lazy whim, his desire to find Gwendolen only pricking sharp enough at last to drag him away from their company. Grandcourt’s passions never blazed hot; they flickered low, unhurried, as unshowy as the careful way he tied his cravats, or the empty expected speeches he made toasting royal dignitaries at dinner. A man could glide through the highest tiers of society, be hailed as the perfect specimen of an English gentleman, full of quiet wit and repressed strong opinion, all on barely a spark of vital energy — and still be stubborn, still hold a quiet, inexplicable pull that made women turn to look twice at him.

He had not been even the least bit annoyed that Gwendolen had run off without a word the day before he planned to call on her to formally ask for her hand. The act had piqued him. He liked to imagine she’d fled out of sharp, proud resentment at his cold, careless behavior to her in Cardell Chase, when he’d drawn her so close to soft, tender feelings for him, then turned and walked away with no further care for winning her. A girl with spirit, that, he thought — just the kind of wife worth the work of taming. He was sure she meant for him to chase her, and chase her he would… eventually. For a whole week, he never so much as asked anyone where she’d gone.

Mr. Lush, his right-hand man, seethed with a glee half-mixed with distrust. Grandcourt had not breathed a single word about Gwendolen to him, his face as blank and unreadable as an alligator’s. But the longer he delayed a decision, the more of his scattered, low-burning energy wasted away, and Lush could not have been happier.

The guests staying at Diplow, though, were bubbling over with curiosity. How was no one hearing word of Miss Harleth? Was it possible she’d actually refused the great, wealthy Mr. Grandcourt? Lady Flora Hollis, a lively, middle-aged woman with an endless appetite for gossip, set off to pay calls all across the neighborhood: stopping first at the rectory, then Offendene, then Quetcham, and gossiped over tea with the Arrowpoints till she’d twice confirmed the same story — Gwendolen had run off to Leubronn with old family friends the Baron and Baroness von Langen. Mrs. Davilow and the Gascoignes, still raw from the shock of her flight, had decided there was no use pretending her trip was some secret, odd disgrace, and the rector had even convinced himself the whole thing was just a classic coquette’s trick. Amaryllis flees the one she loves so he will chase her, he told himself, quoting his old familiar verses. Gwendolen, he thought, was a clever, daring Amaryllis — the only question was if she’d dared too far.

Lady Flora returned to Diplow brimming with news, and did not hesitate to drop it right in front of Grandcourt over dinner, teasing a little that rumor was saying he was a disappointed suitor. He heard it all with perfect quiet, no flicker of surprise on his face, and the very next day told Lush to find a polite excuse to break up the house party at the end of the week — he was off yachting to the Baltic, or somewhere far away, he said, he could not bear to stay cooped up at Diplow like a man on parole with a set of guests he’d never invited in the first place. Lush did not need to be told twice: he knew exactly Grandcourt was heading for Leubronn, and he made himself indispensable enough that he was allowed to tag along. Gwendolen’s sharp, open hatred of him only amused his patron, who liked having Lush close at hand no matter where he went.

When Grandcourt stepped through the doors of the Hotel Czarina at Leubronn, it was five days after Gwendolen had already left. And waiting there, by total coincidence, was his uncle Sir Hugo Mallinger, his wife, their three daughters — and Daniel Deronda. The heir presumptive and the reigning squire of the Mallinger estates were never pleased to stumble across each other by accident. Sir Hugo was an easy-tempered man, but he had carried a lifelong bitter grievance: after three daughters born in quick succession, his wife had borne him no more children at all, and the family will had locked every acre of the Mallinger lands, including his beloved Diplow, in a settlement that meant he only held a life interest. Grandcourt, the cold, tedious nephew he had never liked, stood to inherit everything when he died. For years, Sir Hugo had dreamed of striking a deal: pay Grandcourt a huge sum of ready cash, and buy the Diplow estate from him, so his wife and daughters would have a proper home to retire to after he was gone. He had saved and invested shrewdly for years, hoarding the funds for just that moment, and he was determined to stay on perfectly polite terms with Grandcourt, no matter how much he bored him.

Grandcourt, for his part, thought his uncle a tedious bore he would be glad to see the back of — but he knew full well Sir Hugo wanted Diplow, and the power to refuse what his uncle craved fed his sense of his own importance perfectly. The two men had never been more than perfectly civil to each other, neither willing to risk a falling-out that would spoil their own quiet plans.

On the first evening of Grandcourt’s arrival, all four men — Sir Hugo, Grandcourt, Deronda, and Lush — wandered out to the terrace after the table d’hote dinner, then drifted inside to saunter through the glittering, smoke-heavy gambling rooms.
“Did you gamble much in Baden?” Sir Hugo asked Grandcourt.
“Hardly touched it,” he drawled, flicking a finger against his perfectly curled whisker. “Just watched, bet a little with some Russians. Boring strain, playing these days.”
“What did you win again, Lush?”
“About two hundred pounds,” his man answered.
“Ah, then you’re not here for the tables?” Sir Hugo said. “I never cared for gambling myself. Wraps your brain up in knots, gives me a headache. The air in these rooms is poison, I never stay ten minutes. Deronda, where is that pretty gambling beauty of yours? The one who was playing here a few days back — have you seen her lately?”
“She’s gone,” Deronda said, quiet and short.
“Uncommonly fine girl, a perfect Diana,” Sir Hugo went on, turning back to Grandcourt. “Worth straining your neck to look at. She won a fortune one day, took it as cool as if she’d known every fall of the card beforehand. The day Deronda saw her lose every penny, she didn’t flinch once, carried it like a queen. Must have decided it was time to head home. How do you know she’s left, Deronda?”
“Checked the visitor list,” Deronda said, with a faint, almost unnoticeable shrug. “Vandernoodt told me her name was Harleth, staying with the Von Langens. I saw her name was crossed off the new list.”

Lush already knew Gwendolen was gone, but he had no intention of telling Grandcourt until he asked — but Grandcourt had heard every single word. He paused a beat, then turned to Deronda. “You know those people, the Langens?”
“Talked to them a little after she left. Never met them before.”
“Where has she gone home to?”
“Offendene, near Winchester,” Deronda answered. His voice was cool, like he did not want to say another word on the subject — then he turned, and fixed Grandcourt with that sharp, steady, intense gaze of his, the kind that made strangers stop short, half-convinced he had already said something to them they had not heard. “Not far from your estate at Diplow.”
Grandcourt felt a flicker of sharp, unshown irritation at the way Deronda had turned to stare at him unprompted. He did not let it show, save for a tiny twitch of his eyelid, and drawled, “Yes, I know her,” before turning his shoulder to Deronda to watch a croupier rake in a pile of gold.

Sir Hugo pulled Lush to the side a little, as they wandered out to the open promenade for fresh air. “What’s the story with that girl Harleth? Old Blenny lived at Offendene after the dowager died, didn’t he?”
“Far too much of her,” Lush murmured, low and meaningful. He was happy to let Sir Hugo in on the secret. “He was on the brink of marrying her, a week or two back. Hope it’s off now. She’s the rector’s niece, no fortune at all, living with her widowed mother and a whole brood of younger sisters. Dangerous as gunpowder. The silliest match he could possibly make. She took a freak against him, ran off here without a single warning the day he was meant to call on her. Fact is, he’s come all this way after her — but he was never in any great hurry. Between his caprice and hers, they’ll probably never get back together. That means he’s lost his chance to marry the rich Miss Arrowpoint, too.”
Grandcourt wandered back to them a minute later. “Ghastly hole this place is. Worse than Baden. I’m heading back to the hotel.”

When Sir Hugo and Deronda were left alone, the baronet let out a low chuckle. “Quite a little story. That girl has fire in her. Plenty of unexpected spark. If this romance goes anywhere — or even if it doesn’t, and Grandcourt wastes a year chasing her all over the continent — it makes my chance of buying Diplow all the better.”
“I hope no such foolish marriage ever happens,” Deronda said, his tone sharp with quiet disgust.
“Are you a little smitten yourself?” Sir Hugo teased, lifting his glasses to squint at the younger man. “Wouldn’t surprise me. You could easily out-charm Grandcourt. A girl with that much spirit would pick you over him, easy.”
“On the contrary,” Deronda said, cold. “I should far rather run far away from her.”
“Fine thoroughbred beats a fancy pedigree every time, my boy,” Sir Hugo laughed. “Napoleon said he was his own ancestor, remember? You could be that for her.”
“I’m not sure I have any desire to be anyone’s ancestor,” Deronda said. “That hardly seems like the rarest kind of new beginning.”
“So you won’t be chasing the pretty gambler, then?” Sir Hugo said, setting his glasses back down.
“Decidedly not.”

The answer was perfectly truthful. But for a split second, Deronda had pictured what might have been, if he were a free man — the wild, proud girl he’d watched across the gambling tables, winning and losing with equal grace, the strange pull she had on his curiosity. But his own unspoken, tangled history left him no freedom to chase any romantic adventure, no matter how bright the spark. He had no right to step into this story at all.
