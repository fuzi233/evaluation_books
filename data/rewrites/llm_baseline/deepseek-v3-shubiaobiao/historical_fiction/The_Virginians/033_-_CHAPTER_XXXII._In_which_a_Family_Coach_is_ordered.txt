The secret that Colonel Lambert had whispered to his wife at the close of the previous chapter—one which sent the entire Oakhurst household into delighted anticipation—was now revealed. With the hay safely gathered and the corn not yet ready for harvest, the farm horses stood idle. Why not, then, harness them to the family coach and set out for Tunbridge Wells, stopping first at Westerham to visit their friend James Wolfe?

Mrs. Lambert embraced the idea—and very likely her husband as well—while the children erupted in joy. The girls immediately set about assembling their finest attire: calamancoes, paduasoys, furbelows, and all the delicate fripperies of the day. Even Mrs. Lambert’s most cherished gowns, usually reserved for solemn occasions, were liberated from their lavender-scented seclusion. The Colonel himself dusted off his laced hat and silver-hilted sword, while young Charley was fitted into a repurposed suit of his father’s—the very one in which the elder Lambert had been married.

The coach itself was a relic of bygone grandeur, rumored to have once carried George I from Greenwich to London. To the Lamberts, it remained the most splendid carriage in the kingdom. Brian, the family’s coachman (and, when necessary, ploughman), took his place on the box beside Charley, while the luggage was secured atop. Pistols were stowed within easy reach, and the blunderbuss hung at the ready—though no highwayman dared trouble their journey.

By Saturday evening, they arrived at their lodgings in Tunbridge Wells, secured by young Colonel Wolfe himself. The rooms faced those of Madame Bernstein, whose card parties were already in full swing. From their window, the Lambert ladies watched the parade of powdered beaux and brocaded ladies arriving for the evening’s entertainment.

Colonel Wolfe, ever the obliging guide, named the guests as they passed. The Duchess of Queensberry drew a quotation from Prior from the Colonel’s lips, while the Countess of Yarmouth’s grand entrance—escorted by a bishop and a peer—sparked a hushed scandal.

“Think of the Church and the nobility bowing before *that*,” muttered Colonel Lambert, his old republican blood simmering.

Theo and Hetty observed it all with wide eyes—until Harry Warrington himself appeared, arm-in-arm with a clergyman. Theo’s heart lifted at the sight of him, though he did not notice her.

As evening fell, the Lamberts drew their curtains, shutting out the glow of Madame Bernstein’s candles. Their own lodgings were quieter, filled with simple prayers rather than cards and champagne.

The next morning, the family rose early, strolling through the town before church. Hetty, sharp-eyed, spotted Gumbo at Harry’s window, preening like a peacock. But Harry himself remained unseen.

In church, the Lamberts noted the illustrious company—Madame Bernstein, Lady Maria, Mr. Richardson, and even Dr. Johnson amidst the charity children. But Harry was absent.

“He ought to have been here,” Theo murmured, earning a sharp glance from Hetty.

Later, Colonel Wolfe revealed why: he had found Harry and the parson deep in a game of piquet that very morning. Hetty flared in defense of her friend, while Theo, ever gentle, sought to smooth the tension.

By afternoon, the family walked the common. Harry, radiant with pleasure at seeing them, greeted the Lamberts warmly.

“Why did we not see you sooner today?” Theo asked softly.

Harry looked puzzled.

She pointed toward the church. “There.”

Her reproof was gentle, her smile kind. And in that moment, one could not help but hope that every wayward soul might have such a friend to guide them back to the light.
