A short while later, Mr. Gryce stood alone in the antechamber, his gaze sweeping over the scattered group of individuals with a look of intense scrutiny. A lingering question plagued him, an enigma he hoped the assembly might finally solve. His hope was not misplaced. In a secluded corner of the room, he spotted Miss Butterworth, her demeanor calm and composed, seemingly content to wait out the aftermath of a significant intervention—one in which she had undoubtedly played a pivotal role.

Gryce moved toward her with a warm smile, gently accusing her of orchestrating the scene. She immediately rejected the notion, her voice rising with feigned indignation.

"I was merely present!" she protested. "When the climax arrived—when this young lady discovered her husband had vanished abruptly for New York accompanied by two strangers—it became undeniably clear that a woman, someone who truly grasped her plight and the gravity of her situation, must stand beside her. And I stepped forward to fill that void."

"You are always the woman who steps forward," Gryce remarked gallantly, interpreting her words as a testament to impeccable timing. "So, you are already familiar with Mrs. Adams's account?"

"Yes," Butterworth affirmed, her expression softening. "Her initial frantic ramblings revealed that she was the hand that wielded the knife against Mr. Adams. Subsequently, she was able to articulate the terrifying rationale behind what we initially perceived as a heinous murder. Upon hearing her narrative, Mr. Gryce, I ceased to hesitate regarding either our moral obligations or legal necessities. Pray, do you believe she will be summoned to face justice? Is a trial, and a conviction, inevitable?"

"Madam, let me assure you," Gryce said firmly, "there are not twelve men in this entire city so lacking in common sense as to classify an act of such obvious self-defense as 'crime.' There will be no true bill against young Mrs. Adams. You need have no further anxiety."

The shadow of dread instantly vanished from Miss Butterworth's eyes.

"At last, I may return home in tranquility!" she exclaimed, her composure returning. "Those past five hours have been a harrowing ordeal, and I feel thoroughly rattled. Would you honor me by seeing me to my carriage?"

To Gryce, Miss Butterworth did not appear remotely rattled. On the contrary, she radiated a serenity and comfort that bordered on the unnatural. Yet, as the arbiter of her own state, her judgment was final. Satisfied that her charge was recovering under her husband's attentive care, she accepted his offered arm and glided toward the vehicle waiting outside.

As he helped her inside, he seized the opportunity to inquire about Mr. Poindexter.

"Where is Mrs. Adams's father? Did he permit his daughter to embark on such a perilous mission without offering to accompany her?"

The response was curt and sharp.

"Mr. Poindexter is a man devoid of sentiment," Butterworth declared. "He traveled with us to New York, yet he refused to venture to the Police Headquarters. Sir, you will find that the united fervor of three hearts consumed by righteous wrath, fueled by a vengeance I have rarely encountered, has been squandered on a man utterly incapable of suffering. Do not speak of old John Poindexter to me again. Now, if you would be so kind, instruct the driver to take me directly to my residence in Gramercy Park. I have inserted myself into the machinery of law enforcement for the very last time, Mr. Gryce—positively the last time."

She collapsed onto the carriage cushions with an air of inexorable resolve. Beneath this stern facade, however, lay a subtle, unspoken satisfaction—a quiet pride that suggested she was neither wholly displeased with herself nor with the outcome of her meddling in affairs that typically clashed with the refined sensibilities of a gentlewoman.

Mr. Gryce, stifling a smile, bowed with a courtesy deeper than usual. Under the canopy of this profound bow, the carriage wheels turned away. Walking slowly back along the pavement, he let out a long sigh, lost in thought: *Will a case of comparable intrigue and human complexity ever draw us together in counsel again?*
