The universe conspires against my attempts at originality with a malicious, systematic precision. I had resolved to craft an article on a subject so utterly novel, so remote from the beaten paths of discourse, that it would leave humanity gasping in stunned silence—a topic no mortal tongue had ever touched, allowing me sole proprietorship of the concept. For days, I wandered the corridors of my own mind, scouring the archives of the imagination, yet the blank page remained stubbornly empty.

Then came Mrs. Cutting, our charwoman, yesterday. I shall name her freely, for I am certain she will never encounter this volume; she regards anything less than the Bible and *Lloyd's Weekly News* as a sin against literacy.

"Lor', sir," she observed, eyeing my brow, "you do look worried."

"I am attempting to conceive a subject," I explained, "whose mere discussion shall shock the world into a new era—a topic upon which no human soul has previously spoken, possessing such startling novelty that it invigorates the weary spirit."

She chuckled, deeming me a peculiar gentleman. This is my perpetual misfortune: when I strive for gravity, the audience finds me comic; when I seek humor, they remain deaf. Last week, I constructed a masterpiece of wit regarding Shakespeare's stance on the Reformation. I delivered it with artistic flair at a dinner party, segueing smoothly into a tale from Whitechapel. "Ah," I announced, my voice rising in anticipation, "that reminds me; a truly hilarious incident occurred recently in Whitechapel." "Oh?" they queried. "Oh, it's awfully funny," I replied, already giggling, "it'll bring the house down!"

A deathly silence followed my conclusion—it was, after all, a lengthy jest—and finally, someone asked, "And that was the joke?"

I insisted it was, receiving polite nods of assent from everyone except an elderly gentleman at the far end of the table. He demanded clarification: what was the punchline? Did he say something to her, or vice versa? We engaged in a spirited debate, proving that some minds are simply wired for the opposite frequency. I recall a acquaintance whose reflexive laughter was so potent that serious conversation required a pre-warning label. Without explicit instruction that the matter was grave, he would erupt into hysterics over the most mundane utterance. Once, asked for the time, he halted mid-stride, slapped his thigh, and roared with mirth. With such a man, genuine comedy is fatal; a single good joke would kill him instantly.

Returning to my current predicament, I firmly rejected the charge of frivolity and pressed Mrs. Cutting for substance. After a prolonged period of contemplation, balancing a tea-tray with practiced ease, she ventured the word "samplers"—a fashion she noted had faded since her youth. I politely declined, urging further thought. Finally, she suggested the weather, noting its recent trials.

Thus began my torment. Since that foolish proposal, the weather has haunted my thoughts, displacing all other concerns. Indeed, it is wretched. At the very moment of this writing, it is dismal, and if it remains so when you read this, the sentiment will hold true. To us, the weather is invariably wrong, mirroring the incompetence of government. In summer, it stifles; in winter, it kills; in spring and autumn, it fails to commit to either state, frustrating our desire for decisiveness. If the sun shines, we lament the lack of rain; if it rains, we beg for sunshine. Should December pass without snow, we feel cheated, as if robbed of a purchased commodity, only to descend into linguistic disgrace when the snow actually arrives. We shall never find peace unless every individual generates their own private climate.

Ideally, we would prefer to dispense with it entirely. Yet, I suspect this aversion is unique to urban dwellers. In the countryside, Nature is sublime in every guise. Consider the snow: descending in mysterious, silent softness, it adorns fields and trees in white finery, akin to a fairy nuptial. How delightful a walk becomes when the frozen earth resonates beneath our steps, when the blood sings in the crisp air, and the distant yelp of sheepdogs and children's laughter echoes like Alpine bells across the hills. Then there is skating—gliding on wings of steel across swaying ice, creating a whirring symphony. And spring! Nature at her sweetest eighteen.

As tender buds peek forth, fresh and green like youthful souls tentatively entering the bustle of existence; as blossom trees, pink and white like village maidens in Sunday best, cloak cottages in clouds of fragile beauty; as the cuckoo's cry drifts through the woods! Summer follows with its deep verdant hues and drowsy hum, where raindrops whisper secrets to attentive leaves and twilight lingers in the lanes. And autumn! Ah, how tragically beautiful, glowing gold with the dying grandeur of tinted forests, blood-red sunsets, and ghostly mists, accompanied by the murmur of harvesters, laden orchards, and the calls of gleaners during festivals of praise.

Even rain, sleet, and hail serve as useful attendants in the rural sphere, and the East Wind is merely a boisterous companion between hedgerows. But in the city, where painted stucco blistered under a smoky sun and soot-laden rain creates slurry and mire, where snow piles in filthy heaps and chilling blasts whistle through dingy alleys, Nature offers no charm. Urban weather resembles a skylark trapped in a counting-house—utterly misplaced and obstructive. Cities should be sealed within, heated by piping, and illuminated by electric light. The weather is a rustic maiden who looks best in the hayfields; she loses her allure in Pall Mall. We may have enjoyed flirting with her in the dairy, but meeting her in the metropolis proves exceedingly trying. Her frank, hearty laugh clashes with the artificiality of town life; her presence becomes overwhelming.

Recently, she has favored us with nearly continuous rain for three weeks, rendering me, as Mr. Mantalini phrased it, a "damned damp, moist, unpleasant body." Our neighbor emerges into his backyard periodically to declare that the weather benefits the country—not his own excursion, but the precipitation itself. Having installed a cucumber frame last summer, he views himself as an agriculturist, speaking absurdly to impress the terrace residents with his persona as a retired farmer. I can only hope he is correct this time, that the weather genuinely aids something, for it is inflicting significant damage upon me. It ruins my attire and my disposition. While I possess ample temperament to spare, seeing my beloved hats and trousers succumb prematurely to the cold world's blasts and snows cuts me to the quick.

Consider my new spring suit. A magnificent garment it was, now hanging there, splattered with mud beyond recognition. Blame rests squarely on Jim. I was testing it on when he entered, throwing up his arms in a wild yell upon sighting it: "Got 'em again!"

"Does it fit properly in the back?" I asked.

"Spiffin, old man," he replied, then inquired if I intended to go out.

"No," I answered initially, but he overruled me. "A man with a suit like that has no right to stay indoors," he declared. "Every citizen owes a duty to the public. Contribute to general happiness as far as possible. Go out and treat the girls."

Jim speaks in slang I cannot trace; surely not from me.

"Do you think they'll enjoy it?" I asked.

"It'll be like a day in the country for them," he assured me.

Decided, I stepped out into a lovely evening. Upon returning home, I undressed, rubbed myself with whisky, soaked my feet in hot water, applied a mustard plaster to my chest, consumed gruel and hot brandy-and-water, tallow my nose, and retired to bed. These vigorous measures, bolstered by a robust constitution, preserved my life. As for the suit? It is no longer a suit; it is a splashboard.

I fancied that suit greatly. Yet, this is my fate: I rarely grow attached to anything before disaster strikes. As a boy, I cherished a tame rat with the devotion only a child feels for an old water-rat. One day, it fell into a cooling dish of gooseberry fool in the kitchen. No one noticed its disappearance until the second serving appeared.

I detest wet weather in town. Specifically, it is not the moisture itself but the mud that repulses me. I seem to possess an irresistible gravitational pull toward filth. Merely appearing on a muddy street results in me being half-smothered by it. An old lady once remarked, upon being struck by lightning, that it stemmed from being too attractive. Others traverse dirty days for hours unstained; if I cross the road, I return a disgrace, as my poor mother often warned in my boyhood. Were there but a single patch of mud in all of London, I am convinced I would claim it from every competitor.

I wish to reciprocate this affection, yet I fear I never shall. I harbor a terror of the "London particular." I feel miserable and muggy throughout a dirty day, finding relief only in stripping away my clothes and retreating to bed, escaping the grime. Everything falters in wet weather. Somehow, there appear to be more people, dogs, prams, cabs, and carts, all obstructing my path with greater intensity. Everyone is disagreeable—except myself—which drives me wild.
