*“The unwilling brain Feigns often what it would not; and we trust Imagination with such phantasies As the tongue dares not fashion into words; Which have no words, their horror makes them dim To the mind’s eye.” — SHELLEY*

Dante’s Madonna Pia, taken by her wronged husband to rot in his isolated Maremma castle amid the malarial swamp flats, lingers in memory as one of the purgatory’s most pitiable figures: a woman erased by petty marital cruelty, left only the hope that strangers would remember her gently. We know almost nothing of the small, bitter grievances that curdled that Siennese couple’s marriage, but we can guess for certain the husband was never a pleasant companion, and the desolate flats of the Maremma only amplified his worst, most cutting traits. In his frenzy to punish his wife, he hardly noticed that ridding himself of her also gave her the freedom he thought to steal away.

We need not grieve for that long-departed Tuscan lady half so keenly as we grieve for Gwendolen, whose purgatory has not yet ended. There is no deliverance waiting for her in some afterlife, no cleansing of the fateful choices that have trapped her, tight as a snare. Her worst torments are not written on the surface for others to see: they coil inside her, private and sharp, far more painful than any outward hardship could ever be.

When Grandcourt dragged his wife off on a months-long yachting voyage, he had no intention of getting rid of her. The exact opposite: he wanted to seal the fact that she was entirely his, to do with as he pleased, and to make her feel that truth down to her bones. He loved the unchallenged, dreamy indolence of life at sea, where no social calls, no tedious obligations could intrude on his absolute control, and he never for a second thought to compare the bright Mediterranean to the drear, deathly Maremma flats. He had his reasons for carrying Gwendolen far out of reach, none of them sounding particularly foul on their face: he’d sensed a spark of defiance growing in her, and that soft, hungry look she sometimes turned on Daniel Deronda struck him as something he would have called unforgivable jealousy in any other man. To himself, it was merely a sensible resolution to stamp out the silly little foolery he’d already caught a whiff of when he’d interrupted Deronda’s pre-planned visit to their home.

He could even have argued he was perfectly within his rights to make his wife honor the bargain she’d struck. Their marriage was a contract where every visible advantage was on her side: he’d given her the rank, the luxuries, the steady income that had lifted her mother and sisters out of ruin, and he’d kept every last term of his end of the deal. He knew perfectly well she had never married him out of love, that she’d only swallowed her quiet disgust for his old secrets because of the wealth and status he offered — and she had gotten every penny of what she’d bargained for.

Gwendolen, for her part, was under no illusions. She could not lie to herself and claim their unspoken agreement had included a clause that she would get to rule the roost and have every little wish met. For all her girlhood tendency to dominate everyone around her, she was not the kind of narrow, selfish woman who counts all her own demands as natural rights and every small expectation laid on her a personal insult. She had a living, thrashing root of conscience inside her, and her purgatory had begun long before they ever set sail: she knew she had sinned, selling herself for comfort.

Try then to step inside the skin of that young woman, stranded on that tiny floating plank of a yacht, with nothing but blue Mediterranean stretching between her and the rest of the world. She had sold her truthfulness, her sense of justice, to this man — and he held them throttled into silence, dragged along behind him like a collared hound, forced to watch whatever he chose to do, without a single word of protest.

What was there to complain of, exactly? The yacht was the prettiest thing afloat, its cabin fitted out to perfection, smelling of sweet cedar, lined with silk cushions and gilded mirrors. The crew was hand-picked to be picturesque and unobtrusive, one of the young sailors even had soft ringlets, tanned skin and bright white teeth. Mr. Lush was long gone, packed off back to England the second he’d finished checking all their supplies. Gwendolen herself loved the sea, never got seasick, and could pass hours happily learning the names of the rigging, forecasting shifts in the wind. The weather was endlessly fine, and they cruised slowly south along the coast, where even the sun-cracked clay cliffs glowed purple in the shadow, and you could drift between endless stretches of blue sea and blue sky, fooled into dreaming for a second that the world has forgotten all its sorrows.

But no beautiful view can sate that hungry ache in the heart that makes sweetness turn sour, that turns soft luxury into a weight pressing on your chest. What paradise could quiet the roiling, burning moral repulsion that gnaws at you like a slow poison, concentrating every thought on that one sharp, unbearable pain? While Gwendolen sat propped on her cushions at evening, watching the sunset gild the waves, praying Grandcourt would not stop his pacing, would not look at her, would not speak a single word to her, some other woman half a continent away walked under a smoky London sky counting the cost of eggs for her dinner, waiting for the sound of her beloved’s footstep on the path outside the door. Some young couple leaned cheek to cheek over a piece of half-finished embroidery, counting their small earnings day by day, saving up for a long holiday wandering the moors where the gorse bloomed golden.

Had Grandcourt the faintest clue what was seething in his wife’s breast? He knew she did not love him, but that never mattered to him. He was not the sort of man who needed to convince himself he was universally beloved to feel happy. What he could never, ever fathom was that she could feel a deep, personal revulsion for him. He knew what personal disgust felt like — better than anyone — he spent half his life sneering at the vulgar, smirky, ridiculous manners of other men and women, at their foolish perfume and overbright handkerchiefs and unwanted silly small talk. Before their marriage, that shared sharp eye for other people’s absurdities had drawn them together, and he knew perfectly well why she loathed Lush. But he could not wrap his head around the fact that she could loathe him. He had gone his whole life being told he was handsome, refined, far above every other man any woman would ever meet — he could not believe, if you had spelled it out to him, that a woman could grow so resentful, so disgusted, that even his polished good looks would become more hateful than the ugliest brute’s face, because they hid cruelty under a mask of grace.

No observer on board would have guessed a thing was amiss, not even the foreign maid who was never seasick, not Grandcourt’s long-suffering valet, and certainly not the pretty, uncomplicated crew, who thought the pair a perfect example of elegant high society. Their companionship was all quiet, well-bred silence. Grandcourt never made stupid jokes she could refuse to laugh at, never picked petty fights over nothing. He was perfectly polite, draping an extra shawl over her shoulders when the wind picked up, handing her any book or fan she reached for, and she could not bring herself to be rude enough to snub that small show of courtesy.

He would lift his telescope, nod out towards the shore, and say “There’s a sugar cane plantation at the foot of that rock. Would you like to look?” and she would answer “Yes, please,” forcing herself to care about the cane as something outside her own private torment. Then he would pace the deck smoking for hours, pointing out a distant sail now and then, until at last he would sink down on a seat opposite her and stare, his narrow, unblinking gaze fixed on her as if she were just another fine piece of furniture belonging to the yacht. She would have to strain every nerve not to meet his eyes. At dinner, he would observe that the fruit was going stale and they must dock somewhere to restock, or notice she wasn’t drinking her wine and ask if she’d prefer another vintage. A lady could not very well snap at him over such small, harmless remarks — and besides, fighting Grandcourt was as useless as picking a quarrel with a venomous serpent that had coiled itself uninvited in your cabin. Where could you even storm off to, on a yacht in the middle of the sea?

Grandcourt was intensely, quietly happy to hold his wife captive this way. Their small, floating world was like a tiny absolute kingdom, stripped of all familiar faces, where every person on board had to do exactly as they were told no matter what private grievances they harbored — and that secret, unspoken protest from Gwendolen only made his little despotism taste sweeter.

Gwendolen, who in all her sheltered girlhood had never once seen even a flicker of true heroism or grand purpose in her life, saw the whole world filtered now through the lens of her marriage and the man beside her. The people we are tied to, for good or for ill, become the lenses through which we see every other thing: a silly, shallow man or woman who seems no more than a trivial acquaintance to a stranger can become a whole warped theory of life to the people forced to live with them, like a pane of yellow wavy glass that distorts every shape and turns every bright color into an ache. Their petty opinions, their low suspicions, their loveless boredom can turn a whole life into a walk through a temple full of ugly, false idols. Gwendolen looked at the whole world through that warped pane Grandcourt had placed in front of her eyes. Some unhappy wives find comfort in the hope of bearing a child, but Gwendolen shuddered at the very thought: to long for a baby, to her, would feel like agreeing to make the terrible mistake she’d already made complete, irrevocable. She dreaded becoming a mother. In her worst fits of despair, the only glimmers of hope she could cling to were the thought of some random, happy accident that would set her free. To dream of benign luck stepping in was the only thing that kept far worse, far more terrible temptations from creeping in.

Hatred can grow as unaccountably, as uncontrollably, as the most devoted love, its strength having very little to do with the small outward grievances other people would notice. It feeds on something deep inside, roots down until every current of your life bends to serve it. The most dangerous kind of hatred of all is the kind rooted in fear: it forces you to silence, drives all your rage into hidden, private fantasies where you imagine destroying the thing you loathe, the same dark little secret rites of vengeance that persecuted people have clung to for centuries just to make their suffering bearable. Gwendolen performed those rites every minute she was alone in her head — but they never soothed her. They only stoked a new terror, a dread of herself, terrified of the violent, impulsive urges coiling under her skin. She remembered Deronda’s words, ringing in her ears over and over: Turn your fear into a safeguard. Keep your dread fixed on the idea of increasing your remorse. Take your fear as a safeguard. It is like quickness of hearing. It may make consequences passionately present to you.

Her prayers were wordless, no more than a quiet cry sent out into the vast silence of the sea, broken only by the sound of Grandcourt’s breathing, the splash of waves against the hull, the creak of the masts. Whenever she dared to imagine help coming, it always took the shape of Deronda’s face, his steady calm voice, the gentle guidance he could offer her. It was only after one of those dark, murderous temptations had crept through her thoughts that those soft, tearful moments of desperate clinging for rescue would come, and she would lie awake wide-eyed, knowing with all her heart: I will not mind any suffering, so long as I can keep myself from turning wicked.

The days drifted by, carrying them past the Balearic Islands, around the coast of Sardinia, then slowly drifting north again towards Corsica. But that soft, gentle floating life, so peaceful on the surface, had turned into a waking nightmare for Gwendolen.

“How long are we to stay out yachting?” she dared to ask one day, after they had docked briefly at Ajaccio. The small taste of solid land under her feet had given her a temporary reprieve from the dark thoughts that had started clinging to the very rigging of the ship, that had mixed with the cedar scent in the cabin and turned the smell of salt air into something repulsive.

“What else should we do?” Grandcourt shrugged. “I’m not tired of it. I don’t see why we can’t stay out here indefinitely. There’s far less to bore you out here than anywhere on land. Where else would you go? I’m sick of all the usual foreign resorts. And we’ll get more than enough of Ryelands soon enough. Would you rather be at home?”

“Oh no,” Gwendolen said flatly. Every place on earth felt the exact same, as long as she was forced to share it with him. “I just wondered how long you planned to stay.”

“I like yachting better than anything else,” he said. “I didn’t get to do any of it last year. I suppose you’re starting to get tired of it. Women are so absurdly whimsical, always expecting the whole world to bend to them.”

“Oh, never,” Gwendolen said, a cold little note of scorn in her voice. “I never expect you to give way to anything.”

“Why should I?” he murmured, not even bothering to hide his smirk as he reached for a ripe orange on the table.

She steeled herself to face months more of this endless floating, but the next day, after a sudden squall that had made her seasick for the first time in her life, he came down to her cabin and said, “There was hell to pay last night. The skipper says we’ve got to stay in Genoa for at least a week while we repair all the damage.”

“Do you mind that?” Gwendolen asked, her face still pale under her white linen bedclothes.

“Of course I mind. Who wants to swelter in Genoa?”

“It will be a change,” she said, her languor making her careless enough to say what she truly thought.

*“I* don’t want a change. Besides, the city’s insufferable, the roads are dust and stone. I’ll take a small boat out every day, the way I used to, and sail around the coast on my own. Get a few hours of peace instead of suffocating in some terrible hotel.”

Gwendolen’s heart leaped. That meant hours of time alone, hours where Grandcourt would not be breathing down her neck, watching her every move. That unlooked-for glimmer of hope made all kinds of wild, contradictory fancies bloom in her head: the possibility of slipping away, running off, the thing she had told herself a hundred times would be worse than any suffering she was already enduring, suddenly seeming like a real escape from the dark, violent part of herself.

That new spark of hope revived her, and she went about her days with a lightness, a cheerfulness, so obvious even Grandcourt noticed the shift in her. She sat up late watching the moon sink below the horizon, no longer drowning in that terrible lonely despair she was used to, but with a soft, vague certainty that somewhere in all this vast, indifferent world, rescue might be waiting for her. After all, the storm had just happened to land them exactly in Genoa, just when she needed a break. That small possibility of hope, after months of nothing but fear, felt like the first taste of food to a person who has been starving for weeks.

She was woken the next morning by the anchor dropping in Genoa’s port, pulled out of a strange, vivid dream where she was escaping over the Mont Cenis pass, confused that the snow under the moonlight felt so warm — until suddenly she ran straight into Deronda, who looked at her and told her to go back.

Less than an hour later, she met Deronda in the flesh. Not on a snowy mountain pass, but on the grand marble staircase of the Hotel Italia, where she was walking in her light woolen dress and straw hat, the heat of the city wrapping around her. Grandcourt was right at her side.

Deronda’s face flickered with surprise before he lifted his hat politely and passed on. The moment felt all wrong for a warmer greeting, and the memory of how abruptly Grandcourt had interrupted their last meeting made him doubt the man would welcome any kind of friendly chat.

That doubt would have turned into cold, hard certainty if he could have seen Grandcourt’s face in that moment. The second Deronda vanished around the corner, Grandcourt’s mind was already racing, jumping to the only possible conclusion he could imagine: Gwendolen and Deronda had planned this whole thing, had arranged to meet here in Genoa. It was impossible to piece together how she could have foreseen the storm that damaged the yacht, impossible to imagine her sending secret letters across hundreds of miles of sea to call Deronda here — but none of that mattered. He was already furious, certain the two of them were conspiring behind his back. He sat sipping his coffee later, angled to watch her across the table, and spotted that glow on her face: the unmistakeable light of secret, giddy delight. Her suffering had not marred her beauty — Gwendolen the wife was far more striking than Gwendolen the girl had ever been, her face softened and deepened by all the pain and grief she had carried, making her more vivid, more human than she had ever been before.

That morning, the hope of their unexpected arrival in Genoa seemed to burst out in every line of her, in the easy grace of her movements, in the quiet brightness of her eyes. Grandcourt, sharp and suspicious as a terrier who knows his master is about to go for a walk, picked up every single one of those small signs, and interpreted them in the narrowest, most unforgiving way possible. He knew exactly what she was waiting for: the second his back was turned, she would slip off to find Deronda, for that secret interview she had been daydreaming of for months.

For the first time since the yacht had set sail, Gwendolen dared to believe her rescue might be right at hand.
