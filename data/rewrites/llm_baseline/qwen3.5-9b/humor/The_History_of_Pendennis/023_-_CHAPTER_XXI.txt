To anyone with even a passing familiarity with heraldry and the peerage, the crest of Helen Pendennis's noble lineage is well-known: a nest teeming with tiny pelicans busily pecking at the bleeding chest of a large, maternal bird, which generously replenishes the young with blood drawn from itself. While it is plausible that female pelicans enjoy such a sacrifice, it is undeniable that human women do. There exists a peculiar pleasure, unknown to men, that accompanies the agony of being scarified; indeed, some women would endure such suffering willingly. They derive joy in self-sacrifice for the object their instincts dictate they love. Whether it be a reckless husband, a dissipated son, or a darling rogue of a brother, their hearts are eager to pour out their finest treasures for the sake of the beloved. How readily we, on the other side, offer these soft creatures the luxury of forgiveness! We rarely mind how we live ourselves, yet when the prodigal returns, we rejoice, slaughter the fatted calf, and send Mercy and Forgiveness smiling out to greet the sinner at the first sign of return. May this sentiment hold true forever; otherwise, justice alone would leave us wanting.

During the latter stages of Pen's tenure at Oxford, his uncle's favoritism toward the lad grew considerably. The Major was proud of Arthur, admiring his high spirits, frank demeanor, good character, and aristocratic bearing. It delighted the old London bachelor to watch Pen stroll alongside the university's young patricians. Though stingy to a fault—a reputation that had become something of a club joke among his friends, who envied his constant engagements despite his poverty—he was charmed to treat his nephew and the young lords to cozy dinners at his lodgings, regaling them with excellent claret, sharp wit, and tales so refined they would lose their charm if retold. He paid court to their parents through the youths and honored himself by their company. He visited Oxford multiple times, amusing the students with his presence while they hosted him with parties, breakfasts, and fetes, partly to jest and partly to honor him. He regaled them with stories, adopting a youthful and jovial persona. At a grand debate at the Union, he cheered wildly, tapping his stick in rhythm with the crowd, astounded by the boy's eloquence and fire. He believed he had secured a young Pitt for a nephew.

His affection bordering on paternal, he wrote playful letters filled with town gossip and advice. He bragged at his clubs, introducing Arthur with delight, claiming that "Egad, the young fellows are putting the old ones to the wall!" He spoke of friends like Young Lord Plinlimmon and Young Lord Magnus Charters, predicting they would eclipse their fathers. He begged permission to bring Arthur to a grand fête at Gaunt House, watching with infinite satisfaction as he danced with the ladies of the aforementioned nobility. He scrambled to secure invitations for the lad to prestigious homes with the fervor of a mother seeking a match for her daughter, rather than an old half-pay officer in a wig. Everywhere he boasted of the boy's genius and oratorical prowess, writing repeatedly to Helen about the brilliant degree Arthur was set to achieve. Lord Runnymede would take him on an embassy, or the Duke would seat him in Parliament. Helen, naturally, was too eager to believe anything favorable regarding her son. Yet, all this pride and affection had been trampled by Pen's wicked extravagance and idleness.

One does not envy Pen's position as he reflected on his actions. He had slept while the tortoise won the race. He had sabotaged a potential brilliance at its inception. He had dipped ungenerously into a generous mother's purse, basely and recklessly spilling her small store. Oh, what a cowardly hand strikes and robs such a tender creature! If Pen felt the wrong he had done others, surely a vain young gentleman felt the shame he had brought upon himself even more acutely. There is no cruelty comparable to this remorse, nor groans more pitiful than those of wounded self-love. Like Joel Miller's friend, the Senior Wrangler, who bowed to the audience merely because he and the King entered the theater simultaneously—an act of fatuity far less agreeable to himself—poor Arthur Pendennis was convinced that all England would notice the absence of his name from the examination lists and discuss his misfortune. His wounded tutor, the creditors ("duns"), the skipper and bed-maker who attended to him, and the undergraduates he had once patronized or scorned—all seemed impossible to face.

Rushing to his rooms, he locked himself inside and penned a letter to his tutor, overflowing with thanks, regard, remorse, and despair. He requested his name be removed from the college books and hinted that death would soon end the woes of the disgraced Arthur Pendennis. Slipping out, he wandered mechanically through the unfrequented lanes behind the colleges, clearing the university grounds until he reached the banks of the Cam, now deserted compared to its usual throngs of cheering gownsmen during boat races. Wandering miles away from Oxford, he found himself spotted by acquaintances leaving the city.

Ascending a hill under a drizzling January rain, his ragged academic gown fluttering behind him, a post-chaise rattled up the road. A servant sat on the box, while inside, leaning half-out of the window, sat a young gentleman smoking a cigar and loudly urging the postboy. It was Mr. Spavin, our acquaintance from Baymouth, driving home in triumph in his yellow carriage after earning his degree. Spotting a figure gesticulating madly up the hill with a pale, ghastly face, Spavin roared to the driver. The horses halted abruptly, stopping fifty yards ahead of Pen.

Pen heard his name shouted and saw Spavin thrust his upper body from the window, beckoning violently. Pen stopped, hesitated, nodded fiercely, and pointed onward, signaling the postilion to proceed. Silent, his face desperate, he watched as Spavin stared in blank alarm, jumped out, ran to Pen, and grasped his hand.

"I say—hullo, old boy, where are you going, and what's the row?" Spavin asked.

"I'm going where I deserve to go," Pen replied, with an imprecation.

"This ain't the way," Spavin smiled. "This is the Fenbury road. Don't take on because you're plucked. It's nothing when you're used to it. I've been plucked three times, old boy—and after the first time, I didn't care. Glad it's over, though. You'll have better luck next time."

Pen looked at his former acquaintance—who had been plucked, rusticated, and forced to learn reading and writing correctly only after repeated failures, yet somehow managed to earn a degree. *This man has passed,* he thought, *and I have failed!* It was almost too much to bear.

"Good-bye, Spavin," Pen said. "Very glad you're through. Don't let me keep you; I'm in a hurry—I'm going to town tonight."

"Gammon," Spavin scoffed. "This ain't the way to town; this is the Fenbury road."

"I was just going to turn back," Pen admitted.

"All the coaches are full with the men going down," Spavin noted. Pen winced. "You'd not get a place for a ten-pound note. Get into my yellow; I'll drop you at Mudford, where you can catch the Fenbury mail. I'll lend you a hat and a coat; I've got plenty. Come along; jump in, old boy—go it, leathers!"

Thus, Pen found himself in Mr. Spavin's post-chaise, riding with him to the Ram Inn at Mudford, fifteen miles from Oxford. Here, the Fenbury mail changed horses, and Pen secured a seat to London.

The following day, immense excitement gripped Boniface College. Rumors spread, terrifying Pen's tutor and tradesmen, that Pendennis, maddened by the loss of his degree, had taken his own life. A battered cap, barely legible with his name, and a seal bearing his crest of an eagle gazing at an extinct sun, were found three miles down the Fenbury road near a mill-stream. For twenty-four hours, it was believed poor Pen had thrown himself into the water, until letters bearing the London postmark finally arrived.

The mail reached London at the dreary hour of five. Hastening to his usual inn in Covent Garden, the ever-vigilant porter admitted him and showed him to a bed. Pen scrutinized the man, wondering if "Boots" knew he had been plucked. Unable to sleep, he tossed until the dismal gray of London dawn broke, then sprang up desperately and walked to his uncle's lodgings in Bury Street. The maid, scouring the steps, looked up suspiciously at the unshaven figure in yesterday's linen. She clearly suspected his mishap.

"Good 'eves! Mr. Harthur, what as 'appened, sir?" asked Mr. Morgan, the valet, who had just polished his master's boots and carried in his wig.

"I want to see my uncle," Pen cried in a ghastly voice, collapsing onto a chair.

Morgan recoiled, eyes wide with terror and wonder, and vanished into the Master's apartment. The Major emerged from his bedroom, wig freshly adjusted.

"What? Examination over? Senior Wrangler, double First Class, hay?" the old gentleman exclaimed cheerfully. "I'll come directly;" and his head disappeared again.
