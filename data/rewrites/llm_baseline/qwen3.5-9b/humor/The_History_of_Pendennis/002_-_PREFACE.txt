If this collection of scribbles, representing two years of intermittent effort, fails to achieve artistic perfection—a failure it likely shares with almost every other work of its kind—it does possess one redeeming quality: a certain raw honesty that a more polished masterpiece might sacrifice. By maintaining a constant dialogue with you, the reader, I am compelled toward a frankness of expression, forced to voice my thoughts and feelings exactly as they surge within me. Consequently, I have spotted numerous slips of the pen and the press, along with words hastily uttered, that I wish I could retract upon reviewing this volume.

Think of this less as a formal publication and more as a confidential chat between writer and reader. Such conversations are prone to dragging and losing momentum. As I ramble, inevitably exposing my own frailties, vanities, and eccentricities, you must judge me not by a single outburst or a fleeting mood, but by the general tenor of my conduct. When a man speaks freely, you naturally ask: *Is he truthful? Does he strive to uncover and articulate the reality?* Is he a charlatan feigning sentimentality just for applause? Or is he merely chasing popularity through cheap tricks?

I cannot deny the role of luck in my success any more than anyone else can claim credit for random chance. I have attracted far more readers than I ever dared hope for. While I have no right to demand that you never criticize my craft or nod off while reading, I earnestly ask you to believe that the person holding the pen here is striving to tell the truth. Without that foundation, there is absolutely nothing.

Perhaps those who crave "excitement" should know that this book originally began with a rigid, thrilling blueprint that was promptly discarded. Ladies and gentlemen, you were promised—and both the author's and the publisher's pockets stood to gain—a tale of high-octane horror. Imagine a ruffian (possessing many admirable virtues) lurking in St. Giles's, constantly pursued by a young lady from Belgravia! Picture the electric contrast between high society and the gutter, the clash of slang and polite discourse, the daring escapes, the brutal battles, and the grisly murders! Indeed, until nine o'clock this very morning, my poor friend Colonel Altamont was sentenced to hang, and I only spared him when he appeared at the window.

That "thrilling" plan was abandoned—with great honor shown by the publishers—because, upon trying to execute it, I realized I lacked the necessary expertise. Having never been close to a convict in my life, and finding the manners of criminals and prisoners utterly alien to me, I decided that competing with M. Eugène Sue was a fool's errand. To describe a genuine scoundrel, one must render him so horrifically vile that he becomes unfit for display; conversely, if a painter depicts a villain fairly, I maintain they have no business showing him at all.

Even the gentlemen of our time—this book attempts to portray one such man, neither better nor worse than the average educated male—we cannot expose as they truly are, complete with their notorious pettiness and self-centeredness bred by their upbringing. Since the burial of the author of *Tom Jones*, no fiction writer among us has been allowed to depict a MAN to his fullest potential. We must drape him in convention, forcing him into a polite, artificial smile. Society simply will not tolerate the "Natural" in our art.

Many ladies have protested, and some subscribers have withdrawn, because, during the course of the narrative, I described a young man struggling against and ultimately succumbing to temptation. My intention was to demonstrate that he possessed the capacity to feel passion, yet retained enough manliness and generosity to overcome it. However, you will not hear about it—and it is perhaps best that you don't know—the gritty realities that move the world, the undercurrents of social life, the whispers in clubs, colleges, and mess rooms. This is where the true lives and conversations of your sons unfold.

A degree of frankness greater than custom dictates has been attempted here. With no malicious intent on my part and hoping for no harmful consequences to any reader, I offer this glimpse. If truth is not always pleasant, it remains superior to falsehood, whether argued from the lofty chairs of grave philosophers or delivered from the humble seat of the storyteller as he finishes his labor and bids his kind reader farewell.

KENSINGTON, November 26th, 1850.
