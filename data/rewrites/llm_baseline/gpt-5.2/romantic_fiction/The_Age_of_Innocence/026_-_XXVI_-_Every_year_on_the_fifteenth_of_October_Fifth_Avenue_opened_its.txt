Every year, as surely as the fifteenth of October came round, Fifth Avenue performed its quiet rites of awakening. Shutters were flung back like eyelids after a long sleep; carpets, rolled all summer like stored dough, were uncurled and laid down smooth; heavy curtains—three layers, as if propriety required padding—were rehung so that lamplight might be softened into something becoming. By the first of November the work of reopening had passed from servants’ hands into habit, and by the fifteenth society—having warmed itself, having checked its reflection—began to move in earnest: the Opera, theatres, dinners, dances, and all the carefully-timed collisions that made up the season’s music.

It was at about this point that Mrs. Archer always said New York was very much changed.

From the elevated quiet of a non-participant she listened for discord the way an old farmer listens for a wrong note in weather: not the storm itself but the tremor that foretells it. With Mr. Sillerton Jackson and Miss Sophy to assist her, she would identify each new fissure in the old surface and each weed pushing up between the ordered rows of social vegetables. Newland Archer, as a boy, had waited for this pronouncement the way children wait for the first carving at table—half amused, half hungry. He used to smile and let the lamentations wash over him, as if they were merely the yearly sauce served with familiar dishes.

But this was the winter of the second year of his marriage, and even he—who had once believed the town’s manners as fixed as the stone lintels above its doors—could feel the house settling, ever so slightly, with a creak in the beams.

The topic had risen, as usual, over Mrs. Archer’s Thanksgiving dinner—an occasion on which, officially directed to gratitude, she took instead a mournful inventory of blessings and found them scarce. If there was anything, she seemed to imply, to call down Biblical thunder upon, it was the state of society; and everyone present knew exactly what the Reverend Dr. Ashmore meant when he had chosen his text from Jeremiah. Dr. Ashmore, the new rector of St. Matthew’s, was thought “advanced.” His sermons, like fashionable sauces, were praised for their novelty; his language had a tang to it, and when he denounced the world he called its movement a “trend.” To Mrs. Archer it was terrifying—and irresistibly absorbing—to feel herself part of a community that could be said to be trending, as if the word named an actual crack you might put a finger into.

“There’s no doubt Dr. Ashmore is right,” she said. “There is a marked trend.”

“It was odd, though, to preach about it on Thanksgiving,” Miss Jackson observed.

“Oh, he means us to give thanks for what’s left,” Mrs. Archer answered, dry as a well-kept biscuit.

Archer, listening, found that the “trend” could be tasted now, like metal on the tongue in certain kinds of air. The talk went, as it always did, from general doom to particulars.

“The extravagance in dress—” Miss Jackson began, leaning forward with the energy of someone describing a spoiled preserve. “Sillerton took me to the first night of the Opera, and I can only tell you that Jane Merry’s dress was the only one I recognized from last year; and even that had had the front panel changed. Yet I know she got it out from Worth only two years ago, because my seamstress always goes in to make over her Paris dresses before she wears them.”

“Ah, Jane Merry is one of us,” Mrs. Archer sighed—almost as if membership in that “us” were now a kind of poverty.

“Yes; she’s one of the few. In my youth it was considered vulgar to dress in the newest fashions,” Miss Jackson persisted, the way her generation persisted over a recipe that younger hands kept ruining. “Amy Sillerton has always told me that in Boston the rule was to put away one’s Paris dresses for two years. Old Mrs. Baxter Pennilow—who did everything handsomely—used to import twelve a year: two velvet, two satin, two silk, and the other six of poplin and the finest cashmere. It was a standing order. And as she was ill for two years before she died they found forty-eight Worth dresses that had never been taken out of tissue paper; and when the girls left off their mourning they were able to wear the first lot at the Symphony concerts without looking in advance of the fashion.”

“Boston is more conservative than New York,” Mrs. Archer conceded, as if admitting that another soil produced sturdier roots. “But I always think it’s a safe rule for a lady to lay aside her French dresses for one season. It was Beaufort who started the new fashion by making his wife clap her new clothes on her back as soon as they arrived. I must say at times it takes all Regina’s distinction not to look like… like…”

Miss Jackson’s glance slid round the table, encountered Janey’s bulging interest, and she saved herself in an unintelligible murmur.

“Like her rivals,” Mr. Sillerton Jackson supplied, with the small satisfaction of a man placing the final garnish.

The ladies murmured; and Mrs. Archer, partly to distract her daughter, said: “Poor Regina. Her Thanksgiving hasn’t been a very cheerful one, I’m afraid. Have you heard the rumours about Beaufort’s speculations, Sillerton?”

Mr. Jackson nodded without eagerness. Everybody had heard. In their world, tales did not need retelling to have weight; they floated like smoke in parlours and were breathed in with one’s tea.

A gloom settled, heavy as a thick tablecloth. No one liked Beaufort, and his private hypocrisy would have been a permissible secret pleasure; but dishonour in business was another matter—an unclean flavour that spoiled every dish at once. New York might accept lies in love as part of the social seasoning; but in money it required an unclouded, almost ceremonial honesty. The memory of the last public failure—years ago—still had the force of a warning taste. If Beaufort fell, the Beauforts would fall with him, whatever power he had bought and whatever popularity Regina had earned. Not even the Dallas connection could preserve her if the unlawful rumours were true.

They escaped, as people do, into safer complaints, but each subject led back to the same sense of loosened stitching.

“Of course, Newland, I know you let dear May go to Mrs. Struthers’s Sunday evenings—” Mrs. Archer began.

“Oh, you know everybody goes to Mrs. Struthers’s now,” May put in gaily; “and she was invited to Granny’s last reception.”

Archer, watching the ease with which the younger voice spoke it, thought again how New York managed its transitions: it pretended not to see them until they were complete, and then it remembered—sincerely—that they had always existed. Somewhere there was always a traitor in the citadel; and after the keys had been handed over, what was the use of swearing the gate had never opened? Once people had tasted the convenience of Mrs. Struthers’s Sunday hospitality they were unlikely to sit at home recalling what that hospitality had once been said to be made of.

“I know, dear, I know,” Mrs. Archer sighed. “Such things have to be, I suppose, as long as amusement is what people go out for. But I’ve never quite forgiven your cousin Madame Olenska for being the first person to countenance Mrs. Struthers.”

A sudden blush rose in May’s face—so quick, so hot, that it startled Archer more than it did the rest. She lowered her eyes as if some word had been dropped like a stain.

“Oh, Ellen—” she murmured, with that peculiar note the family had learned to use on Ellen’s name: accusing and yet pleading, as if the person spoken of had embarrassed them by existing.

The mention of Ellen always altered the air, the way the opening of a door lets in a different soundscape—street-noise, the echo of other lives. Mrs. Archer, with less than her usual tact for atmosphere, continued: “I’ve always thought that people like the Countess Olenska, who have lived in aristocratic societies, ought to help us keep up our social distinctions, instead of ignoring them.”

May’s blush deepened and stayed, as though the colour meant more than mere family disapproval.

“I’ve no doubt we all seem alike to foreigners,” Miss Jackson said tartly.

“I don’t think Ellen cares for society,” May went on, as if reaching for a safe phrase and finding none. “But nobody knows exactly what she does care for.”

“Ah, well—” Mrs. Archer sighed again, and the sigh carried with it all New York’s conclusion.

Everyone knew Ellen was no longer in her family’s good graces. Even Mrs. Manson Mingott, who had once defended her with the ferocity of a matriarch defending a wayward calf, could not openly praise Ellen’s refusal to return to her husband. The Mingotts had not proclaimed disapproval; their solidarity was too strong for that. They had simply, as Mrs. Welland said, “let poor Ellen find her own level”—and that level, mortifyingly, was in the dim depths where the Blenkers ruled and “people who wrote” gathered, conducting untidy rites that did not fit into the old vocabulary. It was incredible—and yet true—that Ellen, with all her opportunities, had become simply “Bohemian.”

The fact was used, like a moral, to prove she had made her fatal mistake in not returning to Count Olenski. A young woman belonged, people said, under her husband’s roof—especially when she had left it in circumstances that… well… if one cared to look into them…

“Madame Olenska is a great favourite with the gentlemen,” Miss Sophy said, with her conciliating air that always seemed to flick a concealed barb.

“Ah, that’s the danger a young woman like Madame Olenska is always exposed to,” Mrs. Archer agreed, mournful as a doctor describing a hereditary illness.

With that, the ladies gathered their skirts and went toward the drawing-room, toward the steady glow of carcel lamps and the softened, domesticated hush that old houses perfected. Archer and Mr. Sillerton Jackson withdrew to the Gothic library, where the fire made its own dry commentary and the room held the muffled acoustics of leather and books.

Before the grate, consoling himself for what he considered the dinner’s inadequacy with the undeniable perfection of his cigar, Mr. Jackson grew portentous.

“If the Beaufort smash comes,” he announced, “there are going to be disclosures.”

Archer lifted his head. Beaufort’s name always called up a sharp image: the heavy figure, the expensive fur, the confident tread through snow at Skuytercliff, as if the world had been built to receive him.

“There’s bound to be,” Mr. Jackson continued. “The nastiest kind of a cleaning up. He hasn’t spent all his money on Regina.”

“Oh, well—that’s discounted, isn’t it? My belief is he’ll pull out yet,” Archer said, wanting to change the subject, wanting not to have the bitter taste of scandal on his tongue any longer.

“Perhaps—perhaps.” Mr. Jackson spoke as if measuring a doubtful ingredient. “I know he was to see some of the influential people today. Of course,” he reluctantly conceded, “it’s to be hoped they can tide him over—this time anyhow. I shouldn’t like to think of poor Regina spending the rest of her life in some shabby foreign watering-place for bankrupts.”

Archer said nothing. It seemed to him so natural—however tragic—that money ill-gotten should be expiated. His mind hardly lingered on Regina’s possible exile; instead it went, with the instinct of pain, toward something nearer.

May’s blush. The way Ellen’s name had brought it.

Four months had passed since the midsummer day he and Ellen had spent together, and since then he had not seen her. He knew she had returned to Washington, to the small house she shared with Medora Manson. He had written once—only a few words, asking when they were to meet again. She had replied more briefly still: Not yet.

After that there had been nothing, and in the silence he had built a sanctuary inside himself, a private room where Ellen sat among his secret thoughts and longings. Little by little it became the scene of his real life. Into it he carried the books he read, the ideas that nourished him, the judgments and visions that made the day bearable. Outside it—in the visible rooms of his existence—he moved with a growing sense of unreality, blundering against familiar prejudices and inherited points of view the way an absent-minded man knocks into the furniture in his own darkened house. Absent: that was what he was. So absent from everything most solid and immediate that it sometimes startled him that others still believed he was present.

Mr. Jackson cleared his throat, preparing another course.

“I don’t know, of course, how far your wife’s family are aware of what people say about—well, about Madame Olenska’s refusal to accept her husband’s latest offer.”

Archer was silent.

“It’s a pity—certainly a pity—that she refused it,” Mr. Jackson went on.

“A pity? In God’s name, why?” Archer said, and felt his own voice sharpen.

Mr. Jackson looked down his leg to the neat join of unwrinkled sock and glossy pump, as if the smoothness reassured him.

“Well—to put it on the lowest ground—what’s she going to live on now?”

“Now—?” Archer echoed.

“If Beaufort—”

Archer sprang up, his fist coming down on the black walnut edge of the writing-table so hard that the brass double inkstand jumped, the wells dancing like startled eyes.

“What the devil do you mean, sir?”

Mr. Jackson shifted slightly and turned his calm, hard look full on Archer’s face, as if he were observing a young man’s temper the way one observes a flame that might run along spilled spirits.

“Well—I have it on pretty good authority—in fact, on old Catherine’s herself—that the family reduced Countess Olenska’s allowance considerably when she definitely refused to go back to her husband; and as, by this refusal, she also forfeits the money settled on her when she married—which Olenski was ready to make over to her if she returned—why, what the devil do you mean, my dear boy, by asking me what I mean?”

Archer moved to the mantel and bent to tap his ashes into the grate. The fire gave off a faint resinous smell, and the sound of falling ash seemed too loud in the thick room.

“I don’t know anything of Madame Olenska’s private affairs,” he said, forcing each word, “but I don’t need to, to be certain that what you insinuate—”

“Oh, I don’t: it’s Lefferts, for one,” Mr. Jackson cut in.

“Lefferts—who made love to her and got snubbed for it!” Archer flung back, contempt trembling in his voice.

“Ah—did he?” Mr. Jackson snapped, and Archer felt again that quick sense of stepping into a trap whose spring he had not seen. The old man sat turned sideways to the fire, but his gaze held Archer’s face with the cold grip of steel.

“Well, well: it’s a pity she didn’t go back before Beaufort’s cropper,” Mr. Jackson repeated. “If she goes now, and if he fails, it will only confirm the general impression. Which isn’t by any means peculiar to Lefferts, by the way.”

“Oh, she won’t go back now—less than ever!” Archer said, and even as he spoke he knew he had given Mr. Jackson precisely the certainty he wanted.

The old gentleman considered him, as if tasting the statement and finding it significant.

“That’s your opinion, eh? Well, no doubt you know. But everybody will tell you that the few pennies Medora Manson has left are all in Beaufort’s hands; and how the two women are to keep their heads above water unless he does, I can’t imagine. Of course, Madame Olenska may still soften old Catherine—who’s been the most inexorably opposed to her staying; and old Catherine could make her any allowance she chooses. But we all know she hates parting with good money; and the rest of the family have no particular interest in keeping Madame Olenska here.”

Archer burned with anger that could do nothing. He was in that state where a man feels himself about to act foolishly, and cannot stop, though he recognizes the folly as clearly as a cook recognizes that a sauce has split and will not come back. He saw, too, that Mr. Jackson had instantly noticed his ignorance of Ellen’s quarrels with her grandmother and the others, and had drawn his own conclusions about why Archer was not included in family councils. The knowledge warned Archer to be cautious; but the mention of Beaufort made him reckless. Still, some old training held: Mr. Jackson was under his mother’s roof. Old New York did not permit open quarrels with guests.

“Shall we go up and join my mother?” Archer suggested curtly.

On the drive home May was oddly silent. The city’s night sound—wheels, distant shouts, the muffled beat of hooves—seemed to press against the carriage like a tide. Through the darkness Archer still felt May’s blush about her, as if it were a warmth that had not cooled. He could not guess what the menace of it meant, but he felt warned by the simple fact that Ellen’s name had called it forth.

At home they went upstairs. Archer turned into the library; May usually followed him, but he heard her pass down the passage toward their bedroom.

“May!” he called, impatient without knowing why.

She came back, a slight surprise in her glance at his tone.

“This lamp is smoking again,” he said, seizing on the first domestic annoyance as if it could steady him. “I should think the servants might see that it’s kept properly trimmed.”

“I’m so sorry; it shan’t happen again,” she answered, firm and bright in the manner she had learned from her mother.

The competent cheerfulness exasperated him; it made him feel as though he were already becoming, in her eyes, something to be managed—a younger Mr. Welland with moods that required soothing. She bent to the lamp, lowered the wick with careful fingers. The light struck up on her white shoulders, on the clean curves of her face, and a sudden thought stabbed him with its simplicity: How young she is. For what endless years this life will have to go on.

He felt, with a kind of horror, his own youth, his blood bounding, the strength in him that had nowhere truthful to go.

“Look here,” he said suddenly, “I may have to go to Washington for a few days—soon. Next week perhaps.”

Her hand stayed on the key of the lamp as she turned slowly. The heat of the flame had brought colour back to her cheeks, but it faded as she looked up.

“On business?” she asked, in a tone that implied no other reason could exist, and that she had only supplied the expected phrase to complete his.

“On business, naturally. There’s a patent case coming up before the Supreme Court—” He named the inventor and went on, furnishing details with an ease that surprised him by reminding him of Lawrence Lefferts’s practiced glibness. May listened attentively, saying at intervals: “Yes, I see.”

When he finished she said, simply: “The change will do you good.”

Then, with her cloudless smile, looking him straight in the eyes, she added: “And you must be sure to go and see Ellen.”

The words were ordinary. The tone was the tone she might have used in reminding him not to neglect some irksome family obligation. But the message behind it arrived in him without sound, like a note passed in a crowded room: that she knew what people were saying; that she knew the family’s pressure on Ellen; that she knew, for reasons he had not told her, he had advised Ellen against returning; that he had helped Ellen hold out, and that he must now understand what such encouragement exposed Ellen to—and what it exposed him to. She was telling him she approved his visit, explicitly, and that he was to use it as a tool: to deliver the family’s meaning to Ellen.

Her hand was still on the lamp as the last of this silent speech settled between them. She turned the wick down, lifted off the globe, and breathed on the sulky flame.

“They smell less if one blows them out,” she explained, with her bright housekeeping air, as if the whole question were only a matter of keeping the air sweet.

On the threshold she turned and paused, waiting for his kiss.
