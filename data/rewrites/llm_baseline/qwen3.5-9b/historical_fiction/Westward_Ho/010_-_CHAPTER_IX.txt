"Take aim, you noble musqueteers, And shoot you round about; Stand to it, valiant pikemen, And we shall keep them out. There's not a man of all of us A foot will backward flee; I'll be the foremost man in fight, Says brave Lord Willoughby!"

Thus sang the Elizabethan ballad, a fitting prelude to the scene unfolding beneath the fading light of Christmas afternoon.

In Bideford, the day was drawing to a close. The evening song had finished, and the townsfolk were returning in joyful clusters—fathers with their children, lovers with their sweethearts—bound for cakes, ale, flapdragons, and the lively mummer's plays that marked the holy season. Yet, amidst this tide of merriment, one solitary figure moved differently. Wrapped tightly in a black muffler and trailed by a single maid, a woman walked swiftly yet sorrowfully toward the long causeway leading to Northam.

She was intercepted by Sir Richard Grenville and his wife, who approached her with courteous intent. "You will come home with us, Mrs. Leigh?" Lady Grenville asked gently, inviting her to share a pleasant Christmas night.

Mrs. Leigh offered a sweet, fleeting smile. She placed one hand on her hostess's arm and gestured with the other toward the west. "I cannot well spend a merry Christmas night while that sound is in my ears," she replied softly.

The group turned to look. Above them, the soft blue sky was surrendering to gray, punctuated here and there by misty stars. But to the west, where the downs and woods of Raleigh met those of Abbotsham, the heavens were veiled in delicate white flakes. Iridescent patches marked the sun's final descent, displaying the dying colors of a dolphin, while low on the horizon stretched a band of grassy green.

"What sound troubles you, Mrs. Leigh?" Sir Richard asked, puzzled. "None of us, with our hearts full of joy and ears dulled by the town's clamor, have heard it until this moment. And yet... listen!"

Indeed, the air was filled with a low, deep roar. It hovered over the downs, the woods, the salt marshes, and the river, sounding like the rumble of a thousand wheels, the tramp of endless armies, or perhaps the thunder of a mighty surge battering the pebble ridges.

"The ridge is noisy tonight," Sir Richard observed. "There must have been wind somewhere."

"There is wind now, where my boy is, God help him!" Mrs. Leigh cried out, her voice trembling slightly. Everyone present understood the truth in her words. The spirit of the Atlantic storm had already sent forth its warning—a smooth ground-swell audible two miles inland. By tomorrow, the pebbles currently rattling down with each receding wave might leap to the ridge's summit, hurled like cannonballs onto the marsh by the fury of the advancing hurricane.

"God help my boy!" she whispered again.

"God is as near him by sea as by land," Sir Richard comforted.

"True, but I am a lone mother, and my only desire right now is to return home and pray."

With that, Mrs. Leigh continued up the lane, spending the entire night listening to the thunder of the surging waves, until the sound was utterly drowned out, long before dawn, by the roar of the storm itself.

And where was Amyas on this same Christmas afternoon?

Amyas sat bareheaded in the stern of a small boat in Smerwick Bay. Sea spray whistled through his curly hair as he shouted cheerfully to his crew. "Pull, and with a will, my merry men all, and never mind shipping a sea! Cannonballs are a cargo that doesn't spoil by taking salt-water."

His mother's foreboding had proven accurate. Christmas Eve had been the last of those still, dark, steaming nights typical of early winter, but for the past twelve hours, the western gale had been roaring violently along the Irish coast.

The short daylight of the winter day was fading rapidly. Behind him lay a leaping line of billows whipped into mist by the tempest. Beside him, green, foam-fringed columns rushed up the black rocks only to crash back down in a thousand cataracts of snow. Before him stretched the deep, sheltered bay, though visibility was limited. Some four miles out at sea, a sloping roof of thick gray clouds began, stretching overhead and far inland. It cut the cliffs off at mid-height, hid the Kerry mountains, and plunged the distant firths into the blackness of night. Underneath this awful canopy of swirling mist, the storm howled inward, sweeping before it great foam-sponges and gray salt spray until the entire landscape became hazy, dim, and brown.

Let it howl! For there was more mist than ever salt spray produced, and more thunder than any sea-surge had ever echoed among the cliffs of Smerwick Bay. Along the sandhills, red sparks flashed in the evening gloom—sparks that never came from heaven. These belonged to the fort, now christened by the invaders *Fort Del Oro*, where the hated golden flag of Spain flew proudly alongside San Josepho and eight hundred enemy soldiers. Just three nights prior, Amyas, Yeo, and the finest hands of Winter's command had hoisted four culverins from the Admiral's main deck, floated them ashore, and dragged them up to the battery among the sandhills. Now, the fate of the engagement hung in the balance: would Spanish and Italian condottieri hold their own on British soil against the men of Devon?

Amyas likely gave little thought to his lonely mother at Burrough Court. Instead, his focus was fixed on the quick, bright flashes dancing across the sandhill and the fort, where Salvation Yeo was hurling eighteen-pound shots with deadly precision. He watched with a cool, bitter smile of triumph as the sand flew and the protective gabions crashed.

Amyas and his party had risked their lives aboard ship to secure a fresh supply of ammunition. Winter's battery was out of shot and had been firing stones for the last four hours, lacking better missiles. They ran their boat ashore through the breaking surf, landing in a cove where the beach allowed access. Almost careless whether the vessel capsized or not, they scrambled over the sandhills, each man carrying a pair of shot slung across his shoulder. Leaping into the trenches, Amyas shouted cheerfully to Salvation Yeo:

"More food for the bulldogs, Gunner, and plums for the Spaniards' Christmas pudding!"

"Don't speak to a man at his business, Master Amyas," Yeo grunted, focused intently on his task. "Five mortal times have I missed, but I will bring that accursed Popish rag down, as I'm a sinner."

"Down with it, then! Nobody wants you to shoot crooked. Take good iron to it, not footy paving-stones."

"I believe, sir, that the foul fiend is there, turning my shot aside. I thought I saw him once, but thank Heaven, here comes another ball. Ah, sir, if one could but cast a silver one! Now, stand by, men!"

Once again, Yeo's eighteen-pounder roared, sending a projectile screaming away. Oh, glory! The great yellow flag of Spain, streaming defiantly in the gale, was lifted clean into the air, flagstaff and all, before pitching wildly downward, head-first, far to leeward.

A hurrah erupted from the sailors, answered by the soldiers of the opposing camp, shaking the very clouds above them. But before the echoes faded, a tall officer leaped onto the fort's parapet, clutching the fallen flag. Rearing it as best he could upon the tip of his lance, he held it firmly against the gale while the broken staff was raised anew inside the walls.

Instantly, a dozen longbows were drawn by the defenders. But Amyas shouted from behind, "Shame, lads! Stop and let the gallant gentleman have due courtesy!"

They halted. Amyas sprang onto the battery's rampart, removed his hat, and bowed deeply to the flag-bearer. Once relieved of his burden, the officer returned the bow courteously and descended.

By now, twilight had nearly claimed the sky, and the firing began to slacken. Salvation and his brother gunners, having covered their heavy artillery with tarpaulins, retired for the night. Leaving Amyas, who had volunteered to take the watch until midnight, the rest of the force lay down under arms among the sandhills. Their supper consisted of scanty biscuits, for provisions were running dangerously low. Grumbling softly, they drifted into sleep.

For an hour or more, Amyas paced up and down in the gusty darkness, exchanging occasional words with the sentinel. Suddenly, two men entered the battery, chatting busily. One wore complete armor; the other was clad in the plain short cloak of a man of peace and pen. Yet, their conversation bore no resemblance to sieges, sallies, catapults, bombards, or culverins. Instead, they discussed English hexameters.

Do not imagine, gentle reader, that these two were idling while Rome burned. In that critical year of 1580, the commonwealth of poetry and letters faced dangers far greater than the woes of Ireland (as Raleigh termed it) posed by the Spaniards.
