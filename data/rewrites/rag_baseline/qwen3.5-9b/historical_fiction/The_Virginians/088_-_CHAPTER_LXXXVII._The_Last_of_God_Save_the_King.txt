The air inside the room was thick with the scent of claret and the metallic tang of impending civil war. I sat at the table, watching my mother, a solitary figure in a sea of conflicting loyalties. To the Patriots, I was a Tory; to the Tories, a dangerous Republican. The realm was out of joint, a broken clockwork where every gear turned against the others. I envied the banks of the Waveney, wishing I could retreat from this Virginia chaos, yet duty held me fast. My mother could not face the storm alone, nor could a man of spirit abandon his post. Around us, the well-affected raised their glasses, vowing allegiance to the Crown, their voices rising in a chorus of "God save the King," a mantra that seemed to suspend the gravity of the coming catastrophe.

Among the acquaintances of Virginia, Madam Esmond stood apart, a constant in a shifting landscape. While the gentlemen of Williamsburg prepared for a ball following the news of the Boston Port Bill—a celebration of defiance against Parliament—she refused. Her rejection was absolute. "I go to a ball given by rebels who intend to insult His Majesty!" she declared, her resolve unyielding. She wrote to the stewards, stating clearly that she could not attend. Yet, the irony of perception struck her the moment she stepped out in her chair. A hundred voices, white and black alike, erupted in cheers. They misinterpreted her refusal as an act of supreme patriotism. When she leaned forward, shouting "God save the King" with a voice that cut through the morning air, the crowd echoed her, the chant swelling into a tidal wave of loyalty that drowned out the dissent.

That night, the atmosphere shifted. My brother Harry, dressed in the scarlet coat of Wolfe's regiment, departed with Madam Fanny for the very ball my mother had rejected. We, as strangers to the local politics, were granted entry. I danced with the lady of Mount Vernon, a lively and amiable partner, though my mother's reception of her praise was grim. She viewed the Colonel's choice to dance with Mrs. Washington rather than the Dunmores as a symptom of provincial arrogance, a blindness to the Colonel's former status as a road-surveyor. Pride, she believed, blinded everyone, turning neighbors into rivals.

Three days later, the tension curdled into violence. On the appointed day of fasting and humiliation, my mother hosted a dinner, lighting her house against the city's enforced gloom. She invited Mr. Hardy to sing "God save the King," but when he faltered, she demanded "Britons, strike home!" The command rippled through the room, reaching the ears of the mob gathering outside. A stone flew, shattering the punch bowl before me. My mother did not flinch. Standing by the window, her frilled arms outstretched against the starlit sky, she sang louder, challenging the crowd to throw more stones. The silence that followed was heavy, broken only by the intervention of gentlemen from the tavern who diffused the situation with a mix of deference and threat. The concert ended prematurely, but the damage was done; windows were shattered, and fear settled deep in the bones of my household.

Militia drills commenced, powder barrels stacked in the cellar, and plans for resistance formulated. Yet, beneath the military preparations lay a strange contradiction. Everywhere, the cry was still "God save the King." The patriots claimed they yielded to no men in loyalty, glorying in the name of Britons, even as they plotted separation. I saw the logic in their hesitation: how could a handful of battalions and German mercenaries conquer thirteen million souls on their own soil? It seemed easier now, in retrospect, to trace the errors of the campaign—the paralysis of commanders, the misplaced strategies—but at the time, it felt like a tragedy ordained by Heaven. We watched the rise of a republic, a nation forged in the fires of winter and betrayal, led by a man who possessed a constancy rare among kings. Washington, calm amidst conspiracy, serene before the foe, forgiving despite ingratitude, emerged not just as a general but as a myth. Even he, however, recognized the danger of my work; reading my early drafts, he warned that my portrayal of the conflict would inevitably offend both sides.

This contrariety defined my family. Where Harry, transformed by marriage into a ceremonial figure demanding respect, advocated for the execution of the Governor and the Black Guards, I argued for the inclusion of enslaved men in the fight for liberty. "If you fight for freedom," I reasoned, "surely the negroes may fight, too." Harry's response was visceral, a roar of hatred promising death to any black man he encountered. My mother, however, remained steadfast in her traditionalism, viewing the idea of black insurrection as parricidal. She demanded obedience from all colors, a consistency that separated her from her son's radicalizing nature.

The fracture in our household crystallized around Mr. Gumbo. Once freed by me for his unwavering loyalty, he returned to Virginia only to find himself an outsider among the servants. Free while they were not, he adopted airs of superiority, recounting tales of Europe that bewildered the household. Jealousy festered, particularly between him and Sady, Hal's unfree son. Their conflicts escalated into brutal brawls, a clash of worlds that mirrored the larger political strife. The servants, including Molly, Gumbo's wife, resented his newfound status, accusing him of deceit and infidelity. When my mother, acting as a draconian judge, ordered Gumbo's whipping, the house erupted. Molly attacked her mistress, defending her husband's dignity against the indignity of punishment. I sided with the man; Hal and Fanny supported the lady. The resulting rift was temporary, healing quickly once we resolved to remove the "Standard of Insurrection"—Mr. Gumbo and his wife—from our home, sending them back to Europe where they belonged, leaving behind a house divided by the very lines of the war raging beyond its walls.
