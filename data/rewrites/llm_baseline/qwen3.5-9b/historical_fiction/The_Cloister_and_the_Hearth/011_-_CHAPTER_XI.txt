The piercing gaze of hatred the Burgomaster had fixed upon Gerard, combined with his sudden incarceration, convinced the young man that Ghysbrecht was his implacable foe. Gliding around the corner of the tower, he expected nothing less than a cruel, treacherous trap devised by a wicked man to harm him within those walls, an escape from which would remain unknown to the world. Yet, as he moved stealthily forth, a soft yet courageous hand slipped into his own. Margaret stood beside him, prepared to share this newfound peril.

Before the haunted tower could be fully discerned, a sight struck their vision that froze them in place. Halfway up the structure, a creature with a blazing head, resembling an enormous glow-worm, climbed steadily toward the summit. Its body was shrouded in darkness, though its silhouette was visible against the glare emanating from its luminous head; the entire entity measured nearly four feet in length. At the base of the tower stood a figure clad in white, indistinguishable from a living woman. Gerard and Margaret trembled with awe.

"The rope! The rope!" Gerard gasped. "It is ascending the rope."

As they watched, the glowing form vanished into Gerard's former prison cell, its light flooding the interior and casting a crimson hue across the window. Below, the white figure remained motionless. Those who survive the initial shock of the supernatural often succumb to a peculiar, paralyzing terror—a wild, irresistible urge to throw oneself upon the terrifying object. It captivates the observer with the same hypnotic force a snake holds over a bird. The great tragedian Macready once portrayed this perfectly in *Macbeth*, during Banquo's second apparition; he threw himself backward, head averted, at the horrific shadow. This same primal impulse seized Margaret. She released Gerard's hand, standing bewildered for a moment, before suddenly letting out a wild cry and darting toward the specter.

Gerard, unaware of this natural instinct, instantly believed the Evil One was luring her to her doom. He fell to his knees. "Exorcizo vos! In nomine beatae Mariae, exorcizo vos!"

While the exorcist shrieked his incantations in extreme terror, he received infinite relief when the specter emitted a feeble cry of fear. Discovering that even Hell possessed its minor weaknesses was deeply encouraging. He intensified his prayers, and shortly thereafter, he saw the ghastly shape kneeling at Margaret's feet, begging pitifully for mercy.

Soon, Kate and Giles arrived at the haunted tower. Their surprise was immense upon discovering a new rope dangling from the prisoner's window down to the ground.

"I see how it is," remarked the younger brother, accepting the evidence before him. "Our Gerard has descended this rope. He has escaped. Now, I shall ascend and verify."

"No, Giles, no!" cried the elder, blinded by prejudice. "Do you not see this is sorcery? This rope is a line cast out by the Devil to lure you to destruction. He knows the frailties of every heart; he sees how fond you are of climbing heights. How could our Gerard procure such a rope? How could he fasten it in the sky like this? It defies nature. May the Holy Saints protect us tonight, for Hell is abroad."

"Stuff!" retorted the dwarf. "The road to Hell goes down, and this rope leads up. I have never been lucky enough to climb such a long rope. It might be years before I encounter one so conveniently prepared. Better to be knocked on the head at once than to wait forever for happiness."

With a cry of delight, akin to a cat leaping onto a table laden with fish, he sprang onto the rope. All his athletic energy ignited within him; the only concession Kate managed to extract was permission to fasten a lantern around his neck first.

"A light frightens evil spirits," she explained.

Thus, with his massive arms and legs moving with feather-like agility, Giles ascended the rope faster than his brother had descended. The lantern at the nape of his neck transformed him into a living glow-worm. His sister watched his progress with trembling anxiety. Suddenly, a female figure burst forth from the solid masonry, flying at her with superhuman speed. Kate let out a weak cry—the best effort her tongue allowed, for terror had glued it to her palate. She dropped her crutches, sank to her knees, hid her face, and moaned, "Take my body, but spare my soul!"

Margaret, panting, exclaimed, "Why, it is a woman!"

Kate, quivering, repeated, "Why, it is a woman!"

"How frightened you've made me!" Kate cried.

"I am terrified enough myself. Oh! Oh! Oh!"

"This is strange! But what about the fiery-headed thing? It was with you, and you are harmless! Why are you here at this hour?"

"Nay, why are *you* here?"

"Perhaps we are on the same mission? Ah! You are his dear sister, Kate!"

"And you are Margaret Brandt."

"Yes."

"All the better. You love him; you are here. Then Giles was correct. He has won his freedom."

Gerard stepped forward to confirm the truth, but their conversation was abruptly cut short by a horrifying, unearthly noise, sounding like a sepulcher speaking through a ventriloquist's trick:

With each repetition, the volume increased. Looking upward, they beheld the dwarf, clutching handfuls of parchments, his face alight with fiendish joy and flushed with diabolical fire. The lantern hanging from his neck rendered him a more infernal transparency than any mortal eye had ever witnessed. Simultaneously, the awful imp hurled the parchments at the stunned figures below. Documents rained down like wounded wild ducks; some collapsed immediately, others fluttered helplessly, while still others spread wide, wheeling slowly downward in airy spirals.

Hardly had these settled when the sepulchral roar returned—"Parchment! Parchment!"—and another flock of documents pattered and sailed down. Another followed, until the grass beneath them was whitened by the falling papers.

Finally, the fire-headed imp, with his luminous body and horned hands, slid down the rope like a falling star. Prioritizing business over sentiment, he approached his rescued brother to demand immediate payment for the merchandise he had just delivered.

"Hush!" Gerard warned. "You speak too loudly. Gather them up and follow us to a safer place than this."

"Will you come home with me, Gerard?" asked little Kate.

"I have no home."

"You shall not say that. Who is more welcome than you, after such cruel injustice, to your father's house?"

"Father! I have no father," Gerard replied sternly. "He who was my father has become my jailer. I have escaped his grasp; I will never set foot within his reach again."

"An enemy did this, not our father," Kate insisted, recounting what she had overheard Cornelis and Sybrandt say. Yet, the injury was too fresh to be soothed. Gerard displayed a bitterness of indignation he had previously seemed incapable of feeling.

"Cornelis and Sybrandt are two vile curs who have shown me their teeth and their hearts for a long time, but they could accomplish nothing. My father is the one who granted the Burgomaster authority, or he would not have dared lay a finger on me, a free burgher of this town. So be it. I was his son; I am his prisoner. He has played his part; I shall play mine. Farewell to the borough where I was born, lived honestly, and was imprisoned. While there remains another town in creation, I shall never trouble you again, Tergou."

"Oh! Gerard! Gerard!" Margaret whispered urgently. "Do not provoke him now. Allow his anger to cool!"

Kate turned quickly toward her. "May I look at your face?"

The inspection appeared favorable, prompting her to whisper, "It is a comely face, and not that of a mischief-maker."

"Fear me not," Margaret said in the same gentle tone. "I could not be happy without your love, as well as Gerard's."

"These are comforting words," Kate sobbed. Then, lifting her eyes, she added, "Little did I think I would like you so well. My heart is willing, but my infirmity prevents me from embracing you."

At this suggestion, Margaret gently wrapped her arms around Gerard's sister and kissed her lovingly. "Often he has spoken of you to me, Kate; and often I longed for this moment."

"You, too, Gerard," Kate said, "kiss me before you depart; for my heart grows heavy at parting with you this night."

Gerard kissed her, and she proceeded home on her crutches. The last sound they heard from her was a faint, patient sigh. Then, tears welled thick in Margaret's eyes. But Gerard was a man, and he paid no heed to his sister's sigh.

As they turned toward Sevenbergen, the dwarf nudged Gerard with his bundle of parchments and extended a concave claw. Margaret attempted to dissuade him. "Why take what does not belong to us?"

"Oh, spoil an enemy however you can."
