In Flatland, just as in your own realm, the cardinal directions North, South, East, and West hold absolute sway. Yet, lacking a sun or any celestial beacon to mark the pole, we navigate by a gravity unique to our plane. A natural law dictates a constant pull toward the South, a subtle but unyielding current. In our temperate zones, this tug is faint enough that a healthy woman might wander several furlongs northward without noticing, yet it remains potent enough to anchor our orientation. We rely further on the rain, which descends rhythmically from the North, and on the architecture itself. Our cities are grids of pentagonal dwellings whose walls align strictly north-south, creating roofs that shield inhabitants from the northern deluge. Even in the wilder countryside, where stone is scarce, the trunks of trees offer vertical markers against the infinite expanse.

Yet, navigation is not without peril. In the vast, featureless plains of our temperate regions, where neither house nor tree breaks the horizon, I have found myself stranded for hours, paralyzed until the next rainfall confirmed my bearing. This southern suction weighs heavier on the frail and the elderly, crushing particularly against the delicate constitution of Females. Thus, it is a fundamental code of civility to yield the northern flank of the road to any Lady encountered; a courtesy rendered increasingly difficult in our shifting climates where distinguishing North from South becomes a matter of acute physical sensation rather than mere observation.

Our dwellings possess no windows. Light does not enter; it permeates everything simultaneously, day and night, inside and out, rendering the concept of a window both unnecessary and nonsensical. Centuries ago, our scholars obsessed over the "origin of light," a futile inquiry that eventually flooded our asylum wards with broken minds. The Legislature, weary of suppressing such madness through taxation, finally banned the investigation outright. Alas, I alone among my kin grasp the truth—that light originates from a third dimension—and I am ridiculed as the maddest of fools for daring to speak of spaces beyond our two-dimensional prison.

Turning back to our abodes, the standard form is the pentagon. Two northern sides, RO and OF, slant upward to form the roof, typically devoid of doors. The eastern wall bears a narrow aperture reserved for Women, while the western offers a broad entrance for Men; the southern base remains solid, facing the gravitational pull. Squares and triangles are proscribed. Their sharper angles pose a lethal threat to travelers, whose lines are less distinct than those of living beings. A sudden collision with the corner of a square or triangle could prove fatal. Consequently, since the eleventh century, triangular homes have been illegal, permitted only for military fortifications and powder magazines—structures kept distant from the populace. Squares lingered longer, tolerated under a punitive tax, until three centuries later the Law mandated that in any town exceeding ten thousand souls, the pentagon became the minimum safe angle. Community prudence has since embraced this decree; today, the pentagon reigns supreme, surviving only in the most isolated, backward districts where an antiquarian might stumble upon a forgotten square.
