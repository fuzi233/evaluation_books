Elinor was turning the corner of a half-finished landscape sketch in the drawing room when Marianne sighed, pressing her shoulder to the windowsill overlooking Norland’s wooded park. “What a cruel, terrible pity it is, Elinor,” she said, earnest as ever, “that Edward should have no taste for drawing at all.”

“No taste for drawing?” Elinor repeated, a faint, warm flush rising high on her cheeks that she tried to hide by adjusting the sketch in her lap. “Whatever makes you think that? He does not draw himself, it is true, but he lights up at the sight of other people’s work, and I assure you his natural taste is anything but lacking—he simply never had the chance to nurture it. If he had ever been taught properly, I believe he would have drawn beautifully. He doubts his own judgment so much he hates to speak his mind on any piece of art, but he has an innate, simple, right sense of what is good that almost never leads him wrong.”

Marianne held her tongue, afraid to offend, but she could not reconcile the quiet, polite appreciation Elinor described with the rapturous, soul-shaking delight she believed was the only true mark of taste. She smiled privately at what she thought was her sister’s gentle fond blind spot, even as she honored Elinor for loving Edward so well she would defend his smallest virtues.

“I hope you do not think his general taste is lacking, Marianne,” Elinor went on, teasing softly. “I think I can safely say you do not, for you are always perfectly warm and kind to him. If you truly thought him dull or unfeeling, I know you would never suffer to be civil to him for a minute.”

Marianne fumbled for words. She would never wound her sister for the world, but she could not lie and say she saw in Edward all the tiny, fiery preferences of art and poetry that lit up her own soul. At last she said, “Do not be cross with me, Elinor, if my praise for him does not match every bit of your high opinion. I have not spent the hours you have, picking through his quiet little likes and dislikes, but I think he is the kindest, most sensible man I have ever met. There is no one more worthy of being loved.”

Elinor laughed, soft and relieved. “His dearest friends could not ask for warmer praise than that. I have no right to ask for more.” Marianne was delighted to see her sister so easily pleased.

“Of his sense and his goodness no one who has spoken to him at length could ever doubt,” Elinor continued. “His sharp understanding and steady principles are only hidden by that shyness of his that keeps him quiet half the time. You see enough of him to know how solid and good he is, but you have been so busy doting on mother these past months, I have spent far more time in his company. We have talked for hours about books, about poetry, about all the small things that make a mind feel alive. His imagination is bright, his eye for the world is sharp, his taste is delicate and pure. The more you know him, the better he gets—his manners, his whole face, unfurl the longer you are with him. At first glance he is not striking, not at all, but once you notice how kind and bright his eyes are, how sweet his smile is? I think he is very nearly handsome now. Do you not see it, Marianne?”

“I will think him handsome soon enough,” Marianne promised. “When you marry him, and he is my brother, I will not see a single flaw in his face any more than I see a flaw in his heart now.”

Elinor started at the words, suddenly quiet. She had let her warmth for Edward carry her away, and now she realized Marianne and their mother had already woven a whole future for the two of them, without a single solid promise between them. She knew their natures: what they guessed one minute, they believed the next, and what they hoped for, they expected as a certainty. “I will not pretend I do not think very highly of him,” she said gently. “That I esteem him, that I like him very much.”

Marianne burst out in indignation at once. “Esteem him? Like him? Cold-hearted Elinor! Worse than cold-hearted! You are ashamed to say you love him, and if you use those tame, frigid words one more time I will walk straight out of this room!”

Elinor could not help laughing. “Forgive me,” she said. “I did not mean to hurt you by speaking so quietly of my own feelings. Believe they are far stronger than I have said—strong enough to match his goodness, and the quiet, cautious hope that he might care for me too. But do not go further than that. I am not at all sure of his regard. Some days it feels thin, uncertain, and until I know for certain how he feels, I will not let myself pretend our attachment is something more than it is. There is far more to worry over than just whether he likes me. He is not independent, not at all. We do not know Mrs. Ferrars, his mother, but from Fanny’s little offhand remarks about her, we know she is sharp, greedy, and determined both her sons marry women of great fortune and high rank. Edward knows perfectly well there would be every kind of obstacle in his path if he tried to marry a girl with no money, no connections, like us.”

Marianne stared, stunned, to realize she and her mother’s romantic imaginings had raced so far ahead of the hard, unforgiving truth.

“And you are not secretly engaged to him?” she said. “But it will be, soon, I know it. At least this wait has two good sides: I will not lose you as a sister quite so soon, and Edward will have all the time in the world to nurture that taste for drawing you know he has hidden away. If your love could spur him to learn to paint, how perfectly delightful that would be for the rest of your lives!”

Elinor had spoken her whole, honest truth at last. She knew her regard for Edward was not as secure as Marianne and her mother had thought. Some days he was so quiet, so heavy-spirited, that it felt like more than simple doubt of her affection weighing on him—like the knowledge of his mother’s unforgiving rules was already dragging him down. She could not rest easy about their future, not when he was trapped in a home that would never let him be happy on his own terms. Sometimes, for a few sharp, painful minutes, she feared his affection was nothing more than friendship.

But that quiet fondness had not escaped Edward’s sister Fanny. She seized the first chance to corner Mrs. Dashwood in the breakfast room, dropping hint after hint about her brother’s vast expected inheritance, about Mrs. Ferrars’s unshakable resolve that both her sons make brilliant, wealthy marriages, about how any silly, penniless girl who tried to “trap” Edward would be ruined by the Ferrars family for good. Mrs. Dashwood’s temper flamed hot, she snapped a sharp, scathing reply to Fanny, and stormed out of the room vowing she would not let her beloved Elinor be insulted under this roof for one more week.

It was at that exact moment the post arrived, carrying a letter perfectly timed to her anger. It was from Sir John Middleton, a distant relative of hers, a wealthy, good-natured gentleman who owned a large estate in Devonshire. He offered her a small, cozy cottage on his land, at a rent so low it was barely worth naming, and swore he would make every alteration and repair she could possibly want to make it comfortable. He begged her to come visit his own home, Barton Park, to see for herself if Barton Cottage, a mile down the lane in the same parish, would suit her. The letter was brimming with genuine kindness, exactly the thing a woman who had been stung by the cold cruelty of her own in-laws needed most.

Before that morning, Devonshire, hundreds of miles away from their beloved Norland, would have felt like an unbearable exile. Now it was the best news she could have received. To leave Norland was no longer a grief to mourn—it was a blessing, far better than staying under Fanny Dashwood’s roof, far better than watching the home she had loved for so many years belong to a woman who hated her. She wrote back to Sir John at once, accepting his offer with deep gratitude, then ran to show her daughters the letter to win their approval.

Elinor had long thought it would be far wiser for them to move somewhere far from Norland, rather than stay close by all their old acquaintances and be constantly reminded of what they had lost. She had no cause to argue. The cottage was small, the rent absurdly gentle, and though the thought of leaving Norland, leaving Edward, tugged sharp at her heart, she did not say a word to dissuade her mother from sending the acceptance.
