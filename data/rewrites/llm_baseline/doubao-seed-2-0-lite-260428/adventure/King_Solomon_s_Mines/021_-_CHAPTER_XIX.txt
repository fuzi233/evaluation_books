Ten days after that hellish morning we clawed our way out of the treasure mountain, we were back in our old quarters at Loo. Strange as it sounds, we'd walked away from that nightmare with barely a scratch between us—save for my stubbly shock of hair, which had bleached three full shades grayer the second that rock slammed shut behind us, and Good, who was never quite the same after Foulata died. The poor girl's death hit him harder than any spear wound I'd ever seen him take.

As a man who's knocked about the world long enough to see where all roads lead, I'd be lying if I said her passing wasn't a lucky thing for all of us. She wasn't some ordinary village girl: she was stately, sharp, beautiful as any queen, and there was no way a entanglement between Good and a Kukuana princess would have ended well. She knew it, too. "Can the sun mate with the darkness," she'd said once, "or the white with the black?" No amount of kindness or wit could have untangled that mess.

We never so much as set foot near Solomon's treasure chamber again. After two full days of resting the ache out of our bones, we climbed down into the great pit to hunt for the narrow hole we'd crawled through to escape the mountain—no luck. The rain had fallen and wiped every trace of our tracks clean, and the steep sides of that vast pit were pocked with hundreds of ant-bear and wild animal burrows. There was no telling which one had been our salvation.

The day before we set out back for Loo, we wandered the stalactite caves one more time, a restless, morbid curiosity pulling us even to the Chamber of the Dead. We ducked under the sharp spear of the White Death and stared at the five feet of solid rock that had sealed us in with all that uncounted wealth, Gagool's crushed, mangled remains, and Foulata, lying dead just on the other side. We could not find a single seam or join in that rock no matter how we prodded and pried. The secret of the sliding door was lost to us forever, that massive, inscrutable bit of ancient workmanship unlike anything the modern world could ever build.

Eventually we gave up, disgusted. Even if that rock had suddenly lifted before our eyes, I doubt any of us would have mustered the nerve to step over Gagool's broken body and go back in, not even for all the diamonds in the world. I could have wept leaving all that hoard behind—tens of millions of pounds worth of the finest gems ever piled in one place, likely the greatest treasure in human history. But nothing short of dynamite was moving that slab. Some far-off explorer, a hundred or a thousand years from now, might stumble on the lost "Open Sesame" to the cave, but I doubt it. Those gems will sit locked in their three stone coffers with Foulata's bones, cold and dark, till the end of the world.

We need not have been so glum. I'd had the good sense, you see, to stuff every wide pocket of my old shooting coat and trousers full of loose diamonds before the rock trapped us in that chamber, plus we'd stuffed Foulata's woven basket so full it was bulging, even with the space taken up by our water bottle. A good dozen of the largest stones fell out of my coat pockets when we rolled head over heels down the pit slope, but we still had an unimaginable amount left: ninety-three big stones between 70 and 200 carats apiece, enough to make us all rich beyond any of our wildest dreams, no need to even tell a soul where we found them. We'd not done half badly at all.

When we rode into Loo, Ignosi welcomed us like long-lost brothers. He was well, busy as a bee consolidating his power, rebuilding the regiments that had taken the worst of the casualties in the fight against Twala. He listened to every word of our story, riveted, but when we told him old Gagool was crushed to death under the falling rock, he grew very still.

"Come here," he called to a white-haired ancient induna who sat on the edge of the council circle out of earshot. The old man hobbled over, saluted, and sat. "You are old," Ignosi said.
"Ay, my king. Your father's father and I were born the very same day."
"Tell me—when you were a small boy, did you know Gagool the witch doctress?"
"Ay, my king."
"Was she young then, as you were?"
"Not so. She looked exactly as she did yesterday, and exactly as she looked when my own great-grandfather was a boy. Dried up, ugly, full of nothing but wickedness."
"She is dead now."
"Thank the gods, then. The ancient curse is lifted from the land. I go, my king." The old man bowed and vanished.

"You see, my brothers," Ignosi said to us, "she was no ordinary woman. I rejoice she is gone. She would have left you to die in that dark cave, and sooner or later she would have found a way to kill me too—just as she killed my father, and set Twala on the throne he loved so much. Go on with your tale! I have never heard anything half so strange."

When I finished recounting our escape, I took a breath and broke the news we'd all agreed to share. "Ignosi, the time has come for us to say farewell. We want to see our own land again. Remember your promise to us, now that you are a mighty king: rule justly, respect your laws, never put a man to death without cause. If you do that, you will prosper. Tomorrow at dawn, send us an escort to guide us across the mountains. Is that not right, O king?"

Ignosi covered his face with his hands and was silent for a long minute. "My heart is split in two," he said at last, his voice thick. "What have I done to you three—Incubu, Macumazahn, Bougwan—that you would leave me desolate? You stood at my side through rebellion and battle. Would you abandon me in my hour of victory? Name whatever you want! Wives? Pick any maiden in the land. Homes and cattle? The entire kingdom is yours, as far as your eyes can see. Elephants to hunt, regiments to command? I will give you anything. Anything but you leaving."

"Nay, Ignosi," I said, "we want none of these things. We only want to go home to our own people."

Ignosi's eyes flashed bitter. "So I see now! You care more for those bright shining stones than you care for me, your friend. You have the gems, and now you will sail across the black moving water to sell them and be rich, as all white men want to be. Cursed be those white stones, I say. Any man who steps foot in that cave of Death after you will die. White men, go then."

I laid my hand on his arm. "Ignosi, when you wandered in Zululand, among the white men of Natal, did your heart not ache for the land your mother told you of? The place you were born, the land that was yours by right?"

He nodded, softening. "It was so, Macumazahn."
"Then our hearts ache the same way for our homes, just as yours ached for yours."

Silence hung in the air for a long while before Ignosi spoke again, his voice steady. "You are always wise, Macumazahn. The bird that flies in the air does not want to run along the ground. The white man does not want to live level with the black man, or dwell in our kraals. You must go, and my heart will be sore, for we will be dead to each other—no news will ever pass between our lands again. But listen to me, all three of you. No other white man will ever cross the mountains. I will not let traders come with their guns and their gin, to make my people soft and wicked. I will not let praying men come to stir my people up against my rule, to pave a path for more white men to follow. If a white man comes to my gates, I will send him back. If a hundred come, I will push them back. If armies come, I will fight them with every spear I have, and they will not prevail. I will fill in the pit to the treasure caves, break the white columns, choke every passage with boulders, so no man will ever find those shining stones. But for you three? The path will always be open. You are dearer to me than any living soul."

He paused, his voice cracking. "Farewell, my brave brothers. I will not see you again, for my heart cannot bear it. I will pass a law: your names—Incubu, Macumazahn, Bougwan—shall be *hlonipa*, sacred, like the names of our dead kings. Any man who speaks your name will die. Your memory will live in this land forever, unspoken, for generations. Go now, before I weep like a woman. When you are old, huddled by your fires back in your cold land when the sun no longer gives you heat, you will remember how we stood shoulder to shoulder in the battle we planned, how we killed Twala together, how we won this kingdom. Farewell for ever, my lords and my friends." He threw the corner of his kaross over his head to hide his tear-streaked face, and we left him in silence.

At dawn the next day we rode out of Loo, escorted by our old friend Infadoos, who was heartbroken to see us go, and a full regiment of Buffaloes. Even that early, the whole main street was lined with thousands of Kukuana people, cheering and throwing wild flowers before our horses, blessing us for ridding their land of Twala the tyrant. It was so affecting it made my throat tight, the sort of kindness you never expect from native folk you've fought alongside.

One silly incident broke the heavy mood, though, and gave us something to laugh about. Just before we reached the edge of the town, a pretty young girl with a bouquet of white lilies ran up to Good, who the whole country seemed to adore for his funny eye-glass and single side-whisker. She curtsied, handed him the flowers, and said she had one small boon to ask.

"Speak," Good said, confused.
"Let my lord show his servant his famous beautiful white legs, that I may remember them all my days, and tell my children of them. I have travelled four days' walk just to see them—every person in the land speaks of how white and perfect they are."

"Be damned if I will!" Good spluttered, bright red.
"Come on, old man," Sir Henry laughed, clapping him on the back. "You can't be rude to a lady."
"It's indecent!" Good protested, but eventually he caved, and hiked his trousers up to his knee for her. A roar of delighted gasps went up from all the women watching, and he had to walk with his knee exposed the whole way out of the town before they'd stop staring. Good never got half that level of admiration for his eye-glass or his false teeth. His legs, though? The Kukuana people talked about them for years after, no doubt.

As we rode, Infadoos told us about a secret northern pass over the mountains, far away from Solomon's Great Road. Two years prior, a band of Kukuana ostrich hunters had climbed down it to chase wild ostriches for their plumes, and stumbled on a large, fertile, water-rich oasis far out in the desert. It was that very path Ignosi's mother must have taken when she fled the kingdom with baby Ignosi decades earlier—no wonder we'd never been able to figure out how a woman with a small child could have survived the brutal main desert crossing. This path was easy, and the oasis gave them shelter.

Four days of easy riding later, we stood at the crest of the mountains, staring down at the endless waves of sand stretching out 2000 feet below us, north of the two giant peaks of Sheba's Breasts. At dawn the next day, we reached the edge of the sheer chasm we would climb down to the desert floor, and there we said our goodbyes to Infadoos. The old warrior almost wept, rambling about how he would never see a fight like the one we won ever again, except in his dreams. Good, fumbling in his pocket, handed him a spare eye-glass as a souvenir. The old warrior was delighted, and after three tries managed to screw it right into his eye. Nothing has ever looked more absurd than that grizzled old chief in his leopard skin cloak and ostrich plumes, squinting through a little monocle. He was sure it would make him the most respected man in the whole kingdom.

We shook his hand, took the thunderous royal salute from the Buffalo regiment, and started climbing down the chasm. It was a rough, dangerous scramble, but by sundown we were all safe on the desert floor. That night as we sat by our fire staring up at the steep cliffs above us, Sir Henry laughed and said, "I've had far worse times than the last month, lads. I've never had any weirder ones, though." Good sighed, and said he almost wished he could go back. As for me? I thought, all's well that ends well. I've had a hundred close shaves in my life, but none half as close as that one.

We trudge across the desert for three days, our guides carrying plenty of water, and by the afternoon of the third day we could see the dark green treeline of the oasis on the horizon. By sundown, we were standing on soft grass, listening to the gurgle of running water, at the edge of that forgotten paradise no white man had ever seen before us.
