"Three more days." The words lingered between them as they fell into sleep. But dawn brought no relief—only fever. Trench awoke shivering, his body burning with an illness that grew stronger by the hour. By afternoon, he was delirious, and the roles reversed: Feversham now tended him, just as Trench had once cared for him. The House of Stone, that ancient prison, had claimed its toll. Yet it was no mere chance that struck down Trench at this moment—when freedom, after years of torment, seemed within reach. The sudden surge of hope, the joy of imminent escape, had overwhelmed his weakened frame. His body could not endure the weight of deliverance.

"It’s nothing," Feversham insisted, though his voice trembled. "He’ll be well in a few days."

But Ibrahim shook his head gravely. "It is _Umm Sabbah_," he said—the dreaded typhus that had swept through the prison, claiming lives on the seventh day.

Feversham refused to believe it. "No," he repeated, stubbornly, though fear gnawed at his thoughts. *Will the camel men wait?* Each morning, he walked to the Nile, hoping to see Abou Fatma in his blue robe—always there, always silent. Each time, the man made his sign, but Feversham gave no answer. He could not risk it.

With Ibrahim’s help, he nursed Trench. The boy came daily with food, fetching tamarinds, dates, roots—anything to make cooling drinks. Together, they moved Trench from shade to shade as the sun crossed the zareeba. Some aid was given to Idris’s starving family, and the forty-pound chains were removed. Trench was fed salt-soaked vegetable marrow, his mouth packed with butter, his body oiled and wrapped tightly in camel cloths. The fever ran its course. On the seventh day, Ibrahim whispered: "This is the last. Tonight he will die."

"No," Feversham said fiercely. "Not here. Not like this. 'In his own parish,' he said, 'beneath the trees he knew.' Not here."

His mind had cleared of everything else—the man in blue, the camels, the third feather, Ethne’s fourth—gone. Only Trench mattered. Only the words spoken under the stars on their first night together. "He must not die here," he said again, and stayed by Trench’s side through the long battle between life and death.

At times, the prison seemed to win. At others, Trench’s strong body fought back. That night, life prevailed. The seventh day passed. Even Ibrahim began to hope. On the thirteenth, Trench slept without rambling. When he woke, his head was clear. He found himself swathed in cloths, unable to move, but the heat had passed. The shadow of the House of Stone stretched across the sand. He had no desire to stir—just to lie still, in peace.

Then came shouts from the gate. Prisoners flooded in. Feversham was among them, collapsing beside Trench, exhausted.

"How long have I been ill?" Trench asked.

"Thirteen days."

"I can’t travel yet. You must go. Now. Before it’s too late. Perhaps you can do something for me when you reach Assouan. But I can’t move. Will you go tomorrow?"

"No," Feversham replied. "I wouldn’t leave without you. And it’s too late."

"Too late?" Trench murmured, slow to grasp. He wanted only silence, coolness, rest. But the truth settled in: Abou Fatma had gone.

"The camel men waited no longer," Feversham said. "They feared discovery. They’d return whether we went or not."

"You should have gone," Trench said quietly. For himself, he cared little—so long as he could rest. "Our one chance is lost."

"Not lost," Feversham corrected. "Only delayed. Abou Fatma left me paper, pen, soot mixed with water. He dropped them near me by the river. I hid them. Last night—there was a moon—I wrote to a Greek merchant at Wadi Halfa. He took the letter. He’ll deliver it, get paid. In six months, a year at most, he’ll return to Omdurman."

"Six months?" Trench scoffed. "He’ll ask for another letter. More money. Then say, 'Next year.' I know these people."

"You don’t know Abou Fatma," Feversham said. "He was Gordon’s servant before Khartum fell. He’s been mine since. He waited in Obak while I went to Berber. He risked his life coming here. He’ll come back. Within six months, I swear it."

Trench said nothing. His eyes drifted to a pile of fresh earth in the corner. "What are they digging?"

"A well," Feversham answered.

"A well?" Trench frowned. "So close to the Nile? Why?"

"I don’t know," Feversham admitted. But he did know. Deep inside, he feared the reason. A wall was being built around the prison—high, smooth, unclimbable. Too many had escaped along the Nile bank. Now, they would be locked in forever. The prisoners built it themselves—mud bricks dried in the sun. Feversham helped. Trench joined him as soon as he could stand.

"Our last hope is gone," Trench said.

Feversham didn’t argue aloud. But his heart sank. Day by day, the wall rose—a foot, then two. Twelve feet high, seamless, unbreakable. It stood like a coffin. The palm trees of Khartum vanished from view. Their world was now a square of sky—blue by day, dark and star-strewn by night.

Trench covered his face. "I daren’t look at it," he whispered. "We’ve built our own tomb, Feversham. That’s the truth."

Then he raised his arms and cried out: "Will they never come up the Nile—the gunboats, the soldiers? Have they forgotten us? Good God—have they forgotten us?"

"Hush," Feversham said. "We’ll find a way. We must wait six months. We’ve waited years. Six months—what are they?"

But even as he spoke, he felt the weight of despair.

The next six months passed in silence. No news reached them. The prison was a living hell—scorpions stung at night, guards beat them by day. They lived at the mercy of Idris-es-Saier and Nebbi Khiddr, who always betrayed them when Idris needed money. Religious men came to convert them, and theological debates became their only excitement. One day they’d be freed from shackles; the next, thrown back into darkness.

The sixth month ended. The seventh began. Then the eighth. Still, no word from Abou Fatma.

"He will never come," Trench said.

"Surely he will—if he’s alive," Feversham said.

"But is he?"

One morning, two of the Khalifa’s bodyguards arrived. Idris approached the prisoners.

"Verily God is good to you," he said. "You are to meet the Khalifa. How happy you should be!"

Trench and Feversham rose—not with joy, but dread.

"What does he want?" they thought.

They followed the guards through the streets to the Khalifa’s house. Outside, a white man sat waiting. Inside, the Khalifa sat on a throne, a grey-bearded Greek at his side.

"You are to work in the powder factory," the Khalifa declared. "You will help make gunpowder. Soon, the armies of the Turks will fall."

Feversham opened his mouth to deny it—but Trench stepped forward, speaking fluent Arabic: "There is nothing about gunpowder I do not know."

And so they were assigned to the factory, under the Greek’s supervision.

That man—though unknown to them—would become their savior. Out of pity, he suggested their employment. And when they confessed ignorance, he covered for them.

"I know nothing," Trench admitted. "Except that crystals are used."

"But we leave the prison each day," Feversham said. "That’s something. Who knows when escape might come?"

The factory lay north of the city, on the Nile bank beyond the mud wall, behind the slave market. Every morning, they walked out of the prison, past the storehouses, into the factory. Every evening, they returned—no guard, no escort. Each journey, they searched for Abou Fatma.

But May came, and summer. Still no sign.

"Something has happened to Abou Fatma," Feversham said. "Perhaps he was caught at Berber."

"He will not come," Trench said.

Feversham could no longer pretend otherwise. He did not know that Abou Fatma had been wounded—a sword thrust in the thigh as he fled. He had been recognized by an old gaoler, barely escaped, and spent months recovering in Assouan’s hospital.

Yet hope stirred again. One dusk, as they passed the storehouses, a shadowy figure whispered: "Stop."

Trench feigned injury, kneeling to examine his foot. The man walked past, dropping a slip of paper at their feet. A Suakin merchant—Mohammed Ali—had delivered it.

Trench picked it up, hid it, limped on. Once clear, he read aloud. His voice shook.

"A man will bring you a box of matches. When he comes, trust him.—Sutch."

"Who is Sutch?" Trench asked.

"A great friend," Feversham said. "He’s in Egypt. Probably Suakin."

A man with matches! They might meet him tonight!

But it was a month later when, on the riverbank, an Arab pushed past and said: "I am the man with the matches. Tomorrow, same place, same hour."

He dropped a box of colored matches. Feversham stooped—but Trench stopped him.

"Don’t touch them," he said, pressing the box into the ground.

"Sutch!" Feversham breathed. "He comes to help! How did he know?"

Trench trembled with excitement, but dared not speak. Both men slept fitfully, waking with the feeling that something immense had changed.

Feversham, gazing at the stars, imagined he was in the garden of Broad Place, Surrey—the pines, the house, the hills. He fell asleep at dawn—only to be shaken awake by Trench.

"Suppose they keep us in today," Trench whispered, terrified. "Suppose they do?"

"Why would they?" Feversham asked—but the fear gripped him too.

Idris crossed the yard, unbolted the door. The prisoners surged out—some screaming, some fighting. One man ran, foaming at the mouth, smashing his head against the wall.

"He’s mad!" Trench gasped.

And suddenly, he spoke of madness—his own fear of losing reason. To die was bearable. But to go mad—foaming, raving, cast aside—was horror.

"If this man with the matches fails," he said, "I’ll be near it—very near it."

He buried his face in his hands.

Later, at the factory, a man named Abdul Kader appeared.

"I am here to arrange your escape," he said. "But flight is impossible."

"Impossible?" Feversham asked.

"Yes. I brought three camels—two died. The Effendi at Suakin gave me money—but not enough. If you write a letter asking for two hundred pounds, I’ll return in three months."

Trench turned away. All hope drained from him. Abdul Kader wasn’t risking his life—he was a courier, shuttling between Omdurman and Suakin for money. No escape.

"I have nothing to write with," Feversham said.

Abdul produced paper and pen. "Write quickly," he urged.

Feversham wrote—but knew it was useless.

He handed the letter over, took Trench’s arm, and walked away.

They reached the mud wall at sunset. The Nile shimmered—olive, pink, green. Stars emerged. Drums beat in the town.

They walked slowly, heads down, hearts broken. Hope was dead. Despair had taken root.

Then, an Arab stepped beside them.

"The camels are ready—ten miles west," he whispered.

Feversham looked up. Slowly, the meaning sank in.

"Abou Fatma!" he breathed.

"Hoosh," Abou Fatma said. "The camels are ready. Now."

Trench leaned against the wall, pale, weak. He seemed about to faint.

"Is it true?" he asked faintly.

Before Feversham could answer, Abou Fatma spoke: "Walk slowly. Before you reach the end of the wall, it will be dusk. Cover your heads, wrap rags around your chains—so they don’t rattle. Turn back, go near the water beyond the storehouses. I’ll be there with a blacksmith to remove your chains. Keep your faces hidden. Do not stop. He’ll think you’re slaves."

He handed them rags, held his hands behind his back.

They walked forward slowly. Dusk crept across the river, climbed the sand, enveloped them. They sat, wrapped the rags around their chains, secured them tight.

The sunset faded. Darkness gathered. They turned, walked back.

By the water’s edge, Abou Fatma waited with the blacksmith. Without a word, the chains were knocked off.

"Come," Abou said. "No moon tonight. How long before they notice?"

"Who knows?" Feversham said. "Maybe already. Maybe not till morning. There are many prisoners."

They ran—up the sand slope, through tribal quarters, across the city, through the cemetery.

On the far side stood a disused house. A man rose in the doorway. Abou Fatma entered, returned moments later—each leading a camel.

"Mount," Abou said. "Bring the camel’s head around. Hold it as you climb."

"I know the trick," Trench said.

Feversham climbed behind him. The two Arabs mounted the second camel.

"Ten miles to the west," Abou Fatma said—and struck the camel on the flanks.

Behind them, the lights dimmed. The drums faded.
