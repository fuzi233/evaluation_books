While Baldassarre stood transfixed by the thunderous voice of Savonarola, he remained unaware that another figure had slipped through the rear doorway, standing in the shadows to watch him. It was Piero di Cosimo. The painter cared little for the sermon itself; his purpose was singular—to observe the escaped prisoner. As the preacher paused, allowing the congregation to surrender to a wordless emotional swell, the newcomer stepped forward and laid a hand upon Baldassarre's arm.

Baldassarre turned, his face still streaked with slow-moving tears, though a vigorous sigh suggested the storm within him was spent. The painter spoke softly.

"Shall I cut your cords? I have heard how you were taken."

Baldassarre hesitated, his gaze sharpening with suspicion at the intruder. Finally, he nodded. "If you will."

"Better step outside," Piero suggested.

Baldassarre eyed him warily once more. Seeing the doubt, Piero smiled, drew a knife, and severed the bonds. He began to suspect that the prisoner's madness was not entirely unfounded; there was a peculiar intensity in the man's expression. *Well,* he mused, *if he causes trouble, he will be tied again quickly. Let the poor devil have a chance, at least.*

"You fear me," Baldassarre murmured, his voice low. "You do not wish to know my name."

Baldassarre crossed his arms, savoring the long-departed sensation of muscle and strength. His look softened, taking on a quiet resolve. "No. I have nothing to tell."

"As you wish," Piero replied, though he offered a shrug. "Yet perhaps you seek shelter? We Florentines are hospitable to travelers with torn doublets and empty bellies. There is a hospital for the poor outside our gates. If you like, I can guide you there. The French soldier who captured you has been sent away."

Baldassarre nodded silently, accepting the offer, and the two men walked out together.

As they moved along the Via dell' Oriuolo toward the gate of Santa Croce, Piero broke the silence. "Would you not sit for a portrait? I am a painter, and I would pay well for such a likeness."

Suspicion flared again in Baldassarre's eyes. "No."

"Ah!" Piero said, his tone curt. "Very well. Go straight ahead; you will find the Porta Santa Croce. Outside lies the hospital for travelers. So, you decline my services?"

"I thank you for what you have already done. I require nothing more."

"It is as it should be," Piero muttered, turning away.

*A mysterious old tiger,* the artist thought, watching Baldassarre's retreating back. *Truly worth painting. Ugly, carved with deep lines, as if the plow and the harrow had tilled over his very heart. A stark contrast to my bland, smiling Messer Greco—my *Bacco trionfante*, who has wed the fair Antigone against all history and reason. Ah! His scholar's blood curdled uncomfortably at the sight of that old fellow's grip!*

When Piero re-entered the Piazza del Duomo, the vast multitude that had listened to Fra Girolamo was streaming out through every door. Their haste to resume their daily duties testified to the profound weight of the sermon that had held them captive. The artist leaned against the angle of the Baptistery, observing the departing throng with delight. He marveled at the variety of their garments and the sharp, characteristic faces—faces reminiscent of those Masaccio had captured fifty years prior, and those Domenico Ghirlandaio had not yet fully left behind.

This morning marked a peculiar occasion. The Frate's audience, usually multifarious, displayed an even more complete cross-section of Florence's classes and political factions. There were men of high birth, accustomed to public office, who had become conspicuous not only as enemies of the Medici and champions of popular government but as thorough *Piagnoni*, embracing the Frate's doctrines with fervor. Among them were men of authoritative presence, like Francesco Valori, perhaps sharing a hot, arrogant temperament, gratified by the immediate divine authority to bring about freedom in their own image. Others, like Soderini, possessed less of the ardent *Piagnone* and more of the wise politician.

At their sides stood men of family, like Piero Capponi—brave, unindoctrinated lovers of sober republican liberty who preferred action to argument, holding no specific grudge against any idea that kept the Medici out and made room for public spirit. Beside them were doctors of law, whose studies of Accursius had not extinguished their ardour, preventing them from becoming enthusiastic *Piagnoni*; Messer Luca Corsini, for instance, who was destined to raise his learned arms in street stone-throwing for the cause of religion, freedom, and the Frate.

Among the dignitaries carrying their black *lucco* or furred mantles with habitual authority, there were many with contemplative, sensitive faces: scholars inheriting names like Strozzi and Acciajoli, already minded to take the cowl and join the community of San Marco; artists driven to new heights by Savonarola's teaching, such as the young painter who had recently surpassed himself in his fresco of the divine child in the Frate's bare cell—unconscious yet that he would one day wear the tonsure himself and be known as Fra Bartolommeo. There was the mystic poet Girolamo Benevieni, perhaps hastening to carry tidings of the beloved Frate's imminent arrival to his friend Pico della Mirandola, who was never to see another morning.

Well-born women appeared, attired with such scrupulous plainness that their refined grace was the sole distinction separating them from their less aristocratic sisters. A predominant proportion belonged to the genuine *popolani* or middle class, belonging to both the Major and Minor Arts, conscious of purses threatened by war-taxes. More striking, perhaps, than all other classes were the long stream of poorer tradesmen and artisans. Their faith and hope in his Divine message varied from the rude, undiscriminating trust in him as the friend of the poor and enemy of the oppressive rich, to an eager tasting of biblical subtleties that took a peculiar hold on the sedentary artisan, illuminating the long, dim spaces beyond the board where he stitched with a pale flame that seemed to him the light of Divine science.

Yet, scattered among these devoted disciples were many who were not his followers at all. Some were Mediceans who, from fear and policy, had begun to feign deference to the prevailing spirit of the popular party. Others were sincere advocates of free government who viewed Savonarola merely as an ambitious monk—half sagacious, half fanatical—who had made himself a powerful instrument with the people and must be accepted as an important social fact. There were even bitter enemies: members of the old aristocratic anti-Medicean party determined to seize the reins once more for certain chief families, or licentious young men who detested him as the killjoy of Florence.

The sermons in the Duomo had already become political incidents, attracting the ears of curiosity and malice as well as faith. Men of ideas, like young Niccolò Machiavelli, went to observe and write reports to friends in country villas. Men of appetites, like Dolfo Spini, bent on hunting down the Frate as a public nuisance who made game scarce, went to feed their hatred and lie in wait for grounds of accusation.

Perhaps no preacher ever wielded such massive influence as Savonarola, yet no preacher ever worked with such heterogeneous materials. One secret of this influence lay in the highly mixed character of his preaching. Baldassarre, wrought into an ecstasy of self-martyring revenge, was only an extreme case among the partial and narrow sympathies of that audience. In Savonarola's sermons, there were strains that appealed to the finest susceptibilities of human nature, alongside elements that gratified low egoism, tickled gossiping curiosity, and fascinated timorous superstition. His need for personal predominance, his labyrinthine allegorical interpretations of Scripture, his enigmatic visions, and his false certitude regarding Divine intentions never ceased, in his own large soul, to be ennobled by fervid piety, a passionate sense of the infinite, active sympathy, and a clear-sighted demand for the subjection of selfish interests to the general good—a quality he shared with the greatest of mankind.

But for the mass of his audience, the entire weight of his preaching lay in his strong assertion of supernatural claims, his denunciatory visions, and the false certitude that gave his sermons the urgency of a political bulletin. Having once held that audience in his mastery, it was necessary to his nature—and necessary for their welfare—that he should keep it. The effect was inevitable. No man ever struggles to retain power over a mixed multitude without suffering vitiation; his standard must become their lower needs rather than his own best insight.

The mysteries of human character were seldom presented in a way more fitted to check the judgments of facile knowingness than in Girolamo Savonarola. Yet, we can afford him a reverence that requires no shutting of the eyes to fact if we regard his life as a drama accompanied by great inward modifications during its outward changes. Up to this period, when his direct action on political affairs had only just begun, it is probable that his imperious need of ascendancy burned undiscernibly within the strong flame of his zeal for God and man.

It was the fashion of old, when an ox was led out for sacrifice to Jupiter, to chalk the dark spots and give the offering a false show of unblemished whiteness. Let us fling away the chalk and boldly say: the victim is spotted, but it is not therefore in vain that his mighty heart is laid on the altar of men's highest hopes.
