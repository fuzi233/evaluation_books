"Stop."

The command cut through the morning stillness like a blade, arresting Romola's retreat before she had taken ten paces from the cypress tree.

She froze. The wind died in the leaves above her. Every instinct screamed at her to run, to dissolve into the widening gulf of the countryside that separated her from the suffocating walls of Florence, but her feet remained planted. A voice, familiar and vibrating with a terrifying resonance, had claimed her identity before she could even process the threat.

"You are Romola de' Bardi," the voice stated, devoid of malice yet absolute in its verdict. "The wife of Tito Melema."

Romola did not turn. She knew that cadence; she had heard it echo in her private chambers and in the public squares alike. To acknowledge it would be to grant the speaker the power to define her reality. Instead, she sat rigid on the stone, her spine straightening against the chill, her internal state a war zone of awe and furious resistance.

This was one of the black-robed figures of the Order, a man daring to intrude upon her solitary flight. It was a violation of privacy, yes, but it was also something far more dangerous. It felt as though Destiny itself—a capricious, sceptred deity—had descended from the heavens to grasp her with flesh-and-blood fingers, demanding an accounting she was not prepared to render.

"You are fleeing Florence in disguise," the voice continued, stripping away her pretenses. "I hold a direct command from God to halt you. You are not permitted to leave."

Anger, hot and sharp, flared in Romola's chest. She refused to offer the speaker the satisfaction of seeing her defiance. Sitting perfectly still, she challenged him with a voice that shook with suppressed rage.

"What right do you possess to address me? Or to obstruct my path?"

"The right of a messenger," the voice replied, calm and unyielding. "You have donned religious vestments, yet you harbor no religious intent. You sought the habit solely as camouflage. However, you were not allowed to pass undetected. It has been revealed to me who you are: a woman attempting to escape the station God has ordained. You wish to bury your true name and your true station, to craft a new identity governed solely by your own will. I have orders to recall you. My daughter, you must return to your post."

With every syllable, Romola's resolve hardened. She feared that if she showed even a flicker of submission, she would collapse into paralysis. She spoke with increasing irritation.

"I shall not return. I recognize no jurisdiction in priests or monks to dictate my actions. You wield no power over me."

"I know," the voice conceded, sounding weary rather than defeated. "I know you were raised in contempt for obedience. But it is not this poor monk who seeks to bind you; it is Truth itself that commands you. And Truth cannot be evaded. Either you submit, and it becomes your guide, or you resist, and it becomes a chain you will drag through eternity. But you *will* obey, my daughter. Your former servant returns with the mules shortly; my companion has gone ahead to retrieve him. You are returning to Florence."

Romola surged upward, her eyes blazing with defiance. She faced the speaker directly. It was Fra Girolamo. She recognized him instantly. Their heights were nearly equal, their faces aligned in a confrontation of equals. She had risen to shout words of rejection, but they died in her throat. Fra Girolamo's gaze was not angry; it was unnervingly calm. The impact of that look was alien to her, washing away her anger with a shame that felt entirely irrelevant to her struggle.

There was no ethereal beauty in Savonarola's face. It was rugged, defined by severe features honed by mental rigor and physical discipline. Yet, the reason Romola faltered was not aesthetic. It was the profound sense of genuine interest and care radiating from his stare—a connection based on shared humanity rather than personal affection. It was the first time she had encountered a gaze that expressed simple fellowship as a binding force. Such a look constituted half the vocation of a spiritual leader, and suddenly, questioning his authority felt impossible. She stood silent, staring into his eyes, while he spoke again.

"You claim your freedom proudly, my daughter. But who is so degraded as the debtor who believes himself free?"

A sting struck Romola, and her expression shifted as if a pale lightning bolt had flashed across her features.

"And you are fleeing your debts: the debt of a Florentine woman; the debt of a wife. You are turning your back on the lot assigned to you to forge a different path. But can a man or woman choose their duties? No more than they can select their birthplace or their parents. My daughter, you are running from the presence of God into the wilderness."

As the heat of anger drained from her mind, a strange presentiment took its place. Perhaps there was strength in submission, provided this man possessed a valid law to enforce. But no—he could not possibly understand the forces driving her. Yet, she could not simply refuse guidance anymore. Constrained by a desperate need to remain respectful while resisting, the title she had never previously used rose to her lips without hesitation.

"My father," she whispered, the words foreign yet inevitable. "You cannot comprehend the reasons compelling me to go. Only I can know them. No one else can judge for me. I have been driven by great sorrow. I am resolved to proceed."

"I know enough, my daughter," Savonarola replied, his tone illuminating a truth she had tried to obscure. "My mind has been clarified regarding you. You are unhappy in your marriage; however, I am not a confessor, nor do I seek to pry into matters sealed by the sacrament. I possess a divine warrant to stop you, independent of such secrets. You were warned by a message from heaven, delivered in my presence—warned *before* your marriage, when you could still lawfully choose liberty. But you chose the bond. By deliberately breaking it—and I speak to you as a pagan if the sanctity of matrimony holds no sway for you—you are violating a pledge. Of what grievances will you complain, my daughter, when you yourself commit one of the gravest wrongs a woman and citizen can perpetrate? Withdrawing in secret and disguise from a vow made before God and your fellow men? Of what injustices will you lament when you are shattering the most fundamental law underpinning human trust: faithfulness to the spoken word? Is this the wisdom you have acquired by scorning the Church's mysteries? To fail to perceive the bare duty of integrity, where the Church sees not only integrity but religion?"

Blood rushed to Romola's face, and she recoiled as if struck. "I would not have assumed a disguise," she began, but her voice failed her. She was too shaken by the implication in the friar's words—that her actions mirrored Tito's—to continue.

"And to sever that pledge, you flee Florence: Florence, the only city in the world where you owe the debt of a fellow citizen."

"I should never have left Florence," Romola murmured, her voice trembling, "as long as there was any hope of fulfilling a duty to my father there."

"Do you acknowledge no tie save that of a child to her father in the flesh?" Savonarola countered, his voice rising with intensity. "Your life has been lived in blindness, my daughter. You have dwelled with those perched on a high hill, looking down upon the lives of their neighbors with disdain. I know their hollow discourse. They fill their days with fancied wisdom about the past while scorning God's work in the present. Doubtless you were taught that pagan women once understood living for the Republic, yet you have never felt that you, a Florentine woman, should live *for* Florence. If your own people wear a yoke, will you slip from under it rather than struggle alongside them to lighten it? There is hunger and misery in our streets, yet you declare, 'I care not; I have my own sorrows; I will depart, perhaps to ease them.' The servants of God strive for a law of justice, peace, and charity so that the hundred thousand citizens among whom you were born may be ruled rightly; yet you regard this as trivial, like a bird spreading its wings to fly wherever it pleases in search of food. And yet you have scorned the teaching of the Church, my daughter. As if you, a wilful wanderer following your own blind choices, were inferior to the humblest Florentine woman who stretches out her hands with her own kin, begging blessings for them; who feels a sisterhood with the neighbor kneeling beside her, regardless of bloodline; who contemplates the mighty purpose God has for Florence; and who waits and endures because the promised work is vast, and she feels herself small."

"I was not departing for ease or indulgence," Romola insisted, lifting her head with a desperate urge to defend herself. "I was leaving for hardship. I expect no joy; it has departed from my life."
