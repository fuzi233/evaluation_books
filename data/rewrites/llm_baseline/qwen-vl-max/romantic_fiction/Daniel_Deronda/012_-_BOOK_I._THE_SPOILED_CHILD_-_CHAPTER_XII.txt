CHAPTER XII. “O gentlemen, the time of life is short; To spend that shortness basely were too long, If life did ride upon a dial’s point, Still ending at the arrival of an hour.” —SHAKESPEARE: _Henry IV_.

On the morning after the archery gathering, Mr. Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt sat at breakfast with his companion, Mr. Lush. The room was bathed in summer light, the open windows framing a view of sun-dappled grass and the quiet sweep of parkland beyond, where trees stood like silent sentinels. Inside, every detail spoke of refined comfort—antique furniture, polished wood, and a hushed elegance that seemed to breathe with restraint. Yet the air between the two men carried a different tone: not warmth, but a careful distance.

Grandcourt had drawn his chair away from the table, one leg draped over another, his cigar held loosely as he gazed out at the lawn. His posture suggested ease, but there was something rigid beneath it—the stillness of someone who expects deference without effort. Lush, meanwhile, continued eating, though his attention flickered between his plate and the man across from him. Between them, six dogs wandered in lazy circles—some sniffing at crumbs, others lounging on rugs or pressing their heads against their master’s knees. All except Fetch, the sleek liver-colored water-spaniel, who sat motionless, her dark eyes fixed on Grandcourt with unwavering devotion.

In his lap rested a tiny Maltese, wrapped in soft fur and adorned with a silver collar and bell. Whenever Grandcourt freed a hand from cigar or cup, it would gently stroke the small dog, murmuring nothing, yet radiating a quiet affection that seemed to exclude all else.

Fetch watched. And waited.

At last, she could endure no more. With a slow, deliberate movement, she placed her large, silky paw upon Grandcourt’s knee.

He looked down—not with kindness, nor anger, but with detached observation. For a moment, silence stretched. Then, with visible effort, he set aside his cigar and lifted the Maltese close to his chin, patting its head with practiced care. Throughout this, his gaze never left Fetch. She whimpered—a soft, pleading sound, barely audible—then lowered her head beside her paw, her eyes wide with appeal.

It was a moment any dog lover might have read as heartbreak. But Grandcourt did not respond with tenderness. When the whimper rose into a full howl, he pushed her away sharply, without a word.

The Maltese was dropped onto the table, where its black nose nearly touched a salt cellar. Grandcourt turned back to his cigar, only to find it had gone out. He frowned—not at the extinguished ember, but at the dog whose cry had disturbed the rhythm of his morning.

“Turn out that brute,” he said quietly, not looking at Lush.

Lush rose immediately. Though Fetch weighed more than he liked, he lifted her without complaint and carried her outside, tending to her in some way that took several minutes. When he returned, he lit his own cigar, settled into a position where he could see Grandcourt’s face, and asked, “Shall you ride or drive to Quetcham today?”

“I am not going to Quetcham.”

“You didn’t go yesterday.”

A pause. Grandcourt exhaled slowly. “I suppose you sent my card and inquiries.”

“I went myself at four. Said you’d be there shortly. They’ll assume something prevented you. Especially if you go today.”

Another silence. Then, “Who else is invited here with their wives?”

Lush pulled out a notebook. “Captain and Mrs. Torrington next week. Then Mr. Hollis and Lady Flora. The Cushats. The Gogoffs.”

“A ragged lot,” Grandcourt remarked after a while.

“Why did you ask the Gogoffs?” Lush replied. “When you write invitations in my name, please give me a list. I didn’t know about the giantess until now. She spoils the look of the room.”

“You invited them yourself when you met them in Paris.”

“What has that to do with it? I told you—I want a list.”

Grandcourt’s voice had changed. Earlier, it had been low, languid, almost bored. Now it was steady, precise—cutting through idle talk like a blade. It was the voice of command, and Lush knew it well.

“Are there other couples you’d like to invite?” he asked cautiously.

“Yes. Think of some decent people—perhaps with daughters. And bring one of your musicians. Not a comic fellow.”

Lush hesitated. “Do you think Klesmer would come once he leaves Quetcham? Only first-class music will satisfy Miss Arrowpoint.”

He said it lightly, but his eyes were fixed on Grandcourt, watching for a reaction. For the first time, Grandcourt turned his gaze toward him—slowly, deliberately. After two long puffs from his cigar, he spoke, lower than before, edged with disdain.

“What in the name of nonsense have I to do with Miss Arrowpoint and her music?”

“Well,” Lush replied, half-smiling, “something. You needn’t trouble much, perhaps. But certain forms must be observed before a man can marry a million.”

“Very likely,” Grandcourt said. “But I’m not marrying a million.”

“That’s a pity,” Lush countered. “To throw away such an opportunity—and undermine your own plans.”

“_Your_ plans,” Grandcourt corrected coldly.

“You have debts. Things may turn out inconveniently. The heirship isn’t absolutely certain.”

Grandcourt said nothing. Lush pressed on.

“It really is a fine chance. Her parents couldn’t ask for better. The daughter—she doesn’t need allowances. She’s not beautiful, but she carries herself well. She could hold any rank. And she wouldn’t refuse what you offer.”

“Perhaps not.”

“And her parents would let you do anything with them.”

“But I wouldn’t want to.”

Lush paused. Then, in a tone thick with warning, “Good God, Grandcourt! After everything you’ve seen—will you let a whim ruin your future?”

“Save your speeches,” Grandcourt said. “I know what I’m doing.”

“What?”

Lush put down his cigar, tucked his hands into his pockets, and leaned back. He looked irritated—but controlled.

“I’m going to marry the other girl.”

“Have you fallen in love?” The question dripped with sarcasm.

“I’m going to marry her.”

“You’ve made her an offer already?”

“No.”

“She’s a young woman with a mind of her own,” Lush said. “She’d make a scene. She knows what she wants.”

“She doesn’t like you,” Grandcourt replied, a ghost of a smile touching his lips.

“True enough,” Lush admitted, then added, again with mockery, “But if you two are devoted, that’ll be enough.”

Grandcourt ignored him. He sipped his coffee, stood up, and stepped out onto the lawn, the dogs trailing behind. Lush watched him go, then resumed his seat. He lit his cigar slowly, stroked his beard thoughtfully, and finally said, softly, “Check, old boy.”

Lush was no fool. Over fifteen years, he had learned what worked—and what didn’t—with Grandcourt. Once a scholar, nearly ordained, he had instead become a traveling companion to a marquess, then to young Grandcourt, who had lost his father early. Grandcourt found Lush indispensable—efficient, discreet, always ready. In return, Lush enjoyed a life of leisure, unburdened by labor, thanks to the arrangements he managed for his employer.

Their relationship had grown into something neither honored nor questioned. Grandcourt relied on Lush, but never respected him. He saw him as useful—like a tool—but also as something that could be dismissed with a word, even kicked, if necessary. Yet Grandcourt never actually kicked anyone. That act was too personal, too undignified. A gentleman’s dogs should be kicked for him. So instead, he spoke in ways that hinted at violence—words sharp enough to sting, but safe because Lush lacked the spirit to retaliate.

And why should he? A man born into modest means, raised to sacrifice comfort for education, now lived in luxury—dining well, riding fine horses, indulging every desire—all without lifting a finger. He had once been known for scholarship, but now that knowledge was merely a scent, a remnant of a past he rarely revisited. His sinecure required nothing more than the appearance of learning. And he was content with that.

He was aware, too, that Grandcourt considered him expendable. But he accepted it—as just another quirk in the master’s unpredictable nature. Since he believed himself blameless, he saw no need to resist temptation, especially when it came wrapped in ease.

So today, despite the tension, Lush retreated to his private room and played the violoncello for an hour—his music rising like a quiet defiance, or perhaps simply a reward for enduring another morning with the man who ruled his world without ever needing to raise his voice.
