It’s a well-known fact—though I can’t swear to the source—that when you’re feeling particularly on top of the world, Fate decides it’s time for a little surprise visit. Shakespeare might’ve said it, or maybe it was some other clever fellow with a penchant for poetic doom. But whatever the origin, it’s absolutely true: just as you’re basking in the golden glow of perfect contentment, life sneaks up behind you with a lead pipe and gives you a good whack. And me? I’m a prime example.

Take this whole business with Lady Malvern and her son Wilmot. A moment before they showed up, I was blissfully convinced that everything in my life was exactly as it should be. The sun was shining (or at least the sky wasn’t threatening rain), I’d just emerged from a cold shower feeling like a newborn colt, and—best of all—I’d finally stood up to Jeeves. That’s right. After months of being treated like a slightly confused child, I had *asserted myself*. Not just once, but twice! First, he tried to make me give up a perfectly good suit—fair enough, I suppose, since his taste is impeccable—but then he dared to forbid me from wearing my beloved cloth-topped boots. That was the final straw. Then came the hat crisis: he wanted me to wear the Longacre (as worn by John Drew, apparently), while I insisted on the Country Gentleman (worn by another famous actor, though I forget his name). After a tense standoff involving several rounds of firm refusal and one rather dramatic purchase, I won. I wore the Country Gentleman. I felt like a man again. A free man.

So there I was, standing in the bathroom, towel-drying my spine like a Roman emperor preparing for battle, humming slightly off-key, when the door tapped.

“Hullo?” I called.

“Lady Malvern wishes to see you, sir,” said Jeeves, voice smooth as silk.

“Eh?”

“Lady Malvern, sir. She is waiting in the sitting-room.”

I blinked. “Pull yourself together, Jeeves,” I said, trying to sound stern. “You know perfectly well no one’s in the sitting-room. It’s barely ten o’clock!”

“I gathered from her ladyship, sir, that she arrived early this morning from an ocean liner.”

Ah. That made it marginally less absurd. I remembered arriving in America myself about a year prior—shot out onto a foreign shore at six-thirty in the morning like a startled badger. So perhaps it was possible.

“Who on earth is Lady Malvern, Jeeves?”

“Her ladyship did not confide in me, sir.”

“Is she alone?”

“She is accompanied by Lord Pershore, sir. I believe he may be her son.”

“Oh. Well, put out something suitable, and I’ll be dressing.”

“Our heather-mixture lounge is ready, sir.”

“Lead the way.”

As I dressed, I tried to recall who Lady Malvern could possibly be. It wasn’t until I’d wrestled my shirt over my head and reached for the studs that it clicked.

“I’ve placed her, Jeeves. She’s a friend of Aunt Agatha’s.”

“Indeed, sir?”

“Yes. I met her at lunch one Sunday before I left London. Vicious woman. Writes books. Did one on social conditions in India after the Durbar.”

“Yes, sir. Pardon me, sir, but not that tie!”

“What?”

“Not that tie with the heather-mixture lounge, sir.”

I froze. This was serious. I thought I’d broken the ice with Jeeves. But here he was, back to his old ways—criticizing my fashion choices like I was still a boy who needed guiding.

“What’s wrong with it?” I demanded. “It’s cheerful pink. Nothing more.”

“Too ornate, sir.”

“Nonsense! I’ve seen you glare at it before. Speak plainly!”

“Unsuitable, sir.”

“Jeeves,” I said, clenching my jaw, “this is the tie I wear.”

“Very good, sir.”

He turned away. Dashed unpleasant. I could see the wound in his soul. But I held firm. I tied the tie, slipped into my coat and waistcoat, and marched into the sitting-room.

“Halloa! Halloa! Halloa!” I boomed. “What?”

“Ah! How do you do, Mr. Wooster? You haven’t met my son, Wilmot, I think? Motty, darling, this is Mr. Wooster.”

Lady Malvern was a force of nature—a large, energetic, radiant woman who seemed to occupy more space than the room itself. She sat in my biggest armchair as if it had been custom-built for her, and she radiated warmth and cheer like a furnace. Bright eyes, yellow hair, and fifty-seven front teeth visible at all times. She had the kind of presence that made you feel instantly inadequate—like a small boy caught in a drawing-room full of adults.

Motty, her son, was quite the opposite. Tall, thin, pale, and shy. He looked like a ghost who’d forgotten how to speak. Same yellow hair, plastered down and parted in the middle. Eyes dull grey with pink rims. Chin drooping. No eyelashes. Mild, furtive, sheepish—basically, the sort of chap who would nod politely if someone asked him to sign a confession.

“Awfully glad to see you,” I said. “So you’ve popped over? Making a long stay?”

“About a month,” she replied. “Your aunt gave me your address and told me to be sure and call on you.”

I felt a wave of relief. Aunt Agatha had been distant ever since I’d gone to New York to rescue Cousin Gussie from a music-hall girl—only to discover that Gussie had married her and joined the stage himself. Aunt Agatha had been furious. I hadn’t dared return. So hearing that she was now recommending people to visit me was like finding out I hadn’t been banned from England after all.

“You aunt said you’d do anything in your power to help us,” she continued.

“Rather! Absolutely!”

“Thank you so much. I want you to put dear Motty up for a little while.”

I didn’t get it.

“Put him up? For clubs?”

“No, no! Darling Motty is essentially a home bird. Aren’t you, Motty?”

Motty, who had been sucking the knob of his walking stick, managed a weak:

“Yes, mother.”

“I wouldn’t have him belong to clubs. I mean, have him live with you while I’m away.”

The words landed like a sack of bricks. I stared at her. She seemed utterly unaware of the horror she was inflicting.

I glanced at Motty. He was staring blankly at the wall, mouth still nuzzling his stick. The idea of having this creature lodged in my flat for a month—or longer—was enough to make me consider jumping out the window.

But before I could protest, she went on, rolling over me like a steamroller.

“I’m leaving New York by the midday train. I must visit Sing-Sing prison—I’m very interested in American prison reform. Then I’ll travel across to the coast, visiting points of interest. I’m here primarily on business. You’ve read my book, _India and the Indians_, I hope? My publishers want a companion volume on the United States. I’ll only be here a month, but that should suffice. I was less than a month in India, and Sir Roger Cremorne wrote his _America from Within_ after just two weeks. I’d love to take Motty with me, but poor boy—he gets sick on trains. I’ll pick him up on my return.”

From my vantage point, I could see Jeeves in the dining-room, laying the breakfast table. I wished I could talk to him. I knew he’d have a solution. But instead, I heard the door open and closed again.

“Jeeves!” I called. “What about it?”

“Sir?”

“What’s to be done? You heard it all. That… *excrescence* is coming to stay.”

“Pill, sir?”

“The what?”

“Pill, sir.”

I narrowed my eyes. This was unlike Jeeves. He was usually composed, never petty. But then I realized—ah! The tie. He was still upset about the pink tie. He was getting revenge.

“Lord Pershore will be staying from tonight, Jeeves,” I said coldly.

“Very good, sir. Breakfast is ready.”

I could have wept. There was no sympathy. None. Just silence and cold professionalism. I almost gave in and told him to destroy the hat and tie if he hated them so much—but no. I wouldn’t let him treat me like a serf again.

And so, with a heavy heart, I accepted my fate. If I threw Motty out, he’d tell his mother. She’d tell Aunt Agatha. And Aunt Agatha—well, she could turn a minor indiscretion into a national tragedy. I couldn’t risk it. I’d have to endure.

Midday came, and Motty’s luggage arrived—along with a massive parcel labeled “Books.” I brightened. Maybe this was going to be bearable. A year’s reading material? Perhaps he’d be too busy to cause trouble.

I donned my Country Gentleman hat, adjusted the pink tie, and headed out for lunch with friends. The afternoon passed pleasantly. By dinner, I’d almost forgotten about Motty.

I returned late, expecting to find him asleep. But the book parcel was still unopened. And then—scrabbling at the front door. A thud.

“Better go see what that is, Jeeves.”

“Very good, sir.”

He returned moments later.

“If you would step this way, sir, I think we might carry him in.”

“Carry him in?”

“His lordship is lying on the mat.”

I rushed to the door. There was Motty, curled up on the floor, moaning.

“He’s had a fit,” I said. “Jeeves! Someone’s fed him meat! He’s a vegetarian!”

“Sir?”

“He must’ve eaten steak! Call a doctor!”

“I hardly think so, sir. If you would take his legs—”

“Great Scot! You don’t think—he can’t be—”

“I am inclined to think so, sir.”

And indeed, he was. Once on the right track, you couldn’t miss it. Motty was under the surface.

“You never can tell, Jeeves!”

“Very seldom, sir.”

“Remove the eye of authority and where are you?”

“Precisely, sir.”

“Well, we’d better bring him in.”

We carried him inside. Jeeves tucked him into bed. I lit a cigarette and sat down, dreading what was to come.

Next morning, I went to check on him. I expected a wreck. Instead, he was sitting up, reading Gingery stories, chirpy as a bird.

“What ho!” I said.

“What ho!” he replied.

“What ho! What ho!”

“What ho! What ho! What ho!”

I stopped. Conversation was impossible.

“How are you feeling?” I asked.

“Topping!” he declared. “That fellow of yours—Jeeves—is a corker. I had a frightful headache, but he gave me this dark drink—his own invention—and I was right as rain. I must see more of him. He seems distinctly one of the ones!”

I couldn’t believe it. This was the same boy who’d sucked his stick like a nervous squirrel?

“You ate something that disagreed with you last night?” I ventured.

“No!” he snapped. “I drank too much! Much too much! Lots and lots too much! And I’m going to do it again—every night! If you ever see me sober, tap me on the shoulder and say, ‘Tut! Tut!’ and I’ll apologize and fix it.”

“But I’m responsible for you!”

“Can’t help your troubles,” he said firmly. “This is my first chance to yield to the temptations of a great city. What’s the point of temptations if no one yields? Makes it discouraging for the city. Besides, mother told me to collect impressions.”

I sat on the edge of the bed, stunned.

“I know how you feel,” he said kindly. “But duty first! This is my first real freedom. We’re only young once. Young man, rejoice in thy youth! Tra-la! What ho!”

Well. He had a point.

Over the next few days, Motty became a whirlwind. He started bringing pals back at three in the morning. One night, I came home to find a bull-terrier named Rollo—won in a raffle—grabbing my trouser leg.

“Jeeves!” I yelled.

“That would be Rollo, sir.”

“Eh?”

“His lordship’s dog. His temper is uncertain. I didn’t hear you come in.”

I watched as Jeeves walked into the room—calm, collected, fearless. The dog rolled over, paws in the air, as if hypnotized. Then, seeing me again, it lunged.

And that was the moment I realized: I was living in a house ruled by chaos, with a dog who hated me and a guest who’d turned into a human tornado—all because of a single, ill-advised request from a woman whose idea of kindness was dumping her son on a stranger.

And Jeeves? Still silent. Still judgmental. Still refusing to acknowledge that the pink tie had been a triumph.

I sighed. Life, I decided, was not fair. But at least I had a hat I could wear.
