At the base of the staircase, Mr. Gryce excused himself, summoning two or three men he had stationed outside to remove the valet before ushering Miss Butterworth back into the study. Once the room fell silent once more, granting them the privacy necessary for serious discourse, Mr. Gryce broke the quiet with a declaration of triumph. "One very important thing has been settled by the experiment we have just conducted," he stated firmly. "Bartow is acquitted of any participation in this crime."

Miss Butterworth's shoulders slumped slightly, her hope dimming instantly. "Then we can finally turn our full attention to the young people," she replied, her voice tight with anxiety. "You have heard nothing from them, I suppose?"

"No," Gryce answered bluntly. "Nor have we received word from the old man who laughed."

"No," she confirmed, her disappointment palpable. "I thought... it seemed so probable... that the fragment of writing you discovered would identify these individuals. If it was of sufficient importance for a dying man to risk swallowing it, surely it must provide a clue to his attacker."

Gryce shook his head, a shadow crossing his features. "Unfortunately, it yields nothing. It was a veritable scrawl, madam, resembling this: *'I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!'* Signed simply, *'Amos's son.'*"

"Amos's son! That is Mr. Adams himself," Miss Butterworth exclaimed, the realization striking her with force.

"So we have every reason to believe," Gryce conceded.

"Strange! Unaccountable!" she cried out. "And yet, that paper inscribed with these frantic words was found clinched tightly between his teeth! Was the handwriting recognized?"

"Yes," Gryce replied, leaning forward. "As his own, provided we judge correctly from the specimens we have observed on the fly-leaves of his books."

"Well, mysteries deepen," Miss Butterworth whispered, staring into the gloom of the room. "And the retention of this paper was so vital to him that even in his death throes, he thrust it into this strangest of all hiding places, deeming it the only sanctuary safe from search. And the girl! Her first coherent words upon regaining consciousness were: *'You have left that line of writing behind.'* Mr. Gryce, those words, few and inexplicable as they are, contain the key to the entire situation. Will you repeat them again, if you please, sentence by sentence?"

"With pleasure, madam; I have recited them often enough to myself in solitude," Gryce said softly. "First, then: *'I return your daughter to you!'*"

"So!" Miss Butterworth leaned in, her eyes narrowing. "Mr. Adams had someone else's daughter in his charge whom he is returning. Whose daughter? Certainly not that young man's daughter, for that would necessitate her being a small child. Furthermore, if these words were intended for his assailant, why make such a remarkable effort to conceal them from him?"

"Very true," Gryce admitted. "I have pondered exactly that."

"Yet, if not for him, then for whom? For the elderly gentleman who entered shortly thereafter?"

"It is possible," Gryce mused. "Since learning of his arrival, I have allowed myself to consider this among the viable possibilities, particularly because the subsequent words of this enigmatic communication state: *'She is here.'* Now, the only woman present mere moments prior to that old gentleman's visit was the light-haired girl whom you witnessed being carried out."

"Very true," Miss Butterworth agreed, pacing slightly. "But why do you reason as if this paper had just been penned? It might have been an old scrap, referencing past sorrows or buried secrets."

"These words were written that very afternoon," Gryce corrected gently. "The paper on which they were scrawled was torn from a sheet of letterhead lying directly on the desk, and the pen used to inscribe them—you must have noticed where it rested, quite displaced from its natural position on the extreme edge of the table."

"Certainly, sir," she replied, though her tone suggested she had previously underestimated the significance of such trivial details. "These words are therefore connected to the girl I saw. And she is not Evelyn, or he would not have repeated in this note the bird's catchword, *'Remember Evelyn!'* I wonder if she is Evelyn?" she pressed, gesturing toward the single large portrait adorning the wall. "We may call her so for the nonce. Such a melancholy face may well suggest some painful family secret. But how explain the violent part played by the young man, who is not mentioned in these abrupt, hastily penned sentences! It is all a mystery, madam, a mystery in which we are wasting precious time attempting to unravel."

"I hate to yield without making an effort," Gryce countered. "Those words, now. There were others you have not repeated to me."

"They preceded that injunction, *'Remember Evelyn!'* They spoke of a resolve. *'Neither she nor you will ever see me again.'*"

"Ah!" Miss Butterworth gasped. "But these few words are profoundly significant, Mr. Gryce. Could he have dealt that fatal blow himself? Might he have been a suicide after all?"

"Madam, you possess the right to inquire; but judging from Bartow's pantomime, you must have perceived that it is not a self-inflicted wound he mimics, but a maddened thrust delivered by an outraged hand." Gryce paused, his expression grave. "Let us adhere to our initial conclusions; however—to be fair to every possibility—the chaotic state of Mr. Adams's affairs and the conspicuous absence of all family papers and documents typically found in a wealthy man's desk prove that he had made preparations for a possible death. It may have come sooner than anticipated and in a manner he did not foresee, but it was a thought he had indulged in. Madam, I have a confession to make as well. I have not been quite fair to my most valued colleague. The study—that most remarkable of rooms—contains a secret not yet imparted to you; a very peculiar one, madam, which was revealed to me in a rather startling manner. This room can be, or rather could be, completely severed from the rest of the house; transformed into a death trap, or rather a tomb, wherein this incomprehensible man may have intended to perish. Observe this plate of steel. It is operated by a mechanism that forces it across this open doorway. I was positioned behind that plate of steel last night, and these holes had to be drilled specifically to allow my escape."

"Hah!" Miss Butterworth exclaimed, rubbing her eyes as if waking from a trance. "You detectives have your own brand of experiences! I should not have enjoyed spending that special evening with you. But what an old-world tragedy we are unearthing here! I declare"—she shuddered—"I feel as if transported back to medieval days. Who says we are living in New York, within earshot of the cable car and the singing of the telegraph wires?"

"Some men are perfectly capable of importing the medieval into Wall Street," Gryce replied with a faint smile. "I believe Mr. Adams was one of those men. Romanticism tinged all his actions, even the death he met. Nor did it cease with his passing; it followed him to the tomb. Witness the cross we found lying upon his bosom."

"That was the act of another's hand, the result of another's superstition," Miss Butterworth insisted. "That indicates the presence of a priest or a woman at the precise moment of his demise."

"Yet," Gryce continued, his gaze thoughtful, "he must have possessed a grain of hard sense in his constitution. All his contrivances functioned flawlessly. He was a mechanical genius as well as a lover of mystery."

"An odd combination," she mused. "Strange that we do not feel his spirit infecting the very air of this study. I could almost wish it did. We might then be led to grasp the key to this mystery."

"That," Gryce remarked, "can be accomplished in only one way. You have already pointed it out. We must trace the young couple who were present during his death struggle. If they cannot be found, the case is hopeless."

"And so," she said, rising to her feet, "we circle back to the point from which we started—proof positive that we are lost in the woods." Miss Butterworth stood tall, feeling that for the time being, she had exhausted her resources. Mr. Gryce made no move to detain her; indeed, he appeared eager to depart the premises himself. Nevertheless, they lingered long enough to cast one final, lingering glance around the room. As they did so, Miss Butterworth's finger slowly rose, pointing upward.

"See!" she commanded softly. "You can hardly perceive from this side of the wall the opening created by the removal of that picture on the stair landing. Would you not say that it lies amidst those folds of dark-colored tapestry up there?"

"Yes," Gryce acknowledged, turning his head slightly. "I had already located that specific spot. With the picture hung up on the opposite side, it would be entirely invisible."

"One needs to keep one's eyes moving in a case like this," Miss Butterworth declared, her voice gaining strength. "That picture must have been drawn aside several times while we were in this room. Yet, we failed to notice it."
