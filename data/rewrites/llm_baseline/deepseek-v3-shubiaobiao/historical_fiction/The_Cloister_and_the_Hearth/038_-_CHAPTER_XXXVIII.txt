The road outside the town was trampled with fresh wolf tracks, a grim reminder of the night’s unseen dangers.

“We did well to heed the old man’s warning,” Gerard murmured.

Denys chuckled. “Aye, and I heard them last night—scurrying under our window, howling for a taste of some plump burgher. But no kind soul tossed them a morsel.”

Gerard smiled absently, lost in thought.

“What weighs so heavy on that mind of yours?” Denys prodded.

“Your kindness.”

Denys stiffened, his good humor vanishing. “A civil question deserves a civil answer.”

Gerard blinked. “I meant no offense. But if another claimed you lacked kindness, I’d call him liar. You see only women’s charms, not men’s virtues.” He shook his head. “Last night, you would have wed me to the ‘Golden Head’ and left yourself alone. That was kindness.”

Denys snorted, his temper easing. “Call it friendship, not kindness. Though, truth be told, my conscience pricks me now for letting you turn your back on fortune. A truer friend would’ve hamstrung you—left you nursing at the inn till that lass softened your stubborn heart.” He grinned. “I had the blade ready, but you whimper at a scratch, so I lost my nerve.”

Gerard gaped. Before he could reply, hoofbeats thundered behind them. A rider on a mule galloped wildly toward them, skidding to a halt.

“You’re from the ‘Golden Head’?” the man demanded.

They nodded.

“Which is the younger?”

Denys smirked. “He who was born later.”

The rider squinted. “Your beard’s ripe, his is green. Here, boy.” He thrust out a packet. “Our mistress says you left this.”

Gerard frowned. “I left nothing.”

“Call me liar, will you?” The man scowled. “And where’s my pourboire?”

Denys tossed him a coin, and the rider sped off as fast as he’d come. Gerard unwrapped the packet—layer after layer of silk—until at last, a gold ring with a gleaming amethyst tumbled into his palm. A note fluttered free:

Denys whistled. “The little dove! Though she’d have done better to aim her affections at me.”

Gerard flushed. “Spare me your jests. I’ll not wear it—Margaret’s ring is all I need.” He kissed the simple band on his finger.

Denys rolled his eyes but relented, helping Gerard secure the amethyst inside his bonnet for luck.

The road stretched before them, silent save for the crunch of snow underfoot. Then, cresting a hill, a troop of Burgundian soldiers appeared—fifty strong, banners fluttering.

Denys’s face lit. “The Bastard of Burgundy himself! A leader who fights like a common soldier—my kind of man.”

But their admiration turned to dread as the troop halted before them.

“Halt!” The Bastard’s voice was steel. “Why do you flee south when Burgundy calls north?”

Denys stood firm. “I go on leave to see my kin at Remiremont.”

“Not now. Mount that spare mule—you ride with us.”

Denys tightened his grip on Gerard’s hand. “I cannot. I’ve sworn to see this lad safe.”

The Bastard’s eyes turned icy. “Jarnac! Shoot this dog where he stands.”

Six archers dismounted, leveling their weapons.

Gerard’s heart stopped. “No! He’ll go—I swear it! Only let us say farewell!”

The Bastard gave a curt nod. “Make it swift.”

Gerard clutched Denys, their words tumbling out in desperate whispers.

“Tell Margaret everything,” Gerard begged.

“Strasbourg—then down the Rhine to Rotterdam,” Denys urged. “We’ll meet again.”

But time was a thief. Rough hands tore them apart, forcing Denys onto the mule.

Gerard ran after them, lungs burning, until the troop vanished around a bend. Then his legs gave way. He crumpled to the snow, hollow with loss.

A cavalcade of nobles passed, velvet-clad and laughing. Then—cruel irony—three of their servants circled back, leveling a petronel.

“Your purse, boy.”

Gerard laughed bitterly. “Take it. And tell your master may it freeze his heart and damn his soul.”

Penniless, he staggered on until exhaustion dragged him down at the edge of a wood. The cold seeped into his bones.

The innkeeper’s rhyme whispered in his mind. But home was Margaret, not gold.

Yet how to reach her now?

The snow beckoned, soft as a shroud. His eyelids grew heavy.

With a sigh, he closed his eyes.
