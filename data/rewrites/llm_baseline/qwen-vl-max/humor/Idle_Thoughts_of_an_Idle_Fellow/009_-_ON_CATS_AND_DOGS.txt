Let me tell you, this morning has been a disaster—pure, unadulterated chaos—and it’s all thanks to our household pets. The villain of the piece? Gustavus Adolphus. Yes, that’s his full name. Downstairs, they call him “Gusty” for short, like he’s some kind of heroic figure from a children's book. But I assure you, Gusty is no hero. He’s more like a furry tornado with poor impulse control and an alarming talent for destruction.

Now, don’t get me wrong—I love dogs in general. They’re loyal, affectionate, and always ready to cheer you up when life gets too serious. But Gustavus Adolphus is not one of those dogs. He’s built like a small tank and thinks every room should be a personal playground. When he stretches, two chairs collapse and the what-not goes flying. When he wags his tail, it looks like a hurricane just passed through. And when he breathes? Well, let’s just say the fire dies instantly, as if he’s exhaled a tiny cyclone.

At dinner, he sneaks under the table, lies there quietly for a moment, then suddenly decides to stand up. The first sign we have of this is the table lifting off the floor like it’s doing a backflip. We all grab it, screaming, trying to hold it down while he panics beneath, convinced we’re staging a coup against him. The result? A toppled table, a shattered dinner, and everyone sprawled out like extras in a slapstick film. It’s become a weekly ritual.

This morning was no different. He came in like a storm front—no warning, no grace, just sheer, unbridled energy. His tail swept my coffee cup right off the table, sending liquid directly into the center of my waistcoat. I jumped up, muttering something unprintable (but quite satisfying), and charged toward him. He beat me to the door. There, he met Eliza, who was bringing in the eggs. She saw him coming and said, “Ugh!”—a single syllable of pure horror—and promptly sat down on the floor. The eggs scattered like confetti. Some rolled under the sofa, others cracked open in the middle of the rug, and one even managed to land perfectly in the ashtray. Gusty didn’t stick around. He bolted, and I called after him: “Go downstairs! Don’t come back for at least an hour!” He seemed to agree—he dodged the coal-scoop like a ninja and vanished.

I dried myself off, finished breakfast, and assumed he’d gone outside. Ten minutes later, I checked the passage—and there he was, sitting proudly at the top of the stairs. I ordered him down. He barked, jumped, and refused to move. So I went to investigate. What I found was Tittums.

Tittums is our kitten. She’s about the size of a penny roll—tiny, fluffy, and utterly terrifying. She was perched on the second-to-top step, back arched, fur standing on end, and swearing like a sailor who’d lost his ship and his dignity. I know she’s young, but honestly, the volume and variety of her curses are impressive. I swear myself sometimes—don’t tell anyone, especially your wife—but I’m only an amateur compared to her. Swearing, I believe, is nature’s way of letting off steam. It’s like a safety valve. When you’ve had a bad day, and someone steps on your foot, you can’t just say, “Oh dear, how clumsy of you,” can you? No. You need to unleash a torrent of colorful language—“Bless you, my dear sweet sir, what in the name of all that’s holy made you so careless as to allow your light and delicate foot to descend upon my corn with such force?” That kind of thing clears the mind. It’s cheaper than smashing furniture or throwing things. And let’s face it, a little explosion now and then keeps the soul healthy.

I tried explaining this to my aunt once. She wasn’t impressed. She said I shouldn’t *have* such feelings. Which is why I told Tittums exactly the same thing: “You ought to be ashamed of yourself, brought up in a Christian home like this!” I don’t mind an old cat swearing—it’s part of the charm—but a kitten? It breaks my heart. So I put her in my pocket and returned to work.

I forgot about her. Then I looked up and found her out of the pocket, on the desk, trying to swallow my pen. Then she stuck her paw in the inkwell, knocked it over, licked it clean, and then turned to me and swore again—this time directly at me. I dropped her on the floor. Instantly, Tim started a fight.

Tim is a two-year-old fox-terrier with delusions of grandeur. He thinks he’s a wise old Scottish collie, but he’s really just a dog who likes to interfere. He doesn’t care what Tittums is doing; he feels compelled to correct her. Naturally, Tittums’ mother arrived just in time to scratch Tim’s nose. I was delighted. I threw them all three into the passage, where they’re still wrestling like miniature gladiators.

Meanwhile, I’m covered in ink and in a mood fit to strangle a cactus. If another cat or dog comes near me today, I may just have to hire a funeral director on the spot. But despite all this, I still adore cats and dogs. They’re far better company than most humans.

They never argue. They never talk about themselves—they just sit there, listening intently, pretending to care about your latest existential crisis. They never make awkward comments like, “I always thought Miss Brown liked Mr. Jones,” or mistake your wife’s cousin for her husband. They never ask a struggling writer why he hasn’t written a play yet. They never say cruel things. They never point out your flaws “for your own good.” They never remind you of past mistakes with a smirk. And they never whisper, “Oh yes, a lot of use you are when you’re actually needed.”

But when you’re sad, they don’t lecture. They don’t offer advice. They just come close. A cat will climb onto your shoulder, ruffle your hair, and silently say, “Lor’, I’m sorry for you, old man.” A dog will look up with those big, honest eyes and say, “You’ve got me. Always. Through thick and thin. We’ll face it together.”

Dogs are foolish, yes. They don’t care if you’re rich or poor, wise or silly, saint or sinner. You’re their pal. That’s enough. They’ll follow you into danger, guard you, protect you—even die for you. And yet, we call them animals, as if that’s a flaw. We act like we’re superior—clever, rational, noble beings. But what do we really know? We don’t know where we came from. We don’t know where we’re going. We don’t know what’s beyond this tiny planet. Yet we still think we’re better than a dog who loves us without condition.

Cats, too, get a bad rap. People say they’re selfish, cold, calculating. But that’s not true. Give a cat your trust, and she’ll stay by you through everything. One of mine used to follow me everywhere—so much so that I had to beg her, as a personal favor, not to walk behind me down the High Street. Another one would wait up for me when I was late, meet me at the door, and greet me like a long-lost spouse—except she never asked where I’d been, which was refreshing.

And then there’s the drunkard cat—the one who’d hang around the cellar door all day, waiting to sneak in and lap up beer drippings. She was vain, too. Every time she caught a big rat, she’d bring it into the living room, lay it down proudly, and wait for applause. The girls would scream. Poor rats. They exist only so cats can gain glory and chemists can sell poison.

Still, I find rats fascinating. There’s something eerie about them. They’re cunning, secretive, relentless. They swarm in abandoned houses, whispering secrets in dark corners, gnawing through walls, creeping through cracks. They know when a ship is sinking and leave before anyone else does. They’re the stars of horror stories—gnawing behind the wainscot, glowing red-eyed in the darkness, screaming in the night. I love reading tales about them. Like the story of Bishop Hatto, who hoarded grain and burned starving people alive. Then thousands of rats came, swam across the river, chewed through stone, and ate him alive. “They have whetted their teeth against the stones, / And now they pick the bishop’s bones.” Lovely tale.

Or the Pied Piper of Hamelin. First, he lures the rats away with his magic tune. Then, when the mayor refuses to pay, he pipes the children away too. I wonder what that story means. Is it just a fairy tale? Or is there something deeper? I often imagine the Pied Piper still walking among us, playing softly so only children can hear. That’s why they look so solemn sometimes—like they’ve heard something we can’t. Maybe they’ve seen him. Maybe he’s real.

And maybe, just maybe, we grown-ups hear him too—just faintly, from far away. One day, the music will grow loud, and we’ll drop everything and follow, just like the children did. Our families will try to stop us, but we’ll push gently past, drawn by a wild, strange song. Because deep down, we know what it means.

Now, about pet worship—please, let’s not go there. Some people go too far. Women especially. There are those who read *David Copperfield* and immediately want a long-haired dog that critiques men’s trousers with a sniff of disdain. They talk to it like it’s a person, kiss its nose, press its dirty head to their cheek—mostly when there are young men nearby. Then there are the elderly ladies who dote on fat, flea-ridden poodles. I knew two spinsters once who treated their dog like a human. They washed its face every morning, gave it mutton cutlets for breakfast, and one of them stayed home on Sundays just to keep the dog company while the other went to church.

In some homes, the dog is the center of everything. You want to flirt with the eldest daughter? Start with the dog. Want to borrow the garden roller? Win Fido’s approval first. If he snarls at you, you’re done. “If Fido won’t take to any one,” the father says wisely, “I say that man is not to be trusted.” Bless him. Drat him.

It’s tragic, really. That innocent puppy—full of fun, ambition, and dreams of becoming a great dog—is turned into a grumpy, pampered creature by human excess. The world is like a grinding machine. Fresh, bright things go in one end and come out wrinkled, crabbed, and dull. Look at Pussy Sobersides—sleepy eyes, slow walk, prudish airs. Who’d guess she was once a whirlwind of blue eyes and mad energy?

Kittens are amazing. Full of life, bursting with vitality. They leap, they mew, they roll, they kick. They don’t know what to do with all that energy. Can you remember, reader, when you felt that way? When you were young and the world was yours? Walking home under the moonlight, feeling too full of life to walk normally—you had to jump, wave your arms, shout until farmers’ wives hid behind hedges. You laughed at their fear, and you didn’t know why you were crying. Oh, that glorious, reckless youth!

That’s what I miss. That divine spark. Now, my pulse is slow, my joints ache, and I prefer my armchair and pipe. But oh, to feel that wild, electric life again—just for one moment.
