The silence between us stretched for what felt like an eternity, though I suspect it lasted only two hours. The memory of the horrors we had witnessed was too fresh, too visceral, to permit speech. As twilight bled into the pre-dawn gloom, just as we considered retreating to our beds, the rhythmic crunch of boots broke the stillness. A sentinel's challenge echoed from the kraal gate, met not by voice but by movement; the steps continued, deliberate and heavy. Infadoos emerged, flanked by a procession of six stately chiefs, their presence commanding the dim light of the hut.

"My lords," Infadoos announced, his voice cutting through the haze of exhaustion, "I have fulfilled my word. I bring you these men, great pillars of our nation, each commanding three thousand souls who live solely to execute the King's will. I have recounted to them the sights I have seen and the truths I have heard. Now, let them witness the sacred serpent coiled upon your skin, Ignosi, and listen to your story, that they may decide whether to raise their spears against Twala."

In response, Ignosi once again stripped away his girdle, exposing the intricate tattoo of the snake that wound around his torso. One by one, the chiefs approached, their faces illuminated by the flickering lamp, scrutinizing the mark with solemn intensity before passing silently to the next. When Ignosi resumed his *moocha*, Infadoos pressed the issue.

"Now that you have heard," Infadoos declared, "what is your verdict? Will you stand by this man and aid him in reclaiming his father's throne, or will you remain silent? The land screams against Twala; the blood of our people flows like a swollen river in spring. You have seen tonight. Two other chiefs I intended to consult lie dead beneath the jaws of hyenas. Soon, if you do not act, you shall join them. Choose, my brothers."

The eldest of the group, a stocky warrior with a crown of white hair, stepped forward. "Your words ring true, Infadoos; the land does cry out. My own brother perished tonight. Yet this is a weighty burden, difficult to bear. How can we be certain that lifting our spears is not inviting a thief and a liar to power? This is a matter of uncertain ends. Be assured, rivers of blood will flow before the deed is done. Many will cling to the current king, for men worship the sun that burns brightly in the sky rather than the star that has not yet risen. These white men from the Stars possess great magic, and Ignosi hides beneath their wings. If he is truly the rightful king, let them provide a sign, let the people see a sign, so that all may know the white man's magic stands with us."

"You have the sign of the snake," I interjected.

"My lord," the elder chieftain replied, "that is insufficient. The snake may have been placed there during his childhood. Show us a miracle, and only then will it suffice. We will not move without a sign."

The others nodded in agreement. Turning to Sir Henry and Good, I confessed my perplexity.

"I believe I have found the solution," Good exclaimed, his spirit suddenly buoyant. "Ask them to grant us a moment to consider."

Once the chiefs had withdrawn, Good rushed to his small medicine chest, unlocking it to retrieve a notebook containing an almanack tucked within its fly-leaves. "Look here, gentlemen," he said, tapping the page. "Isn't tomorrow the fourth of June?"

Our records confirmed the date.

"Excellent," Good continued. "'June 4th, total eclipse of the moon commencing at 8:15 Greenwich Mean Time, visible in Tenerife—South Africa, etc.' There is your sign. Tell them we shall darken the moon tonight."

It was a magnificent stratagem, though shadowed by a single peril: the accuracy of Good's calculations. A failed prophecy would shatter our credibility forever and seal Ignosi's fate.

"Suppose the almanack is erroneous?" Sir Henry ventured, watching Good furiously scribble on a blank page.

"I see no reason to doubt it," Good asserted confidently. "Eclipses adhere strictly to their schedules; that is my experience, and the text explicitly notes visibility in South Africa. Having calculated the reckoning to the best of my ability without precise coordinates, I estimate the eclipse will commence here around ten o'clock tomorrow night, lasting until half-past twelve. For ninety minutes, we shall endure near-total darkness."

"Well," Sir Henry conceded, "we had better risk it."

I acquiesced, albeit with lingering doubt; celestial phenomena are fickle beasts—one might encounter cloud cover, or our chronology might be amiss. Summoning Umbopa to recall the chiefs, I waited until they returned.

"Great men of the Kukuanas, and you, Infadoos, listen," I began. "We rarely display our powers, for to do so is to interfere with the natural order, plunging the world into terror. Yet, given the magnitude of this crisis, and our righteous anger against the king for the slaughter we witnessed and the treachery of *Isanusi* Gagool, who sought to slay our friend Ignosi, we have resolved to break our rule. We shall give a sign visible to all mankind."

I guided them to the doorway and pointed to the crimson orb hanging in the sky. "What do you see there?"

"The sinking moon," the spokesman replied.

"Precisely. Can any mortal extinguish the moon before her appointed hour, drawing the curtain of black night upon the land?"

The chief chuckled softly. "No, my lord. No man possesses such power. The moon is stronger than the observer, and her courses are immutable."

"You say so. Yet I declare that tomorrow night, approximately two hours before midnight, we shall cause the moon to be devoured for a span of an hour and a half. Deep darkness shall envelop the earth, serving as proof that Ignosi is indeed the King of the Kukuanas. If we accomplish this, will you be content?"

"Yes, my lords," the old chief smiled, the expression mirrored by his companions. "If you perform this feat, we shall be utterly satisfied."

"It shall be done," I proclaimed. "We three—Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn—have spoken, and it shall be done. Do you hear, Infadoos?"

"I hear, my lord," Infadoos responded, awe coloring his tone. "It is a wonder that you promise to extinguish the moon, the mother of the world, at her zenith."

"Nevertheless, we shall do it, Infadoos."

"It is well, my lords. Today, two hours after sunset, Twala will summon you to witness the girls' dance. One hour after the music begins, the girl deemed fairest by Twala shall be slain by Scragga, the king's son, as a sacrifice to the Silent Ones who guard the mountains yonder," he indicated the three jagged peaks marking the end of Solomon's Road. "Then, let my lords darken the moon, saving the maiden's life, and the people will believe truly."

"Aye," the old chief agreed, still smiling. "The people will believe indeed."

"Two miles from Loo," Infadoos continued, "stands a hill curved like a crescent moon, a stronghold where my regiment and three others commanded by these chiefs are stationed. This morning, we shall devise a plan to relocate two or three additional regiments there. Should my lords truly succeed in darkening the moon, I will take your hands in the darkness and lead you from Loo to this sanctuary, where you shall be secure. From there, we shall wage war upon Twala."

"That is excellent," I affirmed. "Leave us now to rest and prepare our magic."

Infadoos rose, saluted, and departed with his entourage.

"My friends," Ignosi whispered immediately after they left, "can you truly achieve this marvel, or were you merely speaking empty words to the captains?"

"We believe we can, Umbopa—I mean, Ignosi."

"It is strange," he admitted. "Had you not been Englishmen, I would not have believed it. But I have learned that English 'gentlemen' do not lie. If we survive this ordeal, be assured I shall repay you."

"Ignosi," Sir Henry interrupted gently, "promise me one thing."

"I will promise, Incubu, my friend, even before hearing the request," the giant man smiled. "What is it?"

"This: that if you ever ascend to the kingship of this people, you must abolish the hunting of wizards as we saw last night, and ensure that no man dies without trial and judgment."

Ignosi pondered the request, translating the sentiment internally before responding. "The ways of black people differ from those of white men, Incubu; we do not value life with the same reverence. Yet I promise. If it is within my power to restrain them, the witch-finders shall hunt no more, nor shall any man face death without judgment."

"That is a bargain," Sir Henry declared. "Now, let us seek some rest."

Exhausted beyond measure, we fell into a deep sleep, waking only when Ignosi roused us at eleven. We arose, washed, and consumed a hearty breakfast. Stepping outside, we wandered the perimeter, examining the architecture of the Kukuana huts and observing the customs of the women.
