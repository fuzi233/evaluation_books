In the serene landscape of Virginia, where the gentle rhythms of nature harmonized with the quiet traditions of the Old World, Henry Esmond, a man of refined sensibilities and distinguished service, had established a new home. His tenure as a Colonel during the tumultuous reign of Queen Anne had left its mark, but the closure of that era brought with it an opportunity for reflection and a chance to reconnect with the land that had nurtured his ancestors. Esmond's decision to settle in Virginia was a deliberate choice, driven by a desire to escape the complexities of European politics and to create a simpler, more peaceful existence.

As a scion of the esteemed Castlewood family, Esmond could have claimed a title of nobility, but he had chosen instead to eschew such trappings and forge a new path. The Irish peerage, with its hereditary ties, was not something he felt inclined to pursue, and so he focused on building a life free from the entanglements of court politics. The land granted to his ancestor by King Charles I beckoned, a reminder of the enduring legacy of his family's history.

In this tranquil setting, Esmond's children were born, and his wife, a woman of wit and elegance, presided over their household with a warm and generous spirit. Her own past was marked by loss, having been widowed young, but she had found solace in her marriage to Esmond and in the creation of a new life together. The house they built, Castlewood, was a testament to their love and their commitment to the land.

Virginia, a colony of Englishmen, clung to its heritage, proudly upholding the customs and traditions of the motherland. The residents, a mix of gentlemen and traders, maintained a strong sense of loyalty to the crown, and their society was characterized by a patriarchal order, where the landed gentry held sway. The economy thrived on the cultivation of tobacco, with the great rivers providing a bounty of fish and game, and the ships that sailed from the private wharves carried the rich harvest to England, returning with goods and manufactures to sustain the colony.

Hospitality was a virtue in Virginia, and strangers were always welcomed with open arms. The gentry entertained each other in grand style, traveling to each other's estates in a manner reminiscent of a bygone era.
