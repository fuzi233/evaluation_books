Some folk grow old in the heart before their hair is grey; others keep a sap-green courage that no winter can bite. Margaret Van Eyck was of the latter sort. She loved her young namesake with a bright, almost girlish heat—partly because she had a quick eye for what is real in people, and had found in Margaret Brandt a thing rarer than beauty: plain truth. But there was another bond, older than judgment and stronger than gratitude.

It is a crooked piece of human nature that we often cling most warmly to those we have helped. Kindness, once spent, comes back like an echo in the giver’s own breast, and makes a home there.

So it was with the old painter. She had broken her settled habits to go out to Sevenbergen; she had sat by the sickbed; she had warmed broths and sharpened the smell of herbs in the room—sage bruised between finger and thumb, a sting of vinegar in the air, and the slow, comforting whisper of a fire that never quite died. She had coaxed life back with patience that was half art, half mothering; and the very act of saving Margaret had drawn her to her more tenderly than ever she had been drawn even to Gerard—though it was for Gerard’s sake she had crossed that threshold at first.

Now she saw the colour come back to the girl’s cheek, as if some invisible hand had rubbed in a rose-tint; and she saw the cause of it too—the little strip of parchment Margaret held as reverently as if it were a relic.

Margaret Van Eyck read it, and her own long-laid plan yielded without a sigh.

“Sweetheart,” said she, “I did desire he should stay in Italy five or six years, and come back rich, and above all an artist. But your happiness is before all; and I see you cannot live without him. So we must have him home as fast as may be.”

“Ah, madam! you see my very thoughts.” The girl’s voice ran shy; she lowered her eyes and blushed, like one caught tasting honey. “But how to let him know? That passes my skill. He is gone to Italy; but what part I know not. Stay—he named the cities he should visit: Florence was one, and Rome.”

Then sense, which was strong in her, fought down the first wild impulse. A letter flung into the world with no better direction than “My Gerard—Italy” might wander like a lost pilgrim and never reach the shrine. She looked up, beseeching counsel.

“You are come to the right place, and at the right time,” said the old lady. “Here was this Hans Memling with me to-day; he is going to Italy, girl, no later than next week, ‘to improve his hand,’ he says. Not before ’twas needed, I do assure you.”

“But how is he to find my Gerard?”

“Why, he knows your Gerard, child. They have supped here more than once, and were like hand and glove. Now, as his business is the same as Gerard’s, he will visit the same places; and soon or late he must fall in with him. Wherefore, get you a long letter written, and copy out this pardon into it, and I’ll answer for the messenger. In six months at farthest Gerard shall get it; and when he shall get it, then will he kiss it, and put it in his bosom, and come flying home. What are you smiling at? And now what makes your cheeks so red? And what you are smothering me for, I cannot think. Yes—happy days are coming to my little pearl.”

While that hopeful scheme took shape in the upper room, Martin Wittenhaagen sat in the kitchen with a black-jack of ale before him, and Reicht Heynes at her wheel. The distaff hummed; the treadle made its steady, soothing thump, as if the house itself had a pulse. But the old woman’s questions were sharper than any kitchen knife, and she pumped Martin till his answers rose like bubbles and burst in spite of him.

Hans Memling, meanwhile, was a former pupil of Jan Van Eyck and his sister. Painter he was, for all Margaret Van Eyck’s dry gibes; and good-hearted enough—save for one fault that sat on him like a leech. He loved the nipperkin and the brown bowl more than they loved him. It kept his purse light and his feet often at Margaret Van Eyck’s threshold, hungry for a meal and sometimes for a groat. That dependence gave her a hold on him; she knew he would not trifle with any errand she placed in his hand.

So the letter was written—long, urgent, warm with the girl’s very breath—and the duke’s pardon copied within it as carefully as a cook lays pastry over a precious filling, sealing it from all harm. It was left with Margaret Van Eyck; and the following week, sure enough, Hans Memling came back from Flanders. The old artist put the letter into his wallet and slipped him a piece of gold for the road.

He seemed in a mighty hurry to be gone.

“All the better,” said Margaret Van Eyck. “He will be the sooner in Italy.”

But there are horses that fret to start and then, after the first furlong, need whip and spur. Hans’s eagerness cooled into a lazy heat as soon as he reached the chief hostelry of Tergou and spied two boon companions planted in the bay-window like crows on a branch. He went in for a parting glass; and once he lifted the cup, the ale and wine did the rest. When he offered to pay, they cried shame on it—he was bound for a long journey; everybody must treat him, landlord and all.

The more they treated him, the more his tongue ran as if oiled. He boasted he would teach Italians how to paint; then sang of his battle feats—he had handled a pike, he swore; and then, warming further, told of amorous victories with women not present to contradict the tale. In short, he leaked words in every direction; and among the things that oozed out, he blabbed he carried a letter for a townsman, one Gerard—a good fellow; and to prove the point that all present were good fellows too, he slapped Sybrandt on the back with such hearty fellowship it drove the breath from him.

Sybrandt slid round the table to escape further brotherly blows, but he listened. For the first time he learnt Gerard was gone to Italy. To be sure, he affected to doubt it.

“My brother Gerard is never in Italy.”

“Ye lie, ye cur!” roared Hans, the drink turning him quick to wrath. He was too muddled to see the man he now cursed was the one he had praised a moment before. “If he is ten times your brother, he is in Italy. What call ye this? There—read me that superscription!” And he flung the letter down upon the board, where ale had soaked the grain dark.

Sybrandt took it up and peered as gravely as a clerk; then set it down again with a shrug. “I cannot read.”

One of the company, by some happy chance, could; and proud as a minstrel with a new song, he read aloud:

“To Gerard Eliassoen, of Tergou. These by the hand of the trusty Hans Memling, with all speed.”

“’Tis excellently well writ,” said the reader, eyeing each stroke.

“Ay!” cried Hans, swelling. “And small wonder: ’tis writ by a famous hand—by Margaret, sister of Jan Van Eyck. Blessed and honoured be his memory! She is an old friend of mine, is Margaret Van Eyck.”

After that, Hans wandered into forty matters as a drunkard wanders through streets he does not know, laughing at corners and singing to doorposts.

Sybrandt stole away and sought Cornelis. The brothers put their heads together; their talk had the hiss of damp wood on a fire.

Italy was far—an immense distance. If only Gerard could be kept there.

“Keep him there?” said Cornelis. “Nothing would keep him long from his Margaret.”

“Curse her!” Sybrandt’s voice came like spit. “Why didn’t she die when she was about it?”

“She die?” Cornelis snarled. “She would outlive the pest to vex us.”

So the black sheep of the family chewed their envy and fed it, till it grew bold enough to shape a plan: keep Gerard in Italy all his life, and so secure his share of their father’s substance.

They had the wickedness for it, but not the wit. The scheme stuck—until, as if a malicious whisper slid between their two skulls at once, they both said together:

“THE BURGOMASTER!”

They went straight to Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, and he received them at once. The man who sits in suspense catches at knowledge as a starving man catches at crust.

“You have news of Gerard?” he cried.

They told him of the letter, and of Hans Memling with the wallet. Ghysbrecht’s eye flickered with a restless light.

“Who writ the letter?”

“Margaret Van Eyck,” they answered—supposing, naturally, the inside matched the outside hand.

“Are ye sure?” He opened a drawer and drew out a paper Margaret Van Eyck had written in the business of her house. “Was it writ like this?”

“Yes. ’Tis the same writing,” said Sybrandt, boldly lying.

“Good. And now what would ye of me?” Ghysbrecht asked, beating heart carefully masked under a show of indifference so neat it nearly fooled them.

They fumbled with their bonnets and their words, edging round the matter as cats edge round a hot pot. At last it came out: they wanted him to write a letter—something that would keep Gerard in Italy—and they meant to swap it into Hans Memling’s wallet for the true letter.

While they stammered, the old man’s spirit ran a swift race between vengeance and prudence. Vengeance cried: let Gerard return and be crushed by law. Prudence murmured: better a thousand miles between you and him. Prudence also warned: why dirty your hands for these two? why give them power to smear your name? Yet fear tugged too—a fear Gerard held some secret that could wound him to the quick. At last he spoke.

“It is my duty to aid the citizens that cannot write. But for their matter I will not be responsible. Tell me, then, what I shall write.”

“Something about this Margaret.”

“Ay, ay—that she is false, that she is married to another, I’ll go bail.”

“Nay, burgomaster, nay! not for all the world!” cried Sybrandt. “Gerard would not believe it—or but half—and then he would come back to see. No; say that she is dead.”

“Dead! what, at her age, will he credit that?”

“Sooner than the other. Why she was nearly dead: so it is not to say a downright lie, after all.”

“Humph! And you think that will keep him in Italy?”

“We are sure of it, are we not, Cornelis?”

“Ay,” said Cornelis. “Our Gerard will never leave Italy now he is there. It was always his dream. He would come back for his Margaret, but not for us. What cares he for us? He despises his own family; always did.”

“This would be a bitter pill to him,” said Ghysbrecht, tasting the bitterness with a secret relish.

“It will be for his good in the end,” replied Cornelis coolly.

“What avails Famine wedding Thirst?” he added, with a hard little laugh.

“And the grief you are preparing for him so coolly?” Ghysbrecht’s voice held sarcasm, though his heart enjoyed the cruelty.

“Oh, a lie is not like a blow with a curtal axe. It hacks no flesh, and breaks no bones.”

“A curtal axe?” said Sybrandt; “no, nor even like a stroke with a cudgel.” And he shot a sly glance at Ghysbrecht’s broken nose.

The hit landed. The burgomaster’s face darkened; the old hurt burned as if the blow were new. Rage rose, and with it hatred.

“Well,” said he, “tell me what to write for you, and I must write it; but take notice, you bear the blame if aught turns amiss. Not the hand which writes, but the tongue which dictates, doth the deed.”

They agreed eagerly, sneering inwardly. Ghysbrecht drew his inkhorn close, laid the sample of Margaret Van Eyck’s hand before him, and began to ask of the size and fold of the letter—when the door flew open.

Jorian Ketel burst in, quick and flurried, and looked vexed to find company.

“Thou seest I have matter on hand, good fellow,” said Ghysbrecht.

“Ay; but this is grave. I bring good news; but ’tis not for every ear.”

Ghysbrecht rose and drew him aside into the deep embrasure of the window. The brothers heard low, eager murmuring—words clipped and urgent, like knives on a board. It ended with Ghysbrecht sending Jorian out to saddle his mule. Then he turned on the two brothers with sudden coldness that chilled them.

“I prize the peace of households; but this is not a thing to be done in a hurry: we will see about it, we will see.”

“But, burgomaster, the man will be gone. It will be too late.”

“Where is he?”

“At the hostelry, drinking.”

“Well, keep him drinking! We will see, we will see.” He dismissed them; they went off, baffled.

To understand this change, one must step back a little.

That very morning Margaret Brandt had met Jorian Ketel near her door. He passed her with a scowl. It pricked her memory: this was the man who had saved Gerard.

“Stay,” said she. “Yes! it is the good man who saved him. Oh! why have you not been near me since? And why have you not come for the parchments? Was it not true about the hundred crowns?”

Jorian snorted; but her open face made him suspect some misunderstanding. He told her he had come—and how he had been received.

“Alas!” she said. “I knew nought of this. I lay at Death’s door.” Then she led him out into the garden. The air there smelled of damp earth and bruised leaves; a spade stood stuck in the ground, and somewhere a hen muttered to herself. Margaret showed him the place, a patch where the soil lay oddly loose, as if recently turned. “Martin was for taking them up, but I would not let him. He put them there; and I said none should move them but you, who had earned them so well of him and me.”

“Give me a spade!” cried Jorian, eyes bright. Then he checked himself. “But stay! He is a suspicious man. You are sure they are there still?”

“I will openly take the blame if human hand hath touched them.”

“Then keep them but two hours more, I prithee, good Margaret,” said Jorian, and ran off to the Stadthouse of Tergou as if he smelt gold on the wind.

Now Ghysbrecht jogged towards Sevenbergen on his mule, with Jorian striding beside, full of assurances that in an hour the missing parchments would be safe in the burgomaster’s hands.

“Ah, master,” said Jorian, “lucky for us it wasn’t a thief that took them.”

“Not a thief? not a thief? what call you him, then?”

“Well—saving your presence—I call him a jackdaw. This is jackdaw’s work, if ever there was: take the thing you are least in need of, and hide it—that’s a jackdaw. I should know,” he added solemnly, “for I was brought up along with a chough. He and I were born the same year, but he cut his teeth long before me, and wow! but my life was a burden for years all along of him. If you had but a hole in your hose no bigger than a groat, in went his beak like a gimlet; and, for stealing, Gerard all over. What he wanted least, and any poor Christian in the house wanted most, that went first. Mother—she was notable—so if she did but look round, away flew her thimble. Father lived by cordwaining, so about sunrise Jack went diligently off with his awl, his wax, and his twine. After that, make your bread how you could! One day I heard my mother tell him to his face he was enough to corrupt half-a-dozen other children; and he only cocked his eye at her, and next minute away with the nurseling’s shoe off his very foot. Now this Gerard is tarred with the same stick. The parchments are no more use to him than a thimble or an awl to Jack. He took ’em out of pure mischief and hid them, and you would never have found them but for me.”

“I believe you are right,” said Ghysbrecht. “And I have vexed myself more than need.”

But when they reached Peter’s gate, uneasiness crept over him like a draught under a door.

“I wish it had been anywhere but here.”

Jorian reassured him. “The girl is honest and friendly. She had nothing to do with taking them, I’ll be sworn.” He led him into the garden. “There, master—if a face is to be believed, here they lie; and see, the mould is loose.”

He ran for the spade and began to dig. The blade bit with a soft, wet sound; the earth gave up its smell, rich and cellar-cool. Soon the corner of a parchment showed. Ghysbrecht thrust Jorian aside, dropped to his knees, and tore it out as if it were a living thing. His hands trembled; his face shone. He flung parchment after parchment up into the light, while Jorian dusted and shook them, clearing the clinging soil.

But as the heap grew, Ghysbrecht’s expression changed. The shine dulled; his brow drew down. When he reached the last, he clapped both hands to his temples as if the world had struck him.

“What mystery lies here?” he gasped. “Are fiends mocking me? Dig deeper! There must be another.”

Jorian drove the spade in again and turned up hard mould in chunks. In vain.

And even as he dug, Ghysbrecht’s mood snapped from bewilderment to rage.

“Treason! treachery!” he cried. “You knew of this.”

“Knew what, master, in Heaven’s name?”

“Caitiff, you knew there was another one worth all these twice told.”

“’Tis false,” cried Jorian, suspicion flaring because suspicion was shown to him. “’Tis a trick to rob me of my hundred crowns. Oh! I know you, burgomaster.” And he was on the edge of whining.

Then a mellow voice slid into the quarrel, smoothing it like warm oil on choppy water.

“No, good man, it is not false, nor yet is it quite true: there was another parchment.”

“There, there, there! Where is it?” Ghysbrecht panted.

“But,” said Margaret calmly, “it was not a town record—so you have gained your hundred crowns, good man—it was but a private deed between the burgomaster here and my grandfather Flor—”

“Hush, hush!” Ghysbrecht’s command cracked.

“—is Brandt.”

“Where is it, girl? that is all we want to know.”

“Have patience, and I shall tell you. Gerard read the title of it, and he said, ‘This is as much yours as the burgomaster’s,’ and he put it apart, to read it with me at his leisure.”

“It is in the house, then?” Ghysbrecht asked, gathering his face back into control.

“No, sir,” said Margaret gravely, “it is not.” Then her voice faltered, as if some old pain pressed her throat. “You hunted—my poor Gerard—so hard—so close—that you gave him no time to think of aught but his life and his grief. The parchment was in his bosom, and he hath ta’en it with him.”

“Whither, whither?”

“Ask me no more, sir. What right is yours to question me thus? It was for your sake, good man, I put force upon my heart and came out here, and bore to speak at all to this hard old man. For when I think of the misery he has brought on him and me, the sight of him is more than I can bear.” She shuddered, as if a cold sound had passed through her—like a door slammed in a quiet house—and went slowly in, hand to head, crying bitterly.

Remorse, dread of what must come, avarice, fear—these four tugged at Ghysbrecht in a single moment. His head sank; his arms fell heavy.

A coarse chuckle snapped him round. There stood Martin Wittenhaagen, leaning on his bow, grinning as if he chewed mockery for pleasure. At sight of that grin, Ghysbrecht’s worst passions sprang up anew.

“Ho! attach him, seize him, traitor and thief!” he cried. “Dog, thou shalt pay for all.”

Martin answered without a word: he only drew the duke’s pardon from his doublet and thrust it beneath the burgomaster’s nose. Ghysbrecht read—and the law in his own hands turned suddenly to ash.

Martin pressed the advantage, his voice blunt as a cleaver.

“The duke and I are soldiers. He won’t let you greasy burghers trample on an old comrade. He bade me carry you a message too.”

“The duke send a message to me?” Ghysbrecht could scarcely swallow the words.

“Ay! I told him of your masterful doings—of your imprisoning Gerard for loving a girl; and says he, ‘Tell him this is to be a king, not a burgomaster. I’ll have no kings in Holland but one. Bid him be more humble, or I’ll hang him at his own door.’”

Ghysbrecht trembled; he believed the duke capable.

“‘As I hanged the burgomaster of Thingembob.’ The duke could not mind which of you he had hung, or in what part; such trifles stick not in a soldier’s memory. But he was sure he had hanged one of you for grinding poor folk—‘and I’m the man to hang another,’ quoth the good duke.”

To be so handled by a man he counted mean, and yet to find the man untouchable under the duke’s shadow, drove the old burgomaster into an impotent fury. He shook his fist, tried to threaten, but rage choked him. Then he gave a sort of screech, and drew himself up, coiling in face and body like a striking snake, and spat full on Martin’s doublet.

Martin wiped nothing; he only looked at him with a soldier’s contempt.

“Here’s a venomous old toad! he knows a kick from my foot would send him to his last home; and he wants me to cheat the gallows. But I have slain too many men in fair fight to lift limb against anything less than a man; and this I count no man. What is it, in Heaven’s name? an old goat’s-skin bag full o’ rotten bones.”

“My mule! my mule!” screamed Ghysbrecht.

Jorian helped him up, the burgomaster shaking in every joint. But once in the saddle, unnatural vigour came into him; and the figure that fled back to Tergou was like something out of a fever-dream: wizened face under streaming white hair, eye baleful, fury shaking the bent frame as he spurred like mad. His quavering voice carried back on the road like a harsh, persistent crow-call:

“I’ll make their hearts ache. I’ll make their hearts ache. I’ll make their hearts ache. All of them. All—all—all!”

Back at the hostelry, the black sheep sat among the drinkers, sick at heart and staring at Hans Memling’s wallet. For ease, the painter had taken it off and flung it on the table; it lay there, slack-mouthed, ready. How easily they might have slid out the true letter and put in another. For the first time in their lives, they were sorry they had not learned to write, like their brother.

Hans began to talk of going; the brothers whispered and agreed to abandon their plan—for the moment.

They had scarcely settled it when Dierich Brower appeared in the doorway and gave them a wink.

They went out to him.

“Come to the burgomaster with all speed,” said he.

They found Ghysbrecht seated at a table, pale and restless. Before him lay the sample of Margaret Van Eyck’s handwriting.

“I have written what you desired,” said he. “Now for the superscription. What were the words? did ye see?”

“We cannot read,” said Cornelis.

“Then is all this labour lost,” cried Ghysbrecht, anger flaring. “Dolts!”

“Nay, but,” said Sybrandt quickly, “I heard the words read, and I have not lost them. They were: ‘To Gerard Eliassoen, these by the hand of the trusty Hans Memling, with all speed.’”

“’Tis well. Now—how was the letter folded? how big was it?”

“Longer than that one, and not so long as this.”

“’Tis well. Where is he?”

“At the hostelry.”

“Come, then. Take you this groat, and treat him. Then ask to see the letter, and put this in place of it. Come to me with the other letter.”

The brothers took the false letter and went. They had not been gone a minute when Dierich Brower slipped out after them, under orders to keep them in sight until the true letter lay safe in his master’s hands. He watched from outside the hostelry like a hound that has been taught which door to guard.

He did not wait long. The brothers came out almost at once, faces fallen.

“Too late!” they cried. “Too late! He is gone.”

“Gone? How long?”

“Scarce five minutes. Cursed chance!”

“You must go back to the burgomaster at once,” said Dierich Brower.

“To what end?”

“No matter; come!” He hurried them back.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was not the man to accept defeat.

“Well,” said he, when he heard. “Suppose he is gone. Is he mounted?”

“No.”

“Then what hinders you to come up with him?”

“But what avails coming up with him! There are no hostelries on the road he is gone.”

“Fools!” said Ghysbrecht. “Is there no way of emptying a man’s pockets but liquor and sleight of hand?”

A look passed between Ghysbrecht and Dierich—cold, swift, understanding. It carried meaning enough. The brothers paled; the zeal ran out of them as if a bung had been pulled.
