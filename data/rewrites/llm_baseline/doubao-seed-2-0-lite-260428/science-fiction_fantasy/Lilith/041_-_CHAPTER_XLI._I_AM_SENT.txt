The hush of the death-hall hung soft around me, silvered by the faint luminescence of the still forms resting on their couches, when I turned to Eve, my voice raw and worn thin of all its old stubborn pride. “Mother, the couch next to Lona sits empty. I know I am unworthy of even a place here, but may I not sleep this night in your chamber with my dead? Will you pardon both my cowardice and the foolish self-confidence that led me astray, and take me in? I give myself up. I am sick of the weight of my own soul, and I would fain sleep that deep, unbroken rest.”

“The couch beside Lona was prepared for you long before you spoke the wish,” she answered, her smile soft as moonlit moss. “But there is a work waiting to be done before you may rest.”

“I am ready,” I said at once.
Her smile widened, warm and teasing. “How do you know you are equal to the task you do not even know?”
“Because you require it of me,” I replied. “What is it?”

She turned to Adam, his face lined with the weight of uncounted eons of life and loss. “Is he forgiven, husband?”
“From the very core of my heart.”
“Then tell him what he must do.”

Adam turned to Mara, where she sat quiet, her hands folded in her lap. “Give me that hand, my child,” he said tenderly, closing his calloused, soil-stained fingers around hers. He rose, nodding toward me. “Come, we will go to the cottage, and there I will instruct you.”
As we walked the short path, a sudden storm blast tore through the air, loud with the thunder of vast, leathery wings beating against the hall roof, before it died away in a low, keening moan like the cry of a wounded giant. When the cottage door of rough-hewn oak clicked shut behind us, he seated himself on a weathered wooden stool, the scent of turned earth and dried rose cuttings clinging to every beam.
“You will remember the dry rock you climbed after leaving Mara’s house,” he said, “the one pocked with the worn grooves of a cataract that ceased to flow before the first human heart ever beat? Climb that rock now, and step deep into the sandy desert beyond. Do not go more than a dozen paces before you lie down and press your ear to the sand. If you hear the faint murmur of water beneath, go a little farther and listen again. If the sound follows you, you are on the right path. Every few yards, stop, lie flat, and hearken. If the murmur fades entirely at any point, you have wandered off course: turn your head and listen in every direction until you pick the sound up again. Keep to that thrumming undercurrent, never retrace your steps, and the sound will grow louder and louder until it hums in your very bones. That is the spot you seek. Dig with the spade I will give you, dig until you break through to damp, living sand. Lay the hand there, cover it smooth to the level of the desert, and come home.
“Take heed, and carry the hand with infinite care. Never set it down, no matter how safe the place may seem. Let no other living thing touch it. Do not stop, nor turn aside for any obstacle that tries to bar your way. Never look behind you. Speak to no one, answer no call, walk straight onward. The night is still pitch dark, and dawn is hours away, but you must set out at once.”
He pressed the wrapped, warm weight of the hand into my palm, then fetched a dented iron spade, its wooden handle worn smooth from decades of use. “This is my gardening spade,” he said. “With it I have coaxed a thousand small, lovely things out of the dark dirt to meet the sun.”

I stepped out into the night. The cold bit through my thin tunic, the dark so thick I could not see my own outstretched hand. The path to the cataract crag was treacherous even at high sun, lined with sharp heather and loose jagged rocks, and I feared a single misstep would break my ankle before I had gone a hundred paces. But the very moment I took my first stride, a faint cool bioluminescent blue light seeped up from the loam beneath my feet, illuminating every crack and stone before my next step fell. I crossed the heath without a single stumble. The bad burrow of the shadow-things lay utterly still, not a ripple marring its surface, not a snarling head rising to snap at my heels as I crossed. Then the moon peeked over the crag edge, silvering the rest of the path before me. Toward early dawn I was nearly across the dry, empty channels of the old riverbed, and not far, I judged, from Mara’s old cottage.

The moon hung low, nearly touching the horizon, the sun still hours from rising, when I saw a figure wrapped head to toe in a veil of glowing moonlit mist, blocking the narrow path between two granite outcrops. I kept walking as if I saw nothing. The veil fell away.
“Have you forgotten me already?” Lilith said, her voice honeyed, sharp under the sweetness.
I did not hesitate, did not answer, walked straight onward.
“You meant to leave me rotting in that horrible sepulchre, did you not?” she snarled. “Do you not yet understand that I can go wherever I please? Take my hand. I am alive, just as you are.”
I was half a breath from saying “Give me your left hand,” to test her, but bit my tongue and held my peace, stepping steadily forward.
“Give me my hand,” she shrieked so loud the rocks rang with the sound, “or I will tear you to pieces. You are mine!”
She launched herself at me. I shuddered from crown to toe, but did not falter. She passed right through me like cold smoke, and she was gone.

A moment later, a full phalanx of armed men, their armour glinting even in the dim moonlight, marched in measured tread along the path, filling it edge to edge. I walked straight through their ranks, and could not tell if they stepped aside to let me pass, or if they were never solid to begin with. They turned, and followed me. I heard their heavy bootfalls thudding right at my heels, the clank of their armour ringing in my ears, but I never once glanced over my shoulder. Little by little, the sound of their steps and the clash of their metal died away to nothing.

A little farther on, the path fell into deep shadow, and I saw a woman muffled in a wool shawl, seated right across the narrowest stretch of track so that there was no way to go around her.
“Ah,” she said, her voice soft and warm, like the memory of a loving home. “You are come at last! I have waited here more than an hour for you. You have done so well. Your trial is over. My father Adam sent me to meet you, so you might rest a little on the way. Give me the charge you carry, lay your head in my lap, and I will watch over both until the sun is well risen. I am not always bitterness, not to every man.”
Her words were a temptation sharper than any I had yet faced. I was so weary my legs were shaking under me, and it sounded so real. What if I was being a fool, trampling over the Lady of Sorrow herself for the sake of following the letter of Adam’s command? My heart lurched so hard I thought it would burst my ribs. But my will hardened against my own soft longing. I bit down on my tongue to stop any unwise reply, and kept walking. If Adam had truly sent her, he would not blame me for clinging to the order he gave. And Mara, the real Mara, would never love me less for refusing to let even her face turn me from the task.
When I was a foot from her, she pulled the shawl from her face. Her beauty was greater than any I had ever seen, but those eyes were not Mara’s. No illusion could ever hold the quiet, tender grief that lived in Mara’s gaze. I stepped forward, and my foot found empty air. The phantom was no more solid than smoke.

Then a great towering Shadow stepped in front of me, blocking the whole path. I thought he wore a war helmet at first, but as I drew nearer I saw the “helmet” was his own head, twisted and distorted so it bore only a faint, warped resemblance to a human face. A gust of wind hit me, dank and rotting, stinking like the inside of a charnel house. My knees went weak, all firmness fled my joints, and I trembled so hard I nearly collapsed in a helpless heap. It felt not as if I passed through him, but as if he passed through me. For one blinding, terrible second, I knew exactly what it felt like to be one of the damned, trapped in endless, cold dark.
Then a soft wind blew, warm as the very first breath of spring after a thousand years of winter, and the pale pink glow of dawn spilled over the dunes ahead of me.

My path led me past Mara’s cottage. Its door stood propped wide open, and on the wooden table inside I saw a crusty loaf of bread and a pitcher of clear, cool water. No wailing echoed from its hollow rooms, no snarls of the lost rose from its corners. I reached the great precipice that bore the worn marks of the vanished cataract, climbed its smooth, water-worn face, and stepped out into the endless expanse of the desert.
I stopped over and over, pressing my ear to the shifting sand, hunting for the faint murmur of trapped water, until I found the spot where the thrumming was loud enough to hum through my teeth. I slung the hand I carried around my neck to keep it safe, and began to dig. The sand was loose, so I had to carve a wide pit, and the work dragged on for what felt like hours, until at last I hauled up a spadeful of dark, damp sand. I set the spade down on the pit’s edge, laid the hand gently in the hollow, and saw little beads of fresh water already seeping out from between its fingers. I scrambled out of the hole, and shoveled the sand back in until the desert surface was smooth and unmarked, no trace of the pit left behind. Utterly fatigued, I dropped beside the spot, and fell fast asleep.
