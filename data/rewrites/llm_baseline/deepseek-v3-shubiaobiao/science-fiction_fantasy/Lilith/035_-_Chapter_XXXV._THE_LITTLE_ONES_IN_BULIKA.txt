The morning sun had barely crested the horizon when we set out, a vibrant procession cutting across the vast plain—children astride bears and deer, elephants laden with hay, and a fluttering escort of butterflies and dragonflies. The very forest seemed to migrate with us, birds leading the way as if they were heralds of some grand procession. Even the insects stayed with us, a shimmering cloud of life that refused to turn back until the gates of Bulika loomed before us.

The journey was not without its whimsy. Little riders dozed on the backs of their patient mounts, and those perched atop the elephants chattered to their unseen companions below. One child, digging hay from his elephant’s load, murmured, *"Nosy dear, I’m coming! Just a little longer and you’ll poke your trunk up to kiss me!"* The beasts themselves seemed to understand, their deep rumbles and whickers blending with the laughter of the children.

But when the city rose before us—its jagged walls like the teeth of some great beast—a hush fell over the company. For the first time, fear flickered in their eyes. These were creatures of the open sky and soft earth; stone nests were alien, unwelcoming. Yet they pressed on, trusting in Lona and, to my shame, in me.

The thunder of hooves and paws echoed through the archway as we entered, a sound so foreign to Bulika’s streets that even the horses startled at their own noise. The Little Ones, usually so fearless, trembled. The three eldest girls clutched the infants tighter, and even the bears seemed uneasy. Only Lona rode steady beside me, her gaze unshaken.

The city awoke sluggishly. Faces peered from windows, first blank, then horrified as they took in the army of children and beasts. Women crept into the streets, drawn despite their terror—until one boy, spotting a woman who reminded him of his mother, flung himself into her arms. A man wrenched him away—only to be met with the swift justice of a girl’s spear.

*"They are just bad giants,"* Lona said, her voice cold as she urged her horse toward a towering brute who dared raise a club. He faltered, and a hail of stones sent him sprawling. Another tried to intercept us, striking a child’s mount—but an elephant’s foot settled the matter.

Yet the worst blow came when we saw the women of Bulika up close. Lona’s face darkened. These were not the mothers her Little Ones dreamed of. Before we could regroup, two boys were snatched away. We gave chase, only to be met by a white leopardess—familiar, yet terrifying to those who remembered the forest’s battles. But little Odu, brave as ever, ran to her. She tumbled in her haste not to harm him, and before she could rise, he was on her back. The sight of a child mastering their terror struck the crowd dumb.

We found one stolen boy in the bowels of a house, tossed into a pit by a woman who sneered that the leopardess would have him. Instead, the beast carried him to safety. The other child was not so fortunate—slain before we could reach him. Lona’s grief was sharp, but she whispered, *"At least he will never become one of them."*

The leopardess turned her wrath on the murderer, dragging him into the street like a cat with prey. *"Let us leave this place,"* Lona said, her voice hollow. *"There are no mothers here."*

As night fell, the Little Ones curled against their animals, asleep in moments. The city lay quiet, but I kept watch. Bulika was not finished with us yet. The princess had not shown herself—and silence was never a good sign.

The children dreamed, their breaths a soft chorus beneath the stars. The animals dozed lightly, ready to wake at a whisper. And all around us, the city held its breath, waiting.
