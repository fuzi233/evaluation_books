The following day, Mr. Hale approached Margaret with a tentative proposal. "We must return Mrs. Thornton's call," he said. "Your mother is unwell and feels unable to walk so far, but you and I shall go this afternoon."

As they walked, Mr. Hale voiced his concern for his wife's health with a veiled anxiety that finally pierced Margaret's composure. "Did you consult the doctor, Margaret? Did you send for him?"

"No, papa," she replied, her voice steady despite her internal turmoil. "You mentioned his coming to see me when I was well. But if only I knew of a competent physician, I would ask him to come this afternoon; I am convinced mamma is seriously indisposed."

She spoke plainly because her father had previously dismissed her fears as fanciful. Now, however, the situation had shifted. He responded in a despondent tone, haunted by the fear that their move to Milton had caused her decline. "Do you think she has a hidden complaint? Is she truly very ill? Has Dixon said anything? Oh, Margaret! I am terrified that our arrival here has killed her. My poor Maria!"

"Oh, papa! Do not imagine such things," Margaret said, shocked. "She is merely unwell for the time being. With proper advice, she will recover stronger than ever."

"But has Dixon said anything?"

"No! You know Dixon loves to wrap trifles in mystery, and her secrecy regarding mamma's health has alarmed me. Yet, without cause, I dare say. You remember you once told me I was becoming fanciful."

"I hope and trust you are," Mr. Hale admitted quickly. "Forget what I said then. I like you to be fanciful about your mother's health! Do not fear telling me your worries. We shall ask Mrs. Thornton if she can recommend a good doctor. We shall not waste money on anyone but the very best. Stay, we are turning up this street."

The street offered no sign of a residence large enough for Mrs. Thornton. Her son's demeanor gave no clue to his living quarters, yet Margaret had unconsciously assumed that the tall, imposing figure of Mrs. Thornton must inhabit a home of similar grandeur. Instead, Marlborough Street revealed long rows of modest houses and blank walls.

"He told me he lived in Marlborough Street, I'm sure," Mr. Hale said, looking perplexed.

"Perhaps it is one of the economies he practices," Margaret suggested. "But there are people about; let me inquire."

She asked a passer-by, who informed her that Mr. Thornton resided near the mill, pointing out the factory lodge-door at the end of a long dead wall. The lodge resembled a common garden gate, flanked by massive closed gates for wagons. Beyond lay a vast oblong yard containing business offices on one side and the immense, multi-windowed mill on the other, where the deafening clank of machinery and the roaring steam engine created a constant din.

Opposite the wall stood a handsome stone-coped house, blackened by smoke yet meticulously maintained. Paint, windows, and steps were scrubbed clean. It was clearly an old structure, perhaps fifty or sixty years in existence, evidenced by its stone facings, narrow windows, and dual flights of steps leading to the front door. Margaret wondered why those who could afford such a fine dwelling did not prefer a smaller home in the countryside or suburbs, escaping the factory's perpetual whirl and noise.

Standing on the steps, Margaret barely heard her father's voice. The yard itself, bounded by the dead wall, seemed a dismal prospect for the sitting rooms inside. Upon entering the drawing-room, she confirmed her suspicion: the space appeared untouched since the furniture had been carefully bagged, as if prepared for a volcanic eruption. The walls were pink and gold; the floral carpet was covered by a colorless linen drugget. Lace curtains hung heavy, and every chair and sofa was draped in netting or knitting. Alabaster groups sat under glass shades, safe from dust. In the center, beneath the bagged chandelier, a large circular table held smartly bound books arranged like the spokes of a wheel.

Everything reflected light; nothing absorbed it. The room possessed a painfully spotted, spangled appearance that unsettled Margaret. She was hardly conscious of the icy, snowy discomfort required to maintain such purity amidst the smoky atmosphere. Everywhere, there was evidence of labor, but not labor aimed at comfort or tranquility; it was solely for ornamentation and the preservation of that ornament from dirt.

They waited in low voices before Mrs. Thornton appeared. She entered rustling in handsome black silk, her muslins and laces matching the room's pristine whiteness. Margaret explained her mother's inability to accompany them, but in her anxiety to avoid reviving her father's fears, she gave a clumsy account. She left the impression that Mrs. Hale's ailment was a temporary, perhaps fanciful, indisposition of a fine lady, easily dismissed if a strong motive existed, or that the call could simply be deferred. Remembering the horses hired for her own visit and the instruction given to Fanny to pay every respect to the Hales, Mrs. Thornton drew herself up slightly offended. She offered no sympathy and granted little credit for the statement of Mrs. Hale's illness.

"How is Mr. Thornton?" asked Mr. Hale. "I was afraid he was not well from his hurried note yesterday."

"My son is rarely ill," Mrs. Thornton replied smoothly. "When he is, he never speaks of it or uses it as an excuse. He told me he could not find leisure to read with you last night, sir. He regretted it; he values the time spent with you."

"I am sure they are equally agreeable to me," Mr. Hale said. "It makes me feel young again to see his enjoyment of classical literature."

"I have no doubt classics are desirable for those with leisure," Mrs. Thornton countered. "But I confess it was against my judgment that my son resumed their study. The time and place in which he lives demand all his energy and attention. Classics suit men who loiter in the country or colleges; but Milton men must have their thoughts absorbed in the work of today. That is my opinion." She spoke this final clause with a pride that mimicked humility.

"But surely, if the mind is directed too long at one object, it becomes stiff and rigid," Margaret argued.

"I do not understand what you mean by a mind getting stiff," Mrs. Thornton said. "Nor do I admire those whirligig characters who are full of this interest today and utterly forget it tomorrow. Having many interests does not suit the life of a Milton manufacturer. It is enough for him to have one great desire and to bring all his life's purposes to bear on fulfilling it."

"And that is—?" Mr. Hale prompted.

Mrs. Thornton's sallow cheek flushed, her eyes lighting up. "To hold and maintain a high, honorable place among the merchants of his country—the men of his town. Such a place my son has earned. Go where you will—I do not say in England only, but in Europe—the name of John Thornton of Milton is known and respected among all men of business. Of course, it is unknown in fashionable circles," she continued scornfully. "Idle gentlemen and ladies are unlikely to know much of a Milton manufacturer unless he enters parliament or marries a lord's daughter."

Both Mr. Hale and Margaret felt an uneasy, ludicrous consciousness that they had never heard of this great name until Mr. Bell wrote to them. The proud mother's world was distinct from their own, whether it be Harley Street gentilities or country clergymen. Margaret's face, despite her efforts to remain simply listening, betrayed her sensitivity to Mrs. Thornton's tone.

"You think you never heard of this wonderful son of mine, Miss Hale. You think I am an old woman whose ideas are hounded by Milton, and whose own crow is the whitest ever seen."

"No," Margaret replied with spirit. "It may be true that I had hardly heard Mr. Thornton's name before coming to Milton. But since I arrived, I have heard enough to respect and admire him, and to feel the justice in what you have said of him."

"Who spoke to you of him?" Mrs. Thornton asked, mollified yet jealous lest another's words did not do him full justice.

Margaret hesitated, disliking the authoritative questioning. Mr. Hale stepped in. "It was what Mr. Thornton said himself that made us know the kind of man he was. Was it not, Margaret?"

Mrs. Thornton drew herself up. "My son is not the one to tell of his own doings. May I ask again, Miss Hale, from whose account you formed your favorable opinion? A mother is curious and greedy of commendation for her children, you know."

"It was as much from what Mr. Thornton withheld," Margaret replied, "as from what Mr. Bell told us of his previous life. It was more that than what he said that made us feel the reason you have to be proud of him."

"Mr. Bell! What can he know of John? He lives a lazy life in a drowsy college." Mrs. Thornton paused, then added, "But I am obliged to you, Miss Hale. Many a young lady would shrink from giving an old woman the pleasure of hearing her son well spoken of."

"Why?" Margaret asked, looking straight at her in bewilderment.

"Because I suppose they might have consciences telling them how surely they are making the old mother into an advocate for them, in case they have plans on the son's heart."

She smiled grimly, pleased by Margaret's frankness, perhaps realizing she had been asking too many questions. Margaret laughed outright at the notion, a merry sound that grated on Mrs. Thornton's ear, suggesting the words must have been utterly ludicrous. Margaret stopped immediately upon seeing Mrs. Thornton's annoyed look.

"I beg your pardon, madam. But I am very much obliged to you for exonerating me from making any plans on Mr. Thornton's heart."

"Young ladies have, before now," Mrs. Thornton said stiffly.

"I hope Miss Thornton is well," Mr. Hale interjected, wishing to change the subject.

"She is as well as she ever is. She is not strong," Mrs. Thornton replied shortly.

"And Mr. Thornton? I suppose I may hope to see him on Thursday?"

"I cannot answer for my son's engagements. There is uncomfortable work in the town; a threat of a strike. If so, his experience will make him much consulted. But I think he can come on Thursday. At any rate, he will let you know if he cannot."

"A strike!" Margaret asked. "What for? What are they striking for?"

"For the mastership and ownership of other people's property," Mrs. Thornton snorted fiercely. "That is what they always strike for. If my son's workpeople strike, I will only say they are a pack of ungrateful hounds. But I have no doubt they will."

"They want higher wages, I suppose?" Mr. Hale asked.

"That is the face of the thing. But the truth is, they want to be masters and make the masters into slaves on their own ground. They always try; they always have it in their minds. Every five or six years, there comes a struggle between masters and men. They will find themselves mistaken this time, I fancy—a little out of their reckoning. If they turn out, they may not find it easy to go in again. I believe the masters have a thing or two in their heads which will teach the men not to strike again in a hurry."

"Does it make the town very rough?" Margaret asked.

"Of course it does. But surely you are not a coward, are you? Milton is not the place for cowards. I have known times when I had to thread my way through a crowd of white, angry men, all swearing they would have Makinson's blood as soon as he showed his nose out of his factory; and he, knowing nothing of it, someone had to tell him, or he was a dead man. It needed to be a woman—so I went. And when I got in, I could not get out. It was as much as my life was worth. So I went up to the roof, where stones were piled ready to drop on the crowd if they forced the factory doors. I would have lifted those heavy stones and dropped them with the aim of the best man there, but I fainted from the heat. If you live in Milton, you must learn to have a brave heart, Miss Hale."

"I would do my best," Margaret said, rather pale. "I do not know whether I am brave until tried; but I am afraid I should be a coward."

"South country people are often frightened by what our Darkshire men and women call living and struggling. But when you've been ten years among a people who always owe their betters a grudge, waiting for an opportunity to pay it off, you'll know whether you are a coward or not, take my word for it."

That evening, Mr. Thornton came to Mr. Hale's. He was shown into the drawing-room where Mr. Hale was reading aloud to his wife and daughter.

"I am come partly to bring you a note from my mother, and partly to apologize for not keeping to my time yesterday," Mr. Thornton said. "The note contains the address you asked for: Dr. Donaldson."

"Thank you!" Margaret said hastily, holding out her hand for the note, wishing her mother not to hear they had inquired about a doctor. She was pleased that Mr. Thornton seemed to understand her feeling immediately; he handed over the note without further explanation.

Mr. Hale began to discuss the strike. Mr. Thornton's face assumed a likeness to his mother's worst expressions, immediately repelling the watching Margaret.
