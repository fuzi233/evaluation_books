The air in the drawing-room was thick with the scent of beeswax and the low hum of a thousand polite anxieties. For a hundred years, the King's door had swung open daily, a ritual of loyalty where nobility fed on the sight of the sovereign. But now, the war had changed the geometry of things. To avoid the enemy's ships, to prevent the catastrophic loss of the royal person at sea, the monarch had been forced to abandon his native Hanover. He remained here, amongst the Britons he loved reluctantly, a captive of geography rather than choice.

Madam Esmond viewed this displacement with a pragmatic clarity that bordered on cynicism. Her sons, the Virginians, were Jacobites in spirit, yet bound by the hard facts of survival. The King *de facto* was the King; he had been crowned, anointed, and had defeated the claimant *de jure* in battle. Domestic morality mattered less than the throne. If the monarch offered a post or a ribbon, Madam Esmond accepted it as inevitable. She had instructed her sons to present themselves, unaware that George had already stolen Harry's laced coat for the journey to Kensington.

Mr. Warrington arranged the introduction through his uncle, an assiduous courtier, while General Lambert accompanied him to thank the Crown for his promotion. They traveled in a hackney-coach, a modest vessel compared to the grandeur they sought, arriving at the gate of Kensington Palace. The sentries recognized the General, and the pair walked on foot through the portico, past galleries lined with tapestries and busts, until they reached the great black marble staircase. From there, they entered the King's drawing-room, a cavern of art housing Titian's Venus and Rubens' St. Francis.

Here, amidst the murmur of visitors, George Warrington stood transfixed. He had never seen a palace before. The paintings seemed to hold a power that transcended their frames: Van Dyck's portrait of Charles I and his family, Tintoret's Esther before Ahasuerus, all rendered in magnificent Venetian hues. George drank in these images with a hunger that drowned out the whispers of General Lambert listing the names of the powerful surrounding them—the Lords of the Admiralty, the Paymaster Fox, whose waistcoat was rumored to be worth a fortune, the Chief Justice Willes, the learned Doctor Sherlock.

"Yonder," Lambert whispered, guiding George's gaze through the crowd, "are the Admiralty. That stout gentleman belted with gold is Mr. Fox. He carries the *auri fames* on his person; his waistcoat is a perfect Potosi."

"Aliena appetens," George murmured, trying to recall the Latin. "How goes the text?"

"He loves to get money and to spend it," Lambert corrected, his tone flat. "And there is Lord Chief Justice Willes, talking to Lord Salisbury and Doctor Headley. He belongs to your grandfather's time. Loved by Dick Steele, hated by the Dean. Beside him stands Lord London, Doctor Sherlock. The lawn-sleeves have lost half their honors. Remember when I was a boy, kneeling to the Bishop of Rochester? That handsome fair man is Admiral Smith, President of Byng's court-martial. The French broke him when he was a lieutenant for lowering topsails to a French ship, yet the King made him a captain the next day. And that tall, haughty figure? Lord George Sackville. Once I was a Major-General, he might treat me better than a footman. I wish my old Blakeney were here; he is the soldier's darling."

As Lambert spoke, a young gentleman approached, mistaking the General for someone else. "I have the honor of speaking to Mr.—?"

"To Major-General Lambert, at your lordship's service," Lambert replied smoothly. "That, Mr. Warrington, is the first commoner in England, Mr. Speaker Onslow. Where is your uncle? I shall have to present you myself if Sir Miles delays."

Lambert turned his attention entirely to George, ignoring his colleague who stalked away with a startled expression. In another age, a nobleman demanded admiration; here, the hierarchy shifted with the arrival of the sovereign.

Sir Miles Warrington appeared, his red waistcoat bright against the gloom. Cordial greetings erupted between him, his nephew, and the General. The Baronet was drowning in emotion, choked by the sheer generosity of his feelings. "We miss you, we positively miss you!" he cried, wiping his eyes. "My daughters were in love with those lovely young ladies... We feel as if we were old friends already... We love you, on account of your friendship and kindness to our dear nephews." He paused, overwhelmed. "My own flesh and blood, sir; my dear, dear brother's boys!"

While Sir Miles poured out his heart, George restrained his laughter with admirable gravity. Suddenly, the door to the King's apartments opened. Pages swept in, announcing the monarch. He was followed by his son, the Duke of Cumberland, a corpulent prince in a coat of blazing scarlet. Behind them trailed the ministers of state, including the towering figure of Mr. Secretary Pitt, whose eagle eye and beak cut through the room. Even a century later, the image of Pitt struck awe—a colossus striding the narrow island, his gouty shoes silent, his hand wrapped in flannel like a thunderbolt.

Perhaps anger simmered beneath his dark looks. Hours of eloquent, repulsive speeches had likely bored the small, shrewd old gentleman who disliked fine broderie and fine brose alike. The sublime Minister passed solemnly through the crowd; the company ranged along the walls. The King walked the circle, his royal son lagging behind to engage select individuals.

The monarch was a little, keen, fresh-colored man with protruding eyes, dressed in plain, snuff-colored clothes and brown stockings, his only ornament the blue ribbon of the Garter. He spoke with a German accent, addressing those he chose to notice before moving on. He knew General Lambert, who had served under him at Dettingen and in Scotland.

"It is not always," the King said, his voice easy and simple, "that we can do as we like; but I was glad when, for once, I could give myself that pleasure in your case, General; for my army contains no better officer as you."

Lambert blushed, deeply gratified. The King's gaze then fell on Sir Miles Warrington and the young man beside him.

"Who is this?" the Defender of the Faith asked, pointing to George, who stood in a respectful posture wearing Harry's best embroidered suit.

"With the deepest reverence," Sir Miles informed the King, "this young gentleman is my nephew, Mr. George Warrington, of Virginia, who asks leave to pay his humble duty."

"This, then, is the other brother?" the Prince observed. "He came in time, else the other brother would have spent all the money." Turning to Lord Salisbury, he added, "Why do you come out in this bitter weather? You had much better stay at home!"

The King moved on, leaving Sir Miles shaken by the royal condescension. He clapped his nephew's hands. "God bless you, my boy," he cried. "I told you that you would see the greatest monarch and the finest gentleman in the world. Is he not so, my Lord Bishop?"

"That, that he is!" cried the Bishop, clasping his ruffled hands and turning his eyes to the sky. "The best of princes and of men."

Lambert pointed out another young gentleman, Louis, the favorite nephew of Lady Yarmouth. Soon, the Duke of Cumberland approached their group. He held out his hand to Lambert.

"Congratulate you on your promotion, Lambert," the Prince said good-naturedly.

"I owe it, sir, to your Royal Highness's good offices," Lambert replied gratefully.

"Not at all; not at all: ought to have had it a long time before. Always been a good officer; perhaps there'll be some employment for you soon. This is the gentleman whom James Wolfe introduced to me?"

"His brother, sir."

"Oh, the real Fortunate Youth! You were with poor Ned Braddock in America—a prisoner, and lucky enough to escape. Come and see me, sir, in Pall Mall. Bring him to my levee, Lambert." The broad back of the Prince turned, dismissing them.

"It is raining!" Sir Miles shouted, seizing the moment. "You came on foot, General Lambert? You and George must come home in my coach. You must and shall come home with me, I say. By George, you must! I'll have no denial."

He drove them back to Hill Street, presenting the General to his wife and daughters, Flora and Dora, insisting they share a collation. "What, there is only cold mutton? Well, an old soldier can eat cold mutton. And a good glass of my Lady Warrington's own cordial, prepared with her own hands, will keep the cold wind out."

"Delicious cordial! Capital mutton!" Sir Miles declared, explaining the scarcity. "Our own, my dear General, our own from the country, six years old if a day.
