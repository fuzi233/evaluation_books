ON BEING IN LOVE. You have undoubtedly been in love, or fate has already queued you for the inevitable. Love is akin to the measles; it is a universal rite of passage that demands we contract it precisely once. Those who have survived the initial infection possess a peculiar immunity, granting them the audacity to navigate the most treacherous social landscapes with reckless abandon. They may picnicate in shadowed woodlands, wander through verdant alleys, or linger on moss-draped benches watching the sun dip below the horizon, fearing the quiet confinement of a country house no more than they dread their own private clubs. They can descend the Rhine amidst a family gathering or, driven by curiosity, step directly into the gaping maws of matrimony itself. They can endure the dizzying spin of a ravishing waltz and retreat to a dark conservatory, emerging with nothing worse than a mild chill. They can brave moonlit walks along perfumed lanes or twilight expeditions through somber reeds, cross stile gates without peril, scramble through brambled hedges unseen, and traverse slippery slopes without slipping. They can gaze into radiant eyes without being blinded and listen to siren calls while steering their ship with unwavering resolve. They may clasp pale hands in theirs, yet feel no electric, Lulu-esque tether binding them to that delicate pressure. No, we do not succumb to love twice. Cupid fires but one arrow per heart. Love's attendants—respect, admiration, and affection—are our lifelong companions, always welcome at our door, but their grand celestial sovereign, in his royal procession, visits only once before departing. We like, we cherish, we are exceedingly fond of—but we never love again. A man's heart is a firework destined to flash heavenward a single time. Like a meteor, it blazes briefly, illuminating the entire world below with its brilliance, before the night of our mundane existence descends. The burnt-out casing falls back to earth, useless and ignored, slowly smoldering into ash. Once, breaking free from our prison walls, we dare, as the ancient Titan Prometheus did, to climb Olympus and seize the divine fire from Phoebus's chariot. Blessed are those who, hastening downward before the flame expires, can kindle their domestic altars with its spark. Love is too pure a radiance to sustain itself long among the noxious gases we breathe, yet before it is extinguished, we may wield it as a torch to ignite the cozy hearth of affection. Ultimately, that warming glow suits our cramped, chilly parlors far better than the burning spirit of love itself. Love belongs in the Vestal temples of some vast, dim sanctuary where the organ music is the rolling of the spheres. Affection burns cheerfully once the white flame of love flickers out. It is a fire that can be tended daily, piling higher as winter approaches. Elderly couples can sit by it with thin, clasped hands; small children can nestle before it; neighbors find their welcoming corner beside it; and even shaggy dogs and sleek cats can toast their noses against the bars. Let us heap the coals of kindness upon that fire. Cast in your pleasant words, your gentle handshakes, your thoughtful and selfless actions. Fan it with humor, patience, and forbearance. Then, let the wind blow and the rain fall unnoticed, for your hearth will remain warm and bright, casting sunshine upon the faces gathered around it despite the storm outside.

I fear, dear Edwin and Angelina, that you harbor unrealistic expectations of love. You suppose your youthful hearts contain sufficient fuel to sustain this fierce, devouring passion throughout your entire lives. Ah, young fools! Do not place your trust in that unstable flicker. It will dwindle and fade as the months pass, with no means of replenishment. You will watch it expire in anger and disillusionment. To each, it will appear that the other is growing colder. Edwin observes with bitterness that Angelina no longer dashes to the gate to greet him, flushed and smiling; and when he now suffers a cough, she offers only a suggestion of a lozenge, delivered in a tone suggesting she wishes primarily to escape the noise. Poor Angelina, in turn, sheds silent tears because Edwin has ceased carrying her old handkerchief in the inner pocket of his waistcoat. Both are stunned by the decline in the other, yet neither recognizes their own transformation. If they did, they would not suffer as they do. They would seek the cause in the proper direction—in the pettiness of human nature—and join hands over this shared failing to rebuild their home upon a more terrestrial and enduring foundation. Yet, we are so blind to our own flaws while hyper-vigilant regarding those of others. Every misfortune is invariably someone else's fault. Angelina would have loved Edwin eternally had he not become so strange and altered. Edwin would have adored Angelina forever had she remained identical to the girl he first worshipped. It is a bleak hour for you both when the lamp of love has gone out and the fire of affection remains unkindled, forcing you to grope in the cold, raw dawn of life to restart it. May God grant it catches light before the day is fully spent. Many sit shivering by dead coals until nightfall. But why preach? Who, feeling the surge of young love coursing through their veins, believes it will ever flow sluggishly? To a twenty-year-old, it seems impossible that he will not love as wildly at sixty as he does now. He cannot recall any middle-aged or elderly gentleman exhibiting signs of frantic attachment, yet this fails to deter his conviction. His love will never falter, regardless of anyone else's. Nobody has ever loved as he does, so naturally, the rest of the world's experience serves as no guide. Alas! Alas! Ere thirty, he joins the ranks of the cynics. It is not his fault. Our passions, both virtuous and vicious, cease with our blushes. We do not hate, grieve, rejoice, or despair in our thirties as we did in our teens. Disappointment no longer suggests suicide, and we consume success without intoxication. As we age, we conduct all affairs in a minor key. Life's opera lacks majestic passages in its later acts. Ambition sets lower sights; honor becomes reasonable and conveniently adapts to circumstance. And love—it dies. "Irreverence for the dreams of youth" soon creeps like a killing frost upon our hearts. Tender shoots and blooming flowers are nipped and withered, leaving behind only a sapless stump of a vine that once yearned to wrap its tendrils around the globe.

My fair friends will surely deem this rank heresy. Far from believing a man ceases to love after boyhood, it is not until gray hairs abound that they consider his protestations worthy of attention. Young ladies derive their notions of our sex from novels penned by their own, and compared to the monstrosities masquerading as men in that nightmare literature, Pythagoras's plucked bird and Frankenstein's demon were merely average specimens of humanity. In these so-called books, the chief lover—or Greek god, as they admiringly refer to him (though they rarely specify which deity, it might be hunchbacked Vulcan, two-faced Janus, or drooling Silenus, the god of obscure mysteries)—resembles the entire family in being a blackguard. This is likely the intended meaning. However, even the scant masculinity possessed by his classical prototypes, he claims none whatsoever, being a listless, effeminate noodle past the age of forty. Yet, oh! the depth and intensity of this elderly suitor's emotion for some bread-and-butter schoolgirl! Hide your heads, ye young Roméos and Léanders! This *blasé* old beau loves with a hysterical fervor requiring four adjectives for every noun to adequately describe. It is well, dear ladies, that you study only books. Had you read mankind, you would know that a boy's shy stammering tells a truer tale than our bold eloquence. A boy's love springs from a full heart; a man's is more often the result of a full stomach. Indeed, a man's sluggish current cannot be called love compared to the rushing fountain that wells up when a boy's heart is struck by the heavenly rod. If you wish to taste love, drink from the pure stream youth pours at your feet. Do not wait until it becomes a muddy river before stooping to catch its waves. Or is it that you prefer its bitter flavor—that the clear, limpid water is insipid to your palate, and only the pollution of its downstream journey gives it a relish? Must we believe those who tell us that a hand fouled by the filth of a shameful life is the only one a young girl desires to be caressed by? That is the doctrine bawled out day and night from between those yellow covers. Do they ever pause to reflect, I wonder, those devilish lady-helps, what mischief they are causing as they crawl through God's garden, telling childish Evés and silly Adams that sin is sweet and decency is ridiculous and vulgar? How many innocent girls do they not degrade into wicked women? To how many weak lads do they not point out the dirty by-path as the shortcut to a maiden's heart? It is not as if they depict life as it truly is. Speak truth, and righteousness will take care of itself. But their pictures are coarse daubs painted from the sickly fancies of their own diseased imaginations. We wish to envision women—not as their own sex presents them, as Loreleys luring us to destruction, but as good angels beckoning us upward.
