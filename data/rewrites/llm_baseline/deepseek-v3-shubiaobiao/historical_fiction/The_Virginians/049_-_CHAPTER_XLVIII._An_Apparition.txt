Harry Warrington sat brooding in his prison chamber, his pride wounded, his spirit simmering with resentment. The lawyer Draper had come and gone, leaving behind him a sting of humiliation. Harry clenched his fists, replaying the bitter exchange in his mind. He had been trapped—by Maria, by her brother, by his own reckless trust. They had preyed upon his youth, his generosity. Had they ventured to Virginia, he would have shown them a far different welcome.

A knock at the door startled him. Gumbo’s grinning face appeared, announcing a visitor. Before Harry could react, a figure swept past the servant—a lady in a capuchin, her face shadowed by the hood. She rushed to him, her hands trembling as she grasped his.

"Oh, Harry!" she cried, her voice breaking. "Did I ever think to see you like this?"

He recoiled, but she sank beside his bed, pressing his feverish fingers to her lips. Tears streaked her cheeks as she gazed at him with desperate tenderness.

"I—I thought none of you would come," Harry muttered, his voice hoarse.

Her answer was a flood of whispered endearments, her hands clutching his as if she could will strength into him. "I cannot bear to see you suffer," she wept. "Some of this is my fault—my greed, my vanity! I brought them all back—every trinket, every jewel you gave me."

From her reticule, she spilled brooches, rings, a watch, a handful of guineas—all into his lap. Harry’s throat tightened. His pride warred with gratitude, but the sight of her sacrifice undid him.

"Dearest cousin," he murmured, his defiance crumbling.

She hushed him, smoothing his hair as a mother might. "Do not speak. Only know that you are not forgotten."

For a time, they spoke in quiet tones—of debts, of regrets, of small hopes kindled anew. The shadows lengthened before she rose, pressing a final kiss to his brow.

When she left, the room felt colder. But Harry’s heart was lighter.

Then came Draper, his entrance like a gust of foul air.

"Your servant, sir," the lawyer sneered. "Have you packed your trunk?"

Harry stiffened. "I have reconsidered. My word is given."

Draper scoffed. "She lost the paper! There’s nothing binding you!"

"Enough!" Harry’s voice cracked like a whip. "You mistake me for one of your pettifogging clients. I keep my promises."

The lawyer’s face purpled. "Promises? To a scheming old—"

Harry was on his feet in an instant. Draper scrambled back, sputtering threats before fleeing.

Alone again, Harry exhaled sharply. He would not betray her trust, even if it meant rotting in this cell.

Meanwhile, the Baroness de Bernstein waited in her dining room, the untouched meal before her. The chair meant for Harry stood empty.

"Eight o'clock," murmured her servant. "You must eat."

She waved him away. "Bring coffee."

But when the door opened again, it was not the servant who entered.

A figure stood in the threshold—tall, unmistakable in resemblance. The Baroness froze.

"Mr. Warrington!" she gasped.

The stranger bowed. "You expected my brother, madam?"

Her breath hitched. "Who—?"

"I arrived in London tonight. His servant gave me this letter for you." He extended it, his gaze piercing. "I am George Warrington. And I have come to ask—what have you done with my brother?"

The Baroness swayed. "He was to dine here—that chair—!"

George’s expression hardened. "Then I will find him."

"Bring him back!" she pleaded. "Take whatever you need—money, influence—!"

But George was already turning. "Keep your gold, madam. I want only my brother."

With that, he strode into the night, leaving the Baroness trembling in the flickering candlelight.
