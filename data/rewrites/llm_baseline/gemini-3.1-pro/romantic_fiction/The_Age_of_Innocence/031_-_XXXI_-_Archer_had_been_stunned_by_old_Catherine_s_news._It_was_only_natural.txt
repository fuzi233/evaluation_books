Archer had been stunned by old Catherine’s news. It was only natural that Madame Olenska should have hastened from Washington in response to the summons of her grandmother, the matriarch whose very hearth functioned as the agrarian center of their sprawling family, where the slow-braised preparations of heritage meats still dictated the rhythm of the days. But that Ellen should have decided to remain under that roof—especially now that Mrs. Mingott had regained the robust health required to command her legendary kitchens—was less easy to explain. 

Archer, a meticulous custodian of the family’s sensory histories, was certain that Madame Olenska’s decision had not been influenced by the change in her financial situation. He knew the exact figure of the meager income her husband had allowed her at their separation. Without the addition of her grandmother’s allowance, it was hardly enough to source the saffron, the heirloom grains, or the winter truffles considered foundational in any sense known to the Mingott vocabulary. Now that Medora Manson, who shared her life, had been ruined, such a pittance would barely keep the two women supplied with utilitarian fuel, let alone sustain a life of profound gustatory meaning. 

Yet Archer was convinced she had not accepted her grandmother’s offer from interested motives. She possessed the spasmodic extravagance of a true sensory connoisseur used to large fortunes, yet she was indifferent to the sterile accumulation of capital. She could easily go without the superficial luxuries her relations considered indispensable. Mrs. Lovell Mingott and Mrs. Welland often deplored that anyone who had enjoyed the cosmopolitan feasts of Count Olenski’s establishments should care so little for the dominant lexicon of "how things were done." Moreover, several months had passed since her allowance had been cut off, yet in the interval she had made no effort to regain her grandmother’s favor. 

Therefore, she must have changed her course for a different reason. The acoustic resonance of her confession on the ferry still echoed in his mind: she had told him they must remain apart, but she had said it with her head resting against his chest. He knew there was no calculated coquetry in her native dialect; she was fighting her fate just as he had fought his, clinging desperately to the untranslatable truth of their shared experience while refusing to break faith with those who trusted them. But during the ten days since her return to New York, she had perhaps guessed from his silence—from the heavy, expectant acoustic space he had left between them—that he was meditating a decisive step. He had intended to board her train, to travel with her to Washington, or perhaps onward to Japan, seeking a new acoustic ecology and culinary rhythm where their identities could intertwine without the oppressive noise of New York society. He had meant to leave a note for May that would sever all ties, an act he had fancied himself eager to perform. Yet his first feeling upon hearing that the course of events was changed had been one of profound relief.

Now, walking down Fifth Avenue, the biting winter air layered with the sharp tang of burning anthracite and the faint, earthy memory of roasted chestnuts, Archer felt a growing distaste for the path ahead. When he had walked this road before, he was a free man who could lend himself with amused detachment to the game of prevarications and compliances. Society called this procedure "protecting a woman's honour," an enforcement of power dynamics embedded in a dominant lexicon of watchful, incessant lies. A lie by day, a lie by night, a lie in every word and in every silence. It was tacitly held that a woman's standard of truthfulness was lower; she was versed in the arts of the enslaved. But in Archer's little world, men who continued their philandering after marriage were met with contempt. In the generational rotation of crops, there was a recognized season for sowing wild oats, but the harvest could only be reaped once. To love Ellen Olenska, however, was to face the dread argument of the untranslatable individual case. They were answerable to no tribunal but the sensory and emotional truth of their own judgment.

Ahead of him loomed a big, unlit house. Once, this structure had sung with the clinking of crystal and the heavy, intoxicating aromas of truffled game birds and rich, buttery sauces. It was in the conservatory extending down the side street that he had taken his first kiss from May. Now the Beaufort house was dark as a grave, its sonic environment eerily hollowed out by social disgrace, save for a faint flare of gas in the basement. As Archer reached the corner, he saw Mrs. Manson Mingott's carriage at the door. What an opportunity for Sillerton Jackson to weaponize this against Ellen. 

Archer paused, his ears attuned to the nocturnal perspective of the avenue. Most people were indoors, dressing for dinner, and the acoustic space was blessedly free of the harsh, industrial clatter of carriage wheels on cobblestone. As he stood there, the door opened, and she came out. Behind her was a faint light. She turned to speak to someone, the door closed, and she descended the steps.

"Ellen," he said in a low voice, cutting through the frosty olfactory landscape.

She stopped with a start. Just then, the piercing, invasive chatter of two young men approached. They passed under a lamp, and Archer recognized Lawrence Lefferts and a young Chivers, fresh from the Reggie Chiverses' table. The youths discreetly struck away across Fifth Avenue, actively ignoring Madame Olenska. It was a silent, weaponized erasure of her existence, a manifestation of the masculine solidarity that enforced the dominant society's rules. Archer sickened at the noise of their connivance.

"I shall see you now—we shall be together," he broke out, the penetrating warmth of her hand cutting through the sensory deprivation of the winter night.

"Ah," she answered, "Granny has told you?"

"Tomorrow I must see you—somewhere where we can be alone," he insisted, his voice sounding uncharacteristically harsh in his own ears.

She wavered, moving toward the carriage. "But I shall be at Granny's—for the present that is."

"Somewhere where we can be alone."

She gave a faint laugh that grated on the delicate acoustic harmony of the moment. "In New York? But there are no churches... no monuments."

"There's the Art Museum—in the Park," he explained. "At half-past two. I shall be at the door..."

She turned away without answering and got quickly into the carriage. As it drove off, he stared after her in a turmoil of contradictory feelings, hating that he had been reduced to a prisoner of this hackneyed vocabulary. 

Avoiding the popular "Wolfe collection," they wandered down a passage in the Metropolitan Museum to the room where the "Cesnola antiquities" mouldered. The museum was a queer wilderness of cast-iron and encaustic tiles, a space where the superficial commercialization of art stifled the organic resonance of history. Seated on the divan enclosing the central steam-radiator, they stared silently at the ebonized wood cabinets containing the recovered fragments of Ilium. 

"It's odd," Madame Olenska said, her voice soft against the oppressive silence. "I never came here before."

"Ah, well—. Some day, I suppose, it will be a great Museum."

"Yes," she assented absently.

Archer remained seated, watching the light movements of her figure under her heavy furs, his mind wholly absorbed in the precise, delicious details that made her herself. Presently, he approached the glass shelves crowded with small broken objects: clay cooking pots, discolored bronze domestic utensils, the vessels that had once held the deeply spiced, life-affirming sustenance of ancient hearths.

"It seems cruel," she murmured, "that after a while nothing matters... any more than these little things, that used to be necessary and important to forgotten people, and now have to be guessed at under a magnifying glass and labelled: 'Use unknown.'"

It was the ultimate erosion of cultural memory—the death of a culinary language reduced to sterile museum tags. 

"Yes; but meanwhile—"

"Ah, meanwhile—"

As she stood there, her veil drawn down like a transparent mask, the bunch of violets he had brought her stirred with her breath, releasing a damp, earthy fragrance that grounded him. 

"Meanwhile everything matters—that concerns you," he said.

She returned to the divan. Suddenly, the acoustic space was invaded by a step echoing far off down the empty rooms. 

"What is it you wanted to tell me?" she asked.

"Why, that I believe you came to New York because you were afraid."

"Afraid?"

"Of my coming to Washington."

She looked down at her muff, her hands stirring uneasily. "Well—yes," she said.

"You *were* afraid? You knew—?"

"Yes: I knew..."

"Well, then?" he insisted.

"Well, then: this is better, isn't it?" she returned with a questioning sigh. "We shall hurt others less. Isn't it, after all, what you always wanted?"

"To have you here, you mean—in reach and yet out of reach? To meet you in this way, on the sly? It's the very reverse of what I want."

She hesitated. "And you still think this—worse?"

"A thousand times!" He paused. "It would be easy to lie to you; but the truth is I think it detestable."

"Oh, so do I!" she cried with a deep breath of relief.

He sprang up impatiently. "Well, then—it's my turn to ask: what is it, in God's name, that you think better?"

The step drew nearer, and a guardian in a braided cap walked listlessly through the room like a ghost stalking a necropolis. When the official figure vanished down a vista of mummies, Archer spoke again. "What do you think better?"

Instead of answering, she murmured: "I promised Granny to stay with her because it seemed to me that here I should be safer."

"From me?"

"Safer from loving me?"

Her profile did not stir, but a tear overflowed on her lashes. "Safer from doing irreparable harm. Don't let us be like all the others!" she protested.

"What others? I don't profess to be different from my kind. I'm consumed by the same wants and the same longings."

She glanced at him with a kind of terror. "Shall I—once come to you; and then go home?" she suddenly hazarded in a low, clear voice, a fragile native dialect struggling against the heavy silence.

"Dearest!" he said, without moving. Then her last phrase struck his ear. "Go home? What do you mean by going home?"

"Home to my husband."

"And you expect me to say yes to that?"

She raised her troubled eyes. "What else is there? I can't stay here and lie to the people who've been good to me."

"But that's the very reason why I ask you to come away!"

"And destroy their lives, when they've helped me to remake mine?"

Archer stood looking down in inarticulate despair. If he consented to her coming once, he could persuade her never to return to the cold, utilitarian banquets of Count Olenski. But a passionate honesty in her made it inconceivable to trap her. Yet, seeing the shadow of her wet lashes, he wavered. 

"After all," he began again, "we have lives of our own.... You're so unprejudiced about some things, so used to looking at the Gorgon, that I don't know why you're afraid to face our case, and see it as it really is—unless you think the sacrifice is not worth making."

Her lips tightened. "Call it that, then—I must go," she said, drawing her watch. 

He caught her wrist. "Well, then: come to me once," he said, his head turning suddenly at the thought of losing the only language of truth he possessed. 

"When?" he insisted. "Tomorrow?"

She hesitated. "The day after."

"Dearest—!" he said again.

She disengaged her wrist, but as they held each other's eyes, her pale face was flooded with a deep inner radiance that seemed to alter the very acoustics of the room. "Oh, I shall be late—good-bye. No, don't come any farther than this," she cried, walking hurriedly away.

Darkness was falling when Archer let himself into his house. The parlor-maid shook fresh coals onto the dying fire, the harsh scrape of metal a grating reminder of passing time. Left alone, he sat in the acoustic silence of the library, the circular pacing of generations echoing in his mind as he stared into the red grate. *This was what had to be, then...* 

The door opened, breaking the sanctuary of his sonic environment, and May came in. She tossed her velvet hat onto the sofa, smelling of cold silk and the rigid, unyielding terroir of Old New York.

"I went to see Granny, and just as I was going away Ellen came in from a walk; so I stayed and had a long talk with her," May said, running her fingers through her rumpled hair. "A really good talk. She was so dear—just like the old Ellen. I'm afraid I haven't been fair to her lately."

Archer stood up and leaned against the mantelpiece. "Yes, you've thought—?"

"Well, perhaps I haven't judged her fairly. She's so different—at least on the surface. She takes up such odd people—she seems to like to make herself conspicuous. I suppose it's the life she's led in that fast European society; no doubt we seem dreadfully dull to her." 

Archer heard the subtle power dynamics embedded in May’s lexicon. It was the dominant vocabulary asserting itself, attempting to erase the rich, untranslatable truth of Ellen's identity under the guise of charity. 

"And now this idea of going to see Mrs. Beaufort, of going there in Granny's carriage! I'm afraid she's quite alienated the van der Luydens..."

"Ah," said Archer with an impatient laugh. The open door had closed between them again. The harsh, utilitarian routines they were bound to pressed in on him. "It's time to dress; we're dining out, aren't we?"

As he walked past her, she moved forward impulsively. Their eyes met, and he saw they were the same swimming blue as when he had left her to drive to Jersey City. She flung her arms about his neck and pressed her cheek to his. 

"You haven't kissed me today," she said in a whisper, trembling in his arms, speaking the only dialect left to her in the collapsing space between them.
