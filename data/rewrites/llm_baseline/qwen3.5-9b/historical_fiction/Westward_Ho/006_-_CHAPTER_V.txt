The following morning, Amyas Leigh was nowhere to be found. He had not fled to drown his sorrows in the Torridge, nor had he ridden down to lament his fate by the riverbank. Instead, Frank discovered that Amyas had galloped straight to Sir Richard Grenville at Stow. His mother, instantly discerning the truth—that her son had departed to seek a commission in the Irish army—had immediately dispatched Frank after him to retrieve him and urge him to reconsider his rash decision.

Thus, Frank mounted his horse and rode ten miles or more. Finding no inns along the route—a rarity even in later eras—and facing another ten miles of rugged ascent ahead, he turned downhill toward Clovelly Court. In the hospitable spirit of those days, he sought lodging and refreshment for both man and beast from Mr. Cary, the local squire.

Entering the long, dark hall, panelled in oak, Frank felt as though he had stumbled in like the boisterous Menelaus. The first figure that greeted him was the towering form of Amyas, seated at the massive table. He was engaged in a peculiar dance, alternating between burying his face deep in a meat pie and pressing the pastry firmly against his own visage. Clearly, his grief had only sharpened his appetite. Opposite him, kneeling on the bench with elbows planted on the wood, sat young Will Cary. With a gesture of graceful intensity, Will was laying down the law to his friend in a low, fierce whisper.

"Hillo, lad!" cried Amyas, breaking free from the pie. "Come here and rescue me from the grasp of this fire-eater, who I verily believe intends to murder me, unless I allow him to kill someone else first."

"Ah, Mr. Frank!" exclaimed Will Cary. Like all the young gentlemen of the region, he held Frank in the highest esteem, regarding him as an oracle of fashion and chivalry. "Welcome! I was longing for your counsel on half a dozen matters. Sit down, eat, and drink the ale."

"Not so early, thank you," Frank replied politely.

"Avoiding strong ale in the morning?" Amyas mocked, plunging his head into his tankard. Then, mimicking Frank's mannerisms, he continued, "It heats the blood, thickens the animal spirits, and clouds the pure light of reason with frenzied idols. Eh? Young Plato, young Daniel, come forth to judgment! Yet, though I cannot see the bottom of this tankard, I perceive clearly enough that Will shall not fight."

"Shall I not, eh? Who says so? Mr. Frank, I appeal to you; listen."

"We sit in judgment," Frank declared, settling comfortably onto the pasty. "Proceed, appellant."

"Well, I was telling Amyas that Tom Coffin of Portledge... I can tolerate him no longer."

"Let him be," advised Amyas. "He stood quite well on his own when I last encountered him."

"Plague on you, keep quiet! Does he have any right to stare at me whenever I pass?"

"That depends entirely on his gaze; a cat may look at a king, provided she does not mistake him for a mouse."

"Oh, I know exactly what he looks like and what he means. He must stop, or I shall stop him. Besides, the other day, when I mentioned Rose Salterne—" "Ah!" groaned Frank, "Here comes the apple again!" "(Never mind what I said)"—Will continued, "he burst out laughing in my face. Is that not cause for a duel? Moreover, I know he composed a sonnet and sent it to her via a market woman. What right has he to write poetry when I cannot? It is unfair play, Mr. Frank, or I am a Jew, a Spaniard, and a Papist! It is not!" With that, Will struck the table so violently that the plates trembled.

"My dear Knight of the Burning Pestle," Frank interjected, shifting his tone from banter to something far sweeter and more persuasive, "do not imagine that I lack sympathy for you, or that I, like yourself, have not felt the sting of love and the sharper bite of jealousy. But, Mr. Cary, does it not seem to you an awful waste to squander that divine wrath—which, as Plato teaches, is the root of all virtue—upon such petty quarrels? This wrath has been granted to you, along with everything else you possess, to be spent in the service of Her whom all wicked souls fear and all virtuous ones adore: our Peerless Queen. Who dares, while she reigns over England, claim his sword or his courage as his own, rather than hers? Are there no Spaniards to conquer, no wild Irish to liberate from oppression, that two gentlemen of Devon must find no nobler arena for their blades than each other's hearts?"

"By heaven!" cried Amyas. "Frank speaks like a book! As for me, I believe Christian gentlemen ought to leave love-quarrels to bulls and rams."

"And that the heir of Clovelly," Frank added with a smile, "might find more noble examples to emulate than the stags in his own deer park."

"Well," said Will, looking penitent, "you are a great scholar, Mr. Frank, and you speak like one. But gentlemen must fight sometimes, or where lies their honor?"

"I speak," Frank stated with quiet pride, "not merely as a scholar, but as a gentleman who has fought before. Indeed, Mr. Cary, I have taken a life (on whose soul may God have mercy). Yet, it remains my pride that I have never yet fought in my own personal quarrel, and my trust in God that I never shall. For nothing is more noble than fighting for those we love; conversely, fighting for purely selfish reasons is unforgivable for a Christian man, unless refusal implies the total loss of life or honor. Even then, it is preferable to overcome evil with good rather than resist it with force."

"And I can tell you, Will," Amyas added, "I am not afraid of ghosts. But when I severed the Frenchman's head, I told myself, 'If that braggart had been slandering me instead of Her Gracious Majesty, I would expect to see that head resting on my pillow every night.'"

"God forbid!" Will shuddered. "But what am I to do? Tomorrow I will go to the market, even if it is choked with Coffins and contains a ghost in every coffin."

"Leave the matter to me," Amyas assured him. "I have devised a plan, just as Scholar Frank here has. If there is a quarrel in the market tomorrow, watch and see if I do not..."

"You are two fine fellows," Will conceded. "Let us fetch another tankard."

"And let us toast Mr. Coffin and all the gallant lads of the North," Frank suggested. "Now, to my business. I must escort this runaway youth home to his mother. If he refuses to go willingly, I have orders to carry him across my saddle."

"I hope your nag has a sturdy back," Amyas remarked. "But I must depart to see Sir Richard, Frank. It is all very well to jest, but my mind is resolved."

"Stop," commanded Cary. "You must stay here tonight. First, for the sake of fellowship; second, because I require the counsel of our Phoenix, our Oracle, our Paragon." Turning to Frank, he whispered urgently, "Can you decipher this for me? Speak low, gentlemen; my father approaches. You had best return the letter."

"Yes, Father," Will murmured, handing over the note. "Whence came this morning?"

"Eh, company here? Young men are always welcome, especially in these dirty times! How fares your good mother, Frank? Where have I been, Will? Around the farm-house, inspecting the cattle. That sheeted heifer of Prowse's is behaving strangely; her coat looks like a hedgehog's. Tell Jewell to bring her in before nightfall. Then check the forty acres; spring released two coveys, and I picked a brace from them. The Irish hawk flies as wildly as any harrier and will never succeed. I handed her to Tom and kept the little peregrine. A Clovelly hawk is worth its weight in gold against the world, after all! Heigh-ho, I am starving! Half-past twelve and no dinner? What, Master Amyas, ruining your appetite with strong ale? Try sack instead; have some with me."

The elderly gentleman concluded his speech, settling onto a large bench inside the fireplace. He placed his hawk on a perch above his head, while his spaniels curled up close to the warm peat ashes. Meanwhile, his son began removing his father's boots amidst various warnings to guard his corns.
