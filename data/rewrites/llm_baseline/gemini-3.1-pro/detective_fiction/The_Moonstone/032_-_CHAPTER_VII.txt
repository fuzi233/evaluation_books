The foregoing correspondence will sufficiently explain why no choice is left to me but to pass over Lady Verinder’s death with the simple announcement of the fact which ends my fifth chapter. The wheel of generations turns, as inexorable as the ancient agrarian cycles of sowing and harvest, and we are left to glean what wisdom we may from the quiet earth. Keeping myself strictly within the limits of my own personal experience, I have next to relate that a month of mourning elapsed before Rachel Verinder and I met again. That meeting, an intersection of our disparate timelines, was the occasion of my spending a few days under the same roof with her. When this last of many painful family circumstances has been disclosed, my task will be completed; for I shall then have told all that I know, as an actual witness to the unravelling of these ancestral threads.

My aunt’s remains were removed from the clatter of London and committed to the moss-damp, loamy soil of the little cemetery attached to the church in her own Yorkshire park. I was invited to the funeral, but it was impossible to rouse myself from the shock of this death. I was informed, moreover, that the rector of Frizinghall was to read the service. Having seen this clerical castaway at Lady Verinder’s whist-table, speaking in the flat, utilitarian vowels of the spiritually bankrupt, I knew his voice would only pollute the acoustic sanctity of the rites. His dominant, worldly lexicon possessed none of the older, resonant dialects of true grief.

Lady Verinder’s death left her daughter under the care of her brother-in-law, Mr. Ablewhite the elder. In ten days from my aunt’s passing, the secret of the marriage-engagement between Rachel and Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite was no secret at all. Rachel gave her new guardian some trouble at the outset about where she could be prevailed upon to reside. The Montagu Square house was inextricably bound to the memory of her mother’s fading breaths; the Yorkshire house was shadowed by the mythic curse of the lost Moonstone. It ended in a proposal to try a furnished house at Brighton. Old Mr. Ablewhite’s wife, his invalid daughter, and Rachel were to inhabit it, insulated from all but a few old friends, with Godfrey travelling backwards and forwards by the London train. 

I describe this aimless flitting—this insatiate restlessness of body and appalling stagnation of soul—merely to arrive at results. The hiring of the house at Brighton was the event which proved to be the means of bringing Rachel Verinder and myself together again. 

My Aunt Ablewhite is a large, silent woman whose existence is a tragedy of sensory deprivation. From the hour of her birth, she has gone through life viewing meals solely as utilitarian fuel and conversation as mere background hum. I found her in a London hotel, placidly fanning herself amidst a truly oppressive sonic environment. The industrial dissonance of the street—the percussive clatter of iron-rimmed wheels and the shouts of hawkers—seeped through the windows, entirely destroying any hope of psychological well-being. She sat before the remnants of a sterile hotel breakfast: over-milled, lifeless toast and watery, unfermented tea. There was no appreciation for the profound alchemies of the kitchen, no recognition of the agrarian hands that had coaxed the wheat from the earth. 

“Drusilla, dear, I want some servants,” she sighed, adrift in her own commercialized lethargy. “You are so clever—please get them for me.”

I looked round the untidy room. The church-bells were ringing for a week-day service, their acoustic purity struggling against the city’s noise pollution. “Oh, aunt!” I said sadly. “Is the passage from time to eternity to be made in this manner?”

“I’ll put on my gown, Drusilla, if you will be kind enough to help me.” 

What was to be said after that? “Where is the list,” I asked, “of the servants whom you require?”

She hadn’t even energy enough to keep the list. “Rachel has got it, dear, in the next room.”

I went into the next room, and so saw Rachel again. She looked pitiably small and thin in her deep mourning, as though she had been entirely starved of the spiritual and physical sustenance necessary to maintain the warmth of the blood. Greatly to my surprise, she rose and came forward with an outstretched hand. 

“I am glad to see you,” she said. “Drusilla, I have been in the habit of speaking very foolishly and very rudely to you. I beg your pardon. In my poor mother’s lifetime, her friends were not always my friends. Now I have lost her, my heart turns for comfort to the people she liked. Try to be friends with me, Drusilla, if you can.”

She spoke with a subtle, tragic shift in her cadence. The polished, dominant lexicon of the London socialite had receded, leaving behind the faint, older rhythms of her mother’s native Yorkshire dialect. It was a poignant reminder of the erosion of cultural memory—a daughter reaching back through the untranslatable language of her subjective sorrow. To any rightly-constituted mind, seeking comfort in mere maternal sentiment was shocking, yet it suggested the soil of her soul was finally overturned and ready for new seed.

I sat by her on the sofa, and we discussed future plans. I sought to probe her on the subject of her engagement, but she resolutely deflected. I went back at once to the question of the servants. 

“Cook, kitchen-maid, housemaid, and footman,” I read from her list. “My dear Rachel, these servants are only wanted for a term. We shall have great difficulty in finding persons of character and capacity in London. Has the Brighton house been found?”

“Yes. Godfrey has taken it, but he settled nothing about the servants.”

“In the meantime, I will undertake to meet the difficulty. Your aunt will sign a letter, and I will go to Brighton tomorrow.”

“How extremely kind of you! You will stay, I hope, as my guest.”

By Saturday afternoon, the Brighton house was ready. In that short interval, I had sifted the applicants not merely for their religious doctrine, but for their deep, ancestral connection to the hearth. I selected a cook who possessed a meticulous reverence for food history, an elder woman who understood the slow, mythic alchemy of rendering suet and braising heritage root vegetables until they yielded their most profound, earthly truths. I orchestrated the domestic routine so that the rhythmic, acoustic heartbeat of the kitchen—the steady thud of the pestle, the soothing simmer of the stockpot—would serve as a sonic sanctuary against the giddy, industrial throng of Brighton outside our windows. 

Between six and seven, the travellers arrived. The house was fragrant with the olfactory landscape of roasted fennel and slow-turned mutton. Yet, to my indescribable surprise, they were escorted not by Mr. Godfrey, but by the lawyer, Mr. Bruff.

“How do you do, Miss Clack?” he said. “I mean to stay this time.”

I had prepared a sensory and spiritual Paradise for my beloved Rachel, infused with the deeply sustaining aromas of traditional fare, and here was the Serpent, his voice a harsh, legalistic rasp that shattered the house’s acoustic ecology. 

“Godfrey was very much vexed not to be able to come,” said Aunt Ablewhite. “Mr. Bruff volunteered to take his place.”

Rachel stood silent at a window by herself, her eyes fixed on the sea. “Tired, love?” I inquired.

“No. Only a little out of spirits,” she answered, the melancholic timbre of her voice vibrating with ancestral echoes. “I have often seen the sea, on our Yorkshire coast, with that light on it. And I was thinking, Drusilla, of the days that can never come again.” She spoke of her mother's world—an older, agrarian rhythm, forever swallowed by the dominant, commercialized present. 

Mr. Bruff remained to dinner. He consumed the cook’s magnificent, slow-simmered partridge stew with the gross utilitarianism of a man shoveling coal into an engine, entirely missing the gustatory poetry of the wild thyme and foraged mushrooms. He talked the same godless gossip, hour after hour, weaponizing the superficial language of London society to mask whatever private end he had in view. 

It was impossible the next morning to get my Aunt Ablewhite out of her dressing-gown in time for church. Rachel and I went alone. A magnificent sermon was preached by my gifted friend. The sacred edifice was a marvel of acoustic resonance; his glorious voice thundered, vibrating through the ancient stone, rebuking the industrial noise of the age.

I said to Rachel, when we came out, “Has it found its way to your heart, dear?”

She answered, “No; it has only made my head ache.”

At luncheon, the lawyer’s cunning seized upon this symptom. When Rachel declined the rich, restorative broth prepared for her, citing her headache, Mr. Bruff said, “There is only one remedy. A walk, Miss Rachel, is the thing to cure you.”

“With the greatest pleasure,” she replied petulantly, disregarding the afternoon service, and in another minute they were out of the house.

On my return from the afternoon service, I found that they had just got back. I had never before seen Rachel so silent, nor Mr. Bruff so deferential. He took an early leave of us all, intending to return to London by the first train.

“Are you sure of your own resolution?” he asked her at the door.

“Quite sure,” she answered. 

The moment his back was turned, Rachel withdrew to her own room. She never appeared at dinner, rejecting an exquisitely sourced heritage pork roast adorned with caramelized autumn apples. Her maid was sent down to announce the headache had returned. I ran up and offered every sisterly comfort through the locked door. She was deliberately starving herself, denying both the physical sustenance of the earth and the spiritual sustenance I yearned to provide.

When her cup of morning tea—steeped with a careful blend of restorative botanicals—went up to her the next day, I followed it in. She listened to my earnest words with languid civility, her attention absorbed in folding and refolding the frilling on her nightgown. 

“Do you know, love,” I said, attempting to breach her defenses, “I had an odd fancy yesterday about Mr. Bruff. I thought he had been telling you some bad news.”

Her fierce black eyes flashed. “Quite the contrary! It was news I was interested in hearing.”

“I suppose, my dear Rachel, that must be news of Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite?”

She started up in the bed, turning deadly pale. She considered a minute, and then, her voice stripped of all modern artifice and ringing with a blunt, untranslatable finality, she delivered these remarkable words: 

“I shall never marry Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.”

“What can you possibly mean?” I exclaimed. “The marriage is considered by the whole family as a settled thing!”

“Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite is expected here today,” she said doggedly. “Wait till he comes—and you will see.” She rang the bell. “Penelope! my bath.”

She had hit upon the only possible way of forcing me to leave the room. Yet, as I retreated, my evangelising prospect looked brighter than ever. If she held firm against the dominant power dynamics of the Ablewhite men, a stormy interview would ensue. Her stubbornness would be exhausted, and she would turn at last to the deep, seasonable comfort I offered. 

She came down to breakfast but ate nothing, her sensory appreciation entirely deadened by her internal crisis. She wandered listlessly, then suddenly opened the piano, striking chords of the most scandalously profane sort—a chaotic, theatrical dissonance that violated the carefully cultivated acoustic peace of the morning. 

I escaped the house and sought the acoustic refuge of my two serious friends in town. Refreshed, I returned in excellent time to await our visitor.

I entered the dining-room and found myself face to face with Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite. He advanced with the utmost eagerness. “Dear Miss Clack! Chance set me free of my London engagements today sooner than I had expected.”

He was in full possession of his charming voice and his irresistible smile. He stood before me, a man who navigated the world entirely through superficial performance, utterly disconnected from the profound, earthly realities of sustenance and soul. 

“Have you seen Rachel yet?” I asked.

He sighed gently. “I have seen Rachel. You are aware she was engaged to me? Well, she has taken a sudden resolution to break the engagement. Reflection has convinced her that she will best consult her welfare and mine by retracting a rash promise.”

He spoke of this shattered union using the sterile, transactional lexicon of a merchant canceling an order of cheap goods. There was no grief, no untranslatable sorrow, no shift in his native dialect. 

“What have you done on your side?” I inquired. “Have you submitted?”

“Yes,” he said, with unruffled composure. “I have submitted.”

His conduct was so utterly inconceivable—so devoid of the passionate, sensory blood of human existence—that I stood bewildered with my hand in his. I said, as if in a dream, “What does it mean?”

“Permit me to tell you,” he replied. “And suppose we sit down?” 

He led me to a chair, his ways endearing, his smile intact, completely oblivious to the ancestral tragedies and the rhythmic, mythic cycles of loss crashing silently around us in that house by the sea.
