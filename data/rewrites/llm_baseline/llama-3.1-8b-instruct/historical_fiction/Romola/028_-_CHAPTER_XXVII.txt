The memory of his departure lingered, and she couldn't help but feel a pang of longing for his return. The sun-drenched streets outside were alive with the festive atmosphere of the French king's visit, but Romola's window remained shuttered, a rare sight in the normally vibrant city. It was a stark reminder of the isolation that had settled over her since her father's sudden passing, just three months prior.

In the days leading up to his death, Bardo would often ask, "Is Tito coming to write?" – a question that betrayed his deep-seated anxiety about the state of the library's texts. Romola would reassure him, "He's attending a supper at the cardinal's, Father. He's in high demand." Though the cardinal had promised to send word about the library's fate, those words of hope were lost on Bardo, who grew increasingly despondent.

One evening, as the clock struck its familiar hour, Bardo's eyes locked onto Romola with a burning intensity. "Get the pen, child," he commanded, his voice laced with determination. "Tito may not have brought new texts, but I must express my thoughts on the New Platonists. I fear I shall soon depart this world, leaving my work unfinished." Romola hastened to comply, holding the quill poised in her hand. Yet, as the minutes ticked by, the room fell silent, and Bardo's stillness became oppressive. Romola, accustomed to the ebbs and flows of their dictations, initially overlooked the sudden quiet, but when she glanced up, she found her father motionless, his eyes fixed on some distant point.

"I'm ready, Father," she called out, her voice barely above a whisper. Still, the silence persisted, and Romola's heart began to ache with a mix of sorrow and regret. In the days that followed, she couldn't shake the feeling that her life with Tito had been tinged with an imperceptible bitterness. Their marriage, once a radiant tapestry woven from threads of joy and devotion, had become a delicate balance of compromise and sacrifice.
