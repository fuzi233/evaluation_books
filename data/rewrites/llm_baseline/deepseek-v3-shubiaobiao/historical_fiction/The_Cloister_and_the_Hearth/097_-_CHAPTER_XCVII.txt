A priest is never more a priest than when standing at the threshold between this life and the next. Gerard performed the last rites for Margaret with the same solemn care he would have given the humblest soul in his parish, though his hands moved as if guided by some force beyond his own will. The shock of her passing left him numb, the full weight of grief not yet settled upon him. When at last it struck, it came like a storm—first a surge of fervent gratitude for her peaceful, faithful end, then a desperate longing to carry her away from this unfamiliar place, to lay her to rest in the earth of Gouda, where she belonged.

That very evening, a covered cart rolled slowly toward the village, its single horse plodding beneath a sky heavy with unshed tears. Inside lay a coffin, and beside it, Gerard, his arms wrapped around the cold lead as if he could will warmth back into it. The cart’s driver, a silent man with rough hands and a gentle heart, walked beside the horse, stealing glances at the broken figure within but daring not to speak. When night fell and they stopped at an inn, the driver and the landlord tried to coax Gerard away—to eat, to rest, to release his grip on death for even a moment. But he snarled at them, half-wild with sorrow, and clung tighter to the coffin until they relented, draping a cloak over his trembling shoulders and leaving him to his vigil.

At noon the next day, the cart creaked to a halt before the manse, where a cluster of parishioners had gathered, each nursing some small complaint or worry, missing their shepherd already. When they saw the coffin, and their priest hollow-eyed and shattered, a great cry went up among them—not just for Margaret, but for him. The sound roused Gerard from his daze, and he looked upon them with red-rimmed eyes. *"Oh, my children,"* he tried to say, but the words dissolved into a choked silence.

The following day, against all counsel, he buried her himself. His voice, though frayed at the edges, did not falter as he read the service, and the tears of the villagers fell like rain upon the fresh-turned earth. When the last prayer was spoken, he remained standing over the grave, motionless, until the mourners drifted away, leaving him alone with his grief.

He might have stood there till dusk, lost in memory, had the sexton not begun to shovel dirt onto the coffin without warning. The first thud of earth against wood tore a scream from Gerard’s throat—raw, inhuman. The sexton froze. Gerard swayed, clutching at his chest as if something inside had torn loose. The man rushed to steady him, calling for help, and Jorian Ketel came running, his own face streaked with tears. Together, they half-carried Gerard back to the manse.

*"Ah, Jorian,"* he gasped, *"something broke in me—I felt it snap. Here, Jorian. Here."* His hand pressed to his breast, where the pain burned brightest.
