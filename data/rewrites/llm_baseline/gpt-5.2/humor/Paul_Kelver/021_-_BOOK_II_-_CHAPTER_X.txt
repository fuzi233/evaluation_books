Slowly, surely, steadily, I climbed.

Not in the romantic way a hero climbs—moonlight, violins, and a destiny that smells faintly of roses—but in the practical way a man climbs when he has debts that sit on his chest at night and an inconvenient conscience that will not be bribed with fine intentions. I put aside dreams as one puts aside a dish too rich for the stomach and kept my mind on business: plain, nourishing labour, with money at the end of it.

Often my other self—little Paul with the sad eyes—would sidle up to my elbow and whisper of grander things: of literature that would live, of dramas that would “matter,” of a world reformed by a paragraph. He was persuasive, that sentimental imp; and had it not been for two hungers that grew in me day by day until they were almost physical, he might have drawn me off into some genteel ruin.

One hunger was for honesty: I wanted to buy back my self-respect, item by item, like a poor man redeeming his Sunday coat from the pawnbroker’s. The other hunger had a hand in it—Norah’s strong white hand, the feel of it firm in mine, as if it could steady the whole of me with a pressure. I was starving for that again. Hunger is a practical counsellor. It does not urge you toward minor poetry.

Now and again, when my humour made holiday and there was nothing to do but wait for its return, I wrote stories for my own pleasure. They made no mark. Purposeful work has a slower ferment; it rises in its own time. Had I persisted—yes, perhaps. But there was money to be earned, and when one is paying off the past, one does not often have the luxury of cultivating the future. By the time the last debt was paid, I had, without quite noticing it, established a reputation.

“Madness!” said my practical friends, with that comfortable indignation people feel when another man proposes to be uncomfortable. “You’re throwing away a certain fortune for, at the best, a doubtful competence. The one you can do. The other—why, you’d begin all over again.”

“You’d find it almost impossible now,” explained those who meant well and spoke out of experience, which is wisdom seasoned with discouragement. “The world would never listen. Once a humourist, always a humourist. As well might a comic actor insist on playing Hamlet. It might be the best Hamlet ever spoken; the audience would only laugh—or stop away.”

Drawn by our mutual need of sympathy, “Goggles” and I would seek some quiet corner in the Club and pour out our souls to one another like men emptying decanters nobody else is likely to touch. He would lay before me, at length, his conception of Romeo—an excellent conception, no doubt; though, I confess, I could no more picture him to myself as Romeo than I could imagine a walrus playing the flute. But I listened with every sign of encouragement. It was the fee I paid him for listening, in turn, while I unfolded my ideas for monumental literature, helpful to mankind, imagined and built up like a cathedral.

“Perhaps in a future existence,” laughed Goggles one evening, rising as the clock struck seven, “I shall be a great tragedian and you a famous poet. Meanwhile, as your friend Brian puts it, we are both sinning our mercies. After all, to live is the most important thing in life.”

I strolled with him to the cloak-room and helped him into his coat.

“Take my advice,” he said, tapping me on the chest and fixing his funny fishy eyes on me. Had I not known he meant to be serious I should have laughed, he looked so solemnly absurd. “Marry some dear little woman. One never understands life properly till the babies come to explain it.”

He was married himself to a placid lady of about twice his own weight, which perhaps gave his counsel the air of a thing tested in the furnace.

I went back to my easy-chair before the fire. Wife, children, home—was not that the true work of man? Not the dreamer’s work, but the live man’s. I saw them about me: the fair sweet woman, helper and comrade, comforter by the hearth; the little faces fashioned in our image, questioning voices teaching us the answers to riddles we had been too proud to ask. All other hopes, ambitions, dreams—what were they? Phantoms of morning mist fading in sunlight.

It was in this state—dangerous, because it resembles wisdom—that Hodgson came to me one evening.

“I want you to write me a comic opera,” he said.

He held an open letter in his hand and read it as he spoke, as if to show that even while offering a man the chance of his life he could still attend to the post.

“The public seem to be getting tired of these eternal translations from the French. I want something English, something new and original.”

“The English is easy enough,” I replied. “But I shouldn’t clamour for anything new and original if I were you.”

“Why not?” He glanced up.

“You might get it,” I said. “Then you’d be disappointed.”

He laughed. “You know what I mean—something we can refer to as ‘new and original’ on the programme. What do you say? It’ll be a big chance for you, and I’m willing to risk it. I’m sure you can do it. People are beginning to talk about you.”

I had written a few farces and comediettas, and they had gone well. But the chief piece of an evening is a serious responsibility, even when it insists on being funny. A young man may be excused for hesitating; it can make him—and it can do the other thing, which is more final.

I explained my fears. I did not explain that in my desk lay a four-act drama, throbbing with humanity and life, with which I had hoped—each day more faintly—to take the theatre by storm and establish myself as something other than a respectable jester.

“It’s very simple,” urged Hodgson. “Provide Atherton plenty of comic business—you ought to manage that. Give Gleeson something pretty in waltz time, and Duncan a part in which she can change her frock every quarter of an hour, and the thing is done.”

“I’ll tell you what,” he continued; “I’ll take the whole crowd down to Richmond on Sunday. We’ll have a coach, leave the theatre at half-past ten. You can study them—talk with them—learn what they can do. Atherton has ideas in his head; he’ll explain them. Then next week we’ll draw up a contract and set to work.”

It was too good an opportunity to let slip, though I knew that if it succeeded I should be nailed more firmly than ever to my painted post. Still, comic opera is remunerative. It feeds you very well, if it does not always feed your soul.

A small crowd had gathered in the Strand to see us start.

“Nothing wrong, is there?” enquired the leading lady in a tone of anxiety, stepping from her cab a quarter of an hour late. “It isn’t a fire, is it?”

“Merely assembled to see you,” explained Hodgson, without raising his eyes from his letters.

“Oh, good gracious! do let us get away quickly.”

“Box seat, my dear,” returned Hodgson.

The leading lady, accepting the proffered assistance of myself and three other gentlemen, mounted the ladder with charming hesitation, as if ascent were a virtue and not a necessity. Delay was caused by our low comedian, who twice, making believe to miss his footing, slid down into the arms of the stolid door-keeper. The crowd—mostly small boys, connoisseurs of practical mishap—laughed and applauded. Encouraged, he made a third attempt upon hands and knees, gained the roof, and sat down upon the tenor, who smiled with the patient stiffness of a man who has been sat on before and expects to be sat on again.

The first dozen omnibuses we passed the low comedian greeted by rising and bowing profoundly, afterwards falling back upon either the tenor or myself. Except by the tenor and myself his performance appeared highly appreciated. Once Charing Cross was behind us and nobody seemed interested in our progress, he settled down, much to our relief.

“People sometimes ask me,” he said, brushing dust from his knees, “why I do this sort of thing off the stage. It amuses me.”

“I was coming up to London the other day from Birmingham. At Willesden, when the ticket collector opened the door, I sprang out of the carriage and ran down the platform. Of course, he ran after me, shouting to stop me. I dodged them about a minute. You wouldn’t believe the excitement. Fifty people left their seats to see what it was. When they caught me I explained I’d been travelling second with a first-class ticket—which was the fact. People think I do it for attention. I do it for my own pleasure.”

“It must be a troublesome way of amusing oneself,” I suggested.

“Exactly what my wife says. She can never understand the desire that comes over us all, I suppose, to play the fool. As a rule, when she’s with me I don’t do it.”

“She’s not here to-day?” I asked, glancing round.

“She suffers so from headaches,” he answered. “She hardly ever goes anywhere.”

“I’m sorry,” I said, and meant it. One pictures a headache as a small thing until one lives with it.

During the drive his irrepressible desire to amuse himself got the better of him more than once. Through Kensington he balanced the horn upon his nose, as if modern civilization required, above all else, a man who could turn brass into a personal insult. At Kew he stopped the coach to request change for sixpence from a young ladies’ boarding school. At the foot of Richmond Hill he drew a crowd while trying to persuade a deaf old gentleman in a Bath-chair to let his man race us up the hill for a shilling.

At these antics our party laughed uproariously, with the exception of Hodgson, who attended to his correspondence, and an elegant young lady of some social standing who had lately emerged from the Divorce Court with a reputation worth to her, in cash, a hundred pounds a week. She watched Atherton as one watches a street organ: with the faint fear it might begin under one’s window and never stop.

We arrived at the hotel a little before lunch and strolled into the garden. The low comedian, observing an elderly gentleman of dignified appearance sipping Vermouth at a small table, stood for a moment rooted with astonishment; then, making a bee-line for the stranger, seized and shook him warmly by the hand. We exchanged admiring glances.

“Charlie is in good form to-day,” we told one another and followed at his heels like attendants at some eccentric ritual.

The elderly gentleman rose, puzzled.

“And how’s Aunt Martha?” cried Atherton. “Dear old Aunt Martha! Well, I am glad! You do look bonny! How is she?”

“I’m afraid—” began the gentleman.

Atherton started back, as if struck by tragedy. Other visitors gathered round, drawn by that delicious mixture of other people’s emotions and one’s own safety.

“Don’t tell me anything has happened to her! Not dead? Don’t tell me that!”

He seized the bewildered gentleman by the shoulders, presenting a face distorted by terror.

“I really have not the faintest notion what you are talking about,” returned the gentleman, annoyed. “I don’t know you.”

“Not know me? Do you mean you’ve forgotten—? Isn’t your name Steggles?”

“No, it isn’t,” snapped the stranger.

“My mistake,” said Atherton coolly. He tossed off at one gulp what remained of the stranger’s Vermouth and walked away rapidly, leaving dignity to recover itself without its drink.

The elderly gentleman, not seeing the humour, was soothed by one of our party, who explained that it was Atherton—Charlie Atherton.

“Oh, is it,” growled the gentleman. “Then will you tell him from me that when I want his damned tomfoolery I’ll come to the theatre and pay for it.”

“What a disagreeable man,” we said, as we went indoors; for there is nothing people resent so much as refusing to be a public.

During lunch Atherton continued in excellent spirits: he kissed the bald back of the waiter’s head, pretending to mistake it for a face; called for hot mustard and water; made believe to steal the silver; and when the finger-bowls arrived took off his coat and requested the ladies to look the other way.

After lunch he became suddenly serious and, slipping his arm through mine, led me by unfrequented paths.

“Now, about this new opera,” he said. “We don’t want any of the old stale business. Give us something new.”

I suggested it might be difficult.

“Not at all,” he replied. “My idea is this: I’m a young fellow, and I’m in love with a girl.”

I promised to make a note of it.

“Her father—apoplectic old idiot—make him comic: ‘Damme, sir! By gad!’ all that sort of thing.”

By persuading him that I understood, I rose in his estimation.

“He won’t have anything to say to me—thinks I’m an ass. I’m a simple sort of fellow—on the outside. But I’m not such a fool as I look.”

“You don’t think we are getting too much out of the groove?” I enquired.

“The more so the better,” said he.

“Very well. Second act: I disguise myself. I come on as an organ-grinder, sing a song in broken English; then as a policeman; or a young swell about town. Give me plenty of opportunity, that’s the great thing—opportunity to be really funny, I mean. We don’t want any of the old stale tricks.”

I promised him my support.

“Put a little pathos in it,” he added. “Give me a scene where I can show them I’ve something else in me besides humour. We don’t want to make them howl—just feel a little. Let them go out saying, ‘Well, Charlie’s often made me laugh, but I’m damned if I knew he could make me cry before!’ See?”

I told him I thought I did.

The leading lady met us on our return and requested, with a pretty tone of authority, that everybody else should go away and leave us. There were cries of “Naughty!” The leading lady, laughing girlishly, took me by the hand and ran away with me.

“I want to talk to you,” she said, when we had reached a secluded seat overlooking the river, “about my part in the new opera. Now, can’t you give me something original? Do.”

Her pleading was so pretty there was nothing for it but to pledge compliance.

“I am so tired of being the simple village maiden,” she said. “What I want is a part with some opportunity—a coquettish part. I can flirt,” she assured me, archly. “Try me.”

I satisfied her of my perfect faith.

“You might,” she continued, “see your way to making the plot depend upon me. It always seems to me the woman’s part is never made enough of in comic opera. A comic opera built round a woman would be a really great success. Don’t you agree with me, Mr. Kelver?” she pouted, laying her pretty hand on mine. “We are much more interesting than the men—now, aren’t we?”

Personally, as I told her, I agreed.

The tenor, sipping tea with me on the balcony later, beckoned me aside.

“About this new opera,” said he, “doesn’t it seem to you the time has come to make more of the story—that the public might prefer a little more human interest and a little less clowning?”

I admitted that a plot was essential.

“It seems to me,” said the tenor, “that if you could write an opera round an interesting love story, you would score a success. Of course, let there be plenty of humour, but reduce it to its proper place. As a support it is excellent; when it is made the entire structure it is apt to be tiresome—that is my view.”

I replied, with sincerity, that there was truth in it.

“Of course, so far as I am personally concerned,” he went on, “it is immaterial. I draw the same salary whether I’m on the stage five minutes or an hour. But when you have a man of my position in the cast, and give him next to nothing to do—well, the public are disappointed.”

“Most naturally,” I commented.

“The lover,” he whispered, noticing Atherton’s careless approach, “that’s the character they are thinking about all the time—men and women both. Human nature. Make your lover interesting—that’s the secret.”

While we waited for the horses, I found myself standing a little apart with a tall, thin, rather oldish man. He spoke in low, hurried tones, fearful of being overheard—as if ideas were contraband.

“You’ll forgive me, Mr. Kelver,” he said. “Trevor—Marmaduke Trevor. I play the Duke of Bayswater in the second act.”

I could not recall him at once; the second act contained a small army of nobility. But his sensitive face made me shrink from wounding him.

“A capital performance,” I lied. “It has always amused me.”

He flushed with pleasure. “I made a great success some years ago in America with a soda-water syphon,” he said, “and it occurred to me that if you could, in a natural sort of way, drop in a small part leading up to a little business with a soda-water syphon, it might help the piece.”

I wrote him his soda-water scene, and insisted upon it in spite of opposition. Some critics afterwards found fault with the incident as lacking originality; critics are often disappointed when reality intrudes on art. But Marmaduke Trevor was right: it did help a little.

Our return journey was an exaggerated repetition of our morning drive. Atherton produced hideous noises from the horn—noises that seemed devised to frighten birds out of their own memories—and entered into contests of running wit with omnibus drivers, which was a mistake, the score generally remaining with the driver. At Hammersmith, traffic blocking us, he became Cheap Jack, stood on the back seat, and offered all our hats for sale at temptingly low prices.

“Got any ideas out of them?” asked Hodgson, when we said good-night.

“I’m thinking,” I answered, “of going down into the country and writing the piece quietly, away from everybody.”

“Perhaps you are right,” he agreed. “Too many cooks. Be sure and have it ready for the autumn.”

I wrote it with some pleasure to myself amid the Yorkshire Wolds, where the air tastes clean and the nights are quiet enough to hear one’s own doubts moving about. There, away from London’s roar—away from the clatter that makes all thoughts sound alike—I could hear the cadence of my own sentences, whether they rang true or came down false. When I turned the last page at the reading to the company before the close of the season, there followed a dead silence.

The leading lady spoke first.

She asked if the clock on the mantelpiece could be relied upon; because, if so, by leaving at once she could just catch her train. Hodgson, consulting his watch, thought, if anything, it was a little fast. She said she hoped it was, and went.

The only comforting words came from the tenor. He recalled a successful comic opera produced some years before at the Philharmonic; he distinctly remembered that up to five minutes before the curtain rose everybody had regarded it as rubbish. He also had a train to catch.

Marmaduke Trevor, with a covert shake of the hand, urged me not to despair. Atherton, last to go, told Hodgson he thought he might be able to do something with parts of it if given a free hand.

Hodgson and I, left alone, looked at each other.

“It’s no good,” said Hodgson, “from a box-office point of view. Very clever.”

“How do you know it is no good from a box-office point of view?” I ventured.

“I never made a mistake in my life,” said Hodgson.

“You have produced one or two failures,” I reminded him.

“And shall again,” he laughed. “The right thing isn’t easy to get.”

“Cheer up,” he added kindly. “This is only your first attempt. We must try and knock it into shape at rehearsal.”

Their notion of “knocking it into shape” was knocking it to pieces.

“I’ll tell you what we’ll do,” Atherton would say. “We’ll cut that scene out altogether.” And joyously he would draw his pencil through four or five pages of my manuscript, as if slaughter were construction.

“But it is essential to the story,” I would argue.

“Not at all.”

“But it is. It is the scene in which Roderick escapes from prison and falls in love with the gipsy.”

“My dear boy, half-a-dozen words will do all that. I meet Roderick at the ball. ‘Hallo, what are you doing here?’ ‘Oh, I have escaped from prison.’ ‘Good business. And how’s Miriam?’ ‘Well and happy—she is going to be my wife!’ What more do you want?”

“I have been speaking to Mr. Hodgson,” the leading lady would observe, “and he agrees with me that if, instead of falling in love with Peter, I fell in love with John—”

“But John is in love with Arabella.”

“Oh, we’ve cut out Arabella. I can sing all her songs.”

The tenor would lead me into a corner.

“I want you to write in a little scene for myself and Miss Duncan at the beginning of the first act. I’ll talk to her about it. I think it will be rather pretty. I want her—the second time I see her—to have come out of her room on to a balcony and to be standing there bathed in moonlight.”

“But the first act takes place in the early morning.”

“I’ve thought of that. We must alter it to the evening.”

“But the opera opens with a hunting scene. People don’t go hunting by moonlight.”

“It will be a novelty. That’s what’s wanted. The ordinary hunting scene! My dear boy, it has been done to death.”

I stood this sort of thing for a week. “They are people of experience,” I argued to myself; “they must know more about it than I do.” By the end of the week I had arrived at the conclusion that anyhow they didn’t. Added to which I lost my temper—something I advise any lady or gentleman thinking of entering dramatic authorship to lose as soon as possible, because it will be taken from them later without compensation.

I took both manuscripts and entered Hodgson’s private room, closing the door behind me. One parcel was the opera as I had originally written it: neat, intelligible, whatever its merits. The second—scored, interlined, altered, cut, interleaved, rewritten, reversed, turned inside out and topsy-turvy—was the opera as, everybody helping, we had “knocked it into shape”: one long hopeless confusion.

“That’s your opera,” I said, pushing the bulkier bundle across. “If you can understand it, if you can make head or tail of it, if you care to produce it, it is yours and you are welcome to it. This is mine.” I laid the neat parcel beside it. “It may be good; it may be bad. If it is played at all, it is played as it is written. Regard the contract as cancelled, and make up your mind.”

He argued—he argued with force and he argued with eloquence. He appealed to my self-interest; he appealed to my better nature. It occupied him forty minutes by the clock. Then he called me an obstinate young fool; flung the “knocked into shape” opera into the waste-paper basket—which was the only proper place for it—and, striding into the middle of the company, gave curt directions that the damned opera was to be played as it was written, and be damned to it.

The company shrugged its shoulders, and for the next month kept them shrugged. For a while Hodgson stayed away from rehearsals; then, returning, developed by degrees a melancholy interest in the somewhat gloomy proceedings.

So far I had won. My difficulty was to maintain the victory.

Atherton, reciting his lines with meaningless monotony, would pause occasionally to ask me politely whether this or that passage was intended to be serious or funny.

“You think,” the leading lady would enquire, more in sorrow than in anger, “that any girl would behave in this way—any real girl, I mean?”

“Perhaps the audience will understand it,” the tenor would console himself. “Myself, I confess I don’t.”

With a sinking heart concealed beneath an aggressively disagreeable manner, I remained firm in my “pigheaded conceit,” as it was regarded, Hodgson generously supporting me against his own judgment.

“It’s bound to be a failure,” he told me. “I am spending some twelve to fifteen hundred pounds to teach you a lesson. When you have learnt it, we’ll square accounts by your writing me an opera that will pay.”

“And if it does succeed?” I suggested.

“My dear boy,” replied Hodgson, “I never make mistakes.”
