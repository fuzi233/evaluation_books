Spring had edged forward, and, as if the season had remembered some old caprice, it turned suddenly warm. The air lay on the town like a newly-heated cloth laid over bread: close, sweet, and a little enervating. With the change came, for me, that loosening of strength which heat can bring—one restless night making a whole day thin and colourless; the smallest effort drawing the sap out of me as if I had been a plant set too near a window.

On a Sunday afternoon I walked the half-league to the Protestant church. The streets of Villette were quieter than on working days, yet not silent; from open shutters came the clink of crockery, the brisk slap of a carpet shaken, a child’s call flung down a stairwell, and—always—some thin, metallic hum from the city’s industrious heart, as if even devotion could not buy an hour’s true hush. When I returned I was spent; and I fled, as I often fled, to my solitary sanctuary, the first classe. There I sat down with relief, and made of my desk a pillow for my arms and head, thinking only: rest, rest—let me be as still as flour lying in its bin.

A little while I listened to the bees’ low labouring music in the berceau, that long green passage where the sound of wings and leaf is like a domestic hymn. I watched through the glass door and the tender, lightly-strewn foliage of spring: Madame Beck, and a gay party of friends whom she had entertained at dinner after morning mass, were walking in the centre-alley under orchard boughs all in blossom. The bloom had that pure, warm tint which makes one think of snow at sunrise—pink in its whiteness, and white in its pink, as if the tree had chosen to flower in light.

Among that little company my eye, I remember, fastened on one figure: a handsome young girl I had seen before at Madame Beck’s, Mademoiselle Sauveur by name. I had been told—vaguely, as one is told things in a house where many people listen—that she was a filleule, a god-daughter, of M. Emanuel’s; and that between her mother, or her aunt, or some near female kin, and the Professor, there had existed of old a special friendship. She was always richly dressed—wealth, they said, sat behind her like a servant—and to-day her bright silk caught and threw back every tremble of sun and leaf-shade. M. Paul was not of the holiday band, but I had seen her with him before; and, as far as distant observation could judge, she enjoyed him with the frank ease of a ward with an indulgent guardian. I had seen her run up, put her arm through his, hang upon him; and once—only once, but it had struck keenly—there shot through me a curious sensation: an anticipatory discomfort, one of those presentiments that taste like cold iron on the tongue. I refused to dwell on it, as one refuses to put a strange thing into the mouth again.

While I followed the gleam of her robe through blossoms and young emerald leaves, my eyes grew dazzled; they closed. The warmth, the hum of bees and birds, the dim schoolroom odour of chalk and worn wood warmed by sun—everything lulled me. At last, I slept.

Two hours stole over me. When I woke, the sun had slipped behind the high houses; the garden and the room were grey; the bees were gone homeward; the flowers, as if they had folded their napkins, were closing. The party of guests had vanished too; each alley was void, as after a meal when chairs are pushed back and the last glass has been carried away.

I woke not chilled, as I should have been after sitting so still, but strangely at ease. My cheek and arms were not benumbed by the hard desk. No wonder. Instead of bare wood, a thick shawl had been carefully folded beneath my head and arms; another shawl—both taken from the corridor where such comforts hung for any who chose to claim them—was wrapped warmly round me. Someone had come in; someone had seen my dropped state; someone had moved with a step so quiet, a hand so gentle, that even my day-sleep had not been disturbed.

Who had done this? Which teacher? which pupil? None, except St. Pierre, bore me ill-will; yet which of the rest had the habit—the very instinct—of such tender usefulness? As to Ginevra Fanshawe, bright creature, she was not gentle; had she meddled, she would have tugged me up with a laugh, making a jest of my weariness. At last I said: it is Madame Beck’s doing; she has entered, seen me asleep, and thought I might take cold. She would not have her useful machine damaged. She valued me as one values a good oven that bakes evenly: not beloved, perhaps, but not to be allowed to crack.

“And now,” I thought, “I will take a walk. The evening is fresh.”

I opened the glass door and stepped into the berceau. At my own alley I paused. Had it been dark—had it been true dusk—I should scarcely have ventured there; for I had not forgotten the curious illusion of vision (if illusion it were) experienced in that place some months ago. But a ray of declining sun still burnished the grey crown of Jean Baptiste; the birds were not all yet hidden in tufted shrubs and thick wall-ivy. The garden had that late-day sound which is almost as much silence as sound: a far carriage-wheel softened by distance; a leaf turning over; a sparrow scolding once, then quiet.

I paced up and down, and my mind slipped into its old furrow—the same thoughts I had pondered that night when I buried my glass jar: how to make an advance in life, how to take one step more towards independence. The plan had never died; it only lay under other days, like yeast under a cloth, waiting warmth.

“Living costs little,” I said to myself, “in this economical town of Villette, where people are more sensible than I understand they are in dear old England—less worried about appearance, less fevered with display; where no one is ashamed to be homely and saving. House-rent, chosen prudently, need not be high. When I have saved one thousand francs, I will take a tenement: one large room, two or three smaller. I will furnish the first with benches and desks, a black tableau, an estrade for myself; a chair and table; a sponge; white chalks. I will begin with day-pupils, and work my way upwards. Madame Beck began no higher—she has said so often enough—and where is she now? These premises, this garden, bought with her money; a competency secured for old age; an establishment to make careers for her children.”

The thought went on, and with it came a sterner taste—like the bitter edge of burnt sugar.

“Courage, Lucy Snowe. With self-denial and economy now, and steady exertion by-and-by, an object in life need not fail you. Do not complain that such an object is selfish, limited, without interest. Labour for independence until you have proved, by winning that prize, your right to look higher. But afterwards—afterwards—is there nothing more? No true home? Nothing dearer than myself, that might draw from me better things than I care to culture for myself alone? Nothing at whose feet I could lay down the whole burden of human egotism, and take up, gladly, the nobler charge of living for others?”

It was as if I had set a table for one, then looked up and found the place opposite laid for no one.

“I suppose,” I thought, “the orb of my life is not to be so rounded: the crescent-phase must suffice.” Very good. I saw a huge mass of my fellow-creatures in no better circumstance; I saw that many men, and more women, hold their span on conditions of denial and privation. I found no reason to be among the few favoured. Yet I believed in some blending of hope and sunshine sweetening the worst lots. I believed this life is not all; neither the beginning nor the end. I believed while I trembled; I trusted while I wept.

So that account was closed. It is right, now and then, to look one’s life-ledger bravely in the face and settle it honestly; and he is a poor self-swindler who, while he reckons, sets down under “happiness” what is misery. Call anguish—anguish; despair—despair; write both in strong characters with a resolute pen. Falsify—put “privilege” where you should write “pain”—and see if your mighty creditor accepts the coin. Offer the strongest—if the darkest angel of God’s host—water when he has asked blood: will he take it? Not a whole pale sea for one red drop.

I paused before Methusaleh, the giant patriarch of the garden. Leaning my brow against his knotty trunk, I let my foot rest on the stone sealing the small sepulchre at his root. There, once, I had buried a passage of feeling; and now memory, like a hand reaching down into earth, touched it again. Dr. John rose up as he always rose—warm affection; faith in his excellence; delight in his grace. What had become of that curious, one-sided friendship: half marble, half life; truth on one side, perhaps a jest on the other?

Was it dead? I could not tell; but it was buried. Sometimes I thought the tomb unquiet, and dreamed of disturbed earth, of hair still golden and living, thrust through coffin-chinks. Had I been too hasty? The question came with cruel sharpness after some brief chance interview with him. He had still kind looks, a warm hand; his voice still held that pleasant tone for my name. I never liked “Lucy” so well as when he uttered it.

But time taught me what my heart would not: this benignity, this cordiality, this music, belonged in no shape to me. It was part of himself; honey of temper; balm of mellow mood. He gave it as ripe fruit gives sweetness to the rifling bee; he diffused it as sweet plants shed perfume. Does the nectarine love the bee or bird it feeds? Is sweetbriar enamoured of the air?

“Good-night, Dr. John; you are good, you are beautiful; but you are not mine. Good-night, and God bless you!”

The words left my lips in sound; I heard them spoken. Then, close—very close—I heard an echo.

“Good-night, Mademoiselle; or rather, good-evening—the sun is scarce set. I hope you slept well?”

I started, but was only discomposed a moment; I knew the voice, and the speaker.

“Slept, Monsieur! When? where?”

“You may well inquire when—where. It seems you turn day into night, and choose a desk for a pillow; rather hard lodging—?”

“It was softened for me, Monsieur, while I slept. That unseen, gift-bringing thing which haunts my desk remembered me. No matter how I fell asleep; I awoke pillowed and covered.”

“Did the shawls keep you warm?”

“Very warm. Do you ask thanks for them?”

“No.” He said it curtly; then, as if he could not help himself, he added, “You looked pale in your slumbers. Are you home-sick?”

“To be home-sick, one must have a home; which I have not.”

“Then you have more need of a careful friend. I scarcely know any one, Miss Lucy, who needs a friend more absolutely than you; your very faults imperatively require it. You want so much checking, regulating, and keeping down.”

That thought of “keeping down” never left M. Paul’s head. The most habitual subjugation would, in my case, have failed to cure him of it. I listened, and did not strive to be too submissive; his occupation would have been gone had I left him nothing to keep down.

“You need watching, and watching over,” he pursued, “and it is well for you that I see this, and do my best to discharge both duties. I watch you and others closely—constantly—nearer and oftener than you or they think. Do you see that window with a light in it?”

He pointed to a lattice in one of the college boarding-houses.

“That,” said he, “is a room I have hired, nominally for a study—virtually for a post of observation. There I sit and read for hours; it is my way—my taste. My book is this garden; its contents are human nature—female human nature. I know you all by heart. Ah! I know you well—St. Pierre, the Parisienne—cette maîtresse-femme—my cousin Beck herself.”

“It is not right, Monsieur.”

“Comment? it is not right? By whose creed? Does some dogma of Calvin or Luther condemn it? What is that to me? I am no Protestant. My rich father—though I have known poverty, and once starved for a year in a garret in Rome, starved wretchedly, often on a meal a day, and sometimes not that—yet I was born to wealth—my rich father was a good Catholic; and he gave me a priest and a Jesuit for tutor. I retain his lessons; and to what discoveries, grand Dieu, have they not aided me!”

“Discoveries made by stealth seem to me dishonourable discoveries.”

“Puritaine!” He laughed. “I doubt it not. Yet see how my Jesuit’s system works. You know the St. Pierre?”

“Partially.”

He laughed again, sharper. “You say right—‘partially’; whereas I know her thoroughly; there is the difference. She played before me the amiable; offered me patte de velours; caressed, flattered, fawned. Now, I am accessible to a woman’s flattery—accessible against my reason. Though never pretty, she was—when I first knew her—young, or knew how to look young. Like all her countrywomen, she had the art of dressing—she had a certain cool, easy, social assurance which spared me the pain of embarrassment—”

“Monsieur, that must have been unnecessary. I never saw you embarrassed in my life.”

“Mademoiselle, you know little of me. I can be embarrassed as a petite pensionnaire; there is a fund of modesty and diffidence in my nature—”

“Monsieur, I never saw it.”

“Mademoiselle, it is there. You ought to have seen it.”

“Monsieur, I have observed you in public—on platforms, in tribunes, before titles and crowned heads—and you were as easy as you are in the third division.”

“Mademoiselle, neither titles nor crowned heads excite my modesty; and publicity is very much my element. I like it; I breathe in it freely;—but—but—” He checked, and something in his voice shifted, as if a string had been touched too hard. “In short, here is the sentiment brought into action at this very moment; however, I disdain to be worsted by it. If, Mademoiselle, I were a marrying man—which I am not; and you may spare yourself the trouble of any sneer you may be contemplating—and found it necessary to ask a lady whether she could look upon me as a future husband, then would it be proved that I am as I say—modest.”

I believed him then; and in believing, I honoured him with such sincerity of esteem that my heart ached, as if admiration itself could wound.

“As to the St. Pierre,” he went on, recovering his habitual tone, “she once intended to be Madame Emanuel; and I don’t know whither I might have been led, but for yonder little lattice with the light. Ah, magic lattice! what miracles of discovery hast thou wrought! Yes: I have seen her rancours, her vanities, her levities—not only here, but elsewhere. I have witnessed what bucklers me against all her arts. I am safe from poor Zélie.”

He spoke as a man might speak of a dish he had once been tempted to eat—sweet-scented, handsomely garnished, but found, after tasting, to be tainted; and the very remembrance of the flavour now turned his stomach.

“And my pupils,” he recommenced, “those blondes jeunes filles—so mild and meek—I have seen the most reserved romp like boys, the demurest snatch grapes from the walls, shake pears from the trees. When the English teacher came, I saw her, marked her early preference for this alley, noticed her taste for seclusion, watched her well, long before she and I came to speaking terms. Do you recollect my once coming silently and offering you a little knot of white violets when we were strangers?”

“I recollect it. I dried the violets, kept them, and have them still.”

“It pleased me when you took them peacefully and promptly, without prudery—that sentiment which I ever dread to excite, and which, when revealed in eye or gesture, I vindictively detest. To return. Not only did I watch you; but often—especially at eventide—another guardian angel was noiselessly hovering near. Night after night my cousin Beck has stolen down yonder steps, and glidingly pursued your movements when you did not see her.”

“But, Monsieur, you could not from the distance of that window see what passed in this garden at night?”

“By moonlight I possibly might with a glass—I use a glass—but the garden itself is open to me. In the shed at the bottom, there is a door leading into a court which communicates with the college; of that door I possess the key, and thus come and go at pleasure. This afternoon I came through it, and found you asleep in classe; again this evening I have availed myself of the same entrance.”

I could not help saying, “If you were a wicked, designing man, how terrible would all this be!”

His attention seemed incapable of being arrested by that view. He lit his cigar; the tiny scratch and flare sounded loud in the deepening garden, a brief violence against the soft ecology of evening. The smell of tobacco pushed into the damp sweetness of leaves; the faint rasp of his inhalation and exhale made a rhythm like a small machine beginning to work. He leaned against a tree and looked at me with that cool, amused composure he had when his humour was tranquil.

I thought proper to go on sermonizing him. He often lectured me by the hour; I did not see why I should not speak my mind for once. I told him my impression of his Jesuit system—how the knowledge it brought was bought too dear; how coming and going by stealth degraded his own dignity; how it turned the garden, which ought to be a place where one might breathe honestly, into a theatre of spying.

“My dignity!” he cried, laughing. “When did you ever see me trouble my head about my dignity? It is you, Miss Lucy, who are digne. How often, in your high insular presence, have I taken pleasure in trampling upon what you are pleased to call my dignity—tearing it, scattering it to the winds—in those mad transports you witness with such hauteur, and which I know you think very like the ravings of a third-rate London actor.”

“Monsieur, I tell you every glance you cast from that lattice is a wrong done to the best part of your own nature. To study the human heart thus is to banquet secretly and sacrilegiously on Eve’s apples. I wish you were a Protestant.”

Indifferent to the wish, he smoked on. After a space—smiling, yet thoughtful—he said rather suddenly:

“I have seen other things.”

“What other things?”

He took the weed from his lips and threw the remnant amongst the shrubs, where for a moment it lay glowing in the gloom.

“Look at it,” said he. “Is not that spark like an eye watching you and me?”

He took a turn down the walk; and when he returned his voice had a different grain, as if the garden’s damp had sunk into it.

“I have seen, Miss Lucy, things to me unaccountable—things that have made me watch all night for a solution, and I have not yet found it.”

The tone sent a thrill along my veins; he saw me shiver.

“Are you afraid? Whether is it of my words, or that red jealous eye just winking itself out?”

“I am cold,” I said. “The night grows dark and late; the air is changed; it is time to go in.”

“It is little past eight, but you shall go in soon. Answer me only this question.”

Yet he paused before he put it. Dusk had come on with clouds; drops of rain began to patter through the trees, at first like a cautious hand trying the roof, then more frequent. The garden’s soundscape altered: leaf-noise thickened; distant wheels hissed; somewhere water began to drip steadily from a gutter, making a measured, mournful beat. I hoped he would feel the change and relent, but he was absorbed.

“Mademoiselle, do you Protestants believe in the supernatural?”

“There is a difference of theory and belief on that point amongst Protestants as amongst other sects,” I answered. “Why, Monsieur, do you ask such a question?”

“Why do you shrink and speak so faintly? Are you superstitious?”

“I am constitutionally nervous. I dislike the discussion of such subjects. I dislike it the more because—”

“You believe?”

“No: but it has happened to me to experience impressions—”

“Since you came here?”

“Yes; not many months ago.”

“Here?—in this house?”

“Yes.”

“Bon! I am glad of it. I knew it, somehow, before you told me. I was conscious of rapport between you and myself. You are patient, and I am choleric; you are quiet and pale, and I am tanned and fiery; you are a strict Protestant, and I am a sort of lay Jesuit: but we are alike—there is affinity between us. Do you see it, Mademoiselle, when you look in the glass? Do you observe that your forehead is shaped like mine—that your eyes are cut like mine? Do you hear that you have some of my tones of voice? Do you know that you have many of my looks? I perceive all this, and believe that you were born under my star.”

His words had that curious power of dominance, not merely over thought but over the very language one might use to answer him—as if he would have me speak in his lexicon, not mine. It pricked me; and yet, beneath that prick, there was something—some recognition—that made my breath shorten.

“Yes,” he repeated, “you were born under my star. Tremble! for where that is the case with mortals, the threads of their destinies are difficult to disentangle; knottings and catchings occur—sudden breaks leave damage in the web. But these ‘impressions,’ as you say with English caution. I, too, have had my ‘impressions.’”

“Monsieur,” I said, steadier than I felt, “tell me them.”

“I desire no better, and intend no less. You know the legend of this house and garden?”

“I know it. Yes. They say that hundreds of years ago a nun was buried here alive at the foot of this very tree, beneath the ground which now bears us.”

“And that in former days a nun’s ghost used to come and go here.”

“Monsieur—what if it comes and goes here still?”

“Something comes and goes here. There is a shape frequenting this house by night, different to any forms that show themselves by day. I have indisputably seen a something, more than once; and to me its conventual weeds were a strange sight—saying more than they can do to any other living being. A nun!”

“Monsieur, I, too, have seen it.”

“I anticipated that. Whether this nun be flesh and blood, or something that remains when blood is dried and flesh is wasted, her business is as much with you as with me, probably. Well, I mean to make it out; it has baffled me so far, but I mean to follow up the mystery. I mean—”

Instead of finishing, he lifted his head suddenly. In the same instant I did likewise; we both looked towards the high tree shadowing the great berceau, its boughs resting on the roof of the first classe. From that quarter came a strange, inexplicable sound—an inward heave, a rending—like strong arms struggling under a blanket, like a living body wrenching itself free. There scarce stirred a breeze; yet that heavy tree was convulsed while feathery shrubs stood still, as if the disturbance belonged not to weather but to will.

For some minutes among wood and leafage the tearing and labouring went on. Dark as it was, it seemed to me that something more solid than either night-shadow or branch-shadow blackened out of the boles. Then the struggle ceased. What birth succeeded this travail? What Dryad was born of these throes?

We watched, fixed. A sudden bell rang in the house—the prayer-bell—its clang cutting through rain and leaf-sound, commanding the air as a dominant language commands a crowd.

Instantly, into our alley, there came, out of the berceau, an apparition, all black and white. With a sort of angry rush—close, close past our faces—swept swiftly the very NUN herself.

Never had I seen her so clearly. She looked tall of stature, and fierce of gesture. As she went, the wind rose sobbing; the rain poured wild and cold; the whole night seemed to feel her.
