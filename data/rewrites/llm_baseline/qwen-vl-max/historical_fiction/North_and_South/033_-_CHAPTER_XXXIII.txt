“Sleep on, my love, in thy cold bed,
Never to be disquieted!
My last Good Night—thou wilt not wake
Till I thy fate shall overtake.”

The house was unnaturally still after the storm of fear and noise that had shaken it. Her father had seen to everything—warm food, a clean bed, all the comforts needed for her return—and then settled back into his usual chair, drifting once more into one of those quiet, sorrowful reveries that had become his habit since his wife’s passing. Dixon had sent Mary Higgins into the kitchen to supervise the preparations, and though Mary scolded with her usual vigor, she did so in hushed tones, afraid to raise her voice while death still lingered in the home.

Margaret had decided not to speak to her father about the final, terrifying moment—the sudden panic at the station, the near escape. There was no point in bringing it up; it was over. The only lingering dread was that Leonards might somehow manage to borrow enough money to follow Frederick to London, to hunt him down there. But the odds against such a plan were great, and Margaret refused to torment herself with thoughts beyond her control. Frederick would be cautious—she had made sure of that—and within days he would be safely out of England.

“I suppose we’ll hear from Mr. Bell tomorrow,” Margaret said quietly.

“Yes,” her father replied, barely lifting his eyes. “I suppose so.”

“If he can come, he’ll probably arrive by evening.”

“If he cannot,” her father murmured, “I’ll ask Mr. Thornton to accompany me to the funeral. I cannot go alone. I’d break down completely.”

“No, papa,” Margaret said quickly, almost sharply. “Don’t ask Mr. Thornton. Let me go with you.”

“You?” He looked at her, surprised. “My dear, women don’t usually attend funerals.”

“No,” she answered, “not because they’re not allowed—but because they can’t help themselves. Women like us don’t go because we’re too emotional, yet ashamed to show it. Poor women do go—they don’t care if they weep openly. But I promise you, papa—if you let me go, I won’t be a burden. Don’t bring in a stranger and leave me behind. Please, dear papa—let me go. If Mr. Bell can’t come, I will go. I won’t press it if he does.”

But Mr. Bell could not come. His gout had flared up, and his letter was full of sorrow and regret. He longed to be there, to comfort his old friend, but duty called him to Milton. His estate required his presence, and his agent insisted it was essential. He wrote that he hoped to visit soon, if they would have him, and that the only thing making this journey bearable was the thought of seeing them again, perhaps offering some small comfort.

Margaret struggled to convince her father not to invite Mr. Thornton. The idea filled her with a deep, unspoken aversion. She couldn’t explain why—only that the thought of asking him felt like a betrayal, a surrender to something she could not accept.

The night before the funeral, a formal note arrived from Mrs. Thornton: at her son’s request, their carriage would be sent to attend the service, should it not be inconvenient to the family.

Margaret threw the letter toward her father without reading it aloud.

“Oh, don’t let us have these pretenses,” she said. “Let us go alone—you and I, papa. They don longer care for us, or else he would have offered to come himself. Instead, he sends an empty carriage—it’s a mockery.”

Her father blinked, taken aback. “You’ve always been so opposed to him coming, Margaret.”

“And I still am. I don’t want him here. I especially don’t want us to ask him. But this—this is so hollow, so false. I didn’t expect it from him.”

She burst into tears, startling her father. He had never seen her so agitated. In the midst of grief, she had been calm, thoughtful, patient—always putting others first. But now she seemed restless, overwhelmed, and when he reached out to comfort her, she only wept harder.

That night passed in fitful sleep. The next morning brought another letter—from Frederick.

Mr. Lennox was away, his clerk wrote, and wouldn’t return until Tuesday at the latest—possibly Monday. After weighing the options, Frederick decided to stay in London a few more days. He had considered returning to Milton, the temptation was strong—but the memory of Mr. Bell being in his father’s home, and the sudden alarm at the station, made him decide to remain where he was. He assured Margaret he would take every precaution against being followed by Leonards.

Margaret was relieved to read this letter while her father was with her mother. Had he been present, he would have expected her to read it aloud—and that would have stirred up fears she couldn’t soothe. Not only was Frederick delayed, but the letter mentioned the close call at the station, the possibility of pursuit. The mere thought chilled her blood. How would her father react? And how many times had she regretted suggesting they consult Mr. Lennox at all?

At the time, it had seemed wise—just a small delay, a minor risk. But now, every event pointed to it being a mistake. She fought hard against the guilt, knowing it was useless to dwell on what couldn’t be changed. Yet she feared her father’s fragile state would make him fall into despair, consumed by regrets he couldn’t shake.

She gathered all her strength to hold him together.

On the day of the funeral, her father seemed lost in thought, unaware of anything but the fact that his wife’s body would soon be carried away, hidden from sight. As the undertaker arranged the black draperies around the coffin, he trembled. He looked at Margaret, his eyes pleading.

“Pray for me, Margaret,” he whispered. “I have no strength left. I cannot pray. I give her up because I must. I try to bear it—I truly do. I know it is God’s will. But why did she die? Pray for me, Margaret—that I may have faith to pray. It is a great strait, my child.”

She sat beside him in the coach, holding him gently as he leaned into her. She recited verses of comfort, passages of quiet resignation—anything she could remember that spoke of enduring faith. Her voice never wavered. And in speaking, she found strength. Her father repeated the words after her, his lips moving mechanically, his mind struggling to grasp the peace he could not feel.

As the procession moved through the streets, Dixon subtly drew Margaret’s attention to Nicholas Higgins and his daughter Bessy, standing apart but watching closely. Nicholas wore his usual rough clothes, but a strip of black cloth adorned his hat—a mourning token he had never shown before. Yet Mr. Hale saw nothing. He remained lost in silent prayer, repeating the funeral service word for word, his breath catching only once or twice at its end. Then, placing his hand on Margaret’s arm, he walked forward as if blind, trusting her to guide him through the world he could no longer see.

Dixon wept openly, covering her face with her handkerchief. She was so absorbed in her own sorrow that she didn’t notice the crowd dispersing—until someone touched her shoulder.

It was Mr. Thornton.

He had stood behind a group of people, head bowed, unseen. Now he stepped forward.

“I beg your pardon,” he said, his voice low. “Can you tell me how Mr. Hale is? And Miss Hale? I should like to know how they are.”

“Of course, sir,” Dixon replied. “They’re doing as well as can be expected. Master is terribly broken. Miss Hale bears up better than most would.”

Mr. Thornton had wished she were suffering more. He wanted to believe she was grieving deeply, so that he might step in—not as a man of comfort, but as a lover who could console her. A selfish hope, yes—but one that stung like a secret joy. He imagined her leaning on him, needing him, and the thought brought a sharp pang of longing.

But then came the memory of the station—of the young man standing beside her, their posture so intimate, so natural. That image struck him like a wound. At that hour, so far from home—how could she be so free with another? He clenched his fists to keep the pain from showing.

With effort, he forced himself to believe in her purity, to trust her innocence. But the moment he stopped trying, doubt flooded back—wild, irrational fears chasing each other through his mind like shadows.

She bore up better than likely. So she had something to look forward to—some hope bright enough to ease even this grief. Yes—he knew how she loved. He had loved her, and in doing so, had learned the depth of her heart. She would walk in sunlight if any man were worthy enough to earn her love. And yet, even in mourning, she would rest in peace, supported by sympathy.

Whose sympathy? His? Or that other man’s?

The thought turned his pale face colder, sterner. He had to know.

“I suppose I may call,” he said, his tone flat. “On Mr. Hale, I mean. Perhaps after tomorrow.”

He said it as if it mattered little. But it did. Despite the pain, he longed to see her—to stand near her, to breathe the air she touched. Though he hated her sometimes—hated the memory of that gentle closeness—he still craved her presence. He was caught in the whirlpool of passion, spiraling ever closer to the center he could not escape.

“I dare say, sir,” Dixon replied, “Master will see you. He was very sorry to turn you away the other day. Circumstances weren’t right then.”

For reasons unknown, Dixon never told Margaret about this meeting. And so, Margaret never knew that Mr. Thornton had stood at her mother’s funeral, unseen, unheard, a silent witness to her grief.
