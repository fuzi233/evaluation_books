“The soup is hot,” said Gerard.

“But how are we to get it to our mouths?” asked the elder, staring at the little flask as though it were a brazier from some desert tale.

“Father, the young man has brought us straws,” said Margaret; and there was a sly kindness in her smile, as if she took pleasure in small domestic miracles.

“Ay, ay,” grumbled the old scholar; “but my poor bones are stiff; and, by St. John, the fire is too fierce for a body to kneel over with these short reeds. Yet—by all the saints—the young man is adroit!”

Gerard did not answer; he acted. The knot at his breast came undone under his fingers as if it had been waiting for him all day. He snatched off his hat, dropped a stone into each corner to weight it, wrapped his hand in the tail of his jerkin against the heat, whisked the flask from the coals, wedged it safe between the stones, and slid the hat—now a rude tray—beneath Peter’s nose with a cheerful look.

The air that rose from the broth was a whole kitchen’s comfort: wine warmed to sweetness, bread softened into it till it yielded without losing its heart, and a savour of herbs and browned crust that clung to the nostril like a remembered hearth. Peter’s trembling hand guided the rye-straw to his lips; he drew, cautiously at first; then with a longer pull. His face, a moment ago pinched and colourless, began to kindle—as if the heat went not only into his mouth but down into his very will.

“Hippocrates and Galen!” he cried, sucking again as if he would drink youth through a reed. “’Tis soupe au vin—the restorative of restoratives. Blessed be the nation that invented it, and the woman that made it, and the young man that brings it to fainting folk. Here, child—sip while I enlighten our host as to the history and virtues of this sovereign compound.”

Margaret took her turn obediently, but her eyes danced at Gerard over the straw, amused in advance; for she knew the moment that glint came into her father’s eye he was off like a cart with the brake loosed.

“This corroborative, young sir,” Peter began, swelling with his own learning as with the wine, “was unknown to the ancients. We find it neither in their treatises, nor in those popular narratives that betray, by accident, many a remedy both in chirurgery and physic proper. Hector, in the Ilias, if my memory—”

“Alas! he’s off,” murmured Margaret; yet not unlovingly, for she had lived with this torrent of words as with weather.

“—was invited by one of the ladies to drink wine,” Peter continued, as if no mortal had interrupted him. “He declined, pleading he was going into battle and must not weaken his powers. Now if soupe au vin had been known in Troy, he would have added—in the hexameter, mark you—‘But a soupe au vin, madam, I will degust, and gratefully.’ Not only from civility—no perfect commander lacks that virtue—but from prudence. Men going to blows need sustenance; and generals that bring hungry soldiers against fed ones are defeated in all ages by inferior numbers. The Romans lost a great battle in the north of Italy to Hannibal by this neglect alone. Yet this divine elixir gives force to the limbs and ardour to the spirits in one moment. Observe: I was faint and heart-sick a minute ago—now I suck this celestial cordial and behold me brave as Achilles and strong as an eagle!”

“Oh, father—now? An eagle, alack!” Margaret’s laugh was half protest, half delight.

“Girl, I defy thee and all the world. Ready—like a foaming charger—to devour the space between this and Rotterdam, and strong to combat the ills of life, even poverty and old age; which last philosophers call the summum malum. Negatur—unless the man’s life hath been ill-spent, which, by the bye, it generally hath. Now for the moderns!”

“Father! dear father!”

“Fear me not, child; I will be brief—unreasonably brief. The soupe au vin occurs not in modern science; but that is only proof—if proof were needed—that for these few hundred years physicians have been idiots, with their chicken-broth and their decoctions of gold; attributing the highest qualities to that meat which has the least juice of any meat, and to that metal which has less chemical virtue than any metal. Mountebanks! dunces! homicides! Since from these no light is to be gathered, go we to chroniclers. First: Duguesclin, a French knight, being about to join battle with the English—masters then of half France, sturdy strikers by sea and land—drank not one but three soupes au vin in honour of the Blessed Trinity. This done, he charged; and, as might have been foretold, killed a multitude and drove the rest into the sea. And he is but the first of a long list of holy and hard-hitting ones sustentated, fortified, corroborated, and consoled—”

“Dear father,” Margaret interrupted, holding up the hat with both hands, pleading as if she begged a child to sleep, “prithee add thyself to that venerable company ere the soup cools.”

Peter, thus coaxed, put the straw back between his lips; and the reed made a faint whispering sound as he drew—one of those little intimate noises that belong to hunger turning into ease.

It spared them the “modern instances,” and gave Gerard a moment to say, with honest warmth, how proud his mother would be that her simple broth had profited a man of learning.

“Ay,” said Margaret, “but she would like it ill to see her son give all and take none himself. Why brought you but two straws?”

“Fair mistress,” said Gerard, his youth getting the better of his prudence, “I hoped you would let me put my lips to your straw, there being but two.”

She smiled—and blushed, as if the heat from the fire had suddenly leapt to her cheeks. “Never beg that you may command,” she answered. “The straw is not mine; ’tis yours. You cut it in yonder field.”

“I cut it, and that made it mine,” said he, watching her mouth as much as the reed. “But after that, your lip touched it—and that made it yours.”

“Did it? Then I will lend it you. There—now it is yours again; your lip has touched it.”

“No,” he said softly. “It belongs to us both now. Let us divide it.”

“By all means; you have a knife.”

“No; I will not cut it—that would be unlucky. I’ll bite it. There. I shall keep my half: you will burn yours once you get home, I doubt.”

“You know me not. I waste nothing,” she said with quick pride. “It is odds but I make a hairpin of it, or something.”

That thrift—so like a house’s good order—did not tease him as she meant it to; it sobered him, dashed his little triumph, and set him striving for other words. But none came; and soon the bread and soup were done.

Then the old scholar rose to go on; and there came a small difficulty: Gerard the adroit could not tie again the ribbon at his breast as Catherine had tied it. He fumbled; he reddened. Margaret, after watching him sidelong with mischief and pity blended—offered her help; for at her age a girl’s tenderness and sauciness change places like hands in a dance, and she had seen she had put him out of countenance.

She bent her head—fair and stately—so that the auburn hair, glossed and warmed, shone through its silver net as if the day itself had tangled in it. Her fingers—white, supple, careful—played upon the stubborn ribbon. She coaxed it into obedience, drawing the ends, easing them, flattening the knot with touches that were light as flour-dust on a baker’s palm. Then, when the bow was made, she pressed the centre with her convexed hand in a final little pat—a woman’s sure seal upon the work: Now keep.

Gerard felt that gentle pressure not on the ribbon but on his heart. Something opened in him—like a door on a room he had not known existed—full of strange light and trembling sweetness.

“There,” said Margaret, drawing back to take one sharp survey of her handiwork; and, lifting her eyes for the simplest approval, met instead the full, yearning gaze of his adoration. The blood flew to her face. She dropped her lashes as if they were curtains she could pull against daylight. A tremor went through her; she retreated with cheeks that betrayed her more than tears would have done, and took her father’s arm on the far side.

Gerard, ashamed that his eyes had frightened her, took the other arm. So the two young creatures went downcast and conscious, propping along the old man—who, restored by wine-soup and vanity, moved with a defiant spring and the self-belief of his own “eagle.”

They entered Rotterdam by the Schiedamze Poort. Gerard knew nothing of the town; Peter directed him toward the Hooch Straet where the Stadthouse stood. He himself, with Margaret, meant to go to a cousin in the Ooster-Waagen Straet. So, almost as soon as they were inside the gate, their roads must part.

They bade each other friendly farewell; and Gerard plunged into the great town.

At once a deep loneliness fell on him—one of those hushes that can live inside a crowd. The streets were thick with folk, yet he felt as if a door had shut behind him and left him outside all warmth. Then he cursed himself that, out of delicacy, he had not asked his late companions who they were or where they lived.

“Beshrew my shamefacedness!” he muttered. “Their words and their breeding were above their means; and something whispered me they would not be known. I shall never see her more. Oh weary world—I hate you and your ways. To think I must meet beauty and goodness and learning—three pearls of price—and never see them more!”

He walked as his feet chose, his mind elsewhere, and so lost his way. Presently he found himself in a stream of people all moving one way; he joined them, reasoning they must be bound for the Stadthouse. But the current of bodies carried him out upon a wide meadow by the Maas; and there the pull that drew them became plain.

Games were in full cry: wrestling with the thud and grunt of bodies; the sharp clap of palm-play; the ring of archery; the laughter at tumblers—men and, to his astonishment, women—whose feet beat the earth like quick drum-taps. There was legerdemain, and a trained bear that stood on his head, marched upright, and bowed with prodigious gravity; a hare beat a drum; a cock strutted on little stilts with the disdain of a tiny tyrant.

All these things wrung a laugh from Gerard now and then; yet the gay din could not tune his heart. He heard, above the muddle of voices, one young man telling another the Duke had been in the meadow, but was gone to the Stadthouse to entertain the burgomasters and aldermen, the competitors for the prizes, and their friends. Hunger—plain, bodily hunger, that had been forgotten in longing—rose up in Gerard at the thought of a prince’s supper. He left the riverside and found, this time, the Hooch Straet; and it led him straight to the Stadthouse.

But at one door he was refused, and at another; and at last he came to the great gate of the courtyard. Soldiers kept it; and over them presided a major-domo, pompous as a painted saint: embroidered collar, gold chain, white staff tipped with a golden knob. A crowd pressed there, each trying to soften that official rock. They surged up like waves and fell back like foam.

Gerard struggled nearer—and when he was within four heads of the gate his heart gave a leap: Peter was there, Margaret on his arm, begging humbly for entrance.

“My cousin the alderman is not at home; they say he is here.”

“What is that to me, old man?” said the major-domo.

“If you will not let us pass in to him, at least take this leaf from my tablet to my cousin. See, I have written his name; he will come out to us.”

“For what do you take me? I carry no messages; I keep the gate.”

Then he bawled, inexorable, so that his voice cracked like a whip:

“No strangers enter here, but the competitors and their companies!”

“Come, old man,” cried a voice in the crowd, “you have gotten your answer; make way.”

Margaret turned half round, her face eager, her voice clear with distress. “Good people, we are come from far, and my father is old; and my cousin has a new servant that knows us not, and would not let us sit in our cousin’s house.”

At that the crowd laughed—coarse, careless. Margaret shrank as if struck. At that instant a hand closed over hers—warm, sure, sudden as a bell-note in a storm. It was Gerard. The touch steadied her like a hand at the small of the back when one stumbles on a step. A little cry of joy and appeal broke from her; and the tears, that had been held back by pride, came now, prettily, despite her.

The jostling, the fear, the mortification—that her cousin had not even warned his servant, and that caution, however faithful, had turned them into beggars at a stranger’s gate—had all worked upon her; and then this familiar face, this kind grasp, arrived as if Providence itself had elbowed through the crowd.

“All is well now,” remarked a coarse humourist. “She hath gotten her sweetheart.”

“Haw! haw! haw!” went the laughter again.

Margaret dropped Gerard’s hand instantly; and turned, eyes flashing through wet lashes. “I have no sweetheart, you rude men. But I am friendless in your boorish town, and this is a friend; and one who knows—what you know not—how to treat the aged and the weak.”

The crowd fell dead silent. They had been thoughtless, not malignant; and her rebuke, sharp as it was, struck true.

In the sudden quiet Gerard addressed the porter. “I am a competitor, sir.”

“What is your name?” The major-domo eyed him as if weighing a coin.

“Gerard, the son of Elias.”

The man took a slip of parchment and peered. “Gerard Eliassoen can enter.”

“With my company—these two.”

“Nay; those are not your company. They came before you.”

“What matter? They are my friends, and without them I go not in.”

“Stay without, then.”

“That will I not.”

“That we shall see.”

“We will—and speedily.” And Gerard, driven by something fiercer than hunger, raised a voice of such startling power that it struck the street like a trumpet and sent its echo clattering among the stones. “Ho! PHILIP, EARL OF HOLLAND!”

“Are you mad?” cried the porter, turning pale.

“HERE IS ONE OF YOUR VARLETS DEFIES YOU—”

“Hush, hush!”

“—AND WILL NOT LET YOUR GUESTS PASS IN!”

“Hush! murder! The Duke’s there. I’m dead,” whimpered the major-domo; and then, trying to drown Gerard’s thunder, he roared with all his lungs:

“OPEN THE GATE, YE KNAVES! WAY THERE FOR GERARD ELIASSOEN AND HIS COMPANY! (The fiends go with him!)”

The gate swung wide as if on enchanted hinges. Eight soldiers lowered their pikes halfway, making an arch; and under that bristling honour Gerard marched, with Margaret and Peter, triumphant. The moment they passed, the pikes clashed together to bar the gap, almost pinning a citizen who tried to wedge in after them.

A few steps beyond the guarded portal and the world changed, as if they had crossed from street-dust into a tale. The courtyard was laid with tables loaded with rich meats and piled with gorgeous plate. Above, a leafy canopy of fresh-cut branches—plane and lime—was fastened with taste to cords of gold, silver, and blue silk that traversed the space. Fruits of many hues hung among the green; some were no fruit at all but wonders in gold, silver, and wax, peeping like bright eyes through leaves.

The Duke’s minstrels touched their lutes now and then; and the sound—fine, plucked, and quick—ran between laughter and talk like a brook through stones. A fountain played red Burgundy in six jets that met and fought in the air. The evening sun struck through those purple spouts, turning them into molten rubies; and the light, stained with the grape’s blood, flung crimson glories on fair faces, snowy beards, velvet and satin, jewelled hilts, gold that glowed, silver that gleamed, and glass that flashed.

Gerard and his friends stood dazzled, spell-bound; and even the old scholar’s tongue stilled, as if the sight had taken his breath.

A whisper ran: “Salute the Duke! Salute the Duke!” They looked up. Under a dais sat their sovereign, high and easy in his state, bidding them welcome with a kindly wave. The men bowed low; Margaret curtsied with deep grace. The Duke turned his hand again and pointed the newcomers out to a knot of valets.

Instantly seven of his people sprang forward, seated the three at a table, and set before them fifteen many-coloured soups in little silver bowls, and as many wines in crystal vases.

“Nay, father,” said Margaret—only now recovering her manners amid the bustle—“let us not eat till we have thanked our good friend.”

“Girl, he is our guardian angel,” said Peter, his eyes shining.

Gerard, overwhelmed in a different way, hid his face in his hands a moment. “Tell me when you have done,” said he, “and I will reappear and have my supper, for I am hungry. I know which of us three is the happiest at meeting again.”

“Me?” asked Margaret.

“No. Guess again.”

“Father?”

“No.”

“Then I have no guess which it can be,” she said; and gave a little crow of happiness that made even Peter smile.

They tasted soup; and the bowls began to empty as hands moved briskly—bread dipped, spoon lifted, lips warmed. Fish followed in a dozen fashions; patties where lobster and almonds met in strange richness; others of almonds and cream; then little brouets and crisp rissoles, each with its own scent rising—pepper here, saffron there, vinegar sharpened, butter mellowed. Then came a wild boar that smelled divine—fat and spice and the dark sweetness of roasted flesh.

Margaret shrieked twice and sprang back, pinching Gerard in her fright.

Why? The Duke’s cook had been too clever. He had given the dish not only savour but semblance: by burnt sugar and edible colours he had restored to the beast the very hair and bristles taken off by fire and water. The great tusks were set in place; the mouth wore that grim “smile” that tusk lends; and two eyes of coloured sugar glowed in the head.

St. Argus—what eyes! Bright, bloodshot, threatening—eyes that seemed to follow each motion of knife and spoon. And there, on the far side, two gilt valets set the monster down before them with smiling, benevolent pride; old Peter clasped his hands in pious admiration; Margaret wheeled away, horror-struck, fingers still biting Gerard’s arm; Gerard, foolishly delighted at the pinch; and all around, guests grinning at the comedy.

“What’s to do?” shouted the Duke, hearing the signals of female distress. Seven attendants sprang and told him. He laughed. “Give her of the beef-stuffing, then, and bring me Sir Boar.”

Benevolent monarch: the beef-stuffing was his own dish. On these days an ox was roasted whole and reserved for the poor; but this wise prince had discovered that whatever venison, hare, lamb, poultry, and the like you skewered into that beef cavern cooked to perfection—keeping its own juices and taking in the rich breath of the ox. At his word the valets drove silver tridents into the steaming cave at random and speared forth a kid, a cygnet, and a handful of wildfowl. These smoked before Gerard and company; and Peter’s face, which had clouded at the loss of the boar, expanded and shone again, as if the meat itself had smoothed his temper.

Afterwards came tarts—twenty kinds—fruits and herbs, sweet and sharp mingled; and last, confectionery on a scale that mocked church and castle: cathedrals of sugar gilt and painted in the hollows of their carvings; castles with moats and ditches made to the life; elephants and camels and toads; knights on horseback jousting; kings and princesses looking on; trumpeters blowing; all these figures edible, their veins filled with scented sweetness. Art made to be destroyed: guests breached bastions, crunched crusaders, cracked bishops cope and crosier, as remorselessly as children crack comfits; sipping hippocras and spiced drinks, Greek and Corsican wines. And now and then little Turkish boys—turbaned, spangled, jewelled, gilded—came offering on bended knee golden troughs of rose-water and orange-water to cool and perfume the guests’ hands.

Yet long before this final marvel, appetite had yielded. Gerard suddenly remembered the letter he bore to the Princess Marie, and, leaning to a servant, asked in an undertone if he would deliver it. The man took it with deep obeisance: he could not carry it himself, but would place it instantly in the hands of one of the Princess’s suite, several of whom were near.

Peter and Margaret had come not to dine but to find their cousin; yet the old gentleman ate heartily, and being worn with travel and revived by wine-soup and rich meat, he dropped asleep where he sat and forgot his errand. Margaret did not wake him, nor remind him; and we shall hear why.

Meanwhile that cousin—an alderman—sat within a few feet of them, just behind, and had spied them when Margaret screamed at the boar. But he forbore to speak, for municipal reasons. Margaret was plainly dressed, and Peter inclined to threadbare. So the alderman said to himself: It will be time enough to claim them when the sun sets and folk disperse. Then I will take my poor relations home, and none will be the wiser.

Half the courses were lost on Gerard and Margaret. They were no great eaters; and besides, they were feeding on sweet thoughts that steal the edge from hunger. Yet there is another delicacy of sense besides taste—the delight of colour, music, and perfume—and those pleasures worked on them perhaps more strongly than on any pair there. The lute-strings’ clear notes laid a net over the courtyard’s murmur; the fountain’s splash was a cool, continual singing; and above both drifted the smells of roasted meat, crushed herbs, spiced wine, and the green breath of broken branches.

Margaret leaned back, half closing her eyes, and murmured to Gerard, as if the sound of her own words might shatter the spell. “What a lovely scene! The warm sun, the green shade, the rich dresses, the bright music of the lutes and the cool music of the fountain—and all faces so happy and gay! And then—it is to you we owe it.”

Gerard answered only with his eyes; and she, seeing them, said in a languid whisper, “Now, speak not to me. Let me listen to the fountain. What are you a competitor for?”

He told her.

“Very well. You will gain one prize, at least.”

“Which? which? Have you seen any of my work?”

“I? no. But you will gain a prize.”

“I hope so; but what makes you think so?”

“Because you were so good to my father.”

He smiled at the logic, and bent his head at the praise, and again was silent.

“Speak not,” she murmured. “They say this is a world of sin and misery. Can that be? What is your opinion?”

“No,” said Gerard, with the confident rebellion of youth. “That is but a silly old song our elders keep repeating out of custom. It is not true.”

“How can you know?” she asked, suddenly grave beneath her sweetness. “You are but a child.”

“Why, only look round!” he cried, and his voice, for once, had no thunder in it—only wonder. “And then—I thought I had lost you for ever; and you are by my side; and now the minstrels are going to play again. Sin and misery? Stuff and nonsense!”

The lutes burst out afresh; delicate harmony ran through the leafy canopy like wind through grain.

“What do you admire most of all these beautiful things, Gerard?” she asked.

“You know my name?” he said, startled. “How is that?”

“White magic. I am a—witch,” she answered, teasingly.

“Angels are never witches,” said he. “But I can’t think how you—”

“Foolish boy! Was it not cried at the gate loud enough to deave one?”

“So it was. Where is my head?” He drew breath, and looked at her as if all the painted sugar castles and ruby wine-jets were nothing beside the living miracle next to him. “What do I admire most? If you will sit a little more that way, I’ll tell you.”

“This way?”

“Yes—so that the light may fall on you. There.” His voice softened, thickened, as if the words must wade through his own heart. “I see many fair things here, fairer than I could have conceived; but the fairest of all, to my eye, is your lovely hair in its silver frame, and the setting sun kissing it. It minds me of what the Vulgate praises for beauty—‘an apple of gold in a network of silver.’ And oh, what a pity I did not know you before I sent in my poor endeavours at illuminating! I could illuminate so much better now. I could do everything better. There—now the sun is full on it—it is like an aureole. So our Lady looked, and none since her until to-day.”

“Oh, fie!” cried Margaret, shrinking and colouring. “It is wicked to talk so. Compare a poor, coarse-favoured girl like me with the Queen of Heaven? Oh, Gerard! I thought you were a good young man.”

Gerard tried to gather himself, to explain, but his senses were too full—light, music, perfume, the nearness of her—his words came out like bread too quickly baked.

“I am no worse than the rest,” he stammered; “but how can I help having eyes—and a heart? Margaret!”

“Gerard!”

“Be not angry now!”

“Now, is it likely?”

“I love you.”

“Oh, for shame! You must not say that to me,” she whispered, and the blush that flooded her face was fiercer than her rebuke.

“I can’t help it,” said Gerard, low and obstinate as a vow. “I love you. I love you.”
