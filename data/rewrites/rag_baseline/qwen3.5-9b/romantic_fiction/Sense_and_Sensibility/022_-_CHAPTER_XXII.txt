Marianne, possessing a constitution of spirit that brooked no impertinence, vulgarity, or mediocrity, was currently in a frame of mind where the Miss Steeles' presence was not merely unwelcome but actively repulsive. Her icy reception served as a rigid barrier, effectively halting their attempts at familiarity. Yet, it was precisely this rejection that catalyzed a strategic shift in their approach, specifically from Lucy Steele, who seized every opening to bridge the gap with a calculated display of ease and frankness. Lucy possessed a natural acuity; her observations were sharp and often witty, rendering her an agreeable conversationalist for brief intervals. However, Elinor quickly discerned that this charm was unmoored from intellect. Lucy's mind remained stagnant, devoid of the cultivation that would elevate her wit into wisdom. Her ignorance regarding basic facts was impossible to mask, regardless of Elinor's diplomatic efforts to appear magnanimous. While Elinor felt a pang of pity for wasted potential, she viewed Lucy's moral deficiencies with cold detachment. The girl's relentless flattery, her obsessive attentiveness, and her transparent insincerity revealed a profound lack of rectitude. To associate with someone who combined ignorance with duplicity offered no enduring satisfaction.

"You will find my question peculiar," Lucy began one afternoon as they traversed the path from the Park to the cottage, "but pray, are you personally acquainted with your sister-in-law's mother, Mrs. Ferrars?"

Elinor perceived the oddity immediately, her expression reflecting the query's absurdity. "I have never met Mrs. Ferrars," she replied.

"Indeed!" Lucy exclaimed, her eyes narrowing with feigned surprise. "I marvel at that. One assumes you encountered her during visits to Norland. Consequently, you cannot describe her character?"

"No," Elinor responded, carefully withholding her true assessment of Edward's mother and resisting the urge to indulge what appeared to be idle curiosity. "I know nothing of her."

"I am certain you deem me strangely intrusive," Lucy continued, watching Elinor closely. "Yet, perhaps there are reasons. I trust I may venture further; however, I hope you grant me the justice of believing I possess no intent to be rude."

Elinor offered a polite dismissal, and they walked in silence for several minutes until Lucy broke the quiet. "I cannot abide the notion that you view me as curiously impertinent. I would sooner endure anything than offend someone whose esteem is so valuable as yours. I would place absolute faith in you; indeed, I would welcome your counsel on managing this precarious position. Nevertheless, there is no need to burden you. I regret you do not know Mrs. Ferrars."

"I regret that I do *not*," Elinor stated, her astonishment palpable. "If knowing my opinion of her could benefit *you*, I would share it. But truly, I fail to comprehend why you, seemingly unrelated to that family, would inquire so seriously about her character."

"I suppose you are right, and I do not wonder at your reaction. But if I were to reveal the truth, your surprise would diminish. Mrs. Ferrars is irrelevant to me for the moment—the timing of our connection depends entirely on her whims. Soon, however, we shall be intimately linked." She lowered her gaze, adopting an air of bashful innocence, casting a single, assessing glance at Elinor to gauge her reaction.

"Good heavens!" Elinor cried. "What do you imply? Are you acquainted with Mr. Robert Ferrars? Could you possibly be?" The prospect of such a sister-in-law held no delight for her.

"No," Lucy corrected smoothly. "Not Mr. *Robert* Ferrars—I have never laid eyes on him. But," she fixed her gaze upon Elinor, "to his eldest brother."

What surged through Elinor was a mixture of pain and disbelief so potent it threatened to overwhelm her. She turned to Lucy, stunned, unable to decipher the motive behind such a declaration. Though her complexion flushed, she maintained her composure, refusing to succumb to hysteria or fainting.

"You have cause to be surprised," Lucy continued. "Naturally, you knew nothing prior. He never hinted at this to you or your family; it was intended as a profound secret, guarded faithfully by me to this hour. No relation knows except Anne, whom I would never have informed had I not relied utterly on your discretion. I realized my inquiries about Mrs. Ferrars seemed bizarre and required explanation. I am confident Mr. Ferrars will not be displeased that I have entrusted you with this, given his high regard for your entire family—he views you and the other Miss Dashwoods as his own sisters."

She paused. Elinor remained silent, her mind reeling. Eventually, she forced herself to speak, masking her turmoil with a calm demeanor. "May I inquire if your engagement is longstanding?"

"We have been engaged for four years."

"Four years!"

"Yes."

Though shaken, Elinor struggled to accept the reality. "I was unaware you were even acquainted until recently."

"Our acquaintance predates this significantly. He was a pupil under my uncle's care for a considerable period."

"My uncle!"

"Yes; Mr. Pratt. Have you never heard him discuss Mr. Pratt?"

"I believe I have," Elinor admitted, summoning strength as her emotions intensified. "He spent four years with my uncle, residing at Longstaple near Plymouth. It was there our acquaintance began, as my sister and I frequented my uncle's home. Our engagement was formed there, though not until a year after he ceased his studies. Yet, he remained almost constantly with us thereafter. I was reluctant to proceed without my mother's approval, but my youth and affection blinded me to prudence. Though you may not know him as well as I, Miss Dashwood, you must recognize his capability to inspire sincere devotion."

"Certainly," Elinor replied mechanically. After a pause, she added, bolstered by a renewed conviction in Edward's honor and Lucy's deceit, "Engaged to Mr. Edward Ferrars! I confess I am so astounded that I must assume a mistake of identity. We cannot refer to the same Mr. Ferrars."

"We refer to no other," Lucy declared, smiling confidently. "Mr. Edward Ferrars, eldest son of Mrs. Ferrars of Park Street and brother of your sister-in-law, Mrs. John Dashwood, is the individual in question. You must concede I am unlikely to err regarding the name of the man upon whom my happiness rests."

"It is strange," Elinor murmured, trapped in agonizing confusion, "that I have never heard him mention your name."

"No; given our station, it was inevitable. Our primary concern has been secrecy. You knew nothing of me or my family, eliminating any necessity to introduce me. Furthermore, his terror of his sister discovering anything ensured he never mentioned it."

Silence fell between them. Elinor's sense of security crumbled, yet her self-control held firm. "Four years you have waited," she said firmly.

"Yes; and heaven knows how much longer we may endure. Poor Edward! It drains his spirit completely." Pulling a small miniature from her pocket, she added, "To avoid error, please examine this portrait. It fails to capture his true essence, yet I trust you cannot mistake the subject. I have possessed it for over three years."

She handed the image to Elinor. Upon viewing it, any lingering doubt evaporated. It was undeniably Edward's face. She returned it immediately, acknowledging the likeness.

"I have never reciprocated with my portrait," Lucy lamented, "which vexes me greatly, as he has been so eager to obtain it! I intend to send it the first opportunity."

"You act wisely," Elinor replied calmly.

They walked a short distance in silence before Lucy resumed. "I am convinced you will guard this secret faithfully, for you understand its critical importance. My mother would never approve; she is excessively proud, and I possess no fortune."

"I sought no confidence from you," Elinor stated. "Yet, you accord me fair credit. Your secret is safe with me. However, pardon my surprise at such an unnecessary disclosure. Surely, you realize my knowledge adds no protection to your secret."

As she spoke, Elinor scrutinized Lucy's face, searching for cracks in the facade, but found none.

"I feared you would deem I was overstepping boundaries," Lucy said. "Though we have not been personally acquainted long, I have known your family by reputation for years. Seeing you, I felt an immediate kinship. Moreover, in this instance, I believed transparency owed to you following my persistent inquiries about Edward's mother. I am so unfortunate as to lack anyone whose counsel I can seek. Anne is the sole confidante, yet she possesses no judgment; she harms me more than helps. She lacks discretion, and I remain terrified she will betray me. Consider the panic I endured yesterday when Sir John mentioned Edward's name, fearing Anne would expose everything. The mental anguish I endure for Edward's sake over these four years is unimaginable. Everything hangs in suspense; we meet barely twice a year. I wonder my heart remains intact."

She produced a handkerchief, but Elinor felt no compassion.

"Occasionally," Lucy continued, dabbing her eyes, "I consider breaking the engagement entirely." She looked directly at Elinor. "Yet, at other times, I lack the resolve. I cannot bear the thought of causing him misery, nor can I endure the loss of someone so dear to me. What counsel would you offer, Miss Dashwood? What would you do?"
