Shortly after Colonel Altamont's stroke of luck at Epsom, he executed his plan for a grand foreign tour. The chroniclers of high society, those who descend to London Bridge to bid farewell to the fashionable exiles, reported that the *Soho* bound for Antwerp carried "Sir Robert, Lady, and the Misses Hodge; Mr. Serjeant Kewsy, and Mrs. and Miss Kewsy; Colonel Altamont, Major Coddy, etc." The Colonel traveled in full state, dressed in a rich traveling costume, drinking brandy-and-water with gusto, remaining sober while others succumbed to seasickness, and accompanied by his faithful Irish body-servant, the very legionary who had waited on him and Captain Strong in their Shepherd's Inn chambers.

Before departing, the Chevalier shared a copious dinner with his friend the Colonel at Blackwall. Over many toasts to Altamont's health, the Colonel, a generous soul, offered Strong a loan. "Strong, old boy," he said, "if you need a bit of cash, now is your time. I'm your man. You've been a good feller to me, and a twenty-pound note makes no difference to me." But Strong politely declined. "No, thank you," he replied, feigning flushness. "I'm quite flush—well, not flush enough to repay your last loan, Altamont, but sufficient to carry on for a while." Thus, with a cordial but slightly strained handshake, they parted.

Was money truly making Altamont more honest, or merely making him appear so in Strong's eyes? Perhaps wealth had improved him; perhaps Strong simply admired the beauty of riches. Inside himself, Strong reasoned that this "unlucky outcast," a returned convict, was ten times better than his friend Sir Francis Clavering. Altamont possessed pluck and a strange form of honesty; he stuck to friends and faced enemies. Clavering, conversely, lacked the courage to do either. "What cloud hangs over this poor devil?" Strong mused. "He was only a little wild, signing his father-in-law's name. Many have done worse and held their heads high. Clavering does not hold his head up; he never did, not even in his best days." Strong perhaps regretted the lie he'd told the open-handed Colonel—that he wasn't in want of money. It was a lie born of honesty, and the Chevalier, despite his desperate situation, could not bring himself to borrow again from his outlawed friend. Besides, he had hopes. Clavering had promised money, and though the Baronet's promises were notoriously unreliable, the Chevalier was hopeful. He trusted in the chances of intercepting stray remittances, a task for which Mr. Strong was ideally suited.

Life in the Shepherd's Inn chambers felt glum without the Colonel. The solitary existence grated on Strong's social soul, compounded by his own extravagant habits. With a servant at his beck and call to run errands, arrange his toilet, and cook his meals, it became a grand and touching sight to see the portly gentleman painting his own boots and broiling his own mutton chop. The Chevalier had a wife, a Spanish lady from Vittoria, who had returned to her friends after a few months of marriage, having broken his head with a dish. Now, melancholy settled over him, or as he termed it, he was "down on his luck." These moments of depression are the lot of every hero: Marius at Minturnae, Charles Edward in the Highlands, Napoleon before Elba. What great man has not faced evil fortune?

Supplies from Clavering were nonexistent. The twenty-five pounds, or "pony," the exemplary Baronet had received from Altamont, had vanished as swiftly as previous ponies. Clavering had been down the river with a party of sporting gentlemen, dodging the police to land in Essex, where they staged a fight between Billy Bluck and Dick the cabman. The Baronet backed the cabman, who won thirteen rounds until an unlucky blow to the windpipe killed him. "It's always my luck, Strong," Sir Francis lamented. "The betting was three to one on the cabman, and I thought I was as sure of thirty pounds as if it were in my pocket. Dammit, I owe my man Lightfoot fourteen pounds now, which he lent and paid for me. He duns me—the confounded impudent blackguard! I wish to Heaven I knew how to get a bill drawn, or squeeze something from my lady! I'll give you half, Ned, upon my soul and honor, I'll give you half if you can get anyone to do us a fifty."

"Ned" responded sternly that he had given his word of honor as a gentleman to be no party to any future bill transactions involving her husband (who had also given his word). The Chevalier vowed to keep his promise, promising to black his own boots all his life rather than break it. Furthermore, he declared he would advise Lady Clavering that Sir Francis was about to break his faith toward her the moment he suspected such an intention.

Upon receiving this intelligence, Sir Francis Clavering, according to custom, cried and cursed volubly. He spoke of death as his only resource, beseeching his dear Strong, his best friend, his dear old Ned, not to abandon him. Yet, as he descended the stairs of Shepherd's Inn, he swore and blasphemed at Ned as the most infernal villain, traitor, blackguard, and coward under the sun, wishing him in his grave and a worse place, only wishing the ruffian would live long enough for Frank Clavering to have his revenge.

In Strong's chambers, the Baronet met a gentleman whose visits had become frequent: Mr. Samuel Huxter, of Clavering. This young fellow, who had once poached walnuts in Clavering Park and watched the Baronet drive through the street with four horses, possessed immense respect for his Member and a prodigious delight in making his acquaintance. He introduced himself with blushing trepidation as a "Clavering man"—son of Mr. Huxter of the marketplace, whose father had attended Sir Francis's keeper, Coxwood, when his gun burst and took off three fingers. Proud to make Sir Francis's acquaintance, he spoke affably. Honest Huxter talked about Sir Francis to the chaps at Bartholomew's and told Fanny in the lodge that, after all, there was nothing like a thoroughbred un, a regular good old English gentleman of the olden time. Fanny replied that she thought Sir Francis was an "ojous" creature—she didn't know why—but she couldn't abide him; she was sure he was wicked, low, and mean. When Sam countered that Sir Francis was very affable and had borrowed half a sovereign of him quite kindly, Fanny burst into laughter, pulled Sam's long hair (which was not yet of irreproachable cleanliness), patted his chin, and called him a "stoopid, stoopid, old foolish stoopid," noting that Sir Francis was always borrowing money from everybody, and that Mar had actually refused him twice, forcing him to wait three months to get seven shillings.

"Don't say 'er' but 'her,' 'borrer' but 'borrow,' 'actially' but 'actually,' Fanny," Mr. Huxter replied—not correcting her argument, but her grammar.

"Well then, 'her,' and 'borrow,' and 'hactually'—there then, you stoopid," said the other. The scholar made such a pretty face that the grammar master was quickly appeased and would have willingly given her a hundred more lessons on the spot at the price he charged for that one.

Of course, Mrs. Bolton was present, and Fanny and Dr. Sam were on exceedingly familiar and confidential terms. Time had brought Fanny certain consolations and soothed certain regrets, which are deucedly bitter when they occur but are, no more than tooth-pulling or any other pang, eternal.

As you sit, surrounded by respect and affection; happy, honored, and flattered in your old age; your foibles gently indulged; your least words kindly cherished; your garrulous old stories received for the hundredth time with dutiful forbearance and never-failing hypocritical smiles; the women of your house constant in their flatteries; the young men hushed and attentive when you begin to speak; the servants awestricken; the tenants cap in hand, ready to act in the place of your worship's horses when your honor takes a drive—it has often struck you, O thoughtful Dives! that this respect and these glories are for the main part transferred, with your fee simple, to your successor. The servants will bow, and the tenants shout, for your son as for you; the butler will fetch him the wine (improved by a little keeping) that's now in your cellar. When your night comes, and the light of your life is gone down, as sure as the morning rises after you and without you, the sun of prosperity and flattery shines on your heir. Men come and bask in the halo of consols and acres that beams round about him. The reverence is transferred with the estate; of which, with all its advantages, pleasures, respect, and goodwill, he becomes the life-tenant. How long do you wish or expect that your people will regret you? How much time does a man devote to grief before he begins to enjoy? A great man must keep his heir at his feast like a living *memento mori*. If he holds very much by life, the presence of the other must be a constant sting and warning. "Make ready to go," says the successor to your honor; "I am waiting: and I could hold it as well as you."

What has this reference to the possible reader to do with any of the characters of this history? Do we wish to apologize for Pen because he has got a white hat, and because his mourning for his mother is fainter? All the lapse of years, all the career of fortune, all the events of life, however strongly they may move or eagerly excite him, can never remove that sainted image from his heart, or banish that blessed love from its sanctuary. If he yields to wrong, the dear eyes will look sadly upon him when he dares to meet them; if he does well, endures pain, or conquers temptation, the ever-present love will greet him, he knows, with approval and pity; if he falls, plead for him; if he suffers, cheer him—be with him and accompany him always until death is past, and sorrow and sin are no more. Is this mere dreaming, or, on the part of an idle story-teller, useless moralizing? May not the man of the world take his moment, too, to be grave and thoughtful? Ask of your own hearts and memories, brother and sister, if we do not live in the dead; and (to speak reverently) prove God by love.

Of these matters, Pen and Warrington often spoke in many a solemn and friendly converse in after days, and Pendennis's mother was worshipped in his memory and canonized there, as such a saint ought to be. Lucky he in life who knows a few such women! A kind provision of Heaven it was that sent us such; and gave us to admire that touching and wonderful spectacle of innocence, love, and beauty.

But as it is certain that if, in the course of these sentimental conversations, any outer stranger, Major Pendennis for instance, had walked into Pen's chambers, Arthur and Warrington would have stopped their talk, chosen another subject, and discoursed about the Opera, or the last debate in Parliament, or Miss Jones's marriage with Captain Smith, or what not—so, let us imagine that the public steps in at this juncture, stops the confidential talk between author and reader, and begs us to resume our remarks about this world, with which both are certainly better acquainted than with that other one into which we have just been peeping.

On coming into his property, Arthur Pendennis at first comported himself with a modesty and equanimity which obtained his friend Warrington's praises, though Arthur's uncle was a little inclined to quarrel with his nephew's meanness of spirit for not assuming greater state and pretensions now that he had entered on the enjoyment of his kingdom. He would have had Arthur installed in handsome quarters, riding on showy park hacks, or in well-built cabriolets, every day. "I am too absent," Arthur said, with a laugh, "to drive a cab in London; the omnibus would cut me in two, or I should send my horse's head into the ladies' carriage-windows; and you wouldn't have me driven about by my servant like an apothecary, uncle?" No, Major Pendennis would on no account have his nephew appear like an apothecary; the august representative of the house of Pendennis must not so demean himself. And when Arthur, pursuing his banter, said, "And yet, I dare say, sir, my father was proud enough when he first set up his gig," the old Major hemmed and ha'd, and his wrinkled face reddened with a blush as he answered, "You know what Buonaparte said, sir, 'Il faut laver son linge sale en famille.' There is no need, sir, for you to brag that your father was a—a medical man. He came of a most ancient but fallen house, and was obliged to reconstruct the family fortunes as many a man of good family has done before him. You are like the fellow in Sterne, sir—the Marquis who came to demand his sword again. Your father got back yours for you. You are a man of landed estate, by Gad, sir, and a gentleman—never forget you are a gentleman."

Then Arthur slily turned on his uncle the argument which he had heard the old gentleman often use regarding himself. "In the society which I have the honor of frequenting through your introduction, who cares to ask about my paltry means or my humble gentility, uncle?" he asked. "It would be absurd of me to attempt to compete with the great folks; and all that they can ask from us is, that we should have a decent address and good manners."
