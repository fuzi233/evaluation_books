The pang, the curse with which they died,
Had never passed away:
I could not draw my eyes from theirs,
Nor lift them up to pray.

The Mediterranean dawn seeped slow and pale through the hotel cracks, and Deronda had not taken off his clothes once all night. He had barely moved from the chair by Gwendolen's door, his coat still damp at the cuffs from pulling her half-drowned from the waves, the half-moon marks of her fingernails still blotted purple into his wrist where she had clung to him, refusing to let the maids undress her or tend her until she had looked him full in the face again. When she had finally consented to rest, she had only whispered to him, voice thin and tight with suppressed urgency, one request: he must promise he would come to her the second she sent for him come morning.

He could not shake his foreboding. She might descend into fever at any moment, might break into wild raving that would betray the terrible, secret thing he had seen flash in her eyes the second her husband’s empty drifting boat had been towed back to shore. He told her lady’s maid he would remain on call all night, made it clear to all the staff that he was in direct correspondence with her grieving family back in England, and had taken temporary guardianship of her welfare—an easy role to step into, for Grandcourt’s only remaining valet, who had sailed the whole voyage with them, knew Deronda well enough not to question his right to stand watch.

When the tangled, heavy fatigue of the day’s sharp, overlapping emotions finally pulled him under, his dreams were a knotted mess of half-memories: the click of the roulette wheel in Leubronn, Gwendolen’s proud, sharp smile before she lost her fortune, his own mother’s trembling confession of the past he never knew, and finally he woke with a jolt, half-convinced her voice was calling him from the other side of the door.

No summons had come. The fact lightened his chest as he washed and dressed, and he told himself it was too early to send a maid to check on her. When he did inquire an hour later, he learned she had stayed wakeful most of the night, no violent sobbing, no screaming fits, just quiet, rigid rest, and had finally fallen asleep only half an hour prior. He marveled at the stubborn strength of her, this girl who once would have crumpled at a single harsh word, holding herself together by pure will, determined to hide something that ate at her from the inside out. For his own part, the raw emotion of his recent meeting with his mother had left his sympathies feeling dulled, distant, as if his care for Gwendolen was a visit to an old familiar grief rather than a new, urgent weight settling on his shoulders.

He passed the rest of the morning securing formal, signed statements from the fishermen who had rescued her. Their accounts were bare of extra details: Grandcourt’s little tender had been found adrift, sail flapping loose, no sign of the man at all. They told Deronda they thought he had been knocked overboard by the swinging canvas when he turned the boat, and could not swim. They had been some distance out when they first heard a man’s cry for help, had been rowing as fast as they could when they heard a woman’s shriek, and looked up to see Gwendolen hurl herself over the side before they could reach the spot.

When he stepped back inside the hotel, the maid met him at once to say Gwendolen was up, and asking for him.

He was led into a room draped in dark velvet curtains, blinds pulled tight so only a thin silver sliver of dawn filtered through the slats. She sat wrapped in a frayed white cashmere shawl, her eyes fixed on the door like a creature waiting for its judgment, her hair coiled neatly at the nape of her neck even after the nightmare of the waves, the little blue star earrings he had first seen glint in her ears at that Leubronn gaming table still in place. When she stood impulsively to greet him, her whole body sheathed in that pale shawl, her face and neck as white as linen save for the dark purple bruises under her eyes, her lips parted like someone already condemned and helpless, she looked exactly like the unhappy ghost of that proud, sharp girl who had turned away from her losses at the roulette table with her chin held high. The sight pierced him so deep all their old memories flooded back at once, and his dulled sympathies flared hot and raw again.

“Please, rest—don’t stand,” he said, hurrying to her side, and she obeyed, falling back into the deep armchair.

“Will you sit close to me?” she whispered. “I need to speak very low.”

He pulled a small wooden chair right up next to her, close enough that their knees almost brushed. The small, quiet gesture made her pale face turn fully to his, their faces inches apart, and she breathed, in the lowest audible tone, “You know I am a guilty woman.”

Deronda’s own face blanched. “I know nothing,” he said, and dared not say more.

“He is dead,” she said, same steady, suppressed undertone.

“Yes,” Deronda answered, his voice thick with mournful hesitation.

“No one else will ever see his face under the water but me,” she said, hands clenched tight in her lap. “I will never get that sight out of my head.”

It was not delirium. She was perfectly, horribly lucid, and determined to spill every last secret to the only person she trusted not to cast her out. Deronda dreaded what she was about to say, as if the full weight of her soul, sharp with guilt, was about to fall directly onto his shoulders. But she leaned closer, urgency in her whisper: “You will not tell me I ought to confess to the whole world? You will not say I deserve to be disgraced? I could not bear it. Not even if I were dead, I could not let my mother back in England find out. I have to tell you, but no one else. No one.”

“I can say nothing in my ignorance,” Deronda said, sorrowful, “except that I will help you, whatever you need.”

“I told you that from the very start,” the soft, piteous murmur came, “the first time I could, I told you I was afraid of myself. That hatred in me gnawed like an evil spirit, always hatching new plans, every thought of escape growing darker. That is why I begged you to come to me in town before the voyage. I meant to tell you the worst then. I tried. But he walked in before I could get the words out.”

A shudder wracked her, and she went on, “Do you think a woman who cried, and prayed, and fought every day to save herself from her own wicked thoughts… could be a murderess?”

“Great God,” Deronda’s voice shook, deep and rough. “Do not torture me needlessly. You threw yourself into the water to try and save him. This was an accident you could not have prevented.”

“Don’t be impatient with me.” The childlike, trembling beseechment in her words made Deronda turn his head to look at her face. Her poor quivering lips kept moving. “You used to tell me once, that you felt most for the people who had done wrong and were miserable, that they were the ones who could heal, if they were given gentle care. If you had never said that, all of this would have been so much worse. I remembered every word. It was the only thing that kept me going through all those lonely nights on the yacht. If you turn away from me now, forsake me… I have nothing left. Am I worse than the girl you found at the gaming table, who wanted to get better? All that wrong was already in me then. If you had never come, and been patient with me, I would have fallen so much further. Will you leave me?”

Her tightly clenched hands fell limp, trembling, on the arm of her chair. Her lips parted, waiting. Deronda could not find words to answer. He turned his gaze away, reached out, and laced his fingers through one of her cold shaking hands, holding it as gently as if they were two lost children walking through the dark together. It was his only answer: I will never forsake you. All the while he felt as if he were signing his name to a blank page, that might soon be filled with something terrible enough to break him.

That steady, tender grip was something no one had ever given Gwendolen before—no unselfish, gentle care that did not ask anything in return—and it felt to her like a promise of endless patience. The strength it gave her let her pour out the rest of the story, disjointed, wandering, every memory flooding out at once: the dark hateful wishes she had hidden for months, the sharp little dagger she had bought once, hidden in her dressing case, the key to it she had dropped over the side of the yacht weeks prior, to try to rid herself of the temptation, the plan she had made to get the case opened the second they landed in Genoa, so she could find Deronda alone and tell him everything. She broke into her first sob when she spoke of turning the hotel stairs that morning, seeing Deronda, so close to finally confessing, before Grandcourt had dragged her off to sail before she could get a single word out.

Deronda tried to tell her all those dark plans had stayed in her head, that she had never acted on them, but she shook her head, wiped the tears off her cheeks, and swore she would not lie, she would tell him the full truth, exactly as it happened. She told him she never should have married Grandcourt, that she had broken a promise to another woman, chased wealth and pleasure for herself, and got nothing but cold, endless misery, every luxury she had ever bought burning her like hot coals. The only thing that had kept her going, the only thing that had not felt like punishment, was the thought of him.

She told him of that terrible boat ride, how furious she was to be robbed of her chance to speak to him, locked alone on the open water with the man she hated, how all the old dark wishes flooded back, how she had cried out “God help me” and released the tiller for a second, but the cruel longings had come back anyway. Then the gust of wind hit. The sail swung. Grandcourt was knocked overboard.

She leaned in so her breath brushed his ear, whispering every last terrible second of that moment: the way Grandcourt had surfaced, screaming for the rope, the way she had held the coiled rope in her hand, her heart screaming *Die*, and she did not throw it. Then the horror of what she had done had crashed over her, and she had hurled herself over the side, desperate to undo it, but it was already too late. His dead face was bobbing right in front of her in the waves, and she would never be able to unsee it.

She wrenched her hand from his, threw her arms up over her head with a ragged moan. “I have been a cruel woman! What can I do but cry for help? I am sinking. Go down, go down into the dark. No pity. No one will ever care.” She sank back into the chair, breaking into heaving sobs, and for a second Deronda did not exist to her at all.

He was completely unmanned. The raw, raging grief of her confession pierced him far deeper than he could have imagined, coming so close on the heels of his own mother’s terrible secrets, and he knew at that moment he would never choose pleasure for himself again. He would live only to carry the weight of her grief, to help her heal. He had to turn his back on her and walk to the far side of the room for a minute to collect himself.

When he turned back, she was staring at him, eyes wide and dilated, lips parted, timid and forlorn, terrified that he had already forsaken her. Their eyes met fully for the first time since she had said she was guilty, and that long, sad, steady glance told her everything he could not put into words: I know. I will not leave you.

She gave a soft, regretful whisper, her voice soft with love and remorse. “I make you very unhappy.”

Deronda shook his head, gathering his resolve. “There is no question of being happy or unhappy right now. All I want is what will help you most. Tell me anything you feel you need to tell, and I will listen.”

He sat back down beside her, and did not move away, and the last of the terrible secret weight she had carried for months finally began to lift, for he knew the worst of her, and he was not going anywhere.
