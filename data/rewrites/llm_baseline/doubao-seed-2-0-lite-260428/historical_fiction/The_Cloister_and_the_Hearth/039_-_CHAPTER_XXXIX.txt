Rough, unscripted travel holds a strange allure for the English, and the old journals of wandering wayfarers fascinate us still—even when the adventurer is not a pilgrim bound for love. And deep, long-held friendship carries the quiet, solid interest of a fossil unearthed after centuries. But since the true heart of our tale lies in Holland, it is high time we turn back there, to the ordinary people and small, familiar incidents that make up most of human life in every age.

Jorian Ketel came knocking at Peter’s cottage door in Sevenbergen to demand Margaret keep the promise she had made him, only to find her bedridden with a raging fever. The moment Peter heard his errand, he flew into a rage, turned the man out of his house with sharp insults, and the neighbours loitering at the gate threatened to duck him in the village duckpond. Both Peter and Margaret were beloved across the burgh, the whole story of Gerard and their broken betrothal was on every tongue, and Sevenbergen roiled with the hot, unthinking public sympathy that rises to defend its own. Jorian Ketel stormed off in a huff, and regretted ever having done that small good turn for the pair. That sort of repentance is far more common than people admit, and far more sincere than most other kinds.

Not long after, Dierich Brower—Ghysbrecht Van Swieten’s loyal spy—was caught at the “Three Kings” tavern, plying a local stable hand with drink to worm out the hiding place of Martin Wittenhaagen. The villagers seized him on the spot, dumped him headfirst in the horse pond, and threatened far worse if he ever dared show his face in Sevenbergen again. Spurred on by this trespass on their jurisdiction, the Sevenbergen burgomaster sent a formal, stiffly worded letter to his counterpart in Tergou, reminding him he had overstepped the law, and demanding he appeal to Sevenbergen’s own authorities in future if he had any complaint, real or imagined, against one of their townsfolk.

The wily old Ghysbrecht hid his seething rage, sent back a polite reply claiming the man he had pursued to Sevenbergen was a native Tergovian named Gerard, who had stolen critical town records. Since Gerard had fled across the border with the documents, he wrote, the entire matter was closed. He made a virtue of necessity, but his apparent calm was only a thin mask. Foiled at Sevenbergen, he set his spies to question Gerard’s family back in Tergou to find where the young man had run off—only to his infinite surprise, none of them knew. This unnerved him more than ever. He began to fear Gerard was only hiding somewhere close by, biding his time to uncover certain dark secrets and return to take a terrible revenge. From that day on, Dierich and the other servants who waited on him noticed a stark change for the worse in Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. He grew moody, snappish, and haunted. He would cast furtive, darting glances over his shoulder at every sound, like a man waiting for a blow he cannot see coming from any direction. Making other people miserable had never made him happy, and it seldom does.

The small household in Tergou, that would have mended its differences in time without banishing its brightest young hope to the far corners of the world if not for Ghysbrecht’s meddling, still looked the same on the surface—but the simple, happy family the tale opened with was gone, hollowed out from within. Little Kate knew full well the part her half-brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt had played in driving Gerard away. Though she dared not tell her mother for fear of stirring up even more trouble, there were days she shuddered to look at the two of them, and blushed to hear their false, hypocritical expressions of grief. Catherine, sharp as only a mother can be, noticed this at once, and said nothing, only watched and waited quietly for more proof. The two black sheep, for all their efforts to grieve convincingly enough to fool their father and their brother Giles, could not hide the quiet, secret satisfaction glowing beneath their performance from the two sharp-eyed women of the house, who noted every small shift while seeming to notice nothing at all. Mistrust and suspicion now sat at the family dinner table, a poor, bitter substitute for Gerard’s bright, quick wit that had lit up the whole circle before they knew how much they valued it.

As for the old hosier, his pride had been stung by his son’s disobedience, so he bore himself stiffly, and did his best never to speak Gerard’s name. But under that tough, Spartan stoicism, anyone could see Nature yanking hard at his heartstrings. He never tried to hide his one true anxiety. “If only I knew where the boy is, and that he’s safe and well, I would scarcely care about anything else,” he would say, then heave a deep, shaking sigh. It is plain to see that if Gerard had stepped through the door that very moment, he would have been met with tears and embraces, and scarcely a single reproach.

One thing caught the old couple entirely by surprise: how far the story had spread. Less than a week after Gerard fled, his adventures were on every tongue in Tergou, and public sympathy landed firmly on the side of the two lovers, against his cruel parents and the interfering old burgomaster who had stuck his nose into a business that was none of his. “Mother,” Kate said softly one day, “the whole town says Margaret is laid low with a burning fever, and her father fears she will die.” “Margaret? What Margaret?” Catherine asked, putting on a false air of calm indifference. “Oh, mother, who else would I mean? Gerard’s Margaret.” “Gerard’s Margaret!” Catherine shrieked. “How dare you speak that hussy’s name in this house, the girl who has ruined my poor boy, the flower of all my flock. She is the reason he is not a holy priest right here with us, but wandering the wide world while I am a desolate, broken-hearted mother. There, don’t cry, my girl, I am wrong to speak harsh to you. But Kate, you do not know what lives in a mother’s heart. I put on a brave face for all of you, I force myself to swallow all my fears—but at night I see him in my dreams, and there is always some danger close by. Sometimes wild beasts tear at him. Other times he is in the hands of robbers, their cruel knives raised to strike his poor pale face, that even a stone would pity. When I think that while I sit here warm and comfortable, my boy might be lying dead in some savage place, all because of that girl—her very name is poison to me. I shake all over when I hear it.” “I will never say anything to grieve you, mother,” Kate said tenderly, though she sighed.

The girl whose name was forbidden in Tergou was widely spoken of, and deeply pitied, everywhere else. All Sevenbergen grieved for her, and the village’s young men and women would cast pitying glances at the small window where their favourite lay wasting away, as they put it, “dying for love.” That common phrase underestimated her courage and her unselfishness. She knew Gerard was not dead, and was far too loyal to doubt his constancy. She loved her helpless old father dearly, and if she had not been brought so low by illness, she would have tended to all her duties no matter how heavy her heart felt. But the strain of months of anxiety and excitement had brought on a fever so violent only her youth and strong constitution saved her. When the fever finally broke, it left her in that terrible state of utter weakness where life feels nothing but a burden. At times like that, love and friendship at the bedside are like guardian angels, with comfort on their tongues and healing in their hands—but this poor girl had to drag herself back to health alone. For days she lay by herself, the hours rolling over her like heavy leaden waves. She could not find the will to get well. Gerard was gone, and there was no one to get well for. Often she lay still for hours, tears spilling gently out of her eyes and down her cheeks.

One day, she woke from a restless nap to find two women in her room. One was a servant, the other, with deep fur lining on her collar and sleeves, was a woman of high standing. A thin band of silver hair peeking out from under her headdress showed she was well past the age where sensible women bother to hide their years. Both of their faces were kind. Margaret tried to sit up, but the older woman laid a gentle hand on her shoulder. “Lie still, sweetheart. We haven’t come to trouble you, only to comfort you, God willing. Cheer up a little now—can you guess who we are?” “Nay, madam, I know you, though I never met you before,” Margaret said softly. “You are Mistress Van Eyck, and this is Reicht Heynes. Gerard spoke of you often, and how good you were to him. Madam, he has no friend like you near him now.” The thought made her lie back, and tears flooded her eyes at once.

Good-natured Reicht started crying in sympathy, but her mistress chided her. “A fine thing you are to have in a sick room!” she laughed, and set about gently distracting and cheering the girl, with great success. An old woman who has seen every sort of trouble in the world is a blessing beyond price at the side of a sorrowful young one: she knows exactly what to say, and exactly what to avoid, how to soothe and how to interest. Before an hour was up, Margaret’s head was resting on her shoulder instead of her pillow, and her soft, grateful eyes were fixed on the older woman’s face. “Ah, what lovely hair this is,” Dame Van Eyck said, running her fingers through it. “Come look at it, Reicht!” Reicht came and handled it, praising it sincerely. The poor girl who owned it was not so far gone as to be immune to kindness. “Truly, madam, I used to think it was ugly,” she said, “but he praised it, and ever since then I have almost been vain of it, saints forgive me. You know how foolish people in love are.” “They are far bigger fools who are not in love,” the old woman said sharply. Margaret blinked, and looked at her in surprise.

That was only the first of many visits. Mistress Van Eyck or Reicht came to see her almost every day until she was well on the way to recovery, and she improved rapidly under their care. Reicht attributed the change to the nourishing stews and broths she cooked up in Peter’s kitchen, but Margaret knew it was the kind words, the steady proof that she had friends worth living for, that healed her fastest.

Meanwhile, Martin Wittenhaagen had set out for Rotterdam to take the bull by the horns. That bull was no farm animal, but Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, Count of Flanders, and lord of half the Low Countries. When he arrived, Martin learned the court was at Ghent, so he rode straight there to ask for an audience—only to be turned away and fobbed off by pages and lackeys at every turn. So he hid along the edge of the hunting trail the king’s party would pass, and when Philip rode by, he stepped out in front of the whole procession and roared at the top of his lungs for mercy. “Why man, what peril are you in?” the Duke asked, reining in his horse and laughing, glancing around him. “Grace for an old soldier who is being hunted down by burghers!”

Kings may differ from one another in a hundred ways, but they all share one trait: they are almost always surprisingly affable to people of very low station. Such men never compete with them for rank or power, so there is no jealousy between them. Their blunt, unpolished speech amuses royal ears long starved of unscripted, natural words in the stiff, formal court. So Philip the Good drew his horse to a stand, and gave Martin his full, undivided attention. Martin reminded him of a long-ago battlefield, where he had jumped in front of the Duke and taken an arrow meant for him. The Duke remembered the incident perfectly, and was very pleased to hear it recounted (all the more so for not being the one who got shot). Then Martin told him the whole story: of how Gerard had been seized in the church, locked in the Tergou tower, how the two of them had broken him out, and every last detail of the wild manhunt that had followed. Whether he told the story far better than it is written here, or Philip had heard very few good, unvarnished stories in his life, the Duke became so wrapped up in the tale that when a crowd of courtiers came galloping up to interrupt, he swore like a fishmonger, and threatened half-joking to chop the head off the next man who stepped between him and a good story. When Martin finished, he cried out “St. Luke! What sport goes on in my own earldom, in my very woods, and I never see a bit of it! You common folk have all the luck.” He was genuinely indignant that Fortune had never seen fit to give him a manhunt. “Ah, but I was on the hounds’ side of the chase, not the quarry’s,” Martin said bluntly. “Ah! To be sure, I forgot that,” Philip laughed, and felt much better about his own lot. “What do you want, then?” “A full pardon, your highness, for me and for Gerard.” “For breaking out of prison? Pah, every bird flies its cage when it can—it is instinct. Coop a young man up just for loving a young woman? Those burgomasters have lost their wits entirely. What else?” “For knocking down the burgomaster.” “Oh, a hunted boar will turn at bay, that is his right. I would think less of any man who grudge him that. What else?” “For killing the bloodhounds that were set on us.” The Duke’s face fell. “‘Twas their lives or mine, your highness,” Martin said quickly. “Aye, but I cannot have my bloodhounds, my beautiful bloodhounds, sacrificed to—” “No no no, they were not your hounds. They belonged to the ranger.” “Oh. Well then, I am very sorry for the ranger, but I will not have my old soldiers sacrificed to his hounds. You have your pardon.” “And poor Gerard too?” “And poor Gerard too, for your sake. And more—tell that burgomaster his behaviour displeases me. He acts like he is a king himself, not a petty town official. I will have no kings in Holland but one. Tell him to be more humble, or by St. Jude I will hang him on his own doorstep, same way I hanged that burgomaster in… well, some town in Flanders, no, Brabant, no matter, the name escapes me— I hanged him all the same, for oppressing poor folk.”

He called over his portly chancellor, who rode his horse like a sack of potatoes, and ordered him to draw up the formal free pardon for Martin and the young man named Gerard. The precious document was drawn up that day, signed the next, and Martin rode home as fast as his mule could carry him.

Margaret had been out of bed for a few days, sitting pale and thoughtful by the fireside, when Martin burst through her cottage door, waving the parchment over his head. “A full pardon, lass! For Gerard as well as me! Send for him back whenever you want, no burgomaster alive would dare lay a finger on him.” A bright flush of joy spread over her whole face. Her hands trembled as she took the document, devoured it with her eyes, kissed it again and again, then threw her arms around Martin’s neck and kissed him on the cheek. When she had calmed a little, she told him Heaven had sent her a friend in Mistress Van Eyck. “I would so love to consult her about this wonderful news, but I am still too weak to walk all the way to her house.” “No need to walk,” Martin said. “Take my mule.” “Your mule, Martin?” The old soldier laughed, and confessed he had gotten so used to the beast he had stolen from Ghysbrecht he nearly forgot it belonged to the burgomaster. He would turn it over to Ghysbrecht’s stables tomorrow, he said—but tonight, it would carry Margaret to Mistress Van Eyck’s door. Dusk was falling, so Margaret agreed. By seven that evening, she stepped up to Mistress Van Eyck’s door, her cheeks flushed pink with excitement, the precious pardon tucked safe in her bodice, to the utter shock and delight of her new friend.
