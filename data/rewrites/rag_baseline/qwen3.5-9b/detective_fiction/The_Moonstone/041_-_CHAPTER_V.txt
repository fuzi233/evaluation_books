Betteredge seemed to regard the mention of Mr. Candy's assistant as a trivial detour, a minor footnote in the grand ledger of our investigation. With a dismissive wave, he closed the file on that insignificant thread and returned his gaze to the trembling script of Rosanna Spearman's confession.

While he read, I sat by the window, watching the dust motes dance in the slanting light, waiting for the final period to fall. Yet, as the minutes stretched, the profound disquietude that Ezra Jennings had instilled in me began to recede. It was a paradoxical sensation: in a situation as precarious as mine, the fact that another human being could have left such a distinct, indelible mark on my psyche seemed almost supernatural. Slowly, the image of the chemist faded, replaced by the cold, hard reality of my own predicament. I forced my thoughts back into their familiar, channeled river. I resolved to confront the sheer impossibility of my position, to review the strategy I had finally summoned the composure to devise.

My plan was clear, though born of desperation: return to London immediately; lay the entire case before Mr. Bruff; and, above all, secure a private audience with Rachel—by any means necessary, regardless of the cost. There was still an hour before the train departed. Perhaps, in that brief interlude, Betteredge might stumble upon a detail in the unread portions of Rosanna's letter that I needed to know before leaving the house where the diamond had vanished. I waited, holding my breath for that slim possibility.

The letter concluded with a raw, confessional tone:

"You have no need to be angry, Mr. Franklin, even if I harbored a fleeting triumph in knowing I held your entire future in my grasp. Anxiety and fear quickly reclaimed me. Given Sergeant Cuff's scrutiny regarding the diamond's loss, he would inevitably examine our linens and dresses. There was no sanctuary in my room, no corner of the house, that I could trust to keep the evidence safe from his prying eyes. How to conceal the nightgown so thoroughly that not even the Sergeant could detect it? How to achieve this without wasting a single second of precious time? These were insurmountable questions. My hesitation gave way to a solution that might seem absurd to you. I undressed and wore the nightgown myself. You had worn it, and I derived a small, illicit pleasure in claiming it for a moment after you.

"The next intelligence reaching the servants' hall revealed I had secured the garment too late. Sergeant Cuff demanded the washing book.

"I delivered it to him in my mistress's sitting-room. We had crossed paths many times before; I was certain he would recognize me. I was unsure, however, of his reaction upon discovering a servant in a household where a priceless jewel had disappeared. In that suspended animation, I felt relieved simply to endure the encounter and learn the worst immediately.

"He regarded me with the detachment of a stranger when I handed over the book, yet his politeness in thanking me was peculiarly stiff. These were ominous signs. I feared his whispers behind my back, his sudden arrest, and his search. Just then, you returned from escorting Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite to the station. I retreated to your favorite walk in the shrubbery, seizing what I believed was my final chance to speak to you.

"You never appeared. Worse still, Mr. Betteredge and Sergeant Cuff passed the spot where I hid, and the Sergeant spotted me.

"I had no choice but to retreat to my duties before further catastrophe befell me. As I stepped across the path, you emerged from the railway. You walked straight toward the shrubbery, saw me—I am convinced you saw me—and turned away as if I carried a contagion, retreating into the house.

*"NOTE: By Franklin Blake.—The writer is entirely mistaken, poor creature. I never noticed her. My intention was indeed to stroll in the shrubbery, but recalling simultaneously that my aunt might wish to see me upon my return, I changed my mind and entered the house."

"I returned indoors via the servants' entrance. The laundry was empty, and I sat alone. The haunting thoughts of the Shivering Sand resurfaced. I pondered which burden would prove heavier: enduring Mr. Franklin Blake's indifference, or leaping into the quicksand to end it all.

"It is futile to demand an accounting of my actions at this juncture. I strive to understand, yet I cannot.

"Why did I not stop you when you shunned me in such a cruel fashion? Why did I not cry out, 'Mr. Franklin, I have something vital to say; it concerns you, and you must listen'? You were at my mercy; I held the whip-hand. Moreover, possessing the means to assist you in the future, provided only you trusted me, I could have been invaluable. Naturally, I never imagined you, a gentleman, stole the diamond merely for sport. No. Penelope had heard Miss Rachel, and I had heard Mr. Betteredge, discuss your extravagance and debts. It was evident you had taken the diamond to sell or pledge it for cash. Well! I could have directed you to a man in London who would advance a substantial sum for the jewel without asking awkward questions.

"Why did I not speak? Why did I not speak!

"Do the risks of concealing the nightgown exceed my capacity? This might be true for others, but how could it apply to me? In my days as a thief, I navigated dangers fifty times greater, finding escape routes from complexities that this current dilemma seems mere child's play compared to. I was apprenticed to grand-scale frauds, some so clever they graced the newspapers. Could such a trifling task as hiding a nightgown weigh upon my spirit, causing my heart to sink, when I should have been addressing you? Such a query is nonsense. It could not be.

"Where is the point of dwelling on my own folly? The truth is plain. Behind your back, I loved you with every fiber of my being. Before your face, I was terrified. Terrified of angering you, terrified of your response—even if you had taken the diamond—if I presumed to reveal I had discovered it. I approached the limit of my daring when I spoke in the library. You did not turn away then; you did not flee as if I bore disease. I attempted to provoke myself into anger, to summon courage. No! I could feel only misery and humiliation. 'You're a plain girl; you have a crooked shoulder; you're only a housemaid—what right do you have to address Me?' You never uttered these words, Mr. Franklin; yet you said them all the same. Is such madness explicable? No. There remains only confession.

"I apologize once more for this digression of my pen. Fear not its recurrence. I am nearing the end.

"The first to disturb my solitude was Penelope. She had discerned my secret long ago and endeavored gently to restore my sense.

*'Ah!' she said, 'I know why you sit here, fretting alone. The best outcome for your advantage, Rosanna, is the conclusion of Mr. Franklin's visit. I believe he departs shortly.'*

"In all my reveries concerning you, I never considered your departure. I could not meet Penelope's gaze; I could only stare.

*'I have just left Miss Rachel,' Penelope continued. 'A difficult ordeal it was to withstand her temper. She finds the house unbearable with the police present and intends to speak to my lady this evening, visiting her Aunt Ablewhite tomorrow. Should she proceed, Mr. Franklin will undoubtedly find a pretext to depart.'*

"My tongue regained its function. 'Do you mean Mr. Franklin will leave with her?' I asked.

*'Only too gladly, if she permits it; but she will not. _He_ has felt her temper; _he_ is in her black books, despite his efforts to aid her, poor fellow! No! If they do not reconcile by tomorrow, you will witness Miss Rachel go one way and Mr. Franklin another. His destination is uncertain, but he will never remain here after Miss Rachel leaves.'*

"I mastered the despair rising within me at the prospect of your departure. Admitting the truth, I glimpsed a sliver of hope if a serious rift truly existed between Miss Rachel and you. 'Do you know,' I asked, 'the nature of their quarrel?'

*'It lies entirely on Miss Rachel's side,' Penelope replied. 'For all I know, it is solely her temper. I hesitate to distress you, Rosanna, but do not imagine Mr. Franklin is likely to quarrel with _her_. He loves her too dearly for that!'*

"She had barely spoken these cruel words when Mr. Betteredge summoned us. All indoor servants were to assemble in the hall, then enter Mr. Betteredge's room individually to be questioned by Sergeant Cuff.

"When my turn arrived, following the lady's maid and the upper housemaid, Cuff's inquiries, though veiled, revealed that those two women—my bitterest enemies—had uncovered clues outside my door on Tuesday afternoon and again Thursday night. They had provided enough to alert him to partial truths. He correctly surmised I had sewn a new nightgown secretly, but erroneously assumed the paint-stained one belonged to me. From his words, I deduced another unsettling certainty: he suspected my involvement in the diamond's disappearance, yet deliberately signaled that he did not view me as primarily responsible. He seemed to believe I acted under someone else's direction. Who that person was, I could not guess then, nor now.
