The snow outside the town was a fresh, unbroken blanket, save for the chaotic tracks of innumerable wolves that had trampled every foot of the road into a muddy scarlet tapestry.

"We did well to heed the old man's counsel, Denys," Gerard murmured, his voice barely rising above the crunch of hooves and falling flakes.

"Aye, we did," Denys replied, his breath pluming in the freezing air. "For now that I recall, I heard them last night scurrying beneath our window, howling and whining for man's flesh in that market-place. Yet not a single fat burgher showed pity, dropping silver from a window to buy off the poor vagabones."

Gerard offered a smile, but it lacked warmth, clouded by an air of profound abstraction. They continued their plodding journey in a heavy silence, the weight of the world pressing down upon their shoulders.

"What dost thou meditate upon so profoundly?" Denys asked, breaking the quiet.

"Thy goodness," Gerard answered softly.

Denys bristled, his good humor evaporating instantly. Among his many quirks, he possessed a tolerance for genuine impertinence, a trait born of his inherent kindness. Yet, he would flare up at times where not even the shadow of a cause for anger existed.

"A civil question merits a civil reply," he said, his tone dry and sharp.

"Alas, I meant no other," Gerard pleaded.

"Then why pretend thou wert contemplating my virtue, knowing full well there is no goodness beneath my skin?" Denys demanded, his eyes flashing.

"Had another spoken thus, I would have retorted, 'Thou liest,'" Gerard countered gently. "But to thee, I say, 'Thou hast no eye for men's qualities, but only for women's.' Once more, I defy thy unreasonable choler and affirm I was thinking of thy goodness of yesterday. Thou wouldst have wedded me to the 'Tête d'Or,' or rather to the 'tête de veau dorée,' and left thyself solitary."

"Oh, are ye there, lad?" Denys recovered his cheerfulness in a heartbeat. "Well, to speak the truth, I meant it not for goodness, but for friendship and true fellowship, no more. And let me confess, my young master, my conscience pricks me even now for allowing thee to turn thy back upon fortune and peaceful days. A truer friend than I might have taken thee aside and somewhat hamstrung thee. Then hadst thou been fain to lie smarting at the 'Tête d'Or' for a month or so; yon skittish lass would have nursed thee tenderly, and all had been well. I had the blade in hand to do it, but remembering how thou hatest pain, though it be but a scratch, my craven heart failed me at the pinch." Denys wore a look of humble apology, as if lacking virtuous resolution when the path of duty lay so clear was a sin in itself.

Gerard raised his eyebrows in astonishment at this monstrous yet thoroughly characteristic revelation. This new, delicate point of friendship—the question of whether one ought, in all love, to sever the tendon of Achilles of one's friend—was never discussed further. An incident intervened.

"Here cometh one in our rear, riding on his neighbor's mule!" Denys shouted.

Gerard turned. "And how knowest 'tis not his own, pray?"

"Oh, blind fool! Because he rides it with no discretion."

In truth, the man came galloping like a fury. But what astonished the friends most was that upon reaching them, the rustic rider's eyes widened saucer-like. He drew the rein so suddenly and powerfully that the mule stumbled, sticking out her forelegs before sliding between the pedestrians like a four-legged table on castors.

"I trow ye are from the 'Tête d'Or'?" the stranger asked, panting. They assented. "Which of ye is the younger?"

"He that was born the later," Denys winked at his companion.

"Gramercy for the news."

"Come, divine then!"

"And shall. Thy beard is ripe, thy fellow's is green; he shall be the younger. Here, youngster." He held out a paper packet. "Ye left this at the 'Tête d'Or,' and our mistress sends it ye."

"Nay, good fellow, methinks I left nought." Gerard felt his pouch, confirming his emptiness.

"Would ye make our burgess a liar?" the rustic reproached, his voice dripping with indignation. "And shall I have no *pourboire*?" He repeated the demand, even more reproachfully, and charged forward, belly to earth.

"Nay, thou shalt have *pourboire*," Gerard said, pulling a small coin from his belt and tossing it.

"*A la bonne heure*!" cried the clown, his features beaming with disproportionate joy. "The Virgin go with ye; come up, Jenny!" Back he went, stomach to earth, as his nation is pleased to call it.

Gerard undid the packet. It was about six inches square, and inside it he found another packet, which contained a packet, and so on. At the fourth layer, he hurled the whole thing into the snow. Denys took it out and rebuked his petulance. He excused himself on the grounds of hating affectation.

Denys attested, "'The great toe of the little daughter of Herodias'—there was no affectation here, but only woman's good wit. Doubtless the wraps contained something which, out of delicacy, or her sex's lovely cunning, she would not have her hind see bestowed upon a young man; thy garter, to wit."

"I wear none."

"Her own then; or a lock of her hair. What is this? A piece of raw silk, fresh from the worm. Well, of all the love tokens!"

"Now who but thee ever dreamed that she is so naught as to send me love tokens? I saw no harm in her—barring her hands."

"Stay, here is something hard lurking in this soft nest. Come forth, I say, little nestling! Saints and pike-staves! Look at this!"

It was a gold ring with a great amethyst glowing and sparkling, full-colored yet pure as crystal.

"How lovely!" said Gerard innocently.

"And here is something writ; read it thou! I read not so glib as some, when I know not the matter beforehand."

Gerard took the paper. "'Tis a posy, and fairly enough writ." He read the lines, blushing like a girl. They were very naive, and may be thus Englished:

*'Youth, with thee my heart is fledde,
Come back to the golden Hedde!
Wilt not? yet this token keep
Of hir who doeth thy going weep.
Gyf the world prove harsh and cold,
Come back to 'the Hedde of gold.'*"

"The little dove!" purred Denys.

"The great owl! To go and risk her good name thus. However, thank Heaven she has played this prank with an honest lad that will ne'er expose her folly. But oh, the perverseness! Could she not bestow her nauseousness on thee?" Denys sighed and shrugged. "On thee that art as ripe for folly as herself?"

Denys confessed that his young friend had harped his very thoughts. 'Twas passing strange to him that a damsel with eyes in her head should pass by a man and bestow her affections on a boy. Still, he could not but recognize in this the bounty of Nature. Boys were human beings after all, and but for this occasional caprice of women, their lot would be too terrible; they would be out of the sun altogether, blighted, and never come to anything; since only the fair could make a man out of such unpromising materials as a boy.

Gerard interrupted this flattering discourse to beg the warrior-philosopher's acceptance of the lady's ring. He refused it flatly, insisting on Gerard going back to the "Tête d'Or" at once, ring and all, like a man, and not letting a poor girl hold out her arms to him in vain.

"Her hands, you mean."

"Her hand, with the 'Tête d'Or' in it."

Failing in this, he was for putting the ring on his friend's finger. Gerard declined. "I wear a ring already."

"What, that sorry gimcrack? Why, 'tis pewter, or tin at best: and this virgin gold, forbye the jewel."

"Aye, but 'twas Margaret gave me this one; and I value it above rubies. I'll neither part with it nor give it a rival," and he kissed the base metal, bidding it fear nought.

"I see the owl hath sent her ring to a goose," said Denys sorrowfully. However, he prevailed on Gerard to fasten it inside his bonnet. To this, indeed, the lad consented very readily. For sovereign qualities were universally ascribed to certain jewels; and the amethyst ranked high among these precious talismans.
