Happiness, it seems, depends less on what lies outside us than on what stirs within. This may sound like a worn-out truth, but I mention it because I know full well that Mr. Helstone and Mr. Moore stepped out from the mill-yard gates—leading their small band of men—with spirits as high as the stars above. When the lantern light caught Mr. Moore’s face, his eyes sparkled with an unusual animation, and a new energy lit up his dark features. And when the rector’s visage was revealed by the same flickering glow, his usually stern lines softened into a broad grin, alive with glee. Yet here they were, braving a cold, drizzling night and a treacherous journey—circumstances one might think would dampen even the most hopeful soul. If any of the workers stranded on Stilbro’ Moor had glimpsed this party, they might have been tempted to take aim from behind a stone wall. And indeed, both leaders knew that danger lurked in the shadows. But rather than fear, they felt a kind of fierce exhilaration—a thrill born of knowing how close they were to conflict, and how ready they were for it.

I know, dear reader, that you may be quick to point out how unseemly it is for a clergyman to revel in such boldness. You may remind me that a parson should be peaceable, gentle, forgiving. I do not dispute that a minister’s calling is one of mercy and moral guidance. I understand well whose servant he is, whose message he carries, whose example he ought to follow. But let me say plainly: if you despise clergymen, if you see them only as hypocrites or tools of oppression, then you need not expect me to join your chorus of condemnation. I will not rise with you in scornful lamentations, nor echo your bitter denunciations against “the cloth.” I will not raise my hands in horror at the rector of Briarfield as though he were some devil incarnate.

He was no devil. The fault, if there was one, lay not in his nature but in his fate—he had been born into the wrong vocation. He should have been a soldier. Circumstance had made him a priest. That was all. For the rest, he was a man of principle, of grit, of courage. Hard-hearted, perhaps; unyielding, yes; lacking in tenderness, certainly. But he was honest, loyal, and steadfast. A man who did not bend easily, yet never broke. He was not without flaws—prejudices ran deep, and sympathy came rarely—but he stood firm where others wavered. And so, while I may not condone every act of his life, I cannot condemn him either. Not when he walked with integrity, however rigid.

Nor shall I curse Helstone, though many in his parish did just that. Some adored him; others loathed him. Such is often the lot of those who love fiercely and hate just as fiercely, who cling to their beliefs with the same tenacity they wield against their enemies. Men like him are neither wholly good nor wholly bad—they simply choose sides, and once chosen, stand fast.

Helstone and Moore, riding side by side now, were both in high spirits, united in purpose. One might expect them to speak amiably. But no—these two men, both of strong tempers and sharp minds, found little comfort in each other’s company. Their conversations were more often clashes than exchanges. The war was their constant battleground.

Helstone was a staunch Tory, loyal to tradition and monarchy. Moore, though English-born, leaned toward Whig ideals—especially when it came to opposing the war. His politics were not born of ideology, but necessity. He believed Bonaparte invincible, England weak, Europe helpless. He mocked Wellington as a dull, mechanical general, led by a government too ignorant to know its own strength. He argued coolly that surrender to Napoleon was inevitable—and better sooner than later.

Helstone could not abide such talk. It offended him deeply. Yet he refrained from violence—not out of kindness, but because Moore was, in his way, an outsider. Half-Flemish blood ran in his veins, and Helstone respected that. He also admired Moore’s stubborn consistency—the dogged way he held fast to his opinions, even when they angered everyone around him. So he endured the insults, though he burned inside.

As they turned onto the road to Stilbro’, the wind met them head-on, driving rain into their faces. Moore, stirred by the chill air and the sting of the downpour, began again.

“Still pleased with your Peninsular news?” he asked.

“What do you mean?” Helstone replied, stiff and curt.

“I mean—do you still trust that wooden idol of yours, Lord Wellington?”

“And what do you mean now?” the rector snapped.

“Do you still believe this hollow-faced, heartless figure can summon fire from heaven to burn the French army you’re so eager to sacrifice?”

“I believe Wellington will drive Bonaparte’s marshals into the sea the moment he chooses.”

“But you can’t truly believe that,” Moore said, amused. “Bonaparte’s generals are brilliant men, guided by a genius of unmatched power. Your Wellington? A plodding martinet, hampered by a clueless government.”

“Wellington is the soul of England,” Helstone insisted. “He is the rightful champion of a nation that is strong, determined, sensible, and honest.”

“And what is your ‘good cause,’ pray?” Moore countered. “The return of Ferdinand—weak, corrupt, unworthy—to a throne he disgraced? And your hero? A dull farmer’s agent, acting for an even duller master? Against them stands genius, victory, and supremacy.”

“Against legitimacy stands usurpation,” Helstone retorted. “Against quiet, righteous resistance stands arrogant ambition. God defends the right!”

“God often defends the powerful,” Moore replied.

“Oh?” Helstone challenged. “Then tell me—was the handful of Israelites standing dry-shod on the eastern shore of the Red Sea more powerful than Pharaoh’s vast army? Were they stronger? Better armed? More numerous? No. They were slaves, worn down by centuries of bondage. Women and children thinned their ranks. They marched barefoot, armed with crooks and tools. Their leader carried only a rod. And yet—what triumphed? The right! The Lord delivered Israel from the Egyptians. The waters closed over the enemy. ‘The depths covered them, they sank to the bottom as a stone.’ The right hand of the Lord became glorious in power!”

“You forget the true parallel,” Moore said quietly. “France is Israel. Napoleon is Moses. Europe, with its decaying empires and rotten dynasties, is Egypt. France, bold and free, is the Twelve Tribes. And her great Usurper—the Shepherd of Horeb.”

“I scorn to answer you,” Helstone said.

But Moore answered himself, lowering his voice. “Oh, in Italy he was magnificent—greater than any Moses. He was born for leadership, for revolution, for renewal. I still wonder how the conqueror of Lodi could stoop to become emperor—a fool’s title, a vulgar farce. And how a people who once called themselves republicans could descend so shamefully back into slavery. I despise France. If England had advanced as far as France did, she would never have retreated so disgracefully.”

“You don’t mean to say imperial France is worse than republican France?” Helstone demanded, furious.

“I mean nothing. But I can think what I please—about revolutions, regicides, restorations, divine right, non-resistance, war—”

His words were cut short by the sudden roll of a gig approaching rapidly. It stopped abruptly in the middle of the road. Both men had been so absorbed in their argument that they hadn’t noticed it coming.

“Nah, maister—did th’ wagons get home?” a voice called from the vehicle.

“Could that be Joe Scott?”

“Ay, ay!” another voice replied. The gig carried two men, visible by the dim glow of its lamp.

The lantern-bearers had fallen behind; the riders had taken the lead. Now, one of the men in the gig spoke.

“Ay, Mr. Moore—it’s Joe Scott. I’m bringing him back to you in a fine state. Found him on the moor, him and three others. What’ll you give me for returning him?”

“Thanks, I suppose,” Moore said. “Though I could’ve spared a better man. Is that you, Mr. Yorke, by your voice?”

“Ay, lad, it’s me. I was coming home from Stilbro’ market. Just as I reached the center of the moor, whipping along as fast as the wind (for these aren’t safe times, thanks to a bad government!), I heard a groan. I pulled up. Most would’ve driven on. But I’ve nothing to fear. I don’t believe there’s a lad in these parts would harm me—though I’d give them as good as they gave if they tried.”

“I said, ‘Is there anything wrong?’” he continued. “‘Deed is there,’ someone says, speaking out of the ground, like.”

“What’s going on? Be quick about it,” I ordered.

“Nobbut four of us lying in a ditch,” says Joe, as calm as could be.

I told them they had no excuse, and bid them get up—or I’d lend them a crack of the whip. I thought they were just tired.

“We’d ha’ done that an hour ago,” says Joe, “but we’re tied with a bit o’ band.”

So I climbed down and freed them with my penknife. Scott wanted to ride with me to explain what happened. The others are following as fast as their legs can carry them.”

“Well, I’m grateful to you, Mr. Yorke,” Moore said.

“Are you, my lad?” Yorke replied. “You know you’re not. But here come the others. And look—by the Lord, another group with lights, like Gideon’s army! And since we’ve got the parson with us—good evening, Mr. Helstone—we’ll manage.”

Mr. Helstone returned the greeting with icy formality.

“We’re eleven strong,” Yorke declared. “And we’ve horses and chariots among us. If we could only fall upon some of those frame-breakers, we could win a grand victory. We could each be a Wellington—that would please you, Mr. Helstone—and write such thrilling articles for the papers! Briarfield would be famous. But we’ll get a column and a half in the *Stilbro’ Courier* for this job, I daresay.”

“And I’ll write it myself,” the rector promised.

“To be sure!” Yorke laughed. “And mind you recommend that the ones who broke the frames and tied Joe Scott’s legs should be hanged without benefit of clergy. It’s a hanging matter, or should be.”

“If I judged them,” Moore said sharply, “I’d give them short shrift!”

“But I’ll leave them alone this time,” Moore added. “Let them have rope enough—I’m certain they’ll hang themselves.”

“Leave them alone?” Yorke asked. “Do you promise?”

“Promise? No. All I mean is, I won’t go out of my way to catch them. But if one crosses my path—”

“You’ll snap him up, of course,” Yorke finished. “Only you’d prefer they did something worse than merely stopping a wagon before you deal with them. Well, we’ll say no more on that now. Here we are at my door, gentlemen. Come in—I hope you’ll step inside. None of you will be the worse for a little refreshment.”

Moore and Helstone refused at first, but the invitation was so warm, and the night so harsh, and the windows ahead so warmly lit, that they finally agreed.

Yorke dismounted, left the gig in charge of a man who emerged from a nearby outbuilding, and led the way inside.

It might have been noted that Mr. Yorke shifted between dialects—sometimes speaking broad Yorkshire, sometimes pure English. His manner, too, changed with the moment—polite one instant, blunt the next. His station was hard to place by speech or bearing alone. Perhaps his house would clarify it.

He directed the men to the kitchen, saying he’d “see them served wi’ summat to taste presently.” The gentlemen entered through the front door.

They found themselves in a hall lined with pictures almost to the ceiling, carpeted in thick matting. From there, they were led into a large parlour, glowing with warmth from a grand fireplace. It was not opulent, but refined—evidence of a cultivated mind. Italian landscapes adorned the walls—true works of art, carefully selected, genuine and valuable. Even in candlelight, the scenes shimmered: clear skies, soft distances, blue air trembling between hill and eye, fresh tints, balanced light and shadow. All pastoral, all sunlit.

On a sofa lay a guitar and sheets of music. Cameos, delicate miniatures, Greek-style vases graced the mantel. Books stood neatly arranged in elegant bookcases.

Yorke bade them sit. Then he rang for wine, instructing the servant to see the men were well fed in the kitchen.

Helstone remained standing, uneasy. He refused the wine offered.

“E’en as you will,” Yorke said lightly. “I reckon you’re thinking of Eastern customs, Mr. Helstone—fearing we might be forced to be friends. But I’m not so superstitious. You might drink that whole decanter, and I might send you a bottle from your own cellar, and I’d still oppose you at every turn—in vestry meetings, justice meetings, wherever we meet.”

“That’s exactly what I’d expect of you, Mr. Yorke,” Helstone replied.

“Does it agree with you now, Mr. Helstone, to ride out after rioters on a wet night at your age?”

“It always agrees with me to do my duty,” Helstone said. “And in this case, duty is pleasure. To hunt down vermin is noble work—fit for an archbishop.”

“Fit for you, at least,” Yorke smiled. “But where’s the curate? He’s either visiting a sick person or hunting vermin elsewhere.”

“He’s doing garrison-duty at Hollow’s Mill.”

“You left him a glass of wine, I hope, Bob?” Yorke asked, turning to Moore.

“No need to wait,” he said quickly, addressing Moore, who had settled into an old-fashioned chair near the fire. “Move it, Robert! Get up, my lad! That seat is mine. Take the sofa or another chair, but not this one. It belongs to me—and no one else.”

“Why are you so particular about that chair, Mr. Yorke?” Moore asked, rising obediently.

“My father sat in it before me,” Yorke said. “That’s all the reason I’ll give you. And it’s as good a reason as Mr. Helstone has for his beliefs.”

“Moore,” Helstone said, “are you ready to go?”

“Not yet,” Yorke replied. “Robert’s not ready. Or rather, I’m not ready to part with him. He’s a poor lad, and needs correcting.”

“What have I done?” Moore asked.

“Made yourself enemies on every side.”

“So?” Moore shrugged. “What does it matter whether your Yorkshire louts hate me or love me?”

“There it is,” Yorke said. “The lad is an alien among us. His father would never have spoken so. Go back to Antwerp, where you were born and bred, mauvaise tête!”

“Mauvaise tête vous-même,” Moore shot back. “Je ne fais que mon devoir. Quant à vos lourdauds de paysans, je m’en moque!”

“En ravanche, mon garçon,” Yorke replied in nearly perfect French, “nos lourdauds de paysans se moqueront de toi. Sois en certain.”

“C’est bon! C’est bon! Et puisque cela m’est égal, que mes amis ne s’en inquiètent pas.”

“Tes amis! Où sont-ils, tes amis?”

“Je fais écho, où sont-ils? Et je suis fort aise que l’écho seul y répond. Au diable les amis! Je me souviens encore du moment où mon père et mes oncles Gérard appellèrent autour d’eux leurs amis… et Dieu sait si les amis se sont empressés d’accourir à leur secours! Tenez, M. Yorke, ce mot, ami, m’irrite trop; ne m’en parlez plus.”

“Comme tu voudras,” Yorke said, and fell silent.

And as he sits back in his carved oak chair, I shall take this moment to sketch the portrait of this French-speaking Yorkshire gentleman.
