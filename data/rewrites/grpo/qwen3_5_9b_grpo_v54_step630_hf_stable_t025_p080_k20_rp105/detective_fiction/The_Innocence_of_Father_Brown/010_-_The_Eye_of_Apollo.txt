The Thames, that singular smoky sparkle of confusion and transparency, shifted from its usual grey haze to a glittering brilliance as the sun climbed to its zenith over Westminster. Across the bridge, two figures moved in stark contrast: one towering and imposing, the other diminutive and draped in clerical garb. The tall man was M. Hercule Flambeau, private detective, heading toward his new offices in a sleek, American-style skyscraper facing the Abbey. The short man was the Reverend J. Brown, attached to St. Francis Xavier's in Camberwell, traveling from a deathbed to visit his friend.

The building itself was a marvel of modern engineering, a steel skeleton housing a labyrinth of telephones and elevators, yet it remained unfinished, its upper and lower floors echoing with emptiness. But it was the object affixed to the exterior of the floor above Flambeau's that arrested attention. It was a colossal, gilded effigy of a human eye, radiating golden beams, dominating the facade like a warning from the heavens.

"What on earth is that?" Father Brown asked, halting in his tracks.

"Oh, a new religion," Flambeau laughed, dismissing the spectacle. "One of those modern sects that absolve you of sin by claiming you never sinned in the first place. Reminds me of Christian Science. A fellow calling himself Kalon—though I doubt 'Kalon' is his real name—has taken the flat above me. He styles himself the New Priest of Apollo and worships the sun."

"Let him look out," Father Brown murmured. "The sun was the cruellest of all gods. But what does that monstrous eye signify?"

"It's their theory," Flambeau explained casually. "That a man can endure anything if his mind is perfectly steady. Their symbols are the sun and the open eye; they claim a truly healthy man could stare directly into the solar disc."

"If a man were truly healthy," Father Brown countered, "he wouldn't bother staring at it."

"Well, that's all I know," Flambeau shrugged. "They claim to cure all physical ailments."

"And what of the one spiritual disease?" Father Brown asked, his tone sharpening with curiosity.

"And what is that?"

"Oh, thinking oneself quite well," his friend replied.

Flambeau, however, was more captivated by the quiet intensity of the office below him than the flamboyant temple above. He was a man of clear logic, oscillating between Catholicism and atheism, skeptical of bright, pallid new faiths. Yet, humanity, particularly when it possessed striking beauty, always held his interest. The office downstairs was kept by two sisters, both slight and dark, dressed in severe black with masculine collars. The elder, Pauline Stacey, possessed a profile as sharp and clean-cut as the edge of a blade, her eyes blazing with the cold brilliancy of steel rather than diamonds. She was a woman who seemed to cleave her way through life, a modern heiress who had traded castles for a harsher, utilitarian existence. She held her vast fortune not for romantic indulgence, but for practical social engineering, funding a typewriting empire and various women's leagues. Her younger sister, Joan, followed her lead with a dog-like devotion, a tragic shadow to Pauline's rigid light.

Pauline's temper was notorious. On their first meeting, she had refused the lift-boy's assistance, blasting Flambeau with a tirade against those who sought to return to romance and reject mechanical science. She believed everyone should master machines as she mastered the elevator, viewing doctors' aids like glasses or crutches as badges of cowardice. "I was free-born!" she had declared, her voice ringing with abstract anger. "Doctors stick on legs and arms as if we were born cripples. I will open my eyes and stare at the sun whenever I choose."

Flambeau, charmed by her fiery independence, had noted the spiritual bond forming between the floors. Above him lived Kalon, a magnificent creature worthy of Apollo himself. With a golden beard, strong blue eyes, and a mane flung back like a lion's, he possessed an animal beauty heightened by genuine intellect. Despite the vulgarity of his surroundings—a brass plate on a street-facing wall, a clerk in a common suit—Kalon radiated an oppressive, inspiring presence. Three times daily, he stepped onto his balcony to address the sun, his litany piercing the busy streets of Westminster.

It was during one of these noon prayers that the disaster struck. Father Brown, pausing to watch the white-robed prophet gaze unblinking at the blazing disc, heard a sound like a rocket reversing its thrust. A strident yell erupted, followed by a chaotic rush of people. Two figures remained frozen in the aftermath: the fair priest on the balcony above, and the ugly priest of Christ below.

Flambeau burst into the lobby, ordering a surgeon, while Father Brown ducked through the throng. They gathered around the open shaft of the elevator. The lift had not descended; something else had. The body of Pauline Stacey lay at the bottom, her beautiful, defiant form dashed to death.

"She was dead," Flambeau confirmed, his voice hollow. "Suicide? Impossible for such an optimist. Murder? Who could have done it?"

Kalon's clerk confirmed that the prophet had been on his balcony for fifteen minutes prior to the crash. Flambeau, turning to Father Brown with a desperate look, asked, "Then, if he was up there all along, who could have done it?"

"Perhaps we should go upstairs," Father Brown suggested.

They ascended to find the Stacey office empty, then Flambeau's own flat vacant. Returning to the Stacey office, Father Brown took a seat in the entrance, watching the stairs. Soon, three solemn figures emerged: Joan Stacey, her face drawn and prematurely grey; Kalon, descending in utter magnificence; and Flambeau, bewildered.

Joan moved with practiced efficiency to her sister's desk, rallying the room with her cool demeanor. Father Brown turned to Kalon. "Prophet, I wish you would tell me about your religion."

"I shall be proud to," Kalon replied, inclining his crowned head.

"Why," Father Brown asked gently, "do you truly think murder is wrong?"

"Is this an accusation?"

"No," Brown answered softly. "It is the speech for the defense."

Kalon rose, filling the room with his light and life, casting Father Brown's small, dark figure into shadow. "We meet at last, Caiaphas," the prophet declared. "Your church adores the darkening of the sun; I adore the living fire. You are spies seeking confessions of guilt; I seek to convict men of innocence. You would convince them of sin; I would convince them of virtue."

He spoke with the authority of an orator, admitting that Pauline was his love and bride, and that she had written a will leaving him half a million. "Penal servitude will only be like waiting for her at a wayside station," he mused. "The gallows will only be going to her in a headlong car."

He then dismantled the accusation with a flourish. "I could not have committed this crime," he stated firmly. "Witnesses will place me on the balcony from noon until a quarter-past. My clerk sat in my office all morning. I have an alibi complete enough to subpoena half Westminster."

"But," Kalon continued, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, "I believe I do know how my unhappy friend came by her death. Certain adepts have attained the power of levitation... Poor Pauline was of an impulsive temper. She thought herself deeper in the mysteries than she was. In some ecstasy of noble thoughts, she attempted the miracle. Her will, or faith, must have failed her at the crucial instant, and the lower law of matter had its horrible revenge. It is suicide. A heroic failure for the advance of science."

Flambeau, defeated by the sheer force of the argument, accepted the explanation. "If that is so, sir, you need do no more than take the testamentary paper..."

"It will be over there on her desk," Kalon said innocently.

Joan produced a sheet of blue foolscap. It began as a will, but abruptly stopped mid-sentence, ending in scratches with no named legatee. Kalon's mask slipped instantly. His voice dropped to a Yankee shrillness, and he erupted into blasphemies. "That's not all Pauline wrote!" he screamed. "You're a murderess! She was writing a will in my favor; you dragged her to the well before she could finish it!"

Joan remained unnervingly calm. "Your clerk is a respectable young man who knows the nature of an oath. He will swear I was in your office arranging work before and after the fall. Mr. Flambeau will confirm it."

"Then Pauline was alone when she fell, and it was suicide!" Flambeau cried.

"She was alone when she fell," Father Brown said, his voice colorless and sad. "But it was not suicide."

"She was murdered," the little priest insisted.

"But she was alone," Flambeau objected.

"She was murdered when she was all alone," Brown repeated.

Kalon raged, demanding the police, but Father Brown sprang erect, his chair tipping behind him. His face was deathly pale, yet his eyes burned with a sudden, fierce hope. "That's it!" he cried. "That's the way to begin. In Pauline's eyes..."

Kalon recoiled in madness. "What do you mean? How dare you?"

"In Pauline's eyes," Father Brown pressed, "go on. The foulest crime feels lighter after confession."

"Let me go, you devil!" Kalon struggled, throwing the door open.

"No; let him pass," Father Brown sighed deeply. "Let Cain pass by, for he belongs to God."

As Kalon fled, Flambeau turned to the priest. "Which crime?"

"The one we are dealing with, of course."

"We are dealing with two crimes," Brown corrected. "Committed against the same weakness of the same person, in a struggle for her money. The author of the larger crime found himself thwarted by the smaller crime; the author of the smaller crime got the money."

"The truth is one word, and a short one," Father Brown said. "Pauline Stacey was blind."

"Blind!" Flambeau rose slowly.

"She was subject to it by blood," Brown explained. "Her sister would have started eyeglasses if Pauline would have let her; but her philosophy was that one must not encourage such diseases by yielding to them. She refused to admit the cloud, trying to dispel it by will. So her eyes grew worse with straining. The worst strain came with this prophet, who taught her to stare at the hot sun with the naked eye. It was called accepting Apollo. The old pagans knew that the eye of Apollo can blast and blind."

"Whether or no that devil deliberately made her blind," Brown continued in a broken voice, "there is no doubt that he deliberately killed her through her blindness. The simplicity of the crime is sickening. Kalon brought the lift to the girl's landing, saw her writing the will in her slow, sightless way. He called out cheerily that the lift was ready, then pressed a button and shot soundlessly up to his own floor. He walked through his office, out onto his balcony, and was safely praying before the crowded street when the poor girl, having finished her work, ran gaily out to where lover and lift were to receive her, and stepped..."

"Don't!" Flambeau cried.

"He ought to have got half a million by pressing that button," Brown continued, his voice flat. "But that went smash. It went smash because there happened to be another person who also wanted the money, and who also knew the secret about poor Pauline's sight."

"There was one thing about that will that I think nobody noticed," Brown said. "Although it was unfinished and unsigned, Joan had already signed it as a witness, saying Pauline could finish it later. Therefore, Joan wanted her sister to sign the will without real witnesses. Why? Because she wanted her sister to sign it without *real* witnesses. She wanted Pauline to sign in solitude because she had wanted her not to sign at all."

"People like the Staceys always use fountain pens," Brown went on. "By habit and her strong will, Pauline could still write almost as well as if she saw; but she could not tell when her pen needed dipping. Her sister carefully filled all her pens except this one. The remains of the ink held out for a few lines and then failed altogether. And the prophet lost five hundred thousand pounds and committed one of the most brutal and brilliant murders in human history for nothing."

Flambeau opened the door to hear the police ascending the stairs. He turned back. "You must have followed everything devilish close to have traced the crime to Kalon in ten minutes."

Father Brown gave a start. "Oh! to him," he said. "No; I had to follow rather close to find out about Miss Joan and the fountain pen. But I knew Kalon was the criminal before I came into the front door."

"You must be joking!" Flambeau cried.

"I'm quite serious," the priest answered. "I tell you I knew he had done it, even before I knew what he had done."

"But why?"

"These pagan stoics," Brown reflected, "always fail by their strength. There came a crash and a scream down the street, and the priest of Apollo did not start or look round. I did not know what it was. But I knew that he was expecting it."
