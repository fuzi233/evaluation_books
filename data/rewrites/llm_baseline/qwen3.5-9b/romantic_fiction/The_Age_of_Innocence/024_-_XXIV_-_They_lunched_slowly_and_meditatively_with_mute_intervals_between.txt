They ate lunch with a slow, meditative rhythm, punctuated only by brief bursts of conversation. Once the initial spell of politeness was broken, they had so much to say that speech often became merely the background noise to long, shared silences. Archer deliberately steered clear of his own affairs, not out of conscious strategy, but because he feared missing a single detail of her history. Leaning forward, chin resting on her clasped hands, she spoke to him of the eighteen months since they had met.

She confessed a growing weariness with what society called "high life." New York had been kind—almost oppressively so—and she would never forget its warm welcome upon her return. Yet, past the first flush of novelty, she realized she was too "different" to share their interests. Consequently, she had decided to try Washington, a place supposedly filled with greater variety of people and opinions. On balance, she likely intended to settle there, establishing a home for poor Medora, whose patience had finally worn thin with all her relatives precisely when she needed protection from matrimonial dangers most.

"But Dr. Carver," she asked, smiling faintly. "Aren't you afraid of him? I hear he's been staying with you at the Blenkers'."

"Oh, the Carver danger has passed," she replied lightly. "Dr. Carver is a remarkably clever man. He seeks a wealthy wife to fund his grand designs, and Medora serves simply as excellent advertising for his conversion efforts."

"A convert to what?"

"To all manner of new and radical social schemes. But tell me, do you find them interesting? They captivate me far more than the blind adherence to tradition—the *other* people's traditions—that I observe among our mutual friends. It seems absurd to discover America only to turn it into a carbon copy of Europe." She smiled across the table. "Do you suppose Christopher Columbus undertook all that perilous trouble merely to attend the opera with the Selfridge Merrys?"

Archer flushed visibly. "And Beaufort," he asked abruptly, "do you say such things to him?"

"I haven't seen him in ages," she admitted. "But I used to; and he understands."

"Ah," Archer sighed, looking around the sparse room and out at the barren beach lined with stark white houses. "As I've always told you, you dislike us. And you favor Beaufort precisely because he is so unlike us."

"We are dreadfully dull," he observed, breaking into a sudden outburst. "We lack character, color, variety. I wonder," he added, turning back to her, "why you do not return?"

Her eyes darkened, and he braced himself for an indignant rebuke. Instead, she remained silent, lost in thought, causing him a sudden fear that she might agree with him. Finally, she spoke softly: "I believe it is because of you."

It was impossible to phrase the confession more dispassionately, or in a tone less flattering to the vanity of the listener. Archer blushed to the roots of his hair but dared not move or speak. Her words felt like a rare butterfly; the slightest motion might scatter it on startled wings, yet leaving it undisturbed might allow a flock to gather around it.

"At least," she continued, "it was you who taught me that beneath this dullness lie things so fine, sensitive, and delicate that even the people I cherished most in my former life appear cheap by comparison. I struggle to explain myself," she frowned, drawing her brows together, "but it feels as though I never truly understood until now how much hardness, shabbiness, and baseness one pays for the most exquisite pleasures."

"Exquisite pleasures—it is something to have experienced them!" he felt compelled to retort, yet the pleading in her eyes held him silent.

"I wish," she went on, lowering her voice, "to be perfectly honest with you—and with myself. For a long time, I have hoped for this moment: to tell you how you have helped me, what you have made of me—"

Archer stared beneath furrowed brows and interrupted with a nervous laugh. "And what do you conclude you have made of me?"

She paled slightly. "Of you?"

"Yes," he insisted. "Because I am of your making far more than you ever were of mine. I am the man who married one woman solely because another commanded him to."

Her pallor shifted to a fleeting blush. "I thought—you promised—you wouldn't bring up such matters today."

"Ah—how typical of a woman! None of you will ever see a difficult situation through to its conclusion!"

She lowered her voice further. "Is it truly a bad business—for May?"

He stepped into the window, drumming his fingers against the raised sash, feeling in every fiber the wistful tenderness with which she had invoked his cousin's name.

"That is the burden we must always carry," she pressed. "By your own admission?"

"My own admission?" he echoed, his blank eyes fixed on the sea.

"If not," she persisted, following her train of thought with painful intensity, "if it is not worthwhile to sacrifice, to miss out on things, so that others may be spared disillusionment and misery—then everything I returned home for, everything that made my previous life seem so empty and poor because no one acknowledged it—all these things become a sham or a dream."

He turned away without moving from his spot. "In that case," he concluded for her, "there is no earthly reason why you should not go back?"

Her eyes clung to him desperately. "Oh, is there truly no reason?"

"Not if you wagered your entire existence on the success of my marriage." He spoke savagely. "My marriage is not going to be a spectacle worthy of keeping you here."

She offered no reply. He continued, "What is the point? You gave me my first glimpse of a real life, and at the exact same moment, you asked me to continue living a false one. It is beyond human endurance—that is all."

"Oh, do not say that; I am enduring it right now!" she exclaimed, her eyes filling with tears. Her arms dropped to the table, and she sat with her face entirely open to his gaze, as if in the reckless abandon of a desperate peril. That exposed face revealed her completely, exposing the soul behind it. Archer stood dumbfounded, overwhelmed by what it suddenly communicated.

"You too... oh, all this time, you too?"

In response, she allowed the tears on her lashes to overflow, running slowly down her cheeks. Half the width of the room separated them, yet neither made any attempt to bridge the gap. Archer felt a strange indifference toward her physical presence; he would scarcely have noticed it had not been for the hand she had thrown onto the table, which captured his gaze just as it had in the small house on Twenty-third Street, where he had watched it to avoid looking directly at her face.

Now, his imagination swirled around that hand as if it were the edge of a vortex. Still, he made no effort to draw near. He knew the love that feeds on caresses and demands them, but this passion, deeper than bone, refused to be satisfied superficially. His greatest terror was doing anything that might erase the sound and impact of her words; his singular thought was that he should never again feel quite alone. Yet, after a moment, a sense of waste and ruin overtook him.

There they were, close together, safe, and enclosed, yet bound by separate destinies that might as well have placed them halfway across the globe.

"What is the use—when you will go back?" he exploded, a great, hopeless cry rising within him: *How on earth can I keep you?*

She sat motionless, her lids lowered. "Oh—I shall not go yet!"

"Not yet? Then sometime? A time you already foresee?"

At that, she lifted her clearest eyes. "I promise you: not as long as you hold out. Not as long as we can look straight at each other like this."

He sank into his chair. What her answer truly conveyed was: *If you lift a finger, you will drive me back—to all the abominations you know of, and all the temptations you half-guess.* He understood it as clearly as if she had spoken the words, and the realization anchored him to his side of the table in a kind of moved, sacred submission.

"What a life for you!" he groaned.

"Oh—as long as it is a part of yours."

"And mine a part of yours?"

She nodded.

"And that is to be all—for either of us?"

"Well; it *is* all, isn't it?"

At that, he sprang up, forgetting everything save the sweetness of her face. She rose too—not to meet him or flee, but quietly, as though the hardest part of the task was done and she merely waited. So calmly that, as he approached, her outstretched hands acted not as a barrier but as a guide. They fell into his, while her arms, extended but not rigid, kept him at a distance sufficient for her surrendered face to convey the rest.

They may have stood thus for a long time, or perhaps only a few moments; but it was long enough for her silence to communicate everything she needed to say, and for him to realize that only one thing mattered. He must do nothing to make this meeting their final encounter; he must leave their future in her care, asking only that she maintain a firm grip on it.

"Don't—don't be unhappy," she said, her voice breaking as she withdrew her hands.
