“He is brass within, and steel without,
With beams on his topcastle strong;
And eighteen pieces of ordinance
He carries on either side along.”

Take boat at Whitehall-stairs, as Amyas did, and let the Thames draw you down with its brown, tide-smelling breath—tar, bilge-water, and the faint sweetness of malt from riverside brewhouses mingling under the heavier reek of London smoke. The river has its own music: the slap of oars, the creak of thole-pins, chains clinking, hawkers calling from the stairs, and, over all, that unending undernote of water worrying the stones. Even now, when men talk of new worlds, the old world’s soundscape is what steadies the heart. You pass under the shadow of London Bridge, where the current frets and mutters through the starlings like an animal held too long in a pen; and so, with the tide, to Deptford Creek.

There, drawn up high and dry on the sedgy bank, lies the famous ship—Drake’s Pelican—resting like an old fighting-man with his scars shown openly, unashamed. She stands embalmed in river-air and memory; and nailed upon her mainmast are verses and epigrams in her praise, as though words might serve for caulking against time. Here Elizabeth herself once came, banqueted aboard, and made Drake knight with her own hand. Men still tell, with a kind of awe that half believes in lucky planets, how the plank-bridge broke under the press and a hundred tumbled down together, yet none were harmed—as though Fortune had held out her apron to catch them.

And Fortune, this afternoon, seems again at work; for the old ship is dressed for revel. Flags busy the masts; laughter, the scrape of benches, and the bright clatter of pewter come through windows that were once gun-ports; and, more persuasive than any music, savory steams drift out into the river air. The Pelican has long served as a show and, better yet, as a dining-hall for the city’s jovial folk; and to-day a great feast is toward. White-aproned waiters hurry to and fro—quick-footed as gulls—across the lucky bridge from the Pelican Inn, bearing trenchers, pots, and covered dishes; and with them go gay serving-men with the city badge at shoulder, bright as a cockade. The lord mayor gives a dinner to certain gentlemen of Leicester’s party, men who have set their minds on foreign discoveries; and where should such talk be fed more fitly than on the very deck that once carried it round the world?

Look at the company. It is a table worth a chronicler’s ink. If you be an American, look—look well—and reverence these faces; for out of such brows and hands, such stubborn appetites for risk and such patient accounts of victual and venture, your fatherland will one day be made.

At the head sits the lord mayor—Sir Edward Osborne, clothworker, old now, but with the same prompt chivalry that leapt from a window on London Bridge into roaring tide to save the infant who became his wife. Age has not softened him; it has only set the temper. He has the look of a man who has handled both bales and bills, who can taste the honesty of a measure as surely as he can taste a sauce. On his right is my Lord Earl of Cumberland; on his left sits Walter Raleigh; and the three speak low together of vast rich countries still undiscovered between Florida and the River of Canada. Raleigh’s speech runs half on science, half on enchantment, spiced with quotations from Doctor Dee the conjuror; and though such seasoning pleases Cumberland (who has tried many adventures by proxy, and failed in all—because he would send other men to do what he praised), it has less savor to Osborne, who has eaten too much of the world to be fed on words alone. So Raleigh draws in, for witness and weight, the quiet student at his side, Richard Hakluyt of Oxford.

But Hakluyt is deep in talk with a reverend elder, long white beard flowing near to his waist, his face furrowed as if every storm he ever sailed through had taken a turn at carving him. It is Anthony Jenkinson, the great Asiatic traveller, discoursing of reindeer-sledges and Siberian steppes, and of fossil ivory dug by the Tungoos from the ice-cliffs of the Arctic sea—plain proof, he maintains, of Noah’s flood. He speaks, and one hears behind his words the wind’s thin whistle over snow, the crunch of runners, the hush of a world where sound itself is precious. Next to him sits Christopher Carlile, Walsingham’s son-in-law—a valiant captain, a man who will one day drink good wine with a certain Bishop of Carthagena. For now he is busy with Alderman Hart the grocer, Sheriff Spencer the clothworker, Charles Leigh—Amyas’s merchant-cousin—Aldworth the mayor of Bristol, and William Salterne, alderman thereof and cousin to our friend at Bideford. For Carlile, and Secretary Walsingham too, have helped them heart and soul these last two years to gather money for Humphrey and Adrian Gilbert’s great adventures to the North-West; one of which Carlile himself was to have sailed, though for some reason he did not.

Opposite is a knot scarcely less notable: Martin Frobisher and John Davis, the pioneers of the North-West passage, in talk with Alderman Sanderson the geographer and setter forth of globes; with Mr. Towerson, Sir Gilbert Peckham, Captain John Winter (old acquaintance), and, last, Philip Sidney himself. Sidney, with his accustomed courtesy, has given up his rightful place toward the head, that he may have the virtuosi about him like a little court of his own; and with him are Mr. Edward Dyer and Mr. Francis Leigh. They talk of the North-West; and Sidney laments that he is tied to diplomacy and courts, and envies old Frobisher in pretty compliments.

“It’s all very fine,” says Frobisher, “to talk of here—sailing on dry land with a good glass of wine afore you; but you’d find it another guess business, knocking about among icebergs with your beard frozen fast to your ruff, Sir Philip—specially if you were a bit squeamish about the stomach.”

“That were a slight matter,” Sidney answers, “if by it I could win the honor her majesty bestowed on you, when her own ivory hand waved farewell from Greenwich windows.”

“Well, sir,” Frobisher returns, “folks say you’ve no reason to complain of lack of favors, seeing you’ve no reason to deserve lack; and if you can get ’em by staying ashore, don’t you go to sea to look for more, say I. Eh, Master Towerson?”

Towerson’s gray beard wags grim assent; but at that moment a waiter enters, breathless with haste and importance, smelling of hot bread and ale-foam.

“Please my lord mayor’s worship, there is a tall gentleman without would speak with the Right Honorable Sir Walter Raleigh.”

“Show him in, man,” says Osborne. “Sir Walter’s friends are ours.”

Amyas enters and stands for a moment in the doorway, as if the warmth and the smell of the feast—roasted meats, pepper, wine, the sharpness of vinegar on greens—have struck him like a memory rather than a welcome.

“Captain Leigh!” cry half a dozen voices.

“Why did you not walk in, sir?” says Osborne. “You should know your way well enough between these decks.”

“Well enough, my lords and gentlemen. But, Sir Walter—you will excuse me—” And he gives Raleigh a look that needs no tongue.

Raleigh turns pale as death. He rises at once and follows Amyas into an adjoining cabin. They are together scarce five minutes. Then Amyas comes out alone.

In few words he tells them the sad tale we already know. While he speaks, the sounds of the feast—knife on trencher, laughter, the jangle of cups—seem to falter, as if even oak and iron must listen. Ere the story ends, noble tears glisten on some of those stern faces; and the savory steam that had promised comfort now hangs in the air like a reproach, as though food itself had grown ashamed to ask a man to swallow.

“The old Egyptians,” says Sir Edward Osborne, “when they banqueted, set a corpse among their guests, for a memorial of human vanity. Have we forgotten God and our own weakness in this our feast, that He Himself has sent us thus a message from the dead?”

“Nay, my lord mayor,” says Sidney softly, “not from the dead, but from the realm of everlasting life.”

“Amen!” answers Osborne. “But, gentlemen, our feast is at an end. There are those here who would drink on merrily, as brave men should, in spite of private losses; but none here can drink with the loss of so great a man still ringing in his ears.”

So it is. Many at that table have suffered by the failure of the expedition; yet they forget their own hurt under the weight of Sir Humphrey’s death. The feast breaks up sadly and in haste; men rise with chairs scraping like a sudden squall; and each asks his neighbor, “What will the queen say?” Even the river seems louder as they spill out upon the bank—the splash of wherries, the crying of watermen, the city’s ceaseless grind; how thin and brazen it sounds beside a good man gone.

Raleigh returns after a few minutes, silent, pressing honest hands as he passes, and goes out to call a wherry, beckoning Amyas to follow. Sidney, Cumberland, and Frank go after in another boat, leaving the two to talk apart of the sad details.

They land at Whitehall-stairs. Raleigh, Sidney, and Cumberland go toward the palace; and the two brothers turn to their mother’s lodgings.

All the way, Amyas has been shaping words in his mind for Frank about Rose Salterne; but now that the moment comes, courage fails him. He longs that Frank will open the matter first. Frank too shrinks from it—more because he does not know that Amyas has passed through Bideford, nor that he knows aught of Rose’s disappearance. So each is trapped by tenderness, and by fear of what pain the telling will bring their mother.

They go upstairs. It is a relief to both to learn she is at the Abbey; for it is for her sake they dread what must be said. So they stand in the bay-window looking on the river. Light is fading. They speak of indifferent things, and yet look earnestly at each other’s faces, for it is now three years since they met.

Years and events have widened the contrast between them. Frank smiles with affectionate pride at Amyas: no longer the rollicking handy sailor-lad, but a self-confident, stately warrior, reason firm, temperate will, endurance and foresight set in every movement—such a man as Drake and Grenville might begin to fashion, and Raleigh and Gilbert finish. His long locks are cropped close now, but lips and chin are covered with a rich golden beard. His face is browned by a thousand suns and storms; a long scar—trophy of some Irish fight—crosses his right temple. His huge figure has gained breadth with its height; and the hand he lays on the window-sill is hard and massive as a smith’s, as if it had kneaded rope and hammered iron into obedience.

Frank lays his own hand upon it and sighs; and Amyas starts at the contrast. Frank’s fingers are slender, bloodless, all but transparent, like a scholar’s rather than a man’s who should ride and fence at court. Amyas looks anxiously into his brother’s face. It has changed. The brilliant red is still on either cheek, but the white is dull and opaque; lips pale, features sharpened; eyes glittering with an unnatural fire. When Frank tells Amyas he looks aged, Amyas cannot help thinking the words fit Frank more truly.

To keep from seeing what is plain, Amyas talks on of buildings, asking name after name as though stone and timber could distract the heart.

“And so this is old Father Thames, with his bank of palaces?”

“Yes,” says Frank. “His banks are stately enough; yet, you see, he cannot stay to look at them. He hurries down to the sea; and the sea into the ocean; and the ocean Westward-ho, forever. All things move Westward-ho. Perhaps we may move that way ourselves some day, Amyas.”

“What do you mean by that strange talk?”

“Only that the ocean follows the primum mobile of the heavens, and flows forever from east to west. Is it strange to think of that, when I am but now come from a party where we drank success to Westward-ho?”

“And much good has come of it!” Amyas breaks out. “I have lost the best friend and noblest captain upon earth, not to mention all my little earnings, in that same confounded gulf of Westward-ho.”

“Yes,” Frank answers quietly. “Sir Humphrey Gilbert’s star has set in the West—why not? Sun, moon, and planets sink into the West: why not meteors of this lower world? why not a will-o’-the-wisp like me, Amyas?”

“God forbid, Frank!”

“Why then? Is not the West the land of peace and dreams? Do not our hearts tell us so when we look on the setting sun, and long to float away with him upon golden-cushioned clouds? They bury men with their faces to the East. I should rather have mine turned to the West when I die; for I cannot but think some divine instinct made the ancient poets guess Elysium lay beneath the setting sun. It is bound up in man’s heart, that longing for the West. I complain of no one for fleeing away thither beyond the utmost sea, as David wished to flee and be at peace.”

“Complain of no one for fleeing thither?” asks Amyas. “That is more than I do.”

Frank looks at him, questioning; and then—

“No. If I had complained of any one, it would have been of you just now, for seeming tired of going Westward-ho.”

“Do you wish me to go, then?”

“God knows,” says Frank, after a pause that is almost a struggle for breath. “But I must tell you now, I suppose, once and for all. That has happened at Bideford which—”

“Spare us both, Frank; I know all. I came through Bideford on my way hither; and came hither not merely to see you and my mother, but to ask your advice and her permission.”

“True heart! noble heart!” Frank cries. “I knew you would be stanch!”

“Westward-ho it is, then?”

“Can we escape?”

“We?”

“Amyas, does not that which binds you bind me?”

Amyas starts, and holds Frank by the shoulders at arm’s length; and as he does, he feels through the fine cloth how little there is beneath—arms but skin and bone.

“You? Dearest man, a month of it would kill you!”

Frank smiles, and tosses his head in his pretty way, as though he could shake off sickness like a troublesome curl.

“I belong to the school of Thales, who held the ocean mother of all life; and I feel no more repugnance at returning to her bosom than Humphrey Gilbert did.”

“But, Frank—my mother?”

“My mother knows all; and would not have us unworthy of her.”

“Impossible! She will never give you up!”

“All things are possible to them that believe in God, my brother; and she believes. Besides, Doctor Dee—the wise man—gave her this summer I know not what prognostics and diagnostics concerning me. I am born, it seems, under a cold and watery planet, and need, if I am to be long-lived, to go nearer to the vivifying heat of the sun, and there bask out my little life, like a fly on a wall. He has bidden me spend no more winters here in the East; but return to our native sea-breezes, there to warm my frozen lungs; and he has filled my mother’s fancy with stories of sick men given up for lost in Germany and France, yet renewed—like serpent or eagle—by going to Italy, Spain, and the Canaries, that she will be readier to let me go than I to leave her alone. And yet I must go, Amyas. Not merely that my heart pants, as Sidney’s does, as every gallant’s ought, to make one of your noble choir of Argonauts—replenishing the earth and subduing it for God and the queen; not merely that love calls me—love tyrannous, strengthened by absence, deepened by despair; but honor, Amyas—my oath—”

He cannot finish. A violent fit of coughing seizes him; he leans on Amyas’s shoulder while Amyas cries out—

“Fools, fools that we were—that I was, I mean—to take that fantastical vow!”

“Not so,” says a gentle voice behind them. “You vowed for the sake of peace on earth, and good-will toward men, and ‘Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called the children of God.’ No, my sons: be sure such self-sacrifice as you have shown will meet its full reward at the hand of Him who sacrificed Himself for you.”

They turn. Their mother stands there, just come in—quiet as a chapel after the street—her very presence changing the room’s sound, as if harshness dared not speak above a whisper.

“Oh, mother! mother!” says Amyas. “And do you not hate the very sight of me—come here to take away your first-born?”

“My boy, God takes him, and not you. And if I dare believe in such predictions, Doctor Dee assured me that some exceeding honor awaited you both in the West—to each according to his deserts.”

“Ah!” says Amyas bitterly. “My blessing, I suppose, will be like Esau’s—to live by my sword; while Jacob here, the spiritual man, inherits the kingdom of heaven and an angel’s crown.”

“Be it what it may, it will surely be a blessing, as long as you are such, my children, as you have been. At least my Frank will be safe from the intrigues of court and the temptations of the world. Would that I too could go with you, and share in your glory!”

And then, with one of her most winning smiles, she lays her head upon Amyas’s breast and looks up.

“Come now. I have heard of heroic mothers who went forth with their sons to battle, and cheered them on to victory. Why should I not go with you on a more peaceful errand? I could nurse the sick, if there were any. I could perhaps have speech with that poor girl, and win her back more easily than you. She might listen to words from a woman—a woman, too, who has loved—which she could not hear from men. At least I could mend and wash for you. I suppose it is as easy to play the good housewife afloat as on shore? Come, now!”

Amyas looks from one to the other, and his heart sinks; for their courage is all love, and love does not measure cannon.

“God only knows which of the two is less fit to go. Mother! mother! you know not what you ask. Frank! Frank! I do not want you with me. This is a sterner matter than either of you fancies; one that must be worked out, not with kind words, but with sharp shot and cold steel.”

“How?” cry both together, aghast.

“I must pay my men, and pay my fellow-adventurers; and I must pay them with Spanish gold. And what is more, I cannot, as a loyal subject of the queen’s, go to the Spanish Main with a clear conscience on my own private quarrel, unless I do all the harm my hand finds to do, by day and night, to her enemies, and the enemies of God.”

“What nobler knight-errantry?” says Frank, forcing cheer; but Mrs. Leigh shudders.

“What! Frank too?” she says, half to herself; and her sons know what she means. Amyas’s warlike life—honorable and righteous as she knows it—she has borne as a sad necessity; but that Frank, gentle Frank, should become a man of blood, is more than her heart can face at first. That youthful duel of his he has concealed from her, knowing her feeling. She cannot bear to yoke that spirit to battlefield ferocity and carnage. And yet, she thinks, is this but another self-willed idol she must renounce, one by one? Catching at her last hope, she says—

“Frank must at least ask the queen’s leave to go; and if she permits, how can I gainsay her wisdom?”

So the talk drops, sadly enough—like a song cut short. The river outside goes on, indifferent; its noises—oar-splash, call, bell—threading in through the casement, as if the world will not hush even for grief.

But now a fresh perplexity begins in Frank’s soul. At first Amyas takes it for jest; then it nettles him when he finds it earnest. Frank looks forward to asking the queen’s permission with abject despondency and terror. Two or three days pass before he can make up his mind even to seek an interview; and he spends the time making interest with Leicester, Hatton, and Sidney, as if he sued for reprieve from the scaffold.

So says Amyas, adding that the queen cannot cut his head off for wanting to go to sea.

“But what axe so sharp as her frown?” says Frank, lugubrious as a man condemned.

Amyas begins to whistle, rudely and provokingly.

“Ah, my brother, you cannot comprehend the pain of parting from her.”

“No, I can’t. I would die for the least hair of her royal head—God bless it!—but I could live very well from now till Doomsday without ever setting eyes on the said head.”

“Plato’s Troglodytes regretted not that sunlight which they had never beheld.”

Amyas, not understanding the conceit, makes no answer; and there the matter rests for the time.

At last Frank gains his audience. When he returns, two hours later, he is pale and exhausted, as if he has walked against a strong wind.

“Thank Heaven, it is over! She was very angry at first—what else could she be?—and upbraided me with having set my love so low. I could only answer that my fatal fault was committed before the sight of her had taught me what was supremely lovely and only worthy of admiration. Then she accused me of disloyalty in having taken an oath which bound me to service of another than her. I confessed my sin with tears; and when she threatened punishment, pleaded that the offence had avenged itself heavily already—for what worse punishment than exile from the sunlight of her presence, into the outer darkness which reigns where she is not? Then she was pleased to ask me how I could dare, as her sworn servant, to desert her side in such dangerous times as these; and asked how I should reconcile it to my conscience, if on my return I found her dead by the assassin’s knife. At which most pathetic demand I could only throw myself at once on my knees and her mercy, and so awaited my sentence. Whereon, with that angelic pity which alone makes her awfulness endurable, she turned to Hatton and asked, ‘What say you, Mouton? Is he humbled sufficiently?’ and so dismissed me.”

“Heigh-ho!” yawns Amyas.

“If the bridge had been stronger,
My tale had been longer.”

“Amyas! Amyas!” cries Frank, solemn as a preacher. “You know not what power over the soul has the native and God-given majesty of royalty—awful enough in itself—when to it is added the wisdom of the sage and the tenderness of the woman. Had I my will, there should be in every realm not a salique, but an anti-salique law—whereby no kings, but only queens should rule mankind. Then would weakness and not power be to man the symbol of divinity; love, and not cunning, the arbiter of every cause; and chivalry, not fear, the spring of all obedience.”

“Humph! There’s some sense in that,” says Amyas. “I’d run a mile for a woman when I would not walk a yard for a man; and—Who is this our mother is bringing in? The handsomest fellow I ever saw in my life!”

He is not far wrong. Mrs. Leigh enters with a companion—none other than Mr. Secretary, Amyas’s acquaintance at Smerwick Fort; alias Colin Clout, alias Immerito, alias Edmund Spenser. Some half-jesting talk has been passing between poet and saint; for as they come in she says, with a smile that is a little forced—

“Well, my dear sons, you are sure of immortality, at least on earth; for Mr. Spenser has been vowing to me to give your adventure a whole canto to itself in his ‘Faerie Queene’.”

“And you no less, madam,” says Spenser. “What were the story of the Gracchi worth without Cornelia? If I honor the fruit, I must not forget the stem which bears it. Frank, I congratulate you.”

“Then you know the result of my interview, mother?”

“I know everything, and am content,” says Mrs. Leigh.

“Mrs. Leigh has reason to be content,” says Spenser, “with that which is but her own likeness.”

“Spare your flattery to an old woman, Mr. Spenser. When, pray, did I”—and she casts a most loving look at Frank—“refuse knighthood for duty’s sake?”

“Knighthood?” cries Amyas. “You never told me that, Frank!”

“That may well be, Captain Leigh,” says Spenser; “but believe me, her majesty—so Hatton assures me—told him this day no less than that by going on this quest he deprives himself of that highest earthly honor, which crowned heads are fain to seek from their own subjects.”

Spenser does not exaggerate. Knighthood is yet the prize of merit only; so valuable that Elizabeth herself has said, when asked why she did not bestow a peerage upon some favorite, that having already knighted him she had nothing better to bestow. It will remain for young Essex to begin the order’s degradation in his hapless Irish campaign, and for James to complete it by raising money through the sale of baronetcies—an hereditary knighthood, laughing-stock of the day, and, however venerable it may since have grown, reflecting little honor on its first possessors.
