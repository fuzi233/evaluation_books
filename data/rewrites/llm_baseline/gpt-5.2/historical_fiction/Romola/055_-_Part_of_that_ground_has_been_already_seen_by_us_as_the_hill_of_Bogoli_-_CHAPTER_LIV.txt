Romola had gone out with one intent in her like a broth that, left too long over the coals, will not be held back from boiling. All the afternoon it had thickened and risen—no longer a chosen resolve but a necessity, as bodily as thirst. She did not wait for Maso’s slow, careful step, nor measure which streets were safest. She crossed the bridge where the Arno lay black as steeped olives, the last red of day dying behind it, and the wind in the arches made a hollow music that seemed to hurry her feet.

The city had the evening’s usual smells—damp stone, lamp-oil, stale wine from shutters newly barred—but they came to her as if through cloth. Sound was clearer than scent: the scrape of sandals, the mutter of men in doorways, the sudden far clang of a bell that made her heart jump, as if every note were a hand laid on her shoulder. She took the most direct way to the Old Palace. She might meet Tito there. It did not matter. She could not weigh chances; she must empty her heart, as one must lift the lid from a pot before it bursts.

She scarcely knew what she passed—the pillared court, the wide stairs—only that she asked an usher for the Gonfaloniere, gave her name, and begged for a private room. She was left among frescoed figures whose painted lips held their silence, while newly-lit tapers began to smell of warm tallow, a homely, kitchen-like odour in that public grandeur.

The door opened. Bernardo del Nero entered, his white head still carried erect above his silk lucco.

“Romola, my child—what is this?” His voice held the startled tenderness of one who has heard a dish crash in the next room.

She had uncovered her head. She went toward him without speaking. He laid a hand on her shoulder and held her a little away, as if to see whether fever had changed her. Her face was drawn with fatigue and long inward struggle; her hair had loosened and fallen; but her eyes had a bright, strained life, as if mind had for the moment conquered body.

“What has he done?” Bernardo said abruptly. “Tell me everything, child; throw away pride. I am your father.”

“It is not about myself—nothing about myself,” Romola answered, too quickly, as if the words tasted bitter. “Dearest godfather, it is about you. I have heard things—some I cannot tell you. But you are in danger in the palace; you are in danger everywhere. There are fanatical men who would harm you, and—and there are traitors. Trust nobody. If you trust, you will be betrayed.”

Bernardo smiled, the way an old man smiles at a young housewife warning him that knives are sharp. “Have you worked yourself into this agitation, my poor child,” he said, and his hand rose to her head, patting it gently, “to tell such old truth as that to an old man like me?”

“Oh no, no! They are not old truths that I mean,” Romola cried. Her hands clasped hard together, as if that pressure could keep back what must not be spoken. “They are fresh things that I know, but cannot tell. Dearest godfather, you know I am not foolish. I would not come to you without reason. Is it too late to warn you against any one—every one who seems to be working on your side? Is it too late to say, ‘Go to your villa and keep away in the country when these three more days of office are over?’ Oh God! perhaps it is too late! and if any harm comes to you, it will be as if I had done it!”

The last words burst from her as steam breaks through a crust. The confession startled her into silence; it was as if she had spilled something that could never be gathered up again.

“I mean,” she added, faltering, “I know nothing positive. I only know what fills me with fears.”

“Poor child!” Bernardo said. He looked at her a moment with a quiet, discerning gaze, weighing her as he might weigh the truth of a report. Then his tone softened into counsel.

“Go, Romola—go home and rest. These fears may be only big ugly shadows of something very little and harmless. Even traitors must see their interest in betraying; the rats will run where they smell the cheese, and there is no knowing yet which way the scent will come.”

He paused, his eyes turning away, his mind withdrawing into that inward chamber where old men keep their settled decisions. With a slow shrug he added, “As for warnings, they are of no use to me, child. I enter into no plots, but I never forsake my colours. If I march abreast with obstinate men, who will rush on guns and pikes, I must share the consequences. Let us say no more about that. I have not many years left at the bottom of my sack for them to rob me of. Go, child; go home and rest.”

His hand rested again on her head, not as on a petitioner but as on a daughter. Romola could not help clinging to his arm and pressing her brow against his shoulder. That touch was like the last crust of bread left from a happier loaf—plain, necessary, and suddenly precious. All her young filial life, even in its troubles, rose before her as something clean and sweet compared with the hateful confusion into which her days had fallen.

“Is silence best, my Romola?” the old man asked.

“Yes, now; but I cannot tell whether it always will be,” she answered, lifting her head with a look that begged for permission and pardon at once.

“Well, you have a father’s ear while I am above ground,” he said; then he lifted the black drapery and folded it round her head with his own hands, as if to shield her from night air, adding, “and a father’s home; remember that.”

He opened the door. “There—hasten away. You are like a black ghost; you will be safe enough.”

That night, when Romola fell asleep, she sank into a deep sleep, the kind that comes when suffering has spent itself to the edge and the body claims its due. Habit was rigid in her, yet she slept far beyond sunrise, as if she must hoard strength for more pain.

She woke to the sound of guns.

The booming came through the walls in slow shocks, and with each report the air seemed to tremble like a tightened drum-skin. Beneath the cannon there was the constant under-sound of Florence waking wrong—doors slammed not with morning leisure but with haste; feet ran; voices gathered into a hoarse chorus that rose and fell, as when a market quarrel begins at one stall and spreads, except that now it was the whole city that was the market.

Maso was at her door, full of frightened diligence, and his words tumbled out with those circumstantial additions old men make when they cannot bear to know less than others.

“Madonna—Piero de’ Medici, with thirteen hundred men, is before the gate that looks toward Rome. A countryman came in and alarmed the Signoria before it was light—else, they say, the city would have been taken sleeping in its bed like a babe.”

Tito was not in the house; he had been summoned to the Palazzo long ago.

Romola sent Maso out again to gather what news he could, while she went up to the loggia again and again, trying to read the far-off movements as if they were letters she must decipher to save a life. The morning carried a sharpness in the air; the smell of smoke was thin but persistent, like scorched herbs. Yet sound was what ruled her: every volley, every shout, every sudden burst of laughter that seemed out of place, like a cracked note in sacred singing.

Maso returned. The great Piazza, he said, was full of armed men; many chief citizens suspected as friends of the Medici had been summoned to the palace and kept there. Some people did not care whether Piero entered or not; some even said the Signoria had invited him. But whatever bargains men whispered over their tables, the city was giving him an ugly welcome. Soldiers from Pisa were coming against him.

Those hours afterward did not lodge in Romola’s memory as cleanly as ordinary days. They were a churn of dread and recollection, and only a few things stood out as fixed: she knew she had walked, despite alarms in the streets, to the Badia at the appointed time; she knew she had waited there in vain.

And one scene, when she came out of the church and stood a little on the steps while the doors were being closed for the afternoon interval, came back to her afterward as if it had been a wakeful vision.

The streets were full, and not only with men bearing pikes. Faces and tones had changed: a lift at the corner of the mouth, a quickness of eye, an easing in shoulders that had been braced. The guns fired again—but the sound, which had shaken her awake like a sentence, now provoked laughter, as if the city were tasting danger and finding it not so bitter as expected.

She soon learned why.

Piero de’ Medici and his horsemen had turned their backs on Florence and were galloping as fast as they could along the Siena road.

A substantial shop-keeping Piagnone, still holding his pike as if it were part of his arm, told her with a grim, everyday certainty, as one tells of a loaf sold out too dear. “It is true,” he ended, bitterness tightening his emphasis. “Piero is gone, but there are those left behind who were in the secret of his coming—we all know that; and if the new Signoria does its duty we shall soon know who they are.”

The words struck through Romola like a sharp spasm. Yet the evil they foretold was not the nearest thing. When she entered her home again, what pressed on her most was the fear that she had lost sight—perhaps for a long while—of Baldassarre.
