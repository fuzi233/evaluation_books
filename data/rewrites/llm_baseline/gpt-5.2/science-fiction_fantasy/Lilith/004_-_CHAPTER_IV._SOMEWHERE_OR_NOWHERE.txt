The morning came in too hard and white, as if the sun had been scrubbed with lye and set to glare. I did not trust it. Weather, like men, can smile with all its teeth and still mean a blow. At the breakfast-table I turned my hand this way and that, letting the light crawl over the milky sapphire on my finger, hunting for the little star that sometimes held steady like a seed in a pear. It should have been crisp in such brightness. Instead it swam—blurred, as if seen through watered milk.

I pushed back my chair and went to the window. In the night there had been heavy rain; the lawn shone as if someone had glazed it, and from the soil rose that iron-sweet smell of earth rinsed clean. A thrush on the grass was at his work like a careful cook at a stubborn shellfish, tapping and worrying a snail until the hinge gave. Everything sounded close: drip, peck, the soft pull of the bird’s beak. I lifted the ring to the pane and rolled it between thumb and forefinger, trying to coax the star to answer the sun.

That was when a keen black eye looked back at me from the stone.

It was so alive, so intent, that my hand jerked; the ring slipped from my grasp and struck the floor. When I snatched it up, the eye was gone—gone from the sapphire as if it had never been there. At the same instant the light failed. The sun was muffled, swallowed by a dark vapour, and in a breath the whole sky clouded as though a lid had been set over it.

The air thickened, warm and sour, as if the world had begun to sweat. A gust shouldered the house. Then came a flash of lightning and one sharp clap of thunder—clean and immediate, like the crack of a cleaver on a block. The rain followed, not as rain but as poured water, a thin cataract beating the windows and flattening the grass.

I had the window open and stood there looking out at the slanting sheets when I saw him: a raven walking across the lawn with the solemn pace of a man who knows exactly where the kitchen is. The deluge struck his feathers and ran off them; he behaved as if it were no more than a mist from a cooling pot.

I congratulated myself that I was on the ground floor. Even as the thought formed, another thought slid beneath it: if I was not careful, something would happen.

He came on, nearer, nearer, and then, as if the world were a stage and he had his cue, made a profound bow. With a sudden winged leap he landed on the window-sill, stepped over the ledge, dropped into the room, and walked toward the door.

I followed, certain he meant the library, and equally certain that if he once turned toward the stair I would not put one foot after him. But he neither sought books nor ascent. He went instead to a small door opening upon a grass-patch tucked in a nook between two wings of the rambling old house. I hurried and opened it.

He stepped into the creeper-covered porch and stood looking at the rain as one might look at a well-boiling stock. I stood behind him in the doorway. The second flash came, followed by a long roll of thunder retreating into distance.

He turned his head over his shoulder and looked at me as plainly as if he had said, You hear that? Then he swivelled it forward again, and resumed his contemplation with what I could only call approval.

So human were his stance and the restless precision of his head-turning that I found myself speaking almost without will: “Fine weather for the worms, Mr. Raven!”

“Yes,” he answered in the croaky voice I had come to know, “the ground will be nice for them to get out and in!—It must be a grand time on the steppes of Uranus!” He glanced upward. “I believe it is raining there too; it was, all the last week!”

“Why should that make it grand?” I asked.

“Because the animals there are all burrowers,” he said, “like the field-mice and the moles here. They will be, for ages to come.”

“How do you know that—if I may be so bold?” I said, though boldness sat ill with the way the air kept pricking my skin.

“As any one would who had been there to see,” he returned. “It is a great sight, until you get used to it, when the earth gives a heave, and out comes a beast. You might think it a hairy elephant or a deinotherium—but none of the animals are the same as we have ever had here. I was almost frightened myself the first time I saw the dry-bog-serpent come wallowing out—such a head and mane! and SUCH eyes!—but the shower is nearly over. It will stop directly after the next thunder-clap. There it is!”

A flash came with the words, and in about half a minute the thunder arrived, as promised—delayed, like an order called to the table. The rain ceased abruptly, leaving the world steaming. Water hung on every blade of grass, and the whole garden seemed to breathe.

“Now we should be going!” said the raven, stepping to the front of the porch.

“Going where?” I asked.

“Going where we have to go,” he answered. “You did not surely think you had got home? I told you there was no going out and in at pleasure until you were at home!”

“I do not want to go,” I said.

“That does not make any difference—at least not much,” he replied. “This is the way!”

“I am quite content where I am.”

“You think so, but you are not. Come along.”

He hopped from the porch onto the wet grass and turned, waiting. The sun thrust through the cloud like a blade slipped from a sheath; raindrops flashed, each one a tiny mirror.

“I will not leave the house to-day,” I said with obstinacy.

“You will come into the garden!” returned the raven.

“I give in so far,” I said, and stepped down.

The raven walked on the soaked turf as if it were dry. “You will wet your feet!” I cried.

“And mire my beak,” he answered, and immediately plunged it deep into the sod, drawing out a great red worm, thick and shining with the black wet of soil. It wriggled like a live ribbon.

He threw back his head and tossed it into the air.

It spread great wings—gorgeous in red and black—and soared aloft, beating the washed air as if it had always belonged there.

“Tut! tut!” I exclaimed, more startled by the certainty of the act than by the impossibility of it. “You mistake, Mr. Raven: worms are not the larvæ of butterflies!”

“Never mind,” he croaked. “It will do for once! I’m not a reading man at present, but sexton at the—at a certain graveyard—cemetery, more properly—in—at—no matter where!”

“I see! You can’t keep your spade still: and when you have nothing to bury, you must dig something up! Only you should mind what it is before you make it fly! No creature should be allowed to forget what and where it came from!”

“Why?” said the raven, with the coolness of a man turning a roast that is browning too fast.

“Because it will grow proud, and cease to recognise its superiors.”

No man knows it when he is making an idiot of himself. I spoke like one repeating a lesson in a dead language, sure of my grammar and empty of the taste of what I said.

“Where DO the worms come from?” asked the raven, as if curiosity had suddenly opened in him like a cupboard.

“Why, from the earth, as you have just seen!” I answered.

“Yes, last!” he replied. “But they can’t have come from it first—for that will never go back to it!” He looked up.

I looked up also and saw nothing but a little dark cloud with edges red as if with sunset.

“Surely the sun is not going down!” I cried. The light had changed in a way I could not account for; it was as if the day had been seasoned with something bitter.

“Oh, no!” returned the raven. “That red belongs to the worm.”

“You see what comes of making creatures forget their origin!” I said, with warmth enough to cook my own words.

“It is well, surely, if it be to rise higher and grow larger!” he returned. “But indeed I only teach them to find it!”

“Would you have the air full of worms?”

“That is the business of a sexton. If only the rest of the clergy understood it as well!”

In went his beak again through the soft turf, and out came another worm, slick with loam. He tossed it, and away it flew. The air for a moment was full of a faint, clean rustle, like silk brushed the wrong way.

I turned—only to feel my stomach drop. Behind me there was no creeper-covered porch, no familiar door, no nook of house-wall with its domestic angles. There was forest: tall pines crowding close, their trunks dark from rain, their needles combing the wind with a hiss. The smell was resin and wet bark, and beneath it the cold mineral scent of stone.

I cried out, dismay breaking through my throat. I had said, not a minute ago, that I would not leave the house—and already I was a stranger, not even in a garden gone wrong, but in a world that did not bother to pretend it knew me.

“What right have you to treat me so, Mr. Raven?” I demanded. “Am I, or am I not, a free agent?”

“A man is as free as he chooses to make himself, never an atom freer,” answered the raven.

“You have no right to make me do things against my will!”

“When you have a will, you will find that no one can.”

“You wrong me in the very essence of my individuality!” I persisted, clinging to my phrases like a man clutching a recipe he cannot cook by.

“If you were an individual I could not, therefore now I do not,” he said. “You are but beginning to become an individual.”

My eyes were already searching the pines for some unaccountable glimmer—some thread of the old place—some way back. But what was “back” now? How could I call that house HOME, where every door and window opened into OUT, and even the garden refused to stay inside its fences?

I suppose my face betrayed me.

“Perhaps it may comfort you,” said the raven, “to be told that you have not yet left your house, neither has your house left you. At the same time it cannot contain you, or you inhabit it!”

“I do not understand you,” I replied, angry because my words, my inherited words, failed to hold their meaning. “Where am I?”

“In the region of the seven dimensions,” he answered, making a curious noise in his throat and fluttering his tail, as if adjusting his coat. “You had better follow me carefully now for a moment, lest you should hurt some one!”

“There is nobody to hurt but yourself, Mr. Raven! I confess I should rather like to hurt you!”

“That you see nobody is where the danger lies,” he said. “But you see that large tree to your left, about thirty yards away?”

“Of course I do: why should I not?” I answered, too sharply.

“Ten minutes ago you did not see it, and now you do not know where it stands!”

“I do.”

“Where do you think it stands?”

“Why THERE, where you know it is!”

“Where is THERE?”

“You bother me with your silly questions!” I cried. “I am growing tired of you!”

“That tree stands on the hearth of your kitchen, and grows nearly straight up its chimney,” he said.

“Now I KNOW you are making game of me!” I returned, with a laugh of scorn that sounded thin in the pine hush.

“Was I making game of you when you discovered me looking out of your star-sapphire yesterday?”

“That was this morning—not an hour ago!”

“I have been widening your horizon longer than that, Mr. Vane; but never mind!”

“You mean you have been making a fool of me!”

“Excuse me: no one can do that but yourself!”

“And I decline to do it.”

“You mistake.”

“How?”

“In declining to acknowledge yourself one already. You make yourself such by refusing what is true, and for that you will sorely punish yourself.”

“How, again?”

“By believing what is not true.”

The forest listened. In the needles there was a thin, continual whisper, a kind of green static. It was not the honest sound of a hedgerow full of birds; it was a larger hush, an acoustical ceiling pressing down. I found myself craving the homely noises of the house—the clink of crockery, the soft scold of kettle-lid, the steady human rhythm that keeps a mind from roaming into dark places. Here, the silence had teeth.

“Then, if I walk to the other side of that tree,” I said, “I shall walk through the kitchen fire?”

“Certainly,” replied the raven. “You would first, however, walk through the lady at the piano in the breakfast-room. That rosebush is close by her. You would give her a terrible start!”

“There is no lady in the house!”

“Indeed! Is not your housekeeper a lady? She is counted such in a certain country where all are servants, and the liveries one and multitudinous!”

“She cannot use the piano, anyhow!”

“Her niece can: she is there—a well-educated girl and a capital musician.”

“Excuse me; I cannot help it: you seem to me to be talking sheer nonsense!”

“If you could but hear the music!” he said, and his voice softened oddly, as if he were remembering a sound that had fed him. “Those great long heads of wild hyacinth are inside the piano, among the strings of it, and give that peculiar sweetness to her playing!—Pardon me: I forgot your deafness!”

The word struck me. Deafness. Not of ear, perhaps, but of something in me that could not catch what was being offered. I thought of languages I had heard in markets abroad—full of consonants like stones in a bowl—beautiful, alive, and meaningless to me, because I had never learned the mouth-shape of their sense. Here was a world speaking, and I had only the dominant tongue of my own certainties. No wonder everything tasted wrong.

“Two objects,” I said, grasping at a law like a man clutching a salt cellar, “cannot exist in the same place at the same time!”

“Can they not?” said the raven, with mild surprise. “I did not know!—I remember now they do teach that with you. It is a great mistake—one of the greatest ever wiseacre made! No man of the universe, only a man of the world could have said so!”

“You a librarian, and talk such rubbish!” I cried. “Plainly, you did not read many of the books in your charge!”

“Oh, yes!” he answered. “I went through all in your library—at the time, and came out at the other side not much the wiser. I was a bookworm then, but when I came to know it, I woke among the butterflies. To be sure I have given up reading for a good many years—ever since I was made sexton.—There! I smell Grieg’s Wedding March in the quiver of those rose-petals!”

He moved as he spoke, and I found myself beside a rose-bush that, a moment before, I could have sworn was not there. Its leaves were wet and dark; the buds were heavy with rain. I leaned in and listened hard. I heard no music—no thread of melody, not even the faintest ghost of it. Only the pine-hush behind, and the near, intimate tick of droplets falling from petal to leaf.

But I smelt something I had never smelt in any rose: still rose-odour, yes—honeyed, green, with that faint spice at the back of the nose—but altered by something that was not in the blossom. It was as though a procession had passed through the scent, leaving behind an imprint of polished wood, warm cloth, and a sweetness like crushed hyacinth-stems. I could not have named it had he not named it first. His word gave my nose permission to know.

When I looked up, the bird was beside me, close enough that I could see the rain beaded along the scales of his legs.

“Mr. Raven,” I said, and meant it, “forgive me for being so rude: I was irritated. Will you kindly show me my way home? I must go, for I have an appointment with my bailiff. One must not break faith with his servants!”

“You cannot break what was broken days ago!” he answered.

“Do show me the way,” I pleaded.

“I cannot,” he returned. “To go back, you must go through yourself, and that way no man can show another.”

I argued, entreated, tried to bargain with him as if he were a stableman and I a man late for a train. It was all useless. His refusal was as final as the last scrape of a pot when the food is gone.

So I stood there, the resinous air in my mouth, and accepted what I could not alter. How was life to be lived in a world where even place would not keep its word? Yet there would be—must be—adventure. The thought steadied me like bread in the stomach. Whether I found my way home or not, I would at least have the rare knowledge of two worlds.

I had never yet done anything to justify my existence; my former world was nothing the better for my sojourn in it. Here, however, I must earn, or in some way find, my bread—bread that would have its own smell, its own crust and crumb, its own language in the mouth. The body cannot live on notions. If this land meant to keep me, it would have to feed me; and if it fed me, then it would have claim on me in return.

A strange fairness lay in that, as in the old bargains of village life: you eat, you owe; you are sheltered, you work. Only I did not know the measures here, nor the names of the grains.

“There is no hurry,” said the raven, who had been regarding me as if he could hear the clatter inside my skull. “We do not go much by the clock here. Still, the sooner one begins to do what has to be done, the better! I will take you to my wife.”

“Thank you. Let us go!” I answered; and at once he led the way under the pines, while the forest whispered overhead like a great, listening roof.
