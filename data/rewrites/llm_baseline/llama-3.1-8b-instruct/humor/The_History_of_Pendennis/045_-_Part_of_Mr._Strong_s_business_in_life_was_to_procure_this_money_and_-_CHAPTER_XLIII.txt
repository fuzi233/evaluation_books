Alsatia

In the labyrinthine recesses of London, where the Inns of Court sprawl like a network of ancient secrets, Shepherd's Inn stands as a testament to the city's storied past. Nestled behind the black gables and soot-stained chimneys of Wych Street, Holywell Street, and Chancery Lane, the quadrangle lies hidden from the outside world, accessible only through a maze of narrow passages and smoke-shrouded alleys. The air is heavy with the scent of woodsmoke and the murmur of vendors hawking their wares: slop-sellers, brandy-ball purveyors, and dealers in tattered furniture that seems more suited to a dreamer's lair than a place of rest.

As one navigates the crowded courts, the cacophony of sounds assaults the senses: the patter of pattens on the damp pavement, the cries of ballad-singers with their guttural melodies, and the raucous laughter of Punch as he sets up his makeshift stage. Women call out to their children, scolding them for loitering in the gutter or worse, for lingering too long near the gin-shops. Amidst this chaos, the tranquil quadrangle of Shepherd's Inn offers a rare respite, its ancient charm undiminished by the passing of time.

At its center stands the mangy little grass-plat, home to the statue of Shepherd, guarded by iron railings from the grasping hands of rowdy boys. The hall of the Inn, adorned with the founder's proud coat of arms, stretches along one side, while the tall, venerable chambers encircle the remaining two. Above the central archway, which leads into Oldcastle Street and the bustling thoroughfares beyond, the words "Shepherd's Inn" seem to whisper a promise of refuge and tranquility.

Though the Inn's history may be tied to the law, its halls now welcome a diverse cast of characters. The Polwheedle and Tredyddlum Copper Mines occupy one set of ground-floor chambers, while the Registry of Patent Inventions and Union of Genius and Capital Company claim another. Mr. Campion, a gentleman of means, maintains a presence here, albeit a somewhat ambiguous one.
