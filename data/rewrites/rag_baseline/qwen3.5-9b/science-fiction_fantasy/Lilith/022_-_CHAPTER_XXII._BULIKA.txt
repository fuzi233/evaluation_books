"Lost."

The realization hit me with the weight of a collapsing bridge. I had wandered in a state of pure, helpless impatience, my internal compass shattered, until suddenly I stepped directly into the path of a leopardess. She was wading through a torrent of crimson fluid that spilled from her wounded paw. The stream rushed against my ankles with the force of a mountain brook, carrying a scent so metallic and fresh that it triggered an instinctive recoil. Yet, driven by a primal suspicion regarding the source of this arterial flood, I pressed onward, tracking the flow up the bank toward Bulika.

Almost immediately, reason cut through the panic. No leopardess, no elephant, certainly not the leviathans of the primordial age, could sustain such a geyser unless every vessel in their body was ruptured and their circulatory system was refilling from the oceans themselves. This was not blood. I dipped a finger into the rushing current; the liquid was cool, clear, and devoid of viscosity. It was a murmuring rivulet, flowing without channel over the grass. Though the sound was sweet, I dared not quench my thirst. I kept moving, eyes fixed on the horizon, listening for the familiar cadence of the hot streams—a rhythm I had missed for far too long. The mere contact of the water with my skin had restored a measure of vitality, banishing fatigue until the twilight began to thin, signaling the sun's approach.

Moments later, against the pale aurora, the silhouette of a city emerged—wall towers seemingly older than time itself. I knelt to inspect the brook below. It was gone. For some time, I had noted its volume diminishing, but I had ignored the warning signs. Looking back, I saw the grass bent in the direction of its former course, dotted with small, stagnant pools. Beyond that point, toward the city, there was no trace of water. The fountain had paused, or perhaps dried entirely.

Surrounding the fortress were gardens teeming with vegetable varieties I could not name. There were no flowers, no visible animals, no signs of life save for the greenery. These gardens bordered the walls closely, yet were severed from them by massive piles of gravel and refuse dumped from the ramparts. I approached the nearest gate. It stood half-open, unsecured, and devoid of guards or sentinels. The hinges groaned freely; it could swing no wider, nor lock tighter. Stepping through, I descended into a long, ancient thoroughfare. Utter silence reigned, broken only by the crunch of my boots. Was this a necropolis?

I turned and retraced my steps, toiling over dust-heaps and crossing multiple roads, each terminating at a similar gate. I refused to re-enter until the inhabitants stirred. *What was I here for?* The questions hammered at my mind. *What did I expect to find? What was my purpose?* Above all, I needed to see the woman I had resurrected. I did not crave her company; she had awakened terrifying suspicions within me, rendering friendship—and certainly love—impossible. Yet, her presence exerted a strange, magnetic influence. I needed to analyze it, to penetrate the inscrutability of her existence. To understand her mode of being was to glimpse marvels beyond the reach of imagination. But caution demanded restraint; a man must not willingly court temptation for the sake of knowledge. Conversely, I had unleashed an evil force teetering on the brink of extinction. I was accountable for whatever mischief ensued. I had learned she was the enemy of children—the Little Ones. They might be in peril. I had left them seeking answers, receiving only fragments of light. I needed more. I needed to know how to shield them.

At length, a faint commotion rippled through the city. I moved through the next gate, navigating narrow streets lined with towering structures until I reached a small square. Here, I sat upon the base of a pillar, crowned by a grotesque, bat-like creature. Soon, several citizens sauntered past. I addressed one, receiving only a rude glare and a harsh epithet before he vanished into the crowd. Rising, I threaded my way through increasingly crowded alleys filled with loiterers. Notably absent were children. Eventually, near a gate, I encountered a group of youths who resembled the bad giants of old tales. They circled me, staring with predatory interest, then began to shove and hustle me, hurling debris at my person. I endured the assault, unwilling to provoke hostility while needing to stay put. I appealed repeatedly to passersby who appeared more benevolent, but no one halted to listen. To the citizens of Bulika, as to domestic dogs, poverty was an offense punishable by disdain. Deformity and sickness were taxed heavily; indeed, no legislation of their Princess was more enthusiastically embraced than those measures designed to make poverty subservient to wealth.

Desperate, I took to my heels. No one pursued me beyond the gate. However, a lumbering figure sitting nearby, chewing on a hunk of bread, picked up a stone to throw after me. In his stupid eagerness, he hurled not the stone, but the bread. I snatched it; he dared not follow to reclaim it. Beyond the walls, they were cowards. I retreated a few hundred yards, collapsed onto the grass, devoured the bread, and fell into a sound sleep. The hot sunlight renewed my strength.

Night fell. The moon descended, casting a friendly glow, as if claiming an old acquaintance. It was the same silver orb that had witnessed my terror during my first night in this strange world. A cold wind drifted from the gate, carrying an evil odor, yet it failed to chill me, for the sun had plumped me with stored warmth. I crept back into the city. There, I found the few souls remaining outdoors crouching in corners to escape the biting blast.

Walking slowly down the long, narrow street, a huge white shape bounded across my path, flashing briefly in the moonlight before vanishing. Eager to track it, I turned down the next opening—a lane so narrow it barely accommodated passage—which led into a wider thoroughfare. The moment I entered, I spotted the creature on the opposite side, lurking in the shadow. It was following me like a dog, trailing what I initially mistook for a man. Every few moments, the figure glanced over his shoulder at the beast, yet neither spoke nor attempted to drive it away.

Where the figure crossed a patch of direct moonlight, I observed a chilling phenomenon: he cast no shadow. He was himself a flat, superficial shadow, existing in two dimensions. Yet, he remained an opaque void; he did not merely darken objects behind him, he rendered them invisible. Within his own shadow, he was blacker than darkness itself. In the moonlight, he appeared as though he had drawn his shadow up around him; not a hint of it moved beside or beneath him. Meanwhile, the gleaming leopardess that trailed so close at his heels, appearing as the white shadow of his blackness, dragged her own gliding shadow—a solid black smear—across the ground alongside her.

When they passed together from the shade into the moonlight, the Shadow deepened into absolute blackness, while the animal flared into radiant brilliance. I walked abreast of them on the opposite side, my bare feet striking the flat stones. The leopardess never turned her head or twitched an ear. The Shadow seemed to glance at me; for an instant, I lost his profile, seeing only a sharp, upright line. That exact moment, the wind caught me, blowing through my clothes and flesh. I shuddered from head to toe, and my heart hammered against my ribs like a pebble rattling in a child's toy.
