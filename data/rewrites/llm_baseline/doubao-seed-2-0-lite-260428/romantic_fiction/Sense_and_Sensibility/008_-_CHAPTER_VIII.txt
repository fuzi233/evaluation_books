Mrs. Jennings was a cheerful, widowed matron who’d inherited a generous, steady income from her late husband. She’d already seen both of her two daughters marry comfortably and settle into happy homes of their own, leaving her with no greater pastime than playing matchmaker for every young person in her wide circle of acquaintance. She threw herself into this little mission with relentless, warm zeal, never letting a chance to nudge two seemingly compatible hearts together slip by. She had a rare knack for spotting quiet crushes before the people involved had even named them to themselves, and many a shy young lady had blushed bright under her teasing, flattered to have her secret soft spot for some young gentleman called out.

It took barely an evening after her arrival at Barton for Mrs. Jennings to pronounce, with total certainty, that Colonel Brandon was head over heels in love with Marianne Dashwood. She’d first suspected it their very first night gathered round the fire, when the quiet colonel had sat utterly transfixed, hanging on every note Marianne sang for them all. Then when the Middletons came over to dine at the Dashwoods’ small cottage, her suspicion hardened into fact: he’d listened to Marianne sing just as intently again. It was undeniable. This was a perfect match, in her eyes — he was wealthy, she was bright and beautiful, what more could anyone ask for? She’d been eager to see the steady, thoughtful Colonel Brandon happily married ever since her connection to Sir John first introduced them, and she was always hunting for a kind, good husband for every pretty girl she crossed paths with.

This new pairing gave her endless material for gentle jokes, too. She teased the colonel mercilessly up at the Middleton park, and ribbed Marianne just as playfully down at the cottage. The colonel, for his part, barely noticed the raillery when it was only aimed at him. But Marianne was completely confused by it at first, and once she finally grasped exactly what Mrs. Jennings was implying, she hardly knew whether to laugh at how ridiculous it was or bristle at the rudeness of the suggestion. It felt to her like a cruel little jab at the colonel’s advanced years, the sad, lonely state of a confirmed old bachelor.

Mrs. Dashwood, who didn’t think a man only five years younger than herself was nearly as ancient as her romantic, seventeen-year-old daughter imagined, hurried to assure her that Mrs. Jennings had no wish to mock the colonel’s age on purpose.
“Mama, you can argue she’s not ill-intentioned if you like,” Marianne protested, “but you can’t possibly say the accusation isn’t absurd! Colonel Brandon is certainly younger than Mrs. Jennings, but he’s old enough to be my father. Even if he ever had it in him to fall in love, that feeling would have faded out of him decades ago. It’s so ridiculous! If old age and bad health can’t protect a man from this silly, teasing matchmaking, what on earth can?”
“Bad health?” Elinor cut in, amused. “You can find his age much more of a gap between you two than it is for Mama, that’s fair, but you can’t possibly pretend he can’t walk perfectly well, can you?”
“Didn’t you hear him complaining of rheumatism?” Marianne said. “That’s the most common ailment of people in their declining years!”
“My dearest girl,” Mrs. Dashwood laughed, “at this rate you must live in constant fear of me wasting away! It must feel like a total miracle to you that I’ve made it all the way to the advanced age of forty.”
“Mama, that’s not fair,” Marianne said. “I know full well he’s not old enough that anyone’s worried he’ll drop dead any day. He could easily live another twenty years. But thirty-five has no business getting married at all, far as I’m concerned.”
“Fair,” Elinor nodded. “Thirty-five and seventeen are probably not a great match, I’ll grant you that. But suppose there was a twenty-seven year old woman who was still unmarried? I hardly think Colonel Brandon being thirty-five would be any kind of barrier to making her a good husband.”
Marianne paused for a beat, her brow furrowing as she thought it over. “A woman of twenty-seven can never hope to feel or inspire that fiery, true kind of love ever again. If her home is miserable, or she has almost no fortune of her own, I suppose she might force herself to marry, essentially acting as a nurse just for the security and provision that being a wife gives her. That kind of marriage makes sense, as a convenient arrangement, everyone else would be happy with it. But to me? That’s not a real marriage at all. It’s just a commercial trade, each person trying to get what they want at the other’s expense.”
“I know I’ll never convince you a twenty-seven year old could love a thirty-five year old enough to want him as a companion,” Elinor sighed, half smiling. “But do you really have to doom Colonel Brandon and any hypothetical wife to be cooped up in a sickroom forever, just because he happened to mention a little dull ache in his shoulder yesterday, on that freezing, drizzling day?”
“But he talked about flannel waistcoats!” Marianne protested. “Flannel waistcoats only ever mean aches, cramps, rheumatism, every kind of ailment that plagues the old and feeble!”
“If he had a raging fever, I’ll bet you wouldn’t find him half so silly,” Elinor teased, getting up to leave the room. “Admit it, Marianne, there’s something so interesting and romantic to you about a flushed cheek, hollow eye, and a quick, racing pulse when someone’s ill.”

As soon as the door clicked shut behind Elinor, Marianne turned to her mother, her face tight with quiet anxiety. “Mama, I have a horrible worry I can’t keep from you. I’m sure Edward Ferrars is unwell. We’ve been here almost a fortnight, and he still hasn’t come to visit. Nothing but genuine sickness could keep him away this long, what else could possibly be detaining him back at Norland?”
“Had you been expecting him to arrive this soon?” Mrs. Dashwood said, surprised. “I certainly wasn’t. If anything, I’d been a little uneasy thinking back on how unexcited he seemed every time I invited him to come stay at Barton, before we left Norland. Does Elinor expect him already, then?”
“I’ve never mentioned it to her, but of course she must be waiting for him,” Marianne said.
“I think you’re wrong, love,” her mother said. “Yesterday I was talking to her about ordering a new grate for the spare bedroom, and she said there was no rush at all, that it wasn’t likely the room would be needed for a long while yet.”
Marianne stared, confused. “How strange! What on earth does that mean? The way they’ve behaved around each other this whole time has been so impossible to understand! Their last goodbyes were so cold, so collected. They barely spoke to each other the last evening we were at Norland. When Edward said farewell, he wished all of us well exactly the same way, like he was just a kind older brother, no special softness for Elinor at all. Twice I left them completely alone together that last morning, and both times he followed me right out of the room! And Elinor? She didn’t even cry when we said goodbye to Edward, the way I did. Even now she never breaks, never looks sad, never avoids company, never seems restless or unhappy around other people. What is going on?”
