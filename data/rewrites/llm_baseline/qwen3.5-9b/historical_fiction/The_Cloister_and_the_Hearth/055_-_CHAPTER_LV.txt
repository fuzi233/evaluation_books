About two months prior to this scene, in a small coastal town nestled between Naples and Rome, the locals gathered on the beach, their gaze fixed seaward upon a vessel laboring against a fierce gale that blew directly onto the shore. Moments passed where the ship appeared poised to withstand the storm, prompting cheers from the crowd; then, the wind and surf would drive her visibly closer to destruction, granting the spectators a secret, unspoken satisfaction. *Non quia vexari quemquam est jucunda voluptas / Sed quibus ipse malis careas quia cernere suave est.* (It is not pleasant to see anyone tormented, but it is sweet to behold someone free from such evils.) Though not scientifically designed for open-sea navigation, the ship was marvelously constructed for grounding itself, boasting an extravagant stern that caught the wind and lines resembling a cocked hat turned upside down. To the observers on the sand, the battering wooden frame seemed alive, a creature struggling against death with a panting heart. Yet, had they been transported to her deck, they would have discovered she possessed not one beating heart but many, and not a single nature, but a dozen distinct ones revealed clearly in that terrifying hour.

The mariners stumbled wildly across the deck, hauling ropes as each deemed fit, alternating between curses and prayers. Passengers huddled around the mainmast—some seated, some kneeling, others lying prostrate—clutching the bulwarks as the vessel rolled and pitched in the towering waves. Among them stood a handsome young man, his ashen cheeks contrasting with compressed lips that betrayed a fierce internal battle between terror and self-respect. He gripped a shroud tightly, wincing with every swell. This was the ill-fated Gerard.

Meanwhile, cries of prayer and vow rose from the trembling crowd amidships. Listening to them, one might think there were nearly as many deities present as there were humans. The sailors, however, placed their faith in a single Goddess, varying only her titles: "Queen of Heaven," "Star of the Sea," "Mistress of the World," "Haven of Safety." Among the landlubbers, polytheism raged. Even those who accidentally invoked the same deity did not summon the same version of that divinity. An English merchant vowed a pile of gold to Our Lady of Walsingham; a Genoese merchant promised a silver collar worth four pounds to Our Lady of Loreto; a Tuscan noble pledged ten pounds of wax candles to Our Lady of Ravenna. With equal fervor for diversity, they pledged allegiance not merely to the True Cross, but to specific iterations of the Cross associated with various modern cities.

Suddenly, a gust more violent than any before caught the sail at a fatal angle. The rotting shrouds snapped, and the mainsail tore away with a deafening crack. It descended the windward side, shrinking and darkening until it fluttered into the sea half a mile offshore, looking like a discarded sheet of paper. Before the helmsman could adjust the ship's heading, a massive wave struck her quarter, soaking the miserable crew to the bone and offering them a foretaste of chilling death.

In response, vows erupted anew. One man vowed to become a Carthusian monk if Saint Thomas would save him; another promised to pilgrimage barefoot and bareheaded to Santiago de Compostela, clad only in a coat of armor, if Saint James intervened. Others invoked Saints Thomas, Dominic, Denis, and especially Catherine of Siena. Two small Neapolitan traders stood shivering. One shouted at the top of his lungs, "I vow a waxen image of Saint Christopher at Paris, weighing exactly as much as myself, if I reach land safely!" The other nudged him, whispering, "Brother, brother, beware your vow. If you sold all your worldly possessions at public auction, it would not buy enough wax to match his weight." "Hold your tongue, you fool!" retorted the first. Then, in a whisper, "Do you think I am joking? Let me but reach land safely, and I will not give him a single straw dipped in wax."

Others lay flat, praying to the ocean itself. "O most merciful sea! O sea most generous! O bountiful sea! O beautiful sea! Be gentle, be kind, preserve us in this hour of peril!" Others wailed and moaned in pure animal terror each time the doomed ship rolled or pitched more violently than before; she was now a mere toy in the grasp of tremendous waves.

A Roman woman of humble means sat with her child at her half-exposed breast, silent amidst the wailing throng. Her cheek was ashen, her eyes calm, and her lips moved occasionally in silent prayer, yet she neither wept nor lamented nor bargained with the gods. Whenever the ship seemed truly vanishing beneath their feet, causing bearded men to squeak, she simply kissed her child. That was all. Thus, she sat patiently, nursing him in the jaws of death. Why should he lose any joy she could offer him while dying? *Moribundo?* Indeed, I believe Antiquity sat among these medieval figures. Sixteen centuries had not tarnished the old Roman blood flowing in her veins; the instinct of a race she had perhaps scarcely heard of taught her to meet death with decent dignity.

A gigantic friar stood on the poop with feet planted wide apart, like the Colossus of Rhodes, defying the peril surrounding him less than ignoring it. He recited verses from the Song of Songs with a loud, unwavering voice, inviting the passengers to confess. Some knelt before him; he listened and laid his hands upon them, absolving them as if he were in a cozy sacristy rather than a perishing ship. Gerard drew nearer and nearer to him, driven by the instinct that pulls the wavering toward the side of the impregnable. In truth, the courage of heroes facing physical odds might have paled beside that of the gigantic friar and his even more monumental composure. Here, even in extremity, two were found maintaining the dignity of their race: a woman, tender yet heroic, and a monk steeled by religion against mortal fears.

Now, with the sail gone, the sailors cut down the useless mast a foot above the deck, and it crashed over the side with its remaining rigging. This seemed to relieve the strain slightly. However, the hull, no longer propelled by canvas, could no longer outrun the sea. It struck her repeatedly on the poop, delivering tremendous blows that seemed inflicted by a rocky mountain rather than liquid water. The captain abandoned the helm and moved amidships, pale as death.

"Lighten her!" he cried. "Throw everything overboard, or we shall founder before we strike, losing the only slim chance we have of life."

While the sailors executed this order, the captain, pale himself and surrounded by equally pallid faces demanding to know their fate, spoke unlike any English skipper in similar peril. "Friends," he said, "last night, when all was fair, too fair, alas! a globe of fire approached the ship. When a pair appear, it is good fortune, and nothing can drown her on this voyage. We mariners call these fiery globes Castor and Pollux. But if Castor comes without Pollux, or Pollux without Castor, she is doomed. Therefore, like good Christians, prepare to die."

These words were met with a loud wail. When asked tremblingly how long they had to prepare, the captain replied, "She may, or may not, last half an hour; beyond that, it is impossible; she leaks like a sieve. Bustle, men, lighten her!"

The poor passengers seized everything on deck and flung it overboard. Soon, they grabbed a heavy sack; an old man lay upon it, seasick. They pulled it from underneath him. It rattled. Two men dragged it to the side; the owner sprang up with an unearthly shriek and pounced on it. "Holy Moses! What would you do? 'Tis my all! 'Tis the fruit of my entire journey! Silver candlesticks, silver plates, brooches, chalices—"

"Let go, thou hoary villain!" cried the others. "Shall all our lives be lost for thy ill-gotten gear?"

"Fling him in with it!" shouted one. "'Tis this Hebrew whom Christian men are drowning for."

Numbers quickly wrested the object from him and heaved it over the side. It splashed into the waves. Its owner let out one cry of anguish, standing glaring with white hair streaming in the wind, ready to leap after it if it had floated. But it sank, gone forever. He staggered to and fro, tearing his hair, cursing them, the ship, the sea, and all the powers of heaven and hell alike.

Then the captain cried out, "See, there is a church in sight. Steer for that church, mate, and you, friends, pray to the saint, whoever he may be."

So they steered for the church and prayed to the unknown god it honored. A tremendous sea swamped them, breaking the rudder and jamming it immovable, flooding the deck. Wild with superstitious terror, some turned to Gerard.

"Here is the cause of all!" they cried. "He has never invoked a single saint. He is a heathen; here is a pagan aboard."
