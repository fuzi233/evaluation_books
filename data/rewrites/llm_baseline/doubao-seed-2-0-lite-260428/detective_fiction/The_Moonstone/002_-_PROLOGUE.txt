I scribe these lines from a drafty military encampment in India, for my kin back in England. My sole purpose here is to lay bare the reason I have refused to extend the hand of friendship to our cousin, John Herncastle. The quiet distance I have kept between us these months has been misread by members of this family whose good opinion I will not willingly forfeit. I beg you all to hold judgement until you have read every word of this account. On my honor as an officer in His Majesty’s service, every detail I set down here is strictly, unvarnished truth.

This rift between Herncastle and I did not spark from some petty family feud. It was born in the blood and smoke of a public event we both bore witness to: the storming of Seringapatam, under General Baird, on the 4th of May, 1799. To make the full context clear, I must first step back to the days before the assault, and the wild, half-fantastic tales rippling through our camp of the hoard of jewels and gold locked in the Sultan’s palace vaults.

---

One of the most outlandish of those stories centered on a Yellow Diamond, a gem famous enough to have carved its own place in native Indian lore. The oldest surviving traditions say the stone was set in the forehead of the four-handed Hindu god that embodies the Moon. Partly for its unique pale golden hue, partly for the long-held superstition that it drank power from the deity it adorned, waxing and waning in brightness in lockstep with the lunar cycle, it earned the name that sticks to it across India to this day: *the Moonstone*. I have heard a similar old folk belief cropped up in ancient Greece and Rome, though it attached to a lesser, translucent lunar stone, not a sacred diamond dedicated to a god.

The recorded adventures of the Moonstone stretch back to the 11th century. That was when the Mohammedan conqueror Mahmoud of Ghizni swept across India, sacked the holy pilgrimage city of Somnauth, and stripped the centuries-old temple there of every treasure the priests could not hide. Of all the temple’s statues, only the moon-god escaped the raiders’ fury. Three Brahmin priests spirited the unviolated icon, its forehead still set with the Moonstone, away under cover of night, and carried it hundreds of miles to Benares, India’s second sacred city.

They housed the god in a new shrine: a hall inlaid with rare gems, beneath a roof propped up by solid gold pillars. The night the shrine was formally consecrated, Vishnu the Preserver appeared to the three Brahmins in a dream. He breathed divine power directly onto the diamond embedded in the god’s forehead, and the priests fell to their knees, covering their faces in their robes. Vishnu gave them a command: from that hour forward, three priests would take turns watching the Moonstone night and day, for every generation that followed. He also spoke a prophecy of ruin: any mortal fool enough to lay violent hands on the sacred gem, and every heir who received it after him, would meet with unmitigated disaster. The Brahmins ordered that warning carved in gold lettering across the shrine’s main gates.

Centuries passed. Generation after generation, the successors of those three original Brahmins kept their unbroken vigil over the Moonstone. Then, in the early 1700s, the Mughal Emperor Aurungzebe ordered his armies to raze every Hindu temple in the land. The moon-god’s shrine was defiled, its sacred animals slaughtered, its statues smashed to splinters, and a high-ranking officer in Aurungzebe’s army seized the Moonstone for himself.

Too weak to recover their sacred gem by force, the three guardian priests followed the thief in disguise. The generations turned over: the officer who had committed the sacrilege died a horrific, miserable death, the Moonstone passed from one lawless, greedy Mohammedan hand to the next, but the line of guardian priests never let it slip out of their sight, waiting for the day Vishnu’s will would return the gem to its rightful resting place.

By the end of the 18th century, the diamond had come into the possession of Tippoo, Sultan of Seringapatam. He ordered it set as the pommel of his favorite personal dagger, and locked it away with his most prized treasures in the palace armoury. Even there, hidden in the Sultan’s own private quarters, the three guardian priests kept their secret watch. Three unknown officers in Tippoo’s household, men who had earned his full trust by converting (or pretending to convert) to the Muslim faith, were widely rumored to be the disguised Brahmin guardians.

---

That was the strange, fanciful tale of the Moonstone that circulated through our camp in the weeks before the attack. None of us treated it as anything more than a campfire story—none, that is, except my cousin Herncastle, whose obsession with all things supernatural made him swallow the legend whole. The night before the assault, he flew into a absurd, unprovoked rage at me and the rest of our mess for dismissing the story as fiction. A petty argument blew up, and Herncastle’s terrible temper got the better of him. He blustered, loud enough for half the tent to hear, that the second the English army took Seringapatam, that diamond would be on his finger. The whole mess dissolved in roars of laughter, and we all assumed the matter was done for good.

I now skip forward to the day of the attack. Herncastle and I were separated within minutes of the first cannon fire. I did not see him when we waded through the Cauvery river, when we planted the Union Jack in the first breach in the walls, when we fought inch by inch across the ditch beyond and spilled into the town. It was not until dusk, after the fort was fully ours, and General Baird himself had found Tippoo’s corpse buried under a heap of slain soldiers, that we crossed paths again.

We had both been assigned to parties dispatched by the General to stamp out the looting and chaos that followed the conquest. The camp followers had committed unforgivable atrocities, and worse, groups of soldiers had found an unguarded side door to the palace treasury, and were shoving handfuls of gold and jewels into their uniform pockets. It was in the courtyard just outside that treasury that Herncastle and I met, both struggling to rein our own men back into line. I could see plain as day the slaughter he had waded through had driven his fiery temper to the edge of frenzy. In my judgement, he was in no fit state to be given disciplinary duty.

Riot reigned in the treasury, but I saw no outright acts of violence—at first. The men disgraced themselves with rough, giddy jokes, passing barbs back and forth, until the old story of the Moonstone popped up again as a running taunt. “Who’s got the Moonstone?” was the rallying cry that sent groups of looters darting off to hunt the gem the second we had herded them out of one corner of the palace.

I was still fruitlessly trying to restore order when I heard a guttural, terrified scream ring out from the far side of the courtyard. I ran straight toward the noise, certain a new, far deadlier riot had broken loose. I rounded the corner to find two Indians, clearly high-ranking palace officers from their fine silk uniforms, lying dead across the threshold of an open armoury door. A cry from deep inside the room sent me rushing forward. Inside, a third Indian, mortally wounded, was sinking to the floor at the feet of a man whose back was turned to me. The moment I stepped over the sill, the man spun around: it was John Herncastle, a flaming torch in one hand, a dagger dripping warm blood in the other. The large yellow stone set in the dagger’s hilt flared in the torchlight like a spark of living fire.

The dying Indian crumpled to his knees, pointed straight at the dagger in Herncastle’s hand, and gasped out a few words in his native tongue: “The Moonstone will have its vengeance yet on you and yours.” He fell forward, dead, before the last syllable left his mouth.

Before I could speak a single word, the group of soldiers who had followed me across the courtyard crowded into the doorway. Herncastle charged at them like a madman, swinging his torch and the bloody dagger. “Clear the room!” he roared at me. “Post a guard on this door, no one comes in!” The men scattered back out of his path. I set two sentries from my own company, men I knew I could trust, to stand watch over the door. I did not see Herncastle again for the rest of the night.

At dawn the next day, with looting still spreading across the palace, General Baird issued a public proclamation by beat of drum: any man caught red-handed stealing would be hanged immediately, no matter his rank. The provost-marshal stood right at the drum’s side, to make it clear the General’s threat was no empty one. It was in the crowd gathered to hear the proclamation that Herncastle and I met again. He held out his hand to me as if nothing at all had happened. “Good morning,” he said, casual as ever.

I did not take his hand. “Tell me first,” I said, “how that dying Indian in the armoury met his death, and what those last words of his meant, when he pointed at that dagger in your hand.”

“The man died of his wound, I suppose,” Herncastle said, blank-faced. “I have no more idea what his ravings meant than you do.”

I studied him closely. The wild frenzy of the night before had burned completely away, leaving him cold and calm. I gave him one last chance to tell the truth. “Is that all you have to say for yourself?” I asked.

“That is all,” he said.

I turned my back on him, and we have not exchanged a single word since.

---

I want it clearly understood: this account of my cousin’s actions, unless some dire circumstance forces me to make it public, is for the eyes of our family only. Herncastle has given me no excuse to report this incident to our commanding officer. His fellow officers have taunted him more than once about the diamond, referencing his loud brag the night before the assault, but the memory of what I saw him do in that armoury has been more than enough to keep him from ever mentioning the gem again. Word says he intends to exchange into a different regiment, openly to put distance between himself and me.

Whether that rumor is true or not, I cannot bring myself to formally accuse him. I have no hard evidence, only moral certainty. I cannot prove he killed the two men lying dead across the armoury threshold. I cannot even swear my own two eyes saw him strike the fatal blow to the third man, inside. The dying Indian’s curse could be dismissed as the raving of a man delirious with blood loss, and I have no way to contradict that. I leave it to all of our relatives, on both sides of the family, to read this account and judge for themselves if the cold aversion I now hold for John Herncastle is justified, or not.

I do not put any stock in the old, fantastic Hindu legend of the gem’s supernatural curse. But I will admit, before I close these pages, that I hold a superstition of my own. I am convinced unpunished crime carries its own fatal weight. I do not only believe Herncastle is guilty of that triple murder in the armoury. I am even fanciful enough to write here that if he keeps that diamond, he will live to regret the day he ever laid hands on it. And if he ever gives it away, every single person who takes that stolen gem from him will live to regret it too.
