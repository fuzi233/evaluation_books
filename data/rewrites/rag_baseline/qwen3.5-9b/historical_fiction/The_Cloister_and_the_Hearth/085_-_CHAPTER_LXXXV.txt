To comprehend the architecture of Margaret's awakening, one must first understand the absolute sanctity of the space she inhabited—a cathedral under construction, a skeletal framework of faith waiting to be filled with divine light. It was a colossal edifice, far from completion, a testament to human endurance against the slow march of time. While the side aisles bore roofs, the grand mid-aisle and the sacred chancel remained open to the elements; yet, the pillars and arches stood in near-perfect symmetry, some already washed in the purity of fresh lime. Only a single window pierced the darkness with glass; the rest were gaping wounds in the stone, jagged apertures that should have invited chaos. But today, these imperfections forged a new kind of beauty.

It was a glorious summer afternoon, and the sunlight, fractured by the irregular openings, danced across the broken surfaces in marvelous, shifting forms. Beams of glory sliced through the dark, cool shadows of the side aisles, illuminating the white pillars beyond with a dazzling radiance. The central aisle itself was checkered with light and shade, the shadows offering a soothing coolness that defied the heat, while the lights shimmered like patches of amber diamonds animated by heavenly fire. Above, the blue sky vaulted the lofty nave, pressing close as if to embrace the congregation. The white caps of the women created a sea of innocence contrasting exquisitely with the vivid azure dome. Though the massive central aisle was crammed with souls, a profound stillness reigned; the words and the mellow, earnest voice of the preacher held them mute in a collective suspension of breath.

Margaret stood spellbound, captivated by the beauty, the devotion, and that great, overwhelming calm. Seeking refuge from the direct assault of the sun, she moved silently behind a pillar in the north aisle. Even there, unable to catch every syllable, a sweet, devotional languor crept over her, smoothing the turbulent waves of her heart until they ran like oil on water. Leaning against the stone, her eyes half-closed, she felt the goodness of the moment, drifting into a soft, dreamlike reverie.

Presently, she noticed a lady vacating an excellent seat opposite to escape the scorching rays pouring from the window. Seizing the opportunity, Margaret moved swiftly and softly, securing the spot. Now positioned beside a pillar in the south aisle, merely fifty feet from the pulpit, she was close enough to hear every word, though her attention was momentarily fractured by a peculiar sight. A shadow of a man's head and shoulders bobbed up and down with such drollery that she struggled to suppress a smile. Yet, it was not inherently funny; it was the sexton, Jorian Ketel, diligently digging. She confirmed this by glancing backward through the window, tracing the source of the movement.

As she watched Jorian, a specific tone of the preacher's voice struck her ear with such distinct force that it seemed to physically vibrate her very bones. Her hand flew instinctively to her bosom, startled by the sudden intensity of the thrill. Turning sharply, she sought the source, but the preacher's back was turned, revealing only the stark silhouette of his tonsure. She sighed; that shaved crown, being all she could see, effectively contradicted the profound depth of the voice emanating from it. Still, leaning forward with downcast eyes, she hoped for that accent to return. It did not. Instead, the entire voice grew strangely resonant, rising and falling as the speaker warmed, waking faint echoes of a thousand happy memories. She refused to look up, unwilling to dispel the melancholic pleasure the voice inspired.

Then, in the midst of an eloquent passage, the preacher stopped. Margaret almost sighed aloud; the soothing music had abruptly ceased. Could the sermon end already? No; she scanned the room, but the people remained motionless. Suddenly, many faces turned toward her. She looked behind her sharply, expecting a threat, but found nothing. The friar's eyes, naturally large and magnified by the hollow thinness of his cheeks, were dilated to a supernatural size, glaring directly at her from a bloodless visage.

She cringed, turning fearfully, convinced some terrible entity lurked near her. But there was nothing; she was merely the peripheral figure of a listening crowd. In this moment of confusion, the church erupted into commotion. Figures rose everywhere, craning forward; hundreds of agitated faces swung between the friar and Margaret. The rustling of caps turned rapidly created a deafening din, followed by shrieks from nervous women and the buzzing murmur of men. Seeing so many eyes leveled at her, Margaret shrank terrified behind the pillar, casting one scared, hurried glance at the preacher.

In that fleeting instant, she caught an expression on his stricken face that made her shiver violently. She turned faint and sank down onto a heap of chips left by the workmen, burying her face in her hands. The sermon resumed, but she heard only the sound, not the sense. Her mind spun in a whirlwind, refusing to coalesce into any thought other than this: *On that prodigy-struck face, I had seen a look stamped.* The recollection of that gaze now made her quiver from head to toe. For that look was **Recognition**.

After wavering for some time, the sermon concluded in a strain of exalted, feverish eloquence that drove the crowd to forget the preacher's strange pause and ghastly stare. Margaret mingled hastily with the throng and exited the church. As the congregation dispersed toward their homes, she paused at the door and retreated into the churchyard, walking straight to Peter's grave. Despite her poverty, she had provided him with a slab and a headstone. Sitting upon the stone, she kissed it tenderly, then threw her apron over her head to conceal her hair from the world.

"Father," she whispered, addressing the silent earth, "thou hast often heard me say I am wading in deep waters; but now I begin to think God only knows the bottom of them. I will follow that friar round the world, but I will see him at arm's length. And he shall tell me why he looked towards me like a dead man wakened; and not a soul behind me. Oh, Father, you often praised me here: speak a word for me there. For I am wading in deep waters."

Her father's tomb commanded a commanding side view of the church door. Upon that tomb, with her face veiled, she sat, waylaying the holy preacher, ready to confront the mystery that had just shattered her peace.
