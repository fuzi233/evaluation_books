"Rise, my daughter," Father Girolamo finally spoke, his voice cutting through the silence. "Your servant waits with the mules not far hence. 'Tis time I press onward toward Florence."

Romola rose from her kneeling posture. That stillness had become a sacrament to her, a ritual cementing the passive longing into which she had lately stepped. With a single act—the surrender of her plan to flee her husband—her will felt shattered, leaving her needing direction even for trivial matters. She lifted the brim of her hood and spied Maso and the second Dominican standing with their backs turned upon the hill's edge, some ten yards away. Yet she gazed past them, fixing her eyes on Savonarola, silent and expectant, as if the command for Maso to retreat must issue from Savonarola's lips alone, not hers.

"I shall fetch them," he said, reading the quiet plea in her gaze. "And I commend you to the Brother who accompanies me. You seek guidance, seeking that wisdom which hath seemed folly unto you. A primary gateway to this wisdom is the sacrament of confession. You require a confessor, child, and I entrust you to the care of Fra Salvestro, a brother of San Marco in whom I hold deep faith."

"I wish for guidance from you alone, Father," Romola answered, her brow furrowed with anxiety.

"My daughter, I serve not as your confessor. My calling withdraws me from duties requiring constant contact with layfolk, interfering with my specific obligations."

"Then shall I never speak to you privately?" Romola asked, her voice trembling with sudden agitation. "If I falter, if—" She broke off, seized by a sharp alarm. Her newfound fortitude in renunciation threatened to crumble if the immediate, personal influence of Savonarola were severed.

"My daughter, if your soul requires the Word spoken privately by my own mouth, let me know through Fra Salvestro, and I shall meet you in the sacristy or the choir of San Marco. I shall not cease to watch over you. I will instruct my brother regarding you, directing him to lead you into that path of labor for the suffering and the hungry to which you are called as a daughter of Florence in these days of dire want. I desire to behold you among the weaker and less knowing sisters as an apple tree stands among the trees of the forest, that your fairness and natural gifts may serve merely as a lamp through which the Divine Light shines all the more purely. Now, I go to summon your servant."

Once Maso had been dispatched ahead, Fra Salvestro stepped forward, and Savonarola led Romola toward him. She had felt an inner shrinking beforehand, a dread of a new guide who was a total stranger. Yet to resist Savonarola's counsel now would be to assume an attitude of independence at a moment when every ounce of her strength must be drawn from the renunciation of self-will. Consequently, her entire bent of mind leaned toward doing what was painful rather than what was easy. She bowed reverently to Fra Salvestro before looking upon him directly; but when she raised her head and beheld him fully, her reluctance curdled into a palpitating doubt.

There exist men whose presence alone infuses trust and reverence; then there are those to whom we must carry our trust and reverence pre-formed, ready-made. This distinction struck Romola with blinding clarity as Savonarola receded from view, replaced by Fra Salvestro Maruffi. It was not that Fra Salvestro possessed any manifest repulsion in face or manner, nor any air of hypocrisy or tinge of coarseness. His visage was handsomer than Father Girolamo's, his frame slightly taller. He was the long-standing confessor to many among Florence's chief personages, having accrued vast experience as a spiritual director. Yet his face bore the vacillating expression of a mind incapable of concentrating strongly upon one great emotion or belief—a trait fatal to influencing an ardent nature like Romola's.

Such an expression does not necessarily mark insincerity; rather, it stamps a shallow soul. One finds such souls sincerely striving to fill a high vocation, earnestly composing their countenances to utter sublime formulas, only to find their muscles twitch or relax in spite of their beliefs, as prose intrudes where poetry ought to reign. Fra Salvestro harbored a peculiar liability to visions, seemingly dependent on a constitution prone to somnambulism. Savonarola believed in the supernatural character of these visions, whereas Fra Salvestro himself had originally resisted interpreting them thus, even rebuking Savonarola for his prophetic preaching. Another proof, if one were needed, that the relative greatness of men cannot be gauged by their tendency to disbelieve the superstitions of their age. Between these two, there could be no question which was the great man and which the small.

The disparity between them was measured accurately by the shift in Romola's feelings as Fra Salvestro began to address her with words of exhortation and encouragement. After her first angry resistance to Savonarola had passed, she had lost all memory of the ancient dread lest any influence drag her into the circle of fanaticism and sour, monkish piety. But now, once again, the chilling breath of that terror stole over her. It could have no decisive effect against the impetus her mind had just received; it was akin to the closing of grey clouds over a sunrise, rendering her return path monotonous and sombre.

And perhaps of all sombre paths, the one we tread after traversing it with strong resolution is that which most severely tests the fervor of renunciation. As they re-entered the city gates, light snowflakes fell around them. As the grey sister hastened homeward from the Piazza di San Marco, crossing the bridge again and turning into the broad door of the Via de' Bardi, her footsteps left dark marks upon the thin carpet of snow, and her cowl fell laden and damp about her face.

She ascended to her chamber, cast off her serge habit, destroyed the letters of parting, restored all her precious trifles to their places, unbound her hair, and donned her usual black dress. Instead of undertaking a long, stirring journey, she was to sit down in her accustomed seat. Snow beat against the windows, and she stood alone.

She felt the dreariness, yet her courage ran high, like that of a seeker who has stumbled upon new signs of gold. She was about to thread life by a fresh clue. She had poured all the energy of her will into renunciation. The empty tabernacle remained locked, and she placed Dino's crucifix outside it.

Nothing broke the outward monotony of her solitary home until night arrived like a white ghost at the windows. Yet it proved the most memorable Christmas Eve of her life, this one of 1494.
