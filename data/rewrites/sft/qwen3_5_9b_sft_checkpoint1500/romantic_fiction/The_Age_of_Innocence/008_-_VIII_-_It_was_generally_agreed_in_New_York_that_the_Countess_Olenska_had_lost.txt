Across New York, a consensus had formed regarding the Countess Olenska: she had lost her looks.

Her arrival in the city first occurred in the distant past of Newland Archer's youth, when she appeared as a brilliantly pretty child of perhaps nine or ten, a specimen deemed worthy of being painted. Her origins were those of continental drift; her parents, wandering souls, had perished, leaving her to the care of her aunt, Medora Manson—a perpetual nomad herself who sought, with varying degrees of success, to "settle down" in New York. Poor Medora, a widow of multiple counts and marquises, brought a new spouse or an adopted child to each residence, only to abandon both within months, selling her homes at a loss to resume her travels. While New York, indulgent toward her eccentricities due to her lineage as a Rushworth and her final, disastrous union with a Chiver, watched her waltz away, they pitied the fate of the little orphaned niece left in her chaotic wake.

Everyone intended kindness for little Ellen Mingott, despite her dusky red cheeks and tight curls, which bestowed upon her an air of gaiety ill-suited for a child still bound by mourning for her parents. Yet Medora, in a defiance of the rigid laws governing American grief, flouted convention. Upon stepping from the steamer, the family was scandalized to find her wearing a crape veil seven inches shorter than her sisters-in-law's, while little Ellen, dressed in crimson merino and amber beads, resembled a gypsy foundling. However, New York soon grew weary of its moral posturing. Only a handful of elderly matrons frowned upon Ellen's gaudy attire, while the rest were charmed by her high color and spirited nature. She was a fearless, familiar spirit who asked disconcerting questions, offered precocious commentary, and displayed exotic talents—dancing the Spanish shawl dance and singing Neapolitan love songs accompanied by a guitar. Under Medora's tutelage, which involved an expensive but incoherent curriculum including "drawing from the model" and performing in piano quintets with professionals, Ellen flourished.

Naturally, chaos ensued. When Medora's latest husband, the Chivers, finally died in a madhouse, his widow, draped in weeds of a strange cut, packed up again. With her, she took Ellen, who had matured into a tall, slender girl with eyes that stood out sharply against her pale features. They vanished from view until rumors surfaced of Ellen's marriage to an immensely wealthy Polish nobleman of legendary renown. Met at a ball in the Tuileries, he was said to possess princely estates in Paris, Nice, and Florence, a yacht at Cowes, and vast hunting grounds in Transylvania. Ellen disappeared in a kind of sulfurous apotheosis, and when Medora returned to New York, humbled, impoverished, and mourning a third husband, the city marveled that her rich niece had not provided for her. Then came the news that Ellen's marriage had ended in ruin, prompting her return to America to seek solace among her kinsfolk.

A week later, these memories drifted through Newland Archer's mind as he watched the Countess Olenska enter the van der Luyden drawing-room for an evening that promised to be momentous. The atmosphere was solemn; he wondered anxiously how she would navigate it. She arrived slightly late, one hand still bare, fastening a bracelet around her wrist. Yet, she entered without a trace of haste or embarrassment, stepping into a room where New York's most select circle was gathered with a gravity bordering on awe.

In the center of the room, she paused, surveying the assembly with a serious mouth and eyes that held a gentle warmth. In that single instant, Newland Archer dismissed the general verdict on her appearance. It was undeniable that her youthful radiance had faded. The rosy cheeks were gone; she was thin, worn, and perhaps a year older than her actual count, which must have approached thirty. Yet, surrounding her was a mysterious authority born of beauty—a certainty in the carriage of her head, a fluidity in the movement of her eyes. These qualities, devoid of theatricality, struck him as highly trained and imbued with a conscious power. Simultaneously, she possessed a simplicity of manner absent in most of the women present. Many, as he learned later from Janey, were disappointed that her presentation lacked the requisite "style"—for style was the currency New York held above all. Perhaps, Archer mused, it was because her early vivacity had evaporated; she was quiet now, in her movements, her voice, and the soft, low tones of her speech. New York had anticipated a resonance far greater in a young woman with her history.

The dinner proved a formidable affair. Dining with the van der Luydens was rarely a light matter, and doing so alongside their cousin, a Duke, elevated the event to near-religious solemnity. Archer delighted in the subtle distinction an old New Yorker perceived between being merely a Duke and being *the* van der Luydens' Duke. New York accepted stray nobility with a calm, if slightly suspicious, hauteur, but when credentials such as theirs were presented, they were received with an old-fashioned cordiality that might have been misattributed solely to their standing in Debrett. It was precisely for such nuances that Archer cherished his native city, even as he smiled at its pretensions.

The hosts had labored to underscore the occasion's significance. The Du Lac Sevres and the Trevenna George II plate were laid out, along with the van der Luyden "Lowestoft" and the Dagonet Crown Derby. Mrs. van der Luyden looked more majestic than ever, resembling a canvas by Cabanel, while Mrs. Archer, adorned in her grandmother's seed pearls and emeralds, evoked the likeness of an Isabey miniature. Every lady wore her finest jewels, yet, characteristic of the house and the event, these were largely set in heavy, old-fashioned mounts. Even old Miss Lanning, reluctantly coaxed into attendance, wore her mother's cameos and a Spanish blonde shawl.

Though the Countess Olenska was the sole young woman at the table, Archer observed that as he scanned the smooth, plump, elderly faces encircled by diamond necklaces and towering ostrich feathers, they struck him as curiously immature compared to hers. It frightened him to contemplate what must have forged the depth of her gaze.

At his hostess's right hand sat the Duke of St. Austrey, naturally the focal point of the evening. Yet, if the Countess Olenska was less conspicuous than hoped, the Duke was almost invisible. A well-bred man, he had avoided the shooting jacket worn by other recent ducal visitors, but his evening wear was so shabby and baggy, worn with such an air of homespun authenticity, that combined with his stooped posture and a vast beard spilling over his shirt-front, he barely appeared to be in dinner attire. Short, round-shouldered, and sunburnt, with a thick nose, small eyes, and a sociable smile, he spoke rarely. When he did, his tones were so low that, amidst the frequent silences of expectation at the table, his remarks were lost to anyone save his immediate neighbors.

After dessert, when the men joined the ladies, the Duke bypassed the Countess entirely to approach Mrs. Lovell Mingott and Mrs. Headly Chivers. Meanwhile, the Countess engaged in conversation with the amiable hypochondriac, Mr. Urban Dagonet of Washington Square, who had broken his strict rule against dining out between January and April solely for the pleasure of meeting her. They chattered for nearly twenty minutes. Then, the Countess rose and, walking alone across the expansive drawing-room, seated herself at Newland Archer's side.

It was not customary in New York drawing-rooms for a lady to rise and depart from one gentleman to seek the company of another. Etiquette demanded she remain immovable, like an idol, until the gentlemen wishing to speak with her succeeded each other at her side. Yet the Countess seemed oblivious to any breach of protocol. "I want you to talk to me about May," she said.

Rather than answer, he asked, "You knew the Duke before?"

"Oh, yes—we used to see him every winter at Nice. He's very fond of gambling; he used to come to the house frequently." She delivered this with the simplest manner, as if stating, "He is fond of wildflowers." After a pause, she added candidly, "I think he is the dullest man I have ever met."

This pleased her companion so thoroughly that he forgot the slight shock her previous remark had caused. It was undeniably exhilarating to encounter a lady who found the van der Luydens' Duke dull and dared to voice the opinion. He longed to question her further, to hear more about the life glimpsed in her casual words, but he feared to tread on distressing memories. Before he could formulate a question, she had drifted back to her original subject.

"May is a darling; I have seen no young girl in New York so handsome or so intelligent. Are you very much in love with her?"

Newland Archer flushed and laughed. "As much as a man can be."

She considered him thoughtfully, as if reluctant to miss any nuance in his words. "Do you think, then, there is a limit?"

"To being in love? If there is, I haven't found it!"
