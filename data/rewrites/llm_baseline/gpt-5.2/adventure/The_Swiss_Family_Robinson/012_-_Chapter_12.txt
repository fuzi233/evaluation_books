The peril that had lately coiled itself into our lives was, for the moment, ended; yet fear has a way of lingering like the after-scent of a serpent’s track—faint, sour, and stubbornly present in the mind. I could not shake the thought that another of those stealthy enemies might have slipped, unseen, into the morass; or that, as the last had done, it might have glided in from beyond Falconhurst, using that narrow throat of land we called the Gap.

So I planned two journeys, one nested inside the other as daily labor nests inside prayer: first, a close and methodical search of the thicket and swamp; and next, without delay, an expedition to the Gap itself—the one passage by which such a creature could enter our coast territory.

When I called my sons to accompany me into the marsh, I found reluctance in two faces at once. Ernest, who could argue a whole afternoon away on the names of plants, looked as though the reeds themselves might rise to refute him; and Jack, brave in bright weather, swore his ribs still ached at the memory of the last lash of that monstrous tail. He confessed—half angry at himself, half pleading—that cold shivers ran him through whenever he imagined the blow returning.

But there are moments in a household where kindness must wear the stern mask of necessity. I would not yield. We set ourselves to crossing the marsh as we had learned to do: laying planks and wicker hurdles down upon the sucking ground, stepping forward, then lifting what lay behind us to place again before—an awkward, patient dance over treacherous earth, the wet making small, greedy sounds beneath every shift. Even the marsh had its voice: the whisper of reed-tops rubbing, the soft gulp of mud, the occasional plop of unseen frogs—soundscapes that, in their purity, steady the heart far more than the clangor of any city street ever could. In that wild quiet, danger announces itself honestly; it does not hide under iron wheels and shrieking metal.

We discovered nothing new beyond the signs of what had already been: tracks pressed deep into the reeds; the beaten-down lair where rushes, grasses, and bog-plants lay flattened as if a heavy rope had been dragged in circles. The place stank faintly of damp animal heat long gone cold.

At length we emerged beyond the thicket onto firmer ground near the sheer rock wall, and there—like providence poured into a channel—we saw a brook, clear and sparkling, threading out of an opening in the stone. The opening proved to be a cave of considerable size.

We entered. The roof arched above us in pale grandeur, hung with stalactites like frozen drips of time; in places they joined the floor to form pillars that seemed to hold up the very mountain. Underfoot lay a snow-white earth, fine and soft, with a smooth, soapy feel that clung to the fingers.

“This,” I said, rubbing it between thumb and forefinger and feeling it slip like cream, “is fullers’ earth. A fortune, in our way. As good as soap for washing, and it spares me the trouble of turning soap-boiler.”

My wife’s concerns were never far from my mind; clean linen, in a rough life, is not vanity but health. Even the simplest domestic want—fresh water, clean cloth, untainted food—becomes a kind of sacred text, written in the body’s comfort.

The brook slipped onward through an inner opening. Fritz, quick as ever to follow what runs and calls, passed through to trace it, and almost at once his voice came back, magnified by stone.

“Father! The passage widens greatly—come and see!”

I left Ernest and Jack in the outer cave and followed Fritz into the deeper dark. Before we advanced far I fired a pistol-shot; the report leapt away into the black, and the echoes returned in rolling waves as if the cave itself were a great drum. Jack would later mistake those reverberations for falling rock; to my ear they were a measure of distance—a kind of natural acoustics, solemn and uncorrupted. No manufactured hall ever speaks so truthfully as stone that has never known chisels.

I lit the stub of candle I always carried. Its flame burned clear and steady, yet it seemed pitifully small in that vastness. Still, it gave just enough light to catch the gleam on the walls.

Suddenly Fritz stopped, his breath audible in the hush.

“I verily believe this is a second cave of salt! See how the walls glance—how the roof throws back the light!”

I touched the slick surface, then tasted the wetness left by the stream.

“Not salt,” I said. “The water leaves no track and tastes sweet. I rather think we have penetrated a cave of rock crystal.”

Fritz’s eyes shone as if the candle had slipped inside them. “Then we have discovered a great treasure!”

“A treasure,” I agreed, “if it serves us. Otherwise it is like Crusoe’s lump of gold—pretty to behold, useless to eat.”

Yet even as I spoke, I knew the human heart keeps catalogues besides the pantry: wonders nourish a family too, though differently.

Fritz took up a stone. “At least I’ll break off a specimen.”

He struck, and a piece came away—dull and cloudy.

“What a pity,” he said, frowning. “I must knock off another.”

“Gently,” I warned. “You have spoiled the form. These crystals grow in pyramids—six-sided facets. You must not smash what nature has taken ages to shape.”

We explored as far as the candle allowed, turning this way and that, the floor crunching softly underfoot with scattered grit. Fritz secured at last a large lump that showed several crystals in near perfection—sharp as frozen sunlight. Then, our light sinking low, we withdrew. Fritz, unable to resist, fired one more “farewell” shot; the echoes answered like distant thunder trapped underground.

When we returned to the open cave and daylight, we found Jack sobbing bitterly. The moment he saw us he ran, seized me, and clung as if I might dissolve again into stone-shadow.

“My child—what is it?” I asked, alarmed.

“Oh!” he cried, gulping breath. “I thought you were lost. Twice I heard a noise as if rocks had shattered down, and I thought you and Fritz were crushed. It was horrible. Oh, how glad I am to see you!”

I held him and soothed him, explaining that the “shattering” had been only our pistol-shots, magnified by the cave’s throat. His fear, born of that echoing wilderness, touched me; sound is not merely sound—it is a force that shapes the spirit. A clean, honest echo may frighten a child, but it does not grind him down the way constant, meaningless clamor does. There is mercy even in wild alarm, because it ends, and silence returns.

When Jack had steadied, I asked why he had been alone.

“Ernest is over there among the reeds,” he said, pointing. “I daresay he did not hear the shots.”

I found Ernest indeed, seated with calm concentration, weaving reeds into a basket. He had fashioned it cleverly for fishing: a funnel-shaped entrance that invited the fish in and made escape difficult, the wide belly of the basket turning into a living trap. The work smelled of green sap and lake-water; the reeds rasped softly against one another in his hands.

“I shot a young serpent while you were away, father,” he said, with a seriousness that made my blood turn for a moment. “It lies there, covered with rushes. Nearly four feet long, and as thick as my arm.”

“A serpent!” I cried, and hastened to the place, heart hammering with the dread of a brood in the swamp.

But when I drew back the rushes I saw at once the sleek, dark body, the finlike edges—no scales, no coils of venom.

“A fine large eel, my boy,” I said, letting my breath go. “This will provide excellent supper tonight. I am glad you had the courage to kill it instead of fleeing from a supposed serpent.”

Ernest, a little pale still, answered honestly, “I thought it would be so horrid to be pursued and caught that I preferred facing it. My shot took effect—but it was very difficult to kill. It moved about even after the head was smashed.”

“The tenacity of life in eels is remarkable,” I told him. “Some say the best way is to grasp them by the neck and slap the tail smartly against stone or post.”

We returned by keeping close to the cliffs where the ground held firm. At the fountain we found my wife washing clothes, her hands red from the work, her face brightening instantly at our safe return. When she saw the white earth we brought, her practical joy was almost as great as Jack’s relief.

“Good,” she said, feeling it between her fingers. “Soap is nearly gone.”

That evening the eel was cooked for supper, and it proved richer and better than many fish, its flesh firm and white when properly handled. We skinned it cleanly, cut it into pieces, and simmered it with herbs and a pinch of salt until the air of our home grew warm with savory steam. Hunger in such a life is keen, but it is also discerning; after danger, food is never merely fuel—it is reassurance. Around that table the boys listened again to the tale of our plank-walk over the swamp and the crystal cavern’s cold glittering halls, while my wife measured each detail by what it might mean for the household: wash, store, preserve, endure.

Yet my mind returned, as it must, to the larger question: were we safe within the bounds between cliffs and sea? To answer it, preparations began at once for a broader search: beyond the river, as far as the Gap. I wished the whole family to go; and the decision pleased everyone, for travel together, even into uncertainty, was better than waiting at home for news.

Since we meant to be gone for several weeks, we took the small tent and a store of necessities—provisions, firearms, tools, cooking utensils, torches. Everything was packed onto the cart drawn by Storm and Grumble. Jack and little Franz mounted the load and played at once riders and drivers, their laughter adding a bright layer to the morning’s sound. My wife sat comfortably in the cart; Fritz rode ahead; Ernest and I walked. The dogs ranged at our flanks, and Fangs, our tame jackal, went with them—silent, watchful, like a shadow with ears.

We aimed first for Woodlands, and along the way we saw many traces of the serpent’s approach to Rockburg. Where soil was loose, the trail showed like a broad furrow, as if some great plough had passed without wheels.

At Falconhurst we halted and were welcomed, as always, by our poultry—clucking, fluttering, scolding with that domestic insistence that makes even a wilderness feel briefly civilized. The sheep and goats pressed in, nosing for salt and scraps.

By nightfall we reached Woodlands. All lay peaceful; no sign of the boa in that direction, no marks of apes. The little farm looked thriving—orderly as a well-tended kitchen shelf.

We spent the next day surveying the neighborhood and collecting cotton for pillows and cushions, for comfort too is a kind of strength. In the afternoon Franz accompanied me, carrying for the first time a small gun entrusted to him. The weight of it made him stand straighter; responsibility does that.

We took Fangs and Bruno and followed the left bank of the lake, threading among reedy thickets that forced us now and then away from the water. The dogs hunted in all directions, raising duck, snipe, and heron; but the birds flew directly across the lake so that Franz had no safe shot. He grew impatient and proposed firing at the black swans gliding on the glassy surface.

As he spoke, a harsh booming sound struck our ears, deep and hollow as if the earth itself were calling.

I paused, listening. Franz’s eyes widened. “Oh, father—can that be Swift, our young onager?”

“It cannot possibly be Swift,” I said. After a minute of attentive listening, I added, “I believe it is the cry of a bittern—a fine bird of the heron kind.”

Franz looked astonished. “How can a bird make such a roaring noise? One would think it an ox—more like lowing than braying.”

“The voice depends more on the windpipe and lungs, and the muscles that drive the breath, than on size,” I told him. “Consider the nightingale and the canary. Some say the bittern booms with its bill partly thrust into boggy ground, which deepens and muffles the sound.”

Franz longed for his first trophy to be so rare a bird. We sent the dogs into the wood and waited apart, each ready.

A sudden rustling shook the thicket. Franz fired, and his delighted shout rang out.

“I’ve hit him! I’ve hit him!”

“What have you hit?” I called.

“A wild pig!” he cried. “Bigger than Fritz’s!”

“Perhaps it is not a hog,” I said, hurrying to him. “Perhaps it is one of our little pigs from the farm. What will the old sow say to you then?”

But when I reached him I saw an animal certainly pig-like, yet not truly a pig: broad blunt snout, yellowish-grey bristles, no tail; and, on closer examination, webbed feet and curious teeth. It lay still, the life already gone.

“It is a capybara,” I said—“a water-loving creature of South America.”

Franz was beside himself with pride at having shot, as he called it, “a new creature.” But pride did not lighten the carcass. We soon realized carrying it whole would be difficult, and Franz, practical beneath his excitement, proposed opening and cleaning it to reduce its weight. We did so, hands working with the swift, competent motions necessity teaches. The smell was strange—earthy, animal, with an under-note that made me wary. Franz shouldered it in a game-bag until he grew tired, then asked if Bruno would bear it. The good dog accepted the burden willingly, and thus we reached Woodlands.

There we found Ernest surrounded by large rats—dead bodies strewn on the ground like fallen fruit.

“Where can all these have come from?” I exclaimed. “Have you and your mother been rat-hunting instead of gathering rice as you intended?”

Ernest answered with the air of one who has lived a tale and now recounts it.

“We came upon them unexpectedly. In the rice swamp, Knips sprang away to a long mound among the reeds and pounced on something trying to escape into a hole. He chattered and gnashed his teeth; the creature hissed and squeaked. When I ran up, Knips had a big rat by the tail. He would not let go, and the rat could not turn in the narrow entrance to bite him. I pulled it out and killed it with my stick.

“The mound looked curious, so I broke it open—hard work—and dislodged a dozen. Some I killed; many plunged into the water and escaped. Their dwelling was a vaulted tunnel of clay and mud, lined thick with sedges, rushes, water-lily leaves.

“There were other mounds close by. I stretched my game-bag across an entrance and hammered on the roof till a whole lot sprang out—several straight into the bag. I hit right and left, but repented when I found the whole community swarming about in excitement. Some escaped, many stopped in bewilderment, and some actually attacked me.

“It was anything but pleasant. I thought of Bishop Hatto in the Mouse Tower on the Rhine. Knips liked it as little as I did, skipping about desperately, though now and then he seized a rat by the neck. Just as I began to shout for help, Juno came dashing through reeds and water and made quick work. They fled at her. My mother forced her way through the marsh with difficulty and reached me at last. We collected the slain to show you—and for their skins.”

His story roused my curiosity, and I went to the place he described. There I found, to my surprise, a structure much like a beaver dam, though smaller and less complete.

“You have discovered a colony of beaver rats,” I told him. “They resemble beavers in skill and manner of life. Muskrat, musquash, and ondatra are other names. See—their webbed feet, their flattened tails. And we shall find two small glands containing musk. The sooner we strip the skins, the better; they will make caps.”

We returned to the house and met Fritz and Jack coming back from their own excursion. They reported no trace of serpents, great or small. Jack carried about a dozen eggs in his hat, carefully balanced; Fritz had shot two heath fowls, cock and hen.

At supper, Franz was eager to taste his capybara. Even he, in the first bite, could not keep his face from twisting at the peculiar flavor.

“It is the musk you taste,” I said, and I spoke of the odd presence of that scent in various creatures—the musk deer, musk ox, crocodile, and a muskrat of India said to taint even a corked bottle of wine if it merely runs across it—ending with the civet, or civet-cat.

“The civet is a handsome black-and-white animal,” I explained. “The perfume obtained from it was once considered a medicine; now it is chiefly used as a scent. It is formed in a double glandular pouch near the tail. The Dutch keep the creature in captivity for a continual supply. As it is quick and has sharp teeth, they place it in a long narrow cage where it cannot turn. A horn spoon is introduced, and the perfume—a thick oily stuff like butter—is scraped from the pouch. Then the civet is released until the supply is formed again.”

As I spoke, I could not help reflecting how words themselves can trap and handle living things. We name an animal, we classify it, we fit it into a foreign lexicon; yet the taste on the tongue—musk bitterness, leaf-spice sweetness—remains stubbornly personal, never fully carried by any term. Experience is always slightly untranslatable, even between father and son at the same table.

During the meal Jack suddenly ran to fetch his game-bag, producing fruit he had forgotten: several pale green apples, unknown to us. Their skin was smooth, their scent faintly warm, as if sugar and flower had agreed to whisper rather than shout.

“What are those?” I asked. “Are they good?”

“I hope so,” Jack said, “but Fritz and I were afraid of eating some awful poison, like the manchineel. So we brought them for inspection of the learned Master Knips.”

I cut one in two. It held a circle of seeds rather than the single stone of the manchineel. At that moment Knips sidled behind me, snatched up half, and began to munch with lively satisfaction. The boys, taking his appetite as proof, fell upon the fruit in a scramble so eager I had trouble saving two pieces for myself and their mother.

I believed it to be the cinnamon apple of the Antilles. Its flesh was tender and sweet with a faint spice—an aroma like distant baking, an imagined kitchen transported into jungle air.

Weariness settled over us at last. Mattresses and pillows were arranged, and the household of Woodlands slept.

At early light we set out again, steering toward a point between the sugar-brake and the Gap where we had once made an arbor of branches. It still stood in decent condition, so we spread sailcloth over it instead of pitching the tent and made comfortable quarters for the short time I meant to remain.

Our aim was to search for any trace of the boa constrictor or its kind. Fritz, Jack, and Franz went with me to the sugar-cane brake, and we convinced ourselves our enemy had not been there. It had been long since we enjoyed fresh cane-juice. We broke stalks, peeled away the tough rind, and chewed the fibrous sweetness until our mouths filled with cool syrup, spitting out the dry pulp. The air was full of green fragrance, and the canes rustled in the breeze like a thousand thin flutes.

Then the dogs began to bark violently, and we heard loud rustling and rattling through the cane thicket. We could see nothing a yard away. I hurried into open ground; the boys followed, guns ready. We stood, listening, the sound of movement coming nearer in a steady rush.

In a few minutes a herd of little pig-like creatures burst out, trotting briskly in single file as if obedience itself drove their legs. They were uniform grey, with short sharp tusks.

My double-barrel spoke twice; two fell. I was certain they were peccaries. The others did not scatter like deer; they continued after their leader, scarcely turning to avoid the dead, maintaining that same determined pace. Fritz and Jack fired as well and killed several more.

I remembered at once the odoriferous gland on the back must be removed immediately or the meat would be tainted beyond use. With the boys’ help I performed this operation without delay, hands working quickly before heat could set any rankness into the flesh.

Soon after, we heard shots in the direction of our hut where Ernest and my wife remained. I sent Jack to assist them and to fetch the cart for carrying our booty, while we opened and cleaned the animals to lessen weight.

Jack returned with Ernest and the cart. Ernest reported the procession had passed near the hut and that he, with Juno’s help, had secured three. This pleased me greatly, for I had resolved to cure a good store of hams. We loaded the cart; the boys crowned it with flowers and green boughs, and with songs of triumph that made the woods ring—music not purchased, not performed for applause, but rising out of work well done and hunger wisely answered.

Their mother waited anxiously; her relief at the sight of us safe was plain, but her eyes also measured the meat with a cook’s foresight: how much salt remained, how long smoke would hold, how many meals could be stretched across weeks.

After dinner we set to work. We singed and scalded off the bristles, the smell of singeing hair sharp in the air. I cut out the hams, divided the flitches, gave generous portions to the dogs, and cleansed and salted the meat with diligent care, rubbing crystals into every seam until the surfaces grew slick and pale. Meanwhile the boys prepared a shed where it would hang to cure in the smoke of fires made of green wood.

This unexpected labor detained us several days. On the second day, when the smoking-shed was ready, the boys begged to cook the smallest porker in the Otaheitean fashion. They dug a pit and burned dry grass, sticks, and weeds inside it, heating stones which they placed round the sides like a rough oven wall.

While the younger boys tended the fire, Fritz singed and washed the peccary thoroughly, then stuffed it with potatoes, onions, and herbs, adding a liberal sprinkling of salt and pepper. He sewed up the opening and wrapped the animal in large leaves to guard it from ash and dust—an improvisation that was, in its way, a form of culinary history: old techniques reborn in new hands, old knowledge surviving because it fed the living.

When the fire was no longer flame but embers, and the stones glowed hot, we laid the leaf-wrapped pig into the pit, covered it with hot ashes, then with earth, until the place looked like a great molehill.

Dinner was awaited with both appetite and curiosity. My wife, wary of our experiments, prepared plain food as a last resort. Yet even she watched the mound with interest; for a family’s culture is often preserved not in books but in what it dares to cook.

The curing-hut pleased her: roomy enough to hang all our hams and bacon. In the middle, on a wide hearth, we kept a large fire smouldering by heaping it with damp grass and green wood. The hut closed above so the smoke filled it and penetrated the meat. For several days the hams hung there, darkening slowly, absorbing that bitter-sweet preservation.

When Fritz at last announced he would open the earth-oven, everyone gathered. He removed earth and turf and stones, and a delicious odor rose as if summoned from the ground itself: roast pork—yes—but with a surprising spice, warm and intricate, that made my mouth fill with saliva at once. I understood then it came from the leaves.

The peccary was lifted out carefully. Once the cinders were brushed away, it looked beautifully cooked: skin browned, flesh tender, juices sealed within. Fritz received hearty praise—even from his mother.

The leaves, I believed, were from a tree known in Madagascar, called by the natives ravensara—“good leaf”—said to combine the scent of nutmeg, clove, and cinnamon. Its fruit is a nut with a gentler perfume, from which an essence is distilled and valued in native cookery. In speaking of it, I felt again that tug between languages: a native word carries a whole world of taste and practice; translated, it becomes merely “good leaf,” and the old music dims. How much disappears when the dominant tongue names and shelves everything as if it owns it.

During the days of curing, we roamed the neighborhood in all directions. We found no trace of the serpent, but we gained many useful things—among them gigantic bamboos, fifty to sixty feet long and proportionately thick. Cut across near the joints, they became casks, tubs, and pots; the long sharp thorns that girded the stem at intervals proved as strong and handy as iron nails.

One day we visited the farm at Prospect Hill and were provoked to find the vagabond apes had been there, doing mischief as they had done before. The animals and poultry were scattered; the cottage was torn and dirtied beyond any quick repair. With heavy hearts we left the disorder as it was, resolving to set it right later.

When all was ready for continuing our journey, we closed and barricaded the hut where we left the store of bacon. Then, arranging our march in our usual patriarchal fashion, we set out toward the Gap—the thorough defense of that defile being the chief object before us.

Because our last halting-place was hemmed in by shrubs, bamboos, and brushwood, we had during our stay cut a path through the cane thicket in the direction we now meant to travel. This proved invaluable; the loaded cart passed without impediment.

Beyond, the ground opened and lay tolerably level. In a few hours we reached the extreme limit of our coast territory.

We halted on the edge of a little wood. Behind it, to the right, rose the frowning cliffs of the mountain gorge; to the left ran the torrent, leaving between water and rock the narrow pass we called the Gap, and then hurrying onward to mingle with the sea. The place had its own stern music: water’s constant roar, wind stirring leaves, the occasional cry of a bird. Here, sound would warn us long before sight could.

The wood stood high and offered pleasant shelter within gunshot of the pass’s mouth. I chose it for our camp. We unpacked the cart and made our arrangements for safety and comfort, first examining the wood to see if it harbored dangerous animals. We found nothing worse than wild cats; we disturbed several hunting birds and small game, but they fled at our approach.

By the time dinner was ready we were fatigued. The day grew unusually sultry and oppressive, pressing heat onto our skin like a heavy cloth, so we rested until evening. When cooler air returned, strength returned with it. We pitched the tent and prepared for the next day, for I intended then to pass through the defile and make a longer excursion across the savannah than any we had yet undertaken.

Everything was made ready for an early start. My brave wife consented to remain in camp with Franz as her companion, while the three elder boys—and all the dogs except Juno—were to go with me. The night settled around our small human order: smoke from our fire drifting into the trees, salted meat hanging in memory and in store, and beyond the wood the Gap waiting, silent but not mute, its dark throat shaped to carry whatever sound the morning would bring.
