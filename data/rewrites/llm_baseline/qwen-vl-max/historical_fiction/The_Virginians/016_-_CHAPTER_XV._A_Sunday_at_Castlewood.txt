The second day after Harry Warrington’s arrival at Castlewood was a Sunday, and the quiet of the estate was broken only by the distant chime of the chapel bell. The chapel, attached to the castle, served as the village church, and a door from the great hall led directly into the family’s private pew—a stately box with carved woodwork and velvet cushions where the Esmonds had sat for generations. As the hour approached, the household gathered in order: my lord at the head, followed by his sister, Madame de Bernstein, the ladies of the house, and the young Virginian cousin, who watched with curiosity as the family took their places.

Below, the villagers filled the pews—men in smockfrocks, women in wide-brimmed hats, children peering over shoulders. A few ancient banners hung from the rafters, dusty and faded, bearing crests long forgotten by all but the most attentive eye. Harry found himself imagining that these had once flown over battlefields during the Civil Wars, carried by loyal retainers of the Esmond name, whose history he had read with pride in the letters of his ancestors.

At the altar rails stood the effigy of Sir Francis Esmond, who had lived in the time of King James I—the common forefather of every person present in the family pew. He knelt eternally on his stone hassock, hands folded upon a prayer book carved from marble, beneath an emblazoned shield of arms. It was a silent sentinel of memory, watching over the living as they knelt.

Madame de Bernstein, widow of a bishop, never missed a service. She conducted her devotions with solemn dignity, her posture and expression so composed that she seemed almost a match for the stone ancestor beside her. The clergyman, Mr. Sampson, was a tall, handsome man with a high colour and a voice full of life. He read the service with such animation that even the Scripture passages sounded like scenes from a play. His tone rose and fell with dramatic effect, and when he reached the psalm, the organ began to swell—played by one of the younger ladies of the house, her fingers dancing lightly over the keys.

But then came a sudden disturbance. From the servants’ pew, a deep, rich voice broke through the harmony—not in tune, yet so powerful and clear that it silenced the choir. It was Gumbo, Mr. Warrington’s African servant, who had recognized the melody and begun to sing it with astonishing volume and sweetness. The whole congregation turned. Even the parson covered his mouth with his handkerchief, and the London gentlemen blinked in astonishment. Gumbo, unaware of the commotion, sang on—his eyes fixed on the book before him, though he could not read a single word. He sang by ear, and with such passion that the clerk’s voice faded into silence. When the music ended, there was a moment of hushed awe, then a ripple of laughter from the back.

After the hymn, the sermon began. Mr. Sampson spoke plainly, without the stiff phrasing Harry had grown used to from the Calvinistic preachers in Virginia. Instead, he told a story—one he had witnessed just the week before: the execution of a horse-stealer at Assizes. He described the man’s life—the decent farmer, the loving wife, the children clinging to him at the cart. He painted the agony of separation, the walk to the gallows, the final moments under the noose. He did not quote scripture; he spoke as a man to men, warning them of the dangers of vice—drinking, gambling, cockfighting, poaching—and how easily a good man might fall.

He made the law seem alive: watching in the dark woods, setting traps, chasing the thief across highways and fields. He galloped the stolen horse through counties, but always behind came Retribution—swift, relentless, unyielding. He acted out the scene: whispering to the criminal, dropping a handkerchief on the clerk’s head. Harry started as if struck.

Twenty minutes passed, but to him it felt like five. The sermon was gripping, vivid, and real. The gentry in the great pew leaned forward, captivated. Harry noticed that the servants, especially Gumbo, sat rigid with attention, as if afraid to breathe. But the villagers paid little mind. Gaffer Brown and Gammer Jones accepted the tale as part of life. The red-cloaked girls under their broad hats remained untouched.

When the sermon ended, my lord nodded slowly to the pulpit. “Sampson has been strong today,” he said. “He has assaulted the Philistines in great force.”

“Beautiful, beautiful!” murmured Harry.

“Bet five to four it was his Assize sermon,” said William. “He’s been over to Winton to preach, and to see those dogs.”

As the organ played the last notes and the congregation filed out into the golden afternoon light, only Sir Francis Esmond remained—still kneeling, still praying, in stone.

Mr. Sampson stepped from his vestry in his cassock and greeted the gentlemen lingering near the pew. “Come up,” called William, “and tell us about those dogs.”

The chaplain smiled and nodded. They moved into the gallery connecting the church to the house, where they joined my lord and Harry. Mr. Sampson was introduced to the Virginian cousin. He bowed deeply, expressing hope that Harry would take inspiration from his English kin.

“Are you related to Sir Miles Warrington of Norfolk?” he asked.

“Yes,” replied Harry. “He was my father’s elder brother.”

“A pity he has a son,” said the chaplain. “A fine estate, and Mr. Warrington looks well suited to inherit it.”

“Tell me about my uncle,” Harry urged.

“Two more jolly dogs, two more drunken dogs, saving your presence, Mr. Warrington, than Sir Miles and his son, I never saw,” said the parson. “Sir Miles was a staunch friend of Sir Robert’s. He can drink down any man in the county—except his own son and a few others.”

“And the other dogs?” asked William, grinning. “For Heaven hath made him a prey to dogs and all kinds of birds, like the Greeks in the Iliad—”

“I know that line,” Harry interrupted, blushing. “I only know five more, but I know that one.” He looked down, thinking: *Ah, my dear brother George knew all the Iliad and the Odyssey—and nearly every book ever written.*

“What on earth are you talking about now?” asked William.

The chaplain returned to the dogs. Then, naturally, they turned to horses.

William was eager about the Six Year Old Plate at Huntingdon. “Have you brought any news of it, Parson?”

“The odds are five to four on Brilliant against the field,” said the parson gravely, “but mind you, Jason is a good horse.”

“Whose horse?” asked my lord.

“Duke of Ancaster’s. By Cartouche out of Miss Langley,” replied the divine.

“Do you have horse races in Virginia?” asked the chaplain.

“Haven’t we!” cried Harry. “But oh! I long to see a good English race!”

“Do you… bet a little?” continued the parson.

“I have done such a thing,” Harry replied with a smile.

“I’ll take Brilliant even against the field, for ponies with you, cousin!” shouted William.

“I’ll give or take three to one against Jason!” said the clergyman.

“I don’t bet on horses I don’t know,” said Harry, surprised to hear the parson speak so freely, remembering his sermon just half an hour earlier.

“Hadn’t you better write home and ask your mother?” sneered William.

“Will, Will!” called my lord. “Our cousin Warrington is free to bet or not, as he likes. Have a care how you venture on either of them, Harry. Will is an old file, in spite of his smooth face, and as for Parson Sampson, I defy our ghostly enemy to get the better of him.”

“Him and all his works, my lord!” said Mr. Sampson, bowing.

Harry bristled. “I’ll tell you what, cousin Will,” he said. “I manage my own affairs in my own way. I make my own bets on my own judgment. And I don’t need any relation to choose them for me. But since I’m your guest, and you want to show hospitality, I’ll take your bet—there. And so Done and Done.”

“Done,” said William, glancing sideways.

“Of course it’s the regular odds in the paper you give me?” Harry asked.

“Well, no,” growled William. “The odds are five to four—that’s the fact. You may have ‘em if you like.”

“Nay, cousin,” said Harry firmly, “a bet is a bet. And I take you too, Mr. Sampson.”

“Three to one against Jason. I lay it. Very good,” said the parson.

“Is it to be ponies too, Mr. Chaplain?” asked Harry, puffing out his chest as if he held Lombard Street in his pocket.

“No, no. Thirty to ten. It is enough for a poor priest to win.”

“Here goes a great slice out of my quarter’s hundred,” thought Harry. “Well, I shan’t let these Englishmen think I’m afraid of them. I didn’t start it, but for the honour of Old Virginia, I won’t go back.”

With the betting settled, William scowled and headed toward the stables, where he liked to smoke with the grooms. The parson went off to charm the ladies, while my lord and Harry remained alone.

Since Harry’s arrival, my lord had spoken little. He was courteous, but reserved—often sitting at the head of the table without a word.

“I suppose your property is a fine one by now?” he asked.

“I reckon it’s almost as big as an English county,” answered Harry, proud of his inheritance. “And the land’s as good, too, for many things.”

“Indeed?” said my lord, surprised.

“When it belonged to my father, it did not yield much.”

“Pardon me, my lord,” said Harry sharply. “You know how it belonged to your father. It was because my grandfather chose not to claim his right.” [This matter is discussed in the Author's previous work, The Memoirs of Colonel Esmond.]

“Of course, of course,” said my lord hastily. “We of the Virginian house owe you nothing but our own.”

“And our own,” continued Harry, “and the hospitality you’re showing me.”

“You are heartily welcome to both. Were you hurt by the betting?”

“Well,” said the youth, “I am sort o’ hurt. Your welcome is different from ours. At home, we hold out a hand, give you of our best. Here, you take us in, feed us well, but don’t seem to care when we come or go. That’s what I’ve been thinking—and now I’ve said it.”

With that, he knocked a billiard ball across the table and laughed.

“A la bonne heure!” said my lord. “We are cold to the stranger within and without our gates. We don’t cry when we see our cousin. We don’t cry when he leaves—but do we pretend?”

“No,” said Harry, “you try to get the better of him in a bet.”

“Is there no such practice in Virginia? Do sporting men there not try to outwit each other? What was that story you told Aunt Emily about the British officers and Tom of Spotsylvania?”

“That’s fair,” said Harry. “It’s usual practice. A stranger must look out. I don’t mind the parson—if he wins, he may have it. But a relation! To think my own blood cousin wants money from me!”

“A Newmarket man would get the better of his father,” said my lord. “My brother has been on the turf since he rode from Cambridge. If you play cards with him—he will beat you if he can.”

“Well, I’m ready!” said Harry. “I’ll play any game I know. Or jump. Or ride. Or row. Or wrestle. Or shoot—with him—now.”

The older man smiled and held out his hand.

“Anything, but don’t fight with him,” said my lord.

“If I do, I’ll whip him! Hanged if I don’t!” cried Harry.

But a look of displeasure from my lord brought him to his senses.

“A hundred pardons, my lord!” he said, blushing. “I talked ill-mannered, angry and hurt. But to boast about prowess to my host and kinsman—that’s worse. We Americans don’t boast, believe me.”

“You are the first I’ve ever met,” said my lord, smiling. “And I take you at your word. I give you fair warning about cards and betting—that is all, my boy.”

“Leave a Virginian alone!” said Harry. “We are a match for most men.”

Lord Castlewood did not laugh. His eyebrows arched slightly, and his grey eyes turned downward.

“So you can bet fifty guineas and afford to lose them? So much the better for you, cousin. Do those great Virginian estates yield a great revenue?”

“More than sufficient for all of us—for ten times as many as we are now,” replied Harry. (*“What, he’s pumping me,”* thought the lad.)

“And your mother makes her son and heir a handsome allowance?”

“As much as ever I choose to draw, my lord!”

“Peste! I wish I had such a mother!” exclaimed my lord. “But I have only a stepmother—and she draws me.”

There was a knock at the door—the dinner bell.

“Shall we go?” said my lord, taking Harry’s arm.

In the dining room, Mr. Sampson became the centre of attention. He regaled the ladies with stories from London—whispers from Mayfair, scandals from the clubs, the latest gossip about politicians, lovers, and courtiers. He knew who had run away with whom, the last bon mot of Mr. Selwyn, the wildest bet of March and Rockingham. He spoke of the king’s quarrel with Madame Walmoden, the Duke’s new romance, the Princess of Wales’s favourites, and the hangman’s latest victim—who had behaved nobly in the cart.

The ladies laughed freely, unshocked by his plain talk. Not a blush stirred among them. They listened, enchanted, until the chapel bell rang again for afternoon service.

No sermon this time. The chaplain would return after a bottle of Burgundy.

William called for another, and the chaplain tossed off a glass before rushing out. Half an hour later, he burst back in, shouting for another bottle.

Then they joined the ladies, card tables were set, and the evening unfolded in easy play. Madame de Bernstein was a master at piquet—only the chaplain could match her. The game lasted late. The sun sank behind the elms, rooks cawed their evening song, and still they played.

The clock tolled hour after hour, unnoticed. The moon rose, stars appeared. At nine, the groom announced supper.

As they ate, the postboy’s horn echoed through the village. My lord’s letters arrived. He skimmed them, smiled, and handed the paper to William.

“Read that, Will.”

William read aloud. Then he let out a roar.

“Gracious heavens, William! What has happened?” cried one of the sisters.

“Mercy, child, why do you swear so dreadfully?” asked the mother.

“What’s the matter?” inquired Madame de Bernstein, waking from her punch-induced doze.

“Read it, Parson!” said William, thrusting the paper forward.

“Bit, by the Lord!” roared the chaplain, slamming the paper down.

“My lord read aloud: *‘The Six Year Old Plate at Huntingdon was won by Jason, beating Brilliant, Pytho, and Ginger. The odds were five to four on Brilliant against the field, three to one against Jason, seven to two against Pytho, and twenty to one against Ginger.’*

“I owe you a half-year’s income of my poor living, Mr. Warrington,” groaned the parson. “I will pay when my noble patron settles with me.”

“A curse upon the luck!” growled William, reaching for another bumper.

“Nay, cousin Will,” said Harry. “It was but in jest. I can’t think of taking your money.”

“Curse me, sir,” said William, “do you suppose, if I lose, I can’t pay? That I want to be beholden to any man alive? That’s a good joke. Isn’t it, Parson?”

“I think I have heard better,” said the clergyman.

“Hang it,” said William, “let us have another bowl.”

Let us hope the ladies did not wait for it. For certain, they had already had their share.
