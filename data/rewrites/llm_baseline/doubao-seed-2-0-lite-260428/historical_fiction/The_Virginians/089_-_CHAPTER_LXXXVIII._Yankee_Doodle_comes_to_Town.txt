In the days of old British dominion across the Atlantic, one of the quiet uses we made of our American colonies was as a holding ground for all the sinners we had no place for at home. Beyond the convicts and assigned labourers we shipped across the ocean, we unloaded whole droves of scapegraces and disgraced younger sons whose debt, dissipation, and bad behaviour had made England far too hot for them to dwell in. Just as Captain Cook used to hand the native inhabitants of his newly discovered islands stray English livestock and other odd specimens of European civilization, we made a point of sending over all our finest black sheep to browse the colonial pastures as best they could, and propagate their rare, precious breed.

I must own I bore a little guilt for this practice myself, having gone out of my way to secure a living in America for the worthy Hagan, husband to my kinswoman Maria. At the very least I was guilty in that, for we could find no employment for him in England, we were glad to pack him off to Virginia and hand him a colonial pulpit-cushion to thump from the height of a parish. To his credit, he bore himself there as a brave, honest gentleman, doing his full duty by his congregation and his king, and doing no small credit to my small act of patronage. My wife Theo would always urge this fact whenever I confessed my scruples on the matter, proving as was her wont that my conduct on this, as on all other subjects, sprang from the highest possible moral principle. But would I ever have granted Hagan the family living at home, and selected him and his wife to minister to our own parish? I fear not. I never once doubted our cousin Maria’s sincere repentance, but I will admit I was secretly glad when she went to work it out far from polite society in the wilderness. I say this openly, acknowledging my own foolish pride and error. Twice when I needed them most, that kind Maria aided me with steady sympathy and unwavering friendship. She bore her own hardships bravely, and soothed the distresses of others with a devotion no one could fault. And yet I, and some of my family (though never Theo), would look down on her. Shame, shame on our foolish, cruel pride!

Poor Lady Maria was far from the only member of our family we packed off to the American wilderness to be rid of them. After borrowing, lying, and cheating every last person he could reach at home until no one would lend him a penny, or let him run a scam, the Honourable William Esmond, Esquire, was granted a cushioned sinecure in New York. His noble elder brother and his royal master alike heartily hoped they would never set eyes on him again. When the first troubles broke out across the colonies, we began to hear tales of the man and his misdeeds in his new home. Lies and mischief ran ahead of him wherever he travelled. My Lord Dunmore sent word to me that Will was declaring publicly all across New York that the Castlewood estate was only held by our family at his brother’s pleasure: that his father, out of some faint regard for Madam Esmond, his lordship’s half-sister, had granted her the property only for her lifetime, and that he, William, was currently in negotiations with the present Lord Castlewood to buy the full reversion of the estate!

We held the full original deed of gift locked in our strongroom at Castlewood, and it had been properly registered in due legal form at Williamsburg, so we had no fear of losing our home over the lie. But the very audacity of the claim spoke volumes, and Hal and I swore that the first time we ever met Mr. William face to face, we would force him to retract that pretty story outright. I need scarcely describe the full force of Madam Esmond’s fury when she heard the tale. “What? My father, the Marquis of Esmond, was a liar, and I am a cheat, am I?” my mother cried. “He will steal my son’s property the moment I am dead, will he?” She was for writing not just to Lord Castlewood in England, but to his Majesty himself at St James’s, and I only dissuaded her by assuring her that Will’s lies were notorious to every single person who knew him, and no one could possibly expect him to be inconsistent enough, in our case, to tell the truth.

Not long after, we heard he had become one of the loudest Loyalist firebrands in New York, first serving as Captain and then as Major of a volunteer corps that sent glowing addresses out to all the well-disposed gentlemen across the colonies, announcing their full readiness to die for the mother country.

Back in Williamsburg, we found we could not stay in our rented house for all the broken windows left by rioters, so we nailed up the shutters of that ill-starred mansion, and Madam Esmond left the little colonial capital behind, my whole family returning to Richmond, which had been emptied of the members of the recently dissolved Assembly. Captain Hal and his wife returned early to their plantation, and I, not a little vexed by the direction events were taking, split my days between my own household and that of the Governor, who made a great show of being eager for my advice and company. Strong political divides ran through every circle, but there was still no open personal quarrel between the opposing sides. Even after the Assembly was dissolved and its members adjourned to a tavern, where they held that famous meeting where I believe the very idea of a unified congress of all the colonies was first floated, the most vocal opposition leaders remained on friendly terms with his Excellency, dined at his table, and joined him on pleasure rides and excursions. When the session ended, the gentry all rode home to their respective counties and held local meetings; as the assemblies of nearly every other colony had also been abruptly dissolved, it was agreed across the board that a general continental congress must be called. Philadelphia, the largest and most important city on the continent, was chosen as the meeting place, and those famous conferences began: the angry, tense preface to a full-scale war. We were still singing *God Save the King*, still sending humble petitions to the throne, but the air hummed with danger.

I rode out one day to visit my brother Harry at Fanny’s Mount, his new plantation that lay not far from our own Castlewood, with the Rappahannock River running between the two properties, close to the banks of the Mattaponey. Harry rode out on business early that morning, so in the afternoon I decided to take a ride myself, and one of his grooms told me his master had headed towards Willis’s Ordinary. Thinking no harm at all, I set off in that direction to find him. I had just ridden out of the trees into a wide, sunlit clearing not far from Willis’s when I came across Captain Hal on horseback, surrounded by some three-and-thirty countrymen, armed with every sort of weapon you could imagine: pikes, scythe blades lashed to wooden poles, old fowling pieces, and worn muskets. Hal, with two spry young fellows acting as junior officers under him, was putting the whole ragtag group through drill.

A confused, guilty look crossed Hal’s face the moment he saw me. “Present arms!” he barked, and the whole makeshift regiment stumbled through the salute as best they could. “Captain Cade, this is my brother, Sir George Warrington.”

“As a relation of yours, Colonel, the gentleman is more than welcome,” the man addressed as Captain said, holding out a calloused hand to shake mine.

“And — and a true friend to Virginia,” Hal added, his face flushing bright red.

“Thank you for that, gentlemen, please God it is true,” I said, at which the whole ragged little regiment gave a hearty cheer for the Colonel and his brother.

When drill was over, the officers and the men were all eager to adjourn to Willis’s Ordinary for drinks, but Colonel Hal told them he could not stop for refreshment that afternoon, and we trotted away together towards his house.

“So Hal, the cat’s well and truly out of the bag,” I said.

He gave me a hard, determined look. “I’d say there are far wilder cats still waiting to claw their way out. This was always bound to happen, George. And — don’t tell Madam, will you?”

“Good God!” I cried. “Do you truly mean that you and these fellows I saw out there are going to make war against the greatest nation on earth, with the finest, best-trained army in the world?”

“I reckon we’re going to get one awful whipping, that’s the plain truth,” Hal said. “But then, George,” he added, with that sweet, kind smile of his, “we’re young. A whipping or two never hurt any lad, did it? Isn’t that right, old Dolly?” He gave his shaggy old hunting hound, trotting at his horse’s heels, a playful tap of his whip.

I did not try to press our British Loyalist arguments on him then — I had made that case dozens of times before, to no avail. He always brushed my reasons aside, saying, “It’s all very well for you, brother. You speak as an Englishman, and you cast your lot with your country. I cast mine with mine.” There was no arguing that point, I admitted. All two sides could do then was fight it out, and the side that won got to say what was right. Who was right in the old civil wars a century before? King or Parliament? The side that came out on top was right, and the Parliamentary victory was far more humane than the Cavalier triumph would ever have been. Nay, even as a Loyalist, I could not help the strange feeling that if we Tories won the day in America, the triumph would be so bloody and terrible, strung full of gallows ropes and scaffolds and noble heads laid low, that I secretly wanted the Whigs to win.

My brother Hal, who would soon distinguish himself greatly in command of his regiment, never allowed an unkind word to be spoken against the enemy he fought. “The officers of the British army are gentlemen,” he would say. “At least, they have not changed much since the days I served under them. You may find scoundrels and ruffians amongst the King’s troops — I daresay we could find just as many amongst our own lot. Our business is to beat his Majesty’s forces, not to slander them. Any rascal can call names.” From a nickname Mr. Lee gave him that many of his rough backcountry troopers did not fully understand, Harry would soon be known across the Continental Army as “Chevaleer Baird,” a knight truly without fear and without reproach.

When I pressed him how his ragtag militia could ever hope to beat regulars, Hal had a steady answer. “They will beat them, Mr. George. That is exactly what they will do.”

“Great heavens!” I cried. “If you had your old company of the 67th Foot regulars under your command, would you not charge straight at five hundred such men and cut them to pieces without a second thought?”

“With my old company of the 67th, I would go anywhere,” Hal said. “And I agree with you, at this moment I know far more about soldiering than any of these lads. But you put me on that open clearing you just found us on, give me half a dozen of my men with rifles posted in the woods all around the edge of it — who do you think wins, George? You were Aide-de-Camp to General Braddock. You know the answer better than any man alive.”

There was no arguing with that level of quiet determination. “You know exactly where my loyalties lie, Hal,” I said. “And now that I have stumbled across you drilling your militia, I have a duty to report what I saw to the Governor.”

“Of course you must,” he said, easily. “You saw the local county militia out for drill. You will see just the same sight in every colony from here to the St Lawrence River, all the way down to Georgia. Since I’m an old regular soldier, they elected me their Colonel. What could be more natural? Come on, brother, let us trot on. Dinner will be on the table soon, and Mrs. Fanny hates to be kept waiting.”

We rode up to his house, which stood open as all Virginian gentlemen’s houses did in those days, where every friend, neighbour, stranger and traveller was guaranteed a warm welcome.

“So, Mrs. Fanny,” I said, when we walked in. “I have found out what game my brother has been playing out of doors.”

“I trust the Colonel will have all the sport he deserves before long,” she said, tossing her head with a bright, defiant smile. My own wife still thought Harry had been out hunting all afternoon, and I did not have the heart to undeceive her, even as the heavy shadow of coming war settled soft and dark over all of us.
