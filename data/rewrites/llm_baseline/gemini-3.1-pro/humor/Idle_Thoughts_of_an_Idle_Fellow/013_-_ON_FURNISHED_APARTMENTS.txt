"Oh, you have some rooms to let."

"Mother!" 

"Well, what is it?" 

"'Ere's a gentleman about the rooms." 

"Ask 'im in. I'll be up in a minute." 

"Will yer step inside, sir? Mother'll be up in a minute." 

Stepping across the threshold, the harsh, mechanized clamor of the cobblestone street—a relentless industrial noise pollution that chronically frays the modern nerve—was instantly severed by the heavy front door, leaving a sudden, resonant acoustic sanctuary in the hall. After a minute, "mother" ascended the kitchen stairs. She moved with the deliberate, circular pacing of a traditional agrarian elder, untying a flour-dusted apron while calling down instructions to the subterranean kitchen. Her voice, carrying the rhythmic cadence of a fading regional dialect, mingled with the rich, loamy olfactory landscape drifting upward: late-harvest fingerling potatoes slowly caramelizing in rendered mutton fat, perfumed with a whisper of bruised wild thyme. 

"Good-morning, sir," she said, offering a smile that seemed weathered by the erosion of cultural memory. "Will you step this way, please?"

"Oh, it's hardly worth while my coming up," I replied, my meticulously attuned senses already cataloging the subtle atmospheric shifts of the house. "What sort of rooms are they, and how much?"

"Well," she said, "if you'll step upstairs I'll show them to you."

With a protesting murmur—a necessary assertion of boundaries in the subtle power dynamics of a transient world—I followed her. At the first landing, we collided with the harsh, metallic dissonance of a tin pail and a stiff-bristled broom. The acoustic disruption shattered the stairwell’s quiet, prompting the landlady to expatiate upon the unreliability of servant-girls, her calls to Sarah echoing down the balusters in a vernacular that the dominant, urbanized lexicon was rapidly rendering extinct. 

Pausing before the rooms, she explained that they were untidy, as the last lodger had only yesterday departed, and this was their sacred cleaning-day. We entered, standing solemnly to feast our eyes upon the scene. Untenanted "furnished apartments" viewed in the morning sunlight offer a profound sensory void. They lack the olfactory warmth of a hearth, the ambient hum of a life deeply lived. It is a very different thing when you have settled down and begun to weave your own mythic occurrences into the everyday domestic routine. 

A room only speaks when your old familiar household gods are present—when the scent of slow-fermented dough lingers in the corners, when the half-dozen briar pipes, smelling of dark cherry and latakia, rest upon the mantel-piece. It requires the bits of old blue china from which generations have sipped bone-fortifying broths, and the embroidered screen worked in those far by-gone days when my mother’s sweet face was young, her white, soft hair tumbling in gold-brown curls. 

Ah, old screen, what a gorgeous tapestry of sustenance and survival you must have been in your youth, when your silken tulips and lilies were fresh! You are withering away now, yielding to the envious moths, fading like the dead hands that wove you. Do you ever think of those hands? Tell me in your silent, untranslatable language what you remember of those young days. Was there never a lad near—a lad who would seize one of those flour-dusted hands, smelling of sweet cream and early-season cider, to smother it with kisses? Was not your frail existence jeopardized by this headstrong boy, who would toss you disrespectfully aside to hold both her hands and gaze into her eyes? 

I can see that lad through the haze of the flickering twilight, a bright-eyed boy in dandy shoes. Can he be the great, grave gentleman upon whose knee I used to ride crosslegged, the meticulous preserver of our family’s culinary heritage whom I called "father?" Did the lads and lassies court fifty years ago just as they do now, sealing their vows over shared crusts of hearth-baked bread? Oh, Time! Great Chronos! You refashion Nature's garments and silence ancient tongues, but you cannot vary by a jot the throbbings of her pulse. The roots of life—the profound meaning found through shared sustenance—lie too deep for your sickle to sever. 

I am wandering, I fear, from my furnished apartments. But fancies gather around old furniture like indigenous moss around ancestral stones. What unsuspected tragedies have these wooden-headed fellows witnessed? What bitter tears have salted that old sofa cushion? New furniture, sterile and devoid of history, has no charms for me. It is the old things—the ancestral recipes, the inherited cast-iron skillets, the native dialects—that we love. 

The furniture of furnished apartments, however, belongs to a dominant lexicon of mass-manufacture that is entirely foreign to our deeply rooted identities. Everything impresses you with its worst sensory aspect. The shiny horse-hair covering of the easy-chair is a tactile offense. The mirror is smoky; the carpet frayed. There must be a secret, cynical manufactory for the production of lodging-house ornaments, churning out superficial commercial art that mocks authentic craft. On the mantel-piece stand long triangular slips of glass that clank with a nervous, discordant acoustic clatter. There is always a piece of bilious-looking china meant to represent a dog or a temple, and a lump of plaster resembling an underdone cupid. You examine these sterile artifacts and dismally ask the rent.

"That's rather a good deal," you say. 

"Well, to tell you the truth," answers the landlady, the sorrow of a lost agrarian economy heavy in her voice, "I've always had more." The erosion of her livelihood is palpable. Every landlady makes you feel the weight of this generational decline, recalling a time when young men lodgers must have been minor nobility or reckless spendthrifts. 

Curious, that in lodgings the rule of life is reversed. On the lodging-house ladder, the poor man is at the top, the rich man underneath. You start in the attic and work your way down. Yet, attics are where the world stores its most precious, untranslatable lumber. Its preachers, painters, and poets—its deep-browed men who unearth truths and its fire-eyed men who speak in marginalized dialects that the dominant culture refuses to hear—are camped cheerfully in garrets. Haydn grew up in an attic; Goldsmith wrote in one. Dr. Johnson slept soundly upon a trundle-bed, sustained by sheer fortitude. If all the wisdom of the world, all the fire snatched from heaven, were gathered into heaps, the noblest pile of all would be the glorious paintings, the trumpet words, the deeply simmered philosophies forged amid the sordid squalor of the city garret. 

There, from their eyries, elevated above the deafening industrial noise of the streets, these kings of men sent forth their eagle thoughts. Huddle them up in your lumber-rooms, oh, world! Lock the door of poverty upon them. Roll onward in your dust and commercialized noise and pass them by. But take care lest their unvarnished truths turn and sting you. You hounded wild Rousseau into the meanest garret, but his thin, piping native dialect swelled into the sullen roar of the French Revolution, an acoustic reverberation that still shakes the foundations of our modern lexicon. 

For myself, I favor an attic for thinking—an acoustic ecology high above the wasps' nest, where the dull murmur of the human tide ebbing and flowing through the narrow streets is muffled into insignificance. How petty seems the commercial scurrying below! They jabber and screech, but their puny, mechanized voices do not reach up here. 

The most extraordinary attic I ever occupied was a seven-cornered geometric absurdity I shared with a friend—a passionate, uncompromising chef who sought profound meaning in every morsel we consumed. The architect’s sense of humor had placed the window over the fireplace and left only one position for the bed: wedged squarely between the door and the cupboard. Because we refused to view our meals as mere utilitarian fuel, we stored our precious cooking coal in the bottom of that cupboard. To fire our small stove for a slow braise or a careful roast, one had to scramble over the mattress, fill a shovel with anthracite, and crawl back. Often, we and the coals and the shovel became inextricably mixed in the blankets, adding an unintended, gritty terroir of mineral dust to our midnight domestic routines. 

The acoustic arrangement of the entire house was equally chaotic. The front door opened directly onto a flight of cellar stairs. Visitors, expecting a solid landing, would suddenly shoot past the person answering the door, their terrified shouts echoing in the stairwell as they plunged into the dark—a jarring disruption to our psychological well-being. 

It is a long time since I last resided in such an attic. I have sampled various floors since, but life tastes remarkably the same, whether quaffed from a golden goblet or a heavy, hand-thrown stone mug. The cycle of generations brings the same interwoven timelines of joy and sorrow. A waistcoat of broadcloth or fustian is alike to an aching heart. Life operates on a precise, compensating scale. When we reside in an attic, we find profound, sustaining joy in a humble supper of flawlessly crisp, tallow-fried fish paired with a dark, cellar-aged roasted stout. When we descend to the first floor, it merely requires a meticulously sourced, multi-course Continental dinner—complete with clarified consommés and complex, herb-infused reductions—to yield the exact same measure of sensory and spiritual satisfaction.
