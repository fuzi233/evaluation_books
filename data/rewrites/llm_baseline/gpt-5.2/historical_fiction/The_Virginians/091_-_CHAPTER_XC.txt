What was the value of a Colonel when his regiment was no more than a name written fair upon parchment, and his commission—so proudly flourished by the Governor and Council—had been signed by men who now skulked out of sight, lodged aboard ships like rats in a bilge, venturing ashore only for a piratical swoop and a blaze? My Lord Dunmore’s black allies, whom he had summoned round him with promises and proclamations, frightened away the King’s own friends; and, worse, we heard he had sent an envoy to the southern Indians, bidding them come down in numbers to tomahawk our people into obedience.

“And these,” I said to my mother, with a chill that tasted like iron on the tongue, “are to be our allies!”

For as she and I exchanged our ominous look, memory came—sharp as vinegar in a wound—of a painted face bending over mine at Braddock’s Field, and the knife laid to my throat until Florac’s stroke knocked the savage down. We put Castlewood into as good a posture of defence as our country wit could compass: bars on doors, shutters strengthened, men set to watch, muskets cleaned and laid ready. Yet, in truth, it was not so much the Rebels we dreaded as the red men and the blacks—war loosed from its leash, running hungry through the night.

I had never seen Madam Esmond lose heart but once; and that was when she told my wife, and me, with our child beside her knee, how her own husband fell in an Indian foray more than forty years ago. The tale had scarcely ended, the embers scarcely settled into their night-red hush, when figures were seen moving before the house. The owls were calling in the trees, the frogs in the low ground kept up their constant kettle-drum; and, under that uneasy music, the soft scuff of feet on dry grass seemed as loud as marching. Nothing could persuade my mother the shapes were anything but savages. She sank upon her knees, her hands clasped as if to wring the very mercy out of Heaven.

“The Lord have mercy upon us!” she cried. “The Indians—the Indians!”

Yet the painted heroes from the South never came. Dunmore’s negro soldiers melted back to his ships, or wherever pay, rum, and plunder could be found; and the worst terror proved, for a time, to have been a phantom of the mind. Still, I own I looked at my mother’s grey head, at my wife’s brown hair, and at the golden ringlets of our little one, with a pang so keen it was almost a sickness—lest the chance of war should fling them into the hands of men to whom women and children are but spoil. And we—on our side—had summoned such instruments. I dare not set myself up as an interpreter of Providence; yet it seemed to me that the employment of Indians and German mercenaries bore its own punishment. The mercenaries, when struck, yielded; and each yielding stiffened the spirit of the Continentals. And one drunken murder—Miss McCrea, butchered by half a dozen savages—did more harm to the Royal cause than many a lost field.

When the Indian panic ebbed, my mother’s courage returned as flame returns to a lamp when the draught is stopped. But her mind, now steady, fixed itself upon another danger—the danger I ran by remaining in Virginia, and the danger my presence brought upon all who belonged to me.

“What harm can they do me,” says she, “a poor woman? If I have one son a colonel without a regiment, I have another with a couple of hundred Continentals behind him in Mr. Washington’s camp. If the Royalists come, they will let me off for your sake; if the rebels appear, I shall have Harry’s passport. I don’t wish, sir, I don’t like that your delicate wife and this dear little baby should be here, and only increase the risk of all of us! We must have them away to Boston or New York. Don’t talk about defending me! Who will think of hurting a poor, harmless, old woman? If the rebels come, I shall shelter behind Mrs. Fanny’s petticoats, and shall be much safer without you in the house than in it.”

She spoke thus partly because it was reasonable, but more because her heart was set on driving me and mine out of the storm. For herself, danger or no danger, she was rooted like an old orchard tree in the soil where her father was buried and where she had been born. She lived backwards, so to speak—fed on memory as other folk live forward on hope. She had seen the new generation, blessed it, and already begun to bid it farewell.

While we debated Boston, news came that the British had evacuated that luckless town altogether—never having ventured to attack Mr. Washington at Cambridge, though he lay there for months without powder, almost at our mercy; but waiting until he had procured ammunition and seized Dorchester Heights, which commanded the place, and so forced the whole army to retreat. That the King’s troops won Bunker’s Hill is as certain as that they beat the French at Blenheim; yet, after that day, our chiefs appeared to have caught a strange fear of assaulting entrenchments. Why hesitate from July to March to strike an enemy almost defenceless? Why the half-heartedness at Long Island, when the Continental army seemed in our hand? Why that astonishing timorousness later—of Howe before Valley Forge, when a starving, sickening force in rags could scarcely man its lines against a great, victorious, well-appointed army?

It was curious to note how the tones of partisans shifted as fortune rose and fell. When the evacuation of Boston reached us in the country, every little Whig in the neighbourhood came bowing to Madam, counselling a speedy submission. She no longer flaunted her loyalty as openly; yet she never swerved. Night and morning, in private, poor Hagan prayed for the Royal Family in our house; and on Sundays any neighbour might attend the service, where my mother acted as a very emphatic clerk, and the prayer for the High Court of Parliament under our most religious and gracious King was stoutly delivered.

The brave Hagan was a parson without a living, as I was a militia colonel without a regiment. He had continued to pray for King George in Williamsburg long after his Excellency the Governor had run away; but one Sunday he found a corporal’s guard at the church door, who told him the Committee of Safety had put another divine in his place, and he was desired to keep a quiet tongue.

He bade them “lead him before their chiefs”—for our friend loved tall words and a tragic posture—and so he was marched to the Capitol, a jeering chorus of white and coloured blackguards at the skirts of his gown. There he confronted Messrs. Henry and the new State officers—“the robbers,” as he called them, “in their den.” Of course he was ready with a prepared harangue (there are men who would not object to martyrdom so long as it be performed in a full house), but Mr. Henry was too busy to grant him that theatre. After three or four hours’ waiting in an anteroom, in company of negroes, Hagan entered with undaunted mien and began, “Sir, by what authority am I, a minister of the—”

“Mr. Hagan,” says Henry, cutting him short, “I am too busy to listen to speeches. And as for King George, he has henceforth no more authority in this country than King Nebuchadnezzar. Mind you that, and hold your tongue, if you please! Stick to King John, sir, and King Macbeth; and if you will send round your benefit-tickets, all the Assembly shall come and hear you. Did you ever see Mr. Hagan on the boards, when you was in London, General?”

And with that he turned upon General Lee—Mr. Washington’s second in command—then in Virginia on State business; and our good Hagan, reddening, near to tears with shame, was bundled out. After that, we judged his ministrations safer in the country, and removed him from his restive lambs in town.

The new government’s choice of Virginians for the highest posts bribed and flattered many leading men, who, but for the outrageous conduct of our own administration, might have remained faithful to the Crown and made head against the rebellion. Yet though Loyalists were gagged and muzzled, though the Capitol was in Whig hands, and our vaunted levies of loyal recruits were, for the most part, Falstaff’s regiments, the faithful still kept intelligences with one another, and with our neighbours. We did not rise; we ran away. In examinations before committees and justices, some frightened people put on republican airs and vowed perdition to kings and nobles. But we knew each other. According as the chances shifted—according as the master’s hand grew more or less hard—we concealed our colours, showed them, half showed them, or apostatised for the nonce and cried, “Down with King George!”

Our negroes carried tokens and messages from house to house. Plots and schemes ran under the floorboards of society like rats in the granary—silent, constant, and hard to catch. When a battle is over, the neutrals come running to the winning side and shout as loud as patriots. The runaways are not counted. Will any man tell me the ardent signers and well-wishers of the Declaration were not a minority, and that the minority did not win?

We heard that a part of the defeated Massachusetts army was about to make an important expedition southward, on which the greatest hopes were founded. I, longing for action—any movement that was not merely waiting to be seized—put myself in communication with ex-Governor Martin of North Carolina. I proposed joining him, with three or four Virginian gentlemen—officers, like myself, of that notable corps of which we only wanted privates.

We made no particular mystery of leaving Castlewood; the affairs of Congress were not going so well yet that the new government could afford to lay stress or tyranny upon doubtful persons. Gentlemen’s houses remained open; and, in our southern fashion, we visited friends for months. My wife and I, with our infant and a fitting suite of servants, took leave of Madam Esmond as if for a neighbourly stay. We went from plantation to plantation—each table spread with its own smoke and savour: bacon cured in hickory, pone hot from the hearth, peaches that burst at the bite, coffee boiled strong enough to stand a spoon—until at last we reached Wilmington, North Carolina, where we expected to stretch a hand to succours coming to meet us.

Before our arrival, the Carolinian Royalists had shown themselves in force and suffered for it. Their encounters with the Whigs were unlucky. The poor Highlanders fared no better in drawing sword for King George here than when they had drawn against him in their own country.

We reached Wilmington at the end of May, and found Admiral Parker’s squadron there, with General Clinton and five British regiments aboard, bound for a descent upon Charleston.

The General, to whom I immediately made myself known, saw that my regiment consisted of Lady Warrington, our infant (whom she was nursing), and three negro servants; and he received us with a grim welcome. But Captain Horner of the Sphinx frigate, who had served on the Jamaica station and had tasted, like the rest of the world, many kindnesses from our dear Governor there, heard that my wife was General Lambert’s daughter, and eagerly welcomed her aboard, giving up his best cabin to our service. So we were refugees too—like my Lord Dunmore—having waved our flag, pocketed it, and slipped out by the back door.

From Wilmington we bore away quickly to Charleston; and in the passage, and in our delay within the river before the assault, I came to some acquaintance with Mr. Clinton, which grew into further intimacy. It was the King’s birthday when we appeared in the river; we determined it was a glorious day for the commencement of the expedition.

It did not begin for some days; and I leave out purposely all descriptions of Penelope parting from Hector. In the first place, Hector is perfectly well (though a little gouty), and no rascal Pyrrhus has made prize of his widow. And in war, are not such partings occurring every hour? Yet I can see the gentle face still, over the bulwark, as we descended the ship’s side into the boats—the infant smiling on her arm. Old stories indeed. Captain Miles, having no natural taste for poetry, you have forgot, no doubt, Mr. Pope’s verses in which you are described parting from your heroic father; but your mother often read them to you when you were a boy, and keeps the gorget I wore that day somewhere amongst her dressing-boxes now.

My second venture at fighting was no more fortunate than my first. We came back to our ships that evening thoroughly beaten. The madcap Lee—whom Clinton had faced at Boston—met him now at Charleston; and Lee, with the gallant garrison, made a brilliant resistance. Sullivan’s Island was a nut we could not crack. The roar of our frigates, the continual cough and thunder of guns, could not pound its shell; the very air smelled of burnt powder and hot pitch, and the river carried the bitter tang of smoke as if it were a stew left too long upon the fire. The passage by which we moved to the assault was not fordable, as those officers found—Sir Henry at their head, for he was always the first to charge—who tried to wade it. Death by shot, by drowning, by catching my death of cold—these I had braved before I returned to my wife. Our frigate grounded for a time and got off with difficulty, being agreeably cannonaded by the enemy until she slid free of the bank.

A small incident in the midst of this unlucky struggle was the seed of a later intimacy between me and Sir Henry Clinton, and bound me to that gallant officer during the period in which it was my fortune to follow the war. Of his qualities as a leader there may be many opinions; I fear to say, regarding a man I respect and admire, there ought only to be one. Of his personal bearing and courage there can be none. Whether at the final charge on Breed’s Hill, or here before Sullivan’s Fort, or later at Fort Washington, when, standard in hand, he swept up and entered at the head of the stormers, Clinton was always foremost in the race of battle.

We were taking to the water from our boats, intending to force a column toward the fort through a way our own guns had rendered practicable, when a shot struck a boat alongside us—so well aimed that three-fourths of her crew were at once hors de combat, and the officer steering and the flag behind him were knocked down together. I could not help crying out, “Bravo! well aimed!” for no ninepins ever went down more helplessly than those poor fellows.

Then the General turned on me rather grimly. “Sir, the behaviour of the enemy seems to please you!”

“I am pleased, sir,” I answered, “that my countrymen yonder should fight as becomes our nation.”

We floundered on amid the same amiable attentions from small arms and great, until the water was up to our breasts and deepening at every step; then we were fain to take to our boats again and pull out of harm’s way. Sir Henry waited upon Lady Warrington on board the Sphinx after this and was very gracious, and mighty facetious regarding the humble writer of these pages, whom his Excellency always described as a rebel at heart.

May my children live to see no great revolutions—such as that now raging among our miserable French neighbours. Save a very few, the actors in those tragedies do not bear to be scanned too closely: chiefs often ranting quacks; heroes ignoble puppets; heroines anything but pure. The prize is not always to the brave. In our Revolution it fell, for once and for a wonder, to the most deserving. His triumphs were not in rare engagements where he obtained a trifling mastery, but over Congress; over hunger and disease; over lukewarm friends and smiling foes in his own camp. When the struggle was over and our chiefs began to squabble and accuse each other before the nation—what excuses were offered, what pretexts urged, what piteous pleas that fleets came too late, regiments mistook orders, cannon-balls would not fit the guns! Here was a general who beat us sometimes with no shot, no powder, no money; and he never spoke of a convention. Through all the doubt and darkness, I think only the American leader’s indomitable soul remained entirely steady.

Of course our Charleston attempt was trumpeted as a prodigious victory by the enemy, who had learnt—perhaps from their parents—to cry victory if a corporal’s guard were surprised, as loud as if a pitched battle were won. Mr. Lee rushed back to New York, trumpeting his glory, and no man received him with more eager delight than the Commander-in-Chief of the American army. It was “my dear Lee” and “my dear General” between them then; and it has always touched me to note the simple confidence with which the Commander-in-Chief regarded officers who had once served with the King. That mad, flashy braggart (and another Continental general, whose name and luck afterwards were notorious) took advantage of that modesty—advised, blustered, sneered, disobeyed—daily throwing fresh obstacles in the path over which only Mr. Washington’s endurance could have marched.

Whilst we were away in South Carolina, the famous Fourth of July had taken place; and we and the thirteen United States were parted for ever. Virginia distinguished herself too by declaring that all men are equally free; that all power is vested in the people, who have an inalienable right to alter, reform, or abolish their government at pleasure; and that the idea of an hereditary first magistrate is unnatural and absurd.

Our General presented me with this document fresh from Williamsburg as we sailed northward by the Virginia capes; and, with no little amusement, pointed out the faith to which, from the Fourth instant inclusive, I was bound. There was no help for it: I was a Virginian. My godfathers had promised and vowed in my name that all men were equally free (including, of course, the poor race of Gumbo), that monarchy is absurd, and that I had the right to alter my form of government at pleasure.

I thought of Madam Esmond and how she would look when these articles were brought her to sign; how would Hagan receive them? He demolished them in a sermon where all the logic was on his side; but the U.S. Government has not, somehow, been affected by his discourse. And when he came to the point that all men being free, therefore Gumbo and Sady and Nathan had assuredly a right to go to Congress—“Tut, tut, my good Mr. Hagan,” says my mother, “let us hear no more of this nonsense; but leave such wickedness and folly to the rebels!”

By the middle of August we were before New York, whither Mr. Howe had brought the army that had gone to Halifax after its inglorious expulsion from Boston. The American Commander-in-Chief was there, and a great battle inevitable; and I looked forward with an inexpressible doubt and anxiety, knowing that my dearest brother and his regiment formed part of the troops we must attack—and could not but overpower.

Almost the whole American force came over to fight on a small island where every officer on both sides knew they were to be beaten, and from which there seemed no chance of escape. Two frigates, out of the hundred we had, placed to command the enemy’s entrenched camp and the point of retreat across the East River, would have destroyed every bark in which he sought to fly, and compelled him to lay down his arms on shore.

He fought. His hasty levies were utterly overthrown; some of his generals, his best troops, his artillery taken; the remnant huddled into their entrenchments after their rout, the pursuers entering with them. Then the victors were called back. The enemy was pent in a corner, and could not escape.

“They are at our mercy, and are ours to-morrow,” says the gentle General.

Not a ship was set to watch them. Not a sentinel of ours could see a movement in their camp. A whole army crossed, under our eyes, in one night, to the mainland without the loss of a single man. And General Howe was suffered to remain in command after this feat, and to complete his glories of Long Island and Breed’s Hill at Philadelphia. A friend crossed in the night with news that the enemy was ferrying over; but he fell upon a German picket—who could not understand him; their commander was boozing or asleep. By morning, when the spy was brought to someone who could comprehend the American tongue, the whole Continental force had crossed the East River, and the empire over thirteen colonies had slipped away.

My opinions of our chief were by no means uncommon in the army, though perhaps wisely kept secret by those immediately under him. Am I unluckier than other men, or why are my imprudent sayings carried about more than my neighbours’? My rage that such a victory should be so used was no greater than that of scores of gentlemen. Why must my name be given up to the Commander-in-Chief as the guiltiest of grumblers? Personally, General Howe was brave, amiable, good-humoured.

“So, Sir George,” says he to me, “you find fault with me, as a military man, because there was a fog after the battle on Long Island, and your friends, the Continentals, gave me the slip! Surely we took and killed enough of them; but there is no satisfying you gentlemen amateurs!”

And he turned his back, shrugged, and spoke to another. Amateur I might be, and he the most agreeable of men; but if King George had said to him, “Never more be officer of mine,” yonder pleasant Cassio would most certainly have had his desert.
