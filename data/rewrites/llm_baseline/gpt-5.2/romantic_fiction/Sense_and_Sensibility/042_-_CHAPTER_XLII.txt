One other short call in Harley Street—no more than the time it took a footman to announce them and the street-door to breathe out its cold, coach-scented air—completed all that passed between the Dashwood sisters and their brother in town. John congratulated Elinor, with the easy triumph of a man pleased to be economical at another’s expense, on their having travelled so far towards Barton “without costing a shilling,” and on Colonel Brandon’s intention of following them to Cleveland in a day or two. Fanny, with that languid civility which always sounded as if she were doing one a favour by permitting one to exist, let fall a faint invitation to Norland—whenever it should happen to be in their way; which, of all contingencies, was the least likely. John, warmer in private than his wife could endure in public, pressed Elinor’s hand, and assured her—almost with earnestness—that the moment she was settled at Delaford, he would come and see her there.

It amused Elinor, even while it vexed her, to observe how all her friends appeared determined to send her to Delaford; to plant her, like a useful herb, in soil which everyone praised, and which she herself had least desire to tread. Not only did her brother and Mrs. Jennings speak of that parsonage as if her name were already written on its pantry shelves and linen-press, but Lucy too—Lucy, whose friendship had clung to Elinor in London with the persistence of a sweetmeat paper that will not be shaken off—when they parted, had pressed an invitation to visit her there; as if Delaford were a market-town of her own creation, and Elinor’s presence a tribute due.

Very early in April, and early enough in the day for the sun to have some pale strength through the spring mist, the two parties from Hanover Square and Berkeley Street set out from their respective doors, to meet by appointment on the road. For Charlotte’s convenience, and the child’s—whose wants, like a little drum, beat time to every mile—they were to be more than two days upon the journey. Mr. Palmer, travelling more expeditiously with Colonel Brandon, was to join them at Cleveland soon after their arrival.

Marianne, though she had enjoyed few hours of comfort in London, and had long been eager to quit it, could not, when the moment came, bid adieu to the house where she had last been happy in hope—where she had fed her heart on confidence in Willoughby—without pain sharp enough to make every familiar stair and doorway a reproach. Nor could she leave the place in which he remained—busy in new engagements and new schemes, in which she could have no part—without shedding many tears; tears that seemed to her the only language still left that could say what no voice would be allowed to utter.

Elinor’s satisfaction at removal was more positive, more cleanly felt; like the relief of a room aired after a long confinement. She had no such object for lingering thought to fasten on; she left behind no creature whose absence promised even a moment’s regret. She was grateful to be free from the persecution of Lucy’s endearments, grateful, too, to have brought Marianne away without another chance encounter with Willoughby since his marriage; and she looked forward with hope—quiet, practical hope—to what a few months of tranquillity at Barton might do towards restoring Marianne’s peace, and strengthening her own.

Their journey was safely performed. The second day brought them into the cherished—or the prohibited—county of Somerset, for so it was called by turns in Marianne’s imagination; beloved because it led to Barton, forbidden because it led also too near to Combe Magna. In the forenoon of the third day they drove up to Cleveland.

Cleveland was a spacious modern-built house, seated upon a sloping lawn. It had no park; but the pleasure-grounds were tolerably extensive, and arranged with that orderly pretence of wildness which belongs to places that must look “natural” by design. There was an open shrubbery and a closer wood-walk; a road of smooth gravel wound round a plantation and brought the carriage to the front. The lawn was dotted with timber, and the house itself stood as if under the guardianship of fir and mountain-ash and acacia; a thick screen of them together, interspersed with tall Lombardy poplars, shut out the offices, and with them the busy noises and savory breath of work—those homely sounds and smells that make a house feel inhabited.

Marianne entered with a heart swelling from the consciousness of being only eighty miles from Barton, and not thirty from Combe Magna; and before five minutes had passed, while the others were occupied in helping Charlotte exhibit her child to the housekeeper, Marianne slipped away. She stole out through the winding shrubberies, just beginning to be in beauty—those tender leaf-buds like green promises—till she gained a distant eminence. There stood a little Grecian temple, white and cold as an ornament is meant to be, and from it her eye wandered over a wide tract of country to the south-east, resting—fondly, cruelly—on the farthest ridge of hills upon the horizon, and fancying that from their summits Combe Magna might be seen.

In such moments of precious, invaluable misery, she rejoiced to be at Cleveland, because it allowed her to be wretched at liberty. Returning by a different circuit, tasting all the happy privilege of country freedom—the power to wander without being watched, to move from place to place in solitary indulgence—she resolved to spend almost every hour, of every day of her stay, in such rambles; to make of the grounds a kind of silent companion that would never correct her feeling, never argue her out of her grief.

She came back in time to join the others as they quitted the house for an excursion through the immediate premises; and the rest of the morning was easily whiled away in lounging round the kitchen-garden, examining the bloom upon its warm walls, and listening to the gardener’s lamentations upon blights. There were borders where the earth lay dark and crumbling, smelling richly of last year’s decay and this year’s beginnings; there were trained branches and espaliered fruit-trees, tied and guided like domestic passions made obedient. They dawdled afterwards through the green-house, where a few favourite plants—unwarily exposed and nipped by lingering frost—gave Charlotte an opportunity to laugh, as if the very tenderness of the leaves were ridiculous. They visited the poultry-yard too; and in the dairy-maid’s disappointed hopes—hens forsaking their nests, eggs stolen by a fox, a promising young brood rapidly diminished—Charlotte found fresh sources of merriment.

Elinor observed it all with a mind that could appreciate the comfort of such employments, even if her heart was not at leisure to take delight in them. There was a particular pleasure in hearing the small, honest sounds of a place well managed: the scratch and flutter of fowls; the creak of a gate; the faint watery drip from a trough; and, from far off, the steady rhythm of a spade. How different, she thought, from London’s incessant clatter—wheels, cries, and that harsh metallic din that presses upon the spirits till one forgets what quiet feels like. Here, even laughter seemed obliged to make room for birdsong and the wind’s soft brushing through young leaves; the very air had a gentler acoustics.

The morning had been fine and dry, and Marianne, in her plan of employment abroad, had not calculated for any change of weather during their stay. With great surprise, therefore, she found herself prevented, after dinner, by a settled rain from going out again. She had depended on a twilight walk to the Grecian temple—perhaps all over the grounds—and an evening merely cold or damp would not have deterred her; but a heavy, steady rain, that fell with that patient insistence which soaks through even the bravest resolve, could not be fancied into pleasantness.

Their party was small, and the hours passed quietly away. Mrs. Palmer had her child; Mrs. Jennings her carpet-work; they talked of the friends they had left behind, arranged Lady Middleton’s engagements, and wondered whether Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon would get farther than Reading that night. Elinor, however little concerned in the gossip, joined in discourse when it was necessary; and Marianne—who had the knack, in every house, of finding her way to the library, however the family in general might avoid it—soon procured herself a book, as if printed pages might lend her a borrowed language for feelings she could not translate into speech.

Nothing was wanting, on Mrs. Palmer’s side, that constant and friendly good-humour could do to make them welcome. The openness and heartiness of her manner atoned for that want of recollection and elegance which often made her deficient in forms; her kindness, recommended by so pretty a face, was engaging; her folly, though evident, was not disgusting, because it was not conceited. Elinor could have forgiven everything—almost everything—but her laugh; for it broke upon the ear with a suddenness and sameness that flattened every surrounding sound, as if the delicate gradations of the room’s quiet—rain upon glass, the soft rasp of carpet-work, the turning of a page—were nothing compared with that one loud note.

The next day the two gentlemen arrived, to a very late dinner; and their coming enlarged the party with real pleasure, and gave a welcome variety to conversation, which a long morning of the same continued rain had reduced very low. Elinor had seen so little of Mr. Palmer, and in that little had seen such variety in his address to Marianne and herself, that she did not know what to expect of him in his own family. She found him, however, perfectly the gentleman in his behaviour to all his visitors, and only occasionally rude to his wife and her mother. He was capable of being a pleasant companion, and only prevented from being so always by too great an aptitude to fancy himself as much superior to people in general as he must feel himself to be to Mrs. Jennings and Charlotte.

In the rest of his character and habits, as far as Elinor could perceive, there were no traits unusual in his sex and time of life. He was nice in his eating, uncertain in his hours; fond of his child, though affecting to slight it; and he idled away the mornings at billiards which ought to have been devoted to business. He spoke of dishes with a fastidious authority, picking at what he praised, and praising what he hardly tasted; as if appetite were a performance and not a sense. Yet Elinor liked him, upon the whole, much better than she had expected; and in her heart was not sorry that she could like him no more—not sorry to be driven, by the observation of his epicurism, selfishness, and conceit, to rest with complacency on the remembrance of Edward’s generous temper, simple taste, and diffident feelings. With Edward, there had always been something unstudied, a quiet gratitude for what was placed before him, as if he heard—in the clink of a spoon, in the crackle of a fire—the domestic music others were too proud to notice.

Of Edward, or at least of some concerns connected with him, she now received intelligence from Colonel Brandon, who had lately been in Dorsetshire; and who, treating Elinor at once as the disinterested friend of Mr. Ferrars and the kind confidante of himself, talked to her a great deal of the parsonage at Delaford. He described its deficiencies with the same careful exactness with which a conscientious steward might speak of a larder too small or a roof that let in weather; and he told her what he meant to do himself towards removing them—what should be mended, what improved, what made more fit for comfort. There was, in his manner, none of the vulgar eagerness of a man displaying his generosity, but the steady gravity of one who values a home as a place where daily wants are honorably met.

His behaviour to her in this, as in every other particular—his open pleasure in meeting her again after an absence of only ten days, his readiness to converse with her, and his deference for her opinion—might very well justify Mrs. Jennings’s persuasion of his attachment, and might perhaps have been enough to make Elinor suspect it herself, had she not, as from the first, believed Marianne his real favourite. As it was, the notion scarcely entered her head except by Mrs. Jennings’s suggestion; and she could not help believing herself the nicer observer of the two. She watched his eyes while Mrs. Jennings thought only of his behaviour. And when Marianne, from imprudence rather than ill-luck, began to complain—only by look and a slight change of voice—of a heaviness in her head and throat, Elinor saw instantly what Mrs. Jennings did not: the quick feelings and needless alarm of a lover in Colonel Brandon’s anxious solicitude.

Two delightful twilight walks on the third and fourth evenings of Marianne’s being there—not merely on the dry gravel of the shrubbery, but all over the grounds, and especially in the most distant parts, where there was something more of wildness than in the rest, where the trees were oldest and the grass longest and wettest—had, assisted by the still greater imprudence of sitting in wet shoes and stockings, given her a cold so violent as, though for a day or two denied and trifled with, would force itself by increasing ailments on the concern of everybody and the notice of herself.

Prescriptions poured in from all quarters, and as usual were all declined. Though heavy and feverish, with pain in her limbs, a cough, and a sore throat, a good night’s rest was to cure her entirely; the simplest nursing was met with a romantic obstinacy, as if to accept care were to admit defeat. It was with difficulty that Elinor prevailed on her, when she went to bed, to try one or two of the plainest remedies—those humble domestic helps that ask no imagination, only patience: warmth well wrapped about the feet, a mild draught, a quiet room where the rain’s steady tapping might be heard without being answered by the reckless step of one determined to wander out into it.
