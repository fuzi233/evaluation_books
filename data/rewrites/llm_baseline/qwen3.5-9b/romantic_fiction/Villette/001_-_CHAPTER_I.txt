My godmother resided in a stately home within the pristine, ancient town of Bretton. Her husband's lineage had anchored itself there for countless generations, bearing the proud designation of "Bretton of Bretton"—a name that suggested either a fortunate coincidence or perhaps the legacy of a distant ancestor significant enough to imprint his identity upon the neighborhood. Though I cannot verify the truth behind the title, I recall visiting Bretton as a child roughly twice a year, and I cherished those trips dearly. The house and its inhabitants were perfectly suited to my nature. I adored the spacious, tranquil rooms, the tastefully arranged furnishings, the expansive windows that flooded the space with light, and the balcony overlooking a magnificent, antique street. There, Sundays and holidays seemed to dwell permanently; the air was so serene, the cobblestones so immaculate, that everything brought me profound comfort.

In a household of adults, a single child is invariably doted upon, and in a quiet, understated manner, I was similarly cherished by Mrs. Bretton. She had become a widow, leaving behind one son, before I had even known her. Her husband, a physician, had passed away while she was still youthful and striking. As I remembered her, she was no longer a girl, yet she remained handsome—tall, well-proportioned, and possessing a brunette complexion that, though dark for an Englishwoman, radiated a clear vitality. Her cheeks held a healthy glow, animated by a pair of fine, cheerful black eyes. It was often lamented by others that she had not bestowed this radiant hue upon her son. His eyes were blue—piercing even in boyhood—and his long hair, described by cautious friends only as "golden" when the sunlight touched it, lacked her warmth. Yet, he inherited the contours of her face, her perfect teeth, her promising height, and, most importantly, her flawless health and a spirit marked by a steady, equal temperament—a quality far superior to wealth.

It was during the autumn of a certain year that I stayed at Bretton. My godmother had traveled personally to retrieve me from the relatives where I was then permanently residing. I suspect she foresaw approaching events, shadows of trouble that I barely perceived, yet whose faint hint was enough to instill in me a restless melancholy, making the prospect of changing scenes and company a welcome relief. Time moved with gentle ease in her presence—not with turbulent speed, but with the bland, soothing flow of a broad river gliding across a plain. My stays there reminded me of the biblical figures Christian and Hopeful resting beside a pleasant stream, surrounded by green trees and meadows blooming with lilies year-round. There was no variety to excite, no incidents to thrill; indeed, I loved peace so deeply and craved stimulation so little that when it finally arrived, I felt it merely as a disturbance, wishing it had remained at bay.

One day, a letter arrived that clearly startled and concerned Mrs. Bretton. At first, I assumed it was from home and trembled, fearing some dire communication. However, no mention was made of it, and the cloud of worry seemed to dissipate quickly. The following day, returning from a lengthy walk, I discovered an unexpected transformation in my bedroom. Beside my own French bed, nestled in its shaded alcove, stood a small crib draped in white linen. Next to my mahogany chest of drawers sat a tiny chest carved from rosewood. I paused, staring, pondering the meaning of these new objects. The answer was self-evident: *A second guest is arriving; Mrs. Bretton expects visitors.*

Descending for dinner, explanations unfolded. A little girl was to join me as a companion—the daughter of a friend and distant relative of the late Dr. Bretton. It was revealed that this child had recently lost her mother. Although Mrs. Bretton noted that the loss was not as devastating as it initially appeared, the circumstances were tragic. The mother, Mrs. Home, had been beautiful but giddy and careless, neglecting her child and disappointing her husband. Their marriage had proven uncongenial, leading eventually to a separation by mutual consent, avoiding legal entanglement. Shortly thereafter, overwhelmed by exertion at a ball, the lady contracted a cold, developed a fever, and died after a brief illness. Her husband, a man of highly sensitive feelings, was shattered by the sudden news. Consumed by guilt, believing his own lack of patience may have hastened her death, he fell into a deep depression. Medical advice prescribed travel as a remedy, and thus, Mrs. Bretton offered to care for his little daughter until he returned.

"And I trust," my godmother concluded warmly, "that the child will not resemble her mother—that silly, frivolous flirt who led every sensible man astray." She explained that Mr. Home was, in his own way, a sensible man, albeit impractical. Fond of science, he spent half his life in a laboratory conducting experiments—a pursuit his "butterfly wife" could neither understand nor tolerate. Indeed, she admitted, she would not have enjoyed it herself. When I asked further, she shared that her late husband had attributed Mr. Home's scientific inclination to a maternal uncle, a French savant. Of mixed French and Scottish descent, the father-in-law had connections abroad, including nobility who styled themselves with the prefix "_de_" before their names.

That very evening, at nine o'clock, a servant was sent to meet the carriage bringing our new arrival. Mrs. Bretton and I sat alone in the drawing-room awaiting her. John Graham Bretton was away visiting a schoolfriend in the countryside. My godmother read the evening newspaper while I stitched. Outside, the night was wet and stormy; rain lashed against the windowpanes, and the wind roared angrily and restlessly.

"Poor child!" Mrs. Bretton murmured periodically. "Such terrible weather for her journey! I pray she arrives safely."

Just before ten, the doorbell announced Warren's return. Before the door fully opened, I rushed downstairs. In the hall lay a trunk and several bandboxes. Standing nearby was a figure resembling a nursemaid, and at the foot of the stairs, Warren held a shawled bundle in his arms.

"Is that the child?" I asked eagerly.

"Yes, miss."

I reached out to open the shawl and catch a glimpse of the face, but it was swiftly turned away toward Warren's shoulder.

"Put me down, please," a small voice commanded as Warren opened the drawing-room door. With a fastidious haste, the speaker extracted the pin and removed the clumsy wrapping. The creature that emerged attempted to fold the heavy shawl, but her tiny hands and arms struggled to manage the oversized drapery.

"Give it to Harriet, please," she directed, pointing to the nurse. "She can put it away."

Turning immediately, she fixed her eyes on Mrs. Bretton.

"Come here, little dear," the lady invited gently. "Let me see if you are cold and damp. Come, let me warm you by the fire."

The child advanced promptly. Once relieved of her wrappings, she appeared exceedingly diminutive, yet possessed a neat, perfectly formed silhouette—light, slight, and straight. Seated on my godmother's ample lap, she looked like a mere doll; her neck, delicate as wax, and her head of silky curls enhanced the resemblance. Mrs. Bretton spoke in soft, affectionate phrases as she rubbed the child's hands, arms, and feet. Initially regarded with a wistful gaze, the child soon responded with a smile.

Mrs. Bretton was rarely a demonstrative woman; even with her beloved son, her manner was seldom sentimental, often reserved. Yet, when the small stranger smiled at her, she kissed her, asking, "What is my little one's name?"

"Missy."

"But besides Missy?"

"Polly, papa calls her."

"Will Polly be content to live with me?"

"Not _always_; but until papa returns. Papa is gone away." She shook her head expressively. "He will return to Polly, or send for her."

"Will he, ma'am? Do you know he will?"

"I think so."

"But Harriet does not think so—at least, not for a long while. He is ill." Tears filled her eyes. Drawing her hand away from Mrs. Bretton's, she made a movement to leave her lap. It was briefly resisted, but she persisted, saying, "Please, I wish to go: I can sit on a stool."

Allowed to descend from the knee, she fetched a footstool and carried it to a corner where the shadows were deepest, seating herself there. Mrs. Bretton, though commanding and peremptory in serious matters, was often passive in trifles. She granted the child her wish, telling me simply, "Take no notice at present."

Yet, I could not help but observe. I watched Polly rest her small elbow on her small knee, her head propped on her hand. I saw her extract a square inch or two of pocket-handkerchief from the doll-pocket of her skirt, and then I heard her weeping. Unlike other children who cry aloud in grief without shame, this being wept silently. The tiniest, occasional sniff betrayed her emotion. Mrs. Bretton did not hear it, which was fortunate.

Soon, a voice from the corner demanded, "May the bell be rung for Harriet?"

I rang the bell, summoning the nurse.

"Harriet, I must be put to bed," said her little mistress. "You must ask where my bed is."
