CHAPTER LI. Monna Brigida’s Conversion.

When Romola said that someone else awaited her, she meant her cousin Brigida—but she had no idea how deeply that gentle woman was in need of her presence. As they walked back toward the Piazza, they saw a group of young men halt before Tessa, and when Romola, close enough now to see the poor girl’s distress, called out, “Wait for me a moment, cousin,” Monna Brigida quickly replied, “Ah, I will not go on—come for me at Boni’s shop. I shall return there.” The truth was, Monna Brigida carried within her a quiet unease: on one side, the familiar comforts of vanity—rouge, false hair, pearls—clung to her like old habits; on the other, a growing dread that the Piagnoni were right in saying such things weighed upon the soul. Their solemn way of seeing the world had seeped into everything like a scent—nothing tasted quite the same anymore. And then there was Romola, so young and radiant, living a life that made every ordinary indulgence seem like a sin. A widow of fifty-five who had long found joy in her appearance, and in what others thought of it, needed more than mere comfort to keep her spirit lifted. And Monna Brigida was beginning to struggle each morning at her mirror.

If her soul would flourish better without them, was it truly worth wearing the rouge and the braids? But when she looked into the glass, she saw a pale face with heavy cheeks and lines around her eyes that no smile could hide. When she let her grey hair fall simply about her head, as the Piagnoni did, her courage wavered. Monna Berta would surely laugh, call her an old hag—and though Monna Berta was only fifty-two, that age difference stung. Every woman not of their sect would glance away in pity; men would treat her like a grandmother. Yet at fifty-five, a woman was not so very old—only in need of a little care. So the rouge went on, the braids were pinned tight, the embroidered berretta placed firmly atop her head. Her neck, covered by layers of lace and jewels, looked better than Monna Berta’s, if anything. That very day, preparing for the Piagnone Carnival, this inner battle had flared up again. Overwhelmed by fear and longing, she turned back, seeking refuge in the druggist’s shop rather than face the collectors of the Anathema alone.

But she was too slow. Before she could vanish, the white-robed boys at the rear of the procession noticed her. Perhaps Tessa’s surrender came more easily because part of the troop had already confronted someone burdened far more visibly by the sins they sought to purge.

It happened that some of these youths were among the youngest taken into special training. One small boy, ten years old, wore an olive wreath above his cherubic cheeks and wide brown eyes. His imagination was filled with awe—the weight of eternity pressing on every choice between good and evil—yet his heart still yearned for mischief and power. He was the first to reach Monna Brigida, stepping boldly into her path.

She felt anger rise, searched for a doorway to escape—but none was near. To flee now would only worsen the shame. Still, it was not he who spoke first. It was a fifteen-year-old youth holding one handle of a broad basket.

“Venerable mother!” he began, voice clear and earnest, “the blessed Jesus commands you to give up the Anathema you carry. That cap adorned with pearls, those jewels fastening your false hair—let them be surrendered and sold for the poor. Cast the hair itself aside, as a lie fit only for fire. Doubtless, too, you hide other jewels beneath your silk mantle.”

“Yes, madonna,” added the youth at the other end, repeating phrases from Fra Girolamo’s sermons by heart, “they are too heavy for you—like a millstone dragging you down to perdition. Will you adorn yourself instead with the hunger of the poor, and proudly wear God’s curse upon your brow?”

“In truth, you are old, buona madre,” chimed in the cherubic boy in a sweet soprano, “and you look very ugly with the red on your cheeks and that black, glistening hair. Those fine things—only Satan could find pleasure in you. Your angel is sorrowful. He wants you to rub away the red.”

He snatched a soft silk scarf from the basket and held it forward, offering it as a sacred tool for repentance.

Monna Brigida’s anger melted into spiritual dread. Monna Berta and all the fashionable world were not watching—she was surrounded only by these young monitors, dressed in white robes, crowned with olive branches, marked with red crosses, their candor terrifying in its purity. Her Franciscan confessor, Fra Cristoforo of Santa Croce, was not there to steady her against Dominican zeal. She stood helpless, gripped by a sudden, overwhelming sense that divine judgment had come upon her.

With no alternative in mind, she took the scarf and gently rubbed her cheeks, trembling, obedient.

“It is well, madonna,” said the second youth. “A holy beginning. When you remove those vanities from your head, the dew of grace will fall upon it.”

The boy’s mischief grew stronger. Reaching up, he tugged at one of the jeweled pins securing her braids. The heavy plait fell forward, covering her face and pulling the rest of her headgear down with it. There was no longer any hesitation. She raised her hands, unfastened the rest, and threw her beloved crimson-velvet berretta—its seed-pearl embroidery unmatched—into the basket of doom. She stood bare, unrouged, her grey hair pulled back, her face showing the deep lines of age that no powder could conceal.

But the berretta was not left behind. The boys lifted it up, displaying it with cruel delight, the false hair dangling like a grotesque puppet’s wig.

“See, venerable mother,” said the taller youth, “what ugly lies you have freed yourself from! Now you look like the blessed Saint Anna, mother of the Holy Virgin!”

Thoughts of entering a convent immediately, of never showing herself again, rushed through Monna Brigida’s mind. There was nothing left but to save her soul. Of course, people laughed—she didn’t need to turn to know it. Well, perhaps it was better to be forced to think more of Paradise.

Yet at the thought that her familiar world was slipping away forever, tears welled—those quiet, stubborn tears that come to older eyes. She could barely see as a large, rough hand thrust a red cross before her, over the shoulders of the boys. A gruff voice barked:

“Only four quattrini, madonna—blessing and all! Buy it. You’ll find comfort now your wig’s gone. Deh! What are we sinners doing all our lives? Making soup in a basket, and getting only the scum for our stomachs. Better buy a blessing, madonna! Only four quattrini—the profit is less than the smell of a danaro, and it all goes to the poor.”

Still dazed, Monna Brigida reached into her embroidered purse, hidden beneath her silk mantle, when a figure appeared—more welcome than an angel freeing a prisoner.

“Romola, look at me!” she cried, her voice broken, stretching out both hands.

The white-clad troop began to move on, sensing their zeal had gone too far—perhaps a dispensation granted, no further offerings required.

“Dear cousin, don’t be distressed,” Romola said, struck with pity, though she couldn’t help a faint smile at the sight of her kinswoman—plain, natural, utterly unlike the image she’d always known. She removed the dark cloth from her own head and draped it over Monna Brigida.

“There,” she murmured soothingly, “no one will notice you now. Let us turn down the Via del Palagio and go straight home.”

They hurried away, Monna Brigida clutching Romola’s hand tightly, as if to confirm she was real.

“Ah, my Romola, my dear child!” she panted, struggling to keep pace with the tall, graceful figure beside her. “What an old scarecrow I am! I must be good—I mean to be good!”

“Buy a cross!” shouted the gruff voice again, the rough hand thrust forward once more. Bratti was not deterred by Romola’s presence—he followed quietly, determined to make a sale.

“Only four quattrini, blessing and all—and if there was any profit, it would all go to the poor.”

Monna Brigida could not stop, even if she had wanted to. She raised one hand to silence Romola’s protest, and with the other, offered a grosso—worth many white quattrini—saying softly, pleadingly:

“Take it, good man, and begone.”

“You’re in the right, madonna,” Bratti said, taking the coin swiftly and placing the cross in her palm. “I won’t give change—might as well rob you of a mass. We all must be scorched a little, but you’ll come off easier. Better fall from the window than the roof. A good Easter and a good year to you!”

“Well, Romola,” Monna Brigida said, sighing with a touch of despair as Bratti vanished, “if I’m to be a Piagnone, it doesn’t matter how I look!”

“Dear cousin,” Romola replied, smiling warmly, “you don’t know how much better you look than ever before. I can see now how kind and gentle your face really is—just like you. That rouge and finery used to push themselves forward, hiding your expression. Ask Piero or any painter—if they’d paint you now, they’d choose this face over the old one. Every line on a human face has dignity or beauty, unless it comes from base passions. How noble old men are—like my godfather! Why shouldn’t old women look grand and simple?”

“Yes, when one reaches sixty, my Romola,” Brigida murmured, hesitating slightly, “but I’m only fifty-five. Monna Berta and everyone—but it’s no use. I will be good, like you. Your mother, if she’d lived, would be my age—we were cousins. One must either die or grow old. But if I’m a Piagnone, it doesn’t matter.”
