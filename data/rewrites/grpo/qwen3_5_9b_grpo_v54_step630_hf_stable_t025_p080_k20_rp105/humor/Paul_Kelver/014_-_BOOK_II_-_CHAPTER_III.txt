The sun was already pouring through my window when I woke, a bright, mocking intrusion into a room that felt suddenly too small. I sat up, listening to the city roar—a sound that told me the day had begun without me, and perhaps without me ever returning. Reaching for my watch, I found it missing from its usual spot on the rickety dressing table. My clothes were scattered like debris on the floor; one boot sat alone on the chair by the fire, the other vanished into the shadows.

During the night, my head seemed to have expanded by several inches. I wondered idly if I had mistakenly donned Minikin's hat, but the thought was quickly dismissed. Whatever I was wearing now was a top-heavy monstrosity, aching with a dull, confounded throb.

Then, the memory of the previous night crashed over me, shaking me awake. From a neighboring steeple, the chimes rang out. I counted carefully. Eleven o'clock. I sprang out of bed and immediately collapsed onto the floor.

I remembered how, clutching the bedpost, I had felt the room waltz wildly around me. It hadn't quite settled yet. It was still rotating, not whirling now, but staggering feebly, as if exhausted from an all-night orgie. Creeping to the wash-stand, I managed, after a couple of clumsy attempts, to dip my head into the basin. Pulling on my trousers with difficulty and collapsing into the easy-chair, I tried to review the situation, working backward from the present misery.

I felt terrible. Something had disagreed with me.

"That strong cigar," I whispered to the empty room. "I should never have touched it. And that tiny room packed with people. Plus, I've been working myself to the bone. I really need more exercise."

It gave me a twisted sense of pride to realize that, shuffling and cowardly though I was, I wasn't easily bamboozled.

"Nonsense," I told myself brutally. "Don't try to trick me. You were drunk."

"Not drunk," I pleaded, my voice trembling. "Don't use that coarse word. Some people can't handle sweet champagne; it affects their liver. Please, let's call it a liver issue."

"Drunk," I insisted relentlessly. "Hopelessly, vulgarly drunk—drunk as any 'Arry after a Bank Holiday."

"It was the first time," I murmured.

"It was your first opportunity," I replied.

"Never again," I promised.

"The stock phrase," I returned.

"How old are you?"

"Nineteen."

"So you don't even have the excuse of youth. How do you know it won't grow on you? That you won't sink lower and lower until you're a confirmed sot?"

My heavy head dropped into my hands, and I groaned. Sunday afternoon temperance tales flooded back. In the misty air of my imagination, I saw myself rushing toward a drunkard's grave, sometimes struggling heroically, other times weakly yielding as the craving tightened its grip. I saw my father's white face, my mother's sad eyes. I thought of Barbara, and the scorn that could quiver around that bewitching mouth; I thought of Hal, with his tremendous contempt for all weakness. The shame of the present and the terror of the future racked my mind.

"It shall be never again!" I cried aloud. "By God, it shall!" (At nineteen, one is apt to be vehement.) "I will leave this house at once," I continued, pacing the floor. "I will escape this unwholesome atmosphere. I will wipe it from my mind. I will make a fresh start. I will—"

Something I had been dimly aware of at the back of my brain stepped forward and stood before me: the flabby figure of Miss Rosina Sellars. What was she doing here? What right did she have to block my path to regeneration?

"The right of your affianced bride," my inner voice explained, with a grim smile.

"Did I really go that far?"

"We won't go into details," I replied, avoiding the gaze of my reflection. "That was the result."

"I was—I wasn't quite myself. I didn't know what I was doing."

"As a rule, we don't when we do foolish things; but we have to pay the price anyway. Unfortunately, it happened in front of witnesses, and she isn't the type of lady to be easily dismissed. You're marrying her. You're settling down in two small rooms. Her family becomes your family. You'll learn to know them better before many days pass. Among them, she is regarded as 'the lady,' which tells you everything about them. A nice start for your career, isn't it, my ambitious young friend? A nice mess you've made of it!"

"What am I to do?" I asked.

"Upon my word, I don't know," I answered.

I spent a wretched day. Too ashamed to face Mrs. Peedles or even the maid, I locked myself in my room. By dusk, feeling slightly less terrible—or perhaps just less acute—I slipped out and ate a simple meal at a nearby coffee house: tea without sugar and a kippered herring. Another gentleman sat down opposite me, ordered hot buttered toast, and I fled hastily.

At eight that evening, Minikin stopped by the office to ask what had happened. Seeking refuge in shame, I confessed the truth.

"Thought as much," he answered. "Looks like an A1 from the way you're carrying yourself."

"I'm glad it's over, now," I said. "It'll be a lesson I never forget."

"I know," Minikin said. "Nothing like a fair and square drunk to make you feel real good; better than a sermon."

In my distress, I needed advice. Minikin, though younger, was far more experienced in the ways of the world.

"That's not the worst," I confided. "What do you think I've done?"

"Killed a policeman?" Minikin suggested.

"Got myself engaged."

"No one like you quiet fellows for going all out when you do begin," Minikin commented. "Nice girl?"

"I don't know," I answered. "I only know I don't want her. How do I get out of it?"

Minikin removed his left eye and began polishing it on his handkerchief, a habit he adopted when in doubt. From staring into it, he seemed to draw inspiration.

"Take-her-own-part sort of a girl?"

I indicated that he had diagnosed Miss Rosina Sellars correctly.

"Know how much you're earning?"

"She knows I live up here in this attic and do my own cooking," I answered.

Minikin glanced around the room. "Must be fond of you."

"She thinks I'm clever," I explained. "And that I'll make my way."

"And she's willing to wait?"

I nodded.

"Well, I'd let her wait," Minikin replied, replacing his eye. "There's plenty of time before you."

"But she's a barmaid, and she'll expect me to walk with her, take her out on Sundays, visit her friends. I can't do it. Besides, she's right: I mean to get on. Then she'll stick to me. It's awful!"

"How did it happen?" Minikin asked.

"I don't know," I replied. "I didn't know I'd done it until it was over."

"Anybody present?"

"Half-a-dozen of them," I groaned.

The door opened, and Jarman entered; he never bothered to knock. Instead of his usual noisy greeting, he crossed the room in silence and shook my hand gravely.

"Friend of yours?" he asked, indicating Minikin.

I introduced them.

"Proud to meet you," Jarman said.

"Glad to hear it," Minikin replied. "Don't look as if you've got much else to be stuck up about."

"Don't mind him," I explained to Jarman. "He was born like it."

"Wonderful gift," Jarman replied. "D'ye know what I'd do if I had it?" He didn't wait for an answer. "'Ire myself out to break up evening parties. Ever thought of it seriously?"

Minikin said he would consider the idea.

"Make your fortune going round the suburbs," Jarman assured him. "Pity you weren't here last night; might've saved our young friend here a deal of trouble. Has he told you the news?"

I explained that Minikin already knew the facts.

"Now you've got a good, steady eye," Jarman said, fixing his gaze on Minikin's glass orb. "How do you think he's looking?"

"As well as can be expected under the circumstances," Minikin answered.

"Does he know the circumstances? Has he seen the girl?" Jarman asked.

I replied that I hadn't yet enjoyed that privilege. "Then he doesn't know the worst," Jarman said. "A hundred and sixty pounds of her, and still growing! Bit of a load for him, ain't it?"

"Some of 'em do have luck," Minikin rejoined. Jarman leaned forward, taking further stock of his new acquaintance.

"That's a fine head of yours," he remarked. "All your own? No offence," he continued, cutting off Minikin's potential retort. "I was merely thinking there must be room for a lot of sense in it. Now, what do you, as a practical man, advise him: dose of poison, or Waterloo Bridge and a brick?"

"I suppose there's no doubt," I interjected, "that we are actually engaged?"

"Not a blooming shadow," Jarman assured me cheerfully, "so far as she's concerned."

"I shall tell her plainly," I explained, "that I was drunk at the time."

"And how are you going to convince her of it?" Jarman asked. "You think telling her you loved her proves it. So it would to anybody else, but not to her. You can't expect it. Besides, if every girl is going to give up her catch just because the fellow hadn't all his wits about him at the time... well, what do you think?" He appealed to Minikin.

To Minikin, it seemed that if such arguments were allowed, girls might as well close shop.

Jarman, now that he had "got even" with Minikin, became more friendly toward the young man, drawing his chair closer and entering a private argument from which I was entirely excluded.

"You see," Jarman explained, "this ain't an ordinary case. This chap's going to be the future Poet Laureate. Now, when the Prince of Wales invites him to dine at Marlborough House, he don't want to go there tacked on to a girl that carries aitches with her in a bag and doesn't know which end of the spoon to drink her soup from."

"It makes a difference, of course," Minikin agreed.

"What we've got to do," Jarman said, "is get him out of it. And upon my sivvy, blessed if I see how!"

"She fancies him?" Minikin asked.

"What she fancies," Jarman explained, "is that nature intended her to be a lady. And it's no good pointing out the mistake she's making, because she ain't got sense enough to see it."

"No good talking straight to her," Minikin suggested, "telling her it can never be?"

"That's our difficulty," Jarman replied. "It can be. This chap"—I listened as if a prisoner in the dock to the argument of counsel, interested but impotent—"doesn't know enough to come in out of the rain, as the saying is. He's just the sort of chap this sort of thing does happen to."

"But he don't want her," Minikin urged. "He says he don't want her."

"Yes, to you and me," Jarman answered. "And of course he doesn't. I'm not saying he's a natural born idiot. But let her come along and do a snivel—tell him he's breaking her heart, appeal to him to behave as a gentleman, and all that sort of thing, and what do you think will be the result?"

Minikin agreed the problem presented difficulties.

"Of course, if 'twas you or me, we'd just tell her to put herself away somewhere where the moth couldn't get at her and wait till we sent round for her; and there'd be an end of the matter. But with him it's different."

"He is a bit of a soft," Minikin agreed.

"'Tain't his fault," Jarman explained. "'Twas the way he was brought up. He fancies girls are the sort of things one sees in plays, going about saying 'Un'and me!' 'Let me pass!' Maybe some of 'em are, but this ain't one of 'em."

"How did it happen?" Minikin asked.

"'Ow does it happen nine times out of ten?" Jarman returned. "He was a bit misty, and she was wide awake. He gets a bit spoony, and—well, you know."

"Artful things, girls," Minikin commented.

"Can't blame 'em," Jarman returned generously. "It's their business. Got to dispose of themselves somehow. Oughtn't to be binding without a written order dated the next morning; that'd make it all right."

"Couldn't prove a prior engagement?" Minikin suggested.

"She'd want to see the girl first before she'd believe it—only natural," Jarman returned.

"Couldn't get a girl?" Minikin urged.

"Who could you trust?" asked the cautious Jarman. "Besides, there ain't time. She's letting him rest today; tomorrow evening she'll be down on him."

"Don't see anything for it," Minikin said, "but for him to do a bunk."

"Not a bad idea that," Jarman mused. "Only where's he to bunk to?"

"Needn't go far," Minikin said.
