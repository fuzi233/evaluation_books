XXXII. "At the court of the Tuileries," said Mr. Sillerton Jackson, smiling with the air of one recalling a distant, slightly scandalous past, "such things were pretty openly tolerated." The words hung in the quiet air of the van der Luydens’ black walnut dining room on Madison Avenue, where the evening light fell softly over polished silver and delicate porcelain. It was the night after Newland Archer’s visit to the Museum of Art—a quiet moment between moments, when the city still hummed beneath its surface of propriety.

Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden had returned from Skuytercliff, their country retreat, summoned back by the sudden collapse of Beaufort’s fortune. The news had sent shockwaves through society, and it was felt—by those who mattered—that the old guard must reassert themselves. “It will never do,” Mrs. Archer had said firmly, “to let people like Mrs. Lemuel Struthers think they can step into Regina’s shoes.” And so, with solemn duty in their hearts, the van der Luydens had come to town, reopened their doors, and issued invitations for dinners and receptions, as if the world could be restored simply by proper attendance at the Opera.

That evening, they were hosting a small gathering: Sillerton Jackson, Mrs. Archer, Newland, and his wife May. They would go together to see *Faust*, newly performed that winter. The dinner began precisely at seven, as always—no matter how few the guests—so that each course might unfold with measured grace before the men settled into cigars. Archer had not seen May since the previous night. He’d left early for the office, buried in routine work, then detained by an unexpected visit from a senior partner. By the time he arrived home, May had already gone ahead to the van der Luydens’, sending the carriage back without him.

Now, across the table, she looked pale and tired, yet her eyes sparkled with a forced brightness. She spoke quickly, almost too animatedly, as though trying to fill the silence with sound. The conversation had turned—perhaps deliberately—to the Beaufort affair, a topic still fresh in the drawing rooms of New York. After a thorough moral reckoning, Mrs. van der Luyden turned to May with a look of mild inquiry. “Is it possible, dear, that what I’ve heard is true? I was told your grandmother Mingott’s carriage was seen at Mrs. Beaufort’s door.”

There was a subtle shift in tone—the name “Mrs. Beaufort” now spoken without familiarity. May flushed. Mrs. Archer stepped in swiftly. “If it was, I’m certain it was without Mrs. Mingott’s knowledge.” But Mrs. van der Luyden paused, sighed, and glanced toward her husband. “I’m afraid,” he said gently, “Madame Olenska’s kind heart may have led her into imprudence—calling on Mrs. Beaufort.”

“Or her taste for peculiar people,” Mrs. Archer added dryly, her gaze lingering on her son.

“I’m sorry to think it of Madame Olenska,” Mrs. van der Luyden murmured. Mrs. Archer replied softly, “Ah, my dear—and after you’d had her twice at Skuytercliff!”

And then, as if waiting for this moment, Mr. Jackson leaned forward. “At the Tuileries,” he repeated, his voice low but carrying, “the standard was excessively lax in some respects. If you’d asked where Morny’s money came from—or who paid the debts of some of the Court beauties…” He let the implication hang.

“I hope, dear Sillerton,” Mrs. Archer interjected, “you’re not suggesting we adopt such standards?”

“I never suggest,” Jackson returned, unruffled. “But Madame Olenska’s foreign upbringing may make her less particular—”

“Ahh,” the two older women sighed, exchanging glances.

“Still,” Mr. van der Luyden murmured, “to have kept her grandmother’s carriage at a defaulter’s door!” He seemed to regret something unspoken—perhaps the hampers of carnations he’d once sent to the little house on Twenty-third Street.

“Of course,” Mrs. Archer said, “I’ve always said she looks at things quite differently.”

A flush rose in May’s cheeks. She turned to her husband. “I’m sure Ellen meant it kindly.”

“Imprudent people are often kind,” Mrs. Archer observed, as if kindness did not excuse folly.

“If only she had consulted someone,” Mrs. van der Luyden whispered.

“Ah, but she never does!” Mrs. Archer replied sharply.

At that, Mr. van der Luyden glanced at his wife, who inclined her head slightly toward Mrs. Archer. Then, with silent understanding, the three ladies rose and swept out of the room, their trains glimmering behind them, leaving the men to their cigars.

Archer, after the first act at the Opera, slipped away from the group and found himself standing at the back of the club box. From there, he watched the stage—the same scene he had seen two years earlier, the night he first met Ellen Olenska. He half-expected to see her in her grandmother’s box, but it remained empty. He sat motionless, staring at the vacant seat, until suddenly Madame Nilsson’s pure soprano rang out: “*M’ama, non m’ama…*”

He turned to the stage, where the familiar tableau unfolded: the large blonde Marguerite yielding to the small brown Faust, surrounded by giant roses and pansies painted like pen-wipers. His eyes drifted to the horseshoe-shaped balcony, where May sat between two older ladies—just as she had that night, between Mrs. Lovell Mingott and her foreign cousin. She wore white again—white as snow, just as she had on her betrothal evening. Archer hadn’t noticed her dress at first, but now he recognized the blue-white satin and lace of her wedding gown. In old New York, brides wore it for the first year or two of marriage. His mother still kept hers wrapped in tissue paper, hoping Janey might one day wear it—even though poor Janey was nearly grown, and pearl grey poplin was now considered more appropriate.

Archer realized, with a pang, that May had rarely worn this dress since their return from Europe. Seeing her in it now stirred something deep within him. Her figure had filled out slightly, as her graceful build had predicted, but her posture remained upright, her expression still girlish and transparent—except for a new softness, a faint languor he had lately noticed. Without that, she was exactly the girl he had watched that night, playing with her bouquet of lilies-of-the-valley, full of innocent joy.

The sight moved him—not just with nostalgia, but with pity. Such innocence was fragile, like a child’s trust. And then he remembered—her quiet strength, the depth beneath her calm. He recalled her glance when he had insisted their engagement be announced at the Beaufort ball; he heard again her voice in the Mission garden: “I couldn’t have my happiness made out of a wrong—a wrong to someone else.” A longing surged through him, uncontrollable: to tell her the truth, to throw himself on her mercy, to ask for the freedom he had once refused.

Newland Archer was not a man given to drama. Conformity was his nature, shaped by years of living within the strict rules of society. To act impulsively, to break form—was unthinkable. Yet now, the walls of convention crumbled. He walked along the curved passage behind the theater and opened the door of Mrs. van der Luyden’s box as if stepping into a world unknown.

“*M’ama!*” sang Marguerite triumphantly. The occupants looked up in surprise as Archer entered—breaking one of the unwritten laws: no one entered a box during a solo.

He slipped between Mr. van der Luyden and Jackson, leaned over his wife. “I’ve got a beastly headache,” he whispered. “Don’t tell anyone, but come home, won’t you?”

May gave him a glance—understanding, quiet. She whispered to her mother, who nodded sympathetically. Then she murmured an excuse to Mrs. van der Luyden and stood just as Marguerite sank into Faust’s arms.

As Archer helped her on with her opera cloak, he saw the older ladies exchange a knowing smile.

They drove home in silence. May laid her hand gently on his arm. “I’m so sorry you don’t feel well. I’m afraid they’ve been overworking you again.”

“No—it’s not that,” he replied, confused. “Do you mind if I open the window?” He lowered the pane on his side.

He stared out at the passing lights, feeling her presence beside him like a silent question. At the door, she caught her skirt on the step and stumbled against him.

“Did you hurt yourself?” he asked, steadying her.

“No—but my poor dress—see how I’ve torn it!” she exclaimed, bending to gather up the mud-stained fabric.

They climbed the stairs. The servants hadn’t expected them so early—only a dim gaslight flickered on the landing. Archer lit the brackets on either side of the library mantel. The curtains were drawn, and the room glowed warmly, like a familiar face seen in secret. He noticed how pale she was.

“Should I get you some brandy?” he asked.

“Oh, no,” she said, flushing slightly as she removed her cloak. “But hadn’t you better go to bed?”

He opened a silver case and took out a cigarette. Then, abruptly, he dropped it. “No—my head isn’t that bad.” He paused. “There’s something I want to say. Something important—I must tell you now.”

She sat down in an armchair, lifting her head. “Yes, dear?”

Her voice was so gentle, so untroubled, that he wondered at her composure.

“May—” he began, standing a few feet away, as if distance could protect him. “There’s something I’ve got to tell you… about myself…”

She remained still, her lashes unmoving. Pale, yes—but her face held a strange serenity, as if she drew strength from somewhere deep within.

Archer stopped himself from speaking the usual words of guilt and apology. He wanted to speak plainly, without excuses. “Madame Olenska—” he started.

But at the name, she raised her hand—softly, as if to stop him.

The gaslight caught the gold of her wedding ring.

“Oh, why should we talk about Ellen tonight?” she asked, with a slight pout of impatience.

“Because I ought to have spoken before.”

Her face stayed calm. “Is it really worth while, dear? I know I’ve been unfair to her at times—perhaps we all have. You’ve understood her better than we did. You’ve always been kind to her. But what does it matter, now it’s all over?”

He stared at her, stunned. “All over—what do you mean?”

She looked at him with clear, steady eyes. “Why—since she’s going back to Europe so soon. Since Granny approves and understands, and has arranged for her to live independently—she’ll be with Aunt Medora, who’s coming with her. She’s hurrying back to Washington to pack. We sail next week.”

She paused. “I suppose you were kept at the office this evening about the arrangements. It was settled this morning, I believe.”

She lowered her eyes under his gaze, and a fleeting blush crossed her cheeks.

He realized then—his own eyes must be unbearable. Turning away, he rested his elbows on the mantelpiece and covered his face.

Something drummed inside his head—blood? Or the clock ticking on the shelf?

May sat motionless. The clock marked five minutes.

A lump of coal shifted in the grate. She rose to push it back.

Archer turned slowly to face her.

“It’s impossible,” he said.

“Impossible—?”

“How do you know—what you just told me?”

“I saw Ellen yesterday. I told you I’d seen her at Granny’s.”

“It wasn’t then she told you?”

“No. I had a note from her this afternoon. Do you want to see it?”

He couldn’t speak. She left the room and returned almost at once.

“I thought you knew,” she said simply.

She placed a sheet of paper on the table. He picked it up.

It read:

> *May dear, I have at last made Granny understand that my visit to her could be no more than a visit; and she has been as kind and generous as ever. She sees now that if I return to Europe I must live by myself—or rather with poor Aunt Medora, who is coming with me. I am hurrying back to Washington to pack up, and we sail next week. You must be very good to Granny when I’m gone—as good as you’ve always been to me. Ellen.*
>
> *If any of my friends wish to urge me to change my mind, please tell them it would be utterly useless.*

Archer read it twice, then flung it down and burst out laughing.

The sound startled him—like Janey’s midnight fright when he’d rocked with laughter over May’s telegram announcing their marriage date had been advanced.

“Why did she write this?” he asked, forcing the laugh down.

May met his eyes with her quiet honesty. “I suppose because we talked things over yesterday—”

“What things?”

“I told her I was afraid I hadn’t been fair to her—hadn’t always understood how hard it must have been for her here, alone among people who were relations but strangers, who felt free to judge but didn’t know the truth.”

She paused. “I knew you’d been the one friend she could count on. I wanted her to know that you and I—we’re the same—in all our feelings.”

She hesitated, as if waiting for him to respond. Then she added slowly, “She understood my wanting to tell her this. I think she understands everything.”

She stepped toward him, took his cold hand, and pressed it gently against her cheek.

“My head aches too,” she said softly. “Good-night, dear.”

Then she turned and walked to the door, her torn and muddy wedding dress trailing behind her across the floor.
