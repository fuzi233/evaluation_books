Mr. Crisparkle and John Jasper crossed paths daily beneath the shadow of the Cathedral, yet not a word passed between them about Edwin Drood—since that distant moment, more than half a year prior, when Jasper had silently shown the MinorCanon the final entry in his diary. Though they met often, their conversations never touched upon the vanished young man. Still, it is unlikely that either ever met without his thoughts drifting to the other’s hidden intentions. Their positions were too sharply opposed: Jasper, relentless in his pursuit of Neville Landless; Crisparkle, steadfast in his defense. Each must have wondered what the other was plotting, how far he would go, and whether the next move would be a step toward resolution—or ruin. Yet neither spoke.

Crisparkle, honest by nature, could not feign indifference. He made no secret of his readiness to discuss the matter, even to reopen it. But Jasper remained sealed shut—a man of fixed purpose, immovable in silence. Stoic, solitary, consumed by a single obsession, he lived apart from the world. Though his art required harmony with others, his spirit was utterly isolated. He had once confided this truth to his lost nephew—before the darkness took hold.

He knew, surely, of Rosa’s sudden departure—and guessed its cause. Did he believe he had frightened her into silence? Or did he suspect she had told someone—perhaps even Crisparkle—the full horror of their last meeting? Crisparkle could not decide. But as a just man, he could not condemn Jasper for loving Rosa, any more than he could accuse him of sin for choosing love over vengeance.

The dreadful suspicion that had shaken Rosa—Jasper’s possible guilt—never stirred in Crisparkle’s mind. Nor did Helena or Neville voice it aloud. Mr. Grewgious, though openly hostile to Jasper, never hinted at such a motive. Yet he was secretive, and never mentioned the night he warmed his hands at the gatehouse fire, gazing down at a heap of torn, muddy clothes on the floor.

Cloisterham, drowsy and slow to remember, occasionally stirred at the old tale—dismissed long ago by the magistrates. The town split evenly: some believed Edwin had been murdered by his jealous rival; others thought he’d fled willingly. Then, it would notice that Jasper still hunted, still raged, still sought revenge—and then fall back into sleep. That was the state of things when our story now reaches this point.

The Cathedral doors closed for the night. The Choir-master, granted a brief leave for a few services, turned his face toward London. He traveled the same route Rosa had taken, arriving on a hot, dusty evening.

His baggage was light enough to carry himself. He walked to a small, hybrid lodging near Aldersgate Street, behind the General Post Office—a place that called itself hotel, boarding-house, or lodging-house, depending on the guest’s whim. It advertised itself in railway pamphlets as a new kind of enterprise, timidly emerging. With polite reserve, it suggested travelers might prefer to have their boots shined rather than their stomachs filled. For a fixed price, one could have bed, breakfast, service, and a porter all night. From such signs, many melancholy Britons concluded that the times were leveling—except, perhaps, for roads, which would soon vanish entirely from England.

He ate without appetite and left shortly after. Eastward, through the stale city streets, he walked until he reached his destination: a grim court, worse than most.

He climbed a broken staircase, opened a door, peered into a dark, stifling room, and said: “Are you alone here?”

“Alone, deary; worse luck for me, better for you,” came a croaking reply. “Come in, come in—though I can’t see you till I light a match. But I know your voice. We’ve met before, haven’t we?”

“Light your match and see.”

“So I will, deary. But my hand trembles—can’t find the match right away. And I cough so hard, my matches jump like live things when I do. Are you back from sea?”

“No.”

“Not a sailor?”

“No.”

“Well, there’s land customers and water customers. I’m mother to both. Unlike Jack Chinaman across the way—he’s father to neither. No true secret of mixing, though he charges as much as I do, and more if he can get it. Here’s a match—now where’s the candle? If my cough takes hold, I’ll burn twenty before I light one.”

She found the candle and lit it just before the fit struck. She sat rocking, gasping: “O, my lungs are awful bad! Worn down to cabbage-nets!” Until the spell passed. During it, she saw nothing. When it ended, she strained her eyes and suddenly cried out: “Why—it’s you!”

“Surprised to see me?”

“I thought I’d never see you again. I thought you were dead, gone to Heaven.”

“Why?”

“I didn’t think you could stay away so long—alive—from the poor soul who knows the real recipe. And you’re in mourning too! Why didn’t you come for a pipe or two of comfort? Did they leave you money?”

“No.”

“Who died, deary?”

“A relative.”

“Of what?”

“Probably—Death.”

“We’re short tonight!” she laughed weakly. “Short and snappish. But we’re out of sorts without a smoke. Got the all-overs, haven’t we, deary? This is the place to cure them—in here, where the all-overs are smoked off.”

“You may make ready,” he replied, “whenever you like.”

He removed his shoes, loosened his cravat, and lay across the foot of the squalid bed, head propped on his left hand.

“There,” she said, “you look like yourself again. Now I recognize my old customer! Been trying to mix it yourself all this time, poppet?”

“I’ve taken it now and then—my own way.”

“Never take it your own way. Bad for trade. Bad for you. Where’s my ink-bottle? My thimble? My little spoon?” She began her work, blowing gently on the faint spark in her cupped hands. “He’s going to take it in an artful form now, my dear.”

She spoke in snuffles, never stopping. He spoke without looking at her, as if already lost in thought.

“I’ve given you plenty of smokes, first and last, haven’t I, chuckey?”

“A good many.”

“When you first came, you were new to it—weren’t you?”

“Yes. Easily disposed of.”

“But you grew in the world. Soon took your pipe with the best of them?”

“Ah—and the worst.”

“It’s ready for you. What a sweet singer you were when you first came! Used to drop your head, sing yourself off like a bird! Ready now, deary.”

He took the pipe carefully, placed the mouthpiece to his lips. She sat beside him, ready to refill it.

After a few silent drags, he asked doubtfully: “Is it as potent as it used to be?”

“What do you mean, deary?”

“What should I mean but what’s in my mouth?”

“It’s just the same. Always the identical same.”

“It doesn’t taste the same. Slower.”

“You’ve grown used to it.”

“That may be. Look here.” He paused, dreamy, as if forgetting he’d spoken. She leaned close, whispered in his ear.

“I’m listening. You said ‘Look here.’ I said, ‘I’m listening.’ We were talking about your being used to it.”

“I know. I was only thinking. Suppose you had something in your mind—something you meant to do?”

“Yes, deary—something you meant to do?”

“But hadn’t decided yet?”

“Yes.”

“Might or might not do. Understand?”

“Yes.” She stirred the bowl with a needle.

“Would you do it in your imagination, lying here?”

She nodded. “Over and over again.”

“Just like me. I did it hundreds of thousands of times in this room.”

“It’s to be hoped it was pleasant, deary.”

“It _was_ pleasant!”

He said it fiercely, jerking forward. She calmly refilled the pipe, undisturbed. Seeing her focus, he settled back.

“It was a journey—a dangerous one. Over abysses. A slip would mean death. Look down—look down! See what lies below?”

He lunged forward, pointing as if at a chasm. She looked at his face, not his finger. She seemed to know the effect of her calmness. And she was right—he recoiled.

“I told you—I did it millions of times. So often, through such vast time, that when it actually happened, it seemed almost unreal—so fast.”

“That’s the journey you’ve been on,” she murmured.

He glared, then, eyes clouding, answered: “That’s the journey.”

Silence. His eyes flickered open and shut. She stayed beside him, tending the pipe.

“I’ll warrant,” she said after a while, “you made the journey in many ways, since you did it so often?”

“No. Always in one way.”

“Always the same?”

“Ay.”

“In the way it was done at last?”

“Ay.”

“And always took the same pleasure in it?”

“Ay.”

For a time, he could manage no more than monosyllables. Perhaps to test if he was truly conscious, she reversed her question.

“Did you never grow tired, deary? Try to imagine something else?”

He sat up abruptly. “What do you mean? What did I want? What did I come for?”

She laid him back gently, revived the fire in the pipe with her breath, and said softly: “Sure, sure, sure! Yes, yes, yes! Now I understand. You came on purpose. I should have known—by the way it stood beside you.”

He laughed—then clenched his teeth: “Yes. I came on purpose. When I couldn’t bear life, I came for relief. And I got it. It WAS one! It WAS one!” He repeated it, savage, like a wolf’s snarl.

She watched him closely, feeling her way. Then: “There was a fellow-traveller, deary.”

“Ha, ha, ha!” He laughed—more a yell.

“To think,” he cried, “how often fellow-traveller—and never know it! How many times he went the journey—and never saw the road!”

She knelt by the bed, arms crossed, chin resting on them. Watched him. The pipe slipped. She returned it, placed her hand on his chest, moved him slightly. He spoke, as if she had spoken.

“Yes. I always made the journey first—before the colors changed, the landscapes rose, the glittering processions began. They couldn’t start till it was off my mind. No room for anything else.”

Again, silence. Again, she touched him. Again, he spoke.

“What? I told you so. When it finally comes to pass, it’s so short it seems unreal. Hark!”

“Yes, deary. I’m listening.”

“Time and place are both at hand.”

He stood, whispering, as if in darkness.

“Time, place, and fellow-traveller,” she echoed, holding his arm softly.

“How could time be at hand unless the fellow-traveller was? Hush! The journey’s made. It’s over.”

“So soon?”

“That’s what I said. So soon. Wait. This is a vision. I shall sleep it off. Too short, too easy. I need a better vision—this is the poorest. No struggle, no peril, no entreaty—and yet I never saw _that_ before.” He started.

“Saw what, deary?”

“Look at it! Look what a poor, mean, miserable thing it is! _That_ must be real. It’s over.”

He gestured wildly, then sank into stupor. Lied like a log.

But she was not done. She stirred him again—cat-like—listened. Stirred. Listened. Whispered. Found him unresponsive. Slowly rose, disappointed, flicked his face with the back of her hand as she turned.

Yet she did not leave. She sat in the chair by the hearth, elbow on arm, chin in hand, watching him. “I heard you say once,” she croaked, “when I was lying where you lie now, and you were speculating on me—‘Unintelligible!’ I heard you say it of two others. But don’t be too sure, beauty. Don’t be too sure.”

Unblinking, cat-like, she added: “Not so potent as it once was? Ah. Perhaps not at first. You may be right. Practice makes perfect. I may have learned how to make you talk, deary.”

He talked no more. Twitched grotesquely, face and limbs. Silent. The candle burned low. She took the dying end, lit another, jammed the guttering stub deep into the holder, rammed the new one in—like loading a foul weapon. The second candle burned down. Still, he slept.

At last, the last flame died. Daylight crept in.

It had not lasted long when he sat up, chilled, trembling, slowly regained awareness. Prepared to leave. She received his payment with a grateful, “Bless ye, bless ye, deary!” and seemed to begin preparing for sleep.

But seeming lied. The moment his footsteps faded, she followed, muttering: “I’ll not miss ye twice!”

No exit from the court except the entrance. She peered from the doorway, waited. He did not look back. She followed, watched him waver, then held him in sight.

He went to the back of Aldersgate Street. A door opened instantly to his knock. She crouched in another doorway, watching. Understood he was staying temporarily. Her patience held.

For food, she bought bread within a hundred yards, milk as it passed.

He emerged at noon—changed clothes, empty-handed. Not returning to the country yet. She followed a short way, hesitated, then turned confidently and entered the house he had left.

“Is the gentleman from Cloisterham indoors?”

“Just gone out.”

“Unlucky. When does he return?”

“At six this evening.”

“Bless ye and thank ye. May the Lord prosper a business where a poor soul’s civil question is answered so civilly!”

“I’ll not miss ye twice!” she muttered in the street, less kindly. “I lost ye last—when that omnibus carried you near your journey’s end, plied between station and destination. I wasn’t certain you even arrived. Now I know ye did. My gentleman from Cloisterham—I’ll be there before ye. I swear I’ll not miss ye twice!”

That evening, she stood in Cloisterham High Street, staring at the Nuns’ House, passing the time until nine o’clock—when she expected the omnibus passengers to arrive.

The dusk made it easy to watch. And she saw him among them.

“Now let me see what becomes of you. Go on!”

A whisper to the air—but he obeyed, walking along until he reached an arched gateway, where he vanished unexpectedly.

She quickened pace, nearly caught him entering. Saw only a postern staircase on one side, and on the other—an ancient vaulted room. A large-headed, gray-haired man sat writing, open to the thoroughfare, eyeing all who passed as if collecting toll—though the way was free.

“Halloa!” he called quietly, seeing her pause. “Who are you looking for?”

“A gentleman passed in here just now, sir.”

“Of course. What do you want with him?”

“Where does he live, deary?”

“Live? Up that staircase.”

“Bless ye! Whisper. What’s his name, deary?”

“Surname Jasper. Christian name John. Mr. John Jasper.”

“Has he a calling, good gentleman?”

“Calling? Yes. Sings in the choir.”

“In the spire?”

“Choir.”

“What’s that?”

Mr. Datchery rose, stepped to the doorway. “Do you know what a cathedral is?” he asked, jocosely.

She nodded.

“What is it?”

She looked puzzled, then pointed—massive against the dark sky and stars.

“That’s the answer. Go in there at seven tomorrow morning, and you may see Mr. John Jasper—and hear him too.”

“Thank ye! Thank ye!”

Her triumphant cry did not escape the idle buffer nearby. He glanced at her, clasped his hands behind him, and strolled beside her.

“Or,” he suggested, nodding back, “you can go up now to Mr. Jasper’s rooms.”

She smiled cunningly, shook her head.

“Oh? You don’t want to speak to him?”

She repeated her silent “no.”

“You can admire him at a distance three times a day. Long way to come for that, though.”

She looked up quickly. If Mr. Datchery thought to draw her out, he misjudged her. But she saw no trick in his easy manner—just a man idling, gray hair blowing, hands rattling loose coins.

The sound drew her ears. “Wouldn’t you help me pay for my lodging, dear gentleman? I’m a poor soul, indeed—troubled with a grievous cough.”

“You know the travellers’ lodging, I see. Making straight for it,” he remarked, still rattling. “Been here often, my good woman?”

“Once in all my life.”

“Ay, ay?”

They reached the Monks’ Vineyard. The sight stirred a memory. She stopped at the gate.

“By this token, though you mayn’t believe it—” she declared firmly—“a young gentleman gave me three-and-sixpence as I coughed on this very grass. I asked for it, and he gave it.”

“Wasn’t it a bit bold to name the sum?” Mr. Datchery hinted, still jingling.

“I wanted medicine—good medicine I deal in. Told him so. He gave it. I spent every brass farden honestly. I want to spend the same now. If you give it, I’ll spend it just as honest—upon my soul!”

“What’s the medicine?”

“I’ll be honest—before and after. It’s opium.”

Mr. Datchery’s face changed. He stared at her sharply.
