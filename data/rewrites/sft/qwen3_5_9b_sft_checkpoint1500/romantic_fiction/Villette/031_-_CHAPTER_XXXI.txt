The spring was yielding to a sudden, deceptive warmth. For me, this shift in the atmosphere brought a temporary weakening of spirit; slight exertions left me drained, and sleepless nights dragged me through languid days. On a Sunday afternoon, having trudged the half-league to the Protestant church, I returned to my solitary sanctuary, the First Classe, utterly weary. Glad to sit, I propped my arms and head upon my desk, seeking rest.

For a while, I listened to the lullaby of bees humming within the trellised arbour (*berceau*), watching through the glass door and the soft, newly unfurling foliage. Outside, Madame Beck and a lively party of friends strolled down the central alley. They had dined earlier that morning after Mass, and their attire was a riot of pure, warm colours, vibrant as mountain snow at sunrise. Among them, however, caught my eye above all else, was a handsome young girl. I had glimpsed her before as a visitor to Madame Beck's, vaguely told that she was a *filleule*, or god-daughter, of M. Emanuel. Rumor suggested a special bond of old between her and the Professor, though M. Paul himself was not present today. Still, I had seen her with him on other occasions; she moved around him with the frank ease of a ward with an indulgent guardian, once running to him, slipping her arm through his, and clinging to him. Watching her, I felt a strange, disagreeable anticipation ripple through me—a presentiment I quickly suppressed. Following the gleam of her bright silk robe through the emerald leaves, my eyes grew dizzy. The heat, the hum of insects, the scent of blossoms, all conspired to lull me, and soon I slept.

Two hours passed. When I awoke, the sun had dipped behind the towering houses, casting a grey pall over the garden. The guests were gone, the alleys empty. Yet, I felt no chill, no numbness from pressing against the hard wood. Beneath me lay a thick shawl, carefully folded, and wrapped around me was another, both taken from the corridor. A kindness, mysterious and tender.

Who could have done this? Saint-Pierre was kind enough, but who possessed the quiet thought, the gentle habit, to benefit me so subtly without disturbing a sleeper? Ginevra Fanshawe, with her restless energy, would surely have shaken me awake. Finally, I reasoned, "It must be Madame Beck. She sees me as a useful machine, hired for a purpose, and wishes to avoid injury. Her logic is sound, if cold."

Resolving to take a walk as the evening air cooled, I opened the glass door and stepped into the arbour. I ventured toward my own alley. Even in the fading light, the grey crown of Jean-Baptiste still held a ray of sunset, and the birds had not yet retreated entirely to their nests. Pacing up and down, I fell into the same reflections I had harbored months prior when burying my glass jar: how to secure an independent position. Villette was an economical town, less obsessed with display than England, where a prudent rent for a single large room and a few smaller ones could be afforded. With a thousand francs saved, I could set up a school: benches, desks, a blackboard, and an estrade for myself. I would begin with day-pupils, working upward, just as Madame Beck had done. She had bought this estate and garden with her earnings, securing a competency and a flourishing career for her children.

"Courage, Lucy Snowe!" I whispered to myself. "With self-denial and steady effort, an objective need not elude you. Do not complain that it is too selfish or limited; labour for independence until you have proven your right to aspire higher. But what then? Is there nothing more in life? No true home? Nothing dearer than oneself, capable of drawing from me better things than mere self-culture? Where can I lay down the burden of human egotism and take up the noble charge of living for others?"

"I suppose," I continued, "that the orbit of your life is not perfectly rounded for you. The crescent phase must suffice. Very well. I see vast masses of humanity in similar straits, holding their lives on conditions of denial. I find no reason why I should be among the favoured. I believe in a blend of hope and sunshine that sweetens the bitter lot. I believe life is not all, neither beginning nor end. I believe while I tremble; I trust while I weep."

I settled this account honestly. It is wise to look squarely at life's ledger occasionally, naming anguish as anguish and despair as despair, writing them down in bold characters to pay the debt to Doom. To falsify—to insert 'privilege' where 'pain' belongs—is a swindle that no creditor will accept.

Leaning my brow against the knotty trunk of Methusaleh, the giant patriarch of the garden, my foot resting on the stone sealing the small sepulchre at his roots, I recalled the feelings buried there. Dr. John. My warm affection, my faith in his excellence, my delight in his grace. What had become of that curious, one-sided friendship, half marble, half life? Was it dead? Perhaps it slept uneasily, dreaming of disturbed earth and golden hair protruding from coffin cracks.

"Have I been too hasty?" I asked myself, a question that sharpened cruelly after brief encounters with Dr. John. His kindness, his warm hand, his pleasant tone for my name—it all lingered. I had never liked hearing 'Lucy' so well as when he spoke it. Yet, I learned that this benevolence, this cordiality, this music, belonged to him alone. It was the honey of his temper, the balm of his mood, diffused like perfume from sweet plants. Did the nectarine love the bee that fed it? Did the sweetbrier love the air?

"Good-night, Dr. John," I murmured. "You are good, you are beautiful, but you are not mine. Good-night, and God bless you!"

The words left my lips, and then I heard an echo, close at hand.

"Good-night, Mademoiselle; or rather, good-evening—the sun is scarce set. I hope you slept well?"

I started, discomposed for a fleeting moment, then recognized the voice.

"Slept? When? Where?"

"You may well inquire. It seems you turn day into night and choose a desk for a pillow. Rather hard lodging—"

"It was softened for me, Monsieur, while I slept. That unseen, gift-bringing thing which haunts my desk remembered me. No matter how I fell asleep, I awoke pillowed and covered."

"Do the shawls keep you warm?"

"Very warm. Do you ask thanks?"

"No. You looked pale in your slumbers: are you homesick?"

"To be homesick, one must have a home; which I have not."

"Then you have more need of a careful friend. I scarcely know anyone, Miss Lucy, who needs a friend more absolutely than you; your faults imperatively require it. You want so much checking, regulating, and keeping down."

The idea of "keeping down" haunted M. Paul's mind; habitual subjugation would fail to relieve him of it. Yet, I listened, making no effort to be overly submissive. If I resisted, his occupation would vanish.

"You need watching, and watching over," he persisted. "And it is well for you that I see this and do my best to discharge both duties. I watch you and others pretty closely, nearly and constantly, nearer and oftener than you or they think. Do you see that window with a light in it?"

He pointed to a lattice in one of the boarding-houses. "That," said he, "is a room I have hired, nominally for a study—virtually for a post of observation. There I sit and read for hours. My book is this garden; its contents are human nature—female human nature. I know you all by heart. Ah! I know you well—Saint-Pierre, the Parisienne, *cette maîtresse-femme*, my cousin Beck herself."

"It is not right, Monsieur."

"Not right? By whose creed? Does some dogma of Calvin or Luther condemn it? What is that to me? I am no Protestant. My rich father, though I know poverty and once starved for a year in a Roman garret, was a good Catholic. He gave me a priest and a Jesuit for a tutor. I retain his lessons; and to what discoveries, Grand Dieu! have they not aided me!"

"Discoveries made by stealth seem to me dishonourable."

"Puritaine! I doubt it not. Yet see how my Jesuit's system works. You know the Saint-Pierre?"

"Partially."

He laughed. "Right—*partially*; whereas *I* know her *thoroughly*. She played the amiable before me, offered *patte de velours*, caressed, flattered, fawned. Now, I am accessible to a woman's flattery—accessible against my reason. Though never pretty, she was young, or knew how to look young. Like all her countrywomen, she had the art of dressing, a certain cool, easy social assurance which spared me the pain of embarrassment."
