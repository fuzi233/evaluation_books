When Margaret’s senses came back to her, her cheek lay against Catherine’s firm forearm, as if she had been set there like dough to rise under a warm cloth. Around her stood the honest half of the household she had stormed as one might storm a gate—plain faces, plain hands, and plain, hearty counsel spoken with the clumsy kindness of people who have salted much meat and wasted few words. Giles, great-voiced as a market bell, bellowed straight over her.

“Thou’rt not to take on so! Gerard’s alive and well, or he could not ha’ writ thee that letter—aye, the biggest mankind hath seen as yet—and,” he added with a reverence almost comic in his rough throat, “the beautifullest, and most moving, and smallest writ.”

“Ay, good Master Giles,” Margaret breathed, her voice thin as steam from a cooling pot, “he was alive. But how know I what hath since befallen him? Oh, why left he Holland to go among strangers fierce as lions? And why did I not drive him from me sooner than part him from his own flesh and blood? Forgive me—you that are his mother!”

She slid Catherine’s arm away with a gentleness that was more apology than strength, and made a trembling attempt to slip down upon her knees. The attempt ended—after a brief, helpless struggle against hands that would not let her fall—in her being gathered back against Catherine’s bosom, where she clung as if that plain wool gown were the one safe shore in all Europe.

Then, with a little shiver like a leaf in draught, she held out the letter to Eli.

“I will trust it from my hand now,” she whispered—sweetly, faintly. “In sooth, I am little fit to read any more—and—and—loth to leave my comfort.” And she wreathed her free arm round Catherine’s neck, as a child will cling when thunder has passed but the air still trembles.

“Read thou, Richart,” said Eli. “Thine eyes be younger than mine.”

Richart took the letter as if it were a consecrated wafer.

“Well,” said he, turning it this way and that to catch the light, “such writing saw I never. A writeth with a needle’s point; and clear to boot. Why is he not in my counting-house at Amsterdam instead of vagabonding it out yonder!”

And he began.

“When I came to myself I was seated in the litter, and my good merchant holding of my hand. I babbled I know not what, and then shuddered awhile in silence. He put a horn of wine to my lips.”

“Bless him! bless him!” cried Catherine, as if the wine had been poured for her own son.

“Whisht!” said Eli; but his face softened.

“And I told him what had befallen. He would see my leg. It was sprained sore, and swelled at the ankle; and all my points were broken, as I could scarce keep up my hose, and I said, ‘Sir, I shall be but a burden to you, I doubt, and can make you no harmony now; my poor psaltery it is broken;’ and I did grieve over my broken music, companion of so many weary leagues. But he patted me on the cheek, and bade me not fret; also he did put up my leg on a pillow, and tended me like a kind father.

“January 19.—I sit all day in the litter, for we are pushing forward with haste, and at night the good, kind merchant sendeth me to bed, and will not let me work. Strange! whene’er I fall in with men like fiends, then the next moment God still sendeth me some good man or woman, lest I should turn away from human kind. Oh, Margaret! how strangely mixed they be, and how old I am by what I was three months agone. And lo! if good Master Fugger hath not been and bought me a psaltery.”

“Eli, my man,” Catherine broke in, brisk as a cook taking stock of a pantry, “an yon merchant comes our way let us buy a hundred ells of cloth of him, and not higgle.”

“That will I,” said Eli; “take your oath on’t!”

While Richart shifted his stance to go on, Kate stole a glance at her mother and, with a blush that came and went like a quick flame under ash, drew a bit of sewing from beneath her apron. Her head bent lower than need was. Her needle began to travel again—soft, secret, sure. Catherine, catching the look, answered with a look, and drew out her own sewing from her pocket with the same guilty dexterity. They stitched in silence, the thread whispering through linen.

The two pieces—now first betrayed to common sight—were babies’ caps, more than half finished; and horror, for all their tenderness, they were shaped like little monks’ cowls, fine as frostwork.

Richart read on.

“January 20.—Laid up in the litter, and as good as blind, but halting to bait, Lombardy plains burst on me. Oh, Margaret! a land flowing with milk and honey; all sloping plains, goodly rivers, jocund meadows, delectable orchards, and blooming gardens; and though winter, looks warmer than poor beloved Holland at midsummer, and makes the wanderer’s face to shine, and his heart to leap for joy to see earth so kind and smiling. Here be vines, cedars, olives, and cattle plenty, but three goats to a sheep. The draught oxen wear white linen on their necks, and standing by dark green olive-trees each one is a picture; and the folk, especially women, wear delicate strawen hats with flowers and leaves fairly imitated in silk, with silver mixed. This day we crossed a river prettily in a chained ferry-boat. On either bank was a windlass, and a single man by turning of it drew our whole company to his shore, whereat I did admire, being a stranger. Passed over with us some country folk. And an old woman looking at a young wench, she did hide her face with her hand, and held her crucifix out like knight his sword in tourney dreading the evil eye.

“January 25.—Safe at Venice…”

At the name, the room seemed to widen. Even the loom and the hearth, the daily tools that had heard so much human talk, fell attentive. Outside, the house’s small noises—wood settling, a distant cluck, the sigh of winter air—made a kind of homely counterpoint, like a drone under a song.

“A place whose strange and passing beauty is well known to thee by report of our mariners. Dost mind too how Peter would oft fill our ears withal, we handed beneath the table, and he still discoursing of this sea-enthroned and peerless city, in shape a bow, and its great canal and palaces on piles, and its watery ways plied by scores of gilded boats; and that market-place of nations… But I, lying at my window in pain, may see none of these beauties as yet, but only a street, fairly paved, which is dull, and houses with oiled paper and linen, in lieu of glass, which is rude; and the passers-by, their habits and their gestures, wherein they are superfluous. Therefore, not to miss my daily comfort of whispering to thee, I will e’en turn mine eyes inward…”

Margaret listened as if each line were broth spooned into her mouth—too hot to swallow at once, yet necessary.

Gerard’s voice, carried by ink, began to do what his hands could not: set in order the world for the woman he loved. He wrote of nations as a baker speaks of different flours—each with its own temper, its own rising, its own sourness.

He told of Germans who drank till sobriety itself was suspect; of English who sugared their wine; of French courtesy that could be as light as foam and as treacherous as a thin crust over a hollow pie.

“‘Tis sooth! ‘tis sooth!” Catherine burst out at one passage, recognizing a wife in Tergou who gave her man leave to go abroad. “’Twas Beck Hulse, Jonah’s wife. This comes of a woman wedding a boy.”

Denys, lounging with his soldier’s ease, laughed at the French, and then rose with a flourish of hand to breastplate when France was praised, as if he would accept compliments on behalf of a whole kingdom.

Yet Gerard’s letter, for all its jests and observations, kept returning—like a song’s refrain—to the things that struck deeper: what words did in different mouths, how custom could gild cruelty, how nations named their sins with silken names. It was not philosophy in the air; it was the bite and smell of lived places—the stale beer breath of taverns, the clean shine of German windows, the dust of roads where a grape might cost a life.

When Richart read that Gerard had called Denys foul-mouthed, Denys bristled like a cat.

“My foul mouth! Is that so writ, Master Richart?”

“Ay, in sooth; see else.”

Denys peered at the letter with comical gravity, tracing the tiny strokes as if they were battle-maps.

“I read not the letter so.”

“How then?”

“Humph! ahem—why just the contrary.” He straightened, and appealed to the women with injured innocence. “’Tis kittle work perusing of these black scratches men are agreed to take for words. And I trow ’tis still by guess you clerks do go, worthy sir. My foul mouth! This is the first time e’er I heard on’t. Eh, mesdames?”

Margaret, caught between love and laughter, only blushed and lowered her eyes. Kate and Catherine bent over their needlework with a silence that had a sly curl at the edge. Denys studied their faces as intently as if reading a proclamation. Whatever he read there satisfied him; he turned back with patient martyrdom.

“Read on,” said he.

So the letter flowed—Italy polished and subtle; Venice temperate at table, yet crawling with itch-ointment hawked on corners; bread “lovely white,” salt “black;” meat “spoiled” with cheese; innkeepers who would sell clean sheets, and sometimes sell murder; women’s hair turned yellow by sun and art; and the State itself taking tax from sin as from spice.

Eli sighed once, deep in his chest, when Gerard prayed that in little Holland an assassin might be called assassin, and an harlot harlot, till doomsday.

“He should have been a priest,” Eli murmured, “saving your presence, my poor lass.”

At last Richart came to the last dated entry; and the room leaned in, as if nearer words could bring nearer the man.

“January 26.—Sweetheart, I must be brief, and tell thee but a part of that I have seen, for this day my journal ends. To-night it sails for thee, and I, unhappy, not with it, but to-morrow, in another ship, to Rome.

“Dear Margaret, I took a hand litter, and was carried to St. Mark his church…”

As Richart read of the church—of pillars like a forest of stone, and horses that seemed ready to leap, and jewels blazing like fruit set in gold—Margaret’s heart tightened and loosened with each marvel, for all that glittering wealth was only the crust upon another hunger: Gerard’s hunger to share what his eyes had seen, since he could not share it with her in person.

Then came the tale of relics and miracles, told with a man’s mixture of awe and doubt: St. Mark’s body breaking through marble at midnight; the crevice that would gape “while the world lasts;” the Virgin’s chair; the picture carried out to call down rain. There was a shiver in the room when Richart read, “I wish I had not seen it,” as if Gerard’s very voice had faltered.

He wrote, too, of darker civic customs: babes slid through a wall-niche into the State’s net; a corpse carried with bare face and feet; three rotting heads tainting the air upon a porphyry slab; a gallows of alabaster set before the Duke’s own eyes to whisper him memento mori. Even here—amid trade and prayer and music—the city’s soundscape rang with its own harsh bells: the clack of law, the creak of discipline, the steady hum of power saying, in stone, Remember.

He told of four brothers who poisoned each other at one table—wine drugged, marchpane envenomed—so that land might not be quartered, and souls were bartered for acreage. Catherine’s needle paused at marchpane, as if the very name of sugared paste should have been safe from such corruption.

Then, abruptly, the letter turned from poisoned banquets and stone effigies to a small, human sign that could be read without Latin or law.

“And after this they took me to the quay, and presently I espied among the masts one garlanded with amaranth flowers. ‘Take me thither,’ said I, and I let my guide know the custom of our Dutch skippers to hoist flowers to the masthead when they are courting a maid. Oft had I scoffed at this saying, ‘So then his wooing is the earth’s concern.’ But now, so far from the Rotter, that bunch at a masthead made my heart leap with assurance of a countryman. They carried me, and oh, Margaret! on the stern of that Dutch boy, was written in muckle letters,

RICHART ELIASSOEN, AMSTERDAM.”
