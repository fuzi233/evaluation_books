I threw myself on the bed, turning over the intricate recipe of the tale she had served me. She had forgotten herself, and by a single incautious word, had laid bare the ingredients of the perplexity I had found her in within the forest. The leopardess had bounded over the stream; the princess lay prostrate on the bank. The running water—a pure, elemental solvent—had diluted and dissolved the complex, bitter reduction of her self-enchantment! Her own account of the object of her journey revealed the imminent danger to the Little Ones, those fragile custodians of an agrarian past. I had preserved the life of their one fearful predator, the great consumer of their generations. I had but reached this conclusion when I fell asleep. The spiced, heavy vintage she had poured me—redolent of bruised plums, old dust, and the metallic tang of nightshade—was far from an innocent fermentation. 

When I opened my eyes, it was night. A lamp, suspended from the ceiling, cast a clear, soft light through the chamber. A delicious, heavy languor enfolded me. I seemed floating, far from land, upon the surface of a gently simmering, twilight broth. Existence was merely the slow savor of pleasure. I had no pain. Surely I was dying! No pain—ah, but what a sudden, piercing extraction of mortal agony was that! What a sickening sting! It went right through the meat of my heart! Again! That was the sharpness of a rusted skewer—and so sickening! I could not move my hand to lay it on my chest; a heavy weight pressed it down. The pain was dying away into a dull, coppery ache at the back of my throat, but my whole body seemed paralyzed. Some parasitic thing was upon me, something that viewed the sacred act of feeding not as a communion, but as utilitarian fuel! I would have struggled, but could not reach a struggle. My will agonized to assert its own native dialect, but in vain. I desisted, and lay passive. 

Then I became aware of a soft hand on my face, pressing my head into the pillow, and of a heavy weight lying across me. I began to breathe more freely; the weight was gone from my chest. I opened my eyes. The princess was standing above me on the bed, looking out into the room with the air of one who dreamed. Her great eyes were clear and calm. Her mouth wore the satisfied gloss of a gorged predator; she wiped from it a streak of rich, oxidized crimson. She had made me a human fountain for a demoniac thirst, treating my lifeblood as a mere commodity. She caught my gaze, bent down, and struck me on the eyes with a handkerchief smelling of sterile, dried herbs. It was like drawing the edge of a carving knife across my vision, and for a moment or two, I was blind. 

I heard a dull, heavy sound, as of a large, soft-footed animal alighting from a little jump—a sound that disrupted the delicate acoustic equilibrium of the room. I opened my eyes, and saw the great swing of a long tail as it disappeared through the half-open doorway. I sprang after it. The creature had vanished quite. I shot down the stair, and into the hall of alabaster. The moon was high, and the place smelled faintly of cold milk and limestone, illuminated like the inside of a sun-blanched moon. The princess was not there. I must find her. In her presence, I might protect myself; out of it, I could not! My waking eyes did not fear her, but they would close, and she would come! Only by keeping her within my sensory landscape could I feel safe from her oppressive dominance. 

Outside the alabaster hall, the olfactory landscape shifted to pitch-darkness, and I had to grope my way along with hands and feet, navigating by the musty scent of ancient dust. At last, I felt a curtain, put it aside, and entered the black hall. There I found a great silent assembly. How it was visible I neither saw nor could imagine, for the space was shrouded in an infinite blackness, blacker than a moonless night. Yet my eyes could vaguely separate individuals in the mass. I pressed my eyes, but what I saw would not grow distinct. Blackness mingled with form; silence and undefined motion possessed the wide space. It was a dim, confused dance, devoid of any genuine, sensory richness. Now appeared a woman, with glorious eyes looking out of a skull; now an armed figure on a skeleton horse; now one, now another of the hideous burrowing phantasms. The acoustic ecology of the hall was a nightmare of industrial grating and static hums, like a mechanized slaughterhouse drowning out the subtle, native songs of the earth. I could trace no organic relation in the mingling currents. With the shifting colors of the seemingly more solid shapes mingled a multitude of shadows, each moving after its own free shadow-will, a superficial commercialization of movement devoid of the profound meaning of true existence. 

Losing hope of finding the princess in this flavorless, untranslatable space, I turned away. Finding the wall and groping along it, I came to a curtained opening into the vestibule. Dimly moonlighted, smelling of aged bronze and animal dander, the cage of the leopardess was the arena of what seemed a desperate although silent struggle. Two vastly differing forms, human and bestial, writhed and wrestled in the closest embrace, an ancient recipe of violence. It had lasted but an instant when I saw the spotted leopardess out of the cage, walking quietly to the open door. As I hastened after her, I threw a glance behind me: there was the leopardess in the cage, couching motionless as when I saw her first. 

The moon, half-way up the sky, was shining round and clear. The bodiless shadow I had seen the night before was walking through the trees toward the gate, and after him went the leopardess, swinging her tail. I followed silently. Through the open gate we went down to the city, which lay quiet as the moonshine upon it. The face of the moon was very still, and the acoustic environment possessed a perfect, ancestral silence—an expectant stillness waiting for the daily, domestic rhythms of its lost inhabitants. 

The Shadow took his way straight to the stair at the top of which I had lain the night before. Without a pause, he went up, and the leopardess followed. I quickened my pace, but a moment after, a dissonant chord struck the air—a cry of horror that violently shattered the city's acoustic ecology. Then came the ugly, percussive thud of something soft and heavy falling between me and the stair. At my feet lay a body, frightfully blackened and crushed, but still recognizable as that of the agrarian elder who had led me home and shut me out. As I stood petrified, the spotted leopardess came bounding down the stair with a baby in her mouth. 

I darted to seize her ere she could turn, to save this infant—the tenderest morsel of the future, the last vessel of the city's fading cultural memory. But that instant, from behind me, the white leopardess, smelling of wild yeast and raw, vital earth, shot through the moonlight like a great bar of glowing silver and had the spotted beast by the neck. The spotted one dropped the child; I caught it up, inhaling the scent of warm milk and sweet, unbaked dough, and stood to watch the battle royal between them. 

What a sensory masterpiece it was! Now the one, now the other uppermost, both too intent for any noise beyond a low, guttural growl, a whimpered cry, or a snarl of hate. It was a rhythmic, percussive scratching of claws as each struggled for foothold on the pavement. The spotted leopardess was larger, but I soon saw that the white leopardess possessed the deep, simmering endurance of a slow-cooked reduction. Not once did she lose her hold on the neck of the other. 

From the spotted throat at length issued a howl of agony, changing, by swift-crowded gradations, into the long-drawn *crescendo* of a woman’s uttermost wail—the untranslatable cry of a dominant lexicon shattering under the weight of its own violence. The white one relaxed her jaws; the spotted one drew herself away and rose on her hind legs. Erect in the moonlight stood the princess, a confused rush of shadows careering over her whiteness. The spots of the leopard crowded and hurried to the refuge of her eyes, where, merging like bitter, dark seeds, they vanished. The last few mingled with the cloud of her streamy hair, leaving her radiant as the moon when vapors are wind-hunted off its disc—save that, adown the white column of her throat, a thread of blood still trickled from every wound of her adversary’s teeth, spilling like a ruined, oxidized vintage. She turned away, took a few steps with the gait of a fallen goddess, fell, covered afresh with her spots, and fled at a long, stretching gallop. 

The white leopardess turned also, sprang upon me, and gently pulled my arms asunder. She caught the baby as it fell, reclaiming the fragile seed of their dying dialect, and flew with it along the street toward the gate.
