The day after the terrible scene, the little house on the Hoog Straet lay heavy with silence, like a tomb. Catherine, usually so brisk and full of life, moved through it as though her spirit had been drained away. Her eyes were red from weeping when, after dinner, she set out for the convent, hoping to find Gerard and begin the slow work of mending what had been broken. She meant to soften his heart, if only by a single word.

It took some time before the porter could understand whom she sought. When he finally did, he told her that Gerard had left late the previous night and was not expected back. Disheartened, Catherine returned home and broke the news to Margaret.

She found her sitting still, motionless, as if all joy had fled from the world. The child clutched tightly in her arms seemed to be all that remained—her last anchor. She held him close, afraid someone might take even this small comfort from her.

“Come to the Hoog Straet,” Catherine urged gently.

“For what?” Margaret whispered, her voice thin and weary. “You must think I am the cause of all this sorrow.”

“No, no,” Catherine said quickly. “We are not so cruel. And Eli loves you—he may yet be won over.”

“If I can help,” Margaret murmured, “then I will go.”

They arrived to find Eli and a carpenter replacing the old sign—once bearing the names Cornelis and Sybrandt—with a new one: *Margaret Brandt*. Catherine’s heart sank. Though she loved Margaret deeply, this act pierced her like a knife.

“The bane of one is another’s meat,” she muttered.

“You cannot make me spend money unjustly,” Margaret replied coldly.

“You are a good soul,” Catherine said, trying to keep her voice steady.

“Aye,” Margaret answered, “but best, since he is the strongest.”

The next day, Giles dropped in. Catherine told him the tale, painting the brothers as victims—outcast by their father, abandoned by their kin. She pleaded for pity, for justice, for mercy.

But Giles saw things differently. He listened patiently, then spoke plainly: “Those rogues got off too lightly. They deserve to hang at Margaret’s door. And now—hurrah!—my favourite returns.” He smiled broadly, his pride restored. “I’ll show him,” he declared, “what it means to have a brother at court who knows how to serve a friend and guide him right.”

“Bless you, Giles,” Margaret whispered softly.

“You’ve always stood by him, dear Giles,” little Kate added. “But alas, I know not what you can do now.”

Giles left, and silence settled once more—until a quiet knock at the door.

A well-dressed man stepped inside, asking if Margaret Brandt was there.

“Hear this, lass!” Catherine called cheerfully. “You’re wanted!”

“Well, mother,” Margaret said dully, “here I am.”

Then came the shuffle of feet. A frail, pale old man was helped into the room—Ghysbrecht Van Swieten.

At the sight of him, Catherine gasped and threw her apron over her face. Margaret shuddered violently, turning her head sharply away, refusing to look.

A weak voice trembled from the visitor’s lips: “Good people, a dying man has come to beg your forgiveness.”

“Come to see the ruin you made,” Catherine snapped, pulling down her apron and breaking into sobs.

“There, there—she faints! Eli, quick, help her!”

“No,” Margaret said faintly. “It’s just the shock. Let him speak. But he is the murderer of me and mine.”

“Alas,” Ghysbrecht whispered, “I am too weak to stand. No one offers me a seat.”

Eli, who had followed him in, stepped forward, half angry, half ashamed. “Well, burgomaster,” he said stiffly, “it is not our custom to leave guests standing while we sit. But mark this—your actions have done us great harm.”

His voice shook, but he held himself in check—for this was his own home.

Then, unexpectedly, a soft voice broke the tension.

“Father, mother,” little Kate said gently, “I owe you my duty—but this is not right. Death settles all accounts. See how death stares from his face? I shall not live long. His time is shorter than mine.”

Eli hesitated, then hurried to bring a chair. With careful hands, he placed it before the dying man. Ghysbrecht’s attendants helped him sit.

“Fetch the boxes,” he said.

They brought two wooden chests and withdrew, leaving the old man alone among those he had wronged.

All eyes turned to him—except Margaret’s.

With trembling fingers, Ghysbrecht opened the first box and pulled out deeds. “This land and these houses belonged to Floris Brandt,” he said. “They belong to you, his granddaughter, by right. I took them once, for a debt long paid with interest. Now I return them—tears in my heart, and shame in my soul.”

He opened the second box. “Three hundred forty golden angels—the rent and fines I collected beyond what was owed. I kept them… thinking I would give them back one day. But greed held me fast. I was not born dishonest—but I became so. Forgive me, please, against temptation.”

“Indeed!” Catherine exclaimed. “And you, burgomaster—the man who punished thieves all your life! But better late than never. Margaret, can you hear? He’s given you back your own. You’re rich now. Alack, what a mountain of gold!”

“Let him keep land and gold,” Margaret said, her head still turned away. “Give me back my Gerard—the one you stole from me with your treachery.”

“Alas,” Ghysbrecht said, “I wish I could. But what I can do, I have done. It cost me much. I rose from my deathbed to do it myself, lest misfortune prevent you from claiming what is yours.”

“Old man,” Margaret said quietly, “you, whose god is gold, have done this. God and the saints may forgive you, I hope. As for me—I am no saint. I am only a woman, broken-hearted. Speak to him, Kate. I am already dead.”

Kate paused, then spoke in a voice like music, gentle and clear: “My sister carries a grief no riches can heal. Give her time, Ghysbrecht. It is not natural she should forgive you all at once. Her son is fatherless. She is neither maid nor wife nor widow. The blow fell only two days ago—and it laid her heart open, bleeding.”

A deep, silent sob escaped Margaret.

“Therefore, give her time,” Kate continued. “And before you die, she may forgive you—even to please me, who may soon follow you, Ghysbrecht. In the meantime, we—whose wounds are deep, but not as deep as hers—forgive you. You are penitent, and dying. And I, for one, will pray for you from this hour. Go in peace.”

Her words fell like a blessing. Even Eli offered the old man bread and wine, but Ghysbrecht declined.

“What I have done,” he said, “is enough to strengthen me. Man sees only a little way ahead. That land I clung to so tightly—it was a bed of nettles all along. Now it is gone—and I feel lighter, freer, as though I have shed a burden.”

He called for his men. They lifted him into a litter and carried him away.

When he was gone, Catherine lingered over the gold, dazzled by its weight and glow. She had never seen such wealth gathered in one place. She nearly scolded Margaret for staying behind like a statue.

“Oh, come and see!” she insisted, teasing her. “Think of the possibilities!”

“Leave her be, mother,” Kate said softly. “How can she enjoy gold when her heart is heavier than lead?”

But Catherine pressed on. At last, Margaret relented.

She stood before the treasure, staring, wide-eyed. Then suddenly, she wrung her hands and screamed: “TOO LATE! TOO LATE!”

She collapsed into sobs, overwhelmed—not by riches, but by the agony of what might have been. If only a fraction of this wealth, a piece of this land, had come to her a year or two ago—when it was truly hers—Gerard would never have left. He would never have gone to Italy, never have chosen another path.

“Too late! Too late!” she cried again, her voice breaking with sorrow.
