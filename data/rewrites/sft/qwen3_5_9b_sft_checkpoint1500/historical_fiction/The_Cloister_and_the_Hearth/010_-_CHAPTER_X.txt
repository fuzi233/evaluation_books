The legal formalities of matrimony required the reading of the banns three times, a custom unchanged from modern days, save for a local variation wherein the curate, newly arrived in the parish, was readily swayed by the earnest entreaties of the young pair to compress these readings into a single twenty-four-hour span. Consequently, the announcements were proclaimed on Monday at both Matins and Vespers. To the profound relief of the lovers, the church remained empty of the townsfolk of Tergou; the next morning, however, the atmosphere shifted from anticipation to dread. As Gerard and Margaret entered, trembling with anxiety, a stranger rose in the midst of the congregation and, to their horror, interdicted the union. His grounds were twofold: the alleged minority of the parties and the purported lack of parental consent.

Outside, amidst the shadows of the nave, Margaret and Gerard engaged in a desperate, quivering consultation, their hopes seemingly extinguished. Before they could formulate a defense, the officious interloper approached them. With feigned regret, he explained that while his heart inclined toward their happiness, his livelihood depended entirely upon the prohibition of illicit matches. "A crown," he offered, "shall suffice to unravel this tangle; inform the priest that I was mistaken, and the ceremony proceeds as planned."

"A golden angel!" Gerard exclaimed, his spirit igniting at the prospect. The man, equally eager, accepted the sum and presented himself to the curate, confessing a ludicrous error corrected by a mere glimpse of the couple. The priest, relieved, scheduled the wedding for the following day at ten. The professional impediment departed with his payment, a fool indeed among knaves, and sought solace in a local tavern known for its green dedicated to archery and rustic sports. Intoxicated by his success, he boasted of his exploit to the patrons, specifically a frequent visitor named Sybrandt, a man of dubious repute. Sybrandt, absorbing every detail, fled home to deliver the news to his father, who was away in Rotterdam procuring cloth. Sybrandt signaled his elder brother, confiding the tale, and together they became the "black sheep" of a large family—men whose idleness had festered into a venomous obsession with inheritance, transforming their parents' prudence into avarice.

Conspiring in secrecy, the brothers resolved not to burden their mother, whose temperament was volatile, but to seek the Burgomaster, Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. Though they suspected his opposition, they could not discern the motive. Swieten, a man of sharp intellect, saw through their scheme immediately, masking his insight behind a veneer of magisterial indifference. "Since the father is absent, the duty falls to me, the father of the town," he declared, dismissing them with a supercilious air, ashamed of his unwitting accomplices.

Upon returning home, the brothers discovered Gerard seated at his mother's knee, receiving tender affection and promises of support. Yet, the catalyst for her sudden reversal was a moment of female fury: having torn Margaret's portrait to shreds in a fit of irritation, she had witnessed Gerard collapse into a state of ghastly stillness, his eyes fixed on the fragments until tears blinded him. Terror-stricken by her cruelty, her conscience had softened, and she vowed to repair the breach. Unaware of the immediate cause, Gerard, inexperienced in feminine capriciousness, interpreted her kindness as a restoration of maternal warmth, kissing her repeatedly and retreating to his chamber a happy prince, oblivious to the storm brewing within his own household.

The following morning, at ten, Gerard and Margaret stood in the church of Sevenbergen, radiant with joy, flanked only by Peter and Martin Wittenhaagen. Secrecy was paramount; Margaret had refused Italy to remain near her helpless, scholarly father, settling instead on a brief exile in Flanders. As the curate prepared to speak, a harsh voice shattered the sanctity of the moment. "Forbear!" cried a constable of Tergou, seizing Gerard in the name of the law. Martin's long knife flashed, prompting the priest to denounce the impiety of drawing weapons within holy bounds. The burgomaster's servant, however, asserted that the youth married against his father's will and that justice demanded his removal to Rotterdam to await the Duke's sentence.

Margaret screamed in despair, and the lovers collapsed into each other's arms, weeping piteously. One of the constables, stepping forward under the guise of separation, whispered to Margaret, "Rotterdam? 'Tis a lie. We merely escort him to the Stadthouse." True to his word, Gerard was removed on horseback, subjected to a dozen halts and sly detours, until, just outside Tergou, he was placed in a crude canvas-covered cart. By five in the evening, he was clandestinely conveyed to the prison of the Stadthouse, thrust up several flights of stairs into a cramped cell illuminated only by a narrow window barred with vertical iron. The sole furniture was a massive oak chest. Imprisonment in that era was a swift highway to death—a combination of cold, solitude, torture, and starvation. Gerard, realizing he held the grasp of an implacable enemy, knelt to commend his soul to God, fearing he would never see daylight again.

Yet, hope sparked anew when he leaped to the iron bar, clutching it and pressing his knees against the wall to gaze outward. For a fleeting minute, he beheld Martin Wittenhaagen, quietly fishing in the brook beside the Stadthouse. Gerard whistled; Martin instantly abandoned his rod, turning with alacrity to acknowledge the prisoner before vanishing quickly. Gerard, comforted by the sight of his friend, waited, knowing they were not idle. Soon, the burgomaster, Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, appeared stealthily, bringing bread and water. Gerard's initial impulse was to strike the intruder with the iron bar, but Swieten's cough summoned three armed guards. "My orders," Swieten intoned, "are to detain you until you swear an oath to abandon Margaret Brandt and return to the Church." "Death sooner!" Gerard replied. Swieten bowed and withdrew.

Meanwhile, Martin hastened to Sevenbergen, finding Margaret agitated yet resolute, penning a letter to Countess Charolois accusing Swieten of treachery. Upon Martin's entrance, he revealed that he had located Swichten in the haunted tower atop the Stadthouse—a grim place where many had ascended only to descend feet foremost. He described seeing Gerard's face at a slit-like window, noting a pallor that suggested doom. Margaret, questioning whether Gerard looked anxious, was answered by Martin's observation that he appeared as bright as a pewter pot. "He counts on us," Margaret realized. She entrusted Martin with her letter to Rotterdam, but Peter, the elderly scholar, intervened. Having sat silently, observing the turmoil, he proposed a solution born of knowledge rather than wealth. "A Florentine knight," Peter recounted, "was imprisoned in a tower higher than Gerard's, yet his squire freed him using nothing but a bow and arrows."

Dismissing the notion of bribery, Peter outlined a plan relying on wit and dexterity. That same night, under a clear moon, a mysterious figure—Giles, the dwarf—emerged from the nursery, followed by his sister Kate. They had overheard Cornelis and Sybrandt discussing Gerard's incarceration and deduced that their father, currently in Rotterdam, was not responsible. Convinced of treachery, Kate urged Giles to accompany her to the foot of the haunted tower to comfort Gerard and assure him of his father's absence. Carrying a lantern, they ventured forth, the girl seeking courage against the rumored spirits.

Inside the cell, Gerard's hope waned as twilight faded, leaving him famished and despondent. Suddenly, a sharp strike echoed against the wall, followed by the clatter of an object at his feet. An arrow lay there, devoid of a head. Seizing it, Gerard lit a lamp with his tinder-box and discovered a skein of silk tied to the shaft, bearing a message: *"Well-beloved, make fast the silk to thy knife and lower to us: but hold thine end fast: then count an hundred and draw up."*

Seizing the oak chest, Gerard dragged it to the window, standing upon it to survey the scene below. Indistinct figures clustered at the base of the tower. He secured the silk to his knife, lowered it, and counted a hundred beats of his heart before pulling it up. It emerged heavy, revealing a stout whip-cord, which yielded further to a thicker rope as he hauled. The truth dawned on him: a great mass was descending, a "snake" emerging from the shadows. With a shout of joy, he hauled the rope until it reached his hand, threading it through the handles of the chest and securing it. Realizing the chest concealed a secret mechanism, he tested it by leaping upon it. The side burst open, spilling a cascade of parchments, but he quickly reinforced the ropes, bracing the iron bar against the window frame.

Mounting the chest, Gerard positioned himself astride, one leg inside, one outside, and began the perilous descent. The air was thin, the drop immense. He clung to the rope, his hands burning, sliding down stone after stone. Below, thirty feet beneath him, Margaret and Martin stretched their arms, their faces illuminated by the moon, mouths open in a silent plea. "Take care, Gerard!" they cried.
