The peat fire glowed soft and golden on the cottage hearth, gilding the edges of Eli’s ale tankard and the frayed cuffs of his two layabout sons, Cornelis and Sybrandt, who skulked idly in the shadowed corner, listless as lost hounds since they’d received word they believed confirmed Gerard’s death far from home.

Eli looked up when Catherine nudged his elbow. “Eli,” she said, sharp but not unkind, “answer me straight as a man, and I’ll ask no more of you today. What is wormwood?”

Caught off guard mid-sip, Eli blinked, fumbled for a moment for his wits, then offered a slow, sheepish reply: “Why, that’s something that tastes main bitter, is it not?”

“That’s a fair guess, my man, but not the answer I’m after,” Catherine said, tapping the hearthstone with her wooden shoe. “I’ll tell you what it is. Wormwood is having two grown men in this house doing nought but loitering, waiting for thy shoes and mine, waiting for the best friend they ever had’s coffin to be nailed shut, with no work and no purpose to fill their days. It warps folks worse than Nature ever meant them to be. Why not set them up with a trade somewhere, give them a fair chance to turn right?”

Eli groaned, the barb sinking deep. “I’d not be against it,” he said, “but I misdoubt they’d drink and gamble away the very shelves of their shop before the first week was out.”

“Nay, think me no fool,” Catherine huffed. “I’ll watch them close at the start, steer them with a loose rein as the saying goes, so they cannot slip off the path easy.”

“Where would you set ’em up? Not here, that would split our own custom to bits.”

“Not likely!” Catherine scoffed. “Rotterdam’s the place, against the whole world. I’ll go see them settled myself.” She would never admit, even to her own heart, that the real reason she chose Rotterdam was to be close to little Gerard, her lost grandson, the son of the son she still refused to believe was dead.

After a dozen long evenings of haggling and Cornelis and Sybrandt swearing eager, earnest promises of better behaviour, Catherine packed her bags for Rotterdam, and brought gentle, lame Kate along for a change of air. They found a small shopfront right on a busy merchant thoroughfare, shabby and long neglected, its walls streaked with grime and its shelves rotting through, so they haggled the rent down to a fraction of what it would have cost new. The two women set to work at once: scrubbing the floors until the wood shone, hewing sturdy new oak shelves for the shop, whitewashing the walls bright, and kitting out the tiny upstairs dwelling space with warm blankets and a crackling hearth of its own.

All the while, Luke Peterson, that broad, soft-hearted young man who adored Margaret, was forever pestering her to name some task he might do to lighten her heavy, grief-laden days. Again and again she shook her head, her voice soft and sad: “Nothing, Luke. Nothing at all.”

“What, a woman so clever and quick as you, cannot think of one small thing I might do to help you?” he would press, and each time she gave him the same quiet answer. Until at last, weary of his gentle importuning, she varied the reply: “If you could make something to help my sweet sister Kate get about easier, that would be a kindness.”

Luke’s face lit up like a sunrise. The slave to his quiet, unspoken love set to work that very day, and soon fashioned a little light wooden cart, lined it with soft wool cushions, fixed a sturdy leather harness to its front, and yoked himself to it. Each evening at twilight, he would pull Kate out of the city gates and along the tree-lined, flower-dusted boulevard that ran beside the canal, Margaret and Catherine walking slow at his side, little Gerard curled safe and warm in Kate’s lap. The sight of them might have looked idyllic to a stranger’s eye, but there was a quiet weight of unspoken grief hanging over the little party that no passerby could see.

Kate, for her part, enjoyed every moment of the ride: little Gerard was hers to dote on, his soft curls brushing her cheek, and the two of them looked for all the world like a pale white lily cupping a tiny, blushing rosebud, a cherub resting in the lap of a gentle little angel. The coarsest of the town folk jeered at the sight, though: they called out taunts, asked Luke how thistle meat tasted, mocked him that his mistress could not afford a four-legged donkey to pull the cart. Luke did not heed their jeers one bit, but Kate’s tender heart ached for him.

“Good Luke, you made this cart for me, and that is far more than I ever deserved,” she said one evening, her voice trembling. “It was wrong of me to let you pull me yourself. We can easily pay some poor hardworking soul to do that work. I love my rides, and I love holding little Gerard, but I would far rather never ride again than see you mocked for my sake.”

“Little do I care for their foolish tongues,” Luke laughed, his big shoulders rolling. “If their jibes bothered me, I’d knock every last one of their empty heads together. I’ll draw you along this road every evening until my mistress Margaret tells me to stop.”

“Luke, if you will do as Kate bids you, you will oblige me very much,” Margaret said softly, and Luke agreed at once, without a moment’s hesitation.

Only one soul among the onlookers spoke a kind word. That was Joan, wife of Jorian Ketel, the wise old market woman who called out to them as they passed: “That is strength well spent, not wasted! To draw the weak along where their own feet cannot carry them. Her prayers will be your reward, that lass – she carries her pain so gentle, with a smile always on her face, she will not be long in this world.”

Simple, single-minded Luke only shook his head at that, and called back: “I do not need the poor lass’s prayers! I do all this to please my mistress Margaret.”

After that, Luke kept on pestering Margaret week after week, begging her to name some other task he might do for her, and again and again she turned him away. Until one afternoon, he pressed her so hard that she finally turned on him, her eyes glistening, her voice thick with a longing she had kept locked in her breast for years, too deep for words to ever fully capture.

“Find me my boy’s father.”
