The thick, warm air of the monastery refectory reeked of spiced ale, crusty rye bread, and roasting game when talk rippled across Gerard’s table of Brother Werter, the scribe who had dropped dead suddenly only days before, leaving the work of transcribing the abbey’s ancient royal charter half finished. Gerard leaned forward, listening intently, before he spoke up timidly to offer his own services. The monks hesitated, and he misread their quiet doubt as hesitation to accept work for nothing. “Nay, I ask no hire, my lords,” he assured them. “I do this for love, a small token of thanks for the many safe nights your order has granted a poor wandering wayfarer like me.”
One older monk smiled approvingly, but gently pointed out that the late Brother Werter was a master penman, his flowing script nearly impossible for any amateur to match. Trepidatious, Gerard pulled a scrap of cleaned vellum from his wallet: the back of an old deed he’d polished smooth and filled with illuminated calligraphy as a practice sample on the road. The monk’s eyes went wide at first glance, and he hurried up the length of the hall to the high table, genuflecting just enough to brush the fifth and tenth steps as custom dictated, before handing the sample to the prior with a flurry of excited comments. A dozen pairs of knowing eyes fixed on the vellum at once, a low buzz of astonishment rippling out. Soon the first monk returned, grinning as proud as a peacock, carrying a spiced, gravied game crustade for Gerard, and a silver grace cup brimming with rich, honeyed piment wine. Gerard bowed low first to the distant prior, then to his own table, drank deep, and passed the cup to the rest of the company.
To his shock, the whole table cheered him at once, calling him a fellow convent-bred lad. “You cannot hide it!” they laughed. When Gerard blinked and asked how they had guessed, two monks spoke at once, pointing out he had lifted the grace cup to his lips with both hands, an old lifelong habit he had never unlearned.
For some time, the far end of the refectory had been loud with rowdy banter, before a burst of unruly, prolonged laughter rang out so loud the prior dispatched the sub-prior at once to march down the hall and dole out penance to every last monk at that table. Gerard’s face burned hot with shame: he had picked out Denys of Burgundy’s booming trumpet tone dead center of the ruckus, and the rough shout of “Courage!” that had cut through the din.
By mid-afternoon, Gerard was settled in old Werter’s empty cell, supplied with beeswax tapers, a tiltable writing frame that could be adjusted to any angle, and every ink, pigment, and sheet of vellum a scribe could ask for. The charter work was far too great for one evening, and Gerard fretted over how to ask his openly monk-hating friend Denys to linger a day longer. To his great surprise, Denys agreed graciously. “A day’s rest will do neither of us harm,” he shrugged. “Write thou, and I will find some way to pass the time.”
By the time Gerard finished the transcription, the whole monastery sang his praises: they openly agreed the finished charter was far more beautiful than any work the late Werter could have produced. The sub-prior pressed a silver rix-dollar into his palm, plus a handful of fine sable brushes and rare imported pigments pulled from the abbey’s vast, well-stocked scriptorium stores. They set out on the road again soon after, Gerard’s chest light with hope: this was a good omen, the pen he relied on to make his fortune and win back his beloved Margaret had already proven its worth. He nudged Denys playfully, holding up his new gifts. “Do try to be fairer to these good men,” he said. “To be plain with you, it chilled my friendship for you a little when I heard you call my benefactors hypocrites.”
Denys laughed, holding up his hands. “I recant. I was a scurrilous vagabond, no more. We soldiers are rude and hasty, that is all. I give myself the lie, and offer all these men I misjudged my full esteem. It is unjust to slander thousands for the hypocrisy of a few.”
Gerard preened, happy to be proven right, and leaned in to hear exactly what had changed Denys’s mind.
Denys told the tale gaily: the first night at dinner, a broad-shouldered young monk next to him had laughed out loud, bright and unforced, and Denys had thought at last he had found a man, not a shorn ape. He clapped the monk on the back and delivered his usual crude soldier’s jest, only for the monk to look as deadpan sorrowful as King Solomon, and point out that war obviously did nothing to sharpen wits. “What are monks for but to fight the foul fiend?” he had said. “If the fiend is dead, the friars are sped. We will have nothing left to do but turn soldiers, and bring him back to life.” The whole table roared at Denys’s expense, and he hit it off with the lot of them at once.
After dinner, they led him to a private cell instead of the crowded shared dormitory, strictly forbidding him from sleeping. They handed him a bound book of ribald illustrations depicting monks chasing lay women, then marched him barefoot down to a private parlour where a feast fit for a duke was laid out, attended by a dozen of the jolliest, rowdiest monks Denys had ever met, even on campaign. They swapped war stories, toasted, jested, and played cards with a gilded deck where Saint Theresa and Saint Catherine stood in for queens, and the four orders of friars were the knaves. They bet their very rosaries, swearing like troopers when they lost. Around midnight, one sly monk snuck out, and the rest followed him quiet as cats to the garden, to watch him pull a rope ladder from the ivy and scramble up the ten-foot abbey wall—only for a girl in a russet kirtle to pop up on the other side just as fast, the two of them clinging to each other to kiss and cuddle on the wall’s top. Denys mewed like a cat at them, the rest of the monks joined in the caterwauling chorus, and the girl shrieked in surprise. The monk on the wall yelled back that they would pay for it, he would dress every last one of them in a fardingale the next night. The whole lot of them danced in a circle mewing, gave the couple on the wall a mock solemn benediction, and trundled back to the parlour to find two of their brothers dead drunk on the table, two more blubbering over old drinking tales. They hauled the unconscious men off to their cells, dragged the crying ones after, and kicked every last one through their cell doors to sleep it off.
Gerard groaned in disgust as Denys cackled through the tale. The next day, Denys went on, he and the young monks drained the fish ponds, stashing away huge piles of pike, carp, tench, and eel to eat in secret. That night, they led him barefoot down twisted dark paths to the chapel, then down cold stone steps into the crypt under the chapel floor, and suddenly the whole place blazed with light, like paradise itself had opened up.
“The holy fathers retire down there to pray,” Gerard put in, shocked.
“Not that night,” Denys snickered. “Dozens of beeswax candles lit, princely cheer laid out: fifteen ‘Lenten’ soups all secretly laced with venison, grouse, and hare, twenty different artfully cooked fish for the Friday fast. Every monk seated between two buxom local girls, every girl between two hooded lads—except me. I had to court through a translator, who definitely stole three quarters of my lines for himself. The wine flagons were carved to look like breviaries, a monk plucked at a cittern and sang ribald little ballads that turned the girls’ cheeks bright red at first, before they laughed and settled right in.”
Gerard exploded. “Miserable wretches! Corrupters of youth! Perverters of innocence! But for you being there, Denys, who has been taught no better, I wish the whole church had fallen on the entire gang. Impious, abominable hypocrites!”
“Hypocrites?” Denys echoed, genuinely confused. “That is what I called them before I knew them, and you withstood me. Nay, they are sinners—all good fellows are, that—but by Saint Denys’s helmeted skull, they are no hypocrites. No pretense about them at all, just right jolly roaring blades.”
Gerard leaned in, his voice dropping to a spooky, hushed whisper. “Denys, you little know the peril you ran that night. That crypt is haunted. I had it from one of the elder monks. The dead walk there, their light feet pattering soft over the cold stones. Heavenly, unearthly sounds drift out of those vaults at midnight: voices of the dead holding ghostly communion that chill the listener to the bone, chords played by no mortal finger, swept by no mortal hand, ringing faint as echoes deep among the tombs.”
Denys’s face went pale. “Misericorde! If I had known that, no team of mules and wain ropes would have hauled me down there—though,” he sighed, “I would have missed the merriest time of my whole life.”
Before they could argue further, a bearded lay brother from the abbey came spurring up on a mule, waving a scrap of vellum. It was the old lost deed between Ghysbrecht and Floris Brandt, Gerard’s precious memento of home. Gerard turned pale with relief, so overjoyed he not only pressed a coin into the brother’s hand, he leaned down and kissed the mule’s soft nose. He pulled out a needle and thread, sewed the precious deed to the lining of his doublet with neat, deft, almost feminine stitches, his heart and mind drifting all the way back to Sevenbergen, to Margaret.
They kept walking, soon crossing over into Burgundy. Denys was in high spirits, doffing his cap to every woman they passed, firing off his crude soldier’s greeting to every man, before finally leading Gerard to the long-promised inn: Les Trois Poissons, The Three Fishes. “It is small, but I have known it for years, it is a good one,” he said, squinting at the huge new inscription painted across the whole front of the building, sounding the letters out slowly before puffing out his chest. “Ah, there we go: ‘Ici on loge a pied et a cheval’—Here we lodge travelers on foot and on horse.”
Gerard leaned over to read the actual words, and snorted. The full inscription ran: *ON NE LOGE CEANS A CREDIT; CE BONHOMME EST MORT, LES MAUVAIS PAIEURS L'ONT TUE*. No credit given here; the good old innkeeper died, and his bad-paying customers killed him.
