I slipped out of the main street as one might draw a hand from a hot pan—quickly, without thinking—and turned into a narrow alley where the moon lay like spilled milk from wall to wall. The stones underfoot held the day’s chill; the air smelt of damp lime, old soot, and a faint metallic tang as if the city itself had been scraping at buried treasure for too many lifetimes. Wind worried the roofs overhead and combed the alley with a sound like dry reeds shaken in a hollow gourd—an unsteady, rasping hush that made the silence behind it feel watched.

Halfway down I found a small archway set back from the moonlight, no bigger than a pantry door, and stepped into its mouth for shelter. The shadow there was close and cool. I had barely stopped and looked out again when a woman came gliding in after me as if she had been poured from the same darkness. She turned, trembling, and stared into the alley as I did—both of us holding our breath, listening.

In that listening the city showed its true nature. Not the proud front of tall houses, but the hollow between them: the wind’s fretful scrape, the distant clink of something hard on stone far below, as if men were still down in cellars tapping at their wealth. No birds. No dogs. No common household noise that makes a place human—no pot-lids, no children’s shouts, no broom’s whisk on a threshold. Only the wind, and then—soft, padded speed.

A huge leopard flashed across the opening of the archway, white as bone in the moonlight and dappled with dark blots. It did not so much run as cut the air, a living knife. The woman pressed close to me with the instinct of one who has been hunted often, and my heart filled with pity at the smallness of her against such a creature.

I put my arm round her shoulders—not as a gallant, but as one steadies a person who is about to faint. “If the brute comes here,” I said, “I will lay hold of it, and you must run.”

“Thank you,” she murmured, her voice thin with fear.

“Have you ever seen it before?” I asked, keeping my eyes on the slit of moonlit alley.

“Several times,” she answered, still shaking. “She is a pet of the princess’s. You are a stranger, or you would know her!”

“I am a stranger,” I said. Even the word sounded loud in that place, as if the stones themselves disliked it. “But is she, then, allowed to run loose?”

“She is kept in a cage,” the woman said, her words rushing out as if speech were the only thing between her and the leopard’s teeth, “her mouth muzzled, and her feet in gloves of crocodile leather. Chained she is too; but she gets out often—and sucks the blood of any child she can lay hold of.” Her breath hitched. “Happily there are not many mothers in Bulika!”

On that she broke into tears—silent at first, then a sob that seemed to have been stored up and salted like meat against famine. “I wish I were at home,” she wept. “The princess returned only last night, and there is the leopardess out already! How am I to get into the house? It is me she is after, I know! She will be lying at my own door, watching for me!” Her hands fluttered, then clenched. “But I am a fool to talk to a stranger!”

“All strangers are not bad,” I said. The wind shoved at the alley again, making the archway sing faintly like a pipe with a cracked lip. “The beast shall not touch you till she has done with me, and by that time you will be in. You are happy to have a house to go to! What a terrible wind it is!”

“Take me home safe,” she said, swallowing her sobs as if she had learned in this city to swallow everything, “and I will give you shelter from it. But we must wait a little.”

So we waited in that cramped hollow, shoulder against shoulder, and while the moonlight lay in a bright strip beyond us like a tablecloth laid out for a feast no one meant to share, I asked her questions. Talking kept our minds from the padding in the dark; it also filled the empty air with human sound, which in Bulika felt like a kind of defiance.

She told me her people did nothing—nothing—except dig in their cellars for precious stones. The image came to me vividly: families not gathered around bread and stew, but bent under the earth, fingers black with grit, nails split, lanterns smoking in the stale air as they chipped and sifted, listening for the small, satisfying click when a gem struck metal. And above them, kitchens kept cold, hearths barely used, the domestic rituals that carry a language and a people from one generation to the next allowed to starve.

“They are rich,” she said, “and have everything made for them in other towns.”

“Why?” I asked.

“Because it is a disgrace to work,” she replied, as if reciting a proverb every child in Bulika learned before he learned his mother’s lullaby. “Everybody in Bulika knows that!”

I tried to fit such a belief into my mind as one might try to fit a stone into a loaf—hard, wrong-shaped, splitting what should hold together. “How are you rich if none of you earn money?”

“Our ancestors saved for us,” she said. “We never spend. When we want money we sell a few of our gems.”

“But there must be some poor,” I persisted.

“I suppose there must be,” she answered with a shrug that made her shawl rasp softly, “but we never think of such people. When one goes poor, we forget him. That is how we keep rich. We mean to be rich always.”

The wind hissed down the alley, catching on corners like water on stones. There are places where sound carries memory—where the songs and the daily clatter of living preserve names and meanings. Here the very air seemed trained to erase. Forget him, she had said, as simply as one says, throw out the peel.

“But when you have dug up all your precious stones and sold them,” I said, “you will have to spend your money, and one day you will have none left!”

“We have so many,” she returned, “and there are so many still in the ground, that that day will never come.”

“And suppose a strange people were to fall upon you,” I said, “and take everything you have?”

“No strange people will dare,” she answered quickly. “They are all horribly afraid of our princess. She it is who keeps us safe and free and rich!”

As she spoke she would break off, listening, and peer behind her into the alley as if expecting the white shape to pour itself out of the moonlight again. Each pause sharpened my hearing too; the city’s soundscape was all edges—wind, far-off knocks, and now and then the ghost of a soft tread that might have been only my imagination answering her fear.

I asked why her people hated strangers so. The question itself seemed to make the air tighter.

“The presence of a stranger defiles the city,” she said, and the word defiles fell from her lips like ash.

“How is that?” I asked.

“Because we are more ancient and noble than any other nation,” she replied, with the brittle pride of someone repeating a lesson drilled in by those who profit from it. “Therefore we always turn strangers out before night.” Then, as if remembering herself, she added in a lower voice, “We must. We do.”

“How, then, can you take me into your house?” I asked.

“I will make an exception of you,” she said, and there was a strange mingling in her tone—fear, rebellion, and the habit of obedience like a string tied around the heart.

“Is there no place in the city for the taking in of strangers?”

“Such a place would be pulled down,” she answered, “and its owner burned.” She said it without drama, as if speaking of a recipe that everyone knew by heart. “How is purity to be preserved except by keeping low people at a proper distance? Dignity is such a delicate thing!”

Delicate—like a language that dies when it is not spoken at the table, like a song that cannot survive the replacement of voices with decrees. In Bulika even the words for kindness and welcome seemed to have been driven underground with the gems.

She went on to tell me of their princess—how she had reigned for thousands of years; how she had power over the air and the water as well as the earth, and, she believed, over the fire too; how she could do what she pleased and was answerable to nobody. The city around us, with its narrow lanes and closed doors, felt shaped by that thought: a long tyranny made ordinary, like smoke that has so long filled a room the inhabitants no longer smell it.

At length she judged the moment fit—whether because she heard nothing, or because courage had finally boiled up in her despite fear—and we set out. We moved through lanes and narrow passages that twisted like the corridors of a cellar. The wind followed, sometimes funneling into a shriek, sometimes dropping into a deep, uneasy moan. Not once did we meet a living creature. No footfall echoed ours. No voice called. The emptiness itself had a sound: the soft absence where life should have been.

Her door was in a wider street between two tall houses that rose like cliffs. A narrow, steep stair climbed to it, and she began to mount, slowly at first, her hand on the wall as if she could feel, through stone, whether danger lurked. I followed close.

Before we reached the top, she seemed suddenly to take fright, as if some scent had reached her—cold, animal, familiar. She darted up the remaining steps with the desperate speed of someone snatching a pot from a flame.

I reached the landing just in time to have the door closed in my face.

For a moment I stood there, confounded, the wind pushing at my coat, the moonlight whitening the street below. The landing was barely long enough, between the opposite doors of the two houses, for a man to lie down. Weary—too weary to care that Bulika counted my presence a stain—I sank into what shelter there was, poor as it was, and let the city’s thin, scraping wind sing over me while behind the shut door her hurried breathing vanished into the hush of a house that would not admit a stranger.
