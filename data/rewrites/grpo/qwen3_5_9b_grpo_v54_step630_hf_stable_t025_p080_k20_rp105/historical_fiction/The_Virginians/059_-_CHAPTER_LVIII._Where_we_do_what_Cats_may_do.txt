Our Virginians, displaying a wisdom rare in youth, had embraced strong Jacobite sentiments, professing a profound devotion to the exiled royal family. The banished prince had recognized Madam Esmond's father as Marquis of Esmond, and she felt no ill will toward a lineage so eager to honor her house. Scandals regarding her sister, Madame de Bernstein, and the Old Chevalier were dismissed with scorn; she maintained with perfect truth that the first two monarchs of the House of Hanover were no more virtuous in domestic matters than any Stuart. Yet, the king *de facto* was king, crowned and anointed, having decisively defeated the king *de jure* in battle. In her clear judgment, her sons owed it to themselves and the sovereign to appear at his court. If the King offered a lucrative post or a ribbon of blue or red, she would not be surprised. She was certain the King knew the Virginian Esmonds as well as any nobleman. Thus, the lads were commanded to present themselves at court. Had their mother known that George had taken Harry's laced coat for the day he bowed at Kensington, she might have been angry, but she was absent from the scene.

A century prior, the King's drawing-room was open daily to his nobility and gentry. Loyalty, especially since the war began, found satisfaction a dozen times a month in the august sight of the sovereign. A prudent avoidance of enemy ships and a gracious acknowledgment of the potential loss of the royal person at sea caused the monarch to forego visits to his beloved Hanover, compelling him to remain, albeit unwillingly, among his loving Britons. A Hanoverian lady, whose virtues endeared her to the prince, strove to console him for his enforced absence from Herrenhausen. Through the Countess of Walmoden, the revered Defender of the Faith could hear the accents of his native home.

Mr. Warrington requested his uncle, an assiduous courtier, to present him. Since Mr. Lambert also needed to attend court to thank the King for his promotion, the two gentlemen traveled to Kensington together, hiring a hackney-coach as my Lord Wrotham's carriage was required by its rightful owner, who had returned to town. They alighted at the Kensington Palace Gate, where sentries saluted the good General, and proceeded on foot to the summer residence. Walking under the portico, they entered the gallery leading to the great black marble staircase, richly decorated by Mr. Kent, passing through rooms hung with tapestry and adorned with pictures and busts until they reached the King's great drawing-room. Here, amidst masterpieces including Titian's "Venus" and Rubens' "St. Francis adoring the infant Saviour," the gentlemen waited for the King to issue from his private apartments, where he was in conference with his ministers.

George Warrington, never having seen a palace before, had leisure to admire the surroundings and the people. He beheld fine pictures for the first time, delighting in Sir Anthony Van Dyck's portrait of King Charles the First and his family, and Tintoret's "Esther before Ahasuerus," where figures wore magnificent Venetian attire. So enraptured was he by these works that he scarce heard his friend the General whispering the names of the surrounding personages.

"Yonder," said Mr. Lambert, "are two Lords of the Admiralty, Mr. Gilbert Elliot and Admiral Boscawen—the Boscawen whose fleet fired the first gun in your waters two years ago. That stout gentleman belted with gold is Mr. Fox, former Minister, now Paymaster with a great salary."

"He carries the *auri fames* on his person. Why, his waistcoat is a perfect Potosi!" exclaimed George.

*"Aliena appetens"—how goes the text? He loves to get money and to spend it,* continued General Lambert. *That is my Lord Chief Justice Willes, talking to my Lord of Salisbury, Doctor Headley, who, if he serves his God as he serves his King, will be translated to high promotion in Heaven. He belongs to your grandfather's time, loved by Dick Steele and hated by the Dean. With them is my Lord of London, the learned Doctor Sherlock. My lords of the lawn sleeves have lost half their honors now. I remember when I was a boy in my mother's hand, she made me kneel to the Bishop of Rochester; him who went over the water to become Minister to a nameless Perkin. That handsome fair man is Admiral Smith, president of poor Byng's court-martial, who strove in vain to save him; 'Tom of Ten Thousand' they call him in the fleet. The French Ambassador had him broken when he was a lieutenant for making a French man-of-war lower topsails to him, yet the King made Tom a captain the next day. That tall, haughty-looking man is my Lord George Sackville; now that I am a Major-General myself, he will treat me somewhat better than a footman. I wish my stout old Blakeney were here; he is the soldier's darling, as kind and brave as yonder poker of a nobleman is brave. I am your lordship's very humble servant. This is a young gentleman just from America, involved in Braddock's sad business two years ago."*

"Oh, indeed!" said the poker of a nobleman. "I have the honor of speaking to Mr.—?"

"To Major-General Lambert, at your lordship's service, and who served in his Majesty's army before you entered it. That, Mr. Warrington, is the first commoner in England, Mr. Speaker Onslow. Where is your uncle? I shall have to present you myself if Sir Miles delays." As he spoke, the General addressed himself entirely to his young friend, ignoring his colleague, who stalked away with a scared look, amazed at the other's audacity. A hundred years ago, a nobleman was a nobleman, expected to be admired as such.

Sir Miles's red waistcoat appeared shortly, and many cordial greetings passed between him, his nephew, and General Lambert, for Sir Miles was the most affectionate of men. Was the General leaving my Lord Wrotham's house? It was time, as his lordship wished to occupy it. Very good; but consider what a loss for the neighbors!

"We miss you, we positively miss you, my dear General," cried Sir Miles. "My daughters were in love with those lovely young ladies—upon my word, they were; and my Lady Warrington and my girls debated endlessly how to make acquaintance with your charming family. We feel as if we were old friends already; indeed we do, General, if you permit me; and we love you, if I may speak frankly, on account of your friendship and kindness to our dear nephews: though we were a little jealous, I own a little jealous of them, because they went so often to see you. Often have I said to my Lady Warrington, 'My dear, why don't we make acquaintance with the General? Why don't we ask him and his ladies to dine with some other plain country gentlefolks?' Carry my most sincere respects to Mrs. Lambert, I pray, sir; and thank her for her goodness to these young gentlemen. My own flesh and blood, sir; my dear, dear brother's boys!" He passed his hand across his manly eyes, choking almost with generous emotion.

While they discoursed—George Warrington restraining his laughter with admirable gravity—the door of the King's apartments opened, and pages entered, preceding his Majesty. He was followed by his burly son, the Duke, a very corpulent Prince with a coat and face of blazing scarlet. Behind them came various gentlemen and officers of state; among whom George immediately recognized the famous Mr. Secretary Pitt, by his tall stature, eagle eye, and beak, his grave and majestic presence. As I see that solemn figure passing, even a hundred years later, I protest I feel a present awe, a desire to take off my hat. I am not frightened at George the Second, nor dazzled by the portentous appearance of his Royal Highness the Duke of Culloden and Fontenoy; but the Great Commoner, the terrible Cornet of Horse! His figure bestrides our narrow isle of a century back like a Colossus; and I hush as he passes in his gouty shoes, his thunderbolt hand wrapped in flannel. Perhaps, as we see him issuing with dark looks from the royal closet, angry scenes have passed between him and his august master. He has been boring that old monarch for hours with prodigiously long speeches, full of eloquence and noble phrases on commonest topics, utterly repulsive to the little shrewd old gentleman "at whose feet he lays himself," who has the most thorough dislike for fine boody and fine brose! The sublime Minister passed solemnly through the crowd; the company ranged respectfully round the wall; and his Majesty walked round the circle, his royal son lagging a little behind, engaging select individuals in conversation.

The monarch was a little, keen, fresh-colored old man with very protruding eyes, attired in plain, old-fashioned, snuff-colored clothes and brown stockings, his only ornament the blue ribbon of the Order of the Garter. He spoke in a German accent, but with ease, shrewdness, and simplicity, addressing those he wished to notice or passing on with a bow. He knew Mr. Lambert well, who had served under his Majesty at Dettingen and with his royal son in Scotland, and congratulated him good-humoredly on his promotion.

"It is not always," his Majesty said, "that we can do as we like; but I was glad when, for once, I could give myself that pleasure in your case, General; for my army contains no better officer as you."

The veteran blushed and bowed, deeply gratified. Meanwhile, the Best of Monarchs looked at Sir Miles Warrington, whom he knew perfectly as the eager recipient of all favors, and at the young gentleman by his side.

"Who is this?" the Defender of the Faith asked, pointing toward George Warrington, who stood before his sovereign in a respectful attitude, clad in poor Harry's best embroidered suit.

With the deepest reverence, Sir Miles informed his King that the young gentleman was his nephew, Mr. George Warrington, of Virginia, who asked leave to pay his humble duty.

"This, then, is the other brother?" the Venerated Prince observed. "He came in time, else the other brother would have spent all the money. My Lord Bishop of Salisbury, why do you come out in this bitter weather? You had much better stay at home!" And with this, the revered wielder of Britannia's sceptre passed on to other lords and gentlemen. Sir Miles Warrington was deeply affected by the royal condescension. He clapped his nephew's hands. "God bless you, my boy," he cried; "I told you that you would see the greatest monarch and the finest gentleman in the world. Is he not so, my Lord Bishop?"

"That, that he is!" cried his lordship, clasping his ruffled hands and turning his fine eyes to the sky, "the best of princes and of men."

"That is Master Louis, my Lady Yarmouth's favorite nephew," said Lambert, pointing to a young gentleman surrounded by a crowd; and presently the stout Duke of Cumberland came up to their little group.

His Royal Highness held out his hand to his old companion-in-arms. "Congratulate you on your promotion, Lambert," he said good-naturedly. Sir Miles Warrington's eyes were ready to burst with rapture.

"I owe it, sir, to your Royal Highness's good offices," said the grateful General.

"Not at all; not at all: ought to have had it a long time before. Always been a good officer; perhaps there'll be some employment for you soon. This is the gentleman whom James Wolfe introduced to me?"

"His brother, sir."

"Oh, the real Fortunate Youth! You were with poor Ned Braddock in America—a prisoner, and lucky enough to escape. Come and see me, sir, in Pall Mall. Bring him to my levee, Lambert." And the broad back of the Royal Prince turned to their friends.

"It is raining! You came on foot, General Lambert? You and George must come home in my coach. You must and shall come home with me, I say. By George, you must! I'll have no denial," cried the enthusiastic Baronet. He drove George and the General back to Hill Street, presenting the latter to my Lady Warrington and his darlings, Flora and Dora, and insisting they partake of a collation, as they must be hungry after their ride. "What, there is only cold mutton? Well, an old soldier can eat cold mutton. And a good glass of my Lady Warrington's own cordial, prepared with her own hands, will keep the cold wind out. Delicious cordial! Capital mutton! Our own, my dear General," said the hospitable Baronet, "our own from the country, six years old if a day. We keep a plain table; but all the Warringtons since the Conqueror have been remarkable for their love of mutton; and our meal may look a little scanty, and is, for we are plain people, and I am obliged to keep my rascals of servants on board-wages. Can't give them seven-year-old mutton, you know."

Sir Miles, in his nephew's presence, described George's reception at court in such flattering terms that George hardly knew himself, or the scene he had witnessed, or how to look his uncle in the face, or how to contradict him before his family amidst the astonishing narrative. Lambert sat by with open eyes. He, too, had been at Kensington, yet he had seen none of the wonders Sir Miles described.

"We are proud of you, dear George. We love you, my dear nephew—we all love you, we are all proud of you—"

"Yes; but I like Harry best," said a little voice.
