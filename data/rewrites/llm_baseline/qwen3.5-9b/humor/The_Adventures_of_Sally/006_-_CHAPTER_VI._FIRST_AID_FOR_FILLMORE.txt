It wasn't until the following Friday that Sally managed to depart for Detroit. By Saturday morning, she had arrived, driven straight to the Hotel Statler. After confirming that Gerald was staying there and phoning his room to summon him, she headed for the dining hall to order breakfast.

Waiting for the food to materialize, Sally felt rather low. Nursing Mr. Faucitt had drained her, and the train ride hadn't offered much sleep. Yet, the true culprit behind her gloom was Gerald's reception over the phone. He had sounded listless, as if her return after weeks apart was a triviality. Hurt and bewildered, she poured herself a second cup of coffee.

"Coffee does wonders," she muttered. "Men are always like this in the morning. Soon enough, Gerald will burst in, revitalized by a cold shower. Until then, there's food to be eaten."

Just then, a stout young man entered. Sally had glimpsed him earlier in the lobby and found him strikingly similar to her brother, Fillmore. Upon closer inspection, it was indeed Fillmore. Puzzled—she had assumed he lived permanently in New York—she called out to him. After scanning the room (notably avoiding looking right), he spotted her and approached.

"Why, Sally?" His demeanor was nervous, bordering on embarrassed. Sally immediately suspected a guilty conscience. Presumably, he was about to confess that he had gotten engaged without her sisterly blessing and was dreading the conversation. "What brings you here? I thought you were still in Europe."

"I returned a week ago but stayed nursing poor old Mr. Faucitt. He's been terribly ill. I'm here to see Mr. Foster's play, *The Primrose Way*. Is it a hit?"

"It hasn't opened yet."

"Don't be absurd, Fill. Pull yourself together. It opened last Monday."

"No, it didn't. Haven't you heard? All theaters are closed due to this infernal Spanish influenza. Nothing's played this week. You must have seen it in the papers."

"I haven't had time to read the papers. Oh, Fill, what a shame!"

"Yes, it's rough. It's making everyone on edge. I've had the hardest time imaginable."

"What does that have to do with you?" Fillmore coughed. "Well—I er—I forgot to mention. I'm sort of mixed up in the show. Cracknell—he was at college with me—suggested I come down to check it out. Might involve putting money in or something."

"I thought he had endless cash."

"He has plenty, but these fellows love to let a pal in on a good deal."

"Is it a good deal?"

"The play is excellent."

"That's what Mr. Faucitt said. But Mabel Hobson..."

Fillmore's round face showed clear distress. "She's dreadful, Sally! Can't act, and throws her weight around constantly. The other day there was a fuss about a paper-knife..."

"A paper-knife? How could that cause a fuss?"

"One of the props. Got misplaced. I'm sure it wasn't my fault..."

"How could it be yours?" Sally asked, genuinely baffled. Love seemed to cloud Fillmore's judgment.

"Well—you know how it is. An angry woman blames the first person she sees. This paper-knife..." His voice trailed off into painful silence.

"Mr. Faucitt said Elsa Doland was wonderful."

"Oh, she's fine," Fillmore dismissed. "But—" His face lit up. "The girl you need to see is Miss Winch. Gladys Winch. She plays the maid. Only appears in the first act, mostly saying 'Did you ring, madam?' But the way she says it! Sally, that girl is a genius! The finest character actress in a decade! Mark my words, soon her name will shine on Broadway. Personality? Ask me! Charm? She composed the lyrics and music! Looks?..."

"All right, all right! I know all about it, Fill. Now, please explain how you dared get engaged without consulting me?"

Fillmore blushed deeply. "Oh, do you know?"

"Yes. Mr. Faucitt told me."

"Well..."

"Well?"

"Well, I'm only human," Fillmore argued weakly.

"Splendid admission. You've become quite modest, Fill."

He had improved significantly since their last meeting. Someone had seemingly punctured his ego, releasing all his pomposity. If, as Mr. Faucitt hinted, this change was due to Miss Winch's influence, Sally approved of the romance.

"I'll introduce you sometime," Fillmore promised.

"I'd very much like to meet her."

"I must go now. I need to find Bunbury. I thought he might be here."

"Who's Bunbury?"

"The producer. Probably eating in his room. Better go up."

"You're busy, aren't you? Little marvel! Lucky they have you managing them."

Fillmore departed, leaving Sally to wait for Gerald, no longer stinging from his earlier coldness. *Poor Gerald,* she thought. *No wonder he seemed so upset.*

Minutes later, Gerald arrived.

"Oh, Jerry darling," Sally said as he reached the table, "I'm so sorry. I've just heard about it."

Gerald sat down. His appearance matched his voice: a nervous dullness draped over him like a heavy coat.

"My bad luck," he groaned. "Something that happens only to me. Dumb fools! Why shut theaters when there's influenza? People crowd stores all day; surely that spreads it more than a theater? Besides, it's all nonsense. I don't believe in Spanish influenza. People catch colds and think they're dying. It's a fake scare."

"I don't think it's that," Sally replied gently. "Mr. Faucitt suffered greatly. That's why I couldn't come sooner."

Gerald showed no interest in Mr. Faucitt's illness nor in Sally's delayed arrival. He stabbed somberly at his grapefruit.

"We've hung around here day after day, bored stiff. The company is falling apart. Sick of rehearsing endlessly when we don't know if we'll ever open. They were fired up a week ago; now they're sagging. It will ruin the play. Of course. My first chance, just thrown away."

Sally listened with a growing sense of desolation. Trying to be fair, she acknowledged his terrible disappointment and immense stress. Yet, self-pity was a trait she detested in men. Her own vanity was bruised; clearly, her arrival meant nothing to restore him. She couldn't ignore what Mr. Faucitt had said about Gerald. Never noticing before, she now saw he was remarkably self-centered, thrusting this fact upon her.

"That Hobson woman is causing trouble," Gerald continued, poking despairingly at his scrambled eggs. "She shouldn't have the part. Can't handle it. Elsa Doland could play it a thousand times better. I wrote a few lines for Elsa recently, and Hobson flew into a rage. You don't know what a star is until you see one of these promoted Follies props trying to be one. Took me an hour to talk her down from quitting."

"Why not let her quit?"

"For heaven's sake, talk sense!" Gerald snapped querulously. "Do you think Cracknell would keep the show running if she weren't in it? He'd close it instantly, and where would I be? You don't realize this is a huge opportunity for me. I'd look foolish throwing it away."

"I see," Sally said briefly. She had never felt so wretched. Foreign travel, she concluded, was a mistake. Pleasant and broadening perhaps, but it severed ties with reality upon return. Analyzing her feelings, she realized she resented Gerald attempting to hold two attitudes simultaneously. A troubled man is either a stoic captain of his soul or a broken vessel needing comfort. Gerald, it seemed, wanted her sympathy while erecting a wall against it. She felt shut out and useless.

"By the way," Gerald added, "I have to keep her cheerful all the time, so for goodness' sake, don't let slip that we're engaged."

Sally's chin jerked upward. Too much.

"If being engaged to me is a handicap..."

"Don't be silly." Gerald retreated into pathos. "Good God! It's tough! Here I am, worried to death, and you..."

Before he finished, Sally underwent one of her rapid mood shifts. A simple, comforting thought altered her perspective entirely. Tired and gritty from the train, she decided the world's disarray stemmed solely from her lack of a bath and messy hair. Suddenly tranquil, she reasoned that if her grubbiness made Gerald seem distant, all was well. Placing her hand on his with a gesture of contrition, she said, "I'm so sorry. I've been a brute, but I truly sympathize."

"I've had a terrible time," Gerald mumbled.

"I know, I know. But you never said you were glad to see me."

"Of course I am."

"Then why didn't you say so, you poor fish? And why didn't you ask if I enjoyed Europe?"

"Did you enjoy it?"

"Yes, except missing you so much. There! Now my lecture on foreign travel is done, and you can tell me your troubles."
