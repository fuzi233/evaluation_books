In which we both fight and run away

What good was a Colonel without a regiment? The Governor and Council, who had once paraded their gratitude when they gave me my commission, were long gone—fleeing aboard ships, leaving behind only scattered acts of arson and piracy along the coast. My Lord Dunmore’s black allies drove off his own people; and worse still, we heard he had sent envoys to the southern tribes, urging them to descend upon us in force and teach our colonists “good behaviour” with tomahawks. “And these are to be our allies?” I said to my mother, exchanging a look heavy with dread, remembering with chilling clarity the savage whose face loomed over mine on Braddock’s Field, his knife at my throat—until Florac struck him down.

We fortified Castlewood as best we could, but truth be told, we feared less the rebels than the Indians and the enslaved men now armed by the Crown. I never saw my mother lose her courage—except once, when she recalled how our father had been killed in an Indian raid more than forty years before. One night, seeing shadows moving near the house, nothing could calm her; she fell to her knees, crying, “The Lord have mercy upon us! The Indians—the Indians!”

Lord Dunmore’s black troops vanished with his fleet, seeking plunder or pay elsewhere. But the painted warriors from the south never came. Still, I looked at my mother’s silver hair, my wife’s dark curls, and our child’s golden ringlets—and felt a cold fear that they might become victims of this brutal war. And it was we who had raised such forces, enlisted such allies! Though I dare not claim to interpret divine will—nor point out Heaven’s hand in every turn—I would say that using Indians and German mercenaries brought its own retribution. On the field, when the mercenaries faced the Provincials, they broke. Their defeat lifted the spirits of the Continental army. And the murder of one woman—Miss McCrea—by a handful of drunken Indians did more harm to the Royal cause than any lost battle or destroyed regiment.

Once the panic of the Indians passed, Madam Esmond regained her composure—but she grew deeply anxious about the danger I ran, and the risk I placed upon others by staying in Virginia.

“What can they do to me?” she said, “a poor old woman? If I have one son, a colonel without a regiment, I have another leading two hundred Continentals under Washington. If the Royalists come, they’ll spare me for your sake. If the rebels appear, I’ll have Harry’s passport. I don’t want—no, I won’t allow—that your delicate wife and this dear little baby should stay here, only increasing the danger for all of us! We must send them to Boston or New York. Don’t talk about defending me! Who would hurt a harmless old woman? If the rebels come, I’ll hide behind Mrs. Fanny’s petticoats. I’ll be far safer without you in the house than with you.” She spoke partly because it made sense—but mostly because she wanted me and my family out of harm’s way. And though danger threatened, she herself was determined to remain in the land where her father was buried and where she was born. She lived backward, so to speak. She had seen the new generation, blessed them, and bid them farewell. She belonged to the past—to old days and memories.

While we debated sending our family north, news arrived: the British had evacuated Boston entirely. They had never dared attack Washington’s camp at Cambridge—though he lay there for months without powder, at our mercy—waiting until he secured ammunition and seized Dorchester Heights, which commanded the city. Then the whole British army and colony were forced into retreat. Yes, the King’s troops won at Bunker Hill—as surely as they defeated the French at Blenheim. Yet after that, their commanders seemed afraid to attack entrenched Continentals. Why, then, from July to March, hesitate against an almost defenseless enemy? Why delay at Long Island, when the Continental army was within our grasp? Why the astonishing timidity of Howe before Valley Forge—where starving, sick, ragged soldiers barely manned the lines, yet held them against a great, well-equipped army?

As the fortunes of the two sides rose and fell, so too did the tone of their supporters. When word reached us in the countryside of Boston’s evacuation, every Whig in the neighborhood bowed to Madam Esmond and urged her to surrender quickly. She no longer displayed her loyalty as openly as before—no longer flew her flag in defiance—but she never wavered. Each morning and evening, Hagan prayed for the Royal Family in our home. On Sundays, neighbors were welcome to attend service, where my mother served as clerk, and the prayer for the High Court of Parliament under our gracious and devout King was delivered with solemn conviction. Brave Hagan was a parson without a living—just as I was a militia colonel without a regiment. He had continued praying for King George in Williamsburg long after the Governor fled. But one Sunday, when he came to church to perform his duty, he found a corporal’s guard at the door. They told him the Committee of Safety had appointed another minister, and advised him to keep quiet. “Lead me before their chiefs,” he replied—our honest friend always loved grand words and dramatic gestures. So he was marched through the streets, followed by a crowd of white and black ruffians, to the Capitol. There he met Messrs. Henry and the new state officials, confronting the robbers, as he called them, in their den. Of course, he meant to deliver a heroic speech (like many men who would gladly be martyrs, if only they could be roasted before a crowd), but Mr. Henry refused him the chance. After making Hagan wait hours in an anteroom with negroes, the divine entered the magistrate’s chamber with unshaken dignity, beginning: “Sir, by what authority am I, a minister of the—” “Mr. Hagan,” interrupted the other, “I’m too busy to listen to speeches. As for King George, he has no more authority here than King Nebuchadnezzar. Remember that—and hold your tongue. Stick to King John and King Macbeth. If you’d like to send round benefit tickets, the entire Assembly will come to hear you. Have you ever seen Mr. Hagan on the stage when you were in London, General?” Saying this, Henry turned to General Lee, second-in-command to Washington, who had just arrived in Virginia. Shamed and red-faced, Hagan was hurried out of the room, nearly weeping. After that, we decided his services were better kept in the country, removed from the restless flock in the city.

The appointment of Virginians to the highest civil and military offices in the new government flattered and tempted many leaders who, otherwise, might have stayed loyal to the Crown. But even though we Loyalists were silenced, the Capitol was in Whig hands, and our so-called levies of loyal recruits were often little more than Falstaff’s troops—empty shells—we still maintained secret communication across the colony and beyond. Though we did not rise in open revolt, though many fled, and though some, under pressure from committees and justices, feigned Republicanism and cursed kings and nobles—we knew each other well. Depending on how favorable the tide turned, we hid our colors, revealed them, half showed them, or even pretended to abandon them altogether, shouting “Down with King George!” Our enslaved servants carried messages and tokens between homes. Countless covert plots stirred among those who could not afford open rebellion. After battle, the neutrals rushed to join the victors, cheering as loudly as the patriots. The runaways were never counted. Will anyone tell me the signers of the Declaration of Independence were not a minority—and that a minority won?

We knew that part of the defeated Massachusetts army was preparing a major southern expedition, and I, eager for action, made contact with the ex-Governor Martin of North Carolina. I planned to join him, along with several fellow Virginian officers—officers of a corps that lacked only privates. We did not make a secret of our departure from Castlewood. Congress’s affairs were not yet strong enough to persecute those of doubtful allegiance. Gentlemen’s houses remained open; in our southern way, we visited friends for months at a time. My wife and I, with our infant and a small retinue of servants, took leave of Madam Esmond on a visit to a neighboring plantation. From there we moved to another friend’s house, then another, until we reached Wilmington, North Carolina—the place where we expected to meet reinforcements.

Before our arrival, the Carolinian Royalists had already shown strength. Their clashes with the Whigs had been unfortunate. The Highlanders, fighting for King George, fared no better than they had when they fought against him in Scotland. We did not reach Wilmington until late May, by which time Admiral Parker’s squadron had arrived, carrying General Clinton and five British regiments, destined for a landing on Charleston.

General Clinton, whom I approached immediately, received us grimly—my “regiment” consisting of Lady Warrington, our infant she was nursing, and three black servants. But Captain Horner of the frigate Sphinx, who had served in Jamaica and known our beloved Governor well, heard that my wife was General Lambert’s daughter and welcomed her warmly, offering his finest cabin. Thus, we became refugees—like Lord Dunmore—having waved our flag, tucked it away, and slipped out the back door. From Wilmington, we sailed swiftly to Charleston. During the voyage and our delay in the river before the assault, I grew acquainted with Mr. Clinton, and our friendship deepened.

It was the King’s birthday when we appeared in the harbor. We declared it a glorious day to begin the expedition.

But it did not begin for several days, and I omit, intentionally, all descriptions of Penelope bidding farewell to Hector, setting forth on this campaign. First, Hector is perfectly well—though slightly afflicted with gout—and no rogue Pyrrhus has taken his widow. In times of war, such scenes of sorrow and separation occur every hour. I still see her gentle face above the ship’s bulwark as we descended into the boats, and the smile of the infant in her arms. How quaint these old tales sound! Captain Miles, lacking poetic taste, may have forgotten the verses in Mr. Pope’s Homer describing your parting from your heroic father—but your mother read them to you as a boy, and keeps the gorget I wore that day tucked away in her dressing box.

My second attempt at battle proved no more successful than my first. That evening, we returned to our ships thoroughly defeated. The reckless Lee—whom Clinton had faced at Boston—now met him at Charleston. Lee and the gallant garrison put up a brilliant and victorious resistance. The fort on Sullivan’s Island was a nut we could not crack. Our frigates’ fire failed to break its shell; the path to the assault was impassable—too deep for wading, as Sir Henry discovered, leading the charge himself. Death by shot, drowning, or cold—these I had faced before returning to my wife. Our frigate grounded briefly, struggled free, and was cannonaded by the enemy until clear.

Amid this misfortune, a small incident sparked a lasting bond between me and Sir Harry Clinton—a man I came to admire deeply throughout the war. Though opinions vary on his leadership, I dare not criticize one I respect so fully. His personal courage was undeniable—he was always eager to show it. Whether at Breed’s Hill, where he led the rallied troops to carry the Continental lines; here before Sullivan’s Fort; or later at Fort Washington, where he charged with standard in hand and entered the fort at the head of the storming column—Clinton was always foremost in battle. No soldier in the King’s service was more admirable.

We were wading toward the fort, aiming to force a column through a path our guns had cleared, when a shot struck a boat beside ours—so precisely that three-quarters of its crew were disabled, the officer steering knocked down, and the flagbearer fallen. I could not help exclaiming, “Bravo! Well aimed!” For no ninepins ever fell more helplessly than those poor men before the round shot. Then the General turned to me, saying grimly, “Sir, the enemy’s conduct seems to please you?” “I am pleased, sir,” I replied, “that my countrymen yonder fight as becomes our nation.” We struggled forward, still under fire from both small arms and artillery, until the water rose to our chests and deepened with each step. We were forced to return to our boats and pull away.

Afterward, Sir Henry visited Lady Warrington aboard the Sphinx. He was kind and witty, joking about the humble author of these memoirs, whom he described as a rebel at heart. I pray my children may never live through—or take part in—such revolutions as the one raging in France. Save a few, the players in such tragedies bear close scrutiny poorly. The leaders are often mere quacks; heroes, ignoble puppets; heroines, anything but pure. Victory does not always go to the brave. In our revolution, it did—once, miraculously—to the most deserving. But who truly knows his enemies now? His greatest triumphs were not in rare battles where he gained slight advantage—but over Congress, over hunger and disease, over lukewarm allies and smiling foes within his own ranks. When the war ended, and the great leaders began quarreling before the public—what accusations, counter-accusations, excuses! This fleet arrived too late; that regiment misunderstood orders; these cannonballs wouldn’t fit those guns—and so on. Here was a general who beat us with no shots, no powder, no money—and never thought of a convention. His courage never surrendered. Through doubt, darkness, danger, and tempest, I believe it was only the American leader’s indomitable spirit that remained steady.

Of course, our Charleston expedition was hailed as a great victory by the enemy, who learned—perhaps from their parents—to cry “victory” if a corporal’s guard was surprised, as loudly as if we had won a full-scale battle. Mr. Lee rushed back to New York, the conqueror of conquerors, proclaiming his glory. And none received him with greater delight than the Commander-in-Chief of the American Army. It was my dear Lee and my dear General—then, and always. I find it touching in the early history of our Revolution how simply the General-in-Chief regarded officers who had once served the Crown. Like the Mexicans, who stared in wonder at an armored Spanish horseman. And this flashy, boastful general—and another Continental commander, whose name and fate later became infamous—surely exploited the modesty of the Commander-in-Chief, advising, blustering, sneering, disobeying orders, daily adding obstacles to a path only Washington’s extraordinary endurance could navigate.

While we were away on our South Carolina campaign, the Fourth of July had passed. We and the thirteen United States were forever parted. My native Virginia had declared all men equally free, that all power rests in the people, who have the right to alter or abolish their government at will—and that hereditary rule is unnatural and absurd! Our General presented me with this document fresh from Williamsburg as we sailed north past the Virginia capes. Amid laughter and amusement, he pointed out the faith I was now bound to uphold from that date forward. There was no escape. I was a Virginian—my godfathers had sworn, in my name, that all men were free (including poor Gumbo), that monarchy was absurd, and that I had the right to change my government whenever I pleased. I thought of Madam Esmond at home—how she would react when asked to sign such articles. What would Hagan say? He tore them apart in a sermon—logical, passionate, but ultimately powerless. The U.S. Government ignored his words. When he argued that since all men were free, Gumbo, Sady, and Nathan certainly had a right to sit in Congress—“Tut, tut, my good Mr. Hagan,” my mother interjected, “let us hear no more of this nonsense. Leave such wickedness to the rebels!”

By mid-August, we stood before New York, where Mr. Howe had brought his army—the same force that had fled to Halifax after its disgraceful expulsion from Boston. The American Commander-in-Chief was there, and a great battle was inevitable. I faced it with a deep anxiety, knowing my dearest brother and his regiment were among the troops we would attack—and would surely overwhelm. Almost the entire American army crossed to a small island, where every officer on both sides knew they would be beaten and had no hope of escape. Two frigates, positioned to block their retreat across the East River to New York, could have destroyed every vessel they used to flee and forced them to surrender. They fought—hasty levies were crushed, generals captured, artillery seized, the remnants huddled in their entrenched camp. The victors were called back. The enemy was trapped in a corner, helpless. “They are at our mercy,” said the gentle General, “and ours tomorrow.” Not a single ship watched the American camp. Not a sentinel could see their movements. An entire army crossed under our very eyes in one night—without losing a single man. General Howe remained in command after this feat, completing his glories at Long Island and Breed’s5 Hill, and later at Philadelphia!

A spy had crossed during the night to warn us—only to fall upon a picket of Germans who couldn’t understand him. Their commander was drunk or asleep. By morning, when the message reached someone who could comprehend English, the entire Continental force had vanished across the East River—and the empire over thirteen colonies had slipped away.

My views on our chief were not uncommon among the army—though perhaps wisely kept silent by those under Howe’s direct command. Am I more unlucky than others? Or why do my careless remarks travel farther than my neighbors’? My rage at the waste of such a victory matched that of many officers. Why must my name be singled out as the worst of the grumblers? Personally, General Howe was brave, pleasant, and good-natured.

“So, Sir George,” he said, “you fault me as a military man because there was fog after the battle on Long Island, and your friends, the Continentals, escaped? Surely we took and killed enough of them. But you gentlemen amateurs are never satisfied!” And he turned his back, shrugged, and spoke to someone else. Amateur I might be—and he the most amiable of men. But if King George had said to him, “Never again serve me,” that agreeable Cassio would have been dismissed—deservedly.
