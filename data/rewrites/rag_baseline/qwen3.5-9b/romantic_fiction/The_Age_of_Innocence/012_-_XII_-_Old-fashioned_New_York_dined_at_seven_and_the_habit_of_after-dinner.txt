The clock struck seven, and the city exhaled. In Old-Fashioned New York, dining was a ritual, a rigid geometry of time that dictated movement across the grid. Even as the younger set mocked the custom in the salons of Archer's circle, the habit persisted, a gravitational pull dragging the wealthy through the streets.

Newland Archer walked up Fifth Avenue from Waverley Place. The avenue was empty, stripped of its usual noise, save for a cluster of carriages parked before the Reggie Chiverses' house—a gathering for the Duke—and the solitary silhouette of an elderly gentleman in a heavy coat and muffler climbing a brownstone stoop, vanishing into the amber glow of a gas lamp. This was the rhythm of the day. Crossing Washington Square, Archer noted the trajectory of others: old Mr. du Lac heading to the Dagonets, Mr. Skipworth of his own firm visiting the Miss Lannings. Further up, Beaufort emerged from a doorway, a dark projection against a burst of light, descending to a private brougham that rolled away toward a destination shrouded in silence. It was not an Opera night; no parties were being given. Therefore, Beaufort's journey was clandestine, a secret transaction in the daylight. Archer filed this away, connecting the mystery to a small house beyond Lexington Avenue, recently adorned with beribboned curtains and flower boxes, where the canary-colored brougham of Miss Fanny Ring often waited.

Beyond the narrow, slippery pyramid of Mrs. Archer's world lay the unmapped quarter of artists, musicians, and writers. These fragments of humanity refused amalgamation with the social structure. Though deemed respectable in odd ways, they preferred isolation. Medora Manson's literary salon had died quickly, victims of the writers' reluctance to mingle. Other attempts followed—the household of the Blenkers, with their voluble mother and blowsy daughters, where one might meet Edwin Booth, Patti, William Winter, and the new actor George Rignold, alongside magazine editors and critics.

Mrs. Archer and her group viewed these people with a specific timidity. They were uncertain, carrying secrets in the background of their minds. Literature and art were respected, yes, but the era of Washington Irving and Fitz-Greene Halleck was gone. Those gentlemen were of the old school; the successors possessed gentlemanly sentiments perhaps, but their origins, their hair, their intimacy with the stage rendered them incomprehensible under old New York criteria.

"When I was a girl," Mrs. Archer would say, "we knew everybody between the Battery and Canal Street; and only the people one knew had carriages. It was perfectly easy to place anyone then; now one can't tell, and I prefer not to try."

Only old Catherine Mingott might have bridged the gap, with her lack of moral prejudice and indifference to subtle distinctions. Yet she had never opened a book or examined a painting, caring for music only as a memory of gala nights at the Tuileries. Beaufort, matching her in daring, failed to fuse the worlds. His grand house and silk-stockinged footmen were barriers to informality. Worse, he was illiterate, regarding writers as paid purveyors of pleasure for the rich, an opinion never challenged by anyone influential enough to sway him.

Newland Archer had known this structure since childhood, accepting it as the bedrock of his universe. He knew of societies elsewhere where painters and poets were sought after as Dukes, where drawing rooms buzzed with the talk of Mérimée, Thackeray, Browning, or William Morris. Such things were inconceivable in New York, unsettling to contemplate. Archer knew the "fellows who wrote"—he met them at the Century Club or nascent theatrical societies. He enjoyed them there, though bored by their mingling with fervid, dowdy women at the Blenkers', who treated them like captured curiosities. Even after his most stimulating conversations with Ned Winsett, he felt the distance remain. To enlarge either world, he realized, required reaching a stage of manners where they would naturally merge.

This thought surfaced as he approached the door of the Countess Olenska. He recalled the amusement with which she had mentioned her grandmother Mingott and the Wellands objecting to her living in a "Bohemian" quarter. It was not the peril but the poverty her family disliked; literature was merely compromising. She, however, feared nothing. Books scattered in her drawing-room—usually considered out of place—had whetted his interest with names like Paul Bourget, Huysmans, and the Goncourt brothers. Ruminating on this reversal of values, he felt the necessity of imagining conditions incredibly different from his own to be useful in her current difficulty.

Nastasia opened the door, smiling mysteriously. On the bench lay a sable-lined overcoat, a folded opera hat of dull silk with a gold "J.B." on the lining, and a white silk muffler. No mistaking it: these costly items belonged to Julius Beaufort.

Anger flared in Archer, sharp and immediate. He nearly scribbled a word on his card and turned away, remembering only that his previous letters to Madame Olenska had been constrained by excessive discretion. He had no one to blame but himself if she had opened her doors to others. With a dogged determination, he entered the drawing-room, intent on making Beaufort feel obstructed, on outstaying him.

The banker leaned against the mantelshelf, draped with an old embroidery held by brass candelabra containing yellowish church candles. He thrust his chest out, supporting his shoulders against the stone, resting his weight on one large patent-leather foot. As Archer entered, he smiled, looking down on his hostess seated on a sofa perpendicular to the chimney. Behind her, a table banked with flowers formed a screen; against the orchids and azaleas—tributes from the Beaufort hot-houses—Madame Olenska sat half-reclined, her head propped on a hand, her wide sleeve leaving her arm bare to the elbow.

Evening receptions demanded "simple dinner dresses": close-fitting armor of whale-boned silk, slightly open at the neck, lace ruffles filling the crack, tight sleeves revealing just enough wrist for an Etruscan gold bracelet or velvet band. Madame Olenska, heedless of tradition, wore a long robe of red velvet bordered at the chin and down the front with glossy black fur. Archer remembered seeing a portrait by Carolus Duran in Paris, a sensation of the Salon, depicting a lady in such a bold, sheath-like robe, her chin nestling in fur. There was something perverse and provocative in fur worn indoors in a heated room, in the combination of a muffled throat and bare arms; yet the effect was undeniably pleasing.

"Lord love us—three whole days at Skuytercliff!" Beaufort boomed in his loud, sneering voice as Archer entered. "You'd better take all your furs, and a hot-water-bottle."

"Why? Is the house so cold?" she asked, extending her left hand to Archer in a gesture that mysteriously suggested an expectation of a kiss.

"No; but the missus is," Beaufort nodded carelessly.

"But I thought her so kind. She came herself to invite me. Granny says I must certainly go."

"Granny would, of course. And I say it's a shame you're going to miss the little oyster supper I'd planned for you at Delmonico's next Sunday, with Campanini and Scalchi and a lot of jolly people."

She looked doubtfully from the banker to Archer.

"Ah—that does tempt me! Except the other evening at Mrs. Struthers's I've not met a single artist since I've been here."

"Painters? I know one or two painters, very good fellows, that I could bring to see you if you'd allow me," Archer offered boldly.

"Painters? Are there painters in New York?" Beaufort asked, in a tone implying their non-existence since he did not buy their pictures. Madame Olenska turned to Archer with a grave smile. "That would be charming. But I was really thinking of dramatic artists, singers, actors, musicians. My husband's house was always full of them."

She said the words "my husband" as if no sinister associations clung to them, in a tone that seemed almost to sigh over the lost delights of her married life. Archer looked at her perplexedly, wondering if it were lightness or dissimulation that allowed her to touch so easily on the past at the very moment she risked her reputation to break with it.

"I do think," she continued, addressing both men, "that the *imprévu* adds to one's enjoyment. It's perhaps a mistake to see the same people every day."

"It's confoundedly dull, anyhow; New York is dying of dullness," Beaufort grumbled. "And when I try to liven it up for you, you go back on me. Come—think better of it! Sunday is your last chance, for Campanini leaves next week for Baltimore and Philadelphia; and I've a private room, and a Steinway, and they'll sing all night for me."

"How delicious! May I think it over, and write to you tomorrow morning?"

She spoke amiably, yet with the faintest hint of dismissal. Beaufort felt it instantly, unused to dismissals, and stood staring at her with an obstinate line between his eyes.

"Why not now?"

"It's too serious a question to decide at this late hour."

"Do you call it late?"
