Dawn spilled pale and thin over the barren moors the next morning, and we set out at once, pushing hard for the shadowed line of the forest that hung on the horizon. I rode Lona’s dappled mare, her cold, still form swathed in her own wool cloak laid across the saddle before me. I would lay her at her father’s feet: he would give her a place on the soft couches of his dead chamber, I told myself. If he refused her, for she had not returned to him of her own will, I would watch over her body in the desert until it melted back into the dust. But I knew deep in my bones she had died long before this, and he would not turn her away. The thought of humbling myself before him, of confessing every wrong I had done, coiled bitter in my throat.

And Lilith? I had to bring her to Adam too. I had failed. I had no power to wring a single spark of repentance from her. I had no right to slay her, and far less right to loose such a force of hungry, unappeasable hate loose across the world. I was not worthy to be her jailor for all eternity. Again and again along the road I offered her food and water, and every time she only answered with a searing look of ravenous, unblinking malice. Her fiery eyes rolled restlessly in her skull, never closing, not for a moment of rest, until we stepped across the banks of the steaming hot stream. After that they stayed shut, sealed tight, until we stood at the threshold of the House of Bitterness.

One evening as we made camp for the night, a tiny barefoot girl wandered over to where Lilith lay bound on her pallet. I sprinted to stop her, but before I could reach them, the child had pressed a wild berry to the princess’s lips, and let out a sharp, wailing cry of pain. “Please, king,” she whimpered, holding out her pricked, oozing finger, “suck it quick. The bad giantess bit a hole right through it!” I drew the tiny wound clean, and a minute later she was already reaching for a second berry, ready to try again. This time she snatched her hand away fast, and the fruit dropped unharmed to the grass. The little one’s name was Luva.

The next day we crossed the hot stream, and the Little Ones burst into peals of glee, bouncing and cheering to be back on the soil of their home woods. But their nest-village was still miles off, and that day we traveled no further than the ivy-covered hall draped in heavy purple grape clusters I had spotted on my earlier journey. The moment the children caught sight of the fruit they swarmed it, eating greedily, and within minutes every last one of them was fast asleep on the mossy stone floor and in the fern thickets outside. Hoping to watch the ghost dance play out again, certain the Little Ones would sleep straight through the night, I had them leave a wide empty circle clear in the center of the hall. I laid down myself, Lona’s form soft at my side, but I did not sleep.

Darkness fell, and the shadowy company of dancers materialized as if they had stepped out of the very stone walls. I was lingering on the horrid thought that they would dance this hollow, ghostly waltz for all eternity, that my own stiff-necked stubbornness might one day condemn me to join them, when the Little Ones’ eyes all flew open at once. They sprang to their feet wide awake, and every single one of them latched on to the nearest cold, shadowy hand, bounding and skipping along in time to the slow, waltzing rhythm. The spectres did not flinch. They welcomed the children, it seemed—they had long been wandering the path back to their own lost childhood, after all. Their innocent, unruly gambols must have stoked a faint spark of warmth in those weary souls, robbed of their present and with no future but shadow left to them. The children played a hundred merry, never rude pranks, and if they stumbled every now and then and jostled the dance’s rhythm off beat, the hollow spectres, with no mouths left to smile, showed not a flicker of annoyance.

Just before the first pale streak of dawn touched the sky, I froze. There in the doorway stood the skeleton-princess, Lilith, her eyes wide open and blazing, the cursed black mark eating dark into the flesh of her side. She stood motionless a moment, then glided across the stone floor as if drawn to the dancing circle, ready to join. I leaped to my feet with a shout. The Little Ones cried out in sharp, repulsed fear, and all the faint ghostly lights winked out. The low wan moon slanted in through the ivy-framed windows, and I saw the children huddled clinging to each other. The spectres were gone, vanished into thin air. Lilith too had disappeared. I darted back to her pallet, and there she lay, eyes closed, as if she had never moved an inch. When I returned to the hall, the Little Ones were already curling up on the floor, falling back asleep.

We set out again at sunrise, and a little way down the path we spotted two skeletons wandering slowly through a bramble thicket. The Little Ones broke their ranks and ran to greet them. I followed, and even though I now walked free of splints and bandages, I recognized the pair I had seen wandering these woods weeks before. The children made friends with them at once, clinging to their bony arms and stroking the long, smooth bones of their fingers. It was plain the two poor creatures welcomed the attention. Their shared loss of flesh, of everything they had ever known, had drawn them close; the erasure of their old lives had been the start of a strange new one. When the children saw they held handfuls of healing herbs, searching for more to rub into their dry bones (what other nourishment could a being so rudimentary need?), they examined the herbs they held, gathered armfuls of the same, and filled the empty bony hands the skeletons held out to receive them. They said their goodbyes, promising to visit again, and set off chattering to each other about how they never knew such nice, gentle people lived in the woods.

When we reached the nest-village, I stayed one night there to watch the children settle back into their tree-top homes. Lona still looked as if she had just moments since drawn her last breath, and there was no rush to move her. The princess had not eaten a single bite since we set out, and her eyes stayed sealed shut; fearing she would waste away entirely before we reached our destination, I crept to her pallet in the dark of night and laid my bare arm across her lips. She bit down so fiercely the world went black at the edges. I do not remember how I tore away from her, but I came to my senses lying out of her reach, the sun already peeking over the treetops.

I set off immediately after dawn. I chose twelve of the Little Ones—not the biggest or strongest, but the sweetest, merriest of the lot. I seated six of them each on the back of one of six elephants, and took two more of the great gentle “Clumsies” the children loved to bear Lilith’s pallet. I still rode Lona’s mare, her wrapped body held safe before me. I took the straightest path I knew, across the left branch of the dead riverbed, heading for the House of Bitterness. I hoped Mara would show me how to cross the broader, rougher branch of the dead river without harming the elephants, and how to skirt the monstrous basin full of horrors I dreaded even more, for the children’s sake.

We endured one terrible night on the road, the third one, camped in the open desert between the two branches of the dead river. We set the elephants to rest in a sheltered hollow, and laid Lilith’s pallet down between them on the sand. She looked fully dead, but I knew she was not. I laid myself down a little way off, Lona’s body at my other side, keeping watch over both my dead love and the living monster. The moon hung half-way down the west, pale and thoughtful, dappling the sand with soft shadows. Of a sudden it was eclipsed. It stayed visible, but shed no light; a thin, diaphanous dark film stretched across its face, and it looked troubled. The film shifted just a little, and I saw its jagged, torn edge against the moon’s clear silver— the hooked outline of a great bat-like wing. A cold wind blew, one that stung like burning frost, and Lilith was on top of me. Her hands were still bound, but she tore the cloak Lona had woven for me right off my shoulder with her teeth, and sank them deep into my bare flesh. I lay paralyzed. I could feel my life force draining out of me, flowing straight into her, until I remembered the word Mara had taught me. I struck her hard across her bound wrist. She threw back her head and let out a gurgling, unhuman shriek, and I felt her whole body convulse. I flung her off me and sprang to my feet. She was on her knees, rocking back and forth. A second blast of that burning cold wind swept over us, the moon shone bright and clear again, and I saw her face—gaunt, ghastly, smeared red with my blood.

“Down, devil!” I shouted. “Where are you taking me?” she asked, her voice a dull sepulchral echo. “To your first husband,” I answered. “He will kill me!” she moaned. “At least he will take you off my hands.” “Give me my daughter,” she screamed suddenly, grinding her teeth. “Never! Your doom is upon you at last.” “Loose my hands for pity’s sake,” she groaned. “The cords have sunk right into my flesh, I’m in agony.” “I dare not. Lie down,” I said. She fell onto the sand like a heavy log. The rest of the night passed in perfect peace, and by dawn she looked dead again.

Before evening we caught sight of the House of Bitterness on the rise ahead. A moment later one of the elephants ambled up beside my horse, and the Little One riding its neck whispered in my ear. “Please, king, you are not going to that place? That’s where the cat-woman lives!” “Indeed I am,” I answered. “We are spending the night there.” “Oh, please don’t! Nobody ever sees her, the giants say—she has no face! Her head is nothing but back and sides, all round.” “She hides her face from people who are dull and discontented,” I told him. “I met her before. I spent a whole night at her house, and she is good.” “But what if her claws are folded up inside the cushions of her feet, and you never noticed them?” “What if I have claws hidden in my toes?” I teased. “Oh no, you are good, that can’t be!” “Then why do you believe the bad giants’ lies about the lady, when you know they lie about everything else?” The children fell quiet, then one of the little girls spoke up. “If you say she is good, then we believe you. But I don’t want scratches in the dark!” “That is why we have come,” I said. “The princess is her friend. A real friend gives you what you need, not just what you want. The princess sorely needs the kind of hard, firm kindness no one else can give her. If any of you are afraid, you can go home right now. I will not be angry. But I cannot bring anyone with me who believes the giants more than me, or who calls a good lady rude names.” “There she is! In the doorway waiting for us!” one of the children cried, and clapped his hands over his eyes. “Her face is covered, but she’s ugly!” “She has the most beautiful face I have ever seen,” I told them. “As beautiful as Lona’s, hidden under that veil.” Mara stood in the doorway, dressed and swathed all in soft white, waiting for us. “At last!” she said, stepping forward. “Lilith’s hour has been so long on the way, but it has come. Thousands of years I have waited here, and I did not wait in vain.” She came to me, took Lona’s body gently from my arms, carried it into the house, then came back for Lilith. The princess shuddered, but did not fight. The elephants and Clumsies laid down by the door, and we followed Mara inside. She laid Lilith on a rough wooden settle along the wall, cut her bonds, and turned to face us. “Mr. Vane, and all you Little Ones, thank you,” she said. “This woman would never yield to soft, gentle kindness. Hard love is all that is left for her now. I will do everything I can to make her repent.” The tender-hearted Little Ones began to sob softly. “Will you hurt her very much?” the little girl who had spoken earlier asked, slipping her warm hand into mine. “I am afraid I must,” Mara answered. “It would be far crueler to hurt her too little. The work would just have to be done all over again, later, and far worse.” She bent, lifted the small boy Odu up in her arms, turned her back to the rest of us, and drew her veil down from her face, holding him at arm’s length so he could see. I saw the look that spread across his small face, as if he was gazing in a mirror. His mouth fell open, then a look of divine wonder lit up his features, melting into unutterable delight. He stared entranced for a full minute, then Mara set him down. He stood for a moment longer, lost in that vision of bliss he could not name, then ran back to us with the glowing face of a prophet. Mara pulled her veil back into place, and turned to the rest of the children. “Go eat and drink before you sleep,” she told them, “you have had a very long journey.” She set out her house’s hard, dry bread and a jug of cold well water. The children had never seen bread, never tasted water, but they ate it without a single complaint, sipping the water one by one and looking up with faces bright with astonished joy. Then she herded the smallest of them ahead of her, and they all trooped after her to the garret, where she laid them down to sleep on soft piled straw, tucking each one in with her own gentle hands.
