The moon hung low and bruised, a battered disc of oxidized copper staring directly into the canopy. It lacked the usual luminescence, dimmed by an unseen exhaustion, casting a pallid, dispirited light that seemed to question the endurance of the night itself. Unclouded and star-strangled, it cast long, jagged shadows that danced in opposition to my own stride. We moved in parallel courses—she, the fading celestial body; I, the living shadow trailing beneath her gaze. We spoke little, for my focus was anchored to the earth, yet her silent malaise radiated outward, a gravitational pull I felt without needing to witness it.

Then, beneath the sprawling arch of an ancient oak, something halted my progress. A patch of unnatural whiteness fractured the gloom. As I closed the distance, the vague suggestion coalesced into a terrifying certainty: a human form.

"Another casualty," I muttered, dropping to my knees.

My fingers brushed the surface. It was flesh, yes, but stripped of vitality, reduced to a state bordering on skeletal. Lying on its side, the body possessed a chilling specificity—a coldness distinct from the inert chill of stone, the residual frost of something that was once animate and is now extinguished. The closer I examined it, the more the illusion of life dissolved. In a fleeting, bewildered instant, I conjured a grotesque fantasy: a wild dancer, a spectral Cinderella lost in the labyrinthine night of the wilderness, perished before returning home.

Reality snapped back with brutal clarity. The subject was entirely naked, her skin so taut and parchment-thin that every rib was visible, counting themselves like beads strung on a wire of bone. Her skull was a map of terror; teeth, sharp and gleaming, protruded from retracted lips in a ghastly smile. Her hair, thick and finer than silk, cascaded in a black void beyond the length of her limbs.

A tall woman, undoubtedly once graceful. How had she arrived here? Self-inflicted starvation seemed unlikely given the trajectory of her fall. She must have collapsed, succumbing to a rapid depletion of reserves. Yet, the degree of emaciation defied logic, and the nudity demanded explanation. Had savages stripped her? Or had predators claimed her garments? Leaving the corpse exposed was unthinkable. Nature's laws of decomposition were irrelevant; the violation of the body itself was the primary offense. Irreverent eyes, predatory claws, or the slow erosion of years—all were threats I could not permit.

However, the terrain offered no solace. The ground was a hardened lattice of roots, impenetrable to bare hands.

Initial inspection suggested recent death; there was no odor of decay. But what substance remained to rot? Could she be alive? The possibility gnawed at me. In this aberrant world, anomalies were currency. Even if revival was improbable, verification was mandatory. Before burial, I needed absolute confirmation.

Recalling a cluster of ripe grapes I had harvested earlier, I clutched the remaining fruit. Juice might sustain a dying pulse. The mouth was slightly agape, though the head rested at an awkward angle. Sliding my arm beneath her shoulder, I discovered pine needles beneath her cheek were unnervingly warm. Death had not been instantaneous. She *might* still be breathing, though my ears detected no heartbeat and my nose smelled no breath. One hand was clenched tightly around a small object.

I forced a grape into her mouth. No swallow.

Desperation drove me to act. I gathered a thick bed of pine needles and dry leaves, draped my own garment—still retaining the heat of my body—over the makeshift mattress, and lifted her onto it. I covered her completely, trapping whatever thermal energy remained, praying for the sun's return to amplify the effect. Another grape attempt yielded nothing.

"Doubt," I told myself, "is a poor excuse for inaction."

Her skin was drum-tight over bone; friction would cause damage. I crawled into the leaf pile, embracing her as best I could. I shared my dwindling internal heat, spending the remainder of the night in vigil, sleepless and starving. Her cold seeped into me, a parasitic drain, offering no reciprocal warmth.

If I had fled the beautiful sleepers to my silver couches, I reasoned, choosing isolation with such a bedfellow, I would have forfeited a privilege. Instead, I was conscripted into an awful duty. Beneath the setting moon, I lay with the dead, guarding against the dawn.

As twilight faded, the eastern horizon bleached gray. A subtle undulation caught my eye—a large serpent gliding in a straight, unbroken line. Moments later, a doe and her fawn emerged, followed by bear cubs leading a litter of younger ones. Then, the light intensified. A troop of horses trotted past; though diminutive in size, resembling Shetland ponies, their musculature and gait betrayed them as full-grown warhorses. Breeds varied wildly: draft horses, chargers, hunters, racers. Dwarf cattle and miniature elephants trailed behind.

"Why are the children absent?" I asked the silence. "Once this woman is secured, I must retrieve them!"

The destination of this migration remained unclear—an exodus or a morning ritual? Until sunrise confirmed the pattern, I could not abandon her. Placing my hand on her chest, I noted a slight increase in temperature. Perhaps it absorbed the heat I had shed; certainly, it hadn't generated its own. Hope, however fragile, persisted.

The sun breached the horizon, peering down with innocent brilliance. Energized by the light, I removed the protective handkerchief shielding her face from pine debris. Anxiety tightened my chest. Had I saved a jewel or merely its casing?

The body lay motionless. Morning light revealed the horror of her wasting: hollow cheeks, sharp bone edges, teeth piercing the lips. Her clothes were threadbare rags, yet the potential for resurrection lingered.

Sunlight struck her face. I rearranged the cloth, covering her briefly, then departed to follow the creatures. Their trail was well-worn, merging with numerous side paths into a broad artery. The forest receded, giving way to a vast expanse of verdant green. A river flowed along the treeline, shallow yet deep, filled to the brim. A bluish mist rose from its surface, dissipating as it ascended. Small animals grazed on the opposite bank, clearly migrating from the woods to the plains.

I knelt to drink, but recoiled instantly. The water was scalding hot, tasting of metal.

Heat—the first imperative of existence. I sprinted back to my charge.

No one truly understands the psychological weight of redeeming a stranger from death unless they stand in my solitude. "Regardless of her fate," I resolved, "I shall no longer be alone." This water promised to purge the cold death and drown my desolation.

Carrying her to the stream, I marveled at her lightness; her bones were delicate, barely supporting her frame. She was pliable, not stiff, allowing me to cradle her on one arm like a sleeping infant. I moved silently, fearing the wind of my motion might disturb her.

The water was too hot for immersion. Shock could kill a flickering spark. I laid her on the bank and dipped my garment, washing her pitiful form. Age was indeterminate; only the profusion of black hair hinted at her identity. Her eyelids were half-open, a crack in the night sky through which no sunlight penetrated, rendering her appearance even more corpse-like.

As I bathed her, hope waned with every passing minute. Would strength return? Would those lids lift? Still, I worked continuously, ensuring no part cooled while attending to another. Gradually, the body warmed. Finally, I risked submersion. Entering the stream, I drew her in, keeping her face above the surface while the current washed over her. Despite the heat, her clenched hand never relaxed.

Ten minutes later, I lifted her, dried her, and covered her. Running to the forest, I returned with successive loads of leaves.

Now, I could explore. Seeking shelter, I ran upstream toward rocky hills. Upon arrival, I found the river bursting forth from a cavern at the hill's base. It seemed to cascade down an internal staircase, eager to escape, finally finding an exit at the foot.

The opening was narrow. Creeping inside, I discovered the river didn't rush down stairs but rose quietly from the floor like a column, flowing along a deep, confined channel. If I could span this channel with sturdy fallen boughs, creating a platform supported by smaller branches and layered with leaves, the constant flow beneath would maintain a perpetual warmth.

Returning to check on her, I found her unchanged. Heat had not revived her, nor had it halted hope. I cleared boulders from the channel, arranging them at her feet and flanks. Gathering beech boughs with clinging yellow leaves, I constructed a bridge-bed over the torrent. Interlacing twigs and burying them in moss, I built a soft, insulated couch.

Finally, I carried her to the cave. She was so light I feared she might dissolve into dust upon release. Setting her gently on the pathless bridge, the relief of abandoning that fear outweighed the burden of her weight. Covering her with leaves, I attempted to feed her again. To my delight, her jaw allowed a wider opening. The grape lay untouched, but I hoped the moisture would penetrate.

Hours passed. She was no longer cold. The brook's heat had permeated her frame—a mere shell, yet warm to the touch
