The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the library windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Margaret Hale, like the persistent beating of a heart she could no longer control. She sat at her mahogany desk, the heavy scent of beeswax and damp wool filling the room, her gaze fixed upon the thick sheaf of letters that had arrived by the morning post. They were from Mr. Bell, his script sprawling and confident, yet suffocating under the weight of a melancholy she found increasingly difficult to bear.

Dixon stood nearby, a conduit for the trivialities of Milton, her voice carrying the hollow echo of gossip that filled the silence between the thunderclaps. She recounted how Martha had retreated to Miss Thornton's household following the marriage, detailing the bridesmaids' gowns and the breakfasts that followed the ceremony. She spoke of the public's judgment—that Mr. Thornton had thrown away too much dignity on a grand wedding while bleeding financially from the strike, paying exorbitant sums for failed contracts. Then came the auction: the pathetic prices fetched for Dixon's own cherished furniture, a shameful testament to the disparity between the rich and the struggling. Dixon noted how Mrs. Thornton had secured bargains, only for Mr. Thornton to bid against himself the next day, driven by a desperate need to possess something, much to the amusement of the bystanders. "If Mrs. Thornton pays too little," Dixon observed with a shrug, "Mr. Thornton pays too much."

It was a cycle of petty economics, devoid of the gravity of the world outside. Mr. Bell's instructions regarding the books were equally opaque; he was a man of impossible particularity, his letters a labyrinth of confusion that would have been clear if he had simply walked through the door. As for the Higginses, Dixon's memory betrayed her, skewed by an aristocratic bias that rendered the lives of those beneath her status as vague, treacherous dreams. Nicholas was well, she claimed, visiting often to inquire after Miss Margaret—a rarity shared only once by Mr. Thornton. Mary, however, was a "great, stout, slatternly thing," a figure of indeterminate existence. Dixon whispered a rumor, perhaps a dream, that Mary had taken work at Mr. Thornton's mill to learn the arts of cooking, a notion so absurd it bordered on the surreal. Margaret nodded, agreeing that the story was incoherent enough to be a fever dream, yet grateful for someone with whom she could speak of Milton, even if Dixon preferred to dwell on Mr. Bell's speeches, interpreting them as a veiled proposal to make her his heiress.

But Margaret received no encouragement. Her young lady remained silent, refusing to gratify her insinuations, however disguised. Beneath this domestic stagnation lay a strange, undefined longing: the hope that Mr. Bell would finally travel to Milton. At Helstone, they had agreed that any explanation she craved must come from him, delivered orally, never forced. Yet his letters grew shorter, sharper, tinged with a bitter weariness that unsettled her. He spoke of an "old-fashioned complaint called the spleen," a condition he left her to diagnose as either mental or physical, granting himself permission to grumble without sending bulletins. In response, Margaret ceased her inquiries, fearing the truth behind his silence.

One day, Edith dropped a fragment of a conversation, planting a seed that sprouted rapidly in Margaret's mind: Mr. Bell intended to take her to Cadiz to meet her new sister-in-law, to witness the aftermath of the mutiny firsthand. Edith, weary of the questioning, admitted that Mr. Bell had only half-thought of it, suggesting it as a suitable destination for the long vacation. "Why not Spain?" Edith had asked, as if geography held no significance beyond convenience.

Edith's reaction was immediate and defensive. Any pleasure Margaret derived from afar was interpreted as a tacit affront, a proof of indifference. "I know I care much more for Margaret than Margaret does for me," Edith cried, tears welling up. Margaret comforted her, but the distance between them widened with every word. Edith viewed the world through a lens of scarcity, where joy shared with others diminished its value. So Margaret kept her delight hidden, venting it only to Dixon when dressing for dinner.

"She's a Papist, Miss, isn't she?" Dixon asked, her eyes wide with apprehension.
"I believe—oh yes, certainly!" Margaret replied, a flicker of dread dimming her spirits instantly.
"And they live in a Popish country?"
"Yes."
"Then I'm afraid I must say, that my soul is dearer to me than even Master Frederick, his own dear self. I should be in a perpetual terror, Miss, lest I should be converted."

"Oh," said Margaret, trying to steady her voice. "I do not know that I am going; and if I go, I am not such a fine lady as to be unable to travel without you. No! dear old Dixon, you shall have a long holiday, if we go. But I'm afraid it is a long 'if.'"

Dixon recoiled. First, she disliked the diminutive "dear old Dixon," a term of endearment Margaret used freely for those she favored, yet which made Dixon wince, reminding her of her age despite being barely past fifty. Second, she harbored a secret curiosity about Spain, the Inquisition, and the mysteries of the faith. Clearing her throat, she feigned reluctance before probing further. "If you take care never to see a priest, or enter into one of their churches, there would be so very much danger of your being converted?"

Master Frederick, of course, had succumbed to conversion unaccountably. "I fancy it was love that first predisposed him to conversion," Margaret sighed, the words escaping before she could check them.
"Indeed, Miss!" Dixon exclaimed, seizing the logic. "Well! I can preserve myself from priests, and from churches; but love steals in unawares! I think it's as well I should not go."

Margaret feared indulging in this Spanish fantasy, for it served only to distract her from the agonizing impatience of waiting for Mr. Thornton. Mr. Bell remained stationary at Oxford, bound by some secret restraint that prevented him from coming to Milton. Nor could she bring herself to mention Edith's revelation of the potential trip to Spain; it might have been a fleeting fancy, born of a sunny day at Helstone, yet if true, it promised a bright outlet from the monotony that was slowly crushing her life.

A small solace existed in Edith's son. He was the pride and plaything of the household, provided he behaved. But his will was strong, and when he erupted into stormy passions, Edith collapsed into despair, sighing, "Oh dear, what shall I do with him! Do, Margaret, please ring the bell for Hanley." Paradoxically, Margaret found the child more endearing in these moments of turbulence than in his compliant, blue-sashed moods. She would carry him into a private room, engaging in a fierce battle of wills. With a firm power that subdued him into peace, she countered his charms and wiles with arguments for righteousness, until he would rub his hot, tear-smeared face against hers, kissing and caressing until sleep claimed him in her arms. These were Margaret's sweetest moments, brief tastes of a maternal warmth she believed was forever denied to her.

Into this dynamic entered Mr. Henry Lennox, adding a cold brilliance to the household. Margaret found him sharper, less warm than before, yet enriched by strong intellectual tastes and varied knowledge that seasoned the otherwise insipid conversations. She caught glimpses of a slight contempt in his eyes for his brother and sister-in-law, viewing their lives as frivolous and purposeless. Once, in her presence, he queried his brother sharply about abandoning his profession. When Captain Lennox replied that he had sufficient means to live upon, Mr. Lennox curled his lip with disdain. "And is that all you live for?"

Yet the brothers remained attached, bound by the familiar dynamic of the clever leading and the patient being led. Mr. Lennox pushed forward in his career, calculatingly cultivating connections for future advantage. Keen-sighted, far-seeing, intelligent, sarcastic, and proud, he was a force of nature in the room. Since their initial conversation regarding Frederick's affairs, their interactions had been limited, yet enough to erode the shyness on Margaret's side and the mortified pride on his. They met continually, though she sensed he avoided solitude with her. Both perceived that they had drifted strangely apart from their former anchorage, separated by divergent opinions and tastes.

Still, when he spoke with remarkable epigrammatic point, or displayed unusual eloquence, Margaret felt his eye seek the expression of her countenance first, if only for an instant. In the constant family intercourse that threw them together, her opinion was the one to which he listened with a deference that was reluctant, concealed, and painfully complete.
