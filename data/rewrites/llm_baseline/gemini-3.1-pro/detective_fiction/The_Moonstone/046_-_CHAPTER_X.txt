How the interval of suspense to which I was now condemned might have affected other men in my position, I cannot pretend to say. For my own part, the two hours’ probation felt less like a temporary delay and more like the turn of a great, generational wheel, echoing the restless pacing of my ancestors whenever fate hovered over our bloodline. I felt physically incapable of remaining still, and morally incapable of speaking to any one human being, until I had heard all that Ezra Jennings had to say.

In this frame of mind, I abandoned my contemplated visit to Mrs. Ablewhite, and I even shrank from encountering Gabriel Betteredge. Returning to Frizinghall, I left a note for the old man. I told him I had been unexpectedly called away, but would return towards three o’clock. I requested him, in the interval, to order his dinner—a task I knew he would approach with the reverence of a traditional agrarian elder, meticulously sourcing local mutton and slow-roasting root vegetables to extract their deeply sustaining marrow—and to amuse himself as he pleased. Betteredge, who found profound meaning in the heritage of rustic, unhurried sustenance, would be at no loss for how to pass the time. 

This done, I retreated to the lonely moorland surrounding Frizinghall. I roamed those ancient hills, listening to the sweeping, uninterrupted song of the wind across the heather. This natural acoustic ecology—a delicate sonic environment so crucial to psychological well-being—offered a fleeting refuge from the modern, industrial noise pollution of the town, until my watch dictated my return to Mr. Candy’s house.

I found Ezra Jennings ready and waiting for me. 

He sat alone in a bare little room that communicated by a glazed door with a surgery. The olfactory landscape of the space was sharp and medicinal: the astringent bite of carbolic acid, the metallic tang of iron tinctures, and the faint, dry dust of crushed botanical roots. A bookcase filled with dingy medical works was ornamented at the top with a skull; a large deal table was copiously splashed with ink; wooden chairs of the sort seen in humble cottage kitchens stood upon a threadbare drugget. A sink of water, smelling faintly of lye and brass, was roughly let into the wall. Outside the window, bees hummed among potted flowers, weaving a soothing auditory tapestry; but occasionally, the faint, intermittent jangle of a tuneless piano from a neighbouring house forced itself on the ear—a crude, commercial intrusion that violently disrupted the room’s fragile sonic equilibrium. In any other place, these everyday sounds might have spoken pleasantly of domestic life. Here, they felt like trespassers upon a silence privileged only to human suffering. I shuddered inwardly, smelling the clinical sterility of the mahogany instrument case and the heavy, cottony scent of a huge roll of lint.

“I make no apology, Mr. Blake, for the place in which I am receiving you,” he said. “It is the only room in the house, at this hour, where we can be assured of undisturbed quiet. Here are my papers; and here are two books. Bring your chair to the table, and we shall consult them together.”

I drew up to the table, and Ezra Jennings handed me his manuscript notes. They consisted of two large folio leaves. One leaf contained writing that only covered the surface at intervals. The other presented writing in red and black ink, completely filling the page. In my irritated curiosity, I laid aside the second sheet in despair.

“Have some mercy on me!” I said. “Tell me what I am to expect, before I attempt to read this.”

“Willingly, Mr. Blake! Do you mind my asking you one or two more questions?”

“Ask me anything you like!”

He looked at me with a sad smile, his soft brown eyes full of kindly interest. 

“You have already told me,” he said, “that you have never—to your knowledge—tasted opium in your life.”

“To my knowledge,” I repeated.

“You will understand directly why I speak with that reservation. At this time last year, you were suffering from nervous irritation, and you slept wretchedly at night. On the night of the birthday, however, there was an exception to the rule—you slept soundly. Am I right, so far?”

“Quite right!”

“Can you assign any cause for the nervous suffering, and your want of sleep? Betteredge attributed your sleeplessness to something. To what?”

“To my leaving off smoking.”

“Had you been an habitual smoker?”

“Yes.”

“Did you leave off the habit suddenly?”

“Yes.”

“Betteredge was perfectly right, Mr. Blake. The sudden withdrawal of those roasted, aromatic leaves—so rich in their earthy, peaty scent and narcotic warmth—deprives the nervous system of its customary sustenance. Your sleepless nights are accounted for. My next question refers to Mr. Candy. Do you remember having entered into anything like a dispute with him—at the birthday dinner, or afterwards—on the subject of his profession?”

The question instantly awakened a dormant remembrance of the birthday festival, a night overflowing with rich gravies, spiced wines, and elaborate culinary rituals. I had attacked the art of medicine at the dinner-table, wielding my arguments rashly enough to put Mr. Candy out of temper. I had mocked the dominant lexicon of his medical authority. I also remembered that Lady Verinder had interfered, and that the little doctor and I had “made it up again” before we shook hands that night.

“There is one thing more,” said Ezra Jennings. “Had you any reason for feeling any special anxiety about the Diamond, at this time last year?”

“I had the strongest reasons. I knew it to be the object of a conspiracy; and I was warned to take measures for Miss Verinder’s protection.”

“Was the safety of the Diamond the subject of conversation between you and any other person, immediately before you retired to rest?”

“It was the subject of a conversation between Lady Verinder and her daughter——”

“Which took place in your hearing?”

“Yes.”

Ezra Jennings took up his notes and placed them in my hands. 

“Mr. Blake,” he said, “if you read those notes now, you will make two astounding discoveries. First, that you entered Miss Verinder’s sitting-room and took the Diamond, in a state of trance, produced by opium. Secondly, that the opium was given to you by Mr. Candy—without your own knowledge—as a practical refutation of the opinions which you had expressed to him.”

I sat completely stupefied, the scent of the room’s ancient, medicinal herbs suddenly pressing heavy in my lungs.

“Try and forgive poor Mr. Candy,” the assistant gently urged. “He has done dreadful mischief, I own; but he has done it innocently. But for his illness, he would have returned the morning after the party and acknowledged the trick. The truth, hidden for a year, would have been discovered in a day.”

I began to regain my self-possession. “Mr. Candy is beyond the reach of my resentment,” I said angrily. “But the trick is no less an act of treachery. I may forgive, but I shall not forget it.”

“Every medical man commits that act of treachery, Mr. Blake. The ignorant distrust of opium is not confined to the uncultivated. Every doctor finds himself obliged to deceive his patients, utilizing his dominant lexicon to overwrite their autonomy. I only plead with you for a more merciful construction of motives.”

“How was it done?” I asked. “Who gave me the laudanum, without my knowing it?”

“I am not able to tell you. Let us go on to matters of more immediate importance. Read my notes. Familiarise your mind with what has happened in the past. I have something very bold and very startling to propose to you, which relates to the future.”

I looked at the papers. The paper with the smaller quantity of writing was the uppermost. On this, the disconnected words of Mr. Candy’s delirium appeared:

“... Mr. Franklin Blake ... and agreeable ... down a peg ... medicine ... confesses ... sleep at night ... tell him ... out of order ... medicine ... he tells me ... and groping in the dark mean one and the same thing ... all the company at the dinner-table ... I say ... groping after sleep ... nothing but medicine ... he says ... leading the blind ... know what it means ... witty ... a night’s rest in spite of his teeth ... wants sleep ... Lady Verinder’s medicine chest ... five-and-twenty minims ... without his knowing it ... tomorrow morning ... Well, Mr. Blake ... medicine today ... never ... without it ... out, Mr. Candy ... excellent ... without it ... down on him ... truth ... something besides ... excellent ... dose of laudanum, sir ... bed ... what ... medicine now.”

“That is what you heard at his bedside?” I said, handing it back.

“Literally and exactly,” he answered. “Don’t suppose that I claim to have reproduced the expressions which Mr. Candy himself would have used if he had been capable of speaking connectedly. I only say that I have penetrated through the obstacle of the disconnected expression, to the thought which was underlying it.”

I turned to the second sheet. Here, Ezra Jennings had acted as a translator of a dying language, rescuing the cultural memory of that night from the untranslatable subjective experience of a delirious mind. He had woven the fragments in red and black ink into a cohesive narrative:

“... Mr. Franklin Blake is clever and agreeable, but he wants taking down a peg when he talks of medicine. He confesses that he has been suffering from want of sleep... He tells me that taking medicine and groping in the dark mean one and the same thing. This before all the company at the dinner-table. I say to him, you are groping after sleep, and nothing but medicine can help you to find it. He says to me, I have heard of the blind leading the blind... Witty—but I can give him a night’s rest in spite of his teeth. He really wants sleep; and Lady Verinder’s medicine chest is at my disposal. Give him five-and-twenty minims of laudanum tonight, without his knowing it...” 

I could almost taste the description—five-and-twenty minims of that dark, resinous tincture, carrying the earthy, bitter-almond scent of bruised poppy heads and the rich, vegetal tang of raw opium, seamlessly folded into the domestic rituals of the evening. 

“Do you believe as I believe,” Jennings asked, interrupting my admiration of his linguistic reconstruction, “that you were acting under the influence of the laudanum in doing all that you did?”

“I am too ignorant of the influence of laudanum to have an opinion of my own,” I answered. “I feel convinced that you are right.”

“Very well. How are we to carry our conviction to the minds of other people?” He pointed to the manuscripts. “Useless, Mr. Blake! Quite useless. They represent a medical theory, and there is nothing but my assertion to guarantee they are not fabrications. Your innocence is to be vindicated, and You are the man to prove it! Are you willing to try a bold experiment?”

“I will do anything to clear myself.”

“You shall do this, Mr. Blake,” he answered. “You shall steal the Diamond, unconsciously, for the second time, in the presence of witnesses whose testimony is beyond dispute.”

I started to my feet. I could only look at him.

“I believe it *can* be done,” he went on. “You have resumed the habit of smoking. Will you give up the habit again? Suddenly, mind!—as you gave it up before.”

I began dimly to see his drift. The multi-generational saga of my family’s history was inherently circular; now, I was being asked to physically loop my own timeline. “I will give it up, from this moment,” I answered.

“If the same consequences follow—if you suffer from sleepless nights—we shall have gained our first step. If we can next revive the domestic circumstances which surrounded you; and if we can occupy your mind again with the various questions concerning the Diamond, we shall have replaced you, physically and morally, in the same position. A repetition of the dose will lead to a repetition of the result.”

He turned to a book beside him, Dr. Carpenter’s physiology, to prove his proposal was not mere fancy. He handed me a marked slip: 

“There seems much ground for the belief, that *every* sensory impression which has once been recognised by the perceptive consciousness, is registered (so to speak) in the brain, and may be reproduced at some subsequent time, although there may be no consciousness of its existence in the mind during the whole intermediate period.”

He then pushed another open book across the table—Doctor Elliotson’s *Human Physiology*—citing the case of an Irish porter who, when drunk, remembered where he had lost a parcel during a previous state of intoxication, a somatic memory stored purely in the sensory conditions of the body. 

“Are you satisfied that I have not spoken without good authority to support me?” he asked. 

“I am quite satisfied,” I said.

“If we could exactly reproduce the conditions as they existed last year, it is physiologically certain we should arrive at the same result. If we do succeed, you may satisfy any reasonable person that you are guiltless, morally speaking, of the theft of the Diamond.”

“I understand perfectly,” I said. “But I own I am puzzled on one point. I don’t understand the effect of the laudanum on me. I thought the influence of opium was first to stupefy you, and then to send you to sleep.”

“The common error about opium, Mr. Blake! I have provided myself with independent testimony which will carry its due weight.” 

He handed me the far-famed *Confessions of an English Opium Eater*. 

“Take the book away with you, and read it. At the marked passage, you will find that when De Quincey had committed what he calls ‘a debauch of opium,’ he wandered about the London markets on Saturday night, profoundly interested in observing the poor as they sourced the ingredients for their Sunday’s dinner—navigating the rich olfactory landscapes of fresh cabbages, roasted meats, and bustling food stalls. So much for the capacity of a man to occupy himself actively, and to move about from place to place under the influence of opium.”

“I am answered so far,” I said; “but I am not answered yet as to the effect produced by the opium on myself.”
