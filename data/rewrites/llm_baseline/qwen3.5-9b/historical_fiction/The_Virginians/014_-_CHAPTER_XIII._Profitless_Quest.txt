With every mile Harry Warrington traveled toward Pennsylvania, the tales of the British defeat swelled in magnitude and grim detail. Two legendary regiments, veterans of Scotland and the Continent, had reportedly fled from an enemy that seemed invisible. Their famed discipline and valor had crumbled before a mere band of "savages" and a handful of French infantry. Yet, amidst the chaos, the commander of the expedition had displayed supreme courage. His horse had been shot beneath him four times; twice he had been wounded, and finally, mortally injured, he had succumbed three days later.

Harry's informants painted a harrowing picture of the engagement: the crossing of the river, the long, weary advance through the wilderness, the futile frontal assault, and the desperate attempt to use artillery to clear a path. Then came the ambush—fire erupting from behind every bush and tree, followed by a murderous fusillade that claimed at least half of the expeditionary force. But Harry learned that not everyone perished. One of the General's aides-de-camp, a Virginian gentleman, lay suffering from fever and exhaustion at Dunbar's camp. Which one? Harry did not know, but he rushed to the camp immediately.

There, stretched within a tent, he found George Washington—not his missing brother. A sharp pang of disappointment pierced Harry, compounded by the feverish heat of the season. Washington offered no news of George. He dared not speak the full truth. For three days following the battle, his duty had bound him to the General's side. On the fatal ninth of July, he had witnessed George march forward with orders from the chief, never to return. After Braddock himself drew his last breath, the aide-de-camp had managed to retrace his steps to the battlefield. What he found was a scene of horror: the remaining corpses stripped and gruesomely mutilated. He had buried one body, convinced it was George Warrington's. His own illness, perhaps exacerbated by the agony of his search, deepened with every hour spent hunting for the lost youth.

"Ah, George!" Harry cried out, his voice breaking. "If you had truly loved him, you would have found him either dead or alive!"

Nothing satisfied the young man but to walk the ground himself. Armed with money, he hired a couple of guides. They forded the river at the exact point where the army had crossed, traversing the dreadful field from end to end. The Indian raiders were gone, but the air was thick with the carrion birds feasting on mangled, festering carcasses. Except for the serene face of his grandfather, Harry had never encountered Death so visibly. The grotesque spectacle of mutilation forced him to turn away in shuddering loathing. Could the empty woods or those rotting bodies under the trees reveal any sign of his brother?

Desperate, Harry considered approaching the French fort alone, bearing a white flag, knowing the enemy had retreated there after their victory. But his guides refused to accompany him. "Keep your hair for your lady mother, my young gentleman," one advised gently. "'Tis enough that she loses one son in this campaign."

When Harry returned to the English encampment at Dunbar's, it was his turn to fall victim to the fever. Delirium seized him; he lay for days in tents and beds, alternating between unconsciousness and waking confusion. Poor Dempster, who had nursed him through previous illnesses, feared the widow would lose both her children. Eventually, the fever broke. Harry rallied enough to mount his horse, escorted home by Mr. Washington and Dempster.

They approached the gates of Castlewood with heavy hearts. A servant had been sent ahead to announce their arrival. First came Mrs. Mountain and her little daughter, Fanny, who welcomed Harry with tearful embraces. Yet, she barely nodded to Mr. Washington. Little Fanny, however, stopped the young officer cold, her small hands clasped behind her back. "Why have you not brought George back too?" she asked.

Harry did not hear her. The sobs and caresses of his nurse kept him from listening to the child's piercing question. Dempster was greeted warmly by the ladies. "Whatever could be done, we know you would do, Mr. Dempster," Mrs. Mountain said, offering her hand. She instructed Fanny to curtsy to Dempster and express gratitude to their benefactors. Turning to the Colonel, she asked if he would accept refreshment before riding on.

Mr. Washington declined politely. "The time to feed my horse, and a glass of water for myself, and I will trouble Castlewood hospitality no further."

"Hush! Your mother won't see him, child," Mrs. Mountain whispered urgently to Fanny.

"Not see George? Why, he is like a son of the house!" Harry protested.

"She had best not see him," Mrs. Mountain replied firmly. "I don't meddle in family matters anymore. But when the Colonel's servant rode in saying you were coming, Madam Esmond left this room... she said she felt she could not see Mr. Washington."

Harry hesitated, then took his friend's arm. Excusing himself briefly to the Colonel, he left the parlor and ascended to the upper rooms. He moved quickly across the corridor, averting his gaze from the door of his brother's room—a door he dreaded facing. Just as he reached it, Madam Esmond emerged. Without a word, she threw her arms around him, pulling him into her embrace, and led him inside.

The room was frozen in time. A settee sat by the bed, and a book of Psalms lay open on the covers. Everything else remained exactly as George had left it.

"My poor child! How thin you have grown—how haggard!" she exclaimed, smoothing his hair. "Never mind. A mother's care will make you well again. It was nobly done to brave sickness and danger searching for your brother. Had others been as faithful, he might be here now. Never mind, my Harry; our hero will come back to us. I know he is not dead. One so good, so brave, so gentle, and so clever as he was, cannot be lost to us entirely."

(Perhaps Harry thought silently that his mother had not always spoken of her eldest son with such reverence.)

"Dry up thy tears, my dear! He will come back to us, I know he will." When Harry pressed her for the reason behind her certainty, she revealed that she had seen her father in dreams two nights running. In those visions, he had told her her boy was merely a prisoner among the Indians.

Madam Esmond's grief had not crushed her as Harry's had initially; instead, it had sharpened her. Her eyes burned with eagerness, her countenance twisted with anger and a desire for revenge. Harry marveled at her altered state. When he begged her to descend and offer a proper welcome to George Washington, who had accompanied him, the lady's agitation spiked dangerously.

"I should shudder at touching his hand," she declared. "He took my son from me! I cannot sleep under the same roof with him."

"He gave me his bed when I was ill, Mother," Harry argued desperately. "And if our George is alive, how can George Washington be responsible for his death? Ah! Please God, let it be only as you say."

"If your brother returns—and he will—it will not be through Mr. Washington's help," Madam Esmond insisted. "He neither defended George on the field nor brought him out of it."

"But he tended me most kindly during my fever," Harry interjected. "He was still recovering himself when he gave up his bed to me, thinking only of his friend when any other man would have thought only of himself."

"A friend! A pretty friend!" the lady sneered. "Of all His Excellency's aides-de-camp, my gentleman is the only one who returns unwounded. The brave and noble fall, but he remains untouched. I entrusted my boy to him—the pride of my life, whom he swore to defend with his own life—and he leaves my George in the forest, bringing me back himself! Oh, what a welcome I must give him!"

"No gentleman," Harry cried warmly, "was ever refused shelter under my grandfather's roof."

"Oh no—no gentleman!" the little widow retorted sharply. "Let us go down, if you like, son, and pay our respects to this one. Will you please give me your arm?"

Taking an arm that could hardly support her weight, she descended the broad stairs into the apartment where the Colonel sat. She performed a stiff, ceremonious curtsey and placed one of her small, trembling hands in his.

"I wish that our meeting had been happier, Colonel Washington," she said softly.

"You do not grieve more than I do that it is otherwise, madam," the Colonel replied calmly. "I might have wished the meeting spared, that I might not have kept you from friends you are naturally anxious to see—that my boy's indisposition had not detained you. Home, his good nurse Mountain, his mother, and our good Doctor Dempster will soon restore him. 'Twas scarce necessary, Colonel, that you, with so many military and domestic affairs on your hands, should turn doctor as well."

"Harry was ill and weak, and I thought it my duty to ride by him," the Colonel faltered.

"You yourself, sir, have endured the fatigues and dangers of the campaign in a most wonderful manner," the widow said, curtseying again, her dark eyes unreadable. "I wish to Heaven someone else had come back in my place!"
