As dusk bled pink over the island’s ridge, the rich, steamy scent of bear-paw stew coiled out of the fire pot, thick and savory enough to make even our usually patient stomachs growl. We huddled around the crackling flames, my wife and I, craning our ears for the first sound of our four young explorers returning from their dawn ride. We’d fussed over them all morning, and every distant rustle made us lean forward, half expecting to spot them trotting across the clearing.

At last, the sharp thud of hooves on packed earth hit our ears. A tiny troop of riders burst over the far rise at a gallop, and a ringing, whooping cheer split the air. We sprang to our feet to meet them, and they tumbled laughing off their steeds, turning the horses loose to graze on the sweet grass before crowding around us, practically bursting to show off their plunder and tell their tales.

They looked like absolute rapscallions: Franz and Jack each had a fuzzy young fawn slung over their back, the four tied-together little legs sticking straight out under their chins. Fritz’s game bag bulged oddly, round lumps and sharp bumps shifting under the canvas, and every few seconds a tiny twitch proved there was living cargo inside.

“Hurrah for the hunt, father!” Jack yelled, bouncing so hard his fawn shifted. “There’s nothing like real wild chase! Storm and Grumble flew across the grass plain like they had wings! We ran those little antelopes right off their feet before we could catch them!”

“Wait till you hear the rest!” Franz put in, grinning. “Fritz’s got two fluffy angora rabbits in his bag, and we found wild honey! And a clever cuckoo – a honeyguide, Fritz says – led us straight to the hive!”

“My brothers forgot the biggest win,” Fritz cut in, puffing out his chest proudly. “We herded an entire wild herd of antelopes right through the Gap into our protected valley! They’re all shut in there, ours to hunt or breed whenever we want!”

“Well done, lads!” I cheered, clapping him on the back. “But far more precious than all your game is the fact that you’re all back safe and sound. Now sit, and tell us the whole story from the start.”

Fitz launched into his tale: they’d galloped hard down Glen Verdant to the rocky barrier pass, the morning air cool on their faces, the horses running flat out most of the way. Once through the Gap, they slowed, scanning the ground for game, and crested a grassy hill to spot two herds of sleek, horned antelope grazing beside the stream below. He’d had the bright idea not to waste a shot, but to drive the whole herd back through the Gap into our lands, where they’d be a permanent food source. The boys and the two dogs had formed a semicircle behind the biggest herd, yelling and waving their hats to chase the antelope toward the narrow pass. The herd had tried to turn and bolt a dozen times, but they’d blocked every escape route until the lead antelope darted through the Gap mouth, and the rest had followed right after.

“Brilliant!” I said. “But how are you going to stop them wandering back out the Gap whenever they feel like it?”

“We thought of that!” Fritz laughed. “We strung a rope all the way across the defile, strung it with rags, feathers, and shiny bits of shell that dance and flutter in the wind. I read in that Cape travel book how the Hottentots use that exact trick to pen antelopes – those skittish things will never dare cross that spooky fluttering line.”

“Clever of you to remember what you read,” I said, then shook my head. “Though I will warn you about those rabbits in your bag – they breed so fast you’ll have an island overrun with the little pests in six months if you’re not careful.”

“That’s why I’m putting them on Whale Island!” Fritz explained. “There’s tons of clover and grass there, and they’ll never swim all the way back here. It’ll be a perfect little warren, no trouble at all. And these two? My eagle caught them for me, unhurt. I rewarded him with the third one he brought down, dead, for a job well done.”

“Father, listen to my part now!” Jack interrupted, shoving Fritz playfully aside. “He’ll go on for hours if you let him. We were chasing the fawns, right? Fritz had run off after the rabbits, the dogs flushed these two little fawns out of the grass, and off they went, bounding like they were flying. We outran the dogs in ten minutes, chased them till they dropped, completely worn out and too scared to move. That’s when the stupid cuckoo started teasing us, hopping just out of reach and calling at us like it was making faces. Franz thought it was an enchanted princess, I was gonna shoot it to break the spell, but Fritz stopped me, said it was a honeyguide leading us to honey. Sure enough, it stopped right at a hollow tree full of bees! I was in such a hurry to get the honey I tossed a handful of lit matches right into the hole, and –”

He paused, rubbing his puffy, red, bee-stung face. “It didn’t kill them. It made them furious. They swarmed out all over me, stung me every place they could reach. I galloped off on Storm as fast as I could, and it felt like hours before I shook them off. My face is burning – I’m going to make mother slather it with that aloe salve right now!” He darted off before I could tease him about his very bad honey-hunting plan.

I decided then to go along with Fritz’s rabbit plan: Whale Island was remote, it would make a perfect safe little farm for rabbits, and if we ever got trapped there in an emergency, we’d have a steady source of fresh meat waiting for us. The rest of the night passed easy: we feasted on the rich, tender bear paws, their odd vague resemblance to human hands didn’t stop a single one of us from devouring the meal, even when Jack cackled out his “Fee-fi-fo-fum” giant joke right in the middle of dinner. We smoked the rest of the bear meat, rendered down all the fat to store for cooking and lamp oil, and collected a huge pile of sturdy bamboo poles.

At dawn the next day, I roused the boys early. Ernest chose to stay back to help my wife and pore over his natural history books, so the rest of us – Fritz on the mule, me on Lightfoot, Jack and Franz on their usual steeds, the two hunting dogs trotting at our heels – galloped off to harvest the toxic red gum from the euphorbia trees, and check on the ostrich nest we’d spotted half-buried in the sand dunes days earlier.

We made fast time across the Green Valley, passed Turtle Marsh, and filled our water flasks before reaching the sandy rise. I let Jack and Franz gallop ahead to stretch their horses’ legs, while Fritz and I stopped to collect the coagulated red euphorbia gum from the incisions I’d cut in the tree trunks weeks earlier. I rolled the sticky stuff into balls and stuffed it into a sealed bamboo jar.

“Father, didn’t you say this sap is deadly poison?” Fritz asked. “Why collect so much of it?”

“It is – the Cape natives use it to poison water holes to hunt big game,” I told him. “I’m going to use it to drive off any roving apes that try to raid our stores, and to tan our animal skins so insects won’t eat through them.”

Just then, a great flapping of wings burst out from the sand ahead. Four giant ostriches, a glossy black-plumed male leading three ashen grey females, exploded out of their hidden nests and ran straight toward us, so fast their long legs blurred.

Jack and Franz whooped and charged to herd them right into our line. Fritz had already wrapped soft cloth around his eagle’s beak the night before, so the bird couldn’t injure a captured ostrich, and he unhooded it now. The eagle soared high, then dove straight for the lead male ostrich’s head, talons brushing his eyes, panicking the big bird so bad he lost all his speed, stumbling in the sand. Jack hurled his lasso, and the coiled rope wrapped tight around the ostrich’s wings and legs, dragging him crashing to the ground. The rest of the flock fled out of sight over the dunes, and we leaped off our horses to secure our prize.

The ostrich thrashed so hard his powerful legs kicked craters in the sand, one blow nearly grazing my thigh hard enough to shatter bone. I grabbed my heavy wool coat and my hunting bag, and tossed them both over his head, blocking out all the light. As soon as the dark fell over his eyes, he went completely still, all the fight draining out of him. We strapped a wide sealskin harness around his body, tied long stout cords to either side, then looped a loose cord around his legs to stop him from bolting at full gallop.

“Remember how they tame wild elephants in India?” Fritz said, grinning. “They tie the new elephant between two already tame ones. We don’t have two tame ostriches, but Storm and Grumble will do the job just fine!”

We led the two horses up on either side of the now-sitting ostrich, tied the ostrich’s harness cords tight to their saddles. Jack and Franz mounted, whips in hand, and we pulled the cloths off the ostrich’s eyes. He blinked, got to his feet with a bound, and tried to bolt forward – but the stout cords pulled him up sharp, bringing him to his knees. He struggled a few more times, then seemed to accept his fate, falling into a steady fast trot between the two horses, the boys yelling and laughing beside him.

Fritz and I left the boys to lead the ostrich home slow, and we headed back to the ostrich nest. The eggs were still warm, the mother only just having left them. We left half the eggs buried to hatch wild, packed the rest carefully in a lined sack, and slung it across my saddle. We caught up to the rest of the group soon after, and made it back to camp without further incident.

My wife stared at the giant ostrich when we came into view, hands on her hips, half amused and half exasperated. “My dearest husband,” she said, “do you think our stores are so overflowing that we need to bring home a seven-foot bird that eats pebbles and grass and devours whole handfuls of nails? We already have a whole farm full of goats, chickens, antelope, dogs, monkeys – you’ve added an ostrich. Next I suppose you’ll dig up an iron mine to feed it!”

“Useless? No way, mother!” Jack yelled immediately, throwing his arm around the ostrich’s long neck. “He’s going to be my new steed! Faster than any horse on the whole island! I’ll make a saddle for him, and I’ll ride him everywhere.”

The rest of the boys started bickering over who got to claim what part of the ostrich, until I laughed and made a ruling: Jack had been the one to lasso him and bring him down, so if he took full charge of training and taming the bird, the ostrich was his, no arguments.

By dawn the next day, our whole little caravan was packed and heading back to Rockburg. We couldn’t risk the ostrich bolting, so we kept him tied between Storm and Grumble, harnessed the cow to the supply cart so the two horses could stay at their posts, and loaded the cart with all our plunder, plus space for my wife to ride when she grew tired. At the mouth of the Gap, we tore down the fluttering feather line the boys had strung, and built a tall, thick palisade of bamboo poles all the way across the pass, so no antelope could ever escape our valley. We stopped at the sugarcane grove to pick up the peccary hams we’d left smoking, gathered a sack full of sweet-smelling vanilla seeds my wife had spotted growing wild, and paused for the night at our Woodlands farm rest stop.

We woke the next morning to find our little farmyard in perfect condition: the chickens had multiplied, the goats were fat, and we spotted the herd of antelope the boys had herded through the Gap grazing in the trees nearby, completely settled in their new home. We repaired the stalls and the little farm cottage, then pushed on at dawn the next day, reaching Rockburg by midday. We threw open the windows, got all the animals settled in their stalls, and unloaded all our treasures from the cart. For the time being, we tied the ostrich between two stout bamboo posts right outside the front door.

Fritz and I took the boat out the next morning: first to Whale Island, where we turned the two angora rabbits loose to make their new warren, then to Shark Island, where we released the two fuzzy young fawns to graze safe from predators. We got back just in time to find Jack trotting up the path, grinning ear to ear, carrying a wicker basket holding an eel almost as long as he was. He and Ernest had set fishing lines the night before, caught the huge eel, and it turned out so tender and delicious we ate half for dinner, and salted the rest away to store in our root cellar.

We spent the next few days working around the homestead: we planted wheat, barley and maize in a new cleared field, the horses pulling the plow while we dug and hoed the soil, our arms sore and aching by the end of each day, fully living the old scripture of earning your bread with the sweat of your brow. We sowed vanilla, pepper and flowering creeper seeds all around the pillars of our veranda to give us shade and scent in the summer months, built enclosed hen coops to keep the new chicks safe from our curious pet monkey Knips, and hung Ernest’s stinky rat-skins outside the house where the smell wouldn’t make anyone gag – though Jack still pretended to faint every time he walked past them to tease his brother.

The ostrich still refused to tame, though. No amount of gentle petting would calm him down, so I used the same trick that had tamed our wild eagle: I held his head over a pot of smoking tobacco fumes. He fell over completely motionless, and when he woke he was quiet – but he refused to eat for three whole days, growing weaker and thinner by the hour. We all panicked, until my wife stepped in, rolling soft balls of maize meal mixed with melted butter, and slipping them one by one into the ostrich’s beak. He swallowed the first, then the second, then the third, and soon his appetite was back, and all his wild, skittish fight was gone.

After a month of gentle training, the ostrich was completely docile: he’d trot, gallop, stop on command, eat grain right out of our hands. I got to work building a custom rig for him: I made a soft leather hood that fit over his head, with eye holes fitted with spring-loaded blinkers tied to the reins. Pull the right rein, the right blinker shut and the ostrich turned left. Pull the left rein, the left blinker shut and he turned right. Pull both reins, both blinkers slammed shut and he stood stock still instantly. I built him a sturdy, high-backed saddle modeled after an old cavalry trooper’s saddle, fastened tight across his wings and breast so it could never slip off his sloped back, perfect for Jack to ride without ever falling off. By the time we were done, Jack had a steed faster than any horse on the entire island, ready for a hundred new adventures.
