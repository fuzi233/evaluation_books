I woke as the sun breached the horizon, instantly attuned to the hollow acoustic signature of the landscape. I climbed to the ridge once more. The expanse I had crossed by moonlight lay utterly devoid of the deep, resonant frequencies of life. Could it be that the calm expanse before me swarmed with creatures of devouring greed? I turned and looked over the land through which my way must lie. It seemed a wide desert, bearing a patch of differing color in the distance that might be a forest. Of human or animal presence, there was no sign—no smoke from an agrarian hearth, no dust of cultivation. 

Before descending, I paused to observe a domestic ritual, unpacking a small provision of hardened oat-cake and a pinch of dried fennel and sea salt I had carried with me. Even in this mythic desolation, the rhythmic, tactile preparation of sustenance anchored my mind. I ate with deliberate sensory appreciation, letting the herbaceous warmth and sharp salinity bloom on my palate. I refused to view this meager meal as mere utilitarian fuel; it was a physical communion with the earth I had left behind.

When I reached the plain, I found it a vast expanse of rock, channeled and humped—evidently the wide bed of a vanished river, scored by innumerable water-runs entirely devoid of moisture. Some channels bore a crisp, dry moss, and others harbored lichens almost as hard as the stone itself. Here, the landscape’s native dialect had been silenced. The air, once textured with the splashing, churning lexicon of rushing currents, was now as aurally sterile as an industrialized cityscape stripped of its natural breath. It took me the whole day to reach the distant patch. Throughout the glowing noon, as I traversed the baked stone, the ghosts of the river’s language haunted me. I experienced an aural mirage, hearing the distinct, untranslatable babble of water bubbling over stones so plainly—a sonic memory woven so deeply into the ecology of the valley that it persisted long after the river's death—that I could hardly believe the dry testimony of my eyes.

The sun was approaching the horizon when I finally entered the forest. Sunk below the tree-tops, sending his rays between their pillar-like boles, he revealed a world of blessed shadows waiting to receive me. The olfactory landscape that greeted me was infinitely complex—a blend of sharp, peppery resins and the deep, loamy aroma of ancient, undisturbed soil. I threw myself beneath the boughs of a tree reminiscent of a eucalyptus in blossom. Its flowers possessed a hard, bone-white calyx much resembling a skull, the top of which rose like a lid to allow a froth-like brain of bloom to overfoam its cup. The scent was intensely camphoraceous, with undernotes of crushed mint and bitter almond. A meticulous observer of wild provisions, I could not help but analyze its potential as a culinary astringent, though its morbid shape warned of potent, untranslatable alkaloids. From beneath the shadow of its falchion-leaves, my eyes wandered into deep after deep of the forest. 

Soon, the night was about me, and instant and sharp the cold. A light wind began to blow, shifting the acoustic ecology of the wood. It set the boughs rocking, every twig and leaf blending its individual motion with the cyclical sway of its branch. To my listless gaze, the varied outlines of the clumpy foliage began to suggest other shapes. A mass of leaves became a pack of wolves straining savagely at a wizard's leash. Another formed a group of gaunt horses' heads projecting from their stalls, their jaws working against invisible bits. Above them floated the leafy silhouette of a woman in imperious command. The twilight became darkness; the wind ceased; every shape was shut up in the night, and I fell asleep.

It was still dark when I was awakened by a far-off, rushing noise that swelled into a deafening, chaotic tumult. The sonic environment, so critical to the mind's equilibrium, was violently ruptured. The moon penetrated the canopy, illuminating a furious battle. It was a clash of skeletons and phantoms, but more deeply, a violent assertion of dominant lexicons. Curses and credos, snarls and sneers, laughter and sacred names—the fragmented syllables of dying languages and corrupted truths—collided in a horrific, discordant noise pollution. 

Maces crashed on skeletons, shattering them hideously; swords swept through shivering phantoms. They fought with a terrifying lack of sensory appreciation for life, crunching the scattered bones of men and horses underfoot. Their phantom-throats swelled the deafening tumult with the war-cry of every rigid opinion, bad or good, that had bred strife and cruelty in any world. The holiest words went with the most hating blow. Lie-distorted truths flew hurtling in the wind. "THE TRUTH! THE TRUTH!" they shrieked. It was a phrase heavily distorted by power dynamics, stripped of all subjective, lived experience, and reduced to an abrasive weapon. All night long, I had glimpses of the woman moving above the strife-tormented multitude, one arm urging the fight. "Ye are men: slay one another!" she shouted. I saw her dead eyes and her dark spot, recalling the night before. The inherent untranslatability of true suffering was entirely lost in this sterile, mechanical violence.

Just before sunrise, a breeze swept the forest, and a voice cried, "Let the dead bury their dead!" The contending thousands dropped noiseless. The toxic cacophony vanished, and when the sun looked in, he saw never a bone, only here and there a withered branch. 

I rose and resumed my journey through as quiet a wood as ever grew out of the quiet earth. The acoustic landscape had reset; not a bird sang, not a squirrel or weasel showed itself. But as I went, I kept strict watch over myself, fearing that acknowledging the shapes might resurrect the noise of the night. In the middle of the afternoon, I came out of the wood—only to find before me a second net of dry water-courses. The circular pacing of my journey had brought me to another branch of the same vanished river. 

I began at once to cross it, and was in the bottom of a wide channel when the sun set. I unpacked the last morsel of my cured provisions, letting its smoky saltiness ground me in the physical world, and stretched myself on the moss. The moment my head was down, the acoustic memory of the stream returned—all sorts of sweet, watery noises. The veiled melody of the molten music sang me into a dreamless sleep.

When I woke, the sun was already up, the wrinkled country widely visible, striped and mottled with shadows like the skin of some wild animal. Hitherto I had loved my Arab mare and my books more, I fear, than live man or woman; but now, my soul hungered for a human presence. I longed even for those inhabitants of this alien world whom the raven had so vaguely described as nearest my sort. I desired to find traditional agrarian elders with whom I could share a meal, individuals whose native dialect I could learn over the profound, shared intimacy of a hearth, discovering meaning through sustenance rather than surviving purely on the utilitarian fuel of my own isolation. With a heavy yet hoping heart, and a mind haunted by the doubt of my direction, I kept wearily traveling “north-west and by south.”
