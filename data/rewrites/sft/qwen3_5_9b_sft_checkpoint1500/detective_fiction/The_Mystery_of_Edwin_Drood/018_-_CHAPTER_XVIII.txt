About this season, a stranger materialized within Cloisterham. He was a figure of considerable age, distinguished by a shock of white hair and eyebrows of profound blackness. Clad in a surcoat of tight-fitting blue, a buff waistcoat, and trousers of gray hue, he possessed a distinct military bearing. Yet, at the Crozier—the establishment's standard inn, where he deposited a portmanteau—he introduced himself publicly as "an idle dog" subsisting solely upon his inherited means. Furthermore, he declared his intent to secure quarters in the picturesque old city for a duration of one or two months, with the ultimate ambition of establishing a permanent residence there. These pronouncements were delivered with theatrical flair from the coffee-room, standing with his back to a vacant hearth while awaiting a meal of fried sole, a veal cutlet, and a measure of sherry. They were heard by all who happened to listen, or ignore, and absorbed entirely by the waiter, whose business was currently in a chronic state of lethargy.

This gentleman's cranium was disproportionately large, and his mane of white hair was notably thick and abundant. "I presume, waiter," he remarked, shaking his coiffure with the vigorous, preparatory motion of a Newfoundland before sitting down to dine, "that a suitable accommodation for a single bachelor might be secured in these parts, hmm?"

The waiter harbored no doubts regarding this feasibility.

"Some antiquity," stipulated the gentleman. "Retrieve my hat briefly from that peg, will you? No, I need not wear it; examine its interior. What inscription adorns it?"

The waiter recited: "Datchery."

"Now you possess my nomenclature," stated the gentleman. "Resuspend it. I was observing that 'something old' is precisely what I seek; something eccentric, isolated, and removed; something venerable, architecturally imposing, and undeniably inconvenient."

"We boast a splendid selection of inconvenient lodgings within the town, sir, I believe," replied the waiter, displaying a modest confidence in the municipality's resources in that specific regard; "indeed, I am convinced we could accommodate your desires in that respect, however fastidious you may prove. However, an *architectural* lodging!" This concept seemed to perplex the waiter's faculties, causing him to shake his head vigorously.

"Anything with a cathedral-like quality, now," suggested Mr. Datchery.

"Mr. Tope," interjected the waiter, his countenance brightening as he stroked his chin, "would likely be the most competent authority on this subject."

"Who is Mr. Tope?" inquired Dick Datchery.

The waiter elucidated that the individual served as the Verger, noting that Mrs. Tope had formerly let lodgings herself or offered them for lease; however, as no tenant had ever accepted, her window-poster—a long-standing institution of Cloisterham—had vanished. It was presumed it had slipped from its frame one day and remained lost forever.

"I shall pay a visit to Mrs. Tope," declared Mr. Datchery, "following my repast."

Consequently, after consuming his dinner, he received due direction to the location and departed. Yet, owing to the Crozier's tendency toward seclusion and the fatal precision of the waiter's instructions, he quickly became disoriented. He wandered aimlessly around the Cathedral Tower, catching fleeting glimpses of it, burdened by a general impression that Mrs. Tope's dwelling resided somewhere in its immediate vicinity. Like children playing "hot boiled beans and very good butter," he felt the heat of proximity when the Tower was visible, and the chill of distance when it was obscured.

He grew thoroughly chilled when he stumbled upon a fragment of burial ground where an unfortunate sheep grazed. The animal was miserable, for a hideous urchin was stoning it through the railings. Having already crippled one leg, the youth was enthusiastically engaged in the benevolent, sporting endeavor of disabling the remaining three, thereby reducing the beast to the earth.

"'Tis him agen!" shrieked the boy as the poor creature leaped; "and he struck a dint in his wool."

"Cease!" commanded Mr. Datchery. "Do you not perceive you have disabled him?"

"You lie," retorted the sportsman. "'E injured himself. I witnessed it, and I gave 'im a 'alf-warning to desist from bruising 'is master's mutton further."

"Approach me."

"I shall not; I will approach when you can capture me."

"Remain there, then, and indicate the location of Mr. Tope's residence."

"How can I remain here and demonstrate where Topeseses resides, when Topeseses lies across the Kingfreederal, over the crossings, and around countless corners? Stoop-id! Ya-a-ah!"

"Indicate the site, and I shall provide recompense."

"Follow, then."

This brisk exchange concluded with the boy leading the path. After a short interval, he halted at some distance from an arched passage, gesturing.

"Gaze yonther. Observe that window and door?"

"That belongs to Tope?"

"A falsehood; it does not. That is Jasper's."

"Indeed?" observed Mr. Datchery, casting a second glance of genuine curiosity.

"Yes, and I am not proceeding any closer to HIM, I assure you."

"Why not?"

"'Cos I am not prepared to have my legs lifted and my braces burst and my throat constricted; certainly not if I know it, and certainly not by HIM. Wait until I launch a jolly good flint at the back of 'is jolly old 'ead some day! Now gaze across the arch; not the side where Jasper's door stands; the opposite side."

"I comprehend."

"A short distance inward, on that side, there exists a low door descending two steps. That is Topeseses, marked by a hoval plate bearing his name."

"Excellent. Behold," said Mr. Datchery, extracting a shilling. "You owe me half of this sum."

"A lie! I owe you naught; I have never encountered you."

"I insist you owe me half of this, for I possess not a sixpence in my pocket. Therefore, the next time we meet, you shall perform some other service for me, as payment."

"All right, take hold."

"What is your name, and wherein do you reside?"

"Deputy. Travellers' Twopenny, 'cross the green."

Instantly, Deputy fled with the shilling, fearful lest Mr. Datchery repent, though he paused at a safe distance, dancing a demonic jig expressive of the transaction's irrevocability, hoping to provoke unease in the stranger's mind.

Mr. Datchery, removing his hat to grant his shock of white hair another vigorous shake, appeared wholly resigned. He proceeded to the destination indicated.

Mr. Tope's official domicile, connected via an upper stairway to Mr. Jasper's quarters (thus explaining Mrs. Tope's domestic attendance), was of modest proportions and resembled a cool dungeon. Its ancient walls were massive, and the rooms appeared less constructed and more excavated from the masonry itself. The principal door opened immediately onto a chamber of indescribable shape, featuring a groined roof that, in turn, opened into another chamber of similarly formless geometry, also crowned with a groined roof. Their windows were diminutive, embedded deep within the wall thickness. These two chambers, stifling in their atmosphere and dimly lit by natural sunlight, constituted the apartments Mrs. Tope had long offered to an unappreciative urban clientele. Mr. Datchery, however, proved far more appreciative. He discovered that if he seated himself with the main door ajar, he would enjoy the transient sociability of all passersby at the gateway and sufficient illumination. He noted that if Mr. and Mrs. Tope, residing above, utilized a minor side stair descending directly into the Precincts via a door opening outward—to the surprise and inconvenience of a limited pedestrian public traversing a narrow lane—he would exist in solitude, akin to a separate residence. Finding the rent moderate and everything quaintly inconvenient as he desired, he agreed immediately to take the lodging, paying cash down with possession slated for the following evening, on the proviso that reference could be made to Mr. Jasper, who occupied the gatehouse adjacent to the Verger's hole-in-the-wall.

Mrs. Tope described the poor gentleman as exceedingly solitary and melancholy, yet expressed certainty that he would "speak for her." She asked if Mr. Datchery had perhaps heard rumors concerning the events of the previous winter.

Mr. Datchery confessed his knowledge of the matter was as confused as possible when attempting recall. He besought Mrs. Tope's pardon when she found it necessary to correct him on every detail of his summary, pleading that as a single bachelor drifting through life upon his means as idly as possible, and given the constant manner in which individuals dispose of others, it rendered it difficult for a man of easy temperament to keep the circumstances of various cases unmixed in his mind.

Since Mr. Jasper proved willing to vouch for Mrs. Tope, and Mr. Datchery had presented his card, he was invited to ascend the postern staircase. The Mayor was present, according to Mr. Tope, but he was not to be considered as company, as he and Mr. Jasper were intimate friends.

"I beg forgiveness," said Mr. Datchery, executing a leg with his hat tucked under his arm as he addressed both gentlemen equally.
