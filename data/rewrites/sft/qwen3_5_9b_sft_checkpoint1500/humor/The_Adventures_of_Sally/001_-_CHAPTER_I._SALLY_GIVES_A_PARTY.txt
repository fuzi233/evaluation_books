Sally surveyed the long table with a smile that seemed to illuminate the room itself. For the first time, a genuine happiness settled within her. The initial stiffness—the awkward, formal silence born of her brother Fillmore's dazzling, inappropriate white waistcoat—had evaporated. Now, the transient residents and permanent boarders of Mrs. Meecher's boarding house were loosening their ties, their voices rising in laughter and chatter. The party, which had stumbled onto its feet with uncertain steps, was blossoming into the roaring success Sally had envisioned.

At the far end of the table, the conversation had inevitably drifted to the great, life-altering topic: Sally's inheritance and how she intended to wield it. Nothing quite so intoxicates the soul as the prospect of spending other people's money, and Sally's guests were already busy drafting a grand budget for her. Having heard rumors that the sum was staggering, their suggestions were naturally ambitious and expansive.

"Let me tell you," declared Augustus Bartlett, cutting a swathe through the air with his brisk, authoritative gestures. "If I were you, I'd sink two hundred thousand into a solid, safe bond issue. We just released one that you must consider. With the remainder?" He leaned forward, his eyes gleaming with financial fervor. "Play around! Have a flutter on anything promising. Multiple Steel is looking mighty hot. They say it'll hit one hundred and fifty by next Saturday!"

Beside him sat Elsa Doland, a vision of prettiness with large, expressive eyes, who held a different vision entirely.

"Buy a theater, Sally," Elsa urged gently. "Put on something truly excellent."

"And you'll lose every bean you've got," countered a mild young man with a resonant baritone, sitting opposite. "If I possessed a few hundred thousand," he mused dreamily, "I'd stake every penny on Benny Whistler for the heavyweight title. I possess inside information that Battling Tuke has been bought out and intends to fold in the seventh round..."

"Listen here," cut in another voice, eager to join the fray. "Let me tell you what I'd do with four hundred thousand..."

"If I had four hundred thousand," Elsa chimed in, "the first thing I'd do is pay my bill from last week, which is due this morning."

Sally sprang up instantly. Flitting down the length of the table, she draped her arm around Elsa's shoulder and whispered conspiratorially, "Elsa darling, are you truly broke? If you are, you know I'll—"

Elsa laughed, a sound rich with amusement. "You're an angel, Sally. There's no one like you. You'd give your last cent to anyone. But I'm not broke, my dearest friend. I've just returned from the road, and I've saved a fortune. I merely said that to tease you."

Relieved, Sally returned to her seat. She observed that the company had crystallized into two distinct camps. The conservative faction, led by the shrewd Augustus Bartlett, had firmly resolved to allocate three hundred thousand to Liberty Bonds and the balance to safe real estate. Meanwhile, the smaller, more daring contingent, captivated by the mild young man's insider scoop, had already placed Sally's funds on Benny Whistler, carefully parcelling out the bets to avoid moving the market. To these connoisseurs of the inner circle, Battling Tuke's reputation was so formidable that the mild young man was confident a three-to-one payout awaited those who acted cunningly and without haste. Yet, something told Sally it was time to correct these misconceptions.

"I don't know where you're getting your figures," she interjected, her voice cutting through the noise, "but I fear they are incorrect. I have exactly twenty-five thousand dollars."

The announcement produced a chilling ripple through the group. To these masters of half-million-dollar maneuvers, twenty-five thousand seemed almost comically diminutive, a sum suitable perhaps for a taxi fare rather than empire-building. However, the juggernauts of finance adjusted their gears. After all, miracles could be wrought even with a pittance.

"If I had twenty-five thousand," rallied Augustus Bartlett, recovering from the shock, "I'd buy Amalgamated..."

"If I had twenty-five thousand..." Elsa began tentatively.

"If I'd had twenty-five thousand back in 1900," mused a gloomy gentleman with spectacles, staring vacantly into space, "I could have sparked a revolution in Paraguay."

He brooded somberly on the paths not taken.

"Well, I'll tell you precisely what I intend to do," Sally announced, her spirits lifting. "I'm starting with a trip to Europe—France, specifically. I've heard France spoken of in rapturous terms. Once I secure my passport, I'll loaf there for a few weeks, then return to scout for a cozy little business to invest in, ensuring a lifetime of luxury. Any objections?"

"Not a single cent on Benny Whistler," the mild young man countered softly.

"No Benny Whistler for me," Sally replied firmly. "I wouldn't accept him even if you handed him to me personally. If I wish to lose money, I shall go to Monte Carlo and conduct the operation properly."

"Monte Carlo!" The gloomy man perked up immediately at the magical name. "I visited Monte Carlo in '97, and if I'd possessed another fifty dollars... just fifty... I'd have..."

At the far end of the table, a subtle disturbance rippled through the air—a dry cough, followed by the screech of a chair leg against the floor. Slowly, with the effortless grace that actors of the old school mastered in the pre-radio era, Mr. Maxwell Faucitt, the boarding house's most senior resident, rose to his feet.

"Ladies," Mr. Faucitt said, bowing with courtly precision, "... and gentlemen." He paused, his gaze sweeping beneath his white, venerable eyebrows to quell the restless energy of several younger males who showed signs of impatience. "... I feel compelled to say a few words."

His audience did not appear surprised. Life in a great metropolis like New York was prolific with incidents, and perhaps someday Mr. Faucitt might feel capable of remaining silent, but that day had not arrived. From the moment the meal commenced, it had been clear to all that expecting the old gentleman to refrain from speech on the eve of Sally Nicholas's farewell dinner was a delusion of grandeur. Braced for the performance, and buoyed by Miss Nicholas's abundant hospitality, they settled back with equanimity. A potential uprising from the Marvellous Murphys—fresh arrivals who had performed their equilibristic feats in Bushwick the prior week—took shape as an attempt to heckle the speaker, but it collapsed under the cold, piercing stare of their hostess. Though their acquaintance with Sally was brief, both lissome young gentlemen harbored immense admiration for her.

It bore recording that this admiration was well-founded. To please one, it would require a saint, for Sally was difficult not to admire. She was a petite, trim figure, a wisp of humanity with tiny hands and feet, radiating a smile of infinite warmth and a dimple that danced in and out of existence on her rounded chin. When she laughed—which occurred frequently—her eyes vanished, leaving only a mask of pure joy. Her hair was a soft cascade of brown, and she possessed an air of distinction that set her apart from the majority of Mrs. Meecher's eclectic clientele. Above all, she carried youth like a banner. In approving of Sally, the Murphys had demonstrated no lapse from their high critical standards.

"I have been asked," Mr. Faucitt continued, smoothing his vest, "though I am aware that others here are far more worthy of such a task—men who might be termed Brutuses, whereas I am but a Marc Antony—I have been requested to propose a toast to the health of our enchanting hostess, coupled with the name of her brother, our cherished friend, Fillmore Nicholas."

The gentleman in question, seated at the speaker's end, acknowledged the tribute with a brief, condescending nod. It was the nod of a man conscious of being surrounded by social inferiors, yet striving to remain benevolent. Seeing it, Sally momentarily debated the merits of hurling an orange at her brother. One lay conveniently at her hand, and his glistening shirt-front presented a perfect target. Yet, she restrained herself. After all, if a hostess succumbed to primitive impulses, chaos would ensue. Having just subdued the rebellious Murphys with a frown, she realized that initiating a fruit-throwing campaign, even with noble motives, would weaken her moral authority in that sector.

She leaned back with a heavy sigh. The temptation had been formidable. A democratic soul, she despised pomposity above all else. While she loved her brother, she could not deceive herself: since affluence had descended upon him mere months prior, Fillmore Nicholas had become insufferably pompous. If there existed any young men for whom inherited wealth served as an improvement, Fillmore was certainly not one. He now regarded himself as a Man of Destiny. Conversing with him felt akin to receiving an audience from a standoffish monarch. It had taken Sally over an hour to persuade him to abandon his Riverside Drive apartment and descend to the boarding house for this special occasion. Upon arrival, clad in faultless evening attire, he had made the rest of the party resemble a gathering of tramp cyclists.
