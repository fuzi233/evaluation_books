*Perigot: As the bonny lass passed by, Willie: Hey ho, bonny lass! P: She rode at me with glancing eye, W: As clear as the crystal glass. P: All as the sunny beam so bright, W: Hey ho, the sunnebeam! P: Glanceth from Phoebus’ face forthright, W: So love into thy heart did stream.*
—SPENSER, *Shepard’s Calendar*

“The kindliest symptom, yet the most alarming crisis in the ticklish state of youth; the nourisher and destroyer of hopeful wits… the servitude above freedom; the gentle mind’s religion; the liberal superstition.”

The first hint of the unforeseen snowstorm brewing in their quiet corner of the shire looked like nothing more than a wisp of bright white cloud, drifting soft against a perfect blue sky. Anna Gascoigne was the only soul who held Rex’s secret close, even though for the first time in their lives her brother had not breathed a word to her about the thing that consumed every waking thought of his. He simply assumed she knew, as siblings always do. And for the first time, Anna could not bring herself to voice the dread that was coiling tighter in her chest every hour.

If Rex had fallen for someone else more than her, it should have stung, but her worry for him drowned out every other feeling. Anna adored their cousin Gwendolen—she would say with perfect, simple sincerity that Gwendolen was always kind to her, that it was only right she should bend to her cousin’s brilliant, unyielding will. But Gwendolen was a creature of beauty and mystery to her: some wild, glowing thing, whose appetites no one could guess at, that might at any moment devour all the small, tender things Anna loved best. And now Anna was heavy with a truth she dared not speak aloud: Gwendolen would never, ever care for Rex the way he ached for her. All the soft, gentle, earnest things that mattered most to Anna, to Rex, seemed to slide right off Gwendolen like rain off a polished riding boot. It was far easier to imagine her scorning his devotion than returning it—she was always chasing some grand, extraordinary life far away from their quiet country parsonage. Poor Rex! Their father would be furious if he found out, Rex was far too young to be tangled up in a love so fierce it made him reckless, and Anna had always dreamed she would be his quiet little housekeeper for years and years before any romance ever touched him. How cruel a heart must be, she thought, that could not love her sweet, bright brother back. She was starting to hate her too-fascinating cousin, just a little, at the thought of how much he was about to hurt.

It felt to both of them as if those weeks hummed with a loud, obvious passion anyone could see, plain as day. If anyone had asked, Rex would have said he had no wish to hide it—soon he would have an official engagement to announce to his father, bright and proud. But for the first time he was quiet, secretive not just about his feelings but about little actions Anna had never seen him hide before. She, for her part, went tight with nerves every time her mother or father called her aside to speak privately, half-convinced they were about to confront her about Rex and Gwendolen. But none of the elders noticed a single thing. That roiling, tender young drama played out like a silent pantomime, crystal clear to the two people living it, invisible to the grown-ups who pored over their *Guardian* and *Clerical Gazette*, who saw the young ones’ antics as no more meaningful than the scurrying of busy little ants in the garden path.

“Where are you off to, Rex?” Anna called one soft gray morning, when their father and mother had set off in the carriage for the sessions, and she’d spotted her brother pulling on his thick antigropelos, the closest thing to proper hunting gear he owned.

“Going to watch the hounds throw off at the Three Barns.”

“Are you taking Gwendolen with you?” Anna asked, quiet and timid.

“She told you, did she?”

“No, I just thought… Does Papa know you’re going?”

“Not that I know of. I shouldn’t think he’d bother himself over something so small.”

“You’re taking his horse, then?”

“He knows I ride him whenever I get the chance.”

“Don’t let Gwendolen ride after the hounds, Rex,” Anna begged, a strange, sharp premonition settling heavy on her.

“Why not?” he said, smiling, teasing, already half annoyed.

“Papa and Mamma and Aunt Davilow all say it’s not right for her. They don’t want her doing it.”

“Why would you assume she’d do something she shouldn’t?”

“Gwendolen minds no one when she’s set on something,” Anna bolder now, prickling a little with anger.

“Then she won’t mind me trying to stop her,” Rex joked, brushing off her fear like it was trivial.

“Oh Rex, I can’t bear it. You’re going to make yourself so unhappy.” Anna burst into tears right there in the hall.

“Nannie, Nannie, what on earth is the matter with you?” Rex said, impatient now, hat on and whip in his hand, half out the door.

“She won’t care for you even a little bit—I know she never will!” the poor girl sobbed, whispering the words against her sleeve, every scrap of self-control gone.

Rex flushed bright red, turned on his heel, and hurried out the door without another word, leaving Anna miserable, knowing she’d made a fool of herself and said nothing to change his mind.

He thought of her words as he rode, the way you think of an unpleasant fortune-teller’s warning, even when you laugh it off. But he quickly told himself it was just little Anna being soft, being jealous, being overprotective, and soon he felt guilty for rushing off without soothing her. Every other feeling melted into fierce, stubborn certainty that she was wrong—and that very certainty, laced with the quiet, unspoken doubt he wouldn’t admit even to himself, made him resolve to say the words he’d been holding back for weeks, right there, that very day.

Gwendolen was already mounted, trotting her chestnut mare up and down the avenue, when he reached the gate. She’d brought the groom along as a backup, just in case Rex was late and she had to ride out alone, but as soon as she spotted him she dismissed the man, and the two of them set off free as birds. Gwendolen was giddy, bright as a spark, and Rex thought he had never seen her half so lovely: the clean lines of her riding dress showed off her long white throat, the soft curve of her cheek and chin perfectly. He could not imagine a more perfect girl. To a young lover like Rex, goodness, truth, and beauty were all wrapped up in her, alive and glowing right in front of him. Any neutral onlooker would have thought it only fair that Gwendolen should fall just as hard for him—his face was open, warm, handsome, none of that unnameable, sharp, almost dangerous trace of something wild that made people hesitate a little before fully surrendering to Gwendolen’s charm.

It was a perfect January morning, no threat of rain, the pale gray sky soft and kind over the mild winter land: grassy lane edges dusted with frost, hedgerows strung with bright red berries and full of tiny twittering birds, the bare purple branches of elm trees stretching overhead, the furrows in the fields rich dark brown. The horses’ hooves made a light, musical chime in the quiet, weaving in and out of the sound of their laughter. She teased him for his drab, un-fancy hunting gear, the exact opposite of a dandy, and he drank in that laughter like it was wine. The freshness of the morning tangled with the freshness of their youth, every laugh, every quick glance between them bubbling up from a hidden spring of joy. For them, the whole world was still bright, unwritten, still morning, inside and out. It’s hard not to wish, for just a moment, that the two of them could have promised each other everything right there, that they could have kept that bright joy forever, never to break. That goodness Rex believed in so fiercely was real there, tender as a green new blade of grass, ready to be crushed by one sharp gust of wind, one moment of careless choice.

“Anna’s got it into her head you’re going to ride after the hounds today,” Rex said, the words feeling far more daring, far closer to the big confession he was building up to, than he’d planned.

“Did she?” Gwendolen laughed. “What a little clairvoyant she is!”

“Will you?” Rex asked, half convinced she’d never go against what all the grown-ups had forbidden.

“I don’t know. I never know what I’m going to do till I get there. Clairvoyants are always wrong, aren’t they? They predict what’s likely, and what’s likely is always so dreadfully dull. I do the unlikely thing.”

“Ah, that’s a secret then,” Rex grinned. “Once I know what everyone else would do, I know you’ll do the opposite. That’s a pattern all its own. I’ll be able to predict you perfectly soon. You’ll never surprise me again.”

“Easy,” she laughed, her voice like chimes. “I’d turn right around and do the boring, expected thing just to catch you out.”

“See? You can’t escape being predictable. Contrariness is the strongest pattern of all. You have to give up this plan of yours.”

“No, I won’t,” she said, tossing her head, and every line of her pose was so perfect, so charming, that not a single sharp, arrogant word of hers landed wrong for Rex, it all felt like soft, pretty banter he was desperate to steer toward the confession he wanted to make. “My plan is to do exactly what pleases me.”

“Can you only ever feel what pleases you?” he asked, gentle.

“Of course not. Other people ruin that. If the world was nicer, we’d all only ever feel happy things. Girls’ lives are so stupid. We never get to do what we want.”

“I thought it was the opposite for men,” Rex said. “We’re forced to do all the hard, boring things, we get bored out of our minds, we get hurt. But if a man loved a girl more than anything else in the whole world, he’d do whatever she wanted. You’d get your own way, in the end.”

“I don’t believe that. I’ve never met a married woman who got her own way at all.”

“What sort of things would you want to do, then?” Rex asked, genuinely curious, leaning a little toward her in the saddle.

“Oh, I don’t know!” she said, flighty, giddy, her words spilling off her tongue before she could think them over. “Go to the North Pole, or ride steeplechases, or be a queen in the East like Lady Hester Stanhope!”

“You don’t mean you never want to get married, ever?”

“No, I didn’t say that. Only when I do marry, I won’t live the boring little life every other married woman lives.”

“You could do absolutely anything you wanted, if you married a man who loved you more dearly than anything else in the world,” Rex blurted out, his heart thudding, forgetting all his carefully planned words. “I know someone who loves you like that.”

“Oh, don’t talk about Mr. Middleton, for heaven’s sake!” Gwendolen said, her cheeks flushing bright red, sharp and sudden. “That’s Anna’s favorite story. I hear the hounds! Let’s go on.”

She kicked her chestnut mare into a canter, and Rex had no choice but to follow. Still, he felt encouraged. Gwendolen knew perfectly well her cousin was in love with her, but she never thought it mattered, not really. She’d never once felt that sharp, burning pain of loving someone who might not love you back. She wanted Rex’s little gentle devotion to fill up the rest of his vacation at Pennicote, no messy confessions to spoil the fun, and she was half repulsed, half thrilled at the thought of him saying the words out loud to her. For all her love of being adored, there was a sharp, unyielding maidenly fierceness in her that flinched from direct declarations.

But all those thoughts vanished the second they reached the Three Barns, the air thick with the hounds’ baying, the horses pawing at the ground, the hunt men shouting greetings. Several of the local gentry knew Gwendolen, and she laughed and chatted with all of them, so Rex never got the chance to say another private word to her. The thrill of the chase wrapped around her like a cloak. She’d never ridden to hounds before, she’d only ever said she wanted to, which made her mother fear for her safety, and her uncle the rector declare it was unseemly for a lady of good position—no one of quality in their part of Wessex followed the hunt, he said, except Mrs. Gadsby, the yeoman captain’s wife who’d once been a kitchenmaid and still talked like one. That last argument had held Gwendolen back for weeks, caught between her desire to prove she was free and her horror of being lumped in with Mrs. Gadsby.

But today, none of the respectable local ladies who usually came to watch the throw-off were there to stop themselves from following, and Mrs. Gadsby was nowhere to be seen, either. There was nothing to hold Gwendolen back from that wild, thrumming animal excitement: the hounds’ baying, the horses’ stamping, the men’s loud calls, flashes of bright hunting coats against the soft gray and green of the countryside. She felt half centaur, half wild creature, giddy with the thrill of the chase to come.

Rex would have enjoyed it all too, if he could have stayed close to Gwendolen, instead of being stuck watching her laugh and talk with a dozen other men on their lively, fast horses that swerved all around him, keeping him away.

“Glad to see you out this fine morning, Miss Harleth,” Lord Brackenshaw called out to her, the middle-aged peer in his stained pink hunting coat, easy and lazy as if nothing in the world could ever bother him. “We’re in for a first-rate run. Pity you haven’t joined us before. Ever tried that pretty little chestnut of yours over a ditch? I don’t suppose you’d be scared, would you?”

“Not the least in the world,” Gwendolen said, and it was true—she never feared anything when she was on horseback, in the thick of fun. “I’ve taken him over rails and small ditches all the time, near—”

“Ah, there we go,” Lord Brackenshaw said, casually reining his horse away, as the hounds gave tongue all at once. The whole field surged forward like the earth itself was spinning them along, and Gwendolen went with it, without a single backward glance at Rex. He followed right after her, without thinking. Could he let her ride off alone? Under any other circumstances he would have loved the run, but his head was still full of the confession he’d never gotten to say, and he was stuck on his father’s old slow gray nag, Primrose, a good steady horse for a parson, but too old, too stiff for a hard gallop after the hounds.

Gwendolen on her spirited little chestnut was right up with the front of the pack, feeling as invincible as a goddess, never once stopping to think she might get hurt, never once stopping to wonder where Rex was. If she had, she would have laughed at the silly picture of him falling further and further behind, craning his neck looking for gates. She never saw him at all, soon—Rex was so far back he might as well have been miles away. He was searching for a gate along a newly repaired lane when Primrose stumbled, went down hard, broke his knees, and threw Rex clean over his head.

Luckily, the blacksmith’s son Joel Dagge, who followed the hunt on foot (a habit even some frivolous people thought was improper) was trailing at the very back, and saw the whole thing. He ran over to help, and Rex was badly stunned, the first full jolt of sensation when he came to being white-hot pain in his shoulder. Joel was the perfect person to have around: he knew exactly what was wrong with the horse, exactly how far they were to the nearest public house and to the rectory, and assured Rex his shoulder was just dislocated, nothing worse.

“Lord, sir, let me shove that back in for you!” he said cheerfully. “I seen Nash the bone-setter do it a hundred times, done it myself for our little Sally twice over. Shoulders is all the same. If you trust me, hold real still, I’ll have it right in no time.”

“Go on then, old fellow,” Rex said, gritting his teeth, and Joel popped the shoulder back into place, even if it hurt so bad Rex went pale as a sheet. Joel helped him limp the rest of the way home as fast as they could, Rex all the while far more worried that Gwendolen might have had an accident than he was about his own bruises or the broken horse. He told himself everyone would look after her, someone would make sure she got home safe.

Mr. Gascoigne was already back at the rectory, writing letters in his study, when Rex stumbled in, pale and bruised, his face still as handsome as ever, just a little sheepish and hurt. Rex was his secret favorite, the spitting image of him as a young man, though he never showed any partiality to the boy, in fact he treated him with extra strictness. He’d already heard from Anna that Rex had ridden out to the hunt meet with Gwendolen.

“What happened?” he said sharply, not laying down his pen.

“I’m very sorry, sir. Primrose fell and broke his knees.”

“Where on earth did you take him?” Mr. Gascoigne said, his voice tight with severity, though he rarely lost his temper.

“To the Three Barns, to watch the hounds throw off.”

“And you were fool enough to follow them when they ran?”

“Yes, sir. I wasn’t trying to jump fences, but the horse stepped in a hole.”

“Did you hurt yourself, at least? That’ll be some small penance.”

“I dislocated my shoulder, but the blacksmith’s son popped it back in for me. Just a little banged up, that’s all.”
