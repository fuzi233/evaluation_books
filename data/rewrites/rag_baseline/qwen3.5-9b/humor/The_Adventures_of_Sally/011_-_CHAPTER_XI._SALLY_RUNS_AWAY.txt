The air in the boarding house was thick with the scent of starch and damp wool, a static atmosphere where time seemed to measure itself in the slow descent of laundry lines. Ginger Kemp stood on the roof of the Maison Meecher, a structure that loomed over the lower avenue like a cage of brick and iron. Below, the city unfolded in a grid of indifference: Washington Square slept under the weight of somnolent Italians and skaters, while to the right, the great funnels of the *Mauritania* cut a silent path down the river, rising above the rooftops as if the ocean liner were merely another skyscraper traveling down Ninth Avenue. Four stacks. The *Mauritania*. The vessel carrying his past, and perhaps, if the stars aligned, his future.

He had calculated the odds correctly. The weather was a revelation, an Indian Summer that wrapped the city in a velvet blanket, promising that solitude was a luxury available only to the wealthy or the foolish. Ginger, a man of modest means and even more modest ambitions, had decided that today demanded action. He possessed a week's salary, minus Mrs. Meecher's deductions, enough to rent a car and conduct a rescue mission of princely proportions. He imagined Sally in the open car, the wind stripping away the ghosts of New York, the sun illuminating a path forward. It was a plan constructed of logic and hope, a narrative arc where the hero saves the heroine from the mundane.

Then, the universe intervened with a single, jarring movement.

Down in the hallway, the silhouette of Mr. Carmyle blocked the light. His cousin, dressed in the utilitarian serge of a man who believed efficiency was a moral virtue, stood conversing with a janitor. To Ginger, the sight was a physical assault, a violation of the spatial order he had carefully maintained in his mind. There was no drama, no shouting, only the terrible banality of a man who had come to see Sally.

"Hullo," Ginger said, his voice sounding thin against the heavy silence.

"Hullo," replied Carmyle. The greeting lacked warmth, lacking the friction of genuine engagement.

"Have you come to see Miss Nicholas?"

"Why, yes."

"She isn't here," Carmyle stated, the words landing with the finality of a dropped stone. "Apparently..."

For some extraordinary reason, she had dashed over to England.

Ginger tottered. The information hit him not as a sentence, but as a vacuum, sucking the color from the afternoon. He watched as Carmyle turned to the driver of the expensive automobile idling outside—a machine that cost more than Ginger earned in months. The transaction was swift, devoid of emotion. Money changed hands; the car rolled away, leaving behind the smell of gasoline and the echo of a missed connection.

"Gone to England?" Ginger asked, the question hanging in the air like smoke.

"Yes. Gone to England."

"But why?"

"How the devil do I know why?" Carmyle's tone suggested that asking questions was a waste of oxygen. "She sailed on the *Mauretania* this morning."

The tragedy of it was that Ginger had just been watching the boat leave. He had stood on the roof, a spectator to his own loss, unaware that the object of his affection was already crossing the Atlantic, fleeing the very city he had planned to save her from. The warmth of the Indian Summer evaporated instantly, replaced by a chill that seeped into his bones. The city, previously vibrant with potential, became a drab, futile landscape. Without Sally, the streets were merely lines on a map, the park a collection of trees, the sun a useless orb.

Night fell, bringing with it a letter. It was the only thing in the entire day that possessed texture, substance, and life. The envelope bore the crest of the *Mauretania*, a symbol of distance and escape. Ginger snatched it from the rack, clutching it as if it were a lifeline thrown across a chasm.

He retreated to his room, a space defined by the severe simplicity of Mrs. Meecher's decor. A Morris chair, a basin, a wash-stand. Nothing more. Yet, tonight, the room felt expansive. He approached the letter with the ceremonial gravity of a child about to receive a gift, delaying the inevitable consumption to savor the anticipation. First, he produced the stolen photograph of Sally, the only tangible remnant of her presence in this sterile environment. He studied it until the image blurred, then set it aside, filling his pipe and settling into the chair, his feet resting on the bed.

He opened the envelope.

"Ginger, dear."

The handwriting was familiar, intimate, yet written from a thousand miles away. He read, pausing frequently to return to the photograph, as if the ink needed visual confirmation to remain real.

"I'm running away," she wrote. "I haven't even stopped to say good-bye. I can't help it. I know it's weak and cowardly, but I simply can't help it."

The confession stripped away the romantic fantasy he had constructed. This was not a grand adventure; it was an act of survival. New York was too crowded with ghosts, with reminders of what had gone wrong. She had tried to endure, but the pressure had become unbearable. She mentioned Mr. Faucitt, a man whose brother had died and left him a dressmaking business in Regent Street. He needed help, and Sally, desperate for purpose, had run to fill the void.

"There was a letter from Elsa Doland," she continued, her voice seemingly echoing in the empty room. "Bubbling over with affection. And there was a letter from Gerald."

Ginger paused. Gerald. The name was a wound reopened.

"What would you have done, Ginger? Would you have read it?" she asked, anticipating his answer. "I sat with it in front of me for an hour... just looking at the envelope. Then... You see, what was the use? I could guess exactly the sort of thing that would be in it, and reading it would only have hurt a lot more. The thing was done, so why bother about explanations? What good are explanations, anyway? They don't help. They don't do anything... I burned it, Ginger. The last letter I shall ever get from him."

She described the fire in the bathroom, the smoldering paper turning to black ash and a stain on the tiles. A mess. A finality.

"Ginger, burn this letter, too. I'm pouring out all the poison to you, hoping it will make me feel better."

She spoke of fascination, of how easily one could analyze other people's tragedies while remaining blind to one's own. She recounted a girl at the dance place who loved a "mean-mouthed little beast" despite warnings, illustrating how irrational love operates. But the core of her message was personal, raw, and devastating.

"It's my pride that is hurt," she confessed. "I have always been a bossy, cocksure little creature, swaggering about the world like an English sparrow; and now I'm paying for it!"

She detailed the competition between herself and Elsa, the appeal of success to Gerald, the inadequacy of her own offerings against the glittering promise of a career. She admitted to having no pride left, nothing to offer but her own broken heart.

"Oh, Ginger, I'm paying for it!" she cried out in the text. "I wonder if running away is going to do me any good at all. Perhaps, if Mr. Faucitt has some real hard work for me to do..."

She ended with a plea, a request for him not to "make a hash of it," a warning to hold onto his own dignity while she navigated hers. She promised to think of him constantly, to be his stern guardian from afar, even though she would not be there to catch his eye.

Ginger laid the letter down. A sound escaped him, half-sigh, half-oath. For a fleeting moment, the impulse surged within him to travel to Chicago, to break Gerald Foster's neck, to restore the balance of the universe through violence. But the practicalities of geography and law grounded him. He abandoned the scheme, unable to conceive of any other course of action.

He relit his pipe. The flame flickered in the dim room, casting long shadows against the walls. He picked up the letter again, reading it once more, searching for a thread of hope, a sign that she was safe, that she was alive, that the story was not over. The city outside remained silent, the *Mauritania* long since vanished into the night, but inside the room, the letter burned with the heat of her presence, a beacon in the darkness of his isolation.
