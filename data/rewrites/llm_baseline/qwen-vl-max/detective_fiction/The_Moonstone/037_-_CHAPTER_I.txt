In the spring of 1849, I found myself wandering through the East, having recently altered the travel plans I had carefully laid out months earlier—plans that I had already shared with my lawyer and banker in London. This sudden change meant I needed to send one of my servants back to retrieve my correspondence and funds from the English consul in a city no longer on my itinerary. The man was to meet me at a predetermined place and time. But fate intervened: an unforeseen delay—entirely beyond his control—kept him from arriving on schedule.

For a full week, I and my party waited, encamped on the edge of a vast desert. Then, at last, he appeared at the entrance of my tent, letters and money in hand. “I’m afraid I bring you bad news, sir,” he said, handing me a letter marked with a black border, its address penned in Mr. Bruff’s unmistakable hand.

There is nothing more unbearable in such moments than uncertainty. I opened that letter first.

It told me my father had died—and that I was now heir to his considerable fortune. With wealth came duty; Mr. Bruff urged me without delay to return to England. By dawn the next morning, I was already en route home.

Betteredge, my old friend and former servant, later described my departure from England in vivid terms—terms I believe go somewhat too far. He took seriously one of Rachel’s playful jabs at my foreign upbringing, imagining I carried within me distinct French, German, and Italian personalities, when in truth those traits existed only in her satire and in Betteredge’s own generous fancy. Still, apart from this exaggeration, he was right in portraying me as deeply wounded by Rachel’s actions and leaving England in the raw pain of life’s most crushing disappointment.

I went abroad determined—if distance and change could help—to forget her. And I believe it is true that such removal does help. It shifts focus away from one’s own sorrow. I never truly forgot her—but over time, the sharpness of the ache softened, dulled by years, miles, and new experiences. Yet as I turned toward England once more, that healing began to reverse. The closer I drew to her world, the stronger her hold on me became.

When I left England, I would have refused even to speak her name. When I returned, she was the very first person I asked about upon meeting Mr. Bruff again.

He filled me in on everything that had occurred during my absence—everything, that is, except one key detail. At the time, he felt unable to reveal the private reasons behind Rachel’s and Godfrey Ablewhite’s decision to withdraw their marriage promise. I did not press him. To learn that she had reconsidered, that she had freed herself from the engagement, was enough relief after the sting of hearing she had ever considered marrying Godfrey.

Once I knew the past, my thoughts naturally turned to the present. Where was Rachel now? Who cared for her?

She was under the guardianship of Mrs. Merridew, widow of Sir John Verinder—a woman chosen by Rachel’s mother’s executors and who had agreed to take on the role. They were said to be getting along splendidly, and had settled into Mrs. Merridew’s residence in Portland Place for the season.

Within half an hour of learning this, I was already making my way to Portland Place—without even telling Mr. Bruff.

The servant who answered the door hesitated. He wasn’t certain whether Miss Verinder was at home. I sent him upstairs with my card—the quickest way to find out.

He returned with a stony expression. “Miss Verinder is out.”

I might have suspected others of avoiding me. But Rachel? That seemed impossible.

I left word that I would return at six o’clock that evening.

At six, I was told again that she was not at home. Had any message been left for me? No. Had she received my card? The servant apologized—she *had* received it.

The meaning was clear: she refused to see me.

I would not accept such treatment without trying to understand why. I sent my name up to Mrs. Merridew and requested a personal audience at her convenience.

She received me immediately. I was shown into a cozy sitting room and faced a kind, elderly woman—comfortable in every sense. She expressed deep regret and surprise—for my sake, not hers. But she could offer no explanation. The matter, she insisted, concerned private feelings, and she could not interfere.

She repeated this politely, patiently—until I had heard nothing but silence.

My final hope was to write to Rachel. My servant delivered a letter the next day, instructed to wait for a reply.

It came back—just one sentence:
“Miss Verinder begs to decline entering into any correspondence with Mr. Franklin Blake.”

Though I loved her deeply, I felt insulted. Mr. Bruff arrived shortly after, business in mind. I dismissed it at once and laid the whole situation before him.

He offered no more insight than Mrs. Merridew. I asked if anyone had spoken ill of me in Rachel’s presence. He didn’t know of any slander. Had she mentioned me while staying with him? Never. Not once had she asked whether I was alive or dead.

Then I pulled from my pocket-book the letter Lady Verinder had written me from Frizinghall on the day I left Yorkshire. I pointed to two sentences:

“The valuable assistance you rendered in searching for the lost jewel remains an unpardoned offence in Rachel’s mind. Moving blindfold, you have added to her burden of anxiety by threatening her secret with discovery through your innocent efforts.”

“Is it possible,” I asked, “that this same feeling still burns so fiercely against me?”

Mr. Bruff looked genuinely distressed. “If you insist on an answer,” he said, “I can think of no other interpretation.”

I rang the bell. “Have my portmanteau packed. Send for a railway guide.”

“Where are you going?” he asked, alarmed.

“To Yorkshire,” I replied. “By the next train.”

“For what purpose?”

“Because the help I gave in finding the Diamond was an unpardoned offence nearly a year ago—and it still is. I will not stand for it. I must uncover the secret of her silence toward her mother, and her hatred of me. If time, effort, and money can reveal it, I will find the thief who stole the Moonstone.”

He tried to reason with me, to dissuade me. But I was beyond listening. Nothing would shake my resolve.

“I will resume the investigation where I left off,” I declared. “Step by step, until I reach the present. There are gaps in the evidence I left behind—gaps Gabriel Betteredge can fill. I am going to him.”

That evening, as the sun set, I stood once more on the familiar terrace, gazing at the quiet country house. The gardener was the first I saw in the empty grounds. He’d just left Betteredge, sunning himself in his usual spot in the back yard. I remembered it well. I decided to go look for him myself.

I walked the old paths, passed through the open gate. There he was—my dear old friend, seated on the beehive chair, pipe in mouth, *Robinson Crusoe* on his lap, dogs sleeping at his sides.

The setting sun cast my shadow across the yard. Either the dogs sensed it, or their noses caught my scent. They rose, growling.

Betteredge startled, silenced them with a word, then shaded his eyes and looked toward the gate.

Tears filled mine. I had to pause—before I could speak.
