VIII. Search

The room stood undisturbed, save for the chaos of our sudden entry—no sign of struggle, no disarray, nothing to suggest a hasty departure. As the initial shock wore off, our hearts began to slow, and we dismissed the men who had burst in with such haste. Mademoiselle, however, whispered a thought that sent a chill through me: perhaps Carmilla had been awakened by the noise at her door, seized by panic, and fled into hiding—behind a curtain, within a wardrobe—anywhere she might be concealed until the commotion passed and the guards withdrew.

We resumed our search, calling her name again and again, but there was no answer. The silence deepened. Our confusion grew heavier with each passing moment. We checked the windows—barred tightly. I pleaded aloud, my voice trembling: “Carmilla, if you’re hidden, please—please stop this torment. Come out. Let us know you’re safe.” But only the wind replied.

I became certain she was not here. Not in this room. Not in the dressing chamber beyond, whose door remained locked from the inside. She could not have passed it without leaving a trace. Yet where could she have gone?

Had she found one of those hidden passages the old housekeeper once spoke of—secret corridors said to wind through the ancient walls of the château, forgotten now except in ghostly rumor? Such things were possible in a place like this, where stone and shadow held secrets older than memory.

Time crept on. It was past four o’clock. Darkness clung to the halls like smoke. I decided to wait out the night in Madame’s room, though every minute felt like an eternity. Dawn brought no answers.

By morning, the entire household was in turmoil. My father led the search, his face drawn with worry. Every corner of the château was scoured—walls, attics, cellars, closets. The gardens were combed. The stream near the orchard was about to be dragged. Still, no trace of Carmilla. No footprints. No cloth. No clue.

My father was broken. How would he explain this to her mother when she returned? And I—my heart was torn. Not with grief over loss, but with fear, with dread, with something deeper, darker than sorrow. I couldn’t imagine life without her.

It was now past one o’clock. No word. No sign. Desperation rising, I ran up to her chamber, breathless, expecting only emptiness.

And then I saw her.

She stood at the dressing table, pale as moonlight, her hair loose, her eyes wide with terror. I froze. I blinked. Was this real? Then she raised a slender finger, silent, beckoning me forward.

I rushed to her, tears blurring my vision. I threw my arms around her, kissed her cheeks, her forehead, over and over, sobbing with relief. “You’re alive! You’re here!” I cried.

I dashed to the bell and rang it wildly, summoning everyone—servants, family, anyone who could bring news to my father.

“Dear Carmilla,” I gasped, “what happened? Where were you? How did you return?”

She looked at me, her lips trembling. “Last night… was a night of wonders,” she whispered.

“Then tell me everything,” I begged. “Please—explain.”

“It was just after two,” she said, her voice low, “when I fell asleep as usual—doors locked, both the dressing room and the gallery door secured. I slept deeply. No dreams. Nothing. But when I woke—just now—I was on the sofa in the dressing room. The door between the rooms was open. The outer door—forced.”

Her words hung in the air.

“How?” I asked. “How could you have been moved without waking? Without hearing anything? You’re so light—so sensitive. A whisper wakes you!”

She shook her head slowly. “I don’t know. I remember nothing. Only waking there… afraid.”

By then, Madame, Mademoiselle, my father, and several servants had arrived. The room filled with questions, embraces, relief. But Carmilla offered no explanation. She seemed lost, helpless, as if even she didn’t understand what had occurred.

My father paced slowly, hands clasped behind his back. He studied the room, the doors, the locks. I noticed Carmilla’s gaze follow him—a fleeting glance, sharp and dark, like a shadow slipping across a wall.

When the servants were dismissed—Mademoiselle having gone to fetch valerian and salvolatile—the room narrowed to only four: my father, Madame, Carmilla, and me.

He approached her gently, took her hand, guided her to the sofa, and sat beside her.

“May I venture a guess?” he asked softly. “A question?”

“Of course,” she said. “Ask anything. I’ll tell you all I know. But remember—mamma has set limits. I cannot speak of certain things.”

“I understand,” he said. “I won’t touch those subjects. Now—last night’s mystery: how were you removed from your bed, from your room, without waking? The windows were secure. The doors were locked from the inside. Yet you ended up in the dressing room. What do you think?”

She leaned on her hand, her face drained of color. “I don’t know,” she murmured. “I truly don’t.”

“Have you ever walked in your sleep?” he asked.

“Not since I was very small,” she replied. “But yes—I did. My nurse used to say I’d wander in the night, sometimes fall down stairs, wake up confused.”

My father nodded, a faint smile touching his lips. “Then I believe I can solve this.”

He paused, letting the silence grow heavy.

“You rose in your sleep. You unlocked the door—not leaving the key, but taking it with you. You carried it outside, locked it from the other side. Then you wandered—perhaps to another room, perhaps upstairs or downstairs. This house is full of forgotten chambers, cluttered corners, dusty closets. It would take weeks to search every inch. You may have drifted far before returning—still asleep—back to the dressing room. That’s where you woke.”

“And why wasn’t I disturbed?” I asked, still stunned.

“Because sleepwalking,” he said, “can happen without awareness. No pain. No alarm. Just movement in darkness. You came here after we searched, still unconscious. Then—finally—awoke. Found yourself there. As surprised as anyone.”

He laughed lightly. “And so we are spared witches, thieves, poisoners. A simple dream-walk explains it all. A miracle of innocence.”

Carmilla sat quiet, her beauty more haunting than ever—her skin luminous, her eyes deep with unspoken things. Her stillness unnerved me.

My father turned to her, his tone softening. “I wish Laura looked half as well as you do,” he said, sighing. “She’s been so pale lately.”

I glanced at him, then at Carmilla—her presence filling the room like a breath of cold air.

Our fears were over. The mystery solved. Carmilla was back.

But as I watched her, I couldn’t shake the feeling that something had changed. That the night had not ended as it should have. That somewhere, beneath the surface of this peaceful reunion, something ancient and terrible stirred—quietly, patiently—waiting.
