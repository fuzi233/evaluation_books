A month after the Carnival, on a morning near the end of March, Tito descended the marble steps of the Old Palace, heading toward San Marco with a purpose that carried more weight than it seemed. For some reason, he avoided the direct path—a straight line from the palace—and instead chose a longer route through the Piazza di Santa Croce, where the crowds were emerging from church after the early Lenten sermon.

It was in the grand cathedral of Santa Croce that the Lenten sermons had lately drawn the largest audiences. Savonarola’s voice was no longer heard even in his own church, San Marco—silenced by a hostile Signoria acting under orders from the Pope. The pontiff had threatened an immediate interdict upon Florence if the “wretched worm” and “monstrous idol” were not forbidden to preach and sent to Rome to beg forgiveness. In response, the city had taken up the challenge: not with silence, but with mockery. The most thrilling part of Lent now was not hearing Savonarola himself, but listening to those who attacked him, ridiculed him, and called for proof of his claims.

And so, at Santa Croce, the Franciscan assigned to deliver the Quaresimal sermons had declared a bold wager: he would walk through fire alongside Fra Girolamo, to test whether the Dominican’s prophetic words were truly divine. After all, Savonarola had claimed that miracles would accompany his message at the proper moment. Now was that moment. If Savonarola walked through the flames unharmed, his doctrine would be proven true. If he perished, his falsehood would be laid bare. And to show no fear of death, the Franciscan offered to go into the fire himself—willing to burn for the sake of truth.

Savonarola, as always, ignored the provocation. But fate had a twist: the fiery preacher was none other than Fra Francesco di Puglia, who had once faced off against Savonarola’s follower, Fra Domenico, in Prato. Then, the conflict had been purely rhetorical—until Fra Francesco was recalled by his superiors. Now, when news reached Fra Domenico, preaching to women along the Via del Cocomero, he stepped forward to defend his master. He accepted the challenge—declaring himself ready to walk through the fire with Fra Francesco.

The people, already stirred by the spectacle of faith tested by flame, began to take sides. But Savonarola, sensing the danger of such public debate, ordered Fra Domenico to withdraw. The Franciscan, however, insisted: his challenge was not to a disciple, but to Fra Girolamo himself.

But this morning, when Tito entered the Piazza di Santa Croce, he found, as expected, that the crowd was thick and restless, pouring out of the church in great numbers. Instead of scattering, many gathered near the entrance of the Franciscan monastery. Tito followed their gaze, moving through the throng with casual ease, yet watching closely for any sign of movement from within the cloistered gates.

It was not a person they awaited. Their attention was fixed on a large placard nailed to the wall by order of the Signoria, its surface covered in clear, official handwriting. But curiosity was frustrated by the fact that most of the text was in Latin. Though nearly every man could guess what it said, they craved precision, and the inability to read the script bred irritation—especially among those who knew just enough Latin to be annoyed by their ignorance. For while pulpit quotes were often explained by preachers, written Latin remained alien to the common eye. Spelling itself was chaotic, and few could decipher it with confidence.

“It’s the Frate’s doctrines,” said Goro, a familiar figure in the crowd, “that he’s to prove by being burned. The Signoria’s put it up so we know. That’s what the Padre’s been saying in his sermons.”

“Nay, Goro,” replied a sleek shopkeeper, “you’ve got your legs twisted. The Frate must prove his teachings by _not_ being burned—he walks through the fire and comes out whole.”

“Yes, yes,” chimed in a young sculptor, cap jauntily tilted, “but Fra Girolamo refuses to walk through the fire. He’s already sound and whole. Why should he risk it just to come out unchanged? He leaves such tasks to Fra Domenico.”

“Then he’s a coward!” cried Goro, voice rising. “Suffocation! That’s what he did at Carnival—had us all gather in the Piazza to see lightning strike him. Nothing happened.”

“Hush,” said a tall shoemaker, slippers dangling over his shoulder. “You’re as wise as a calf with water on its brain. The Frate won’t flinch. He may say nothing now, but when the time comes, he’ll step into the fire alone. But I’d give a shoestring to know what this Latin means.”

“There’s too much of it,” sighed the shopkeeper. “Is there no scholar here?”

A general turn of heads revealed Tito approaching from behind.

“There he is,” said the sculptor, smiling and lifting his cap.

“The secretary of the Ten,” said the shopkeeper, doffing his own hat. “He’s going to the convent, no doubt. Make way for him.” Though Florentines rarely showed such deference, Tito’s presence commanded it. His black mantle, fastened with a gold fibula, seemed regal; his velvet cap, ordinary in form, looked like something rare and noble. His face, hardened since arriving in Florence, only deepened his air of quiet authority. He raised his cap and said, “Thanks, my friend. I merely wished to see what the placard says—ah, as I expected.”

He had been informed that the government allowed anyone willing to volunteer for the trial by fire—provided they signed at the Palazzo. A generous gesture, worthy of the Signoria. Naturally, many believers might step forward. What was fire to one whose faith was firm? A man feared fire because he believed it would burn him. But if he believed otherwise?

Tito lifted his shoulders and paused, letting the thought settle. “For which reason,” he continued, “I have never doubted the Frate when he said he would enter the fire to prove his doctrine. In his place—if you believed the fire wouldn’t burn you—would any of you hesitate to walk through it, as easily as across the dry bed of the Mugnone?”

As he spoke, the crowd shifted. Some faces brightened with sudden understanding. Others tightened with hesitation. Faith was one thing—practice another. The prospect of real fire made them cautious.

“Then, Messer Segretario,” said the sculptor, “Fra Francesco is the greater hero. He offers to enter the fire for truth, knowing it will burn him.”

“I don’t deny it,” Tito replied smoothly. “But if he is mistaken, he’ll be burned for the wrong side. The Church does not count such deaths as martyrdom. We must wait until the trial happens.”

“True,” said the shopkeeper, impatiently. “But will you interpret the Latin for us?”

“Of course,” said Tito. “It lists the doctrines the Frate teaches—the ones the fire will prove or disprove. You’ve likely heard them before. First: *Ecclesia Dei indiget renovatione*—the Church of God needs renewal.”

“That’s true,” several voices agreed.

“It means the priests should live better lives,” said the shoemaker. “No miracle needed. That’s what the Frate has always said.”

“*Flagellabitur*, then,” Tito continued. “It will be scourged. *Renovabitur*: purified. *Florentia quoque post flagellam renovabitur et prosperabitur*: Florence, too, after the scourging, shall be renewed and prosper.”

“That means we’ll get Pisa back,” said the shopkeeper.

“And trade with England again,” added an old man in a berretta. “There’s been enough scourging already—our trade is sinking.”

At that moment, a tall figure wearing a red feather emerged from the monastery door. He exchanged a glance with Tito, who casually tossed his becchetto over his shoulder and turned back to the placard. The bystanders stepped aside respectfully for Messer Dolfo Spini.

“*Infideles convertentur ad Christum*,” Tito read on. “The infidels shall be converted to Christ.”

“Turks and Moors,” said the shopkeeper. “I’ve no quarrel with that.”

“*Haec autem omnia erunt temporibus nostris*,” Tito went on. “All these things shall happen in our times.”

“Why else would they?” asked Goro.

“*Excommunicato nuper lata contra Reverendum Patrem nostrum Fratrem Hieronymum nulla est*: the excommunication against our reverend father, Fra Girolamo, is null. *Non observantes eam non peccant*: those who ignore it do not sin.”

“I’ll judge that after the trial,” said the shopkeeper.

“Which will settle everything,” said Tito. “That’s all the Latin—just the doctrines to be tested. The rest is a Tuscan proclamation calling for volunteers to sign up. Can I help you further?”

He bowed slightly, cap raised, and moved away with effortless grace.

Once clear of the piazza, Tito quickened his pace. After a few turns, he stopped before a quiet door and gave a light, distinctive knock. It opened to reveal a young woman, whom he playfully tapped under the chin as he asked if the Padrone was in. Without ceremony, he passed through a half-open door into a spacious but cluttered room, where Dolfo Spini sat playing with a fine stag-hound—one moment sniffing at a basket of puppies, the next licking Spini’s hands with affectionate obliviousness to her master’s character.

Spini barely glanced up. Tito stood quietly, thumbs in belt, waiting.

“A fine *bracca*,” he remarked calmly.

Then, in a tone cool and clear, yet commanding attention, he added: “When you finish such distractions that can’t possibly wait, my Dolfo, let us speak of business. My time, though I wish it were eternity at your service, is not entirely mine this morning.”

“Down, Mischief, down!” Spini snapped, pushing the dog aside. “Malediction!” He rose abruptly, placing a hand on Tito’s shoulder. “I hope your sharp mind sees all the angles of this scheme. To me, it’s as dark as the bottom of a sack.”

“What troubles you, my cavalier?”

“These accursed Frati Minori at Santa Croce. They’re backing down. Fra Francesco himself seems afraid—worried the Prophet might use magic to fake a miracle. He fears he’ll be dragged in and burned, while the Prophet emerges unscathed by sorcery. And still, not a single lay brother offers to stand beside poor Fra Domenico.”

“It’s the stupidity of the tonsured skull,” said Tito. “They fail to see that whether they burn or not matters little. Have you sworn to them they’ll not touch the fire?”

“No,” Spini admitted, puzzled. “Because one must go in with Fra Domenico, who thinks it’ll take a thousand years to gather the fagots.”

“Not at all,” Tito said. “Fra Domenico won’t go in. I’ve told you before—only your brilliant mind needs repetition. Now that the Signoria has taken charge, all we need is a day, fuel prepared in the Piazza, and a crowd expecting a miracle. If the Prophet leaves without a sign, he’s ruined. The people will drive him out. The Signoria can banish him. The Pope can do as he pleases. So, my Alcibiades, swear to the Franciscans their grey robes will never come near the flames.”

Spini rubbed his head, tapped his sword against his leg—trying to grasp the invisible web of strategy.

“But,” he said, looking up, “unless we seize him in the Piazza when the people are enraged, Valori and the Salviati will rise for him. I know they talked of it after Ascension Sunday. And the people might turn. Rumors of the French king returning—some chance in his favor—could save him. The city won’t be safe until he’s gone.”

“He *will* be gone soon,” Tito assured him. “Without any effort beyond this little farce of the Trial by Fire. Wine turns to vinegar without shouting—your Florentine sages would say so. You’ll rid your city of an incubus with cleverness, not clumsy swords.”

“But suppose he *does* walk through the fire, with magic or the devil’s help?” Spini asked, grimacing. “How do you know there’s nothing in such things? Scholars believe in them. This Frate is capable of anything.”

“Oh, of course such things exist,” Tito shrugged. “But I know the Frate isn’t in league with the devil. The only magic he relies on is his own skill.”

“Skill?” Spini scoffed. “Calling it skill to make Florence clash with the Pope and all Italy—beckoning the French king who never comes? You may call him able. I call him a hypocrite who wants to rule everyone and become Pope.”

“You judge with your usual insight, my captain,” Tito replied. “But our views don’t clash. The Frate wants power. To oppose the Pope, he needs a foreign lever—and Florence as a fulcrum. Once I saw him as a narrow bigot. Now I see a shrewd, ambitious man who knows his goal and aims with the same precision you aim a ball in *maglio*.”

“Yes, yes,” Spini grinned. “I can aim well.”

“True,” Tito said gravely. “And I mention this only so you may see how this victory will enhance your reputation in Rome and Milan—useful when the city changes its policy.”

“Well, thou art a good little demon,” Spini said, patting Tito’s shoulder. “Thou shalt have good pay.”

“Of course,” Tito smiled, “any advantage to me depends entirely on your—”

“We’ll dine at my palace tonight,” Spini interrupted, nodding significantly. “I’ll explain the new plan to them all.”

“Pardon, my magnificent patron,” Tito said, “the plan has been the same from the start—unchanged except in your memory. Are you certain you hold it now?”

Spini repeated it.

“One thing more,” he said as Tito turned to leave. “There’s Ser Ceccone, the sharp-nosed notary. He’s been useful lately. Tell me—you who can see a man wink from behind, can I keep using him?”

Tito hesitated. He knew Spini too well to offer honest advice when vanity was involved. “Doubtless,” he said quickly. “I have nothing against Ceccone.”

That mention stung Tito. He had once feared Ser Ceccone. His nature made him alert to dangers—small things that could grow. He remembered vividly the October morning when Romola appeared at Nello’s shop and forced him to admit he knew Fra Girolamo wouldn’t leave the city. Ceccone had witnessed it. Worse, Tito sensed the notary disliked him. Now, after fleeing Medicean plots and returning to the city, Ceccone had allied himself with the Arrabbiati and Spini. And Spini, fond of boasting to intimates, might reveal secrets—especially if Ceccone’s jealousy sharpened his mind.

To be undone by a petty notary—by the sting of an insect offended by accident—was pitiful. “But,” Tito thought, “his dislike is no deeper than a hungry dog’s grumble. I’ll win him over with prosperity.” He had recently secured Ceccone a temporary post as extra *cancelliere* under the Ten—believing that bribe would silence the cur.

But perfect schemes require perfect foresight. Tito didn’t know that Ceccone’s envy had turned to hatred for reasons unknown to him. That evening, after Tito left the Special Council, he brushed past Ceccone on the stairs. The notary, fresh from Pistoja, had learned of the arrests. Like Tito, he had been involved in the Medicean plot—privy to plans, eager to betray. But he had been too late. His offer was coldly rejected. The council already had the information. And since he had meddled in sedition, he was advised to stay out of harm’s way.

Ceccone blamed Tito. He held his tongue, but his bitterness grew. And worse, he had begun receiving small payments from the Milanese court—spying on Florence. But his reports were dismissed as outdated. He suspected Tito of secret ties to Milan—though he had no proof. Still, he chewed the suspicion like a bitter root.

And so, as Tito walked away, the seeds of future trouble had already been planted—not in the fire, but in the shadows of ambition and resentment.
