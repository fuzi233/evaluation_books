V. A Wonderful Likeness

That evening, a messenger arrived from Gratz—dark-faced, solemn, the son of the picture restorer—bringing with him a horse and cart laden with two massive packing cases, each crammed with portraits. The journey had taken ten leagues, and whenever a traveler came from our distant capital, we gathered in the hall, eager for news. This time, the arrival stirred something deeper than curiosity in our isolated home. The cases were left in the great hall while the man was ushered to supper by the servants.

Afterward, armed with hammer, chisel, and screwdriver, he returned with assistants, and we all assembled to watch as the contents were unveiled. Carmilla sat apart, her gaze distant, uninterested, as one after another the old paintings emerged—mostly portraits, many of them centuries old, their faces obscured by grime and time. My mother came from an ancient Hungarian line, and most of these works had passed down through her bloodline. My father held a list, reading aloud the numbers as the artist pulled forth each restored piece.

I cannot say the art was masterful, but it was undeniably aged, some of it curiously strange. For me, they were revelations—faces I’d never seen before, now emerging from decades of dust and shadow.

“There is one portrait I have not yet seen,” my father said. “In the corner, I believe, is the name ‘Marcia Karnstein,’ and the date 1698. I am curious to see how it has turned out.”

I remembered it—a small painting, nearly square, about a foot and a half high, frameless, so blackened by time that it was nothing more than a silhouette. Now, the restorer brought it forward, his hands trembling slightly with pride.

And then I saw it.

It was breathtaking. Not merely a likeness—but alive. It was Carmilla.

“Carmilla, dear,” I whispered, “this is impossible. Look—here you are, smiling, breathing, as if you could speak at any moment. Isn’t it extraordinary? Even the mole on your throat—just like yours.”

My father chuckled. “Yes, it’s a remarkable likeness,” he said, though his eyes lingered elsewhere, speaking instead to the restorer about technique, pigments, restoration methods. He seemed unimpressed. But I stood transfixed, unable to tear my gaze away.

“May I hang this in my room, Papa?” I asked softly.

“Of course, my dear,” he replied, smiling. “If it means so much to you, it must be even more beautiful than I imagined.”

Carmilla did not respond. She remained still, her head tilted slightly, her long-lashed eyes fixed on me with quiet intensity. A faint smile curled her lips—not of amusement, but of something deeper, darker. Then she looked down, as if caught in thought.

“And now,” I said, stepping closer, “you can read the name clearly. It’s not Marcia. It’s written in gold. Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. And there’s a tiny coronet above and below the date: A.D. 1698. You know—my mother was descended from the Karnsteins.”

“Oh?” she murmured, almost dreamily. “So am I. Very far back. Very ancient. Are there any Karnsteins living now?”

“None who carry the name,” I said. “The family fell during civil wars long ago. But the ruins of their castle are only three miles from here.”

“How fascinating,” she sighed, her voice barely audible. Then she glanced toward the open door of the hall. “Look—the moonlight. So beautiful. Would you like to walk outside? Just a little way, down to the drawbridge?”

“It’s so like the night you came to us,” I said.

She smiled, but it was slow, sorrowful. “Do you remember?” she breathed.

“I remember everything,” I answered. “And I’m glad you came.”

She drew closer, her arm slipping around my waist. “You asked for a portrait of me,” she whispered, her voice soft as silk. “To hang in your room.”

“Yes,” I said, my heart quickening. “Because it looks just like you.”

She leaned into me, pressing her cheek against mine, her breath warm. “How romantic you are,” I murmured. “When you tell your story someday, it will be full of grand passion.”

She kissed me—quietly, without sound.

“I am sure,” I said, “you’ve loved someone. There must be a secret love in your heart.”

“No,” she whispered. “I have loved no one. Never. Unless… unless it should be you.”

She looked radiant under the silver light. Then, suddenly, her face changed. Her eyes flickered—once, twice—and then dimmed. She hid her face in my hair, her body trembling, her hand clenched tightly in mine. Sobs rose in her chest, silent but deep.

“Darling,” she murmured, her voice breaking. “I live in you. And you would die for me. I love you so.”

I stepped back, startled.

Her face was pale, expressionless. Her eyes were blank, lifeless.

“Is it cold?” she asked drowsily. “I feel… faint. As if I’ve been dreaming. Let us go inside. Come. Come in.”

“You’re ill, Carmilla,” I said, alarmed. “You look weak. You must drink something—wine, perhaps.”

“I’ll be fine,” she insisted, though her voice wavered. “Just a moment. Yes—give me wine. I’ll be myself again soon.”

We walked slowly toward the door, but she paused once more.

“Let us look one last time,” she said. “Perhaps this is the final time I’ll see the moonlight with you.”

I watched her closely. “Are you truly better?”

She nodded, forcing a smile. “Yes. I am well. Only weakness—nothing more. They call me languid. I tire easily. I can scarcely walk as far as a child. But it passes. In a moment, I am whole again.”

And indeed, she was. By the time we reached the drawing-room, she was animated, laughing, speaking freely. The strange stillness had vanished.

But that night, as I lay awake, I felt a shift—an unsettling change in the air. Something had happened. Something unseen. And though Carmilla appeared normal, I sensed a new darkness stirring beneath her skin.

A darkness that had begun, perhaps, with the portrait.
