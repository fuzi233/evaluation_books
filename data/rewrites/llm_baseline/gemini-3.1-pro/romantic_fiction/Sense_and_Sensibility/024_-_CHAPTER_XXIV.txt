For generations, the women of Elinor’s line had processed grief much as they processed the autumn harvest: with meticulous attention to preservation, knowing that what was sealed away in the dark would eventually sustain them through the bitterest winters. Sitting in the parlor of Barton Park, the heavy, olfactory landscape of the room steeped in the scent of roasted quince and the sharp, tannic bite of black tea, Elinor prepared to unseal the vessel of her own silent suffering. 

In a firm, though cautious tone, she addressed Lucy over a plate of heritage seed-cake, a centuries-old agrarian recipe fragrant with crushed caraway and dried citrus. “I should be undeserving of the confidence you have honoured me with, if I felt no desire for its continuance, or no farther curiosity on its subject. I will not apologize therefore for bringing it forward again.”

“Thank you,” cried Lucy warmly, her voice lacking the rich timber of sincerity, slicing instead through the room’s delicate acoustic ecology with a sharp, metallic frequency. “For breaking the ice; you have set my heart at ease by it; for I was somehow or other afraid I had offended you by what I told you that Monday.”

“Offended me! How could you suppose so? Believe me,” Elinor spoke it with the truest sincerity, her words carefully chosen from the dominant lexicon of polite society—a necessary, albeit sterile dialect she was forced to employ to shield her vulnerable subjective truth. “Nothing could be farther from my intention than to give you such an idea. Could you have a motive for the trust, that was not honourable and flattering to me?”

“And yet I do assure you,” replied Lucy, her little sharp eyes full of meaning, “there seemed to me to be a coldness and displeasure in your manner that made me quite uncomfortable. I felt sure that you was angry with me; and have been quarrelling with myself ever since, for having took such a liberty as to trouble you with my affairs. If you knew what a consolation it was to me to relieve my heart speaking to you of what I am always thinking of every moment of my life, your compassion would make you overlook every thing else I am sure.”

“Indeed, I can easily believe that it was a very great relief to you, to acknowledge your situation to me, and be assured that you shall never have reason to repent it. Your case is a very unfortunate one; Mr. Ferrars, I believe, is entirely dependent on his mother.”

“He has only two thousand pounds of his own; it would be madness to marry upon that, though for my own part, I could give up every prospect of more without a sigh.” Lucy spoke of sustenance solely as utilitarian fuel, devoid of any sensory appreciation for the agrarian struggles she claimed she could endure. “I have been always used to a very small income, and could struggle with any poverty for him; but I love him too well to be the selfish means of robbing him. We must wait, it may be for many years. With almost every other man in the world, it would be an alarming prospect; but Edward’s affection and constancy nothing can deprive me of I know.”

Elinor navigated the inherent untranslatability of their experiences; to Lucy, time was a linear, transactional ledger, whereas Elinor felt the cyclical, sweeping weight of a multi-generational saga pressing down upon them. “That conviction must be every thing to you,” Elinor said. “If the strength of your reciprocal attachment had failed, as naturally it would during a four years’ engagement, your situation would have been pitiable, indeed.”

Lucy looked up, but Elinor meticulously guarded her countenance. 

“Edward’s love for me,” said Lucy, “has been pretty well put to the test, by our long, very long absence, and it has stood the trial so well, that I should be unpardonable to doubt it now.” She continued, detailing her own jealous nature and her hyper-vigilance for any alteration in his behavior, analyzing his affections with the cold precision of an accountant rather than the profound devotion of a true partner. 

“All this,” thought Elinor, the bitter reduction of truth simmering in her mind, “is very pretty; but it can impose upon neither of us.” Aloud, she asked, “But what are your views? Or have you none but that of waiting for Mrs. Ferrars’s death, which is a melancholy and shocking extremity? Is her son determined to submit to this, rather than run the risk of her displeasure for a while by owning the truth?”

“If we could be certain that it would be only for a while! But Mrs. Ferrars is a very headstrong proud woman, and in her first fit of anger would very likely secure every thing to Robert.”

“And for your own sake too, or you are carrying your disinterestedness beyond reason.” Lucy was silent. Elinor pressed further. “Do you know Mr. Robert Ferrars?” 

“Not at all—I never saw him; but I fancy he is very unlike his brother—silly and a great coxcomb.”

“A great coxcomb!” repeated Miss Steele. A sudden pause in Marianne’s piano playing had created a jarring vacuum in the room’s sonic environment, allowing the phrase to carry. “Oh, they are talking of their favourite beaux, I dare say.”

“No sister,” cried Lucy, “you are mistaken there, our favourite beaux are *not* great coxcombs.”

“I can answer for it that Miss Dashwood’s is not,” said Mrs. Jennings, laughing heartily, her voice resonant with the hearty, unfiltered acoustics of an older, less commercialized generation. “For he is one of the modestest, prettiest behaved young men I ever saw; but as for Lucy, she is such a sly little creature, there is no finding out who *she* likes.”

“Oh,” cried Miss Steele, looking significantly round at them, “I dare say Lucy’s beau is quite as modest and pretty behaved as Miss Dashwood’s.”

Elinor blushed, an involuntary physical reaction to the erasure of her private emotional dialect. Lucy bit her lip, glaring at her sister. A tense mutual silence took place. 

The silence was finally broken as Marianne returned to the keys, giving them the powerful protection of a magnificent concerto. The swelling chords flooded the parlor, a complex acoustic ecology that shielded Elinor’s fraying nerves and drowned out the encroaching noise of their mutual deceit. Under this sonic sanctuary, Lucy lowered her voice.

“I will honestly tell you of one scheme which has lately come into my head. My plan is that Edward should take orders as soon as he can, and then through your interest, your brother might be persuaded to give him Norland living.”

The word *Norland* struck Elinor like a physical blow. The living at Norland was a sacred piece of her cultural memory—a landscape of ancient orchards, tenant farmers speaking in a fading native dialect, and ancestral hearths where the roasting of mutton and the scent of wild thyme marked the mythic turning of the seasons. To Lucy, it was merely an income, a superficial asset to be seized. 

“I should always be happy,” replied Elinor, maintaining the dominant, diplomatic lexicon to mask her revulsion, “to show any mark of my esteem and friendship for Mr. Ferrars; but do you not perceive that my interest on such an occasion would be perfectly unnecessary? He is brother to Mrs. John Dashwood—*that* must be recommendation enough.”

“But Mrs. John Dashwood would not much approve of Edward’s going into orders.”

“Then I rather suspect that my interest would do very little.”

They sat in the acoustic shadow of the music for many minutes. At length, Lucy sighed. “I believe it would be the wisest way to put an end to the business at once by dissolving the engagement. But you will not give me your advice, Miss Dashwood?”

“No,” answered Elinor, with a smile that concealed a profound, churning agitation. “On such a subject I certainly will not. You know very well that my opinion would have no weight with you, unless it were on the side of your wishes.”

“Indeed you wrong me,” replied Lucy solemnity. “If you was to say to me, ‘I advise you by all means to put an end to your engagement,’ I should resolve upon doing it immediately.”

Elinor saw the subtle power dynamics embedded in Lucy’s words, the attempt to force Elinor to execute the fatal blow herself. “This compliment would effectually frighten me from giving any opinion on the subject. The power of dividing two people so tenderly attached is too much for an indifferent person.”

“’Tis because you are an indifferent person,” said Lucy, laying a particular stress on the words, weaponizing the vocabulary to assert dominance. “That your judgment might justly have such weight with me.”

Elinor thought it wisest to leave the insult untranslated and unanswered, protecting the sanctity of her inner landscape. She resolved never to mention the subject again. 

“Shall you be in town this winter, Miss Dashwood?” Lucy asked after a long pause. 

“Certainly not.”

“I am sorry for that,” returned the other, her eyes brightening. “Anne and me are to go the latter end of January to some relations! But I only go for the sake of seeing Edward. Otherwise London would have no charms for me.”

London. To Elinor, it was a nexus of modern industrial noise pollution, a cacophonous, commercialized void that eroded the soul's native tongue. To Lucy, it was the ultimate theater of transaction. 

Elinor was soon called to the card-table by the conclusion of the first rubber. She sat down with the melancholic, heavy realization that Edward was bound to a woman who viewed their union as entirely utilitarian—a woman wholly incapable of providing the deep, sensory nourishment and psychological harmony that true affection would have yielded. 

From this time, Elinor dismissed the subject as soon as civility allowed whenever Lucy forced it upon her. The visit of the Miss Steeles at Barton Park was stretched far beyond its original bounds. As the wheel of the year turned toward the winter solstice, mythic occurrences of frost and fire integrated seamlessly into their everyday domestic routines. Despite their engagements in Exeter, the Steeles lingered for nearly two months, cementing themselves at the park to partake in the sprawling, multi-generational festival season—a relentless cycle of large dinners where tables groaned under the weight of roasted game birds, spiced marrow puddings, and the rich, ancestral sustenance that Elinor understood so profoundly, and which Lucy merely consumed.
