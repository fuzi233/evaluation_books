Behind the house on Rue Fossette lay a garden—large for a city, and to my memory now it seems serene and lovely. Time, like distance, softens certain places; and when all around is stone, blank walls, and sun-baked pavement, how precious becomes a single bush, how enchanting an enclosed patch of earth where green things grow and flowers bloom.

There was a whisper among the neighbors that Madame Beck’s home had once been a convent. Long before the city crept into this quarter, when fields and avenues stretched far and wide, and deep shade wrapped the land as it should embrace a holy house, something terrible had happened here—something that stirred fear and left behind a tale of ghosts. A legend lingered: of a nun in black and white, seen sometimes on certain nights, wandering through the gardens. Though the ghost must have faded centuries ago—houses now rose all around—certain relics remained: ancient fruit trees, giants of the orchard, still rooted in the soil like living monuments. At the base of one—a pear tree so old it seemed to have outlived time itself, its trunk mostly dead but a few branches still bearing spring blossoms and autumn fruit—you could scrape away the mossy earth and glimpse beneath the roots a slab of smooth, hard, black stone. It was said, though never proven, that this was the cover of a vault beneath, where a young girl had once been buried alive by a cruel monastic order for breaking her vow. Her spirit, they whispered, haunted the place long after her body turned to dust; her shadow, her dark robe and white veil, flickered through moonlight and mist, visible only to those with trembling eyes.

But beyond such romantic tales, the garden held real charm. On summer mornings, I rose early to walk alone, savoring the quiet. In the evenings, I lingered, waiting for the moon to rise or catching a breath of the cool night air, imagining the dew falling like a gentle kiss. The grass was thick and green, the gravel paths bright and clean. Sunlit nasturtiums clung to the roots of the ancient trees like bursts of flame. There was a large arbor shaded by an acacia, and a smaller, more hidden bower nestled in vines that wound along a high gray wall, their tendrils weaving a crown above, their clusters hanging in abundance where jasmine and ivy met and entwined.

No doubt, at midday, when Madame Beck’s school emptied and pupils and boarders scattered like birds, the garden became crowded and noisy—noisy enough to drown even the bells of St. Jean Baptiste. But at dusk, when the externes had gone home and the boarders sat quietly at their studies, it transformed into a sanctuary. Then it was peaceful, hushed, almost sacred. I often walked there in the evening, drawn by the stillness, the mellow air, the scent of flowers breathing softly under the fading light.

One such evening, I stayed later than usual. The twilight deepened, the air cooled, and the blooms, no longer touched by sun, seemed to exhale their fragrance in reply to the dew descending from the sky. A light glowed in the oratory window—Madame Beck’s household was gathered for evening prayer. As a Protestant, I sometimes chose not to attend. “Just a little longer,” solitude murmured, and the pale moon overhead whispered, “stay—everything is calm now. No one will miss you. You’ve been worn down by the day’s heat and noise. Savor these moments.”

The garden’s backside was bordered by the rear of a row of buildings—the boarding houses of the neighboring college. These were plain stone, bare except for a few attic windows high up, belonging to servants’ rooms, and one lower casement said to belong to a master. Yet despite this, one narrow alley ran parallel to the high wall on that side—and it was forbidden to the girls. Called *l’allée défendue*, any pupil who entered would face the strictest punishment allowed by Madame Beck’s otherwise mild rules. Teachers might pass freely, but the path was narrow, overgrown with thick shrubs, their branches forming a canopy that let only scattered shafts of sunlight through. Even during daylight, few ventured there; after dark, it was avoided entirely.

From the beginning, I found myself drawn to it. Its seclusion, its dimness, intrigued me. At first, fear of seeming odd kept me away. But slowly, as people grew accustomed to me and my quiet ways—my habits, my subtle differences, which were neither striking nor offensive, but simply part of who I was—I began to visit the path regularly. I tended some colorless flowers growing between the shrubs, cleared away last autumn’s leaves that choked a wooden seat at the end, and cleaned it with a pail of water and brush borrowed from Goton, the cook. Madame saw me and smiled—whether truly or not, I couldn’t say—but she seemed sincere. “Look,” she said, “how tidy Miss Lucy is! You like this path, then?” “Yes,” I replied, “it’s quiet and shady.” “That’s right,” she answered kindly, and added that since I wasn’t responsible for supervision, I could stay there as much as I liked. She even suggested I allow the girls to come and speak English with me.

On that night, I sat on the seat I’d restored, hidden from view, listening to what sounded like distant city noises. They weren’t distant at all—this school stood in the heart of the city. Five minutes brought you to the park, ten to palatial homes. Wide streets nearby sparkled with lights, filled with carriages heading to balls or opera. While our dormitory rang with curfew and silence, the world outside celebrated. But I felt none of that joy. My nature lacked such instincts. I had never seen a ball or opera, though I’d heard them described. Not with longing—to reach them, to shine in them—but merely with curiosity. I wanted only to see, not to participate.

Above me, a young crescent moon hung low in the sky. Through a gap in the branches, I caught sight of it, and beside it, stars I knew from childhood. That same golden curve, with its dark sphere, had once leaned against an old thorn atop a field in England. Now it leaned beside a spire in this foreign capital. Oh, my childhood! I had feelings—even if I lived quietly, spoke little, appeared cold. When I thought of the past, I *could* feel. But the present? Better to be stoic. And the future—mine, especially—better to be numb. So I guarded the pulse of my soul carefully, keeping it still.

I remembered how, once, a thunderstorm broke with fury. A hurricane shook the building. The Catholic girls panicked, praying aloud. I, however, was seized by the storm. It forced me awake, roused me violently. I dressed, slipped out the casement near my bed, and sat on the ledge, feet resting on the roof below. It was wet, wild, pitch-black. Inside, the dormitory trembled with prayers. I couldn’t return. The storm held me—its thunder roared like a hymn no human voice could sing. Clouds split open, lightning blinding. For twenty-four hours afterward, I ached for escape—for something to lift me from my life and carry me forward. That ache, I had to crush. Like Jael driving a nail through Sisera’s temples, I silenced it. Unlike Sisera, it didn’t die—it only stunned. And sometimes, it woke again, wrenching against the nail, making my head bleed, my nerves thrum.

But tonight, I was not restless. My inner turmoil slept. An angel-like presence hovered near—perhaps just the ideal, the dream—pouring balm on my temples, holding a mirror before my closed eyes, showing visions that repeated themselves in dreams. A soft glow from her wings and robe spread over me, over the tent, over the landscape. Jael, the stern woman, sat apart, softened slightly by her captive, yet thinking more of Heber’s return.

By which I mean: the cool night, the sweet dew, filled me with quiet hope—not for anything specific, but a general sense of peace, of ease. Such a mood, so rare and sweet, ought to have heralded good things. Alas, no good came.

Then, abruptly, the harsh reality intruded—ugly, crude, as it so often does. Amid the silence of the stone buildings, trees, and high wall, I heard a sound. A casement creaked. Before I could look up, a tree above me shook—as if struck. Something fell at my feet.

Nine o’clock chimed from St. Jean Baptiste’s clock. Day was fading, but it wasn’t dark. The crescent moon offered little light, but the lingering gold of sunset and the clear sky sustained the twilight. Even in my shadowed path, I could read small print if I approached an opening.

I saw at once what had fallen: a box—small, made of ivory, white and tinted. The lid opened easily. Inside, violets lay piled over a folded pink note, addressed: *Pour la robe grise*. I wore a dress of French grey. Good.

Was this a love letter? A *billet-doux*? I’d heard of such things, but never held one. Was this it?

Not likely. I didn’t believe it for a moment. I had no admirer, no suitor. The other teachers dreamed of lovers—one even believed in a future husband. The older girls knew of suitors; two or three were already betrothed. But I had never stepped into that realm. When others went to church or strolled the boulevards, they always claimed to meet someone of the opposite sex whose gaze told them they were desirable. I never experienced that. I walked, I prayed, I attended mass—but no one seemed to notice me.

Everyone else in Rue Fossette had received a glance from the young doctor’s blue eyes at some point. But not me. His eyes, I must admit, were guiltless, calm as the sky they resembled.

So while I listened to their stories, I wondered at their confidence, their joy—but never thought to look ahead myself.

This, then, was no love letter. I opened it calmly, convinced it wasn’t.

It read—translated:

“Angel of my dreams! A thousand thanks for keeping your promise. I scarcely dared hope you would. I thought you half-joking, and you seemed to think the plan too dangerous—the hour too late, the path too secluded, so often haunted by that dragon, the English teacher—*une véritable bégueule Britannique*, as you say—*espèce de monstre, brusque et rude comme un vieux caporal de grenadiers, et revêche comme une religieuse*” (the reader may forgive my modesty in allowing this flattering sketch of myself to remain in the original).

“You know,” it continued, “that little Gustave has been moved to a master’s room—that favored chamber whose lattice overlooks your prison-ground. I, the best uncle in the world, am allowed to visit him. How I trembled approaching the window, peering into your Eden—an Eden for me, though a desert for you! How I feared to see emptiness—or worse, the dragon! But when I saw, through gaps in the branches, the gleam of your straw hat and the flutter of your grey dress—your dress I’d recognize anywhere—my heart leapt. Why won’t you look up? Cruel, to deny me one glance of those dear eyes! One look would revive me!

I write this quickly, while the doctor examines Gustave. I’ve sealed it in this small casket, with a bouquet of the sweetest flowers—though less sweet than you, my Peri, my all-charming! Ever yours—you well know whom!”

“I wish I did know whom,” I muttered.

And indeed, I wished I knew who *she* was—the girl in the grey dress, the one addressed as “angel.” The note was meant for someone wearing grey and a straw hat. But both were common. The hat was ordinary, worn by many. The grey dress—Madame Beck herself wore one. Another teacher, three boarders—many wore the same shade. It was simply fashionable.

Still, I knew I had to go in. Lights moved in the dormitory—prayers were over, girls were going to bed. Soon, doors would lock, lights extinguish. The front door stood open, letting in the night’s coolness. From the porter’s lodge, a lamp shone, illuminating the long vestibule, the drawing-room doors, and the great street door.

Suddenly, a bell rang—quick, quiet, a cautious tinkle. Rosine darted from her lodge and opened the door. Someone entered, stayed a moment, then left. Rosine came to the garden gate, lamp in hand, looking around. “What a joke!” she laughed. “No one was there.”

“Let me pass,” a familiar voice pleaded. “Only five minutes.” A tall, commanding figure emerged from the house and strode into the garden.

A man in the garden at night—unthinkable. But he knew his privilege. Perhaps he trusted the darkness.

He wandered the paths, searching, stepping on flowers, breaking branches. Finally, he entered the forbidden walk.

There I met him—like a ghost, perhaps.

“Dr. John! It’s found.”

He didn’t ask who found it. With a glance, he saw I held it.

“Do not betray her,” he said, staring at me as if I were the dragon.

“If I were ever tempted to betray, I couldn’t—because I don’t know,” I replied. “Read the note. You’ll see how little it reveals.”

“Perhaps you’ve already read it,” I thought. But I couldn’t believe he wrote it—his style was too different. Besides, I foolishly imagined it would hurt him to call me such names.

His face flushed as he read. “This is too much,” he said. “Cruel. Humiliating.”

I thought it *was* cruel, seeing him so affected. Whether he was at fault or not, someone must be more to blame.

“What will you do?” he asked. “Tell Madame Beck? Cause a scandal?”

I thought I should tell. I said so. But added I didn’t think it would cause a stir—Madame was too discreet to make a fuss about such matters.

He stood silent, thinking. Too proud, too honorable, to beg me to keep silent when duty demanded disclosure.

I wanted to do right, but dreaded hurting him.

Then Rosine glanced out through the open door. I could see her clearly—her dress was grey, like mine.

That detail, combined with earlier events, gave me an idea: perhaps no student was involved. If so, I had no obligation to interfere.

“I’ll keep silent,” I said, “if you can assure me no pupil is implicated. Take the casket, the flowers, the note. I’ll forget it all.”

“Look there!” he whispered, grabbing the casket and pointing through the branches.

I looked.

There was Madame—wrapped in shawl, gown, slippers—descending the steps softly, creeping like a cat around the garden. In two minutes, she’d reach Dr. John.

Yet if she was a cat, he was a leopard—silent, swift, graceful. He watched her turn a corner, then crossed the garden in two noiseless bounds. She reappeared—he was gone.

Rosine helped him, closing the door between him and his pursuer.

I could have fled—but I chose to face Madame openly.

Though I often spent twilight in the garden, I’d never stayed so late. I knew she’d noticed my absence. She’d come to find me, to catch me unawares.

I expected scolding.

But she was kind. Not a word of reproach. No surprise. With her flawless tact—greater than anyone’s, I believe—she claimed she’d simply come out to enjoy “the evening breeze.”

“What a beautiful night!” she exclaimed, gazing at the stars—the moon now hidden behind the tower of Jean Baptiste. “How pleasant! How fresh the air!”

Instead of sending me in, she stayed, walking a few turns with me down the main path. When we returned, she leaned gently on my shoulder as we climbed the steps. At parting, she pressed her cheek to mine. “Good night, my dear friend. Sleep well.”

I smiled, lying awake later—smiling at Madame.

Her sweetness, her grace—so perfect, so practiced—were proof, for one who knew her, that suspicion stirred within her mind. From some hidden vantage—through parted leaves or an open window—she had likely seen something: a glimpse, a shadow, a footfall, a murmur. Though Dr. John had spoken softly, the hum of his voice seemed to echo across the grounds.

She hadn’t seen *what* happened—perhaps not yet. But a delicate, tangled plot lay before her, begging to be unraveled. And at its center, wrapped in cobwebs, was I—Miss Lucy—clumsily caught, like a foolish fly.
