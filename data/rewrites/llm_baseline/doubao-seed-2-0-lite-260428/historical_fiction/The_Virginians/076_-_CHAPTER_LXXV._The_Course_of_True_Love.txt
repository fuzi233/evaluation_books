Had your father, dear young folks, possessed even the smallest modicum of common prudence, not only would this chapter of his history never have been written—you yourselves would never have drawn breath in the world to plague him a hundred ways. You would never come thundering and shouting through the passages when he wants quiet to pore over his books; never rouse him from that doze every healthy country gentleman earns after dinner; never mislay his spectacles right when he needs them, or steal away his newspaper before he’s finished the leading piece. You would never have run him ragged over tailor’s bills, mantua-maker’s bills, and tutor’s fees, never broken his rest mid-night when you took it into your heads to fall ill, sending your silly mother into such a tizzy that she never sat still for half an hour. What use is there in Darby pulling on his nightcap when Joan won’t rest? Every trifling ailment any one of you ever caught scared her half out of her wits, and I protest I never knew a moment’s peace. Were I not the most long-suffering man alive, I’d have been glad to pack the whole rambunctious brood off to boarding school for good.

Now that you’ve outgrown childhood—long petticoats, chickenpox, smallpox, whooping cough, scarlet fever, all the other charming mishaps of juvenile life—your unconscionable mother has gone and proposed turning the whole south wing of the house into a nursery for hypothetical grandchildren. She’s determined to marry off your brother Captain Miles early, just as we were, fretting that he spends too much time loitering at Brookes’s and Goosetree’s when he’s up in London. She has the perversity to hint that while an invitation to Carlton House may sound very grand, it’s far too dangerous for a young man, and that Miles ought to live somewhere far from temptation, sow his few wild oats, and settle down just as we did.

Marry? My dear creature, we never ought to have married at all! By every rule of prudence and duty, I ought to have wriggled out of my little engagement to Miss Theo (who would have found some other perfectly good husband), and wedded a wealthy heiress. Your Uncle John was a parson and could not duel, poor Charley was still a boy at school, and your grandfather was far too old a man to call me out with sword and pistol. There never was a more foolish match in all the world than ours, and all our relations were perfectly right to fly into a rage over it. What are relations made for, after all, if not to be angry and find fault? When Hester marries, mind you, Master George: quarrel with her if she does not pick a husband of your exact choosing. And when George gets his church living, after making senior wrangler and fellow of his college, Miss Hester, toss that pretty little nose of yours right up at whatever young lady he fancies for himself. As for you, my little Theo, I will not part with you. You must never leave your old father: he likes you to play Haydn for him after dinner, and peel his walnuts when the meal is done.

While they had the great “blessing” of meeting and billing and cooing every single day, your two parents drifted through a fool’s paradise, barely heeding the world around them, all its tattle and meddling. Rinaldo was as brave a warrior as ever slew a Turk, but you know he could not resist lingering in Armida’s enchanted garden. Pray, my Lady Armida, what spell did you cast over me in youth, so that glory, fashion, the gaming tables, and the company of every witty man I ever met could not keep me long from your apron strings, or out of reach of your dear, simple prattle? What did we even talk about, through all those endless hours we spent together? I never napped after dinner back then. Which of us was the witty one? Was it me, or was it you? And how on earth did every conversation feel so perfectly delightful?

I remember that year I did not even bother to go gawk at the Earl of Ferrers’s trial and hanging, when all the rest of London was crowding to see the spectacle. The King of Prussia’s capital had fallen; if the Austrians and Russians had camped right outside the Tower of London, the city could not have buzzed more. But Miss Theo and her young gentleman felt no great rush of pity or indignation over the news. What was the fate of Leipzig or Berlin to us? The plain truth is, that dear little old house on Dean Street was a garden of delights all its own. I have been idle in the years since, but never half so happy. Shall we order the postchaise tomorrow, my dear, leave the children to keep house, and drive all the way back to London to see if those old lodgings are still to let? You shall sit in your old spot in the window, and wave a little handkerchief as I walk up the street. Call what we did imprudent all you like. We would do it all over again, would we not? My good friends, if Venus herself had walked into the room to claim the golden apple, I was so infatuated I would have handed it straight to your mother. And had she been the one choosing, she would have picked me, in my threadbare old coat, over Lord Clive with every single diamond he brought back from India.

Once, for a terrible little stretch of that year, I half-went mad. I daydreamed of turning highwayman just to get caught and hanged like Lord Ferrers, or enlisting in the Prussian army and begging one of his enemies to knock my brains out, or signing up for the India service to perform some reckless exploit that would end in my utter destruction. Ah me! That was a dreadful time. Your mother scarce dares speak of it now save in a whisper of terror, and even thinking of it makes her flinch—such cruel, sharp pain it was. She was unhappy years later on that same anniversary, until one of you was born on that exact date. Suppose we had been parted? What would have become of us? What would my lot have been without her? When I let myself dwell on that possibility, the whole world goes blank. I do not speak of a parting now, after thirty happy years of union God has granted us. We are in the autumn of our days, our successors are grown and ready, and whichever of us is called away first knows the other will follow before long. But we were actually parted once, in our youth, and I tremble still to think what ruin might have fallen on us if a dearest friend had not stepped in to bring us back together.

All unknown to me, my relatives in England—very likely thinking they were acting for my own good—chose to write to Madam Esmond in Virginia, and lay out what they called the utter folly of the engagement I had entered into. Every last one of them sang the exact same song. I saw all those letters years later, when my mother showed them to me at home in Virginia, and burned the whole cursed stack to ash. Aunt Bernstein was first in line with her advice: a young person with no remarkable good looks, no grand family, no money? No match could be more imprudent, it was their duty to break it off for dear George’s sake. She had a dozen eligible heiresses lined up for me, what a shame to throw myself away on this girl, her sister ought to step in at once.

Lady Warrington had to write too, in her own peculiar style. Her ladyship’s letter was crammed full of scripture texts, dressing all her worldly disapproval in fancy phylacteries. She pointed out that I was keeping low company, running about with actors and other such people who, she would not say were absolutely ungodly (Heaven forbid!), but who were deplorably worldly. She would not say outright that some artful woman had inveigled me for her daughter, after having failed to capture my younger brother Harry first. She was far from speaking ill of the young woman I had chosen, but it was undeniable that Miss Lambert had no fortune, no expectations, and her parents were clearly eager to compromise me. Sir Miles, that wise judge of worldly affairs, fully agreed with her, even begged her to write and entreat Madam Esmond to interfere before the ill-advised match could go forward.

And then the new Countess of Castlewood had to stick her finger in the pie too, writing a majestic letter to Madam Esmond stating that as Providence had placed her at the head of the Esmond family, it was her sacred duty to warn her kinswoman to put a stop to this marriage. I am certain the three women laid their heads together beforehand, sending packet after packet of warnings across the Atlantic to the Virginian lady.

One raw April morning, I set off as usual to pay my call on my dearest girl, expecting to find her waiting with that familiar soft smile to welcome me. Instead I found Mrs. Lambert, her eyes bright red with crying, and the General as pale as death. “Read this, George Warrington!” he said, as his wife dropped her head in her hands. I recognised the handwriting at once. Even to this day, the sound of fire irons clattering against a hearth in a room overhead gives me a tremor—for I heard exactly that noise that day, coming from the girls’ bedchamber where they were sobbing together. Poor, gentle child! Poor Theo!

“What can I do after this, George, my poor boy?” the General asked, pacing the room in desperation. I did not even finish reading all of Madam Esmond’s letter, for a faint, sick dread washed over me, though I fear I could recite great chunks of it by heart to this day. Its tone was polite enough, its words temperate, but every line implied that Mr. and Mrs. Lambert had inveigled me into the marriage, that they knew full well such a union was unworthy of me. Madam Esmond wrote that, as she understood it, they had first wanted the exact same match for her younger son Harry, a plan they had only abandoned when they learned Harry would not be the heir to the Virginian estate. If Mr. Lambert was a man of spirit and honour, as he was said to be, she could scarcely imagine he would persist in pushing for the match after her warning. She would not lay commands on her son, whose temper she knew well, but for the sake of Miss Lambert’s own reputation and comfort, she insisted the dissolution of the engagement must come from her family, not from the just unwillingness of Rachel Esmond Warrington of Virginia.

“God help us, George!” the General said, “and give us all strength to bear this grief, these cruel charges your mother has seen fit to bring! They are hard, but they do not matter half so much as sparing my poor girl all the pain we possibly can. She has never given a soul a moment’s unhappiness since the day she was born, and it is wicked that she should be made to suffer.” He passed his hand over his own dry, glistening eyes.

“It was my fault, Martin! My fault!” poor Mrs. Lambert wept.

“Your mother spoke fairly to us once, and gave her promise,” the General said.

“And do you think I will withdraw mine?” I cried, swearing a thousand frantic oaths that I was bound to Theo before Heaven, that nothing on earth would ever part me from her.

“She herself will demand the parting,” the General said. “She is a good, dutiful girl. She will not have her father and mother called schemers, and treated with public scorn. Your mother most likely did not realise what harm she was doing, but the harm is done. You may see the child, and she will tell you the same. Of course, George, you will give me your word of honour not to try and see her afterwards. As soon as my military business allows, we will leave London, and all be together somewhere quiet. It is you, perhaps, who ought to leave first. But give me your word, at any rate, that you will not seek her out. We must spare her pain, sir! We must spare her pain!” The good man sat down, so deep in his own anguish that I, still not yet crushed fully under the weight of my own grief, felt his suffering, and pitied it.

It could not be that the dear lips I had kissed only yesterday would speak to me but once more. We were all in that familiar room, the one we met in every day, my own sketchbook sitting on the table next to her little workbox. She was in the chamber upstairs, and she would have to come down soon. The door creaked open, and I saw her sweet face, pale as our little Mary’s was when we thought she would die of her fever. There was even a faint, brave smile on her lips. She came up to me, and kissed my cheek. “Good-bye, dear George!” she said.

Great Heaven! I sit here now, an old man, with my wife’s old workbox across the table from me, and she is but five minutes away in the kitchen serving out tea to her pensioners, and my eyes grow so dim and full I can not see the words on the page before me. I am three and twenty years old again, reliving every step of that agony. Who dared sully her sweet, innocent love with suspicion? Who had any right to stab such a soft, gentle bosom? You can see those three ladies sharpening their knives, and that poor child baring her breast to the blade.

“What makes you look so angry, papa?” my wife says, stepping into the room.

“My love,” I tell her, “it is the thirteenth of April.” A flash of old pain shoots across her face, then melts into a soft, tender smile. She bore that martyrdom, and out of the old pang comes a halo of forgiveness. I can not forgive, not until I grow so doting in old age I can no longer remember a single thing. “Hal will be home for Easter,” she says, “he will bring two or three of his Cambridge friends with him.” And straight away she starts chattering of plans for little parties, treats, walks to amuse the boys. When is she ever doing anything but plotting to make everyone around her happy?

An old man in spectacles scribbling down his own pitiful old miseries over his ledger is a quaint, ridiculous sight, I will own. My corns ache, I know, but I suspect every man’s neighbour has his own pinching shoes. I will not howl over my old grief for too long. Many a man, I dare say, has had the light of his day snuffed out suddenly, the joy of his life extinguished, left to wander in dark, vague torment. To this day, when I pick up Howel’s Letters and stumble on the passage about Prince Charles in Spain, that whole old tragedy comes leaping back to life. I fled to Brighthelmstone then, took a room facing east, and watched the sun rise every morning for weeks after nights of total wakefulness, smoking my pipe of Virginian tobacco right in his face. If I happen to see that same sun rise there now, I shake my fist at him, remembering what horrible grief and savage wrath he once watched me suffer. Even though my wife is mine for good now, I am still cross about it. Who dared make us hurt so?

I kept my promise. I never set foot inside the General’s house after that last, terrible parting. But at night I would creep past, and stare up at her window, watching the lamp burn long into the dark. I would visit her little brother at the Chartreux school, stuff him full of cakes and hand him half-crowns. I would invite her elder brother to dine with me, and almost kiss him goodbye when he left. I breakfasted every morning at a little coffee house in Whitehall just to see General Lambert walk to his office, and we would nod at each other sadly, and pass on without a word. The women never came out. They were with her, trying to talk her into forgetting me. Oh, the weary, weary days! Oh, the maddening, endless stretch of time!

At last I saw a doctor’s chariot drawing up outside the General’s door every single day. Was she ill? I am half ashamed to own that I was almost glad she was ill. My own suffering was so infernal, that I greedily wanted her to share my pain. And of course she would—what gentle, compassionate heart like hers would not feel every last pang of my own grief, what pain would she not bear to spare me even a single twinge?

I hunted that doctor down. I poured my whole story out to him, laid my heart bare with such passionate sincerity that he won his sympathy. My confession let him understand his young patient’s malady, the one no dose of his physic could cure. I told him everything: that I would marry her no matter what risks I had to brave, that without her I was utterly wrecked and ruined, and cared not a farthing what became of me. My mother had once given her consent, and to take it back now, when the bond between us was drawn so tight it outstripped any duty I owed her, was nothing less than cruelty.

That good man must have been unhappy in love himself once, so gently did he know how to succour the miserable. He would not act as a secret messenger between two forbidden lovers outright, but he said he could safely tell Miss Lambert that he had seen me, and was prescribing for my broken heart as well as hers. He said he had never seen anyone bear suffering with such angelical sweetness as Theo. He prescribed Jesuit’s bark for her, he told me, but he suspected hearing news of me did her more good than all his medicine put together. I had a little ring set with a cameo of Hercules, too small for his finger, and the good old man never wore trinkets anyway, but I made him hang it to his watch chain, in the hopes that Theo would see it, and know the token came from me.

I clung to my friend Spencer in those days, the man from the Temple who had also made his own ill-fated love match, and we would walk for miles, back and forth between my lodgings and his chambers, our conversation never straying far from the women we loved.
