Home had the hush of a kitchen after the fire has been damped down—no clatter of pans, no kettle’s homely singing to stitch the hours together—only the thick, stunned silence that follows a day of shouting and running and dread. Even the house’s ordinary breath seemed afraid to make a sound, as if the very boards underfoot knew there lay, behind the closed door upstairs, a stillness beyond all scolding, beyond all calling.

Mr. Hale, who had made sure, with the anxious care of one who can do nothing else, that there should be some little refreshment ready for Margaret’s return, dropped at last into his accustomed chair. His hand rested upon the worn arm as it always had; yet he sat as if the chair belonged to another man—sunk in one of those waking dreams that were not rest, only a slow drowning.

Dixon had taken refuge in action. She had fetched Mary Higgins to the kitchen, and between them they made a kind of stern domestic order out of disorder—bread cut, broth warmed, linen laid straight. Mary’s tongue was in full employ; but she drove it in an angry whisper, as though the very idea of speaking above breath might be a desecration while death was in the house. Her murmured scoldings went on like a suppressed simmer, the sort of sound that ordinarily would have comforted Margaret with its familiarity—yet now only sharpened the sense of what was missing: the old, gentle household noises, the easy talk, the freedom to call up the stairs.

Margaret, for her part, had resolved on one silence within another. She would not tell her father of the last crowning terror—how close peril had come, and with what sickening suddenness. It had ended well. There was no use in laying that weight on him when it could not now alter anything. The only fear worth fearing was that Leonards might scrape together money enough to follow Frederick to London and hunt him there. Yet the chances were many against such a pursuit. Frederick would be on his guard. In a day or two, at most, he would be safely out of England. Margaret forced herself to accept this as one accepts the day’s bread: not because it is delicious, but because one must live.

“I suppose we shall hear from Mr. Bell to-morrow,” she said at length, grasping for any thread of ordinary expectation.

“Yes,” her father answered, without moving his eyes. “I suppose so.”

“If he can come, he will be here to-morrow evening, I should think.”

“If he cannot come,” Mr. Hale went on, the words heavy as if lifted one by one, “I shall ask Mr. Thornton to go with me to the funeral. I cannot go alone. I should break down utterly.”

“Don’t ask Mr. Thornton, papa. Let me go with you,” Margaret cried, too quickly—her feeling outrunning her caution.

“You!” he said, startled. “My dear, women do not generally go.”

“No; because they can’t control themselves. Women of our class don’t go because they have no power over their emotions, and yet are ashamed of showing them. Poor women go, and don’t care if they are seen overwhelmed with grief.” Her voice shook once, but steadied. “But I promise you, papa, if you will let me go, I will be no trouble. Don’t have a stranger, and leave me out. Dear papa—if Mr. Bell cannot come, I shall go. I won’t urge my wish against your will, if he does.”

Mr. Bell could not come. The letter arrived with its kind, affectionate pain in every line—gout had laid him holdfast. He was truly grieved; he hoped, if they would have him, to come soon, for his Milton property needed his personal looking after. His agent had written that his presence was absolutely necessary. He confessed, too, with that honest self-knowledge which is almost tenderness, that he had avoided Milton as long as he could; and that now the only thought that reconciled him to the journey was the hope of seeing and perhaps comforting his old friend.

When Mr. Hale spoke of inviting Mr. Thornton nevertheless, Margaret felt something in her draw back as if from a cold iron. She could not give it a tidy name. It was not mere dislike; it was a bodily repugnance, as if some flavour had been suddenly tainted and would spoil all the dish. She pleaded, coaxed, reasoned—anything rather than have that request go from their door.

On the night before the funeral there came a stately note from Mrs. Thornton to Miss Hale, saying that, at her son’s desire, their carriage should attend the funeral, if it would not be disagreeable to the family.

Margaret read it, and in a motion half impatience, half helplessness, tossed it to her father.

“Oh, don’t let us have these forms,” she said. “Let us go alone—you and me, papa. They don’t care for us; or else he would have offered to go himself, and not have proposed this sending an empty carriage.”

“I thought you were so extremely averse to his going, Margaret,” Mr. Hale said, looking up with mild surprise.

“And so I am. I don’t want him to come at all; and I should especially dislike the idea of our asking him. But this—this seems such a mockery of mourning that I did not expect it from him.”

Her own words broke something in her. Tears came, hot and sudden, as if the day’s iron restraint had at last found its weakest seam. She startled her father by the burst; for she had been so quiet in her grief, so thoughtful for others, so patient and gentle in all things, that he could not understand this restlessness now. He tried to soothe her with a tenderness that had been almost absent from him in the first stunned hours—not from want of love, but from being overborne; yet the more he sought to comfort, the more she wept. The night would not settle. It passed in broken dozes and listening, as if she waited for some familiar sound that would never come again: the soft step on the stair, the faint clink of china, the little domestic music by which a household knows itself alive.

In the morning, when she was already ill prepared for any new shaking of the nerves, a letter came from Frederick.

Mr. Lennox was out of town. His clerk had said he would return by the following Tuesday at latest, perhaps on Monday. After consideration, Frederick had determined upon remaining in London a day or two longer. The temptation to come down to Milton again had been very strong; but the thought of Mr. Bell domesticated in his father’s house, and the alarm at the last moment at the railway station, had decided him. Margaret might be assured he would take every precaution against being tracked by Leonards.

She was thankful—thankful with a kind of shame at being thankful—that she received this letter while her father was absent in her mother’s room. Had he been there, he would have expected her to read it aloud; and the allusions, slight as Frederick made them, to recognition and pursuit would have raised such a nervous alarm in him as she could not have soothed away. Even she felt her blood run cold as her eyes went over the lines again: London; delay; Leonards. How could she have urged the plan of consulting Mr. Lennox? At the time it had appeared prudent—so small a delay, so slight an added risk. Yet since then everything had conspired to make it seem folly. She fought against the regret because it could do nothing but sting; yet it stung all the same. She knew, too, how differently such remorse would prey upon her father. His mind, in its weakened state, would not cast off morbid self-accusation as one shakes off dust; it would cling like soot.

So she gathered herself, summoning every force. Her father, when he came down, seemed to have forgotten that they expected a letter at all. He was absorbed in a single idea—that the last visible token of his wife’s presence, the poor familiar shape of her body in the house, was to be taken away and hidden from his sight for ever.

When the undertaker’s man began to arrange the crape draperies about him, Mr. Hale trembled pitifully, as if the stiff black folds were not cloth but some living thing binding him. His eyes went to Margaret with the beseeching look of one who cannot bear his own weakness.

When the man at last released him, he tottered towards her, murmuring brokenly, “Pray for me, Margaret. I have no strength left in me. I cannot pray. I give her up because I must. I try to bear it; indeed I do. I know it is God’s will. But I cannot see why she died. Pray for me, Margaret, that I may have faith to pray. It is a great strait, my child.”

In the coach Margaret sat close, almost supporting him with her arm as if he were a sick man on a jolting road. She spoke to him in the low steady voice one uses to keep a kettle from boiling over—no flourish, no dramatics, only what must be done to prevent a breaking. She repeated the noble verses of comfort she could remember, and the texts that speak of resignation and rest. Her voice did not falter; and in the act of ministering she seemed to draw strength as a hearth draws heat when a log is set rightly. Her father’s lips moved after her. He repeated the familiar words when her voice prompted them, in that terrible patient struggle of one who knows what he ought to feel and yet cannot force the feeling to grow in him.

At the graveside her fortitude nearly gave way. Dixon, with the smallest motion of her hand—more a twitch than a gesture—directed Margaret’s notice to Nicholas Higgins and his daughter, standing a little aloof, yet deeply attentive to the ceremonial. Nicholas wore his usual fustian; but there was a bit of black stuff sewn round his hat—an awkward, homely mark of mourning, all the more touching because he had never shown such outward sign for his own Bessy. It was as if this death, in the master’s house, had reached into the worker’s life too, stitching a black band round a rough hat in silent acknowledgment of a shared human loss.

But Mr. Hale saw nothing. He went on repeating, mechanically, the funeral service as the clergyman read. When all was ended, he sighed twice or thrice, like a man surfacing only to sink again. Then he laid his hand upon Margaret’s arm and, without words, entreated to be led away, as if he were blind and she his guide.

Dixon sobbed aloud, her handkerchief over her face. She was so wrapped in her own grief that she did not perceive the crowd dispersing—those inevitable lookers-on who gather wherever death makes a public pause—until someone spoke close to her.

It was Mr. Thornton.

He had been there all the time, standing with bent head behind a group of people, so that no one had recognised him. His voice, when he spoke, was low and constrained, as if grief had turned it to stone.

“I beg your pardon—but can you tell me how Mr. Hale is? And Miss Hale, too? I should like to know how they both are.”

“Of course, sir,” Dixon answered, wiping her eyes with an angry jerk, as if tears insulted as well as hurt. “They are much as is to be expected. Master is terribly broke down. Miss Hale bears up better than likely.”

Mr. Thornton would rather, in his selfish heart, have heard that she had been overcome. There was enough of passionate egotism in him to take fierce pleasure in imagining her leaning on him for comfort, as a drooping child nestles into its mother, dependent upon her warmth for life. A few days ago he would have indulged that delicious vision even after all Margaret’s repulse.

Now it was miserably disturbed—no, haunted, torn through—by what he had seen near the Outwood station. The remembrance of the handsome young man beside her, and the attitude of familiar confidence in which she had stood, shot through him like agony until he clenched his hands hard to master the pain.

At that late hour—so far from home! To galvanise his old trust in Margaret’s pure maidenliness into life cost him a great moral effort; and when the effort relaxed, the trust fell dead again, and wild fancies chased each other through his mind like fever-dreams. Dixon’s words gave him a little miserable gnawing confirmation.

“She bore up better than likely.” Then she had hope to look to, some bright prospect that could lighten even the newly-made motherless daughter’s hours. He knew how she would love. He had not loved her without gaining the instinct that her heart, once given, would fill the world with sunlight for the one who deserved it. Even in mourning she would rest with a peaceful faith upon sympathy.

His sympathy? Whose? That other man’s.

The thought made his pale, grave face grow doubly wan and stern.

“I suppose I may call,” he said, coldly. “On Mr. Hale, I mean. He will perhaps admit me after to-morrow or so.”

He spoke as if the answer were indifferent. It was not. For all his pain he longed to see the very author of it. Though he could hate Margaret at moments, when that gentle, familiar attitude rose before him, he yet had a restless desire to renew her image, a craving for the very air she breathed, as if the soundscape of his life had been altered and he must seek the lost note again though it cut him. He circled and circled nearer to the fatal centre of his own passion.

“I dare say, sir, master will see you,” Dixon said. “He was very sorry to have to deny you the other day; but circumstances was not agreeable just then.”

For some reason—chance, or stubborn pride, or the curious power that grief has of rearranging what one tells and what one holds back—Dixon never named to Margaret this little meeting by the grave. So it came about that Margaret never knew Mr. Thornton had stood unseen among the mourners at her mother’s funeral.
