Clavering Park had once known a gloom so complete that the very shutters seemed to have gone into mourning; when its master fled abroad, the house mourned after him like a deserted soup-tureen—huge, silver, and useless without a hand to stir it. Yet Sir Francis, returning to his own walls, did not bring spring with him. He inhabited only a few ground-floor rooms, as a man camps in the ruins of his own magnificence; and the rest of the mansion—passages, saloons, long galleries made for laughter—was closed up, silent, and cold, like an overbuilt larder in a hard winter.

His housekeeper and a helper from the lodge-gate attended him with that dutiful briskness which is half pity and half professional habit, and cooked a portion of the game he spent his dreary mornings in shooting. Even that comfort had a melancholy savor: the birds were roasted, yes, but without triumph—no bustling household to applaud the carving, no bright table-talk to make the gravy seem richer. The crack of the gun, the thud of a falling pheasant, the thin music of knives on plates in a half-empty dining-room: such were the domestic acoustics of Clavering Park now, where once the voices of guests had rolled through the corridors like a generous chorus.

Lightfoot, Sir Francis’s man, had slipped over to my Lady’s service; and (as Pen learned by letter from Mr. Smirke, who performed the ceremony) had carried out his prudent intention of marrying Mrs. Bonner, my Lady’s woman. Mrs. Bonner—mature, saving, and resolute—had long kept her feelings in a tidy drawer, like good napery; but at last, being struck by the charms of youth, she brought them out with her bank-book and her person, and bestowed both upon Lightfoot. Their joint ambition was to be landlord and landlady of the Clavering Arms; and it was agreed they should remain in Lady Clavering’s service until quarter-day, then take possession of their inn and its beer, its beds, its bar, its unavoidable quarrels, and its perpetual clatter of arriving boots.

Pen promised graciously that when Sir Francis vacated his seat in Parliament in the young man’s favor, he would give his election dinner at that very Clavering Arms. And as Pen’s uncle had arranged it—Clavering seeming able to refuse him nothing—Arthur came down in September to Clavering Park. The Baronet was very glad of a companion: solitude makes a man listen too hard to his own thoughts, and Sir Francis preferred other people’s purses and laughter. Within a couple of days, Pen provided him with the small ready money he desired; and no sooner did these modest funds warm Sir Francis’s pocket than his health and spirits, in the most miraculous manner, demanded business at Chatteris and the neighboring watering-places, of which the county boasts many—chiefly when there are race-grounds and billiard-rooms to transact it in. Off he went to “see to his affairs,” leaving Arthur master of the great silent house and its echoing respectability.

Arthur could live alone well enough. He had the uncommon talent of being able to dine with his own company and find it tolerable—especially when he had books, paper, and a cigar. He could walk with the gamekeeper of a morning and read of an evening; and if the Park’s emptiness had a sound, it was not a human one but a natural one—the wind in the trees, the distant caw, the soft, satisfied sigh of a fire settling down after it has done its duty.

In truth, Pen found the society of Sir Francis perfectly intolerable within two or three days; and with a mischievous satisfaction he had offered the pecuniary aid which the other solicited, providing him at once with the means of fleeing his own house. Besides, our ingenious friend had work to do: he must ingratiate himself with the townspeople of Clavering and the voters of the borough he hoped to represent. He set himself to it with the more eagerness because he remembered how unpopular he had been there before—and because his sense of humor made him relish the whole performance.

Naturally rather reserved and silent in public, he became all at once as frank, easy, and jovial as Captain Strong. He laughed with anybody who would exchange a laugh; shook hands right and left with a cordiality that deserved a patent; appeared at market-day and the farmers’ ordinary; and in fine behaved like a consummate hypocrite, which is to say, like a gentleman of spotless integrity bent on gaining a seat and willing to pay for it in smiles.

The town accepted him with that practical appetite with which it accepted everything: as one takes a free sample from a shop, knowing perfectly well it is meant to tempt you into buying a whole pound. A glib tongue costs the distributor nothing; the receiver likes to taste it. Pen went about laboriously simple, adroitly pleased, a wholly different being from the scornful young dandy the inhabitants remembered ten years ago. He had the air of a man who not only asked after your wife but could imagine her making decent broth; who not only admired your harvest but could smell the wheat. It was astonishing how much more human a candidate becomes when he pretends to possess a palate.

The Rectory was shut up. Doctor Portman was off to Harrogate with his gout and his family—an absence Pen deplored in a letter in which, in a few kind words, he expressed regret at missing his old friend, whose advice he wanted and whose aid he might one day require. But Pen consoled himself by making acquaintance with Mr. Simcoe, the opposition preacher; with the two partners of the cloth-factory at Chatteris; and with the Independent preacher there—all of whom he met at the Clavering Athenaeum, which the Liberal party had set up in the “advanced spirit of the age,” and perhaps in opposition to the aristocratic old reading-room where the Edinburgh Review had once scarcely been admitted and where no tradesmen were allowed an entrance, as if print itself became contagious when handled by a man with a shop.

Pen propitiated the younger cloth partner by inviting him to dine at the Park; he complimented the Honourable Mrs. Simcoe with hares and partridges from the same quarter, along with a request to read her husband’s last sermon. And being a little unwell one day, the rascal took advantage of the circumstance to show his tongue to Mr. Huxter, who sent medicines and called the next morning. Old Pendennis would have been delighted with his pupil’s dexterity. Pen himself was amused with the sport, and his success put him in a wicked good-humor, as of a man who has found the secret spice by which plain fare is made palatable to any company.

Yet when, at night, he walked out of Clavering after presiding at an Athenaeum meeting or enduring an evening with Mrs. Simcoe (who, with her husband, was properly awed by the young Londoner’s reputation), other thoughts came on him. There are places that do not let you be merely a politician; they make you a son.

Crossing the old familiar bridge over the rushing Brawl, he heard the water’s ceaseless talk—an ancient, honest sound that no committee had ever voted into being and no fashionable doctrine could silence. And there among the trees stood his cottage of Fairoaks, its dark outline clear against a starlit sky. In the window of the room he remembered best, there was still a light.

It seemed to pursue him with a wan reproach, as though it were his mother’s spirit watching and warning. The night was very clear; the stars shone keen as newly-whetted knives; the river ran on, tireless. The old home trees whispered and moved gently over the cottage roof. In the faint glimmer lay the terrace where, as a boy, he had walked on summer evenings—ardent, trustful, unspotted, untried, sheltered as yet from the world’s contamination in the pure and anxious bosom of love. The clock of the near town struck midnight with a clang that broke his reverie like a dropped dish in a hushed room; and he went onwards through the lodge into the Clavering avenue and under the dark arcades of rustling limes.

When next he saw the cottage, it was smiling in sunset. The bedroom windows were open where the light had burned the night before. Captain Stokes of the Bombay Artillery—Pen’s tenant (whose mother, old Mrs. Stokes, lived in Clavering)—received his landlord with cordiality; showed him over the grounds and the new pond he had made in the back garden from the stables; talked confidentially of roof and chimneys; and begged Mr. Pendennis to name a day when he would do himself and Mrs. Stokes the pleasure to, etc.

Pen, who had been a fortnight in the country, excused himself for not calling sooner by frankly owning he had not had the heart to do it.

“I understand you, sir,” the Captain said; and Mrs. Stokes, who had slipped away at the bell (how odd it seemed to Pen to ring the bell!), presently came down in her best gown, surrounded by her children. The young ones clambered about Stokes; the boy jumped into an arm-chair.

It was Pen’s father’s arm-chair. Arthur remembered the days when he would as soon have thought of mounting the King’s throne as of seating himself in that chair. He asked if Miss Stokes—who was the very image of her mamma—could play: he should like to hear a tune on that piano.

She played. The old piano sounded again—enfeebled by age, its notes thinned and tremulous, as if the instrument itself had grown old in remembering. He did not listen to the player. He listened to Laura singing as in the days of their youth; he saw his mother bending and beating time over the girl’s shoulder. Memory has its own dialect, perfectly understood by the heart and perfectly untranslatable to any other listener in the room. No dictionary in the world can render it.

The dinner at Fairoaks, given in Pen’s honor by his tenant, was very stupid and melancholy for Pen at first. Old Mrs. Stokes was there; Captain Glanders; Squire Hobnel; and the clergyman and his lady from Tinckleton. The talk went round and round like a dish passed too often—polite, lukewarm, and growing duller at each circuit—until a waiter from Clavering arrived to assist the captain’s stable-boy and Mrs. Stokes’s butler. Pen remembered the fellow as a street boy; he was now barber in the place, and, as if to prove that advancement does not at once improve one’s grip, he dropped a plate over Pen’s shoulder.

“I suppose, Hodson, your hands are slippery with bear’s-grease,” Mr. Hobnell remarked (who also employed him). “He’s always dropping the crockery about, that Hodson is—haw, haw!”

Hodson blushed and looked so disconcerted that Pen burst out laughing; and by that lucky crack of china, good-humor and hilarity were the order of the evening. For the second course there was a hare and partridges top and bottom; and when, after the withdrawal of the servants, Pen said to the Vicar of Tinckleton, “I think, Mr. Stooks, you should have asked Hodson to cut the hare,” the joke was taken instantly by the clergyman, then by Captains Stokes and Glanders, and then by Mr. Hobnell—who arrived rather late in the laughter, but with an immense guffaw, as men do who like mirth best when it has been chewed for them.

While Mr. Pen was in the country practicing his agreeable hypocrisies, the lady of his choice—if not of his affections—came up to London from the Tunbridge villa bound upon shopping expeditions and important business. She was accompanied by old Mrs. Bonner, her mother’s maid: a woman who had lived with Blanche since infancy, quarrelled with her at every stage of growth, and loved her at all of them; and who, being about to quit Lady Clavering’s service for the hymeneal state, was anxious—like a good soul—to bestow some token of respectful kindness upon her old and young mistress before she left them altogether, to take up her post as the wife of Lightfoot and landlady of the Clavering Arms.

Mrs. Bonner took advantage of Miss Blanche’s taste to make the purchase she intended for her ladyship. She requested Blanche to choose something for herself, something that should be to her liking and remind her of her old nurse who had attended her through wakeful nights, teething, childish fever, and all the small tragedies by which babies educate their elders in fatigue. The purchases were made; and as the nurse insisted on buying an immense Bible for Blanche, the young lady suggested that Bonner should purchase a large Johnson’s Dictionary for her mamma. Each of the two women might certainly profit by the present; and Blanche, who liked to do mischief under the guise of moral improvement, enjoyed the thought.

Then Mrs. Bonner invested money in bargains in linen-drapery that might be useful at the Clavering Arms, and bought a red-and-yellow neck-handkerchief which Blanche recognized at once as intended for Mr. Lightfoot. Younger than herself by at least five-and-twenty years, Mrs. Bonner regarded that youth with a fondness at once parental and conjugal and loved to lavish ornaments on his person, which already glittered with pins, rings, studs, chains, and seals purchased at the good creature’s expense. One could almost hear the little metallic jingle of those vanities, as if Lightfoot had been fitted with a harness of courtship.

It was in the Strand that Mrs. Bonner made her purchases, aided by Blanche, who liked the fun very well. And when the old lady had bought everything she desired and was leaving the shop, Blanche—with a smiling face and a sweet bow to one of the shopmen—said, “Pray, sir, will you have the kindness to show us the way to Shepherd’s Inn?”

Shepherd’s Inn was but a few score yards off; Old Castle Street was close by. The elegant young shopman pointed out the turning. Blanche and her companion walked off.

“Shepherd’s Inn!” Bonner inquired. “What can you want in Shepherd’s Inn, Miss Blanche? Mr. Strong lives there. Do you want to go and see the Captain?”

“I should like to see the Captain very well. I like the Captain; but it is not him I want. I want to see a dear little good girl, who was very kind to—to Mr. Arthur when he was so ill last year, and saved his life almost; and I want to thank her and ask her if she would like anything. I looked out several of my dresses on purpose this morning, Bonner!”

And she looked at Bonner as though virtue were a ribbon and she had tied it on herself with uncommon skill. Blanche was very fond of sugar-plums; she would have fed the poor on them when she had had enough, and given a country girl a ball-dress when she had worn it and tired of it.

“Pretty girl—pretty young woman!” mumbled Mrs. Bonner. “I know I want no pretty young women to come about Lightfoot.” And in imagination she peopled the Clavering Arms with a harem of the most hideous chambermaids and barmaids, all elbows and freckles, none with a tempting mouth.

Blanche, in pink and blue with feathers, flowers, and trinkets—no chatelaine existed yet, or she would surely have worn one like a portable shop—entered Shepherd’s Inn like a radiant proclamation of fashion. Her shot-silk dress whispered as she moved; her mantle fell with studied grace; her parasol made a little private rustle against the city’s harsher noises. She bewildered Mrs. Bolton, who was scrubbing the lodge-floor and whose ears were full of the wet slap of brush on stone and the sour hiss of brown-soap water. Betsy-Jane and Ameliar-Ann looked at Blanche with delight, as children look at iced cakes behind glass.

Blanche smiled upon them with ineffable sweetness and protection; like Rowena going to see Rebecca; like Marie Antoinette visiting the poor in a famine; like the Marchioness of Carabas alighting from her carriage-and-four at a pauper-tenant’s door and taking from John No. II the packet of Epsom salts for the invalid’s benefit, carrying it with her own imperial hand into the sick-room. Blanche felt a queen stepping down from her throne to visit a subject and enjoyed all the bland consciousness of doing a good action.

“My good woman! I want to see Fanny—Fanny Bolton; is she here?”

Mrs. Bolton, seeing such splendor in such a place, suspected at once it must be a play-actor or something worse.

“What do you want with Fanny, pray?” she asked.

“I am Lady Clavering’s daughter—you have heard of Sir Francis Clavering? And I wish very much indeed to see Fanny Bolton.”

“Pray step in, miss. Betsy-Jane, where’s Fanny?”

Betsy-Jane said Fanny had gone into No. 3 staircase, on which Mrs. Bolton said she was probably in Strong’s rooms and bade the child go and see.

“In Captain Strong’s rooms! Oh, let us go to Captain Strong’s rooms,” cried Miss Blanche. “I know him very well. You dearest little girl, show us the way to Captain Strong!” For the lodge-floor reeked of scrubbing, and the goddess did not like the smell of brown-soap; it offended her as industrial odors offend poets—by reminding them that cleanliness has a price and a sound.

And as they passed up the stairs, a gentleman by the name of Costigan, who happened to be swaggering about the court and gave a very knowing look with his “oi” under Blanche’s bonnet, remarked to himself, “That’s a devilish foine gyurll, bedad, goan up to Sthrong and Altamont: they’re always having foine gyurlls up their stairs.”

“Hallo—hwhat’s that?” he presently said, looking up at the windows. From them issued piercing shrieks.

At the sound of a distressed female voice, the intrepid Cos rushed up the stairs as fast as his old legs would carry him, nearly overthrown by Strong’s servant descending. Costigan found the outer door of Strong’s chambers open and began to thunder at the knocker. After many fierce knocks, the inner door was partially unclosed and Strong’s head appeared.

“It’s oi, me boy. Hwhat’s that noise, Sthrong?” asked Costigan.

“Go to the d——!” was the only answer, and the door was shut on Cos’s venerable red nose. He went downstairs muttering threats, vowing he would have satisfaction for the indignity—though indignities, like cheap spirits, were a commodity he had taken in often and seldom paid for.

Meanwhile, the reader, more fortunate than Captain Costigan, may be made acquainted with the secret withheld from that officer.

It has been said how generous Mr. Altamont could be; and when well supplied with funds, how liberally he spent them. Of a hospitable turn, he had no greater pleasure than drinking in company; so there was no man more welcome at Greenwich and Richmond than the Emissary of the Nawaub of Lucknow. Now it chanced that on the very day when Blanche and Mrs. Bonner climbed to Strong’s room in Shepherd’s Inn, the Colonel had invited Miss Delaval of the ——— Theatre Royal and her mother, Mrs. Hodge, to a little party down the river; it had been agreed they were to meet at chambers and then walk down to a landing in the Strand to take water.

So when Mrs. Bonner and Mes Larmes came to the door, where Grady, Altamont’s servant, stood, the domestic said, “Walk in, ladies,” with the utmost affability, and led them into a room arranged as if they had been expected. Indeed, two bouquets from Covent Garden—Altamont’s tender gallantry—waited upon the table.

Blanche smelt at a bouquet and put her dainty nose into it; she tripped about, peeped behind curtains, examined books and prints, and noticed—among these bachelor curiosities—the plan of the Clavering estate hanging on the wall like a recipe for property. She asked after Captain Strong and almost forgot him in the pleasure of the adventure: the odd, delightful, droll little notion of being in bachelor chambers in a queer old City place! The whole inn had its own soundscape—doors thudding, distant kettles singing, feet and voices muffled by stairwells—but here there was perfume in the air, as if flowers had been used to translate the room into a more fashionable language.

Grady, meanwhile, having produced a pair of ample varnished boots, disappeared into his master’s room. Blanche had hardly leisure to remark how big the boots were and how unlike Mr. Strong’s.

“The women’s come,” said Grady, helping his master into them.

“Did you ask ’em if they would take a glass of anything?” asked Altamont.

Grady came out. “He says, will you take anything to drink?” the domestic asked.

Blanche, amused with the artless question, broke into a pretty little laugh and asked Mrs. Bonner, “Shall we take anything to drink?”

“Well, you may take it or lave it,” said Mr. Grady, who thought his offer slighted and did not like the contemptuous manners of the newcomers, and so left them.

“Will we take anything to drink?” Blanche asked again, and laughed again—her laughter, in that confined space, sounding too bright for the dingy staircase beyond, like a silver spoon clinking in a cracked mug.

“Grady!” bawled a voice from within—a voice that made Mrs. Bonner start.

Grady did not answer. His song was heard far off from the kitchen, his upper room, where he was singing at his work.

“Grady, my coat!” roared the voice again.

“Why, that is not Mr. Strong’s voice,” said the Sylphide, still half laughing. “Grady, my coat! Bonner, who is Grady, my coat? We ought to go away.”

Bonner still looked puzzled at the sound—puzzled, and then frightened, as if a forgotten word from a dead dialect had suddenly been spoken aloud.

The bedroom door opened, and the individual who had called for his coat appeared without it. He nodded to the women and walked across the room.

“I beg your pardon, ladies. Grady, bring my coat down, sir! Well, my dears, it’s a fine day, and we’ll have a jolly lark at—”

He said no more, for here Mrs. Bonner—staring at him with scared eyes—suddenly shrieked, “Amory! Amory!” and fell back screaming and fainting in her chair.

The man, so apostrophised, looked at her an instant; then rushed up to Blanche, seized her, and kissed her.

“Yes, Betsy,” he said, “by G—it is me. Mary Bonner knew me. What a fine gal we’ve grown! But it’s a secret, mind. I’m dead, though I’m your father. Your poor mother don’t know it. What a pretty gal we’ve grown! Kiss me—kiss me close, my Betsy? D—— it, I love you: I’m your old father.”

Betsy—or Blanche—looked quite bewildered and began to scream too: once, twice, thrice. It was those piercing shrieks Captain Costigan had heard from the court below.

At the sound, the perplexed parent clasped his hands—his wristbands open, and on one brawny arm letters tattooed in blue—and rushed to his room. He came back with an eau-de-Cologne bottle from a grand silver dressing-case, and began liberally sprinkling Bonner and Blanche, as though a flood of scent could wash away shock, as though fragrance were an absolution. The room, already perfumed by flowers, became a cloud of sharp, spirituous sweetness; the air stung the nose and caught at the throat—too much elegance at once, like too much seasoning flung into a stew to disguise doubtful meat.

The screams brought in the other occupants: Grady from the kitchen, and Strong from his apartment in the upper story. Strong, one glance at the women’s faces, saw what had occurred.

“Grady,” he said, “go and wait in the court; and if anybody comes—you understand me.”

“Is it the play-actress and her mother?” said Grady.

“Yes—confound you—say there’s nobody in chambers, and the party’s off for to-day.”

“Shall I say that, sir? and after I bought them bokays?” asked Grady.

“Yes,” said Amory with a stamp of his foot.

And Strong, going to the door too, reached it just in time to prevent Captain Costigan’s entrance, as that officer mounted the stair with his dignity inflamed and his curiosity galloping.

The ladies from the theatre did not have their treat to Greenwich. Nor did Blanche pay her visit to Fanny Bolton that day. And Cos, taking occasion majestically to inquire of Grady what the mischief was and who was crying, received for answer that ’twas a woman—another of them—and that they were, in Grady’s opinion, the cause of ’most all the mischief in the world.
