The day had hardly begun its full blaze, yet the heat already lay on Romola’s shoulders like a woollen mantle. Coming from San Marco, she kept close to the wall where the stones still held a night-coolness; even so, the sun pressed into every open street, and the air tasted of dust warmed into a faint bitterness. At Santa Croce she turned in among the intersecting lanes, where the light fell in long blades between high houses.

Borgo la Croce was strangely still. No hammer rang, no cart rattled, no vendors cried their wares. Only her own steps answered her, a measured tapping on the pavement that made the quiet seem deeper. As she neared a bend she saw, a little way before her, a child—no more than three years old—running with the uncertain, rolling haste of a young creature loosed from watchful hands. He wore only a white shirt that reached to his knees; his bare legs were already filmed with street-dust. He stopped, looked about, and for an instant she saw only his back: square and sturdy, with a cloud of reddish-brown curls like sun-touched chestnut husk.

Then he turned. Dark eyes brimmed and spilled. His lower lip thrust forward, trembling; fat brown fists clutched his shirt as if it were the only thing that could keep him from falling out of the world. Romola’s tall black figure cast a shadow on him, and the shadow, more than her, broke his last thread of courage: he drew breath and let out a sobbing cry that seemed too large for so small a throat.

Romola uncovered her head at once, as if to show she brought no menace, and went down on the hot stones beside him. She gathered him with both arms and laid her cheek to his, speaking in the soft, coaxing cadence that rises in women before they have time to think. The sobs swelled louder, then—like a sudden shower that ends in a moment—ceased. His face unknotted, and he stared at her open-mouthed, as if he had never seen a human face so near.

“You have lost yourself, little one,” she murmured, kissing his damp cheek. “Never mind. We will find the house again. Perhaps mamma will meet us.”

It seemed plain he had slipped away when a mother’s eyes were elsewhere; and Romola expected the pursuing footsteps, the frantic call, any moment. None came.

“Oh, what a heavy, heavy boy!” she said, trying to lift him. He was dense as a small sack of grain. “I cannot carry you. Come then—you must toddle by my side.”

He did not speak. One fist still clung to his shirt with stubborn tenacity; the other placed itself in her hand with a grave consent, as if their bargain were settled.

“You have a mamma?” Romola asked, looking down at him with a yearning she did not name.

He gave no answer. This was no chirping little bird of a child, but a square-shouldered tiny man, serious in his distress and serious in his silence.

Yet he knew his way. At the turning by San Ambrogio he dragged her towards the front with a determined pull, head thrust forward like a small ox in harness.

“Ah—that is the way home?” Romola said, letting the corner of her mouth soften into a smile.

He only tugged harder: go faster.

Another turning earned the same decisive judgement, and then the street opened into a quieter reach where garden ground lay beyond the walls and the air smelled faintly of damp earth and leaves. At the end stood a house, and before it the boy halted, drawing her toward a flight of worn stone steps. He would not let go her hand, and she would not leave him until she was sure she put him into familiar arms.

They mounted into dimness—the sudden withdrawal from sun into shadow made the air feel cooler, almost wet on the skin. At the landing, light spilled from an open doorway. They passed a small lobby and came to another open door. Romola paused on the threshold. No one had heard her.

At the far end of the room, opposite the light, sat Tessa on a low chair with one hand resting on the cradle’s edge. Her head hung a little to one side; she slept fast, the sleep of someone worn with rocking and watchfulness. Near the window, her back to the door, Monna Lisa worked at a bowl—pulling apart leaves, stripping away limp outer edges, rinsing the crisp hearts, as if preparing salad were a prayer said with fingers. Her deafness made the room doubly still: the soft scrape of leaf against earthenware, the faint drip of water, were all the sound.

There was barely time for Romola’s eyes to take in the scene before the boy snatched his hand free and ran to Tessa’s side. He did not shake her, only leaned his head against her arm and turned those solemn eyes back toward Romola, as if to judge what would come next.

The pressure woke Tessa. She blinked, bewildered; then her gaze found the figure at the doorway. In an instant she started up, blushed so deeply it seemed to climb into her hairline, and began to tremble—neither advancing nor speaking, held between fear and gratitude.

“Ah—we have seen each other before,” Romola said, smiling as she came forward. “I am glad it was your little boy. He was crying in the street; I suppose he had run away. We walked together a little way, and then he knew where he was and brought me here. But you had not missed him? That is well—else you would have been frightened.”

At the thought of how near loss had been, Tessa’s colour fled. With a half sob she seized Lillo’s arm and hurried to Monna Lisa, speaking loudly into the old woman’s ear.

“Oh, Lisa, you are wicked! Why will you stand with your back to the door? Lillo ran away ever so far into the street.”

“Holy Mother!” Monna Lisa said, in her meek, thick tone, letting the spoon drop from her hands. “Where were you, then? I thought you were there, and had your eye on him.”

“But you know I go to sleep when I am rocking,” Tessa answered with pettish helplessness—the plaint of one who is blamed for being what she is.

“Well, well,” Monna Lisa said, recovering the spoon, “we must keep the outer door shut, or else tie him up; for he’ll be as cunning as Satan before long, and that’s the holy truth. But how came he back, then?”

The question brought Tessa back to Romola, and she turned, blushing and timid again. Monna Lisa’s eyes followed, and the old woman made a low reverence.

“Doubtless the most noble lady brought him back,” she said; and then, stepping nearer, she added with domestic frankness, “It’s my shame for him to have been found with only his shirt on; but he kicked and wouldn’t have his other clothes this morning, and the mother—poor thing—will never hear of his being beaten. But what’s an old woman to do without a stick when the lad’s legs get so strong? Let your nobleness look at his legs.”

Lillo, aware that his legs were under discussion, pulled his shirt up higher and looked down at their olive roundness with a solemn curiosity. Romola laughed—the sound broke the tightness in Tessa’s throat—and she bent to give him a caressing shake and a kiss.

That easy laughter loosened something in Tessa too. When Naldo had been told of the Carnival adventure, and she had asked who the heavenly lady was who came when she was wanted and vanished again—whether it could be the Madonna herself—he had answered, “Not exactly, my Tessa; only one of the saints,” and would say no more. So Romola had remained, in Tessa’s mingled scraps of memory and church-pictures, like a figure from painted walls. Now the figure laughed at a child’s legs, and touched him as women touch children, and the awe softened into trust. Tessa’s fears tended more toward ugly things and harsh voices than toward anything good.

“Ninna’s in the cradle,” she said. “She’s pretty too.”

Romola went to the cradle. The infant lay with a damp curl on her forehead, her little mouth puckering in sleep; and as Romola leaned in, the baby stirred and opened blue eyes, startled into life by a new shadow.

“Ah—she is waking: she has opened her blue eyes,” Romola said. “You must take her up, and I will sit down in this chair—may I?—and nurse Lillo. Come, Lillo!”

She sat in Tito’s chair. The fact struck her only as she lowered herself: the familiar weight of possession about it, the sense of a man’s usual place. She held out her arms. Lillo hesitated, then pointed at her with small fingers, half puzzled, half indignant.

“That’s Babbo’s chair,” he said, as if the chair itself were a rule.

“But Babbo is not here, and I shall go soon. Come—let me nurse you as he does,” Romola answered.

As she lifted him she wondered, sharply and for the first time, what sort of Babbo he might be—what sort of man had a wife dressed like a contadina and yet carrying about her a daintiness that suggested ease, a little softness of body and of hand that belonged to plenty and to idleness rather than to field-work. Lillo, discovering the lap to be as comfortable as any throne, settled into it and began to explore Romola’s dress and fingers, searching for ornaments beyond her rosary with the serious greed of a child who knows there are hidden treasures in the world.

Tessa, occupied with coaxing Ninna past her waking peevishness, now sat down again on her low chair near Romola’s knee, arranging the baby’s tiny person as if setting out a dish to be admired—tilting the little head, smoothing the shift, jealous that the strange lady’s eyes, like Naldo’s, seemed to return always to the boy.

“Lillo was going to be angry with me, because I sat in Babbo’s chair,” Romola said, bending to kiss Ninna’s little foot, warm and milk-scented. “Will he come soon and want it?”

“Ah, no!” Tessa said. “You can sit in it a long while. I shall be sorry when you go. When you first came to take care of me at the Carnival, I thought it was wonderful; you came and went away again so fast. And Naldo said, perhaps you were a saint, and that made me tremble a little—though the saints are very good, I know; and you were good to me, and now you have taken care of Lillo. Perhaps you will always come and take care of me. That was how Naldo did, a long while ago; he came and took care of me when I was frightened, one San Giovanni. I couldn’t think where he came from—he was so beautiful and good. And so are you.”

“Naldo is your husband,” Romola said. It was not a question; it was the quiet fitting together of what she saw. “His eyes are like Lillo’s.”

“Ah! you know him!” Tessa paused, wonder widening her. “Perhaps you know Nofri and Peretola, and our house on the hill, and everything. Yes—like Lillo’s; but not his hair. His hair is dark and long—ah! if you know it—ecco!”

She slipped her hand to a thin red silk cord at her neck and drew from her bosom the tiny old parchment breve, the horn of red coral, and, with them, a long dark curl tied carefully at one end. She held the curl toward Romola, keeping it out of Ninna’s grasping hand.

“It is a fresh one. I cut it lately. See how bright it is!” She laid it against the white of Romola’s fingers so that its darkness seemed to drink the light. “They get dim, and then he lets me cut another when his hair is grown; and I put it with the breve, because sometimes he is away a long while, and then I think it helps to take care of me.”

A shiver went through Romola as if the curl were not hair but a cord pulled across her nerves. At Tessa’s first words of a husband who came she knew not whence, a possibility had risen—one of those faint, terrible shapes that leap up instantly in a mind that has been searching. Now, seeing the curl laid on her hand, it seemed for an instant a mocking likeness of the lock she herself had cut to wind with one of her own, years ago. But she held herself still. She wanted truth, and she wanted it without striking pain into this simple, trusting creature—this woman with the child’s mind lodged inside her.

“It is a beautiful curl,” Romola said, mastering the impulse to draw her hand away. “Lillo’s curls will be like it, perhaps, for his cheek too is dark. And you never know where your husband goes to when he leaves you?”

“No,” Tessa said, tucking away her treasures beyond the children’s reach. “But I know Messer San Michele takes care of him, for he gave him a beautiful coat, all made of little chains; and if he puts that on, nobody can kill him. And perhaps, if—”

She hesitated; the old dreamlike awe began to rise again, now that talk had led her into deeper water. “If you were a saint, you would take care of him too, because you have taken care of me and Lillo.”

Romola’s certainty came like a blow that made the blood rush. A flush sprang up, quick and hot; but she had bent her cheek against Lillo’s head so that the child’s curls hid the tremor of her mouth. The feeling inside that flush was strange—something like exultation at the thought that the wife’s burden might be lifted from her shoulders at last; that this ignorant little being might be Tito’s lawful wife. Exultation—shameful word for a proud woman; yet it rose, because it promised a way out of a duty that had become an insoluble knot.

And yet Tessa’s appeal, so artless, so full of the need to be guarded, pierced her. Romola lifted her head and spoke with a steadiness that sounded like authority even to herself.

“I will always take care of you, if I see you need me. But that beautiful coat—your husband did not wear it when you were first married? Perhaps he used not to be so long away from you then?”

“Ah, yes! he was. Much—much longer. So long, I thought he would never come back. I used to cry. Oh me! I was beaten then—a long, long while ago at Peretola, where we had the goats and mules.”

“And how long had you been married before your husband had that chain-coat?” Romola said.

Her heart began to beat so fast it seemed each pulse must betray her. She watched Tessa’s hands as if they were counting out her fate.

Tessa looked up, meditative, and began to count on her fingers.

“The chestnuts were ripe when we were married,” she said, marking thumb and fingers as she spoke; “and then again they were ripe at Peretola before he came back, and then again, after that, on the hill. And soon the soldiers came, and we heard the trumpets, and then Naldo had the coat.”

Romola drew in her breath. “You had been married more than two years. In which church were you married?”

Tessa gave a small start. The directness of Romola’s question made her feel, dimly, that she was being led where Naldo had once forbidden her to go. She looked up with hesitating doubt.

“Naldo said I was never to tell about that,” she whispered. “Do you think he would not be angry if I told you?”

“It is right that you should tell me,” Romola said, mild and firm. “Tell me everything.”

If Naldo’s command had been fresh, her obedience might have held; but the years had blurred it into something like a shadow on the mind. And the very strangeness of speaking what she had never spoken before made her voice sink as if she were confessing.

“It was not in a church—it was at the Natività, when there was a fair, and all the people went overnight to see the Madonna in the Nunziata, and my mother was ill and couldn’t go, and I took the bunch of cocoons for her; and then he came to me in the church and I heard him say, ‘Tessa!’ I knew him because he had taken care of me at the San Giovanni; and then we went into the piazza where the fair was, and I had some berlingozzi, for I was hungry and he was very good to me; and at the end of the piazza there was a holy father, and an altar like what they have at the processions outside the churches. So he married us, and then Naldo took me back into the church and left me; and I went home, and my mother died, and Nofri began to beat me more, and Naldo never came back. And I used to cry, and once at the Carnival I saw him and followed him, and he was angry, and said he would come some time, I must wait. So I went and waited; but, oh! it was a long while before he came; but he would have come if he could, for he was good; and then he took me away, because I cried and said I could not bear to stay with Nofri. And, oh! I was so glad, and since then I have been always happy, for I don’t mind about the goats and mules, because I have Lillo and Ninna now; and Naldo is never angry, only I think he doesn’t love Ninna so well as Lillo, and she is pretty.”

As she spoke, the room seemed to fill with more than words: the remembered sounds of fair and church—the muffled chant, the shuffle of feet, the sudden flare of trumpets in her story—rose and fell like distant echoes, until the present quiet returned with the soft rustle of salad leaves and the baby’s small, fretful sigh.

Tessa forgot her earlier caution and fell to showering Ninna with kisses, devouring her as if love were food and the child were sweet bread. Romola sat in silence, eyes turned inward and seeing nothing of the cradle, nothing of the salad bowl, nothing even of the child on her lap. The tale had not brought the simple release she had felt for an instant in her pulse. The marriage had been no lawful knot, no clear external law that could untie hers. The difficulty remained—only changed in shape.

Lillo felt the change at once. He had given no caress back to her, yet he demanded them as his due; and when her arm slackened he tugged her head toward him with both brown arms.

“Play with me again!” he said.

Romola started as if wakened. She clasped him anew, pressing him to her, and looked from him to Tessa. Tessa had paused in her kissing and was again gazing up at Romola with that placid, worshipping delight, as if Romola’s face itself were a protection.

Something in Romola’s expression altered—slowly, like a lamp turned and trimmed so that it gives a warmer light. Her mouth softened; her eyes lost their hard inward stare and took in the room, the two children, the poor domestic order. Presently she drew her scissors from her scarsella. The three pair of wide eyes—Lillo’s, Tessa’s, even Ninna’s newly opened blue glance—followed the gleam of metal with the intentness of kittens.

Romola lifted one of her long wavy locks and cut it off cleanly. The severed hair fell heavy into her hand like a relinquished tie.

“I must go away from you now,” she said, “but I will leave this lock of hair, that it may remind you of me; because if you are ever in trouble you can think that perhaps God will send me to take care of you again. I cannot tell you where to find me; but if I ever know that you want me, I will come to you. Addio.”

She set Lillo down quickly, as if staying another moment might undo her steadiness, and held out her hand. Tessa kissed it, awe and sorrow mingled in her breath. Romola’s mind pressed on her like the heat outside; she needed solitude as one needs water. But she did not forget the least among them: she turned aside to lay her hand on Monna Lisa’s shoulder and make a friendly farewell sign.

Before the old woman had finished her deep reverence, Romola had gone.

Tessa and Monna Lisa moved toward each other by the same impulse. The two children clung to their mother’s skirts, as if they, too, felt that something invisible had passed through the room.

“Do you think she was a saint?” Tessa asked in Lisa’s ear, showing the lock.

Monna Lisa rejected the notion with a backward flick of her fingers; then, stroking the rippled gold as if weighing it, she said—

“She’s a great and noble lady. I saw such in my youth.”

Romola went home and sat alone through the sultry hours, while the city’s sounds thickened and thinned outside—distant bells, far-off calls, the flutter of pigeons like a soft brushing in the air. In that slow, breathing soundscape, each noise seemed to measure her solitude. By evening she held the heavy certainty that her lot was unchanged.

She was flung back on the conflict between an outward law—sacred, far-reaching, binding the household to the city as the city was bound to God—and the inward facts that had grown more peremptory day by day. Savonarola’s teaching had entered her like a stern music: return to the place appointed; do not cast off bonds because they gall; do not make private ease the judge over public duty. The holiness attached to close relations, and most of all to the closest, was the outward shape of a deeper human good; and to abandon inherited or chosen ties lightly, because they had ceased to please, was to tear up virtue by the roots. What else had Tito’s crime toward Baldassarre been—if not abandonment driven to its last horror of falsity and ingratitude?

And yet—what was she to do with a bond that had become nothing but the stifling predominance of a nature she despised? She had striven, long, to make union possible; each attempt only sharpened the impossibility. The marriage had turned into degrading servitude, and her soul recoiled as from a tainted dish set on her table day after day—food that one is commanded to eat, and that sickens one even while one strives to be grateful.

Law was sacred. Yes. But rebellion might be sacred too.

The thought flashed on her with a dreadful clarity: her problem was the same in kind as Savonarola’s—where obedience ends and where rebellion begins; where the soul must act on its own warrant without appeal to any tribunal, even against a law not unarmed with divine lightnings—lightnings that may fall if the warrant is false.

Before the sun went down she had taken her resolve. She would ask counsel neither of her godfather nor of Savonarola until she had made one determined effort to speak freely with Tito and obtain his consent that she should live apart from him. She did not desire to leave him clandestinely again; she did not desire to forsake Florence. She would tell him that if ever he felt a real need of her, she would come back. Could more faithfulness be demanded?

A shudder ran through her at the image of his refusal clothed in a sneer—at his suggestion that the only scandal-proof escape was a convent. He knew how her mind revolted from that narrow rule, not only from personal repugnance, but because her father’s dearest memories forbade it: the cloister was linked with his deepest griefs and bitterest dislike, like a word in a hated tongue that cannot be sweetened by pious use.

Tito had announced he would come home this evening. She would wait and speak at once; during the day his ear was hard to catch. If he so much as scented personal words, he slipped away with an ease that looked unpremeditated, leaving her speaking into emptiness.

She sent for Maso to say she would wait for his master. The old man looked at her and lingered—hesitation and wondering anxiety written in the lines of his face—but when she asked no question he turned away slowly. Why ask? Perhaps Maso only knew, or guessed, what she already knew.

Tito came late. Romola had been pacing up and down the long room that had once been the library. The windows stood open; night had not cooled the air, only thickened it. She wore a loose white linen robe instead of her usual black—something washed and plain, that let the skin breathe. The slight coolness after her long hours of heat and motionless thought made her more wakeful, not less; and when at last she took up the lamp and went to open the door herself, her eyes must have startled him—bright with sleeplessness and with pain turned into resolve, so unlike her usual quiescent self-restraint before him.

But it seemed this was what he expected.

“Ah! it is you, Romola. Maso is gone to bed,” he said gravely, quietly, stepping in and closing the door for her with a deliberate hand. Then he turned and looked at her more fully than he was wont, as if measuring the change in her.

“You have heard it all, I see.”
