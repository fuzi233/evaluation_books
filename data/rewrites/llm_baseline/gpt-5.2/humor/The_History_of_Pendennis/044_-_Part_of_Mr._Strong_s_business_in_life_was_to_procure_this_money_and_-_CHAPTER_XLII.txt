Some time ago—when Arthur Pendennis had been beaten by Oxbridge, and had retired home to nurse his pride with ink—he produced, among other smaller calamities, the larger portion of a novel. Youth, being unable to pickle itself properly, will often try preserving in German vinegar; and Pen, who had lately been learning that language, had steeped his hero in a marinade of imported misery. The result was a Walter Lorraine saturated with Byronic black pepper, Wertherian vinegar, and a Mephistophelean dash of bitter herbs, all pounded together with the hot impatience of nineteen, and served up smoking, in a dish too large for the appetite.

If the secret history of books were written—if you could see, alongside each paragraph, the author’s private weather, and what he had eaten, and what he had not eaten, and whom he had met at tea, and whom he had not—many a thin volume would suddenly acquire the richness of a stew that has simmered all night. As it was, Pen, now older and with his emotions no longer kept at a perpetual rolling boil, looked back over his pages with the odd expression of a cook who has discovered the recipe-book of his apprenticeship: here he had salted too fiercely; there he had forgotten the salt altogether; and there—yes, there was a passage he had once thought original, which now tasted undeniably of somebody else’s kitchen.

He could remember, too, the very hour and place where certain sentences had been thrown in, as one remembers where one first tasted a sharp cheese, or drank a too-sweet punch, and vowed never again. The feeling returned like a ghostly flavour on the palate—faint, undeniable, and slightly ridiculous. And what were those stains? Not ink alone. There are blots in young men’s manuscripts as there are rings on a table where hot cups have stood: signs that something once scalding was there. The spring had run, and dried.

One morning he described all this to Warrington—who sat entrenched behind a pipe and a book, making a private fog of Lamb Court, as content as any city-dweller can be who pays for air and receives coal-smoke. Pen, excited in his way, talked with hands as well as tongue; and as he brought the manuscript down on the table, the tea-things rattled, and the blue milk shivered in the jug like a small frightened lake.

He had fetched the packet the night before from a long-neglected chest crammed with relics: old shooting-jackets, old Oxbridge scribbling-books, an obsolete surplice, a cap and gown battered as if by the very logic of his examiners—mementoes that smelt of camphor, stale cloth, and the dead, sweet dust of bygone ambition. He had read the beginning in bed until he fell asleep, partly because the commencement was dull, and partly because he had returned late from a London party and the room itself seemed to yawn.

“By Jove!” he cried now, thumping the papers again, “when I think these lines were written only a few years ago, I’m ashamed of my memory. I wrote this when I believed myself eternally in love with that little coquette, Miss Amory. I used to carry down verses to her, and put them into the hollow of a tree, and dedicate them ‘Amori.’”

“That was a sweet little play upon words,” Warrington said, letting out a puff as if he were offering incense to the god of Scholarship. “Amory—Amori. It showed proof of learning. Let us hear a bit of the rubbish.”

And with the fire-tongs—just employed to coax a coal into his pipe—he reached across, seized the manuscript as if it were a joint to be carved, and began to read aloud from the title-page with an air of mock ceremony: Leaves from the Life-book of Walter Lorraine.

“‘False as thou art beautiful! heartless as thou art fair! mockery of Passion!’ Walter cried, addressing Leonora; ‘what evil spirit hath sent thee to torture me so? O Leonora—’”

“Cut that part!” Pen sprang forward, crimson. “Don’t read it out, at least. That’s about my other flame—my first—Lady Mirabel that is now. I saw her last night at Lady Whiston’s. She asked me to a party at her house and said that, as old friends, we ought to meet oftener. She’s been seeing me any time these two years in town and never thought of inviting me before; but seeing Wenham talking to me, and Monsieur Dubois—the French literary man—with a dozen orders on, like a walking pastry-shop of decorations, she condescended to remember I existed. The Claverings are to be there the same evening. Won’t it be exciting to meet one’s two flames at the same table?”

“Two flames!” Warrington said. “Two heaps of burnt-out cinders.”

“Both beauties are in the book, then?”

“Both, or something like them,” Pen admitted. “Leonora, who marries the Duke, is the Fotheringay. I drew the Duke from Magnus Charters, with whom I was at Oxford; it’s a little like him. Miss Amory is Neaera. By gad, that first woman! I thought of her walking home from Lady Whiston’s in the moonlight, and the whole early scenes came back as if they’d happened yesterday. When I got in, I pulled out the story I wrote about her and the other three years ago; and do you know, outrageous as it is, it has some good stuff in it. If Bungay won’t publish it, I think Bacon will.”

“That’s the way of poets,” Warrington said. “They fall in love; they are jilted, or jilting; they suffer; they proclaim no other mortal ever suffered so exquisitely; and when they have experienced feelings enough, they bottle them and carry the bottle to market. All poets are humbugs. All literary men are humbugs. Directly a man begins to sell his feelings for money he’s a humbug. If a poet gets a pain in his side from too good a dinner, he bellows Ai! Ai! louder than Prometheus.”

“I suppose a poet has a greater sensibility than another man,” Pen said, with spirit—rather as if defending the cook’s right to taste. “That’s what makes him a poet. He sees and feels more keenly: that’s why he speaks of what he sees and feels. You speak eagerly enough in your leading articles when you spy a false argument, or detect a quack in the House. Paley, who doesn’t care for anything else on earth, will talk for an hour about a question of law. Give another man the privilege you take yourself; let him use his faculty freely; let him be what nature has made him. Why should not a man sell his sentimental thoughts as well as you your political ideas, or Paley his legal knowledge? Each alike is experience and practice. It isn’t money that makes you perceive a fallacy or Paley argue a point; it’s aptitude. A poet sets down his thoughts and experiences on paper as a painter sets down a landscape, to the best of his ability, according to his gift. If ever I think I’ve the stuff to write an epic, by Jove I’ll try. If I only feel I’m good enough to crack a joke or tell a story, I’ll do that.”

“Not a bad speech, young one,” Warrington said, “but it does not prevent all poets from being humbugs.”

“What—Homer, Aeschylus, Shakspeare and all?”

“Their names are not to be breathed in the same sense with you pigmies,” Warrington answered. “There are men and men, sir.”

“Well, Shakspeare wrote for money, just as you and I do,” Pen said.

Warrington, who loved him, and yet could not bear his impudence without punishing it by silence, resumed his pipe and his reading.

There was no doubt that Walter Lorraine contained a great deal of Arthur Pendennis in disguise—like a ragout that pretends to be hare while every guest knows it is chiefly yesterday’s mutton. The tale would never have been cooked at all but for Pen’s private griefs, passions, and follies. As we already know those ingredients, there is no need to ladle out extracts; and besides, the narrator has a tenderness for the reader’s digestion.

Pen had kept the manuscript in his box for nearly half the length of time which a certain Horatian maxim recommends for the ripening of works of art—a maxim, by the way, which is easier to quote than to practise, and easier still to doubt. He had not laid it by because he believed it would improve in a crypt, like a great wine. He laid it by because he did not know where else to put it, and had no urgent desire to taste it again. A man who says he will hide his composition for ten years should be very sure it is a cheese meant for keeping; otherwise, when he opens it, he may find it has not grown richer, only blander.

Pen had never imagined, even in his most feverish days, that his novel was a masterpiece, or that he stood shoulder to shoulder with the giants he admired. Reading it now, he was sufficiently alive to its faults and modest enough about its merits. It was not very good; but it was, he thought, as good as most of the things that rolled through the circulating libraries like so many carriages in a fashionable street—some handsome, some rickety, all going somewhere because people paid to ride.

He had looked critically into more than one popular book of the day and thought, with the cheerful insolence of youth, that he could handle English as well as those ladies or gentlemen who were being praised for it; and as he ran over his own pages he was pleased to find here and there passages with a certain vigour—flashes, if not of genius, at least of genuine heat.

This, too, was Warrington’s verdict. After half an hour’s perusal and the consumption of two pipes, that severe critic laid the manuscript down with a yawn that seemed to open the very cellar of boredom.

“I can’t read any more of that balderdash now,” he said; “but there’s some good stuff in it, Pen, my boy. There’s a greenness and freshness in it I like. The bloom disappears off the face of poetry after you begin to shave. You can’t get up that artless rosy tint later. Your cheeks are pale now, faded by evening parties; you’re obliged to take curling-irons, and macassar, and the Lord knows what to your whiskers; they curl ambrosially, you’re very grand and genteel; but, ah, Pen, the spring-time was the best.”

“What the deuce have my whiskers to do with the subject?” Pen cried—nettled, for it was true: those whiskers were treated less like hair than like a delicate sauce, coaxed and perfumed and made to stand up as if presenting arms. “Do you think we can do anything with Walter Lorraine? Shall we take him to the publishers, or make an auto-da-fé of him?”

“I don’t see what good there is in incremation,” Warrington said, “though I’ve half a mind to put him into the fire to punish your atrocious humbug. Shall I burn him indeed? You have too great a value for him to let a hair of his head be harmed.”

“Have I? Here goes,” Pen said; and Walter Lorraine went off the table and onto the coals.

But the fire, having done its honest duty of boiling the breakfast-kettle, had given up work for the day and gone out—exactly as Pen knew. Warrington, with a scornful smile, took up the manuscript with the tongs from the harmless grey cinders.

“Oh, Pen, what a humbug you are!” he said. “And worst of all, a clumsy humbug. I saw you look to see the fire was out before you sent Walter Lorraine behind the bars. No, we won’t burn him. We’ll carry him to the Egyptians and sell him. We’ll exchange him for money—yea, for silver and gold, and for beef, and for liquors, and for tobacco, and for raiment. This youth will fetch a price in the market: he’s a comely lad though not over strong. We’ll fatten him up, give him the bath, curl his hair, and sell him for a hundred piasters to Bacon or Bungay. The rubbish is saleable enough. My advice: next time you go home for a holiday, take him in your carpet-bag, give him a more modern air, prune away—sparingly—some of the green passages, add a little comedy, cheerfulness, satire, and that sort of seasoning, and then we’ll take him to market and sell him. It’s no wonder of wonders, but it’ll do very well.”

“Do you think so, Warrington?” Pen asked, delighted; for praise from that cynical mouth was like a rare spice, expensive and therefore treasured.

“You silly young fool! I think it’s uncommonly clever,” Warrington said, in a kind voice. “So do you.”

And with the manuscript in his hand he playfully struck Pen on the cheek. That cheek turned red as ever it had done in the earliest days of blushing; Pen seized his friend’s hand and said, “Thank you, Warrington,” with all his might; and then retired with his book to his room, and spent the greater part of the day on his bed rereading and altering—adding where he could, cutting where he must, whisking in a little gaiety, a little salt of satire—until Walter Lorraine stood pretty much in the shape in which, as the respected novel-reader knows, it subsequently appeared.

While Pen was thus at his cookery, Warrington—good-natured in his fashion, and crafty as a man who has lived by noticing the appetites of the public—set to work upon the two gentlemen who “read” for Messrs. Bacon and Bungay, and inspired them with a lively curiosity regarding Walter Lorraine and the peculiar merits of its author. The time was that in which The Fashionable (that well-known dish of the season) had set all mouths going; and Warrington did not fail to insist that Pen was fashion itself: received in the houses of the greatest people, dining as easily with a Marchioness as with a mutton-chop.

Percy Popjoy, kind-hearted and simple, was brought to bear upon Mrs. Bungay, and informed that his friend Pendennis was engaged upon a work of the most exciting nature—full of wit, genius, satire, pathos, and every conceivable good quality, as if the book were a Christmas pudding containing every coin and jewel ever lost in England. Bungay knew no more of novels than he did of Hebrew or Algebra; he neither read nor understood the wares he published and paid for; but he took opinions from his advisers and from Mrs. B., and, smelling commerce, asked Pendennis and Warrington to dinner again.

Bacon, hearing that Bungay was about to treat, grew anxious—as one rival cook sniffs another’s kitchen. Was anything settled between Mr. Pendennis and that odious house “over the way”? Mr. Hack, the confidential reader, was told to inquire; and the result of that diplomatist’s inquiries was that one morning Bacon himself toiled up the stairs of Lamb Court to the door on which the names of Mr. Warrington and Mr. Pendennis were painted.

For a gentleman of fashion as poor Pen was represented to be, the chambers were not suitable—unless one admits that fashion in London consists of living near the roof and paying for soot. The carpet, long ragged, had grown only more ragged in two years. A constant odour of tobacco perfumed the sitting-room—a thick, browned atmosphere, as if the place had been smoked like a ham. Bacon tumbled over the laundress’s buckets in the passage. Warrington’s shooting-jacket was tattered at the elbows as usual. The chair offered to Bacon broke down under him with a crack that sounded like the snapping of a dry biscuit.

Warrington burst out laughing, said Bacon had got the game chair, and bawled to Pen to fetch a sound one from his bedroom. Then, seeing the publisher looking round the dingy room with profound pity and wonder, Warrington asked whether he didn’t think the apartments elegant, and whether he would like, for Mrs. Bacon’s drawing-room, any of the articles of furniture.

Mr. Bacon knew Warrington’s reputation as a humourist. “I never can make that chap out,” he was heard to say; “or tell whether he’s in earnest or only chaffing.”

It is very possible Bacon would have set the two down as impostors altogether, had there not chanced to be upon the breakfast-table certain cards of invitation brought by the morning post for Pen—bright, crisp, and aristocratic as wafers. Looking down, Bacon saw that the Marchioness of Steyne would be at home to Mr. Arthur Pendennis on a given day, and that another lady of distinction proposed dancing on a certain future evening. The vulgar noise of the Court below—doors slamming, pails clinking, the iron grind of the city’s daily mechanism—seemed for a moment checked by the hush with which Bacon regarded those cards; for in London the soundscape itself changes when a man believes he hears money.

Warrington saw him eyeing the documents.

“Ah,” Warrington said, with an air of simplicity, “Pendennis is one of the most affable young men I ever knew, Mr. Bacon. Here is a young fellow that dines with all the men in London, and yet he’ll take his mutton-chop with you and me quite contentedly. There’s nothing like the affability of the old English gentleman.”

“Oh no, nothing,” said Mr. Bacon.

“And you wonder why he should go on living up three pair of stairs with me, don’t you now? Well, it’s a queer taste. But we are fond of each other; and as I can’t afford to live in a great house, he comes and stays in these rickety old chambers with me. He’s a man that can afford to live anywhere.”

“I fancy it don’t cost him much here,” thought Bacon—who did not arrive at his fortune by fancy alone.

And the object of these praises presently entered from his adjacent sleeping-room.

Then Mr. Bacon, having tested the chair and the carpet and the general solvency of the air, began to speak on the business of his visit. He had heard Mr. Pendennis had a manuscript novel. He professed anxiety to see the work, and no doubt they could come to terms. What was Pen’s price? Would he give Bacon the refusal? He would find their house liberal; their hearts warm; their cheques—possibly warmer.

Pen, delighted, put on an air of indifference, like a cook pretending he has no need to sell his pies. He said he was already in treaty with Bungay and could give no definite answer. This piqued Bacon into liberal—though vague—offers; and Pen began to fancy Eldorado was opening to him, with a banker at the gate and a horse and cab in the yard.

The sum Mr. Pendennis finally received for the first edition of Walter Lorraine shall not be mentioned, lest other young aspirants, smelling profit, forsake their honest callings and rush fatally into romance-writing as if it were a gold-field where nuggets lie in the street. Let no young people be misled. For one book that succeeds, remember the many that fail; and, if you will venture, do so at your peril. Those who have already written novels need no warning; the ink is spilt, the cake is baked. Let them take their wares to market; let them apply to Bacon and Bungay, and all the Row; and may they be happy in their ventures. The world is wide, tastes are various, and there is always a chance: by genius, by luck, by a friend’s diplomacy. One man goes over ice that bears him, and a score who follow flounder in.

By merit then, or good fortune, or Warrington’s skilful playing off of Bungay against Bacon (a trick any amateur novelist may attempt on any two publishers), Pen’s book was sold for a sum so considerable that Pen, dizzy, began to plan like a man who has unexpectedly found a turkey in his larder. He thought of opening an account at a banker’s, keeping a cab and horse, descending from his third-floor eyrie in Lamb Court to the first floor into newly furnished rooms, or migrating to the fashionable end of town where the streets are quieter only because the noise is more expensive.

Major Pendennis advised the latter move strongly. He opened his eyes with wonder when he heard of the luck that had befallen Pen—news which the young man, full of it as a kettle of steam, hastened to pour into his uncle’s ear. The Major was almost angry that Pen should have earned so much.

“Who the doose reads this kind of thing?” he thought, hearing the bargain. “I never read your novels and rubbish. Except Paul de Kock—who certainly makes me laugh—I don’t think I’ve looked into a book of the sort these thirty years. Gad! Pen’s a lucky fellow. I should think he might write one of these in a month now,—say a month,—that’s twelve in a year. Dammy, he may go on spinning this nonsense for the next four to five years and make a fortune. In the meantime I should wish him to live properly, take respectable apartments, and keep a brougham.”

Arthur, laughing, told Warrington what his uncle’s advice had been; but he had a more reasonable counsellor than the Major in Warrington—and in his own conscience, which, speaking plainly, said: Be grateful; don’t plunge into extravagance. Pay back Laura.

So he wrote to her—tenderly, modestly—thanking her, telling her his regard, and enclosing such an instalment of his debt as nearly wiped it off. The widow and Laura might well be affected. There was no flourish, no fashionable varnish: the letter had the honest warmth of bread sent to a hungry house. Old Dr. Portman, reading a passage in which Pen, with a grateful heart, humbly thanked Heaven for his present prosperity and for sending him such dear friends to support him in ill fortune—when the Doctor came to that, his voice faltered, and his eyes twinkled behind his spectacles. And when he had finished, and taken his glasses off his nose, and folded the paper and returned it to the widow, he held Mrs. Pendennis’s hand a minute; then, by a sudden impulse more eloquent than his quotations, he drew that lady towards him and fairly kissed her.

At which salute Helen burst out crying on the Doctor’s shoulder—for her heart was too full to answer any other way. And the Doctor, blushing a great deal after his feat, led her with a bow to the sofa, seated himself by her, and mumbled in a low voice some words of a Great Poet he loved, about making “the widow’s heart to sing for joy.”
