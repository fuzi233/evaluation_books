Thus it befell to Chaucer's knight in prison; thus it befell to Don Guzman; and thus it happened on this particular evening in Bideford.

After his release on parole, the Spaniard settled into quarters of considerable comfort, a stark contrast to the lean days of his captivity. He adopted the demeanor of a seasoned soldier of fortune, accepting the flow of events with a quiet resilience. It was barely a month into his stay when Old Salterne, the Mayor of Bideford, arrived for supper.

Though Don Guzman initially bristled at the peculiar English custom of inviting burghers to dine "above the salt" alongside nobility, his innate sense of gentility prevailed. He recognized that Richard Grenville, his host, acted with strict adherence to propriety. Shrugging off his initial discomfort, the Spaniard submitted to the arrangement, sharing the table with a merchant who managed ledgers and trained apprentices. To his surprise, finding the Mayor conversant and unpretentious, Don Guzman found himself engaging in dialogue with him. He discovered Salterne to be a prudent and courteous man, acutely aware of the Spaniard's superior rank yet confident in asserting his own wisdom as an elder statesman—a manner of speaking reminiscent of the merchant princes of Genoa and Venice.

At supper's end, Salterne invited the Don to return the following night. Turning to the Spaniard, he acknowledged the humility required for a nobleman of Spain to dine with a simple merchant, yet assured him that the fare was fit for any rank. Graciously accepting the invitation, Don Guzman secured an excellent supper and, if he chose to drink, fine wine.

Mr. Salterne, a shrewd merchant eager for adventure abroad—evidenced later by his pivotal role in settling Virginia—was equally eager to extract knowledge from guests who seemed familiar with distant lands. He attempted first to loosen the Spaniard's tongue with generous sack, then to interrogate him regarding the Spanish Main, Peru, the Moluccas, and the Indies. The first scheme failed; the Spaniard was as abstemious as a monk, drinking little but water. The second met with limited success; Don Guzman was as cunning as a fox, offering little but wind.

Into this verbal stalemate stepped the Rose of Torridge, radiant as ever. Hearing the topic of conversation, she artlessly added her own questions to her father's. Don Guzman, unable to ignore her inquiries, provided answers that amused both host and hostess without revealing vital commercial secrets.

Little Eros, though sometimes called the eldest of the gods, is by other authorities described as the child of idleness and full bellies. This calumny resonated deeply with Don Guzman. Idle by necessity as a captive, and well-fed by Sir Richard's generous table, he had begun to seek amusement in matters of the heart. Lady Grenville, the nearest option, was considered first; yet, as a man of honor and mindful of his host, he suppressed the desire for such a pursuit, dismissing it as a dangerous chase. He did not, however, dwell on its sinfulness, a moral blindness shared by many Southern Europeans of his time and, regrettably, by the English in the Stuart era. Having discarded Lady Grenville, he allowed the Rose of Torridge to occupy his thoughts. It was not with malicious intent, but rather to amuse himself and because he could not help it. There was an innocent freshness about Rose, fond of admiration yet possessing a charm that was new and captivating to him. "The train of the peacock, yet the heart of the dove," he mused. If he could persuade her to love only him, perhaps he might foolishly reciprocate. This thought struck him with a panic of prudence, resolving him to keep his distance, yet the days dragged on slowly. Lady Grenville, when at home, spoke of nothing but her husband; when she departed for Stow, leaving the Don alone in the great house, what choice did he have but to lounge in the butt-gardens, display his fine black cloak and feather, play rackets with the youth, bowls with the elders, and eventually receive an invitation to supper from Mr. Salterne?

There, he ruled the roast with royal authority, driven not only by rank but by the value of his travels. These were the dawn days of English commerce. Every merchant in Bideford and England burned with imagination regarding discoveries, companies, patents, and settlements. They rivaled the adventures of Sir Edward Osborne and his London Company of Turkey Merchants; they celebrated the privileges granted by Sultan Murad Khan; they honored the Levant voyages of Roger Bodenham, John Fox, Lawrence Aldersey, and John Rule. The crushing of Venetian power at Famagusta and the alliance between Elizabeth and the Grand Turk had opened vast doors for Mediterranean trade. Not a word fell from the Spaniard about the Mediterranean that did not take root in fertile soil. Furthermore, Master Edmund Hogan had successfully embassied to the Emperor of Morocco; John Hawkins and George Fenner had traded in Guinea for musk, civet, gold, and grain; and just two months prior, Captain Hare had sailed for Brazil with adventurers carrying English hardware and textiles, facilitated by John Whithall, who held an ingenio and slaves in Santos. Drake had proven the viability of plundering American coasts and establishing an East Indian trade; Frobisher and Davis were boring through northern ice seeking a passage to China; and Anthony Jenkinson had penetrated deep into Russia, Persia, Armenia, and the Arctic wastes. Everywhere, under Elizabeth's rule, English commerce spread its roots.

As Don Guzman conversed with his new friends, he perceived clearly that they belonged to a race that must be extinguished if Spain were to remain the mistress of the world. Seizing the New World in the Pope's name, crushing the Hollanders, and reducing the Venetians and Genoese to servitude was insufficient. These fierce islanders remained to assert, with cunning policy and scripture, or with sharp shot and cold steel, the principle of free seas and free trade. He saw it, and his countrymen saw it too; hence the Spanish Armada. But of that, another time. Don Guzman also knew, from hard experience, that these islanders, who spoke broad Devon through their noses, were not mere money-counters. Though they preferred profit to fighting, they could fight with a dogged ferocity surpassing the blue blood of Spain. They sent merchant ships armed to the teeth, manned by men trained from childhood to wield those arms, ordered to use them without mercy against any who dared stop their commerce.

One evening, after civilly hinting that Englishmen venturing where they had no right might be sent back, he was met with a volley of retorts: "We'll see that, sir." "Depends on who says 'No right.'" "You found might makes right when you claimed the Indian seas; we may find right makes might when we try." "Try them, then, gentlemen, if it pleases your worships; and find the sacred flag of Spain as invincible as ever was the Roman eagle." "We have, sir. Did you ever hear of Francis Drake?" "Or of George Fenner and the Portugals at the Azores, one against seven?" "Or of John Hawkins, at St. Juan d'Ulloa?"

"You are insolent burghers," Don Guzman declared, rising to depart.

"Sir," interjected Old Salterne, "as you say, we are burghers and plain men, and some of us have forgotten ourselves, perhaps. We beg your forgiveness for our want of manners, attributing it to the strength of my wine. We never meant to be insolent, especially to a noble gentleman and a foreigner."

But the Don refused to be pacified. He walked out, cursing himself as an ass and a blinkard for demeaning himself to such company, forgetting that he had provoked the incident.

The next day, prompted by Mammon, Salterne approached him, begging pardon again and promising that no such rude individuals would be invited if he would deign to visit once more. Appeased, the Don returned the very next evening, sneering at himself throughout the journey.

"Fool that I am!" he muttered. "That girl has bewitched me. I must go and eat my share of dirt for her sake."

Arriving, he cunningly hinted to Old Salterne that he had developed a fondness for the host and felt bound by his courtesy. He suggested he might reveal certain things he would not share with everyone, noting that Spaniards were not jealous of single traders but of general attempts to deprive them of their hard-earned wealth. He implied that opportunities for individual enrichment still existed.

Old Salterne, shrewd though he was, had a weakness, and the Spaniard had touched it. Delighted by the prospect of learning the mysteries of the Spanish monopoly, the merchant often encouraged Rose to draw out the Don. Blind as money made men, he feared nothing lest she be drawn in herself. First, he deemed it impossible she would consider marrying a Popish Spaniard; second, he believed it impossible the Spaniard would marry a burgher's daughter. He trusted that religious difference and family pride would keep them as separate as beings of different species. As for love without marriage, the thought was rendered absurd: on Rose's part by her virtue, on which the old man would stake every farthing; and on the Don's part by a certain human fondness for the continuity of life, for which Mr. Salterne gave him credit. Thus, for weeks and months, the merchant's house became the Don's favorite haunt. He saw the Rose of Torridge daily, and she heard him.

For her part, the poor child had never seen such a man. He possessed the high-bred grace of Frank yet was cast in a manlier mold. He held just enough of his nation's proud self-assertion to make a woman bow before him, yet possessed the tact to prevent it from degenerating into the boastfulness often attributed to Spaniards. He had marvels to tell of flood and field, surpassing even Amyas in number and grace. He was present, whereas the Leighs and her old lovers were not. What choice did she have but to amuse herself with the only person available?

Soon, more ladies in the country found the same attraction. The north was bare of young gallants due to the Netherland and Irish wars. The Spaniard became welcome at every house for miles around, receiving unwonted attention from fair young dames. His head might have turned, and Rose Salterne might have escaped, had not Sir Richard delicately signaled that, despite the free manners of English ladies, brothers were as jealous and ladies' honors as inexpugnable as in the land of demureness and duennas. Don Guzman took the hint well, maintaining good terms with country gentlemen and their daughters. The cunning soldier of fortune found it advantageous to be intimate with all the ladies he could, preventing Old Salterne from suspecting any peculiar predilection for Mistress Rose.

Nevertheless, Mr. Salterne's parlor remained his most common haunt. While he discoursed for hours about "Antres vast and deserts idle" and cannibals, satisfying Rose's fancy, he seasoned his tales with scraps of mercantile information, keeping the old merchant expectant and eager to invite him again.

His stories were indeed worth hearing. He seemed everywhere: born in Peru, sent home to Spain at ten, raised in Italy, a soldier in the Levant, an adventurer in the East Indies, back in America, then Rome, then Ireland. Shipwrecked, captive among savages, gazing into volcanic craters, fighting Turks, Indians, lions, elephants, and alligators. By thirty-five, he had seen enough for three lives and knew how to make the best of it.

He had shared the horrors of the siege of Famagusta, escaping the victorious Turks who would have flayed or impaled captive officers, avoiding a life as a Janissary. He had witnessed the Battle of the Three Kings, seen Stukely borne down by a hundred lances unconquered even in death, and held upon his knee the head of the dying King of Portugal.

"What have I left on earth, but a heart trampled as hard as the pavement?" he asked Rose one evening. "Whom do I have to love? Who loves me? I have nothing for which to live but fame: and even that is denied to me, a prisoner in a foreign land."

"Have you no kindred, then?" asked pitying Rose.

"My two sisters are in a convent; they had neither money nor beauty, so they are dead to me. My brother is a Jesuit, so he is dead to me. My father fell by the hands of Indians in Mexico; my mother, a penniless widow, is a companion or duenna, carrying fans and lapdogs for some princess in Seville, of no better blood than herself. And I—devil! I have lost even my sword—and so fares the house of De Soto."

Don Guzman intended to be pitied, and he was accordingly. Then he would turn the conversation to Italian stories, tailored to his audience: the pathetic ones when Rose was present, the racy ones when she was absent. Rose wept over the sorrows of Juliet and Desdemona long before they were enacted on an English stage, while the ribs of the Bideford worthies shook to many a jest that Cinthio and Bandello's ghosts must revisit if they wished them remembered.

Before a year had passed, Rose Salterne was far more in love with Don Guzman than he with her. Both suspected each other's minds, though neither hinted at the truth; she from fear, he from sheer Spanish pride of blood. He soon realized he must compromise that blood by marrying the heretic burgher's daughter, or all his labor would be thrown away.

He observed with astonishment and practiced with pleasure the graceful old English fashion of saluting every lady on the cheek at meeting—a custom that often caused scandal to coarse-minded Romish visitors. He saw more than one Bideford burgher, redolent of onions and profane in manner, fume with jealous rage as they kissed the velvet cheek of Rose Salterne.
