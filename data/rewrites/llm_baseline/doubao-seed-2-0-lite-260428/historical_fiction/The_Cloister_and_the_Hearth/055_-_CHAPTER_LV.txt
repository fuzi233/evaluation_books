Some two months before the quiet scene unfolding in Eli’s cottage hearth, the inhabitants of a tiny coastal hamlet tucked between Naples and Rome flocked to the shingle beach, every eye fixed seaward on a merchant vessel fighting for its life against a gale blowing straight for the shore. At moments, she clawed far enough off the coast that the crowd cheered her aloud, certain she would outrun the danger; at others, wind and waves drove her visibly closer, and the onlookers nursed a quiet, unacknowledged thrill they would never admit even to their own hearts. It is not that the torment of others brings joy, but that to stand safe while watching perils you yourself are spared feels a soft, sweet pleasure all its own.
The ill-fated craft, never built for sharp sailing, was almost perfectly engineered to run aground: her absurdly tall, overhanging poop caught every gust like a sail, and her hull lines curved like a cocked hat flipped upside down. To the watchers on the beach, her battered, labouring frame seemed almost alive, a panting thing fighting tooth and nail against death. Had they stepped aboard her deck, though, they would have seen a score of beating hearts, and a dozen different natures laid bare in the terror of that hour.
Sailors stumbled wildly across the tilting planks, hauling at ropes as each saw fit, cursing and crying for mercy in the same breath. Passengers huddled in a trembling mass around the main mast: some sitting, some kneeling, some flat on their bellies clinging to the bulwarks as the ship rolled and pitched through mountainous seas. A comely young man, his cheeks ash pale but his lips clamped tight to hold back any whimper of fear, stood a little apart, white-knuckled on a thick shroud, flinching every time a wall of green water crashed over the rails. It was Gerard, luckless and far from home.
Vows and desperate prayers rose from the huddled throng, so many different names of the divine called out it seemed there were nearly as many gods on deck as there were men and women. The sailors at least clung to one goddess, though they called her by every title they knew: Queen of Heaven, Star of the Sea, Mistress of the World, Haven of Safety. The landsmen, though, ran wild with their devotions: an English merchant swore a mountain of gold to Our Lady of Walsingham, a Genoese trader vowed a four-pound silver collar to Our Lady of Loretto, a Tuscan noble promised ten pounds of beeswax candles to Our Lady of Ravenna. Not a one of them swore on the fragment of the true cross itself, but on the fragment of the true cross housed in this city, that town, the other market square across the sea.
A sudden, brutal gust caught their remaining sail at a bad angle, the rotten shrouds snapped with a crack like thunder, and the canvas tore free to go cartwheeling down the wind, shrinking smaller and blacker until it fluttered into the waves half a mile off like a scrap of charred paper. Before the helmsman could swing the ship’s head to run before the gale, a great wave slammed into her quarter and drenched every soul aboard to the bone, a cold foretaste of the death waiting to swallow them. One man screamed a vow to become a Carthusian monk if St. Thomas spared him. Another swore to walk barefoot, bareheaded, clad only in a coat of mail, all the way to Compostella if St. James would see him safe to shore. Others called on Dominic, Denys, and above all Catherine of Sienna.
Two shivering Neapolitan traders huddled side by side. The first shouted at the top of his lungs, “I vow to St. Christopher of Paris a wax statue the exact weight of the saint, if I win to dry land!” His companion nudged him sharply. “Brother, have a care what you swear! You could sell every last thing you own at public auction and never raise enough wax for that.” “Shut your fool mouth,” the first snapped, then whispered, “You think I’m in earnest? Let me step foot on shore, and I won’t even give him a single rush-dip candle.”
Some lay flat on the deck, flattering the sea itself like a petty, cruel king. “Oh most merciful sea, oh most generous sea, oh bountiful, beautiful sea, be kind, spare us in this hour!” Others wailed and moaned in raw animal terror every time the ship lurched harder than the last, now little more than a plaything in the arms of the storm.
A humble Roman woman sat half-bared at the mast, her baby at her breast, silent amid the screaming throng. Her cheeks were ash pale, but her eyes were calm, her lips moving only in quiet, unspoken prayer. She did not weep, did not lament, did not bargain with any saint for her life. Every time the ship sank so far under a wave that bearded men squeaked like children, she only kissed her child. That was all. She sat there patient, nursing him even in the jaws of death: why should he lose any small joy she could give him, even as they both might die? There, unmistakeably, sat the old blood of Antiquity, running untainted through her veins 1600 years after the fall of Rome, an instinct to die with quiet dignity most of the medieval passengers around her could not fathom.
A gigantic Dominican friar stood on the poop, feet spread apart like the Colossus of Rhodes, not defying the storm so much as simply ignoring it. He chanted verses from the Canticles in a loud, unwavering voice, inviting all passengers to confess their sins to him. A handful knelt in the sloshing seawater at his feet; he heard their confessions, laid his hands on their heads, and absolved them as calmly as if he stood in a dry, snug sacristy rather than on a ship breaking apart under his feet. Drawn by instinct to the only steady, unshaken man aboard, Gerard drifted slowly closer, and the courage of any warrior facing mortal men would have paled next to the friar’s gigantic, unshakable composure. Even here, two souls upheld the dignity of the human race: the tender, heroic Roman mother, and the monk steeled by his faith against every mortal fear.
With the sail gone, the sailors hewed down the useless mast a foot above the deck, its remaining tangled rigging falling over the side to drag in the waves. For a few moments, it lightened the ship’s labour a little. But now the hull, no longer pushed by canvas, could not outrun the following seas, which crashed over the poop again and again, blows so heavy they felt like the impact of a rocky mountain, not water.
The captain left the helm, his face as white as death. “Lighten her!” he roared. “Throw everything overboard, or we’ll founder before we ever hit the reef, and lose the only small chance we have to live.” As the sailors scrambled to obey, the captain turned to the crowd of pale, staring passengers, and spoke in a tone no English skipper in the same peril would ever have used. “Friends, last night when the sea was calm, too calm, alas, a globe of fire hovered right beside the ship. When two of those fiery globes appear, sailors call them Castor and Pollux, and no ship can drown that voyage. But if only one comes, no Castor with no Pollux? The ship is doomed. Make your peace, good Christians, for we have no long time left.”
A great wail went up at his words. To a trembling question of how long they had to prepare, he replied, “She might last half an hour, no longer. She leaks like a sieve. Hurry, men, lighten her.”
The passengers seized every loose object on deck and heaved it over the rails. Before long, they grabbed a heavy sack an old man had been lying on, seasick, and dragged it out from under him. It rattled. Two men hauled it to the gunwale, until its owner leaped up with an unearthly shriek and threw himself on top of it. “Holy Moses, what do you think you’re doing? That’s all I have! The whole profit of my journey! Silver candlesticks, silver plates, brooches, goblets-”
“Let go, you grey old villain,” the rest roared. “Do you want all our lives lost for your ill-gotten gains?” “Throw him in with it,” one man yelled. “It’s this Hebrew that’s drowning all us Christians!” They wrenched the sack out of his arms and heaved it over the side. It vanished under the waves with a soft splash. Its owner gave one cry of unutterable anguish, stood staring out at the water, his white hair streaming in the gale, and would have leaped after it if the sack had floated. Instead it sank, gone forever. He staggered back and forth, tearing at his hair, cursing the passengers, the ship, the sea, and every power in heaven and hell alike.
“Look!” the captain shouted. “There’s a church in sight. Steer for that church, mate! And all of you, pray to whatever saint it’s named after.”
They turned the ship’s last bit of steerage towards the shoreline church, and prayed to whatever unknown saint watched over it. A giant sea crashed over the stern, snapped the rudder clean off and jammed it immovable, flooding the entire deck waist-deep. Wild with superstitious terror, a crowd of passengers swarmed around Gerard. “This is the cause of all our ruin!” they screamed. “He never called on a single saint! He’s a heathen, a pagan, right here on our ship!”
“Alas, good friends, do not say so,” Gerard stammered, his teeth chattering with cold and fear. “Call those who pray to the sea heathens, not me. Friends, I honour the saints, but I dare not waste time going through them now. Dominic, Thomas, Catherine- what use are they to me? St. Peter sits closer to God’s throne than all of them, and I’d drown before he had time to plead my cause. I must go straight to the One who made the sea, and the saints, and me. Our Father which art in heaven, save these poor souls and me that cry for bare life! Oh sweet Jesus, pitiful Jesus, who walked the waves of Genezaret when Peter sank, who wept for Lazarus when the apostles were still dry-eyed, save poor Gerard- for dear Margaret’s sake!”
At that very moment, the sailors were seen clambering into the tiny jolly-boat every ship carried, ready to desert the sinking hull. A rush of selfish, desperate men followed, and thirty souls crammed themselves into the tiny craft, pushing off for shore. Left behind were three souls too dazed to move, two paralyzed with terror, sat like heaps of wet rags on the sloshing deck. One man crawled on his knees after the full-sized wooden statue of the Virgin Mary the sailors had pried reverently from the mast, clinging to it and praying aloud as the statue washed back and forth across the deck in the rising seawater. The Jew sat cross-legged, past even the petty passion of fear, wailing over his lost bag of silver, shaking his fist at every spray that lashed his face and cursing the Nazarenes, their gods, their devils, their ships, and their seas for all eternity. The giant Dominican, having shriven every soul left aboard, stood calmly communing with his own spirit. The Roman woman sat pale and unflinching, only drawing her baby closer to her bosom as death crept nearer.
Gerard saw her, and the sight woke his manhood. “See, see!” he cried. “They took the boat and left this poor woman and her child to drown.” His mind raced, searching for something that would float. There were no loose casks, no spare planks. His eyes fell on the wooden Virgin. He caught it up in his arms, ignoring the wail that burst from the praying man like a child robbed of his favourite toy, and ran aft with it. “Come, wife,” he called to the Roman woman. “I’ll lash you and the child to this. It’s worm-eaten, but it will hold long enough.”
She turned her great dark eyes on him, and asked one single word, soft, full of tenderness: “Thyself?”
“I am a man,” he replied, “with no small child to care for.”
“Ah,” she said, and the spark of hope lit her face, chasing away a little of her calm resignation. He tied the statue tight to her side, and the hope of life made her tremble a little, though her eyes never wavered. The ship sat so low in the water now that Gerard could lever her over the gunwale with an oar, easing her into the waves. “Go,” he said, “while there is still time.”
She turned her Roman eyes, now wet with tears, to him. “Poor youth! God forgive me! My child!” He pushed her out into the surf, guiding her away from the hull with his oar so she would not be dashed against it.
A heavy hand fell on his shoulder. A deep, booming voice sounded in his ear. “It is well done. Now come with me.” It was the giant friar. Gerard turned, and the friar took two long strides, grabbed the broken stump of the mast, and Gerard obeyed by instinct, laying hold of the other end. With one enormous, straining effort, they hoisted the heavy timber and heaved it over the side. One of the dazed passengers had run after them, and leaped first, landing on one end of the mast. Gerard seized the other end, and the friar settled himself in the middle.
It was a terrible ride. The mast bucked and plunged like a kicking horse with every wave, spray lashing their faces so hard it blinded them, threatening to knock them off and under the water. Presently a long, grinding grating sound echoed ahead: the ship had struck the reef. A moment later, their mast was hurled against the stationary hull with tremendous force. The bewildered passenger’s head struck the jagged broken rudder with a sickening crack, smashed like a coconut under a sledgehammer. He sank immediately, leaving nothing but a red stain on the water, a clump of white matter on the rudder, and his dying cry ringing in their ears as their mast drifted clear under the lee of the black hull. The friar uttered a short Latin prayer for his soul, then settled back calmly on the spar.
They rolled along the troughs of the waves, one moment sunk so low they saw nothing but water all around them, the next catching glimpses of the shore lined with cheering villagers waving their arms in wild Italian gestures to encourage them. The jolly-boat the thirty men had crammed into drifted past them, bottom upwards. Not far off, the Roman woman rose and fell on the waves, holding a stray paddle she had found, her baby tucked tight under her left arm as she paddled gallantly with her right.
After what felt like an eternity, the friar spoke quietly. “I touched the ground.”
“Impossible, father,” Gerard gasped. “We are still a hundred yards from shore. Do not leave our mast, I beg you.”
“My son, you speak prudently,” the friar replied. “But I have business for the Holy Church waiting for me in Rome, and I will not waste time floating when I can walk in her service. There, I felt the sand under my toes again! The advantage of wearing sandals, not heavy shoes. The water here is shallow. You are shorter than I am: stay with the mast as long as you need to. I walk.”
He stepped off the spar, spreading his great powerful arms, and charged through the surf. Gerard followed close behind him. At every huge breaking wave, the friar stood like a stone tower, mouth closed, head thrown back to meet the wall of water, vanishing entirely under it for a heartbeat before emerging again, ploughing steadily onward. At last they were almost to the shore, but the outward drag of the surf foiled every attempt to wade to dry land. The villagers waded out to meet them, stout fishermen linked arm in arm in a triple chain holding long spears, and dragged them all to safety.
The friar shook the seawater off his robes, gave the gathered crowd a short paternal benediction, and set off on the road to Rome, his eyes bent to the ground as his order required, never pausing, never glancing back at the sea that had nearly swallowed him whole, but had no power to harm him without his Master’s leave.
While he stalks onward alone, I am not bound by the rules of the Dominican order to march straight on unseeing. The crowd left Gerard to dry himself beside their enormous bonfire, with a delicacy he would never have met in the cold north, and it was only then that he pulled two precious parchments from the breast of his tunic, laying them out carefully to dry before he even thought of stripping off his soaked clothes. Only once they were fully dry did he consent to change into a spare fisherman’s tunic, leaving his own waterlogged garments by the fire, and walk down to the shoreline to watch the rest of the wreck wash in.
The captain had stayed aboard his ship, not out of gallantry, but convinced it was useless to fight the fate the fiery single Castor had marked for him. Even so, the sea tore the poop section free of the rest of the hull, and swept him safe ashore, still clinging to the timbers. Gerard helped haul him out of the surf. The disconsolate Hebrew landed on another chunk of floating wreckage, and the moment his feet touched dry land he offered a large reward for his lost bag of silver, a plea that won no sympathy from the villagers, only a little amusement. Two more passengers survived, washed in on other pieces of the broken hull. The thirty men who had crammed into the jolly-boat came ashore too, but one at a time, all dead. One still drew breath when they pulled him out, and the villagers, acting with the very best intentions, dragged him too close to the roaring bonfire to warm him. He, too, soon breathed his last.
As Gerard stood at the edge of the waves, half horrified half curious to see the bodies of his late shipmates washed onto the shingle, a soft hand touched his shoulder. He turned. It was the Roman matron, glowing with unspoken gratitude. She lifted his hand gently, raised it to her lips, and kissed it- a gesture so noble, it felt less like a show of thanks than an honour conferred on a man who had earned it. Then, her face bright with tears, she held her baby up and made the little boy kiss his preserver’s cheek. Gerard kissed the child again
