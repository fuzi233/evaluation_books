A month after the Carnival, toward the end of March, Tito descended the marble steps of the Old Palace. He carried a weighty purpose in his heart, directing him toward San Marco. Yet, driven by some unspoken instinct, he refused the direct route—a simple, slightly curved path from the palace. Instead, he chose a circuitous journey through the Piazza di Santa Croce, anticipating a surge of humanity spilling forth from the great church following the early Lenten sermon.

Indeed, the Grand Church of Santa Croce had recently become the theater of the largest crowds in Florence. Fra Girolamo's voice, once thunderous in his own sanctuary of San Marco, had been silenced by a hostile Signoria. Obedient to a fresh decree from the Pope, who threatened an immediate interdict upon the city unless this "wretched worm" and "monstrous idol" ceased preaching and sought pardon in Rome, the friar was forbidden to speak. Consequently, the most electrifying occupation of the Lenten season had shifted: it was no longer listening to Fra Girolamo, but watching him dismantled, argued against, and vilified. This spectacle was available at Santa Croce, where the Franciscan assigned to deliver the Quaresimal sermons had proposed a dramatic climax: a trial by fire alongside Fra Girolamo.

Had not the schismatical Dominican claimed that his prophetic doctrine would be proven by a miracle at the proper time? Here, apparently, was that time. The Franciscan proposed that Savonarola walk through the flames; if emerged unscathed, the divine origin of his teachings would be irrefutable. If consumed, his falsehood would be exposed. To ensure the Franciscan could not evade this test, he declared his willingness to share the pyre, offering himself as a sacrifice to secure the necessary premise for the logic of the trial.

True to his nature, Savonarola had ignored these pulpit assaults. However, the zealous preacher of Santa Croce was none other than Fra Francesco di Puglia. The previous year, at Prato, he had faced a similar challenge from Savonarola's fervent follower, Fra Domenico, but had been recalled by his superiors before the rhetoric heated into physical confrontation. When honest Fra Domenico, currently preaching to women in the Via del Cocomero, heard of this renewed threat, he instantly seized the gauntlet on behalf of his master. He proclaimed his readiness to walk through the fire with Fra Francesco.

The populace, eager for what seemed a swift and decisive method of argumentation, began to swell with anticipation. Yet, Savonarola, acutely aware of the perils inherent in mere debate, commanded Fra Domenico to withdraw his acceptance and sever ties with the affair. The Franciscan acquiesced, noting that his challenge had been directed solely at Fra Girolamo himself, not his subordinate.

Following this, the fervor surrounding the Lenten sermons waned slightly. But that morning, as Tito entered the Piazza di Santa Croce, he found exactly what he anticipated: a throng pouring from the church. Rather than dispersing, many gathered near the entrance of the Franciscan monastery. Tito moved through the crowd with a careless, leisurely gait, yet his eyes remained fixed on the monastic doors, scanning for any sign of interest.

His expectations were misplaced. The crowd's focus was not on the monks emerging from the gate, but on a large placard affixed by order of the Signoria. Covered in legible official handwriting, it promised answers to their questions. Curiosity, however, was dampened by the fact that the document was primarily in Latin. While nearly everyone knew roughly what the notice contained, a gnawing hunger for precise interpretation lingered, creating an irritating sense of superiority regarding their neighbors' inability to decipher the learned tongue. Familiarity with Latin phrases picked up from sermons offered little aid when confronted with written script. Furthermore, the orthography of the vernacular was in a chaotic state for the literate masses, leaving the majority of those closest to the board unable to read it at all.

"It is the Frate's doctrines that he must prove by being burned," observed Goro, a prominent figure among the onlookers. "The Signoria has intervened, and the writing confirms it. It matches what the Padre preached."

"Nay, Goro," replied a slick shopkeeper with compassionate correction. "Thou hast twisted thy legs into hose. The Frate must prove his doctrines by *not* being burned. He is to walk through the fire and emerge sound and whole on the other side."

"Yes, yes," chimed in a young sculptor, wearing his white-streaked cap and tunic with jaunty pride. "But Fra Girolamo objects to walking through the fire. Being sound and whole already, he sees no need to undergo the ordeal to remain in the same condition. He leaves such odd jobs to Fra Domenico."

"Then I say he flinches like a coward!" Goro wheezed. "Suffocation! That was his trick at the Carnival. He had us all in the Piazza to watch lightning strike him, and nothing happened."

"Hold your tongue," interjected a tall shoemaker, balancing bunches of slippers over his shoulders. "Friend, you are as wise as a calf with water on its brain. The Frate will flinch from nothing. Perhaps he says nothing beforehand, but when the moment arrives, he will walk through the fire without asking a grey-frock to accompany him. Though, I'd give a shoestring to know what this Latin actually says."

"There is so much of it," sighed the shopkeeper. "Unless I'm good at guessing, I'm lost. Is there no scholar nearby?"

A collective turn of heads revealed Tito approaching from behind.

"Here is one," smiled the young sculptor, raising his cap.

"It is the Secretary of the Ten," announced the shopkeeper, bowing respectfully. "He is surely heading to the convent; make way." Such deference was rare among Florentines, reserved usually for the highest officials. The exceptional reverence was demanded by the splendor and grace of Tito's appearance; his black mantle, adorned with a gold fibula, resembled a royal robe, and his ordinary black velvet cap looked like a unique crown. The hardening of his cheeks and mouth, the primary change in his visage since arriving in Florence, seemed to superficial observers merely to add a masculine edge to his beauty. Tito acknowledged the greeting with a polite lift of his own cap.

"Thank you, my friend," he said smoothly. "I merely wished, as you did, to inspect the contents beneath this placard. Ah, as I suspected. I was informed that the government permits anyone to subscribe their name as a candidate to enter the fire—an act of liberality worthy of the magnificent Signoria—while reserving, of course, the right to select the participants. Doubtless many believers will be eager to sign. What is it to enter the fire for one whose faith is firm? A man fears fire because he believes it burns him; but if he believes otherwise?"

Tito lifted his shoulders, pausing oratorically. "For this reason, I have never hesitated to believe the Frate when he stated he would enter the fire to prove his doctrine. In his place, if you believed the fire would not burn you, which of you, my friends, would not step into it as readily as you would walk along the dry bed of the Mugnone?"

As Tito surveyed his audience, a transformation rippled through them, akin to an eager dog sensing a pungent scent. With the matter of burning becoming practical, few were rash enough to commit to a general theory on faith and flame. The scene might have overwhelmed someone less composed than Tito.

"Then, Messer Segretario," the young sculptor remarked, "Fra Francesco appears the greater hero, offering to enter the fire for the truth despite knowing it will burn him."

"I do not deny it," Tito replied blandly. "But if it transpires that Fra Francesco is mistaken, he will have been burned for the wrong cause, and the Church has never counted such victims as martyrs. We must suspend judgment until the trial truly takes place."

"It is true, Messer Segretario," the shopkeeper murmured with subdued impatience. "But will you favor us by translating the Latin?"

"Assuredly," Tito smiled. "It expresses the specific conclusions or doctrines the Frate teaches, which the trial by fire aims to verify. They are doubtless familiar to you. First, that Florence—"

"Let us have the Latin phrase by phrase, then explain the meaning," requested the shoemaker, a frequent listener to Fra Girolamo.

"Willingly," Tito agreed. "You may then judge if I render the correct meaning."

"Yes, yes; that is fair," nodded Goro.

"_Ecclesia Dei indiget renovatione_," Tito recited. "That is, the Church of God needs purification or regeneration."

"It is true," several voices chorused.

"That means the priests ought to live better lives; no miracle is needed to prove that. That is what the Frate has always said," noted the shoemaker.

"_Flagellabitur_," Tito continued. "It will be scourged. _Renovabitur_: it will be purified. _Florentia quoque post flagellam renovabitur et prosperabitur_: Florence also, after the scourging, shall be purified and shall prosper."

"That means we shall regain Pisa," the shopkeeper declared.
