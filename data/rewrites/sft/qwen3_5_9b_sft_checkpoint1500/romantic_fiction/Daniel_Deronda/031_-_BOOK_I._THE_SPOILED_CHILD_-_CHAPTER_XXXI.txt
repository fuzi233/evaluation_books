To unpath'd waters, undreamed shores."

On the morning that Gwendolen Harleth became Mrs. Grandcourt, the air was crisp with a slight frost, though the sun hung low and bright in a clear sky. The procession moving toward the church was a spectacle, drawing crowds from nearly every corner of Pennicote. A close friend of the rector officiated, while the clergyman himself served as the father, lending a dignified gravity to the parade. Yet, amidst the brilliance, two faces bore the shadow of sorrow: Mrs. Davilow's and Anna's. Mrs. Davilow's eyes were swollen and pink, betraying nights of weeping, and no one blamed her for grief at the departure of her beloved daughter. It was harder to understand why Anna, adorned in her sister's honor, wore such a troubled countenance, but the villagers' curiosity quickly turned to gossip regarding the union itself.

Of the couple, Gwendolen was the undeniable star. Her carriage and figure bespoke a "lady o' title," though perhaps her complexion lacked the rosy bloom such status usually commanded. Grandcourt, however, shared her pallor—a trait noted by the miller's wife, who found comfort in their similarity. The villagers, innocent of deeper truths, assumed he loved her deeply, hoping he would never resent her past as a governess or her mother's humble cottage at Sawyer's. The miller's fourteen-year-old daughter, listening to tales of high gentry treating wives poorly, received stern correction from her mother, who spoke of Squire Pelton's brutal habits. The tailor, overhearing this, wisely warned Mrs. Girdle that quarrels often begin with the tongue, not the whip. "The Lord gave it 'em to use," Mrs. Girdle replied, noting that men rarely intended for women to wield it. They speculated that Grandcourt, known for his silence, was a tyrant who demanded everything be done "dummy-like" without question.

As the pair emerged together, the tailor marveled at Gwendolen's long, pointed eyes, remarking how they made people feel comical when she looked at them. In truth, Gwendolen displayed an electric vitality, her gaze shining with the lustre of intense excitement. She felt no pain, but rather a thrilling intoxication akin to standing at a gambling table after a massive loss. Whatever uneasy conscience she harbored was drowned out by the gratification of her ambition and desire for luxury. She did not repent accepting Grandcourt; instead, she faced the world with a defiant exultation, daring to win or lose with equal éclat. She believed she was stepping into a realm of full power, armed with the official strength of marriage. The intoxicating fumes of youthful egoism, previously shaken by shame and guilt, had returned stronger than before. She walked among illusions, unaware that she was already a little drunk on the prospect of greatness.

Back at home, Mrs. Davilow helped Gwendolen remove her bridal white for traveling clothes. "Thank God you bear it so well, my darling!" the mother whispered, her trembling hands showing her deep anxiety. Gwendolen laughed, pressing her hands to her mother's cheeks with affectionate mockery. "If I were going to Mrs. Mompert's, you would have said that, dear, sad, incorrigible mamma!" she declared, spreading her arms to display herself as "Mrs. Grandcourt." She teased her mother about being ready to die of vexation if Gwendolen refused the match, promising that her future included splendid houses, horses, diamonds, and court life. She vowed to love her mother better than anyone else, though she dismissed the notion of her husband expecting primacy with a pretty grimace, claiming she wouldn't treat him ill unless he deserved it.

The journey to Ryelands, fifty miles by rail, left the sky veiled in twilight. As they drove through the park, Gwendolen chatted incessantly, ignoring any shift in their dynamic, while Grandcourt remained ecstatically quiet. She seized his hand with renewed vivacity, like a kitten refusing to be petted quietly. Her heart beat fast, not merely from the novelty of the estate, but from the terrifying fulfillment of her girlish dreams. She imagined herself the heroine of an admired play, surrounded by lackeys who revered her insolence, living behind corridors of immense height where painted springs bloomed. Yet, beneath this exultation lay a sharp infusion of dread, ready to curdle at any sign.

As they approached the gates, Gwendolen fell silent. When Grandcourt kissed her, she barely registered it, lost in the absorbing show of the estate. Inside, the hall glowed with warmth, mats, carpets, and statues, staffed by few but efficient servants. Led by Grandcourt down a scented corridor, she entered an ante-room where an open doorway spilled rich light. "These are our dens," he said, kissing her hand before departing to prepare for dinner. More in love than he had ever expected, he left her to solitude.

Gwendolen threw herself into a chair by the glowing hearth, admiring her reflection in glass panels amidst her faint-green satin. Impatient for privacy, she dismissed the housekeeper, instructing her to leave everything until Hudson arrived. Moments later, the housekeeper returned, holding a packet wrapped carefully. "I was ordered to give this into nobody's hands but yours when you are alone," she explained, citing Grandcourt's specific instructions. "It is a present particularly ordered by him, but he was not to know of its arrival until you wore it."

Gwendolen took the packet, letting it rest on her lap as the doors closed. She suspected it contained the diamonds Grandcourt had mentioned. In a moment of confused feeling and creeping luxury, she welcomed the diversion. Inside the sealed covering was a box, and within it, a jewel-case. As soon as she opened it, she saw the diamonds gleaming—but instantly, she noticed a letter lying atop them. Recognizing the handwriting immediately, she felt a chill as if an adder had coiled over the jewels. Her heart leaped with a shock that drained her strength, and her hands trembled violently as she unfolded the thin paper.

*"These diamonds, which were once given with ardent love to Lydia Glasher, she passes on to you,"* the letter read, *"You have broken your word to her, that you might possess what was hers. Perhaps you think of being happy, as she once was, and of having beautiful children such as hers, who will thrust hers aside. God is too just for that. The man you have married has a withered heart. His best young love was mine: you could not take that from me when you took the rest. It is dead: but I am the grave in which your chance of happiness is buried as well as mine. You had your warning. You have chosen to injure me and my children. He had meant to marry me. He would have married me at last, if you had not broken your word. You will have your punishment. I desire it with all my soul."*

*"Will you give him this letter to set him against me and ruin us more—me and my children? Shall you like to stand before your husband with these diamonds on you, and these words of mine in his thoughts and yours? Will he think you have any right to complain when he has made you miserable? You took him with your eyes open. The willing wrong you have done me will be your curse."*

At first, Gwendolen stared at the words, spellbound by the horror of them, as if reading a sentence of penance. Suddenly, a fresh spasm of terror seized her. She leaned forward, stretching the paper toward the fire, desperate to destroy the evidence before anyone could see it. The letter flew like a feather from her trembling fingers, caught instantly in a great draft of flame. In her frantic movement, the casket fell from her lap, scattering the diamonds across the floor. She paid no attention to the glittering stones; instead, she collapsed back into her chair, utterly helpless. For a long time, she sat in a stupor, feeling ill, haunted by the repeating words of the letter. Truly, these were poisoned gems, and the poison had already seeped into her blood.

Finally, a tap at the door announced Grandcourt's return, dressed for dinner. The sight of him sent a new, violent shock through her, causing her to scream again and again with hysterical force. He had expected her dressed and smiling, ready to descend for the meal. Instead, he found her pale, shrieking as if possessed by terror, with the jewels scattered around her on the floor. It was as if the furies themselves had crossed his threshold.
