When the sun sprang, as it did in those latitudes, at one bound from night into a glaring, merciless day, the Rose was already thundering close-hauled through a heaving sea. Amyas Leigh paced the deck like a man pursued—hair blown into ropes, coat torn, eyes raw with rage and salt tears—his grief not the quiet kind that sinks, but the kind that staggers and strikes at shadows. He spoke aloud to no one and to all: he would go back; he would burn the villa; he would take La Guayra and pay blood for blood.

“We can do it, lads!” he cried hoarsely. “If Drake took Nombre de Dios, we can take La Guayra.”

A roar answered him, fierce as hunger. “Yes!”

“We will have it, Amyas, and have Frank too, yet,” cried Cary; but Amyas only shook his head, as if some sure knowledge—he could not have told whence it came—had settled, cold and heavy, under his ribs: that all the ports of New Spain could never give him back one face.

He stood a moment staring toward the coast, where the green mountains rose sharp as knives behind the shore haze. Then his hand went out.

“There—there is the first crop of our vengeance.”

Between them and the now-distant shoulders of the Silla three sails showed, white as gulls’ wings and yet as ill-omened as carrion birds, creeping up to windward.

“There are the Spanish bloodhounds on our heels—the same ships we saw yesterday off Guayra. Back, lads, and welcome them, if they were a dozen.”

The older men answered with a cheer that made the younger swallow their first misgiving. Salvation Yeo’s great voice rolled over all, as steady as a bell in a storm.

“If there were a dozen, the Lord is with us, who hath said, ‘One of you shall chase a thousand.’ Clear away, lads, and see the glory of the Lord this day.”

“Amen!” cried Cary; and the Rose was kept closer yet into the wind, her timbers complaining as she climbed each sea and slid down again with a hiss of foam.

The sight of battle worked in Amyas like strong drink. He no longer felt wounds, nor the weight of the night behind him. Even Frank’s last look—so bright, so unearthly—went dim at the edges as the familiar needs of ship and fight came crowding in. In a quarter of an hour his voice rang firm again, as of old.

“Now, my masters, let us serve God, and then to breakfast, and after that clear for action.”

Jack Brimblecombe read the daily prayers and the prayers before fight at sea; and his honest voice shook when, in the Prayer for all Conditions of Men, he added—against the very grain of Amyas’s despair—“and especially for our dear brother Mr. Francis Leigh, perhaps captive among the idolaters.” They rose from their knees, the boards under them smelling of tar and wet rope and yesterday’s powder-smoke.

“Now then,” said Amyas, “to breakfast. A Frenchman fights best fasting, a Dutchman drunk, an Englishman full, and a Spaniard when the devil is in him—and that’s always.”

“And good beef and the good cause are a match for the devil,” said Cary. “Come down, captain; you must eat too.”

Amyas would not stir. He took the tiller from the steersman and sent him below with a sharp nod. But Cary, who knew how a body fails when the belly is empty, was down and up again in five minutes with a plate and a jack—brown bread already damped by the sea air, and great slices of salted beef, the fat edged with last night’s cold, and a draught of ale that smelled of malt and old oak. He coaxed it into Amyas as one forces food into a child that has cried itself sick. Then he scuttled below again, his own tears dropping into his beard where no man might see.

Amyas stood steering, chewing without tasting, the hard salt of the beef on his tongue like penance. His face had aged seven years in a night; the grief had set into it like a scar. A calm had come over him—not peace, but the stillness of a drawn sword.

“There are three of them, you see, my masters,” he said when the men were again on deck. “A big ship forward, and two galleys astern of her. The big ship may keep; she is a race ship, and if we can but recover the wind of her, we will see whether our height is not a match for her length. We must give her the slip, and take the galleys first.”

“I thank the Lord,” said Yeo, eyes bright beneath his weathered brow, “who has given so wise a heart to so young a general; a very David and Daniel, saving his presence. And if any dare not follow him, let him be as the men of Meroz and of Succoth. Amen! Silas Staveley—smite me that boy over the head, the young monkey; why is he not down at the powder-room door?”

And Yeo went to his guns as to a sacrament, with a terrible certainty that this, too, was work laid on him.

There was not much to mend; the Rose had slept, as it were, with her teeth bared. Yet there were always a hundred small tasks that made the difference between living and dying: decks cleared; nettings laced; bulwarks strengthened; waist-cloths fitted; tops armed; pikes tallowed so hands would not stick with salt and blood; yards slung; sheets and tacks doubled. The ship’s ordinary sounds—block and tackle creak, mallet taps, the rasp of rope—made their own rough music, and over it the sea’s breathing and the wind’s thin whistle through the rigging. In such a soundscape men held their hearts steady; silence bred imagining.

Amyas took the poop; Cary the forecastle; Yeo the main-deck; Drew the waist. All was ready, and more than ready, before the big ship was within two miles.

Her golden flag floated at her poop. Her trumpets shouted defiance up the breeze from a dozen brazen throats. The Rose answered from her own, and the flags told their tale plain: England; and at the fore, Leigh and Cary side by side, with the ship and bridge of Bideford above them, as if home itself had come to sea to watch.

“Now, silence trumpets,” cried Amyas. “Waits, play up! ‘Fortune my foe!’ And God and the Queen be with us!”

So the music rose—cornet and sackbut, fife and drum—and Parson Jack, planted among the players on the poop, worked away at his violin as if it were a weapon.

“Well played, Jack; thy elbow flies like a lamb’s tail,” said Amyas, forcing a jest across the hard place in his throat.

“It shall fly to a better fiddle-bow presently, sir, an I have the luck—”

“Steady, helm!” Amyas snapped. “What is he after now?”

The Spaniard, who had been driving down upon them under a press of sail, took in light canvas.

“He don’t know what to make of our waiting for him so bold,” said the helmsman.

“He does though, and means to fight us,” cried another. “See—he is hauling up the foot of his mainsail, but he wants to keep the wind of us.”

“Let him try, then,” quoth Amyas. “Keep her closer still. Let no one fire till we are about. Man the starboard guns; to starboard and wait, all small-arm men. Pass the order down to the gunner: fire high, and take the rigging.”

One of the Spaniard’s bow guns spoke; the shot went wide. Then another, and another—angry, impatient cracks, the sound carried off by wind and sea. The men of the Rose fingered primings and match-cords; the archers loosened arrows in the sheaves, the goose-feather fletching damp with spray.

“Lie down, men, and sing a psalm,” said Amyas. “When I want you, I’ll call you. Closer still, if you can. We will try a short ship against a long one. We can sail two points nearer the wind than he.”

He had read the Spaniard’s mind. The great race ship would gladly have crossed the Rose’s bows, but dared not, knowing the English hunger for a rake. If he did not mean to shoot past to leeward, he must come up close and wait on the same tack.

Amyas smiled without mirth. Hold, hold—there are more ways of killing a cat than choking her with cream.

They closed until the enemy filled the eye—timbers, men, painted sternwork, the flash of a breastplate. Then Amyas’s voice cut like an axe.

“Ready about!”

The Rose went about like an eel. Her yards creaked, her canvas shivered, and she ran upon the opposite tack—swift, clean, insolent—under the Spaniard’s stern. The Spaniard hesitated, astonished at the quickness; then tried to follow, lumbering as a cart in a narrow lane. Too late. His long body hung in the wind’s eye, helpless, while the Rose’s bowsprit all but scraped his quarter and she slid across his stern at ten yards.

“Now then!” roared Amyas. “Fire, and with a will! Have at her, archers! Have at her, muskets all!”

Bar and chain-shot, round and canister swept from stem to stern. Through the smoke musket-balls hissed, and the cloth-yard arrows—silent, cruel—drove their way into flesh and wood. The steersman and all who crowded the poop went down. The mizzen topmast snapped; stern-windows and quarter-galleries burst inward. The gorgeous painting of the Madre Dolorosa on the enemy’s stern—heart pierced with seven swords, gilded and bedizened—shivered into splinters. And, crowning all, the golden flag of Spain, which had flaunted so proudly an instant before, drooped and trailed in the sea.

Her tiller was shot away; her helmsman lay dead. She staggered like a blinded beast and fell up into the wind.

“Well done, men of Devon!” shouted Amyas as cheers tore the air.

“She has struck!” cried some.

“Not a bit,” said Amyas. “Hold on, helmsman. Let her patch her tackle while we settle the galleys.”

Away they shot, and long before the great ship could right herself, the Rose was two good miles to windward, with the galleys sweeping down fast.

They were venomous craft, those galleys—long and low, slicing the short chopping sea on forty oars apiece, their beaks like sword-fish snouts nosing forward. A strong square forecastle was packed with soldiers; cannon muzzles grinned not only from the sides but straight ahead, so that a ship before them might be hammered without respite. Down the waist were the slaves—five, six to an oar—half naked, sun-browned, their backs striped and shining. Between the banks ran a narrow gangway, and along it the drivers paced, whips in hand.

Now the Rose’s men heard them: the roll and rattle of oars, the hoarse “Ha!” that came with every stroke; the crack of lashes; the answering yells, not human so much as driven out of humanity; the curses of the drivers. And over all came a stench on the wind—musky, rank, like a kennel where hounds have been shut too long in heat and filth—so that more than one young sailor turned sick, not from fear but from pity and loathing. Even the sea’s own clean salt could not master it.

“Must we fire upon the slaves?” someone muttered, and more than one thought the same, for who knew what tongues were among those panting wretches—what English cries swallowed by the lash?

Amyas drew breath as if it hurt.

“Spare them all you can, in God’s name. But if they try to run us down, rake them we must, and God forgive us.”

The two galleys came abreast, forty yards apart. Amyas knew well he could not out-turn their oars as he had out-witted canvas; to run was to be caught between them and the big ship astern. So he chose, as he often did, the desperate road.

“Lay her head up in the wind, helmsman. We will wait for them.”

They opened with their bow-guns; but the sea’s chop threw their aim awry, and the balls went wide, slapping water and skipping off. Amyas held his own fire. The men stood at quarters, lips pressed tight, hearing every sound—the creak of leather, the clink of a buckle, the breathing of their mates—because when the guns are silent the world’s small noises grow loud and sharp.

The Spaniards, seeing him wait, shouted with joy. Mad Englishman! The galleys converged, meaning to strike him full, one on each bow.

Within forty yards—another minute and the shock would come—Amyas’s helm went up; the yards swung; the Rose gathered way and plunged straight upon the larboard galley.

“A dozen gold nobles to him who brings down the steersman!” shouted Cary from the forecastle, knowing his moment.

A flight of arrows hissed and rattled upon the galley’s quarter-deck.

Hit or no, the steersman flinched at the oncoming bulk. The galley sheered; her helm went up; her beak slid almost harmlessly along the Rose’s bow. There came a long dull grind, then crack on crack—hideous as breaking bones—as the Rose sawed through the bank of oars from stem to stern. Oars snapped like dry sticks; slaves were hurled in heaps; the yells rose in a wave that made English hearts clench.

Before the other galley could swing to strike in the Rose’s new position, Amyas had poured his whole broadside—great and small—into her at pistol-shot. The answer was a scream of rage and agony that seemed to tear the day.

“Spare the slaves! Fire at the soldiers!” cried Amyas; but the work was too hot for nice choosing. The crippled larboard galley, brave as a mad dog, swung across the Rose’s stern and hooked herself on.

It was bold, and folly with it; for it checked the other galley from returning without offering herself again to the English broadside. Spaniards tried to board through stern-ports and up the quarter; but they were met with such a storm of steel and shot that in three minutes they were flung back upon their own poop—and with them, to their disgust, came Amyas Leigh and twenty English swords.

There followed five minutes of hard cutting, hand to hand. The Rose’s lofty stern gave her men an advantage; arrows and muskets from above raked the galley’s open decks, and the forecastle soldiers could scarce lift a head without paying for it. Amyas fought as if grief had made him weightless—his blade rising and falling with a dreadful steadiness—until the poop was clear.

Then he drove along the central gangway, shouting in Spanish, his words cutting through the lash-cracks and the slaves’ “Ha!” like a new kind of thunder.

“Freedom to the slaves! Death to the masters!”

He clambered into the forecastle, his men swarming after him like wasps; and he showed them so hot an example how to sting that in three minutes more there was not a Spaniard aboard who was not dead or dying.

“Let the slaves free!” he shouted. “Throw us a hammer down, men. Hark—there’s an English voice!”

And indeed there was. From amid the wreck of broken oars and writhing limbs, a voice shrieked out—broad Devon, as plain as a hearth in winter—up to the Rose’s master looking over the side.

“Oh, Robert Drew! Robert Drew! Come down, and take me out of hell!”

“Who be you, in the name of the Lord?” cried Drew, staring as if at a ghost.

“Don’t you mind William Prust, that Captain Hawkins left behind in the Honduras, years and years agone? There’s nine of us aboard, if your shot hasn’t put ’em out of their misery. Come down, if you’ve a Christian heart—come down!”

All discipline went from Drew like a cloak flung off. Hammer in hand, he leapt down; and the two old comrades, amid blood, splintered oars, and the raw stink of the galley, caught each other fast in their arms as if the sea itself had turned in a circle and brought the past home again.
