It is a desolate and terrifying retreat. No vessel dares venture into this bay. Children from our fishing village, Cobb's Hole, never approach here to play. Even the birds of the air seem to avoid the Shivering Sand.

Most genteel individuals face a peculiar hazard: the tyranny of idleness. Lives spent searching for purpose often lead blindly into destructive pursuits. Frequently, those claiming an intellectual bent resort to tormenting nature—capturing newts, beetles, and spiders only to dissect them with cold indifference, convinced they are cultivating their minds while merely creating a mess. One sees young masters and mistresses examining spider guts through magnifying glasses or watching headless frogs descend the stairs, rationalizing this cruelty as a passion for natural history. Similarly, flowers are mutilated with sharp tools out of a morbid curiosity regarding their structure, despite the destruction of their beauty. These activities stem from an emptiness of mind and a lack of constructive labor. Compare the hardship of honest toil to the degradation of splitting petals and poking into insect bellies; gratitude should be felt that one's mind has something essential to occupy it.

Fortunately, Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel chose a path of pure chaos rather than torture. Their only victim was the wood paneling of a door.

Mr. Franklin, a man of boundless, albeit indiscriminate, genius, turned his attention to "decorative painting." He claimed to have invented a novel liquid medium, a "vehicle," to thin the paint. Its composition remains unknown to me, but its odor was potent enough to induce sneezing in passing dogs. Eager to participate, Miss Rachel donned an apron and bib. Under Mr. Franklin's guidance, she decorated her boudoir, starting with the interior of the door. He scoured the existing varnish with pumice-stone to create a raw surface, upon which Miss Rachel applied intricate patterns of griffins, birds, flowers, and cupids, copied from designs by a renowned Italian artist (whose name escapes me). The work was tedious and filthy, yet the pair displayed infinite stamina. Whenever they were not riding, receiving guests, dining, or singing, they were huddled together, busier than bees, defacing the door. As the old adage suggests, "Satan finds some mischief still for idle hands to do"; had I occupied my position in the family observing this scene, I could have penned no truer verse.

The next notable date was Sunday, June 4th.

That evening, the servants' hall buzzed with speculation regarding a domestic matter that, like the door decoration, foreshadowed future developments. Observing the evident affection between Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel, combined with their striking physical compatibility, we naturally wondered if their collaboration extended beyond art. Opinions varied: some predicted a wedding before summer's end, while others, including myself, acknowledged the likelihood of marriage but harbored doubts that Mr. Franklin would be the groom.

Mr. Franklin's affection was undeniably sincere; the challenge lay in deciphering Miss Rachel's feelings. Allow me to introduce her.

Her eighteenth birthday arrived on June 21st. If you appreciate dark-complexioned women of substantial presence, Miss Rachel stands as one of the most captivating figures imaginable. She possessed a slender, perfectly proportioned frame; her movements were graceful, proving that her charm was intrinsic to her flesh, not merely her attire. Her hair was jet-black, matching her eyes, while her nose was slightly too broad for perfection. Yet, her mouth and chin were divine, and her complexion radiated a warmth akin to sunlight, yet without the sun's harsh glare. She carried her head with the erect dignity of a spear, exuding a spirited, thoroughbred energy. Her voice was clear and resonant, and her smile began in her eyes before reaching her lips.

Regarding her character, she possessed as many flaws as any woman, neither more nor less. Most notably, she was fiercely independent. Unlike most girls of her age, she held firm opinions and dared to defy societal conventions if they conflicted with her views. While admirable in trifles, this autonomy occasionally bordered on recklessness in serious matters. She rarely sought counsel, seldom announced her intentions, and shared few secrets, even with her mother. She navigated life's joys and sorrows on her own terms. As my mistress often remarked, "Rachel's best friend and her worst enemy are, one and the same: Rachel herself."

However, amidst this stubbornness, there existed not a shred of deceit. I recall no instance where she broke a promise or lied. On numerous occasions in her youth, she had absorbed the blame for a friend's transgression, enduring punishment silently. When confronted with evidence, she would shake her head defiantly and declare, "I won't tell you!" Even when punished for her silence, she never confessed to the original fault. Stubborn, yes—even devilishly so at times—but fundamentally, the finest creature to tread this earth.

This brings us to her matrimonial prospects. On June 12th, my mistress issued an invitation to a London gentleman to assist in celebrating Miss Rachel's birthday. This was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, a cousin of hers and the suitor upon whom I believed her heart was secretly fixed.

Context requires a brief explanation of the Ablewhite family. My mistress's younger sister, the Honorable Caroline, had previously suffered a romantic disappointment. Adhering to the "neck or nothing" philosophy, she married Mr. Ablewhite, a banker from Frizinghall. Although initially considered a misalliance due to his humble origins, time and social progress validated the union. The Ablewhites were wealthy, respectable, and highly regarded in the neighborhood. Mr. Godfrey, the second son, was the focus of our attention.

Despite Mr. Franklin's efforts, his chances against Mr. Godfrey appeared slim. Mr. Godfrey was physically superior, standing over six feet tall with a handsome ruddy complexion, a smooth-shaven face, and magnificent flaxen hair. Professionally a barrister and temperamentally a ladies' man, he was also a celebrated philanthropist. He served as vice-president and manager for various charitable organizations dedicated to aiding women, earning him immense popularity. He was a public figure capable of moving crowds to tears and generosity at Exeter Hall. Compared to such a polished, beloved giant, Mr. Franklin—an average man of good reputation but lesser stature—seemed ill-equipped to compete.

On June 14th, Mr. Godfrey responded. He accepted the invitation from Wednesday through Friday evening, citing prior commitments to his charitable duties. I countered that Miss Rachel had misled me onto a false trail, predicting that Mr. Ablewhite's poetry would soon be followed by his person.

June twenty-first, the day of the birthday, dawned cloudy and unsettled, but by noon the sky had cleared with remarkable bravery. In the servants' hall, we commenced this annual celebration as custom dictates: presenting our modest gifts to Miss Rachel, accompanied by the traditional speech delivered yearly by me as the senior steward. I adhere to the strategy employed by Her Majesty when addressing Parliament—a method of delivering essentially the same sentiments annually. Prior to delivery, my address is awaited with the fervor reserved for groundbreaking revelations; yet once spoken, and found wanting in novelty despite expectations, the household grumbles briefly before turning hopeful eyes toward next year's potential innovation. An easily governed populace, whether in the House of Commons or the kitchen—is the lesson learned.

Following breakfast, Mr. Franklin and I held a private consultation regarding the Moonstone. The time had arrived to transfer the gem from the bank at Frizinghall into Miss Rachel's personal custody. Whether Mr. Franklin had attempted renewed courtship of his cousin and met with rejection, or whether his chronic sleeplessness was exacerbating the erratic fluctuations in his temperament, remains unclear. Certain, however, is that on the morning of the birthday, Mr. Franklin displayed none of his usual composure. Within moments, he oscillated through twenty distinct opinions concerning the diamond. The griffins, cupids, and myriad other figures were undeniably magnificent, though so numerous, so entwined in floral motifs, and so contorted in posture that they lingered uncomfortably in the mind long after viewing ceased. If art demands such sacrifices—even from my own daughter—I maintain that art must prevail.

Mr. Franklin seized a bite from the luncheon table and departed for Frizinghall, ostensibly to escort his cousins, though privately understood by himself and me to be fetching the Moonstone. Taking my station at the sideboard to oversee the dining preparations, I possessed ample mental space while Mr. Franklin was absent. Having attended to the wines and reviewed the staff, I retreated to compose myself before the guests arrived. A scent of tobacco and a perusal of a particular volume previously mentioned in these pages steadied both body and mind.

Dismounting without assistance, they bounced upon the ground as if crafted of India rubber. He nodded, tapping the breast pocket of his coat. "Have you seen any sign of the Indians?" "Not a glimpse," he replied, immediately requesting my lady. Upon learning she was in the small drawing-room, he proceeded directly there.

Before I could withdraw, Miss Rachel, ever mindful of the elderly servant present since her birth, called out, "Look, Gabriel!" Half an hour later, I presented myself in my lady's chamber as instructed. Our conversation largely mirrored our previous discussion at the Shivering Sands, save that I carefully withheld information regarding the "jugglers," deeming it unjustified to alarm my lady given the lack of immediate danger. The rest of the household filed in behind the Ablewhite family, completing the gathering until we numbered exactly twenty-four souls. It was indeed a magnificent spectacle to behold them all seated around the dinner table, ready for the Rector of Frizinghall to rise and offer grace with his characteristic eloquence.

There is no need to burden you with a roster of the guests; you are unlikely to encounter them again within the scope of my narrative, save for two specific individuals. These two sat flanking Miss Rachel, the undisputed queen of the evening and the focal point of every gaze. Her presence was heightened by the magnificent birthday gift she wore: the Moonstone. Though originally unset, the gem's brilliance was amplified by the ingenious workmanship of Mr. Franklin, who had secured it to her white bodice using delicate silver wire. Its sheer size and radiance captivated everyone, yet only the two guests beside Miss Rachel offered commentary that deviated from mere awe.

On her right sat the renowned traveler, Mr. Murthwaite. A tall, gaunt, wiry figure with a weathered complexion and an intense, unblinking stare, he appeared weary of domestic life and eager to return to the wilds of the East. Miss Rachel, secure in England, found this alarming news delightful. The servants erupted in delight, dropping their cutlery to exclaim, "Oh, how fascinating!" As the meal progressed, I sensed a palpable shift in the atmosphere; the festivities were failing to thrive as previous celebrations had. Looking back with the benefit of hindsight, I am convinced the cursed Diamond cast a shadow over the entire assembly. I circulated among the guests, encouraging them to drink, hoping to liven the mood. While they politely credited my efforts to my loyalty, the result was merely silence. Gaps in conversation stretched uncomfortably long. When speech resumed, it was often innocuous yet disastrously ill-timed.

Mr. Candy, for instance, ventured remarks far darker than usual. Consider the incident involving Mrs. Threadgall, the widow of the late Professor Threadgall. This lady, unable to suppress her grief, frequently referred to her husband as if he were still alive, assuming everyone knew he was deceased. Such was the behavior of Mr. Candy; the others were equally frustrating. Mr. Godfrey, usually eloquent, retreated into silence, perhaps sulking over his earlier humiliation in the rose garden. Then there was Mr. Franklin. One might expect him to salvage the evening, but he was in a state of manic energy, bouncing between languages and topics with bewildering results. They traded barbs until Mr. Candy lost all composure defending his profession. My lady was forced to intervene, ordering a cessation of hostilities. This authoritative command extinguished the remaining spark of liveliness. The Devil—or perhaps the Diamond—had clearly possessed the dinner party. Just as I arranged the decanters before Old Mr. Ablewhite, a startling sound echoed from the terrace: the beat of an Indian drum. Here they were, the jugglers returning with the Moonstone!

I cannot detail the tricks they performed, nor how they executed them. Amidst the frustration of the failed dinner and the shock of the thieves' timely return, I momentarily lost my wits. His face, previously bronzed, had turned ash-gray. He bowed to my lady and declared the exhibition concluded. The Bouncers groaned in disappointment, blaming Mr. Murthwaite for ruining the show. The lead Indian reiterated that the performance was over. I slept fitfully, plagued by insomnia similar to Mr. Franklin's. Sunrise finally claimed me. Waking at half-past seven to find a brilliant sun, I stepped out to secure the dogs when a frantic rustling of skirts echoed on the stairs behind me.

Accompanied by Mr. Franklin, I rode to the stables to inquire how the intruders could have entered.

"One likely slipped into the hall amidst the confusion of the departing guests," Mr. Franklin theorized. "He may have waited beneath the sofa while we discussed the Diamond's placement. Once the house settled, the cabinet was open game."

Calling the groom to open the gate, Mr. Franklin spurred his horse forward. This seemed the only logical explanation, yet a nagging doubt persisted: how had the thief escaped? The front door was locked and bolted as I had left it. The other exits remained secure. The dogs? Had they been drugged? As these questions raced through my mind, the dogs themselves bounded past, full of life and spirit, easily tamed and chained once more. Mr. Franklin's theory felt increasingly insufficient.

Breakfast proceeded as usual, regardless of the robbery or potential murder. Afterwards, Lady Verinder summoned me. I disclosed everything I had withheld concerning the Indians' plot. The South Spit was already submerged by the incoming tide, the waters churning over the hidden quicksand below. With an almost terrifying patience, Cuff tested the boot against the tracks repeatedly. Every time, the alignment pointed unequivocally *toward* the rocks. Yet, despite his exhaustive search, he found absolutely no signs of footsteps leading *away* from them.

Finally, he ceased his hunt. Turning his gaze from the tracks to the churning sea, he looked at me, then back out at the deepening waves. I mirrored his gaze and read the terrible realization in his eyes. A cold, numb tremor swept through me, and I collapsed to my knees on the beach.

"She has returned to the hiding place," Cuff murmured to himself. "A fatal accident must have occurred on those rocks."

Memories flooded my mind: Rosanna's changed demeanor, her hollow words, the deadened way she had accepted my presence just hours earlier. These warnings confirmed his suspicion, though perhaps not the full extent of the tragedy. I tried to explain that her death was self-inflicted, that she had sought it out, but the words refused to form. The paralysis held me fast. I felt neither the driving rain nor the rising tide. In a vivid, dreamlike vision, I saw her again as I had on the morning I brought her home. I remembered her confession that the Shivering Sand drew her irresistibly, her wonderment that her grave might wait for her *there*. The horror of it pierced me deeply, echoing through my own grief for my daughter, who was Rosanna's age. Even a life as miserable as hers could have ended in such a dreadful manner.

Gently, the Sergeant lifted me up and turned me away from the site of her demise. Only then did I begin to breathe again, forcing myself to view the world as it truly was. Looking toward the sandhills, I saw the outdoor servants and a fisherman named Yolland rushing down the slope, shouting inquiries about the body.

In brief, grim terms, Cuff explained the evidence of the footprints and declared that a fatality had undoubtedly occurred. He singled out the fisherman and posed a critical question, turning his attention back to the sea: "Could a boat have taken her off in weather like this, from those rocks where her tracks end?"

Yolland gestured wildly at the crashing rollers and the massive waves leaping against the headlands. "No boat ever built could reach her through that," he answered firmly.

Cuff studied the fading footprints, now being washed away by the rain. "Here," he stated, "is proof she cannot have left by land. And here," he added, nodding at the fisherman, "is proof she could not have fled by sea."

Pausing to consider, he addressed Yolland. "She was seen running toward this spot half an hour before I arrived. Let us assume an hour has passed since then. How high was the water on this side of the rocks at that time?" Pointing to the southern side, free of the quicksand, the fisherman replied that there wouldn't have been enough water to drown a kitten. On the northern side, covered by the shifting sand, the water level would have been negligible.

"Therefore," Cuff concluded, turning to me, "the accident must have happened on the side of the quicksand."

My tongue finally loosened. "Not an accident!" I cried out. "When she reached this place, she was weary of life and chose to end it here."

The Sergeant recoiled slightly. "How do you know?" he asked.

The group pressed closer, but Cuff swiftly pushed them back. "He is an old man," he said calmly. "The discovery has shaken him. Leave him be for a moment."

Turning to Yolland, he asked if there was any chance of recovery when the tide receded. "None," the fisherman replied sadly. "What the Sand takes, it keeps forever."

Stepping forward, Yolland addressed me directly. "Mr. Betteredge, regarding the young woman's death... four feet out, broadside along the Spit, lies a shelf of rock about half a fathom beneath the sand. My question is: why didn't she strike it? If she slipped accidentally from the Spit, she would have fallen into shallow water with footing at the bottom, barely covering her waist. Unless she deliberately waded or jumped into the Deep beyond, she would not be missing now. It was no accident, sir. The Deeps of the Quicksand claim her, and they took her by her own hand."

With this authoritative testimony, the Sergeant fell silent, and so did we all. Together, we turned back up the beach slope. Just as we reached the sandhills, the under-groom rushed to meet us from the house. He was a loyal lad with a natural respect for me, handing me a folded note with a solemn expression.

"Penelope sent this for you, Mr. Betteredge," he said softly. "She found it in Rosanna's room."

It was her final farewell to the old man who had strived tirelessly to care for her. *"You have often forgiven me, Mr. Betteredge, in times past. When you next see the Shivering Sand, try to forgive me once more. I have found my grave where my grave was waiting for me. I have lived, and died, sir, grateful for your kindness."*

Those were the only words, yet they broke me completely. Tears flow easily when one is young and starting out; they flow just as readily when one is old and leaving everything behind. I burst into uncontrollable sobbing.

Sergeant Cuff stepped closer, intending kindness. I flinched and backed away. "Don't touch me," I whispered. "It is the dread of you that drove her to this."

"You are mistaken, Mr. Betteredge," he replied quietly. "But there will be time to discuss this when we are indoors."

Supported by the groom's arm, I followed the others back through the driving rain, returning to the house to face the turmoil and terror that awaited us within.

Those ahead of us had already carried the news. By the time we arrived, the household was in a state of absolute panic. As we approached my mistress's door, it flew open violently from within. My lady emerged amidst the chaos, accompanied by Mr. Franklin, who strove in vain to compose her agitation. She was utterly beside herself, consumed by the sheer horror of the discovery.

"You are answerable for this!" she shrieked, pointing a trembling finger at the Sergeant with wild intensity. "Gabriel! Give that wretch his money—and release me from the sight of him!"

The Sergeant was the only soul among us capable of holding his ground, being the only one entirely composed. "I am no more responsible for this distressing calamity, my lady, than you are," he replied. His tone was respectful yet unyieldingly firm. "Should you still insist upon my departure from this house in half an hour, I shall accept your ladyship's dismissal, but certainly not your ladyship's money."

The words struck home, affecting both my mistress and myself. She allowed Mr. Franklin to guide her back inside, closing the door behind them. Once they were gone, the Sergeant cast an observant glance around the group of female servants. While the others trembled in fear, he noted that Penelope was weeping openly.

"When your father has finished changing his wet clothes," the Sergeant instructed her gently, "come and speak to us in your father's room."

Before the half-hour elapsed, I had dressed in dry garments and provided the Sergeant with the change of attire he required. Penelope entered to hear what he sought. Never before had I felt so acutely the strength of my bond with my daughter. I drew her onto my lap, clasped her close, and prayed silently for her protection. She buried her face in my bosom, wrapping her arms tightly around my neck, and we remained in silence for a moment. In my heart, I believed the poor deceased girl must have been the catalyst for this tragedy, alongside my daughter and myself.

The Sergeant moved to the window, gazing outward. I felt compelled to express my gratitude for his consideration of our situation, and I did so. There is a peculiar luxury reserved for those of high station: the freedom to indulge their emotions. For those of lower standing, necessity offers no such reprieve. Hardship spares the privileged, yet shows no mercy to us. We are taught to suppress our feelings, to internalize them, and to endure our duties with patient stoicism. I make no complaint of this condition; I merely observe it.

Soon, both Penelope and I were prepared to receive the Sergeant. When questioned regarding what might have driven her fellow-servant to such a desperate act, my daughter answered—as one might expect—that it was due to her affection for Mr. Franklin Blake. Next, asked if she had shared this suspicion with anyone else, she replied, "I have not mentioned it, for Rosanna's sake."

I deemed it necessary to interject. "And for Mr. Franklin's sake, my dear, as well," I added softly. "If Rosanna indeed perished because of her love for him, it was neither known nor caused by him. Should he depart the house today, let him do so without the unnecessary pain of bearing this truth."

Sergeant Cuff nodded approvingly, "Quite right," before falling silent again. To me, it appeared he was weighing Penelope's theory against some private hypothesis of his own, keeping the latter strictly to himself.

At the conclusion of the half-hour, my mistress's bell rang. En route to answer it, I encountered Mr. Franklin exiting his aunt's sitting-room. He informed me that her ladyship was eager to confer with Sergeant Cuff, in my presence as previously arranged, though he wished to address the Sergeant privately first.

On our return journey to my chamber, he paused to examine the railway timetable hanging in the hall. "Are you truly intending to leave us, sir?" I asked. "Miss Rachel will surely recover if you simply grant her time?"

"She will recover," Mr. Franklin responded, "once she realizes I have departed and that she will never see me again."

I initially interpreted his words as resentment stemming from how my young lady had treated him. However, this was not the case. From the moment the police arrived, my mistress had observed that even the mere mention of his name ignited Miss Rachel's temper. Having loved his cousin too deeply to admit it to himself until reality forced the truth upon him, he had retreated to his aunt's residence. Now, with his eyes opened to the cruel reality—a transformation familiar to those who know him—Mr. Franklin had made his decision. It was the single resolution a man of spirit could possibly take: to leave the house.

What he intended to tell the Sergeant was spoken in my hearing. He conveyed that her ladyship was willing to acknowledge her previous haste. He then asked if the Sergeant would agree, under these circumstances, to accept his fee and withdraw from the matter of the Diamond as it currently stood.

"No, sir," the Sergeant replied. "My fee is compensation for performing my duty. I decline to accept payment until that duty is fulfilled."

"I do not understand you," Mr. Franklin said.

"I shall explain, sir," the Sergeant continued. "Upon arriving here, I undertook to shed necessary light on the disappearance of the Diamond. I am now ready and waiting to honor that pledge. Once I have presented the current state of affairs to Lady Verinder and clearly outlined the course of action required to recover the Moonstone, the burden of responsibility will shift to her shoulders. Only then, after she decides whether to permit me to proceed further, will I consider my task complete. At that point, I shall have done exactly what I promised, and I will claim my fee."

In those words, Sergeant Cuff reminded us that even within the Detective Police, a man possesses a reputation to safeguard. His stance was undeniably correct, leaving no room for further argument.

As I rose to escort him to my lady's room, he inquired if Mr. Franklin wished to remain. Mr. Franklin answered, "Unless Lady Verinder desires it." Then, lowering his voice so only I could hear, he whispered, "I know what that man intends to say about Rachel; and I am too fond of her to listen and maintain my composure. Leave me alone."

I departed, finding him leaning miserably against the window sill, his face concealed in his hands, while Penelope peered through the doorway, yearning to offer comfort. Had I been in his position, I would have summoned her immediately. When wronged by one woman, there is profound solace in confiding in another, for nine times out of ten, the listener will stand by your side. Perhaps, while my back was turned, he did call her in? If so, it is only fair to declare that my daughter would stop at nothing to console Mr. Franklin Blake.

Meanwhile, Sergeant Cuff and I proceeded to my lady's room. During our last consultation, we had found her reluctant to look up from the book resting on the table. This time, however, there was a marked improvement. She met the Sergeant's gaze with a steadiness equal to his own. A fierce family spirit illuminated every line of her face, and I knew that Sergeant Cuff would find a worthy adversary in a woman like my mistress, especially when she was primed to hear the worst he could possibly say.

The first words spoken after we took our seats were addressed by my lady. "Sergeant Cuff," she said, "there was perhaps some excuse for the inconsiderate manner in which I spoke to you half an hour since. I have no wish, however, to claim that excuse. I say, with perfect sincerity, that I regret it, if I wronged you."

The grace of voice and manner with which she offered this atonement had its intended effect upon the Sergeant. He requested permission to justify himself, framing his defense as an act of respect toward my mistress. It was impossible, he argued, that he could be in any way responsible for the calamity that had shocked us all, for a simple reason: his success in concluding his inquiry depended entirely on his ability to avoid saying or doing anything that might alarm Rosanna Spearman. He appealed to me to testify whether he had indeed carried out this objective. I could, and did, bear witness that he had. And there, as I thought, the matter might have been wisely allowed to conclude.

However, Sergeant Cuff took the conversation a step further, evidently (as you shall soon judge) with the purpose of forcing the most painful of all possible explanations to occur between her ladyship and himself.

"I have heard a motive assigned for the young woman's suicide," said the Sergeant, "which may possibly be the correct one. It is a motive quite unconnected with the case I am currently conducting. I am bound to add, however, that my own opinion points the other way. Some unbearable anxiety concerning the missing Diamond, I believe, has driven the poor creature to her own destruction. I do not pretend to know what that specific anxiety may have been. But I think (with your ladyship's permission) I can identify a person capable of determining whether I am right or wrong."

"Is the person now in the house?" my mistress asked, after a brief pause.

"The person has left the house, my lady."

That answer pointed as directly at Miss Rachel as any accusation could possibly aim. A heavy silence descended upon us, one I feared would never lift. Lord! how the wind howled outside, and how the rain lashed against the windowpanes, as I sat there waiting for one or the other of them to speak again!

"Be so good as to express yourself plainly," commanded my lady. "Do you refer to my daughter?"

"I do," replied Sergeant Cuff, in so many words.

My mistress had her cheque-book on the table when we entered the room—undoubtedly intending to pay the Sergeant his fee. She now placed it back into the drawer. It broke my heart to see how her poor hand trembled—the hand that had bestowed so many benefits upon her old servant; the hand that, I pray God, may take mine when my time comes, and I leave this world forever!

"I had hoped," said my lady, very slowly and quietly, "to have recompensed your services, and to have parted with you without Miss Verinder's name having been openly mentioned between us as it has been mentioned now. My nephew has probably conveyed some message regarding this before you entered my room?"

"Mr. Blake delivered his message, my lady. And I gave Mr. Blake a reason—"

"It is needless for you to tell me your reason. After what you have just said, you know as well as I do that you have gone too far to retreat. I owe it to myself, and I owe it to my child, to insist on your remaining here, and to insist on your speaking out fully."

The Sergeant glanced at his watch. "If there had been time, my lady," he answered, "I should have preferred submitting my report in writing rather than communicating it verbally. But, if this inquiry is to proceed, time is of too much importance to be wasted on paper. I am ready to discuss the matter immediately. It is a very painful topic for me to broach, and for you to hear."

There, my mistress stopped him once more. "I may possibly make it less painful to you, and to my good servant and friend here," she said, "if I set the example of speaking boldly on my side. You suspect Miss Verinder of deceiving us all by secreting the Diamond for some personal purpose? Is that true?"

"Quite true, my lady."

"Very well. Now, before you begin, I must tell you, as Miss Verinder's mother, that she is *absolutely incapable* of doing what you suppose her to have done. Your knowledge of her character dates from only a day or two ago. My knowledge of her character dates from the very beginning of her life. State your suspicion of her as strongly as you please—it is impossible that you can offend me by doing so. I am sure, beforehand, that (despite all your experience) the circumstances have fatally misled you in this instance. Mind! I possess no private information. I am as completely shut out from my daughter's confidence as you are. My sole reason for speaking so positively is the reason you have already heard: I know my child."

She turned to me and extended her hand. I kissed it in silence.

"You may go on," she said, facing the Sergeant again as steadily as ever.

Sergeant Cuff bowed. My mistress had produced but one effect upon him. His hatchet-like face softened for a fleeting moment, as if he felt pity for her. As to shaking him from his own conviction, it was plain to see that she had not moved him by a single inch. He settled himself comfortably in his chair and began his vicious attack on Miss Rachel's character with these words:

"I must ask your ladyship to look this matter squarely in the face, from my point of view as well as from yours. Will you please to imagine yourself coming down here in my place, armed with my experience? And will you allow me to mention very briefly what that experience entails?"

My mistress signaled that she would do so. The Sergeant continued:

"For the last twenty years," he said, "I have been extensively employed in cases of family scandal, acting in the capacity of a confidential agent. The one result of my domestic practice that bears any relevance to the matter at hand is a fact I can state in two words. It is well within my experience that young ladies of rank and position do occasionally incur private debts which they dare not acknowledge to their nearest relatives and friends. Sometimes, the milliner and the jeweler are at the bottom of it. Sometimes, the money is required for purposes which I do not suspect in this case, and which I shall not shock you by mentioning. Bear in mind what I have said, my lady—and now let us see how events in this house have forced me to revert to my own experience, whether I liked it or not!"

He paused to consider, then continued—with a horrible clarity that compelled you to understand him; with an abominable fairness that favored no one.

"My first information regarding the loss of the Moonstone," said the Sergeant, "came to me from Superintendent Seegrave. He proved to my complete satisfaction that he was utterly incapable of managing the case. The one thing he said which struck me as worth listening to was this—that Miss Verinder had declined to be questioned by him, and had spoken to him with a perfectly incomprehensible rudeness and contempt. I found this curious—but I attributed it mainly to some clumsiness on the Superintendent's part which might have offended the young lady. After that, I set it aside in my mind and applied myself, single-handed, to the case. It ended, as you are aware, in the discovery of the smear on the door, and in Mr. Franklin Blake's evidence satisfying me that this same smear, and the loss of the Diamond, were pieces of the same puzzle. So far, if I suspected anything, I suspected that the Moonstone had been stolen, and that one of the servants might prove to be the thief. Very good. In this state of affairs, what happens? Miss Verinder suddenly emerges from her room and speaks to me. I observe three suspicious appearances in that young lady. She is still violently agitated, though more than forty-eight hours have passed since the Diamond was lost. She treats me as she has already treated Superintendent Seegrave. And she is mortally offended with Mr. Franklin Blake. Very good again. Here (I say to myself) is a young lady who has lost a valuable jewel—a young lady, furthermore, as my own eyes and ears confirm, who possesses an impetuous temperament. Under these circumstances, and with that character, what does she do? She betrays an incomprehensible resentment against Mr. Blake, the Superintendent, and myself—otherwise, the very three people who have all, in their different ways, been trying to help her recover her lost jewel. Having brought my inquiry to that point—*then*, my lady, and not until then, I begin to look back into my own mind for guidance from my own experience. My own experience explains Miss Verinder's otherwise incomprehensible conduct. It associates her with those other young ladies that I know of. It tells me she has debts she daren't acknowledge, that must be paid. And it sets me asking myself, whether the loss of the Diamond may not mean—that the Diamond must be secretly pledged to pay them. That is the conclusion which my experience draws from plain facts. What does your ladyship's experience say against it?"

"What I have said already," answered my mistress. "The circumstances have misled you."

I remained silent on my side. *Robinson Crusoe*—God knows how—had somehow gotten into my muddled old head. If Sergeant Cuff had found himself, at that moment, transported to a desert island, without a Man Friday

With my mistress gone, I finally had the quiet to reflect upon Sergeant Cuff. I located him tucked away in a cozy corner of the hall, poring over his memorandum book while twisting his lips into a scowl of displeasure.

"Taking notes on the case?" I inquired.

"No," replied the Sergeant. "Checking my schedule for the next professional engagement."

"Oh!" I exclaimed. "You believe it's all concluded here, then?"

"I believe," answered Sergeant Cuff, "that Lady Verinder is one of the sharpest women in England. I also believe a rose is far superior to a diamond. Where is the gardener, Mr. Betteredge?"

He refused to utter another word regarding the Moonstone. His interest in his own investigation had evaporated completely; instead, he remained fixated on locating the gardener. An hour later, I overheard them arguing heatedly in the conservatory, the dog-rose once again serving as the root cause of their dispute.

Meanwhile, it became my task to ascertain whether Mr. Franklin intended to depart via the afternoon train. After learning of the conference in my lady's room and its outcome, he promptly resolved to wait for news from Frizinghall. While this natural shift in plans would have been inconsequential for anyone else, it produced a peculiar effect on Mr. Franklin. It left him adrift, burdened with idle hours, causing every facet of his foreign nature to surface simultaneously, tumbling out of him like rats from a sack. At moments he seemed an Italian-Englishman, at others a German-Englishman, and at times a French-Englishman. He drifted from room to room with nothing to discuss but Miss Rachel's treatment of him, addressing only me.

I found him in the library, seated beneath the map of Modern Italy, seemingly believing that complaining was the sole remedy for his troubles.

"I possess several noble aspirations, Betteredge; but what am I to do with them now? I brim with dormant virtues if only Rachel would assist in awakening them!"

His eloquence in depicting his own neglected talents, coupled with his pathos in lamenting them, left me utterly at a loss for comfort. Suddenly, the idea struck me to apply a dose of *Robinson Crusoe* to the situation. I shuffled to my own chamber and returned with that timeless volume.

The library was deserted. The map of Modern Italy gazed at me, and I gazed back at it. I checked the drawing-room; his handkerchief lay on the floor, evidence that he had drifted in, while the emptiness of the room proved he had drifted out again. In the dining-room, Samuel stood with a biscuit and a glass of sherry, silently surveying the vacant space. Just a moment prior, Mr. Franklin had rung violently for refreshments. Once served hurriedly by Samuel, Mr. Franklin had vanished before the bell downstairs had ceased its frantic ringing.

Finally, I tracked him to the morning-room. There he sat by the window, tracing hieroglyphics in the condensation on the glass.

"Your sherry awaits you, sir," I announced.

It was as if I were speaking to a wall; he was sunk in the abyss of his own thoughts, beyond recall.

"How do *you* explain Rachel's conduct, Betteredge?" was the only response I received.

Lacking a prepared answer, I produced *Robinson Crusoe*, hoping a solution might lie buried within its pages if only we searched diligently enough. Mr. Franklin snapped the book shut and erupted into his German-English gibberish.

"Why not investigate?" he demanded, as if I had personally forbidden inquiry. "Why waste your patience, Betteredge, when patience alone reveals the truth? Do not interrupt. Rachel's behavior is entirely logical if you grant her the basic courtesy of considering the Objective view first, followed by the Subjective, and concluding with the Objective-Subjective synthesis. What facts do we possess? That the loss of the Moonstone on Thursday morning plunged her into nervous agitation from which she has not yet recovered. Deny the Objective view? Very well. Proceeding from within outward, what conclusion do we reach? The Subjective view. I challenge you to refute it. Consequently, the Objective-Subjective explanation follows naturally: Rachel, strictly speaking, is not Rachel, but 'Somebody Else.' Would I mind being mistreated by 'Somebody Else'? You are unreasonable enough, Betteredge, but hardly accusatory. Thus, it concludes—in defiance of your confounded English narrowness—with me feeling perfectly content. Where is the sherry?"

By this time, my mind was so befuddled I could scarcely determine whose head I possessed. In this dire state, I managed three objective actions: I procured Mr. Franklin's sherry, retreated to my room, and indulged in the finest calming pipe of tobacco I have ever smoked.

However, do not assume I escaped Mr. Franklin's influence so easily. Drifting from the morning-room into the hall, he discovered my office, smelled my pipe, and instantly recalled his foolish vow to abstain from smoking for Miss Rachel's sake. In a flash, he burst in with his cigar-case and launched into his eternal topic, delivered in his crisp, witty, incredulous French style.

"Light me, Betteredge. Can it truly be that a man has smoked as long as I have without realizing there exists a complete system for treating women hidden in his cigar-case? Listen closely, and I shall demonstrate it in two steps. Select a cigar, test it, and if it fails, discard it and try another. Observe the application! Choose a woman, test her, and if she breaks your heart, fool that you are, learn from your cigar-case: discard her and try another!"

I shook my head. Clever, undoubtedly, yet my own experience contradicted it.

"In the days of the late Mrs. Betteredge," I remarked, "I frequently felt tempted to adopt your philosophy, Mr. Franklin. Yet the law compels one to smoke the cigar once selected, sir."

I punctuated this with a knowing wink. Mr. Franklin burst into laughter, and we merried like crickets until another facet of his character emerged.

Thus, my young master and I continued our interlude while the Sergeant and the gardener argued over roses. Half an hour earlier than anticipated, the pony-chaise arrived. My lady had decided to remain temporarily at her sister's residence. The groom delivered two letters—one for Mr. Franklin, the other for me.

I dispatched Mr. Franklin's letter to the library, where his wandering had recently deposited him. I read my own letter in my room. A cheque fell out before I could fully grasp its contents, informing me that Sergeant Cuff's dismissal from the Moonstone inquiry was now final.

I summoned the Sergeant to the conservatory. He appeared, his mind occupied with the gardener and the dog-rose, insisting that no one had ever matched Mr. Begbie in obstinacy, nor ever would. I requested he dismiss such trivialities and attend to a serious matter. He obliged sufficiently to notice the letter in my hand.

"Ah!" he sighed wearily. "You have heard from her ladyship. Does this concern me, Mr. Betteredge?"

"You shall decide for yourself, Sergeant."

Reading the letter aloud with care and emphasis, I conveyed Lady Verinder's message:

"My dear Gabriel, I request you inform Sergeant Cuff that I have fulfilled my promise to him, with these results concerning Rosanna Spearman: Miss Verinder solemnly declares she has never spoken a word privately to Rosanna since that unfortunate woman entered my home. They never met, even accidentally, on the night the Diamond was lost. No communication whatsoever occurred between them from Thursday morning, when the alarm was raised, until this Saturday afternoon when Miss Verinder departed. After abruptly informing my daughter of Rosanna Spearman's suicide—"

Reaching this point, I paused to ask the Sergeant his opinion thus far.

"I would only offend you if I voiced my own opinion," the Sergeant replied. "Proceed, Mr. Betteredge," he added with exasperating resignation.

Recalling the Sergeant's audacity in complaining about the gardener, my tongue itched to continue in words other than my mistress's. However, my Christian forbearance held firm. I continued steadily:

"After appealing to Miss Verinder in the manner the officer deemed most appropriate, I subsequently addressed her in the manner I believed most likely to impress her. On two separate occasions before my daughter left my roof, I privately warned her that she exposed herself to suspicion of the most unbearable and degrading nature. I have now plainly stated that my apprehensions have been realized.

'Her answer,' she affirms solemnly, 'is as clear as words can be. First, she owes no money privately to any living soul. Second, the Diamond is neither now nor has it ever been in her possession since placing it in her cabinet on Wednesday night. The confidence my daughter places in me extends no further. She maintains an obstinate silence when I ask her to explain the Diamond's disappearance. She refuses, with tears, when I implore her to speak for my sake. "The day will come when you will understand why I am indifferent to suspicion, and why I remain silent even toward *you*. I have done much to earn my mother's pity—nothing to make her blush for me." These are my daughter's exact words.

Following the exchange between the officer and myself, I believe—even stranger as he is—that he must be apprised of what Miss Verinder has stated, alongside you. Read this letter to him, then place the enclosed cheque in his hands. In relinquishing all further claim on his services,

I had the pony-chaise standing ready, anticipating that Mr. Franklin might insist on departing by the train that very night. However, the sudden appearance of his luggage, trailed downstairs by Mr. Franklin himself, made it abundantly clear that he had finally resolved to stay put—for once in his life.

"So you have truly made up your mind, sir?" I asked as we met in the hall. "Why not wait a day or two longer and afford Miss Rachel another opportunity?"

The veneer of composure seemed to have completely stripped itself from Mr. Franklin now that the moment of departure had arrived. Instead of offering a verbal response, he pressed the letter his ladyship had addressed to him into my hand. Much of its content merely reiterated points already covered in previous communications, but there was a final addition regarding Miss Rachel that explained the steel in Mr. Franklin's resolve, should it explain anything at all.

"You will likely wonder," her ladyship wrote, "that I allowed my own daughter to keep me entirely in the dark. A diamond valued at twenty thousand pounds has vanished, and I am left to deduce that the mystery of its disappearance is no mystery to Rachel. Furthermore, some unknown party appears to have imposed an inexplicable oath of silence upon her for reasons I cannot fathom. Could I possibly permit myself to be trifled with in such a manner? It seems entirely plausible, given Rachel's current state. She is in a condition of nervous agitation that is truly pitiful. I dare not broach the subject of the Moonstone again until time has worked to calm her. To aid this recovery, I have not hesitated to dismiss the police officer. The puzzle that confounds us also baffles him. This is not a matter where a stranger can assist; he only adds to my suffering and drives Rachel mad whenever his name is mentioned.

"My plans for the future are as settled as circumstances allow. My intention is to take Rachel to London—to soothe her mind through a complete change of scene and to seek the counsel of the finest medical experts. May I request that you join us in the city? My dear Franklin, you must, in your own way, emulate my patience and wait for a more opportune moment. The invaluable assistance you previously offered in investigating the missing jewel remains, in Rachel's present dreadful state of mind, an unforgivable offense. Acting blindly in this affair, you inadvertently threatened her secret with exposure through your efforts, thereby adding to the heavy burden of anxiety she has endured. It is impossible for me to excuse the perversity that holds you responsible for consequences neither you nor I could have foreseen. She cannot be reasoned with; she can only be pitied. I am grieved to say it, but for the present, you and Rachel are better apart. My sole advice is to give her time."

I returned the letter, feeling genuine sorrow for Mr. Franklin, knowing how deeply he cherished my young lady. I observed that her mother's assessment had struck him to the core. "You know the proverb, sir," I told him. "When things are at their worst, they are certain to improve. Things cannot possibly be much worse, Mr. Franklin, than they are now."

Mr. Bruff folded the document and then cast a glance in my direction, seemingly uncertain whether I intended to remain or depart. I possessed a mission of mercy to fulfill, and my satchel of sacred literature rested upon my lap. He might just as reasonably expect to topple St. Paul's Cathedral by mere inspection as he could move me. However, he possessed one virtue, likely acquired through his worldly training, which I cannot deny: he was keenly observant. He perceived nearly the same impression in me that the cabman had earlier noted. He too muttered a profanity and retreated in a violent hurry, leaving me undisputed mistress of the situation.

Immediately upon being alone, my aunt reclined upon the sofa and, with evident confusion, broached the subject of her Will. "I trust you do not feel neglected, Drusilla," she remarked. "I intend to deliver your small legacy personally, my dear."

Here lay a golden opportunity! Seizing it without hesitation, I opened my bag and extracted the foremost publication. It was an early edition—number twenty-five—of the renowned anonymous treatise (attributed to the estimable Miss Bellows) titled *The Serpent at Home*. Its purpose, perhaps unfamiliar to the secular reader, is to demonstrate how the Adversary lurks within the most innocuous acts of daily existence. The chapters particularly suited for female contemplation include "Satan in the Hairbrush," "Satan Behind the Looking-Glass," "Satan Under the Tea-Table," and "Satan Out of the Window," among others.

"Attend closely, dear aunt, to this precious volume," I instructed, handing it to her open at a passage brimming with fiery eloquence concerning "Satan Among the Sofa Cushions." "And you shall receive everything I request."

Poor Lady Verinder, reclining thoughtlessly upon her own cushions, glanced at the book and returned it to me, appearing more bewildered than before. "I fear, Drusilla," she replied, "that I must await my recovery before attempting such reading. The physician—"

At the mention of the doctor's name, I recognized the obstacle. Time and again, in my past endeavors among the dying, the members of that notoriously irreligious profession of medicine have interposed themselves between me and my divine mandate, falsely claiming that patients require silence and that the greatest disturbance to their peace comes from Miss Clack and her writings. This same blinded materialism, operating treacherously behind my back, now sought to deprive me of the sole property right my poverty could claim: my spiritual inheritance in my perishing aunt.

"The doctor informs me," my misguided relative continued, "that I am not well today. He forbids me to see strangers and commands that, should I read at all, I choose only the lightest and most amusing volumes. 'Do nothing, Lady Verinder,' he said, 'to weary your head or quicken your pulse.'"

There was no choice but to yield temporarily. An open declaration of the infinite superiority of my ministry over that of the medical man would only provoke the doctor to exploit the human frailty of his patient and threaten to abandon the case. Fortunately, there are myriad methods of sowing the good seed, and few are more skilled in their application than myself.

"You might feel stronger in an hour or two," I suggested gently. "Or perhaps you will awaken tomorrow with a sense of something lacking, and even this modest volume might supply it. May I leave the book, aunt? The doctor could hardly object!"

I slipped it beneath the sofa cushions, positioned half-in and half-out, adjacent to her handkerchief and smelling-bottle. Each time her hand reached for either item, it would brush against the book; sooner or later—who knows?—the book might touch her. Having secured this arrangement, I deemed it prudent to withdraw. "Allow me to leave you to rest, dear aunt; I shall return tomorrow."

As I spoke, I glanced toward the window. It was filled with flowers in boxes and pots. Lady Verinder held these perishable treasures in high esteem and frequently rose to admire and scent them. A new inspiration struck me. "Oh! Might I take a flower?" I asked, moving to the window unsuspected.

Instead of removing a bloom, I introduced another book from my bag, placing it among the geraniums and roses to surprise my aunt. The thought followed immediately: *Why not replicate this in every room she enters?* I bid farewell promptly, crossed the hall, and slipped into the library. Samuel, ascending to escort me out and assuming I had already departed, descended again. Upon the library table, I spotted two of the "amusing books" recommended by the infidel doctor. Instantly, I concealed them from view using two of my own sacred publications.

In the breakfast-room, I found my aunt's favorite canary singing in its cage. She invariably fed the bird herself. Some groundsel was strewn on a table directly beneath the perch. I deposited a book among the weeds. In the drawing-room, further opportunities presented themselves. My aunt's beloved musical scores lay upon the piano; I inserted two more books among the sheets. Another was placed in the back drawing-room, hidden beneath unfinished embroidery known to be Lady Verinder's own work.

A third small chamber opened from the back drawing-room, separated by curtains rather than doors. My aunt's plain, old-fashioned fan rested on the mantelpiece. I opened my ninth book to a particularly significant passage and tucked the fan inside as a bookmark to preserve the page. Then arose the question: Should I ascend further, risking potential insult if the occupant of the upper rooms—the lady with the cap-ribbons—were to discover me? But what of that? A poor Christian fears no insult. I ascended the stairs, prepared to endure anything.

The house was silent and solitary; it was likely the servants' tea-hour. My aunt's room lay ahead. A miniature of my late uncle, Sir John, hung on the wall opposite the bed, seeming to smile benevolently, as if whispering, *"Drusilla! Deposit a book."* Tables flanked the bed. Whether she sought light or sustenance, a sacred publication awaited her gaze or grasp, silently declaring, *"Come, try me! Try me!"*

One book remained in my bag, and one apartment remained unvisited: the bathroom, accessible from the bedroom. Peering inside, the holy inner voice that never errs whispered, *"You have encountered Her everywhere else; meet Her at the bath, and the work is complete."* Observing a dressing-gown draped over a chair, I noted its pocket. Into that pocket I placed my final book.

Can words convey the exquisite satisfaction of duty fulfilled when I slipped out of the house, unnoticed by anyone, emerging into the street with my empty bag? Oh, my worldly friends, chasing the phantom of Pleasure through the sinful labyrinths of Dissipation, how easily happiness is attained if one chooses only to be good! Folding my things that night, reflecting on the true riches I had scattered lavishly from the roof to the foundation of my wealthy aunt's home, I declared myself as free from anxiety as a child. So light-hearted was I that I sang a verse of the Evening Hymn, falling asleep before completing a second. Like a child again! Like a child again! Thus I passed that blissful night.

Resolving to restrict my future narration strictly to my own direct observations, I proceed to relate that a full month elapsed between my aunt's demise and the day Rachel Verinder and I encountered one another again. Once this final, painful episode of our family history is revealed, my task shall be complete; for I shall have recounted everything I witnessed as an unwilling observer of these tragic events.

Lady Verinder's remains were transported from London and interred in the small cemetery adjacent to the church within her own estate grounds. I received an invitation to the funeral alongside the rest of the family. However, given my religious convictions, it was impossible for me to summon the strength to overcome the profound shock of her death within such a short timeframe. Furthermore, I was informed that the rector of Frizinghall was to deliver the eulogy. Recalling that in years past I had observed this particular ecclesiastical castaway playing whist with Lady Verinder, I doubted—even had I been physically capable of traveling—that I would feel morally justified in attending the ceremony.

Following Lady Verinder's death, her daughter fell under the guardianship of her brother-in-law, Mr. Ablewhite the elder. Appointed as guardian by the will until his niece either married or reached majority, Mr. Godfrey presumably apprised his father of this new familial dynamic. Consequently, within ten days of my aunt's passing, the previously concealed nature of the engagement became public knowledge within the family circle. The pressing dilemma for Mr. Ablewhite Senior—another confirmed castaway—became how to render himself and his authority as palatable as possible to the wealthy young lady destined to wed his son.

Initially, Rachel presented difficulties regarding her place of residence. The house in Montagu Square was tainted by the memory of her mother's death, while the Yorkshire estate was forever linked to the scandalous disappearance of the Moonstone. Her guardian's home at Frizinghall offered no such objections, yet Rachel's presence there following her recent bereavement acted as a dampener on the gaiety of her cousins, the Misses Ablewhite. Moreover, she herself requested that her visit be postponed to a more favorable season.

Ultimately, Old Mr. Ablewhite proposed renting a furnished house in Brighton. His wife, an invalid daughter, and Rachel were to inhabit it together, with the expectation that he would join them later in the season. **II. The Statement of the Captain of the *Bewley Castle* (1849)**

At the request of Sergeant Cuff, I commit to writing the following account concerning three passengers, believed to be Hindus, who traveled under my command last summer on the *Bewley Castle* en route to Bombay. These men boarded at Plymouth. Throughout the outward voyage, I received no complaints regarding their behavior; they were accommodated in the forward section of the ship. Personally, I observed them rarely.

Towards the end of the journey, we encountered severe bad luck, remaining becalmed off the Indian coast for three full days and nights. Lacking the ship's logbook to verify exact coordinates, I can only recall generally that the currents drifted us closer to land. Once the wind returned, we reached port within twenty-four hours.

As any sailor knows, prolonged calms inevitably relax shipboard discipline. This occurred on my vessel. Several passenger gentlemen lowered the smaller boats and amused themselves rowing and swimming whenever the evening air cooled sufficiently. Protocol dictated these boats be hoisted back into their davits afterward, yet they were left moored alongside. Amidst the oppressive heat and the frustration of the stillness, neither officers nor crew seemed inclined toward their duties.

On the third night, the watch reported nothing unusual. However, when dawn broke, the smallest boat was missing, and shortly thereafter, reports surfaced that the three Hindu passengers were also absent. Had they stolen the boat just after dark—as I am convinced they did—we were too close to shore for a pursuit to be viable once the loss was discovered in the morning. I am certain they landed during the calm before daylight, accounting for potential exhaustion and clumsy navigation.

Upon arriving at port, I learned for the first time why these passengers sought to escape. I conveyed only what I have stated here to the local authorities. They deemed me culpable for permitting the relaxation of discipline. I have expressed my regrets to both the officials and the shipowners. Kattiawar is a rugged territory; even the farmers till the soil armed to the teeth. The populace remains fanatically devoted to ancient Hinduism, worshipping Brahma and Vishnu. The sparse Muslim families scattered in the interior dare not eat meat. Should a Muslim be suspected of harming the sacred cow, he is summarily executed by the surrounding devout neighbors. To bolster this fervor, two of Hinduism's most renowned pilgrimage sites lie within Kattiawar's borders: Dwarka, the birthplace of Krishna, and the holy city of Somnath, sacked and destroyed in the eleventh century by the conqueror Mahmud of Ghazni.

Finding myself in these romantic lands for the second time, I resolved not to depart Kattiawar without witnessing once more the magnificent desolation of Somnath. Traveling on foot toward the sacred city, I calculated I was roughly three days' journey away. Soon, I noticed others traveling in my direction, appearing singly and in small groups. To those who addressed me, I claimed to be a Hindu-Buddhist from a distant province on pilgrimage. Dressed appropriately, speaking the language fluently, and possessing the lean, tanned complexion of a local, I easily blended in—not as a native, but as a traveler from afar.

By the second day, dozens and then hundreds of Hindus were moving in my direction. By the third day, the throng swelled to thousands, all converging slowly toward Somnath. A minor favor I rendered to a fellow pilgrim on the third day introduced me to members of the higher castes. Through them, I learned the multitude was heading to a grand religious ceremony on a nearby hill, scheduled for that very night, honoring the God of the Moon.

As we approached the site, the crowd detained us. By the time we reached the hill, the moon hung high. My Hindu guides possessed special privileges granting access to the shrine, and they graciously permitted me to join them. Upon arrival, the shrine was concealed behind a curtain strung between two majestic trees. Beneath these trees, a flat rock formation projected outward, creating a natural platform. Standing below with my guides, I gazed upward.

Looking back down the slope, I beheld the most spectacular union of nature and humanity I have ever witnessed. The lower hills faded imperceptibly into a grassy plain where three rivers converged. On one side, the graceful curves of the water snaked away, alternating between visibility and concealment beneath the trees until the horizon. On the other, the ocean lay motionless in the night's calm.

Imagine this serene landscape populated by tens of thousands of humans, all clad in white, cascading down the hillside, spilling onto the plain, and lining the riverbanks. Illuminate this tableau with the wild red glow of cressets and torches flickering intermittently throughout the vast crowd. Envision the unclouded eastern moonlight pouring its glory over everything. Such was the vision that greeted me from the hilltop.

A mournful melody played on strings and flutes drew my attention to the hidden shrine. Turning, I saw three figures on the rocky platform. In the center stood the man I had spoken with in England when the "Indians" appeared on the terrace at Lady Verinder's house. His two companions from that occasion were undoubtedly present here as well.

One spectator near me noticed my startle. Whispering, he explained the significance of the three figures. They were Brahmins who had forfeited their caste in service to the god. The deity had decreed their purification must be achieved through pilgrimage. That night, the three men were to part ways forever. Setting out in three distinct directions as pilgrims to various shrines across India, they were never to see each other's faces again, nor rest from their wanderings from the day of their separation until the day of their death.

As these words were whispered, the mournful music ceased. The three men prostrated themselves on the rock before the curtained shrine. Rising, they looked upon one another, embraced, and then descended separately into the crowd. The people parted for them in absolute silence. In three different directions, the masses split simultaneously. Slowly, the great white sea of humanity closed back together, erasing the tracks of the doomed men. We saw them no more.

Then, a new strain of music, loud and jubilant, erupted from the hidden shrine. The crowd around me shuddered and pressed tighter. The curtain between the trees was drawn aside, revealing the shrine. There, seated high on a throne atop his symbolic antelope, with four arms stretching toward the corners of the earth, loomed the God of the Moon, dark and awe-inspiring in the mystical heavenly light. And there, in the forehead of the deity, gleamed the yellow diamond whose brilliance had last illuminated me in England, emerging from the bosom of a woman's dress!

Yes! After eight centuries, the Moonstone shines forth once more over the walls of the sacred city where its tale began.
