I stand before the page, trembling not from fear but from wonder. The sun blazes above, gilding the sky like molten gold; beneath it, the sea stretches in endless blue, calm and ancient. This world—this familiar world of trees, birds, breath, and memory—is all I have ever known. Yet now I know it is only a veil, thin as mist, hiding truths far older and stranger than any myth. At any moment, that veil might lift—and what lies beyond could shatter everything I believe.

It was shortly after my father’s death that I found myself sitting alone in the library. The air was still, thick with dust and silence. My gaze drifted to the portrait hanging among the books—a stern-faced man in dark clothing, whom I had always assumed to be some distant ancestor. I wished I knew more about him. Then, distracted, I reached for a book on the shelf, opened it, and began reading.

When I looked up again, he was there—not walking toward me from the door, but from between me and the portrait.

A thin man, pale as ash, dressed in a black coat worn threadbare at the edges. His face was sharp, his eyes bright with intelligence, and his nose—long, curved, and pointed—reminded me instantly of the jug my sisters used to call Mr. Crow.

“Finding myself near you, Mr. Vane,” he said, voice low and measured, “I took the liberty of calling.”

I blinked. “You’re…?”

“Raven,” he replied, smiling faintly. “Though once upon a time, they called me by another name. But you nearly guessed it—your family has a gift for intuition.”

I frowned. “I don’t recall seeing you before.”

“Oh, but you have,” he said. “Once. When you were a child. In this very room.”

I couldn’t place him. Not clearly. But something in his presence stirred a half-remembered dream.

“You’re saying you were here?” I asked.

“I was,” he said. “And I’ve been here many times since. Though your grandfather—your father’s father—did not care for my company. He never met me. But your great-grandfather? Sir Up’ard, they called him—though no one knows why—was my closest friend.”

My breath caught. “You knew him?”

“I did,” Raven said softly. “And he taught me things no one else could have. Things that changed how I saw the world—and worlds beyond it.”

He paused, then continued: “That closet there”—he gestured toward a small, locked chamber near the back—“held his private collection. A hundred manuscripts, untouched by print. One morning, I sat inside, cataloguing them, when he appeared in the doorway. ‘Come,’ he said. I laid down my pen and followed him—across the hall, down a steep stair, through a passage beneath the house, until we reached a tower he had built in secret. It stood high, hidden, with a single room at the top. The door had a lock so massive it seemed made for giants—but he opened it with a key no bigger than a needle.”

I leaned forward. “What happened?”

“He stepped inside,” Raven said. “And then—before my eyes—he began to shrink. Not just in size, but in presence. Like smoke drawn into wind. Within moments, he was no longer a man, but a speck—then nothing. And beyond him, I saw mountains—blue, clear against a sky of deeper blue. I recognized the land. I had been there before. But never by such a path.”

He looked at me then, his eyes piercing. “Years later, I passed on what he taught me to one of his descendants. And even now, when I wish to return home, I use your house. I must confess—I’ve done so without your permission. But I’ve earned a right of passage, if you will allow it.”

I stared. “So you go through my house… to another world?”

He smiled. “Not *through* it. Into it. That is the difference. Space is not a wall—it is a door.”

I shook my head. “This is madness.”

“Is it?” he said gently. “Or is it simply truth you have not yet learned to see?”

I hesitated. “Then tell me—how do you pass?”

“There is a door,” he said. “One step through it, and you are elsewhere. Not across distance, but across reality.”

“Another world?” I whispered.

“Yes,” he said. “A world where laws differ. Where light behaves differently. Where thought shapes matter in ways we cannot yet imagine. Morals? They remain constant. But physics? Mind? Perception? All change.”

I laughed, though it was not meant to mock. “You speak as if you’ve seen it.”

“I have,” he said. “And I can show you.”

He rose, walked calmly across the room, and ascended the stairs to the garret. I followed, watching his back—the long, dark hair, the wide coat that brushed the floor, the shoes too large for his feet.

The garret was dim, lit only by slivers of sunlight filtering through cracks in the roof. Some parts had no flooring; we stepped carefully from beam to beam. In the center stood a partition, with a door.

Beyond it, a small chamber sloped upward, its ceiling slanting into the roof. And there, on the floor, leaning against the wall, stood a mirror.

Old. Dusty. Its surface dull, save for the eagle carved at the top—wings outstretched, eyes fixed forward, as if watching.

“That is the door,” Raven said.

I approached slowly. The reflection showed us both—me, hesitant, and him, calm as stone. But something felt wrong. The mirror didn’t feel like glass. It felt like a threshold.

“It’s old,” I said. “Useless.”

“Not useless,” he replied. “Only waiting. Its clarity depends not on polish, but on light.”

“But there’s no light here.”

He turned, pulled a chain on the opposite wall. With a creak, the ceiling began to rotate. Slowly, steadily. He checked his watch, then tugged again.

“We arrive almost at noon,” he murmured. “The moment is near.”

After a few minutes, he pulled two more chains—left, right, left again. Then silence.

Suddenly, a patch of sunlight fell onto another mirror on the opposite wall. The rays reflected, crossed the chamber, and struck the dusty surface of the first mirror.

But no light returned.

No glow. No echo.

Just darkness.

“The light,” I said, “where did it go?”

Raven smiled. “Back, perhaps, to where it came from. Or into a realm we have not yet learned to perceive. Light is not just sight—it is sense. And we have senses yet unborn.”

He spoke then of dimensions—more than three, not just length, width, and depth, but others woven into the fabric of existence. Powers within us, dormant, unknown. He spoke of mind and matter as threads in a tapestry, interlaced but not bound. Of doors not made of wood or iron, but of thought, of vision, of memory.

I listened, but understood little. My mind rebelled. This was fantasy. Madness.

Yet as he spoke, I noticed the mirror—once clear, now clouded. A white mist filled its surface.

Then, slowly, the mist thinned.

And beyond it—mountains. Blue. Jagged. Real.

A vast heath stretched beneath them. And on it—a figure, moving fast, vanishing into the distance.

I turned to Raven.

He was gone.

I whirled back to the mirror.

There, reflected in its depths, was a man—his coat flapping, his hair lifted by a wind I could not feel.

It was Raven.

Running away.

I stumbled backward, heart pounding, hands shaking.

The mirror was empty now.

The chamber silent.

The door closed.

And I stood alone—on the edge of a world I had not known existed.

But the question remained:

Was it real?

Or had I, at last, crossed into a dream too vast to wake from?
