Christmas Eve in Cloisterham. The streets held a peculiar mixture of strangers and returnees, those whose childhood memories of the town had faded into the vague recollections of adults visiting a place they knew only by reputation. To these visitors, the cathedral bell and the cawing of the rooks sounded like the distant, comforting voices of a nursery long forgotten. To others, standing in the shadows where the city seemed to shrink under the weight of time, the rustling leaves and the scent of the Close stirred dreams of a final, peaceful reunion between the beginning and the end of life.

The festive tokens were abundant. Red berries glinted from the lattices of Minor Canon Corner, and Mr. and Mrs. Tope diligently pressed sprigs of holly into the carved stalls of the Cathedral, treating them with the reverence due to the coats of the Dean and Chapter. The shops overflowed with lavish provisions: currants, raisins, spices, candied peel, and moist sugar hung in profusion. An air of gallantry and dissipation permeated the air, marked by a massive bunch of mistletoe hanging above the greengrocer's door and a pitiful Twelfth Night cake, crowned with a Harlequin, offered for a shilling a ticket. Public entertainments awaited: the waxworks, a marvel to the reflective mind of the Emperor of China, were open for viewing, and a new comic pantomime promised at the theatre, heralded by the portrait of Signor Jacksonini, the clown, uttering "How do you do tomorrow?" with a visage both large and miserable. Cloisterham was alive with activity, save for the High School, whose scholars had departed in love with Miss Twinkleton's pupils, and Miss Twinkleton's school itself, where the handmaidens fluttered in the windows, becoming notably skittish when entrusted with the physical representation of their own sex.

Tonight, three figures were destined to converge at the gatehouse. The narrative of each man's day follows.

Neville Landless, freed from his academic duties by Mr. Crisparkle, spent the afternoon in a room of intense concentration. After two hours past noon, he cleared his desk, arranged his books, and methodically tore up every stray paper, leaving only notes essential to his studies. He organized his drawers and turned to his wardrobe, selecting sturdy shoes and socks for travel. He packed these into a new knapsack bought that morning in the High Street, alongside a heavy walking-stick with an iron-shod tip. He tested the stick, swinging it and weighing it in his hand, before laying it beside the bag on a window seat.

Dressing for the journey, Neville paused on the staircase just as Mr. Crisparkle emerged from his bedroom. Seeing Neville retrieve the stick, the Minor Canon smiled and asked how he chose it.

"Really, I do not understand the subject," Neville replied. "I chose it for its weight."

"It is *too* heavy, Neville. *Much* too heavy," Mr. Crisparkle insisted, adopting a pedantic posture. "You do not lean upon it; you merely balance with it."

"To rest upon in a long walk, sir?" Neville asked.

"Rest upon?" Mr. Crisparkle repeated, demonstrating a casual stride. "You balance!"

"I shall learn with practice, sir. I have not lived in a walking country."

"A truth," said Mr. Crisparkle. "Train yourself, and we shall cover dozens of miles together. Do you return before dinner?"

"I doubt it, as we dine early."

With a bright nod expressing absolute confidence, Mr. Crisparkle bid him farewell. Neville proceeded to the Nuns' House, requesting that Miss Landless be told of his arrival by appointment. He waited at the gate, refusing to cross the threshold, adhering to his parole to avoid obstructing Rosa's path.

His sister Helena arrived promptly, mindful of their mutual obligations. They met with affection, avoiding lingering, and walked toward the inland countryside.

"I am not treading on forbidden ground, Helena," Neville said as they turned. "Yet I cannot avoid mentioning my infatuation."

"Had you not better avoid it, Neville? You know I hear nothing."

"You hear what Mr. Crisparkle hears, and approves," Neville countered. "Yes, I hear it. The issue is this: I am unsettled and unhappy, and I fear I am unsettling others. Without me, perhaps you and the others, barring our guardian, would be dining cheerfully in Minor Canon Corner tomorrow. I am clearly not high in the old lady's estimation, and I am an irksome burden on her hospitality, especially now, when I must be kept apart from that person, whose reputation precedes me. I have explained this gently to Mr. Crisparkle, noting my internal struggle and the need for change and absence to overcome it."

"When do you return?" Helena asked.

"In a fortnight."

"And alone?"

"I am far better without company, even if you were there."

"Mr. Crisparkle agrees?"

"Entirely. At first, he thought it a moody scheme, harmful to a brooding mind. But last Monday, we took a moonlight walk to discuss it. I showed him I sought to conquer myself, and that being away tonight would serve me better than staying. Meeting people walking together here would do no good; it is not the way to forget. In a fortnight, chances will have passed. Furthermore, I feel hopeful of bracing exercise and wholesome fatigue. Mr. Crisparkle values health highly, and his justice suggests he applies natural laws equally to us. Convinced of my earnestness, he grants his full consent."

Helena considered the plan. Though Mr. Crisparkle's approval swayed her, she ultimately endorsed it as a healthy project denoting sincere self-correction. She pitied him slightly for traveling alone on Christmas, yet felt compelled to encourage him.

"Will you write?" she asked.

"Yes, every alternate day, detailing all adventures."

"Do you send clothes ahead?"

"My dear Helena, no. I shall travel like a pilgrim, with wallet and staff."

He handed her the knapsack. She noted its heaviness and asked what wood it was. "Iron-wood," he replied.

Until this point, Neville had been cheerful, perhaps buoyed by presenting his resolve so brightly. But as twilight fell and city lights ignited against the encroaching gloom, his spirits sank.

"I wish I were not going to this dinner, Helena," he murmured.

"Dear Neville, is it worth caring so much? Think how soon it will pass."

"How soon it will pass!" he repeated, gloomily. "Yes. Yet I dislike it."

A moment of awkwardness passed, but Helena reassured him of his certainty.

"I wish I felt as sure of everything else as I do of myself," he answered.

"How strangely you speak, dear! What do you mean?"

"Helena, I do not know. I simply dislike it. There is a strange, dead weight in the air."

She pointed to the copper-hued clouds beyond the river, noting the rising wind. He spoke little more until they reached the gate of the Nuns' House. He hesitated twice before entering, reluctant to cross the threshold. Finally, as the Cathedral clock struck a quarter past twelve, he hurried inside.

Thus, he ascended the postern stairs.

Edwin Drood passed a solitary day. Something profound had slipped from his life, and in the silence of his chamber, he wept for it the previous night. Though the image of Miss Landless lingered in his mind, occupying a stronghold, he harbored doubts regarding his own unworthiness. He wondered what they might have been if he had valued her sooner, if he had accepted his fate with appreciation rather than passive resignation. Despite this sharp heartache, the vanity and caprice of youth sustained her handsome figure in his mind.

Rosa's look at their parting haunted him. Had she seen beneath the surface of his thoughts, into their twilight depths? Or was it merely a look of astonished, keen inquiry? He decided he could not decipher it, though it remained remarkably expressive.

Waiting only for Mr. Grewgious before departing, Edwin took a saunter through the ancient city. He recalled the days when Rosa and he, mere children, walked with the dignity of engagement. "Poor children," he thought with pitying sadness.

Finding his watch had stopped, he entered a jeweler's shop to have it wound. The jeweler, knowledgeable about jewelry, suggested a bracelet suitable for a young bride, particularly one of a diminutive style. When Edwin regarded it coolly, the jeweler shifted to a tray of rings, recommending a chaste signet ring often chosen by gentlemen changing their station, engraved with their wedding date.

"The rings are as coldly viewed as the bracelet," Edwin remarked, telling the tempter he wore no jewelry save his father's watch, chain, and shirt-pin.

"That I was aware of," the jeweller replied, recalling that Mr. Jasper had visited recently to inspect a watch-glass. "I showed these items to him, remarking that if he wished to make a present to a gentleman relative... But he smiled, saying he had an inventory in his mind of all the jewelry his gentleman relative ever wore: his watch, chain, and shirt-pin."
