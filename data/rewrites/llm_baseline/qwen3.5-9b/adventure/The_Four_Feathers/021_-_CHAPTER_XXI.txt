Mrs. Adair watched the horizon of her own life with growing unease, speculating on the fallout of the confidences she had poured into Durrance. She hovered in uncertainty regarding his reaction. Would he finally lay bare the truth to Ethne? Would he confess that he had seen through the illusion of her affection for him, revealing instead the stark reality of her devotion to Feversham? If he chose this path, however gently he attempted to shield her from the full weight of his discovery, he would surely fail. Ethne would be forced to confront him, and Mrs. Adair dreaded the inevitable moment when her companion's direct gaze would meet hers, demanding answers she could not provide.

Consequently, it was a temporary relief to Mrs. Adair that the surface of things remained undisturbed between Ethne and Durrance. They met and conversed as if the day Willoughby arrived at the garden, and the evening Ethne performed the *Musoline Overture*, had been erased from memory. Yet, as the initial shock of potential danger faded, Mrs. Adair found herself increasingly puzzled, then angry, and finally disappointed.

Durrance, however, had already resolved his course of action. Knowing that Ethne wished him ignorant of the truth, he found solace in the belief that he had brought happiness to the one man who was truly her friend. Durrance, perceptive as ever, recognized this sentiment and decided not to dismantle it—for the present. He needed absolute certainty regarding the nature of the rift between Ethne and Feversham, as well as concrete proof of Harry's fate, before taking decisive steps. Thus, he adopted a facade of ignorance. He abandoned his usual scrutiny, feigning contentment, making light of his own misfortunes, and claiming that Ethne's company was a sufficient compensation.

"You see," he told her, "one learns to accept blindness as a natural state. But one never gets used to you, Ethne. With every encounter, I discover something new and delightful. Besides, there is always the slim possibility of a cure."

His strategy yielded results. Ethne, sensing that his suspicions had been laid aside, countered his relentless cheerfulness with her own profound sorrow. Her grief was immense. Though she had briefly regained the lightness of heart she possessed before the three white feathers arrived at Ramelton, she now felt the heavy burden of the despair that followed them. There was a distinction, however. Her pride had been restored, and Durrance's words had sparked a faint hope that Harry might yet be saved. Still, she knew the toll of sleepless nights and the dull, burning agony of waiting for dawn. She could no longer convince herself that Harry Feversham was merely a lost friend; he was alive, trapped in unknown straits, and she thirsted desperately to know his condition.

Occasionally, her patience frayed. "I suppose escape is possible from Omdurman," she remarked one day, forcing her voice into a tone of detached indifference.

"Possibly? Yes, I believe so," Durrance replied optimistically. "Naturally, it is difficult and requires time. Attempts have been made to extract Colonel Trench and others, but they haven't succeeded yet. The obstacle lies in the intermediary."

Ethne glanced sharply at him. "The intermediary?" she asked, then paused, realizing the implication. "I think I begin to understand," she said, pulling herself upright abruptly. "You mean the Arab who can move freely between Omdurman and the Egyptian frontier?"

"Yes," Durrance confirmed. "Usually a Dervish pedlar or merchant trading with Sudanese tribes. He slips into Wadi Halfa, Assouan, or Suakin to conduct the exchange. Naturally, the risk is immense. If caught in Omdurman, he faces severe punishment. Hence, many shy away from the danger at the last moment. Furthermore, they are often rogues. You arrange the deal in Egypt, handing over the funds. Six months or a year later, he returns alone with excuses. Perhaps it was summer, and conditions were unfavorable. Perhaps the guards tightened. Perhaps he was suspected. Or perhaps he demands more money. His story may be true, you pay him more, and he returns empty-handed again."

Ethne nodded slowly. "Exactly."

In doing so, Durrance had inadvertently clarified a mystery that had plagued her. She was certain Harry Feversham intended to aid Colonel Trench, but she couldn't fathom how his own capture served that purpose. Now she understood: he planned to become his own intermediary. This revelation strengthened her hopes, suggesting that Harry had acted with meticulous forethought. He would be eager for the second feather to reach her, and if he successfully extracted Trench, he would not stay behind.

They sat on the terrace as the sunset painted the creek waters red. "Life would not be easy in the prison of Omdurman," Ethne observed, striving to maintain her composure.

"Easier?" Durrance exclaimed. "No, it wouldn't. A hovel crammed with Arabs, devoid of light or air, with a ceiling perhaps two feet above your head, locked from dusk until dawn. Likely, the prisoners would stand all night in that filthy den, packed so tightly together. Imagine it, even here in England on a night like this! Think of what it would be on an August night in the Sudan! Especially if you held memories of places like this, turning the confinement into a torment."

Ethne gazed across the cool garden. At this very moment, Harry Feversham might be gasping for breath in that dark, stinking cell, parched and feverish, with visions of Ramelton's grassy slopes and the liquid music of the Lennon River playing in his mind.

"One would pray for death," Ethne murmured slowly, "unless..." She hesitated, cutting off the thought that followed: *"unless one went there deliberately with a fixed purpose."*

Durrance finished the sentence for her. "Unless there was a chance of escape," he said. "And there is a chance—if Feversham is in Omdurman."

Fearing he had revealed too much about the horrors of the prison, he hastily added, "Of course, what I've described is mere hearsay, unverified. Prisoners' experiences vary; they may not fare as badly as we imagine." Then, letting the subject drop, he changed the topic. Neither Ethne pursued it further.

She occasionally wondered how Durrance had interpreted her sudden disappearance from the drawing room the night he revealed his meeting with Feversham. He never addressed it, and she wisely mirrored his silence. The marked shift in his demeanor—the lack of the caution that had previously troubled her—soothed her fears. It appeared he had concocted a simple, natural explanation for himself. She also questioned why he had informed her of the meeting in Wadi Halfa and Feversham's journey south. Again, she found a satisfying answer: she believed the news was a message delivered solely for her understanding, conveyed by Durrance as an instrument of a higher power. She never sought to uncover his true motives.

Throughout September, they maintained the pretense. Every morning, whenever Durrance traveled to Devonshire, he crossed the fields to meet Ethne at The Pool. Mrs. Adair, observing them laughing and talking without a trace of awkwardness or distance, grew angrier and struggled harder to suppress her anger and allow the charade to continue. It was a month of intense strain for all three parties, and everyone breathed a sigh of relief when Durrance announced his frequent trips to London to consult his oculist. These visits multiplied in frequency and duration. Even Ethne was grateful; she could momentarily remove her mask, tire herself out, and find solitude to gather the strength needed to resume her cheerful persona when Durrance returned.

Despair sometimes seized her completely. "Will I be able to sustain this pretense when we are married, when we are constantly together?" she asked herself. But she pushed the question away, refusing to look ahead lest her reserves collapse.

After the third trip, Durrance addressed her: "Do you recall that I once mentioned a renowned oculist in Wiesbaden? It seems prudent for me to visit him."

"You are being recommended to go?"

"Yes, and I must go alone."

Ethne looked up, her expression sharp and quick-witted. "You think I would be dull company in Wiesbaden?"

"There is no cause for concern. I can find a relative to accompany me."

"No," Durrance corrected firmly. "It is for my own sake. I may need to enter a sanatorium. It is best to remain quiet and avoid visitors for a time."

"You are certain?" Ethne asked.

"It would pain me greatly if I thought you proposed this separation because you believed I would be happier at Glenalla."

"No, that is not the reason," Durrance answered truthfully. He felt compelled to separate them. Like Ethne, he suffered under the tyranny of constant deception. Only because he respected her determination to uphold her vow—that two lives should not be ruined by her actions—could he restrain himself from shouting the truth.

"I return to London next week," he added. "Upon my return, I will be in a position to decide whether to proceed with the Wiesbaden trip."
