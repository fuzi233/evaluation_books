A grave, white-haired seneschal approached their table and inquired with polite formality whether Gerard Eliassoen was among them. When Gerard affirmed it, the man said, “The Princess Marie desires to speak with you, young sir. I am to escort you to her presence.”

At once, every face within earshot turned sharply, drawn by curiosity and envy toward the young man summoned by a princess. Gerard rose at once to comply.

“I wager we shall not see you again,” Margaret said calmly, though a faint blush touched her cheeks.

“That you will,” he replied, then leaned close and whispered, “This is my good princess—but you are my queen.”

Aloud, he added, “Wait for me, I pray you. I shall return shortly.”

“Ay, ay!” Peter muttered, suddenly rousing himself as if from a dream.

Once Gerard had departed, the two who had seemed so plain in dress—yet were now linked to one summoned by royalty—became the center of attention. The eyes of all nearby followed them, and William Johnson stepped forward, feigning surprise. “And to think,” he said, “I stood right behind you, and you did not see me!”

“Nay, cousin Johnson,” Margaret replied coolly, “I saw you long ago.”

“You saw me—and spoke not?” he asked, wounded.

“Cousin,” she replied, “it was your duty to welcome us to Rotterdam, as it is ours to welcome you to Sevenbergen. Your servant refused us a seat in your house.”

“The fool!” Johnson muttered.

“And I wanted to see if ‘like maid, like master’ held true,” Margaret added, “for there is truth in old sayings.”

Johnson flushed deeply. He sensed her sharpness and suspected her intent. In that moment, he chose wisdom over words. He insisted they come home with him at once, and he would show them whether they were truly welcome in Rotterdam or not.

“Who doubts it, cousin? Who doubts it?” the scholar chimed in.

Margaret thanked him graciously but hesitated. “I would like to hear the minstrels again,” she said.

After about a quarter of an hour, Johnson returned to the matter. “See,” he said, “many guests have already left.”

Then came her true reason: “It would be ill-mannered to abandon our friend. He does not know where we are staying in this large city, and we’ve already parted once.”

“Oh!” Johnson exclaimed. “Then let us fix that. My young man—ahem! I mean my secretary—shall remain here and wait. He shall bring Gerard to my house. He shall lodge with me, and nowhere else.”

“We would not wish to be such a burden,” Margaret said.

“Nay, nay,” Johnson insisted. “You shall see whether you are welcome—or not. You, your friends, even your friends’ friends, if need be. And I shall learn what the Princess has to say to him.”

Margaret felt a sudden warmth at the thought of Gerard staying under the same roof as she. But then a flicker of unease crossed her mind. “But what if your young man should be careless, go off playing, and Gerard miss him?”

“He go play?” Johnson laughed. “Leave the spot I set him in? Ho! Stand forth, Hans Cloterman!”

A figure clad in black serge and dark violet hose rose slowly, took two deliberate steps, and stood before them without moving a muscle—solemn, precise, the very image of disciplined propriety.

At the sight of him, Margaret, whose heart was light, could barely suppress a smile. Yet she leaned close to Johnson and whispered, “I would stake my hand in fire on his loyalty. We are at your command, cousin, when you have given him his orders.”

Hans was instructed to remain at the table and await Gerard’s return, then guide him to Ooster-Waagen Straet. He obeyed not with words, but by silently taking the seat indicated.

Margaret, Peter, and William Johnson then departed together.

“And indeed,” Margaret said gently, “it is time you were abed, father, after all your travel.” That had been her intention all along.

Hans Cloterman sat waiting—calm, unflinching, businesslike. Minutes passed, yet no impatience stirred in him. Johnson had done him justice when he mocked the idea of his secretary abandoning post for sport or youthful folly. As Gerard delayed, the patient Hans—now free from his employer’s watchful eye—used the time wisely. He drank wine solemnly, silently, at measured intervals: goblets of strong Corsican wine, which he consumed with quiet dignity.

The wine was potent, but so was Cloterman’s head. An hour passed before the model secretary began to feel the notion that Creation expected him to drink a toast—to all good fellows, and especially to the Duke of Burgundy present.

With that thought, he filled the ninth bumper and rose with careful slowness, as if balancing a sacred object. Once upright, he toppled sideways onto the grass, goblet still in hand, spilling wine across several ankles—whose owners jumped back in alarm—yet not a muscle in his long, grave face shifted. Even in total loss of reason, he retained his composure, stiffness, and infallibility.

Meanwhile, the seneschal led Gerard through winding corridors to the pavilion’s door, where young noblemen in embroidered silks and plumed hats sat guard, playing cards by torchlight. A whisper passed between them, and one reluctantly rose, stared at Gerard with haughty surprise, and entered the pavilion.

He soon returned, beckoning Gerard forward. Through a few passages, they arrived in an antechamber where three more young gentlemen sat—feathered, furred, and adorned like elaborate tapestries—deep in the noble art of dice.

“You can’t see the Princess,” one declared. “It is too late.”

Another added, “She passed this way moments ago with her nurse. She’s gone to bed, doll and all. Deuce—ace again!”

Gerard prepared to leave.

The seneschal smiled skeptically. “The young man is here by the Countess’s order. Kindly conduct him to her ladies.”

A magnificent Adonis rose, looking affronted, and led Gerard into a room where eleven ladies sat or lounged, chattering like magpies. Two, more industrious than the rest, played cat’s-cradle with fingers as nimble as their tongues.

At the sight of a stranger, all voices ceased abruptly—like machinery tripped by a single lever—and all eyes turned to Gerard as if guided by the same string. He had already been uneasy; now this sudden scrutiny unnerved him. His gaze dropped to the floor.

The ladies, finding a creature they could intimidate, giggled with delight.

Then a duenna stepped forward and said sternly, “Mesdames!”

Instantly, they fell silent—abashed as if a modesty string had been pulled.

The duenna took Gerard by the arm and marched him forward in silence. His heart sank. Halfway through the hall, he almost turned and fled.

“What must princes be,” he thought, “when their courtiers are so cold? Doubtless they inherit their manner from those they serve.”

His thoughts were interrupted when the duenna led him into a room where three ladies sat sewing, and a small girl tuned a lute.

The ladies wore rich but tasteful garments. The duenna approached the woman hemming a kerchief and whispered a few words.

She turned to Gerard with a gentle smile and gestured for him to approach. She did not rise, but laid aside her work. Her simple motion carried grace and ease.

She began at once. “Margaret Van Eyck is an old friend of mine, sir. I am delighted to receive a letter from her hand, and grateful to you for delivering it safely.”

“Sir, I thank you a thousand times,” said the young lady.

“I am glad you feel indebted to her, sweetheart,” the Countess said. “Our friend asks us to do you a favor in return.”

“I will do anything on earth for him,” the girl replied fervently.

“Anything on earth is nothing in the world,” the Countess murmured quietly.

“Well, then,” the girl said, “what would you have me do, sir?”

Gerard now understood the rank of the company he was in.

“My sovereign demoiselle,” he said softly, voice trembling slightly, “where there is no effort, there needs no reward.”

“But we must obey mamma. All the world must obey.”

“That is true,” the Countess replied. “Then, our demoiselle, reward me—if you will—by letting me hear the song you were about to sing when I interrupted you.”

“What! You love music, sir?” the girl asked.

“I adore it.”

She glanced at her mother, who nodded with a smile.

Then she took up her lute and sang a popular romaunt of the day. Though only twelve, she was well trained and diligent. Her small hand swept the strings with courage and precision, striking arpeggios clear and bright as stars. But the true magic was her voice—not loud, but round, pure, full, and ringing like a bell.

She sang with modest eloquence, unburdened by affectation. Too young for theatrics or sentiment, she poured out emotion naturally—everything flowed. Her little mouth seemed the mouth of Nature itself.

The song, too, was as pure as her voice—no false divisions, no whining slurs, no overwrought cadences or empty flourishes. No trite embellishments to drown melody or meaning. It was honest, simple, and radiant.

As the tender melody poured from her young throat, Gerard’s eyes filled with tears.

The Countess watched him closely. Most praised the princess loudly, but not with the depth of feeling in his gaze. When the song ended and the last note faded, she asked demurely, “Was he content?”

Gerard started. The spoken word broke the spell and brought him back to reality.

“Oh, madam!” he cried, “Surely it is thus that cherubs and seraphs sing—charming the saints in heaven!”

“I am somewhat of your opinion, my young friend,” the Countess said, moved. She gazed at her daughter with love and quiet pride—a look said to be granted only to the brief-lived when beholding the brief-lived.

She continued: “My dear friend has asked me to assist you. It is the first favor she has honored us with—and therefore sacred. You are in holy orders, sir?”

Gerard bowed.

“I fear you are not a priest—you look too young.”

“Oh no, madam,” he replied. “I am not even a sub-deacon. I am merely a lector. Next month I shall become an exorcist, and soon after, an acolyte.”

“Well, Monsieur Gerard,” the Countess said, “with your talents, you may pass quickly through the lower ranks. And I beg you to do so—for the day after you celebrate your first mass, I shall appoint you to a benefice.”

“Oh, madam!” he exclaimed.

“And, Marie, remember—I make this promise in your name as well as my own.”

“Fear not, mamma,” the princess said. “I shall not forget. But if he follows my advice, he will be Bishop of Liege. The Bishop of Liege is a beautiful bishop. Do you not remember him, mamma, when we visited Liege? He was braver than grandpapa himself. He wore a high crown, split down the middle, studded with jewels so brilliant! His gown was stiff with gold, his mantle adorned with pictures—above all, his gloves! They were embroidered with jewels, scented with the sweetest perfume. I smelled them all the time he blessed me. Dear old man—he will surely die soon, as most old people do. Then, sir, you may be bishop—and wear—”

“Gently, Marie, gently,” the Countess interjected. “Bishoprics are for older gentlemen. This is a young man.”

“Mamma! He is not so very young.”

“Not compared to you, Marie, eh?”

“He is of good birth, dear mamma. And I am certain he is good enough for a bishop.”

“Alas, mademoiselle,” Gerard said, “you are mistaken.”

“I know not that, Monsieur Gerard,” the Countess said, “but I am puzzled how you can judge so boldly.”

“Alas, mamma,” the princess said, “you have not looked at his face.”

She raised her eyebrows, as if amused by her mother’s simplicity.

“I beg your pardon,” the Countess said, “I have. Well, sir, if I cannot go quite so fast as my daughter, attribute it to my age, not to lack of interest in your welfare. A benefice will be a good beginning. And I must ensure it is near—what is the name of your place?”

“Tergou, madam.”

“A priest gives up much,” the Countess continued, “often, I fear, he learns too late how much.” Her gaze rested on Gerard with quiet pity—half-surprised that he would surrender his sex, all the joys of love and family, for a life of service. “At least you shall be near your friends. Do you have a mother?”

“Yes, madam, thanks be to God!”

“Good. You shall have a church near Tergou. She will thank me.”

Now, sir, we must not keep you longer from those who claim your company more than we do. Duchess, please send a page to guide him to the banquet hall—the way is difficult to find.”

Gerard bowed low to the Countess and the princess, then backed toward the door.

“I hope it will be a nice benefice,” the princess said with a pretty smile as he left. Then, shaking her head with solemn warning, “But you had better have been Bishop of Liege.”

Gerard followed his new guide, heart warm with gratitude. But before reaching the hall, a chill settled over him.

A quiet soul unaccustomed to extremes finds it hard to hold two emotions at once. While with the Countess, joy had overwhelmed him—the unexpected promise, the honor it would bring to his family. But now, passion reasserted itself.

What! Give up Margaret? Her soft hand still felt in his, her deep eyes burned in his heart. Resign her, and all the world of love she had opened to him today?

The reversal was so powerful he resolved instantly to say nothing at home about the benefice.

“The Countess is kind,” he thought. “She has many ways to help a young man. She will not force me to become a priest when she learns I love a woman. One might almost think she knows it—she looked at me strangely and said, ‘A priest gives up much, too much.’ Perhaps she will give me a position at court.”

With that comforting thought, his spirits lifted. Now at the banqueting hall’s entrance, he thanked his guide and hurried toward Margaret—eyes alight with joy.

He reached the table—she was gone. Peter was gone too. No one remained. Only a soberly dressed citizen lay unconscious beneath the table, being lifted away by others.

Gerard did not recognize the importance of this drunken man. He was searching for “Beauty” and ignored the “Beast.”

He ran wildly around the hall, now nearly empty. She was not there.

He left the palace. Outside, a crowd gathered before two newly lit fan-lights over the gate.

He asked urgently if anyone had seen an old man in a gown and a lovely girl pass by.

They laughed. “We were staring at these new lights—turning night into day. We didn’t care about old men and young wenches. Common sights.”

From another group, he learned a Mystery play was being performed nearby, drawing all of Rotterdam.

Hope flared. He went to see it.

The play featured divine beings—too sacred to name—descending clumsily from heaven to debate with cardinal virtues, the nine Muses, and the seven deadly sins—all portrayed in human form, indistinguishable from one another.

To enliven the scene, the Prince of the Power of the Air appeared, tormented by a mischievous imp who struck him with a bladder. Each thwack sent the crowd into laughter.

When the Vices had delivered their obscenities and the Virtues their platitudes, the celestial figures retreated one by one to heaven—on a single cloud, operated by two artisans with a winch, visible to all.

Then the bottomless pit opened, blazing in the center. The carpenters and Virtues pushed the Vices inside, and the Virtues, Beelzebub, and the imp danced merrily around the infernal flames to the tune of fife and tabor.

This play, written by the Bishop of Ghent to spread religious feeling through sensation, was typical of clerical theater in its time. But eventually, the laity took over—and the stage, I’m told, became profane.

Margaret was nowhere in the crowd. Gerard could not enjoy the performance. He left mid-act, during a celebrated exchange where Justice out-quibbled Satan.

He wandered through the streets, unable to find her. At last, exhausted, he found a hostelry and slept until dawn.

All day, heavy and sorrowful, he searched—never finding her or her father, never gaining a clue.

He began to believe she had changed her mind—or worse, deceived him.

Anguish turned to irritation. More fortune fell upon him—and he almost resented it.

On the third day, after retracing every street once more, he said aloud, “She is not in the town. I shall never see her again. I will go home.”

He started for Tergou—royal favor promised, fifteen golden angels in his purse, a golden medal on his chest, and a heart as heavy as lead.
