John Jasper, going home through the Close, was pulled up short by a picture that seemed at once too common for wonder and too monstrous for indifference: Stony Durdles, dinner-bundle tucked under his arm as if it were part of his ribcage, leaned his back against the iron railing of the burial-ground; and in the clear moonlight a small ragged boy—sharp as a thorn and as ugly—stood off and flung stones at him as at a chalked target.

Some stones struck—thudding on coat or shoulder with a sound duller than it ought to have been, as if Durdles were half wall himself. Some missed—spatting on paving, ticking off iron, and skittering away into the Close’s hush where every little ricochet spoke too loudly. Durdles seemed equally unmoved by hit or miss. The boy, on the other hand, when he hit, put his mouth to a jagged gap where teeth were wanting and blew a whistle of triumph that split the night like a butcher’s blade on bone; and when he missed, he yelped, “Mulled agin!” and took a more vicious, more careful aim.

Jasper stepped out of shadow into moonlight, his own tread softened by the Close’s ancient stones and the great Cathedral’s patient listening.

“What are you doing to the man?” he demanded.

“Making a cock-shy of him,” returned the boy, with the readiness of a creature that had never been taught to fear words.

“Give me those stones in your hand.”

“Yes, I’ll give ’em you down your throat, if you come a-ketching hold of me,” the boy answered, shaking loose and backing, his boots a ruin of rags and broken leather that slapped the ground. “I’ll smash your eye, if you don’t look out!”

“Baby-Devil that you are,” said Jasper, with disgust more controlled than his anger, “what has the man done to you?”

“He won’t go home.”

“What is that to you?”

“He gives me a ’apenny to pelt him home if I ketches him out too late,” said the boy; and then, half stumbling, half dancing—like some wild refrain that had learned to live in his knees—he chanted:

“Widdy widdy wen!
I—ket—ches—Im—out—ar—ter—ten,
Widdy widdy wy!
Then—E—don’t—go—then—I—shy—
Widdy Widdy Wake-cock warning!”

With a comprehensive sweep on the last word, he let fly again at Durdles. The chant had the air of a customary signal—warning enough to tell Durdles, if he chose, to stand clear or to go.

Jasper beckoned the boy with his head to follow—feeling it useless to seize him or plead with him—and crossed to the iron railing where Durdles, stoned and stony, stood sunk in meditation among the tombs as among familiar tools.

“Do you know this thing—this child?” Jasper asked, at a loss for a word that would put a proper name to such a specimen.

“Deputy,” said Durdles, nodding.

“Is that its—his—name?”

“Deputy,” Durdles assented.

“I’m man-servant up at the Travellers’ Twopenny in Gas Works Garding,” the creature explained, as if that settled every question of birth and breeding. “All us man-servants at Travellers’ Lodgings is named Deputy. When we’re chock full and the Travellers is all a-bed I come out for my ’elth.”

Then he withdrew into the road, took aim, and began again:

“Widdy widdy wen!
I—ket—ches—Im—out—ar—ter—”

“Hold your hand!” cried Jasper. “Don’t throw while I stand so near him, or I’ll kill you. Come, Durdles; let me walk home with you to-night. Shall I carry your bundle?”

“Not on any account,” replied Durdles, adjusting it with jealous possession, as if what lay inside were as personal as breath.

“Durdles was making his reflections here when you come up, sir,” he went on, with the slow gravity of beery suddenness, “surrounded by his works, like a poplar Author.—Your own brother-in-law,” introducing with a motion of his head a sarcophagus within the railing, white and cold in the moonlight. “Mrs. Sapsea,” indicating the monument of that devoted wife. “Late Incumbent,” indicating the reverend gentleman’s broken column. “Departed Assessed Taxes,” indicating a vase and towel, standing on what might represent the cake of soap. “Former pastrycook and Muffin-maker, much respected,” indicating a gravestone. “All safe and sound here, sir, and all Durdles’s work. Of the common folk, that is merely bundled up in turf and brambles, the less said the better. A poor lot, soon forgot.”

The Close held those words strangely. It was a place made for echoes—organ-notes, children’s hymn-singing, the scrape of a verger’s shoe—and now it had to hold, too, the boy’s stone-clicks and wolfish yelps, and Durdles’s blunt verdict on memory. The Cathedral’s shadow lay over them like an old language that few could speak properly now, and fewer still could translate.

“This creature, Deputy, is behind us,” said Jasper, glancing back. “Is he to follow us?”

The relations between Durdles and Deputy were capricious. Durdles turned with the sluggish grandeur of a man who had drunk and worked among stone-dust all his life, and Deputy instantly made a wide circuit into the road and stood on the defensive, his head cocked for danger like a street-dog’s.

“You never cried Widdy Warning before you begun to-night,” said Durdles, as if unexpectedly reminded of an injury—or inventing one on the spot.

“Yer lie, I did,” said Deputy, in his single form of polite contradiction.

“Own brother, sir,” observed Durdles, turning again, and as suddenly forgetting his grievance as he had recalled or imagined it, “own brother to Peter the Wild Boy! But I gave him an object in life.”

“At which he takes aim?” Jasper suggested.

“That’s it, sir,” returned Durdles, satisfied. “At which he takes aim. I took him in hand and gave him an object. What was he before? A destroyer. What work did he do? Nothing but destruction. What did he earn by it? Short terms in Cloisterham jail. Not a person, not a piece of property, not a winder, not a horse, nor a dog, nor a cat, nor a bird, nor a fowl, nor a pig, but what he stoned, for want of an enlightened object. I put that enlightened object before him, and now he can turn his honest halfpenny by the three penn’orth a week.”

“I wonder he has no competitors.”

“He has plenty, Mr. Jasper, but he stones ’em all away. Now, I don’t know what this scheme of mine comes to,” pursued Durdles, with the same sodden consideration, “I don’t know what you may precisely call it. It ain’t a sort of a—scheme of a—National Education?”

“I should say not,” replied Jasper.

“I should say not,” assented Durdles. “Then we won’t try to give it a name.”

Still, behind them, Deputy kept his distance, and with it his noise—picking up stones and flinging them at every wall, post, pillar, and shuttered surface as they went by the deserted way. The quiet of the Close was not empty; it was peopled with small sounds that mattered—the nesting-stir of birds in ivy, the faint hush of wind in old carvings, the far-off tick of a clock—and Deputy’s clatter was like the intrusion of cheap, tinny commerce into a sacred concert, a kind of pollution that could not be unseen once heard.

“Is there anything new down in the crypt, Durdles?” Jasper asked.

“Anything old, I think you mean,” growled Durdles. “It ain’t a spot for novelty.”

“Any new discovery on your part, I meant.”

“There’s a old ’un under the seventh pillar on the left as you go down the broken steps of the little underground chapel as formerly was; I make him out (so fur as I’ve made him out yet) to be one of them old ’uns with a crook. To judge from the size of the passages in the walls, and of the steps and doors, by which they come and went, them crooks must have been a good deal in the way of the old ’uns! Two on ’em meeting promiscuous must have hitched one another by the mitre pretty often, I should say.”

Jasper offered no correction. He watched Durdles—powdered head to foot with mortar, lime, and stone-grit—as if the man’s daily intimacy with hidden masonry and buried names had begun to wear a romantic colour. The dead were Durdles’s pantry and ledger; he knew what lay where, and how close it lay, with a tradesman’s certainty that had nothing poetic in it, yet made poetry possible.

“Yours is a curious existence,” Jasper said.

Durdles replied, gruffly, “Yours is another,” and gave no clue whether he meant it as praise or rebuke.

“Well! inasmuch as my lot is cast in the same old earthy, chilly, never-changing place, yes,” said Jasper. “But there is much more mystery and interest in your connection with the Cathedral than in mine. Indeed, I am beginning to have some idea of asking you to take me on as a sort of student, or free ’prentice, under you, and to let me go about with you sometimes, and see some of these odd nooks in which you pass your days.”

“All right,” returned the Stony One, in general. “Everybody knows where to find Durdles, when he’s wanted.” Which, if not strictly true, came near enough, for Durdles was always in vagabondage somewhere within reach of stone and shadow.

“What I dwell upon most,” Jasper pursued, “is the remarkable accuracy with which you would seem to find out where people are buried.—What is the matter? That bundle is in your way; let me hold it.”

Durdles had stopped and backed a little. Deputy, attentive as a carrion-bird to any sign of action, instantly skirmished into the road. Durdles looked for some ledge or corner to place his bundle on; when Jasper relieved him of it, he said:

“Just you give me my hammer out of that, and I’ll show you.”

Clink, clink. The hammer changed hands, and Durdles’s grip on it had the comfort of habit—the way a cook’s hand finds the weight of a knife without looking, the way an elder finds the staff that makes the ground answer.

“Now, lookee here. You pitch your note, don’t you, Mr. Jasper?”

“Yes.”

“So I sound for mine. I take my hammer, and I tap.” He struck the pavement—tap—tap—tap—and the dull report ran along the stones and died against iron and wall. Deputy widened his range, as if supposing his own head might be called for in the demonstration.

“I tap, tap, tap. Solid! I go on tapping. Solid still! Tap again. Holloa! Hollow! Tap again, persevering. Solid in hollow! Tap, tap, tap, to try it better. Solid in hollow; and inside solid, hollow again! There you are! Old ’un crumbled away in stone coffin, in vault!”

“Astonishing!” said Jasper.

“I have even done this,” Durdles went on, drawing out his two-foot rule. Deputy crept nearer again, his hunger for mischief turning into a hunger for treasure; his imagination already licking its lips at the notion of discovery, accusation, and the “delicious treat” of seeing others pay for it.

“Say that hammer of mine’s a wall—my work. Two; four; and two is six,” measuring on the pavement. “Six foot inside that wall is Mrs. Sapsea.”

“Not really Mrs. Sapsea?”

“Say Mrs. Sapsea. Her wall’s thicker, but say Mrs. Sapsea. Durdles taps, that wall represented by that hammer, and says, after good sounding: ‘Something betwixt us!’ Sure enough, some rubbish has been left in that same six-foot space by Durdles’s men!”

Jasper opined, “Such accuracy is a gift.”

“I wouldn’t have it at a gift,” returned Durdles, ill-pleased. “I worked it out for myself. Durdles comes by his knowledge through grubbing deep for it, and having it up by the roots when it don’t want to come.—Holloa you Deputy!”

“Widdy!” shrilled Deputy, standing off again.

“Catch that ha’penny. And don’t let me see any more of you to-night, after we come to the Travellers’ Twopenny.”

“Warning!” returned Deputy, catching it; and that mystic word, tossed back into the night, seemed his assent to the bargain.

They crossed what had once been the monastery vineyard—ground that had fed men who once spoke their prayers in an older tongue, now paved over with later meanings—and came into the narrow back lane where stood the crazy wooden house of two low stories known as the Travellers’ Twopenny. It was warped and distorted, like the morals of the travellers; a lattice-porch lingered in scant remains above the door, and a rustic fence in scant remains before its stamped-out garden, because the travellers were so tenderly attached to the premises—or so fond of having a roadside fire by day—that nothing could persuade or threaten them into departure without their violently possessing themselves of some wooden forget-me-not and bearing it off.

The place attempted the semblance of an inn with fragments of conventional red curtaining in the windows. Those rags were made muddily transparent in the night by feeble lights of rush or cotton dip burning dully in the close air within, giving out that stale, greasy smell of cheap flame and damp boards—an odour that speaks of bellies never properly warmed, of broth stretched thin, of bread that has forgotten wheat. Over the door an inscribed paper lantern addressed them with the house’s purport, swaying a little, whispering paper against wire in the faintest draft like a tired insect.

As Durdles and Jasper drew near, half-a-dozen other hideous small boys—twopenny lodgers, followers, hangers-on, who could say—started into the moonlight as if Deputy’s presence had sent out a carrion-scent. Instantly they fell to stoning him and one another, the lane alive with yells and the hard bright chatter of pebbles.

“Stop, you young brutes!” cried Jasper angrily. “Let us go by!”

The remonstrance was received with yells and flying stones, in the comfortable custom of late years whereby Christians are stoned on all sides as if Saint Stephen’s days were revived. Durdles, with some point, remarked, “They haven’t got an object,” and led the way down the lane.

At the corner, Jasper, hotly enraged, checked his companion and looked back. For a moment all was silent. Then a stone came rattling at his hat; and far off a yell of “Wake-Cock! Warning!” rose, followed by a crow as from some infernally-hatched Chanticleer, proclaiming under whose victorious fire he stood. Jasper turned the corner into safety and took Durdles home, Durdles stumbling among the litter of his stony yard as if he were about to turn head-foremost into one of the unfinished tombs.

Jasper returned by another way to his gatehouse. Letting himself in softly with his key, he found his fire still burning—a small domestic red eye, watching. He went to a locked press and took from it a peculiar-looking pipe, which he filled—but not with tobacco—and with a little instrument adjusted the contents of the bowl very carefully, as if measuring and seasoning something that must be exact to the grain.

Then he ascended an inner staircase of only a few steps, leading to two rooms. There was a light in each. In one, his nephew lay asleep, calm and untroubled, as if the world’s noises could not reach him. Jasper stood looking down upon him for some time, the unlighted pipe in his hand, his attention fixed and deep, listening—perhaps—to the boy’s even breathing, perhaps to the Close’s distant hush, perhaps to something else that had no name in any living dialect.

At last, hushing his footsteps, he passed to his own room, lit his pipe, and delivered himself to the Spectres it invoked at midnight.
