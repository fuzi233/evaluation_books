I got up and the world wavered, as if the ground itself had been lifted and set down again a little aside from where it belonged. For a heartbeat I could not find her. The place where she had been—so near that her cold seemed to have entered my skin—was empty. Loneliness rolled back over me like a fog bank returning after a clear hour, smothering the taste of air.

Gone.

She whom I had dragged—by my own will, by my own stubborn hope—back from the verge of death, had fled and left me in the same desolation I thought I had bought my way out of. I could not endure that kind of solitude even for a moment. It had a sound: not silence, but a hollow ringing, like a room after the fire has gone out and the chimney keeps talking to itself. Had I wronged her? If I had compelled her to carry again the weight of living, then my life must be spent under that weight with her, not standing aside congratulating myself for a rescue.

I caught sight of her at last, moving swiftly over the grass, angling away from the river. The water behind me gave a low continuous voice, a dark, bread-thick murmur that had steadied me earlier; I plunged once into it for a last cold restorative—like swallowing a mouthful of bitter herb to wake the body—and then went after her.

The white leech’s visit and the woman’s blow had left me thin as a scraped bone, but strength was already creeping back, warm and insistent. I kept her in sight without trouble. Still, my heart carried a miserable refrain: Is this the end? Her eyes as I had last seen them—angry, hating—kept floating up before me, as if burned onto the underside of my lids.

I could understand her resentment at being forced to breathe again; I could not understand her loathing. What had I done beyond service? Could modesty itself take offense at help freely given? Every touch of mine had been reverent. I had borne her as a father bears his motherless child—careful of her weight, careful of her dignity, careful of the thin boundary between saving and taking. Had all my labor, all my despairing hope, purchased nothing but ingratitude?

No, I answered myself, as if speaking to an elder at a hearth who would not let me indulge in self-pity. Beauty must have a heart. It might be buried deeper than any seed in winter ground, but it must be there. And the deeper it lay, the more true would be its waking—if it ever woke. To rouse that heart would be a gift beyond the happiest life: it would be a nobler life.

She climbed a gentle slope ahead, walking straight and steady as one who knew her destination. Then I realized with a start that she was drawing away from me. I called on my strength as on a sleeping dog, and it came in a rush. My veins filled as if some new, clear broth had been poured into them. My body felt suddenly lighter—almost as though the air had become a buoyant medium meant to carry me. I moved after her like an easy wind and gained on her quickly.

Not once did she glance back.

She went swiftly, but without the agitation of haste—gliding, self-possessed, like a statue that has learned to walk. I was within three yards when she turned sharply and yet with unbroken grace, and stood. She showed neither fatigue nor heat; her paleness was not sickly but pure, like milk just skimmed, like flour sifted into a white bowl. Her breathing was slow and deep. Her eyes seemed to fill the heavens and cast light down into the world.

It was nearly noon, yet the sense upon me was of a great night: an invisible dew making the stars look close and large, so that one might almost smell their cold metal.

“Why do you follow me?” she asked—quietly, sternly, with the remote tone of a stranger meeting me for the first time.

“I have lived so long,” I said, “on the mere hope of your eyes, that I must want to see them again.”

“You will not be spared,” she returned, cold as a knife laid flat against the tongue. “I command you to stop where you stand.”

“Not until I see you in a place of safety will I leave you,” I said.

“Then take the consequences.” She turned and resumed her swift, gliding walk.

But as she turned she cast me one glance, and I stopped as if run through with a spear. Scorn had failed her. She would kill me with her beauty—kill me as surely as if she fed me sweetness until I forgot to breathe.

Despair brought my will back like a snapped cord mended in haste. The spell broke. I ran, caught up with her again.

“Have pity upon me!” I cried.

She gave no sign she had heard. I followed her as a child follows a mother who pretends to abandon him, who walks on without turning, trusting the child’s terror to keep him close.

“I will be your slave,” I said, and laid my hand on her arm.

She wheeled as if I had touched her with something venomous. The blaze of her eyes drove me inward; still I could not look away.

“Pity me,” I cried again.

She turned away and went on.

All day I followed her. The sun climbed, paused as if listening to the grass breathe, then went down the other side. She never stopped. I never ceased. Her pace did not relax. She did not once turn her head. There was nothing between us but the constant soft sound of our feet in thick, yielding turf—no stone, no road, no human artifact; only the world’s old carpeting under an immense sky.

When the sun slid under, night rose up. I kept close to her, because the thought of losing her even for a moment struck me like hunger. If she passed out of my sight, I knew, it might be forever.

We had walked the whole day over that thick, soft grass when suddenly she halted and threw herself down upon it. There was still light enough to show she was utterly weary. The sight struck me with an odd domestic tenderness—as if, for a moment, she were not a curse or a queen but simply a woman whose strength had given out at last, like a cook sinking onto a bench after feeding a household through harvest and winter.

I stood behind her, staring down. Did I love her? I knew she was not good. Did I hate her? I could not leave her.

I knelt beside her.

“Begone! Do not dare touch me,” she cried. Her arms lay on the grass at her sides, as if numb.

Then suddenly they rose and closed about my neck—rigid, inhuman, like the arms of an instrument built for pain. She drew my face down to hers. Her lips clung to my cheek.

A sting of pain shot through me and pulsed, a sharp, clean agony as if some hidden needle had found a vein. I could not stir even a hair’s breadth. The pain drained away. In its place came a slumberous weariness, and with it a dreamy pleasure—like the heavy comfort that follows a draught too sweet, too strong, taken in ignorance. Then I knew nothing.

All at once I woke.

The moon sat a little above the horizon, but gave no radiance; it was only a bright thing pinned into blackness, like a coin pressed into velvet. My cheek smarted. I lifted my hand and found it wet. My neck ached; there too my fingers came away damp. I sighed and felt emptied out, as if whatever had filled me earlier had been drawn off.

I turned my eyes dully and saw what had become of the moonlight: it was gathered about the lady. She stood wrapped in a shimmering nimbus, as if the darkness itself had agreed to honor her outline.

I rose and staggered toward her.

“Down!” she cried, imperiously, as one speaks to a dog that has forgotten its place. “Follow me a step if you dare!”

“I will,” I murmured, and it cost me like lifting a stone with broken arms.

“Set foot within the gates of my city, and my people will stone you: they do not love beggars!”

Her words hit my ears without entering me. Weak as water and only half awake, I did not know I moved; still the distance lessened.

She stepped back once, lifted her left arm, clenched her hand, and seemed to strike me on the forehead.

It was as if an iron hammer had fallen. I went down.

And then—instant as a snapped string—I sprang to my feet, cold and wet, clear-headed and strong. The blow had left no wound, no pain. Had it revived me? But how was I wet? I could not have lain long, for the moon was no higher.

The lady stood some yards away, her back to me, doing something I could not make out. Then by her sudden gleam I knew she had thrown off her garments and stood naked-white in the dazed moon.

For one moment she stood.

Then she fell forward.

A streak of white shot away from where she had been, drawn out in a swift line. In the same instant the moon seemed to recover herself and flashed full, and I saw that the streak was a long-bodied thing, rushing in great low-curved bounds over the grass. Dark spots streamed down its back as it went, as if it had been running under the edge of a wood and gathering leaf-shadows without ever touching the trees.

“God of mercy!” I cried. “Is the terrible creature speeding to the night-infolded city?”

And as I stared after it, fear turning my mouth to dust, it seemed to me that from far away there came a sudden bloom of sound—an outcry bursting and spreading—terror leaping from house to house as the pale savage bounded among them, rending and slaying. The thought had its own noise inside my skull: a modern, merciless clamor, like the crash of metal and the shriek of machines, drowning the delicate, living acoustics of a place where human voices ought to carry memory instead of panic.

While I gazed, another creature shot past me from behind—swift, almost noiseless as an arrow. It was large, pure white. Its path went straight for the place where the lady had fallen.

My tongue clove to the roof of my mouth. I sprang forward after it.

But the spot I aimed for fell behind the beast almost at once. It moved with a wasteless speed—long, level, skimming leaps that seemed to spend nothing, like a dancer who never lands, or a knife thrown true.

“It was well I could not cry out,” I thought wildly; “if she had risen, the monster would have been upon her!”

When I reached the place, the lady was not there. Only the garments she had dropped lay dark in the moonlight—cloth without a body, like a language after its last speaker has gone: the familiar shapes remain, but the living meaning has vanished, and one stands staring, suddenly illiterate.

I looked after the second beast. It tore over the ground even faster than the first, following the same line. I watched it grow smaller and smaller until it disappeared into the uncertain distance.

But where was the lady?

Had the first beast surprised her—creeping noiselessly? I had heard no shriek. There had not been time to devour her. Could it have caught her up as it ran and borne her away to its den? But burdened, it could not have flown so fast—and I should have seen it carrying something.

Horrible doubts woke in me like rats in a pantry.

I searched thoroughly, circling, combing the grass with my eyes and hands as if I might find a dropped pin, a lost seed, a trace of warmth. Nothing—no body, no mark, no sign beyond the abandoned clothes and the lingering wrongness in the air.

At last, with the night’s hush pressing close and the distant imagined cries still echoing in my nerves, I set out in the track of the two animals.
