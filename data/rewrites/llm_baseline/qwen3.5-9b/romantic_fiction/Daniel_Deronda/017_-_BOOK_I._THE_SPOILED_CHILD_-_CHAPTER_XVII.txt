"This is true the poet sings,
That a sorrow's crown of sorrow
Is remembering happier things."
—TENNYSON: *In Memoriam*.

On a fine evening near the end of July, Daniel Deronda was rowing alone on the Thames. It had been nearly a year since he returned to England, ostensibly to finish his education and secure his place in society. Though he had begun reading law to satisfy Sir Hugo's wishes and ward off idleness, the endeavor had only deepened his internal indecision. Now, living with the Mallingers in London, his old passion for boating had surged anew. The river offered a seclusion unavailable anywhere else in the city. He owned a boat at Putney, and whenever Sir Hugo did not require his services, it became his chief holiday to row until past sunset, returning only when the stars appeared.

Deronda was not in a sentimental mood, nor was he lost in idle reverie. Rather, he inhabited a contemplative state common among certain young men of his generation: a questioning of whether it was worthwhile to engage in the "battle of the world"—a struggle fought by those who possess a small fraction of capital accumulated by others' labor. Sir Hugo found Deronda's hesitation puzzling. How could a youth who stood in such splendid contrast to the sickly and whining types be paralyzed by spectral illusions? Especially since Deronda rejected authorship, a profession often viewed as converting foolish thinking into profit.

Rowing in a dark-blue shirt and skull-cap, with his curls closely clipped and his mouth framed by abundant, soft waves of beard, Deronda bore only disguised traces of the seraphic boy who once trailed clouds of glory. Yet, even someone who had not seen him since childhood might have recognized him through a slow familiarity, perhaps triggered by the peculiar intensity of his gaze—a look Gwendolen once called "dreadful," though it possessed a remarkably mild scrutiny. His voice, occasionally heard in subdued fragments of song, was merely a high baritone. To judge by his lithe, powerful frame and the firm gravity of his face, an observer would quickly deduce he lacked the rare, ravishing tenor that nature sacrifices so reluctantly.

Look at his hands: they were neither small and dimpled nor possessing tapering fingers suited only for a deprecating touch. They were long, flexible, and firmly grasping—hands reminiscent of those Titian painted to combine refinement with force. There was a striking resemblance between these hands and the face that wore them: the uniform pale-brown skin, the perpendicular brow, and the calmly penetrating eyes. He was no longer seraphic; he was thoroughly terrestrial and manly, yet of a type capable of recognizing human dignity even in the poorest relations. Such figures appear occasionally among average conditions, like a workman whistling over a measurement and lifting his eyes to answer a query about the road. Often, the profound meanings of faces, as well as written words, reside chiefly in the immediate impressions they evoke. For Deronda, rowing on the Thames in ordinary attire beneath Kew Bridge, passing with no intention of seeking adventure, these impressions were paramount.

Indeed, Deronda strongly objected to the notion, which others had forced upon him, that his appearance naturally drew attention. Complimentary hints regarding this trait resonated angrily within him, stemming from mixed experiences hinted at previously. For years, his reflection in the glass had associated him with thoughts of someone he resembled deeply—a figure whose character and fate he constantly wondered about but never dared inquire.

Between six and seven o'clock near Kew Bridge, the river was no longer a solitude. People sauntered on the towpath, and boats plied the water. Deronda had been rowing hard to clear this area when he noticed a large barge approaching. Guiding his boat aside, he rested his oar within a couple of yards of the riverbank. Throughout, he continued the low-toned chant haunting his throat since leaving the bridge: the gondolier's song from Verdi's *Otello*, setting Dante's immortal words, *"Nessun maggior dolore / Che ricordarsi del tempo felice / Nella miseria"* ("No greater sorrow / Than to recall the happy times / In misery"). As he paused, the soft, melodic wail of *"nella miseria"* drifted clearly over the water's edge.

Three or four bystanders stopped to watch the barge pass the bridge, and undoubtedly included the young gentleman in the boat. However, the low vocal sounds likely reached only one ear with significance greater than the ambient noise. Deronda, waiting for the barge, turned his head toward the riverside and spotted a figure a few yards away that seemed an embodiment of the misery he was unconsciously voicing. It was a girl, hardly more than eighteen, of a low, slim build, with a most delicate face. Her dark curls were pushed back behind her ears beneath a large black hat, and a long woolen cloak draped her shoulders. Her hands hung clasped before her, and her eyes were fixed on the river with an immovable, statue-like despair.

This arresting sight caused Deronda to stop singing. Apparently, his voice had penetrated her inner world without her noting its origin, for when it abruptly ceased, she shifted her stance slightly. Looking around with a frightened glance, she met Deronda's face. The exchange lasted only a couple of moments, yet it felt like an eternity for two strangers locked in mutual gaze. Her look resembled that of a fawn or other gentle animal before it flees: no blush, no specific alarm, only a timidity that could not prevent a prolonged stare before she turned away. It seemed Deronda perceived she was only half-conscious of her surroundings—was she hungry, or bewildered by some other cause? A surge of interest and compassion welled up in him, but instantly she turned and walked to a nearby bench under a tree.

He felt he had no right to linger. Poorly dressed, melancholy women were common sights; it was the delicate beauty, the picturesque lines, and the vivid coloring of this particular image that made it exceptional—and thus made it more imperative that he not intrude. He began to row away, soon distancing himself upriver, yet no other thoughts were potent enough to banish the pale image of the unhappy girl. He repeatedly speculated on the romance hidden behind her loneliness and desolation, then smiled at his own bias that interesting faces must harbor interesting adventures. Finally, he justified his feeling that sorrow was more tragic when it befell delicate, childlike beauty.

"I should not have forgotten the look of misery if she had been ugly and vulgar," he told himself. Yet, the attractiveness of the image ensured its persistence. To him, she was clear as an onyx cameo: the brown-black drapery, the white face with minute features and dark, long-lashed eyes. His mind swept over the girl-tragedies occurring worldwide, hidden and unheeded, as if they were plays in a coppice where helpless creatures dragged wounded wings and stained the shadowed moss with the crimson mark of their own deaths.

Recently, Deronda's solitary excursions had been dominated by uncertainties regarding his own path. These doubts, given ample leisure, expanded to encompass vast connections with life and history. Consequently, the new image of helpless sorrow blended seamlessly with his reasons for shrinking from the world's routine—a system that made men apologize for wrongdoing and treated opinions as mere professional tools. Why should he pull strongly on any thread of the hopelessly entangled scheme of things? He used his oars sparingly, content to drift with the tide and be carried back by it. It was his habit to indulge in a solemn passivity that arose with lengthening shadows and mellow light, when thinking and desiring melted imperceptibly together, transforming argument into passionate vision.

By the time he returned with the tide past Richmond Bridge, the sun was nearing setting. The approach of his favorite hour, with its deepening stillness and darkening masses of trees and buildings caught between the double glow of sky and river, disposed him to linger as if listening to an unfinished musical strain. Seeking a perfectly solitary spot to moor his boat, he chose a bend opposite Kew Gardens, offering a broad stretch of water reflecting the sky's glory while keeping him in shadow. Lying on his back with his head propped on cushions, he could observe everything without being seen. For a long while, he did not turn his eyes from the view directly ahead.

He was forgetting everything else in a half-speculative, involuntary identification with the landscape, pondering how far he might shift his center of gravity until his own personality existed outside him as surely as the scenery. Suddenly, the sensation of movement on the opposite bank, bordered by willow bushes, drew his glance. In the first moment, he felt a darting presentiment concerning the moving figure. Soon, he could see the small face illuminated by strange, dying sunlight. Fearing to frighten her with sudden motion, he watched her with motionless attention.

She looked around, seemingly gathering security from the apparent solitude, hid her hat among the willows, and immediately removed her woolen cloak. Shortly thereafter, she sat down and deliberately dipped the cloak into the water, holding it there briefly before pulling it out with evident effort, rising as she did so. Deronda realized she intended to wrap the wet cloak around herself like a drowning shroud. There was no time to hesitate. He rose, seized his oar, and paddled across; fortunately, her position lay slightly below him.
