The two young men linger long enough to watch their charges vanish through the Nuns’ House courtyard, then turn to find the brazen doorplate glinting like a sharp, disapproving eye, as if the frock-coated old beau with his monocle trapped on the street side of the metal is glowering at them for loitering. They glance at each other, then down the moon-silvered length of Cloisterham’s quiet thoroughfare, and fall into step slowly, unspoken friction thrumming between them.

“Do you stay here long, Mr. Drood?” Neville asks, his tone sharper than he intends.
“Not this trip,” Edwin answers, dismissive. “I’m back to London tomorrow. But I’ll pop in off and on till next Midsummer, then I’m saying goodbye to Cloisterham, and England too, for a good long stretch.”
“Off abroad?”
“Going to rouse Egypt a little,” Edwin says, condescending. “No reading, mind you—working. Engineering. My father was a partner at the firm I’m joining, left my small patrimony tied up as part of their capital. I’m on their payroll till I come of age, then I step into my modest share of the concern. Jack—you met him at dinner—is my guardian and trustee till then.”
“Mr. Crisparkle told me of your other fine fortune too.”
Edwin’s brow lifts, sharp and unpolite. “What do you mean, my other good fortune?”
Neville had phrased the line with a furtive, watchful edge, that strange air of being both hunter and hunted that clings to him even now. The two stop dead, and trade a heated, wary look.
“I hope I gave no offence,” Neville says, flushing. “I only innocently referenced your betrothal.”
“Good God!” Edwin snaps, picking up his pace. “Every gossiping soul in this mouldy old town won’t leave it be. I half expect some tavern to hang a sign with my face under *The Betrothed’s Head*—or Pussy Rosa’s. One or the other.”
“I’m not to blame for Mr. Crisparkle mentioning it openly to me,” Neville begins.
“No, that’s true enough,” Edwin allows.
“But I chose to bring it up to you,” Neville goes on, “because I assumed you’d be impossibly proud of it.”

Two quiet, unspoken currents of anger are feeding this fight now, invisible to both men. Neville is already half lost to quiet, smitten admiration for Little Rosebud, and seethes that Edwin—far less worthy of her than anyone he’s ever met—treats his prize like a trivial afterthought. Edwin, for his part, has already been struck by Helena Landless’s fierce, unyielding grace, and burns that her ragged, unpolished brother dares talk down to him like he’s some foolish green boy who doesn’t know his own luck.
Edwin scoffs. “I don’t reckon people gush most loudly about the things they’re proudest of, or want every stranger chattering about them. I live a busy life, after all—you, as the well-read newcomer, can correct me if I’m wrong, I’m sure you know better.”

They’re both savage now. Neville’s rage is out in the open, raw and unhidden; Edwin hides his own behind half-hummed snatches of popular tunes, and fake stops to gawk at “picturesque moonlit effects” as if he hasn’t even noticed they’re fighting.
“It’s hardly civil of you,” Neville bursts out at last, “to sneer at a stranger who came here with none of your advantages, trying to make up for lost time. I wasn’t raised in that ‘busy life’ of yours, you see. My ideas of politeness were formed among heathens.”
“Then the best civility anyone can learn, no matter where they grew up,” Edwin retorts, “is to mind their own business. Set me that example, and I’ll follow it faithfully.”
“You take far too much upon yourself!” Neville snaps, fists clenched. “Back where I come from, you’d be called to account for that kind of arrogance.”
“By who, exactly?” Edwin stops dead, staring at him with open disdain.

A heavy, unexpected hand lands on Edwin’s shoulder. Jasper steps out from the shadowed side of the lane, between the two of them, as if he’d been strolling past the Nuns’ House too and caught their raised voices mid-rant.
“Ned, Ned, Ned,” he chides, soft but firm. “None of this. I don’t like it. You two have been shouting loud enough to wake the cathedral tombs. Remember, my boy, you’re almost the host here tonight. You belong to this town, represent it to our new guest. Mr. Neville is a stranger, and hospitality demands you show him respect. And you, Mr. Neville,” he adds, resting his other hand on Neville’s inner shoulder, so he walks sandwiched neatly between the two young men, a hand on each, “you must pardon me, but I beg you to rein in your temper too. What is there even to fight over? Let’s just say there’s nothing amiss, and we can forget the whole thing. We’re all three on good terms, aren’t we?”

After a long, silent standoff where both young men fight to be the last to speak, Edwin speaks first. “No hard feelings from me, Jack.”
“Nor from me,” Neville concedes, though his tone is far less easy, far more tight. “Though if Mr. Drood knew everything I’ve left behind me back home, he might understand how sharp-edged words cut deeper for me than they do for others.”
“Ah, now, let’s not put conditions on our good mood,” Jasper soothes, steering them toward his gatehouse cottage only a few yards away. “That sounds far too much like a remonstrance, and that’s hardly generous. Frankly, no anger on Ned’s side—frankly, no anger on yours, Mr. Neville?”
“None at all, Mr. Jasper.” It still rings a little stiff, a little forced.
“Perfect! My bachelor rooms are right there, the heater’s on the fire, the wine and glasses are all laid out, and it’s barely a stone’s throw from Minor Canon Corner. Ned’s off to London tomorrow, after all. We’ll drag Mr. Neville in with us for a proper stirrup-cup.”
“With all my heart,” Edwin says, grinning.
“And with all mine,” Neville says, though he’d far rather be anywhere else. He can feel his temper slipping out of his grip, and Edwin’s lazy, unshakable cool only stokes the fire hotter.

Jasper hums the refrain of a raucous drinking song as he leads them inside. When he lights the lamp next to the hearth fire, the first thing they all see is Edwin’s portrait of Rosa, hanging over the mantel. It’s a disastrous thing to put between two men already at each other’s throats—it drags their fight back to the exact subject that sparked it. Both glance at it consciously, neither speaking. Jasper, who seems to have missed the root of their argument entirely, points it out cheerfully.
“You recognise that painting, Mr. Neville?” He shades the lamp to throw full light on the canvas.
“I recognise it. It doesn’t do the original any favours.”
“Oh, you’re too harsh! Ned painted it, gave it to me as a gift.”
“I’m sorry to hear that, Mr. Drood,” Neville says, sincerely apologetic. “If I’d known I was speaking to the artist—”
“Joke, man, just a joke,” Edwin cuts in, yawning so wide his jaw cracks. “Just messing around with Pussy’s little quirks. I’ll paint her properly, serious and proper, one of these days, if she’s a good girl.”
He leans back in his chair, lacing his fingers behind his head to prop it up, that lazy, patronizing air thick enough to cut. Neville burns with indignation so obvious it’s as bright as the firelight. Jasper glances between the two, a tiny, unreadable smile on his face, then turns his back to stir a jug of mulled wine at the hearth, lingering over the mixing far longer than he needs to.
“I suppose, Mr. Neville,” Edwin says, already picking a new fight, “if you were going to paint the girl you loved—”
“I can’t paint,” Neville snaps, cutting him off.
“That’s your misfortune, not your fault. You would if you could. But if you could, I expect you’d turn her into Juno, Minerva, Diana, Venus—all four goddesses rolled into one, wouldn’t you?”
“I’ve no lady love, so I can’t say.”
“If I ever tried my hand,” Edwin crows, boyish and boastful now, “at a serious portrait of your sister Helena—mind you, dead serious—you’d be blown away by what I could do.”
“My sister would never consent to sit for you,” Neville shoots back. “Since that’s never going to happen, I’ll never get to see what you’re capable of. I’ll survive the loss.”

Jasper turns from the fire, hands a huge brimming goblet to each young man, then pours one for himself. “Come on, Mr. Neville—we drink to Ned tonight. His foot’s in the stirrup, off on his grand adventure tomorrow. The stirrup-cup’s all for him. Ned, my dearest fellow—to you, my love!”
Jasper drains nearly his whole glass, Neville follows suit, and Edwin laughs and does the same. The spiced, strong wine hits their heads fast. Both their faces flush bright red, speech already thickening around the edges.
“Look at him!” Jasper says, gesturing to Edwin with admiring, playful fondness. “See how he lounges so easily, Mr. Neville! The whole world is laid out before him. A life of stirring, interesting work, endless travel and excitement, a home full of quiet ease and love. Just look at him!”
Edwin’s eyes are glassy now, still leaning back in his chair. “See how little he even cares for all that!” Jasper teases. “Hardly worth his trouble to pluck the ripe golden fruit hanging right off the tree for him. What a contrast to the rest of us! You and I, Mr. Neville—we’ve got no grand adventures, no bright domestic future ahead of us. Unless you’re far luckier than me, we’re stuck in this tedious old town, turning the same small round forever.”
“Upon my soul, Jack,” Edwin says, complacent and slurring, “I almost feel bad for having everything handed to me like that. But you know what I know, right? It might not be as easy as it looks after all. Isn’t that right, Pussy?” He snaps his fingers at the portrait of Rosa. “We still haven’t quite got it all figured out, have we? You know what I mean, Jack.”

His words are thick, muddled. Jasper, still perfectly calm and sober, glances over at Neville, waiting for him to speak. Neville’s speech is just as slurred, eyes blazing.
“It might have done Mr. Drood a world of good to have known some real hardship,” he defies.
Edwin only rolls his eyes in his direction. “Pray why’s that?”
“Ay,” Jasper says, leaning in, fake interested. “Do tell us why.”
“Because it would have made him actually appreciate his good luck,” Neville snarls. “Luck that’s never been earned by a single one of his own merits.”
Jasper’s eyes dart to Edwin, waiting for his retort. “Have you known a lot of hardships, may I ask?” Edwin sits bolt upright, suddenly sharp.
Jasper’s eyes dart back to Neville. “I have.”
“And what did they teach you to appreciate?”
The game of glances between the two young men never stops. “I told you once already tonight.”
“You did no such thing.”
“I tell you I did. That you take far too much upon yourself.”
“You added something else to that, didn’t you?”
“Yes. I said that back where I come from, you’d be called to account for it.”
“Only there?” Edwin laughs, contemptuous. “A million miles away, isn’t it? I see! That’s a very safe distance to talk big from.”
“Say here, then!” Neville leaps to his feet, shaking with rage. “Say anywhere! Your vanity is unbearable, your conceit’s past all endurance. You strut around like you’re some rare precious prize, not just a common loud-mouthed boaster. You’re nothing but a common fellow, a common fraud.”
“Pfft, easy now,” Edwin snaps, just as furious but far more collected. “What would you know? You might be able to spot a common black fellow or a common black boaster from all your acquaintances back home, but you’re in no position to judge white men.”

The insult to his dark colonial heritage shatters Neville’s last thread of control. He flings the dregs of his spiced wine straight in Edwin’s face, and hefts the empty goblet to bash his skull in. Jasper lunges in the nick of time, catching his arm mid-swing.
“Ned, my dear boy!” he shouts, loud and commanding. “I entreat you, I command you—stay still! Mr. Neville, for shame! Give me that glass. Open your hand, sir. I will take it!”
Neville wrenches Jasper’s grip off him, pauses for one wild, raging second with the goblet still raised over his head, then hurls it under the grate so hard the glass shatters into thousands of flying shards. He storms out of the house, slamming the door so hard the walls rattle.

When he staggers out into the night air, nothing is steady, nothing looks like itself. The whole world spins in a red haze around his head, and he half-expects someone to jump out of the dark and force him to fight to the death. When nothing comes, and the moon glares down at him like he’s already dead from the fit of wrath, he clamps a hand to his throbbing skull, staggers down the lane. He half-notes he heard the door behind him bolt and bar shut, like he’s a dangerous animal locked outside.
Wild, mad impulses to throw himself in the river fizzle out when he looks up and sees the cathedral spire glowing white over the graves, remembers his sister Helena waiting for him back home, thinks of Mr. Crisparkle, the good man who had won his trust and sworn to look after him only that very day. He stumbles to Minor Canon Corner, and knocks softly on the door.

Mr. Crisparkle is still up, the last person in the household awake, plucking soft, quiet notes on his piano so he doesn’t disturb anyone else’s slumber. He answers the knock himself, candle in hand, and his cheerful face drops the second he sees Neville, dishevelled, wild-eyed, his shirt soaked with wine.
“Mr. Neville! In this state! Where have you been?”
“I was at Mr. Jasper’s. With his nephew.”
“Come in.” The Minor Canon steers him inside with a strong, steady hand, and leads him to his small bookroom, shutting the door behind them.
“I’ve started dreadfully ill, sir. Terribly ill.”
“Too true. You’re not sober, Mr. Neville.”
“I’m afraid I am not, sir—though I could prove to you later I barely drank anything at all, it just overcame me fast, in the strangest way possible.”
“Mr. Neville, Mr. Neville,” Crisparkle says, shaking his head with a sorrowful smile. “I’ve heard that line before.”
“I think—my mind’s muddled, but I think—Mr. Jasper’s nephew is just as drunk as I am, sir.”
“Very likely,” Crisparkle says, dry. “We quarrelled, sir. He insulted me most grossly. He had that hot tigerish blood I told you about churning long before that final line.”
“Mr. Neville,” Crisparkle says, gentle but firm. “I must ask you not to clench that right hand at me. Unclench it, please.”
“He goaded me past the point I could bear!” Neville obeys instantly, the words bursting out of him. “I don’t know if he meant to at first, but he did it. He definitely meant to by the end. In the rage he lashed me up into, I would have cut him down if I could. I tried to do it.”
“You’ve clenched that hand again,” Crisparkle says, quiet as a breath.
“Pardon me, sir.”
“You know your room, I showed it to you before dinner. I’ll walk you up there again. Give me your arm. Keep quiet, the whole house is asleep.” He guides Neville up the stairs with that steady, practiced grip on his elbow, the kind he uses for his morning wrestling and rowing training, so Neville can barely stumble even in his state. They reach the neat, quiet guest room prepared for him, and Neville collapses into a chair, flings his arms over his reading desk, and buries his head in them, racked with sobs of self-loathing.
Crisparkle almost slips out the door without a word, but turns back to pat his shoulder softly. “Good night.” A sob is the only answer, and it is far better than any apology Neville could have spoken.

As Crisparkle walks back down the stairs, there’s another soft knock at the outer door. He opens it to find Jasper standing on the step, holding Neville’s hat in his hand.
“We’ve had an awful, murderous scene with him,” Jasper says, voice low.
“Surely it’s not that bad!”
“Murderous!” Jasper insists, earnest as anything. “He could have laid my dear boy dead at my feet on the hearth. He would have, too, if I hadn’t been quick and strong enough to stop him. Seeing what I saw tonight, I’ll never sleep easy if those two are ever left alone together again. There’s tiger’s blood in that dark blood of his.”
Crisparkle’s blood runs cold. Those were Neville’s exact words, barely five minutes before.
“You, my dear sir,” Jasper goes on, squeezing his hand, “even you, have taken on a very dangerous charge.”
“You’ve no need to fear for me, Jasper,” Crisparkle says, smiling quiet. “I’m not worried for myself.”
