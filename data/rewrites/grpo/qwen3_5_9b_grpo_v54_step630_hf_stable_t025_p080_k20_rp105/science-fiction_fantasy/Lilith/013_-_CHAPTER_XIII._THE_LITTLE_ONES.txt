I had scarcely begun my labor when the air grew thick with the sound of tiny voices. From the dense undergrowth of miniature trees, which carpeted the forest floor like living moss, emerged the Little Ones. Within moments, a swarm of them surrounded me. I signaled that the giants had only just departed and remained nearby, but they laughed—a sound like a chorus of silver bells—and declared the wind pure and clean.

"They are too blind to see us," one chirped.

"Do you like the rope binding your ankles?" another asked, her voice bright with curiosity.

"I wear it to convince them I cannot remove it," I lied, hoping to maintain the illusion of captivity.

"They can barely see their own toes!" she giggled. "Just take short steps, and they will believe the rope is secure."

As she spoke, she danced with boundless joy. A larger girl knelt to inspect the clumsy knot at my feet. I smiled, expecting her delicate fingers to fail, but in an instant, the bond was loose. They seated me upon the earth and fed me with fruits of exquisite sweetness. Then, the smallest among them began to play with me in a frenzy of chaotic delight, rendering my work impossible. As one grew weary, another took her place, and this cycle continued until the sun dipped below the horizon, casting long shadows over the clearing. Heavy footsteps approached, signaling the giants' return. The children scattered instantly. I quickly re-tied the rope around my ankles.

"We must be careful," the girl who had freed me whispered urgently. "A single trampling of one of their clumsy feet could crush a tiny life!"

"Can they not see you at all?" I asked.

"They might spot movement," she replied, her tone grim. "And if a pile of them landed on you, it would be terrible; they hate every living thing except themselves. Not that they are very alive either!"

She whistled a sharp, bird-like note. Instantly, the forest fell silent; not a child was seen or heard, and she vanished into the foliage.

My master arrived to reclaim me. He untied my ankles, dragged me to the threshold of his hut, threw me onto the dirt, re-bound my feet, delivered a kick, and departed.

At that moment, escape was within my grasp. Yet, having found friends, I hesitated. They were so enchanting, so full of whimsical grace, that I felt compelled to learn more of them. "Tomorrow," I promised myself with a surge of hope, "I shall see them again!" But from the silence of the huts until sleep claimed me, I heard their whispers drifting through the night, knowing I was watched with affectionate intensity. After that, they rarely allowed me solitude.

I never truly understood the giants. They possessed no malice, only a profound stupidity. Often, I avoided their blows by seizing their feet and toppling them, a trick that usually ended their aggression for the day.

In contrast, the Little Ones were a constant source of wonder. Their actions delighted and surprised me daily. My loathing for my labor faded as they brought amusement and solace to my monotonous existence. Soon, my love for them became overwhelming. Though I knew little of the world, they seemed wise beyond their size, capable of learning anything. I had no bed but the bare ground, yet I frequently awoke nestled in a cradle of children—some in my arms, others curled tightly around me, the smallest drawn nearest to my warmth. I rarely suffered the chill of the night. Before dawn and after dusk, they always brought me sustenance.

One morning, I awoke to find myself alone. Moments later, subdued sounds approached. Lona, the tallest and most solemn of the group, regarded by all as their mother, emerged from the woods. She was followed by the multitude, moving in joyful silence to avoid waking the giant at my door. In her arms, she carried a boy-baby. Previously, the youngest had been a girl, roughly a year old. Three older girls served as her nurses, though they shared the infant's care with the entire community. Among the Little Ones, dolls were unknown; the older tended the younger, who in turn cared for the tiniest.

Lona approached and placed the infant in my arms. The baby opened his eyes, gazed at me, closed them, and drifted back to sleep.

"He loves you already," Lona said softly.

"Where did you find him?" I asked.

"In the wood, of course," she answered, her eyes shining. "That is where we always find them. Isn't he beautiful? We searched all night. Sometimes it is difficult!"

"How do you know when one is there?"

"I cannot explain," she replied. "Everyone rushes to tell the others, yet we never discover who spoke first. Sometimes I think someone whispered it in sleep, and another heard it half-awake. When a baby appears in the wood, no one stops to ask questions. By the time we find it, it is too late."

"Do more boys or girls arrive?"

I noticed that asking the same precise question twice caused them to furrow their brows.

"I do not know," she admitted.

"You can count them, surely!"

"We never do that. We would not like to be counted."

"Why?"

"It would not feel smooth. We prefer not to know."

"Where do the babies originate?"

"From the wood—always. There is no other place."

She knew their origin perfectly and considered it the only truth required.

"How often do you find one?"

"Such a happy event consumes all our joy, and we forget the previous one. You too are glad to have him, are you not, good giant?"

"Yes, indeed!" I replied. "But how do you feed him?"

"I will show you," she said, departing briefly to return with two or three ripe plums. She offered one to the baby's lips.

"He would open his mouth if he were awake," she noted, lifting him. She squeezed a drop of juice onto the fruit's surface and held it to his lips. Without stirring, the infant began to suckle. She continued slowly until only skin and stone remained.

"There!" she cried, her voice triumphant. "A world of big apples with nothing for the babies! We would not stay in it—would we, darling? We would leave it to the bad giants!"

"But what if you let the stone fall into his mouth?" I asked.

"No mother would do that," she replied firmly. "I would not be fit to hold a baby!"

I marveled at the woman she would become. But what happened when they grew up? Where did they go? This led me back to the mystery of their arrival.

"Will you tell me where you lived before?" I asked.

"Here," she answered.

"Have you NEVER lived elsewhere?" I ventured cautiously.

"Never. We all came from the wood. Some believe we dropped from the trees."

"How is it there are so many of you, so small?"

"I do not understand. Some are smaller, some larger. I am very big."

"The baby will grow bigger, won't he?"

"Of course!"

"And will you grow bigger?"

"I do not think so. I hope not. I am the biggest. It frightens me sometimes."

"Why should it frighten you?"

She offered no answer.

"How old are you?" I persisted.

"I do not know what you mean. We are all just that."

"How big will the baby grow?"

"I cannot tell," she said, her voice trembling slightly. "Some," she added, "begin to grow after we think they have stopped. That is a frightful thing. We do not speak of it!"

"What makes it frightful?"

She paused, then answered, "We fear they may be beginning to grow giants."

"Why should you fear that?"

"Because it is so terrible. I do not want to talk about it!"

She pressed the baby to her chest with such anxiety that I dared not press further.

Soon, I noticed traces of greed and selfishness in a few of the smaller children. The older girls cast worried glances at them frequently. None of them assisted with my work; they would do nothing for the giants! Yet, they never ceased their tender care for me. They sang to me for hours, climbed trees to pop fruit into my mouth, and maintained a constant vigil against the giants' approach.

Occasionally, they gathered to tell stories—mostly childish and often seemingly meaningless. On one occasion, a moody little fellow sang a strange, crooning song with a refrain so poignant that, though unintelligible to me, it brought tears to my eyes. This reaction puzzled the onlookers. Then, I realized I had never seen water fall, lie, or run in that world. There had been plenty in some ancient age, but the Little Ones had never witnessed it before seeing my tears. Nevertheless, they seemed to possess a dim, instinctive understanding of their origins. A very small child approached the singer, shook his clenched fist in his face, and muttered something like, "Squeeze the juice out of the good giant's seeberries! Bad giant!"

One day, as Lona sat with the baby at the foot of my tree, I asked, "Why do I never see children among the giants?"

She stared, searching for meaning in my words, then replied, "They are giants; there are no little ones among them."

"Have they never any children?" I asked.

"No; there are never any in the wood for them. They do not love them. If they saw ours, they would stamp them."

"Is the number of giants always the same? I thought, before I knew better, that they were your fathers and mothers."

She burst into the merriest laughter, then said, "No, good giant; WE are THEIR firsters."

As the words left her lips, the joy vanished from her face, replaced by fear.

I stopped working and gazed at her, bewildered.

"How CAN that be?" I exclaimed.

"I do not say; I do not understand," she answered. "But we were here and they were not. They go from us. I am sorry, but we cannot help it. THEY could have helped it."

"How long have you been here?" I asked, increasingly confused, hoping for a clue.

"Always, I think," she replied. "I think somebody made us always."

I returned to my scraping.

She saw my confusion.

"The giants were not made always," she resumed. "If a Little One does not care, he becomes greedy, then lazy, then big, then stupid, then bad. The dull creatures do not know they come from us. Very few believe we exist anywhere. They say NONSENSE! Look at little Blunty: he is eating one of their apples! He will be the next! Oh, oh! He will soon be big and bad and ugly, and not even know it!"

The child stood apart, eating an apple nearly as large as his head. I had often thought he looked less healthy than the others; now, he looked repulsive.

"I will take the horrid thing from him!" I cried.

"It is no use," she answered sadly. "We have done all we can, and it is too late! We feared he was growing because he would not believe us. But when he refused to share his berries and claimed he had gathered them for himself, we knew! He is a glutton, and there is no hope. It makes me sick to see him eat!"

"Could not some of the boys watch him and prevent him from touching those poisonous things?"

"He may have them if he wants: it is all the same—to eat the apples, or to be a boy who eats them if he can. No, he must go to the giants! He belongs to them. You can see how much bigger he is than when you first arrived! He is bigger since yesterday."

"He looks as much like that hideous green lump in his hand as a boy could!"

"It suits what he is becoming."

"His head and it might swap places!"

"Perhaps they do!"

"Does he want to be a giant?"

"He hates the giants, but he is making himself one anyway; he likes their apples! Oh baby, baby, he was just such a darling as you when we found him!"

"He will be very miserable when he realizes he is a giant!"

"Oh, no; he will like it well enough! That is the worst part."

"Will he hate the Little Ones?"

"He will be like the rest; he will not remember us—most likely, he will not believe we exist. He will not care; he will eat his apples."

"Tell me how it happens. I understand your world so little! I come from a world where everything is different."

"I do not know about WORLD. What is it? What more but a word in your beautiful big mouth? That makes it something!"

"Never mind the word; tell me what happens next to Blunty."

"He will wake one morning and find himself a giant—not like you, good giant, but like any other bad giant. You will hardly recognize him, but I will tell you which. He will think he has always been a giant and will not know you, or any of us. The giants have lost themselves, Peony says, and that is why they never smile. I wonder if they are glad because they are bad, or bad because they are not glad. But they cannot be glad when they have no babies! I wonder what BAD means, good giant!"

"I wish I knew no more about it than you!" I returned. "But I try to be good, and intend to keep trying."

"So do I—and that is how I know you are good."

A long silence followed.

"Then you do not know where the babies come from into the wood?" I asked, making one final attempt.

"There is nothing to know there," she answered. "They are in the wood; they grow there."

"Then how is it you never find one before it is fully grown?" I asked.

She furrowed her brow and fell silent for a moment.

"They're not there till they're finished," she said.
