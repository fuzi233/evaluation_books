The three or four "To Let" boards had clung to the low paling fence as long as anyone in the little triangular Square could recall, though if they had ever stood upright, it must have been centuries prior. Now they sagged precariously, overhanging the wood like a row of rotting axe heads poised to cleave a passerby's skull, yet failing to sever a single soul from the old house. There was little traffic to begin with; the real stream of humanity flowed a furlong distant, bypassing the labyrinthine tenements and alleys that had strangled the neighborhood since the house was built, leaving it a hollow throat waiting to be choked. Probably, the crumbling brick edifice merely survived because the leases required for its eviction hadn't yet expired, a temporary suspension before the inevitable clearance of the entire block.

It was a blooming ruin of faded red brick, its walls scarred with the defunct crests and clasped hands of insurance firms long dust. Children of the Square had once swung on the low iron gate at the alley's end until nothing remained but a solid, jagged top bar. The alley itself ran past basement windows boarded tight, where tramps scrawled their cryptic, fading warnings in chalk. Water spilled constantly from the encroaching roof of the neighbor's house, washing the path into an uneven, muddy ribbon where stray cats and dogs held court. The chances of finding a tenant here were so slim that the agents had abandoned all pretense of maintaining the "To Let" signs; they leaned drunkenly, illegible and broken.

For six months, Barrett Oleron had crossed this desolate square twice daily on his commute from his cramped lodgings to his workspace ten minutes away. For six months, no splintering sign had ever threatened his path. Perhaps he simply avoided the side running past the broken gate and the rain-lashed entrance alley. But fate, or perhaps mere chance, compelled him one morning to cut across the broken gate. Pausing before one of the leaning notices, he saw the agent's name and a message, scratched in ink that dated back to Oleron's boyhood, stating that the key could be obtained at Number Six.

Barrett lived a life of peculiar inconvenience. He paid exorbitant rents for a separate bedroom and workroom, a luxury most struggling authors without private means couldn't justify. Beyond that, he rented space in a warehouse to store the majority of his grandmother's furniture, a hoard of antiques that grew heavier with every passing season. His books, needed in bed, often sat half a mile away in his studio; urgent notes meant to be sent during the day frequently ended up buried in the pockets of a coat hanging behind his bedroom door. Divided domiciles bred chaos. So, brought abruptly to a halt by the hatchet-like notice-board, Barrett looked down through sparse privet bushes at the boarded basements, then upward at the blank, grimy windows of the first floor, and finally to the second floor and the flat stone coping of the lead roof. He stood for a minute, thumbing his lean, freshly shaven jaw, before glancing at the board again and walking slowly across the square to Number Six.

He knocked. He waited three minutes. The door stood ajar, yet silence answered. Knocking again, a long-nosed man in shirt sleeves emerged.

"I was arsking a blessing on our food," the man said, his voice thick with severe explanation.

Barrett asked for the key to the old house. The man retreated. Five more minutes passed on the step; the man returned, mouth full of whatever sustenance he had claimed to bless, and announced that the key was lost.

"But you won't want it," the man muttered. "The entrance door isn't locked, and a push opens any of the others. I'm the agent for it, if you're thinking of taking it—"

Barrett recrossed the square, descended the two steps at the broken gate, and passed down the alley to turn into the old wide doorway. To the right, immediately inside, stairs plunged into the roomy, shadowed cellars. Before him rose a staircase broad, handsome, and filthily grand, with a carved railing. Barrett ascended, carefully avoiding the grime-stained rail and the dirty walls, stopping at the first landing. A door facing him was boarded up, but he pushed at one to his right, and an insecure bolt or staple yielded with a sickening snap. He stepped into the empty first floor.

He lingered there for a quarter-hour, letting the silence of the dust-choked air settle over him, before descending and returning to the house of the key-less man.

"How much is the rent?" Barrett asked.

The man named a sum, a figure rendered low by the ghastly reputation of the neighborhood and the abominable, unrepaired state of the premises.

"Is it possible to rent just a single floor?" Barrett pressed.

The long-nosed man shook his head uncertainly. "They might..."

"Who are they?"

"The firm of lawyers in Lincoln's Inn," the man replied, giving Barrett the name.

"You might mention my name—Barrett," Barrett added, trying to plant a sense of urgency.

Work pressures prevented Barrett from visiting Lincoln's Inn that afternoon, but the following day he made the trip. Instantly, the lawyer offered him the entire house for purchase at fifty pounds down, the balance remaining on mortgage. It took Barrett half an hour to disabuse the lawyer's mind of the notion that he sought to buy, convincing him instead that Barrett desired only to rent a single floor. This shift created a series of hesitant pauses, murmurs, and stutters regarding feasibility; the lawyer was far from certain he could accommodate such a request. Finally, however, Barrett extracted a promise: provided the "To Let" boards remained standing, and provided an agreement was reached that if the whole house were ever let, the arrangement would terminate automatically without further notice, something could be arranged. To Barrett, the specter of the old place suddenly leasing over his head seemed the slimmest of risks, a trivial gamble compared to the chaos of his current existence. He promised a decision within a week.

On the morrow, he returned to inspect the house from cellar to garret, then went home to bathe.

He was utterly captivated by the section he had decided to claim as his own. Scoured clean, repainted, and filled with his grandmother's dusty treasures, it ought to be a sanctuary of charm. He rushed to the storage warehouse to jog his memory of his forgotten belongings and to take precise measurements, then headed to a decorator's. He was currently drowning in his regular work, wishing the notice-board had caught his eye months earlier or later, but the quickest path forward was to suspend work entirely until after his move.

Two weeks later, the first floor gleamed in a tender, elderflower white. The paint was dry, and Barrett stood in the midst of his installation, animated and delighted. He rubbed his hands together as he polished and arranged his grandmother's effects—the tall lattice-paned china cupboard heavy with Derby and Mason and Spode porcelain, the large folding Sheraton table, the long, low bookshelves (two of which he had had copied), the chairs, the Sheffield candlesticks, the riveted rose-bowls. He set these treasures against walls of wood panelled in the happiest proportions, moulded and coffered around the low-seated window-recesses in a mood of gaiety and rest that modern builders no longer understood. The ceilings soared, faintly painted with an ancient pattern of stars; even the tapering mouldings of his iron fireplace were delicately designed, like jewelry. Barrett walked about, hands still rubbing, frequently pausing for the sheer, irrational joy of glimpsing one white room give way to another.

"Charming, charming!" he whispered to himself, a smile playing on his lips. "I wonder what Elsie Bengough will think of this!"

He purchased a bolt and a Yale lock for his door, sealing off his quarters from the rest of the silent, echoing house. Now, if he needed a book for bed, it was merely a step away into the next room. Throughout the process, he felt a swelling tide of luck, convinced he had stumbled upon a treasure. He installed a hat-rack in the little square hall, hanging up his hats, caps, and coats. Late at night, passersby traversing the small triangular square, looking up over the little serried row of wooden "To Let" hatchets, could see the warm light within Oleron's red blinds. Or they could witness the sudden darkening of one blind followed by the illumination of another, as Barrett, candlestick in hand, moved from room to room, making final adjustments to his furniture or preparing to resume the work his removal had interrupted.

Paul Oleron regarded the world with a benevolence that the world rarely returned, though at forty-four, he rarely bothered to calculate the deficit in his ledger. To weigh the scales would not change the outcome; it would only depress him further. He had walked this narrow path, chosen in the fevered, idealistic days of his youth when he believed a life devoid of nobility and selflessness was unworthy of existence. Now, the illusion was thinning, revealing a starker reality, yet he refused to anticipate the inevitable twilight of his powers where he might regret his rigid, lofty standards.

For now, his displacement into the old house, marked by insurance stamps embedded in the brick, served only as a brief interruption to *Romilly Bishop*. At fifteen chapters, the novel lay dormant, waiting for the dust to settle. As the tall man with the ascetic, monk-like face rearranged his new domain, he projected a spinster-like precision. Having lived twenty years in a dozen shifting accommodations—from garrets to flats—he had learned that method saved both time and temper. He had arranged with Mrs. Barrett, a stout Welshwoman whose voice retained the falsetto and heavy Merionethshire accent despite her London residency, to manage his breakfast and the Saturday cleaning. Indeed, he welcomed domestic labor as a necessary respite from the strain of creation.

His kitchen, adjacent to a strip containing a modern bath, overlooked a narrow alley. At one end stood a large closet with a square sliding hatch near the top—a former powder chamber where an elaborately dressed head had once received the click of a pistol. Puzzling over this relic, Oleron smiled faintly, moved by a sentiment he could not name. The space would need a new function, likely serving as his larder. While rummaging on an upper shelf, deep within the wall, he uncovered two mushroom-shaped wooden wig stands. Their origin was a mystery; perhaps the painters had unearthed them. With such limited storage, these finds offered a sliver of ingenuity for housing his linens and the accumulated, fragile treasures of his papers.

It was early spring when Oleron took possession, driven by an anxiety to publish *Romilly* before the following autumn. Yet, he resisted the urge to force the hand of his art. He sensed the book's crucial importance in his evolution and trusted it to dictate its own tempo. In his previous workroom, the narrative had begun to breathe on its own; he hoped the distraction of moving would soon fade, allowing the story to resume its march. On a crisp March morning, he stepped out, returning laden with two great bunches of yellow daffodils. He placed one on the mantelpiece beside a pair of Sheffield sticks and the other on his desk, then opened the half-finished manuscript.

Before diving into the words, however, he consulted his cheque-book and pass-book in a small rosewood cabinet. The arithmetic brought a thoughtful furrow to his brow. His installation costs had exceeded his estimates, leaving a balance of just under fifty pounds with no immediate influx.

"Hm! I'd forgotten rugs and chintz curtains," he murmured, recalling the expenses. "A pity to ruin the place for want of ten pounds... Well, *Romilly* simply *must* be out by autumn. So, here goes."

He drew his papers forward, but the ink remained dry. The square outside possessed a clamorous life that threatened to drown his focus. Hawkers cried from their carts; at noon, a troop of schoolchildren swung on his gate; later, an itinerant musician strummed a mandolin beneath his window. Oleron pushed up the sash and tossed a penny, hoping for silence, but the noise persisted. He returned to his table, only to find himself drifting. Moments passed in which he stared at the room, reconstructing its former life: a buttercup satin settee? A crystal chandelier hanging from the high molding? Where had the tambour-frame stood? The mental fatigue was useless. Frank idleness, he realized, was preferable to this futile exhaustion. He planned a walk but succumbed to a doze in his chair.

"Waking at half-past four is no triumph," he yawned upon stirring. "I must do better tomorrow." A wave of delicious laziness washed over him, tempting him to break an evening appointment.

The next morning, however, he sat down without answering any of his three letters—two bills and a note from Miss Bengough. The air was bright with white and blue skies, a noisy wind carrying a subtle shift in the colors of growing things. Light flooded the room in fitful bursts as clouds rolled northeast, reflecting off the polished table and worn floor. The street noises resumed their rhythm.

Oleron dotted patterns on his page, then stopped to align his jar of daffodils precisely against a creamy panel. He wrote a sentence that flowed for two lines before fragmenting into notes and jottings. For a moment, he convinced himself this was work, then paced the room. An idea struck him: the space needed color. It was currently too pale, mild and sweet like a kindly old face, yet devitalized and wan. It required a robust note, warmer flowers, perhaps gay fabrics for the window seats.

"Of course, I really can't afford it," he muttered as he measured the width of a window recess with a two-foot ruler.

Suddenly, his posture shifted to intense curiosity. He rose, rubbing his hands with gentle glee. "Oho, oho! These look like window boxes, nailed up. We must investigate! Yes, this is an adventure!"

Two windows faced the wall, and beyond the open bedroom door, another existed on the same plane. The seats had been repainted repeatedly, yet Oleron's probing finger detected faint traces of old nail heads beneath the fresh layers. Below the ledge, an old keyhole had been puttied over. Pulling out his penknife, he worked carefully for five minutes, then retreated to the kitchen for a hammer and chisel.

Driving the chisel under the seat, he prised the lid loose. Using the knife to sever the hinge and wedges, he positioned a wooden block and struck it gently.

"Now for our little mystery—"

The impact of the mallet sounded strangely brutal in the sweet, pale apartment. The panelling rang and vibrated like a sounding board. Echoes seemed to awaken from the cellar to the garrets above, creeping along his nerves. Pausing to dampen the noise with a duster, he pried the lid open. The paint flaked, the rusty nails squeaked, and the lid revealed the hollow space beneath. Inside, save for a few inches of scurf, mold, and ancient cobwebs, there was nothing.

"No treasure there," Oleron noted, amused at his fleeting fantasy. "*Romilly* will still have to see the autumn. Let us inspect the others."

He turned to the second window. Raising the remaining seats occupied most of the afternoon. Like the first, the bedroom seat yielded nothing. But from the second seat in the sitting room, he extracted a yielding, folded mass, thick with dust. Carrying it to the kitchen, he brushed away the grime and spread it out.

It was a large bag of ancient, frieze-like fabric, shaped like an irregular triangle with wide flaps and remnants of leather straps. The top layer was a faded yellowish-brown, while the underside revealed rich, varying shades of crimson depending on how the folds lay.

"Now, whatever could that have been?" Oleron mused, staring at the enigma. "I give it up. Whatever it is, it has settled my work for today..."

He folded the object carelessly and shoved it into a corner of the kitchen. Taking pans, brushes, and an old knife, he returned to the sitting room to clean the newly discovered compartments, lining them with paper. Once finished, he transferred his spare boots, books, and papers into the boxes, closing the lids. Amused by his little adventure, yet slightly anxious for the return to serious labor, he stood amidst the quiet of the room, the mystery of the crimson bag lingering like a shadow over his thoughts.

A flicker of irritation pricked at Oleron when Miss Bengough dismissed the room itself with a casual glance, treating the space he had found so uniquely captivating as mere background noise. Indeed, she rarely raised her eyes to look around, her attention fixed elsewhere. Yet, this indifference suited him; she had always been a creature of the immediate, careless of decorum, appearing more vibrant when devouring biscuits from a paper sack than when adhering to social conventions. At thirty-four, unmarried, and possessing a robust, florid complexion—the hue of a fresh butter cake or a deep pink dog-rose—she cut a striking figure. She moved with sudden, expansive gestures, leaving trails of swirling air where her veils and scarves caught the breeze like trapped spirits. While Oleron earned his modest living through the labor of his pen, Miss Bengough "pulled a better living out of the pool," leveraging her cunning connections among drapers and milliners to acquire a wardrobe that was both varied and ostentatious.

When Oleron first arrived in his new lodging, a month prior, he had heard the frantic rustle of her skirts ascending the stairs, followed by a single, resonant knock. Now, standing in the doorway, the scent of the street clung to her garments. She tossed a bundle of journals onto a chair without ceremony.

"Do not bother to stop for me," she declared, chewing rapidly on a large hatpin as she stripped off her hat and veil. "I wasn't sure if you were settled yet, so I brought sandwiches. Coffee, I presume? No need to rise—I'll handle the kettle myself."

"Oh, quite unnecessary," Oleron replied, a welcome relief washing over him. "I'm actually relieved to be interrupted."

He cleared his desk, stacking papers away, and watched her vanish into the kitchen, the sound of running water filling the silence. Ten minutes later, he returned bearing a tray laden with coffee and food. They seated themselves opposite one another, separated by the small table holding their sustenance.

"Well," Oleron asked as she poured the dark liquid, "how does the new place strike you?"

"Hm..." She paused, considering. "One would think you were preparing for a wedding, Paul."

He chuckled softly. "Nonsense. But surely it's superior to some of our previous haunts?"

"Perhaps. I certainly preferred the last one, despite the pitch-black ceiling and the lack of a tap. How is *Romilly* progressing?"

Oleron rubbed his chin thoughtfully. "Hm... I must confess, I am not thriving with it. However, as you often say, it will likely be perfect by the time it sees the light."

"Stuck?"

"Precisely stuck."

"Would you mind reading a portion of it to me?"

Oleron hesitated. For years, he had shared fragments of his work with Miss Bengough. Her feedback was invariably sharp, practical, and occasionally illuminating, though she steadfastly shielded him from knowledge of her own writing, dismissing it as mere filler compared to his "real work."

"I fear I cannot," he admitted, continuing his dry shave. "In fact, Elsie, I haven't written a single new line of it recently—not a word. Of course, that implies progress in a certain, alarming direction. I intend to reconstruct the entire narrative."

Miss Bengough gasped. "Reconstructing?"

"Yes. I plan to alter Romilly's fundamental nature. Lately, I feel I am failing to capture her essence. As she currently stands, I have grown distant from her."

"But—" She protested, her voice rising. "You had her so vivid, so undeniably alive, Paul!"

Oleron offered a faint smile. He expected this reaction. There was undoubtedly a mirror image of Miss Bengough within his fictional creation, which explained why she found her so "real."

"But are you being serious, Paul?" she asked, her eyes wide and round.

"Absolutely."

"You mean you intend to discard those fifteen chapters?"

"Not exactly."

"The magnificent love scene?"

"I would hesitate greatly, and only do so to replace it with something I deem superior."

"And that exquisite, beautiful description of Romilly on the shore?"

"It need not be lost," he murmured, feeling a twinge of unease.

But Miss Bengough waved a broad, airy hand and let loose a torrent of words. "Truly, you are pushing yourself too hard!" she exclaimed. "I wish you would remember you are flesh and blood, living in a world of men! Do not mistake me; I would never urge you to lower your standards. Yet, bringing your art down to a level humanity can comprehend would not be a lowering—it would be a betrayal. You are sometimes so godlike! To destroy those fifteen chapters would be a wicked, criminal squandering of your gifts. Think sensibly. You have labored for nearly two decades, standing on the precipice of your triumph. Your finances are precarious—you need not admit it, I know you are near destitution—and here you propose to excise the element that will secure your fame, replacing it with something nine times out of ten that no soul will desire. You are testing my patience!"

As she spoke, Oleron shook his head slowly. This dynamic was ancient between them. The boisterous, capable journalist was an ideal companion until they reached a certain threshold, beyond which they stood alone. Elsie often claimed that if she possessed even a tenth of Oleron's genius, she could achieve anything, treating talent as a divisible commodity. She failed to grasp that his gift was qualitative, essential, and indivisible—a spirit that informed his work but remained opaque to her. Their minds diverged at that point.

"Yes, yes, yes," he said finally, weariness creeping into his tone. "Practically, you are entirely correct, and I have no rebuttal. If I could entrust *Romilly* to you, you would undoubtedly craft a masterpiece. But that is impossible. And frankly, I begin to doubt if she is worth the trouble. You understand what follows."

"What does it follow?" she pressed, her tone blunt.

"Well," he said, a wan smile touching his lips, "when one becomes convinced a task is unworthy of effort, what happens? One simply ceases."

Miss Bengough cast her gaze upward, searching the ceiling for divine intervention against such an obstinate man.

"Nonsense!" she cried eventually. "Last time I saw you, you were practically drowning in *Romilly*, churning out four chapters a week. Had you not moved, you would have completed three-quarters of the book by now. What drove you to abandon your most vital project right in the midst of it?"

Oleron attempted to deflect her with a list of grievances, but she refused to budge. Perhaps subconsciously, she suspected the truth: he was utterly exhausted by the squalor of his existence. Twenty years of garrets, attic rooms, and dingy flats had worn him down. He was tired of the grime and the shabbiness. The reward seemed infinitely distant, or if it lay close, he no longer felt the desperate hunger to seize it. To tell a man on the brink of collapse that one final push suffices is cruel if he lacks the strength to make it.

"Regardless," Oleron summarized, "I am happier here than I have been in ages. That is some form of justification."

"And you produce no work," Miss Bengough countered sharply.

Then, a simmering petulance in Oleron's chest boiled over. "And pray, why must I toil endlessly?" he demanded. "Does not happiness matter? I do not claim to hate my work when finished, but the act of creating it has become an unbearable yoke. Occasionally, in moments amidst many weeks, it ignites with a glow and thrill I recall from my youth. Those were the golden days. Now, at forty-four, it has devolved into drudgery. Few people desire it, and increasingly, neither do I. Were any sensible man to ask if I am a fool for persisting, I believe I would concede the point."

Miss Bengough's rosy face grew solemn. "Yet you foresaw all this, Paul, many years ago—and you chose it anyway."

"How could I have known?" he retorted. "I did not know. My heart whispered it to me, and I trusted it. Youth believes it possesses wisdom; then, suddenly, one discovers they are nearly fifty."

"Forty-four, Paul—"

"—Forty-four, then. And the realization strikes that the enchantment lies not ahead, but behind. Yes, I knew and I chose, if that constitutes knowing and choosing... but what a costly price youth demands for its errors!"

Her eyes dropped to the floor. Without looking up, she asked, "Do you regret it, Paul?"

"Do I not?" he seized the question. "On my honor, I have recently begun to suspect I do! What recompense do I receive for all this sacrifice?"

"You know precisely what you obtain," she answered.

He understood her implication. A nod of the finger, and she would reveal herself. She knew the truth—that he could have done no better for himself—but she dared not speak it aloud. Had he proposed marriage during the past decade, she would have answered calmly, "Very well; when?" Yet, he had never considered it.

"Your endeavor is genuine work," she continued gently. "Without artists like you, we jackals would cease to exist. You and a handful of others bear the weight of everything upon your shoulders."

By the time the embers of Oleron's fire glowed like dying stars against the encroaching dark, his mind was already a battlefield. He sat hunched over the heat, wrestling with Miss Bengough's prophecy that shadows waited for him in his work. A cold, hard certainty crystallized within him: it would have been infinitely kinder if she had kept her warnings locked inside her head. To speak of difficulty, he realized with a sinking stomach, was to conjure it. Words themselves became a kind of mortar, binding together the doubts and fears that otherwise might remain scattered. By voicing the obstacles, she had built them into walls. She had inadvertently birthed an enemy dedicated to the ruin of *Romilly*.

And, in a manner both absurd and irrefutable, women possessed a strange power to attach this malignancy directly to the surroundings where one lived. *"You'll never finish *Romilly* here,"* she had declared. But why? Was it the sheer luxury of the house—the gentle, suffocating comfort—that drained the vital juices of ambition and reduced a man to sloth? The cottage was undeniably charming, a haven of beauty, yet it held no such seductive poison. No, Elsie had simply missed the mark.

Driven by a sudden need to refute her, Oleron shifted his chair, turning to survey the room. It seemed to smile at him, a benevolent, knowing grin illuminated by the firelight. He mirrored the expression, though it tasted of pity for a space that had somehow been maligned in her eyes. Even the slight pallor he had noted earlier vanished under the soft, amber glow. The chintz curtains, heavy with floral patterns and trellises, hung in long, silent folds; the leather-bound volumes in the cases drank the light with a richness that spoke of age and wisdom. If anything had been lacking, it was Elsie herself, standing out of step with the quiet dignity of the room.

A strange thought seized him, and he allowed it to expand. Yes, the room had, quite unintentionally, played a trick on Miss Bengough that afternoon. It had acted as a mirror, reflecting a stark contrast between her nature and the atmosphere around her. If one were to grant the ridiculous notion that the room lacked vigor—though it hardly did—it stood in opposition to Elsie's overwhelming abundance. She was a creature of redundancy, of "muchness," whereas Oleron, in his soul, leaned toward the austere.

In that moment, a startling revelation struck him. He visualized Elsie again as she had stood before him: large, showy, a moist, pink flush radiating from her skin like the bloom of a prize flower. Instantly, the image suffered in his mind. He recognized now, with a jolt of horror, that he had been critiquing her all along, unconsciously dissecting her machinery. Her humid, melting appearance was not natural; it was the product of analyzable processes. Lurking behind that façade, he saw now, was a vague, shadowy shape emblematic of mortality—a grim counterpoint to the vitality he needed. For ten years, they had shared intimacies, yet he had never dared propose. Now, sitting in the firelight, a profound gratitude washed over him for having never taken that step.

Sudden shame flushed his face. Had Elsie been there, witnessing this internal scrutiny, he would have crumbled before her. What right did he have to dissect the woman who had been his anchor, his loyal comrade, the one whose support he would count on if the world fell away? To treat her thoughts as a puzzle to be solved was to betray a sacred trust. He was an ingrate, a cad.

For ten minutes, he sat frozen, staring into the flames as the humiliating red faded from his cheeks. The only sound in the silence was a faint, musical tinkling drifting from the kitchen—the rhythmic drip of water from a poorly tightened tap into a vessel below. His fingers began to beat mechanically against his knee, counting the falls, trying to drown out the rhythm with a trivial motion. Slowly, the shame receded, leaving him cool and detached. When he finally resumed his meditation, he was unaware that he had returned to exactly where he had left off.

His thoughts drifted back to the root of his unease. It was not merely her physical floridity that he had judged; it was the vast chasm between their minds. There was no comfort in realizing how different they truly were. He felt no relief in remembering that they had once walked side by side in understanding; instead, a torrent of questions surged forth. Their intellects diverged sharply. Looking back, he could no longer deny that their paths had never truly coincided. True, he had read his writings to her, and she had spoken with apparent comprehension, but what happens when the foundation of friendship is revealed to be built on a lie? He questioned everything now. For a fleeting second, the thought crossed his mind that a man who demands more from a friend than they can give risks losing them forever, but he pushed the idea aside.

Silence returned, broken only by the distant tap. And then, slowly, the truth emerged. If these observations applied to Elsie Bengough, they applied with even greater force to the creation she inspired: Romilly Bishop. Since he could not bring himself to speak harsh words of Elsie aloud, he gave free rein to his thoughts regarding the fictional character.

There was no doubt lingering in his heart anymore. He hated the central figure of his novel. As he had described her, she overwhelmed the senses; she was coarse-fibred, over-colored, rank. The moment he articulated this hatred, it became absolute. Just as Gulliver had mocked the Brobdingnagian maids-of-honour, so Oleron now despised Romilly. Mentally and spiritually, she was a void—unsensitive, limited, common. The model, which had stuck out through fifteen vulgar and blatant chapters, had blinded him to the flaws until now. He marveled that it had taken so long for dawn to break upon him.

And *this* was meant to be his Beatrice, his vision! As Elsie, she was destined to enter the furnace of his art and emerge transformed into the Woman all men desire. Her thoughts were to be distilled from his finest imaginings, her form carved from his dearest dreams, set in a landscape worthy of her worth. He had brooded over her for months, feeling her stir within him like a mother sensing a child's life, before penning chapter after chapter.

And those fifteen sodden chapters were what he had produced!

A surge of determination shook him. They must go. All fifteen chapters of her. It was settled. For what would replace them, his mind was a blank canvas, but he focused on the immediate necessity. A man was not absolved of taking the wrong path simply because the right one had not yet revealed itself. Something better would surely follow if it was fated to arrive; meanwhile, action was required.

He rose, retrieved the manuscript pages, and spread them on his desk. Before casting them into the fire, he read them over once more, letting the weight of his failure settle over him. Then, instead of tossing them, he let them slip from his hand, catching nothing but the air.

His consciousness snapped back to the kitchen tap. It had a gamut of four or five notes, ringing irregular changes that were foolishly sweet, like a dulcimer. In his mind, Oleron could see every gathering drop, the tiny tremble on the lip of the brass, the percussion of its fall—"Plink... plunk..."—minimized almost to silence. Following the lowest note, there seemed to be a brief phrase, repeated irregularly, and soon Oleron found himself waiting for its return. It was quite pretty, a haunting melody born of water.

Yet it offered no wakefulness. Instead, it pulled him under. Oleron dozed over the fire, the rhythm lulling him into sleep.

When he awoke, the fire had burned low, its embers glowing like coals in the hearth, while the candleflames licked the rims of the Sheffield iron stands. With sluggish movements, he rose, yawned, and performed his nightly ritual of locking doors and fastening windows. He passed into his bedroom and sank into a deep, soundless slumber.

But the following morning brought a curious sequel. Mrs. Barrett did not knock at his door, as she often did, but tapped gently on the wooden panel beyond it, where his bed lay. Oleron rose, donned his dressing gown, and admitted her. He was not aware that, as the door swung open, he had been humming a tune. Mrs. Barrett lingered, her hand resting on the knob, her face averted slightly, a soft smile playing on her lips.

"My dear!" her soft falsetto trilled. "But that will be a very *old* tune, Mr. Oleron! I shall not have heard it for forty years!"

"What tune?" Oleron asked, bewildered.

When the pitch of life drops so low that the spirit turns inside out, reversing every sweetness into a horror, the connection between human beings dissolves. "I remember the number clearly!" he cried, his eyes wide with excitement. "Three ones and three threes! Eleven-one-three-three-three!"

One of the inspectors waved him aside dismissively. "Has anyone entered?" he asked the constable.

"No, sir."

"Then you, Brackley, come with me. You, Smith, hold the line. A squad is on its way."

Brackley followed the inspectors down the alley and into the shadow of the house. They ascended the wide, ornately carved staircase, the wood groaning under their weight.

"This fellow hasn't stepped out much lately," one inspector muttered, kicking aside a pile of rotting leaves and crumpled paper lying outside Oleron's door. "We shouldn't need to knock. Break a pane, Brackley."

The door featured two glazed panels. With a sharp *crunch*, glass shattered, and Brackley shoved his arm through the jagged hole to pull back the latch.

"Faugh!" one inspector gagged, recoiling as they crossed the threshold. "Ventilate this place, quickly! It smells like a hearse!"

Outside, the crowd watched as the red blinds were rolled up and the windows thrown wide, admitting the blinding daylight.

"That's better," an inspector sighed, leaning out of the window to draw a ragged breath of fresh air. "Seems to be the bedroom. Will you go in, Simms, while I check the rest?"

Inside the room, the blinds were drawn tight, though the windows offered no respite. On the bed lay a man who appeared waxen, his skin stretched tight over emaciated bones. He raised a trembling hand to block the torrent of sunlight pouring in, squinting against the torture of the brightness. His mind seemed fractured; he could not believe his ears, for there were two uniformed men bending over him, demanding to know where "she" was. He shook his head mutely.

"The woman Bengough... she goes by the name of Miss Elsie Bengough, do you hear? Where is she?" Simms asked, his voice strained. "No use, Brackley. Get him up. Be gentle with him. I think I'll stick my head out the window myself..."

Simms had already cleared Oleron's study, finding nothing of note, and was now in the kitchen. He kicked aside a mountain of vegetable refuse that cluttered the floor, the stench of decay rising to meet him. The kitchen window lacked a blind and was cast in shadow by the blank wall of the house opposite across the alley. At first glance, the room appeared empty.

However, as the inspector kicked at the withered flowers, his eye caught a disturbance. A shuffling track, clearly not made by his boot, had been dragged across the floor and stopped abruptly at a cupboard in the far corner. Above the door of this cupboard sat a square panel that appeared to slide on metal runners. The door itself was shut.

The inspector approached slowly, extending a hand toward the tiny knob. He slid the hatch along its groove with a soft, metallic squeak.

Instantly, he jerked backward involuntarily.

Framed within the opening, falling forward slightly before jamming back into its frame, was an object that resembled a large, lumpy pudding, wrapped in a pudding bag of faded, blood-red frieze.

"Ay!" the inspector breathed, the word barely audible.

To reseal the hatch, he would have to push the "pudding" back with his bare hand. Some unspoken, primal horror prevented him from doing so. Instead, he reached for the handle of the cupboard itself. It yielded with immense resistance, revealing so much weight behind it that he had to brace his shoulder against it merely to close it again. As he forced it shut, however, a triangle of black-and-white checked skirt stuck out, hovering a few inches above the floorboards.

Stepping back into the small hall, he called out, "All clear!"

Downstairs, Oleron had finally been dressed. Still using his hands to shield his eyes from the light, his mind was a chaotic storm. Too many things were happening. He could not comprehend the extraordinary accumulation of dead flowers scattered throughout the rooms, nor the presence of the police officers. He couldn't fathom why one of them was summoning a four-wheeler and a stretcher, nor what the heavy, mysterious article being moved about in the kitchen might be—it was surely *his* kitchen.

"What's the matter?" he mumbled, his voice thick with sleep and confusion.

Then, a murmur rose from the square below, accompanied by the clatter of wheels as a four-wheeler stopped outside. An officer leaned close to his ear, and as Oleron whispered something in response, the officer erupted into a rapid string of words—something about "used in evidence against you." They hoisted him to his feet, supporting his weight as they guided him toward the door.

No, Oleron realized, the pieces simply did not fit.

They carried him down the stairs and out into the alley. Oleron sensed angry shouts, confused and indistinct, gathering momentum. He surmised that a mob sought to lynch someone.
