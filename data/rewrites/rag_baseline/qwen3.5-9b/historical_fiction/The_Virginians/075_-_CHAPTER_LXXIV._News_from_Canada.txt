The silence in the castle was heavy, a physical weight that pressed against the ribs of the young man who had just returned. Two weeks had elapsed since his departure, a duration that stretched into an eternity for someone consumed by obsession. He stepped back into the orbit of Madame de Bernstein, finding her relieved to shed the constraints of her father's household. As the carriage wheels rattled away from the estate, the dam broke. She spoke with a newfound freedom, though the specific contours of her confrontation with her niece remained locked behind a veil of silence.

Her tone shifted, however, becoming brittle and nervous whenever the subject of the niece arose. The old lady laughed, a sharp, jagged sound devoid of malice, yet tinged with a frantic energy. She dismissed the notion of molding the girl, speaking instead of Eugene, her nephew, whom she declared doomed to eternal subservience under a wife's rule. Order, she insisted, was the only medicine available. Her assessment of the American gentleman, Mr. Van den Bosch, was pragmatic: a shrewd steward, a man of small means but large utility. She treated the Countess's lineage with a casual indifference, brushing aside rumors of a convict mother transported from England, a woman who had supposedly beaten hemp in various prisons. To the old lady, birth was a trivial variable; the Countess possessed a name of sufficient magnitude to purchase entry into any salon. Whether her father was a grocer, a smuggler, or a dealer in human souls mattered less than the fact that the Countess of Castlewood could afford to be anyone's daughter. Once the nephew presented her, the duty was clear: stand by her.

Mr. Warrington, observing the deep currents binding his aunt to her nephew, hoped she would extend similar warmth to the niece. He mentioned his recent visits to Mr. Hagan and his wife, seeking his aunt's blessing. The request hit a wall of obstinate refusal. Madame de Bernstein demanded that Lady Maria's name remain buried, then launched into a monologue lasting two hours, populated exclusively by ghosts of the past. She recounted anecdotes regarding her niece that, given the innocence of the current generation, I shall omit. Yet, I must note the complexity of the creature: if she sinned, she was not alone in her transgression; if she repented, others ought to follow suit. Hagan, after shedding the stage, lived a life of exemplary piety, distinguished by elegance and eloquence in the pulpit. His wife, conversely, adopted extreme sectarian views, earning great respect within her fold. When I last saw her, she spoke of a peculiar spiritual illumination—a glow I suspected at the time stemmed from the liberal consumption of spirits. Still, I remembered the kindness they showed me and mine during a season when sympathy was scarce, when many a Pharisee turned away.

My standing with my fickle aunt fluctuated like the tides, dependent on the whims of the moment. Each brother, in turn, was embraced and then cast adrift. My zenith of favor coincided with the success of my play. Introduced to the town wits, I held my ground among them, deemed sufficiently bred by the macaronis and the fashionable set. Had my purse been deeper, or my ambition sharper, I might have carved a career among them. Instead, I loved a pair of kind eyes more than the brightest orbs of the Gunnings or Chudleighs, or all the painted beauties of the Ranelagh ring. Because I favored your mother, children, my tastes were branded low, a source of lamentation for my genteel family. My godly Lady Warrington and my worldly Madame Bernstein united in their disapproval, laying their elderly heads together in sorrow.

"Why, with his name, he might marry anybody," murmured Meek Religion, keeping one eye on Heaven and the other on the main chance. "I meddle with no man's affairs, and admire genius," replied Uncle, "but it is a pity you consort with poets and authors... and that, when you might have had a lovely creature with a hundred thousand pounds, you let her slip and make up to a country girl without a penny-piece."

"But if I had promised her, uncle?" I asked.

"Promise, promise!" he retorted, spinning on his heel. "These things are matters of arrangement and prudence, demanding a careful lookout. When you first committed yourself to little Miss Lambert, you had not yet seen the lovely American lady your mother wished you to marry, as a good mother naturally would. Your duty to your mother, nephew—your duty to the Fifth Commandment—would have warranted your breaking with Miss L. and fulfilling your excellent mother's intentions regarding Miss... What was the Countess's Dutch name? Never mind. A name is nothing; but a plum, Master George, is something to look at! Why, I have my dear little Miley at a dancing school with Miss Barwell, Nabob Barwell's daughter. I do not disguise my wish that the children may contract an attachment enduring through their lives! I tell the Nabob so. We went from the House of Commons one dancing day and saw them. 'Twas beautiful to see the young things walking a minuet together! It brought tears into my eyes, for I have a feeling heart, George, and I love my boy!"

"But if I prefer Miss Lambert, uncle, with twopence to her fortune, to the Countess, with her hundred thousand pounds?"

"Then, sir, you have a singular taste, that's all," the old gentleman said, dismissing me. I understood his vexation; he could not comprehend my inability to view the world through his lens of calculation.

Neither did Aunt Bernstein approve of my engagement or the family I frequented. Their simplicity wore thin on the old woman of the world, annoying her as much as holy water annoys a certain person who is not quite as black as he is painted. She chafed at my constant presence at my sweetheart's side. Rising mightily in her favor, I began to fall again. Once more, Harry became the favorite, and his brother, heaven knows, was not jealous.

Harry was now our family hero. He wrote brief dispatches from the seat of war, simple accounts that Madame Bernstein initially ignored, finding them lacking in drama. We had learned in London of the action on the glorious first of August at Minden, where Wolfe's old regiment was one of the British six securing victory. At that same hour, the young General lay in his bed near Quebec, stricken by fever, perhaps compounded by rage and disappointment at the setback his troops had just suffered.

Arriving in the St. Lawrence in June, the fleet carrying Wolfe and his army disembarked on the last day of the month onto the Island of Orleans. Opposite rose the great cliff of Quebec. Following the great action where his General fell, the dear brother who accompanied the chief wrote home a simple letter. He described his modest role in that glorious day but offered no new details on the action of the 13th of September, save for a recollection from a brother aide-de-camp: the General never spoke after receiving his death wound. Thus, the famous phrases attributed to the dying hero are no more authentic than an oration of Livy or Thucydides.

From his vantage point on the island, situated in the great northern channel of the river, the General hungered for a chance to strike. He landed above the city and below it, shifting constantly, bent on attacking wherever an opening appeared. It was surely a prodigious error on the part of the Marquis of Montcalm to accept battle on equal terms, for the British General lacked artillery. When we executed our famous scaling of the heights and reached the Plains of Abraham, we were closer to the city, certainly, yet as distant as ever from entering it.

The game played between the brave chiefs of those two gallant little armies, spanning from July until Mr. Wolfe won the crowning hazard in September, must have been as riveting as any match between eager players. On the very first night after landing, the sport commenced. At midnight, the French sent a squadron of fireships down upon the British vessels discharging stores at Orleans. Our seamen found it good sport to tow the fireships clear of the fleet and grind them ashore, where they burned out.

Upon learning our ships had entered the river, the French commander marched to Beauport, ahead of the city, establishing a strong position. Once our stores and hospitals were secured, the General crossed from his island to the left shore, drawing nearer to the enemy. He had the ships behind him, but the entire countryside facing him was armed. Indians in the forest seized our advanced parties as they cleared the path, murdering them with horrible tortures. The French matched their Indian allies in savagery. The Montmorenci River rushed between Wolfe and the enemy, blocking direct assault. Neither the Indians nor the city could be taken directly.

Determined to find another point of vulnerability, the General circled the town of Quebec, skirting the left shore beyond. Everywhere was guarded, including his immediate front. Having run the gauntlet of the batteries up and down the river, he returned to his post at Montmorenci. To the right of the French position, across the Montmorenci River—which was fordable at low tide—stood an enemy redoubt. He wanted it. Perhaps defending it would force the French chief out of his lines, bringing on a battle. Wolfe decided to gamble these odds. He planned to ferry the bulk of his army from the Island of Orleans to attack via the St. Lawrence. Timing was critical; at shallow water, his lieutenants, Murray and Townsend, would cross the Montmorenci. On the last day of July, he played this desperate game.
