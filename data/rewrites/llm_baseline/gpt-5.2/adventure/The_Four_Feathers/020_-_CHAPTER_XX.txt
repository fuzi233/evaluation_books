When Durrance came across the dark fields to Guessens, his body-servant was waiting in the passage with that patient, trained stillness which belongs to a house that has long learned its master’s moods.

“You can put the lights out and go to bed,” Durrance said.

He did not need lamps to find his way to the room they called his study, though the name had always been a jest between the walls. It was less a place of books than of barrels and locks, oil-flasks and leather cases, the faint, clean tang of gun-grease living in the boards as a spice lives in a mortar. In the dark he sat awhile, upright and listening—listening not with the leisure of comfort, but with the taut attention of one who must make a map out of sound: the settling of the old house, the far breath of the creek, the thin ticking of a clock as if it were measuring out not time but limitation.

Then he began to move, gently, as though courtesy were due even to furniture. His hands reached for the scattered cups and goblets he had once won, and the metal met his fingers with different temperatures and weights, each one a remembered note. It was odd—how a polished rim could be recognized by a nick no larger than a grain of salt, how a stem could tell its own story by a twist no eye need witness. He drew a kind of comfort from their shapes, and yet the comfort came with a sting: the trophies were still there, but the shining of them was now only a rumor told to him by other people.

He lifted a thin-stemmed goblet and held it as though it were a warm glass just rinsed and set down in a kitchen after dinner. Colchester came back at once—clouds low and grey, ploughed fields dull between hedges, the sound of hooves striking wet ground with that steady, cooking-pot rhythm of a chase. He set it down in its old place and reached for the pewter on the writing-table, a blunt, homely weight, which had once been a convenient holder for pens when pens had been of use. Oxford returned with it: voices in quadrangles, laughter, the scraped chair-legs of rooms where young men spoke too loudly because they believed time was endless.

On the mantelpiece his fingers found the silver-mounted hoof of a favorite horse. It was cold and smooth and obstinately alive in his palm, as if the animal were still stamping somewhere behind the plaster. All of it together—cup, pewter, hoof—made the room a gigantic diary, and he, blind, read it by touch the way a food historian reads a vanished cuisine from a scorched pan dug out of soil: not merely what was eaten, but who ate it, and with what hunger.

At last his hands came to the racks.

He took down the guns and rifles one by one. They had their own speech, as Ethne’s violin had once spoken—wood and metal that carried a private history in their grain. He sat with a Remington across his knee, and the room filled with a remembered heat: a long day in the hills west of Berenice, sun hammering stone until the air itself tasted of iron, the distant cough of a lion moving through open country. He could feel again, in the old grooves of his hands, the patience of the stalk, the slow tightening of intention, the moment just before sunset when the sight settled and the shot went true.

Another weapon called up the Khor Baraka and his first ibex, the dry, peppered smell of rock, the thin wind that carries sound like a string carries vibration. Another murmured of antelope northward of Suakin. Then the little Greener gun, used on midwinter nights in a boat upon this very creek of Salcombe: salt breath, black water with a skin of starlight, the muffled wing-beat and the sudden heavy drop of the first mallard he had ever brought down. He slid his left hand along the barrel’s underside, a familiar, almost domestic caress, and let the butt settle into the hollow of his shoulder. The gesture was perfect; the world it belonged to was not.

For the weapons began to talk too loudly, as Ethne’s violin had once talked too sharply in those first raw days after Harry Feversham went away and left her alone with notes that pricked like nettles. Each lock he handled, each place his thumb knew by instinct, told him the same truth: he could no longer see the sights. The loss stood up before him, definite as a door barred from the inside.

He put the guns away.

And then, with a suddenness that shook him, he was seized by a desire—not to accept, not to endure, but to defy. To disregard his blindness. To pretend it was no hindrance, and to pretend so fiercely that pretense might harden into fact. The craving rose like thirst and carried him, winged, out of the dim, star-sown country of his narrowed nights straight into the East.

The East came to him first by scent and sound. He smelled hot stone and damp rope, dust and sweat and spices bruised underfoot—cumin, coriander, pepper—odors that clung to cloth the way songs cling to memory. Noise gathered itself into images: domes of mosques under a steel-blue sky, a rabble in streets, vendors calling in tongues whose cadences meant more than their words, because the voice is always the first homeland.

He was no longer in Guessens. He was in Port Said, marshalled in the long procession of steamers down the canal. He heard the Arabs coaling the ship—their chant monotonous as work and as necessary as bread—and in the volume of it he could see them in his mind as plainly as if his eyes were whole: an endless chain of naked figures going up and down planks between barges and deck, lurid in the red glare of braziers. The sound was an ecology of labor: the scrape of coal, the slap of feet, the human voice holding men together against fatigue as salt holds meat from spoiling.

Out of the canal he travelled, past the red headlands of Sinai, into the chill of the Gulf of Suez. The Great Bear swung low above the quarterdeck rail; later the Southern Cross began to blaze in the south. He touched at Tor and at Yambo. He saw Yeddah lift its tall white houses from the sea, admired the dark, brine-withered woodwork of carved casements. In the dusk of its roofed bazaars he walked as the homesick walk when they come back after long years: every sound a familiar utensil placed back in its rightful drawer, every scent a remembered dish brought steaming to the table. From Yeddah he crossed between narrowing coral-reefs into the land-locked harbor of Suakin.

And westward from Suakin stretched the desert—his inheritance and his exile both—full of meanings no map could translate. He heard again the quiet padding of camels’ feet in sand, a soft percussion that soothed even as it desolated. He saw, inwardly, the great rock-cones rising sheer from the plain like islands from a rippleless sea, towards which you march all day and get no nearer. He felt the gorgeous, momentary blaze of sunset in the west; the rustle of wind through the short twilight when the west becomes pure pale green and the east the darkest blue; the planets swooping down out of nothing to the earth with an inevitability that made a man feel small and yet strangely kept.

So he tramped to and fro in his room, forgetful of the dark around him, parched with desire as with fever—until, unexpectedly, the dream broke on the homely sound of England: blackbirds and swallows bustling and piping in the garden. Their thin, bright notes, threaded through dawn air, were as domestic as a kettle beginning to sing. He stood still and knew that outside his windows the world was white with morning.

He woke from the East into his own house, and the knowledge came with a heavy quiet: there were to be no more journeys for him. Affliction had caged him and soldered a chain about his leg. He felt his way by the balustrade up the stairs and went to bed. As the sun rose, he fell asleep.

But at Dongola, on the great curve of the Nile southward of Wadi Halfa, the sun had been blazing for hours and the town was awake.

Under the few palm trees before the house of the Emir Wad El Nejoumi, sport was prepared—holiday cruelty, as ordinary there as the baking of bread might be elsewhere. A white prisoner, captured a week before near the wells of El Agia on the great Arbain road by a party of Arabs, had been brought in during the night and now waited for the Emir’s pleasure. The news flew through the streets like a spark through dry grass. Men, women, children poured towards the palms, drawn by the promise of spectacle as surely as flies come to sweetness.

In front of the trees an open space stretched to the gateway of Nejoumi’s house. Behind them sand sloped down, flat and bare, to the river, which lay there shining and indifferent, carrying away all voices as it carried away all blood.

Harry Feversham stood under the palms, guarded by four Ansar. His clothes had been stripped; he wore only a torn, ragged jibbeh and a twist of cotton on his head to shield him from the sun. His bare shoulders and arms were scorched and blistered as meat blisters when held too close to fire. His ankles were fettered; his wrists bound with a rope of palm fibre. An iron collar was locked about his neck with a chain attached, and one soldier held the chain like a leash.

Feversham stood and smiled at the crowd that mocked him, and seemed well pleased—like a lunatic. It was the part he had chosen. If he could sustain it—if he could baffle them into uncertainty whether he was truly daft or an agent carrying promises of help and arms to the disaffected tribes of Kordofan—then there was a chance they would fear to dispose of him themselves and would send him on to Omdurman. It was hard work. Madness, to be believed, must be performed with a steady hand.

Inside the house the Emir and his counsellors debated his fate. On the river-bank, within Feversham’s view, a high gallows stood black and stark against the yellow sand.

He was very glad of the chain about his neck and the fetters on his legs. They helped him betray no panic by assuring him of its futility. The waiting—while the sun climbed and no one came from the gateway—was the worst he had endured. In Berber, through the fortnight, a hope of escape had sustained him; when the lantern shone on him from behind in ruined acres, what had to be done was done so quickly there was no time for fear. Here there was time, and too much of it. Time to anticipate, to see beforehand.

His heart sank until he was faint, as in those distant days when he had heard hounds scuffling and whining in covert and had sat shaking on his horse. He glanced furtively at the gallows and foresaw vultures perched upon his shoulders, fluttering around his eyes. Yet the man had grown during his years of probation. The fear of physical suffering was not uppermost; nor even the dread that he would walk unmanfully to the scaffold. A greater fear gnawed at him: if he died now, here at Dongola, Ethne would never take back the fourth feather, and his fierce hope of the afterwards would never be fulfilled.

Again he was glad of collar and fetters. He summoned his wits and, alone amid enemies, laughed and scraped and grimaced at his tormentors as though their hatred were a clumsy joke told in a foreign accent.

An old hag danced before him, gesticulating, singing a monotonous song. Her gestures were pantomime and menace—promises of mutilation. The words, in a plainness that needed no translation, described the death agonies awaiting him and the tortures of hell thereafter. Feversham understood; something in him shuddered as a body shudders at the smell of blood. But he only imitated her, nodded and mowed as though she were singing of Paradise.

Others pressed close, lifted their war-trumpets, and placed the mouths against his ears. They blew with all their might. The noise was not music but violence—sound used as a club, an acoustic assault that made the air itself bruise. A child danced with delight.

“Do you hear, Kaffir?” it cried. “Do you hear our ombeyehs? Blow louder! Blow louder!”

Feversham clapped his hands, crying out that the music was good, as though the blasting were a wedding tune.

At last a tall warrior came forward with a long, heavy spear. A cry rose, and a space was cleared as for a game. The warrior poised the spear and swung it backward and forward to supple his arm, like a bowler preparing to deliver at cricket. Feversham glanced wildly about; seeing no escape, he suddenly flung out his breast to meet the blow.

But the spear never reached him. As the warrior lunged, one of the guards jerked Feversham’s neck-chain violently from behind, and the prisoner was flung half throttled on his back. Three times—each time to a roar of delight—the pastime was repeated.

Then a soldier appeared in the gateway of Nejoumi’s house.

“Bring him in!” he cried.

Dragged under the arch, across a courtyard, into a dark room, Feversham could see nothing at first. Then his eyes adapted to the gloom. He distinguished a tall bearded man sitting upon an angareb, and two others squatting beside him on the ground. The man on the angareb was the Emir.

“You are a spy of the Government from Wadi Halfa,” Nejoumi said.

“No, I am a musician,” Feversham returned, and laughed happily, like a man who has made a jest and is pleased with his own wit.

Nejoumi made a sign. An instrument with many broken strings—a zither—was handed to the captive. Feversham sat on the ground, bent over it. His fingers moved slowly, fumbling, as though the strings were threads he must find by feel. He breathed hard. Then, coaxed from what was nearly wreckage, a wavering melody emerged.

It was the tune Durrance had listened to in the street of Tewfikieh on the eve of his last journey into the desert; the tune Ethne Eustace had played only the night before in the quiet drawing-room at Southpool. It was the only melody Feversham knew. In that dim room at Dongola, it sounded thin, like a remembered language spoken by someone with an accent—yet it was still itself, stubbornly itself, untranslatable into any other.

When he had done, Nejoumi began again.

“You are a spy.”

“I have told you the truth,” Feversham answered, stubborn.

Nejoumi changed his tone. He called for food, and they set before Feversham the raw liver of a camel, covered with salt and red pepper. The smell of blood rose warm and metallic; the pepper stung the nostrils; the salt lay upon it like frost. Seldom has a man had less inclination to eat, but Feversham ate nonetheless—even that. He knew reluctance would be construed as fear, and fear might condemn him. He chewed with deliberation, forcing the body to obey the role, as one might force down a harsh ration because pride will not permit refusal.

While he ate, Nejoumi questioned him in the silkiest voice about the fortifications of Cairo, the strength of the garrison at Assouan, rumours of dissension between the Khedive and the Sirdar.

To each question Feversham returned the same answer, as if it were a refrain learned by heart.

“How should a Greek know of these matters?”

Nejoumi rose from the angareb and gave an order, rough now.

The soldiers seized Feversham and dragged him back into the sunlight. Water was poured upon the palm-rope that bound his wrists so that the thongs swelled and bit into the flesh, tightening as dough tightens when it is worked and left to set. Pain sprang bright and immediate.

“Speak, Kaffir. You carry promises to Kordofan.”

Feversham said nothing. He clung doggedly to the plan he had pondered so long and carefully. He could not improve upon it now by any alteration suggested by fear at a moment when his mind could not afford to wobble.

A rope was flung about his neck. He was pushed and driven beneath the gallows.

“Speak, Kaffir,” said Nejoumi. “So shall you escape death.”

Feversham smiled and grimaced and shook his head loosely from side to side, as though all were a jest between friends. It astonished him that he could do it—that he did not fall to his knees and beg. More astonishing still, he felt no temptation to. His heart beat very fast, but with a kind of excitement, like a man about to step into cold water.

He did not even think of Ethne then; the great dread that his hope might die with him did not trouble him. He had an allotted part to play, and he played it. That was all.

Nejoumi looked at him sourly. He turned to the men ready to draw away the angareb on which Feversham stood.

“To-morrow,” he said, “the Kaffir shall go to Omdurman.”

Only then did Feversham feel, clearly and fiercely, how the palm-fibre rope tortured his wrists.
