The dawn after that extraordinary Midsummer night broke soft and bright, no common, ordinary sort of day—no fiery portents streaked the sky, no storms tore across the earth, no whirlwinds or floods tore through the streets of Villette. The sun rose laughing, warm and golden as a perfect July day should be, while morning draped herself in rubies at the horizon and spilled whole armfuls of roses across her path, petals drifting down so thick they stained the cobblestones pink. The Hours woke dewy as nymphs, emptied their vials of mist over the distant hills, and stepped out free of fog: shadowless, blue, and glorious, they led the sun’s horses on a cloudless, burning course across the sky.

It was the finest summer day any soul could wish for, and I doubt I was the only one in the Rue Fossette who noticed it—but everyone else’s thoughts were far too occupied to stop and admire the light. The same preoccupation hummed under my skin too, of course, though it held none of the shock, none of the overwhelming, unravelling mystery it bore for the rest of the pensionnat, leaving me half free to notice the roses and the sun while I turned the same secret over in my head. Still, as I wandered the garden, feeling the warmth sink into my shoulders and watching the blooms unfurl, I pondered the news every other tongue in the house was chattering about.

What news? Only this: when the bell rang for matins, there was an empty spot in the first row of boarders. When breakfast was laid, one coffee cup sat unclaimed, cold, untouched. When the housemaid stripped the beds, she found Ginevra Fanshawe’s bolster propped lengthwise under a cap and nightgown, meant to look like a sleeping girl. And when her music mistress arrived for their usual early morning lesson, the bright, flighty young pupil she expected was nowhere to be found.

They searched high and low, turned every room upside down, ransacked the entire house from attic to cellar, but it was useless. No trace, no note, no scrap of a hint rewarded their efforts: the nymph had vanished, swallowed whole by the dark of the previous night, like a shooting star burning out mid-flight. The supervising teachers were horrified, but Madame Beck’s terror was far deeper. I had never seen her so pale, so shaken: this was a blow struck straight at her most vulnerable spot, her reputation, her carefully guarded business. None of them could work out how it had happened—every casement was latched, every pane unbroken, every door bolted fast. To this day, no one but Lucy Snowe has ever known the truth of it: I had not forgotten how, the night of the park fête, I had stepped softly to the grand front door and drawn it closed to its lintel, shut, but never bolted, never locked. I remembered too, the thunder of the carriage and pair we had encountered on the dark lane, the flash of a white handkerchief waving from its window.

From those clues, and a handful of small, private observations no one else had made, I could draw only one conclusion: it was an elopement. Certain of that, and seeing Madame Beck so distraught, I finally told her what I knew. When I mentioned M. de Hamal’s long courtship of Ginevra, I found she had long been aware of the affair—she had discussed it at length with Mrs. Cholmondeley before, and washed her hands of all responsibility for the girl, laying it at the other woman’s feet. Now she sent at once for Mrs. Cholmondeley and M. de Bassompierre.

We soon learned the Hôtel Crécy had already heard the news. Ginevra had sent a vague note to her cousin Paulina, hinting at marriage, and the de Hamal family had already reached out with their own inquiries. M. de Bassompierre set off at once to hunt the fugitives down, but he arrived too late to stop the ceremony.

A week later, the post brought me a letter. I transcribe it here, for it explains every last mystery of those strange ghostly months:
> DEAR OLD TIM (short for Timon, you sour old thing),
> I’m off, as you see—gone like a shot! Alfred and I planned to elope almost from the very start. We never meant to get spliced the boring, humdrum way everyone else does: Alfred has too much spirit for that, and so do I, thank God!
> You’d never guess, that man who used to call you “the Dragon” has spent so much time around you these past few months he’s actually become quite fond of you. He begs you not to miss him now he’s gone, and sends his apologies for all the little trouble he put you to. He’s terribly sorry he startled you that day he snuck up on you in the grenier when you were reading that very important letter of yours—he couldn’t resist making you jump, you looked so completely wrapped up in it. To make up for it, he says you once gave him quite the fright when you burst into the room looking for a shawl or a bit of ribbon just as he’d struck a light to smoke a quiet cigar while waiting for me.
> Do you get it now? The Comte de Hamal was your so-called Nun of the Attic, sneaking in to visit little old me! I’ll tell you how he pulled it off. You know he has free run of the Athénée, where his three nephews go to school. The Athénée’s courtyard sits right on the other side of the high wall that borders the forbidden allée in our garden. Alfred climbs as well as he dances or fences: he got over that outer wall first, then pulled himself up the big old tree that spreads its boughs right over the grand arbour, branches resting on the roof of the pensionnat’s lower wings, until he could climb up to the first floor and the grand salle. One night he fell right out of that tree, tore half its branches off, nearly broke his neck, and ran off so spooked he almost got caught by Madame Beck and M. Paul Emanuel walking in the alley below.
> From the grand salle it was no trouble to climb the last flight of stairs up to the top garret. The skylight there is always left half open for air, day and night, right? He slipped right in through it. Nearly a year ago I happened to tell him our old legend of the ghost nun, and that’s when he came up with his brilliant idea for the spectral disguise. You have to admit, he carried it off perfectly.
> If it hadn’t been for that black gown and white veil, you and that “tiger Jesuit” M. Paul would have caught him a dozen times over. He thinks you two are very brave ghost-seers, but what surprises him most is how quiet you kept it all. How could you stand having that long spectre visit you over and over without screaming, rousing the whole house, waking the whole neighbourhood?
> Oh, and how did you like the nun as a bedfellow? *I* dressed her up, didn’t I do a perfect job? I’d have screamed myself hoarse if I’d woken up to that, but you—you have nerves of iron, real leather-hard! I swear you feel nothing at all, no pain, no fear, no grief. You’re a proper old Diogenes.
> Are you terribly cross at my moonlight flit and my runaway wedding? It’s been excellent fun, and I did it partly just to spite that minx Paulina and that bear Dr. John—show them that with all their lofty airs, I could get married just as well as they could. M. de Bassompierre was in a terrible temper at first, threatening to prosecute Alfred for kidnapping, all sorts of nonsense. He was so dead serious I had to put on a little melodrama: go down on my knees, sob, drench three whole handkerchiefs. Of course my uncle caved right away! What’s the use of making a fuss? I’m married, that’s all there is to it. He still huffs that the wedding isn’t legal because I’m not of age, as if that makes any difference! I’m just as married as if I were a hundred years old. But we’re going to have a second, proper wedding now, I’m getting a full trousseau that Mrs. Cholmondeley is overseeing, and uncle has promised to give me a decent dowry. That’s very convenient, because dear Alfred has nothing but his noble birth and his army pay. I just wish uncle would be generous about it, no strings attached. He’s being so annoying, making the dowry conditional on Alfred signing a promise he’ll never touch cards or dice again. They say my angel has a weakness for gambling? I don’t know anything about that—all I know is he’s the dearest, most adorable creature alive.
> Alfred planned our elopement like a genius, didn’t he? He picked the night of the park fête on purpose, because he knows Madame Beck’s habits, knew she’d be out at the concert all hours. I assume you went with her? I watched you leave the dormitory at eleven that night. I can’t imagine how you ended up walking home alone on foot. That surely was you we passed in the narrow old Rue St. Jean, wasn’t it? Did you see me wave my handkerchief out the carriage window?
> Goodbye! Rejoice in my good luck, congratulate me on my perfect happiness, and believe me, dear cynical misanthrope,
> Yours, in the highest of spirits,
> P.S. — Don’t forget I’m a Countess now! My parents and all the girls back home are going to lose their minds when they hear it. “My daughter the Countess!” “My sister the Countess!” Doesn’t that sound so much better than plain old Mrs. John Bretton?

Most readers would assume a wild, heedless elopement like Ginevra’s would end in bitter, years-long payback for her frivolity, that a great deal of suffering lay in store for her future. But all I can tell you of the years that followed is this: I saw her right at the end of her honeymoon, when she stopped by the pensionnat with her new husband to call on Madame Beck, and sent for me to join them in the salon. She flew straight into my arms laughing, blooming and beautiful, her curls longer than ever, her cheeks flushed brighter than any rose in the garden. Her white bonnet, fine Flanders veil, and wedding dress stitched with orange blossom suited her perfectly.
“I got my dowry!” she cried the second she saw me—Ginevra had always had a good head for practical things, no matter how much she scorned the boring bourgeoisie. “Uncle de Bassompierre is fully reconciled now. I don’t care if he calls Alfred a nincompoop, that’s just his stuffy Scotch upbringing! I swear Paulina envies me, and Dr. John is so wild with jealousy he’s fit to blow his own brains out. I’m so happy I hardly have anything left to wish for—except maybe a private carriage and a fancy hotel, and oh! I have to introduce you to my husband. Alfred, come here!”
The Comte de Hamal stepped out from the inner salon where he had been talking to Madame Beck, listening to her half felicitations, half scolding lectures. He was every bit as polite and charming as she had described him, and apologized for all the ghostly pranks with neat, well-turned words, ending by saying the best excuse for every one of his misdeeds was standing right beside him, his bride.
Then Ginevra sent him back to Madame Beck’s side, dragged me off to a corner, and nearly suffocated me with her giddy, girlish, nonsensical high spirits. She held up her wedding ring to show me a dozen times, crowing over the sound of “Madame la Comtesse de Hamal.” I said very little, gave her only the dry, quiet, reserved part of myself—but she expected no more of me. She knew me too well to look for flowery compliments, and my dry little jibes only made her laugh harder.
Not long after their marriage, M. de Hamal left the army, all to wean him away from his gambling companions and bad habits, and he was given a post as a diplomatic attaché that took the couple abroad. I thought Ginevra would forget me then, but she never did. For decades, she sent me a capricious, uneven stream of letters. The first couple of years, every line was all about herself and Alfred. Then Alfred faded slowly into the background, replaced by a new little person: Alfred Fanshawe de Bassompierre de Hamal, their son. She wrote pages and pages of boastful tales about his impossible, extraordinary precocity, and scolded me relentlessly for being so phlegmatic and unimpressed with his supposed genius. I didn’t know what it was to be a mother, she huffed, the whole depth of maternal feeling was lost on my hard, unfeeling heart.
In time, the little boy went through teething, measles, whooping cough: that stretch of months was a perfect whirlwind of dramatic distress in her letters. No woman on earth had ever endured so much hardship, no human being ever needed sympathy so badly. At first I was alarmed, and wrote back soft, comforting notes—but I soon learned there was far more noise than actual trouble in all her lamentations, and I lapsed back into my usual dry, unflappable self. The little boy weathered every illness like a hero, too: five times she swore he was on his deathbed, and five times he made a miraculous full recovery.
As the years passed, the letters began to carry quieter, more ominous notes about Alfred the First. M. de Bassompierre had to be called in over and over again to pay off his debts, many of them those dark, shameful “debts of honour” tied to cards. Small, plaintive troubles popped up over and over. No matter what storm blew up, though, Ginevra cried loudly for help and never once faced a problem alone. One way or another, from some generous relative or indulgent friend, she always got exactly what she wanted. She fought the whole battle of life by proxy, and in all the years I knew her, she suffered less than almost any other human soul I have ever met.
