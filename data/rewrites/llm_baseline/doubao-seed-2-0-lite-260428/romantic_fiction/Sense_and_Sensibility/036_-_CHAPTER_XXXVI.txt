Within days of their last chance encounter with Willoughby, the London newspapers carried a small, triumphant announcement: Thomas Palmer, Esq.’s wife had safely given birth to a son and heir, a tidbit that was only truly thrilling to the handful of close connections who had known of the coming birth for weeks.

The event sent Mrs. Jennings into peals of delighted excitement, reshaping her whole schedule at once. Eager to dote on Charlotte and the new baby every waking hour, she left for the Palmers’ house first thing each morning after dressing, and rarely returned home until well after dark. At the Middletons’ earnest request, Elinor and Marianne found themselves spending every full day at their Conduit Street townhouse as well. Left to their own devices, the sisters would have far rather lingered in the quiet of Mrs. Jennings’ empty home, at least for the mornings—but it was impossible to object to the combined wishes of their hosts.

Their company was scarcely more welcome to Lady Middleton and the Steele sisters than the arrangement was welcome to them. Elinor and Marianne had far too much good sense to fawn over Lady Middleton’s every trivial whim or coo over her spoilt children, so Lady Middleton decided they must be ill-natured; their habit of curling up with books for quiet reading struck her as vaguely satirical, a trait she disliked without ever bothering to define it. Lucy and her sister Anne viewed the Dashwoods as interlopers, trespassing on the ground they had worked so hard to claim as their own, stealing the favour of the Middletons they meant to keep all to themselves.
Their presence was a constant irritation: Lady Middleton felt ashamed to laze about doing nothing in front of the sharp-eyed Dashwood girls, and Lucy dared not lay on her usual thick, cloying flattery for fear Elinor and Marianne would see through it and despise her for it. Only Anne Steele saw a path to turn the situation to her favour. If either Dashwood girl would just spill every last, salacious detail of Marianne’s heartbreak over Mr. Willoughby, she would happily give up her favourite spot by the after-dinner fire to make it worth their while. But the sisters never indulged her: every pointed, pitying remark Anne tossed Elinor’s way about “poor broken Marianne” only got a blank look in response, and every careless jab about how flighty beaux could be earned Marianne’s full, unmasked disgust. Even a far smaller favour would have won her over—all she wanted was for them to laugh with her about the silly doctor who was rumoured to be courting her, but they never did, leaving her to make all her own jokes on the topic on the days Sir John was out at dinner.

None of this quiet jealousy was visible to the ever-cheerful Mrs. Jennings, who insisted the girls getting to spend so much time together was a perfect delight, and teased the Dashwoods every night for getting a break from her “stupid old woman” company. She would pop in to join the group sometimes at the Middletons, sometimes back at her own house, brimming over with stories of Charlotte’s recovery, convinced the new mother’s good health was entirely down to her own careful tending. The only thing that soured her mood was Mr. Palmer’s stubborn refusal to admit his son was the most beautiful baby who had ever lived. No matter how many times she pointed out the baby’s exact resemblance to every relation on both sides of the family, he only shrugged and said all infants looked identical to him, a very unfatherly opinion that she complained of daily.

It was around this time that poor Mrs. John Dashwood suffered her own small, ridiculous misfortune. She was paying a call with Elinor and Marianne at her Harley Street home when a mutual acquaintance dropped by, and on hearing the two Miss Dashwoods introduced as Mr. John Dashwood’s sisters, the woman immediately assumed the girls were permanent guests in Harley Street. Within two days, she sent out musical party invitations addressed to Elinor and Marianne alongside John and Fanny Dashwood’s, a misstep that threw Fanny into a quiet fury. She was now forced to go to the enormous inconvenience of sending her carriage to collect her sisters-in-law and escort them to the party, and worse still, had to pretend to treat them with public kindness—who was to say they would not now expect to be invited out with her again? She could always snub them later, of course, but that was little comfort: when you are determined to act unkindly, nothing stings worse than other people expecting you to be generous.

Marianne by this point had grown so used to going out every evening to fill the empty, aching hours after Willoughby’s rejection that she no longer cared where she was taken. She would dress quietly and mechanically for every engagement, never expecting even a scrap of amusement, and often did not even ask where the night’s event was until the carriage pulled up at the door. She cared so little for her appearance these days that she spent less than half the time adjusting her hair and gown than Anne Steele did staring at her, the moment she was done dressing. Anne missed nothing: she memorised the cost of every ribbon, the number of gowns Marianne owned, and made constant, nosy attempts to find out how much Marianne spent on laundry every week, and what her annual allowance was. Every one of these rude little inspections ended with the same aggravating compliment: “Upon my word, you look vastly smart tonight, I do declare you’ll make a dozen new conquests!”

That very evening, after enduring that exact remark, the sisters climbed into John Dashwood’s carriage five minutes after it arrived at the door, a punctuality that left Fanny fuming—she had reached the party first, and been hoping they would be late enough to inconvenience her coachman, or give her an excuse not to save them seats.

The musical party itself was unremarkable, like all parties of its kind: full of a small handful of guests who actually loved music, and a far larger crowd who could not tell a pianoforte from a hurdy-gurdy, while the performers all considered themselves the finest amateur musicians in the whole of England. Elinor, who had no taste for performative displays of skill, did not bother to pretend to care about the grand piano, and let her eyes wander freely around the room. It was then she spotted a familiar face: the young man who had lectured them at length about fancy toothpick cases at Gray’s the stationer’s shop, months before. He soon caught her staring, said a few words to John Dashwood, and the two of them walked over to her, as John introduced him as Mr. Robert Ferrars.

He bowed so preeningly, twisting his head at an absurd angle, that Elinor knew at once he was exactly the vain, silly coxcomb Lucy had described him to be. How lucky it would have been, she thought ruefully, if her love for Edward Ferrars depended on the merits of his family! His mother’s sharp temper and his sister’s cruelty would have done half the work of turning her off him, and this brother’s ridiculous vanity would have finished it entirely. But Robert’s empty conceit did nothing to dim her affection for the quiet, gentle Edward at all, as he proved over the next quarter hour of conversation. He lamented Edward’s awkwardness, his inability to mix in high society, and insisted it was all their mother’s fault for sending him to a private tutor as a boy instead of Westminster School like he had been. “It’s all irremediable now, of course,” he said, puffing out his chest. “I tell my mother that all the time. If she’d only ignored my uncle Sir Robert’s bad advice and sent Edward to public school, he’d be as polished a man as I am today.”

He then prattled on, learning they lived in Devonshire, expressing shock that anyone could live there without being near Dawlish, and gushing endlessly about how much he adored small cottages. He told her he had once thrown three expensive architect’s plans straight into the fire for his friend Lord Courtland, and told him to build a cottage instead, and had rearranged an entire cottage for a dance for Lady Elliott once, fitting eighteen couples in the dining room, card tables in the drawing room, tea in the library, and supper in the small saloon. Elinor agreed with every word he said; he was too ridiculous to deserve the effort of a rational argument.

On the ride home, John Dashwood, who cared as little for music as Elinor did, had a sudden thought inspired by the evening. Remembering how Mrs. Dennison had mistaken his sisters for his permanent guests, he suggested that they invite Elinor and Marianne to stay in Harley Street for a few days while Mrs. Jennings was busy tending to Charlotte. The cost would be negligible, the inconvenience almost nothing—and it would finally lay his father’s deathbed promise to rest, and clear his conscience entirely.

Fanny was horrified. “I can’t possibly do that without offending Lady Middleton,” she said quickly. “The girls are her guests every single day. Otherwise I would be only too happy to have them, you know I am—look how I took them out this very evening! But they’re Lady Middleton’s visitors, I can’t very well steal them away.”

John Dashwood meekly pointed out the sisters had already spent a full week at Conduit Street, and Lady Middleton would hardly begrudge them a few days with their own brother.

Fanny paused, then hit on the perfect excuse. “My love, I would ask them at once, truly I would, but I just this very day decided to invite the Miss Steeles to stay with us for a few days. They are such well-behaved, sweet girls, and their uncle did so very much to help Edward get his start in life. We owe them that kindness. The Dashwood girls can come visit us next year, you know, but the Steeles might leave town at any moment. You already like them, don’t you? My mother adores them, and little Harry is completely besotted with them.”

John Dashwood was fully convinced. He agreed the Steeles needed to be invited right away, and his conscience was soothed by the plan to invite his own sisters the following year—though he secretly suspected that by next year, Elinor would be married to Colonel Brandon, and Marianne would be visiting her sister at the Colonel’s estate, making the invitation entirely unnecessary.

Fanny, delighted to have escaped the burden of hosting her sisters-in-law with such a clever little lie, wrote to Lucy first thing the next morning, inviting her and Anne to stay in Harley Street as soon as Lady Middleton could spare them. The note made Lucy giddy with joy: it felt as if Fanny herself was now championing her secret engagement to Edward, clearing the path straight to the Ferrars family’s good graces. Such a chance to be under the same roof as Edward and win over his mother and sister was the greatest possible advantage she could have hoped for. She at once declared that their visit to the Middletons, which had never had a set end date, was always meant to wrap up in exactly two days’ time.

When Elinor saw Fanny’s note, she realised for the first time that Lucy’s carefully laid plans might actually succeed. Such unheard-of kindness, extended to her after so short an acquaintance, could only mean Fanny’s favour was shifting to Lucy entirely, driven by far more than petty spite towards the Dashwoods. Lucy’s flattery had already softened Lady Middleton’s cold pride, and wormed its way into Fanny’s narrow, selfish heart—and there was no telling what greater favours she would win next.

Sir John called on the Steeles at Harley Street a few days later, and came home full of wild stories of how beloved they were. Fanny Dashwood swore she had never met two more charming young women in her life. She had given each of them a hand-sewn needle book made by a displaced French emigrant, called Lucy by her first name, and announced that she did not think she could ever bear to let them leave.
