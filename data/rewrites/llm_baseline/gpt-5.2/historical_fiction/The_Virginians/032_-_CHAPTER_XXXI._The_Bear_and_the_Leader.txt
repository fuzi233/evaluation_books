Tunbridge had a way of turning lives into a dish served cold and then reheated with relish. What had been, in truth, a confused little sorrow between Harry Warrington and the luckless Cattarina became, in the mouths of watering-place matrons, a grand ragout of vice—richly seasoned, over-stirred, and passed from cup to cup as easily as the medicinal draughts at the Well. The old ladies, who took their sips of iron water with pinched faces and then loosed their tongues with astonishing vigour, could no more resist a scandal than they could resist a sweetmeat. They supposed the Virginian to be as dissipated as any young English lordling in lace ruffles and debt; and of all of them Madame de Bernstein was the most determined to believe it.

Her ladyship’s mind, like a pot kept too long at the fire, liked to bubble. She had known enough of wickedness in her day to be quick at recognising its smell—even when she mistook honest broth for something corrupt. Harry’s innocence displeased her almost as much as it would have surprised her; she preferred, from old habit and old Roman temper, that her nephew should do as Rome did. Let him be no better than his neighbours, since neighbours made the rule. So she accepted every story of his “Lovelace adventures” with a complacency that was almost maternal. The very idea of him being tempted, pursued, or fallen upon was comfortable to her. It made him common enough to manage.

He had, it is true, given tea and music at the Wells; and in those little assemblies, where china clinked and harpsichords tinkled and fiddles scraped in rooms heavy with perfume and damp woollen cloaks, he had been gallant and gentle to many ladies—ladies of far better figure and repute than the unfortunate dancer. By degrees Madame de Bernstein’s sharp watch over Lady Maria slackened. Old vigilance dozes, like an old cook who has stood too long by the hearth: the hand that once snatched away a child from the kettle grows slow; the eye that once saw everything through steam and smoke begins to ask for sleep.

The Baroness had her own concerns—her cards, her dinners, her rheumatic twinges that came like a knife between the bones. She could not for ever keep another’s heart under lock. Perhaps she thought the danger past; perhaps she grew indifferent to whether it existed or not; perhaps Maria, with that quiet craft young women learn as early as they learn to braid a ribbon, had soothed and cajoled her guardian until even the old Dragon’s scales felt soft. Dragons age. Teeth drop out. The claws, once fit to rake a reputation to ribbons, fall at last with a feeble pat. Their venom is no longer fatal; it is merely disagreeable, like wine turned sharp. And at roosting-time they trail their languid tails homeward, as helpless as any old creature that has spent its fire.

If good folks at Oakhurst had been ready enough to credit what was said against Harry, why should Madame Bernstein—who had seen more of the world’s evil than all Oakhurst put together—be less credulous? In such a place as Tunbridge, where women of fashion, women of reputation and demi-reputation, virtue and no virtue, all mixed as promiscuously as spices in a market-stall, no tale was too coarse to be whispered, no slander too bold to be believed. A century earlier, our ancestors met at Epsom, Bath, Tunbridge, Harrogate, as freely as modern folk go to foreign watering-places; they danced to the same fiddles, drank from the same glasses, and took health and pleasure in the same rooms until the air grew close with candle-smoke and wet silk.

So when the little dancer went back to London—as she did—why, it must be because heartless Harry had deserted her. Deserted her for somebody else, of course; and the company at the Wells could provide not only one name, but half-a-dozen, and would choose whichever best suited the whisperer’s spite. Each name was passed like a morsel from mouth to mouth; each came out altered—salted, peppered, overdone.

Harry’s bad reputation, thus manufactured, comforted his old aunt exceedingly. It eased her mind about his passion for Lady Maria. If he were wicked in the common way, he might be diverted in the common way; and the thought that he was like other young men was, to her, a pillow. She grew so easy that when the chaplain announced he had come to escort her home, she did not even care to separate Maria from her side. She preferred to keep her under her own eye, to gossip at her about her “wicked young cousin,” to speak with that indulgent cynicism old people call wisdom—boys would be boys—and to confide, with a smile that had more steel than mirth, her intention of finding Harry a proper wife: suitable age, suitable fortune, suitable everything that could be measured like flour.

Maria endured these pleasantries as best she might, as a hungry person endures talk of food that is not for them.

There lived in the last century a certain French duke and marquis, a man who made a profession of conquest and did posterity the unkindness of writing down, in sprightly memoirs, the names of many court-beauties he had ruined. Those pages, no doubt, are delightful reading for grandsons who discover their ancestresses there, like preserved fruit labelled and shelved for public tasting. In the course of his travels that nobleman visited North America, and, as in Europe, set himself promptly to fall in love and to win. Yet it is curious that, among the plain colonial people, he seems not to have made a single conquest—else he would certainly have recorded it. Travellers have complained loudly of transatlantic roughness; but one may say, from what one has seen, that American gentlemen are commonly modest in speech, and American women—at least to the best of belief—pure in life.

Harry Warrington had brought that colonial modesty with him. He lived among men of pleasure; he heard talk as free as wine; yet he sat silent through much of it, uneasy when double meanings were flung like bones under the table. He had no victories to boast. If others narrated their own exploits with laughter and coarse triumph, he blushed as if some hot stew had been suddenly splashed upon him. Mr. Sampson had observed this at Castlewood, when cousin Will, with his choice stories, had made Harry shift and redden; and my lord had even rebuked his brother, bidding him keep such jokes for the usher’s table at Kensington and not offend their kinsman. Hence the chaplain’s “Reverentia pueris” at the ordinary on Harry’s first appearance: Sampson, if he had not strength enough to do right, had at least enough grace not to revel in indecency before an innocent youth.

He was touched, too, by Harry’s gift of the horse; and gratitude, that honest appetite, warmed him. “You see, sir,” he said, “I am of the world, and must do as the rest of the world does. I have led a rough life, Mr. Warrington, and can’t afford to be more particular than my neighbours. Video meliora, deteriora sequor, as we said at college. I have a little sister at boarding-school not far from here; and as I keep a decent tongue in my head with my little Patty, and expect others to do the same, sure I may try to do as much by you.”

To Madame de Bernstein the chaplain was loud in praise of Harry. She liked praise of him as she liked good seasoning—liberal, unmistakable. She was as fond of him as she could be of anything; he pleased her eye and amused her temper. His good looks, his manly bearing, his bright eyes, his deep young voice, and—most of all—his blushes, which rose so readily, delighted her. Had he been clever, learned, witty, she would have tired of him. His shrewd simplicity entertained her like a plain dish well made. “We must find a good wife for him, Chaplain,” she said. “I have one or two in my eye who will suit. We must set him up here. He never will bear going back to his savages again, or to live with his little Methodist of a mother.”

On that point Sampson had his own private eagerness. He too had a wife in his eye for Harry. One may suppose he had had conversations at Castlewood with the Earl, who had hinted at rewarding his chaplain with a living or some provision, if only Sampson’s good offices could help my Lady Maria to a husband. Therefore he watched now, waiting to see how best to bring influence to bear.

And indeed Sampson could be agreeable. Within a few hours he and Harry were intimate. The parson rejoiced in high spirits, good appetite, and good humour; he had no sanctified drawl and no hypocritical squeamishness. Yet he took care not to shock Harry with needless grossness; whether from policy or compunction he initiated his pupil only into minor mysteries, keeping back the darker secrets of his own unlucky knowledge. With Harry he was brisk and jolly—ready for bottle, cards, cock-fight, shooting-match, a gallop across the common—yet his talk remained decent. He aimed rather to amuse than to corrupt. His success was easy: he had animal spirits, natural wit, and long apprenticeship in the business of toad-eating, which had been his livelihood since he first entered college as a servitor and began to seek whose favour might feed him.

Toad-eaters were by no means extinct; they only changed their manner. In those days a gentleman’s dependent made no pretence of equality. He openly paid court; called his patron “sir;” stood until bid to sit; ran errands, any errands; flattered ex officio. Mr. Sampson felt no shame in speaking of Harry as his young patron, recommended to him by his noble patron, the Earl of Castlewood. He was proud to appear at Harry’s side; he talked him up to company; he gave orders to Harry’s tradesmen—taking, no doubt, his perquisites as naturally as cooks take tastes from the pot—and played aide-de-camp with cheerful obedience. Since coming to Europe, poor Harry had not been uniformly lucky in his friendships.

“What a reputation, sir, they have made for you in this place!” cried Sampson, bursting in from the coffee-house. “Monsieur de Richelieu was nothing to you!”

“How do you mean, Monsieur de Richelieu? Never was at Minorca in my life,” said downright Harry, who knew nothing of the Frenchman’s famous home victories.

Sampson explained, laughing. The pretty widow Patcham, newly arrived, was certainly desperate about Mr. Warrington; her behaviour at the rooms last night proved it. As for Mrs. Hooper—there was no doubt. The Alderman had dragged his wife back to London for no other reason. It was the talk of the whole Wells.

“Who says so?” Harry broke out, colour rising in his face as quickly as heat under a lid. “I should like to meet the man who dares say so, and confound the villain!”

“I should not like to show him to you,” said Sampson, amused. “It might be the worse for him.”

“It’s a shame to speak with such levity about the character of ladies or of gentlemen either,” Harry continued, pacing the room, anger ringing in his voice like a struck pan. “It’s a shame, I say, to talk away the reputation of any man or woman as people do here. In our country a fellow’s ears would not be safe; and a little before I left home three brothers shot down a man for speaking ill of their sister.”

“Serve the villain right!” cried Sampson.

“Already they have had that calumny about me set a-going here, Sampson—about me and the poor little French dancing-girl.”

“I have heard,” said Sampson, shaking powder out of his wig with a care as delicate as a pastry-cook dusting sugar.

“Wicked; wasn’t it?”

“Abominable.”

“They said the very same thing about my Lord March. Isn’t it shameful?”

“Indeed it is,” Sampson answered, with an expression of astonishing gravity—though the truth was that indignation on such grounds had not visited him.

“I don’t know what I should do if these stories were to come to my mother’s ears. It would break her heart. Only a few days before you came, a military friend of mine, Mr. Wolfe, told me how the most horrible lies were circulated about me. Good heavens! What do they think a gentleman of my name and country can be capable of—I a seducer of women? They might as well say I was a horse-stealer or a housebreaker. I vow if I hear any man say so, I’ll have his ears!”

“I have read, sir, that the Grand Seignior of Turkey has bushels of ears sometimes sent in to him,” said Sampson, laughing. “If you took all those who had heard scandal against you or others, what basketsful you would fill!”

“And so I would, Sampson, as soon as look at ’em—any fellow’s who said a word against a lady or a gentleman of honour!” Harry cried, his voice thick with outraged pride.

“If you’ll go down to the Well you’ll find a harvest of ’em. I just came from there. It was high tide of Scandal. Detraction at its height. You may see the nymphas discentes and the aures satyrorum acutas,” the chaplain said, shrugging.

“That may be as you say,” Harry answered, “but if ever I hear any man speak against my character I’ll punish him. Mark that.”

“I shall be very sorry for his sake,” Sampson returned; “for you’ll mark him in a way he won’t like, and I know you are a man of your word.”

“And now shall we go to dinner, and afterwards to my Lady Trumpington’s tea?” Harry said, forcing himself down from anger as one forces a boil to subside.

“You know, sir, I can’t resist a card or a bottle,” said Sampson. “Let us have the last first, and then the first shall come last.”

So they went off to their accustomed place of refection.

It was an age when men drank as steadily as they breathed; and since General Braddock’s army had come through Harry’s native country, the young Virginian had learned to relish the filling of bumpers and the calling of toasts, having been taught it was a point of honour among officers never to decline a challenge. Thus he and his chaplain drank their claret in peace and plenty, naming with each glass some favourite lady, as easily as one names herbs while chopping them for a sauce.

Sampson had reasons of his own for wishing to know how far the affair between Harry and Lady Maria had gone—whether it advanced, whether it had been checked, whether it had ended. The claret warmed them to that dangerous friendliness which makes a man open his heart as if it were a larder door in a hungry house. The room had the thick, agreeable smell of wine and warm wood; outside, through the window, Tunbridge made its own music—wheels on gravel, distant laughter, the faint calling of sellers—sounds that rose and fell like a tide, and then were smothered again by the closeness indoors.

When the other diners had gone, and Monsieur Barbeau brought a bottle at dessert, Sampson found himself in an eloquent temper, with an urge to preach moralities; while Harry felt an almost physical need to pour out his private history as if it were wine that must be decanted before it soured.

“I am glad to hear what your conduct has really been with regard to the Cattarina, Mr. Warrington,” cried the chaplain, leaning forward with shining eyes. “I am glad from my soul. The wine is with you. You have shown you can bear down calumny and resist temptation. Ah, my dear sir, men are not all so fortunate. What famous good wine this is!” He drained a glass, then held it up. “A toast from you, dear sir, if you please?”

“I give you Miss Fanny Mountain, of Virginia,” said Harry, filling a bumper; and at the name his thoughts flew home, over sea and years, to a child in his mother’s household.

“One of your American conquests, I suppose?” Sampson asked.

“Nay, she is but ten years old, and I have never made any conquests at all in Virginia,” Harry said, almost sternly.

“You are like a true gentleman, and don’t kiss and tell, sir.”

“I neither kiss nor tell. It isn’t the custom of our country, Sampson, to ruin girls, or frequent the society of low women. We Virginian gentlemen honour women: we don’t wish to bring them to shame.” The words came out proud and simple, as if he were stating how bread should be baked or meat salted. “The young lady whose name I mentioned hath lived in our family since her infancy, and I would shoot the man who did her a wrong—by Heaven, I would!”

“Your sentiments do you honour!” Sampson cried, carried away either by the wine or by the usefulness of such sentiments in a patron. “Let me shake hands with you! I will shake hands with you, Mr. Warrington. And let me tell you ’tis the grasp of honest friendship offered, and not merely the poor retainer paying court to the wealthy patron. No! with such liquor as this, all men are equal—faith, all men are rich whilst it lasts! Tom Sampson is as wealthy with his bottle as your honour with all the acres of your principality!”

“Let us have another bottle of riches,” Harry laughed. “Encore du cachet jaune, mon bon Monsieur Barbeau!” And away went Barbeau to the caves below, where the bottles lay in their dusky coolness like stored summers.

“Another bottle of riches! Capital! How beautifully you speak French, Mr. Harry!”

“I do speak it well,” Harry said. “At least, when I speak, Monsieur Barbeau understands me well enough.”

“You do everything well, I think. You succeed in whatever you try. That is why they have fancied here you have won the hearts of so many women, sir.”

“There you go again about the women!” Harry snapped, though the flush rose in him more from unease than anger. “I tell you I don’t like these stories. Confound me, Sampson, why is a gentleman’s character to be blackened so?”

“Well—at any rate—there is one, unless my eyes deceive me very much indeed,” Sampson said, lowering his voice with an air of discretion.

“Whom do you mean?” Harry asked, colouring to the temples.

“Nay, I name no names,” the chaplain replied. “It isn’t for a poor chaplain to meddle with his betters’ doings, or to know their thoughts.”

“Thoughts! What thoughts, Sampson?”

“I fancied I saw, on the part of a certain lovely and respected lady at Castlewood, a preference exhibited. I fancied, on the side of a certain distinguished young gentleman, a strong liking manifested itself: but I may have been wrong, and ask pardon.”

“Oh, Sampson, Sampson!” Harry burst out, as if something tight had been cut. “I tell you I am miserable. I tell you I have been longing for some one to confide in, or ask advice of. You do know, then, that there has been something going on—something between me and—help Mr. Sampson, Monsieur Barbeau—and—and some one else?”

“I have watched it this month past,” said the chaplain.

“Confound me, sir, do you mean you have been a spy on me?” Harry flared, wounded pride flinging up again like a flame.

“A spy! You made little disguise of the matter, Mr. Warrington, and her ladyship wasn’t a much better hand at deceiving. You were always together—in the shrubberies, in the walks, in the village, in the galleries of the house—you always found a pretext for being together, and plenty of eyes besides mine watched you.”

“Gracious powers! What did you see, Sampson?” Harry cried, half terrified now at what the world might have harvested from their moments.

“Nay, sir—’tis forbidden to kiss and tell,” the chaplain said again, with a knowing smile.

Harry turned yet redder, the wine and shame mixing in him like heat and spice. “Oh, Sampson,” he said, voice dropping low, “can I—can I confide in you?”

“Dearest sir—dear generous youth—you know I would shed my heart’s blood for you!” exclaimed Mr. Sampson, squeezing his patron’s hand, and rolling his bright eyes upward as if appealing to Heaven for witness.
