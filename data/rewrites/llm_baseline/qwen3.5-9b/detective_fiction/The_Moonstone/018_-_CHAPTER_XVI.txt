We found Lady Verinder alone in the room, illuminated only by the harsh glow of her reading lamp. Its shade was screwed down tight, casting a shadow that completely obscured her face. She did not lift her gaze to greet us as she usually did; instead, she sat hunched over the table, her eyes fixed stubbornly on the pages of an open book.

"Officer," she said, her voice steady despite the tension in the air, "does the inquiry you are conducting require to know beforehand if anyone currently residing in this house intends to depart?"

"It is of paramount importance, my lady," Sergeant Cuff replied.

"I must inform you," she continued, "that Miss Verinder has decided to travel to stay with her aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite, at Frizinghall. She has arranged to leave us first thing tomorrow morning."

Sergeant Cuff glanced sharply at me. I stepped forward instinctively to intercede on behalf of my mistress, but a sudden failure of courage seized me. My heart hammered against my ribs with a violence that would have belied my age had I been twenty-five again. I stumbled backward, unable to speak.

"May I inquire, your ladyship," the Sergeant prompted gently, "at what precise moment Miss Verinder informed you of her intention to visit her aunt?"

"Approximately an hour ago," my mistress answered calmly.

Cuff looked at me once more, his dark eyes unreadable. Then he addressed her again. "I have no authority, my lady, to command Miss Verinder's movements. However, I earnestly request that you delay her departure until later today. I intend to proceed to Frizinghall myself tomorrow morning, and I promise to return by two o'clock, or sooner. If Miss Verinder can remain here until that hour, I wish to speak to her unexpectedly before she leaves."

Lady Verinder nodded. She turned to me and gave strict instructions to the coachman: the carriage was not to collect Miss Rachel until two o'clock sharp.

"Is there anything further you wish to add?" she asked the Sergeant.

"There is one condition, your ladyship," Cuff said. "Should Miss Verinder express surprise at this alteration of plans, please ensure she does not attribute the delay to me."

My mistress raised her head abruptly from her book, as though preparing to speak, but checked herself with a visible effort. She cast a final glance at the open page and dismissed us with a wave of her hand.

"That is a remarkable woman," Sergeant Cuff murmured once we were safely out in the hall. "Had it not been for her extraordinary self-command, the mystery that baffles you, Mr. Betteredge, would have been solved tonight."

At those words, the terrible truth finally crashed into my dull, aging brain. For a fleeting moment, I imagine I lost my senses entirely. I grabbed the Sergeant by the lapel of his coat and pinned him firmly against the wall.

"Damn you!" I roared. "There is something dreadfully wrong regarding Miss Rachel—and you have been concealing it from me this entire time!"

Sergeant Cuff looked up at me, standing flat against the plaster, without flinching a single muscle of his somber countenance.

"Aha," he said simply. "You have guessed it at last."

My grip loosened, and my head sank onto my chest. Please consider my service to this family for fifty years as partial excuse for my outburst. Miss Rachel had often climbed onto my knees as a child, pulling at my whiskers. To me, despite her flaws, she remained the dearest, prettiest, and most virtuous young mistress I had ever served. I apologized profusely to the Sergeant, though I fear my tears rendered the apology somewhat undignified.

"Do not distress yourself, Mr. Betteredge," the Sergeant said, his tone surprisingly kind. "In my profession, those who are quick to take offense are rarely worth their daily bread. If it brings you any solace, feel free to grab my collar again. You lack the technique, but I shall overlook your clumsiness out of sympathy for your feelings." He curled the corners of his mouth upward, delivering a grim joke in his own bleak fashion.

I ushered him into my small sitting-room and shut the door behind us.

"Speak plainly, Sergeant," I demanded. "What do you suspect? It is cruel to keep secrets from me now."

"I do not merely suspect," Cuff replied. "I know."

My bad temper surged again. "Do you mean to tell me, in plain English," I shouted, "that Miss Rachel has stolen her own diamond?"

"Yes," the Sergeant confirmed without hesitation. "Miss Verinder has possessed the Moonstone in secret from the beginning to the end. She enlisted Rosanna Spearman in her confidence solely because she calculated that we would naturally suspect Rosanna of the theft. That is the entirety of the case, Mr. Betteredge. Collar me again if it vents your anger; I welcome it."

God forgive me, my feelings were not so easily assuaged. "Give me your reasons!" I pleaded.

"You shall hear my reasoning tomorrow," Cuff stated firmly. "If Miss Verinder refuses to postpone her trip to her aunt—which you will discover she indeed will—I shall be compelled to present the full case to your mistress tomorrow. Since the outcome is uncertain, I request your presence to witness the proceedings. Let the matter rest for tonight. No, Mr. Betteredge, you shall extract no further words from me concerning the Moonstone."

He gestured toward the dining table, which was set for supper. "That is one of the many human weaknesses I always treat with tenderness. If you will ring the bell, I shall say grace: 'For what we are about to receive...'"

"My appetite has vanished, Sergeant," I replied. "I shall wait to see you served, and then I shall beg to withdraw and attempt to compose myself alone."

I watched him eat the finest meal imaginable, wishing with every fiber of my being that the food might choke him. Just then, the Head Gardener, Mr. Begbie, entered with his weekly accounts. Immediately, the conversation shifted to roses and the comparative merits of grass versus gravel paths. I excused myself, leaving the two men deep in debate, and walked out with a heart weighed down by despair.

This was the first genuine trouble I had faced in many years that tobacco could not soothe, nor could the solitude of *Robinson Crusoe* resolve. Restless and miserable, with nowhere specific to go, I paced the terrace, reflecting in silence. I felt ancient, exhausted, and utterly unfit for my station. For the first time in my life, I wondered when God might finally relieve me of this burden. Yet, amidst this gloom, my conviction in Miss Rachel's goodness remained unshaken. Even if Sergeant Cuff, clad in the wisdom of King Solomon, had accused my young lady of a base and guilty conspiracy, my answer would have been singular: "You do not know her; I do."

My reverie was broken by Samuel, who brought me a written note from my mistress. Entering the house to fetch a candle, Samuel noted a shift in the atmosphere. My troubled mind had previously blinded me to it, but now, alerted by the barking dogs and the low moan of the wind, I looked up to see the cloud racks turning black and racing swiftly across a watery moon. Stormy weather was approaching—Samuel was correct.

The message from my lady revealed that the magistrate at Frizinghall had written to remind her of the three Indian prisoners. Early next week, they were to be released to wander freely. If we had further inquiries to pose to them, there was no time to waste. Having neglected to mention this during her earlier meeting with Sergeant Cuff, my mistress now instructed me to rectify the oversight.

The Indians had slipped from my mind entirely, as they likely had from yours. I saw little utility in reopening that subject, yet I obeyed my orders dutifully. I found Sergeant Cuff and Mr. Begbie engaged in a heated argument over a bottle of Scotch whisky, debating the cultivation of roses. The Sergeant was so engrossed that he signaled me not to interrupt when I arrived.

As best as I could discern, the crux of their disagreement was whether the white moss rose required budding onto the dog-rose to thrive. Mr. Begbie insisted it did; Sergeant Cuff vehemently denied it. They appealed to me with the fervor of schoolboys. Knowing nothing of horticulture, I adopted a neutral stance, much like Her Majesty's judges when the scales of justice hang perfectly balanced.

"Gentlemen," I remarked diplomatically, "much can be argued on either side."

The brief pause created by my impartiality allowed me to slide my lady's written message onto the table beneath Sergeant Cuff's gaze. By this time, I harbored a distinct hatred for the man. Yet, I must concede that his mental agility was astonishing. Within thirty seconds of reading the note, he had retrieved Superintendent Seegrave's report from memory, isolated the section concerning the Indians, and formulated his response.

"A distinguished traveler, expert in Indian languages and customs, appears in Mr. Seegrave's report, does he not?" Cuff asked.

"Indeed," I replied.

"Then, pray, provide me with his name and address," Cuff commanded.

"I shall write them on the reverse of your lady's message," I offered.

"Certainly. I shall consult this gentleman upon my arrival at Frizinghall tomorrow."
