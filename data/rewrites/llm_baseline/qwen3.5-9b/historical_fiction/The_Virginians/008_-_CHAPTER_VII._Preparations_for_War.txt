Surely no man can claim more sympathy than one endowed with bravery, youth, good looks, and misfortune. Madam Esmond might have borne twenty sons, yet she possessed every right to admire her young soldier. Her attention toward Mr. Washington was now undivided; she raved of him and praised him in every assembly. She eagerly highlighted his sterling virtues to her sons, drawing sharp contrasts between his noble character and Harry's penchant for pleasure—whom she dismissed as "the wild boy!"—and George's listless contemplation of books.

George, however, was not swayed by his mother's extravagant encomiums. Instead, the jealous demon within him festered until he became a torment to himself and everyone around him. He offered jests so profound that his simple-minded mother failed to grasp their meaning, sitting bewildered amidst his sarcasms, unable to comprehend his moody, saturnine humor.

Meanwhile, public events were unfolding that would soon dictate the fate of their humble household. The quarrel between the French and English in North America had evolved from a provincial dispute into a national conflict. Reinforcements from France had already reached Canada, and English troops were anticipated in Virginia.

"Alas! my dear friend!" wrote Madame la Presidente de Mouchy from Quebec to her young correspondent, George Warrington. "How contrary is destiny to us! I see you leaving the embrace of an adored mother to throw yourself into the arms of Bellona. I foresee you returning wounded from combat. I hesitate almost to wish victory to our lilies when I behold you serving under the banners of the Leopard. There are enmities the heart does not recognize—ours remain at peace amidst the tumult. All here love and salute you, as well as Monsieur the Bear-Hunter, your brother (that cold Hippolyte who preferred the chase to the soft conversation of our ladies!). Your friend, your enemy, the Chevalier de la Jabotière, burns to meet his generous rival on the field of Mars. M. Du Quesne spoke of you last night at supper. M. Du Quesne, my husband, sends affectionate greetings to his young friend, accompanied by those of your sincere Presidente de Mouchy."

Indeed, the banner of the Leopard was unfurled to the wind, rallying numerous King's soldiers. It was resolved to wrest from the French all conquests made upon British dominions. Two regiments were raised and funded directly by the King in America, while a fleet carrying two additional regiments was dispatched from home under an experienced commander.

In February 1755, Commodore Keppel, aboard the famous *Centurion*—the same vessel in which Anson had circumnavigated the globe—anchored in Hampton Roads with two ships of war. Onboard were General Braddock, his staff, and a portion of his troops. Appointed by the Duke, the General awaited reinforcements. A century prior, the Duke of Cumberland had been hailed as "The Duke par excellence" in England; though not quite so renowned, he was certainly no worse than the caricatures drawn by his enemies. A fleet of transports swiftly followed, laden with stores, men, and money.

General Braddock landed his forces at Alexandria on the Potomac River and proceeded to Annapolis, Maryland, where he convened the colonial governors. Urging unity, he exhorted them to mobilize their respective provinces for the common cause. The arrival of the General and his small army sparked immense excitement throughout the provinces, nowhere more intense than at Castlewood.

Harry immediately departed to witness the troops encamped at Alexandria. The sight of their lines delighted him, as did the inspiring music of fifes and drums. He quickly befriended the officers of both regiments, longing to join the expedition. He became a welcome guest at their mess. Madam Esmond was pleased that her sons could enjoy the company of fashionable gentlemen from England. She believed their association would refine them, noting that these English officers were vastly superior to the horse-racing and cock-fighting Virginian squires, the lawyers, pettifoggers, and "toad-eaters" she encountered at the lieutenant-governor's table.

Madam Esmond possessed a keen eye for detecting flatterers in others' households. She was particularly satirical regarding the small circle of officials in Williamsburg, lacking patience for their etiquette and squabbles over precedence. Regarding the King's officers, both Harry and his elder brother smiled at their mother's compliments concerning the elegance and propriety of the camp's gentlemen. Yet, had the good lady known the truth—if she could have heard their raucous jokes and songs over wine and punch, or witnessed the disreputable state of many as they staggered to their lodgings—she would hardly have recommended their company so readily.

Men and officers swaggered through the countryside, frightening peaceful farmers and villagers with their riotous behavior. The General raged against his troops' disorder and the provincials' "traitorous niggardliness." The soldiers treated the land almost as a conquered territory, scorning the locals and insulting the wives of their Indian allies who had traveled to join the English. Consequently, the General was forced to bar Indian women from his camp. Amazed and outraged, their husbands withdrew, and within months, their invaluable services were lost to him.

Had Madam Esmond heard some of the stories circulating about the camp's gentlemen, she might have hesitated, but she rejected them outright. After all, soldiers were soldiers; those officers who visited Castlewood on her son's invitation were, in fact, most polite gentlemen. She received them graciously, offering them the finest sporting opportunities the country provided. Soon, the General himself sent polite messages to Mistress of Castlewood. Citing his father's service under the glorious Marlborough and the enduring respect for Colonel Esmond's name in England, he requested permission to visit Castlewood and pay his respects to the daughter of such a meritorious officer.

If Madam Esmond had understood the true motive behind General Braddock's politeness, her charms might not have been so easily won. The Commander-in-Chief held levees at Alexandria, attended by the local gentry. Among those presenting themselves were the twin brothers of Castlewood, mounted on their best horses, wearing their latest London suits, escorted by their two negro boys in smart liveries.

Braddock was sulky and irritable with the provincial gentry, barely acknowledging the young gentlemen. At dinner, he casually asked his aide-de-camp who the "young Squire Gawkeys" in blue, gold, and red waistcoats were. Mr. Dinwiddie, the Lieutenant-Governor of Virginia, along with the agent from Pennsylvania and a few others, were dining with his Excellency.

"Oh!" exclaimed Mr. Dinwiddie. "Those are the sons of the Princess Pocahontas."

With a tremendous oath, the General retorted, "Who the deuce was she?"

Dinwiddie, who harbored no love for her due to her previous imperiousness, launched into a disrespectful and ridiculous account of Madam Esmond, mocking her pomposity and immense pretensions. He entertained General Braddock with anecdotes until his Excellency fell asleep. Upon waking, Dinwiddie had departed, but the Philadelphia gentleman remained, deeply engaged in conversation with the officers.

The General resumed the topic, speaking of Madam Esmond in curt, disrespectful terms typical of soldiers of that era. He demanded again the name of the "old fool" Dinwiddie had mentioned, breaking into expressions of contempt for the gentry and the country at large. Then, Mr. Franklin of Philadelphia interjected. He repeated the widow's name but presented a vastly different view of her character, demonstrating extensive knowledge of her, her father, and her estate—as he did with every subject under discussion. He explained that Madam Esmond possessed cattle, horses, and ample stores that would prove highly useful at this critical juncture, strongly recommending that the General conciliate her.

Already convinced that Mr. Franklin was a shrewd and intelligent man, the General graciously ordered an aide-de-camp to invite the two young men to dine the following day. They arrived promptly; the General was pleasant and good-natured, and the members of his family made much of them. Behaving with the modesty and breeding befitting their station, the twins returned home delighted. Nor was their mother less pleased at the civilities shown to her boys.

In response to Braddock's message, Madam Esmond penned a note in her finest style, acknowledging his politeness and requesting that he specify a time for her to receive him at Castlewood.

The arrival of the army and the looming campaign became the constant topic of conversation within the Castlewood household. To embark on the campaign was Harry's dearest wish. He dreamed only of war and battle, spending his time with officers in Williamsburg, scouring and polishing every gun and sword in the house, reviving childhood amusements, and drilling the negroes.

His mother, possessing a gallant spirit herself, knew that the time had come for one of her sons to leave her and serve the King. She dared not decide which of them the lot would fall upon. She admired and respected the elder, yet she felt she loved the younger boy with all the passion of her heart. Though eager for war, Harry too hesitated to broach the subject nearest his heart. Whenever he ventured to mention it to George, the older brother's countenance wore an ominous look.
