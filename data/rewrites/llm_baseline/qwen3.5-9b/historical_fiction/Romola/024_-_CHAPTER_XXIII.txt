"You are easily frightened, though," Piero remarked, letting out another scornful laugh. "My portrait does not quite capture the essence of the original. Still, the old fellow certainly possessed a tiger's gaze; I must return to the Duomo and study him once more."

"It is hardly pleasant to be seized by a madman, if indeed he is one," Lorenzo Tornabuoni replied, offering a polite defense for Tito's reaction. "Though perhaps he is merely a ruffian. We shall soon learn the truth. I believe we ought to determine if we possess sufficient authority to halt this disturbance between our citizens and your countrymen," he added, turning his attention to the Frenchman.

They moved forward toward the throng, swords drawn, while the quiet onlookers formed an escort around them. Tito followed suit; it was imperative that he ascertain what others already knew regarding Baldassarre. The initial paralysis of terror was rapidly giving way to the quick-witted strategies that mortal danger forces upon the timid. The mob of men and boys, more inclined to jeer at the soldier and torment him than to deal him serious wounds, parted instantly at the sight of gentlemen bearing drawn blades. The French soldier was immediately questioned.

He and his companions had merely escorted their prisoners into the city to solicit funds for their ransom. Two of the captives were Tuscan soldiers captured in Lunigiana; the third, an elderly man, had been traveling with a group of Genoese, coming to blows with French foragers near Fivizzano. While the old man appeared mad, he proved harmless. The soldier knew nothing further, being unable to comprehend a single word the elder spoke. Tito listened intently, yet remained deaf to everything else until specifically addressed.

It was Tornabuoni who broke the silence. "Will you accompany us back, Melema? Or, since Messere is departing for Signa, would it be wise to follow the current fashion and attend the Friar? He will be speaking with the force of a torrent at flood stage this very morning. It is what we must all do, you know, if we wish to preserve our Medicean skins. *I* myself would go, had I the leisure."

Tito's face had regained its color, and he managed a display of gaiety. "Naturally, I number myself among the admirers of the inspired orator," he smiled. "Unfortunately, however, I shall remain occupied with the Segretario until the time of the procession."

"I intend to enter the Duomo to examine that savage old man again," Piero declared.

"Then, Piero mio, have the charity to show him to one of the hospitals for travelers," Tornabuoni instructed gently. "The monks may discover whether he requires confinement within a cage."

The group dispersed, and Tito set off for the Palazzo Vecchio, where he expected to meet Bartolommeo Scala. Though the distance was short, for Tito it felt stretched out like the agonizing minutes of a waking dream. The brief stretches of street and piazza were filled with memories, forebodings, and torturing fears that might have spanned months. He felt as though a serpent had begun to coil tightly around his limbs.

Baldassarre alive and present in Florence represented a living vengeance—a coiling snake that would not rest until it had crushed its prey. Such a man did not allow injuries to pass unavenged; his love and hatred burned with a passionate fervor that subjugated every other aspect of his being, driving him to sacrifice himself to his passions as if worshipping a deity capable of self-destruction. Baldassarre had loosened his grip and vanished. Tito understood this perfectly: it meant the vengeance was being calculated to ensure its certainty.

If he had not uttered those decisive words—"He is a madman"—if he could have summoned the mental fortitude necessary to openly acknowledge his recognition of Baldassarre, would the risk not have been diminished? He could have claimed he possessed positive evidence of Baldassarre's death. The only individuals capable of contradicting him were Fra Luca, who was dead, and the crew of the companion galley that had delivered news of the pirate encounter. The odds were infinitely against Baldassarre having reunited with anyone from that crew. Tito thought bitterly that a timely, well-crafted falsehood might have spared him from fatal consequences. Yet, uttering such a lie would have demanded perfect self-command during a moment of convulsive shock. He realized now that he had spoken without preconception; the words had leaped forth like a sudden birth, conceived and nurtured in the dark.

Tito was confronting an inexorable law of the human soul: we prepare ourselves for sudden deeds through the repeated choices of good or evil that gradually shape our character. His only remaining hope lay in Baldassarre's failure to find his revenge. And then, Tito grasped a thought more actively cruel than any he had previously entertained: Could his own unpremeditated words contain a grain of truth? Enough truth, at least, to support his denial should Baldassarre attempt to identify him?

The old man looked strange and wild. With his eager heart and sharp mind, suffering was likely enough to have driven him mad. If this were true, the vengeance seeking to inflict disgrace might be thwarted. However, there existed another form of vengeance that no clever lying could deflect. Baldassarre belonged to a lineage for whom the thrust of a dagger feels as natural an impulse as the spring of a tiger's claw.

Tito shrank with shuddering dread from disgrace, yet he also harbored a physical terror inseparable from a soft, pleasure-loving nature—one that prevents a man from facing wounds and death as a welcome release from shame. His thoughts instantly raced toward some hidden defensive armor that might protect him from a vengeance no subtlety could parry. He marveled at the power of the passionate fear that consumed him. It was as if he had been struck by a blighting disease, suddenly transforming the joyful senses of youth into pure pain.

One resource remained open to Tito. He could have turned back, sought out Baldassarre again, and confessed everything—to him, to Romola, to the entire world. Yet, he never considered this path. True repentance, which severs all ties to evil, demands more than mere selfish fear. He possessed no conviction that truth offered strength or safety; the only strength he trusted lay in his ingenuity and his dissimulation.

Now that the initial shock, which had revealed the treacherous signs of fear, had passed, he hoped to navigate all emergencies through cool deceit and defensive preparation. It was a defining feature of Tito's experience at this critical juncture that no direct plan to rid himself of Baldassarre ever crossed his mind. Every other possibility ran through his thoughts, even fleeing Florence itself, but he never devised a scheme to remove his enemy. His dread generated no active malice, and he would still prefer not to cause pain to any mortal. He had simply chosen to make life easy for himself—to carry his human burden in a way that pinched him nowhere. That choice had, at various points, landed him in unexpected predicaments.

The question now was no longer whether he should share the common pressure of destiny with his suffering fellow-men, but whether all the resources of lying would suffice to save him from being crushed by the inevitable consequences of that habitual choice.
