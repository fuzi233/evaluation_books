The cold thickened as night drew itself tighter about me. The dead horse beneath my hands had already turned from warmth to board and stone; even the stink of him seemed to freeze in the air, as if scent itself could be seized and held. In the dark I could hear the land at work: pebble ticking under soft pads, the quick rasp of hungry breath, and then—threaded through it all—the wolves’ voices, a chorus tightening its circle.

Their eyes came first, green sparks hung low among the rocks. The half-moon of them narrowed. I tried to rise with some show of steadiness, but my limbs were wooden, and I had not so much as a stick—no staff, no branch, not even a stone big enough to swing with any hope. They rushed.

For an instant they checked at the horse, as if weighing carrion against living flesh; then the line bent toward me and the throats opened. I stood, a foolish upright morsel, waiting for the weight of them.

A sound like swiftness itself—almost silence, yet edged with a thin, fierce cry—slashed in from their flank. Another ring of eyes, brighter and nearer, fell upon the wolves. Bodies hit bodies. Teeth snapped. Claws tore. The wolves’ howl broke into snarls that rose and failed, and through the feeble-yet-fierce caterwauling I knew the new-comers: cats. They came as if poured out of darkness, led by a great grey one I could see only in his eyes—and yet, by those eyes, knew his bulk and colour as if he stood in noon-day.

I could not see the battle; I heard it. The night became a kind of music—no melody, only the harsh ecology of hunting, every sound finding its place: the scraping rush, the short scream, the thud of bodies on stone. For a moment I thought to run—then knew the first step would likely be a fall, and in such blackness both factions could read me by scent.

All at once the wolves’ voices ceased. The cats’ uproar swelled, wild and victorious. Soft padding followed—flight, not pursuit. The cats had beaten the wolves.

My relief lasted a breath.

Teeth—needle-sharp—closed on my legs. Then another mouth. Then a dozen. A living cataract of fur and fury climbed me; claws found purchase everywhere, pincers of curved keratin hauling at cloth and skin. They hissed hot into my face without biting it—until, in despair, I flung myself down, and the instant I did, they went straight for my features. I sprang up, and they abandoned my face as if it were a game, returning to my legs with renewed zeal.

I fought them with hands made savage by pain. I tore them away, strangled at them, crushed them under heel, jabbed at eyes—yet nothing loosened; when I tried to fling one off, it clung to my fingers like a limpet. They kept finding fresh space for fresh bites, as if my body were a board continually set with new morsels. Their assault had rhythm: when I ran, they surrounded me like a torrent and tormented me no more; when I fell—and I fell often—they waited with an uncanny patience; but if I slackened from fear of falling, they flew at my legs again, driving me onward.

So I ran through that miserable night, cleaving the solid dark, herded by silent paws and occasional pain. Yet even in persecution there was a strange order: the way underfoot was comparatively smooth; I pitched into no gully. We passed the Evil Wood without my seeing it, and left it behind as one leaves a noisome kitchen—by thrusting the door to and trusting the latch.

When morning thinned the darkness to a grey that showed the world again, I found myself beyond the channels and on the edge of the orchard valley. Joy lifted me so suddenly I would have made friends with my tormentors—but not a cat was anywhere to be seen. As if the night had only lent them to the task.

I threw myself on moss and slept at once.

A kick woke me.

I opened my eyes to daylight and bondage: hands tied, feet tied—my whole self parcelled like a bag for carrying. The giants stood over me.

“What fitter?” I said in my own mind. “To whom else should I belong?” And I laughed, not merry but sick with self-disgust, triumphant in the ugliness of it.

Another kick ended that false laughter. With their help—each “help” arriving in the shape of a boot—I got upright. There were six of them. They untied the rope that bound my legs together only to fasten a rope to each ankle, like leading-strings for a reluctant animal, and dragged me away. When they pulled both ropes at once I went down; when I lay there, they kicked me up again.

They took me straight back to my old labour. They tied my leg-ropes to a tree, undid my arms, and thrust the hateful flint into my left hand. Then they flung themselves down at ease and began to pelt me with fallen fruit and stones. Most missed; a few struck with the dull, bruising honesty of thrown things.

If I could have freed my legs and seized a stick lying a couple of yards away, I might have rushed the six of them in a madness of hunger and rage. But I looked past the stick, past the giants, into the oncoming night as one looks toward the hearth when the day has been long.

“The Little Ones will come at night,” I said to myself, and that hope fed me better than the fruit I had no chance to eat.

All day I worked. The flint’s edge—always too eager—bit the rind and stem; sap and bruised sweetness rose and mingled with the iron scent of my own scraped skin. The orchard had its own soundscape: leaves whispering like many soft tongues, branches tapping together, the occasional drop of a heavy fruit landing with a thick, domestic thud. Against that, the giants’ presence was a kind of industrial noise—blunt, intrusive, without listening—kicks and grunts that drowned the finer speech of the place.

When darkness came they tied my hands again and left me fast to the tree. I slept in pieces. Each time I woke it was from a dream of lying in the heart of a heap of children—small bodies warm as loaves just drawn from an oven, the smell of milk-breath and crushed herbs, a language of laughter around me that no grown tongue could ever translate without losing its life.

With morning my enemies returned—kicks first, then their bestial company. By noon my strength was running out through hunger and labour, and I had begun to feel as if I were hollow inside, a man made of rind.

Then the brushwood burst into commotion, and over it rang the bell-like laughter that had become dearer to me than any promise.

I cried out—delight, welcome, relief in one voice.

Answering my shout came a whole storm of noises: trumpeting as of baby-elephants, neighing as of foals, bellowing as of calves—but it was the riders making the clamour, not the animals. Through the bushes poured the Little Ones: some on diminutive horses, some on small elephants, some astride little bears. Beside the mounted troop walked older boys and girls, and among them a woman carrying a baby that crowed as if the day were a cup and it had discovered sweetness in it.

The giants heaved themselves up, slow as ill machinery. But the children moved like a practiced kitchen brigade: every small body knew its place, every hand its task. A storm of sharp stones greeted the giants. The little horses charged their legs like determined rams. The bears rose and hugged at their waists, pinning them in awkward helplessness. The elephants looped trunks around thick necks, dragged them down, and trampled them with the very lesson the giants had taught—only now received instead of given.

My ropes were undone in an instant. Arms—seemingly innumerable—closed around me. I ceased to see the giants at all.

They made me sit. Lona came close, her face intent, and without a word began feeding me red and yellow fruits—lovely as if the orchard had been saving its best for this moment. The skins gave beneath her fingers; the flesh broke with a clean, wet sigh; juice ran cold and sharp-sweet over my tongue. Hunger is a brute that speaks one language; yet even while I ate I could not help listening to the orchard’s gentler sounds returning as fear withdrew—the birds tentative, the leaves resuming their whisper, the ground no longer jarred by heavy feet.

The whole colony mounted guard until I had eaten my fill.

Then they brought two of their largest elephants and placed them side by side. They hooked their trunks together and tied their tails, arranging the great bodies parallel. The elephants could have undone the knots with a shake, could have unhooked trunks by forgetting the task; but they did neither. It was plain they understood: they held stillness as one holds a bowl steady for pouring.

I rose and laid myself in the hollow between their backs. As my weight tried to press them apart, they leaned into each other and made a living litter, firm and warm. My feet projected beyond their tails, but my head lay pillowed on an ear of each—soft, leathery, faintly dusty with the day’s travel.

Some of the smaller children mounted along the back of each bearer as a bodyguard. The rest formed a train. And so the procession began to move.

Where they carried me I did not try to guess. I yielded myself to their pleasure, almost as happy as they were. At first they chattered and laughed and played a thousand glad tricks, their voices stitching the air into a fabric that wrapped me better than any blanket. But the moment they saw I was going to sleep, they fell silent—still as judges, as if they understood the ecology of rest: too much noise can wound the mind like bright light wounds the eye.

When I woke, a sudden musical uproar greeted the opening of my eyes. We were passing through the forest where they found the babies—the forest I had suspected must stretch all the way from the valley to the hot stream. The light was different here: filtered, green-gold, like sun through a bottle.

A tiny girl sat with her little feet close to my face, looking down at me coaxingly, as if she meant to draw me into a secret. For a while she only watched me; the others seemed to hang upon her waiting.

“We make a petisson to king,” she said.

“What is it, my darling?” I asked.

“Shut eyes one minute,” she answered.

“Certainly I will! Here goes!” I said, and shut my eyes tight.

“No, no! not fore I tell oo!” she cried, scandalized.

I opened them again, and we talked and laughed for quite another hour, as if time had gone circular and returned to childhood, where promises are renewed by mere being together.

At last she said suddenly, “Close eyes!”

I closed my eyes and kept them closed.

The elephants stopped.

I heard a soft scurry, a little rustle—then a silence so complete it became audible. In that world, some silences are heard: they have edges; they ring like a bowl that has just been struck and now holds its note without sound.

“Open eyes!” shouted twenty voices from a little way off.

I obeyed.

Not a creature was visible except the elephants that bore me.

I knew the children were marvellously quick at getting out of the way—no doubt the giants had taught them, by cruelty, the art of vanishing. But when I raised myself and searched the open, shrubless forest and could descry neither hand nor heel, I stared in blank astonishment.

The sun had set; dusk gathered fast. And presently a multitude of birds began to sing.

I lay down to listen, pretty sure that if I left the hiders alone they would soon come out. The singing grew until it was a little storm of bird-voices, layered and bright, as if the forest were repairing something in the spirit with sound. I found myself thinking how different such music was from the hard noise of the giants—how it did not demand attention like a shout, but offered it like food set on a table, to be taken according to hunger.

“Surely the children must have something to do with it!” I thought. “And yet how could they set the birds singing?”

Then, happening to look up into the tree under which my elephants stood, I thought I saw motion among the leaves. I looked more keenly. Sudden white spots appeared in the dark foliage. The music died down. A gale of childish laughter rippled the air. And white spots came out everywhere.

The trees were full of children.

In wild merriment they began to descend, some dropping from bough to bough so rapidly I could scarce believe they had not fallen. I slid from my litter, and instantly I was surrounded—target for all the artillery of their joy. With stately composure the elephants walked away to bed, as if the day’s work were done and their own domestic routines called them.

“But,” I said when their uproar had run far enough to empty itself a little, “how is it that I never before heard you sing like the birds? Even when I thought it must be you, I could hardly believe it!”

“Ah,” said one of the wildest, “but we were not birds then! We were run-creatures, not fly-creatures! We had our hide-places in the bushes then; but when we came to no-bushes, only trees, we had to build nests! When we built nests, we grew birds, and when we were birds, we had to do birds! We asked them to teach us their noises, and they taught us, and now we are real birds!—Come and see my nest. It’s not big enough for king, but it’s big enough for king to see me in it!”

I told him I could not get up a tree without the sun to show me the way; when it came, I would try.

“Kings seldom have wings!” I added.

“King! king!” cried one. “Oo knows none of us hasn’t no wings—foolis feddery tings! Arms and legs is better.”

“That is true,” I said. “I can get up without wings—and carry straws in my mouth too, to build my nest with!”

“Oo knows!” he answered, and went away sucking his thumb.

A moment after I heard him calling from high up in a walnut tree of enormous size, his voice small as a remembered word in a dying dialect: “Up adain, king! Dood night! I seepy!”

And I heard no more of him until he woke me in the morning.
