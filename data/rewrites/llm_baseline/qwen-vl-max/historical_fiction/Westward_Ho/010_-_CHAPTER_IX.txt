“Take aim, you noble musqueteers, And shoot you round about; Stand to it, valiant pikemen, And we shall keep them out. There's not a man of all of us A foot will backward flee; I'll be the foremost man in fight, Says brave Lord Willoughby!”

Elizabethan Ballad.

It was Christmas afternoon, and the light was fading fast. The evening service had ended, and the people of Bideford were returning home in cheerful groups—fathers with their children, lovers arm-in-arm—headed for cakes and ale, flapdragons, mummer’s plays, and all the joyful customs of the season. But one woman walked alone, wrapped tightly in a black muffler, her maid close behind. She moved quickly, yet with a heavy heart, toward the long causeway that led to Northam town. Sir Richard Grenville and his wife caught up with her and stopped her courteously.

“You’ll come home with us, Mrs. Leigh,” said Lady Grenville. “We’d be honored to share a merry Christmas night.”

Mrs. Leigh smiled gently, placing one hand on Lady Grenville’s arm and pointing westward with the other.

“I cannot truly celebrate Christmas while that sound lingers in my ears.”

The others turned in the direction she indicated. Above them, the soft blue sky was turning gray, and here and there a misty star blinked through the dusk. But to the west, where the downs of Raleigh met those of Abbotsham, the sky was veiled in delicate white flakes. Iridescent streaks marked the path of the sun’s descent, glowing with the colors of a dying dolphin. Low on the horizon, a band of greenish haze stretched across the land. Yet what was this sound that troubled her? None of them—distracted by the noise and cheer of the town—had noticed it until now. And yet, listen! It was perfectly still. Not a breath stirred the grass. Still, the air thrummed with a deep, low roar—like the rumble of a thousand wheels, the march of endless armies, or, as it truly was, the thunder of waves crashing against the pebble ridge.

“The ridge is noisy tonight,” said Sir Richard. “There must have been wind somewhere.”

“There is wind now, where my boy is,” whispered Mrs. Leigh. “God help him.”
And everyone knew she spoke truth. The spirit of the Atlantic storm had sent its first warning—a smooth swell felt two miles inland. By tomorrow, the pebbles now tumbling back with each retreating wave might leap to the top of the ridge and be hurled like cannonballs onto the marsh, driven by the fury of the western gale.

“God help my boy,” she repeated.

“God is just as near him at sea as he is on land,” said Sir Richard kindly.

“True,” she replied, “but I am a mother alone, and my heart can only rest when I’m home, kneeling in prayer.”

And so Mrs. Leigh continued on her way, spending the entire night listening between prayers to the growl of the surf—until it was drowned, long before dawn, by the full fury of the storm.

Meanwhile, where was Amyas?

Amyas sat bareheaded in the stern of a boat in Smerwick Bay, the spray whistling through his curls as he shouted cheerfully:

“Pull, and with a will, my merry men all, and never mind shipping a sea. Cannon balls are a cargo that don’t spoil by taking salt-water.”

His mother’s fears had proved true. Christmas Eve had been the last of the still, dark, steamy nights of early winter. For twelve hours now, the western gale had raged along the Irish coast.

The short daylight of winter was fading fast. Behind him, waves leapt into the air, shattered into mist by the tempest. Beside him, green foam-fringed columns surged up the black cliffs and crashed down in cascades of snow. Before him lay the deep, sheltered bay—but not far beyond it, four miles out to sea, a thick gray cloud loomed like a sloping roof, stretching over their heads and cutting off the cliffs halfway up, hiding the Kerry mountains, and darkening the distant firths into night. Beneath that swirling veil, the storm howled across the land, sweeping great foaming masses and salt spray, blurring the world into a hazy, dun-gray blur. Let it howl! For the mist was thicker than any spray, the thunder louder than any wave. On the sand-hills, red sparks flashed in the gloom—not from heaven, but from the fort now called Fort Del Oro, where the hated golden flag of Spain flew proudly. Inside, San Josepho and eight hundred Spaniards held out. Three nights earlier, Amyas, Yeo, and Winter’s best men had dragged four culverins from the Admiral’s main deck, floated them ashore, and hauled them up to the battery among the dunes. Now they would see whether Spanish and Italian soldiers could hold their ground against the men of Devon.

No blame to Amyas if his thoughts wandered from his lonely mother at Burrough Court to the bright flashes on the sand-hills and the fort, where Salvation Yeo was hurling eighteen-pound shot with deadly precision, watching with cold triumph as sand flew and gabions crumbled.

Amyas and his crew had just returned to the ship for fresh ammunition—their supply exhausted, and the guns firing stones for the past four hours. They ran the boat ashore through the surf, landed in a cove, and scrambled over the dunes, each man carrying a pair of cannonballs slung across his shoulder. Amyas leapt into the trench and called out to Yeo:

“More food for the bull-dogs, Gunner—and plums for the Spaniards’ Christmas pudding!”

“Don’t talk to a man at work, Master Amyas. I’ve missed five times already—but I’ll bring down that cursed Popish rag, by God!”

“Then bring it down. No one wants crooked shots. Use good iron, not paving stones.”

“I swear, sir, the devil himself is twisting my shot aside. I thought I saw him once. But thank Heaven, here’s real ball again. Ah, sir—if only I could cast a silver one! Now, stand by, men!”

And once more, Yeo’s eighteen-pounder roared. The great yellow flag of Spain—flapping wildly in the wind—was lifted clean from its staff, soared into the air, and then plunged headfirst, far downwind.

A roar of triumph from the sailors echoed across the bay, answered by the soldiers in the opposing camp. But before the cheers faded, a tall officer sprang onto the fort’s parapet, seized the fallen flag, and raised it high upon his lance, holding it firm against the gale as the staff was re-erected within.

At once, a dozen longbows were drawn. But Amyas shouted:

“Shame, lads! Hold your fire—let the gallant gentleman have due courtesy!”

They paused. Amyas bounded onto the battery rampart, doffed his hat, and bowed to the officer. The man, once relieved of his duty, returned the bow with dignity and stepped down.

By now, it was nearly dark, and the firing slowed. Yeo and his gunners covered their weapons with tarpaulins and withdrew for the night. Amyas, having volunteered to take watch until midnight, remained. The rest of the force, after a meager supper of biscuit—provisions running dangerously low—lay down under arms among the dunes, grumbling themselves to sleep.

For an hour or more, Amyas paced back and forth in the gusty darkness, exchanging occasional words with the sentinel, when two figures entered the battery, talking earnestly. One wore full armor; the other, a plain short cloak typical of a scholar or peaceful man. But their conversation was not of war—of siege or sally, catapult or cannon—but of English hexameters.

Do not suppose, gentle reader, that they were trifling while Rome burned. In that critical year, 1580, the very soul of English poetry was in greater peril from these classical imitations than Ireland was from the Spanish threat.

Imitating ancient meters—called “versifying” to distinguish it from rhyme—was becoming fashionable among the learned. Stonyhurst and others had tried their hands at hexameter translations of Latin and Greek epics, which sounded more like doggerel. Young wits broke into iambics, sapphics, elegiacs—damaging the queen’s English and the ears of her subjects.

I know not if Mr. William Webbe had yet published his hints for “Reformation of English Poetry,” but Gabriel Harvey, the Cambridge Malvolio, had already persuaded Spenser, Dyer, Sidney, and perhaps even Sidney’s sister, to follow his model:

“What might I call this tree? A laurel? O bonny laurel! Needes to thy bowes will I bowe this knee, and vail my bonetto;”

after ridiculing the first book of “that Elvish Queene”—then in manuscript—as a base retreat from classicism to romance.

Now Spenser, perhaps in melancholy defiance (he had just been jilted by a Kentish maid), wasted his genius on clumsy lines he imagined antique. Some pirate publisher—Tom Nash swore it was Harvey himself—had just released “Three proper wittie and familiar Letters, lately past between two University men, touching the Earthquake in April last, and our English reformed Versifying,” a correspondence between Spenser and Harvey meant to prove the superiority of lines like:

“For like magnificoes, not a beck but glorious in show, In deede most frivolous, not a looke but Tuscanish always.”

Let them pass—Alma Mater has seen worse since. But then it was serious. There is a story (true or not) that Spenser was half-bullied into rewriting the *Faerie Queene* in hexameters—had not Raleigh, a true romanticist whose verse was “most lofty, insolent, and passionate,” convinced him otherwise.

The great dramatists had not yet risen to form the English school Spenser, unknowingly, was laying the foundation for. It wasn’t until Daniel, twenty years later, demolished Campian’s “eight several kinds of classical numbers” in his defense of rhyme, that the matter was settled. So we may forgive Raleigh’s sharp reply to a quote from the three letters of “Immerito and G. H.”:

“Tut, tut, Colin Clout, much learning has made thee mad. A good old fishwives' ballad jingle is worth all your sapphics and trimeters, and 'riff-raff thurlery bouncing.' Hey? Have I you there, old lad? Do you mind that precious verse?”

“But, dear Wat, Homer and Virgil—”

“But, dear Ned, Petrarch and Ovid—”

“But, Wat, what have we that we do not owe to the ancients?”

“Ancients, quotha? Why, the legend of King Arthur, and Chevy Chase too—of which even your fellow-sinner Sidney cannot deny that every time he hears it, even from a blind fiddler, it stirs his heart like a trumpet blast. Speak well of the bridge that carries you over, man! Did you find your Redcross Knight in Virgil, or such a dame as Una in old Ovid? No more than you did your Pater and Credo, you renegado baptized heathen, you!”

“Yet, surely, our younger and more barbarous taste must bow before divine antiquity, and imitate afar—”

“As dottrels do fowlers. If Homer was blind, lad, why dost not poke out thine eye? Ay, this hexameter is of an ancient house, truly, Ned Spenser, and so is many a rogue: but he cannot make way on our rough English roads. He goes hopping and twitching in our language like a three-legged terrier over a pebble-bank, tumble and up again, rattle and crash.”

“Nay, hear, now—

'See ye the blindfolded pretty god that feathered archer, Of lovers' miseries which maketh his bloody game?' *

True, the accent gapes in places, as I have often confessed to Harvey, but—”

* This distich is Spenser’s own; the others are authentic.

Harvey be hanged for a pedant, and the whole gang of versifiers—from Lord Dorset (though he’s long past hanging) to yourself! Why delude yourselves into playing Procrustes, stretching one word till its joints snap, squeezing the next into a heap like the Inquisitors do heretics in their banca cava? Out upon him and you, and Sidney, and the whole kin. You have not made a verse among you, and never will, which is not as lame a gosling as Harvey’s own—

'Oh thou weathercocke, that stands on the top of Allhallows, Come thy ways down, if thou dar'st for thy crown, and take the wall on us.'

“Hark! There’s our young giant comforting his soul with a ballad. You’ll hear rhyme and reason together now. He won’t miscall ‘blind-folded,’ ‘blind-fold-ed,’ I warrant; nor make an ‘of’ and a ‘which’ and a ‘his’ carry a whole verse on their wretched little backs.”

As he spoke, Amyas, who had been humming a Christmas carol to himself, burst into song:

“As Joseph was a-walking He heard an angel sing— 'This night shall be the birth night Of Christ, our heavenly King.

His birthbed shall be neither In housen nor in hall, Nor in the place of paradise, But in the oxen's stall.

He neither shall be rocked In silver nor in gold, But in the wooden manger That lieth on the mould.

He neither shall be washen With white wine nor with red, But with the fair spring water That on you shall be shed.

He neither shall be clothed In purple nor in pall, But in the fair white linen That usen babies all.'

As Joseph was a-walking Thus did the angel sing, And Mary's Son at midnight Was born to be our King.

Then be you glad, good people, At this time of the year; And light you up your candles, For His star it shineth clear.”

“There, Edmunde Classicaster,” said Raleigh, “does not that simple strain go deeper into the heart of him who wrote *The Shepherd’s Calendar*, than all your artificial, foreign verses—like—

'Wote ye why his mother with a veil hath covered his face?'

Why dost not answer, man?”

But Spenser was silent for a moment, then said:

“Because I was thinking not of the rhyme, but of the rhymer. Good heavens! How that brave lad shames me—singing here the hymns his mother taught him, right before the muzzles of Spanish guns, instead of lamenting unmanly, as I have done, the love he holds, I doubt not, as dear as I did my Rosalind. This is his welcome to the winter storm; while I, who dream of heavenly inspiration, can only see in it a mirror of my own cowardly despair.

'Thou barren ground, whom winter's wrath has wasted, Art made a mirror to behold my plight.'*

Pah! Away with frosts, icicles, tears, and sighs—”

* From *The Shepherd’s Calendar*.

“And with hexameters and trimeters too, I hope,” interrupted Raleigh. “And all the tricks of self-pitying sorrow.”

“—I will set my heart to higher work than barking at the hand that chastens me.”

“Will you put the lad into the *Faerie Queene*, then, by my side? He deserves as good a place as Guyon, or even Arthegall. Let us hail him. Hallo! Young chanticleer of Devon! Are you not afraid of a chance shot, that you crow so lustily on your own mixen?”

“Cocks crow all night long at Christmas, Captain Raleigh, and so do I,” Amyas replied cheerfully. “But who’s there with you?”

“A penitent pupil of yours—Mr. Secretary Spenser.”

“Pupil of mine?” said Amyas. “I wish he’d teach me a little of his art. I could fill my time here with making verses.”

“And who would be your theme, fair sir?” asked Spenser.

“No ‘who’ at all. I don’t want to write sonnets to blue eyes or black ones. But if I could put down some of the things I saw in the Spice Islands—”

“Ah,” said Raleigh, “he would beat you out of Parnassus, Mr. Secretary. Remember—you may write about Fairyland, but he has seen it.”

“And so have others,” said Spenser. “It is not so far off. Wherever there is love and loyalty, great purposes, and lofty souls—even in a hovel or a mine—there is Fairyland.”

“Then Fairyland should be here, friend. You represent love, and Leigh loyalty; as for great purposes and lofty souls—who better than I? Unless my enemies and conscience both lie, I am as ambitious and proud as Lucifer himself.”

“Ah, Walter, Walter, why must you always slander yourself thus?”

“Slander? Tut—I merely give the world a fair challenge: ‘Here—know the worst of me. Come on and try a fall.’ Slander? Ask Leigh here—he’s known me a fortnight—whether I’m not as vain as a peacock, as selfish as a fox, as imperious as a bona roba, ready to use anyone as a cat’s paw if there’s a chestnut in the fire. And yet the poor fool can’t help loving me, running my errands, believing every scheme and dream I utter as gospel. He probably thinks I’ll be the man in the moon someday—and he my big dog.”

“Well,” said Amyas, half-apologetically, “if you’re the cleverest man in the world, what harm in my thinking so?”

“Hearken to him, Edmund! He’ll know better when he grows out of this callow honesty and learns from the goddess Detraction how to appear wiser than the wise, by showing the fool’s motley beneath the philosopher’s cloak. Go to, lad! Slander your equals, envy your betters, pray for an eye that sees spots in every sun, and a vulture’s nose to scent carrion in every rose-bed. If your friend wins a battle, say he wasted his men; if he loses, hint he sold it; if he rises, accuse favor; if he falls, claim divine justice. Believe nothing, hope nothing, endure all—especially kicking—if anything can be gained. Then you’ll wear purple and fine linen, sit in kings’ palaces, and feast sumptuously every day.”

“And wake with Dives in torment,” said Amyas. “Thank you for nothing, captain.”

“Go to, Misanthropos,” said Spenser. “You haven’t tasted the sweets of this world’s comfits yet, and you rail at them?”

“The grapes are sour, lad.”

“And will be forever,” said Amyas, “if they grow on such a devil’s tree. I really think you’re out of your mind, Captain Raleigh, sometimes.”

“I wish I were—for it’s a troublesome, hungry, windy mind, curse it. But come in, lad. We were sent by the lord deputy to invite you to supper. There’s a dainty lump of dead horse waiting for you.”

“Send it out then,” said practical Amyas. “And tell his lordship that, with his leave, I won’t stir from here until morning, if I can stay awake. There’s a stir in the fort—I expect they’ll come out.”

“Tut, man! Their hearts are broken. We know it from their deserters.”

“Seeing’s believing. I never trust runaways. If they’re false to their masters, they’ll be false to us.”

“Well, go thy ways, old honesty. And Mr. Secretary shall give you a book in the *Faerie Queene*—‘Sir Monoculus or the Legend of Common Sense,’ eh, Edmund?”

“Monoculus?”

“Ay, Single-eye, my prince of word-coiners—won’t that fit? And give him the Cyclops head for a device. Heigh-ho! They may laugh that win. I’m sick of this Irish work. Were it not for advancement, I’d rather drive a team of red Devons on Dartside. And now I’m angry with the dear lad because he isn’t sick of it too. What a plague business he has, paddling up and down, contentedly doing his duty like any city watchman! It’s an insult to the mighty aspirations of our nobler hearts—eh, my would-be Ariosto?”

“Ah, Raleigh! You can afford to confess yourself less than some, for you are greater than all. Go on and conquer, noble heart! But as for me, I sow the wind, and I suppose I shall reap the whirlwind.”

“Your harvest seems already come—what a blast that was! Hold on by me, Colin Clout, and I’ll hold on by thee. So! Don’t tread on that pikeman’s stomach, lest he take thee for a marauding Don and slit Cohn’s pipe and Colin’s weasand too.”

And the two stumbled away into the darkness, leaving Amyas to pace as before, wrestling with Raleigh’s wild words and Spenser’s melancholy, until he concluded there was a strange bond between cleverness and unhappiness. Thanking his stars he was neither scholar, courtier, nor poet, he said grace over his lump of horseflesh when it arrived, devoured it as if it were venison, and returned to his patrol—this time in silence, for the night deepened, and no need to alert the Spaniards that he was awake.

So he began to think of his mother—how she might be spending Christmas. Then of Frank, wondering what grand festival he attended amid bright lights, sweet music, and gay ladies—how he dressed, whether he thought of his brother far away on the dark Atlantic shore. Then he said his prayers and his creed. Then he tried not to think of Rose Salterne—and, of course, thought of her all the more.

The hours passed slowly, past eleven o’clock. Lights were out in the battery and the ships. The only sounds were the steady tramp of the sentinels beside him, and now and then a grunt from the men sleeping under arms twenty yards behind.

He paced back and forth, scanning the strip of dune between him and the fort. All was black and blank. Then it began to rain fiercely.

Suddenly, he thought he heard a rustle in the coarse sand-grass. True, the wind whistled through it loudly—but that sound was not quite like the wind. Then a soft sliding noise—something had slipped down a bank, dragging sand with it. Amyas froze, crouched beside a cannon, and pressed his ear to the rampart. Clearly, he heard approaching feet—rabbits or men, he couldn’t tell—but he suspected the latter.

And so he waited.
