To grasp the architecture of this tragedy, one must first confront the absolute volatility of a single, calculated falsehood. A lie is not merely a blow with a curtal axe; its consequences are unpredictable, ramifying far beyond the scope of human calculation. While a physical strike leaves a wound that can be anticipated, a lie breeds chaos without bounds, often costing lives and laying waste to entire households. In this story, the repercussions of one heartless fabrication were tremendous, yet the architects of that deceit reaped little reward. The brothers, who concocted the lie solely to diminish the number of claimants to their father's estate, found themselves displaced rather than enriched. They watched as little Gerard seized the place in his mother's heart that rightfully belonged to them. Worse still, Eli publicly declared that, believing Gerard lost and likely dead, he had already provided by will for both the orphaned boy and Margaret, his son's widow. At this revelation, a silent, lethal warning passed between the betrayers—a glance that spoke volumes. Cornelis stood on the brink of speaking the truth—that Gerard was most likely alive—but caught his mother's gaze and swallowed his words.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the other partner in this treachery, was now a failing man. He sensed the period approaching when all his accumulated wealth would slip from his grasp, leaving only his misdeeds to cling to his soul. Too intelligent to fully deceive himself, he had never been free from gusts of remorse. In delivering Gerard's letter to Margaret, he had compromised his integrity. "I cannot give up land and money," his giant Avarice commanded. "I will cause her no unnecessary pain," his dwarf Conscience pleaded. Thus, after tampering with the seal and discovering there was not a syllable regarding the deed, he delivered the document himself, making a merit of it to balance his conscience on a scale common to the self-accusing judge. The birth of Margaret's child shocked him, casting his treachery in a new, damning light. If his forged letter were to take effect, he would be responsible for the dishonor of a woman who was the daughter of one friend, the granddaughter of another, and from whom he was unjustly withholding her ancestral lands. These thoughts preyed upon him during that twilight of life when bodily strength decays and the memory of old friendships resurfaces, filling him with gloomy horrors. Yet, he dared not confess, for the cure was an honest man who would have forced him to disgorge everything. With him, Avarice was an ingrained habit, while Penitence remained but a fleeting sentiment.

Matters reached a breaking point one day when Ghysbrecht, returning from the town hall to his home, found a woman waiting in the vestibule, a child cradled in her arms. Veiled, she appeared ashamed, prompting him to address her with magisterial condescension. Upon hearing his tone, she lowered her veil and met his gaze directly. It was Margaret Brandt. Her sudden appearance and intense manner startled him, and he could not conceal his confusion. "Where is my Gerard?" she cried, her bosom heaving. "Is he alive?" "For aught I know," he stammered. "I hope so, for your sake. Prithee, come into this room. The servants!" "Not a step," Margaret retorted, seizing him by the shoulder with the energy of an excited woman. "You know the secret of that which is breaking my heart. Why does not my Gerard come, nor send a line for these many months? Answer me, or all the town shall hear, let alone your servants. My misery is too great to be sported with."

In vain he insisted he knew nothing of Gerard. She countered that those who had sent her to him had told her a different tale. "You do know why he neither comes nor sends," she stated firmly. At this, Ghysbrecht turned paler and paler, summoning all his remaining dignity. "Would you believe those two knaves against a man of worship?" "What two knaves?" she asked keenly. He faltered, "Said ye not...?" Realizing his failure, he broke down. "There I am, a poor old broken man, whose memory is shaken. And you come here, confusing me so, I know not what I say." "Ay, sir, your memory is shaken, or surely you would not be my enemy," she replied sharply. "My father saved you from the plague when none other would approach; he was ever your friend. My grandfather Floris helped you in your early poverty and loved you, man and boy. Three generations of us you have known; and here stands the fourth. This is your old friend Peter's grandchild, and your old friend Floris' great-grandchild. Look down on his innocent face, and think of theirs!"

"Woman, you torture me," Ghysbrecht sighed, sinking onto a bench. But she saw her advantage immediately. Kneeling before him, she placed the boy on his knees. "This fatherless babe is poor Margaret Brandt's, who never did you ill, and comes of a race that loved you. Nay, look at his face. It will melt you more than any word of mine. Saints of heaven, what can a poor desolate girl and her babe have done to wipe out all memory of thine own young days, when thou wert guiltless as he is, that now looks up in thy face and implores thee to give him back his father?" Holding the child high with her arms beneath him, she smiled under the old man's eyes. He cast a wild look of anguish upon the child, then another upon the kneeling mother, before starting up and shrieking, "Avaunt, ye pair of adders!" The sting of her words gave his old limbs a momentary vigor. He walked rapidly, wringing his hands and clutching at his white hair. "Forget those days? I forget all else. Oh, woman, woman, sleeping or waking I see but the faces of the dead, I hear but the voices of the dead, and I shall soon be among the dead. There, there, what is done is done. I am in hell. I am in hell." Unnatural force ended in prostration. He staggered, and but for Margaret's support with one disengaged arm, would have fallen. She cried for help. Servants rushed in and carried him away in a state bordering on syncope. The last sight Margaret had of him was his old, furrowed face, white and helpless as the hair hanging down over the servant's elbow. "Heaven forgive me," she whispered. "I doubt I have killed the poor old man."

Yet, this attempt to penetrate the torturing mystery left it as dark, or darker, than before. As she pondered every word, her suspicion was confirmed that Ghysbrecht did know something about Gerard. "And who were the two knaves he thought had done a good deed, and told me? Oh, my Gerard, my poor deserted babe, you and I are wading in deep waters." The visit to Tergou cost more than she could afford, and a customer fled owing her money. Compelled once more to unfold Catherine's angel, she descended the stairs with it in hand only to find loose silver on the table accompanied by a written line: *For Gerard his wife*. She fell with a cry of surprise upon the writing, which soon rose into a cry of joy. "He is alive. He sends me this by some friendly hand." She kissed the note repeatedly and tucked it into her bosom.

Time rolled on, bringing no further news of Gerard, yet about every two months a small sum in silver found its way into the house. Sometimes it lay on the table; once it was flung through the bedroom window in a purse; another time it rested at the bottom of Luke's basket. He had stopped at the public-house to speak with a friend. The giver or his agent was never detected. Catherine disowned it; Margaret Van Eyck swore she had no hand in it; so did Eli. Whenever the funds arrived, Margaret would say to little Gerard, "Oh, my poor deserted child, you and I are wading in deep waters." She applied at least half of this modest, yet vital supply, to dressing the little Gerard beyond his station in life. "If it does come from Gerard, he shall see his boy neat." All the mothers in the street began to sneer, particularly those with children dressed in rags. Months rolled on, and a dead sickness of heart succeeded these keener torments. She returned to her first thought: "Gerard must be dead. She should never see her boy's father again, nor her marriage lines." This last grief, somewhat allayed by Eli and Catherine recognizing her betrothal, now revived in full force, as others refused to look favorably upon her story. Often she moaned over her boy's illegitimacy. "Is it not enough for us to be bereaved? Must we be dishonored too? Oh, that we had ne'er been born."

A change took place in Peter Brandt. His mind, clouded for nearly two years, seemed now to be clearing; he experienced intervals of intelligence, during which he and Margaret talked of Gerard until he wandered again. One day, returning after an absence of several hours, Margaret found him conversing with Catherine in a way he had not done since his paralytic stroke. "Eh, girl, why must you be out?" she asked.
