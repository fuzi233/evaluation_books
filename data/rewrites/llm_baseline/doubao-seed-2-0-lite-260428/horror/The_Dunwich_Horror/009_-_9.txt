The sun hung bleached and sickly over Dunwich at 1 p.m. that Friday, when Armitage, Rice, and Morgan pulled their motor car to a stop outside Osborn’s general store. The three Arkham academics had left their well-lit, book-lined offices at dawn, but the weight of the region’s ill omen settled over them before they’d even spotted the first of the hunchbacked, domed hills. Even in full midday light, the deep ravines gaped like unblinking shadowed mouths, and the jagged circles of standing stones perched on the highest crests jutted against the pale sky like fragments of some long-broken blasphemous altar.

The second they stepped into Osborn’s store, they could taste the hushed, wide-eyed terror hanging thick in the air. No one met their gaze directly, and the few muttered words they got told them the full of the horror: Elmer Frye, his wife, and all three of their children were gone. The afternoon blurred into a parade of mounting, cold dread as they drove the rutted back roads of the valley, questioning skittish natives and staring at the unholy wreckage left in the monster’s wake: the Frye home collapsed into splinters reeking of tarry, alien stickiness, barrel-sized prints sunk six inches into packed yard mud, Seth Bishop’s half-flayed cattle lowing in their pasture with wounds no earthly claw could tear, and wide swathes of forest where every pine and oak had been snapped clean in half like dry straw. Armitage traced the broad, crushed path leading up and down Sentinel Hill, then stood staring for long minutes at that flat, weathered altar-stone on its summit, as if he could see the ancient evil that had knelt there, waiting, for generations.

It was only then that they learned five State Police troopers had driven in from Aylesbury that morning, answering the first frantic calls about the Frye massacre. No one could find them. Their car sat empty by the Frye ruins, doors hanging open, half-smoked cigarettes still smoldering on the dash, no sign of a struggle. The natives’ confusion curdled into new, sharper terror when old Sam Hutchins went bone white, nudged Fred Farr hard in the ribs, and pointed a shaking finger at the dark, damp maw of Cold Spring Glen, so deep the midday sun never touched its floor. “Gawd,” he gasped, “I telled ’em not ter go daown there. I never thought nobody’d dew it, not with them tracks, that smell, the whippoorwills a-screechin’ daown in there even in full daylight.” A cold, windless shudder rippled through every person gathered there, and every head tilted back unconsciously, as if straining to hear the low, heavy breathing they swore was echoing up from the glen’s hidden depths.

Armitage’s hands shook as he realized the full weight of the responsibility resting on his shoulders. Night was coming fast, the hour the mountainous, unseen blasphemy lumbered out on its unholy path. *Negotium perambulans in tenebris*… he mouthed the old Necronomicon line he’d memorized months prior, clutching the crumpled sheet of paper with the alternative rites he’d not yet committed to memory, and fumbled to confirm his flashlight still worked. Rice hauled a heavy metal insecticide sprayer out of their valise, loaded with the strange, iridescent reagent they’d brewed back at Miskatonic, and Morgan unlatched the action of his big-game rifle, ignoring Armitage’s quiet warning that no earthly bullet could kill a thing that had no business existing in this dimension at all. Armitage had read Wilbur Whateley’s hideous diary cover to cover, he knew exactly what they were facing, but he said nothing to stoke the Dunwich villagers’ panic further. He hoped they could destroy the thing before the wider world ever learned the apocalyptic horror it had narrowly escaped.

As the sun dipped below the hills, the locals scattered for their cabins, slamming their doors and bolting them tight, even though every one of them had seen the creature crush a whole house as easily as a man crumples a paper bag. They shook their heads at the three academics’ plan to stand guard by the glen, none of them bothering to voice their certainty that the three would be torn to pieces before dawn.

That night, the hills rumbled like a living thing shifting under the ground, and the whippoorwills’ screams never once fell silent. Every few minutes, a gust of wind billowing out of Cold Spring Glen carried a thick, oily, unnameable stench, the exact fetor the three men had breathed when they stood over Wilbur Whateley’s half-uncloaked, dying body on the Miskatonic library floor. But the monster never emerged. Armitage told his companions venturing down into the glen in the dark was a death sentence, that they could never fight what they could not see in that pitch-black, unhallowed gully.

Dawn came wan and gray, drizzle drizzling down from a sky stacked heavy with roiling black clouds piling up over the northwest hills. The three men huddled for shelter under the only intact Frye outbuilding, debating their next move – wait the storm out, or march down into the glen to hunt their nameless quarry? Thunder boomed far off, then sheet lightning bleached the sky white, and a forking bolt stabbed straight down into the depths of Cold Spring Glen. The sky turned so dark it felt like twilight in mid-morning, and for a minute they thought the storm would pass quickly.

Then they heard it: a dozen villagers running screaming down the road, sobbing, half out of their minds, their clothes torn and caked in mud. The first man in the lead collapsed to his knees, gasping out the words that made Armitage and his friends go rigid: “Oh Gawd, it’s a-goin’ agin, an’ this time by day! It’s out, it’s movin’ right now, the Lord alone knows when it’ll tear through all of us.”

The tale poured out, disjointed and hysterical: Luther the hired hand had been chasing the cows out of the storm after the lightning strike, when he’d seen every single tree at the mouth of the glen bend sideways at once, smelled that same unholy tar stench, and heard a swishing, lapping sound no earthly wind or beast could make. He’d heard the planks of the Bishop’s Brook bridge creak, strain, and snap in two, and when he’d run back to check the mud, the rain hadn’t yet washed away the same barrel-sized prints he’d found on the road the Monday prior. Then the whole valley had been on the party line, listening when Seth Bishop’s housekeeper Sally called screaming: the trees by his road were bending for no reason, the dogs were howling so hard their throats bled, the out shed caved in like it was made of tissue paper, the picket fence crumpled into splinters, and something huge, invisible, kept slamming itself against the front walls of the Bishop house, over and over. Then there was a crash, a chorus of screams, and the line had gone dead. Not a sound after that. The villagers were sure it was divine judgment, no mortal thing could possibly stop it.

Armitage stepped forward, his voice steady and firm, reassuring the faltering crowd. “We can stop it,” he told them. “I’ve read Wilbur Whateley’s diary, I know the old rites the wizard used, rites that can banish this thing back to the spaces between the hills it crawled out of. That sprayer will make the invisible monster glow bright, just for long enough to finish the spell. This isn’t even half of what Wilbur would have unleashed on the whole world if he’d lived. We can’t hesitate.”

Earl Sawyer pointed out the shortest path to the Bishop ruins, cutting across the lower meadow, wading the brook, and climbing through the gnarled old timber lot at the foot of Sentinel Hill. The sun broke through the clouds halfway through their climb, but the ancient twisted trees around them loomed like groping hands, and the air grew thick with that familiar, unwholesome pressure. When they emerged on the far side of the treeline, the full ruin of the Bishop property lay before them: splintered rubble, no bodies, nothing but that clinging tarry stench, and a wide, clear path of crushed underbrush leading straight toward the rotting Whateley homestead, and up the slopes of Sentinel Hill.

Armitage handed his high-powered pocket telescope to Morgan, whose eyesight was sharper. After a second of staring, Morgan gasped, passing the glass to Earl Sawyer. It took the farmer a minute to focus the lenses, and when he did, his yell was raw, unhinged: “Gawd almighty! The grass and bushes is a-movin’! It’s crawlin’ slow up the hill, right now, heading straight for that altar stone on the summit. Heaven knows what it’s plannin’ to do up there.”

Panic pricked through the whole crowd of searchers. They had been chasing a story, a rumor – but now they could see the rippling path of the hundred-foot-tall, unseen monstrosity creeping ahead of them, huge, hungry, brimming with a malevolence no human had ever been meant to face. Every man there could feel it, that they were inches away from stepping over a line no sane person should ever cross, into the forbidden, unnameable spaces outside all human understanding.
