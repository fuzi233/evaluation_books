Thirty days after leaving Dawson, the Salt Water Mail, with Buck and his companions at the lead, stumbled into Skaguay. They were a wreck, utterly drained. Buck, once a sturdy one hundred and forty pounds, had shrunk to one hundred and fifteen. His mates, though naturally lighter, had shed proportionately more. Pike, the habitual liar, now limped with genuine injury; Sol-leks hobbled, and Dub bore a torn shoulder blade.

Every step was a labor of agony. Their feet fell heavily, jarred their bodies, and doubled the exhaustion of the day. It was not the fatigue born of sudden, frantic exertion, from which recovery takes hours. It was the slow, relentless draining of months' toil, a "dead tired" that consumed the last reserve of strength. Every muscle, fiber, and cell was spent. In less than five months, they had traversed twenty-five hundred miles, resting for only five days. Arriving at Skaguay, they appeared to be on their last legs, barely managing to keep the traces taut as they tottered down the main street.

"Mush on, poor sore feets," the driver coaxed, his voice flat. "Dis is de las'. Den we get one long res'. Eh? For sure. One bully long res'."

The drivers, having covered twelve hundred miles with merely two days' rest, believed justice and reason demanded a long stopover. Yet, the mail was clogged with Alpine congestion due to the flood of prospectors rushing north, leaving behind families who stayed home. Official orders mandated that fresh, vigorous Hudson Bay dogs replace the worn-out, worthless teams. The old dogs, counting for little against dollars, were to be sold.

Three days passed, confirming the depth of their weakness. On the fourth morning, two men from the States approached and purchased the team, harness included, for a song. One was named Charles, a middle-aged man with watery eyes and a mustache that curled fiercely upward, defying the drooping lip beneath. The other was Hal, a boy of nineteen or twenty, armed with a Colt's revolver and a belt bristling with cartridges—an advertisement of his sheer callowness. Both men were hopelessly out of place, mysteries that baffled understanding.

Buck observed the transaction, sensing that the Scotch half-breeds, Perrault, François, and the former drivers were fading from his life. Driven to the new owners' camp, Buck saw a scene of slovenly disorder: a tent half-stretched, unwashed dishes, and chaos everywhere. There was a woman, whom they called "Mercedes"—Charles's wife and Hal's sister.

As the men began packing, their lack of method was glaring. The tent was rolled into an awkward bundle three times larger than necessary; dirty dishes were shoved aside; Mercedes fluttered in and out, offering endless, useless advice. She insisted a clothes-sack go on the front of the sled, then the back, then uncovered items hidden inside, forcing them to unload and reload repeatedly. Neighbors watched, grinning and winking, offering blunt counsel: "I wouldn't tote that tent along if I was you." Undeterred, Charles and Hal piled on the last oddments, creating a mountainous, top-heavy load.

"The lazy brutes!" Hal shouted, raising his whip as the dogs strained against the breast-bands but failed to budge the sled. "I'll show them!"

Mercedes seized the whip, snatching it from his hand. "Oh, Hal, you mustn't! Promise you won't be harsh, or I won't go a step."

"Precious lot you know about dogs," Hal sneered. "You've got to whip 'em. Ask anyone."

"They're weak as water," a bystander interjected wisely. "They need a rest."

"Rest be blanked!" Hal swore, his oath drawing a gasp of pain from Mercedes. Defending her brother, she declared, "You're driving our dogs; do what you think best."

Again, the whip fell. The dogs threw themselves forward, digging their feet into the packed snow, but the sled held firm like an anchor. After two futile efforts, they stood panting, the whip whistling savagely until Mercedes dropped to her knees before Buck, weeping. "You poor, poor dears," she sobbed, embracing him. "Why don't you pull hard?"

An onlooker stepped forward. "For the dogs' sake, break out that sled. The runners are frozen fast. Throw your weight against the gee-pole."

Following the advice, Hal broke the runners free. The overloaded sled lurched forward, the dogs struggling frantically under the storm of blows. Just as they gained momentum, the path curved steeply. An experienced handler might have saved them, but Hal did not. The sled tipped, spilling half its cargo onto the street. The dogs, angry and abused, broke into a run, scattering the remaining gear.

Kind-hearted citizens caught the fleeing animals and gathered the spilled belongings. "Half the load and twice the dogs," they advised, if they hoped to reach Dawson. Reluctantly, the men pitched the tent and sorted the wreckage. Laughter erupted over the canned goods, a luxury forgotten on the Long Trail. "Blankets for a hotel!" one joked. "Throw away that tent and those dishes—who's washing them? Do you think you're traveling on a Pullman?"

Thus began the inexorable elimination of the superfluous. Mercedes wept as clothesbags were dumped and necessities discarded, rocking back and forth, refusing to budge an inch. Eventually, she even threw out her own essentials, attacking her husband's and brother's belongings in a frenzy.

To supplement the exhausted team, Charles and Hal bought six "Outside" dogs. Added to the original six and Teek and Koona from the Rink Rapids, the team numbered fourteen. Yet, these newcomers were useless. Three were short-haired pointers, one a Newfoundland, and two indeterminate mongrels. They knew nothing of the trail, bewildered and spirit-broken by the savage environment. Buck and his comrades viewed them with disgust.

The outlook was grim. The old team, worn out by twenty-five hundred miles, faced the future with hopelessness. The two men, however, were cheerful and proud, boasting of their fourteen-dog sled. They had calculated the journey with a pencil: so much food per dog, so many dogs, so many days. Mercedes nodded in agreement, unaware that fourteen dogs could not possibly eat enough to sustain a sled of such weight.

The next morning, Buck led the long line up the street. There was no snap, no go. They were starting dead weary. Having crossed the distance four times already, the thought of facing it again filled Buck with bitterness. His heart was not in the work, nor was theirs. The Outsides were timid; the Insides lacked faith in their masters.

These two men and the woman were incompetent. It took them half the night to pitch a sloppy camp and half the morning to pack it away, wasting the day in rearranging the load. Sometimes they made ten miles; often, they didn't start at all. They rarely exceeded half the daily distance used by seasoned travelers.

Consequently, they ran short on dog-food. To hasten the crisis, they overfed the dogs initially. The Outsides, untrained by famine, had voracious appetites. When the worn-out huskies pulled weakly, Hal doubled the orthodox ration. Even when Mercedes, tearful, tried to coax him into feeding them more, she secretly stole scraps from the fish-sacks. But what the dogs needed was rest, not food. The heavy load sapped their strength, and progress was agonizingly slow.

Then came the underfeeding. Hal realized his supply was half-gone with only a quarter of the journey completed. With no means to buy more, he slashed the rations. His sister and brother-in-law supported him, but their heavy outfits and incompetence frustrated every attempt to increase travel time. They could not feed the dogs enough, yet they could not make them run faster. Worse, they did not know how to work themselves, delaying starts until daylight faded.

First to fall was Dub, the clumsy thief who had worked faithfully despite constant punishment. His untreated, worsening shoulder injury left him unable to pull. Hal shot him with the Colt's revolver. Next, the Newfoundland succumbed to the starvation rations, followed by the three short-haired pointers. The two mongrels hung on with grit, but eventually, they too died.

By this time, all the gentleness of the Southland had vanished. The glamour of Arctic travel evaporated, replaced by a harsh, brutal reality. Mercedes stopped weeping for the dogs, focusing instead on her own misery and quarreling endlessly with her husband and brother. Irritable, stiff, and aching, they were sharp-tongued and cruel. Charles and Hal wrangled constantly, each convinced he did more than his share, dragging in arguments about art, politics, and distant relatives. The fire remained unbuilt, the camp half-pitched, the dogs unfed.

Mercedes harbored a grievance of sex. Soft and pretty, accustomed to chivalry, she found the treatment from her male relatives unbearable. Her custom of being helpless only fueled their complaints, which in turn drove her to hysterics. She persisted in riding the sled, weighing one hundred and twenty pounds—a crushing burden for the starving beasts. When they forced her off by main strength, she sat on the trail like a spoiled child. They returned three miles later, and by force, placed her back on the sled.
