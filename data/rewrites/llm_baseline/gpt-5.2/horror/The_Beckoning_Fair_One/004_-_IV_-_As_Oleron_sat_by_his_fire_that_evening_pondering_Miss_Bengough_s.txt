As Oleron sat by his fire that evening, turning over Miss Bengough’s prophecy as one turns a bruised apple in the hand to find the rotten spot, he decided it would have been kinder if she had kept her certainties to herself. A man does not set a fine sauce the better for being told beforehand it will split; the warning itself becomes the heat under the pan. Speak of difficulties, and you season the air with them until the tongue has made them almost inevitable.

He heartily confounded her.

Some hostile influence to the finishing of *Romilly* had, by her mere utterance, been brought to birth; and in that brisk, illogical manner women sometimes possess, she had fastened the thing upon his new abode as though she had pinned an evil charm to the curtains.

“You’ll never finish *Romilly* here.”

Why not? Was this her notion—that comfort, like over-rich pastry, cloys the appetite for work and leaves a man slack, dropped out of the race? The place was well enough—entirely charming, for that matter—but not so enervating as all that. No; Elsie had missed the mark.

He shifted his chair to take the room in again, and the room, in firelight, looked as though it smiled back—positively smiled, as a hearth-smile answers a hand held out to it. He smiled too, as if pity were due to a maligned apartment. In the soft glow even that slight lack of robust colour he had noticed by day was gone; the chintz curtains, flowered and trellised with baskets and oaten pipes, fell in long quiet folds to the window-seats; the old bookcases showed their rows of bindings as richly as jars on a pantry shelf catch lamplight; and whatever sallowness the daylight had insisted upon seemed to have been cooked away by the fire’s kindly heat.

If the truth must be told, it had been Elsie herself who had seemed a little out of the picture.

That thought, once tasted, returned with an after-flavour he could not at first name. The room had, quite accidentally, done Miss Bengough a disservice that afternoon; it had placed her—set her down—so that a contrast declared itself. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the room in which he sat were indeed characterised by a certain spareness, a lack of vigour; then so much the worse for Elsie, who certainly erred on the side of redundancy, of general muchness. If one must set qualities side by side, Oleron knew his taste ran to the austere.

It came on him with the sharpness of discovering too much spice in a dish: he wondered he had not found it before. He saw her again as she had appeared—large, showy, moistly pink, with that prize-bloom quality exuding from her as if she were perpetually just out of the hothouse—and instantly she suffered in his thought. He even recognised now that, at the time, he had noticed something odd, and that unconsciously his attitude, even while she had been there, had been one of criticism.

The mechanism of her was a little obvious. Her melting humidity seemed the outcome of processes that could be analysed, like a glaze that shines because of sugar and heat, not because of any miracle; and behind her—behind the florid, healthy surface—there had lurked, dimly, some emblem of mortality, as though the very abundance foretold the slackening and collapse that follow it. In ten years of intimacy he had never once dreamed of asking her to marry him; none the less, for the first time he felt a distinct thankfulness that he had not.

Then, suddenly and swiftly, his face flamed at the meanness of his own mind. What was he doing—dissecting Elsie Bengough, his friend? Elsie, with whom he had spent weeks and weeks of afternoons; the good chum on whose help he would have counted if the rest of the world failed him; the woman whose loyalty he knew would not swerve while she drew breath. To turn her, even in thought, into a thing to be judged and sliced open—he was an ingrate, a cad.

Had she been there at that moment he would have humbled himself before her.

For ten minutes and more he sat staring into the fire while that humiliating red ebbed slowly from his cheeks. All was still within and without—save for a tiny musical tinkling from the kitchen, the drip of water from a tap not quite turned off into the vessel beneath it. It was so domestic, so small, so like a neglected detail after washing up, that it might have belonged to any house in any street; yet in this quiet it was as present as a clock. Mechanically he began to beat with his finger to the faint falling of the drops, as though keeping time for a tune he did not know he knew; and the little regular motion seemed to hasten the retreat of shame. He cooled again.

And when he took up his meditation, he did it without any consciousness that he returned precisely to the place he had left.

It was not only Elsie’s florid superfluity he had approached in a critic’s attitude; there were also the wide differences between her mind and his. Looking back, he felt no gratitude that, up to a certain point, their natures had run companionably side by side; he was full of questions beyond that point. Their intellects diverged. Had there been any real coincidence at all? He had read his work to her; she had seemed to understand, to speak to the point—yet what can a man do who has assumed another sees as he does, until a single phrase falsifies and discredits everything that has gone before? He doubted all now, and the doubt spread like a stain in hot water.

It did occur to him, briefly, that the man who demands more of a friend than can be given risks losing that friend; but he put the thought aside. Again he ceased to think, and again his finger moved in time to the distant dripping—one small sound in an otherwise merciful hush, a little bright pulse against the room’s calm.

And now, if these things were true of Elsie Bengough, they were also true of the creation of which she was the prototype—Romilly Bishop.

Since he could say of Romilly what decency would not let him say of Elsie, he let his mind go. He did so there in that smiling, fire-lighted room, with the faint tap-music for accompaniment. There was no longer any doubt about it.

He hated the central character of his novel.

Even as he had described her physically, she overpowered the senses: coarse-fibred, over-coloured, rank—like a fruit that has been forced too quickly and sweats its sweetness out into cloy. The moment he named the thought, it became true beyond argument. Gulliver had spoken of Brobdingnagian maids-of-honour so; and mentally and spiritually Romilly corresponded—unsensitive, limited, common. The model—the model stuck out through fifteen vulgar and blatant chapters so plainly that, without knowing why, he had been unable to begin the sixteenth.

He marvelled that it had only just dawned upon him.

And this—this was to have been his Beatrice, his vision. As Elsie she was to have been fed into the furnace of his art and to have come out the Woman all men desire: her thoughts culled from his own finest; her form from his dearest dreams; her setting wherever he could find one fit for her worth. He had brooded long before making the attempt; then, one day, he had felt her stir within him as a mother feels a quickening, and he had begun to write, and so had added chapter to chapter—

And those fifteen sodden chapters were what he had produced.

Again he sat, his finger softly moving to the drip.

Then he bestirred himself, decisively, as a cook will when he has tasted and knows the whole pot is spoiled. She must go. All fifteen chapters of her. That was settled. For what was to take her place his mind was blank; but one thing at a time. A man is not excused from taking the wrong course because the right one is not immediately revealed. Better would come if it was to come; in the meantime—

He rose, fetched the fifteen chapters, and read them over before he should drop them into the fire.

But instead of putting them into the fire, he let them fall from his hand.

The drip asserted itself again. It had, he realised, a tinkling gamut of four or five notes, on which it rang irregular changes; it was foolishly sweet and dulcimer-like, as though some small, bright instrument were being plucked in the kitchen by invisible fingers. In his mind he could see each drop gathering, swelling, trembling on the lip of the tap, and then the tiny percussion of its fall—plink, plunk—minimised almost to nothing. Following the lowest note there seemed to be a brief phrase, irregularly repeated; and presently he found himself waiting for the recurrence of that phrase as one waits for a refrain.

It was quite pretty.

It was also, in its way, unnerving—not by loudness, but by how it insinuated itself into attention, like a remembered air creeping in under the door. It did not conduce to wakefulness. Sitting there with the papers in a heap, with the fire lowering, Oleron dozed.

When he awoke again the fire had burned low, and the flames of the candles were licking at the rims of the Sheffield sticks. Sluggishly he rose, yawned, went his nightly round of locks and fastenings—door, window, latch by latch—and passed into his bedroom. Soon he slept soundly.

A curious little sequel followed on the morrow.

Mrs. Barrett usually tapped, not at his door, but at the wooden wall beyond which lay his bed; and then Oleron rose, put on his dressing-gown, and admitted her. He was not conscious that, as he did so that morning, he hummed an air; but Mrs. Barrett lingered with her hand on the door-knob, her face a little averted, smiling as though at some old intimacy.

“De-ar me!” her soft falsetto rose. “But that will be a very o-ald tune, Mr. Oleron! I will not have heard it this for-ty years!”

“What tune?” Oleron asked.

“The tune, indeed, that you was humming, sir.”

His thumb was in the flap of a letter. It stayed there, arrested.

“I was humming?… Sing it, Mrs. Barrett.”

Mrs. Barrett prut-prutted with modesty. “I have no voice for singing, Mr. Oleron; it was Ann Pugh was the singer of our family; but the tune will be very o-ald, and it is called ‘The Beckoning Fair One.’”

“Try to sing it,” said Oleron, his thumb still holding the envelope as though the paper had suddenly grown heavy; and Mrs. Barrett, with dimpling confusion, hummed the air.

“They do say it was sung to a harp, Mr. Oleron, and it will be very o-ald,” she concluded.

“And I was singing that?”

“Indeed you wass. I would not be likely to tell you lies.”

“Very well—let me have breakfast,” Oleron said, and opened his letter; but the trifling circumstance struck him as odder than he would have admitted. For the phrase he had hummed—this ancient scrap with its title like a hand held out in the dark—had been that which, the evening before, he had associated with the falling from the tap.
