Thursday night passed without incident. By Friday morning, however, two distinct pieces of news had arrived.

The first concerned the baker's boy, who reported meeting Rosanna Spearman the previous afternoon. She was wearing a heavy veil and walking toward Frizinghall via the path across the moor. It seemed peculiar that anyone should mistake Rosanna, whose broad shoulders were unmistakable, yet the boy must have been wrong. As everyone knew, Rosanna had been confined to her sickbed all Thursday afternoon, unable to leave her room.

The second piece of news arrived via the postman. Worthy Mr. Candy, having driven off in the rain on the birthday night despite his claim that a doctor's skin is waterproof, had indeed succumbed to the elements. He had contracted a severe chill and was now suffering from a fever. Recent reports indicated he was delirious, babbling nonsense with the same fluency he displayed in his sober moments. While we all sympathized with the little doctor, Mr. Franklin expressed regret primarily on behalf of Miss Rachel. During breakfast, he suggested that unless the mystery surrounding the Moonstone was resolved quickly, Miss Rachel urgently required the finest medical advice available.

Shortly after breakfast, a telegram from Mr. Blake, the elder, arrived in response to his son's message. It announced that, with the assistance of the Commissioner, he had identified the ideal candidate to assist us: Sergeant Cuff. He was expected to arrive on the morning train from London.

Upon reading the name, Mr. Franklin started visibly. He recalled hearing remarkable anecdotes about Sergeant Cuff from his father's lawyer during his time in London. "I begin to hope we are nearing the end of our anxieties," he remarked. "If half the stories I've heard are true, there is no equal in England for unraveling mysteries like Sergeant Cuff!"

Excitement and impatience rippled through the household as the anticipated arrival approached. Superintendent Seegrave, returning at the appointed hour and learning of the Sergeant's imminent arrival, immediately secluded himself in a room with pen, ink, and paper to draft the report he surely awaited. I considered fetching the Sergeant personally, but my lady's carriage and horses were unavailable, even for someone of Cuff's renown; the pony-chaise was reserved for Mr. Godfrey. He deeply regretted departing his aunt during such anxious times and postponed his journey until the last train, hoping to hear the London detective's assessment of the case before heading to town. However, he was bound for the city that Friday night, as a Ladies' Charity in financial difficulty awaited his counsel for Saturday morning.

When the time for the Sergeant's arrival came, I descended to the gate. A fly pulled up from the railway station, and a grizzled, elderly man stepped out. He was so emaciated he appeared to possess not an ounce of flesh on his bones. Dressed entirely in decent black with a white cravat, his face was sharp as a hatchet, his skin yellow, dry, and withered like an autumn leaf. His eyes, a steely light grey, possessed a disconcerting habit of seeming to expect more from you than you realized you possessed. His walk was soft, his voice melancholy, and his long, lanky fingers hooked like claws. He might have been a parson, an undertaker, or anything but what he truly was. He was the antithesis of Superintendent Seegrave and, for a family in distress, a far less comforting figure to behold.

"Is this Lady Verinder's?" he asked.
"Yes, sir."
"I am Sergeant Cuff."
"This way, sir, if you please."

On the road to the house, I introduced myself and my position within the family to assure him he could discuss the matter with me. Yet, he spoke not a word regarding the business. Instead, he admired the grounds, commenting on the briskness of the sea air. Privately, I wondered how the celebrated Cuff had acquired his reputation. Upon reaching the house, we moved in the awkward manner of two strange dogs yoked together for the first time.

Seeking my lady, who was believed to be in one of the conservatories, we circled to the rear gardens and dispatched a servant to locate her. While we waited, Sergeant Cuff peered through the evergreen archway, spotted our rosery, and strode straight in, displaying the first sign of genuine interest he had shown. To the gardener's astonishment and my dismay, this renowned policeman revealed himself to be a veritable mine of knowledge concerning rose gardens.

"Ah, you have secured the proper exposure here, facing south and southwest," the Sergeant noted, wagging his grizzled head with a trace of pleasure in his mournful tone. "This is the ideal shape for a rosery—nothing better than a circle set within a square. Yes, with walks between all the beds. But they ought not to be gravel walks like these. Grass, Mr. Gardener—grass walks between your roses; gravel is too harsh for them. That is a lovely bed of white and blush roses. They always blend beautifully, don't they? Here is the white musk rose, Mr. Betteredge—our old English variety standing tall alongside the newest imports. Very dear!" he continued, fondling the bloom with his claw-like fingers, treating the plant as if it were a child.

"What a pleasant sort of man to be recovering Miss Rachel's diamond and identifying the thief!" I muttered inwardly.

"You seem fond of roses, Sergeant?" I ventured.
"I haven't much time to be fond of anything," replied Sergeant Cuff. "But when I *do* have a moment to spare, Mr. Betteredge, the roses usually receive it. I began my life among them in my father's nursery garden, and I shall end it among them, if fate permits. Yes. One of these days (God willing) I shall retire from catching thieves and attempt growing roses. There will be grass walks, Mr. Gardener, between my beds," he added, clearly unsettled by the gravel paths of our current rosery.

"It seems an odd taste, sir," I dared to suggest, "for a man in your line of work."
"If you will look around you (which most people fail to do)," said Cuff, "you will see that a man's tastes are often as diametrically opposed to his profession as possible. Show me two things more contrary than a rose and a thief, and I shall adjust my tastes accordingly—if it is not too late in my years." He paused, addressing the gardener. "You consider the damask rose a good stock for most tender varieties, do you not, Mr. Gardener? Ah! I thought so. Here comes a lady. Is it Lady Verinder?"

He had spotted her before either I or the gardener had, though we knew exactly where to look and he did not. I began to suspect he was sharper than he initially appeared. Whether it was his appearance or his mission that caused my lady slight embarrassment, she found herself momentarily at a loss for words in the presence of a stranger. Sergeant Cuff, however, put her at ease immediately. He inquired if anyone else had been engaged regarding the robbery prior to hiring him. Learning that another officer was present in the house, he requested permission to speak with him before proceeding further.

My lady led the way back. Before following, the Sergeant offered a final comment to the gardener regarding the gravel paths. "Get her ladyship to try grass," he said with a sour glance at the stones. "No gravel! No gravel!"

I cannot fully explain why Superintendent Seegrave appeared diminished in stature upon being introduced to Sergeant Cuff, but the fact remains undeniable. They withdrew together and remained sequestered for a considerable duration. When they emerged, the Superintendent was agitated, while the Sergeant was yawning.

"The Sergeant wishes to see Miss Verinder's sitting-room," announced Mr. Seegrave with great pomp and eagerness. "The Sergeant may have inquiries to make. Attend the Sergeant, if you please!"

As I was thus commanded, I cast a glance at the great Cuff. On his side, the Sergeant regarded the Superintendent with that quiet, expectant stare I had previously noted. I could not confirm he was watching for his colleague to appear foolish, but I strongly suspected it.

I led the way upstairs. The Sergeant moved softly throughout the Indian cabinet and around the boudoir, posing questions—sometimes directed solely at the Superintendent, but constantly at me. The substance of his inquiries seemed equally unintelligible to both of us. Eventually, his tour brought him to the door adorned with the decorative painting. He placed one lean, questioning finger on the small smear beneath the lock, which the Superintendent had already observed while reprimanding the maids for crowding into the room.

"That is a pity," said Sergeant Cuff. "How did this occur?"
He posed the question to me. I explained that the women-servants had crowded into the room the previous morning and that some of their petticoats had caused the damage. "The Superintendent ordered them out, sir," I added, "before further harm was done."
"Correct!" affirmed the Superintendent in his military fashion. "I ordered them out. The petticoats did it, Sergeant—the petticoats did it."
"Do you recall which petticoat caused the mark?" asked Cuff, still addressing me rather than his fellow officer.
"No, sir."
He turned to the Superintendent. "_You_ noticed, I presume?"
The Superintendent looked slightly taken aback but salvaged the situation. "I cannot rely on my memory, Sergeant," he admitted. "It was merely a trifling detail—a mere trifle."
