For seven days and nights, we were battered by a tempest of unprecedented fury. Darkness swallowed us repeatedly, only to reveal scenes of greater terror when the light returned. By the seventh day, all hope had evaporated.

Driven far from our intended course, we knew not where we drifted. The crew, once disciplined, had succumbed to exhaustion and despair. Their masts were splintered, leaks tore through the hull from bow to stern, and the rising sea threatened to swallow us whole. In place of curses, the sailors now cried out to God for mercy, making frantic vows to perform any task if delivered from this nightmare. Each man committed his soul to Heaven, desperately searching for a way to survive.

My heart ached as I surveyed my family amidst the chaos. Our four young sons were paralyzed by terror. "Dear children," I urged, "if the Lord wills, He can save us from this dread; if not, let us calmly surrender our lives to His hands, remembering the eternal joy of being reunited in heaven. Death is not bitter when it keeps us together."

At my words, my weeping wife lifted her head with newfound bravery. Clinging to her, the boys listened as she spoke with calm, loving encouragement. Seeing her fortitude brought me solace, though my own heart nearly broke. Together, we knelt, praying with profound earnestness. Little Fritz, in particular, begged for deliverance for his parents and brothers, seemingly forgetting his own fear. Such childlike trust soothed our souls, making the horrors of our plight feel slightly bearable. *Perhaps*, I thought, *the Lord hears our prayers!*

Suddenly, above the thundering waves, a cry rang out: "Land! Land!" Simultaneously, the ship struck with a bone-jarring force that threw everyone onto the deck, threatening immediate destruction. Shouts of breaking timber and the roar of pouring water signaled the ship's demise.

"Lower the boats!" the Captain shouted over the tumult. "We are lost!"

"Lost!" I gasped, the word piercing my heart like a dagger. Yet, seeing my children's renewed panic, I forced a cheerful demeanor. "Take courage, my boys! We are still above water! Look—the land is near! Let us strive to reach it. Remember, God helps those who help themselves!" I turned to my wife and children, then climbed the slippery deck.

Waves hurled me down, but I regained my footing. The ship was shattering everywhere; a massive hole gaped in the hull. The crew, abandoning the passengers, crowded into the lifeboats. As the last man boarded, he severed the ropes, casting the boats adrift, ignoring my desperate pleas to include us. The wind drowned my voice, and the towering waves made a return impossible.

Despair washed over me until I noticed the ship's stern, where our cabin lay, wedged tightly between two jagged rocks. While the bow was smashed against the breakers, the rear remained elevated. Through gaps in the driving mist, I glimpsed a rugged coastline—a sign of salvation.

Returning to my family, I masked my anguish with a smile. "Courage, dear ones! Though our ship sails no more, our cabin remains dry. If the winds calm by morning, we shall surely reach shore."

My children, accustomed to trusting my judgment, instantly transformed their fear into hope, even enjoying the cessation of the violent rocking. My wife, however, sensed my hidden distress. I confessed our true predicament, fearing the blow to her nerves, but her unwavering faith restored my own resolve.

"We must find food for supper," she declared. "Starving will weaken us for tomorrow."

Night descended, the storm raging as fiercely as before. Crashes echoed from the wreck, signaling further damage. We feared the lifeboats had perished beneath the waves.

"God will help us soon, Father?" asked my youngest.

"You silly child," scolded Fritz, my eldest, "do not presume to dictate God's plans. We must wait patiently."

"I wish you'd spoken kinder," I murmured.

Finally, a meal was ready. The boys ate heartily and fell asleep quickly. Fritz, aware of the danger, stayed awake to keep watch. After a long silence, he suggested, "Father, couldn't we make swimming belts for Mother and the younger boys? You and I can swim."

"That is an excellent idea," I replied. "Let us prepare them tonight, just in case."

Scouring the wreckage, we found empty flasks and tin cans, linking pairs together to create buoyant floats. My wife and the young boys readily accepted theirs. I gathered matches, tinder, knives, and cord, hoping that if the ship broke apart before dawn, we might reach shore with essential supplies.

While the boys slept, my wife and I prayed vigilantly throughout the night, bracing for disaster.

Dawn broke, revealing a moderating gale and a brilliant sunrise. I woke the boys, and we gathered on the remaining deck. To our astonishment, it was empty—no sailors remained.

"Hallo, Papa! Where are they? Did they take the boats? Why leave us?" the children cried in alarm.

Dispersing, we scavenged the wreck. My wife tended to the suffering animals with her youngest son, Franz. Fritz retrieved weapons and ammunition from the armory. Ernest collected tools—nails, scissors, axes, hammers, and chisels. Even little Franz, usually timid, produced a box of sharp fishhooks, earning my praise despite his brothers' scoffs. My wife shared better news: livestock survived—a donkey, goats, sheep, a ram, a cow, and a pregnant sow. She had saved them with food, though she worried about preserving milk in the heat.

We located four sturdy wooden casks floating nearby. Hauling them aboard, I sawed them horizontally, creating eight shallow tubs. Exhausted from labor, we refreshed ourselves with goat's milk, wine, and biscuits.

Arranged in a row, the tubs looked promising. Yet, my wife hesitated. "I shall never muster the courage to enter one!"

"Do not worry," I promised. "Once you see my design, you may prefer it to this sinking wreck."

Using a long plank as a keel and bending its ends upward, I constructed a narrow boat from the tubs, dividing it into eight compartments. It proved too heavy and clumsy to move manually. Summoning a capstan bar and using makeshift rollers, I applied the principle of leverage to raise the bow, allowing the boys to slide a roller underneath. Explaining Archimedes' law to Ernest, who vowed to study mechanics once safe, we successfully launched the craft.

She slid swiftly into the water, leaning dangerously to one side. Adding weight balanced her, delighting the boys who raced to climb in. Realizing stability was crucial, I attached outriggers—long poles capped with empty brandy casks—to prevent capsizing.

Testing the vessel, I cleared a path from the wreck and prepared oars. Too late to depart, we postponed the journey until morning.

Another night in the precarious wreck was unpleasant, but necessity prevailed. We ate a simple supper and rested, donning float belts again. Persuading my wife to wear a sailor's dress from a midshipman's chest, I assured her it offered greater comfort. Her initial embarrassment faded as she embraced the practicality, and we retired to our hammocks, ready for the day ahead.

Rising early, we knelt in prayer. "With God's help, we escape today," I announced. "Feed the animals we leave behind; perhaps we can return for them later. Gather everything useful."

The boys obeyed joyfully. From their haul, I selected canvas for a tent, carpenter's tools, firearms, fishing gear, pots, and provisions. These items replaced the ballast I had discarded earlier, yet we still had to leave some treasures behind.

Just as we settled into our tubs, roosters crowed and chickens clucked, protesting our abandonment. "Why not take the fowl?" I decided. "If we starve, they can feed us!" Ten hens and two roosters were secured in a tub with netting. Ducks and geese swam freely, while pigeons flew toward the shore.

"So they are," I replied. "When young and fresh. As they ripen, the milk congeals and eventually solidifies into a kernel. This kernel dries as you see, but if dropped on fertile soil, the germ within swells, bursts through the shell, and sprouts a new tree."

Not long after, we were summoned to dinner, and all willingly obeyed the call. During the meal, I engaged the boys by proposing to decide on suitable names for the different spots we had visited on this coast.

"For," I said, "it will become increasingly troublesome to explain what we mean unless we do so. Besides, we shall feel much more at home if we can talk as people do in inhabited countries. Instead of saying, 'the little island at the mouth of our bay where we found the dead shark' or 'that wood where we found coconuts and caught the monkey,' let us begin by naming the bay in which we landed. What shall we call it?"

"Oyster Bay," suggested Fritz.

"No, no—Lobster Bay!" cried Jack, "in memory of the old fellow who took a fancy to my leg!"

"I think," observed my mother, "that, in token of gratitude for our escape, we should call it Safety Bay."

This name met with general approval and was forthwith fixed upon. Other names were quickly chosen: our first place of abode became Tentholm; the islet in the bay, Shark's Island; and the reedy swamp, Flamingo Marsh. It took some time to decide on a name for our leafy castle, but finally, it was entitled Falconhurst. We then rapidly named the remaining points: Prospect Hill, the eminence we first ascended; Cape Disappointment, from whose rocky heights we had strained our eyes in vain search for our ship's company; and Jackal River, for the large stream at our landing place. Thus concluded our geographical nomenclature.

Fritz finished his cases, and Jack asked for my assistance in carrying out his plan of making a cuirass for Turk, the dog, out of the porcupine skin. Juno strongly objected to his friendly approaches, darting out of his way as fast as she could. It was clear that, protected by armor that was both defensive and offensive, he would easily put to flight the fiercest animal he might encounter. I determined to make a helmet for Jack out of the remainder of the skin, which he received with infinite delight.

Others were bent double, with only a few remaining standing upright. We lifted the fallen trees, dug deep holes at their roots, and drove sturdy bamboo props into the earth. Using strong, broad fibers, we lashed the trees securely to these supports.

"Papa," Franz asked, handing me a bundle of fibers as I needed them, "are these wild or tame trees?"

"Oh, these are wild trees! Most ferocious!" Jack laughed. "We are tying them up lest they run away. In a little while, we'll untie them, and they'll trot after us everywhere, giving us fruit wherever we go. Oh, we will tame them! They'll have rings through their noses just like the buffalo!"

"That isn't true," Franz replied seriously. "But there are wild and tame trees. The wild ones grow out in the woods, like crab-apples, and the tame ones grow in gardens, like the pears and peaches at home. Which are these, Papa?"

"They are not truly wild," I answered. "They are grafted, or cultivated—what you call tame trees. No European tree bears good fruit unless it is grafted." Seeing a puzzled look cross the little boy's face at the new word, I explained further.

"Grafting," I continued, "is the art of inserting a slip or twig of one tree into an 'eye'—a knot or scar—in the branch of another. That twig then grows and produces fruit not like the original stock, but exactly like the tree from which the slip was taken. For instance, if we have a sour crab-tree and an apple tree bearing fine Ribston Pippins, we take a slip of the apple tree, insert it into an eye of the crab-tree, and in a year or two, that branch will be laden with delicious apples."

"But," Ernest asked, "where did the slips of good fruit come from, if none grow without grafting?"

"From foreign countries," I replied. "It is only in the cold climate of our part of the world that such grafting is necessary. In many southern regions, the most luscious fruit trees grow naturally in the soil, flourishing and bearing sweet fruit without any human care. Conversely, in England, Germany, and even France, these same trees require the utmost horticultural skill to produce a single berry.

"When the Romans invaded England, they found nothing but crab-apples, nut bushes, and brambles. Yet, by grafting onto these stocks, they produced fine apples, filberts, and raspberries. It was the same in our beloved Switzerland—all our fruit trees were imported."

"Were cherries, Father? Can't we even call cherries Swiss? I always thought they grew nowhere else," Fritz interjected.

"I fear we cannot even claim cherries as our own, not even their name. They are called *cherries* from *Cerasus*, a region in Asia Minor known as Pontus, from whence they were brought to Europe by Lucullus, a Roman general, about seventy years before Christ. Hazelnuts also originate from Pontus; walnuts came originally from Persia. Grapes, however, are of the greatest antiquity. You recall the story of Noah cultivating vines; they have traveled from place to place until they are now found throughout the civilized world."

The once-muddy swamp had been drained into a splendid lake, teeming with black swans, geese, and colorful ducks. Among the water plants, vibrant flamingos, demoiselle cranes, and herons moved in harmonious peace, unafraid of their human masters. Even our giant frogs, Grace and Beauty, had grown to the size of rabbits, responding eagerly to Jack's calls to catch flies. Old Hurry, the ostrich, marched with regal dignity beneath the trees, while countless pigeons nested in the rocks, their soft coos adding to the domestic tranquility.

Access to Rockburg was restricted to a single bridge; upstream, a dense thicket of citrus trees, figs, and thorny shrubs formed an impassable barrier. Our farms thrived: the rabbit warrens on Shark Island provided ample food and fur, while antelopes grazed in the groves on Whale Isle, which was reserved for industrial tasks like candle-making and tanning. The livestock at Woodlands supplied abundant meat, and my wife's dairy was a bustling hub of activity.

True to their nature, our sons loved naming their animal companions. A creamy-white cow became "Blanche"; a bull with a booming voice was dubbed "Stentor"; two swift onagers were named "Arrow" and "Dart"; and Jack claimed a jackal descendant as "Coco," insisting that no word could be pronounced without an 'o' sound. Despite occasional minor fevers and accidents, everyone remained in excellent health.

Though we lived in seclusion, I maintained a firm belief that we would eventually reconnect with civilization. Yet, as time carried our sons toward manhood, it also aged us, stirring anxious thoughts about our future isolation. On such days, I hid my sorrow from the family, finding solace only in prayer.

One afternoon, Fritz was missing. His *cajack* (canoe) was gone, and he had ventured out to sea. Anxious, I joined Ernest and Jack on Shark Island to watch for his return. After a tense wait, we spotted a small speck approaching. To our relief, it was Fritz, towing a heavy sack.

"Welcome back, wherever you come from!" I cried. "You seem to have quite a cargo!"

"Yes, Father," Fritz replied, "my trip yielded both booty and fascinating discoveries. Come, let us unload, and while I rest, I will tell you my adventure."

Gathering around him, Fritz explained that he had long dreamed of exploring the coast beyond the point where he had slain the walrus. Fully prepared with supplies, weapons, and his faithful eagle, Pounce, he had set sail at dawn. He described rounding the corner near the wreck, where he could see the rusted cannons and ironwork lying undisturbed on the seabed.

He navigated past rugged cliffs teeming with sea-fowl, seals, and walruses, eventually reaching a high, precipitous cape. There, he found a magnificent natural archway leading into a vast cavern. Inside, thousands of tiny swallows nested on platforms made of a strange, sponge-like material. Recognizing them as edible birds' nests—highly valued in China and worth their weight in silver—he collected samples for us.

"I suggest you try them, Mother," he urged. Though hesitant, my wife agreed to experiment.

Leaving the cave, Fritz entered a secluded, lake-like bay. Here, he discovered beds of enormous oysters attached to the rocks. Upon examining them, he found they contained numerous pearly spheres of varying sizes.

"You have discovered treasure!" I exclaimed. "These are beautiful pearls! While useless to us now, they could become a source of immense wealth if we ever meet the civilized world again."

Fritz named this hidden cove **Pearl Bay**. Sheltered by reefs and sandbanks, it was a perfect harbor. As he crossed the exit reef, he was attacked by a flock of albatrosses. In his frustration, he struck one down with his boat-hook.

However, the story took a dramatic turn the next morning. Fritz confessed a secret discovery regarding the fallen bird. Attached to its leg was a piece of rag bearing English words: *"Save an unfortunate Englishwoman from the smoking rock!"*

The message sent a shockwave through him. Could someone else truly be alive in this lonely region? The phrase "smoking rock" likely referred to a volcano, yet he refused to accept despair. He wrote a reply on a strip of his handkerchief: *"Do not despair! Help is near!"* He tied it to the bird's leg and released it, hoping it would carry his message westward.

"We must find her," Fritz declared, his resolve hardening. "Will my note ever reach her? Can I save her?"

I advised caution, suggesting the message might be ancient, but Fritz's hope burned too brightly to extinguish. We agreed he must prepare for a major expedition. First, he needed to modify his canoe to carry two passengers and sufficient provisions for a long journey. Meanwhile, the boys, eager to help, focused on harvesting the pearl oysters.

"It seems fitting to establish a pearl fishery immediately!" they cheered, building a temporary hut on the bay's shore.

Finally, the day arrived. Taking leave of my wife and younger brother Franz, Fritz and I boarded the yacht, accompanied by the dogs. Jack proudly took the newly installed seat beside Fritz, acting as pilot alongside his older brother.

We navigated safely through the rocks near Walrus Island, entering a calm expanse where we spotted the elusive paper nautiluses gliding over the water. We secured several specimens, naming the location **Nautilus Creek**. Continuing our journey, we rounded a flat promontory we named **Cape Pug-Nose**, and soon beheld the grand cliffs ahead.

Instead of going around, Fritz steered us directly toward the majestic archway. I marveled at the Gothic-like architecture of the pillars and pinnacles, proposing the name **Cape Minster**. As we passed through the tunnel, flocks of swallows darted out, creating a living curtain before retreating to their nests. The tide carried us effortlessly into the dazzling sunlight of Pearl Bay.

The bay was breathtaking—a romantic haven dotted with islets, surrounded by fertile meadows, shady groves, and murmuring brooks. We sought a suitable landing place near the shallow oyster beds, finding a spot beside a sparkling streamlet. As twilight descended, we lit a watchfire, ate a hurried supper, and left the dogs and Coco the jackal to guard the shore. Anchoring the yacht within gunshot of the land, we prepared for the long and perilous voyage ahead, driven by the hope of saving a lost soul.

Upon our return, my wife and Franz were initially startled by Fritz's sudden disappearance, but their relief at our safe arrival quickly turned to eager anticipation as we recounted our harrowing adventures. My wife shuddered uncontrollably at the tale of our desperate struggle with the lion and his mate, her pale complexion reflecting the terror of the encounter. Jack's miraculous survival also left her trembling; he still bore the marks of his ordeal, making it difficult for her to believe he was truly unharmed. Tears welled in Franz's eyes as he learned of the tragic death of our beloved dog, Juno. Tenderly asking if her remains had been retrieved, he insisted they be buried near the home she had cherished for so many years.

The following day, Franz oversaw her burial with great care. At his request, Ernest crafted an epitaph inscribed upon a stone slab resting above her grave:

*"JUNO / A servant true lies here: / A faithful friend, / A Dog, / To all most dear; / Who met her end / Fighting right bravely in her master's cause."*

The meat from the wild boar and the gathered truffles were handed to my wife, who delighted in the prospect of future feasts. Though she yearned for the boar's head, I honored my pledge to Ernest that it would adorn his museum. Despite my own craving for it roasted in the Hottentot style, I held firm to my word. Consequently, the magnificent boar's head, alongside the lion skins, were transported to the tannery on Whale Island for cleaning and preservation.

Five days elapsed, yet Fritz remained missing. My anxiety grew unbearable, compelling me to decide on a rescue mission. Everyone cheered the plan, and even my wife, hearing we would sail in the pinnace, volunteered to join us.

On a bright morning with favorable winds, the five of us, accompanied by the dogs, boarded the boat and raced toward Cape Minster. Our little yacht danced merrily over the water, buoyed by the sunshine and refreshing sea breeze. As we approached the archway, I spotted a dark, shadowy mass beneath the surface. "A sunken rock," I muttered, puzzled that I had never noticed it before. I lowered the helm instantly, but momentum carried us forward. With a violent shock, we struck the hidden obstacle. Miraculously, the danger passed immediately. Glancing backward, I saw the rock vanish; where its green shadow had been a moment prior, only clear water remained.

Before we could recover, Jack shouted, "Another one, Father! To starboard!" Indeed, a second submerged rock lay ahead. "The rock is moving!" Franz screamed. Suddenly, a massive black body erupted from the sea, spouting a towering column of water that rained down around us. The mystery solved itself: it was a Cachalot whale. Enraged by our earlier scratches on its back, the giant beast retreated slightly before charging. Fearful tales of this creature's destructive power flooded my mind—of boats sunk and ships destroyed. Desperate, I grabbed a musket; Jack took the other. Simultaneously, we fired. Both shots hit their mark. The whale thrashed violently for a few seconds before plunging deep and disappearing.

Refusing to lose such a valuable prize, we scanned the horizon, reloading as we watched the shoreward direction of its retreat. Soon, we sighted it again in shallow waters, lashing its tail and staining the waves crimson with blood. Daring to approach the infuriated monster, we fired once more. Its struggles intensified, becoming frantic, until finally, with a convulsion from head to tail, it lay motionless—dead.

Just as the boys prepared to cry out in triumph, a shout froze them mid-cheer. Behind a rocky outcrop, they spotted a canoe manned by a tall, muscular savage. Standing up in his skiff, the native examined us intently. Noticing our gaze, he paddled back into hiding. Panic seized me; surely a tribe of blacks lurked here, and Fritz must have fallen into their hands. Determined not to be easily captured, we readied our guns.

Soon, dusky faces peeked from high and low rocks, vanishing as quickly as they appeared. The canoe ventured out again for reconnaissance. Anxiety gripped everyone, including Jack, who looked to me for command. "Hoist a white flag," I ordered, "and fetch the speaking-trumpet." Holding the device, I spoke peaceful words in Malay, but the gesture and speech yielded no result; the savage prepared to withdraw.

Impatient, Jack snatched the trumpet. "Come here, you black son of a gun!" he roared. "Board our ship and make friends, or we'll blow you and your—"

"Stop! Foolish boy!" I interrupted sharply. "Your wild words will only alarm him."

"No! Look!" Jack cried. "He's paddling straight for us!" True to his warning, the canoe sped toward us. Then, Franz's terrified shriek cut through the air: "Look! It's in Fritz's canoe! I see the walrus head!"

Only Ernest remained calm. Seizing the trumpet, he called out, "Fritz, ahoy! Welcome, old fellow!"

Scarcely had the words left his lips when I recognized the familiar face beneath the darkened disguise. Within minutes, the brave boy was aboard. Ignoring his soot-stained visage, we greeted him with heartfelt embraces. Immediately, a storm of questions erupted from all sides regarding his whereabouts, his delay, and his transformation into a "blackamoor."

"The last question," Fritz smiled, "is the only one I will answer now. The rest must wait until I recount my adventures. Hearing gunfire, I assumed Malay pirates were attacking. Never dreaming you were here in the yacht, I disguised myself as you see me to scout. Your greeting in Malay only heightened my terror, confirming my suspicion that you were enemies."

After describing our own encounter with the whale, I asked if he knew of a suitable anchorage. "Certainly," he replied, his eyes gleaming with significance. "I can guide you to an island with splendid harbors, teeming with strange wonders." Removing the stains from his skin, he jumped back into his canoe and piloted us to a picturesque islet within the bay.

Now confident in the success of his expedition, I revealed the truth to my wife, preparing her for the surprise awaiting us. As expected, she was deeply moved, almost overwhelmed by the prospect of meeting a human being, specifically a woman, after so long. "Why didn't you tell me sooner?" she asked. "Why wait until the last moment with such joyful news?"

"I feared raising hopes that might never be fulfilled," I explained. "But now, thank Heaven, he has succeeded, and secrecy is no longer needed."

The boys struggled to comprehend the mystery and suppressed excitement shared by their mother, Fritz, and me. Curious glances swept toward the island. Once the sails were furled and the anchor dropped, they eagerly disembarked. Following Fritz silently through the thickets, we eventually emerged into a clearing dominated by a hut constructed from sheltering boughs, with a cheerful fire burning at its entrance.

Fritz slipped inside, leaving his brothers outside, stunned into silence. Moments later, he emerged leading a slender, handsome youth dressed as a young English naval officer. The pair approached us, and Fritz, his face radiant with joy, introduced his companion as Edward Montrose.

"And," he continued, turning to my wife and me, "will you not welcome him as a friend and brother to our family?"

"That we certainly will!" I exclaimed, stepping forward to shake the stranger's hand. "Though our wild life may have coarsened our appearances, I trust it has not hardened our hearts."

My wife embraced the apparent youth warmly. The lads and even the dogs expressed their delight at the new addition—the boys thrilled at the prospect of a fresh companion, while the dogs were charmed by her gentle demeanor. Fritz hinted that the girl wished her true gender remain unknown until my wife could provide attire more suitable for her nature.

The young men then rowed to the yacht to retrieve supplies for supper and prepare a campsite for the night. Once finished, my wife served a hearty meal. Eager to make their new guest feel at home, the boys entertained her tirelessly. Soon, the initial sense of strangeness faded, and she laughed and chatted as merrily as anyone else, admiring our dishes and tasting our mead without mentioning her past life.

Meeting any human after years of isolation excited the boys immensely, but discovering someone so handsome, so vibrant, and so charming completely captivated them. When I signaled the end of the feast, and the new guest was about to be led to her quarters aboard the yacht, a toast was raised to "Edward Montrose," drunk in fragrant mead amidst universal cheering.

Once silence returned, Jack broke the quiet. "Now, Fritz, tell us exactly how you found this jolly fellow. Was it a deliberate search or a chance encounter? Spill the beans while we sit by the fire." Adding more wood to the blaze, he settled down expectantly.

During the long, grey evenings of our captivity, the boys and Jenny gathered in our cozy study to trade stories and read aloud. It was a season of profound happiness, a stark contrast to the confinement of our rocky walls. When the relentless rains finally ceased and the sun broke through to smile upon the waking earth, stepping outside felt like a miracle. The balmy breath of spring reminded us that months had passed since we last felt the freedom of open skies.

Life surged back into motion. The fields, gardens, and orchards demanded the energy of the lads, while Mother and Jenny found endless tasks in the poultry yard and household management. We attended to our scattered settlements—Falconhurst, Woodlands, Prospect Hill, and the islands of Sharks and Whales. Specifically, Jack and Franz were assigned to the island battery.

By evening, after a day of repairing the flagstaff, re-hoisting the Union Jack, and polishing the cannons, the rest of us strolled along the beach, enjoying the cooling sea breeze. We watched as Jack and Franz finished their duties. They loaded their guns, paddled their canoe (cajack) out to sea with an empty tub as a target, fired, and cheered triumphantly as the wooden barrel shattered.

Scarcely had they secured their weapons when a startling boom echoed across the water from the west. Three distinct gunshots.

We froze, speechless. Was it a trick of the wind? Had we imagined the sound? Could it be the boys firing again? No—they were already sprinting toward us, leaping into their canoe with frantic urgency. They had heard it too.

A whirlwind of emotions seized us: anxiety, joy, hope, and doubt. Was it a European vessel, signaling the end of our exile? Or a Malay pirate bent on robbery and murder? Would these strangers be saviors or threats?

Before we could formulate a response, the canoe touched shore. Jack and Franz gasped, "Did you hear them? What shall we do?"

"Oh, Fritz!" my youngest son cried, seizing his brother's hands in an embrace of pure elation. "It must be a European ship! We shall find her! We shall see our Fatherland once more!"

Until that moment, I had not realized how deeply the sight of their European sister had ignited a longing for civilization within the brothers. All eyes turned to me for counsel.

"At present," I advised calmly, "we can do nothing. Night is falling. We must prepare and pray for guidance."

Excitement kept everyone awake until late. We took turns watching the verandah, hoping for further signals, but around midnight, the sky darkened. A terrifying hurricane erupted, raising such a monstrous sea that no boat could survive. If a ship had fired a broadside at the bay's entrance, the noise would have been drowned by the roaring blast.

The storm raged for two days and nights. On the third day, the sun returned, and the sea began to subside. Filled with anxiety, I joined Jack and Franz at the fort to fire the warning guns again. Perhaps the vessel had been driven onto the rocks or had fled the coast; if so, it might never return.

"One... Two..." we fired, waiting.

Minutes passed in tense silence, then a distant reply rolled back to us. Doubt vanished. The strangers were still nearby and aware of our presence. We waved the flag—a signal that all was well—and returned to the house. The entire family was in a frenzy of anticipation. I tried to calm them, knowing I couldn't promise everything would go according to their dreams.

Fritz and I immediately launched a reconnaissance. Armed with guns, pistols, cutlasses, and a spyglass, we paddled out past the high cliffs, heading toward the source of the gunfire. For an hour, we saw only frowning rocks and restless waves. Reaching Cape Pug-Nose, we rounded the bluff.

Suddenly, our doubts dissolved. Beyond the cape, in a sheltered cove, lay a brig-of-war flying the English colors. Her sails were furled, her anchor dropped. Through the glass, I saw figures on deck and tents pitched on the shore, smoke rising from fires.

A cold misgiving struck me. *Why is an English vessel so far from normal shipping lanes?* I recalled tales of mutineers hiding in secluded bays to disguise their ships and raid the seas.

"I see the captain, Father!" Fritz exclaimed, handing me the glass. "He is speaking to an officer. His face is clearly English, and the flag confirms it!"

Keeping hidden behind the cliff, I scrutinized the scene. Neatness and order reigned supreme. Spotless decks, burnished brass, disciplined movements—it was impossible for this to be a band of pirates. Satisfied that the ship would remain anchored for days, we decided to return unseen. We wanted to approach them properly, presenting ourselves not as desperate castaways, but as the rightful masters of this land.

Back at Rockburg, we briefed the family. Even Jenny, whose heart soared at the prospect of meeting countrymen, agreed to wait until the following day. That day was dedicated to preparation. We scrubbed the decks of our yacht, polished the brass, cleared away unused junk, and hoisted the English flag to the peak.

Mother and Jenny selected their finest clothes. Though they had always dressed simply for practical reasons, we knew we needed to impress a naval officer. We discarded our rough, utilitarian gear for smarter attire.

At dawn, the eventful morning arrived. Hearts heavy with emotion, we ate breakfast in near silence. Then, Fritz and Jack quietly fetched baskets overflowing with the island's freshest fruits as gifts. With the anchor weighed and sails set, our little yacht bounded happily over the waters of Safety Bay, avoiding the foaming reefs, and headed straight for the cove.

To the surprise of the crew on the brig, we rounded Cape Pug-Nose and hailed them. Every eye on board and ashore turned toward us. To a military crew expecting wild savages or empty shores, a pleasure yacht carrying a family of settlers was an anomaly they hadn't anticipated.

Fritz and I rowed to the brig. Within moments, we stood on the deck. The captain, a man of simple British frankness, welcomed us warmly and led us to his cabin.

"Good fortune brings you to a coast usually deemed uninhabited," he asked politely.

I recounted our story—the wreck, our survival, and crucially, the rescue of Miss Montrose.

"Then let me thank you in my own name and that of Colonel Montrose!" the officer declared, shaking Fritz's hand. "Finding a trace of that brave girl was my mission. The disappearance of the *Dorcas* devastated the Colonel. Despite three years of silence, he never lost hope. Weeks ago, I met three survivors who claimed their boat was the only one to reach land safely. From them, I learned the details, applied for permission to cruise these waters, and hoped to find the rest of the crew. My efforts have been rewarded beyond expectation."

Fritz accepted the praise modestly. Soon, the captain sent a boat for the rest of our party. Mother, Jenny, and the three boys boarded the brig, greeted with warmth and honor by the officers.

Over lunch, the captain mentioned an invalid passenger, Mr. Wolston, and his family, who had sailed with him from Sydney. The sea voyage had failed to improve Mr. Wolston's health, and the recent storm had worsened his condition. He was eager to rest on land.

Curious to meet them, we visited their camp later that afternoon. Pitched under shady trees, Mr. Wolston and his family were delighted to see us. Their conversation was so engaging that evening found us still on the shore. Too tired to return to Rockburg, the captain kindly offered tents for those unable to sleep on the yacht. The boys spent the night on land.

That night, I held a serious discussion with my wife. Should we truly wish to return to Europe? Was it wise to leave simply because an opportunity arose? Neither of us voiced our true feelings initially, fearing disagreement. Gradually, it became clear that neither harbored a strong desire to leave. Our shared dream was to make "New Switzerland" our permanent home.

"My dear wife," I said, "you desire nothing more than to spend your remaining days here, provided I and at least two of my sons choose to stay."

She nodded. She would willingly let the others return to Europe, asking only that they establish a prosperous colony and ensure the island retained the name of our homeland, welcoming future settlers from both England and Switzerland.

I approved wholeheartedly. Next came the difficult question: which sons would stay, and which would go? We left it to them to decide.

The following morning, Captain Littlestone planned to bring his ship to Safety Bay so we could show him our settlement. We invited Mr. Wolston and his family to Rockburg, hoping the change of scenery would aid his recovery.

As the visitors approached, rounding the rocky cape, their amazement grew. Before them lay the magnificent domain of Rockburg. Suddenly, eleven guns boomed from the battery on Shark Island, saluting the Royal Standard fluttering proudly in the morning breeze.

Surprise and delight illuminated every face. Poor Mr. Wolston's spirits visibly revived at the sight of such peace and happiness. He was carried ashore with extreme care and settled comfortably in my room, with a camp-bed arranged for his wife nearby.
