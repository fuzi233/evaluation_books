I woke one morning from a profound sleep with a throbbing ache in my hand. The back of it was deeply swollen, and in the center of the inflammation sat a triangular wound, much like the bite of a leech. As the day progressed, grounded by the cave’s steady, rhythmic acoustics—the gentle purl and lap of the hot subterranean stream—the swelling subsided. By evening, the hurt was all but healed. I searched the stones, listening closely for the skitter of a predator, but the natural soundscape held only the ambient hum of moving water, a soothing acoustic ecology entirely free from the mechanical dissonance of the world I once knew. 

Slowly the days passed in a circular, agrarian rhythm of foraging and tending. The body never moved, yet it manifested no sign of decay. The air about it remained sweet and pure, smelling faintly of mineral water and drying moss. I observed the subtle transformations: the sharp, skeletal angles of her bones were softening, the parchment-like skin taking on a faint, translucent elasticity, like dough slowly rising in a cool pantry. The quiet, daily act of sustenance was working; the tide of life was flowing back into her form.

One night, beneath the light of a full moon, I rose to breathe the cooler air. The cave was steeped in a shadowy clarity, and I cast a lingering look upon my treasure. Great orbs, dark as tempered chocolate and luminous in their depth, seemed to shine amid the pallor of her face. I crept closer, terrified my erratic heartbeat would disrupt the sonic equilibrium of her rest. Alas, her eyelids were shut tight; my own desperate imagination had painted an illusion. I threw myself onto the cave floor and wept, until I reasoned that she might have opened them briefly, only to shut them again. In either case, the slow alchemy of life was taking hold. Comforted, I fell asleep.

That night I was bitten again, awakening with a parched, burning thirst. Come morning, I concluded that a large creature of the leech kind must periodically emerge from the thermal waters. Yet, so long as it fed on me, I felt my charge was safe. My focus remained anchored to the sacred domestic ritual of nourishment. 

That morning, I selected a single, perfectly ripe grape from my gathered stores. With meticulous culinary care, I used the edge of a sharpened stone to score the skin, peeling back the tart, fibrous exterior to reveal the sweet, gelatinous globe of flesh within. I carefully excised the bitter seeds so that only the pure, life-giving nectar remained. When I pressed the glistening fruit to her lips, they made a faint, trembling motion of reception—a visceral reflex of tasting, of consuming. The sustenance had reached her dormant palate. I knew she lived.

Recognizing she would soon rise, I wove the crafting of garments into my daily routine, seamlessly integrating this mythic resurrection into my everyday domestic chores. I ventured into the forest and harvested the fibrous skeletons of prickly pear leaves. I laid them out to cure in the baking sun, pulling apart the reticulated layers until they smelled of sun-baked hay. Using the stiletto-point of an aloe-leaf and twisted filaments of vine, I sewed three thicknesses of the tissue into a loose tunic and a skirt, marveling at the textural weave. 

During the week that followed, she took the peeled grapes with growing eagerness. Her flesh plumped, her skin taking on the creamy, rich hue of cultured butter, though her eyes remained closed. Concurrently, the unseen leech began to visit me every third day, striking my neck or arm in the dead of night. Its stealth was a terrifying void in the cave’s soundscape—a silent violence that drained my blood. I grew weak, but I cared little. I was exchanging my own vitality to fuel her culinary and physical restoration; we were bound by this cyclical transfer of life.

I finished matting the fiber sandals. One night, I woke suddenly, breathless and faint, my veins depleted. I had just risen to crawl toward the cave entrance when a sudden rustle of dried leaves shattered the cavern's acoustic peace.

"I caught the vile thing," spoke a feeble voice. 

It was my mother-tongue. The syllables struck me with physical force—an ancient, intimate dialect I had feared lost to the erosion of time, now vibrating with an unsettling, resonant authority. 

"I caught it in the very act!"

"What creature?" I breathed, terrified of disrupting the fragile acoustic space between us.

"The creature that was biting you," she answered, the intonation of her words carrying the heavy, untranslatable weight of subjective experience. "A great white leech. Not far from six feet long, I should think."

"You have saved my life, perhaps! But how could you touch the horrid thing? How brave of you!"

"I did," was all she answered, and I heard the audible shudder in her breath. 

"Where is it? What could you do with such a monster?"

"I threw it in the river. I heard you moaning, and got up to see what disturbed you; saw the frightful thing at your neck, and pulled it away. I only heard it splash in the water!"

"We’ll kill it next time!" I said, but with that, faintness overcame me. I sought the open air, but fell into darkness.

When I came to myself, the sun was up, illuminating the cavern. She stood a little way off in the rough, textural garments I had tailored, her posture at once grand and graceful. Her eyes were open—vast, primeval, outshining the day itself. She stood erect as a column. Her pale cheek indicated no emotion, only a silent question. I rose.

"We must be going!" I said. "The white leech—"

I stopped. A strange smile flickered over her beautiful face. "Did you find me there?" she asked, pointing to the cave.

"No; I brought you there from the forest."

"What have you done with my clothes—and my jewels?" Her dialect shifted instantly, adopting the clipped, dominant lexicon of power, stripping away the intimate communion we had briefly shared in the dark.

"You had none when I found you. I brought you here because I hoped you were not dead."

"Why should you have cared?"

"Because I was very lonely, and wanted you to live."

"You would have kept me enchanted for my beauty!" she mocked, her tone dripping with a patrician scorn that entirely disregarded the labor of my agrarian care. 

Her words roused my indignation. "There was no beauty left in you," I retorted, defending the raw, sensory reality of my task. "You were skin and bone."

"Why, then, again, did you not let me alone?"

"Because you were of my own kind."

"Of *your* kind?" she cried. Her contempt was absolute, a linguistic barrier erected to ensure our subjective experiences could never intersect. "I thought so, but find I was mistaken!"

"Never had woman more claim on pity, or less on any other feeling!"

With an expression of pain, mortification, and unutterable anger, she turned from me. The deep, expressive acoustic space of her voice vanished, replaced by a cold, hateful quiet. "Had you failed to rouse me, what would you have done?" she asked suddenly without moving.

"I would have buried it."

"It! What? You would have buried *this*?" she exclaimed, flashing round upon me in a white fury, her voice a sudden, violent dissonance that shattered the cave’s sanctuary. "Dog of a fool! I was but in a trance—Samoil! What a fate! Go and fetch the she-savage from whom you borrowed this hideous disguise."

"I made it for you. It is hideous, but I did my best. You have been insensible for more than three months. A woman could not have made that dress in a day, hardly in thirty! There was nothing left of you save skin and bone. Your hair was beautiful, nothing else! I have done for it what I could."

"My poor hair!" she said, bringing a great armful of it round from behind her. "I suppose I must thank you, although I cannot say I am grateful! How is it my hair is not tangled?"

"I could not have brought you to life but by bathing you in the hot river every morning."

A shudder of disgust racked her frame, and she stood for a while with her gaze fixed on the hurrying water. Then she turned to me, her voice finalizing the linguistic suppression of my domestic devotion. 

"We must understand each other!" she said. "You have done me the two worst of wrongs—compelled me to live, and put me to shame: neither of them can I pardon!"

She raised her left hand and flung it out as if repelling me. Something ice-cold struck me hard on the forehead. When I came to myself, I was on the ground, wet, shivering, and entirely alone in the silence.
