To reconstruct the architecture of my own suspension, one must first acknowledge the absolute paralysis of my faculties during the initial shock. My consciousness was severed; the bridge between stimulus and response had been burned away. Consequently, I possess no memory of the precise moment Betteredge joined me on the shore, nor of the actions that followed. It is solely on his testimony that I recall laughing—a grotesque, involuntary sound—at his inquiry regarding my state, handing him the stained nightgown with a detached command to decipher the riddle inscribed upon it.

Between the beach and the fir plantation, where my senses slowly returned to the realm of the tangible, lies a void of total amnesia. I cannot recall a single syllable exchanged between us amidst the crashing surf. The first coherent image of my recovery emerges as we walked back toward the house, Betteredge offering a grim reassurance: that we could both endure the ordeal, provided we fortified ourselves with a measure of grog.

The scene transitions to the sanctuary of Betteredge's small sitting-room, a space defined by its cool shadows and profound quiet. Here, my resolve to avoid Rachel's domain evaporates, replaced by a grateful acceptance of refuge. I consume the grog—a liquid luxury unknown to me at that hour, mixed with ice-cold well water. While such a mixture would typically induce stupor, in my current state of fractured nerves, it acts as a stimulant, sharpening rather than dulling my awareness. I begin, as Betteredge prophesied, to "face it"; and he, in turn, adopts the same posture of confrontation.

The portrait I now present of myself may seem, to the discerning eye, a study in absurdity. Placed in a scenario devoid of parallel in history, what is the primary recourse of a rational mind? Does one retreat into isolation? Does one subject oneself to a rigorous autopsy of the impossible facts confronting me? Or does one hasten to London to summon the highest authorities? None of these occurred. Instead, I accepted the shelter of a home I had sworn never to debase myself by entering again. I sat, consuming spirits and water in the company of an aging servant, at ten o'clock in the morning. Is this the conduct of a man in my horror-stricken position? I can only plead that the visage of old Betteredge served as an anchor against the tides of madness, and that his grog sustained me when nothing else could have held me upright against the complete prostration of body and soul. I offer this defense, and I extend admiration for the unyielding dignity and logical consistency displayed by every individual who reads these lines, regardless of their station, in every crisis from cradle to grave.

"Now, Mr. Franklin, there is one certainty, at any rate," Betteredge declared, casting the nightgown onto the table between us as if it were a living, breathing entity. "_He_ is a liar, to start with."

Such a comforting reduction of the problem did not align with the turmoil in my mind.

"I am as innocent of any knowledge concerning the theft of the Diamond as you are," I replied. "Yet here stands the witness against me! The paint on the nightgown, and the name stitched into it—are these not irrefutable facts?"

Betteredge lifted my glass, pressing it insistently into my palm.

"Facts?" he echoed, his tone shifting to a conspiratorial whisper. "Consume another drop, Mr. Franklin, and you shall overcome the delusion of objective reality! Foul play, sir!" he continued, leaning closer. "That is the key to the riddle. Foul play exists somewhere—and you and I must unearth it. Was there nothing else in the tin case when you reached inside?"

The question triggered a sudden recollection. I withdrew the letter from my pocket and unfolded it. It was a document of considerable length, penned in cramped, hurried script. My eyes scanned frantically for the signature at the conclusion. *Rosanna Spearman.*

As the name registered, a bolt of illumination struck my mind, followed immediately by a rising tide of suspicion.

"Stop!" I cried out. "Rosanna Spearman emerged from a reformatory? Rosanna Spearman was once a thief?"

"There is no denying that, Mr. Franklin. So what of it now, pray tell?"

"So what of it now? How do we know she did not steal the Diamond after all? How do we know she did not deliberately smear my nightgown with the paint?"

Betteredge laid a restraining hand upon my arm, halting my tirade before I could escalate further.

"You shall be cleared of this charge, Mr. Franklin, beyond the shadow of a doubt. Yet, I trust you will not be cleared in _that_ specific manner. Examine the letter, sir. Out of respect for the girl's memory, examine what she writes."

I perceived the intensity in his voice, interpreting it as a stern, albeit friendly, admonition. "You shall form your own judgment on her correspondence," I stated firmly. "I will read it aloud."

I began, and the following lines resonated through the silent room:

"Sir—I have something to confess to you. A declaration that brings immense suffering may sometimes be distilled into very few words. This confession requires but three words. I love you."

The letter slipped from my grasp. I stared at Betteredge, bewildered. "By Heaven," I stammered, "what does this signify?"

He recoiled slightly, shrinking from the weight of the answer.

"You and Limping Lucy were alone together this morning, sir," he ventured cautiously. "Did she utter a single word regarding Rosanna Spearman?"

"She never even mentioned the name Rosanna Spearman."

"Return to the letter, Mr. Franklin. I implore you, I lack the heart to distress you further after the trials you have endured. Let her speak for herself, sir. And resume your grog. For your own sake, resume your grog."

I picked up the paper again and continued reading.

"It would be a profound disgrace for me to disclose this if I were a living woman when you read it. I shall be dead and gone, sir, by the time you discover my letter. It is this impending death that emboldens me. Not even my grave will remain to testify to my existence. I may own the truth—with the quicksand waiting to swallow me the moment the ink dries.

"Furthermore, you will find my nightgown concealed in my hiding-place, bearing the smear of paint upon it. You will wonder how it came to be hidden by me, and why I remained silent throughout my lifetime. I have but one explanation. I committed these bizarre acts because I loved you.

"I shall spare you the details of my life prior to your arrival at my lady's house. Lady Verinder rescued me from a reformatory. I entered the reformatory fleeing from prison. I was imprisoned because I was a thief. I became a thief because my mother descended into the streets when I was merely a child. My mother turned to the streets because the gentleman who claimed to be my father abandoned her. There is no necessity to recount such a commonplace tragedy at length; it is chronicled frequently enough in the newspapers.

"Lady Verinder was exceedingly kind to me, and Mr. Betteredge was likewise kind. Those two, along with the matron at the reformatory, constitute the only virtuous souls I have encountered in my entire existence. I might have progressed in my station—not happily, perhaps—but I might have advanced, had you not arrived to visit. I do not blame _you_, sir. It is my fault—entirely my fault.

"Do you recall the morning you emerged from the sandhills, searching for Mr. Betteredge? You appeared like a prince in a fairy tale. You resembled a lover in a dream. You were the most adorable human creation I had ever beheld. A sensation akin to the blissful life I had never known surged within me the instant my eyes met yours. Do not laugh at this sentiment if you can restrain yourself. Oh, if only I could convey the gravity of this feeling to _you!_

"I returned to the house and inscribed your names in my work-box, drawing a true lovers' knot beneath them. Then, some demon—or perhaps a benevolent angel—whispered, 'Go and look in the glass.' The mirror revealed truths best left unsaid. I was too foolish to heed the warning. I grew increasingly infatuated with you, treating you as if we belonged to the same social stratum, as if you were gazing upon the most beautiful creature your eyes had ever rested upon. I endeavored—Oh, dear, how I struggled—to draw your attention to me. Had you known the agony of my nights, crying in misery and humiliation at your perpetual indifference, you might have pitied me, granting me a fleeting glance to sustain me.

"It would have been a cruel glance, perhaps, had you understood my hatred for Miss Rachel. I believe I discovered your affection for her before you realized it yourself. She used to gift you roses to adorn your buttonhole. Ah, Mr. Franklin, you wore _my_ roses far more often than either you or she imagined! My sole solace at that time was placing my rose secretly into your glass of water in lieu of hers, then discarding her offering.

"If she had truly possessed the beauty you attributed to her, I might have endured it better. Nay, I suspect I would have grown even more spiteful toward her.
