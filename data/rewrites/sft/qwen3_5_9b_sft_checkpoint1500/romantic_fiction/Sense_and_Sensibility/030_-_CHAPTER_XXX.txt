As soon as Mrs. Jennings returned, she bypassed the customary knock and swept into the room, her face etched with genuine alarm. "How do you do, my dear?" she cooed to Marianne, who averted her gaze, offering no reply. "And how is she, Miss Dashwood? Poor creature, she looks dreadfully ill. It's no wonder. Ay, 'tis plain enough. He is wedded very shortly—a scoundrel! I have no patience for him. Mrs. Taylor confided this to me an hour past, sworn to it by a close confidante of Miss Grey herself, or I'd never have swallowed it. I nearly fainted upon hearing it. 'Well,' said I, 'if this be true, he has abused a young lady of my acquaintance abominably, and I pray with all my soul that his wife may torment his heart until it breaks.' And so I shall always say, my dear, trust me. I cannot abide men behaving thus; if I cross paths with him again, I'll give him a beating he hasn't known for years. Yet, there is solace, my dear Miss Marianne: he is not the only eligible young man alive, and with your lovely visage, you shall never lack suitors. Alas, poor soul! I'll trouble her no further; she needs to weep it out and be done with it. Fortunately, the Parrys and Sandersons arrive tonight, which ought to cheer her up."

Stepping out on tiptoes, she moved as if fearing her young friend's grief might swell under the weight of noise.

To her sister's astonishment, Marianne resolved to dine with them. Elinor gently dissuaded her, but Marianne insisted, "No, I shall descend; I can endure it, and the commotion will distract me." Elinor, delighted to see her sister yield to practical reasoning for once, though doubting Marianne's ability to sustain the meal, offered no further protest. She adjusted Marianne's gown as best she could while her sister lay recumbent, preparing to escort her to the dining hall at the summons.

Seated there, though appearing wretched, Marianne ate more than anticipated and displayed a composure that surprised Elinor. Had she attempted speech or been conscious of Mrs. Jennings' well-intentioned yet clumsy attentions, this tranquility would have vanished. Instead, not a syllable escaped her lips; the deep abstraction of her mind kept her ignorant of everything surrounding her.

Elinor, appreciative of Mrs. Jennings' kindness despite its often distressing and occasionally absurd nature, returned her civilities, acting as a shield for her sister who could offer none. Her friend observed Marianne's unhappiness and sought to alleviate it through indulgence: seating her by the warmest fire, tempting her with every delicacy available, and regaling her with the latest gossip. Had Elinor not witnessed the shadow on her sister's countenance, she might have found amusement in Mrs. Jennings' efforts to cure a broken heart with sweets, olives, and tales. However, when the relentless repetition of these comforts became unbearable for Marianne, she could stay no longer. With a sharp cry of "Misery!" and a gesture bidding Elinor not follow, she rose hastily and fled the room.

"Poor soul!" cried Mrs. Jennings, following her exit. "It grieves me to see her! And I declare she departed without finishing her wine! Nor the dried cherries! Lord! nothing seems to benefit her. If I knew what she craved, I'd scour the town for it. Well, 'twas odd to me that a man could treat such a pretty girl so poorly. But when there's ample fortune on one side and scarcely a penny on the other, Lord bless you! they care naught for sentiment!"

"The lady, then—Miss Grey, I believe you termed her—is she wealthy?"

"Fifty thousand pounds, my dear. Have you seen her? They say she's smart and stylish, though not particularly handsome. I recall her aunt, Biddy Henshawe, who wed a very wealthy man; the entire family is affluent. Fifty thousand pounds! And by all accounts, it won't materialize until it's needed, for they say he's utterly ruined. No wonder! Dashing about in his curricle with his hunters! Well, 'tis useless discussing it, but when a young man, whoever he be, courts a pretty girl and promises marriage, he hath no business to flee his vow merely because poverty strikes and a richer maiden offers herself. Why doesn't he, in such a plight, sell his horses, lease his estate, dismiss his staff, and reform thoroughly? I warrant you, Miss Marianne would have waited for matters to mend. But 'tis not so nowadays; nothing regarding pleasure shall be relinquished by the lads of this age."

"Do you know the sort of girl Miss Grey is? Is she reputed amiable?"

"I've heard no ill of her; indeed, I scarce hear her mentioned save that Mrs. Taylor remarked this morning Miss Walker hinted to her she believed Mr. and Mrs. Ellison would welcome Miss Grey's marriage, as she and Mrs. Ellison could never agree."

"And who are the Ellisons?"

"Her guardians, my dear. But now she is of age and may choose her own path; and a dreadful choice she has made! Pray," pausing briefly, "does your poor sister intend to retreat to her chamber to mourn in solitude? Is there no balm for her? 'Tis cruel to let her linger alone. Well, ere long we shall gather friends to amuse her. What shall we play? She detests whist, I know; but does she favor any other game?"

"My dear madam, this kindness is superfluous. Marianne, I suspect, will not leave her room again this eve. I shall endeavor to persuade her to retire early, for she plainly requires repose."

"Aye, that suits her best. Let her dictate her supper and retire. Lord! No wonder she has looked so pale and desolate for the last fortnight; this matter has hung over her head all this time. And so the letter arrived today to seal her fate! Poor soul! I'm certain if I'd suspected it, I'd not have jesting about it for all my wealth. But then, how could I guess? I took it for a common love letter, and you know young folk like to laugh at such trifles. Lord! How concerned Sir John and my daughters will be when they learn the truth! If I had my wits about me, I might have visited Conduit Street on my way home to inform them. But I shall see them tomorrow."

"It would be needless for you to warn Mrs. Palmer and Sir John against mentioning Mr. Willoughby or alluding to our past to my sister. Their innate goodness will surely recognize the cruelty of feigning knowledge when she is present. The fewer words spoken to me concerning this subject, the more my feelings shall be spared, as you, my dear madam, readily understand."

"Oh! Lord! yes, truly. 'Twould be terrible for you to hear it discussed; and as for your sister, I assure you, I would not utter a word about it to her for the world. You noted I held my tongue all dinner. Neither will Sir John, nor my daughters, for they are all thoughtful and considerate, especially if I give them a hint, which I certainly shall. For my part, I deem the less said of such matters, the better, the swifter it blows over and fades. And what good does chatter accomplish, you know?"

"In this affair, it serves only harm; perhaps more than in many similar cases, for it has been accompanied by circumstances rendering it unfit for public discourse, for the sake of every person involved. I must grant this justice to Mr. Willoughby—he has broken no formal engagement with my sister."

"Law, my dear! Cease defending him. No formal engagement indeed! After touring Allenham House with her and selecting the specific chambers wherein they were to dwell henceforth!"

For Marianne's sake, Elinor could not press the topic further, hoping it was not required for Willoughby's defense either. Though Marianne stood to lose greatly, he could gain little by enforcing the grim reality. After a brief silence, Mrs. Jennings, buoyed by her natural vivacity, broke forth again.

"Well, my dear, 'tis a true adage: an ill wind blows no good. It will prove beneficial for Colonel Brandon. He shall have her at last; ay, verily. Mark me, if they're not married by Midsummer. Lord! How he'll chuckle over this tidings! I hope he arrives tonight. 'Twill be far superior for your sister. Two thousand a year, debt-free, barring the little love-child, indeed; ay, I had forgotten her, but she may be apprenticed out cheap, and then what matters? Delaford is a charming spot, I can attest; precisely the sort of old-fashioned haven filled with comforts and conveniences. Enclosed by high gardens brimming with the finest fruit trees in the realm, and a magnificent mulberry tree in one corner! Lord! How Charlotte and I stuffed ourselves during our sole visit! Furthermore, there is a dove-cote, delightful stew ponds, and a lovely canal; indeed, everything one could desire. Moreover, it lies near the church, but a quarter-mile from the turnpike, ensuring it's never dull. Sit in the ancient yew arbor behind the house, and you'll behold every carriage passing. Oh! 'tis a delightful place!
