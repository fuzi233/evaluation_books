Elinor watched the dulness that had settled on her friend’s spirits with an uneasiness she could not season away. His coming ought to have been like the first savour of broth on a winter morning—comforting, restoring; yet the warmth of it reached her only in patches, as if some cold draught kept slipping in through a crack she could not find. He was attentive, he was present, and still he seemed not wholly there. One moment, a look—quick, bright, almost tender—promised the old distinction; the next, his manner drew back, reserved and careful, contradicting what his eyes had nearly confessed. If affection remained, it moved in him like a note half-muted beneath too much noise.

The next morning he came down early and joined them in the breakfast-room before the rest of the household had stirred. The cottage was quiet in the way of a place that still remembered fields: the softened creak of a stair, the faint tick of the clock, and beyond the window the hedgerows holding the wind as a sieve holds flour. Mrs. Dashwood’s breakfast things were in readiness—tea steaming in the pot, bread cut and set out, butter pale as primroses—so homely and exact that even Edward’s troubled air seemed out of place among such honest provisions.

Marianne, alive to every opportunity of advancing other people’s happiness, was scarcely seated before she contrived to leave them to themselves. She rose with an alacrity that had once been merely her nature and now, perhaps, her effort, and began to go upstairs; but before she was half-way, the parlour door opened behind her. She turned, expecting Elinor, and saw instead Edward come out with a hurried step.

“I am going into the village to see my horses,” said he. “As you are not yet ready for breakfast, I shall be back again presently.”

He was gone before Marianne could make sense of it; and Elinor, though she said nothing, felt the small wound of it as distinctly as if a pin had pricked her finger while slicing bread—nothing fatal, nothing dramatic, yet sharp enough to make one careful afterwards.

When he returned, his countenance had altered—not into ease, but into that forced brightness by which people try to persuade themselves. He spoke with fresh admiration of the country. In his walk, he had found vantage points in the valley; and the village, lying higher than the cottage, had given him a broad view of the whole which had exceedingly pleased him. It was a subject sure to catch Marianne, who heard landscape the way others hear music: instantly, thoroughly, with her whole being.

She began to answer him in kind, describing her own delight in the scenes around them, questioning him with eager minuteness about every particular that had struck him; but Edward, with a sudden laugh that sounded like a spoon against china—too light for the weight of the room—interrupted her.

“You must not enquire too far, Marianne—remember I have no knowledge in the picturesque, and I shall offend you by my ignorance and want of taste if we come to particulars. I shall call hills steep, which ought to be bold; surfaces strange and uncouth, which ought to be irregular and rugged; and distant objects out of sight, which ought only to be indistinct through the soft medium of a hazy atmosphere. You must be satisfied with such admiration as I can honestly give. I call it a very fine country—the hills are steep, the woods seem full of fine timber, and the valley looks comfortable and snug—with rich meadows and several neat farm-houses scattered here and there. It exactly answers my idea of a fine country, because it unites beauty with utility—and I dare say it is a picturesque one too, because you admire it; I can easily believe it to be full of rocks and promontories, grey moss and brushwood, but these are all lost on me. I know nothing of the picturesque.”

“I am afraid it is but too true,” said Marianne; “but why should you boast of it?”

Elinor, pouring the tea with the steadiness of one used to keeping things from boiling over, looked up at him. There was something in the very way he refused the language of admiration—too deliberate, too pointed—that suggested not simplicity but defence, as if he feared the commerce of words might commit him.

“I suspect,” said she, “that to avoid one kind of affectation, Edward here falls into another. Because he believes many people pretend to more admiration of the beauties of nature than they really feel, and is disgusted with such pretensions, he affects greater indifference and less discrimination in viewing them himself than he possesses. He is fastidious and will have an affectation of his own.”

“It is very true,” Marianne replied, warming, “that admiration of landscape scenery is become a mere jargon. Every body pretends to feel and tries to describe with the taste and elegance of him who first defined what picturesque beauty was. I detest jargon of every kind, and sometimes I have kept my feelings to myself, because I could find no language to describe them in but what was worn and hackneyed out of all sense and meaning.”

There was something in that—this fear of borrowed phrases, this hunger for a tongue that could still mean what it said—that made Elinor’s heart tighten. Words were power; words were also traps. To name a feeling in another person’s coin was to let them set the value.

“I am convinced,” said Edward, “that you really feel all the delight in a fine prospect which you profess to feel. But, in return, your sister must allow me to feel no more than I profess. I like a fine prospect, but not on picturesque principles. I do not like crooked, twisted, blasted trees. I admire them much more if they are tall, straight, and flourishing. I do not like ruined, tattered cottages. I am not fond of nettles or thistles, or heath blossoms. I have more pleasure in a snug farm-house than a watch-tower—and a troop of tidy, happy villagers please me better than the finest banditti in the world.”

Marianne stared at him, astonishment plain in her face; then her eyes shifted to Elinor with a sort of compassionate alarm, as if she had witnessed a beloved dish spoiled by an unexpected ingredient. Elinor, who could bear much but could not help her own perception of humour, laughed outright. Edward’s seriousness, so grave in everything that touched the heart, could be almost arch when he chose; and it was as if he had taken a knife to the inflated language of taste and pared it down to something plain and serviceable.

The subject went no farther. Marianne fell into a thoughtful silence, and the morning would have continued so, softened only by the domestic sounds—the cup set carefully on a saucer, the faint stirring of the fire—had not a new object suddenly caught her eye.

She sat beside Edward; and as Mrs. Dashwood passed him his tea, his hand moved so directly before Marianne that a ring, with a plait of hair in the centre, showed conspicuously on one of his fingers. In a moment, and with her usual ungoverned sincerity, she cried out—

“I never saw you wear a ring before, Edward. Is that Fanny’s hair? I remember her promising to give you some. But I should have thought her hair had been darker.”

The words, once spoken, could not be taken back. Marianne had uttered what she truly felt, careless of consequence; and the consequence came at once. Edward coloured deeply—so suddenly that Elinor saw the blood rise, as when heat catches on a pan too quickly. He gave a momentary glance at Elinor—an appeal, an apology, a fear—then answered, with an effort at steadiness:

“Yes; it is my sister’s hair. The setting always casts a different shade on it, you know.”

Elinor met his eye and could not pretend she had not understood. In that instant she was as satisfied as Marianne, and more wounded. The hair was her own. Marianne concluded, in her innocence, that it was a free gift from sister to brother; Elinor knew—by the plain fact that she had never given it—that it must have been procured by some theft or contrivance, done without her knowledge and therefore without her consent. The little plait, so domestic in its fashioning, so intimate in its nearness to his skin, became at once a token and an insult; not because Edward wore it, but because someone had taken from her what she had not chosen to surrender and turned it into an emblem.

Yet her temper was not in a humour to take offence as others might. She would not grant the triumph of her pain to anyone’s eyes. Affecting to notice nothing, she began instantly to talk of something else—of bread, of the weather, of the arrangement of the day—while inwardly she resolved to catch every opportunity of seeing that ring again, to satisfy herself beyond all doubt that the hair was exactly the shade of her own. It was not curiosity; it was the need to place certainty where words had been used to cloud meaning.

Edward’s embarrassment lasted some time and settled at last into an absence of mind still more fixed. He was particularly grave through the whole morning, as if every sound in the room—each murmur of teacup, each footstep—troubled him. Marianne, acute in feeling when she had once committed an error, severely censured herself for what she had said. Her remorse would have been shorter, perhaps, had she known how little offence her thoughtlessness had given Elinor compared with what Elinor had already been forced to swallow in silence.

Before midday was half spent, Sir John Middleton and Mrs. Jennings arrived, brisk and full, as people are who live amid plenty and think it a kindness to distribute bustle like extra servings. Hearing of a gentleman at the cottage, they came to survey the guest; and Sir John, aided by his mother-in-law, was not long in discovering that the name Ferrars began with an F. It promised them a mine of raillery against Elinor, which nothing but the newness of their acquaintance with Edward prevented from being sprung at once. Even so, Elinor learned, from certain significant looks—made more significant by Mrs. Jennings’s inability to keep anything silent—how far their penetration, founded on Margaret’s previous instructions, extended.

Sir John never entered the Dashwoods’ parlour without offering an invitation, as if hospitality were a dish that must be pressed upon everyone until accepted. On this occasion, eager to entertain their visitor and feeling himself bound to contribute to the amusement of the party, he insisted on engaging them for both tea and dinner.

“You must drink tea with us to-night,” said he, “for we shall be quite alone—and to-morrow you must absolutely dine with us, for we shall be a large party.”

Mrs. Jennings enforced the necessity with cheerful tyranny. “And who knows but you may raise a dance,” said she. “And that will tempt you, Miss Marianne.”

“A dance!” Marianne cried. “Impossible! Who is to dance?”

“Who! why yourselves, and the Careys, and Whitakers to be sure.—What! you thought nobody could dance because a certain person that shall be nameless is gone!”

“I wish with all my soul,” cried Sir John, “that Willoughby were among us again.”

The name, thrown into the room with such careless force, acted on Marianne like a sudden loud sound in a place that had been quiet. Colour rushed into her face. She tried to command herself, and could not.

This, and Marianne’s blush, gave new suspicions to Edward. His eyes, which had been heavy all morning, lifted quickly; he looked from the visitors to Marianne and then, leaning a little towards Elinor, asked in a low voice, by whom he was sitting, “And who is Willoughby?”

Elinor answered briefly, as briefly as truth and prudence would allow. Marianne’s countenance, however, told more than Elinor’s words. Edward saw enough, in the language she could not suppress, to comprehend not only the meaning of others but many of Marianne’s former expressions that had puzzled him. When their visitors at last left them, he went immediately round to Marianne and said, in a whisper, with a quiet, almost playful archness that yet carried the edge of knowledge—

“I have been guessing. Shall I tell you my guess?”

“What do you mean?” she said, startled.

“Shall I tell you?”

“Certainly.”

“Well then; I guess that Mr. Willoughby hunts.”

Marianne was surprised and confused; yet she could not help smiling at the manner in which he spoke—as if he had taken a great secret and reduced it to something ordinary, domestic, safe. After a moment’s silence, she said with warmth that was all her own, “Oh, Edward! How can you?—But the time will come I hope… I am sure you will like him.”

“I do not doubt it,” he replied, rather astonished at the earnestness of her assurance; for had he not imagined it to be a joke contrived for the benefit of general acquaintance—founded only on a something or a nothing between Mr. Willoughby and herself—he would not have ventured to mention it at all.
