In the architecture of the soul, there exists a higher justice, one that allows gratitude to eclipse resentment, a hallmark of regal natures endowed with a broader life and a deeper capacity for joy. These are not the minds that gleefully dissect virtue until it dissolves into pulp, deeming it unworthy of thanks; those are the devil's friars, sworn to poverty in love and trust, yet forced to beg from a world that hoards human property.

Deronda, as he parted from Gwendolen, carefully withheld the declaration, *"I shall not see you again for a long while; I am going away,"* fearing that Grandcourt might interpret this absence as significant to her. In truth, he departed under circumstances of monumental weight to himself. As he set out to keep his promise of visiting her, he carried within him a solemn emotion that resurrected the most profound experiences of his existence.

Sir Hugo had summoned him to his chambers with a terse note: *"Come immediately. Something has happened."* This summons brought a measure of relief when Deronda entered the baronet's study and was met not with the dread he anticipated, but with grave affection.

"It is nothing to grieve you, sir?" Deronda asked, his tone vibrating with a restored confidence rather than inquiry, as he grasped the hand extended to him.

There was an unusual intensity in Sir Hugo's gaze, a suppressed turmoil in his voice, as he replied, "No, Dan, no. Sit down. I have something to say."

Deronda complied, though a presentiment weighed upon him. It was exceedingly rare for Sir Hugo to display such profound seriousness. "Not to grieve me, my boy, no. At least, if there is nothing in it that will grieve you too much. But I hardly expected that this—just this—would ever occur. There have been reasons why I never prepared you for it. Reasons why I never spoke of your parentage. Yet, I strove in every way to ensure this did not become an injury to you."

Sir Hugo paused, but Deronda remained silent. He could not articulate, *"I have never felt it an injury."* Even if that sentiment were true, his throat tightened, rendering speech impossible. More hung in the balance than anyone but himself could comprehend as the veil of secrecy began to tear. Sir Hugo had never witnessed the grand face he cherished so dearly grow so pale, nor the lips pressed together with such agonizing restraint.

Continuing with an anxious tenderness, as if dreading a fresh wound, Sir Hugo confessed, "I acted in obedience to your mother's wishes. The secrecy was hers. But now she desires to remove it. She wishes to see you. I will place this letter in your hands; you may examine it later. It will simply state what she bids you do and where you may find her."

He handed over a letter penned on foreign stationery. Deronda shoved it into his breast pocket, grateful for the reprieve from immediate reading. The storm of emotion on Daniel's face had overtaken the baronet's composure, visibly shaking him. Finding it difficult to proceed further, Sir Hugo waited.

But Deronda's entire being was seized by a question, the most arduous in the world to voice. Yet, he could not endure the delay. This was a sacramental instant; if he allowed it to slip, he could never again access the spiritual gravity required to speak the words and receive the answer. For several moments, their gazes dropped, and it seemed as though unspoken thoughts drifted in the air between them. Finally, Deronda lifted his eyes to Sir Hugo, speaking with a tremulous reverence, terrified of inadvertently conveying the reproach that years of stifled affection had bred: "Is my father also living?"

The response came instantly, low and emphatic: "No."

In the turbulent mix of emotions that followed, it became impossible to disentangle joy from sorrow. A new illumination had fallen upon the past for Sir Hugo as well during this encounter. After a silence in which Deronda felt akin to someone whose faith has crumbled before they have fully embraced a new creed, the baronet spoke in a tone of confession, "Perhaps I was wrong, Dan, to undertake what I did. And perhaps I liked it a little too well—having you all to myself. But if you have suffered any pain which I might have alleviated, I ask you to forgive me."

"The forgiveness has long been there," Deronda replied. "The chief pain has always stemmed from someone else—whom I never knew—whom I am now to know. It has not prevented me from feeling an affection for you that constitutes a vast portion of every life I recall."

It appeared to be a single, shared impulse that drove the two men to clasp each other's hands for a fleeting moment.
