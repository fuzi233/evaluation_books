George Warrington had written in his letter—quoted earlier—that despite Lord Castlewood’s past dealings with Harry, the nobleman and George remained on cordial terms, bound by a kinship that transcended petty grievances. If George needed letters of introduction, access to court circles, or even a seat in the House of Lords to witness a debate, his cousin was always ready to assist. He was a man of charm and wit, generous in spirit when it came to advancing the interests of those close to him—provided such actions did not undermine his own.

Now, Lord Castlewood went so far as to promise George he would use his influence to secure him a position in government or society, an offer eagerly embraced by the Virginian. For George, England was no longer just a temporary refuge—it had become home. He found the life of London infinitely more stimulating than anything Virginia could offer. Though he might have accepted a planter’s existence if it meant true independence, in reality, he was merely the first among many subjects under his mother’s rule, and one who felt especially burdened by her expectations. The thought of returning with his young bride to a life of rigid routine, constant subordination, and inevitable disputes filled him with dread. Better, he believed, to remain in England—poor but free, surrounded by congenial company and the faint possibility of future distinction—than endure the suffocating monotony and social constraints of colonial life.

Thus, Lord Castlewood’s pledge carried great weight for George. True, the earl had done little for his own brother, and his own standing as a peer was far from enviable. But hope often distorts perception, and George chose to believe in his cousin’s sincerity. Unlike the Warringtons, who were reserved and distant, Lord Castlewood proved remarkably gracious upon learning of George’s engagement to Miss Lambert. He visited her parents, complimented both George and Theo, and behaved with such charm and warmth that the family, already inclined toward kindness, forgot the rumors that followed him like shadows. To them, he seemed transformed—witty, lively, almost innocent in their presence. As if shedding his worldliness with his coat, he entered their drawing-room a different man—one who left behind the storms of high society to rest in a haven of peace.

It was no pretense, either. In truth, Lord Castlewood may have been better while among these good people, at least for a time. And perhaps, in that moment, he was not a hypocrite at all.

His admiration for Miss Theo was open and unreserved, though modestly expressed.

“It doesn’t trouble my sleep,” he said, “nor will I break my heart if Miss Lambert chooses another. But I wish, madam, that when I was young I’d met someone as pure and kind as your daughter. She might have kept me out of much harm. Yet such girls never fell into my path—or if they had, they might have made me better than I am.”

“Surely, my lord,” Mrs. Lambert replied gently, “it is not too late?”

He started, startled by the suggestion.

“Not too late?” he echoed.

She blushed. “Not too late to win your daughter’s hand, my lord—but not too late to repent. Scripture says it is never too late to turn from sin. If others are welcomed at the eleventh hour, why should you despair?”

“I know my own heart better than you do,” he answered, voice tinged with melancholy. “I speak French and German fluently—because I learned them in childhood. A man who picks them up later can never truly master them. So it is with virtue: I cannot learn it now. I can only watch others practice it and admire them. When I stand on the other side of Lazarus’ gate,” he continued, “will Miss Theo give me a drop of water? Don’t frown—I know I shall be there. Some men are born doomed. I think some of our family are among them. Others waver, uncertain which way the scale will fall. But some are predestined angels—born to soar, naturally drawn to Heaven, no matter what they do.”

“Oh, my lord,” she said, “why should you not be one of the predestined? While there is a day, an hour—there is still hope!”

“I see what lies behind your words, dear lady,” he said softly. “I read your prayers in your eyes. But how can they help? You don’t know all. You don’t understand what our lives are—how early they begin, how selfish nature, necessity, and upbringing shape us. Fate holds the reins of our chariot; we cannot escape our destiny. I know this. I see goodness in others. I go my own way—but it is not mine. It is Fate’s. And yet, Fate has mercy: it allows us, from time to time, to meet souls like yours.” He took her hand, looked deeply into her eyes, and bowed with quiet dignity.

Every word was true. The greatest mistake is to assume that flawed men lack feeling or sensitivity. They feel deeply—but their emotions do not last. Sometimes, tears themselves become a form of indulgence, much as old libertines once found sorrow in their victims’ grief to heighten their pleasure. But Mrs. Lambert knew nothing of these inner struggles. To her, he was a sinner in need of grace—and so she prayed for him with tender persistence. He was, she believed, much better than he appeared. He was interesting, compelling. There was still hope—for him, for all.

Which of them judged him more rightly? Time would tell. But if the gentleman spoke the truth, then the lady’s prayers, though powerless to change him, could do no harm. Kind-hearted women—like doctors tending a dying patient—care for the reprobate not because they expect recovery, but because compassion compels them. Once healed, the patient is forgotten; the physician moves on.

Yet while the illness endured, Mrs. Lambert could not resist seeing more of her sick man. A genuine intimacy grew between Lord Castlewood and the Lamberts. One might suspect worldly motives mingled with spiritual ones—even in such a devout woman. After all, who can say where worldliness won’t slip into Eden, even if guarded by flaming swords?

Her son was preparing for ordination. Lord Castlewood feared his current chaplain, Mr. Sampson, whose careless habits and unorthodox views might lead him to resign. Should that happen, the small parish would become vacant—and the earl hinted it might be offered to a young clergyman of sound principles and gentle manners, content with modest income and a small but faithful congregation.

Thus began a connection between the two families. The ladies of Castlewood, ever mindful of propriety, paid visits to Mrs. Lambert’s drawing-room. They were polite, courteous, respectful. My Lady Castlewood invited the Lamberts to her card parties. There was gambling, certainly—everyone gambled, from the king to bishops, peers to peeresses. But no one was forced to play. And surely, no one should object when such lofty figures indulged freely.

Mrs. Lambert herself attended several of these gatherings, grateful for the welcome and pleased by the attention her daughters received.

Earlier, mention had been made of an American family of Dutch descent, brought to England by Madam Esmond’s recommendation to her sons abroad. Her letter had been clear enough for Mrs. Lambert to grasp its intent. George, however, was already engaged. And poor Hetty’s suitor, Harry, was away on military duty—a circumstance for which Hetty’s mother may not have regretted. With a laugh, she told George he ought to heed his mother’s advice: break off with Theo and pursue Miss Lydia, who was ten times—no, a hundred times—richer than her daughter, and undoubtedly more beautiful.

“Yes, indeed,” George replied, “she is handsomer, richer, perhaps even cleverer.”

(These praises, Mrs. Lambert half resented.)

“But suppose she is all these things,” he continued, “so is Mr. Johnson cleverer than I. So is Mr. Hagan the actor taller and finer-looking. So is Sir James Lowther wealthier. Yet, pray, ma'am, do you think I’d be jealous of any of them—or believe Theo would leave me for their sake? Why should I deny that Miss Lydia is lovely, rich, clever, vivacious, well-bred—if you insist—and even angelic, if you wish? Theo is not afraid. Are you, child?”

“No, George,” Theo replied, her gaze honest and steady—so sincere it could shame suspicion itself.

And if, after such words, Mrs. Lambert leaves the room—on some errand, real or imagined—to fetch scissors, thimbles, slippers, or even the cross atop St. Paul’s—then returns minutes later to find George near Theo, her face flushed, his hand just released from hers—would she really know what passed between them?

Would you, madam? Do you remember what happened when Mr. Grundy came courting? And you, who weren’t present, dare cry *fie* and *for shame*? Then fie and for shame upon you, Mrs. Grundy!

With Harry gone and Theo and George irrevocably betrothed, Madam Esmond’s matchmaking plans were thwarted. But why should Mrs. Lambert not have her own? If a rich, handsome, beautiful girl crossed Jack Lambert’s path, why shouldn’t he claim her? So thought the ever-marriage-minded mother—though General Lambert, as usual, called her a goose.

At any rate, beauty and wealth were no objection. Madam Esmond had urged hospitality to this family. It wasn’t her fault Harry had departed for Canada. Would the General want him to return, abandon his career, come to England, marry the American, and break Hetty’s heart? Let us spare further debate—and refrain from suggesting Mr. Lambert was right to call his wife such a foolish bird.

In those days, distinctions between court and city were vast. Mr. Van den Bosch, upon arriving in London, associated only with city folk. He lived near his agent in the commercial district. When his daughter returned from school for holidays, they took walks to Islington or Highgate, or strolled through the Artillery Ground in Bunhill Fields. They attended Baptist services in Finsbury Fields, and once or twice, secretly, watched Mr. Garrick or the mischievous Mr. Foote at the Little Theatre. A Lord Mayor’s feast was a grand event for a man of his rank; dancing with a young mercer at Hampstead Assembly gave the daughter immense joy.

When George first visited his mother’s friends, he found Mr. Draper of the Temple, attentive and eager. The married lawyer warned George: “Look out—this young lady has a fortune.”

Mr. Drabshaw, a Quaker gentleman and nephew of Madam Esmond’s Bristol agent, also frequented the household, growing anxious when George arrived.

To honor his mother’s neighbors, George hosted a gathering at his own apartments. Naturally, his Soho friends joined them. Not one failed to praise Miss Lydia’s beauty. She had the foot of a fairy, the arms and neck of a huntress, flashing eyes that sparkled with life. She carried a soft, plaintive accent from America—one I, speaking now, have heard mocked a hundred times in Cockney imitation, yet which, when spoken by a charming woman, is enchanting. Who dares say otherwise? Miss Whittington, whose h’s are lost? Miss Nicol Jarvie, with her northern burr? Miss Molony, with her Dublin lilt? All accents are sweet when spoken by beauty. Who sets the standard—rose, thistle, shamrock, or star and stripe? Miss Lydia’s accent, I believe, was never offensive. Had it been, George, a man of taste, would have noticed—and her schoolmistress at Kensington would have failed her.

After six months at the Kensington Academy, Miss Lydia returned to her grandfather, eager to enter society. At first, her world was narrow—but she was determined to expand it. Wherever she led, her grandfather followed. He had been thwarted in youth; he’d punished his son too harshly, with unfortunate results. For Lyddy, he vowed, life would be as pleasant as possible.

“Do you think I’m wrong, Mr. George?” he asked.

There was talk in Virginia—though he didn’t know its source—that the Castlewood boys might have been happier if Madam Esmond had allowed them more freedom. George could not contradict that rumor, nor persuade the kindly old man to change his plans.

As for the Lamberts, how could they refuse such a generous, loving father, a friend of Madam Esmond?

When Lydia returned from school, her grandfather moved from Monument Yard to a fine house in Bloomsbury. Their city friends followed—merchants from Virginia Walk, respectable tradesmen with whom he dealt, and their families, all gracious toward Miss Lyddy.

But gradually, the old circle faded. No more outings to Belsize or Highgate, no Saturday trips to Deputy Higgs’ villa, no country dances at Mr. Lutestring’s in Hackney. Even Sunday worship changed—oh, the horror! Mr. Van den Bosch left Bethesda Chapel in Bunhill Row and actually took a pew in Queen Square Church!

Queen Square Church—and Mr. George Warrington lived nearby in Southampton Row! It was obvious whom Miss Lyddy favored.

Mr. Draper, once full of praise for the grandfather and granddaughter, now warned George with new caution. Mr. Van den Bosch, he claimed, was no noble Dutchman—he was from Albany, nobody’s son. He’d made his fortune through land speculation, privateering (nearly piracy), and the Guinea trade. His son had married an assigned servant—marriage being questionable—and was disinherited, falling into vice, dying quietly in his bed.

“Mr. Draper has told you bad things about me,” the old man said calmly to George. “We are all sinners. Some truths may be said of all, and many falsehoods. Did he tell you my son was unhappy with me? I told you that myself. Did he spread wicked tales about my family? He liked it so much he wanted to marry Lyddy to his brother. Bless her! I’ve had many offers. And you—you’re the man I would have chosen. I like you none the less for preferring someone else—though I must say, I fail to see what you find in your Miss compared to my Lyddy.”

“There is no accounting for tastes, sir,” George replied, with his most elegant air.

“No, sir,” the old man agreed. “It’s a wonder of nature, happening every day. When I ran my store in Albany, a gentleman of your class might have married my dear daughter—had she lived—along with a good fortune. But since my father and I quarreled, Lyddy would have been penniless. Instead of my beautiful Bella, he chose a plain creature, no prettier than your Miss, without a dollar. The fool of it, saving your presence, Mr. George.”

“Please don’t save my presence,” George laughed. “I suppose the gentleman had promised the other lady, seen her first—hence, indifferent to your charming daughter.”

“Ah, yes,” the old man mused. “When a young man gives his word to commit folly, he sticks to it. But Lord, Lord, what am I talking about? Twenty years ago! I was well-off then, but Heaven has blessed my store—now I’m three times richer. Ask my agents how much they’ll pay for Joseph Van den Bosch’s bill at six months on New York—or sight, perhaps, for forty thousand pounds. I warrant they’ll discount it.”

“Happy he who holds the bill, sir,” George said, bowing with amusement.

“Lord, Lord,” the elder exclaimed, “how mercenary you young men are! Always thinking of money! Happy he who has the girl, I should say—the money isn’t the point when it comes with such a lovely young thing as that—though I humbly say it, who shouldn’t, and who is her fond, silly old grandfather.”

“We were talking about you, Lyddy darling—come, give me a kiss, my blessing!” he said, embracing her. “We were discussing you and Mr. George said he wouldn’t take you with all the money your poor old grandfather can give you.”

“Nay, sir,” George protested.

“Well, you’re right to say nay,” the old man chuckled. “I didn’t mean all. When Heaven takes me to a better place, Lyddy will be a rich little Lyddy. But she doesn’t want me to go yet, does she?”

“Oh, you darling dear grandpapa!” she cried.

“This young gentleman won’t have you,” he declared. (Lyddy sent him a playful, arch “Thank you, sir” from her brown eyes.) “But at least he’s honest—more than we can say of some folks in this wicked London. Oh, Lord, how mercenary they are! Did you know, in Monument Yard, they all swarmed around my poor little Blessing for her money? Tom Lutestring, Mr. Draper—your precious lawyer—Mr. Tubbs of Bethesda Chapel—all buzzing like flies around honey.”

“To avoid the flies, of course!” Miss Lydia said, tossing her head proudly.

“Where my brother-tradesmen live,” the old man continued. “Else why should I consort with your grandees and fine folk? I don’t care for fashion, Mr. George; I don’t care for plays or poetry, begging your pardon. I’ve never been to a play in my life—except to please this little minx.”

“Oh, sir,” she exclaimed, “it was lovely! And I cried so, didn’t I, grandpapa?”

“At what, my dear?”

“At—Mr. Warrington’s play, grandpapa.”

“Did you, my dear? I dare say. It was mail day—my letters had come, my ship *Lovely Lyddy* had arrived in Falmouth, Captain Joyce reported escaping a French privateer—my head was full of gratitude for that escape, which saved me much money, Mr. George—ships insured at scandalous rates these days, so I often prefer to risk rather than insure—that I barely listened to the play. I only went to please this little Lyddy.”

“And you pleased me, dearest Gappy!” she cried.

“Bless you! Then it’s all I want. What more does a man need here below than to please his children? Especially me, who knew unhappiness when young, and repented of treating this darling’s father too harshly.”

“Oh, grandpapa!” she whispered, clinging to him.

“Yes, I was too hard with him, dear. That’s why I spoil my little Lydkin so!”

More kisses followed. The girl flung her arms around the old man’s neck, pressed her lips to his wrinkled cheek, showered his wig with powder, and glanced at George as if to say: *See, sir? Would you not like me to do the same for you?*

We confess—we confess much. George certainly told Theo of his visit with Lyddy and Gappy, and the old man’s revelation of his granddaughter’s wealth. But did he tell all? For Theo remained amused, warm, and entirely unbothered when next the two young ladies met.
