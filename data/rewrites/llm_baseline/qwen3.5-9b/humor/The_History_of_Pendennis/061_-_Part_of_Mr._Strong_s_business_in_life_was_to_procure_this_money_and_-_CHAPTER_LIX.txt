It happened, amidst the grand festivities of the English season when all London descends upon Epsom Downs, that a motley assembly of the characters we have previously encountered gathered to witness the Derby. Among them, in a commodious open carriage drawn by a spirited pair of horses, sat Mrs. Bungay of Paternoster Row, resplendent in attire befitting King Solomon in all his glory. Beside her walked the modest Mrs. Shandon, a friend to whom the worthy publisher's wife had maintained a steadfast affection since their first meeting. Having refreshed himself with a copious luncheon, Mr. Bungay was currently engaged in furiously shying at the nearby sticks, his efforts causing sweat to pour down his bald pate. Meanwhile, Mrs. Shandon ambled among the drinking tenants and gypsies, while Finucane remained constantly at the service of the two ladies, intercepting gentlemen of acquaintance connected with the publishing house who sought to pay their respects.

Mr. Archer soon approached to offer his compliments and inform Mrs. Bungay of the events unfolding on the course. "Over there," he announced, pointing toward the Prime Minister, "His Lordship has just instructed me to back *Borax*. However, I personally prefer *Munmeer*." He then proceeded to identify countless dukes and grandees to the delighted Mrs. Bungay. "Gaze yonder in the Grand Stand," he directed. "Seated there is the Chinese Ambassador, accompanied by his Mandarin suite. Fou-choo-foo procured letters of introduction from the Governor-General of India for me; we were close friends, and I even ensured chopsticks were laid for him whenever he visited to dine. Yet, he insisted on bringing his own cook. Would you believe it, Mrs. Bungay? One day, while I was absent and the Ambassador was dining in our garden with Mrs. Archer, feasting on gooseberries—a delicacy the Chinese adore—that beast of a cook, spotting my wife's beloved Blenheim spaniel (an inheritance from the Duke of Marlborough, whose ancestor's life was saved at Malplaquet by Mrs. Archer's great-great-grandfather), seized the poor little creature, slit its throat, skinned it, and served it up stuffed with forcemeat as the second course!"

"Law!" exclaimed Mrs. Bungay.

"You can imagine my wife's agony upon learning the truth!" Archer continued. "The cook screamed upstairs, claiming he had found Fido's skin in the yard just as we were tasting the dish! She never spoke to the Ambassador again—not a word—and, truly, he hasn't dined with us since. The Lord Mayor, however, enjoyed the meal immensely; paired with green peas, it bears a striking resemblance to duck."

"You don't say so!" cried the astonished publisher's lady.

"It is fact, upon my word. Observe that lady in blue seated beside the Ambassador; that is Lady Flamingo. Rumor has it she intends to marry him and return to Pekin with His Excellency. She is deliberately squeezing her feet to prepare for the journey. Alas, she will only cripple herself and never walk properly again. My wife possesses the smallest feet in England, wearing shoes suitable for a six-year-old, but compared to a Chinese lady's foot, what difference does it make?"

Mrs. Bungay shifted her gaze. "Who is that carriage Mr. Pendennis is conversing with? He and Mr. Warrington arrived just moments ago. He seems haughty, though he has cause to be, keeping such top-tier company. Does he possess a substantial fortune, Mr. Archer? I notice he remains in mourning."

"Eighteen hundred pounds annually in land, plus twenty-two thousand five hundred in Three-and-a-Half Per Cent consols," Archer replied.

"Law! You know everything, Mr. A!" the lady of Paternoster Row exclaimed.

"I happen to know, for I was consulted regarding poor Mrs. Pendennis's will," Archer explained. "Her uncle, the Major, rarely acts without my counsel; fearing his extravagance, we have secured the property to prevent him from squandering it. —Good day, my lord. Do you recognize that gentleman, ladies? You've surely read his speeches in the House; it is Lord Rochester."

"Lord Fiddlestick!" shouted Finucane from the box. "Surely it's Tom Staples of the *Morning Advertiser*, Archer."

"Is it?" Archer responded simply. "Well, I am quite short-sighted, and truly, I mistook him for Rochester. That gentleman with the double opera-glass (nodding politely) is Lord John; and the tall man accompanying him, don't you recognize him? It is Sir James."

"You know them merely because you spot them in the House," grumbled Finucane.

"I know them because they are kind enough to consider me their most intimate friend," Archer persisted. "Behold the Duke of Hampshire; what a model of a fine old English gentleman! He never misses 'the Derby.' 'Archer,' he remarked to me only yesterday, 'I have attended sixty-five Derbies! I first appeared on the field at age seven on a piebald pony alongside my father, the Prince of Wales, and Colonel Hanger. I missed only two races: one due to measles at Eton, and another during the Waterloo campaign, when I was serving with my friend Wellington in Flanders.'"

"And who is that yellow carriage adorned with pink and yellow parasols, to which Mr. Pendennis is speaking, surrounded by so many gentlemen?" Mrs. Bungay inquired.

"That is Lady Clavering of Clavering Park, adjacent to my friend Pendennis's estate. On the box sits the young son and heir; he is dreadfully tipsy, that little scoundrel! Beside him is Miss Amory, Lady Clavering's daughter from a former marriage, who is remarkably sweet toward my friend. Though I suspect his affections lie elsewhere. You recall young Mr. Foker, the great brewer? He attempted suicide out of a fatal passion for Miss Amory, who rejected him, but was rescued just in time by his valet. He is now abroad under guard."

"How fortunate that young man is!" sighed Mrs. Bungay. "Who would have guessed that the quiet, demure lad who dined with us three or four years ago would evolve into such a distinguished figure? I saw his name listed at Court recently, presented by the Marquis of Steyne and all, and in every noble gathering, his name appears as certain as a gunshot."

"I introduced him significantly when he first arrived in town," Mr. Archer noted. "His uncle, Major Pendennis, completed the rest. Hallo! There is Cobden, of all people in the world! I must go speak with him. Farewell, Mrs. Bungay. Good morning, Mrs. Shandon."

An hour prior, at a distant section of the course, a battered old stagecoach known as Wheeler's (the Harlequin's Head) drag had arrived, carrying a crew of ragged but spirited fellows from Bow Street, complete with a lavish lunch stored in the boot. As the magnificent Derby race thundered across the greensward, watched by millions, the occupants of the drag bellowed the names of their chosen horses—"The Cornet!" "It's Muffineer!" "Blue Sleeves!" "Yellow Cap!"—until the fluttering signal revealed *Podasokus* as the victor. Instantly, one of the gentlemen leaped onto the roof of the precarious coach, shouting, "Hurray, hurray! *Podasokus* wins! Supper for ten, Wheeler, my boy! Ask around, and damn the expense!" The others cheered, whispering that the Colonel stood to win fifteen hundred pounds on favorable odds. Suspicion rippled through the group; each man eyed his neighbor warily, fearing a plot to isolate the winner and borrow money, ensuring no one could be alone that afternoon.

Elsewhere, a far more modest, if equally shabby, vehicle—cab number 2002—carried a gentleman and two ladies from the Strand. Inside, one lady, seated on the box with her mother and companion, enjoyed a feast of lobster salad and bitter ale. Her beauty was so striking that many splendid young dandies, previously distracted by the fashionable crowds on the hill, cast envious glances at the rosy-cheeked girl. Youthful blushing and good humor flushed her face like clouds across a serene sky. Her mother's cheeks were permanently mottled with the color of the lobster she devoured, deepening further with every sip of pale ale and brandy-and-water.

The gentleman escorting them was indefatigably attentive, cracking jokes throughout the journey. He fearlessly bantered with solemn guardsmen in heavy drags and humble dustmen driving donkey-chaises. He fired volleys of "chaff" into endless windows, grinning girls' schools, shouting urchins behind academy railings, and casements where maidservants, nurses, and stern dissenting matrons peered out. Both the girl in the straw bonnet with pink ribbons and her lobster-devouring mother agreed that when "in spirits," there was no one like Mr. Sam. He had filled the cab with trophies stolen from bankrupt stick players, pincushions, wooden apples, jack-in-the-boxes, and toy soldiers. He even brought a gypsy with a tawny child to tell fortunes.
