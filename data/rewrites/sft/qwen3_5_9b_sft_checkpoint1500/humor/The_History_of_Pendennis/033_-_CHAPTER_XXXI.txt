Eager with the intoxicating prospect of real life, Pen embarked on a tour of London's stranger quarters. He took a peculiar pride in believing himself to be mingling with the broad spectrum of humanity—he observed coal-heavers in their taverns, pugilists in their lodging-house parlors, and respectable burghers enjoying themselves by the river or in the suburbs. In truth, he would have loved nothing more than to hob-nob with a gang of celebrated pickpockets or share a tankard of ale with a crew of burglars, provided fate offered him the chance. There was a certain gravitas in watching Warrington listen intently to the tales of the "Tutbury Pet" or the "Brighton Stunner" at the Champion's Arms, and a genuine fascination in observing his attention toward the coal-heavers gathered at the Fox-under-the-Hill. Pen's familiarity with the metropolis's pubs and their diverse patrons grew prodigious. He was the personal confidant of landlords and landladies alike, welcomed at the bar as readily as in a private club. He confessed a far greater affection for this rough society than for his own, finding the manners of his peers annoying and their conversation tedious. "In polite society," he would remark, "everyone is a carbon copy: same clothes, same food, same words. One young dandy at a club mimics another, and one debutante at a ball looks like her neighbor. But *character* exists here. I'd rather speak to the strongest man in England, or the man who can consume the most beer, or that magnificent republican hatter who believes Thomas Hardy was the greatest figure in history. I prefer gin-and-water to claret. I love a sanded floor in Carnaby Market better than a chalked one in Mayfair. And yes, I admit it, I prefer snobs." Indeed, this gentleman was a social republican; and while conversing with Jack and Tom, it never crossed his mind that he was superior to them, though perhaps the deference they showed him pleased him secretly.

Pen trailed behind Warrington to these various refuges with great glee and diligence. Yet, he was considerably younger, and consequently, much more pompous and stately than his companion. He was, in essence, a young prince in disguise, touring the impoverished provinces of his father's kingdom. These men respected him as a high-flier, a fine fellow, a regular swell. About him hung an air of imperious good humor, a royal frankness, and a majesty, despite the fact that he was merely the heir-apparent to twopence-halfpenny and descended from a galleypot. If life places us in such positions, we accept them with ease and are quick to assert superiority over those who are equal to us. Pen's condescension at this stage of his life was a spectacle worth witnessing. Among capable men, this assumption and impudence pass unnoticed due to extreme youth; but it is curious to observe the conceit of a generous and clever lad—there is something almost touching in that early display of simplicity and folly.

After spending mornings engrossed in heavy reading—fearfully not limited to law, but encompassing politics, general history, and literature, deemed necessary for a young man's advancement alongside dry legal texts—they would apply diligently to letters, reviews, elementary lawbooks, and above all, the newspapers. By the time dinner approached, these young gentlemen would sallied out into town with boundless spirits and appetites, determined to enjoy a merry night following a pleasant forenoon. It was the jovial era of twenty-four, when every faculty of mind and body operated in healthy vigor, when the world felt new, and one moved forward spurred by good cheer and a delightful capacity for enjoyment. If we ever feel young again later in life, it is with the companions of that time; the tunes we hum in old age are those we learned then. Sometimes the festivities of that period revive in memory, but how dingy the pleasure gardens have grown, how tattered the garlands appear, how sparse and aged the company, and how many lights have extinguished since that day! Gray hairs have arrived like daylight streaming in—bringing daylight and a headache with it. Pleasure has retired to bed with rouge still on her cheeks. Very well, friend, let us walk through the day, sober and somber, yet friendly.

One wonders what Laura and Helen would have said, could they have witnessed what they might occasionally have seen if they had been in London during the early morning when bridges began to blush in the sunrise and the tranquil city streets shone in the dawn. They would have seen Mr. Pen and Mr. Warrington rattling over echoing flags toward the Temple, following one of their wild nights of carousing. Nights wild, but not so wicked as they sometimes are, for Warrington was a woman-hater; and Pen, as noted, was too lofty to stoop to vulgar intrigue. Their Young Prince of Fairoaks could never address anyone of the sex without respectful courtesy and shrank from coarse words or gestures with instinctive delicacy. Though we have seen him fall in love with a fool, as superiors and inferiors have done, and likely more than once in his life, for the duration of any delusion he always regarded the object as a Goddess, choosing to wait upon her. Men serve women kneeling; when they stand, they depart.

"That," an acquaintance of Pen's declared to him in his hard, homely fashion—"that is what happened." This speaker was an old friend with whom Pen had reunited in London, none other than the honest Mr. Bows of the Chatteris Theatre, now employed as a pianoforte player accompanying the eminent lyrical talents nightly delighting the public at the Fielding's Head in Covent Garden, where the little club known as the Back Kitchen resided.

Numerous friends of Pen frequented this merry gathering. The Fielding's Head had been a house of entertainment almost since the time when the famous author of 'Tom Jones' presided as magistrate in the neighboring Bow Street; his seat was pointed out and remained occupied by the president of the night's entertainment. The worthy Cutts, landlord of the Fielding's Head, generally filled this post when not disabled by gout or other ailments. His jolly appearance and fine voice were memorable to many male readers; he sang profusely during the harmonic meetings, his songs belonging to what may be termed the British Brandy-and-Water School—such as 'The Good Old English Gentleman,' 'Dear Tom, this Brown Jug,' and so forth. Songs blending pathos and hospitality, chanting praises of good liquor and social affections in a baritone voice. The charms of our women, the heroic deeds of naval and military commanders, were often sung in these ballads. On many youthful occasions, I have admired how Cutts the singer, after working everyone up to patriotic enthusiasm by describing how Abercrombie received his death-wound, or bringing tears to our eyes as he shed them liberally himself, recounting how autumn's falling leaf "proclaimed the old man he must die"—how Cutts the singer transformed into Cutts the landlord, and before the applause, struck by fistfuls on his table, faded away, he would call out, "Now, gentlemen, give your orders, the waiter's in the room—John, a champagne cup for Mr. Green. I think, sir, you said sausages and mashed potatoes? John, attend on the gentleman."

"And I'll thank ye give me a glass of punch too, John, and take care the wather boils," a voice would cry, familiar to Pen, causing the lad to blush and start upon first hearing it—that of the venerable Captain Costigan, now established in London and one of the great pillars of the harmonic meetings at the Fielding's Head.

Captain Costigan's manners and conversation attracted many young men. He was a character, his fame spreading rapidly after his arrival in the metropolis, particularly after his daughter's marriage. He was great in his conversation regarding "me daughther." He recounted her marriage, the events surrounding it, the carriages she kept, Mirabel's adoration for her and him, and the hundreds of pounds he was at perfect liberty to draw from his son-in-law whenever necessity urged. Having stated his firm intention to "draw next Saturday, I give ye me secret word and honor next Saturday, the fourteenth, when ye'll see the money will be handed over to me at Coutts's, the very instant I present the cheque," the Captain would frequently propose to borrow a half-crown from his friend until the arrival of that day of Greek Calends, when, on the honor of an officer and gentleman, he would repay the trifling obligation.

Sir Charles Mirabel did not possess that enthusiastic attachment to his father-in-law which Costigan sometimes boasted of (though in other emotional stages, Cos would inveigh, with tears in his eyes, against the ingratitude of the child of his bosom and the stinginess of the wealthy old man who had married her). Nevertheless, the pair had acted kindly toward Costigan, settling a small pension on him, paid regularly, and forestalled with even greater regularity by poor Cos. The payment dates were always well known to his friend at the Fielding's Head, to whom the honest Captain repaired, bank-notes in hand, calling loudly for change amidst the full harmonic meeting. "I think ye'll find that note won't be refused at the Bank of England, Cutts, my boy," Captain Costigan would say. "Bows, have a glass?
