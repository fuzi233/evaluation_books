Ever since that unforgettable evening in Grosvenor Place—when the stars aligned, the candles flickered, and Arthur Pendennis vanished into the night like a man who’d just witnessed something deeply unsettling—Mr. Harry Foker had been in a state of spiritual distress so profound it would have made even a seasoned philosopher weep. And yet here was Harry Foker, a man whose wisdom had once been the envy of Oxford dons and clubmen alike, now reduced to trembling at the mere thought of a woman.

You see, this young gentleman, though raised on a diet of cynicism, self-indulgence, and a fair amount of gin, had always prided himself on being above such vulgar passions. He’d given Pen advice about love, marriage, and the dangers of sentimentality with the air of a sage dispensing truth from Mount Parnassus. “Love is a fool’s game,” he’d declared over brandy one night. “It’s for poets and fools—and those who’ve never met a real woman.” But fate, that mischievous old busybody, had other plans.

The moment he left Pendennis at the door of his chambers in Lamb Court, Foker’s world tilted sideways. Back at the Back Kitchen—the den of wit, wine, and questionable morality—he found himself utterly unmoved by the usual delights: devilled turkey, gin-twists, and Hodgen’s latest musical masterpiece, “The Cat in the Cupboard,” which, if you’re not familiar, was a horror-comedy ballad so absurdly macabre that it should have come with a warning label. Foker sat through it all, nodding politely, mouthing “Bravo, Hodgen!” as required by social etiquette, while inside, his mind was screaming, *She’s real. She’s alive. She’s got grey eyes.*

That night, he retired early, exhausted, only to be tormented by dreams of Miss Amory—no longer just a name, but a vision, a siren, a full-blown literary heroine sprung from the pages of some romantic novel he’d never read but suddenly wished he had. When he woke, she was there, standing beside his bed in the dim morning light, whispering, “Here I am. I’m your princess. You’ve found me. Now forget everything else.”

And so he did.

His former life—a tapestry woven from billiards, brawls, and banter—now seemed as dull as a Sunday sermon. The French prints on his walls, once vibrant symbols of joy and freedom, now looked like relics of a past life. There was Mademoiselle Petitot dancing over a rainbow? Ha! What nonsense. That was nothing compared to the way Miss Amory smiled when she sang “Yet round about the spot, ofttimes I hover.” He began humming it softly under his breath, as if trying to summon her back into the room.

Foker had never been much of a lady’s man—well, not the kind of man who sought out ladies of high society anyway. He called them “modest women,” which, while technically accurate, also sounded like an insult disguised as praise. His mother was sweet but boring. His cousins—those daughters of Lord Rosherville—were worse. One studied geology (a subject so dry it could make a desert blush). Another rode horses and smoked cigars like a pirate captain. A third fasted every Friday and turned her wardrobe into a chapel. The fourth, meanwhile, wore a nightcap during daylight hours and debated theology like it was a sport. Foker had no patience for any of them. Drummington, their ancestral pile, was worse than a prison. He swore he’d rather walk the treadmill at Newgate than spend another weekend there.

Lord Erith, the heir to the earldom, despised him. Foker returned the favor with equal vigor, calling him “the nightcap of the House of Commons” and “the Speaker’s personal shame.” Their relationship was strained, especially after the infamous billiards incident where Foker poked his uncle in the ribs with the cue and said, “Well, old cock, I’ve seen many a bad stroke in my life, but I never saw such a bad one as that there.” The Earl played on, smiling angelically, but spent the rest of the night gasping for breath and refusing to speak to his nephew again. For years afterward, he avoided Foker like the plague—though he never quite severed ties, because, well, family loyalty and banking agreements are hard to ignore.

And then there was Lady Ann Milton—his cousin, his fiancée, and the future Mrs. Foker. Since childhood, it had been decided that she would marry him. Her parents and aunt had arranged it before she could even tie her own shoes. She was good blood, they said. Good looks. Good breeding. No money. But what did that matter? “Your mother’s blood, sir,” the Brewer once said proudly. “Your own blood, sir. Nothing finer in England.”

Harry, ever obedient, agreed without hesitation. “If Ann’s agreeable,” he said, “I say ditto. She’s not a bad-looking girl.”

“Not a bad-looking girl?” the Brewer replied. “She’s a beauty!”

“Well, then,” Harry said, “I’ll wait. But give me time. I want to have my fling first.”

And so he did. He filled his rooms with French art, actresses, dancers, and scenes of romance and passion. He hung up a tiny portrait of Lady Ann among them—just a simple round face with ringlets—but it looked lost next to Mademoiselle Redowa grinning in red boots or Miss Calverley in tight pantaloons, playing a page boy. It was clear: Lady Ann wasn’t exactly the star of the gallery.

But now, all that changed.

Because when Foker woke the morning after Lady Clavering’s dinner, it wasn’t Lady Ann’s face he saw—it was Blanche’s. Clear grey eyes. A smile that could melt stone. And that tune—“Yet round about the spot…”—ringing in his ears like a ghost haunting a haunted house.

He sat up in bed, wrapped in crimson silk, and sighed. His life, once full of excitement, now felt empty. Conkey Sam? The Worcestershire Nobber? What were they to him now? The French prints stared at him mockingly. Even Miss Calverley’s letter—calling him “dear Hokey-pokey-fokey”—seemed rude, childish, and utterly irrelevant.

Lady Agnes, his devoted mother, noticed his pallor. “Why do you go to Spratt’s so often?” she asked. “Those billiards will kill you.”

“It isn’t the billiards,” he muttered.

“Then it’s the Back Kitchen,” she guessed. “I’ve thought of writing to Mrs. Cutts—ask her to reduce the wine in the negus.”

“No, ma’am,” Harry said. “It’s not the Back Kitchen either.”

And so, with a sigh that could have shattered glass, he confessed everything—not directly, of course, but through a series of carefully crafted lies and half-truths. He praised the Claverings’ dinner, their furniture, their plate. He complimented Sir Francis on his post-dinner brilliance. He suggested inviting them to dinner soon—only two weeks away, which felt like an eternity.

And then came the coup de grâce: “We must ask Miss Amory,” he insisted. “I—I want to ask Pendennis. He’s very sweet upon her. Don’t you think she sings well?”

Lady Agnes hesitated. “She seemed rather forward,” she said.

“But she sings beautifully,” Harry insisted. “And she’s charming.”

So Miss Amory’s name was added to the invitation.

Triumphant, Harry embraced his mother and retreated to his private sanctum, where he lay on his ottoman, staring at the ceiling, planning how to meet her again. He’d brought back from his Grand Tour a valet named Anatole—a polyglot of uncertain origin who spoke every language badly but served Harry with perfect precision. This morning, however, something was different. Harry tried on pants of every stripe and color. Boots too shiny. Shirts too loud. He scented himself extravagantly. Then, with a blush, he asked:

“I say, Anatole… when you engaged, didn’t you say you could… dress hair?”

“Yes, sir,” Anatole replied, confused.

“Cherchy alors une paire de tongs,—et—curly moi un peu.”

Anatole, thinking his master was either going to a masquerade or falling in love, fetched the tongs from the old butler and the lady in charge of Lady Agnes’s hair. Within minutes, Harry’s locks were curled like a West Indian diplomat’s. He dressed with extreme care—jacket, waistcoat, cravat, gloves—and stepped out into the world, transformed.

As he prepared to leave, Anatole whispered, “At what dime shall I order the drag, sir, to Miss Calverley’s door?”

“Confound her!” Foker snapped. “Put the dinner off—I can’t go!”

“No,” he said, catching himself. “Hang it—I must go. Poyntz and Rougemont are coming. Drag at Pelham Corner at six o’clock.”

But before that, he had one more stop: Lamb Court. He drove down, passing Grosvenor Place, and paused long enough to peek up at Miss Amory’s window—pink curtains fluttering in the breeze like the wings of a butterfly. Satisfied, he continued to Pendennis’s chambers.

Now, why visit Pendennis? Why rush to see him after not seeing him in months? Why feel such urgency?

Ah, young ladies—if you’ve ever experienced the same sudden shift in affection, you’ll understand. When love strikes, people change. The friends you once adored become tedious. The places you loved lose their charm. But the ones connected to your beloved—oh, they become precious. You start caring about their family. Their sister. Their dog. Their father’s favorite cigar. You want to know everything.

And so it was with Foker. Pendennis, once merely a friend, was now the key to the kingdom. He lived near Miss Amory. He knew her. He’d seen her. He’d heard her sing. He was, in effect, the bridge between Foker and the object of his desire.

What would Lady Ann Milton have said if she’d known? Probably something along the lines of, “Oh dear. Not again. First the billiards, now this. Can’t he just be normal?”

But then, love is never normal. It’s ridiculous, irrational, and utterly irresistible. And poor Harry Foker—once the most cynical, jolly, fast-living gentleman in London—was now a prisoner of his own heart, waiting for a chance to see Pendennis, to hear news of Miss Amory, and perhaps, just perhaps, to find a way to steal a glimpse of her again.

And if you ask why he was so eager to see Pendennis, well, let me tell you: because sometimes, the best way to get close to someone you love is to become friends with the person who knows them best. Especially when that person happens to be a man who lives in the same neighborhood, has dinner parties with her family, and might, just might, know where she keeps her spare handkerchiefs.

Love, as they say, makes fools of us all. And Harry Foker, bless him, was now a fool of the highest order.
