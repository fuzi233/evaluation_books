I traveled on with the moon as my constant companion. She hung full, as she always did – I had never once seen her crescent or dark in this strange land – and on that night, as she sank low toward the horizon, I could have sworn I caught a soft, secret smile tugging at the edges of her craters. When her lower rim dipped just below the line of the sands, a cottage bloomed clear in the very center of her glowing disc, painted as if by brushstroke against her silver: door and windows stood wide, and her own light poured straight through them. The certainty hit me like a soft hand on my shoulder: I was expected there.

In the next breath the moon winked out, and the cottage vanished with her. The dark came on fast, and the road ahead snaked through a jagged chain of small, shadow-choked ravines. I decided to make camp right where I stood. I stretched out in a soft sandy hollow, supped on the leftover fruit the little Lovers had pressed on me at parting, and fell asleep almost instantly.

I woke abruptly. New constellations blazed overhead, patterns no Earth astronomer had ever mapped, and I lay staring up at them for long minutes before I noticed the figure seated on the rise of ground a little way above me. The shock of realizing I was not alone pricked at my skin. The silhouette was backlit against the alien sky, looming larger than any human ought to be from my vantage low in the hollow. It turned its head, and I saw its back was fully turned to me.

“Will you not come with me?” The voice was sweet, mellow, unmistakably a woman’s.

“Thank you,” I called back, not wanting to seem rude, “but I’m quite comfortable here. Where do you mean to take me? I’ve always liked sleeping under open sky.”

“There is no harm in the air,” she replied. “But the night creatures that roam these wastes are not the sort a man would choose to have sniffing at his blankets while he sleeps.”

“I haven’t been disturbed yet,” I said.

“No. I’ve sat beside you ever since you laid down.”

That took me aback. “That is very kind of you. How did you find me? Why do me such a favour?”

“I saw you,” she answered, still not turning to show me her face, “in the last sliver of the moon’s light before she set. I see very poorly in the day, but perfectly at night. The shadow of my cottage would have hidden you from view, but both its doors were standing wide. I was out wandering the waste, and watched you slip down into this hollow. I did not want to wake you – people get frightened when I creep up on them unannounced. They call me the Cat-woman. It is not my name.”

I remembered the children’s warnings: that she was hideously ugly, that she scratched strangers who crossed her. But her voice was gentle, almost apologetic, and I could not bring myself to fear her. “I will not call you that,” I said. “Please tell me what I ought to call you.”

“When you know me well enough, give me the name that feels like it fits,” she said. “That will tell me what kind of man you are. People rarely give me the right one. It is a good day when they do.”

“Then I suppose you live in that cottage I saw, glowing right in the heart of the moon?”

“I do. I live there alone, save for the guests that find their way to me. It is a humble little place, but I do what I can for them. Sometimes their sleep there is sweeter than any they have known elsewhere.”

Her voice seeped into my bones, and I felt an unexpected stillness settle over me. I climbed to my feet. “I will come with you, madam.”

She rose at once, and led the way without a single glance over her shoulder. I could just make out her outline well enough to follow. She was taller than me, but not nearly as giant as she had looked at first from the hollow. That she refused to turn to face me sparked my curiosity, not fear – her voice rang too true for me to doubt her. But how could I ever find a name that fit a woman I could not see? I tried to match her stride, to slip beside her, but no matter how fast I stepped, she matched my pace and kept a steady easy lead a few feet ahead. After a while a faint prickle of unease crept in. Why was she so determined not to be seen? Extreme ugliness would explain it – she might fear terrifying me. A sudden, cold horror of some inconceivable monstrosity snaked through my chest: was I trailing some unnameable abomination through the dark? I almost regretted agreeing to follow her. The silence stretched too long, thick and unbearable, so I broke it.

“I am trying to find a place I have heard of, but do not know its name,” I said. “Perhaps you can point me the way?”

“Describe it, and I will guide you. The stupid Bags know nothing of such things, and the careless little Lovers forget almost everything.”

“Where do those two peoples live?”

“You just came from their lands,” she said.

“I never heard those names before.”

“Of course you did not. Neither people knows its own true name.”

“How strange,” I said.

“Perhaps. But hardly anyone anywhere knows their own real name. Many a fine proud gentleman would stare in shock if someone called him by the name that actually belongs to him.”

I fell quiet, wondering what my own true name might be.

“You are thinking of yours right now, aren’t you?” she said, as if she could read my thoughts. “But never mind it. It is of no consequence. Your real name is written on your forehead, but right now it whirls around too fast for anyone to read. I will help it steady. Soon it will slow, and settle, I hope, at last.”

That startled me, and I said no more. We had left the ravines far behind, but there was still no sign of her cottage. “The Little Ones – the Lovers – told me of a smooth green country, soft underfoot,” I said.

“Yes?”

“They spoke too of a girl giantess who is queen there somewhere. Is that her land?”

“There is a city in that grassy realm,” she said, “ruled by a woman they call their princess. The city is named Bulika. But she is no girl. She is older than this world, and came here from yours, with a terrible history that has not yet run its course. She is evil, and holds great favour with the Prince of the Power of the Air. The people of Bulika were once simple folk, tilling the soil and pasturing sheep. They welcomed her when she arrived, and she rewarded their kindness by teaching them to dig for diamonds and opals to sell to strangers. She made them abandon their fields and their flocks, and build that city of stone. One day they found a great serpent and killed it, and that drove her to rage. She declared herself their princess, and became a terror to them. Back then, this whole region was called The Land of Waters. All those dry, empty river beds you have crossed were once full of rushing, living torrents, and the valley where the Lovers’ fruit trees grow was a great lake that fed them. But the wicked princess gathered half the waters of the whole land into her lap, sealed them up inside a hollow egg, and carried it away. The rest of the water fled straight underground the moment she was gone, leaving the country as dry and dusty as her own heart. If not for the hidden waters still flowing beneath the sand, every living thing here would have perished long ago. Where there is no surface water, no rain ever falls, and where no rain falls, no springs rise. Ever since, she has ruled Bulika, holding her people in constant fear, doing all she can to stop them from growing in number. And yet they boast of their prosperity, think themselves so very self-satisfied. They are good at bargaining, good at cheating, they stick close together when their interests align, and will betray each other without a second thought the moment those interests clash. They are proud of their cruel princess, and despise everyone they can outwit. They have not the faintest doubt that they are the most honourable nation alive, and each man among them thinks he is better than every other. No one who has not walked their streets can understand how deep their worthlessness runs, how high their vainglory towers.”

“Thank you for telling me,” I said. “Now tell me of the Little Ones, the Lovers. I want more than anything to help them. They are the greatest wonder I have found in this world of wonders.”

“Perhaps you will find answers to that in Bulika. There is an ancient poem locked in the palace library, no one alive there can read it anymore, but it says clearly that after the Lovers pass through great trials and learn their own true name, they will fill the land, and make the giants their slaves.”

“By that time they will have grown a little, I suppose?” I said.

“They will have grown. And yet, I think they will also not have grown. It is possible to grow and not grow at the same time, to grow smaller and bigger all at once – even to grow by means of not growing at all.”

“Your words are very strange, madam,” I said. “But I have heard it said that some words seem to mean less, because they actually hold far more meaning than we can grasp.”

“That is true. Those kinds of words have to be felt to be understood. It would serve the princess of Bulika well if she could hear what the very silence of this land is shouting in her ears all day long. But she is far too clever to ever understand anything that matters.”

“Then when the little Lovers are grown, the water will come back to the land?”

“Not exactly. When they are thirsty enough, they will find the water. And when they have water, they will begin to grow. To grow, they must have water. And deep beneath the sand, it is still flowing.”

“I heard that water twice before,” I said. “Once when I laid down to wait for the moon, and woke to sunlight, once when that cruel bad giant almost killed me. Both times the water’s voices came, and healed me.”

She never turned her head, never broke her steady pace a few feet ahead, but every word she spoke was clear as a bell, and her voice reminded me sharply of the soft healer woman I had met in the house of death. Much of what she said I could not parse, and so cannot now recall, but I had long since forgotten every last flicker of fear I had felt for her. We crossed a wide stretch of soft dune sand before the cottage finally came into view. It sat half sunk in the dunes, its foundations hewn out of solid rock. Its walls were thick, thicker than the old sexton’s cottage I had visited earlier, and its door stood open to us. We stepped straight into a large bare room, with two small, unglazed windows set opposite each other.

My hostess walked straight to the far corner, lifted a long white linen cloth from the floor, and wound it tight around her head and face, covering all her features. Then she shut the second door, trimmed a small horn lantern sitting on the hearth, and turned to face me.

“You are very welcome, Mr. Vane,” she said, calling me by the name I had realized I could not remember just a little while before. “Your entertainment will be scanty. The night is almost spent, but dawn is not yet here, and it is far safer for you to be inside these walls. A little lack of comfort is no great harm.”

“I thank you with all my heart, Mara,” I said, and the name came to my lips unbidden. At once I remembered the old sexton, and the little black cat he had kept by his hearth.

“Some people take me for Lot’s wife, still turned to salt and lamenting over Sodom,” she said. “Some think I am Rachel, weeping for her lost children. But I am neither of those.”

“Mara, may I lie here on your floor until morning?” I asked.

“Up that narrow stair you will find a bed,” she said. “Some who have slept there woke feeling lighter than they ever had, some lay awake all night and slept straight through the next full day. It is not soft, but it is far better than the sand – and there are no hyenas sniffing around its legs.”

The stair was steep and tight, leading straight up to an unceiled garret with one wide, low dormer window. A narrow bed stood under the sloped roof, its white linen coverlet sending a sharp shiver down my spine, so vividly it recalled the couches in that strange chamber of death. On the small table beside it sat a dry loaf and a cup of cold clear water. After months of eating nothing but wild fruit, it looked like a feast.

“I will leave you in the dark,” Mara called up from the bottom of the stair. “This lantern is the only light I have, and there are things I must tend to tonight.”

“It is no trouble at all, thank you,” I called back. “Eating, drinking, lying down and sleeping are all things one can do perfectly well in the dark.”

“Rest in peace,” she said.

I ate the whole loaf, drank every drop of the water, and laid down. The bed was hard, the covers thin, the night cold, and I dreamed I was back in the chamber of death, lying between the great fallen warrior and the lady with the healing wound. I woke deep in the night, hearing faint distant wild animal noises on the wind. “Desert creatures sniffing for me,” I told myself, and was about to roll over and fall back asleep when a rough, deep purr rose outside my window, climbing up into a full, thundering howl. I sprang from the bed to see what kind of beast made that sound.

Outside the cottage door, bathed in the full silver glow of the moon, stood Mara, tall and clad all in white, her back turned to me. She was kneeling, stroking a huge white panther-like creature that purred under her hands, one hand patting its head, the other pointing straight up to the moon hanging half-way up the sky, then drawing a straight line down to the distant horizon. The creature bolted off at impossible speed, straight in the direction she had pointed. I followed it with my eyes for a moment, then looked back for Mara – but she was gone. I had still never seen her face. When I looked for the great cat, I could not tell if the faint white speck far off on the sand was real, or just a trick of the moonlight. What did it mean? What mission had she sent that monstrous cat out to complete? I shuddered, and climbed back into bed. Then a thought hit me: the moon had been setting when I laid down in the sandy hollow, and now a few hours later it was halfway up the sky, full and bright. Everything in this world was unknowable, even the paths of the stars. I learned later this world had a whole host of moons, each bound to different rules, and no mortal could ever map their orbits.

I fell asleep again, and did not wake until full dawn. Downstairs, Mara had left more bread and water waiting for me. The loaf was so large I could only eat half of it. She sat across from me, still fully muffled in her white cloth, and did not speak until I asked her the way to Bulika. She told me to follow the high bank of the dry river bed until it vanished, then veer right until I reached a thick forest. I could spend one night there, but I had to leave the woods facing the rising moon. Keep that same path, she said, until I found a running stream of living water, cross it at a right angle, and walk straight on until the spires of Bulika rose over the horizon.

I thanked her, and mentioned that I had looked out my window in the night, and been astonished to see her great cat messenger understand her signs so perfectly, race off so fast. “If I had a creature like that to guide me,” I said, fishing for what she might tell me of its mission, but she cut me off.

“It is heading straight for Bulika. The shortest possible way.”

“It looked so intelligent.”

“Astarte knows her work well enough to be trusted with it,” she said.

“Do you have many messengers like her?”

“As many as I need.”

“Are they difficult to teach?”

“They need no teaching. They are all of one strange breed, but no two of them are ever the same. Their origin is so natural, you would think it impossible if I told you.”

“Will you tell me how they come to be?”

“A new one was born just last night,” she said. “Out of your own wandering thoughts, while you slept in the sand.”

I laughed. “Everyone in this world seems to love their mysteries,” I thought to myself. “Some stray thought of mine must have caught root, and she gave it that big cat form.”

“Then that creature is mine?” I said.

“Not at all,” she replied. “Nothing is truly yours unless your will helped make it exist.”

I smiled to myself, surprised. A metaphysician too, I thought. “May I take the rest of the loaf with me?” I asked.

“You will not need more food today,” she said.

“But I might need it tomorrow!”

She rose and walked to the door. “It has nothing to do with tomorrow – but take it if you wish.” She held the door open for me. I picked up the leftover bread, but lingered, burning with the desperate wish to see her face. “Must I go then?” I asked.

“No one sleeps in my house two nights in a row,” she said, her voice soft and muffled through the cloth.

“Thank you for your hospitality, farewell,” I said, turning to step across the threshold.

“The time will come when you will have to house with me for many days and many nights,” she murmured sadly.

“I will come willingly,” I said.

“No,” she said. “You will not come willingly.”

I thought to myself that she was right – I never would have chosen to return here a second time of my own free will. But then my heart rebuked me, and before I had fully crossed the line out of the cottage, I turned back.

She was standing in the middle of the room, the loose white linen of her outer clothes pooling at her feet like foaming ocean waves, and the cloth that had wrapped her face lay discarded among them. Her face was as beautiful as a night of unbroken starlight. Her great gray eyes were lifted up to the sky
