Behold my lady’s carriage stop the way,
With powdered lacquey and with charming bay;
She sweeps the matting, treads the crimson stair,
Her arduous function solely “to be there.”
Like Sirius rising o’er the silent sea,
She hides her heart in lustre loftily.

The Grandcourts had settled into their Grosvenor Square mansion just in time to receive an invitation to Lady Mallinger’s musical soirée. Sir Hugo had known for weeks that his less-than-beloved nephew would be arriving in London on pressing business, and the third evening after their move, Gwendolen could barely summon the attention to admire her ornate new ceilings and rich silk draperies. Her whole mind was fixed on two certainties: she would speak to Daniel Deronda again that night, and she would finally meet the Miss Lapidoth he had spoken of so tenderly—the girl who had suffered so much, and who he said was “capable of submitting to anything in the form of duty.”

Gwendolen had not forgotten a single word Deronda had said about Mirah, and that phrase looped bitterly in her head, sharp as a pin. She would have been forced to admit, if pressed, that the weight she carried could never bear the name of duty. It was a yoke, forged by a shameful, reckless mistake she had made months before, pulled tight around her neck by her own selfish choices, no room left for any gentle, noble purpose to ease its weight.

Lady Mallinger’s Park Lane drawing rooms, all dressed in white paint, gilded trim, and soft deep crimson upholstery, were half full of guests when the Grandcourts arrived. The stretch of formal instrumental music had just ended, and the room hummed with low, cheerful chatter. Klesmer stood at the piano, preparing to accompany Mirah in her performance of Leo’s *O patria mia*—a piece he had personally recommended to her, far more suited to her rare, haunting voice than any of the overplayed society favorites. Mirah was poised beside the instrument, all quiet grace, when Gwendolen was ushered to a seat of honor that gave her an unobstructed view of both of them. Draped in pale green velvet, the famous “poisoned” diamond necklace glittering at her throat, her sharp sight and iron self-command let her map the entire room in seconds. She gave Klesmer a small, cool bow as she passed, and the flash of their shared smile transported them both back to that morning, not long ago, when Gwendolen’s greatest ambition had been to stand exactly where Mirah stood now: an artist, commanding the adoration of a grand audience by the power of her talent, not just by the glitter of her jewels. A faint, bitter resentment curled in her chest. *He thinks I’ve found the proper path now, the life I was always meant for*, she thought, the silk of her gown feeling like a cage.

She had not spotted Deronda on her way to her seat, and while she made polite small talk with Sir Hugo, her eyes drifted lazily around the room, bowing to acquaintances here and there, careful not to let anyone—least of all her husband—guess she was searching. She dared not risk him later sneering at her for a behavior he’d dismiss as “damnably vulgar.” But a slow, sweeping glance across a crowded room often lands on things you never wanted to see. One pair of eyes met hers, forcing her to give a tiny, involuntary bow: it was Mr. Lush, the amateur of Meyerbeer she had all but banished from her life, the man Sir Hugo still found useful as a go-between with the less reputable set of gentlemen in town. He stood right at Grandcourt’s elbow, while her husband pointedly turned his shoulder to him, pretending to listen intently to Lord Pentreath.

A cold, unpleasant jolt shot through Gwendolen. She had banished Lush from her sight after their wedding, and had not given him a single thought since, pushed completely out of her mind by the turbulent, secret drama playing behind her own eyes. Now there he was, hovering at her husband’s side, and a sudden, vivid certainty bloomed in her like a memory pulled straight from a dream: this man knew every shameful, hidden detail of Grandcourt’s past. He knew the secrets that made her own new life feel like a slow, suffocating punishment. It took every ounce of her self-control to turn her head away from him, pretending to admire the painting on the wall as she continued her slow scan until she found Deronda. He was not looking at her, and she pulled her gaze back, consoling herself that he must have seen her come in.

He was lingering near the door with Hans Meyrick, who Deronda had personally added to Lady Mallinger’s guest list, and both were thrumming with quiet anxiety that Mirah would not get the attention she deserved. Deronda felt his chest tight with emotion: Mirah’s presence was tangled up in a hundred hopes and memories, all centered on the dying brother Mordecai he was soon to reveal to her. He had only just escaped Lady Pentreath, who had boomed in her deep, cello-like voice, “Well, your Jewess is pretty—there’s no denying that. But where’s her Jewish impudence? She looks as demure as a nun. I suppose she learned that trick on the stage.” Indignation flared hot in his chest, the same fierce protective anger he had felt as a boy when Sir Hugo had once asked him if he wanted to be a great singer, when strangers treated talent and vulnerable people like a commodity to be judged carelessly by the fashionable crowd. He winced to think how much Mordecai would hate to hear the word “Jewess” tossed around like nothing more than a stamp on a cheap imported silk scrap.

That was when he saw the Grandcourts sweep into the room, and Hans immediately leaned over to exclaim over Gwendolen, calling her “that Vandyke duchess of a beauty.” For a split second, old, ungenerous repulsion for Gwendolen flickered in Deronda’s chest, as if her glittering beauty and her sharp, unhappiness were somehow to blame for the world undervaluing soft, good Mirah. It was a petty, class-driven resentment, the kind that makes you bristle when something or someone you love deeply is dismissed by people who have never had to fight for anything, and he snapped at Hans, “I thought you swore you could only admire your Berenice style of woman.”
“That’s the one I worship, not merely admire,” Hans laughed back. “Other women I might make myself wicked for, but for Berenice? I’d make myself pretty good, which is far harder.”
“Quiet,” Deronda said, nodding toward the piano, where Mirah was about to start singing. He was relieved when Hans wandered off to get a better view, still unsettled by his own unkindness.

He had never heard Mirah sing *O patria mia* before. He knew every line of Leopardi’s ode to Italy by heart— the poem of a disconsolate mother in chains, hiding her face on her knees and weeping— and the few lines set to music hummed with the grandeur of the whole work, wrapping the room in that heroic, lonely passion. Mirah sang it perfectly. When the applause broke out, Klesmer leaned in to whisper to her, “Good, good—the crescendo was far better than before,” but her eyes scanned the crowd immediately for Deronda. Nothing else mattered half so much as making him proud tonight, singing in the house that was his home, after he had pulled her from destitution and given her a new life.

The crowd of admirers parted to make way for Gwendolen, who Mrs. Klesmer was leading over to introduce to Mirah. Now that his worry for Mirah had faded, Deronda’s heart softened toward the glittering, unhappy Gwendolen, and he felt a little ashamed of his earlier petty hardness. He thought of all the confessions she had made to him, of how badly she too needed rescue—far harder rescue than pulling a lost girl from the banks of the river, and he had felt so helpless to give it. *Cowardly to turn away from her for that*, he thought, resolving to go to her at the first chance, to honor the trust she had placed in him, even against Sir Hugo’s unsubtle hints that he should keep his distance.

Gwendolen sat down beside Mirah on the plush crimson divan, the contrast between the two of them sharp and vivid to Klesmer, who lingered nearby smiling his quiet, lopsided smile. “You must let me say how very much I owe you,” Gwendolen said warmly to Mirah. “Mr. Deronda told me I was in for a great treat, but I could never have imagined how beautiful your singing would be.”
“You are far too kind,” Mirah replied, her eyes wide as she stared at Gwendolen. To her, a real grand lady with real diamonds and a life of luxury felt like a character stepped straight out of some unknown, dramatic play, where the happy parts were only surface deep, and the tragedy waited just around the next line.
“I would give anything to learn to sing like that,” Gwendolen went on, glancing playfully up at Klesmer. “I sing very badly, he will tell you, and I once hated the idea of being merely middling at anything. I was told that was a foolish attitude— that’s a very different lesson from the one you must have learned, isn’t it?”
“Not at all,” Klesmer cut in quickly. “If Miss Lapidoth would be willing to teach you, it would be a pleasure for her, and a fine chance for you to study properly.” He walked away, and Mirah, earnest and guileless, said, “If I can help teach you, I would be so glad. I am only just starting out myself, and I plan to pass on every good lesson my master ever gave me.”

Gwendolen was caught off guard by her sweet, simple eagerness, and to shift the conversation away from singing she asked, “You haven’t been in London very long, have you? Did you meet Mr. Deronda abroad?”
“Oh no,” Mirah said. “I never saw him once before I came to England this summer.”
“But he’s heard you sing so many times, and knows you so well, doesn’t he?” Gwendolen pressed, half eager just to hear anyone talk about Deronda, half fumbling for something to say to fill the silence. “He spoke of you in the highest terms. It seems like you are great friends.”
Mirah’s face softened with that reverent, grateful glow that always came over her when Deronda’s name was mentioned. “I was poor. I was in terrible trouble, no friends at all. He took pity on me, and gave me all the good friends I have now. That is the only way he came to know me— he was kind to a stranger in need. I owe every good thing in my life to him.”

Gwendolen could not explain the bright, strange joy those words gave her. She knew what that kindness felt like, too: the same ready, gentle compassion he had once extended to her, in the darkest hour of her own life. She moved away soon after, certain she had nothing to fear from Mirah, no premonition that this quiet young Jewish protégé would ever alter the course of her life more deeply than a few singing lessons, even if the gilded world of Mrs. Grandcourt ever left her the time and peace of mind to practice.

On a sudden reckless impulse, she chose not to return to her old seat, but settled herself on an empty settee where there was only room for one other person. She was far closer to Deronda now than she had been before. So it felt almost like fate when he walked over to shake her hand before Klesmer started his next piece, then lingered for a moment by the empty end of the settee, until the roaring, crashing swell of his bass and treble made the trivialities of society manners feel absurd, and he sat down beside her.

But when the music ended and the room erupted in chatter, the moment Gwendolen had been waiting for to speak freely was spoiled completely. Mr. Lush was leaning against the wall not ten feet away, well within earshot. She felt a hot flush of anger rise up her neck, but she kept her face carefully neutral as she said, “Miss Lapidoth is every bit as wonderful as you described her to be.”
“You were very quick to spot that,” Deronda said, a little dry.
“No, I don’t mean I’ve found all the deeper excellences you told me of,” Gwendolen hurried on. “But her singing is charming, and so is she. Her face is so lovely, nothing common about it at all. She’s such a complete, lovely little person. I am sure she will be a huge success.”

The words grated on Deronda, and he did not reply, only stared gravely ahead of him. Gwendolen knew instantly she had displeased him, and the frustration of being trapped, unable to say a single honest word with Lush hovering nearby, made her heart race. They sat in stiff, painful silence for what felt like an eternity, neither of them looking at the other, until Lush finally pushed off the wall and wandered off to join a group across the room.

Gwendolen turned to him at once, voice low and urgent. “You despise me for talking artificially.”
“No,” Deronda said, his voice cool. “That sort of polite small talk is perfectly excusable sometimes. I did not think what you were saying was entirely artificial, though.”
“There was something in it that upset you,” she insisted. “Tell me what it was.”
“Those small niceties of feeling around words and tone are impossible to explain,” Deronda said. “You cannot make someone else understand them if they have not felt them for themselves.”
“You think I am too dense to ever get it,” Gwendolen said, a faint tremor in her voice she could not quite hide. Her eyes were glistening with unshed tears, fixed on his face. “Have I ever been that blind to anything you have ever said to me?”
“Not at all,” Deronda said, softening immediately. “Different people have different experiences, that is all. We do not all wince at the same hurts. I have more than enough proof that you are not dense.” He gave her a small, gentle smile.
“But feeling something is not the same as being able to make yourself better,” Gwendolen said, not returning his smile. The distance his words put between them chilled her. “I think the only way we ever get better is to have people around us who bring out the good feelings in us. You must not be surprised by anything you see in me. I think it is too late for me to change. I do not know how to be wise, like you told me to be.”
“I almost never do any good with all my unsolicited preaching,” Deronda said, sadly thinking of the ill-fated necklace incident, and how she had ended up trapped in a marriage far more dangerous than any roulette table. “I ought never to have meddled at all.”
“NO—don’t say that,” Gwendolen said, panic rising in her throat, sure this was the only chance she would ever get to say the words. “If you give up on me, I will have nothing left. You telling me I did not have to stay selfish and ignorant was the only strength I had. If you say you wish you never spoke to me— that means you despair of me, that you are going to forsake me. And if you do that, I will never be good. You are the one who could have made me different, if you just stayed near me, and kept believing in me.”

She did not wait for him to reply. She rose quickly and walked back to her old, empty seat, just as Mirah began to sing *Per pietà non dirmi addio*—the melody rising soft and searching, every note throbbing with emotion, as if the tune itself was made only of unspoken longing.

For Deronda, the song wove itself straight into the pleading of Gwendolen’s voice, a painful, urgent demand for something he could not name, something that clashed with every sacred promise he had made to Mordecai, every duty he owed to Mirah. He saw her clearly in his mind then: glittering in her silk and gems, her wounds all hidden, terrified of herself, reaching out for him to hold her up. It felt like he was being dragged out to sea by a strong tide, one hand grasped by the dying Mordecai pulling him toward a far-off, sacred purpose, the other caught by Gwendolen’s outstretched, desperate fingers, begging him not to leave her behind. He shook his head a moment later, laughing at his own foolishness. He was not that important. He could not possibly hold that much power over Gwendolen Grandcourt’s life.

“Lucky devil you are,” Hans clapped him on the shoulder. “Sitting on that settee with that Vandyke duchess, having a dramatic little lovers’ quarrel! She stormed off in the most perfect regal fashion. I want to paint her and her husband together. He’s got exactly the handsome physique the Duke should have in Lucrezia Borgia— if only he had a fine baritone voice to go with it.”
Deronda only grunted, hoping Hans had not seen nearly as much of their private conversation as he thought.

Gwendolen, for her part, was halfway home in the carriage before she realized she had spent the whole evening half-waiting for Grandcourt to reproach her for her tête-à-tête with Deronda, for losing that perfect, frozen air of social equanimity she was supposed to keep up at all times in public. But he said nothing about her behavior at all. He only turned to her, his voice flat and cold, as their carriage rattled through the dark London streets, and said, “Lush will dine with us tomorrow, among the other guests. You will treat him civilly.”

Gwendolen’s heart began to hammer so hard she could barely breathe. The words leapt to her tongue, ready to strike back like a blow: *You are breaking your promise to me. The very first promise you made after we married.* But she could not say them. She was terrified of the fight, as if she could already feel his hands tightening around her throat. After a long, heavy silence, she said, in the defeated tone of a woman who has just realized her chains are far heavier than she ever guessed, “I thought you did not intend for him to ever step foot in our house again.”
“I need him right now. He’s useful to me. And he will be treated civilly.”
