I write these lines in India, and I direct them to my relatives in England, because silence has begun to taste sour in my mouth.

There are things that, when they are left untold, are seasoned by other people with whatever spice best suits their own palates—pity, pride, convenience. My refusal to take my cousin John Herncastle by the hand has been served up at home as stubbornness, as hauteur, as some private whim. I cannot afford, with those whose good opinion keeps a roof over the name we share, to let that false dish be carried from table to table.

Read what follows before you judge me. I pledge, on my honour, that it is strictly and literally true.

The breach between Herncastle and me began not in some drawing-room quarrel, but in a public calamity we passed through together—the storming of Seringapatam under General Baird, on the 4th of May, 1799. If you would understand why I now turn away from my cousin’s offered friendship as if it were meat handled by unclean hands, you must allow me to go back a little—to the days before the assault, and to the talk that simmered in camp about the Sultan’s hoarded treasure of gold and jewels shut up in the palace.

Among all those camp-stories, one, the wildest, persisted like a strong smell that clings to cloth after the fire has died: a tale of a yellow diamond famous in the native annals—a stone that did not merely glitter, but belonged, by belief older than our own records, to a god.

They said it had once been set in the forehead of the four-handed deity who typifies the Moon. The stone’s colour—like clarified butter held up to lamplight, like saffron seeped into warm water—had given it its name. There was more: a superstition, repeated with solemn mouths by men who otherwise scoffed at everything unseen, that the gem’s lustre waxed and waned with the moon itself, as if the stone were a living thing answering to its master in the sky. In India, they call it to this day the Moonstone.

I have heard, too, that old Greece and Rome had their own lunar superstition—not of a diamond consecrated to a god, but of an inferior, semi-transparent stone that collectors still label by the moon’s name. The human mind, it seems, will always try to give a voice to what has none, and to translate light into feeling. But in India the story had teeth.

The first clear adventure of this Yellow Diamond begins, as the natives recount it, in the eleventh century. Mahmoud of Ghizni crossed India; seized Somnauth; stripped the famous temple—shrine of Hindoo pilgrimage and wonder of the Eastern world—until the very air must have rung with the clatter of torn metal and the cries of the dispossessed. Of all the deities worshipped there, the moon-god alone escaped the rapacity of the Mohammedans. Three Brahmins, keeping their wits when others lost their lives, preserved the god and the gem. By night they removed the idol, the yellow diamond still bright on its brow, and carried it in secret to Benares.

There, so the tradition runs, they set it up in a new shrine: a hall inlaid with precious stones, under a roof held by pillars of gold. On the night that shrine was completed, Vishnu the Preserver appeared to the three Brahmins in a dream. He breathed—so they said—the breath of his divinity upon the diamond in the forehead of the moon-god, and the priests knelt and hid their faces in their robes. Vishnu commanded that the Moonstone should be watched from that time forth by three priests in turn, night and day, to the end of the generations of men.

More dreadful than the command was the warning that came with it: disaster—certain disaster—to the mortal who laid hands on the sacred gem, and to all of his house and name who received it after him. The priests caused the prophecy to be written over the gates of the shrine, in letters of gold: a sentence set up like a brand, meant to burn the eye.

Ages passed, and the watch continued. Generation after generation, successors of the three Brahmins kept their vigil—by day under heat that makes the skin smart, by night when the insects’ chorus turns the air into a vibrating veil. Then came Aurungzebe, Mogul Emperor, in the early years of the eighteenth century. Again havoc and rapine were let loose among the temples of Brahmah’s worship. Sacred animals were slaughtered; images broken; the shrine polluted. The Moonstone was seized by an officer of rank in Aurungzebe’s army.

The three guardian priests were powerless to recover their treasure by open force; they followed it instead. They watched it in disguise, and so, through the turns of time, the stone passed—carrying its curse, if curse there was—from one lawless Mohammedan hand to another. The sacrilegious warrior perished miserably; the gem changed masters; and still the successors of the three priests kept their silent, patient watch, waiting for the will of Vishnu to restore what had been taken.

So time rolled on to our own century. At last the diamond fell into the possession of Tippoo, Sultan of Seringapatam. He caused it to be set as an ornament in the handle of a dagger, and kept it among the choicest treasures of his armoury. Even in Tippoo’s own palace, they said, the three priests remained near, hidden in plain sight. There were three officers of the Sultan’s household, strangers to the rest, who had won confidence by conforming—or appearing to conform—to the Mussulman faith. Camp gossip, eager for a pattern, pointed to these three as the priests in disguise.

Such was the tale, told and retold. It ran through our tents as naturally as talk of rations. Between coarse bread and salted meat, men spoke of a jewel that answered to the moon; between the scrape of bayonets on stone and the groan of wheels, they spoke of guardians who never slept. The camp itself had its own music then—the crackle of cooking fires, the slap of water in buckets, the little domestic clinks and murmurs that try to make a life out of a temporary place; and over it all, the steady background of war: distant drums, shouted orders, the metallic rasp of arms. It was a soundscape that left no mind untouched. Some grew calm under it, as if the noise filled the hollow places. Others—my cousin among them—seemed only more fevered, as if every clang of metal struck on a nerve already raw.

The story made no serious impression on any of us—except Herncastle. His love of the marvellous, always his weakness, induced him to believe it. On the night before the assault he grew absurdly angry with me, and with others, because we treated the whole thing as a fable. There was a wrangle—foolish in itself, and more foolish for the hour at which it took place. Herncastle’s temper, never a small thing, got the better of him.

In his boastful way, he declared that we should see the diamond on his finger if the English army took Seringapatam.

The sally was met with a roar of laughter. Men, busy sharpening steel and folding blankets and making what small arrangements they could against death, found relief in the joke. “Who’s got the Moonstone?” became a catchword before the night was out, tossed about with the same careless air as a soldier’s jest at his own hunger. And there, as we all supposed, the matter ended.

It did not.

The next day came—the day of the assault. Herncastle and I were separated at the outset. I did not see him when we forded the river; when we planted the English flag in the first breach; when we crossed the ditch beyond; when, fighting every inch, we entered the town. The world contracted to shouting men and smoke and stone. I remember sound as much as sight: the strange hollow boom when cannon speaks close by; the wet slap of boots in mud; the high, thin screaming of someone who could no longer keep terror inside his chest. Even now, when I try to recall faces, it is often a noise that returns first, and with it the feeling—the way the ear becomes a gate through which panic walks straight into the mind.

It was only at dusk, when the place was ours, after General Baird himself had found the dead body of Tippoo under a heap of the slain, that my cousin and I met again. We were each attached to a party sent out by the General’s orders to prevent the plunder and confusion that followed our conquest.

And plunder there was—deplorable excesses committed by camp-followers; and worse, the soldiers finding their way through an unguarded door into the palace treasury, loading themselves with gold and jewels as if the bright things could pay for what their eyes had seen that day. The air in the courtyards had a sickly mixture in it—smoke and blood and trampled fruit from broken stalls, and the stale sweetness of spilled spirits. On such a night, even a clean loaf would have tasted of horror.

It was in the court outside the treasury that Herncastle and I came together, both trying to enforce discipline upon our own men. His fiery temper, I could plainly see, had been exasperated to something like frenzy by the slaughter through which we had passed. He was, in my opinion, very unfit to perform the duty entrusted to him.

There was riot enough, but no violence that I saw in the treasury at first. The men disgraced themselves good-humouredly. They shouted rough jests, thrust jewels into their mouths to bite and test, stuck chains about their necks like children dressing in their mother’s finery. The catchword about the diamond sprang up again, wickedly apt to the hour: “Who’s got the Moonstone?” It became a rallying cry. The moment plundering was checked in one place, it broke out in another, drawn by that phrase as if by a bell.

While I was still vainly trying to establish order, a frightful yelling rose on the far side of the courtyard—different from the rowdy laughter, sharper, torn with fear. I ran at once toward it, dreading some new outbreak of pillage and violence. At an open door I stopped short.

Two Indian bodies lay across the entrance, dead—by their dress, officers of the palace. The sight was like a sudden silence in music: it cut through everything else.

Another cry from within hurried me into a room that appeared to serve as an armoury. There, a third Indian, mortally wounded, was sinking at the feet of a man whose back was toward me.

He turned at the instant I came in.

It was John Herncastle.

He held a torch in one hand and a dagger in the other—its blade wet, dripping. In the end of the dagger’s handle a stone was set like a pommel; and as he moved, it flashed in the torchlight like a little captive flame. Yellow—unmistakably yellow.

The dying Indian sank to his knees. He lifted an arm that could hardly keep its own weight, pointed to the dagger in Herncastle’s hand, and spoke in his native language words I understood well enough from their tone, even before sense caught up with sound:

“The Moonstone will have its vengeance yet on you and yours!”

He said those words, and fell dead upon the floor.

For a moment I stood as if struck deaf. Not by the prophecy—though the old camp-tale came back with a chill in it—but by the simplicity of the scene: three men dead or dying, and my cousin in the middle of it with that torch, that dagger, and the yellow fire at the hilt. Myth had stepped out of story and into a room any man could enter by an open door.

Before I could move—before I could speak—the men who had followed me across the courtyard crowded in.

Herncastle rushed at them like a madman. “Clear the room!” he shouted to me. “And set a guard on the door!”

The men fell back as he threw himself on them, brandishing torch and dagger. The torchlight flared, throwing hard shadows on the walls hung with arms. I did what immediate duty required. I put two sentinels of my own company, men I could rely on, to keep the door.

Through the remainder of the night I saw no more of my cousin.

Early in the morning, the plunder still going on, General Baird made proclamation by beat of drum that any thief detected in the fact—be he whom he might—should be hung. The provost-marshal attended, to show that the General was in earnest. The drum’s harsh, regular thud drove itself into the skull; it was an ugly sound, but a necessary one—an attempt, by measured noise, to impose law on chaos.

In the throng that followed the proclamation, Herncastle and I met again. He held out his hand to me, as usual, and said, “Good morning.”

I waited before I gave him mine.

“Tell me first,” I said, “how the Indian in the armoury met his death—and what those last words meant, when he pointed to the dagger in your hand.”

“The Indian met his death, as I suppose, by a mortal wound,” said Herncastle.

“What his last words meant I know no more than you do.”

I looked at him narrowly. The frenzy of the previous day had calmed. He stood collected, even indifferent, as if the night had been no more than a change of weather.

I determined to give him another chance.

“Is that all you have to tell me?” I asked.

“That is all,” he answered.

I turned my back on him; and we have not spoken since.

Let it be understood that what I write here about my cousin—unless necessity should arise for making it public—is for the family only. Herncastle has said nothing that can justify me in speaking to our commanding officer. He has been taunted more than once about the diamond by those who remember his angry outbreak on the night before the assault; but his own remembrance of the circumstances under which I surprised him in the armoury has been enough, I think, to keep him silent.

It is reported that he means to exchange into another regiment, avowedly to separate himself from me. Whether that is true or not, I cannot prevail upon myself to become his accuser; and I think with good reason.

If I made the matter public, I have no evidence but moral evidence. I have no proof he killed the two men at the door. I cannot even declare that he killed the third man inside—for I cannot say my own eyes saw the deed committed. It is true that I heard the dying Indian’s words; but if those words were pronounced the ravings of delirium, how could I contradict the assertion from my own knowledge? How does one translate the last taste of fear on another man’s tongue into a language a court will accept?

Let our relatives on either side form their own opinion on what I have written, and decide whether the aversion I now feel toward this man is well or ill founded.

Although I attach no sort of credit to the fantastic Indian legend of the gem, I must acknowledge before I conclude that I am influenced by a superstition of my own. It is my conviction—call it conviction or delusion as you please—that crime brings its own fatality with it. I am not only persuaded of Herncastle’s guilt; I am even fanciful enough to believe he will live to regret it if he keeps the diamond—and that others will live to regret taking it from him, if he gives the diamond away.
