Upon the library wall of one of America's most celebrated authors hang two crossed swords, relics worn by his kin during the Great War of Independence. One blade was gallantly drawn in the service of the Crown; the other, the weapon of a brave and honored republican soldier. The possessor of these harmless trophies has earned a name equally revered in his ancestral homeland and his own, where genius such as his is always welcomed in peace. This history serves as a reminder of those very swords in the historian's study at Boston. During the Revolutionary War, the subjects of this tale—native-born Americans and children of the Old Dominion—found themselves arrayed on opposing sides of a bitter quarrel. Yet, at its conclusion, they reunited peacefully as brethren should, their enduring love having survived the anger that once divided them.

In the wainscoted parlour of the Warringtons in England, the portrait of a colonel in scarlet hangs beside that of a general in blue and buff. A descendant of these brothers recently showed me these images, alongside numerous letters, books, and papers belonging to them. Within the Warrington family—and to distinguish them from other members of that respectable lineage—these figures have always been known simply as "The Virginians"; indeed, their memoirs bear this title. Both spent considerable time in Europe, living on the very verge of that Old World from which we are now drifting so swiftly. Familiar with myriad varieties of men and fortunes, their lives brought them into contact with personages found only in books, yet who seem vividly alive through the letters they penned. Reading these yellowed pages, scored with the boyish tears of disappointed passion, dutifully dispatched after grand balls and ceremonies, scribbled by campfires or from prison cells, one almost hears their voices. There is even one letter pierced by a bullet, much of its text blotted out with the blood of its bearer. Had it not been for the affectionate thrift of one devoted correspondent, these missives might never have been preserved.

From the very beginning, the mother kept all her sons' letters. Early among them, Henry, the younger twin, writes his love to his brother, then recovering from a sprain at his grandfather's estate, Castlewood, in Virginia, thanking his grandpapa for a horse he rides with his tutor. The final entry reads, "from my beloved son," reaching her mere hours before her death. This venerable lady never visited Europe save once, accompanying her parents during the reign of George the Second. She took refuge in Richmond when Castlewood was burned during the war and was thereafter known as Madam Esmond. She cared little for the name or family of Warrington, holding it in slight esteem compared to her own.

As the reader shall soon discover, the letters of the Virginians are far from exhaustive. They offer hints rather than full descriptions, indications and outlines primarily. Perhaps the narrator has mistaken certain details or filled in colors incorrectly; yet, poring over these documents, he has strived to imagine the writer's situation, the company he kept, and the conversations he overheard. Drawing figures as he fancied them and setting down dialogue as he believes he might have heard it, he has endeavored, to the best of his ability, to revivify bygone times and people. Whether this task has succeeded, and whether it brings profit or amusement to the reader, remains for the kind reader to determine.

One summer morning in 1756, during the reign of His Majesty King George the Second, the *Young Rachel*, a Virginian ship commanded by Master Edward Franks, ascended the Avon River on her joyful return from her annual voyage to the Potomac. Proceeding with the tide toward Bristol, she moored as close as possible to Trail's Wharf, her designated destination. Mr. Trail, a part-owner who could survey his vessel from his counting-house windows, immediately took a boat to approach her side. The owner of the *Young Rachel*, a large, grave man with gray hair and a demure demeanor, offered a welcoming handshake to Captain Franks, standing on the deck, and congratulated him on the swift and fortunate journey. Turning promptly to business, he inquired about the cargo and passengers, noting that gratitude to Heaven for its mercies should precede such inquiries.

Captain Franks, a cheerful man who loved a jest, replied, "We have, but yonder ugly negro boy fetching the trunks, and a passenger who has the state cabin to himself." Mr. Trail frowned as if Heaven itself had granted fewer mercies than desired. "Confound you, Franks, and your luck!" he exclaimed. "The *Duke William*, which arrived last week, brought fourteen, and she is not half our tonnage."

"And this passenger, who occupies the entire cabin, pays nothing," continued the Captain. "Swear now, it will do you good, Mr. Trail; indeed it will. I have tried the medicine."

"A passenger takes the whole cabin and pays not a penny?" Mr. Trail gasped. "Gracious mercy, are you a fool, Captain Franks?"

"Ask the passenger himself, for here he comes."

As the master spoke, a young man of approximately nineteen emerged from the hatchway. Clad in deep mourning, with a cloak draped over his shoulders and a sword tucked under his arm, he called out, "Gumbo, you idiot, why don't you fetch the baggage out of the cabin? Well, shipmate, our journey is ended. You will see all the little folks tonight whom you have been talking about. Give my love to Polly, Betty, and Little Tommy; do not forget my duty to Mrs. Franks. I thought yesterday the voyage would never conclude, and now that it is over, I am almost sorry. That little berth in my cabin looks remarkably comfortable now that I am leaving it."

Mr. Trail scowled at the unpaid passenger, offering only a barely perceptible nod. Just then, Captain Franks introduced him: "This here gentleman is Mr. Trail, sir, whose name you have undoubtedly heard of."

"It is pretty well known in Bristol, sir," Mr. Trail declared majestically.

"And this is Mr. Warrington," continued the Captain, "Madam Esmond Warrington's son, of Castlewood."

Instantly, the British merchant removed his hat, while the owner of the beaver cap began bowing prodigiously, as if addressing a crown prince. "Gracious powers, Mr. Warrington! This is a delight indeed! What a crowning mercy that your voyage should have been so prosperous! You must accept my boat to go ashore. Let me cordially and respectfully welcome you to England. Let me shake your hand as the son of my benefactress and patroness, Mrs. Esmond Warrington, whose name is known and honored on Bristol Exchange, I warrant. Isn't that so, Franks?"

"There's no sweeter tobacco comes from Virginia, and no better brand than the Three Castles," said Mr. Franks, producing a large brass tobacco box and stuffing a quid into his jolly mouth. "You don't know what a comfort it is, sir! You'll take to it, bless you, as you grow older. Won't he, Mr. Trail? I wish you had ten shiploads of it instead of one. You might have ten shiploads; I've told Madam Esmond so. I've ridden over her plantation; she treats me like a lord when I visit the house. She doesn't grudge me the finest wine, nor keeps me waiting in the counting room as some folks do. She is a real born lady, she is; and might have a thousand hogsheads as easily as her hundreds, if there were but hands enough."

"I have lately engaged in the Guinea trade," said Mr. Trail, speaking obsequiously, "and could supply her ladyship with any number of healthy young negroes before next fall."

"We are averse to purchasing negroes from Africa," the young gentleman replied coldly. "My grandfather and my mother have always objected to it, and I cannot bring myself to think of selling or buying the poor wretches."

"It is for their good, my dear young sir! For their temporal and spiritual good!" cried Mr. Trail. "We purchase these poor creatures solely for their benefit. Let me discuss this matter further at my own house. I can introduce you to a happy home, a Christian family, and the honest fare of a British merchant. Cannot I, Captain Franks?"

"Can't say," growled the Captain. "He never asked me to take a bite or sup at your table. He invited me to psalm-singing once and to hear Mr. Ward preach; I don't care for such entertainments."

Choosing to ignore this remark, Mr. Trail lowered his voice. "Business is business, my dear young sir. I know it is merely my duty—the duty of us all—to cultivate the fruits of the earth in their season. As the heir of Lady Esmond's estate—for I believe I am speaking to the heir of that great property—I would urge upon you, at the earliest opportunity, the propriety and duty of increasing the ample means with which Heaven has blessed you. As an honest factor, I could not do otherwise; as a prudent man, should I scruple to speak of what tends to your profit and mine? No, my dear Mr. George."

"My name is not George; my name is Henry," the young man said, turning his head away as tears filled his eyes.
