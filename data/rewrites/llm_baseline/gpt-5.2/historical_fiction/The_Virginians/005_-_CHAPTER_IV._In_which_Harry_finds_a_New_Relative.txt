Kind neighbours, civil speeches, a house filled to the rafters with what a country life counts riches—linen, plate, horses, orchards, a long cellar of bottles sleeping in dust, a library with the smell of calf and glue; a score of hands, black and white, ready at a clap; good health, two sons, and a cook who could coax comfort out of Indian corn and a stringy fowl—these seem, on paper, the very inventory of content. Madam Esmond Warrington, at Castlewood in Westmoreland County, had them all, and in her prayers morning and night she ticked each blessing as carefully as she ticked her household accounts.

Yet in the midst of plenty she moved like a small crowned creature for whom ease is not rest, but merely more territory to govern. The country gentry paid court, the lesser planters yielded to her opinions as they yielded to her physic, and at backgammon they let her win with that obedient smile which is only another form of tribute. Still, few envied “the little Princess Pocahontas,” as the neighbourhood called her half in jest, half in awe. She had been bred to command; to be crossed was to be affronted; and if a soul resisted her, she worried the point as a terrier worries a rat, until one or the other gave way. In church she spoke the public confession of human sin in a voice so clear and resolute that it sounded like an accusation against the congregation rather than humility before Heaven; and at home she seldom admitted to herself—never to others—that she could be wrong.

Her father, Colonel Esmond, in his old age had watched those imperious fits with a dry amusement that disconcerted her. She always felt his eye, and his humour—which she scarcely possessed—held her in check by making her, for once, uncertain. But the Colonel was gone, and with him the single presence she could not quite master; and Castlewood, left to her rule, stiffened beneath it.

She had long managed the estate; now she tightened every screw. She dismissed the English factor her father had trusted and chose another; she appointed a new overseer; she set men to building, improving, planting; she measured tobacco-fields with her own quick step; she imported a new tutor, as one imports a tool. Much as she loved the Colonel, there were maxims of his she would not continue. Had she not obeyed papa and mamma all her life, as a dutiful daughter? So must her children obey her, that their days might be long in the land. The princes she had borne were her first subjects.

Before long she laid aside her husband’s name, and in the country she would be Madam Esmond. The family pretensions were known; she had no objection to the Marquisate King James had once given her father and grandfather being spoken of with respect. Her father’s enormous magnanimity might make him resign titles to the younger branch—to her half-brother, Lord Castlewood, and his children—but she and her sons were of the elder blood, and were to be treated accordingly. Lord Fairfax alone, in all Virginia, might take precedence of her. She demanded the pas before lieutenant-governors’ ladies and judges’ wives; to a governor’s lady, as the sovereign’s representative, she would yield—formally, and no more. The family papers held, as in vinegar, accounts of battles she fought on such questions of ceremony; for etiquette, with her, was not silk and bowing, but a language of power, in which a single place at table could say “master” or “subject” without any man daring to translate it aloud.

Of the Warringtons in England—her husband’s people—she thought almost nothing. She had married a younger son of a Norfolk baronet to please her parents, when she was still a boarding-school chit, ready, she said, to jump overboard if papa ordered it. “And that is always the way with the Esmonds,” she would add, as if obedience were not duty but blood. Once a year, with great solemnity, she sent letters to the Warringtons and to her noble Esmond kin in Hampshire; but a judge’s lady, with whom Madam had quarrelled, returned to England, met Lady Warrington in London whilst Sir Miles attended Parliament, and repeated some of the Princess’s speeches about her English relatives. The tale went to my Lady Castlewood; and after that, the letters from Virginia were not answered. Madam Esmond’s surprise hardened into wrath; she left off writing, too, and carried the silence like a grievance pinned inside her stays.

So she fell out with neighbours, with kindred, and—most bitterly—with her sons. The first breach between the Queen and her Crown Prince came over the dismissal of Mr. Dempster, the lad’s tutor and the late Colonel’s secretary. During the Colonel’s life she had endured Dempster as one endures a thorn in a cushion; or rather, Dempster had hardly been able to endure her. She was jealous of books, as if the very smell of ink concealed a plot, and thought “bookworms” dangerous: subtle men, whispering principles into boys’ ears. She had heard—how does such a rumour ever die in a province?—that Dempster was a Jesuit in disguise. Out he went; and the poor dominie went to build a cabin in a clearing and make himself useful teaching, and doctoring, and living where he could among the sparse folk of the country. Master George vowed he would never forsake his old tutor, and kept his vow, trudging to him and from him as faithfully as some men go to their church. Harry, who loved fishing and a good horse better than declensions, had liked Dempster well enough, but not with that grave, hungry affection his brother gave.

Then came the money.

By an aunt’s death, and at his father’s demise, young George Warrington—heir of Castlewood—became entitled to six thousand pounds in England, of which Madam Esmond was one trustee, and a London lawyer the other. She could never be made to understand, in her bones, the difference between holding and owning. The sum, in her mind, was not a trust but a thing withheld from her by insolence; and when Mr. Draper, the lawyer, refused to send it across at her order, she burned with indignation.

“Is not all I have my sons’?” she cried. “Would I not cut myself into little pieces to serve them? With that six thousand pounds I would have bought Mr. Boulter’s land and negroes, and had a thousand a year out of it—ay, and made a handsome provision for my Harry.”

Mr. Washington of Mount Vernon—young then, and already with that steady way of looking at a question as if it were a field to be measured—could not convince her that Mr. Draper was right, and that trust is not possession. She wrote to London with a sharp pen, and, as some women season even their paper with pepper, called the lawyer an insolent pettifogger, and said he deserved punishment for doubting the honour of a mother and an Esmond.

When the matter was referred to George, and his mother demanded his declaration as if it were a confession in church, the boy sided—miserably, yet honestly—with Washington and Draper. He said he could not help himself; he did not want the money; he would gladly give it to his mother if he had power; but the trust was the trust, and the law the law.

Madam Esmond would hear none of it. Feelings were her reasons; tears her evidence; scorn her judgement. Here was Harry—dear Harry—left with a younger brother’s slender portion, and the wretches in London would not help him; and George, who inherited the Colonel’s estate, would not help him. “Mean at fourteen!” she cried, as if thrift or honour were meanness when they opposed her wish. Add long estrangement, bitter outbreaks, passionate appeals to Heaven, and the widow’s mind may be imagined: for when a person makes of affection a whip, every stroke seems to her an act of love.

That quarrel turned her into a saver for the younger son. The fine buildings the Colonel had begun at Castlewood were stopped short, like a story interrupted mid-sentence. Ships no longer came from New York freighted with Dutch bricks; no more carved cornice-work arrived; no more sashes and glass, no more carpets and costly upholstery from home. No more books were bought. The agent had orders to discontinue the wine. Madam Esmond regretted, with a sharp inward groan, the great coach she had ordered from England, and would ride in it only to church, sitting stiffly in silks, and crying across to the boys opposite her as the wheels ground the sandy road:

“Harry, Harry! I wish I had put by the money for thee, my poor portionless child—three hundred and eighty guineas of ready money to Messieurs Hatchett!”

“You will give me plenty while you live,” Harry answered gaily, “and George will give me plenty when you die.”

“Not unless he changes in spirit, my dear,” said Madam Esmond, with a grim glance at her eldest. “Not unless Heaven softens his heart and teaches him charity, for which I pray day and night; as Mountain knows; do you not, Mountain?”

Mrs. Mountain—Ensign Mountain’s widow, Madam Esmond’s companion and manager—sat the fourth in the coach on these Sundays: a plain-spoken woman with a mouth set like a door that would not be forced open by rank. She said, “Humph! I know you are always disturbing yourself and crying out about this legacy, and I don’t see that there is any need.”

“Oh no! no need!” cried the widow, rustling. “Of course I have no need to be disturbed, because my eldest born is a disobedient son and an unkind brother—because he has an estate, and my poor Harry, bless him, but a mess of pottage.”

George looked at her as if struck, until his eyes brimmed. “I wish you would bless me, too, O my mother!” he said, and broke into passionate weeping.

In a moment Harry’s arms were round his brother’s neck; he kissed him again and again, quick as a boy comforting a hurt dog. “Never mind, George. I know whether you are a good brother or not. Don’t mind what she says. She don’t mean it.”

“I do mean it, child!” cried the mother. “Would to Heaven—”

“HOLD YOUR TONGUE, I SAY!” Harry roared out. “It’s a shame to speak so to him, ma’am.”

“And so it is, Harry,” Mrs. Mountain said, catching his hand in approval. “You never said a truer word in your life.”

“Mrs. Mountain, do you dare to set my children against me?” Madam cried. “From this very day, madam—”

“Turn me and my child into the street? Do,” Mrs. Mountain returned. “That will be a fine revenge because the English lawyer won’t give you the boy’s money. Find another companion who will tell you black is white, and flatter you: it is not my way, madam. When shall I go? I shan’t be long a-packing. I did not bring much into Castlewood House, and I shall not take much out.”

“Hush! the bells are ringing for church, Mountain. Let us try, if you please, and compose ourselves,” said the widow; and she looked with eyes of extreme affection, certainly at one—perhaps at both—of her children.

The bells—iron tongues set swinging in the little belfry—threw their sound across the fields like a net. Their peal, clear and measured, rinsed the air of the coach’s quarrel for a moment, as if the very countryside took a long breath. Under that clean ringing, which belonged to old custom and not to any man’s temper, George kept his head down; and in church, while the minister’s voice rose and fell, Harry drew close and sat with his arm round his brother’s neck, steadying him as if warmth could be a language truer than words.

It was after such scenes—after years of them, so that affection and offence went round and round like the seasons—that Harry, now in England, found himself telling his tale in a wainscoted parlour where meals had been eaten “in ordinary” for two centuries. The room had the settled smell of old oak, beeswax, and the faint ghost of dinners long since vanished: roast meats, gravy, nut-brown ale; a sweetness of apples stored in a distant pantry; and, from the hearth, that honest reek of coal which London gentry pretended not to notice and country folk always did. Outside, the place had its own hush—no drum, no hammer, no city din; only the soft domestic acoustics of a great house: the click of knitting-needles; the faraway fall of a door; a footman’s step padded to be unheard; and, now and then, a carriage on gravel like a brief shower.

Madam Bernstein sat in a great chair beneath a brilliant Kneller, knitting as she listened, never tired of the boy’s voice. Her hostess and the young ladies of the house came more than once to ask if she would ride, or walk, or take tea, or play at cards; but she declined all, saying she found infinite amusement in Harry’s conversation. And whenever any of the Castlewood family were present she redoubled her caresses, insisted the lad speak close to her ear, and called out to the others, “Hush, my dears! I can’t hear our cousin speak.” They would withdraw, striving still to look pleased, as persons do who have learned that even kindness can be made a weapon.

“Are you my cousin, too?” Harry asked her at last, honest bewilderment in his face. “You seem kinder than my other cousins.”

They were sitting under the portraits of the race—men in armour and lace, women with heavy lids and jewelled throats, all looking down with that painted confidence which outlives the flesh. Over Madam Bernstein’s chair, the Kneller showed a young lady of three or four-and-twenty in the easy flowing dress of Queen Anne’s time: a hand on a cushion; auburn hair parted from a fair forehead and falling over pearly shoulders and a lovely neck. The painted lips almost smiled, as if she knew a secret the room could not hear.

“That picture is by Sir Godfrey,” Madam Bernstein said, her needles clicking like small castanets. “He thought himself the greatest painter in the world. But he was not so good as Lely, who painted your grandmother—my—my Lady Castlewood, Colonel Esmond’s wife; nor he so good as Sir Anthony Van Dyck, who painted your great-grandfather yonder—and who looks, Harry, a much finer gentleman than he was. Some of us are painted blacker than we are. Did you recognise your grandmother in that picture? She had the loveliest fair hair and shape of any woman of her time.”

“I fancied I knew the portrait from instinct, perhaps,” said Harry, “and a certain likeness to my mother.”

“Did Mrs. Warrington—I beg her pardon; I think she calls herself Madam, or my Lady Esmond now—?”

“They call my mother so in our province,” the boy answered.

“Did she never tell you,” Madam Bernstein asked, her voice low, “of another daughter her mother had in England, before she married your grandfather?”

“She never spoke of one.”

“Nor your grandfather?”

“Never. But in his picture-books, which he constantly made for us children, he used to draw a head very like that above your ladyship. That, and Viscount Francis, and King James the Third, he drew a score of times, I am sure.”

Madam Bernstein’s hands paused an instant, and the room’s small sounds seemed to grow louder in the pause—the fire’s faint snap; the distant clink of china; the house’s own breathing. “And the picture over me reminds you of no one, Harry?”

“No, indeed.”

“Ah! Here is a sermon!” she said, with a sigh, and began knitting again as if to brace herself with the familiar motion. “Harry, that was my face once—yes, it was—and then I was called Beatrix Esmond. And your mother is my half-sister, child, and she has never even mentioned my name!”
