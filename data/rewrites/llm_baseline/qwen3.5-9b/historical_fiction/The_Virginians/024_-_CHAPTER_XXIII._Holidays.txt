Mrs. Lambert's little daydream had finally evaporated. Both Miss Theo and her mother were forced to admit, deep in their hearts, that their hero was merely an ordinary mortal. Though they exchanged few words on the subject, each knew the other's thoughts with the intuitive clarity of those who love deeply; and Mama, through an extra measure of tenderness and a specially caressing manner toward her daughter, sought to soothe her disappointment.

"Never mind, my dear," the maternal kiss whispered against the filial cheek. "Our hero has turned out to be but an ordinary mortal, and none such is good enough for my Theo. Thou shalt have a real husband ere long, if there be one in England. Why, I was scarce fifteen when your father saw me at the Bury Assembly, and while I was yet at school, I used to vow that I never would have any other man. If Heaven gave me such a husband—the best man in the whole kingdom—sure it will bless my child equally, who deserves a king if she fancies him!"

Indeed, I am not sure that Mrs. Lambert—who, of course, knew the age of the Prince of Wales and was fully aware of how handsome and virtuous the young prince was—did not secretly expect that he too would ride past her gate, tumble down from his steed, be ushered inside, be cured, and thereby cause his royal grandfather to grant Martin Lambert a regiment, all while falling hopelessly in love with Theo.

For his part, the Colonel, along with his second daughter, Miss Hetty, took the laughing, scornful, and unbelieving side. Mama was always prone to matchmaking; indeed, Mrs. Lambert was much addicted to novels and cried her eyes out over them with great assiduity. No coach ever passed the gate without her expecting a husband for one of her girls to alight from it and ring the bell.

As for Miss Hetty, she allowed her tongue to wag in a more than usually saucy manner, peppering the conversation with a hundred sly allusions to their guest. She dragged Prussia and Persia into their discourse with abominable pertness and frequency. She asked whether the current King of Prussia was called the Shaw or the Sophy, and queried how far it was from Ispahan to Saxony—which his Majesty was currently invading—and about which war Papa was so busy studying with his maps and newspapers. She retrieved *The Persian Tales* from her mother's closet and laid them slyly on the parlor table where the family sat.

"I would not marry a Persian prince for my part," she declared. "I prefer a gentleman who might not have more than one wife at a time." She referred to their young Virginian guest as "Theo's prince" and asked her mother if she wished Hetty to take the other visitor, the "black prince," for herself. Indeed, she rallied her sister and her mother unceasingly on their sentimentalities, refusing to stop until she had driven them to anger, at which point she would burst into tears, kiss them violently one after another, and coax them back into good humor.

Simple Harry Warrington, meanwhile, knew nothing of the jokes, the tears, the quarrels, the reconciliations, the hymeneal plans, and all the rest of the domestic drama of which he was the innocent occasion. A hundred allusions to Prussians and Persians were shot at him, yet those Parthian arrows failed to penetrate his hide at all. Was he a Shaw? A Sophy? Very likely he thought a Sophy was a lady and would have deemed it the height of absurdity that a man with a great black beard should bear such a name.

We often find ourselves dropping like a stone into a quiet pool, perfectly compact and cool, little knowing the flutter and excitement we create beneath the surface, disturbing the fish, frightening the ducks, and agitating the entire expanse of the water. How should Harry know the effect his sudden appearance produced in this little, quiet, sentimental family? He thought quite well enough of himself on many points, but was still diffident regarding women, being of that age when young gentlemen require encouragement and to be brought forward. Having been raised at home in very modest and primitive relations toward the opposite sex, he remained oblivious to the subtext.

So Miss Hetty's jokes played around the lad, whom she considered stupid, and he minded them no more than so many summer gnats. It was not that he lacked intelligence; rather, he was simple, too occupied with himself and his own honest affairs to consider others. Why, what tragedies, comedies, interludes, intrigues, farces, and dramas go on under our noses in friends' drawing-rooms where we visit daily, leaving us utterly ignorant, self-satisfied, and blind!

As these sisters sat combing their flowing ringlets of night or conversed in the great bed—a custom of the day where they lay together—how should Harry know that he held such a large share in their thoughts, jokes, and conversation?

Three days after his arrival, his new and hospitable friends walked with him through the fine park belonging to my Lord Wrotham, where they were free to wander. Upon reaching a stretch of water, they encountered some swans, which the young ladies were accustomed to feeding with bread. As the birds approached the women, Hetty cast a queer look at her mother and sister, followed by a glance at her father, who stood nearby, honest and happy in a red waistcoat.

"Mamma's swans are something like these, Papa," Hetty said.

"What swans, my dear?" Mama asked.

"Something like, but not quite. They have shorter necks than these, and there are scores of them on our common," continued Miss Hetty. "I saw Betty plucking one in the kitchen this morning. We shall have it for dinner, with apple-sauce..."

"Don't be a little goose!" interrupted Miss Theo. "...and sage and onions. Do you love swan, Mr. Warrington?"

"I shot three last winter on our river," replied the Virginian gentleman. "Ours are not such white birds as these—they eat very well, though."

The simple youth had not the slightest idea that he himself was an allegory at that very moment, and that Miss Hetty was narrating a fable concerning him. In some exceedingly obscure Latin work, I have read that long before Virginia was discovered, other folk were equally dull of comprehension. Thus, it was a premature sentiment on the part of Miss Theo—that little tender flutter of the bosom we have acknowledged she felt upon first beholding the Virginian, so handsome, pale, and bleeding. This was not the great passion she knew her heart was capable of feeling. Like the birds, it had awakened and begun to sing at a false dawn.

"Hop back to thy perch, and cover thy head with thy wing, thou tremulous little fluttering creature! It is not yet light, and roosting is as yet better than singing. Anon will come morning, and the whole sky will redden, and you shall soar up into it and salute the sun with your music."

One little phrase, some thirty-three lines back, perhaps the fair and suspicious reader has noticed: "Three days after his arrival, Harry was walking with," etc., etc. If he could walk—which it appeared he did perfectly well—what business did he have to be walking with anyone but Lady Maria Esmond on the Pantiles at Tunbridge Wells? His shoulder was set; his health was entirely restored; he had not even changed his coat, owing to the Colonel's generosity. Surely a young man in such a condition had no right to linger at Oakhurst and was bound by every tie of duty, convenience, love, relationship, and a gentle heart waiting for him, alongside the washerwoman, to depart for Tunbridge.

Why did he stay behind, unless he was in love with either of the young ladies (and we maintain he was not)? Had the gracious reader grasped the meaning of the mystic 'S' with which the previous chapter commenced, wherein the designer feebly endeavored to depict the notorious Sinbad the Sailor surmounted by that odious old man of the sea? What if Harry Warrington were that sailor, and his fate that choking, deadening, inevitable old man? What if, for the past two days, he had felt those knees throttling him round the neck? If his fell aunt's purpose was answered, and if his late love was killed as dead by her poisonous communications as fair Rosamond was by her royal and legitimate rival...

Is Hero then lighting the lamp and preparing supper, while Leander sits comfortably with some other party, never thinking of taking to the water? Ever since that coward's blow was struck in Lady Maria's back by her own relative, surely kind hearts must pity her ladyship. I know she has faults—yes, and she wears false hair—but a woman in distress, shall we not pity her? A lady of a certain age—are we to laugh at her because of her years? Between her old aunt and her unhappy delusion, be assured, Lady Maria Esmond is having no very pleasant time at Tunbridge Wells. There is no one to protect her. Madam Beatrix has her all to herself. Lady Maria is poor and hopes for money from her aunt; Lady Maria possesses secrets which the old woman knows and brandishes over her.
