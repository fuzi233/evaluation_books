When the curtain had fallen and the last claps were scattered into the gasping rafters, our friends took coach for Mr. Warrington’s lodgings, where the two Virginians had ordered a supper as carefully as if they meant to argue England into liking them by the mouthful. The streets still rang with theatre-noise—the iron-rimmed wheels skreeling over stones, link-boys calling, chairmen chanting their bargains—yet in the narrow stair and passage of the lodging-house there was already another dominion: the warm, domestic kingdom of roasting and spice.

The landlady’s door opened on a rush of savour. A joint had been sent up and carved; chickens lay trussed and shining, their skins tight as parchment where the fat had rendered; a ham, glazed and scored, gave out a sweet smoke as if it had kept the memory of its wood in its flesh. There were pickles in glass, and a bowl of salad with the sharp green smell of bruised leaves; there was bread with a crust that crackled when broken; and in the midst, in a dish that steamed like a little cloud, something had been sauced with wine till it wore the colour of amber.

General and Mrs. Lambert accepted the invitation with that frank good humour which turns even hired rooms into company. Their son from college came with them; their two daughters, Theo and Hetty, blooming with that spring-time confidence which needs no sunshine in Dean Street or elsewhere; and Mr. Spencer of the Temple, a new acquaintance of George’s, who had the air of a man trained to taste words as carefully as other gentlemen taste claret.

How they sat exactly cannot now be sworn to; but the supper itself arranged them with a sort of natural providence. Miss Theo fell somehow into the place beside the chickens and Mr. George Warrington; and Miss Hetty, by a decision as prompt as appetite, kept the ham within her command and Mr. Harry within her conversation. Mrs. Lambert must have been on George’s right; and so the General, young Mr. John Lambert, and the Temple gentleman filled the remaining ground as they might, leaving their voices to cross and re-cross above the plates.

They began, as people will, with the play; and if the tragedy had meant to follow them home and sit among the dishes, it could not have found a worse company for solemnity.

“As for what our gentlemen say, sir,” cries Mrs. Lambert to Mr. Spencer, “you must not believe a word of it. ’Tis a delightful piece, and my husband and Mr. George behaved as ill as possible.”

“We laughed in the wrong place, and when we ought to have cried,” the General owned, filling his glass.

“You caused all the people in the boxes about us to look round and cry ‘Hush!’ You made the pit folks say, ‘Silence in the boxes, yonder!’ Such behaviour I never knew, and quite blushed for you, Mr. Lambert!”

“Mamma thought it was a tragedy, and we thought it was a piece of fun,” says the General, with a relish for his own confession, as though it were a sauce. “George and I behaved perfectly well, didn’t we, Theo?”

“Not when I was looking your way, papa!” Theo answers; and the boldness of it made even the cutlery seem to listen.

“Was there ever such a saucy baggage seen?” cries the General. Theo, who could be saucy and modest in one breath, says, “You know, sir, I didn’t speak till I was bid. I own I was very much moved by the play, and the beauty and acting of Mrs. Woffington. I was sorry that the poor mother should find her child, and lose him. I am sorry, too, papa, if I oughtn’t to have been sorry!” And she smiled as if to excuse the very softness she defended.

“Women are not so clever as men, you know, Theo,” cries Hetty from her end of the table, and casts a glance down the board at Harry which pricked like a pin. “The next time we go to the play, please, brother Jack, pinch us when we ought to cry, or give us a nudge when it is right to laugh.”

“I wish we could have had the fight,” says General Lambert, carving with the satisfied air of a man who prefers a good cut to a good speech. “The fight between little Norval and the gigantic Norwegian—that would have been rare sport: and you should write, Jack, and suggest it to Mr. Rich, the manager.”

“I have not seen that,” says Harry, very gravely, as he set down his fork as if it were a musket and he had been ordered to speak. “But I saw Slack and Broughton at Marybone Gardens!” He paused, wondering perhaps if he had done a clever thing, for all the company laughed; then added, warming, “It would require no giant to knock over yonder little fellow in the red boots. I, for one, could throw him over my shoulder.”

“Mr. Garrick is a little man,” says Mr. Spencer, “but there are times when he looks a giant. How grand he was in Macbeth, Mr. Warrington! How awful that dagger-scene was! You should have seen our host, ladies! I presented Mr. Warrington, in the greenroom, to Mr. Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard, and Lady Macbeth did him the honour to take a pinch out of his box.”

“Did the wife of the Thane of Cawdor sneeze?” asks the General in an atrociously hollow voice.

“She thanked Mr. Warrington, in tones so tragic, that he started back, and must have upset some of his rappee, for Macbeth sneezed thrice.”

“Macbeth, Macbeth, Macbeth!” cries the General, and shakes his head like a prophet at a fair. “And the great philosopher who was standing by—Mr. Johnson—says, ‘You must mind, Davy, lest thy sneeze should awaken Duncan!’ who, by the way, was talking with the three witches as they sat against the wall.”

“What! Have you been behind the scenes at the play? Oh, I would give worlds to go behind the scenes!” cries Theo; and to hear her, you would think she longed for the place not for its splendour but for its honest mechanics—the ropes and pulleys, the sweat and wax, the human labour that makes a moment’s illusion.

“And see the ropes pulled, and smell the tallow-candles, and look at the pasteboard gold, and the tinsel jewels, and the painted old women, Theo?” says George, demurely drinking his hock. “No. Do not look too close. You were angry with your papa and me.”

“Nay, George!” Theo protests.

“Nay? I say, yes!” He turned to the General with a bow, as though to bring his own levity under a gentleman’s discipline. “If I may speak for you, sir, as well as myself, I think we were not inclined to weep, like the ladies, because we stood behind the author’s scenes of the play, as it were. Looking close up to the young hero, we saw how much of him was rant and tinsel; and as for the pale, tragical mother, that her pallor was white chalk, and her grief her pocket-handkerchief. Own now, Theo, you thought me very unfeeling?”

“If you find it out, sir, without my owning it,—what is the good of my confessing?” says Theo, and the answer came as neatly as a stitch drawn straight through linen.

“Suppose I were to die,” George continues, and at the word the room’s small noises—knife on plate, a chair’s leg shifting, the faint boil of a pot in some lower room—seemed suddenly louder, “and you saw Harry in grief, you would be seeing a genuine affliction, a real tragedy; you would grieve too. But you wouldn’t be affected if you saw the undertaker in weepers and a black cloak!”

“Indeed, but I should, sir!” says Mrs. Lambert, who had a mother’s tenderness for ceremony as well as for flesh and blood; “and so, I promise you, would any daughter of mine.”

“Perhaps we might find weepers of our own, Mr. Warrington,” says Theo, and there was a quick light in her face which, as quickly, she half hid; for Hetty’s bright eyes were watching.

“Would you?” George cries; and his cheek, like Theo’s, took colour as if some hot wine had been spilled within.

The Temple gentleman, who liked to hear himself, and who had snuff at the ready like punctuation, steered the talk into criticism. “The elder writers understood but little of the pathetic,” he declared. “What do you think of Sophocles and Antigone?” cries young Mr. John Lambert.

“Faith, our wits trouble themselves little about him, unless an Oxford gentleman comes to remind us of him!” says Mr. Spencer. “I did not mean to go back farther than Mr. Shakspeare, who, as you will all agree, does not understand the elegant and pathetic as well as the moderns. Has he ever approached Belvidera, or Monimia, or Jane Shore; or can you find in his comic female characters the elegance of Congreve?” and he passed his snuff right and left, as if to season their judgments.

“I think Mr. Spencer himself must have tried his hand?” asks some one.

“Many gentlemen of leisure have. Mr. Garrick, I own, has had a piece of mine and returned it.”

“And I confess that I have four acts of a play in one of my boxes,” says George.

“I’ll be bound to say it’s as good as any of ’em,” whispers Harry to his neighbour, with the devoted confidence of a brother who will applaud in any weather.

“Is it a tragedy or a comedy?” asks Mrs. Lambert.

“Oh, a tragedy, and two or three dreadful murders at least!” George replies. “Let us play it, and let the audience look to their eyes! Yet my chief humour is for a tyrant,” adds the General, delighted with the prospect of roaring.

“The tragedy, the tragedy! Go and fetch the tragedy this moment, Gumbo!” calls Mrs. Lambert to the black servant.

Gumbo makes a low bow. “Tragedy? yes, madam.”

“In the great cowskin trunk, Gumbo,” says George with superior gravity, as though the very trunk were an actor waiting in the wings.

“Yes, sir,” says Gumbo, equally grave.

“But my tragedy is at the bottom of I don’t know how much linen, packages, books, and boots, Hetty.”

“Never mind, let us have it, and fling the linen out of window!” cries Hetty, who would have sacrificed wardrobes to amusement with a laugh.

“And the great cowskin trunk is at our agent’s at Bristol,” says George. “So Gumbo must get post-horses, and we can keep it up till he returns the day after to-morrow.”

The ladies groaned their comic “Oh!” and the General, perhaps more sincerely than he wished to show, said, “Let us be thankful for the escape. Let us be thinking of going home too. Our young gentlemen have treated us nobly, and we will all drink a parting bumper to Madam Esmond Warrington of Castlewood, in Virginia. Suppose, boys, you were to find a tall, handsome stepfather when you got home? Ladies as old as she have been known to marry before now.”

“To Madam Esmond Warrington, my old schoolfellow!” cries Mrs. Lambert. “I shall write and tell her what a pretty supper her sons have given us: and, Mr. George, I won’t say how ill you behaved at the play!”

And with this last toast—wine bright on the tongue, salt and smoke lingering from ham and fowl—the company took leave. The General’s coach waited, his servant holding a flambeau whose flame snapped and hissed in the draught, and whose small crackling seemed, for a moment, the only civilised sound amid the street’s rough chorus.

After such an entertainment as that which Mr. Warrington had given, what could be more natural—more decently neighbourly—than for him to call next day, to ask whether his guests had reached home and rested? London after dark was not a thing to be trusted; behind Montague House the open country still offered fields for footpads; and a young lady might as easily catch cold from a night’s excitement as from a draught under a door.

So George shut his books early at the Sloane Museum; and if he had been reading, it was with an eye that slid from the page to some inward thought until the letters meant nothing. The city’s soundscape followed him out: the museum’s hush, then the street’s clang, then the hum of talk at doors, then the far-off calling of a watchman. He walked as though each noise had a hand upon his sleeve, now urging, now warning. When he came to Mr. Lambert’s lodgings in Dean Street and lifted the knocker, there was a tremor in him that no reasoning could quiet, and a hesitation that made the brass cold under his fingers.

A footman opened. George’s bow was as fine as if it had been practised, and yet it had the slight stiffness of a man whose body knows too well what his mind pretends not to.

They followed young Mr. John Lambert upstairs to the General’s apartments. There was only one lady in the room, seated near a window where the light, such as Dean Street grudgingly gives, lay thin and grey. She was no especial beauty—if by beauty one means the painted perfection men praise in public—and yet it was spring-time in her face: a bloom like fresh roses in an honest cheek, eyes that might be called violets by any poet who had ink enough and impudence enough to make a garden out of a girl.

She was hemming at a piece of boyish apparel—stitch after careful stitch—while she talked, as if plain work kept her courage steady. In those days young ladies were not ashamed of naming shirts, nor of making them; the needle had its own kind of authority in a household, as old as any family Bible.

What could mamma have meant, an hour before, by insisting Theo was pale and tired and had best not go out? Mrs. Lambert and Miss Hetty were gone to pay compliments to my Lord Wrotham’s ladies and thank them for the loan of a house in their absence; papa was at the Horse Guards; and Theo, left behind, sat like a guarded treasure with her needle as sentinel.

“Your brother and mine are gone to see our little brother at his school at the Chartreux,” Theo says, when George has made his respects. “My brothers are both to be clergymen, I think.” She bent again to her hemming, drawing the thread through with a faint little whispering sound, a domestic music set against the street below.

So Harry has been with them?

“He often comes, almost every day,” Theo answers, and looks up into George’s face with that frankness which is itself a kind of daring. “Poor fellow! He likes us better than the fine folks, who don’t care for him now—now he is no longer a fine folk himself,” she adds, smiling; and in the smile there was pity without condescension, which is rarer than either.

“Why have you not set up for the fashion, and frequented the chocolate-houses and the racecourses, Mr. Warrington?”

“Has my brother got so much good out of his gay haunts or his grand friends, that I should imitate him?” George replies; and there was in his tone a salt edge—his reputed sarcasm—which women were said to dislike, though Theo did not flinch from it.

“You might at least go to Sir Miles Warrington; sure his arms are open to receive you. Her ladyship was here this morning in her chair, and to hear her praises of you! She declares you are in a certain way to preferment. She says his Royal Highness the Duke made much of you at court. When you are a great man will you forget us, Mr. Warrington?”

“Yes, when I am a great man I will, Miss Lambert.”

“Well! Mr. George, then—”

“—Mr. George!”

“When papa and mamma are here, I suppose there need be no mistering,” Theo says quickly, turning her eyes to the window as if the street might have overheard. “And what have you been doing, sir? Reading books, or writing more of your tragedy? Is it going to be a tragedy to make us cry, as we like them, or only to frighten us, as you like them?”

“There is plenty of killing,” George says, “but, I fear, not much crying. I have not met many women. I have not been very intimate with those. I daresay what I have written is only taken out of books, or parodied from poems which I have read and imitated like other young men. Women do not speak to me, generally; I am said to have a sarcastic way which displeases them.”

“Perhaps you never cared to please them?” Theo asks, and the blush that rose then was not all modesty; some part of it was anger on behalf of a sex dismissed.

“I displeased you last night; you know I did?”

“Yes; only it can’t be called displeasure,” she says, and then, softer, “and afterwards thought I was wrong.”

“Did you think about me at all when I was away, Theo?”

“Yes, George—that is, Mr.—well, George!” The slip of name fell like a dropped spoon—small, loud, undeniable—and both of them heard it. “I thought you and papa were right about the play; and, as you said, that it was no real sorrow, only affectation, which was moving us. I wonder whether it is good or ill fortune to see so clearly? Hetty and I agreed that we would be very careful, for the future, how we allowed ourselves to enjoy a tragedy. So, be careful when yours comes! What is the name of it?”

“He is not christened,” George says; and his voice took that half-smiling seriousness men use when they are afraid to be too earnest. “Will you be the godmother? The name of the chief character is—”

At that very instant the door opened, and mamma and Miss Hetty arrived from their walk, bringing in with them the outer world’s brisker air and the scrape of shoes and the faint street-smell of damp stone. Mrs. Lambert began at once, protesting in a flutter that she never expected to see Mr. Warrington at all that day—that is, she thought he might come—that is, it was very good of him to come; and that the play and the supper had been charming, except that Theo had a little headache this morning.

“I dare say it is better now, mamma,” says Hetty promptly.

“Indeed, my dear, it never was of any consequence; and I told mamma so,” says Theo, with a toss which was meant for her mother and perhaps half meant for George, as if to say she would not be managed into frailty.

Then they fell to talking of Harry. He was very low. He must have something to do. He was always going to the Military Coffee-House, and perpetually poring over the King of Prussia’s campaigns. It was not fair upon him, to bid him remain in London after his deposition, as it were. He said nothing, but you could see how he regretted his previous useless life, and felt his present dependence, by the manner in which he avoided his former haunts and associates.

Passing by the guard at St. James’s, with John Lambert, he had said to brother Jack, “Why mayn’t I be a soldier too? I am as tall as yonder fellow, and can kill with a fowling-piece as well as any man I know. But I can’t earn so much as sixpence a day. I have squandered my own bread, and now I am eating half my brother’s. He is the best of brothers, but so much the more shame that I should live upon him. Don’t tell my brother, Jack Lambert.”

“And my boy promised he wouldn’t tell,” says Mrs. Lambert to George, her eyes softening; and no doubt he did. The girls were both out of the room when she spoke so plainly.

George, for his part, said he had written home to his mother—that half his little patrimony, the other half likewise, if wanted, were at Harry’s disposal, for purchasing a commission, or for any other project which might bring him occupation or advancement.

“He has got a good brother, that is sure,” sighs Mrs. Lambert. “Let us hope for good times for him.”

“The Danes always come pour qui scait attendre,” George said in a low voice; and the foreign phrase, like a seasoning from another kitchen, had the power to call back a moment—words Theo had used, and the exact turn of her tongue upon them.

“What, you heard that?” cries Mrs. Lambert, pleased to the brink of tears. “Ah, George! my Theo is an—Ah! never mind what she is, George Warrington. Bah! I am going to make a gaby of myself, as I did at the tragedy.”

Yet George had been turning a scheme in his mind—private, clever, meant to nourish his brother’s future as carefully as one feeds a man back to strength. Since his presentation to his Royal Highness at Kensington, more than one person—General Lambert among them—had told him that the Duke had inquired regarding him, and had asked why the young man did not come to his levee. Such august importunity could not be slighted. A day was appointed between Mr. Lambert and his young friend; and they went to pay their duty to his Royal Highness at his house in Pall Mall.

The very approach had its own harsh music: the roll of coaches, the shouted names, the stamping of horses, the clink of steel at doors—sounds of power that overrule quieter human notes as thoroughly as a drum overrules a flute. Inside, men waited and watched and arranged their faces for fortune.

When it came to George’s turn to make a bow, the Prince was especially gracious. He spoke at some length about Braddock and the war, and seemed pleased with the modesty and intelligence of the young gentleman’s answers. George, who had seen men die in woods where the birds were suddenly silenced by gunfire, spoke without boasting; and he ascribed the failure of the expedition to the panic and surprise certainly, but more especially to the delays occasioned by the rapacity, selfishness, and unfair dealing of the people of the colonies towards the King’s troops who were come to defend them.

“Could we have moved, sir, a month sooner, the fort was certainly ours, and the little army had never been defeated,” Mr. Warrington said; and in this observation his Royal Highness entirely concurred.

“I am told you saved yourself, sir, mainly by your knowledge of the French language,” the Duke then affably observed.

George modestly mentioned how he had been in the French colonies in his youth, and had opportunities of acquiring that tongue. The Prince, who had great urbanity when well pleased, and a fine sense of humour, condescended to ask who had taught Mr. Warrington the language; and to express his opinion that, for pronunciation, the French ladies were by far the best teachers.

The young Virginian made a low bow. “It is not for me to gainsay your Royal Highness.”

Upon which the Duke was good enough to say, in a jocose manner, that Mr. Warrington was a sly dog. Mr. W. remained respectfully silent; and the Prince continued, most kindly:

“I take the field immediately against the French, who, as you know, are threatening his Majesty’s Electoral dominions. If you have a mind to make the campaign with me, your skill in the language may be useful, and I hope we shall be more fortunate than poor Braddock!”

Every eye was fixed on the young man to whom so great a Prince offered so signal a favour. And now it was that George thought to make his cleverest speech—clever, too, in his own notion, because it would turn the gift aside to the brother who hungered for employment and honour.

“Sir,” he said, “your Royal Highness’s most kind proposal does me infinite honour, but—”

“But what, sir?” says the Prince, staring.

“But I have entered myself of the Temple, to study our laws, and to fit myself for my duties at home. If my having been wounded in the service of my country be any claim on your kindness, I would humbly ask that my brother, who knows the French language as well as myself, and has far more strength, courage, and military genius, might be allowed to serve your Royal Highness; in the place of—”

“Enough, enough, sir!” cries out the justly irritated son of the monarch. “What? I offer you a favour, and you hand it over to your brother? Wait, sir, till I offer you another!”

And with this the Prince turned his back upon Mr. Warrington as abruptly as he would turn it upon the French some months after.

George felt, in that instant, how a word may be refused not because it lacks sense, but because it trespasses upon power’s chosen language—the dialect of patronage, which will not be corrected by private affection. He and General Lambert withdrew; and the stir of levee and antechamber closed behind them like a door shutting on a sudden draught.

“Oh, George! oh, George!” groaned the General, when they were in the street again and the common roar of London swallowed their disgrace. “Here’s a pretty kettle of fish!”
