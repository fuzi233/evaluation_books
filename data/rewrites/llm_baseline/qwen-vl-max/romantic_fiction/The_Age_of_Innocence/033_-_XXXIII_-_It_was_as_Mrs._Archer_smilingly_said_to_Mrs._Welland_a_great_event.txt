It was, as Mrs. Archer smiled to Mrs. Welland, a momentous occasion for a young couple to host their first grand dinner.

Since the Newland Archers had established their household, they’d entertained informally—Archer fond of inviting a few friends for supper, May welcoming them with the same warm grace her mother had shown in her own marriage. He often wondered if she would have invited anyone at all without tradition’s guidance, but long ago he’d stopped trying to uncover the real woman beneath the carefully shaped façade society had molded her into. It was expected that well-to-do young couples in New York should entertain freely, and a Welland married to an Archer carried double the weight of expectation.

But this dinner—this was different. With a hired chef, borrowed footmen, Roman punch, roses from Henderson’s, and menus printed on gilt-edged cards—it was not merely a gathering, but a declaration. As Mrs. Archer observed, it was the Roman punch that truly made the difference—not because of the drink itself, but because it signaled everything else: canvas-backs or terrapin, two soups, a hot and cold dessert, decolletage and short sleeves, and guests of proper standing.

There was always something thrilling about a young couple sending out their first formal invitations in the third person. Few refused, even among the most seasoned and sought-after. Yet it was still considered a triumph when the van der Luydens, at May’s request, delayed their departure just to attend Ellen Olenska’s farewell dinner.

The two mothers-in-law sat in May’s drawing room on the afternoon of the event. Mrs. Archer wrote out the menus on thick, gold-edged Tiffany paper, while Mrs. Welland supervised the placement of palms and standard lamps.

Archer arrived late from his office to find them still there. His mother-in-law had moved on to name-cards for the table, and Mrs. Welland was considering whether to reposition the large gilt sofa to create another conversational corner between the piano and the window.

May, they told him, was in the dining room, inspecting the mound of Jacqueminot roses and maidenhair ferns at the center of the long table, and checking the arrangement of Maillard bonbons in openwork silver baskets between the candelabras. On the piano sat a large basket of orchids sent by Mr. van der Luyden from Skuytercliff. Everything, in short, was exactly as it should be on the eve of such an important evening.

Mrs. Archer ran her eye over the guest list, ticking off names with her sharp gold pen.

"Henry van der Luyden—Louisa—the Lovell Mingotts—the Reggie Chiverses—Lawrence Lefferts and Gertrude—(yes, I suppose May was right to include them)—the Selfridge Merrys, Sillerton Jackson, Van Newland and his wife. (How time flies! It seems only yesterday you were my best man, Newland)—and Countess Olenska—yes, I believe that’s everyone."

Mrs. Welland looked at her son-in-law with affection. "No one can say, Newland, that you and May aren’t giving Ellen a fitting send-off."

"Ah," said Mrs. Archer, "I understand May wants her cousin to tell people abroad we’re not quite savages."

"I’m certain Ellen will appreciate it," Mrs. Welland replied. "She was supposed to arrive this morning. It’ll make a lovely final impression. The night before sailing is usually so dreary."

Archer turned toward the door, and his mother-in-law called after him: "Do go see the table. And don’t let May overexert herself." But he pretended not to hear, and hurried up the stairs to his library. The room stared back at him like a stranger’s face, politely composed. It had been ruthlessly tidied—ash trays placed strategically, cedar boxes arranged neatly—as if ready for gentlemen to smoke.

"Ah, well," he thought, "it won’t last long—" and walked on to his dressing room.

Ten days had passed since Madame Olenska left New York. In that time, Archer had received no word from her—only the return of a key wrapped in tissue paper, delivered to his office in a sealed envelope bearing her handwriting. This quiet refusal might have seemed like a familiar move in a game they both knew, but he chose to interpret it differently. She was still resisting her fate. She was going to Europe. And she wasn’t returning to her husband. That meant nothing stood in the way of his following her. Once he took that step—once he proved it irrevocable—he believed she wouldn’t turn him away.

This belief gave him strength. It kept him silent, steady, unbroken. He didn’t write. He showed no sign of pain or humiliation. To him, the silence between them was a deadly game, and he still held the trumps. He waited.

Yet there had been moments that tested him—like when Mr. Letterblair summoned him the day after Madame Olenska’s departure to discuss the trust Mrs. Manson Mingott wished to establish for her granddaughter. For hours, Archer reviewed the legal terms with his senior partner, all the while sensing that his consultation was less about kinship than about something else—something he feared would be revealed at the end.

"Well," Mr. Letterblair concluded after reviewing the settlement, "the lady can’t deny it’s a generous arrangement. In fact, I must admit she’s been treated very well overall."

"All round?" Archer echoed, with a hint of irony. "Do you mean her husband’s offer to return her money?"

Mr. Letterblair raised his eyebrows slightly. "My dear sir, the law is the law. Your wife’s cousin was married under French law. One assumes she understood the implications."

"Even so," Archer began, then paused. Mr. Letterblair had rested his pen against his nose, adopting the look of a virtuous elder who wished to convey that virtue did not equal ignorance.

"My dear sir," he said, "I have no wish to excuse the Count’s behavior—but on the other side… I wouldn’t swear to it… well, there may have been some give and take… with the young gentleman…" He unlocked a drawer and slid a folded report across the desk. "This came from discreet inquiries…"

Archer made no move to read it or reject the suggestion. Mr. Letterblair continued flatly, "I don’t say it’s conclusive, of course. Far from it. But straws show the wind. And on the whole, it’s eminently satisfactory for everyone that this dignified resolution has been reached."

"Oh, eminently," Archer agreed, pushing the paper back.

A few days later, summoned by Mrs. Manson Mingott, he found her depressed and restless.

"You know she’s abandoned me?" she said at once, without waiting for a reply. "Oh, don’t ask why! She gave so many reasons I’ve forgotten them all. My private opinion? She couldn’t stand the boredom. At least that’s what Augusta and my daughters-in-law think. And I don’t entirely blame her. Olenski’s a scoundrel, yes—but life with him must have been far livelier than here on Fifth Avenue. Not that the family would admit it—they think Fifth Avenue is heaven with the rue de la Paix thrown in. Poor Ellen, of course, refuses to go back. She holds firm. So she’s settling in Paris with that silly Medora. Well, Paris is Paris. You can keep a carriage there on next to nothing. But she was as bright as a bird, and I’ll miss her." Two dry tears rolled down her cheeks and vanished into the folds of her dress.

"All I ask," she finished, "is that they leave me alone. I must digest my gruel in peace…" And she twinkled faintly at Archer.

That evening, when he returned home, May announced her plan to host a farewell dinner for her cousin. Madame Olenska’s name hadn’t been spoken between them since the night she fled to Washington. Archer looked at his wife, surprised.

"A dinner—why?" he asked.

Her cheeks flushed. "Because you like Ellen—I thought you’d be happy."

"It’s very kind of you to say that," he replied, "but I really don’t understand—"

"I’m doing it, Newland," she said quietly, rising to her desk. "Here are the invitations. Mother helped. She agrees we should." She hesitated, embarrassed yet smiling, and Archer suddenly saw before him the full embodiment of the Family.

"Oh, all right," he said, staring blankly at the guest list she handed him.

When he entered the drawing room before dinner, May was bending over the fireplace, coaxing logs to burn in their unfamiliar setting of pristine tiles.

The tall lamps were lit, and Mr. van der Luyden’s orchids adorned various modern porcelain vases and knobby silver containers. Mrs. Newland Archer’s drawing room was widely admired. A gilt bamboo *jardinière*, filled with primulas and cinerarias, blocked the bay window (where old-fashioned taste might have preferred a bronze Venus); pale brocade sofas and armchairs were artfully grouped around little plush tables covered with silver ornaments, porcelain animals, and ornate photo frames; and rosy-shaded lamps rose like tropical blooms among the palms.

"I don’t think Ellen has ever seen this room like this," May said, rising flushed from her struggle, and casting a proud glance around. The brass tongs she’d leaned against the chimney fell with a crash, drowning her husband’s reply. Before he could retrieve them, Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden were announced.

The other guests followed quickly, for it was known the van der Luydens liked punctuality. The room was nearly full, and Archer was showing Mrs. Selfridge Merry a small, highly varnished Verbeckhoven painting of sheep—given to May by Mr. Welland for Christmas—when he felt Madame Olenska beside him.

She was unnaturally pale, her dark hair seeming heavier and denser in contrast. Perhaps it was the amber beads wound tightly around her neck, or the memory of the little Ellen Mingott he’d danced with as a child when Medora Manson first brought her to New York.

The beads clashed with her complexion, or her dress was ill-fitting—her face looked dull, almost harsh. Yet in that moment, he loved her more than ever. Their hands touched, and he thought he heard her whisper, “Yes, we sail tomorrow on the *Russia*.” Then came the noise of opening doors, and soon May’s voice: “Newland! Dinner’s announced. Will you please escort Ellen?”

Madame Olenska placed her hand on his arm, and he noticed it was ungloved. He remembered how he’d fixated on that same hand the night he sat with her in the little Twenty-third Street drawing room. All the beauty that had faded from her face seemed to have gathered in the long, pale fingers and faintly dimpled knuckles resting on his sleeve. He thought: *If it were only to see her hand again, I would follow her.*

Only at a gathering ostensibly honoring a "foreign visitor" could Mrs. van der Luyden accept being seated on the host’s left. The emphasis on Madame Olenska’s foreignness was subtle but unmistakable—and Mrs. van der Luyden accepted the shift with quiet approval. There were things that had to be done, and if done at all, they had to be done thoroughly. One such thing, in old New York’s code, was rallying around a kinswoman about to leave the tribe. The Wellands and Mingotts would do anything to proclaim their unwavering love for the Countess Olenska now that her passage was booked. Archer, seated at the head of the table, watched in awe as her popularity was restored, grievances hushed, her past excused, and her present glorified by the family’s collective embrace. Mrs. van der Luyden shone upon her with the faintest warmth she allowed, and Mr. van der Luyden, seated at May’s right, cast glances down the table that clearly justified every carnation he’d sent from Skuytercliff.

Archer felt suspended—floating somewhere between chandelier and ceiling. He marveled at his own role. As his gaze swept the placid faces, he saw them all engaged in devouring May’s canvas-backs like a band of silent conspirators, himself and the pale woman beside him at the heart of their plot. Then it struck him—a sudden flash of understanding—how to everyone at the table, he and Madame Olenska were lovers. Not just lovers, but lovers in the deepest sense reserved for foreign tongues. He realized he’d been the object of countless watching eyes and listening ears for months. He understood that, through means unknown to him, the separation between himself and the woman he loved had been achieved—and now the entire tribe had gathered around his wife, united in the assumption that nothing had ever happened, that no one had ever suspected, and that this dinner was simply May’s natural gesture of affection toward her friend and cousin.

It was the old New York way—taking life without effusion of blood. A way that feared scandal more than illness, valued decency over courage, and deemed nothing more vulgar than “scenes,” except the conduct of those who caused them.

As these thoughts raced through him, Archer felt like a prisoner in the midst of an armed camp. He glanced around the table and sensed the unyielding resolve of his captors in the tone with which they discussed Beaufort and his wife over Florida asparagus. *They’re showing me what would happen to me,* he thought. And a chilling certainty settled over him—the power of implication, analogy, and silence outweighed any direct action. It closed in on him like the doors of a family vault.

He laughed, meeting Mrs. van der Luyden’s startled gaze.

“You find it amusing?” she asked, her smile tight. “Of course, poor Regina’s idea of staying in New York does seem rather absurd.”

“Of course,” Archer muttered.

At that moment, he became aware that Madame Olenska’s other neighbor had been talking to the woman beside him for some time. Simultaneously, he saw May, serene between Mr. van der Luyden and Mr. Selfridge Merry, glance swiftly down the table. Clearly, the host and the woman beside him couldn’t sit in silence all evening. He turned to Madame Olenska, and her pale smile met him—*Oh, let’s see this through,* it seemed to say.

“Did you find the journey tiring?” he asked, surprised by the naturalness of his voice.

On the contrary, she replied, she had rarely traveled with fewer discomforts.

“Except,” she added, “the dreadful heat in the train.”

He remarked that she wouldn’t suffer that in the country she was going to.

“I never,” he declared with intensity, “was more nearly frozen than once, in April, on the train between Calais and Paris.”

She said she didn’t wonder, but noted that one could always carry an extra blanket. Every form of travel had its trials, she said.

To which he replied abruptly, “I think they’re all insignificant compared to the joy of getting away.”

She flushed. He added, his voice rising: “I mean to travel myself—soon.”

A tremor crossed her face. Leaning toward Reggie Chivers, he exclaimed, “Say, Reggie, what do you think of a trip around the world? Next month? I’m game if you are!”

Mrs. Reggie chimed in that she couldn’t bear to let Reggie go before the Martha Washington Ball she was organizing for the Blind Asylum in Easter week. Her husband calmly added that by then he’d need to be practicing for the International Polo match.

But Mr. Selfridge Merry had caught the phrase “around the world,” and having sailed the globe in his steam-yacht, he launched into vivid descriptions of Mediterranean ports’ shallowness. Though, he added, it didn’t matter—once you’d seen Athens, Smyrna, Constantinople, what else was there?

And Mrs. Merry said she’d always be grateful to Dr. Bencomb for preventing them from visiting Naples due to the fever.

“But you need three weeks to do India properly,” her husband conceded, eager to prove he wasn’t a frivolous traveler.

Then the ladies retired to the drawing room.

In the library, despite the presence of others, Lawrence Lefferts dominated.

As usual, the conversation had turned to the Beauforts. Even Mr. van der Luyden and Mr. Selfridge Merry, seated in the honorary armchairs reserved for them, paused to listen to Lefferts’ impassioned critique.

Never had Lefferts been so full of the virtues of Christian manhood and the sanctity of home. Indignation lent him eloquence. If others had acted as he spoke, society would never have admitted a foreign upstart like Beaufort—not even if he’d married a van der Luyden or a Lanning instead of a Dallas. And what chance would he have had, Lefferts thundered, of marrying into such a family if he hadn’t already wormed his way into certain houses—just as people like Mrs. Lemuel Struthers had done afterward?

If society opened its doors to vulgar women, the harm was minimal—though the gain doubtful. But once it tolerated men of obscure origin and tainted wealth, the end was inevitable—total disintegration, and soon.

“If things continue,” Lefferts roared, looking like a young prophet dressed by Poole, “we’ll see our children begging for invitations to swindlers’ houses, marrying Beaufort’s bastards.”

“Oh, come on—draw it mild!” Reggie Chivers and young Newland protested, while Mr. Selfridge Merry looked alarmed and Mr. van der Luyden’s face twisted in pain.

“Has he got any?” Mr. Sillerton Jackson asked, pricking up his ears.

While Lefferts tried to laugh it off, the older gentleman whispered to Archer: “Funny, those men who always want to fix things. The ones with the worst cooks are always claiming they’re poisoned when they dine out. But I hear there are pressing reasons for our friend Lawrence’s speech—typewriter this time, I understand…”

The talk rushed past Archer like a river running endlessly because it didn’t know how to stop. He saw expressions of interest, amusement, even laughter on the faces around him. He heard the younger men laugh, praised the Archer Madeira, which Mr. van der Luyden and Mr. Merry were sipping thoughtfully. Through it all, he sensed a general warmth directed at him—as if the guards of his imagined prison were trying to ease his captivity. And that only deepened his longing for freedom.

In the drawing room, where they joined the ladies, he met May’s triumphant gaze. He read in it the certainty that everything had gone perfectly. She rose from Madame Olenska’s side, and immediately Mrs. van der Luyden beckoned the countess to the gilt sofa, where she throned. Mrs. Selfridge Merry crossed the room to join them. Archer saw clearly that another conspiracy was underway—one of restoration and erasure. The silent machinery that upheld his world was determined to record that it had never doubted Madame Olenska’s conduct, nor questioned his marital happiness. These amiable, inexorable people were pretending to each other that they had never heard, suspected, or even imagined anything otherwise. And from this web of mutual pretense, Archer grasped anew that New York believed him to be Madame Olenska’s lover. He caught the gleam of victory in May’s eyes—and for the first time, realized she shared that belief. The revelation stirred a wild laughter within him, echoing through his efforts to chat about the Martha Washington ball with Mrs. Reggie Chivers and little Mrs. Newland. The evening flowed on, a senseless river rushing forward, unaware of its destination.

Finally, he saw Madame Olenska rise to leave. He knew she would be gone in moments. He tried to recall what they had said at dinner—but nothing came to mind.

She approached May, the others forming a circle around her. The two women clasped hands; then May bent and kissed her cousin.

“Certainly our hostess is much the handsomer of the two,” Reggie Chivers murmured to young Mrs. Newland, and Archer remembered Beaufort’s crude sneer at May’s lackluster beauty.

A moment later, he stood in the hall, draping Madame Olenska’s cloak around her shoulders.

Through all his turmoil, he clung to the resolve to say nothing that might disturb her. Convinced no force could deter him, he allowed events to unfold as they would. But as he followed her into the hall, he suddenly yearned—to be alone with her, just for a moment, at the door of her carriage.

“Is your carriage here?” he asked.

At that instant, Mrs. van der Luyden, being eased into her sables, said gently: “We’re driving dear Ellen home.”

Archer’s heart lurched. Madame Olenska, holding her cloak and fan in one hand, reached out the other.

“Good-bye,” she said.

“Good-bye—but I’ll see you soon in Paris,” he answered aloud—shouting it, it seemed.

“Oh,” she murmured, “if you and May could come—!”
