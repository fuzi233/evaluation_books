Harry Feversham arrived in Dublin by the night mail after his ride with Durrance in the Row. The next morning, he crossed Lough Swilly aboard a small cargo steamer that made its weekly journey up the Lennon River to Ramelton. As the boat docked, he spotted Ethne waiting for him on the quay, her hands resting lightly on the reins of her dog-cart. She greeted him with a smile—warm, but with the ease of a comrade rather than the tenderness of a lover.

"You didn’t expect me," she remarked, noting his expression.

"I never do," he replied. "You always surpass what I imagine."

At that, her smile softened, deepening into something more intimate. She flicked the reins, urging the horse forward. "I’ve brought no groom on purpose," she said. "We’ll take our time. Guests arrive tomorrow—today is just for us."

They moved along the riverside causeway, the town unfolding around them—the steep, narrow streets, the old stone bridge arching over the swift, dark water, the trees crowding the far bank. Feversham absorbed it all with quiet fascination. This was Ethne’s world, and every detail of it seemed to belong to her.

She was twenty-one then, tall and strong, with a grace that defied the rigid expectations of her time. Her hair, dark brown and thick, was coiled at the nape of her neck, and her clear grey eyes met his without hesitation. There was a steadiness to her, an unshakable honesty that made her seem older than her years. She was fearless, too—Feversham had heard the story of how she had once stopped a bolting horse with nothing but her own quick wits and nerve.

For a while, they rode in silence. Then, abruptly, she turned to him.

"I saw in the papers this morning," she said, "that your regiment has been ordered to Egypt. If not for me, you might have gone. There would have been chances for distinction. I’ve held you back, and I’m sorry for it."

Feversham stiffened. His voice, when he spoke, was rough. "You needn’t be."

"But I am," she insisted. "And I blame myself all the more because I don’t see things as others do. If you had gone, and if the worst had happened, I would have grieved—of course—but I would have known, absolutely known, that we’d meet again."

She said it simply, as if stating an obvious truth. Feversham felt the words like a blow. He wanted to speak, to confess, but the weight of what he carried choked him.

Ethne, unaware, continued. "I don’t feel loss as others do. When people I care about are gone, it’s never truly an ending. Somewhere, somehow, we’ll find each other again."

"Unless," Feversham said slowly, "one has been deceived. What if the face you trust is only a mask?"

She shook her head. "No. I don’t believe in mistakes like that. Not in the end."

Her certainty was a knife twisting in him. He opened his mouth—*Ethne, you don’t know*—but before he could speak, she reined in the horse, laughing, and pointed ahead.

They had reached the top of a hill. A scarlet letter-box stood embedded in the stone wall.

"That’s where I posted my letters to you," she said. "During the worst of it."

Feversham swallowed hard. "Convenient," he managed.

She grinned. "And that gap in the wall—I used to climb through it at night to reach the post. Half a mile down the avenue in the dark."

"You might have been hurt."

"Only by the thorns," she replied lightly, turning the horse through the gates of Lennon House.

---

The house was old and sprawling, its grey stone weathered by time. As they approached, the door swung open, and Ethne’s father, Dermod Eustace, emerged—a gaunt, fierce-eyed man whose demeanor softened slightly at the sight of Feversham.

"Welcome," he said brusquely. "Make yourself at home. If you want whiskey, stamp twice. The servants know." And with that, he retreated inside.

Dermod Eustace was a relic of another era, a man who clung to the old ways with a stubborn pride. He ruled his household with a mix of tyranny and hospitality, compelling guests to stay whether they wished to or not. Yet for all his bluster, he was oddly fond of Feversham—and, despite himself, a little afraid of his daughter.

That afternoon, Feversham and Ethne wandered down to the Lennon River, where the water rushed over rocks in a smooth, unbroken sheet. They sat by the bank, talking little, listening to the river’s voice. The hours slipped by, golden and slow, until the sunset painted the sky and the shadows stretched long across the grass.

It was a moment Feversham would remember forever—because the next night, everything changed.

---

The ball was in full swing when Ethne took his arm and led him from the crowded drawing room into the empty hall. The front door stood open, letting in the cool night air. From the ballroom behind them came the swell of music, the laughter of dancers.

"The post is in," Ethne said, crossing to a side table. "Letters for you—and this."

She held out a small white box. It was light, too light. Feversham took it, frowning at the unfamiliar handwriting. He broke the seal and lifted the lid.

Three white feathers drifted out, settling soundlessly on the floor.

His face went pale.

Ethne stared at them, then at him. "What does this mean?"

"That some things can’t be hidden," he said hoarsely.

She shook her head, bewildered. "It’s a joke—a cruel one, but still—"

"No." His voice was steady now, his eyes locked on hers. "They were sent in earnest."

"By whom?"

Feversham hadn’t considered that. The message itself was the blow; the messengers hardly mattered. But Ethne snatched up the box, pulling out three visiting cards.

"Captain Trench. Mr. Castleton. Mr. Willoughby." She looked up. "Do you know them?"

"All three were officers in my regiment."

She knelt, gathering the feathers in her hand as if touching them might make sense of this. "Were they right to send these?"

"Yes."

The word was final. He offered no defense.

Ethne’s voice was very quiet. "You would have married me without telling me any of this, wouldn’t you? If these feathers hadn’t come?"

He didn’t answer. He didn’t need to.

Slowly, deliberately, she twisted the ring from her finger and laid it on the table. "Take it back."

Feversham picked it up, the metal cold in his palm.

Then she pointed to the feathers. "These are yours too."

He hesitated. "There are four."

She said nothing, but her fan—white feathers set in ivory—was missing one of its plumes.

She had added her own to the three.
