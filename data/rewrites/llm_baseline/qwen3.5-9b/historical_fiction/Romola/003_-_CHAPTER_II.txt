After joining the cluster of chatterers, the young stranger grew impatient. Hopeless of discovering the cause of the local uproar—and indifferent to matters that concerned only those born of Florence—he decided to wander the piazza alone. He sought a vendor of food who might possess less curiosity about public affairs than the others. Suddenly, struck by a thought, he reached into the pouch hanging at his waist, rummaging through it with a look of growing frustration.

"Not a single coin!" he muttered in a language neither Tuscan nor Italian. "I thought I had one poor piece left. Very well, then—I must buy my breakfast for love!"

He hadn't walked far before locating a quiet corner of the market where such currency would surely be accepted. There stood two mules, decorated with red tassels and collars. One bore wooden vessels for milk; the other carried panniers overflowing with herbs and salads. Leaning her elbow against the milk-mule's neck was a young girl, no older than sixteen, her face framed by a red hood that concealed her hair entirely. Her features were so infantile in their prettiness that the total hiding of her hair enhanced the effect. Perhaps weary from her morning labor preparing for the trek from a castle several miles away, the child appeared to have fallen asleep in that half-standing, half-leaning posture.

Yet, the stranger felt no hesitation in waking her. His method, however, was so gentle that to the sleeping damsel, it seemed merely as if a sprig of thyme had brushed her lips as she bent to gather herbs. The dream shattered instantly; she opened wide, baby-blue eyes and sat up in astonishment and confusion, seeing the young man standing close before her. She heard his voice, strange yet soft, and even if she had been composed, she assumed he spoke words utterly unintelligible to her. Her first instinct was to turn her head slightly away and lift a corner of her green serge mantle as a shield.

He repeated his words gently: "Forgive me, pretty one, for awakening you. I am dying of hunger, and the scent of milk makes breakfast seem more desirable than ever."

He had chosen the phrase *"muoio di fame"* (I die of hunger) knowing it would be familiar to her ears, delivering it with the playful intonation of a beggar. This time, she understood. The corner of the mantle dropped, and within moments, a large cup of fragrant milk was offered to him. He took no further compliments before lifting it to his lips. As he drank, the little maiden gathered the courage to look up at the long, dark curls of this singular stranger. Though his clothes were ragged, he bore no resemblance to any beggar she had ever known.

While this observation occurred, another current of feeling moved her hand into a bag hanging beside the mule. When the stranger set down his empty cup, he saw a large piece of bread held out toward him, accompanied by a glance of encouraging blue eyes urging him to accept this extra gift.

"But perhaps that is your own breakfast," he said. "No, I have had enough without payment. A thousand thanks, my gentle one."

There was no verbal reply, but the bread was pushed closer, as if impatient at his refusal. As the stranger's long dark eyes rested on the baby-like face, it seemed the girl was gathering more courage to meet his gaze.

"Ah, then, if I must take the bread," he said, placing his hand upon it, "I shall grow bolder still, and beg for another kiss to make the bread sweeter."

His speech was becoming wonderfully clear despite the strange timbre of his voice, which had initially seemed enough to make her cross herself. She blushed deeply and lifted the corner of her mantle to cover her mouth once more. Just as the presumptuous stranger leaned forward, his fingers brushing the arm holding the shielding mantle, he was startled by a harsh voice close behind his ear.

"Who are *you*—and murrain to you! No honest buyer, I'll warrant, but a hanger-on of the dice-gamblers—or something worse. Go! Dance off and find fitter company, or I'll give you a tune at a tempo you won't like!"

The young stranger drew back, meeting the speaker's glare with a look provocatively free of alarm or apology. A slight expression of saucy amusement broke into a broad, beaming smile as he surveyed the figure of his threatener. She was a stout, brawny woman wearing a man's jerkin over her green serge gown, with the peaked hood of a discarded mantle fastened around her sunburnt face. Beneath all coarseness and premature wrinkles, her face displayed a half-sad, half-ludicrous maternal resemblance to the tender baby-face of her daughter—a kind of likeness that often serves as a more terrifying prophecy than the grim skull of Death.

There was something irresistibly charming in that bright young smile, but Monna Ghita was not a woman to show weakness. Speaking with heightened exasperation, she continued:

"Yes, yes, you can grin as well as other monkeys in caps and jerkins. You're a minstrel or a mountebank, I'll be sworn; you look as silly as a tumbler who has been upside down and managed to land on his feet. What fool's tricks have you been playing, Tessa?" she added, turning to her daughter, whose frightened countenance seemed even more inviting to abuse. "Giving away milk and victuals, it seems? Ay, ay, you'd carry water in your ears for any idle vagabond who wouldn't stoop to pick it up, you silly staring rabbit! Turn your back and lift the herbs from the panniers, or I'll make you recite a few Hail Marys without counting!"

"Nay, Madonna," said the stranger with a pleading smile, "do not be angry with your pretty Tessa for showing pity to a hungry traveler who found himself unexpectedly without a copper coin. Your handsome face looks so striking when it frowns that I long to see it illuminated by a smile."

"_Va via!_" (_Go away!) "I know what paste you are made of. You may tickle me with that straw for a long while before I laugh, mark my words. Get along with a bad Easter, or I'll put a beauty spot or two on that face of yours that will ruin your kissing prospects before Advent."

As Monna Ghita raised her formidable hands to enforce her command, Bratti, who had arrived a moment earlier, remarked to his companion, "What do you think of this pretty parrot, Nello? Does his tongue not smell of Venice?"

"Nay, Bratti," whispered the barber, "your wisdom contains much of the ass, as I told you just now; especially regarding the ears. This stranger is a Greek, or else I am not the barber who has exclusively shaved the excellent Demetrio and extracted more than one sore tooth from his learned jaw. And this youth could easily be taken as having come straight from Olympus—at least since he received a touch of my razor."

"_Orsù!_ Monna Ghita!" continued Nello, delighted to see some sport, "what storm has caused this commotion? Has this young stranger behaved badly?"

"By San Giovanni!" said the cautious Bratti, still harboring his original suspicions about the shabbily-dressed man possessing fine jewelry, "he was right to flee from *me*, if he intended to get into trouble. I can swear I found him beneath the Loggia de' Cerchi, wearing a ring on his finger such as I have seen on Bernardo Rucellai himself. Not another rusty nail's worth do I know about him."

"The truth is," said Nello, eyeing the stranger good-naturedly, "this *bello giovane* has been a little too presumptuous in admiring the charms of Monna Ghita and attempted to kiss her while her daughter's back was turned. Observe how busy the pretty Tessa is; she cannot look this way at present. Was it not so, Messer?" Nello concluded in a tone of courtesy.

"You have divined the offense like a soothsayer," laughed the stranger. "Only that I had not the good fortune to encounter Monna Ghita immediately. I begged a cup of milk from her daughter and accepted this gift of bread, for which I was making a humble offering of gratitude, before enjoying the higher pleasure of facing these riper charms—which I confess I may have admired too boldly."

"_Va, va!_ Be off, every one of you, and stay in purgatory until I pay to extract you, will you?" shrieked Monna Ghita fiercely, elbowing Nello and driving her mule forward to force the stranger aside. "Tessa, thou simpleton, bring the mule forward; the cart is coming upon us."

As Tessa turned to take the mule's bridle, she cast one timid glance at the stranger, who was now moving with Nello out of the path of an approaching market cart. Her glance lasted just long enough to catch the beckoning movement of his hand, indicating he had been waiting for this chance to bid farewell.

"Ebbene," said Bratti, raising his voice to speak over the cart, "I leave you with Nello, young man, for I cannot push my bag and basket any farther and have business at home. Remember our bargain, for if you found Tessa without me, it was not my fault. Nello will show you my shop in the Ferravecchi, and I shall not turn my back on you."
