The Blind Scholar and his Daughter.

The Via de' Bardi, a thoroughfare etched into the annals of Florence, winds through Oltrarno, the district draped along the southern bank of the Arno. Stretching from the Ponte Vecchio to the Piazza de' Mozzi, it is flanked on the right by a steep ascent known in the fifteenth century as the Hill of Bogoli—a former stone quarry that supplied the city's pavement, now treacherous when soaked by rain. To the left, the river laps against a row of buildings with irregular, pierced façades, their reflections softened in the water as the sun dips toward the western horizon. Though quaint, these structures feel to the historical eye like a modern substitute for the legendary palaces of the Bardi family, razed by popular fury in the mid-fourteenth century.

Those Bardi were a proud, vigorous lineage, prominent among the early Florentine factions that clashed in the shadow of high noble towers. They possessed hands capable of gripping a sword with lethal force, yet also fingers attuned to the delicate pleasure of handling minted gold. By the mid-1300s, they had evolved from commoners into feudal Counts of Vernio, acquiring lands and strongholds that stirred the jealousy of their republican peers. Their ambition was matched by their prowess in commerce; akin to the Rothschilds of their era, they financed the wars of Edward III, only to be undone by a sudden demand from Sicilian creditors. Their bankruptcy sent shockwaves through the Mediterranean, yet unlike modern bankrupts, they did not hide in shame. Instead, they stood taller, becoming arrogant grandees who, in 1343, drew the wrath of the armed populace. They held their narrow street inlets like panthers at bay until an assault from the hill forced them to retreat. Their twenty-two houses were sacked and burned, and many of their chiefs fled the city. Yet, like a multi-rooted tree, the family survived, rising again and again through the vicissitudes of Florentine history, oscillating between dignity and disgrace, wealth and poverty. However, they never reclaimed their old street on the riverbank, which by 1492 belonged to others, notably the Neri.

Within one of these Neri houses, however, resided a descendant of the Bardi, specifically the branch that had once been Counts of Vernio. This descendant inherited the family's pride, energy, and desire to leave a lasting mark upon the swirling earth, yet his passions had shifted under new conditions. He was no longer a warrior or a daring merchant-general. He was a blind, elderly scholar, Bardo de' Bardi, whose hands were cramped from decades of copying manuscripts, who ate sparing meals, and wore threadbare clothes out of choice and necessity. He sat among his books and marble fragments, illuminated only by the distant glow of his memories, a moneyless man to whom Nello, the barber, had promised to introduce the young Greek, Tito Melema.

Bardo's home was situated near the hillside, a large, sombre mass of stone pierced by small windows and topped by a roofed terrace. Grim doors with scrolled hinges opened onto a groined entrance court, empty save for a massive lamp-iron hanging from the center. A smaller door led to a stone staircase and the ground-floor rooms, which served as a warehouse. Both floors were filled with precious stores destined for the Scheldt, the shores of Africa, the Aegean, or the Euxine. Maso, the old servant, had to climb slowly to the second storey to reach his master's door, where we enter shortly after Nello's conversation with the Greek.

We follow Maso into the ante-chamber and through the left-hand door. He nods briefly as a clear, young voice greets him: "Ah, you are come back, Maso. It is well. We have wanted nothing."

The voice emanates from the far end of a long, spacious room lined with shelves holding books and antiquities in scrupulous order. Scattered on separate stands are a beautiful feminine torso, a headless statue wielding a bladeless sword, severed infantine limbs inviting a kiss, well-preserved Roman busts, and vases from Magna Grecia. A large central table holds antique bronze lamps and dark pottery vessels. The room's palette is pale and sombre; the vellum bindings offer little relief to the marble, which is livid with age, and the once-splendid carpet is worn to dimness. The dark bronzes lack sunlight to reveal their green tinge, for the sun has not yet climbed high enough to send gleams through the narrow windows facing the Via de' Bardi.

The only splash of bright color comes from the hair of a tall maiden, seventeen or eighteen years old, standing before a carved reading-desk. Her hair is a reddish gold, enriched by an unbroken ripple reminiscent of sunset clouds on a grand autumn evening. Confined by a black fillet above her small ears, it ripples forward to veil her neck above a square-cut gown of black serge. Her eyes are fixed on a large volume before her; one white hand rests on the desk, the other clasping the back of her father's chair.

Her blind father sits with his head uplifted, turned slightly toward her as if gazing at her. His delicate paleness, framed by a black velvet cap atop his drooping white hair, highlights the striking resemblance between his aged features and those of the young maiden. Both lack the tinge of rose on their cheeks, sharing a refinement of brow and nostril, balanced by a full, firm mouth and a powerful chin that conveys proud tenacity and latent impetuosity. This expression is mirrored in the backward poise of the girl's head and the grand line of her neck and shoulders. One cannot tell if such a face inspires love or only an unwilling admiration mixed with dread; the answer lies in the eyes, which often seem charged with a direct message from the soul. But the father's eyes have long been silent, while the daughter's are bent on the Latin pages of Politian's 'Miscellanea,' reading aloud from the eightieth chapter:

"There was a certain nymph of Thebes named Chariclo, especially dear to Pallas; and this nymph was the mother of Teiresias. But once when in the heat of summer, Pallas, in company with Chariclo, was bathing her disrobed limbs in the Heliconian Hippocrene, it happened that Teiresias coming as a hunter to quench his thirst at the same fountain, inadvertently beheld Minerva unveiled, and immediately became blind. For it is declared in the Saturnian laws, that he who beholds the gods against their will, shall atone for it by a heavy penalty... When Teiresias had fallen into this calamity, Pallas, moved by the tears of Chariclo, endowed him with prophecy and length of days, and even caused his prudence and wisdom to continue after he had entered among the shades, so that an oracle spake from his tomb: and she gave him a staff, wherewith, as by a guide, he might walk without stumbling... And hence, Nonnus, in the fifth book of the 'Dionysiaca,' introduces Actreon exclaiming that he calls Teiresias happy, since, without dying, and with the loss of his eyesight merely, he had beheld Minerva unveiled, and thus, though blind, could for evermore carry her image in his soul."

At this point, the daughter's hand slips from the chair back to meet her father's, which he has just uplifted. She does not look around, continuing with a voice slightly altered by suppressed feeling to read the Greek quotation from Nonnus, when the old man interrupts.

"Stay, Romola; reach me my own copy of Nonnus. It is a more correct copy than any in Poliziano's hands, for I made emendations in it which have not yet been communicated to any man. I finished it in 1477, when my sight was fast failing me."

Romola walks to the far end of the room with a queenly step, natural to her tall, finely-wrought frame, requiring no conscious adjustment.

"Is it in the right place, Romola?" asks Bardo, perpetually seeking assurance that the outward fact corresponds with the image stored in his mind.

"Yes, father; at the west end of the room, on the third shelf from the bottom, behind the bust of Hadrian, above Apollonius Rhodius and Callimachus, and below Lucan and Silius Italicus."

As she speaks, a fine ear would detect a faint suggestion of weariness struggling with habitual patience in her clear voice. But as she approaches her father and sees his arms stretch out with nervous excitement to seize the volume, her hazel eyes fill with pity. She hastens to lay the book on his lap and kneels beside him, looking up as if believing her love must penetrate the darkness that shuts out everything else. At that moment, the doubtful attractiveness of Romola's face, where pride and passion quiver against native refinement and intelligence, is transfigured into lovable womanliness by mingled pity and affection. It is evident that the deepest fount of feeling within her has not yet reached her less changeful features, finding outlet only through her eyes.

The father, unaware of this soft radiance, looks flushed and agitated as his hand explores the edges and back of the large book.

"The vellum is yellowed in these thirteen years, Romola."

"Yes, father," she says gently; "but your letters at the back are dark and plain still—fine Roman letters; and the Greek character," she continues, opening the book on his knee, "is more beautiful than that of any of your bought manuscripts."

"Assuredly, child," says Bardo, passing his finger across the page as if hoping to distinguish line and margin. "What hired amanuensis can be equal to the scribe who loves the words that grow under his hand, and to whom an error or indistinctness in the text is more painful than a sudden darkness or obstacle across his path? And even these mechanical printers who threaten to make learning a base and vulgar thing—even they must depend on the manuscript over which we scholars have bent with that insight into the poet's meaning which is closely akin to the *mens divinior* of the poet himself; unless they would flood the world with grammatical falsities and inexplicable anomalies that would turn the very fountain of Parnassus into a deluge of poisonous mud. But find the passage in the fifth book, to which Poliziano refers—I know it very well."

Seating herself on a low stool close to her father's knee, Romola takes the book on her lap and reads the four verses containing Actreon's exclamation.

"It is true, Romola," says Bardo when she finishes; "it is a true conception of the poet; for what is that grosser, narrower light by which men behold merely the petty scene around them, compared with that far-stretching, lasting light which spreads over centuries of thought, and over the life of nations, and makes clear to us the minds of the immortals who have reaped the great harvest and left us to glean in their furrows? For me, Romola, even when I could see, it was with the great dead that I lived; while the living often seemed to me mere spectres—shadows dispossessed of true feeling and intelligence; and unlike those Lamiae, to whom Poliziano, with that superficial ingenuity which I do not deny to him, compares our inquisitive Florentines, because they put on their eyes when they went abroad, and took them off when they got home again, I have returned from the converse of the streets as from a forgotten dream, and have sat down among my books, saying with Petrarca, the modern who is least unworthy to be named after the ancients, 'Libri medullitus delectant, colloquuntur, consulunt, et viva quadam nobis atque arguta familiaritate junguntur.'"

"And in one thing you are happier than your favourite Petrarca, father," says Romola, affectionately humouring the old man's disposition to dilate; "for he used to look at his copy of Homer and think sadly that the Greek was a dead letter to him: so far, he had the inward blindness that you feel is worse than your outward blindness."

"True, child; for I carry within me the fruits of that fervid study which I gave to the Greek tongue under the teaching of the younger Crisolora, and Filelfo, and Argiropulo; though that great work in which I had desired to gather, as into a firm web, all the threads that my research had laboriously disentangled, and which would have been the vintage of my life, was cut off by the failure of my sight and my want of a fitting coadjutor. For the sustained zeal and unconquerable patience demanded from those who would tread the unbeaten paths of knowledge are still less reconcilable with the wandering, vagrant propensity of the feminine mind than with the feeble powers of the feminine body."

"Father," says Romola, with a sudden flush and an injured tone, "I read anything you wish me to read; and I will look out any passages for you, and make whatever notes you want."

Bardo shakes his head, smiling with a bitter sort of pity. "As well try to be a pentathlos and perform all the five feats of the palaestra with the limbs of a nymph. Have I forgotten thy fainting in the mere search for the references I needed to explain a single passage of Callimachus?"

"But, father, it was the weight of the books, and Maso can help me; it was not want of attention and patience."

Bardo shakes his head again. "It is not mere bodily organs that I want: it is the sharp edge of a young mind to pierce the way for my somewhat blunted faculties. For blindness acts like a dam, sending the streams of thought backward along the already-travelled channels and hindering the course onward. If my son had not forsaken me, deluded by debasing fanatical dreams, worthy only of an energumen whose dwelling is among tombs, I might have gone on and seen my path broadening to the end of my life; for he was a youth of great promise.
