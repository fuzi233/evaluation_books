At the sight of the room, pristine and untouched save for the violent breach of our entry, a sudden chill of relief washed over us. We quickly regained our composure, dismissing the men who had assisted us. It dawned on Mademoiselle that Carmilla, startled by the clamor at her threshold, might have fled her bed in a primal panic, retreating into the depths of a wardrobe or behind a heavy drape. From such concealments, she could not emerge until the majordomo and his minions had departed. We resumed our frantic search, calling her name with desperate urgency, but the silence remained absolute. Our confusion curdled into agitation. We inspected every window; they were bolted shut. I pleaded with Carmilla, imploring her to cease this cruel game, to reveal herself and end our torment. It was futile. By then, I was convinced she was nowhere in the chamber, nor in the adjoining dressing room, whose door remained locked from within. She could not have slipped past it.

A cold dread seized me. Had Carmilla discovered one of the labyrinthine secret passages whispered about by the old housekeeper, routes whose precise locations had been lost to time? Only daylight could unravel this knot, yet for now, we were paralyzed by uncertainty. Past four o'clock, and I resolved to spend the encroaching darkness in Madame's room, hoping the morning would offer clarity.

Daybreak brought no answers. The entire household, led by my distraught father, descended into a feverish state of alarm. Every corner of the château was ransacked; the grounds were scoured. No trace of the missing lady appeared. The stream was prepared to be dragged; my father stood on the brink of collapse, terrified of the nightmare he would have to recount to the grieving mother upon her return. I, too, was nearly undone, though my anguish stemmed from a different, sharper terror. The morning dissolved into a haze of suspense.

Then, the clock struck one. Still, no news. In a surge of instinct, I rushed to Carmilla's room. She stood before her dressing table, bathed in the pale morning light. I was stunned, unable to reconcile the vision with reality. She beckoned me with a delicate finger, silent and commanding. Her face wore an expression of profound, raw fear. Overcome by an ecstatic release, I threw myself at her, kissing and embracing her repeatedly. I scrambled to the bell, ringing it violently to summon others to share the burden of my father's worry.

"My dear Carmilla, what has become of you?" I gasped, tears blurring my sight. "We have suffered agonies! Where have you been? How did you return?"

"Last night was a night of wonders," she replied, her voice trembling. "For mercy's sake, explain yourself."

"It was past two," she continued, her eyes wide with confusion, "when I retired to my bed as usual, both doors locked—the bedroom and that leading to the gallery. My sleep was uninterrupted, dreamless, until I awoke just now on the sofa in the dressing room. I found the door between the rooms ajar and the outer door forced. How could this happen without waking me? Such commotion must have been deafening, and I am notoriously sensitive to the slightest disturbance. And how could I have been carried from my bed without my slumber breaking? Even the faintest stir startles me!"

By then, Madame, Mademoiselle, my father, and a throng of servants filled the room. Carmilla was besieged by inquiries, congratulations, and warm embraces, yet she seemed the least capable of explaining the impossible. My father paced the floor, his brow furrowed in deep thought. I caught Carmilla's eye following him, tracking his movements with a sly, dark glint that unsettled me.

When the servants were finally dismissed, and Mademoiselle had sought a vial of valerian and sal volatile, leaving only my father, Madame, and myself with Carmilla, my father approached her gently. He took her hand, guided her to the sofa, and sat beside her.

"Will you forgive me, my dear, if I risk a conjecture and pose a question?" he asked softly.

"Who possesses a better right?" she replied immediately. "Ask whatever you wish, and I shall tell you everything. Yet my story is one of sheer bewilderment and darkness. I know absolutely nothing. Question me freely, but remember the strict limitations Mother has imposed regarding certain topics."

"Quite so, my dear child. I need not tread upon ground where silence is demanded. The marvel of last night lies in your removal from your bed and room without awakening, occurring while the windows were secured and the interior doors fastened. Let me offer my theory and ask a single question."

Carmilla leaned on her hand, dejected; Madame and I listened, breath held tight.

"Now, my question: Have you ever been suspected of somnambulism?"

"Never, since I was very young," she answered.

"But you *did* walk in your sleep when you were young?"

"Yes; I know I did. My old nurse told me so countless times."

My father smiled, nodding knowingly. "Very well. Here is what transpired. You arose in your sleep, unlocking the door—not leaving the key in the lock as custom dictates, but removing it and securing it from the outside. You took the key with you, carrying it to one of the twenty-five rooms on this floor, or perhaps ascending or descending stairs. This old house is a labyrinth of chambers, closets, and accumulated lumber; it would take a week to search it thoroughly. Do you see now?"

"I understand partially, but not fully," she admitted. "And how do you account for my discovery on the sofa in the dressing room, which we had already searched so meticulously?"

"She came there after you had finished searching, still asleep. Eventually, she awoke spontaneously, just as surprised to find herself there as any of us." My father laughed, a sound of pure relief. "Let us congratulate ourselves. The most natural explanation requires no drugging, no tampering with locks, no burglars, poisoners, or witches—nothing to alarm Carmilla or anyone else concerning our safety."

Carmilla looked radiant, her beauty seemingly enhanced by a graceful languor unique to her. I sensed my father silently comparing her features to my own, for he sighed, saying, "I wish my poor Laura looked more like herself."

Thus, our alarms were happily quelled, and Carmilla was restored to her circle of friends, the mystery dissolved by the simplest of explanations.
