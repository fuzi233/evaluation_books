Feversham had taken the night mail for Dublin after that ride with Durrance in the Row, and, before the next day had finished turning, he had crossed Lough Swilly on a squat little cargo steamer that smelt of tar, wet rope, and salt fish. Once a week it nosed its way up the Lennon River as far as Ramelton, pushing through water that held the colour of peat and old stories. The engine beat a steady iron pulse, but it was the river’s own voice he noticed most—the soft scold of current against piling, the slap where eddies worried the stones—as if the place had its music ready before he arrived.

On the quay Ethne waited in a dog-cart. She gave him her hand, and with it a smile that belonged to comrades who had shared weather and worry.

“You are surprised to see me,” she said, catching the look he tried to hide.

“I always am,” he answered. “For always you exceed my thoughts of you.”

Her smile altered—no longer merely comradeship; it warmed, and it wounded.

“I shall drive slowly,” she said when his traps were stowed. “I brought no groom on purpose. There will be guests to-morrow. We have only to-day.”

They went along the wide causeway by the riverside, and turned up the steep, narrow street. Feversham sat quiet beside her, letting his eyes take the town in with a curious, possessive pride, because this drowsy little place—its grey bridge, the dark thicket of trees climbing the far bank, the water’s continual singing above the shallows—was part of her daily life. Ramelton lay as if listening to itself: cartwheels were few, voices softened by habit; even a door-latch, lifted and dropped, seemed to have its proper note.

Ethne at twenty-one was tall and strong, supple as a young ash, square in the shoulders without losing grace. She moved with a deer’s lightness, hair dark brown and coiled low at her neck, cheeks bright with health as if the wind had seasoned her. Her clear grey eyes met other eyes frankly, as though she despised the little tricks of looking away. Her nature answered her looks: honest, simple with the straightforward simplicity of strength that can afford gentleness and has no need of violence. People in Ramelton still told, with a shiver of admiration, how once a horse bolted down that very steep hill at the clatter of a pail. The reins had fallen; she sat for a breath helpless. Then, cool as if measuring flour, she climbed over the front of the dog-cart as it pitched and raced, balanced along the shafts, reached the reins at the horse’s neck, and stopped it ten yards from the sharp curve above the bridge. There were thorns in her courage, too, though Feversham had not yet pricked his fingers on them.

For the first part of the drive she was almost as silent as he, and when she spoke it was with an absent air, as though a larger matter paced her thoughts. Not until they were clear of the town and out upon the undulating road towards Letterkenny did she turn quickly, as if she could bear it no longer, and say:

“I saw this morning that your regiment was ordered from India to Egypt. You could have gone with it, had I not come in your way. There would have been chances of distinction. I have hindered you, and I am very sorry. Of course you could not know there was any possibility—still, I can understand it is hard to be left behind. I blame myself.”

He stared at the road ahead until the hedgerows blurred. Then, in a voice that rasped as though something had caught in his throat:

“You need not.”

“How can I help it?” she went on. “I blame myself the more because I do not see things quite like other women. If you had gone to Egypt, and the worst had happened, I should have been lonely, of course, all my days—but I should have known quite surely that when those days were over, you and I would see much of one another.”

She spoke as evenly as if stating the time of tide. The sentence, simple as bread, struck him like a blade. He caught his breath, and sat rigid, keeping his face smooth by force. Her eyes were on him, waiting, confident that his first word would set all right.

“You see,” she said, “I can put up with the absence of the people I care about a little better, perhaps, than most. I do not feel I have lost them at all.” She hunted for her phrase, as if her thought were something delicate that might break if handled roughly. “You know how things happen. One goes along in a dull sort of way, and then suddenly a face springs out from the crowd of one’s acquaintances, and you know it at once—certainly—for the face of a friend. Rather, you recognise it though you have never seen it before. It is almost as though you had come upon some one long looked for and now gladly recovered. Such friends—they are few, no doubt, but only the few really count—such friends one does not lose, whether they are absent, or even—dead.”

“Unless,” he said slowly, “one has made a mistake. Suppose the face in the crowd is a mask—what then? One may make mistakes.”

She shook her head, decisive as a knife coming down on a board.

“Of that kind, no. One may seem to have made mistakes, perhaps for a long while. But in the end one would be proved not to have made them.”

Her faith took hold of him and tortured him. He could not keep his silence.

“Ethne,” he cried, “you don’t know—”

But she reined in, laughed, and pointed with her whip.

At the top of a hill, a couple of miles from Ramelton, the road ran between stone walls. On the left were open fields; on the right a wood of oak and beech held a cool, leaf-filtered darkness. Built into the left-hand wall was a scarlet letter-box, its paint bright as a berry.

“I wanted to show you that,” she interrupted. “It was there I used to post my letters to you during the anxious times.”

And his chance of speech slipped away like a fish under shadow.

“The house is behind the trees to the right,” she added.

“The letter-box is very convenient,” he said.

“Yes,” she answered, and drove on. A little farther the park wall had crumbled, and she stopped again.

“That’s where I used to climb over to post the letters. There’s a tree on the other side as convenient as the letter-box. I used to run down the half-mile of avenue at night.”

“There might have been thieves,” he exclaimed.

“There were thorns,” she said, as if that were the more practical danger.

They turned through the gates and drove up to the porch of a long, irregular grey house that seemed to have grown, like a family, by additions and accidents. Ethne flicked the reins lightly.

“Well, we have still a day before the dance.”

“I suppose the whole countryside is coming,” he said.

“It daren’t do anything else,” she laughed. “My father would send the police to fetch them if they stayed away—just as he fetched your friend Mr. Durrance here. By the way, Mr. Durrance has sent me a present—a Guarnerius violin.”

The door opened and a thin, lank old man came out upon the steps. His face was fierce and peaked, like a bird of prey’s; yet at the sight of Feversham it softened into friendliness. A stranger might have thought him winking, but in truth his left eyelid drooped continually, giving him a look of perpetual irony.

“How do you do?” he said briskly. “Glad to see you. Must make yourself at home. If you want any whiskey, stamp twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand.” And he went straight back into the house as if the matter were settled.

Dermod Eustace, master of Lennon House, was already becoming legend while still hardly cold in the future grave of which people spoke with an almost proprietary certainty. Facts, however, held firm beneath the barnacles of invention. He practised an ancient, tyrannous hospitality on the road to Letterkenny—forced bed and board on strangers and acquaintances alike, as Durrance had once discovered. He belonged to another century, and glowered at the new one with anger, and could only be reconciled to it after much alcohol. He was like an intoxicated Coriolanus, believing people should be shepherded with a stick, yet never forgetting his manners even to the lowliest woman. Ramelton townsfolk said with pride that even at his worst—galloping down the steep cobbled streets on a big white mare, his inseparable collie trotting beside him, swaying with drink yet by miracle keeping his saddle—he had never ridden down any one except a man. Two further truths were known: he was rather afraid of his daughter, who wisely kept him uncertain whether she disapproved of him or not; and he had conceived a great liking for Harry Feversham.

Harry saw little of him that day. Dermod retired into the room he called his office, and Feversham and Ethne spent the afternoon fishing for salmon in the Lennon. The afternoon lay with a Sabbath stillness; even the birds seemed to hush as if the air itself were listening. From the house the lawns fell steeply, shaded by trees and dappled with sun, into a valley where the river ran swift and black under overarching boughs. There was a fall where water slid over rocks with such unbroken smoothness it looked solid, save where one sharp spur lifted and the river broke back on itself in an amber wave lit through by the sun.

Opposite that spur they sat a long while, sometimes speaking, but most often listening—the roar of water, the softer shiver of leaves, the distant, domestic clink from the house as if some kitchen door had been shut, some utensil laid down, ordinary life keeping time to the river’s loud insistence. The soundscape wrapped them; it soothed, and it made his thoughts, for once, less clamorous. Sunset came; shadows lengthened like slow hands. They stood, looked at each other, smiled, and walked back.

It was an afternoon he would remember with a pain sharpened by its calm, for the next night was the night of the dance.

As the band struck the opening bars of the fourth waltz, Ethne left her post at the drawing-room door and, taking Feversham’s arm, led him out into the hall. The hall was empty. The front door stood open to the cool summer night, and the garden’s trees whispered to one another, their speech threaded with the far-off pulse of crickets. From the ballroom came the swaying lilt of music, the beat of feet on boards, laughter riding on the strings. It was all sound and breath and human bodies in motion—life made audible.

Ethne drew a breath of relief at her reprieve from duty, then dropped his arm and crossed to a side table.

“The post is in,” she said. “There are letters—one, two, three—for you, and a little box.”

She held it out: a small white jeweller’s cardboard box. The instant it touched her hand she frowned.

“It must be empty,” she said.

Yet it was carefully sealed and tied. Feversham broke the seals, unfastened the string. He looked at the address; it had been forwarded from his lodgings, and he did not know the hand.

“There is some mistake,” he said, and lifted the lid.

Three white feathers fluttered out, rocked and swayed for a moment in the lamplight, and then drifted down one after another to the dark polished boards. They lay like flakes of snow. But they were not whiter than Harry Feversham’s cheeks. He stood staring at them until he felt a light touch upon his arm—Ethne’s gloved hand on his sleeve.

“What does it mean?” she asked. There was perplexity in her voice, no more than perplexity; her smile and her loyal confidence seemed to promise that his first word would clear the air. “What does it mean?”

“That there are things which cannot be hid, I suppose,” he answered.

She did not speak at once. The music floated languorously into the hall; the garden’s trees whispered through the open door, their sound clean and living beside the band’s glossy strain. Then she shook his arm gently, gave a breathless little laugh, and spoke as if pleading with a child.

“I don’t think you understand, Harry. Here are three white feathers. They were sent to you in jest—oh, of course in jest. But it is a cruel kind of jest—”

“They were sent in deadly earnest.”

He looked straight into her eyes as he spoke. Ethne’s hand fell from his sleeve.

“Who sent them?” she asked.

He had not thought of that. The message was everything; the senders were nothing. But Ethne reached out, took the box, and turned it over. Three visiting cards lay at the bottom. She drew them out and read aloud:

“Captain Trench, Mr. Castleton, Mr. Willoughby. Do you know these men?”

“All three are officers of my old regiment.”

For a moment she seemed dazed, as if the words had changed the room’s air. She knelt and gathered the feathers into her hand—perhaps thinking that to touch them would make them comprehensible. They lay on the palm of her white glove. She blew gently, and they rose and hung fluttering, rocking like small, indecisive birds. As they drifted down she caught them again, and slowly, as if feeling her way by touch, she asked:

“Were they justly sent?”

“Yes,” said Harry Feversham.

He did not think of denial. The dreaded thing, the thing he had anticipated for years with a sick foreknowledge, had happened. He was known for a coward. That word—long blazing across the wall of his thoughts—was now written in public places. He stood as though again before the portraits of his fathers, mute under sentence. It was the girl who denied it. Still kneeling, she said:

“I do not believe that is true. You could not look me in the face so steadily were it true. Your eyes would seek the floor, not mine.”

“Yet it is true.”

“Three little white feathers,” she said slowly; then with a sob tightening her throat, “This afternoon we were under the elms down by the Lennon River—do you remember, Harry?—just you and I. And then come three little white feathers, and the world’s at an end.”

“Oh, don’t!” he cried, and his voice broke. Until then he had spoken steadily, as steadily as his eyes. But her words pulled up the image of the river and the elms, the roar and hush of water, the whole clean, listening afternoon—its simple, domestic peace—and set it against these small accusatory things. It caught him by the heart.

Ethne, however, seemed not to hear the appeal. She was listening with her face turned toward the ballroom. Chatter and laughter grew louder and nearer. She understood the music had ceased. She rose quickly, clenching the feathers in her hand, opened a door—her sitting-room door.

“Come,” she said.

He followed her. She closed the door, shutting out the noise as one shuts a lid on boiling.

“Now,” she said, “will you tell me, if you please, why the feathers have been sent?”

She stood quietly before him. Her face was pale, but her expression showed little beyond a desire—almost a determination—to reach the truth. Her voice held the same quiet.

He answered directly, without softening.

“A telegram came. It was sent by Castleton. It reached me when Captain Trench and Mr. Willoughby were dining with me. It told me my regiment would be ordered on active service in Egypt. Castleton was dining with a man likely to know; I did not question the message. He told me to tell Trench. I did not. I thought it over with the telegram in front of me. Castleton was leaving that night for Scotland, and would go straight from Scotland to rejoin the regiment. He would not see Trench for weeks, and by then the telegram would likely be forgotten or its date confused. I did not tell Trench. I threw the telegram into the fire, and that night sent in my papers. But Trench found out somehow. Durrance was at dinner too—good God, Durrance!” he broke out suddenly. “Most likely he knows like the rest.”

It struck him, sharp and strange, that Durrance—who had once looked up to him—would likely count him a thing of scorn. But Ethne’s voice cut through that thought. What did Durrance matter, what did anyone matter, now that she knew?

“And is this all?” she asked.

“Surely it is enough,” he said.

“I think not,” she answered. Her voice lowered a little. “We agreed, didn’t we, that no foolish misunderstandings should ever come between us? We were to be frank, and to take frankness each from the other without offence. So be frank with me. Please.” There was pleading now. “I could, I think, claim it as a right. At all events I ask for it as I shall never ask for anything else in all my life.”

There was an explanation, he remembered—such as it was—but it seemed futile beside the consequence. Ethne had unclenched her hand. The three feathers lay upon the table between them, small and immaculate, impossible to reason with. He wore cowardice like a label. Besides, he could never make her understand; there were parts of fear that had no honest vocabulary. Yet she asked, and she had the right.

So he gathered his wits and spoke warily, because there was something he must still hide—for her sake, he told himself, at all costs.

“All my life I have been afraid that some day I should play the coward, and from the very first I knew I was destined for the army. I kept my fear to myself. There was no one to tell it to. My mother was dead, and my father—” He stopped, drew in a deep breath, and saw with painful clarity his father sitting at that moment in his mother’s favourite seat upon the terrace, looking over moonlit fields towards the Sussex Downs, dreaming of honours his son might win in Egypt—honours worthy of the Fevershams. The thought of the blow he had dealt that stern old heart made him bend forward and groan aloud.

“My father,” he resumed, “would, nay, could, never have understood. I know him. When danger came his way, it found him ready; but he did not foresee. That was always my trouble—I foresaw. Any peril to be encountered, any risk to be run, I foresaw them. And I foresaw something else. My father used to talk in his matter-of-fact way of the hours of waiting before the commencement of a battle, after the troops had been paraded. The mere anticipation of the suspense and strain of those hours was torture to me. I foresaw the possibility of cowardice. Then one evening, when my father had his old friends about him on one of his Crimean nights, two dreadful stories were told—one of an officer, the other of a surgeon—who had both shirked. I was confronted with the fact of cowardice. I took those stories to bed with me. They never left my memory; they became a part of me. I saw myself behaving now as one, now as the other, perhaps in the crisis of a battle bringing ruin on my country, certainly dishonouring my father and all the dead men whose portraits hung ranged in the hall. I tried to get the best of my fears. I hunted—but with a map of the country-side in my mind. I foresaw every hedge, every pit, every treacherous bank.”

“Yet you rode straight,” Ethne interrupted quickly. “Mr. Durrance told me so.”

“Did I?” he said vaguely. “Perhaps I did, once the hounds were off. Durrance never knew what the moments of waiting before the coverts were drawn meant to me. So when this telegram came, I took the chance it seemed to offer and resigned.”

He ended. He had spoken, but not wholly; his carefulness left a shape unfilled, a silence like an unspoken dialect between them. At once she suspected it.

“Were you afraid, too, of disgracing me?” she asked. “Was I in any way the cause that you resigned?”

He looked her in the eyes and lied.

“No.”

“If you had not been engaged to me, you would still have sent in your papers?”

“Yes.”

Slowly Ethne stripped a glove from her hand. Feversham turned away, as though the small sound of leather over skin accused him.

“I think I am rather like your father,” she said. “I don’t understand.”

In the silence that followed he heard something whirr and rattle softly on the table. He looked and saw she had slipped her engagement ring from her finger. It lay there, the stones winking as if trying to speak a language he could not bear.

“And all this—all that you have told me,” she exclaimed suddenly, her face stern now, “you would have hidden from me? You would have married me and hidden it, had not these three feathers come?”

She had held the words back from the beginning, giving him every chance, still hoping—by some miracle—there might be an explanation that would restore him in her mind. Now she struck and laid bare the worst of his disloyalty. He flinched and did not answer; his silence consented.

Ethne, however, was just; she was curious too, wanting the very bottom of the matter before she thrust it away into the back of memory.

“But yesterday,” she said, “you were going to tell me something. I stopped you to point out the letter-box.” She laughed, queerly empty. “Was it about the feathers?”

“Yes,” he answered wearily. What did these questions matter, since her ring lay there and the feathers had come? “Yes. I think what you were saying rather compelled me.”

“I remember,” she said, cutting him off as if afraid of where the memory might lead, “about seeing much of one another—afterwards. We will not speak of such things again.”

He swayed slightly, as though the room had shifted under his feet.

“I remember, too, you said one could make mistakes. You were right; I was wrong. One can do more than seem to make them.” She drew a breath, held her voice steady. “Will you, if you please, take back your ring?”

He picked it up and held it in his palm, very still. He had never cared for her so much; never seen her value so thoroughly as now, at the moment of losing her. In the quiet room she seemed to gleam—bright flowers in her hair, the fall of her dress, the white slipper on her foot—as if she belonged to some older, truer order of things, one not easily translated into his own broken speech. It seemed incredible he had ever won her; more incredible that he had lost her by disloyalty.

Then her voice came again.

“These, too, are yours. Will you take them, please?”

She pointed with her fan to the feathers on the table. He reached out obediently—and drew his hand back, startled.

“There are four,” he said.

Ethne did not answer. He looked at the fan and understood. It was ivory and white feathers. She had broken off one of its feathers and added it—quietly, deliberately—on her own account to the three.
