Mrs. Haller at Home

Rebecca the mare did not slacken her pace; instead, she seemed to gather new momentum, thundering down the lane toward Baymouth. Pen, riding with the reckless abandon of a boy who believes he is invincible, put her up at the inn stables and bolted directly to Mr. Foker's lodging. These apartments sat precariously over a chemist's shop, where the stock of cigars and soda-water vanished with alarming speed, courtesy of his youthful tenants.

Upon arriving, Pen discovered only Mr. Spavin, Foker's friend and co-owner of the tandem carriage that had recently ploughed through Chatteris. Spavin was smoking a cigar with the air of a man who had conquered the world, while simultaneously teaching a diminutive terrier tricks with a scrap of biscuit.

The contrast was jarring. Pen's face was flushed a vibrant, healthy red from the gallop, glowing with vitality. Beside him, Spavin's features were waxy, pale, and distinctly debauched. Spavin glanced up, his eyes narrowing. "Who's that man?" he muttered internally, comparing Pen's robust energy to his own lethargy. "He looks as fresh as a bean. His hand doesn't shake in the morning. I'd bet five to one."

"Mr. Pendennis!" Spavin announced, peering at the card Pen produced with some pride. "A fair tap of ale, I assure you."

Having failed to find Foker, who was nowhere to be seen, Pen felt a sudden, hollow disappointment. He walked down to the beach, biting his nails as he stared at the vast, bright expanse of the sea. The blue waters rolled into the bay, foaming and roaring hoarsely. Pen looked at them with blank, unfocused eyes, hardly registering the noise. Inside, however, a tidal wave of frustration was crashing against his mind, and he possessed no power to hold it back. He flung stones into the surf, but the ocean kept coming. Rage at not seeing Foker boiled within him. He *must* see Foker.

"Suppose I go on," Pen thought, "along the Chatteris road, just to catch him."

Rebecca was saddled within the hour. Galloping along the grass verge of the Chatteris road, Pen reached the point where the Clavering branch usually appeared. Naturally, the horse turned, but Pen cut her sharply over the shoulder, forcing her onto the turnpike. He rode on, scanning the horizon for the black tandem and its distinctive red wheels, finding nothing but empty road.

At the turnpike, logic suggested continuing. So, Pen rode to the George Inn. The hostler confirmed that Mr. Foker was indeed present, having made a "tremendous row" the previous night—drinking, singing, and attempting to fight a postboy, though, according to the hostler, likely losing that bout. With a satirical grin directed at Foker's domestic, Stoopid, the hostler summoned Pen.

Stoopid emerged carrying Foker's immaculately brushed clothes, looking grave despite the chaos. "Show Mr. Pendennis up to 'un."

Inside the apartment, amidst an immense feather bed and towering bolsters, Mr. Harry Foker lay reposing. Only a sallow face and a red silk nightcap peeked out from the mountain of pillows.

"Hullo!" cried Pen.

"Who goes there? Brother, quickly tell!" sang out the voice from the depths. "What! Pendennis again? Is your Mamma acquainted with your absence? Did you sup with us last night? No stop—who supped with us last night, Stoopid?"

"There was the three officers, sir, and Mr. Bingley, sir, and Mr. Costigan, sir," Stoopid replied with perfect, unblinking gravity.

"Ay, the cup and merry jest went round," Foker chorused, yawning and throwing his lean arms over his head. "We chanted. I remember I wanted to fight a postboy. Did I thrash him, Stoopid?"

"No, sir. Fight didn't come off, sir."

"Do it some other day." Foker yawned again. "But there was chanting. Bingley chanted, I chanted, the General chanted—Costigan I mean. Did you ever hear him sing 'The Little Pig under the Bed,' Pen?"

"The man we met yesterday," Pen stammered, trembling slightly, "the father of—"

"Of the Fotheringay—the very man. Ain't she a Venus, Pen?"

"Please sir, Mr. Costigan's in the sitting-room, sir," Stoopid interrupted, pointing the way. "Says you asked him to breakfast. Called five times, sir, but wouldn't wake you on no account. Has been here since eleven."

"How much is it now?"

"One, sir."

"What would the best of mothers say," cried the little sluggard, "if she saw me in bed at this hour? She sent me down here with a grinder. She wants me to cultivate my neglected genus." Foker burst into a boyish laugh, then beckoned Pen to follow him into the sitting room to talk to the General while he dressed. "Ask him to sing you 'The Little Pig under the Bed'; it's capital."

Pen fled in great perturbation to meet Mr. Costigan. Meanwhile, Foker began his toilette.

To understand Foker, one must look to his lineage. One grandfather was a brewer, the other an Earl, both sources of immense wealth. The Fokers had attended Cistercian School from father to son. There, young Foker had been dreadfully bullied for his trade, his uncomely countenance, his inaptitude for learning, and his gluttony. But freedom from school transformed him. Within months, he blossomed from a silent sneak into the humorous, sarcastic, brilliant Foker who now inhabited the room. Though a dunce by academic standards, he was now a dandy, scented and arrayed in fine linen, perfectly splendid.

In the sitting room, General—or Captain, as he preferred—Costigan sat by the window with a newspaper held at arm's length. His eyes were dim, and he spelled the words with his lips, a rare and difficult occupation for a man whose brain seemed permanently muzzy. His hat was cocked jauntily on one ear, revealing a quantity of lank, iron-grey hair that fell over his pate. His boots were shabby, a testament to poverty attacking the extremities first. Whisky had spoiled whatever complexion he once possessed, lending his face a copper tinge. He wore a high stock, scarred and stained, and a dress coat tightly buttoned where the fabric hadn't surrendered to wear.

As soon as he saw Pen, the Captain descended from the window seat. He saluted with a military flourish, conveying a couple of fingers covered by a broken black glove to his hat before removing it altogether.

"The young gentleman to whom I had the honour to be introduced yesterday in the Cathedral Yard," the Captain said, offering a splendid bow. "I hope I see you well, sir. I marked ye in the theatre last night during me daughter's performance; and missed ye on my return." He recounted the jollity of the night, pulling out an old snuff-box to present to his new acquaintance.

Arthur Pendennis was flurried. This shabby-looking buck was her father. Perfumed with the recollection of last night's cigars, the Captain twisted the tuft on his chin as jauntily as any young dandy.

"I hope, Miss F--, Miss Costigan is well, sir," Pen stammered, flushing. "She—she gave me greater pleasure, than—than I—I—I ever enjoyed at a play. I think, sir—I think she's the finest actress in the world," he gasped.

"Your hand, young man! For ye speak from your heart," cried the Captain. "Thank ye, sir, an old soldier and a fond father thanks ye. She is the finest actress in the world. I've seen the Siddons, sir, and the O'Nale—they were great, but what were they compared to Miss Fotheringay? I do not wish she should assume her own name while on the stage. Me family, sir, are proud people; and the Costigans of Costiganstown think that an honest man, who has borne Her Majesty's colours in the Hundred and Third, would demean himself, by permitting his daughter to earn her old father's bread."

"There cannot be a more honourable duty, surely," Pen agreed.

"Honourable! Bedad, sir, I'd like to see the man who said Jack Costigan would consent to anything dishonourable." The Captain's voice dropped to a pathetic whisper. "I have a heart, sir, though I am poor; I like a man who has a heart. You have: I read it in your honest face and steady eye. And would you believe it"? he added, "that that Bingley who has made his fortune by me child, gives her but two guineas a week: out of which she finds herself in dresses, and which, added to me own small means, makes our all?"
