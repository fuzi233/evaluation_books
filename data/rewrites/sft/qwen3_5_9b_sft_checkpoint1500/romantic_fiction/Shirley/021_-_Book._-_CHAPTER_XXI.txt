Mrs. Pryor.

As Shirley conversed with Moore, Caroline ascended the stairs to rejoin Mrs. Pryor. She discovered the older lady in a state of profound despondency. While Mrs. Pryor refused to admit that Miss Keeldar's precipitous actions had wounded her pride, it was undeniably clear that an internal grievance gnawed at her spirit. To anyone lacking a sympathetic nature, she might have appeared immune to the quiet, tender attentions with which Miss Helstone endeavored to offer solace; yet Caroline, perceptive of her friend's hidden depths, knew that although Mrs. Pryor appeared unmoved or barely stirred, she felt, treasured, and was ultimately healed by these gestures.

"At last," Mrs. Pryor confessed, "I find myself deficient in self-confidence and decision. These qualities have always eluded me. Nevertheless, I believe Miss Keeldar should have known my character sufficiently by now to realize that I invariably feel a painful urgency to do what is right, to act for the best. The unprecedented nature of the demand upon my judgment perplexed me, particularly following the terrors of the night. I could not venture to act promptly for another, but I trust no serious harm will result from my lapse of firmness."

A gentle knock interrupted her reflection. The door swung open halfway.

"Caroline, come here," a low voice commanded.

Miss Helstone stepped out. Standing in the gallery was Shirley, appearing contrite, ashamed, and sorrowful as any penitent child.

"How fares Mrs. Pryor?" Shirley asked gently.

"A little out of spirits," Caroline replied.

"I have behaved most shamefully, most ungenerously, most ungratefully toward her," Shirley declared. "How insolent of me to turn upon her for what, in truth, was no fault—merely an excess of conscientiousness on her part. Yet I regret my error most sincerely. Please convey this to her and inquire if she will grant me forgiveness."

Caroline delivered the message with heartfelt delight. Mrs. Pryor rose and approached the door. Lacking the courage for dramatic scenes, which terrified all timid souls, she spoke haltingly, "Come in, my dear."

Shirley entered with some impetuosity, throwing her arms around her former governess. As she kissed her warmly, she insisted, "You know you *must* forgive me, Mrs. Pryor. I could not function at all if a misunderstanding persisted between us."

"I have nothing to forgive," Mrs. Pryor responded. "Let us pass it over now, if you will. The ultimate conclusion of this incident is that it proves, more plainly than ever, how unequal I am to certain crises."

That painful realization would remain etched in Mrs. Pryor's mind. No effort by Shirley or Caroline could erase it. She could forgive her offending pupil, but she could not forgive her innocent self.

Soon afterward, Miss Keeldar, destined to receive constant visitors throughout the morning, was summoned downstairs once more. The Rector arrived first, offering a lively welcome followed immediately by a livelier reprimand. He expected both, and, buoyed by high spirits, accepted them with equal grace.

During his brief visit, he completely forgot to inquire after his niece. The riot, the rioters, the mill, the magistrates, and the heiress had consumed all his thoughts, leaving no room for familial bonds. He recounted the roles he and the Curate had played in defending the Hollow.

"The vials of Pharisaical wrath will surely be emptied upon our heads for our share in this business," he proclaimed, "but I defy every calumniator. I was there solely to uphold the law, to fulfill my duty as a man and a Briton; characters I consider entirely compatible with those of the Priest and Levite in their highest sense. Your tenant, Moore," he continued, "has won my approval. A cooler commander I could not wish to see, nor one more determined. Furthermore, the man has demonstrated sound judgment and common sense—first, in being thoroughly prepared for the event that transpired, and secondly, when his well-coordinated plans secured success, in knowing how to wield his victory without abusing it. Some magistrates are now thoroughly frightened, and, like all cowards, display a tendency toward cruelty. Moore restrains them with admirable prudence. He has hitherto been unpopular in the neighborhood, but mark my words: the tide of public opinion will soon turn in his favor. People will discover they have misappreciated him and will hasten to remedy their error; and when he perceives the public disposed to acknowledge his merits, he will exhibit a more gracious demeanor than that with which he has hitherto favored us."

Mr. Helstone was on the verge of adding some half-jesting, half-serious warnings to Miss Keeldar regarding her rumored partiality for her talented tenant when a ringing bell announced another visitor. This newcomer appeared as a white-haired elderly gentleman with a truculent countenance and a disdainful eye—in short, their old acquaintance and the Rector's arch-enemy, Mr. Yorke. Instantly, the Priest and Levite seized their hats, offered the briefest of farewells to Miss Keeldar, and exchanged the sternest of nods with her guest before departing abruptly.

Mr. Yorke was in no mild mood, nor did he express his opinions on the night's transactions in measured terms. Moore, the magistrates, the soldiers, and the mob leaders each received a share of his invectives. However, he reserved his strongest epithets—and genuinely racy Yorkshire Doric adjectives—for the benefit of the "fighting parsons," labeling the Rector and Curate "sanguinary, demoniac." According to him, the cup of ecclesiastical guilt was now brimming to the rim.

"The church," he declared, "is in a bonny pickle now. It was time it came down when parsons took to swaggering among soldiers, blazing away with bullets and gunpowder, taking the lives of far honor-sturdier men than themselves."

"What would Moore have done if nobody had assisted him?" Shirley asked.

"Drunk as he'd brewed, eaten as he'd baked."

"This implies you would have left him alone to face that mob. Excellent! He possesses immense courage, but however vast the gallantry that ever garrisoned one human breast, it could scarce avail against two hundred."

"He had the soldiers, those poor slaves who hire out their own blood and spill other folk's for money."

"You abuse soldiers almost as much as you abuse clergymen. In your eyes, all who wear red coats are national refuse, and all who wear black are national swindlers. Mr. Moore, according to you, did wrong in securing military aid, and did still worse in accepting any other assistance. Your mode of speaking amounts to this: he should have abandoned his mill and his life to the rage of a set of misguided madmen, while Mr. Helstone and every other gentleman in the parish should have looked on, witnessed the building razed and its owner slaughtered, and never stirred a finger to save either."

"If Moore had behaved to his men from the beginning as a master ought to behave, they never would have entertained their present feelings toward him."

"Easy for you to talk," exclaimed Miss Keeldar, beginning to warm to her tenant's cause—"you, whose family has resided at Briarmains for six generations, to whose person the people have been accustomed for fifty years, who know all their ways, prejudices, and preferences—it is easy, indeed, for *you* to act in a manner that avoids offending them. But Mr. Moore arrived as a stranger to the district; he came here poor and friendless, with nothing but his own energies to back him, nothing but his honor, his talent, and his industry to forge his path. It is a monstrous crime indeed that, under such circumstances, he could not instantly popularize his naturally grave, quiet manners; could not be jocular, free, and cordial with a strange peasantry as you are with your fellow-townsmen! It is an unpardonable transgression that when he introduced improvements, he did not conduct the business in the most politic way, nor did he graduate his changes as delicately as a wealthy capitalist might! For errors of this sort, is he to become the victim of mob outrage? Is he to be denied even the privilege of defending himself? Are those who possess the hearts of men in their breasts (and Mr. Helstone, say what you will of him, possesses such a heart) to be reviled like malefactors simply because they stand by him, because they dare to espouse the cause of one against two hundred?"
