The church held its coolness like water in a stone jar. Sunlight fell in long, slanted bars through the painted glass, laying bright lozenges on flagstones worn to a polish by generations of knees. High above, the purple in the window’s saints seemed not glass at all, but a bruise of heaven, softening the air with color. It soothed Margaret where she stood, and it soothed Father Clement too; more than soothed—set his mind, as a hand sets a bowl on a table, straight before the Maker of all clean joys.

Yet it was not only the light that governed the place. The church had its own living sound: the hush that gathers when a thousand bodies breathe at once; the minute rustle of linen caps as women inclined their heads; the faint creak of a pew under an old man shifting a stiff hip; a child checked with a finger to the lips. Even the swallows outside stitched the silence with their quick, bright cries. Clement heard it all as if it were the church’s second scripture, an acoustic shelter against the world’s harsher clamor—a world where hammers and carts and bargaining tongues could make a man’s thoughts as ragged as a torn sleeve.

He looked down the great aisle, crammed with his country folk: a sea of snowy caps, many of them edged and fretted with gold thread as fine as spider-work. He had traveled many a hundred leagues and seen nothing like that pale multitude, save snowfields under winter sun. In the morning he had thundered at them; the hour had demanded it, and his own zeal had driven him. But this sweet afternoon, this tempered light and the soft, communal breathing, made threats sound out of tune. His belly—his very bowels—yearned toward them. Poor souls; they heard almost as little of God’s love from pulpits in those days as the heathen were said to hear. So he changed the dish he served them. He set down, not the bitter herbs of warning, but the warm bread of glad tidings.

He spoke of salvation as of a table spread for the hungry: not for the proud alone, nor for the well-shod, but for those whose hands were chapped with digging and whose mouths knew mostly coarse rye and onions. He did not whirl about like a weathercock, chasing his own wind. He hardly moved; he let the words do the walking. The people leaned toward his gentle earnestness. Even those in the bad places—those with consciences like smoky chimneys—he did not quite neglect. He touched them too, as a careful cook will scrape the bottom of the pot where the last sweetness sticks, so none be wasted.

And then, warmed by that mercy, eager that no soul in all that crowd should miss the good news, he turned his face toward the south aisle.

There, caught full in a stream of sunshine, was a face that stopped his breath as surely as if a hand had closed his throat.

Margaret Brandt.

At first he only stared. There was no feeling, no thought, no prayer—only a benumbing, soul and body, as if he had stepped barefoot onto sudden ice. Her auburn hair was bathed in the sun and glittered, not like common hair, but like a saint’s ring of light painted in gold leaf. Her face glowed doubly: with its own bloom, and with the light that framed it. She leaned very lightly against a white pillar, as if the stone were a friend. Her lashes lay downcast; she listened with that tender stillness he had seen a hundred times—her whole attention like a bowl held steady to catch every drop.

There was no change in her. This was the blooming Margaret he had left, only riper by a shade, more lovely for it, like fruit that has taken one more day of sun. Clement’s cheeks went bloodless. His eyes grew monstrous. The people died out of his sight. The church’s sounds warped, as in a dream: a rising rustle ran over the nave—folk standing, shifting, crossing themselves—but he could not turn from that face, all life and color and beauty, and that hair glistening in the sun like flame tamed into silk.

He gazed, waiting for her to vanish. She did not.

Then, in a blink as simple as swallowing, she lifted her eyes and looked full at him.

Her own violet eyes.

His lips parted to cry out her name. The name was already in his mouth like a sob. But she turned her head swiftly away; and soon after she vanished from his sight, slipping behind the pillar and out of the sun. Yet not without one more glance—rapid as lightning—that met his and left her crouching inward, mind all whirl and startled questioning, and him panting, hands gripping the pulpit’s edge as if it were the only solid thing in a world gone fluid. For that glance was not recognition. It was the look that comes before recognition: startled, inquiring, nameless, indescribable, as when a tongue tastes a spice it cannot place and yet knows it has been eaten before.

He made a mighty effort and muttered words nobody could understand. His voice had gone thin and far. He resumed his discourse feebly, stammering, babbling, casting about for the thread he had dropped. Bit by bit—forcing himself, now she was out of sight, to treat the thing as a vision permitted from another world—he rose into an unnatural excitement. His closing words spilled out in a torrent that bordered on rhapsody. The simple folk, who could not have told you why, felt the hair rise on their arms. The air itself seemed charged, as if a storm had passed close without rain.

When at last it ended, he sat down on the pulpit stool shaken through and through, as if his bones were reeds.

And then an idea, very much of that age, took him as firmly as a hand takes a shoulder. He had sought her grave at Sevenbergen and found nothing. She had been permitted to appear to him—so the thought went—and show him she was buried here, probably hard by that very pillar where her spirit had shown itself. Once the notion entered, it settled with the certainty of a fact. In his mind it was as plain as a name on a wooden trencher: Margaret lay here.

He had only to ask the sexton—whom, to his great disgust, he had seen working during the sermon, shovel and all, as if holy words were no more than wind—to learn the spot.

By now the church was empty. The last footfalls had faded; the last whisper had died. The soundscape changed: no longer the soft massed breathing, only the clean echo of his own steps and the distant birds outside. He came down from the pulpit, passed through a small aperture in the south wall, and stepped onto the grass.

The smell of earth met him at once—damp, iron-dark, threaded with the sharpness of newly cut sod. A man was there with a spade: Jorian, digging with the steady rhythm of one used to opening the ground for the dead as another man opens a sack of grain for the living. Clement knew him in a moment. But Jorian never suspected the poor lad whose life he had once saved in this holy friar. The loss of Clement’s shapely beard had altered the very outline of his face; more than tonsure or close-cropped hair dusted with premature grey, more than cheeks thinned and paled by fasts and vigils. A beard that has never known a razor grows into a man’s features like an old habit; take it away, and the face is a stranger.

“My son,” said Friar Clement softly, “if you keep any memory of those whom you lay in the earth, prithee tell me—is any Christian buried inside the church, near one of the pillars?”

“Nay, father,” said Jorian, pausing only long enough to look up. “Here in the churchyard lie buried all that buried be. Why?”

“No matter,” Clement answered; and his voice, though steady, had the hollow note of a man speaking across water. “Prithee tell me then, where lieth Margaret Brandt.”

“Margaret Brandt?” Jorian stared stupidly, as if the name were a coin worn smooth. “She died about three years ago, and was buried here.”

“Oh, that is another matter,” he added after a moment, scratching at the thought like a dog at a flea. “That was before my time. The vicar could tell you, likely—if so be she was a gentlewoman, or at the least rich enough to pay him his fee.”

“Alas, my son,” Clement said, and the word had the weight of stone, “she was poor—and paid a heavy penalty for it—but born of decent folk. Her father, Peter, was a learned physician. She came hither from Sevenbergen—to die.”

When he had uttered it, his head sank upon his breast. It was as if speech had spent him. The present—the grass, the spade, the open sky—fell away. He was lost in the past, where voices spoke in dialects that warmed the blood, where a familiar name carried more than meaning: carried a whole life no other tongue could translate.

Jorian, in the grave, put down his spade with a thud that sounded too loud in the afternoon quiet. He stood upright, set his arms akimbo, and said sulkily, “Are you making a fool of me, holy sir, or has some wag been making a fool of you!”

And having flung out his irritation, he took up the spade again and dug with a vigor that showed his temper ruffled—metal biting earth with a blunt, offended rhythm. Clement gazed at him with a puzzled, gently reproachful eye; the tone was rude, and the words, in their sharpness, almost unintelligible to a man who had been living more with prayers than quarrels.

Good-natured though crusty, Jorian had not thrown up three spadefuls before shame cooled him. The churchyard’s quiet—broken only by spade and breathing—seemed to rebuke him better than any sermon.

“Why, what a base churl am I,” he said, “to speak thus to thee, holy father; and thou standing there, looking at me like a lamb.” He paused, then his face cleared as if a shutter had been opened. “Aha! I have it. ’Tis Peter Brandt’s grave you would fain see, not Margaret’s. He does lie here—hard by the west door. There; I’ll show you.”

He laid down his spade, climbed out, and pulled on his doublet and jerkin to go with the friar.

He did not know there was anybody sitting on Peter’s tomb. Still less that she was watching for this holy friar.

Pietro Vanucci and Andrea, too, did not recognize him without his beard.
