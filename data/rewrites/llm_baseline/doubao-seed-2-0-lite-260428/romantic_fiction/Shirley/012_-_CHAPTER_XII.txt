Shirley Keeldar proved her words no empty courtesy by haunting the lanes between Fieldhead and the rectory, turning up unannounced at Caroline Helstone’s door so often that the maids began to smile and set an extra teacup out on the sill of every fine afternoon. Not that Caroline would ever have sought her out first: the quiet, diffident girl could never shake the ingrained belief that no one could truly want her company, that she was too slow, too uninteresting, too little acquainted with the bright, sparkling world the young heiress moved in. She could not fathom how Shirley, surrounded by half a dozen eager neighbouring families all vying for her notice, could ever prefer the stillness of her small parlour to their noisy dinner parties and dances.

Shirley was bright, and for the most part happy, but no soul alive can thrive without the kind of society that seeps into your bones like warm sunlight. She had, in the space of a month, made polite acquaintance with every family for three parishes round: she laughed with the Misses Sykes, gossiped with the Misses Pearson, and traded witticisms with the two much-celebrated Miss Wynnes of Walden Hall, but not one of them, as she put it, felt like kin. “If I were indeed Shirley Keeldar, Esquire, lord of Briarfield manor,” she told her old governess Mrs. Pryor one soft May morning, leaning against the wide window frame overlooking Fieldhead’s lawn, “there is not a single lady within twenty miles I would ask to step up and be mistress of the manor beside me.”

Mrs. Pryor, darning a fine linen handkerchief in her lap, did not look up at once, her fingers moving steady over the stitch. “My dear, do not let that odd habit of referring to yourself as a gentleman take root. Strangers hearing you speak so will think you affect masculine manners, and that will do you no credit.”

Shirley never laughed at Mrs. Pryor. She had too much respect for quiet, unassuming worth to ever turn her governess’s little harmless peculiarities into a jest. So she said nothing, only stood still, her eyes fixed on the great ancient cedar at the lawn’s edge, watching a plump little sparrow hop along its lowest bough. Before long her lips parted, and she began to chirp softly at it, clear and trilling, till the chirp swelled into a whistle, sweet and light, winding through the old Scottish air she had heard only the night before, drifting over the hedge from the lane where Robert Moore had been walking after closing his mill.

“My dear!” Mrs. Pryor expostulated, lifting her head.

“Did I whistle?” Shirley said, flushing a little. “I quite forgot. I beg your pardon, ma’am. I promised myself I would never do that in your hearing.”

“And where did you pick up that habit?” Mrs. Pryor asked, mild but curious. “You never once whistled when you were in my charge as a girl.”

“I learned it long ago, by ear,” Shirley said, leaning her shoulder against the window frame. “I’d forgotten I knew how, till last night. I was walking up our lane, and heard a gentleman whistling that very tune on the other side of the hedge, and it all came back.”

“What gentleman?”

“There’s only one young gentleman in this part of the world who is not gray-haired, isn’t there? Mr. Moore. Though I will say my two old favourites, your rector Mr. Helstone and Mr. Yorke, are far finer company than any of the silly lads our age round here.”

Mrs. Pryor fell quiet for a long moment, then said, “You walk out this morning, my dear?”

“I am going straight to the rectory,” Shirley said, already reaching for her bonnet. “I am going to drag Caroline Helstone out for a walk over Nunnely Common. The wind is fine and bracing, and she mopes indoors far too much.”

“Then do remind her to wrap her shawl tight,” Mrs. Pryor said. “The air has a sharp bite to it, and she looks as though she catches cold easily. Will you not ask me to come with you?” Shirley added, smiling. “We would make a very merry party.”

“No, my love,” Mrs. Pryor said, shaking her head. “I am too stout, and I cannot keep pace with your long legs. I should only be a restraint on you.”

Shirley had no trouble at all persuading Caroline to come out with her. The two of them stepped out onto the quiet road, the wide, empty sweep of Nunnely Common stretching out before them covered in new green turf and dotted with the first purple buds of heather, and before long all Caroline’s shyness melted away. The first few casual words they exchanged were enough for both of them to know they had stumbled on something rare, something no other friend had ever given them. Shirley talked of the Scottish border moors she had travelled the summer before, that long sultry afternoon where they had driven from noon till sunset across endless heath, seeing nothing but wild sheep, hearing nothing but the cries of curlews, the sky heavy and livid with coming storm.

Caroline nodded, her eyes softening. “I know that exact hue. The heath turns purple-black, deeper even than the storm cloud hanging low over it. You wait for lightning to split it any second.”

They talked of the distant thunder muttering over the hills, of the rain pouring down in sheets so thick the mountain peaks vanished entirely from sight, as if they had been washed clean off the face of the earth, and then the quiet that comes after, the soft sliver of sun breaking through the clouds like a promise the world has not ended.

“Stop here a minute,” Caroline said, tugging gently at Shirley’s sleeve, and they paused on the high green brow of the common. Below them Nunnely Dale lay draped in all its May finery: meadows pearled over with daisies, blazing golden with kingcups, the old remnant of ancient British forest Nunnwood draped in a soft drifting cloud shadow, the distant hills dappled blue and purple, the horizon tinted like mother-of-pearl, faint rose and pale green melting into fleeces of white cloud so pure they looked like fresh snow fallen on the sky. The wind blew cool and sweet on their faces, scented with gorse and wild violet.

“England is a bonny isle,” Shirley said softly. “And Yorkshire is its fairest corner. I was born here, you know. Five generations of my family are buried under the aisles of Briarfield church. I drew my first breath in that old black stone hall back behind us.”

Caroline held out her hand, and Shirley took it, shaking it firmly, a smile tugging at her lips. “Then we are countrywomen,” Caroline said.

“And that dark stretch of trees down there is Nunnwood?” Shirley asked, pointing.

Caroline nodded, and told her how the trees were old giants, trunks as thick as castle pillars, their tops so high they seemed to touch another sky, the ruins of an ancient nunnery tucked away in a green dell at its heart. Shirley’s eyes lit up. “Then we will go there, just the two of us, one long bright summer day. We’ll bring sketchbooks, and a book of old ballads, and two little baskets Mrs. Gill can pack full of provisions, and we’ll wander all day, no one to bother us.”

Caroline’s face lit up, and she began to list all her favourite spots in the wood: the glades carpeted with moss bright as gemstones, the wild strawberry patches, the gnarled old oaks draped in ivy, the little dell where the nunnery ruins half-buried in ferns lay. “There is no third person alive whose company would not spoil that,” Caroline said. “Not even the nicest gentleman. The moment a man joins the party, you forget all about the trees, don’t you? Nature herself seems to draw a veil over her face, and all that quiet joy vanishes, replaced by excitement and restlessness that makes the day fly by too fast, leaving nothing behind when it’s gone.”

They fell to talking then, quiet and earnest, of marriage. Shirley spoke of the horror she felt at the thought of being trapped, of being tied to someone who might one day grow tired of her, till her presence became a burden she could not escape no matter how badly she wanted to slip away into solitude. Caroline laughed a little, and said her uncle thought every man who married was a fool, and that Shirley was brave to hope for better, and then slowly, shyly, the two of them found themselves talking of Robert Moore. Shirley’s eyes sparkled, her cheeks flushing warm, when Caroline told her he was the one the stray dogs and the rectory’s black cat adored, the one who never pushed an animal roughly away, who was gentle even to the poorest beggar at his door.

“He is a noble being,” Shirley said, her voice soft with enthusiasm. “When a man is good, and kind, and handsome, there is nothing finer in all creation. I would never dream of competing with such a soul for precedence. It is not demeaning to look up to someone, not at all. It is glorious. There is no joy greater than finding a soul far higher and better than your own to admire.”

They had reached the rectory gates by then, the ivy climbing high over the stone pillars. “Don’t come in today,” Caroline said, smiling. “My uncle will be in his study, and he will badger you for half an hour about politics.”

“Then I will come fetch you tomorrow, to spend the evening at Fieldhead,” Shirley said, bending to press a soft, warm kiss to Caroline’s cheek. “I have never talked to any girl the way I have talked to you today, Caroline Helstone. I think you and I are going to suit very well. Goodbye, for now.”

* * * * *

Mrs. Pryor had no less eager a desire to be Caroline’s friend than Shirley did. A few days later, she paid a rare call at the rectory, stepping in through the door on a close, warm afternoon when the rector was safely out visiting his parishioners. She was flushed from the walk, and strangely fluttered, trembling a little as she sank down on the parlour sofa, fanning herself with her handkerchief, as if the strange house had stripped away all her usual composure. Caroline, surprised, went to her at once, gently lifting off her bonnet, loosening the fastenings of her heavy wool shawl, her light soft hands soothing the nervous tremor out of Mrs. Pryor’s limbs as no one else’s could. She recovered in a few minutes, and soon the two of them were talking easily, Mrs. Pryor’s conversation thoughtful and well-spoken, her sentiments fair and gentle, far more pleasant than Caroline had ever expected.

Their eyes fell on the portraits hanging on the opposite wall then: two gentlemen in dark old formal coats on either side of a lovely lady’s face over the mantelpiece. “That is a remarkably beautiful face,” Mrs. Pryor said, pausing in their talk. “Perfect features, no sculptor could improve on them. That is Mrs. Helstone, your uncle’s wife, is it not?”

Caroline nodded, and told her the story: how the quiet, silent woman had been the beauty of the district, had married the rector, and died only five years later. “He always says he could never have borne a talkative wife,” Caroline said. “He claims you go out to gossip, and you come home to read in quiet.”

Mrs. Pryor stood up to go, smoothing her skirts, and let Caroline wrap her shawl around her shoulders. “I hope you will come to Fieldhead very often, my dear,” she said softly. “You must be so lonely here, with no other woman in the house. And if you ever wish for help with your studies, languages, drawing, anything at all, you have only to ask me.”
