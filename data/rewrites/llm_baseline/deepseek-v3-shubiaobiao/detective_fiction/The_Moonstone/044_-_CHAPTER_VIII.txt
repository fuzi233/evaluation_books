The hour was late when Mr. Bruff appeared unexpectedly at my door. The man was altered—his usual brisk assurance replaced by a weary silence. He took my hand without a word.

“You’ve come from Hampstead?” I ventured.

“From Hampstead, yes,” he replied, his voice low. “I’ve just left Rachel at Portland Place. You must understand, Mr. Franklin, that I would rather have left you in ignorance forever than see her pay this price for the truth.”

“You’ve spoken with her?”

“I brought her back myself. She was in no state to travel alone.” His gaze sharpened. “I don’t blame you—you acted under my roof, with my consent. But I will not permit a repetition of this disaster. She is young, resilient. Time and quiet will mend what’s been broken—provided you do not interfere. Will you swear to make no further attempt to see her without my approval?”

The answer came without hesitation. “After what she has endured—after what I have endured—you have my word.”

“Your promise?”

“My promise.”

The tension in his frame eased. He set aside his hat and drew nearer.

“Good. That’s settled. Now, to the future—*your* future.” He leaned forward. “The situation, as it stands, is this: Rachel has told you the truth, plainly and completely. And while we know there must be some monstrous error at work, we cannot fault her for believing the evidence of her own eyes—corroborated, as it was, by circumstances that damn you utterly.”

I cut in. “I do not blame Rachel. I only wish she had spoken more plainly when she had the chance.”

Mr. Bruff gave a dry laugh. “You might as well wish her to be another woman entirely. Even then, I doubt any lady of delicacy—one who had hoped to marry you—could bring herself to accuse you to your face. It is not in Rachel’s nature. In another matter, unrelated to yours but not dissimilar in principle, she acted from the same motive. Besides”—his voice hardened—“she told me herself tonight that had she confronted you then, she would have disbelieved your denial just as she disbelieves it now. What answer can you make to that? None. So let us be practical, Mr. Franklin. The past is a tangle we cannot unravel. Our only path lies forward.”

“You forget,” I said, “that for me, this is entirely a matter of the past.”

“Is it?” His eyes gleamed. “Answer me: is the Moonstone the root of all this misery?”

“Of course.”

“Then consider. What do we believe happened to the Moonstone in London?”

“It was pledged to Mr. Luker.”

“We know *you* did not pledge it. Do we know who did?”

“No.”

“Where is the Moonstone now?”

“In the vaults of Luker’s bankers.”

“Precisely. Now mark this: we are in June. By month’s end, a full year will have passed since the gem was pledged. There is a chance—a slim one, but a chance—that the thief may redeem it when the term expires. If so, Luker must retrieve it personally. My proposal is simple: we watch the bank at the critical hour and learn who claims it.”

I frowned. “It’s daring. Ingenious. But—”

“But you object to the delay.”

“A fortnight is an eternity in my position, Mr. Bruff. I cannot endure this limbo without acting.”

He exhaled sharply. “Then act. Have you a plan?”

“Sergeant Cuff.”

“Retired. Useless.”

“I know where to find him.”

A pause. Then, grudgingly: “Try. The case has twisted into something even Cuff might find intriguing. But hear this—the key lies in discovering who pawned the Diamond. Let me know what the Sergeant says.” He rose, collecting his hat. “And if you’ve made no progress by month’s end, I’ll set my watch at the bank—unless you’ve relieved me of the necessity.”

With a thin smile, he was gone.

---

At first light, I set out for Dorking—Betteredge’s directions in hand. The Sergeant’s cottage lay beyond the town, a quiet refuge buried in roses. The gate was locked. Peering through the trellis, I saw blossoms crowding every inch of the garden, framing the windows, spilling over the path. Here, the great detective had retreated into horticultural oblivion.

The woman who answered my ring dashed my hopes at once. “He’s gone to Ireland, sir. Left yesterday.”

“On business?”

She smiled. “Roses are his business now. Some gardener’s made a discovery—off he went to investigate.”

No certainty of return. I left a message—*Moonstone. Urgent.*—and turned back to London, restless as a caged beast.

That night, logic gave way to fevered theories, each more absurd than the last. I woke tangled in philosophical doubts, questioning the very existence of the Diamond—until chance intervened. Rummaging for a handkerchief, I found Betteredge’s forgotten letter.

The act of replying—mundane as it was—cleared my mind. I turned my focus to the birthday dinner, probing memory for any overlooked detail. The guest list was essential. Betteredge would have it. I resolved to return to Yorkshire at once.

With hours to kill before the next train, I sought those few London guests: Murthwaite (gone abroad), Miss Clack (fled to France), and Godfrey Ablewhite—whose recent misfortunes I learned at his club. A broken engagement, a sudden legacy of £5,000, and a hasty departure for the Continent. Another dead end.

Defeated, I boarded the train to Frizinghall. Tomorrow, Betteredge would join me—my last hope. That evening, I prepared to question the remaining guests, starting with Mr. Candy.

The doctor was a shock. The vivacious little man I remembered was a specter—eyes dim, hair white, frame shrunken. Only his gaudy clothes remained unchanged.

“Mr. Blake!” he cried, seizing my hand. “Command my services, sir! Anything!”

I launched into my rehearsed explanation—the Diamond, the need to reconstruct that fateful evening. But his gaze drifted, fingers plucking at nothing. My words meant nothing to him.

A change of tack: “Betteredge mentioned you wished to see me.”

At that, he brightened. “Ah! Yes! Yes!”

But the spark faded as quickly as it came. Whatever message he’d intended was lost in the ruin of his mind.

I left, heavy-hearted. Three witnesses beyond reach, and a fourth useless. All now depended on Betteredge—and the ghosts of Lady Verinder’s circle.
