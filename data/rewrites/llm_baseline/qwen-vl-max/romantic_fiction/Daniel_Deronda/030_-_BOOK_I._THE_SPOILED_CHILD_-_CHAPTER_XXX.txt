No confession, no absolution—no priest to guide or forgive. Yet they sit, burdened by the ashes of years that have burned away all hope.

Picture a sprawling, uneven house, its finest section built from gray stone and topped with red tiles, a round tower jutting out at one corner, its conical roof darkened by time and crowned with a weather-vane that catches the eye whether summer sparkles through the trees or winter’s clouds hang low over snow-laden branches. The ground is shaded by wide-spreading oaks; on one side, a great tree thrives near a broken bank where Scotch firs lean, their roots exposed like pale fingers clutching at the earth, beyond which lies a rookery. On the other side, a pool edged with thick bushes, where ducks flutter and cry. Around it all stretches a vast meadow—almost a park—bordered by an old wood and guarded by stone lodges that resemble small prisons. Beyond the gate, the countryside once peaceful and green now bears the scars of coal mines, blackened and desolate. Here, men walk with candles stuck in their hats, faces grim and sullen, their presence a constant source of unease for the children of Gadsmere—Mrs. Glasher’s four beautiful children, who had lived here for nearly three years.

In November, when flowerbeds are bare, trees stripped of leaves, and the pool shivers under a cold sky, the place seems to mirror the bleakness of the land around it—except when the children play on the gravel, dogs bounding beside them. But Mrs. Glasher, in her present state, found peace in Gadsmere as she might have in any retreat. Its isolation, ensured by the unappealing landscape, suited her perfectly. When she drove her two ponies pulling a waggonet full of children, she met no aristocrats in carriages—only businessmen in gigs. At church, no eyes watched her with suspicion; the curate and his wife either knew nothing of her past or chose to ignore it. To them, she was simply a widow, tenant of Gadsmere. The name Grandcourt meant little here compared to Fletcher or Gawcome, the colliery lessees.

Ten years had passed since the elopement of an Irish officer’s beautiful wife with young Grandcourt, followed by a duel that left only empty air wounded. That affair had stirred some talk, but now most remembered it only vaguely. Where was that Mrs. Glasher? Once famed for her beauty and charm, known abroad as the companion of Grandcourt, she had vanished from memory.

It seemed natural enough that Grandcourt would leave her behind. A man of his rank and taste could not be expected to remain bound to a woman who had abandoned her child along with her husband. It was assumed she had slipped further into obscurity. Grandcourt, after all, was known for his pursuit of women. Now, he was likely seeking a suitable marriage—perhaps with a noble daughter, fair and young. No one spoke of Mrs. Glasher anymore. She was like a ship lost at sea, forgotten, unrecovered. But Grandcourt remained visible—returning to harbor, flags flying, declared seaworthy as ever.

Yet, in truth, Grandcourt had never freed himself from her. His passion for her had been deeper, more enduring than any other. Though now extinguished—like music from a cracked flute—it still left behind a dull ache. Three years earlier, when her husband died, Grandcourt had briefly considered marrying her, echoing a promise made during the early days of their affair. Then, he had been willing to pay for a divorce, but her husband refused—unwilling to remarry and unwilling to expose his private life in court.

Mrs. Glasher, however, had changed. At first, she cared little about marriage. Escape from a harsh husband, the intoxication of love with a young, handsome, passionate man—these were enough. She had been vivacious, craving admiration, worn down by five years of marital neglect. Freedom was sweet, and she didn’t mind the uncertainty of her position. She saw no value in being a conventional wife, overlooked and dull. The only shadow on her joy was the thought of leaving her three-year-old son—though he died two years later. His voice calling “mamma” remained distinct in her memory, different from the sounds of the children who came after.

But time had altered her. The lines of her face deepened, her body softened. And now, the idea of Grandcourt marrying her had become her deepest desire. What once mattered little now weighed heavily: the dignity of her children, the future they deserved. Her love for him had transformed into a fierce need for legitimacy—not for herself alone, but for them. If he married her, they would be secure. Her son could inherit his father’s estate. That dream gave her life purpose. She longed only for this: to see her children raised in respect, her own worth affirmed before them. Marriage to Grandcourt wasn’t just a wish—it was the only happiness she could imagine. For that, she was ready to endure anything quietly, even silence and pain. She understood his temper—how he hardened under pleading, how his moods shifted unpredictably. So she kept her feelings hidden, refusing to beg, to make scenes. Reticence cost her dearly. There was venom growing in her silence, bitter and slow.

She was utterly dependent on him. He had always paid for her upkeep, but never made it permanent. Everything remained voluntary. Still, she would accept nothing less than marriage. He had said he would never settle property until death—by will only. And sometimes, when thinking of the future, she wondered: if he never had a son, perhaps hers could inherit. At that age, no child could yet claim the strength of his father. But becoming his wife was not a fantasy—Lush himself had once believed it possible. In fact, Lush had encouraged Grandcourt’s interest in Miss Arrowpoint, seeing it as a sign that Grandcourt had given up on Mrs. Glasher. But both plans collapsed with Gwendolen’s arrival. And so Mrs. Glasher eagerly embraced Lush’s scheme—to warn Gwendolen against Grandcourt, to build a barrier between them. She entered it with fervor, passion mingled with purpose, letting some of her pent-up bitterness spill forth.

Afterward, she heard from Lush that Gwendolen had left—and that the danger was gone. But she received no word that the threat had returned. She wrote to Grandcourt, as she always did. He took longer than usual to reply. She began to hope—what else is hope but a bold interpretation of another’s thoughts? Perhaps he, too, felt tired of that failed pursuit and would return to the old path.

Grandcourt came to Gadsmere with two purposes: to tell her personally of his impending marriage, making the end final; and to reclaim the diamonds he had entrusted to her years ago, meant for his bride. They were not large stones—small peas and beans for ears, neck, hair—but valuable. He wanted them for Gwendolen. Years before, when he asked her to return them, saying they’d be safer in a bank, she refused. “If you marry another woman,” she said, “I’ll give them to her. Are you going to marry another?” At the time, Grandcourt had no reason to press. He had a certain grace—he rarely imposed his will on her, though others might say it was because she was helpless, a constant reminder of his power. But there was more: a lingering echo of the influence she once held over him. That faint pull toward the familiar, toward what had once brought him joy, had often made him hesitate before new ventures. But now, novelty had seized him. He wanted to move forward.

Mrs. Glasher sat in her favorite room, where mornings passed with her children gathered close. The window faced broad gravel and grass sloping to a brook feeding the pool. An old oak table, tawny leather chairs, a low black cabinet—all littered with toys, books, garden clothes. Above, a portrait of a gentle woman in pastel smiled indulgently down. The children were all there. The three girls, seated near her by the window, were tiny reflections of her—dark-eyed, delicate-faced brunettes with rich color in their cheeks, finely shaped brows and nostrils, each one a miniature woman. The eldest was barely nine. The boy, Henleigh, sat apart on the carpet, bent over animals from a Noah’s ark, commanding them sharply, occasionally licking the spotted ones to test the paint.

Josephine was having her French lesson. The others sat still, dolls in their laps, solemn as images of the Madonna. Mrs. Glasher had dressed carefully—each day she told herself Grandcourt might come. Her head, though thinner, still held a striking beauty: sharp profile, crisp curls, defined eyebrows. She wore bronze silk and velvet, a gold necklace Grandcourt had placed around her neck years ago. Not that she enjoyed dressing—the reflection in the mirror reminded her only of how much she had changed. But she preserved what she could. If her wish were granted, she imagined herself one day looking like a matron fit for high society. The children’s faces, so like her own, seemed to whisper of the bloom she once had, now replaced by pallor. But they kissed her cheeks without hesitation. Their love was now her only goal.

Suddenly, she turned from Josephine’s book and listened. “Hush, dear. I think someone is coming.”

Henleigh jumped up. “Mamma, is it the miller with my donkey?”

He got no answer. He moved to her knee, repeating the question insistently. But the door opened, and the servant announced Mr. Grandcourt.

Mrs. Glasher rose, agitated. Henleigh frowned—disappointed it wasn’t the miller. The girls lifted their dark eyes to him, timid. None of them truly liked this visitor. When Grandcourt took her hand and then touched Henleigh’s head, the boy struck at his arm with his fists. The girls submitted to kisses and pats, but soon were sent outside, dancing and chatting with the dogs on the gravel.

“How far have you come?” she asked as he removed his hat and coat.

“From Diplow,” he replied slowly, seating himself opposite her, gazing at her without focus—though she noticed every glance.

“You’re tired?”

“No. I rested at the Junction—a hideous place. Railways are always a bore. But I had coffee and smoked.”

He pulled out his handkerchief, wiped his face, tucked it back in, glancing at his crossed knee and polished boot as if addressing a stranger. But she trembled, every word, every look a potential key to hope or despair. He was already planning the conversation, calculating what would follow. Imagine the contrast: her, worn by time into sharper longing, more aware of dependence; him, dulled into stubborn neutrality.

“I expected you,” she said. “It’s been so long since I heard from you. Do the weeks seem longer at Gadsmere than at Diplow?”

“Yes,” he drawled. “But you received the money in the bank.”

“Oh, yes,” she answered curtly, tingling with impatience. Always before—she thought—Grandcourt had shown more interest in her, in the children. Today, he seemed distant.

“Yes,” he said, playing with his whisker, not looking at her at first. “Time has flown for me—usually it drags. But a lot has happened, as you know.” Then he looked at her.

“What do I know?” she asked sharply.

He paused. Then, without changing tone: “That I was thinking of marrying. You saw Miss Harleth?”

“She told you that?”

Her cheeks grew paler, perhaps from the fire in her eyes above.

“No,” he said slowly. “Lush told me.” It was like watching hands creep in with thumb-screws and iron boots, within sight of the victim.

“Good God,” she burst out, “say at once that you’re going to marry her!” Her knees shook, her hands clenched tightly.

“Of course, these things happen,” he said, not wanting to prolong the pain. “Lydia, it’s inevitable.”

“You didn’t always see it as such.”

“Perhaps not. I do now.”

In those quiet words, she felt absolute resistance—as if pushing against a locked iron door. She knew her helplessness. She couldn’t plead, couldn’t cry, couldn’t cling—only feel the immovable face, the rigid limbs. She didn’t weep. She didn’t speak. She was overwhelmed by sudden certainty—cold, sickening, heavier than thought or emotion. The collapse of hope left her with a horror. At last, she rose, spasmodically, pressed her forehead against the cold glass of the window. The children, playing outside, ran forward, faces upturned, expecting her attention. She shook her head, waved them away, and sank into the nearest chair, overcome.

Grandcourt had risen too. He was annoyed—both by the scene and by the feeling that nothing he could do would prevent it. But the task remained: to arrange things so that future discomfort was minimized. He leaned against the fireplace. She looked up.

“All this means nothing to you,” she said bitterly. “Me and the children—we’re nuisances. You just want to go back to Miss Harleth.”

“Don’t make this worse than it needs to be,” he said. “Lydia, it’s no use dwelling on what can’t be changed. Of course, it’s unpleasant to see you suffering. I came to tell you what you must accept—you and the children will be provided for as before. That’s all.”

Silence. She dared not respond. This woman, once vibrant, now carried the steel of maternal grief. She could suppress pain better than scream. But beneath the silence, hatred burned. She wished the marriage would bring misery to others—two others, besides herself.

He continued. “It will be better for you. You can stay here. I’ll settle a good sum on you and the children. You can live wherever you like. Nothing to complain of. Things moved quickly. No time for preparation.”

He stopped. He didn’t expect gratitude. He thought she might accept it. But she showed no change. After a moment, he said:

“You’ve never had reason to fear I’d be stingy. I don’t care about the money.”

“If you did care,” she said, sarcasm slipping out, “you wouldn’t give it to us.”

“That’s unfair,” he replied, voice lower. “And I advise you not to say things like that again.”

“Would you punish me by leaving the children in poverty?” The venom escaped, despite her control.

“There’s no question of that,” he said firmly. “I advise you not to say things you’ll regret.”

“I’m used to regretting,” she said. “Perhaps you’ll regret. You’ve already regretted loving me.”

“This only makes it harder for us to meet again. Who else do you have?”

“Quite true.”

The words came like a low moan. At that moment, she wished he might, after promising a better life, find himself lonely and wretched—driven back to her, searching for memories of youth and joy. But no—he would go untouched. Only she would suffer.

The words ended. Grandcourt had planned to stay until evening, but now he wanted to leave. No earlier train was available. He still needed to speak to her about the second matter—like a second surgery, requiring space. Time dragged on. Meals were eaten. The children came in. Life went on, stiff and constrained, like any household quarrel. For Lydia, the children’s presence was a relief—a savage pride in their beauty, a silent taunt to Grandcourt’s indifference. He handled Antonia gently, promised Henleigh a fine saddle and bridle. Only the older girls remembered him clearly—now shy, intimidated by his demeanor. They exchanged only polite remarks in front of servants. Otherwise, silence. Grandcourt’s mind wandered to the diamonds—why had he given them to her? It was a nuisance. He valued his caste, his possessions. He disliked asking for anything. Yet, he had created a past that now bound him. He must ask for what he had promised.

At last, they were alone, candles lit, facing each other. He glanced at his watch, then said, casually,

“There’s one thing I had to mention, Lydia. My diamonds—you have them.”

“Yes,” she answered, rising, arms folded, fingers laced. She had expected this. She had decided how to handle it. She would not provoke him. During the silence, she had wished she could take back the words that widened the chasm.

“They’re in the house?”

“No. Not in the house.”

“I thought you said you kept them close.”

She stood still. The moment hung.
