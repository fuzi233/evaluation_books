Paulina might have sworn off any further correspondence with Graham until her father gave his full blessing to their bond, but Dr. Bretton could not possibly live within a league of the Hôtel Crécy without finding every excuse he could to call. For a little while, the pair of them meant to keep a polite distance, to hold back from any grand, overt show of courtship as they’d promised themselves—but their hearts drew closer all the same, soft and unforced as the lengthening golden light of a June afternoon.

Every noble, best part of Graham woke and leaned toward Paulina; even the dazed, superficial fancy he’d once harbored for silly Ginevra Fanshawe faded entirely, for that old crush had never once engaged his sharp, hungry intellect. Now every part of him, every keen taste and curious faculty, lit up in her company, starved no longer for conversation that fed his soul. Paulina never once set out to reform him, or to school his mind—she thought him perfect already, far too wonderful to be improved by anyone. It was Graham himself who first stumbled into talking of a book he’d been reading, and when her response hummed with a harmony of sympathy he’d never known before, he spoke longer and better than he ever had in his life. She listened bright-eyed, answered with wit that sparkled, and every exchange uncovered some new hidden corner of his own mind he’d never noticed, some unacknowledged goodness in his heart he’d never had cause to nurture. Their thoughts fit each other like carefully strung pearls, matching line for line, and where Paulina was usually quiet and pensive left to her own devices, now she was as merry as a lark, glowing soft as diffused sunlight. All that careful, shy reserve she’d wrapped herself in for years thawed completely under Graham’s generous, warm influence, as if it had never been there at all.

They talked over the old days at the Bretton house, first with shy, smiling diffidence, then with open, unguarded candor. Graham never needed Lucy to prompt his memories of “little Polly”—every last recollection was his to tell, far sweeter coming straight from his own kind lips. Paulina would later recount to me, wonder in her voice, how vivid his memory was: he could remind her how she’d once grabbed his face in her small hands, stroked his thick dark curls and cried out “Graham, I *do* like you!” He could recall the weight of her little footstool dragged up beside his chair, the soft press of her small fingers smoothing his cheek, the way her tiny forefinger prodded the cleft in his chin and lisped that it was “a pretty dimple,” then looked up to tell him he had a “nice, strange face, far nicer than your mamma’s or Lucy’s.”

“To think I was so bold then!” she said to me once, cheeks pink. “He seems almost sacred to me now—his curls feel too lofty to touch, and when I look at his firm, marble chin, his straight, perfect Greek features, I almost feel afraid. People call women beautiful, Lucy, but he is nothing like a woman. Do other people see him as I do? Do *you* admire him?”

“I’ll tell you what I do,” I answered her, sharp and quick to stop the flood of tender confidences that would otherwise turn molten lead in my ear. “I never see him. I looked at him twice or thrice a year ago, before he recognized me, and then I shut my eyes. If he passed across them twelve times between sunset and sunrise, I would never be able to tell you what shape had gone by, apart from old memory.”

“Lucy, what do you mean?” she breathed.

“I mean I value my sight too much to risk being struck stone blind.”

It silenced her. She did not gush over his beauty to me again, though she still spoke of him sometimes, shy and soft, her voice strung with a tenderness that chafed at my lonely heart until I turned away with a sharp look. She only laughed, calling me her spartan, proud Lucy, and said Graham thought I was the quirkiest, most excellent little woman he had ever met, that the two of them wanted me to share their beautiful, bright life together. I told her I had no use for the kind of shared life they imagined. Solitude was sad, I admitted—but heartbreak was far sadder.

Lovers are selfish in their happiness, determined to have a witness no matter what it costs that onlooker. Paulina had forbidden letters, yet Graham wrote; she had sworn not to reply, yet she answered, if only to chide him for breaking their promise. She made me read his notes, and I could not blame her for being proud: they were fine, manly letters, tender and modest, gallant without being foolish. Her own letters were even lovelier—she had tried so hard to hide her love, to bridle his ardor, but every line glowed with the invisible magnet pull he had over her, warm from greeting to farewell.

“I wish papa knew,” she would murmur, anxious, over and over. “I so wish he did, but I fear it too. Graham is ready to go and tell him tomorrow, but I know he will be angry at first. It will be a shock, I know. I can hardly bear to think what he will say.”

M. de Bassompierre, who had walked through the last years blind to the growing attachment under his roof, was finally stirring awake. He said nothing to Paulina, but when she was not looking, I saw him stare at her, quiet and thoughtful, as if noticing for the first time that his little girl was no longer a child.

One evening, Paulina had gone up to her dressing room to write that very letter to Graham, leaving me in the library reading, when he walked in and asked me not to leave. He pulled out an old pocket memorandum book from his desk, and studied an entry for a long minute before turning to me.

“Miss Snowe,” he said, “do you know how old my little girl is?”

“About eighteen, sir, is it not?”

“So the book tells me. She was born the fifth of May, eighteen years ago. It is strange—I had forgotten to count the years right. I always thought of her as twelve, fourteen, some indefinite little age, a child forever.”

He sat quiet, heavy with thought. “My little jewel. She is the only pearl I have. Now others are starting to see how pure and precious she is. They will want to take her from me.”

I said nothing. Graham had dined with us that day, glowing, all his manners bright with the high hope he could no longer hide, and I knew Mr. Home had at last put the pieces together, working his slow, logical way through the whole story. When I told him Paulina was writing letters, not just to me or to Graham’s mother, he grumbled that the young man had no right to steal his only treasure—why could he not have set his sights on Ginevra Fanshawe, whom he would have handed over to Graham gladly, instead of his Polly? He could not fathom that any man could be good enough for her, that any title or fortune could ever measure up to what she was. I pressed him gently, asking if any of the stuck-up Villette barons or vicomtes would ever please him more than Graham, and he admitted he could not stand a single one of them. I reminded him that Graham had known them both his whole life, that he did not care a fig for her heiress fortune, that he had seen her wit and grace that night all the learned French scholars crowded around her at dinner, calling her *pétrie d’esprit et de graces*—all wit and grace.

He remembered then, how when he was deathly ill, little Polly had nursed him through, growing stronger and tenderer as he worsened, his only sunbeam in the sickroom as he recovered. He sighed, and admitted he had known Graham’s mother Louisa since they were children, that she had been a tall, blooming girl fit for a princess, that he had always thought well of the boy. It still felt like robbery, though.

The door opened then, and Paulina stepped in, all dressed for the June evening, her cheeks flushed summer pink, her curls falling loose and long over her lily-white neck, the unsealed half-written letter still in her hand to show me. She froze when she saw her father, the color rising all over her face. He teased her for blushing, and she stammered that she had not expected him to be there, that the letter was just a short note to a friend. When he pressed her to tell the truth, her courage suddenly broke through.

“Oh papa, I will tell you everything!” she cried, trembling all over. “I hate hiding things from you. I love you more than anything but God.” She laid the letter in his lap. He read it, his own hand shaking, his eyes glistening, and when he finished, he looked at her as if he could not believe that the little child who once stood at his knee could write such words, feel such things. He was gruff at first, pretending to call Graham a scamp, a robber who had dived deep into waters he had no business wading, teasing that he would send the lad off to Siberia. Paulina scolded him for being prejudiced, called him a naughty old Scotsman, before all her dignity melted away, and she flung herself sobbing around his neck, swearing she would never leave him, never make him unhappy. He choked out two words, rough and tender: “My lamb! My treasure!”

The room was dimming now, so I slipped quietly out to the antechamber to stop any servant bringing candles from interrupting. No servant waited there. Graham stood by the table, setting down his hat, pulling off his gloves slowly, lingering, waiting. He did not call to me, but his eyes said Lucy, come here, and I went. A soft, bright smile played across his face, the same brave charm he had always carried, even with his breath caught tight in suspense. He asked if he was about to be called to judgment, if Mr. Home had understood his intentions at last. I told him his star must be fortunate. He called me his old faithful little god-sister, asked me to say amen to his great venture. I did, and he turned to walk back into the library, me following at his heels.

“Sir,” he said, stepping up to Mr. Home, “what is my sentence?”

The father looked at him, Paulina’s face still hidden against his shoulder.

“Well, Bretton,” he said, gruff but softening already. “You have given me the usual reward of hospitality. I welcomed you into my house, and you have taken my best thing. I will not call it robbery—but I am bereaved, and what I have lost, you, it seems, have won.”

“Sir,” Graham said, steady, “I cannot repent.”

“Repent? Not you! You triumph, no doubt. You get that old Highlander Celt blood from your ancestors, don’t you—all that cunning, that charm, the clever tongue and the wily brain, passed straight down your line.”
