Under the calico draperies of a window that had been meant, no doubt, for moonlight and sighs, Arthur Pendennis established himself like a sulky house-cat—half-hidden, wholly offended with the universe—and set his face to practise that excellent accomplishment of youth, namely, looking as if one has been wronged by everybody, particularly by Providence. From this shaded embrasure he watched Miss Laura Bell go into her first quadrille with Mr. Pynsent.

Mr. Pynsent danced as a conscientious Englishman walks to his pew: straight, grave, and with the air of a man discharging a duty to the constitution. If the fiddle had burst into flame or the floor had opened and swallowed the set, he would have completed the figure before falling in. But Laura—Laura was all June and music. The lamps, the crowd, the scrape and swing of the band put a brightness into her honest rosy face; her brown ringlets shook back with every turn; her white gown spread and gathered as if it too had a heart; and she smiled on partner and neighbour with such open good-humour that half the room, being male, began to admire her for the best of reasons—because she looked as if she enjoyed herself.

Lady Fogey, who kept a London house and carried her fashion down into the country like a travelling bonnet-box, asked Lady Rockminster who the young person might be; compared her to some reigning beauty (as though girls were patterns cut from one expensive paper), and pronounced that she would “do.”

Lady Rockminster, with her eyeglass and her manner of judging the universe as if it were linen on a line, would have been vastly astonished if any protégée of hers did not “do,” and thought Lady Fogey’s presumption in pronouncing on the point a sort of social poaching. She surveyed Laura with majestic approval. The girl’s manner was artless and gay; her tone good; her arms—well, a trifle red; but youth is allowed its own colouring, as kitchens are allowed their own heat.

Opposite Laura in the dance stood Miss Blanche Amory, who smiled at her “dearest friend” with such killing sweetness that one could almost see the little daggers glitter behind the dimples. Blanche’s shoulders were the whitest in the room; and never, for a single instant, were they at rest in their frock. Nor were her eyes, which rolled and searched and flashed like two small mirrors set to catch attention. Her whole little figure seemed to call out, over the heads of the set: Come and look at me—not at that pink, healthy, bouncing country lass. This is the true Parisian manner—this is the prettiest little foot, and the prettiest little chaussure. Look at it, Mr. Pynsent. Look at it, Mr. Pendennis, you behind the curtain. I know you are pining to dance with me.

Laura danced on, yet kept an attentive eye on Pen in his calico confessional. He stayed there through the first quadrille, and would have stayed longer, had not good-natured Lady Clavering beckoned him to the dais, where the dowagers sat in state, like an array of richly dressed casseroles waiting to be served. Up went Pen—blushing, exceedingly awkward, and with that injured vanity most conceited young men wear when summoned from their sulk to society. He performed a haughty salute to Lady Rockminster, who returned it with the smallest possible acknowledgment, such as one gives to a door one must pass through. Then he paid his respects to Mrs. Amory, widow of the late gentleman, who glittered with diamonds, velvet, lace, feathers, and the sort of millinery and goldsmith’s ware that seems designed to warn younger women what marriage may end in.

Young Mr. Fogey—fifth form at Eton, ardently expecting his beard and his commission—was presently honoured with Miss Bell’s hand for the second dance. He regarded her as if she were a revelation. “I like you much better than the French girl,” he said candidly (having danced with Miss Amory before). Laura laughed—laughed the more, because in the midst of it she caught sight of Pen still looking absurdly pompous and sulky, as though he were presiding over a funeral instead of a ball.

The next dance was a waltz. Fogey sighed that he did not know how, and vowed to hire a master in the holidays, as men vow to be saints on Sunday and forget by Monday. Mr. Pynsent claimed Laura again, and Pen, watching her whirl round the room with that gentleman’s arm about her waist, felt a fury as fresh as if jealousy had been invented that evening for his exclusive use.

He had not always been angry at such sights. At Clavering Park, in summer, when chairs and tables were removed, the governess called down to the piano, and Chevalier Strong—who could dance anything from British hornpipe to Spanish fandango with the ease of a man slicing an orange—helped the two girls improvise little balls, Pen had stood twirling and laughing and had thought the world a tolerable place. But now Laura, so animated that she even animated Mr. Pynsent, looked altogether too happy; and happiness, witnessed from behind a curtain, is an offence.

Blanche, who danced beautifully, had drawn for her partner Captain Broadfoot of the Dragoons at Chatteris—a heavy honest man who threw energy at his task but possessed not the smallest ear for music. He moved too slowly and did not know it; he waltzed as if the tune were a government regulation to be consulted. So, in quadrille and in waltz, Blanche saw Laura carrying off the honours with ease, and was not pleased. After a couple of turns with the dragoon, she pleaded fatigue and begged to be led back near her mamma—where Pen was talking—and immediately demanded, with a shrug, why he had not asked her to waltz and had left her to the mercies of that great odious man in spurs and a red coat.

“I thought spurs and scarlet were the most fascinating objects in the world to young ladies,” Pen said. “I should never dare put my black coat in competition with that splendid red jacket.”

“You are very unkind and cruel and sulky and naughty,” said Miss Amory, as if listing his crimes in order of elegance. “You had better go away. Your cousin is looking at us over Mr. Pynsent’s shoulder.”

“Will you waltz with me?” Pen asked.

“Not this waltz. I can’t, having just sent away that good Captain Broadfoot. Look at Mr. Pynsent—did you ever see such a creature? But I will dance the next waltz with you, and the quadrille too. I am promised, but I will tell Mr. Poole I had forgotten my engagement to you.”

“Women forget very readily,” said Pen.

“But they always come back,” Blanche replied, “and are very repentant and sorry for what they’ve done. See—here comes the Poker, and dear Laura leaning on him. How pretty she looks!”

Laura approached and gave Pen her hand with the same frank kindness she had been wearing all evening, as if her heart were a warm loaf to be broken and shared. Mr. Pynsent made a stiff bow—indeed as little graceful as that domestic implement to which Blanche had likened him—while Laura said, delighted, “I am so glad to have come, dear Pen. I can speak to you now. How is mamma? The three dances are over, and I am engaged to you for the next, Pen.”

“I have just engaged myself to Miss Amory,” Pen said; and Blanche nodded, curtseyed, and looked as if she had signed a treaty.

“I don’t intend to give him up, dearest Laura,” Blanche said.

“Well, then, he’ll waltz with me, dear Blanche,” Laura answered, with a quiet firmness that made her good nature look like strength. “Won’t you, Pen?”

“I promised to waltz with Miss Amory.”

“Provoking!” Laura said; and curtseying, she went and placed herself under the ample wing of Lady Rockminster.

Pen was delighted with his own spite. The two prettiest girls in the room were quarrelling about him: he flattered himself he had punished Laura. He leaned against the wall with a dandified elbow, talked to Blanche, and quizzed the men and women with an unmerciful liveliness—the dragoons in tight jackets; country dandies in curious attire; ladies with toilettes that looked as if they had been dressed by imaginative pastrycooks. One had a bird’s nest for a head; another carried what might have been six pounds of grapes in her hair besides her false pearls.

“It’s a coiffure of almonds and raisins,” Pen declared. “It might be served up for dessert.”

He kept this satire up through the quadrille with unflinching bitterness and considerable wit, and Blanche laughed continually—at his wickedness, at his jokes, and because Laura, opposite again as vis-à-vis, could see how merry and confidential they were. Across Cornet Perch’s shell-jacket Blanche whispered to Laura, while Pen drawled through a figure with thumbs in both waistcoat pockets, “Arthur is charming to-night.”

“Who?” Laura asked.

“Arthur,” Blanche answered in French. “Oh, it’s such a pretty name!”

Now the ladies changed places; Cornet Perch performed his own pas seul, hands hanging large and swollen in front of him, since his tight little jacket supplied no convenient pockets for attitude. In the interval before the succeeding waltz, Pen took care to notice Laura only enough to wound her: he asked whether Cornet Perch was amusing, and whether she liked him as well as her other partner, Mr. Pynsent. Having planted these two daggers in her gentle bosom, he returned to his loud jokes with Blanche, good or bad, but always audible.

Laura could not understand her cousin’s sulkiness, and did not know where she had offended him. She was not angry in turn—she was too forgiving; and besides, jealousy in a man is not always displeasing to a woman, being a compliment with teeth. Since Pen would not dance with her, she was glad to take up with the brisk Chevalier Strong, who danced even better than Pen; and when the waltz began, she set off with all her heart, as a young girl should, as naturally as she breathes.

Captain Broadfoot now occupied the floor with a lady of proportions scarcely inferior to his own—Miss Roundle, large and helplessly conspicuous in a strawberry-ice coloured crape dress, daughter of the lady with the grapes. If ever a colour begged to be eaten rather than worn, it was that; and the band scraped on, while the room filled with the soft thunder of feet, the whisper of skirts, the little cries and laughter, the whole cheerful ecology of sound that makes a ballroom bearable. It was only now and then, when a chair scraped harshly or a boot-heel struck too sharply, that one felt how easily pleasure is jarred by clumsy noise, as a delicate sauce is spoiled by a careless hand.

Pen, with Blanche hanging on the arm which encircled her waist, set out upon his waltzing career at last, feeling that they performed brilliantly indeed. Perhaps he looked to see if Miss Bell admired the spectacle; but Laura would not or did not see him, being engaged with Strong and very content. Pen’s triumph was not destined to last long. Blanche was doomed another discomfiture.

For while Pen and Blanche spun rapidly, as light as opera-dancers in their own opinion, Broadfoot and Miss Roundle revolved with majestic leisure, taking time as a principle. They were in everybody’s way—but more particularly in Pendennis’s. Pen and Blanche, executing their brisk gyrations, came bolt up against the heavy dragoon and his strawberry-ice planet with such force that the common centre of gravity deserted them all in terror. Broadfoot and Miss Roundle were fairly upset; Pen, less lucky than Blanche, came down sprawling upon the floor among the general ruin, while Blanche herself was flung upon a bench against the wall.

Captain Broadfoot, heavy but good-natured, burst out first into a loud laugh at his own misfortune; and as nobody can long be angry with a man who laughs at himself, his fall passed almost as an entertainment. But Miss Amory was savage; Miss Roundle, seated again and looking pitifully round, presented an object which very few people could behold without giggling; and Pen, hearing the giggles, turned furious. He was one of those sarcastic young gentlemen who can laugh at the universe but cannot endure the universe laughing back. Of all things he feared ridicule most; and now it hopped and tittered all around him like a chorus.

As he scrambled up, Laura and Strong were laughing; everybody was laughing; Pynsent and his partner were laughing. Pen boiled with wrath against them, and could have stabbed them both on the spot for daring to be amused. He turned away, blundering out apologies to Blanche: it was the other couple’s fault—the woman in pink—the whole calamity had been produced by strawberry-ice and gravity. Was Blanche hurt? Would she not have courage for another turn?

Miss Amory, in a pet, declared she was very much hurt indeed, and would not take another turn. A cavalier in a blue ribbon and a three-pointed star rushed to fetch her a glass of water. She accepted it, drank, smiled graciously on the bringer, and then—turning her white shoulder upon Pen with a marked haughtiness—besought the gentleman with the star to conduct her to her mamma, holding out her hand to take his arm.

The man with the star trembled with delight, bowed over her hand, pressed it fervidly to his coat, and looked round with triumph. He was no other than Mirobolant.

Blanche had never fairly looked into the artist’s face since he had been employed in her mother’s family, and had no idea—none whatever—that the distinguished person to whose arm she clung was the chef. She took the decorations, the dark eyes, the air of foreignness, and made—like many people in society—an instant translation into nobility. Pen, seeing her go, forgot his humiliation in astonishment, and cried out, “By Jove, it’s the cook!”

The moment the words were out he regretted them; for it was Blanche who had invited Mirobolant, and the man could hardly refuse a lady’s command. Blanche in her flutter did not hear; but Mirobolant heard, and cast back over his shoulder a glance so furious it might have roasted Pen where he stood. It amused Pen, who was in that mischievous, sour humour that longs for a quarrel; and besides, the notion that a cook—or any man under a cook’s title—could have feelings of honour did not conveniently enter the mind of this lofty young aristocrat, the apothecary’s son.

But it had never entered poor Mirobolant’s head that he was not equal, as a man, to any other mortal; nor that his position was so degrading it should forbid him to offer his arm to a lady who asked it. In his own country he had seen fine ladies join dances with Blaise or Pierre; and he might even, in his simplicity, have taken Blanche up to Lady Clavering and asked her to dance too—had not Pen’s exclamation struck him as if it had been a shot. It cruelly humiliated him; anger rose hot and fast, like a pot boiling over.

Blanche did not know what made him start and grind a Gascon oath between his teeth. But Chevalier Strong—who, having received interesting information from Madame Fribsby, understood the poor fellow’s state—was luckily near, and spoke rapidly to Mirobolant in Spanish, which the other understood. Strong then begged Miss Amory to come and take an ice before she returned to Lady Clavering. At that, the unhappy Mirobolant relinquished the arm he had held for a minute, bowed most profoundly and piteously, and fell back as if struck out of the scene.

“Don’t you know who it is?” Strong asked Blanche as he led her away. “It is the chef Mirobolant.”

“How should I know?” Blanche said. “He has a croix; he is very distingué; he has beautiful eyes.”

“The poor fellow is mad for your beaux yeux,” Strong replied. “He is a very good cook—but he is not quite right in the head.”

“What did you say to him in the unknown tongue?” Blanche asked.

“He is a Gascon, from the borders of Spain,” Strong answered. “I told him he would lose his place if he walked with you.”

“Poor Monsieur Mirobolant!” said Blanche. “Did you see the look he gave Pendennis?”

Strong, enjoying the mischief, said, “I think he would like to run little Pen through with one of his spits.”

“He is an odious, conceited, clumsy creature, that Mr. Pen,” Blanche said.

“Broadfoot looked as if he would like to kill him too; so did Pynsent,” Strong answered. “What ice will you have—water ice or cream ice?”

“Water ice. Who is that odd man staring at me—he is décoré too.”

“That is my friend Colonel Altamont,” Strong said. “A very queer character, in the service of the Nawaub of Lucknow. Hallo! what’s that noise? I’ll be back in an instant.” And he sprang out, for a scuffle and high voices were heard in the ballroom.

The refreshment-room in which Miss Amory now found herself was the place set apart for supper, which Mr. Rincer the landlord had provided at five shillings a head for those who liked to pay for midnight courage. In this room, too, superior refreshments were ready for the county families; and the commoner sort were kept out by a waiter at the portal who announced, like a doorkeeper to Paradise, that this was a select room for Lady Clavering and Lady Rockminster’s parties, not to be opened to the public until after midnight. The distinction was as carefully preserved as if it were a recipe: some people might taste at once; others must wait until the hour when everybody is hungry and dignity is weak.

Pynsent, who danced with his constituents’ daughters, took them and their mammas in for refreshment there. Strong, manager and master of revels wherever he went, had of course the entrée. And the only person now occupying the room was the gentleman with the black wig and the orders in his buttonhole—the officer in the service of his Highness the Nawaub of Lucknow.

He had established himself early in the evening, declaring himself confoundedly thirsty, and called for a bottle of champagne. The waiter, hearing champagne in a select room, instantly supposed he had to do with a grandee. The Colonel sat down, began to eat his supper and absorb his drink, and entered affably into conversation with anybody who came in, as if hospitality were a uniform. Sir Francis Clavering and Mr. Wagg had found him there when they left the ballroom early—Sir Francis to smoke a cigar and look at the people gathered outside on the shore (which he declared was much better fun than remaining within), Mr. Wagg to hang on a baronet’s arm, as he loved to do with the greatest man in company.

Colonel Altamont had stared at them in so odd a manner that Sir Francis asked the landlord who he was, and hinted a strong opinion that the Nawaub’s officer was drunk. Mr. Pynsent, too, had conversed with the same gentleman; Pynsent’s cue was to speak to everybody (and he did it, to do him justice, in the most ungracious manner), and he took the man in the black wig for some constituent, some merchant captain, or other outlandish local specimen. Coming in with a lady on his arm, Pynsent was asked by the Colonel if he would try a glass of Sham. Pynsent, with great gravity, bowed, tasted, pronounced it excellent, and retreated. This decorum surprised and routed the Colonel more than rudeness would have done: he stared after Pynsent stupidly and told Mr. Rincer over the counter that he was a rum one.

Mr. Rincer blushed, hardly knowing what to say. Mr. Pynsent was a county earl’s grandson, aiming at Parliament. Colonel Altamont wore orders and diamonds, jingled sovereigns in his pocket, and paid like a man. Not knowing how to reconcile pedigree with payment, Mr. Rincer escaped by addressing somebody else: “Yes, Colonel—yes, ma’am—did you say tea? Cup a tea for Mr. Jones, Mrs. R.,” and thus slid away from discussion.

For, in plain truth, Mr. Altamont, having remained at the buffet almost all night and employed himself actively there, had flushed his brain considerably by drinking, and was still going on when Strong ran out and left Blanche alone with her water ice.

She was absorbed in absorbing it—for it was very fresh and good: cold, clean, sharply sweet, the sort of taste that makes the mouth feel newly made. Or perhaps she was not curious about the noise in the next room, though the waiters were, and ran after Strong. So when she looked up from her glass she beheld the strange man with the little red eyes staring at her.

He rose and advanced with a rather unsteady gait, and after gazing said, with a familiarity that smelt of champagne and empire, “And so you’re Betsy Amory. Betsy Amory, by Jove!”

“Who—who speaks to me?” said Betsy, alias Blanche.

But the noise in the ballroom had grown so loud—voices clashing, feet stamping, the bright social music turning suddenly into the rougher racket of a quarrel—that we must rush back thither at once and see what has caused the disturbance.
