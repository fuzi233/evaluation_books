We found Lady Verinder sat in a room lit by nothing but a single reading lamp, its shade twisted low to cast her whole face in deep shadow. Unlike her usual frank, direct way of greeting us, she huddled close to the table, her eyes fixed stubbornly to the pages of an open book.
“Officer,” she said, “does it matter to the investigation you are running, to know in advance if anyone in this house plans to leave?”
“Most important, my lady,” Sergeant Cuff replied.
“Then I must tell you: Miss Verinder means to go and stay with her aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite, in Frizinghall. She has arranged to leave first thing tomorrow morning.”
Sergeant Cuff turned his gaze straight to me. I took a step forward to speak for my mistress, and then—ashamed as I am to own it—my heart failed me entirely, and I stepped back again, not a single word passing my lips.
“Might I ask your ladyship when Miss Verinder told you of this plan to visit her aunt?” the Sergeant inquired.
“About an hour ago,” Lady Verinder answered.
Cuff looked at me a second time. They say old men’s hearts don’t stir easy, but mine was hammering so hard I swear it would have burst if I was still five and twenty!
“I have no right, my lady,” said Cuff, “to control Miss Verinder’s actions. All I ask is that you put off her departure, if you can, till later in the day. I must travel to Frizinghall myself tomorrow morning, and I will be back no later than two o’clock. If Miss Verinder can be kept here until then, I would like two unexpected words with her before she goes.”
Lady Verinder ordered me to tell the coachman not to bring the carriage round for Miss Rachel until two in the afternoon. “Have you anything more to say?” she asked the Sergeant once the instruction was given.
“Only one thing, your ladyship. If Miss Verinder is surprised by the change in plans, do not tell her I am the reason her trip is delayed.”
My mistress’s head snapped up off her book, as if she was about to burst out with something unguarded—then she forced herself to clamp down hard on the words, fixed her eyes back on the open page, and waved us both off with a sharp flick of her hand.
“That is a remarkable woman,” Cuff said once we were out in the hall. “If not for her iron self-control, the whole mystery that has stumped you, Mr. Betteredge, would have been solved tonight.”
At those words, the truth finally crashed through my thick old head. I think I must have gone clean mad for a minute: I seized Cuff by the collar of his coat and pinned him hard against the wall. “Damn you!” I shouted. “There is something wrong with Miss Rachel—and you have been hiding it from me this whole time!”
Sergeant Cuff stared up at me, flat to the wall, not moving a single muscle of his melancholy face, not lifting a hand to push me off. “Ah,” he said, quiet as ever. “You guessed it at last.”
My hands fell away from his collar, and my head sank to my chest. Bear with my outburst, won’t you? I served this family for fifty years. Miss Rachel used to climb right up on my knees and yank at my whiskers when she was tiny. For all her sharp little ways, she was the dearest, prettiest, best young mistress an old servant could ever hope to love. I apologized to Sergeant Cuff, though I’m afraid my eyes were streaming, and I cut a very unseemly figure doing it.
“Do not distress yourself, Mr. Betteredge,” Cuff said, kinder than I had any right to expect. “In my line of work, if we took offence easy, we would not be worth the salt on our porridge. If it helps you work off your temper, grab my collar again. You do not do it half right, but I will overlook the clumsiness for the sake of your feelings.” The corners of his mouth twitched up, his own dreary version of a joke he was very proud of.
I led him to my small sitting room and shut the door. “Tell me the truth, Sergeant,” I said. “What do you suspect? There is no kindness in hiding it from me now.”
“I do not suspect,” Cuff said. “I know.”
My bad temper flared up again before I could stop it. “Do you mean to tell me plainly,” I snapped, “that Miss Rachel stole her own Diamond?”
“Yes,” he said. “That is exactly what I mean. Miss Verinder has kept the Moonstone in her secret possession from the very first, and she drew Rosanna Spearman into her confidence, counting on us to suspect the poor maid of the theft. That is the whole case, wrapped up tight. Go on, grab my collar again, if it makes you feel better.”
God help me, that was not the sort of comfort I needed. “Give me your reasons,” was all I could choke out.
“You will hear them tomorrow,” Cuff said. “If Miss Verinder refuses to put off her trip to her aunt—and you will find that she will refuse— I will have no choice but to lay the full case before your mistress. I do not know what will come of that, so I want you there, present, to hear every word that is said from both sides. Leave the matter rest tonight. You will not get another single word about the Moonstone out of me. Now look, your supper is laid out on that table. I am a man who treats that particular human frailty very tenderly. Ring the bell, and I will say grace. ‘For what we are about to receive——’”
“Enjoy your supper, Sergeant,” I said. “My own appetite is gone entirely. I will see you served with the best of everything, then you will excuse me if I step out and try to bear this misery on my own.”
I made sure he had every good thing on the table, and I will own I half wished the lot of it would choke him. The head gardener, Mr. Begbie, wandered in at that very moment with his weekly accounts, and Cuff immediately steered the conversation round to roses, arguing the merits of grass paths over gravel walks before they dived headlong into a shouting match about white moss roses, and whether they needed to be budded to dog-rose stock to grow properly. Mr. Begbie swore you had to do it, Cuff swore you did not, and they appealed to me like a pair of brawling boys. I know nothing at all about growing roses, so I took the neutral path judges take when the scales of justice hang perfectly even. “Gentlemen,” I said, “there is much to be said on both sides.”
In the quiet lull that impartial line bought me, I laid Lady Verinder’s written note down on the table under Cuff’s nose. He read it in half a minute, and was immediately sharp and focused again: he recalled Superintendent Seegrave’s report, fished out the part that spoke of the three Indian jugglers, and asked me for the name and address of the famous traveller who knew the Indian languages and customs that Seegrave had mentioned. I wrote the details on the back of my lady’s note, and Cuff said he would track the man down first thing when he went to Frizinghall in the morning.
“Do you expect that to turn up anything?” I asked. “Superintendent Seegrave swore those Indians were as innocent as unborn babies.”
“Superintendent Seegrave has been wrong about every single conclusion he has drawn so far,” Cuff said. “It is well worth finding out if he was wrong about the Indians, too.” With that, he turned right back to Mr. Begbie and picked up the rose argument exactly where he left off. I shut the door on them, and left them to it.
I met Penelope loitering in the passage, waiting for Miss Rachel to ring for her so she could finish packing for the trip. She told me Rachel had declared the house was utterly unendurable now there was a policeman under the same roof, that she could not stand his presence for another hour. When she heard her departure was delayed till two, she flew into a screaming, violent passion. Lady Verinder rebuked her sharply, then sent Penelope out of the room, having some private thing to say to her daughter that no one else could hear. My girl was low as mud. “Nothing is right, father,” she said. “Nothing is the way it used to be. I feel like some terrible misfortune is hanging over all of us.”
I felt the exact same way, but I put a brave face on it for her. Miss Rachel’s bell rang just then, and Penelope ran up the back stairs. I headed for the hall to check the glass to see if the weather was shifting, and just as I reached the swing door leading from the servants’ quarters, it flew open violently from the other side. Rosanna Spearman ran right past me, her face streaked with pain, one hand clamped hard to her heart like a red hot pang had stabbed her through there.
“What is the matter, my girl?” I asked, stopping her. “Are you ill?”
“For God’s sake, do not speak to me,” she gasped, twisting herself right out of my grip and running for the servants’ staircase. I called out to the cook, who was nearby, to go look after her. Two other people had heard the ruckus too: Sergeant Cuff slipped soft as a shadow out of my sitting room and asked what the noise was, and I told him it was nothing. Then Mr. Franklin pulled open the swing door, and beckoned me into the hall, asking if I had seen Rosanna.
“I did, sir,” I said. “She just passed me, looking distraught, acting very strange.”
“I am afraid I am the innocent cause of that upset, Betteredge,” he said. “I cannot explain it, but if the girl is mixed up in the loss of the Diamond, I swear to you she was two minutes away from confessing everything to me, of all people, not two minutes ago.”
As he said that, I thought I saw the swing door creak open a tiny crack from the other side. Someone was listening. The door clicked shut before I could reach it, and I caught a glimpse of the hem of Sergeant Cuff’s neat black coat vanishing round the far corner of the passage. I did not want to stir up more mischief than we already had, so I told Mr. Franklin it was only one of the dogs that had wandered in, then asked him what had happened between him and Rosanna.
He pointed to the billiard table. “I was knocking the balls about,” he said, “trying to get this whole miserable Diamond mess out of my head. I looked up, and there was Rosanna Spearman standing right beside me, like a ghost. I was so startled I did not know what to do at first. She looked so anxious, I asked if she wanted to speak to me. She said, ‘Yes, if I dare.’ I knew the suspicion hanging over her, so I felt awkward, did not want to push her. I held my billiard cue and kept tapping at the balls to make the situation less awkward, and I think I mortified her without meaning to. She turned away, and I heard her whisper, ‘He would rather look at billiard balls than look at me!’ and before I could stop her she was gone. Tell her I meant no unkindness, will you? And if she still wants to speak, no matter what trouble I get into, send her to me in the library.”
I went to the servants’ quarters, and found Rosanna had locked herself in her room, turned down every offer of help, and only asked to be left alone to rest. I reported back to Mr. Franklin, who went up to bed. I was putting out the last of the lights and latching the windows when Samuel came to tell me the gardener had gone home, and Sergeant Cuff was nowhere to be found downstairs.
I went up the stairs to check if he had turned in to the bedroom we had prepared for him. Halfway up the second landing, I heard quiet, steady breathing coming from the corridor that led straight to Miss Rachel’s bedroom. There he was: curled up on three chairs pushed tight across the passage, a red handkerchief tied round his grizzled head as a pillow, his black coat rolled up under his neck. He woke instantly, quiet as a hound, the moment I stepped near.
“Good night, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “And mind, if you ever take to growing roses, the white moss rose grows far better if you do not bud it on the dog-rose, no matter what the gardener says.”
“What are you doing here?” I asked. “Why are you not in your proper bed?”
“I am not in my proper bed,” Cuff said, “because this miserable world is full of people who cannot earn their money easily and honestly at the same time. Rosanna Spearman came back from the Shivering Sand tonight, exactly the same hour Miss Verinder announced she was leaving the house. Whatever Rosanna hid, it is plain to me Miss Rachel could not run off until she knew it was hidden safely. The two of them have already communicated once in secret tonight. If they try to meet again when the whole house is asleep, I want to be right here in their path to stop it. Do not blame me for spoiling your sleeping arrangements, Mr. Betteredge. Blame the Moonstone.”
“I wish to God the Moonstone had never stepped foot in this house!” I burst out.
Sergeant Cuff looked down ruefully at his lumpy three-chair bed, and nodded, dead serious. “So do I,” he said.
