Bound by a custom I hold dearer than life itself, though my spirit chafes against its constraints, I pause here to gather the scattered remnants of humanity I have left behind along the road. Let me be clear: these were never intended for the stage's center. They are merely the footnotes, the background figures who, if nothing else, proved that Gerard did not traverse the journey from Holland to Rome as a ghost passing through mannequins, but as a man walking among flesh and blood.

Eli and Catherine lived out days measured in centuries. Their longevity stretched the fabric of memory so thin that, eventually, both faded into the twilight of Gerard's own recollection. Even Giles, who seemed destined for immortality, survived long enough to walk the courts of Bavaria at the tender age of ninety. Yet, time had not been kind to his form; he had withered into a leathern husk, his voice reduced to a trumpet-toned rasp, a skeleton of a man clinging to existence.

Cornelis, however, found his peace only in waiting. With every rival gone and his mother's love growing fierce as she clung to him while her children dispersed to the winds, he sat upon the shores of patience. As Catherine had foreseen, before the lovers could truly wear themselves down, Cornelis simply rusted away. At sixty-five, in the quiet embrace of his eighty-six-year-old mother, he succumbed to the slow decay of old age. Though he had lain unconscious for hours, he returned to consciousness in that final, sacred interval between breaths. Seeing her face hovering above him, he spoke of his grand design—the transformation of the shop and premises once hers and his were united. "Yes, my darling," cooed the frail old woman, smoothing his brow, and within a heartbeat, he was dust. That dust was swept into the earth by the very feet he had waited to serve, leaving no trace but a silence that the world eventually filled.

Denys, shattered by the loss of his comrade, sought solace in returning to Burgundy. There, a meager pension from the court sustained him, keeping him alive until fortune, in its mysterious turns, bestowed upon him a considerable inheritance from a distant relative. For many years, he remained known in his homeland as a crusty veteran, a storyteller who could spin tales of war with a grim relish, and a relentless critic of womankind, his bitterness hardening into the armor of age.

Jerome, weary of the northern lassitude, fled south to Italy. His noble connections opened doors that others could not fathom, and by the ripe age of seventy, he sat upon a mitre as an Abbot. He tightened the screw of discipline upon his order; his monks loved him with a terrifying reverence and loathed him with equal fervor. For ten years, he ruled with an iron rod, unyielding and absolute. Then, one night, he died alone. He had failed to find the path to a single human heart, save perhaps God's. The Vulgate lay open on his pillow, the crucifix gripped in his hand, and upon his lips rested something resembling a smile—a look unseen during his earthly life. It seems that in those awful final moments, he was not quite solitary. *Requiescat in pace.* The Master serves many masters, and they possess many minds; sometimes, the most faithful servant is the most surly soul, a truth as ancient as the mortal houses we inhabit.

And now, the golden-haired lad, Gerard Gerardson, steps forward. He does not belong to the realm of fiction, but to history. The ink of the chroniclers has already marked his birth, inscribing over the tailor's house in Brede Kirk Straet the words: *"Here stands the small house where the Great Erasmus was born."* Scribes have penned half a dozen lives of him, yet even they admit they cannot fully grasp the magnitude of *Magnum Erasmum*. No pygmy can comprehend a giant, nor a partisan understand a judge, and so few have truly understood him.

He was first and foremost the scholar and divine of his era, but he was also the heaven-born dramatist of his century. Many of the finest scenes woven into this new tapestry are drawn directly from his medieval pen, illuminating the pages where they dwell. For the words of a genius so towering as his are not born to perish. Once their immediate work upon mankind is done, they may seem to lie dormant, buried under the dust of libraries. But with every fresh shower of intelligence that Time pours upon the students of the past, these words awaken. They prove their immortal lineage again and again. They rise from the ashes, they bud, they flower, they bear fruit, and they sow seeds, generation after generation, age after age, ensuring that the light they cast shall never truly fade.
