It was close on four o’clock in the afternoon, when low golden light slanted through Tergou’s cobblestone lanes. Eli the hosier was busy in his shop at the front of the house, his two eldest sons out running errands for the day. Catherine and her little crippled daughter Kate had fretted over their missing son Gerard for hours, and had wandered a little way down the high road, craning their necks for any glimpse of him coming over the distant hill. Left entirely alone in the family sitting room was Giles, whose odd form and favourite trick of the moment we will pause to sketch in full.

The Dutch have long been an innovative, leading people—they lay claim to inventing woodblock printing, oil painting, modern liberty, banking, formal gardening, and above all, a famously rigorous standard of cleanliness long before the rest of Europe caught on. Where across the Channel, English gentry in velvet jerkins and pointed-toed shoes trod over floor rushes that sat unchanged for months, crusted with gnawed bones, rotting scraps, spittle, eggshells and all manner of foul refuse, this humble hosier’s sitting room was floored with bright, glazed Dutch tile, washed so often and carefully that you could eat a meal straight off its surface. One large leaded window took up most of the far wall, its central cross-shaped stone mullion carved so bold and raised that the whole household crossed themselves and murmured a short prayer whenever they caught sight of it. Its small lozenge-shaped panes were soldered together with thin strips of lead, the same design you can still spot in rural Dutch cottages to this day. All the chairs in the room were simple, rough-hewn pieces, save for one towering oak armchair: its back stood so perfectly perpendicular to its seat that the sitter’s head would stop a full two feet short of its intricately carved acanthus crest. Beside it sat a waisted copper pail of holy water, with a small hand aspergillum for sprinkling it over the household and its goods, and a long, heavy oak dining table.

It was this table that little Giles clung to now, his jaws clamped tight around its rim, his eyes bulging, his stubby claw-like hands flailing in the air like a vampire mid-leap. Nature, it seemed, had not made Giles a dwarf out of deliberate cruelty: she had crafted his head and torso with all her usual care, only to be distracted halfway through shaping his lower half, leaving the rest to random chance. The result was a human wedge, an inverted cone, that made no sense by any ordinary rule of proportion. Bisected at the waist, the top half of Giles would have outweighed the three times the lower half. That very disproportion, though, let him pull off feats that would have stumped even the legendary strongman Milo. His powerfully brawny arms had almost no lower body weight to drag behind them, so he could shin up a vertical pole like a squirrel, and hang for hours from a bough by one hand like a cherry dangling from its stalk. If he could have created a vacuum with his palms the way lizards supposedly do with their toes, he would have been able to walk across the ceiling. This pocket-sized athlete’s greatest joy, lately, had been to grip the edge of the dining table and swing back and forth, then—at the peak of delight—he would release his hands and hang suspended by nothing but his strong, sharp teeth.

All pleasures, though, are prone to interruption. Little Kate hobbled into the room and caught Sampsonet mid-swing, and stood aghast. She was every inch her mother’s daughter, her heart set on protecting the fine furniture, not on marvelling at a twelve-inch gymnast’s tricks.
“Oh Giles! How can you do that? Mother will be home any minute. You’ll dent the table edge for good.”
“Go on then, run tell her, you little tale-bearer,” Giles snarled, not bothering to release his grip. “You’re the biggest mischief-maker in all Tergou, everyone knows it.”
“Am I?” Kate asked calmly. “That is new to me.”
“Biggest there is,” he growled, chomping down harder on the oak.
“Oh, indeed,” she said drily.

That sally of unusual satire landed, and Giles was still very much unblasted, so Kate simply sat down on a plain wooden chair and began to cry. Her mother stepped through the door at that very moment, and Giles scrambled to hurl himself under the table, where he glared out from the shadows.
“What on earth is the matter now?” Catherine said sharply. She glanced from Kate’s tear-streaked face to the telltale shadow under the table, and lifted a threatening hand to hint at cuffed ears.
“Nay, mother,” Kate said quickly. “Giles only spoke a foolish word. I would not have noticed it on any other day, but I am tired, and I have been so worried for Gerard, you know.”
“Let no one waste their worry on me,” a faint, dusty voice called from the doorway. Gerard tottered in, pale, caked in road dust, and worn thin, and before anyone could speak he dropped exhausted into the nearest chair, surrounded by a storm of half-shouted cries of delight, curiosity, and relief.
He had combed every corner of Rotterdam for Margaret, beating through the city like it was a thick game covert, and the long, unbroken walk home had completely drained him. But youth is elastic, and within minutes he was bright again, the eager centre of his whole family’s circle. First they badgered him to tell every last detail of the prize competition.
“Oh mother, oh Kate,” he gushed, “when I walked into the great hall where all the competitors’ work was laid out before the judges, I nearly fainted where I stood at the goldsmiths’ displays. I did not think all the goldsmiths in the world could ever gather so much gold, silver, gemstone, and sheer clever, beautiful craftsmanship. Every art, truly, is divine.” He went on to describe the gleaming reliquaries, feretories, chalices, croziers, monstrances, fine jewelled goblets, pocket clocks, chains and brooches, until his mother and sister sat transfixed, their mouths soft with wonder. “But when I reached the illuminated manuscripts from Ghent and Bruges, my heart sank. My own little submission looked like smudged dirt beside them. For a minute I thought I would cry, but I prayed for a better spirit, and soon enough I could only feel grateful, awed by those patient, skilful craftsmen I count as my masters. I was so lost in the beauty of the coloured pages I quite forgot all about the plain black and white writing entries. But the next day, after every other prize had been handed out, they came around to the writing category, and whose name do you think the herald shouted first?”
“Yours!” Kate cried immediately.
The rest of the family laughed at her certainty.
“You may laugh all you like,” Gerard said, grinning, “but it was Gerard Eliassoen of Tergou he called. I stood there stupid, frozen, until they pushed me forward. Everything swam before my eyes, until I found myself kneeling on a velvet cushion at the feet of the Duke. He spoke some kind words to me, but I was so flustered I could not answer a single one. Then he reached to his side, and instead of drawing his dagger to cut off my dull, stammering head, he hung a heavy gold medal around my neck. Here it is.”
A roar of excitement went up, and the whole family crowded close to see it. “And then he gave me fifteen bright, heavy gold angel coins. I had seen one of those before, but I never once got to hold one. Here they are.”
“Oh Gerard! Oh our Gerard!”
“I’ve split them all fair,” he said, sorting them out. “One for you, Cornelis, one for you Sybrandt, one for you, Giles the Little Mischief. Two for you, my little crippled Lily, because God has laid that burden on you. One for myself, to buy the best vellum and the brightest new coloured inks. The last nine are for you, mother, who nursed all of us, and risked two whole crowns to fund my trip when no one else would.”
The gold pulled every quirk of their characters right to the surface. Cornelis and Sybrandt snatched their coins, their faces tight with greed, then glowered at Kate out of the corners of their eyes, furious that she had been given two pieces. Giles grabbed his, rolled it across the gleaming tile floor, and went whooping after it. Kate set down her crutches, sat soft and slow, and held out her little arms to Gerard in a gesture brimming with tender, uncomplicated love. Catherine, who had been half stunned at first by the shower of gold spilling into her apron, suddenly cried out, “Stop kissing him a minute, Kate, he is my son first! Ah Gerard, my brave boy, I never thought you would grow to be half so worthy.”
Gerard knelt down beside her, and she wound her arms around his neck and wept there, half for joy, half for pride.
“Good lad! Good, steady lad!” Eli the hosier cried, his voice thick with emotion. “I’m off to tell all the neighbours. Lend me that medal a minute, Gerard, I’ll go show my old friend Peter Buyskens. He’s forever badgering me with stories about how his son Jorian won a cheap tin mug at the archery butts.”
“Aye, go show him,” Gerard laughed. “Take one of the angels to show off too. Tell him there’s fourteen more just like it where that came from. But mind you bring it straight back!”
“Wait, father,” Gerard said, flushing bright with joy at the delight he had already brought them. “There is far better news still to come.”
“Better? Better than winning a Duke’s prize and all this gold?”
Gerard told them then of his audience with the Countess, who had secured a fine, well-paid benefice for him to take up once he was ordained. The whole house rang with cheers. “God bless that good lady, and bless the old dame Van Eyck too!” Catherine cried. “A benefice for our son! All my old cares are gone. Eli, my old love, we two can die happy whenever our time comes now. This dear boy will be the mainstay of the family, none of the people we love will ever go wanting for a home or a friend.”
From that hour on, Gerard was regarded as the undisputed pillar of the family. He was a son set apart, always in the right, and nothing was considered too good for him. Cornelis and Sybrandt’s jealousy curdled deeper, and they began counting the days until he left to take up his post at the benefice, certain their favourite brother’s priest’s purse would be wide open to fund all their whims. Gerard, for his part, had no objection to speeding his ordination. The sharp pain of Margaret’s supposed ingratitude he had carried back from Rotterdam throbbed fainter and fainter. His public success, and the open adoration of his parents, made him hold his head higher, and he resented more sharply still the slight he believed Margaret had dealt him. Even so, no other woman could hold a candle to her in his mind, and for every reason he was eager to take holy orders as quickly as he could, moving through the required ranks step by step. He already knew the Vulgate better than most of the local parish clergy, and studied the Church’s rubrics and dogma every evening with his friends the local monastery monks. The very next time the bishop made his visitation to Tergou, Gerard petitioned to be admitted to the order of exorcist, the third of the minor holy orders. The bishop quizzed him at length, astonished at his depth of learning, and ordained him on the spot. Gerard knelt, the bishop said a short blessing prayer, then handed him a small handwritten manuscript full of exorcism rites. “Take this, Gerard,” he said, “and receive power to lay hands on the possessed, whether they be baptized or catechumen.” Gerard took the little book reverently, walking home with the formal authority of the Church to cast out demons settled soft and weighty on his shoulders.
He had barely stepped over his own threshold when little Kate hobbled up to him on her crutches, breathless.
“Oh Gerard! Guess who has been to the house looking for you? The burgomaster himself.”
“Ghysbrecht Van Swieten? What could he possibly want with me?”
“I do not know, Gerard. But he is most urgent to see you. He says you are to come to his house at once.”
“He is the burgomaster, so I will go,” Gerard said, his face darkening. “But I do not like this. I have seen him cast a look at me no friend would ever wear. No matter. Such looks are a warning to the wise. Though I suspect—”
“Suspect what, Gerard?”
“Nothing,” he said, shaking his head. “Nothing at all. Come, little one. I will go.”
