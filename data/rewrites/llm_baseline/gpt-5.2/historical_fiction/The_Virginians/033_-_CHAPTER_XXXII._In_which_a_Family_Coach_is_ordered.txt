The secret which Colonel Lambert had whispered—half in mirth, half in that hopeful domestic daring which passes for romance in a country-house—could not be held long within Mrs. Lambert’s ear. It flew from her like the scent from a torn bunch of lavender; and in a moment the whole of Oakhurst seemed to inhale it and grow brisk.

The hay was stacked, the corn still stood green and unready, and the farm horses, for once, were not dragged and lathered by the day’s labour. “Why,” asked the Colonel, with a simplicity that made a holiday sound like a duty, “should we not put them into the coach, and carry all of you to Tunbridge Wells? We will take friend Wolfe at Westerham on the way.”

It was proposed as one proposes a feast when the pantry is not bare—an act of management as much as indulgence. Mrs. Lambert embraced the plan with a tenderness that very likely included the planner. The children, who could never keep still when joy came in, clapped and leapt as if the very flags of the hall had turned to a dancing-floor.

Instantly the girls were in a rustle and flutter of gear: calamancoes and paduasoys, falbalas and furbelows, capes, cardinals, sacks, caps, ribbons, mantuas, clocked stockings, and those high-heeled shoes which make a country step seem more courtly than it is. Mrs. Lambert’s best gowns were brought out from their lavender darkness—those grave, sweet-smelling robes that came forth only on solemn days, as if cloth could keep a Sabbath. The Colonel fetched his laced hat and waistcoat and his silver-hilted hanger; Charley, radiant, got into a holiday suit that had once been his father’s wedding bravery, and which Mrs. Lambert cut and altered with a little sigh, as though a garment could feel time passing through it.

Even the horses were dressed for the occasion. Ball and Dumpling had their tails and manes tied with ribbon, as if they too must be made conscious of company. Old Chump, the white cart-horse, was pressed into service as unicorn-leader, to give a steady pull up the first five hilly miles from Oakhurst towards Westerham, where a coach’s pride is apt to become a coach’s complaint.

The coach itself was ancient—one of those heavy, high-swung family chariots which seem built not to go but to be seen going. It was believed at Oakhurst, with a faith as firm as any creed, that this very vehicle had once been in the procession which brought King George the First from Greenwich to London. It had belonged to Mr. Lambert’s father; and the household, from the oldest down to the newest-born, had always spoken of it as one of the finest coaches in the three kingdoms. A family may be poor in ready money and yet rich in remembered splendours; and this coach was a kind of moving monument to what the Lamberts had been.

Brian—coachman, and, truth to tell, ploughman too—mounted the box; and Charley, by a right which only the young can claim without paying for it, sat by his side. The precious clothes were packed in imperials on the roof. The Colonel’s pistols were stowed into the pockets of the carriage, and the blunderbuss hung behind the box within Brian’s reach; for he was an old soldier, and liked the feel of defence as much as another man likes the feel of a warm loaf.

No highwayman, however, took the trouble of trying them. Not even an innkeeper made war upon Colonel Lambert’s slender purse; for there is a kind of poverty so plainly honest, and so plainly burdened with children, that even knavery passes it by. Young Colonel Wolfe, who had engaged for them a modest lodging at Tunbridge in the house where he himself commonly stayed, rode with them on horseback, and saw them safely to their quarters.

It happened that these lodgings stood opposite Madame Bernstein’s; and the family had scarcely set down their bags on a Saturday evening when the windows of the great world opened itself before them as if it were a show. Chair after chair came shouldering through the Pantiles, discharging powdered beaux and patched and brocaded beauties at the Baroness’s door; for she was holding one of those card-parties that were her trade as much as her pleasure.

The sun was not yet down. Our ancestors began their dissipations early, and were at meat, drink, or cards any time after three o’clock until any time in the night or morning. From their window, Mrs. Lambert and the girls could see the arrivals as distinctly as if they themselves had been invited. Colonel Wolfe, attentive, named the characters as they passed; it was almost as delicious, Hetty and Theo thought, as being in the room—perhaps more delicious, because one could watch without being watched.

When the Duchess of Queensberry went by, Mr. Wolfe told them who she was, and Martin Lambert, never at a loss for the garnish to any dish of life, was ready with lines out of his beloved Prior about “Kitty, beautiful and young.” The Colonel, pleased with his own recollection and with the girls’ bright listening faces, cried, “Think that that old lady was once like you, girls!”

“Like us, papa?” said Hetty, tossing up her little head. “Well, certainly we never set up for being beauties!”

“Yes, like you, you little baggage,” he answered, laughing; “like you at this moment, who want to go to that drum yonder:—‘Inflamed with rage at sad restraint / Which wise mamma ordained, / And sorely vexed to play the saint / Whilst wit and beauty reigned.’”

“We were never invited, papa,” Hetty returned. “And I am sure if there’s no beauty more worth seeing than that, the wit can’t be much worth the hearing.”

“Oh, but he’s a rare poet, Mat Prior!” the Colonel persisted, pleased to teach and tease at once. “Though, mind you, girls, you’ll skip over all the poems I have marked with a cross. A rare poet! and to think you should see one of his heroines! ‘Fondness prevailed, mamma gave way’—she always will, Mrs. Lambert!—‘Fondness prevailed, mamma gave way, / Kitty at heart’s desire / Obtained the chariot for a day, / And set the world on fire!’”

“I am sure it must have been very inflammable,” said Mrs. Lambert, smiling.

“So it was, my dear, twenty years ago—much more inflammable than it is now,” the Colonel remarked, with the air of a philosopher who measures time by the burn of youth.

“Nonsense, Mr. Lambert.”

“Look, look!” cried Hetty, darting forward, and pointing to the little square and the covered gallery where the door to Madame Bernstein’s apartments stood, ringed by street-urchins, idlers, and country yokels craning to see.

“It’s Harry Warrington!” Theo exclaimed; and she waved her handkerchief with that frank, impulsive joy which cannot help proclaiming itself. But Warrington did not see her. He walked arm-in-arm with a portly clergyman in a crisp, rustling silk gown, and the two passed in beneath Madame Bernstein’s door.

“I heard him preach a most admirable sermon here last Sunday,” said Mr. Wolfe. “A little theatrical, but striking and eloquent.”

“You seem to be here most Sundays, James,” Mrs. Lambert observed gently.

“And Monday, and soon till Saturday,” added the Colonel.

“See, Harry has beautified himself already,” said the Colonel, peering, “hath his hair in buckle, and I have no doubt is going to the drum too.”

“I had rather sit quiet generally of a Saturday evening,” said sober Mr. Wolfe, “at any rate, away from card-playing and scandal; but I own, dear Mrs. Lambert, I am under orders. Shall I go across and send Mr. Warrington to you?”

“No,” said meek Mrs. Lambert. “Let him have his sport. We shall see him to-morrow. He won’t care to be disturbed amidst his fine folks by us country-people.”

“I am glad he is with a clergyman who preaches so well,” Theo said softly; and her eyes pleaded on Harry’s behalf, as if virtue were something one might gather for another, like herbs for a broth.

“The clergyman has a very kind, handsome face.”

“Here comes a greater clergyman,” cried Mr. Wolfe. “My Lord of Salisbury, with his blue ribbon, and a chaplain behind him.”

“And whom a mercy’s name have we here?” Mrs. Lambert broke in, as a sedan-chair came up all gilding and insolence, topped with five earl’s coronets, carried by bearers in richly laced clothes, and preceded by three footmen in the same splendour.

The Bishop, who had been about to enter, stopped, turned, and ran back with bows and curtseys so low that respect seemed like servility. He gave his hand to the lady who stepped from the chair.

“Who on earth is this?” asked Mrs. Lambert, amazed.

“Sprechen sie Deutsch? Ja, meinherr. Nichts verstand,” said the Colonel, wagging his head with mocking gravity.

“Pooh, Martin.”

“Well, if you can’t understand High Dutch, my love, how can I help it? Your education was neglected at school. Can you understand heraldry?—I know you can.”

“I make,” cried Charley, eager as if reciting a recipe learnt by heart, “three merions on a field or, with an earl’s coronet.”

“A countess’s coronet, my son. The Countess of Yarmouth, my son.”

“And pray who is she?” asked Mrs. Lambert.

“It hath ever been the custom of our sovereigns,” the Colonel began solemnly, “to advance persons of distinction to honour; and this eminent lady hath been so promoted by our gracious monarch, to the rank of Countess of this kingdom.”

“But why, papa?” the daughters asked together.

“Never mind, girls,” said mamma quickly, with that instinct which tries to keep the young from learning too early the vocabulary by which power disguises itself.

But the Colonel was incorrigible. “Y, my children, is one of the last and the most awkward letters of the whole alphabet. When I tell you stories, you are always saying Why. Why should my Lord Bishop be cringing to that lady? Look at him rubbing his fat hands and smiling into her face! It’s not a handsome face any longer. It is all painted red and white like Scaramouch’s in the pantomime. See, there comes another blue-riband, as I live. My Lord Bamborough. The descendant of the Hotspurs. The proudest man in England. He stops, he bows, he smiles; he is hat in hand, too. See, she taps him with her fan. Get away, you crowd of little blackguard boys, and don’t tread on the robe of the lady whom the King delights to honour.”

“But why does the King honour her?” the girls insisted.

“There goes that odious last letter but one!” cried the Colonel. “Did you ever hear of her Grace the Duchess of Kendal? No. Of the Duchess of Portsmouth? Non plus. Of the Duchess of La Vallière? Of Fair Rosamond, then?”

“Hush, papa!” Mrs. Lambert pleaded, putting a finger to his lips. “There is no need to bring blushes on the cheeks of my dear ones, Martin Lambert!”

“’Tis not I; it is their sacred Majesties who are the cause of the shame,” the Colonel cried, his old republican blood rising. “Think of the bishops of the Church and the proudest nobility of the world cringing and bowing before that painted High Dutch Jezebel. Oh, it’s a shame! a shame!”

“Confusion!” broke out Colonel Wolfe; and snatching at his hat, he hurried from the room. He had seen the young lady he admired and her guardian walking across the Pantiles on foot toward the Baroness’s party; they came up while the Countess was still in talk with the two blue-ribboned lords, spiritual and temporal, who made the lowest reverences and waited to escort her in upon the Bishop’s arm.

Theo turned away from the window, her face suddenly grave, as if a cold draft had gone through her. Hetty stayed, rigid with indignation, a little red spot flaming on each cheek.

“A penny for little Hetty’s thoughts,” said mamma, coming to draw her from the casement.

“I am thinking what I should do if I saw papa bowing to that woman,” said Hetty, between anger and fear, as if she tasted something bitter and could not spit it out.

Tea was brought in; the kettle hissed like a small domestic serpent, and the family gathered to their evening meal. The table—plain enough, but honest—was set with that careful housewifery which makes even a lodging look for an hour like home. If across the way there were champagne and chickens, here there was the clean scent of brewed leaves, warm bread, and the mild comfort of being together. Yet Hetty, from her chair, begged that the window should not be closed, so she might keep her command of the show.

Charley had been down amongst the crowd, no doubt to read armorial bearings on sedans with as much relish as if they were chapters in a chronicle; and by his mother’s order and his own appetite, he invested sixpence in a cheese-cake. He came back with it wrapped in paper, the sweet warm smell rising as the crust broke under his fingers.

“Look, mother,” he said, mouth already hopeful, “do you see that big man in brown beating all the pillars with a stick? That is the learned Mr. Johnson. He comes to the Friars sometimes to see our master. He was sitting with some friends just now at the tea-table before Mrs. Brown’s tart-shop. They have tea there, twopence a cup; I heard Mr. Johnson say he had had seventeen cups—that makes two-and-tenpence—what a sight of money for tea!”

“What would you have, Charley?” asked Theo.

“I think I would have cheese-cakes,” said Charley with a sigh of devotion, as his teeth closed on a large slice; and, speaking as if the cake did not fill all his world, he went on: “and the gentleman whom Mr. Johnson was with was Mr. Richardson who wrote—”

“Clarissa!” cried all the women together, and ran to the window as if called by a bell.

By now the sun was down, and the stars pricked through; opposite, the footman began lighting candles in the Baroness’s rooms, so that the great chamber became a bright aquarium, with figures moving and glittering behind glass. Down by Mrs. Brown’s tart- and tea-shop there was a small illumination too, and by it the Lamberts could see one lady fetching Mr. Richardson’s hat and stick, and another tying a shawl about his neck before he walked away.

“Oh dear me! he does not look like Grandison!” cried Theo, dismayed, as if the author’s body had betrayed his book.

“I rather think I wish we had not seen him, my dear,” said mamma, who loved novels with a tender, hungry faith, as if printed words could keep one warm.

“Never mind that little fat man,” Hetty cried sharply, “but look yonder, mamma.”

And they looked, and saw Mr. Warrington receiving the honour of a presentation to the Countess of Yarmouth, who was still followed by her obsequious peer and prelate. The Countess sat down to a card-table; the Bishop, the Earl, and a fourth person took places as her partners. Presently Mr. Warrington stepped into the embrasure of a window with a lady whom they recognised—Lady Maria Esmond, whom they had seen for a few minutes at Oakhurst.

“How much finer he is!” remarked mamma. “How he is improved in his looks! What has he done to himself?”

“Look at his grand lace frills and ruffles!” cried the matron, half amused, half a little wounded. “My dear, he has not got on our shirts any more.”

“What are you talking about, girls?” asked papa from his sofa, where a doze had perhaps stolen upon him in the honest way of household fathers.

They told him Harry Warrington was visible, talking with his cousin Lady Maria.

“Come away!” cried the Colonel, rousing. “You have no right to be spying the young fellow. Down with the curtains, I say!”

And down the curtains went, shutting out that brilliant, noisy world as if one had closed a lid upon a glittering box.

If you will allow a remark, only by way of contrast between the two opposite houses: while Madame Bernstein and her guests—bishop, dignitaries, noblemen and such—were gambling or talking scandal, or devouring champagne and chickens (which I hold to be a venial sin), and doing honour to the King’s favourite, our country friends knelt around their own table. Even Brian the coachman came in as silently as his creaking shoes could manage; and Mr. Lambert, standing, read in a low voice the prayer that Heaven would lighten their darkness and defend them from the perils of the night, and grant the request of those few gathered together. The words had an old cadence, like a remembered dialect—something that does not glitter, but sustains. Outside, the Pantiles murmured with chairs, laughter, and the continual metallic clip of a town that sells amusement; within, the small, human hush of a family made its own music.

On Sunday morning the young folks were up betimes, in their smart dresses meant to fascinate Tunbridge. With brother Charley as escort, they paced the little town and the quaint Pantiles and the pretty common long before the company was at breakfast or the bells had rung to church. The morning had that clean sweetness which seems to taste of milk before it is even poured, and of grass before it is cut—a countryside flavour that Tunbridge could not quite buy away.

It was Hester who discovered where Harry Warrington lodged, by spying Mr. Gumbo at a window, in undress, with his lovely hair set in curl-papers. He drew aside a pair of red curtains and opened the sash, thrusting out his head to inhale the breeze as if it were a delicacy. He did not see the Oakhurst party, though they saw him well enough. He leaned gracefully; he waved a great feather-brush, and condescended to dust the furniture within; he chatted with a cherry-cheeked milkmaid lingering under the casement, and kissed his lily hand to her. That hand glittered with rings; his person blazed with jewellery—gifts, no doubt, from those who found novelty more moving than virtue.

Once or twice before breakfast the girls passed that window again. It stood open, but the room behind it was blank. No Harry appeared. Neither sister spoke of what both were thinking. Hetty, already in a mood, grew provoked with Charley, who clamoured for breakfast and seemed to her to care for nothing but eating.

“You are always thinking of it!” she cried.

He, artless as a child with a full mouth, owned he should like another cheese-cake.

Good-natured Theo laughed. “I have a sixpence,” she said. “If the cake-shop is open on a Sunday morning, Charley shall have one.”

The cake-shop was open. Theo drew out her little purse—netted by her dearest friend at school—and there lay her pocket-piece, her grandmother’s guinea, a scant store of shillings, and some copper at one end. She treated Charley to the thing he loved; and the tart, warm smell of it, the slight crack of the crust, the soft sweet within—these small, edible certainties made the morning feel safe, even when hearts were not.

A great deal of fine company was at church. There was the funny old Duchess; old Madame Bernstein with Lady Maria beside her; Mr. Wolfe, of course, close by Miss Lowther, singing with her out of the same psalm-book; and Mr. Richardson with a bevy of ladies. One of them, papa told them afterwards, was Miss Fielding, Harry Fielding’s sister.

“Oh, girls, what good company he was!” the Colonel cried after church, his spirits rising as if the sermon had been a glass of wine. “And his books are worth a dozen of your milksop Pamelas and Clarissas, Mrs. Lambert; but what woman ever loved true humour? And there was Mr. Johnson sitting amongst the charity children. Did you see how he turned round to the altar at the Belief, and upset two or three of the scared little urchins in leather breeches? And what a famous sermon Harry’s parson gave, didn’t he? A sermon about scandal. How he touched up some of the old harridans seated round! Why wasn’t Mr. Warrington at church? It was a shame he wasn’t at church.”

“I really did not remark whether he was there or not,” said Miss Hetty, tossing her head.

But Theo, who could not help being true even when truth pricked, said quietly, “Yes, I thought of him, and was sorry he was not there; and so did you think of him, Hetty.”

“I did no such thing, miss,” Hetty persisted.

“Then why did you whisper to me it was Harry’s clergyman who preached?”

“To think of Mr. Warrington’s clergyman is not to think of Mr. Warrington,” Hetty declared, fencing with words as if they were weapons. “It was a most excellent sermon, certainly, and the children sang most dreadfully out of tune.”

And indeed the little choir’s piping and the charity children’s dragged notes made a kind of rough music—honest, but painful—like a countryside dialect mangled by those who only half know it. Theo winced at it, not from pride, but from pity; for there is something in wrong sound that makes the heart uneasy, as if peace itself depends upon harmony.

“There is Lady Maria at the window opposite, smelling at the roses,” Hetty went on, eager to change the subject. “And that is Mr. Wolfe’s step—I know his great military tramp. Right left—right left! How do you do, Colonel Wolfe?”

Mr. Wolfe entered with a face overcast.

“Why do you look so glum, James?” asked Colonel Lambert. “Has the charmer been scolding thee, or is thy conscience pricked by the sermon. Mr. Sampson, isn’t the parson’s name? A famous preacher, on my word!”

“A pretty preacher, and a pretty practitioner!” said Mr. Wolfe with a shrug.

“Why, I thought the discourse did not last ten minutes,” said the Colonel, “and madam did not sleep one single wink during the sermon, didst thou, Molly?”

“Did you see when the fellow came into church?” Mr. Wolfe demanded, his indignation gathering voice. “He came in at the open door of the common, just in time, and as the psalm was over.”

“Well, he had been reading the service probably to some sick person,” Mrs. Lambert said, always charitable first, as if charity were her native tongue. “There are many here.”

“Reading the service! Oh, my good Mrs. Lambert!” Mr. Wolfe cried. “Do you know where I found him? I went to look for your young scapegrace of a Virginian.”

“His own name is a very pretty name, I’m sure,” burst out Hetty, hotly. “It isn’t Scapegrace! It is Henry Esmond Warrington, Esquire.”

“Miss Hester,” said Mr. Wolfe, stung into plainness, “I found the parson in his cassock, and Henry Esmond Warrington, Esquire, in his bedgown, at a quarter before eleven o’clock in the morning, when all the Sunday bells were ringing, and they were playing over a game of piquet they had had the night before!”

“Well, numbers of good people play at cards of a Sunday. The King plays at cards of a Sunday,” cried Hetty, reckless with her own anger.

“Hush, my dear!”

“I know he does,” Hetty insisted, “with that painted person we saw yesterday—that Countess what-d’you-call-her?”

“I think, my dear Miss Hester,” said Mr. Wolfe, “a clergyman had best take to God’s books instead of the Devil’s books on that day—and so I took the liberty of telling your parson.”

Hetty looked as if she thought it a liberty indeed.

“And I told our young friend,” Mr. Wolfe went on, “that I thought he had better have been on his way to church than there in his bedgown.”

“You wouldn’t have Harry go to church in a dressing-gown and nightcap, Colonel Wolfe?” Hetty cried, fiercely. “That would be a pretty sight, indeed!”

“I would have my little girl’s tongue not wag quite so fast,” remarked papa, laying a hand on her flushed cheek.

“Not speak when a friend is attacked, and nobody says a word in his favour? No; nobody!” Hetty cried.

Her lips clamped shut; her whole small frame shook. She flung a last look of defiance at Mr. Wolfe and hurried out, just in time to reach the stair before her self-command broke into sobbing.

Mr. Wolfe stood discomfited. “I am sure, Aunt Lambert, I did not intend to hurt Hester’s feelings.”

“No, James,” she said kindly—he had called her Aunt Lambert since early days—and she gave him her hand.

Mr. Lambert, to cover what could not be mended, began whistling “Over the hills and far away,” tapping a little drum accompaniment on the window with his fingers.

“I say, you mustn’t whistle on Sunday, papa!” cried Charley, literal as a rubric; and then, with a logic as steady as hunger, suggested it was three hours from breakfast and he should like to finish Theo’s cheese-cake.

“Oh, you greedy child!” cried Theo; yet even her scold had laughter in it.

But at a small sound outside—half sob, half strangled breath—Theo slipped away, closing the door behind her. We will not pursue her. That noise was the overflow of Hetty’s heart; and though we cannot see it, one may be sure the child flung herself on her sister’s neck and wept into Theo’s kind bosom, as into the only safe place in Tunbridge.

Hetty did not walk out in the afternoon when the family took the air upon the common. She pleaded a headache and lay upon her bed, while her mother watched her with that soft patience which is a sort of silent nursing. Charley found a comrade from Grey Friars; Mr. Wolfe, of course, paired off with Miss Lowther; and Theo, taking her sober walk with her father in the Sabbath sunshine, met Madame Bernstein herself basking on a bench under a tree, with her niece and nephew in attendance.

Harry ran up to greet his dear friends. He was radiant at seeing them—his pleasure so honest that for a moment all the talk of cards and bedgowns fell away like a bad dream. Madame Bernstein and Lady Maria were most gracious to the Colonel and his wife, thanking them by looks and words for their kindness to Harry. In that clear light, among the faint rustle of leaves and the distant murmur of Tunbridge voices—wheels far off, a hawker calling, the small, natural sound of a place breathing—Theo thought how noble and handsome he looked. She called him by his Christian name as if he were truly her brother.

“Why did we not see you sooner to-day, Harry?” she asked.

“I never thought you were here, Theo,” he said.

“But you might have seen us if you wished.”

“Where?” asked Harry.

“There, sir,” she said, pointing to the church.

She lifted her hand as if in reproof; yet a sweet kindness shone in her face, as though she scolded only because she cared. And if a young man is to be saved from the louder world—its glittering rooms, its false languages of honour and advancement—perhaps it is not by sermons only, but by the remembered look of a pure heart that calls him by his true name.
