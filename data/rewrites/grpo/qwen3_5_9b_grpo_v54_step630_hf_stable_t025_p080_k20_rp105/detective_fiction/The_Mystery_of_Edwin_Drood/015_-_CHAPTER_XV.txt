Neville Landless had risen before the dawn, his boots clicking a steady rhythm against the cobblestones of Cloisterham. By the time the church bells tolled for morning service, he was already eight miles out, his stomach growling in protest against the crust of bread he had eaten on the road. He pulled his coat tighter and sought refuge at the nearest roadside inn, the *Tilted Wagon*.

The establishment lived up to its name in every sense of the word. It sat precariously atop a hill, a painted facade promising comfort that the reality beneath denied. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of damp fagots and stale ale. A scolding landlady, her apron stained, slapped a baby in the corner—one sock red, the other missing—while the cheese on the shelf seemed to weep alongside a moldy tablecloth. It was a place where men and beasts alike were treated as transient inconveniences, yet Neville, weary and hungry, accepted the meager tea and toast with a grateful silence.

Once fed, he stepped outside to survey the path ahead. The road stretched straight and level, but a cart track cut through the heath, winding between high hedgerows. Neville chose the latter, the ascent steep and rutted, demanding every ounce of his strength. Halfway up, the silence of the heath was broken.

He sensed them first—a pressure in the air, a shift in the wind. Turning, he saw four figures approaching rapidly, their strides long and purposeful. Behind them, four more slowed their pace, lagging as if reluctant to commit. Further back, a dozen others turned and retreated into the shadows.

Neville froze against the bank, watching the procession. Four passed him, then four more emerged from the brush, closing the gap. They formed a phalanx, eight men surrounding him on the open slope. There was no escape.

"Why do you attend upon me in this way?" Neville called out, his voice trembling with a mix of fear and indignation. "Are you a pack of thieves?"

"Don't answer him," one of the men muttered, his voice low and urgent. "Better be quiet."

"Better be quiet?" Neville repeated, his anger flaring. "Who said so?"

No one replied. The silence was heavier than the threat.

"It's good advice, whichever of you skulkers gave it," Neville spat, stepping forward. "I will not submit to be penned in between four men there and four men here. I wish to pass, and I mean to pass!"

The men did not move. Neville, enraged by their stony defiance, shouldered his heavy walking stick and charged toward the lead group. The largest man among them shifted instantly, intercepting Neville with a practiced ease. The clash was sudden and brutal; Neville's stick descended with a sharp crack, but the attacker caught him mid-swing.

"Let him be!" the man commanded, his voice suppressed but firm. "Fair play! His is the build of a girl to mine, and he's got a weight strapped to his back besides. Let him alone. I'll manage him."

A scuffle ensued, rolling across the grass until both men were breathless and bloodied. Finally, the attacker disengaged, wiping a smear of crimson from his cheek. "There! Now take him arm-in-arm, any two of you!"

Immediately, two of the men seized Neville's arms, holding him fast.

"As to our being a pack of thieves, Mr. Landless," the leader said, spitting blood onto the ground, "you know better than that at midday. We wouldn't have touched you if you hadn't forced us. We're going to take you round to the high road, anyhow, and you'll find help enough against thieves there, if you want it. Wipe his face, somebody; see how it's a-trickling down him!"

As his face was cleansed, Neville recognized the speaker. It was Joe, the driver of the Cloisterham omnibus, a man he had seen only once before, on the day of his arrival.

"And what I recommend you for the present is, don't talk, Mr. Landless," Joe instructed. "You'll find a friend waiting for you at the high road—gone ahead by the other way when we split into two parties—and you had much better say nothing till you come up with him. Bring that stick along, somebody else, and let's be moving!"

Bewildered and speechless, Neville allowed himself to be led. Walking between his captors, he drifted like a dreamwalker until they reached the main road, where a small group of people waited. Among them were Mr. Jasper and Mr. Crisparkle. The men released Neville with a deferential bow to the Minor Canon.

"What is all this, sir? What is the matter? I feel as if I had lost my senses!" Neville cried, collapsing slightly as the crowd closed in.

"Where is my nephew?" Mr. Jasper demanded, his voice wild with panic.

"Where is your nephew?" Neville echoed, confused. "Why do you ask me?"

"I ask you," Jasper retorted, his eyes narrowing, "because you were the last person in his company, and he is not to be found."

"Not to be found!" Neville gasped, horror dawning on him.

"Stay, stay," Mr. Crisparkle interjected, placing a calming hand on Neville's shoulder. "Permit me, Jasper. Mr. Neville, you are confounded; collect your thoughts; it is of great importance that you should collect your thoughts; attend to me."

"I will try, sir, but I seem mad," Neville admitted, clutching his head.

"You left Mr. Jasper last night with Edwin Drood?" Crisparkle asked gently.

"Yes."

"At what hour?"

"Was it at twelve o'clock?" Neville asked, turning to Jasper for confirmation.

"Quite right," Crisparkle confirmed. "The hour Mr. Jasper has already named to me. You went down to the river together?"

"Undoubtedly. To see the action of the wind there."

"What followed? How long did you stay there?"

"About ten minutes; I should say not more. We then walked together to your house, and he took leave of me at the door."

"Did he say that he was going down to the river again?"

"No. He said that he was going straight back."

The bystanders exchanged uneasy glances. Then, Jasper's gaze locked onto Neville's clothes. "What are those stains upon his dress?"

All eyes turned to the dark smears on Neville's trousers.

"And here are the same stains upon this stick!" Jasper accused, snatching the walking stick from one of the men. "I know the stick to be his, and he carried it last night. What does this mean?"

"In the name of God, say what it means, Neville!" Crisparkle urged.

"That man and I," Neville pointed to Joe, "had a struggle for the stick just now, and you may see the same marks on him, sir. What was I to suppose, when I found myself molested by eight people? Could I dream of the true reason when they would give me none at all?"

They admitted that they had thought it discreet to remain silent and that the struggle had indeed occurred. Yet, even as they spoke, the men who had witnessed it looked darkly at the drying blood on Neville's clothes, their expressions grim.

"We must return, Neville," Crisparkle said firmly. "Of course you will be glad to come back to clear yourself?"

"Of course, sir," Neville replied.

"Mr. Landless will walk at my side," Crisparkle announced, scanning the crowd. "Come, Neville!"

They set off, the others straggling behind. Jasper walked on Neville's other side, never breaking contact. He remained silent while Crisparkle pressed Neville for details, his face a mask of fixed determination. When they approached the city and Crisparkle suggested calling on the Mayor immediately, Neville nodded sternly but said nothing until they stood in Mr. Sapsea's parlour.

Mr. Sapsea, informed by Crisparkle of the circumstances, broke the tension by declaring his absolute reliance on his own penetration. He argued that there was no conceivable reason for his nephew's sudden disappearance unless Mr. Sapsea could suggest one, and no likelihood of accidental drowning unless it appeared probable to him. He washed his hands of all suspicion, placing the burden of judgment entirely on the Mayor's shoulders.

Mr. Sapsea declared the case had a "dark look," an "Un-English complexion." After wandering through a dense haze of nonsensical legalistic ramblings, he arrived at the brilliant discovery that to take the life of a fellow-creature was to take something that didn't belong to you. He hovered on the brink of issuing a warrant for Neville's arrest, but Crisparkle's indignant protest saved the young man, undertaking to keep him in his own house until produced. Jasper interpreted this as a suggestion to scour the river, examine the banks, send notices to London and the countryside, and publish placards imploring Edwin Drood to reveal himself. These measures were enacted immediately.

It was difficult to determine who was more oppressed by horror: Neville Landless or John Jasper. Jasper's position forced him to be active, while Neville's forced him to be passive, yet both were bowed down and broken.

With the earliest light of the next morning, the search began. Men worked upon the river with barges, poles, drags, and nets. Others combed the muddy, rushy shores with jack-boots, hatchets, spades, ropes, and dogs. Even at night, the river was specked with lanterns, lurid with fires; far-off creeks and lonely causeways were guarded by watchers listening to the lapping stream, but no trace of Edwin Drood revisited the light of the sun.

All day long, the search continued. Now in boats, now ashore among the osiers, tramping amidst mud and jagged stones where solitary watermarks showed like spectres, John Jasper worked and toiled. But to no purpose; still, no sign of Edwin. Setting his watches for the night to ensure vigilant eyes watched every change of tide, Jasper went home exhausted. Unkempt, disordered, bedaubed with dried mud and torn clothing, he collapsed into his easy-chair just as Mr. Grewgious stood before him.

"This is strange news," Grewgious said.

"Strange and fearful news," Jasper replied, lifting his heavy eyes before dropping them again, drooping over the side of his chair.

Grewgious smoothed his head and face, standing silently by the fire.

"How is your ward?" Jasper asked after a time, his voice faint and fatigued.

"Poor little thing! You may imagine her condition," Grewgious answered.

"Have you seen his sister?" Jasper inquired.

"Whose?"

The curtness of the question, coupled with Grewgious's slow movement of his eyes from the fire to Jasper's face, might have been exasperating under normal circumstances. But in his depression, Jasper merely opened his eyes to say, "The suspected young man's."

"Do you suspect him?" Grewgious asked.

"I don't know what to think. I cannot make up my mind."

"Nor I," Grewgious said. "But as you spoke of him as the suspected young man, I thought you *had* made up your mind. I have just left Miss Landless."

"What is her state?"

"Defiance of all suspicion, and unbounded faith in her brother."

"Poor thing!"

"However," Grewgious pursued, "it is not of her that I came to speak. It is of my ward. I have a communication to make that will surprise you. At least, it has surprised me."

Jasper, with a groaning sigh, turned wearily in his chair.

"Shall I put it off till to-morrow?" Grewgious asked, warning him. "Mind, I warn you, that I think it will surprise you!"

Attention sharpened in Jasper's eyes as he caught sight of Grewgious smoothing his head again, looking at the fire with a compressed, determined mouth.

"What is it?" Jasper demanded, sitting upright.

"To be sure," Grewgious said, provokingly slowly, keeping his eyes on the fire. "I might have known it sooner; she gave me the opening; but I am such an exceedingly Angular man, that it never occurred to me; I took all for granted."

"What is it?" Jasper demanded again.

Grewgious, warming his hands by the fire and looking fixedly at Jasper sideways, replied. "This young couple, the lost youth and Miss Rosa, my ward, though so long betrothed, and so long recognising their betrothal, and so near being married—"

Grewgious saw a staring white face and two quivering white lips in the easy-chair, and two muddy hands gripping its sides.

"—This young couple came gradually to the discovery (made on both sides pretty equally, I think), that they would be happier and better, both in their present and their future lives, as affectionate friends, or say rather as brother and sister, than as husband and wife."

Grewgious saw a lead-coloured face in the easy-chair, and on its surface dreadful starting drops or bubbles, as if of steel.
