Elinor discovered, at last, the cruel distinction between bracing oneself for an unhappiness and having it laid, warm and weighty, into one’s hands. She had schooled her mind for months to call Edward’s marriage to Lucy a settled certainty; yet some obstinate portion of her heart—unbidden, unreasoned—had kept a small, living hope like a covered ember: that something would intervene. A resolution of his own; a friend’s mediation; an opening elsewhere for Lucy that would draw her away as surely as a new market draws the most calculating buyer. While he remained unmarried, that ember could be tended in secret. Now, with the report of his marriage, she reproached herself for ever having allowed its heat to exist, because it made the pain sharper, more immediate, as if the news had scorched her.

It startled her, too, that it should have happened so soon—so hurriedly—before he could, as she had imagined, be in orders, and therefore before any living could be his. But the momentary surprise dissolved into something colder and clearer: Lucy’s haste was exactly of a piece with Lucy’s prudence. In securing what she wanted, she would overlook every consideration except the peril of delay. Married in town; already travelling down, as the message said, to her uncle’s. Elinor could not prevent her mind from setting the scene with the exactness of a cook who sees a kitchen she has never entered and yet knows where every pot will be hung: the turn of their future rooms at Delaford; the parsonage hearth; Lucy brisk and busy, contriving at once smart appearance and strict saving—paring, stretching, substituting—ashamed only of being suspected of it, and yet never ashamed of its intention. She saw her courting favour with Colonel Brandon, with Mrs. Jennings, with every person whose purse might be useful—sweetening each connection as one sweetens a tart fruit, for ease of swallowing.

Of Edward in that picture she could not form any steady image. Whatever she tried to see—his contentment, his regret—nothing would stay in place. Every sketch displeased her, and she turned her thoughts away as if from a dish that, once beloved, had been spoiled past appetite.

Days went by without letter or detail. She had expected some hand in London to send particulars, to confirm, to explain; but the post brought nothing that would settle her mind. Though she could not, with justice, fix blame, she found herself resenting the silence of every absent friend, as if their indolence were a personal affront. Her impatience needed action, however small, the way an anxious housekeeper needs the sound of something being done—water set to boil, knives set to work—only to bear the waiting.

“When do you write to Colonel Brandon, ma’am?” she asked one morning, with a restlessness she could not disguise.

“I wrote to him, my love, last week,” Mrs. Dashwood replied, “and rather expect to see him, than to hear from him again. I pressed his coming earnestly, and should not be surprised to see him walk in today or tomorrow, or any day.”

Something to look forward to. Something approaching along the road, like the first faint hoofbeats that reach a quiet house before the rider is seen. Colonel Brandon must know more; Colonel Brandon must, by the very steadiness of his character, bring a steadier account.

She had scarcely settled into that expectation when her eye, turning to the window, caught the figure of a man on horseback at their gate. The animal’s stamp on the hard ground, the brief jingle of tack, the crunch of gravel under the descending boot—those little sounds, so ordinary, fell on her mind with unnatural force. He stopped. For an instant she was sure it was the Colonel. Then her heart corrected her with a shock: the air, the height—no. Again she looked. He had dismounted; the horse was being managed with familiar hands; the very movement of his shoulders was known.

It was Edward.

She moved away and sat down, as if the room had shifted and she must secure herself against falling. The thought came with a desperate command, like an order given in a kitchen when a crisis threatens to ruin the whole service: He comes from Mr. Pratt’s purposely to see us. I will be calm. I will be mistress of myself.

In the same instant she perceived that the others had made the same first mistake and the same correction. She saw her mother and Marianne change colour; saw them glance at her, whisper quickly together. Elinor would have given anything—anything—to speak then, to make them understand that she wished no coolness, no reproach, no awkwardness in their welcome. Whatever he had done or not done, whatever pain he brought with him, she could not bear that he should be met with a studied slight. But her throat was locked; her tongue would not obey. She could only sit and leave their behaviour to their own discretion.

Not a syllable was spoken aloud. They listened. His footsteps came along the gravel path—distinct, unhurried, each step sounding through the quiet like a spoon against a cup in a silent room. He was in the passage; then, almost immediately, before them.

His face, as he entered, held no joy that could have eased Elinor even had she been prepared to see it. He was pale with agitation; his manner had the guarded fear of a man uncertain of his reception and conscious that he did not deserve a kind one. Mrs. Dashwood, in the warmth of her heart resolving to be guided by Elinor’s supposed wishes, went forward with forced composure, gave him her hand, and wished him joy.

He coloured and stammered a reply that could scarcely be understood. Elinor felt her own lips move with her mother’s words, as if they would join them; and once the moment passed, she wished intensely that she too had held out her hand. But it was too late; to do it now would have been pointed, unnatural. With an expression meant to be open, she sat again and spoke of the weather—of the dryness of the season—as if the state of the fields were all that occupied her.

Marianne had retreated as far as she could, half hidden, to conceal distress that would not be governed. Margaret, catching fragments of meaning without comprehending the whole, believed dignity required distance and silence; she placed herself as far from Edward as possible and sat immovably still.

When Elinor had exhausted the subject of the season’s dryness, a pause fell—heavy, awful, filling the room with the kind of quiet in which every breath is heard. Mrs. Dashwood, feeling herself obliged to proceed, asked, with an effort at steadiness, whether he had left Mrs. Ferrars very well.

In a hurried manner he answered yes.

Another pause—longer, more oppressive.

Elinor forced herself to speak again, though she feared the sound of her own voice. “Is Mrs. Ferrars at Longstaple?”

“At Longstaple!” He looked up with real surprise. “No—my mother is in town.”

“I meant,” said Elinor, taking up some work from the table to give her hands occupation, “to enquire for Mrs. Edward Ferrars.”

She did not dare lift her eyes. She felt, rather than saw, the movement beside her; she knew her mother and Marianne were both looking directly at him.

Edward coloured again. He seemed perplexed—hesitating as if he doubted what he had heard and what he was expected to say. At last, with an uncertainty that made the words stumble out, he answered, “Perhaps you mean—my brother—you mean Mrs.—Mrs. Robert Ferrars.”

“Mrs. Robert Ferrars!” Marianne repeated, and her mother with her, in an accent of astonishment so complete it almost seemed to change the air of the room. Even Elinor, who could not utter a sound, fixed her eyes on him with the same impatient wonder.

He rose abruptly and walked to the window, as if he did not know where to place himself. There, with a restless hand, he took up a pair of scissors that lay near, and while—without thought—he cut and mangled the sheath as he spoke, spoiling both in his agitation, he said in a hurried voice, “Perhaps you do not know. You may not have heard that my brother is lately married to—to the youngest—to Miss Lucy Steele.”

The words were echoed—brokenly, incredulously—by all but Elinor. She remained bent over her work, yet the room swam; the meaning struck her with such sudden force that for a moment she scarcely knew where she was.

“Yes,” Edward added, “they were married last week, and are now at Dawlish.”

Elinor could endure the restraint no longer. She rose so quickly that her chair scraped the floor, and almost ran from the room. The moment the door shut behind her, the flood she had held back broke loose. She wept—not with the aching, hopeless tears of loss, but with a joy so sharp and relieving it seemed as if it might never end, as if her whole heart, long compressed, had been given space to breathe again.

Behind her, in the parlour, Edward—who until then had looked anywhere rather than at her—saw her sudden flight, and perhaps he saw, perhaps he even heard, what it meant. After that, he fell into a silence so deep that no remark, no question, no affectionate address from Mrs. Dashwood could reach him. At last, without speaking at all, he left the room and walked out towards the village, leaving Mrs. Dashwood, Marianne, and Margaret in a bewilderment that had no remedy but conjecture—astonished and perplexed by a change in his situation so extraordinary and so swift that it made every former certainty seem like a story misremembered.
