“Correct” was the word you could lay like a clean napkin over Doctor Florret and find it fitted him at every corner.

He was large in the approved manner—built, as it were, to preside over boys and benches; stout, but in the sense that a well-fed joint is stout: comfortable, respectable, meant to be carved without surprise. His hands were white and shaped as though they had been taught penmanship by a dancing-master. On his left finger he wore a diamond that did not so much flash as behave. His voice could give dignity to the rain. His clean-shaven face shone with benevolence—benevolence with a latch on it, so that it should not swing open upon you too far.

So with his household. His wife was his feminine echo: you looked at them together and you felt that nature, having once achieved Doctor Florret, had decided to keep the pattern and merely vary the trimming. Their furniture was solid, like school rules: made to sit upon, not to admire. Their pictures were the pictorial equivalent of “quiet colours.” Everything about him said, “This is how it is done,” and if you waited long enough, you would learn the correct thing to do by observing him do it; at which point it became suddenly obvious that anything else would have been indecent.

In practice, as I discovered at the cost of four years that had the air of being both long and short—long in the evenings, short in the results—Doctor Florret followed the methods considered correct by English schoolmen from somewhere about Edward VI., and, I should imagine, by a good many Egyptian priests before that.

Heaven knows I worked. I wanted to learn. A boy’s ambition is like yeast: it rises in every basin it is put into, and overflows in directions nobody asked it to. If I passed a man haranguing a street-corner—hatless, damp, pointing at Utopias as if they were just round the next lamp-post—I instantly became Demosthenes, then a statesman, then Prime Minister, then a figure so praised that even the Opposition (had I known of the Opposition) would have been forced into reluctant hymn-tunes about me. If a distant drum beat, it became the pulse of battle; and there, in the centre of all carnage and glory, was myself—always myself—conspicuously isolated, promoted with a speed that would have shocked the Horse Guards, returning Commander-in-Chief by sheer merit and the proper arrangement of dreams.

Vast crowds filled the streets in that agreeable roar that is half welcome and half the sound of one’s own blood when one is happy. I rode a charger—snow-white, or coal-black; the question plagued me like an unsolved sum, for I was anxious my day of triumph should not suffer from a bad colour choice. I paced, very slowly, between waves of faces, and then—because dreams are economical—walked into a lamp-post or on to the toes of some irascible old gentleman, and woke.

A drunken sailor, storming from between swing doors, could at once become the captain of my vessel. A factory chimney, belching smoke, turned into a mast. The costers’ shouts were “Ay, ay, sir!” and I, rolled together out of Columbus and Drake and Nelson, discovered new continents and defeated the French—those useful French!—died in the moment of victory, was mourned by the nation, and buried in Westminster Abbey. Alternatively, I lived and was made a Duke. I had no fixed preference; both endings were handsome.

On November the ninth certain boys, by letters from their mothers read aloud by Doctor Florret with a snort, developed severe toothaches. On November the tenth they described the Lord Mayor’s Show to us, and their voices faded as if from the far end of a corridor. The bells of Bow rang in my ears. I became a merchant prince while still young; I lent money to nobles and married their daughters; I listened to people discussing a new book and instantly became a famous author. All men praised me (reviewers, in those days, were still mythical creatures with no teeth). Poetry, fiction, history—I wrote everything, and everyone read and wondered.

Only one small thorn sat in the cushion of my greatness: penmanship was my private tragedy, and spelling a personal riddle. My fame was scratched out and rewritten in blots. More than once I considered abandoning literature in favour of something that did not require me to persuade letters to stand in line.

But whichever road led to the Elysian Fields of applause, education—dimly, stubbornly—I understood as necessary equipment. So I bent myself over books with an aching head and a heart that, at times, felt as if it had been sat upon.

I see that small bedroom still: my elbows planted on a shaky table that had one leg less than it pretended, the solitary candle making a brave show and occasionally frizzling my hair when I leaned too near. On cold nights I wore my overcoat turned up about the neck, and wrapped a blanket round my legs, looking like a refugee from my own bed. Often I put my fingers in my ears—not to shut out thought, but to shut out life: the voices and clatter from below, the domestic racket that rose like steam.

“A song, of a song, to a song—A song, O song!” came up insistently; and then I, in a small buzzing voice of my own that began to seem detached from me, repeated: “I love. Thou lovest. He, she, or it loves. I should or would love,” over and over till my head felt as if it were shrinking, and I put my hands up in alarm to see whether it was still there.

Was I more stupid than the average boy, or is a boy’s brain simply not built to carry the load our educational system packs upon it, like a dishonest porter?

“Latin and Greek,” I can hear Doctor Florret purring, with that solemn croak of Correctness, “are useful as mental gymnastics.” My dear Doctor Florret and your compatriots, could you not select your apparatus from the vast storehouse of actual knowledge, and choose something that strengthens without tearing? You, with your grown brain and your practised wit, take down your Shakespeare or Browning—your own tongue, your mother speech. Construe this passage; parse it, analyse it, divide it into joints as you would divide a tough fowl for soup. Now tell me it is sensible to set a tear-stained, ink-bespattered Tommy to do the same with AEsop or Ovid, as if his little mind were meant to lift one-hundred-pound dumb-bells.

We were not princes training for polite leisure. We were the sons of City men, minor officials, clerks, shopkeepers; our roads led through the workaday world of ledgers and counters. Yet half our time went to studies that, for our likely lives, were as much use as teaching a hungry boy the Latin names of dishes he will never taste.

How I hated them, those youth-tormenting Shades. Homer—how I wished the fishermen had put that absurd riddle to him earlier. Horace—why could not that shipwreck have succeeded? It would have, in any other case.

Then, one blessed day, a talisman came into my hands.

Hearken, ye heavy-burdened little brethren: waste not your substance upon tops and marbles, nor yet upon tuck—if you still call it tuck—but scrape and save. For near Paternoster Row dwells a magician who, for silver, will sell you a Key that opens wide the gates of Hades.

By it, Aristophanes’ Frogs became my cheerful companions. With Ulysses I wandered through Wonderland and understood what I wandered in. Doctor Florret was charmed with my sudden progress—which was real, because at last I did the sane thing: I understood first and explained afterwards. Let youth, whom the folly of age would imprison in ignorance, provide itself with Keys.

But the credit belongs to Dan.

Dan of the strong arm and soft smile; Dan the wise hater of useless labour; sharp-witted, easy-going Dan, who discovered that common sense—properly concealed—could be smuggled even into school.

He followed me a term later into the Lower Fourth, and before he had been there a week he handled Latin verse with an ease suggestive of unholy assistance. One day, in a lonely corner of Regent’s Park—after looking round to make sure no prig was within earshot—he revealed his magic.

“Don’t tell the others,” he commanded; “or it will get out, and then nobody’ll be any the better.”

“But is it right?” I asked.

“Look here, young ’un,” said Dan, with that practical morality of his, “what are you here for—what’s your father paying school fees for? For you to play a silly game, or to learn something?

“Because if it’s only a game—we boys against the masters—then let’s play by rule,” he continued. “But if we’re here to learn—well, you’ve been in the class four months and I’ve just come, and I’ll bet I know more Ovid than you do already.”

Which was true.

So I thanked him and shared his Key; and whatever Latin I remember—whatever good it may ever do me—I take it I owe to Dan.

And more than Latin, I owed him. His sound mother-wit was always at my disposal to correct my dreamy unfeasibility; he was my friend from first to last, and to be the chosen friend of Dan—shrewd judge of man and boy—seems to me no trifling feather in my cap.

He “took to” me, he said, because I was “jolly green”—“such a rummy little mug.” He would never give me a better reason, except a sweet smile and a rough tumble of my hair with his great hand. But I think I understood, in my small way. I loved him because he was big and handsome and kind; no one but a little boy knows how brutal or how kind a big boy can be.

I was still rather an effeminate little chap—nervous and shy—with a pink-and-white face and hair that no amount of wetting could persuade into straightness. I was growing too fast; the speed of it seemed to steal what strength I had. And the daily journey, with school work and home work, was perhaps too much for my years. Every morning I was up at six, out before seven, to catch the 7.15 from Poplar; then, from Chalk Farm, another couple of miles on foot. Yet I did not mind the walking, because at Chalk Farm station Dan was always waiting. In the afternoon we walked back together, and when I was tired and my back ached—as if somebody had cut a piece out of it—he would put his arm round me, for he always knew. How strong and restful it was to lean on! One walked as in an easy-chair.

Remembering how I walked thus by his side, shyly looking up into his face, thinking him so solid and good and feeling so glad he liked me, I can understand a little how a woman loves. With his arm around me, it was sweet to feel weak.

At first we were in the same class, the Lower Third. He had no business there. He was head and shoulders taller than any of us, years older. It was a disgrace that he was not in the Upper Fourth. The Doctor told him so before us all twenty times a week. Old Waterhouse—“Old Waterhouse,” because “Mister Waterhouse, M.A.” would convey nothing to my mind—liked him cordially and was honestly grieved. We, his friends, though it pleased us to have him among us, suffered in our pride of him.

The only person quite contented was Dan himself. That was his way. Other people held opinions on what was good for him; he had his own. And his own was the only opinion that influenced him. The Lower Third suited him. The Upper Fourth had no charm.

Even in the Lower Third he was always at the bottom—by preference. He selected the last seat and kept it in spite of allurements and reproaches. It was nearest the door, first out and last in. It also gave him a sense of retirement: screened a little from observation, the boy at the back becomes, with practice, almost forgotten. To Dan’s philosophical temperament its practical advantages outweighed every sentimental objection.

Only once do I remember him losing it. Generally, tiresome questions—past participles, square roots, meridians—never reached him, being snapped up on the way by arm-waving enthusiasts. The few that trickled to the back he ignored. They bored him and he never pretended they did not.

But one day, off his guard, he gave a correct answer—quite unconsciously—and found himself promoted to the front bench. I had never seen Dan out of temper before, but at that moment I am convinced that any whispered congratulation would have earned a punch.

Old Waterhouse thought he had achieved reform. “Come, Brian,” he cried, rubbing his long thin hands together, “after all you’re not such a fool as you pretend.”

“Never said I was,” muttered Dan, with one backward glance of regret towards his lost seclusion; and before the day was out he had manoeuvred himself back again.

As we went out together, Old Waterhouse passed us on the stairs. “Haven’t you any sense of shame, my boy?” he asked sorrowfully, laying a kind hand on Dan’s shoulder.

“Yes, sir,” answered Dan, with his frank smile; “plenty. It isn’t yours, that’s all.”

He was an excellent fighter. In a school of over two hundred boys, not half a dozen—Upper Sixth fellows who came in top hats with umbrellas, and who wouldn’t, out of regard for their own dignity—could have challenged him with any chance of success. Yet he fought very seldom, and then in a bored, lazy fashion, as though he were doing it purely to oblige the other boy.

One afternoon, just as we were about to enter Regent’s Park by the wicket opposite Hanover Gate, an errand boy with an empty basket, supported by two smaller boys, barred our way.

“Can’t come in here,” said Basket.

“Why not?” inquired Dan.

“’Cos if you do I shall kick you,” explained Basket simply.

Without a word Dan turned away, prepared to go on to the next opening. Basket, encouraged by this, followed us.

“Now I’m going to give you your coward’s blow,” he said, stepping in front; “will you take it quietly?” The Outer Circle is lonely on a winter afternoon.

“I’ll tell you afterwards,” said Dan, stopping short.

Basket slapped him lightly on the cheek. It could not have hurt; but the indignity—according to our code—was enormous. No boy of honour was supposed to accept it without retaliation.

“Is that all?” asked Dan.

“That’s all—for the present,” replied Basket.

“Good-bye,” said Dan, and walked on.

“Glad he didn’t insist on fighting,” Dan remarked cheerfully as we went. “I’m going to a party tonight.”

Yet on another occasion, in a street off Lisson Grove, he insisted on fighting a young rough half as big again as himself, who, brushing against him, had knocked his hat into the mud.

“I wouldn’t have said anything about his knocking it off,” Dan explained afterwards, tenderly brushing the bruised hat with his sleeve, “if he hadn’t kicked it.”

And another time—one broiling summer afternoon—three or four of us, Dan among us, were on our way to Hadley Woods. Just beyond Barnet we turned into the fields. Dan drew from his pocket an enormous pear, so juicy and fat it seemed indecently prosperous for a schoolboy.

“Where did you get that?” asked Dudley.

“From that big greengrocer’s opposite Barnet Church,” answered Dan. “Have a bit?”

“You told me you hadn’t any more money,” Dudley reproached him, taking the offered slice at the end of Dan’s pocket-knife.

“No more I had,” Dan replied.

“Then you must have had some, or you couldn’t have bought it.”

“Didn’t buy it.”

“Do you mean to say you stole it?”

“Yes.”

“You’re a thief,” Dudley denounced, wiping his mouth and throwing away a pip.

“I know it. So are you.”

“No, I’m not.”

“What’s the good of talking nonsense? You robbed an orchard only last Wednesday at Mill Hill and gave yourself the stomach-ache.”

“That isn’t stealing.”

“What is it?”

“It isn’t the same thing.”

“What’s the difference?”

Nothing could make Dan comprehend the difference. “Stealing is stealing,” he maintained, “whether you take it off a tree or out of a basket. You’re a thief, Dudley; so am I. Anybody else say a piece?”

The thermometer was at that point where morals grow slack and appetites preach louder than consciences. We all had a piece. We were all shocked at Dan, and told him so. It did not agitate him in the least.

To Dan I could speak my inmost thoughts, certain he would understand me; and sometimes he helped, and sometimes he left me deliciously confused. The yearly examination was approaching. My father and mother said nothing, but I knew how they waited for the result—my father to see what I had accomplished, my mother to see how hard I had tried. I had worked, and yet I doubted, for prizes depend less on what you know than on what you can persuade others you know; which applies to prizes beyond those of school.

“Are you going in for anything, Dan?” I asked as we crossed Primrose Hill one bright June morning. The question was absurd; I asked it because I wanted him to ask it of me.

“They’re not giving away anything I particularly want,” he murmured in his lazy drawl, looking at prizes with the practical eye of a boy who saw through gilt edges.

“You’re sweating yourself, young ’un, of course?” he asked next, as I expected.

“I mean to have a shot at the History,” I admitted. “Wish I was better at dates.”

“It’s always two-thirds dates,” Dan assured me, to my discouragement. “Old Florret thinks you can’t eat a potato till you know the date that chap Raleigh was born.”

“I’ve prayed so hard that I may win the History prize,” I told him. With Dan I never felt shy; he never laughed at me.

“You oughtn’t to have done that,” he said.

I stared. “But we’re told to pray for things we want.”

“Beastly mean way of getting ’em,” retorted Dan. “It isn’t fair to the other fellows. That won’t be your winning the prize; that’ll be your getting it through favouritism.”

“But they can pray, too,” I reminded him.

“If you all pray for it,” said Dan, “then it’ll go—not to the fellow that knows most history—but to the fellow that’s prayed the hardest. That isn’t old Florret’s idea, I’m sure.”

No argument, neither then nor later, ever brought him round on that point. Dan judged everything for himself. In his opinion Achilles was a coward, not a hero.

“He ought to have told the Trojans they couldn’t hurt any part of him except his heel, and let ’em have a fair shot at that,” he argued. “King Arthur and all the rest with their magic swords—it wasn’t playing the game. There’s no pluck in fighting if you know you’re bound to win. Beastly cads, I call them all.”

I won no prize that year. Dan, oddly enough, did—arithmetic; the only subject in the Lower Fourth that interested him.

“I like to see things coming right,” he explained.

My father shut himself up with me for half an hour and examined me himself.

“It’s very curious, Paul,” he said; “you seem to know a good deal.”

“They asked me all the things I didn’t know,” I blurted out, and laid my head upon my arm. “They seemed to do it on purpose.”

My father crossed the room and sat down beside me.

“Spud!” he said—it was long since he had used that childish nickname—“perhaps you are going to be with me, one of the unlucky ones.”

“Are you unlucky?” I asked.

“Invariably,” he answered, rumpling his hair. “I don’t know why. I try hard—I do the right thing, but it turns out wrong. It always does.”

“But I thought Mr. Hasluck was bringing us such good fortune,” I said, looking up. “We’re getting on, aren’t we?”

“I have thought so before, so often,” he replied, “and it has always ended in a— in a collapse.”

I put my arms round his neck, for I always felt towards my father as towards another boy: bigger than myself and older, but not so very much older.

“You see, when I married your mother,” he went on, “I was a rich man. She had everything she wanted.”

“But you will get it all back,” I cried.

“I try to think so,” he said. “I do think so—generally speaking. But there are times—you would not understand—they come to you.”

“But she is happy,” I persisted. “We are all happy.”

He shook his head.

“I watch her,” he said. “Women suffer more than we do. They live more in the present. I see my hopes; but she—she sees only me, and I have always been a failure. She has lost faith in me.”

I could say nothing. I understood, but dimly.

“That is why I want you to be an educated man, Paul,” he continued after a silence. “You can’t think what a help education is to a man. I don’t mean it helps you to get on in the world; I think for that it rather hampers you. But it helps you to bear adversity. To a man with a well-stored mind, life is interesting on a piece of bread and a cup of tea. I know. If it were not for you and your mother I should not trouble.”

And yet at that time, so far as I remember, our fortunes were at their brightest; and when they grew dark again he would be full of fresh hope, planning and scheming and dreaming once more. It was never acting. A worse actor never trod our stage. His occasional attempts at cheerfulness he did not feel always ended with the three of us crying in one another’s arms. It was only when things went well that experience rose up against him. Child of misfortune, he was like Antaeus: he drew strength by touching his mother earth.

Nor must it be supposed that his despondency was frequent. Generally speaking, as he would say, he was full of confidence. Already he had fixed upon our new house in Guilford Street—then still good and residential—and at the same time sufficiently central for office purposes, near Lincoln’s Inn and Gray’s Inn and Bedford Row, those pavements worn by weary footsteps of the Law’s sad courtiers.

“Poplar,” said my father, “has disappointed me. It seemed a good idea—a rapidly rising district, singularly destitute of solicitors. It ought to have turned out well, and yet somehow it hasn’t.”

“There have been a few come,” my mother reminded him.

“Of a sort,” admitted my father. “A criminal lawyer might gather something of a practice here, no doubt. But for general work, of course, you must be in a central position. Now, in Guilford Street people will come to me.”

“It should certainly be a pleasanter neighbourhood to live in,” agreed my mother.

“Later on,” said my father, “in case I want the whole house for offices, we could live ourselves in Regent’s Park. It is quite near to the Park.”

“Of course you have consulted Mr. Hasluck?” asked my mother, who was, of the two, far more practical.

“For Hasluck,” replied my father, “it will be much more convenient. He grumbles every time at the distance.”

“I have never been quite able to understand,” said my mother, “why Mr. Hasluck should have come so far out of his way. There must surely be plenty of solicitors in the City.”
