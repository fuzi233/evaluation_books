A priest finds his truest vocation not in the pulpit, but in the chamber of death. Gerard performed the final rites for the departed with the same mechanical precision he would have applied to his humblest parishioner. Once the duties were done, however, he sat stunned, paralyzed by the sudden, terrible blow, unable yet to comprehend the depth of his loss. In a fleeting surge of religious fervor, he knelt to thank Heaven for her Christian passing. Immediately after, every thought turned to removing her from the gaze of strangers and laying her to rest in his own churchyard.

That very evening, a covered cart drawn by a single horse set off toward Gouda. Inside lay a coffin and a brokenhearted man, his arms and chin resting heavily upon it. The mourner's brief reserve of strength had been spent on the necessary preparations; now he lay crushed, clutching the cold iron latch that secured her remains. The driver, hired for the journey, walked beside the horse's head in silence, offering not even a word of comfort. When the horse needed rest, the driver whispered respectfully to the man, acknowledging the mute agony before him.

When they halted for the night, both the driver and the innkeeper made a kindly attempt to urge the mourner to leave the cart and seek rest and food. Gerard repelled them fiercely. When they pressed further, he snarled at them like a loyal dog guarding its master, refusing to let go of the cold metal all through the long night. Finally, they draped a heavy cloak over him and left him undisturbed.

At noon the following day, the sorrowful cart arrived at the rectory. A dozen parishioners had gathered, drawn by some trivial concern, only to find the parson missing. Upon seeing the scene—their healer so utterly stricken—they erupted in a loud wail of grief. This sound tore through Gerard's lethargy of despair. He looked around, recognizing the familiar faces, and tried to speak. "Oh, my children! My children!" he cried, but choked back the words against the tide of his anguish.

Yet, despite all protests, he buried her himself the next day. He read the burial service with a voice that trembled only occasionally, though many tears fell upon the fresh earth. When the service concluded, he remained standing amidst the graves like a statue, while the people departed out of deep respect. He stood frozen in a trance until the sexton—a man of simple wit—began filling the grave without warning. At the first sound of earth falling upon her, Gerard let out a piercing scream. The sexton paused immediately. Gerard staggered, pressing a hand to his breast. The sexton rushed to support him and summoned aid.

Jorian Ketel, who had lingered nearby mourning his benefactress, ran into the churchyard. Together, the two men helped Gerard back to the manse. "Ah, Jorian! Good Jorian!" he gasped, gripping the other's arm. "Something has snapped within me; I felt it, and I heard it break. Here, Jorian, right here..." He pressed his hand firmly against his chest.
