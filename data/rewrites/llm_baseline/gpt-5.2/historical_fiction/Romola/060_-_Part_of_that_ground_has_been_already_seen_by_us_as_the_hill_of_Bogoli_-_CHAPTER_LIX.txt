Morning had only just settled into its early brightness when Romola set out again for San Marco. The light lay fresh on the baked stones, pale gold in the joints of the pavement, while from open doorways came the day’s first domestic breath—wood-smoke that still clung to last night’s hearth, wet laundry hanging in shaded courts, the faint sour-sweet of fermenting must from a vintner’s back room. The city was waking as a body wakes: with a stretch, a groan, a sharpening of appetite.

She had obtained, through Fra Salvestro the evening before, a promise of an interview with Fra Girolamo in the chapter-house. Savonarola’s life was fenced about with such rigid care—he would not give calumny even a pretext—that private interviews were rare, and when they occurred they were arranged so that they could not be mistaken for secret plotting. The hour chosen was one when other visitors were likely to be in the outer cloisters, the convent’s quiet crossed by ordinary footsteps, the harmless murmur of lay voices.

Romola chose her way through the heart of Florence, not because it was the shortest, but because she wanted—she needed—to taste the air of the city, to feel what temper was rising, as a cook reads the moment when a pot begins to turn: first a faint change of scent, then a quickening, then the boil that will not be denied. She listened to Florence as to a kitchen before a feast: the clatter of shutters, the ring of a hammer in a smithy, the scrape of a chair dragged out into a loggia where men would sit and argue. Everywhere there were knots of talkers. Trade was languid, and when hands are idle, tongues labor harder.

Every loggia, every convenient corner of the piazza, every shop-front that made a rendezvous for gossips was astir with the excitement of gratuitous debate. The three days’ interval before the conspirators’ fate would be sealed was being spent like coin: both sides meant to spend it to the last farthing, to purchase the popular mood.

Handbills were already in circulation. Some offered, in black capitals large enough to be read by a man striding past, the alternative of justice upon the conspirators or ruin to the Republic; others, in equally emphatic print, urged observance of the law and the granting of the Appeal. Around those jutting islets of heavy type there lay lakes of smaller characters: arguments, reasons, insinuations, names. In that time—so proud of the new triumph of printing—there was a belief that no reasoning was so widely convincing as question-begging phrases in large letters. Romola, who had been trained among books and knew how often the truth hid where the eye was least tempted to look, cared far more for what was set down in the small print. She hurried, yet her gaze kept straying, anxiously searching for a loose sheet she could secure before it was pasted up high or ripped down by an officer’s hand.

For a long way she saw none except those clutched by eager readers, or plastered on walls from which, in places, sbirri were already tearing them down, the paper coming away with a dry rasp and leaving wet paste like a bruised mark on the plaster. At last, slipping behind San Giovanni with a quickened pace—she wanted to avoid the many acquaintances who frequented the piazza—she saw Bratti with a stock of handbills, exchanging them for small coin as briskly as a fruit-seller counts figs.

Bratti was no stranger to her. Florence’s humble lives were not unknown to Romola; she had learned, with her father, that the city’s mind was not only in council chambers and libraries, but at the bread-stalls, the oil-sellers, the corners where boys bawled news. Turning to him, she said, “Have you two sorts of handbills, Bratti? Let me have them quickly.”

“Two sorts,” Bratti replied, separating the wet sheets with a slowness that scraped her nerves. The paper still held the press’s damp coldness and a sharp smell of ink, like crushed walnuts mixed with iron. “There’s ‘Law,’ and there’s ‘Justice.’”

“Which sort do you sell most of?”

“‘Justice’—‘Justice’ goes the quickest,—so I raised the price, and made it two danari.” He held up the sheet as if it were a cut of meat and he was the butcher. “But then I bethought me the ‘Law’ was good ware too, and had as good a right to be charged for as ‘Justice;’ for people set no store by cheap things, and if I sold the ‘Law’ at one danaro, I should be doing it a wrong. And I’m a fair trader. ‘Law,’ or ‘Justice,’ it’s all one to me; they’re good wares. I got ’em both for nothing, and I sell ’em at a fair profit. But you’ll want more than one of a sort?”

“No, no: here’s a white quattrino for the two,” Romola said, folding the bills quickly, as if to keep their poison from spilling into the air, and hurrying away. The thin paper crackled between her fingers, and the ink smudged faintly on her glove like soot.

Soon she was in the outer cloisters of San Marco. The noise of the city fell back as if someone had shut a door on it; here the sound was of sandals on stone, of a low cough, of voices softened by arches. Fra Salvestro was awaiting her under the cloister, but did not notice her light step at first; he was chatting, according to his habit, with lay visitors. Under a government friendly to the Frate, the timidity about frequenting San Marco—which had followed the first shock of the Excommunication—had been gradually giving way. There were always faces now: earnest, curious, calculating.

In one of the visitors Romola recognized a well-known satellite of Francesco Valori, Andrea Cambini, whose hands never rested when he spoke, as if he must knead the air to make his meaning rise. Fra Salvestro listened with that air of trivial curiosity which tells that the listener cares very much about news and very little about its quality. Romola had always found this trait repulsive; now it was exasperating. The disjointed words reaching her ear made it plain Cambini was expounding something about the conspirators’ fate. The talk had the harsh relish of scandal, like cheap garlic rubbed on stale bread: it stings, it fills the mouth, it leaves no nourishment.

Romola chose not to approach the group. As soon as she saw that she had caught Fra Salvestro’s attention, she turned towards the door of the chapter-house. Fra Salvestro made a sign of approval and disappeared within the inner cloister. A lay Brother stood ready and opened the door for her; it swung on its hinges with a heavy sigh and shut behind her with a dull finality.

The chapter-house received her as it always did: cool air holding the faint odor of old plaster, candle-wax, and stone that had drunk prayers for generations. The frescoed figures on the walls looked down with their perpetual sorrow, those painted faces fixed in a grief that time could not soften. Once, long ago, those mournful saints had seemed to mourn with her when she came here with the wound of Dino’s death raw in her. The place could not help dragging back some of that memory; but the present pressed so strongly that the recollection was less a clear picture than a recurrence of pressure and chill, as if the same yearning had come again to be met by the same marble refusal.

She would not let the remembrance grow. She opened the handbills at once, thinking she might read in the interval before Fra Girolamo appeared. The paper that urged observance of the law was set forth with arguments in smaller type: the right of appeal, the danger of precedent, the dignity of a Republic that does not strike in haste. Romola’s eyes moved quickly, devouring line after line, while her ears kept their own vigil for the door.

By the time she reached the end, the door was opening. She doubled up the sheets and stood expectant. When Savonarola entered, she knelt by custom, as those did who saw him in private; but at his benedictory greeting she rose and stood facing him from a few yards’ distance.

It had been an unusually long while since she had seen him. His seclusion since the Excommunication had made his presence rarer, and the late months had visibly deepened in his face the marks of overtaxed mental labor and bodily severity: the skin drawn tighter over the bones, the eyes set as if they had stared too long into firelight. Yet Romola was less conscious of these changes than of something harder to name. Was it that the expression of serene elevation and pure human fellowship which had once moved her was now less present? Or was it that the new division between them—born of her feeling about her godfather—roused slumbering sources of alienation and clouded her own sight? Perhaps both currents ran together. So often our relations are decided by such coincident streams: the inexcusable word seldom comes until affection has already been worn thin by the strain of repeated excuses.

Savonarola’s glance at her had a hardness, the hardness of a mind preoccupied with itself. He divined why she had sought him: it would be about the conspirators. On that subject he had already had to quell inner voices that might grow loud again if Romola’s pleading encouraged them. Seated in his cell correcting sheets of his Triumph of the Cross, it had been easier to rest on a resolution of neutrality.

“It is a question of moment, doubtless, on which you wished to see me, my daughter,” he began. The tone was gentle, but its gentleness had the feel of control, not of spontaneous softness. “I know you are not wont to lay stress on small matters.”

“Father, you know what it is before I tell you,” Romola said. Once she began, everything else fell away as if a table had been swept clear for one dish only. “You know what I am caring for—it is for the life of the old man I love best in the world. The thought of him has gone together with the thought of my father as long as I remember the daylight. That is my warrant for coming to you, even if my coming should have been needless. Perhaps it is: perhaps you have already determined that your power over the hearts of men shall be used to prevent them from denying to Florentines a right which you yourself helped to earn for them.”

“I meddle not with the functions of the State, my daughter,” Fra Girolamo said. The words came with a firmness that was less anger than resistance. He was strongly disinclined to reopen outwardly a debate he had already fought inwardly. “I have preached and laboured that Florence should have a good government, for a good government is needful to the perfecting of the Christian life; but I keep away my hands from particular affairs which it is the office of experienced citizens to administer.”

“Surely, father—” Romola broke off. The first word had burst out almost impetuously, but she checked herself, stung by the sudden sense that she was remonstrating with the man who had once been her guide, her pillar. In rebelling she bruised her own reverence.

Savonarola was too quick not to see the conflict arresting her. He was too noble at this moment to take refuge in that calm evasiveness into which he was often hurried in public by the pressure of a crowd. “Say what is in your heart; speak on, my daughter,” he said. He stood with his arms laid one upon the other, looking at her with quiet expectation.

“I was going to say, father, that this matter is surely of higher moment than many about which I have heard you preach and exhort fervidly.” Her voice gathered again. “If it belonged to you to urge that men condemned for offences against the State should have the right to appeal to the Great Council—if you count it a glory to have won that right for them—can it less belong to you to declare yourself against the right being denied to almost the first men who need it? Surely that touches the Christian life more closely than whether you knew beforehand that the Dauphin would die, or whether Pisa will be conquered.”

A subtle movement passed over Savonarola’s lips, like a subdued sign of pain. Then he answered, “My daughter, I speak as it is given me to speak—I am not master of the times when I may become the vehicle of knowledge beyond the common lights of men. In this case I have no illumination beyond what wisdom may give to those who are charged with the safety of the State. As to the law of Appeal against the Six Votes, I laboured to have it passed in order that no Florentine should be subject to loss of life and goods through the private hatred of a few who might happen to be in power; but these five men, who have desired to overthrow a free government and restore a corrupt tyrant, have been condemned with the assent of a large assembly of their fellow-citizens. They refused at first to have their cause brought before the Great Council. They have lost the right to the appeal.”

“How can they have lost it?” Romola said. Her breath came faster; her cheeks warmed as if she stood too close to a brazier. “It is the right to appeal against condemnation, and they have never been condemned till now; and—forgive me, father—it is private hatred that would deny them the appeal; it is the violence of the few that frightens others; else why was the assembly divided again directly after it had seemed to agree? And if anything weighs against the observance of the law, let this weigh for it—that you used to preach more earnestly than all else that there should be no place given to hatred and bloodshed because of these party strifes, so that private ill-will should not find its opportunities in public acts. Father, you know there is private hatred concerned here: will it not dishonour you not to have interposed on the side of mercy, when there are many who hold that it is also the side of law and justice?”

“My daughter,” Fra Girolamo said, and now emotion showed more plainly, as heat shows through the thin skin of a fruit, “there is a mercy which is weakness, and even treason against the common good. The safety of Florence—which means even more than the welfare of Florentines—now demands severity, as it once demanded mercy. It is not only for a past plot that these men are condemned, but also for a plot which has not yet been executed; and the devices that were leading to its execution are not put an end to: the tyrant is still gathering his forces in Romagna, and the enemies of Florence, who sit in the highest places of Italy, are ready to hurl any stone that will crush her.”

“What plot?” Romola said. The question leapt out of her as if her mind had struck against something sharp. She reddened and trembled, alarmed surprise shaking her.

“You carry papers in your hand, I see,” Savonarola said, pointing to the folded handbills. “One of them will, perhaps, tell you that the government has had new information.”

Romola opened the second handbill hastily. The small print swam for a moment, then steadied. It proclaimed that the government now had positive evidence of a second plot, to be carried out in this August time. The words were meant to harden hearts; they lay on the page like salt thrown on raw flesh.

To Romola it read like a confirmation of something more intimate and more vile—that Tito, always slipping between nets, had won his safety by foul means. His recent pretence of wishing the Frate to exert himself on behalf of the condemned now turned rancid in her mind, as if she had tasted honey and found it smeared over rot. She crushed the paper in her hand, the crisp sheet wrinkling into a hard knot.

“Father,” she said, and a new passion broke through, “what safety can there be for Florence when the worst man can always escape?” Then, as memory flashed from the thought of her husband, she went on more bitterly: “And have not you yourself encouraged this deception which corrupts the life of Florence, by wanting more favour to be shown to Lorenzo Tornabuoni, who has worn two faces, and flattered you with a show of affection, when my godfather has always been honest? Ask all Florence who of those five men has the truest heart, and there will not be many who will name any other name than Bernardo del Nero. You did interpose with Francesco Valori for the sake of one prisoner: you have not then been neutral; and you know your word will be powerful.”

“I do not desire the death of Bernardo,” Savonarola said, colouring deeply. The redness rose like a sudden tide.

“It would be enough if he were sent out of the city,” he added, as if offering a compromise to his own conscience.

“Then why do you not speak to save an old man of seventy-five from dying a death of ignominy—to give him at least the fair chances of the law?” Romola burst out. Her impetuosity swept away prudence; she forgot everything but the sting of what seemed to her injustice. “It is not that you feel bound to be neutral; else why did you speak for Lorenzo Tornabuoni? You spoke for him because he is more friendly to San Marco; my godfather feigns no friendship. It is not, then, as a Medicean that my godfather is to die; it is as a man you have no love for!”

When she paused, cheeks glowing, lips quivering, there was dead silence. The chapter-house seemed to hold its breath. In that stillness, Romola heard her own words echoing back inside her, and in the echo they clashed painfully with the memories that clung to Savonarola’s presence: the voice that had once sounded like clear water, the hand she had thought strong and clean. The silence expanded with compunction and with a sudden self-doubt that hurt like an over-tight band. Had she committed sacrilege in her passion?

Yet she could retract nothing. Her whole being clung to her plea, and she could not make her mind submit to Savonarola’s refusal. All the more she needed, in this very rebellion, to satisfy the reverence that still lived in her like a native dialect she could not wholly forget.

She moved suddenly towards him. “Forgive me, father; it is pain to me to have spoken those words—yet I cannot help speaking. I am little and feeble compared with you; you brought me light and strength. But I submitted because I felt the proffered strength—because I saw the light. Now I cannot see it. Father, you yourself declare there comes a moment when the soul must have no guide but the voice within it, to tell whether the consecrated thing has sacred virtue. And therefore I must speak.”

Savonarola had a susceptibility to opposition that was hardly separable from a power-loving and powerful nature—accustomed to seek great ends that cast a reflected grandeur on the means. His sermons could burn with that red flame. If he had been meaner, he would have shown irritation at Romola’s freedom, so unlike the deference he usually received. But now those feelings were swallowed up in the harder struggle that made half the tragedy of his life: a mind hungry for purity and simplicity, yet caught in a tangle of demands, false ideas, and outward conditions that made simplicity impossible.

He listened, alive to every suggestion in her words, and rapidly surveyed again—as he had done before—the courses open to him and their likely results. But it was a question where arguments could seem decisive only in proportion as they were charged with feeling, and she had given him no new impulse that could shift his bias. He looked at her and said, “You have full pardon for your frankness, my daughter. You speak, I know, out of the fulness of your family affections. But these affections must give way to the needs of the Republic. If those men who have a close acquaintance with the affairs of the State believe, as I understand they do, that the public safety requires the extreme punishment of the law to fall on the five conspirators, I cannot control their opinion, seeing that I stand aloof from such affairs.”

“Then you desire that they should die? You desire that the Appeal should be denied them?” Romola said. Again she felt repelled, as if he had held out a cup and then withdrawn it with an explanation that tasted like excuse.

“I have said that I do not desire their death.”

“Then,” Romola said, and indignation rose again like a second fermentation, “you can be indifferent that Florentines should inflict death which you do not desire, when you might have protested against it—when you might have helped to hinder it, by urging the observance of a law which you held it good to get passed. Father, you used not to stand aloof: you used not to shrink from protesting. Do not say you cannot protest where the lives of men are concerned; say rather you desire their death. Say rather you hold it good for Florence that there shall be more blood and more hatred. Will the death of five Mediceans put an end to parties in Florence? Will the death of a noble old man like Bernardo del Nero save a city that holds such men as Dolfo Spini?”

“My daughter, it is enough.” Savonarola’s voice had the hard edge of a knife laid down on a board. “The cause of freedom, which is the cause of God’s kingdom upon earth, is often most injured by the enemies who carry within them the power of certain human virtues. The wickedest man is often not the most insurmountable obstacle to the triumph of good.”

“Then why do you say again that you do not desire my godfather’s death?” Romola said. Anger and despair mingled until she could not separate them. “Rather, you hold it the more needful he should die because he is the better man. I cannot unravel your thoughts, father; I cannot hear the real voice of your judgment and conscience.”

A moment’s pause—thick, strained. Then Savonarola spoke with keener emotion than before. “Be thankful, my daughter, if your own soul has been spared perplexity; and judge not those to whom a harder lot has been given. You see one ground of action in this matter. I see many. I have to choose that which will further the work intrusted to me. The end I seek is one to which minor respects must be sacrificed. The death of five men—were they less guilty than these—is a light matter weighed against the withstanding of the vicious tyrannies which stifle the life of Italy, and foster the corruption of the Church; a light matter weighed against the furthering of God’s kingdom upon earth, the end for which I live and am willing myself to die.”

Under other circumstances, Romola might have been touched by the appeal at the beginning of his speech. But she was now utterly opposed to him. What he called perplexity seemed to her sophistry and doubleness; the more he spoke of sacrificing “minor respects,” the more she felt the bitter taste of a language that makes killing sound like prudence, as if words themselves were being used to salt and preserve cruelty. His phrases seemed to belong to a lexicon of power—grand, dominant, certain—under which the untranslatable tremor of one old man’s life, one family’s love, could be flattened and made negligible.

She answered almost with bitterness. “Do you, then, know so well what will further the coming of God’s kingdom, father, that you will dare to despise the plea of mercy—of justice—of faithfulness to your own teaching? Has the French king, then, brought renovation to Italy? Take care, father, lest your enemies have some reason when they say that in your visions of what will further God’s kingdom you see only what will strengthen your own party.”

“And that is true!” Savonarola said, and his eyes flashed. In that instant Romola’s voice seemed to him the voice of his enemies. “The cause of my party is the cause of God’s kingdom.”

“I do not believe it!” Romola said. Her whole frame shook with passionate repugnance, as if her very senses rejected the sound of it. “God’s kingdom is something wider—else, let me stand outside it with the beings that I love.”

The two faces were lit, each by an opposite certainty, each by an opposite emotion. Further words were impossible. Romola drew her veil over her head with a haste that was almost a gesture of self-defense, and she went out in silence, leaving behind her the cool air, the mourning frescoes, and the sound of her own footfall—brief, sharp, and then swallowed by the convent’s stone.
