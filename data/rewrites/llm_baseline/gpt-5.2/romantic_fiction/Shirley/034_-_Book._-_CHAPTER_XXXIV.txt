Martin Yorke, having once tasted the sharp relish of consequence, craved it again as a boy craves the first illicit spoonful from a cooling pan. Authority had sat on his tongue like a warm spice, and now he could not endure the thin, insipid bread-and-milk of common days. Miss Helstone—she whom he had always, with schoolboy cruelty and family candour, set down as ugly—had crossed, for one electric moment, into the circle of his notice; and since then her face haunted him as persistently as a scent clings to wool: in lamplight, in frost-glare, in the soot-dim of afternoon, always the same remembered outline, always the same provoking life in her eyes.

He was no ordinary schoolboy, though he sat among ordinary schoolboys. At his desk in the grammar school he appeared idle, his slate untouched; but within, he was measuring and mixing, like a cook with a new dish in mind, trying this pinch of boldness and that pinch of revenge, seeking the right proportion to draw her again within reach. Many a life-romance, he did not yet know, is set going with a grand first course, and then—through want of fire, or want of courage—never reaches the second.

On the Saturday half-holiday he went to the wood with a book of fairy legends in his pocket, and another book—unwritten, inexhaustible—opening and shutting in his head. The branches above him clacked and whispered; the brook made its thin, clear music under ice; a jay scolded; distant axes bit wood with a steady, muffled thud. Those sounds, clean and various, were meat and drink to him. The town’s mill-clank and loom-roar, heard faintly when the wind set that way, had always seemed an offence—noise that did not belong to place or season, noise that drove thought like smoke drives bees.

He harboured, too, a frank and almost irreligious reluctance for Sunday. His father and mother, while protesting that the Establishment was no better than any other contrivance for saving souls, never failed, once on the sacred day, to fill their large pew in Briarfield Church with the whole blooming family. Mr. Yorke in theory set all sects level as ploughed land; Mrs. Yorke awarded her palm to Moravians and Quakers for their plainness, as though humility were a homely broth best served without garnish. Yet neither had ever been known to set foot in a conventicle.

Martin disliked Sunday because the morning service was long and the sermon commonly to his taste as thin ale is to a boy who has smelt stronger drink. Yet this Saturday, out among the stripped trees, his musings found a new charm in the coming day; and the charm had a name.

Sunday came in deep snow—snow that made the world look baked in white, as though a patient hand had sifted flour over every hedge and furrow. At breakfast Mrs. Yorke, surveying the drifted lanes through the window, announced her conviction that the children, boys and girls, would be better at home; and her decision that, instead of going to church, they should sit silent for two hours in the back parlour, while Rose and Martin alternately read a succession of sermons—John Wesley’s. Wesley, being a reformer and agitator, had a place in both their parents’ favour; his words, like mustard seed, had bite.

“Rose will do as she pleases,” said Martin, not lifting his eyes from the book he was studying over his bread and milk. He broke the bread slowly, as if he were cutting measured portions for a recipe; he sipped the milk with the same deliberate calm. Even as a boy he ate like one who listened to food—its temperature, its texture—more than to talk.

“Rose will do as she is told, and Martin too,” observed the mother.

“I am going to church.”

The quietness with which he said it was the quietness of a true Yorke: a will set like yeast to work, unseen, until the dough must rise. If pushed to the wall he would let himself be crushed, if there were no gap; but he would not capitulate while a single crevice remained.

“It is not fit weather,” said the father.

No answer. Martin read; he broke and sipped; the fire popped; outside, the wind worried the windows.

“Martin hates to go to church, but he hates still more to obey,” said Mrs. Yorke.

“I suppose I am influenced by pure perverseness?”

“Yes, you are.”

“Mother, I am not.”

“By what, then, are you influenced?”

“By a complication of motives, the intricacies of which I should as soon think of explaining to you as I should of turning myself inside out to exhibit the internal machinery of my frame.”

“Hear Martin! hear him!” cried Mr. Yorke. “I must see and have this lad of mine brought up to the bar. Nature meant him to live by his tongue. Hesther, your third son must certainly be a lawyer; he has the stock-in-trade—brass, self-conceit, and words—words—words.”

“Some bread, Rose, if you please,” requested Martin, with intense gravity. His voice was naturally low, plaintive; in his dour moods it sank to a lady’s whisper, and the more stubborn the humour, the softer, sadder the tone—as though he would not waste breath on opposition, but would hoard it, like a miser hoards spice, for some future feast.

He rang the bell and gently asked for his walking-shoes.

“But, Martin,” urged his sire, “there is drift all the way; a man could hardly wade through it. However, lad,” he continued, seeing the boy rise as the church bell began to toll, “this is a case wherein I would by no means balk the obdurate chap of his will. Go to church by all means. There is a pitiless wind, and a sharp, frozen sleet, besides the depth under foot. Go out into it, since thou prefers it to a warm fireside.”

Martin quietly assumed cloak, comforter, and cap, and deliberately went out.

“My father has more sense than my mother,” he pronounced to himself, as he shut the door on the warmth and the smell of toast. “How women miss it! They drive the nail into the flesh, thinking they are hammering away at insensate stone.”

The lane was half buried; the snow had drifted in waves; sleet, like grit, stung his cheeks. Yet even that harsh weather had its own voice—wind keening round gables, the hollow hush of fields muffled deep, his own footfall crunching and complaining. Better this, he thought, than the ceaseless mechanical din of the mill: that other kind of sound which did not change with season or mercy, but went on with the obstinacy of money.

He reached church early.

“Now, if the weather frightens her,” he said, looking up at the leaden sky, “or if that Mrs. Pryor objects to her going out, and I should miss her after all, it will vex me; but tempest or tornado, hail or ice, she ought to come; and if she has a mind worthy of her eyes and features she will come. She will be here for the chance of seeing me, as I am here for the chance of seeing her. She will want to get a word respecting her confounded sweetheart, as I want to get another flavour of what I think the essence of life—a taste of existence, with the spirit preserved in it, and not evaporated. Adventure is to stagnation what champagne is to flat porter.”

The church was cold, silent, empty but for one old woman, who sat hunched in the free sittings like a sparrow enduring winter. As the chimes subsided and the single bell tolled slowly, another and another elderly parishioner came dropping in—grey heads, bent shoulders, hands reddened with washing and frost. It is always the frailest, the oldest, and the poorest who brave the worst weather, to prove and maintain their constancy to dear old mother church. Not one affluent family attended; not one carriage party appeared; all the lined and cushioned pews stood bare. Only on the hard oaken seats sat ranged the elders and feeble paupers, their coughs small in the vast chill, their shawls smelling faintly of peat smoke and damp wool.

“I’ll scorn her if she doesn’t come,” muttered Martin, and his breath made a brief cloud.

The rector’s shovel-hat passed the porch. Mr. Helstone and his clerk were in the vestry. The bells ceased; the reading-desk was filled; the doors were closed; the service commenced.

Void stood the rectory pew. She was not there.

Martin scorned her—scorned her as only a boy can, with the extravagance of a first disappointment.

“Worthless thing! vapid thing! commonplace humbug! Like all other girls—weakly, selfish, shallow!” Such was Martin’s liturgy. “She is not like our picture. Her eyes are not large and expressive; her nose is not straight, delicate, Hellenic; her mouth has not that charm I thought it had, which I imagined could beguile me of sullenness in my worst moods. What is she? A thread-paper, a doll, a toy, a girl, in short.”

So absorbed was the young cynic that he forgot to rise from his knees at the proper place, and remained in an exemplary attitude of devotion when, the litany over, the first hymn was given out. To be caught thus did not soothe him; he started up red, for he was as sensitive to ridicule as any girl.

To make the matter worse, the church door reopened and the aisles began to fill: patter, patter, patter—many little feet trotting in, quick as mice. The Sunday scholars. According to Briarfield winter custom, these children had been kept till now where there was a warm stove, then led into church just before the communion and sermon. Their small bodies brought with them the warm scent of damp mittens, the faint sweetness of cheap treacle, the steam of breath caught in woollen scarves. They were settled first, and at last—when the boys and younger girls were all arranged—when the organ was swelling high and the congregation rose to lift a spiritual song—a tall class of young women came quietly in, closing the procession.

Their teacher, having seen them seated, passed into the rectory pew.

The French-grey cloak and small beaver bonnet were known to Martin; it was the very costume his eyes had ached to catch. Miss Helstone had not suffered the storm to prove an impediment. After all, she was come to church.

Martin probably whispered his satisfaction to his hymn-book; at any rate, he hid his face in it for two minutes, as if the paper could cover the sudden heat that rose through him like wine.

Satisfied or not, he had time to get very angry with her again before the sermon was done. She had never once looked his way; at least he had not been so lucky as to encounter a glance.

“If,” he said—“if she takes no notice of me, if she shows I am not in her thoughts, I shall have a worse, a meaner opinion of her than ever. Most despicable would it be to come for the sake of those sheep-faced Sunday scholars, and not for my sake—or for that long skeleton Moore’s.”

The sermon ended. The benediction was pronounced. The congregation dispersed, sounds waking again in the nave: boots scraping, a child’s suppressed yelp, the creak of a pew-door, a woman’s whisper like paper rubbed together.

She had not been near him.

Now, indeed, as Martin set his face homeward, he felt that the sleet was sharp and the east wind cold. His nearest way lay through some fields—a dangerous way, because untrodden; the drifts might hide a ditch, the crust might break underfoot. He did not care. He would take it.

Near the second stile rose a clump of trees. Was that an umbrella waiting there? Yes—an umbrella, held with evident difficulty against the blast; behind it fluttered a French-grey cloak.

Martin grinned as he toiled up the steep, encumbered field, difficult to the foot as a slope on Etna. When he gained the stile he seated himself coolly upon it, as though it were a chair set out for him at a table, and thus opened a conference which, for his own part, he was willing to prolong indefinitely.

“I think you had better strike a bargain. Exchange me for Mrs. Pryor.”

“I was not sure whether you would come this way, Martin, but I thought I would run the chance. There is no such thing as getting a quiet word spoken in the church or churchyard.”

“Will you agree?—make over Mrs. Pryor to my mother, and put me in her skirts?”

“As if I could understand you! What puts Mrs. Pryor into your head?”

“You call her ‘mamma,’ don’t you?”

“She is my mamma.”

“Not possible—or so inefficient, so careless a mamma; I should make a five times better one. You may laugh. I have no objection to see you laugh. Your teeth—I hate ugly teeth; but yours are as pretty as a pearl necklace, and a necklace of which the pearls are very fair, even, and well matched too.”

“Martin, what now? I thought the Yorkes never paid compliments?”

“They have not done till this generation; but I feel as if it were my vocation to turn out a new variety of the Yorke species. I am rather tired of my own ancestors. We have traditions going back for four ages—tales of Hiram, which was the son of Hiram, which was the son of Samuel, which was the son of John, which was the son of Zerubbabel Yorke. All, from Zerubbabel down to the last Hiram, were such as you see my father. Before that there was a Godfrey. We have his picture; it hangs in Moore’s bedroom; it is like me. Of his character we know nothing; but I am sure it was different to his descendants. He has long, curling dark hair; he is carefully and cavalierly dressed. Having said that he is like me, I need not add that he is handsome.”

“You are not handsome, Martin.”

“No; but wait awhile—just let me take my time. I mean to begin from this day to cultivate, to polish, and we shall see.”

“You are a very strange, a very unaccountable boy, Martin. But don’t imagine you ever will be handsome; you cannot.”

“I mean to try. But we were talking about Mrs. Pryor. She must be the most unnatural mamma in existence, coolly to let her daughter come out in this weather. Mine was in such a rage because I would go to church; she was fit to fling the kitchen brush after me.”

“Mamma was very much concerned about me; but I am afraid I was obstinate. I would go.”

“To see me?”

“Exactly; I thought of nothing else. I greatly feared the snow would hinder you from coming. You don’t know how pleased I was to see you all by yourself in the pew.”

“I came to fulfil my duty, and set the parish a good example.”

And, though he spoke with that habitual, provoking gravity, he felt the words she had given him settle warmly inside—like a spoonful of something rich swallowed in secret.

“And so you were obstinate, were you? I should like to see you obstinate, I should. Wouldn’t I have you in good discipline if I owned you? Let me take the umbrella.”

“I can’t stay two minutes; our dinner will be ready.”

“And so will ours; and we have always a hot dinner on Sundays. Roast goose to-day, with apple-pie and rice-pudding. I always contrive to know the bill of fare. Well, I like these things uncommonly; but I’ll make the sacrifice, if you will.”

“We have a cold dinner. My uncle will allow no unnecessary cooking on the Sabbath. But I must return; the house would be in commotion if I failed to appear.”

“So will Briarmains, bless you! I think I hear my father sending out the overlooker and five of the dyers, to look in six directions for the body of his prodigal son in the snow; and my mother repenting her of her many misdeeds towards me, now I am gone.”

“Martin, how is Mr. Moore?”

That name—spoken with such guarded hunger—was the true seasoning of her errand. The wind, for a moment, seemed to hush to listen; then it came again, driving the sleet in fine needles across the field.

“That is what you came for, just to say that word.”

“Come, tell me quickly.”

“Hang him! he is no worse; but as ill-used as ever—mewed up, kept in solitary confinement. They mean to make either an idiot or a maniac of him, and take out a commission of lunacy. Horsfall starves him; you saw how thin he was.”

“You were very good the other day, Martin.”

“What day? I am always good—a model.”

“When will you be so good again?”

“I see what you are after; but you’ll not wheedle me—I am no cat’s-paw.”

“But it must be done. It is quite a right thing, and a necessary thing.”

“How you encroach! Remember, I managed the matter of my own free will before.”

“And you will again.”

“I won’t. The business gave me far too much trouble. I like my ease.”

“Mr. Moore wishes to see me, Martin, and I wish to see him.”

“I dare say,” he returned coolly, as though her wish were a common thing like wanting salt.

“It is too bad of your mother to exclude his friends.”

“Tell her so.”

“His own relations.”

“Come and blow her up.”

“You know that would advance nothing. Well, I shall stick to my point. See him I will. If you won’t help me, I’ll manage without help.”

“Do; there is nothing like self-reliance, self-dependence.”

“I have no time to reason with you now; but I consider you provoking. Good-morning.”

Away she went, the umbrella shut, for she could not carry it against the wind; she held her cloak close, and the snow took the edge of her outline as she moved, turning her into something half-real, half-legendary—a figure that might have stepped from the fairy tales in his pocket and yet would be expected, quite practically, at a cold Sunday table.

“She is not vapid; she is not shallow,” said Martin, watching her. “I shall like to watch, and mark how she will work her way without help. If the storm were not of snow, but of fire—such as came refreshingly down on the cities of the plain—she would go through it to procure five minutes’ speech of that Moore.

“Now, I consider I have had a pleasant morning. The disappointments got time on; the fears and fits of anger only made that short discourse pleasanter, when it came at last. She expected to coax me at once. She’ll not manage that in one effort. She shall come again, again, and yet again. It would please me to put her in a passion—to make her cry. I want to discover how far she will go—what she will do and dare—to get her will. It seems strange and new to find one human being thinking so much about another as she thinks about Moore.”

He turned at last towards home; and now his appetite, practical as a clock, reminded him of the hour. He could already imagine the kitchen warmth, the smell of goose fat crisping skin, the tart breath of apples under pastry, the thick comfort of rice-pudding—simple fare, but honest; the sort of Sunday abundance that held a family together more surely than any sermon read aloud in a back parlour.

“It is time to go home,” he concluded. “Won’t I walk into that goose? and we’ll try whether Matthew or I shall get the largest cut of the apple-pie to-day.”
