Captain Durrance found his servant waiting by the door when he emerged from the fields toward Guessens.

"Turn off the lights and go to bed," Durrance commanded, his voice steady despite the weight of his condition. He moved through the hall and entered his study—a room that had always served more as a gun-room than a place for thought.

He sat for a long time, letting the darkness settle around him, before beginning to pace slowly. The room was cluttered with the spoils of his youth: cups, goblets, and trophies scattered across surfaces where light once fell freely. He knew every object by touch alone—their shape, weight, and the familiar spot they occupied. Running his hand over a thin-stemmed goblet, he recalled the regimental steeple-chase at Colchester, the grey skies, the ploughed earth between the hedges. He traced the pewter pen-holder on his desk, bought long ago at Oxford, and ran his fingers along the silver hoof of a favorite horse on the mantelpiece. These items formed a tactile diary of his life before the fall, a record of victories and adventures.

Then he reached for his guns. To Durrance, they were as intimate as Ethne's violin had been to her. He lifted a Remington, resting it across his knee, and closed his eyes—not in surrender, but in memory. He relived the heat of the hills west of Berenice, the stony ground, the lion falling at three hundred yards just before sunset. Another weapon whispered of the first ibex in the Khor Baraka, the antelope stalked near Suakin. There was the small Greener, used on freezing winter nights aboard a boat in the Salcombe estuary, the one that had brought down his first mallard. He slid his left hand beneath the barrel, feeling the stock nestle comfortably into the hollow of his shoulder. Yet, as he manipulated the locks, the silence of his blindness pressed in. Without sight, the world of his achievements collapsed into a single, undeniable reality: he could no longer see the sights. The sum of his losses settled over him, heavy and final.

Putting the weapons back, a sudden impulse seized him. He wanted to deny his blindness, to act as if it were merely a minor inconvenience, a temporary shadow. This desire surged through him like a fever, dragging him out of the dim realm of his present and throwing him straight into the East. The scent of spices, the clamor of markets, the domes of mosques, the blinding sun, the throngs in the streets, and the steel-blue sky—all these things pulled at him until he was forced from his chair and pacing frantically.

In his mind, he stood in Port Said, marching down the canal with a procession of steamers. He heard the chanting of Arab coalers, so vivid he could see their naked forms moving up and down the planks between barges and decks, lit by the red glow of braziers. He traveled onward, past the red cliffs of Sinai, into the chill waters of the Gulf of Suez. He zigzagged down the Red Sea, watching the Great Bear dip low in the northern sky while the Southern Cross burned bright in the south. He touched at Tor and Yambo; he beheld the towering white houses of Yeddah rising from the sea, marveling at the dark, weathered wood of their carved windows. He wandered through the shaded bazaars with the joy of a homesick traveler returning after many years, crossing the narrow coral reefs to reach the landlocked harbor of Suakin.

Beyond Suakin lay the desert, vast and unwelcoming—a landscape that had both created and cast out the man who now dwelt within it. He dreamed of the soft padding of camel feet in the sand, the jagged rock cones rising abruptly from the dunes, the fleeting brilliance of sunset, the rustle of wind through the short twilight when west turns pale green and east deepens to blue. He tramped endlessly through this mental wilderness, forgetting his blindness, parched with a thirst that mimicked fever, until suddenly the sounds of blackbirds and swallows filled his ears. Outside his window, the world was breaking into dawn.

He awoke to the gentle noise of birds. There would be no further journeys for him; illness had confined him, binding his leg with chains. He found his way by touch along the balustrade to his bed and sank into sleep as the sun climbed higher.

Meanwhile, at Dongola, where the Nile bends southward past Wadi Halfa, the sun beat down with relentless force, and the town was already stirring. Sport awaited the people this morning beneath the sparse palm trees before the residence of Emir Wad El Nejoumi. A white prisoner, captured a week prior near the wells of El Agia on the great Arbain road by a band of Arabs, had been brought in during the night and now waited for judgment. News of his arrival spread swiftly through the town, drawing crowds of men, women, and children eager to witness the spectacle. An open space lay before the palms, leading to the Emir's gate; behind them, a sandy slope dropped flat toward the river.

Harry Feversham stood beneath the trees, guarded by four Ansar soldiers. His clothes had been stripped away, leaving him in nothing but a torn, ragged jibbeh and a twisted cloth wrapped around his head for shade. Sunburn and blisters marred his bare shoulders and arms. His ankles were shackled, his wrists bound with coarse palm fiber, and an iron collar locked tightly around his neck, connected by a chain held by one of the guards. Despite the mockery of the surrounding crowd, Feversham stood with a strange, unsettling smile, appearing almost pleased, like a madman.

This was the mask he had chosen to wear. If he could maintain the illusion of insanity, baffling his captors into uncertainty—were he truly deranged, or an agent offering aid to the rebellious tribes of Kordofan?—there existed a slim chance they would hesitate to execute him personally and instead send him forward to Omdurman. It was difficult work. Within the house, the Emir and his advisors debated his fate; outside, visible to him, a high gallows loomed black and ominous against the yellow sands. Feversham was relieved by the chain and fetters; they anchored him, preventing any slip into panic, assuring him of their uselessness.

These hours of waiting, as the sun climbed higher and the gate remained shut, were the most terrible he had endured. For twenty days in Berber, a flicker of escape hope had sustained him, and when the lantern had illuminated him in the ruins, action had demanded immediate focus, leaving no room for fear. Now, however, time stretched endlessly.

Time allowed him to anticipate, to foresee. His heart sank, nearly fainting, recalling those distant days when hounds scuffled in a covert and he sat trembling on his horse. He glanced nervously at the gallows, imagining vultures perching on his shoulders, circling his eyes. Yet the man had changed since his years of trial. Physical suffering held no terror, nor did the prospect of walking unmanly to execution weigh heavily upon him. Instead, a far greater dread consumed him: if he died here at Dongola, Ethne would never retrieve the fourth feather, and his enduring hope for an "aftermath" would remain unfulfilled. Grateful for the collar and fetters that masked his despair, he summoned his wits, laughing, scraping, and making grimaces at his tormentors.

An old woman danced before him, gesticulating wildly, singing a monotonous song. Her movements were theatrical, threatening abhorrent mutilations; her words, crude and unvarnished, described the agony awaiting him and the eternal torments of hell. Feversham understood and shrank inwardly, yet outwardly he mimicked her gestures, nodding and bobbing as if she sang of paradise. Others approached with war trumpets, pressing them to his ears and blowing with all their might.

"Do you hear, Kaffir?" a child cried, dancing with delight. "Do you hear our ombeyehs? Blow louder! Blow louder!"

The prisoner merely clapped his hands, declaring the music excellent.

At length, a tall warrior bearing a long, heavy spear approached. A cry erupted, clearing a space. The warrior stood before the captive, swinging the spear back and forth to loosen his arm, like a bowler preparing to deliver a ball. Feversham scanned wildly for an exit, finding none, and suddenly threw out his chest to meet the blow. But the spear never struck. As the warrior lunged, one of the four guards yanked the neck chain violently from behind, hurling the prisoner half-throttled onto his back. Three times, to roaring delight, this game was repeated. Finally, a soldier emerged from the Emir's gateway.

"Bring him in!" he shouted. Followed by the curses and threats of the crowd, the prisoner was dragged under an archway across a courtyard into a dark chamber.

For a few moments, Feversham saw nothing. Then his eyes adjusted to the gloom, revealing a tall, bearded man sitting on an angareb—a traditional Sudanese bedframe—and two others squatting nearby. The man on the angareb was the Emir.

"You are a spy of the Government from Wadi Halfa," Nejoumi declared.

"No, I am a musician," Feversham replied, laughing happily, as if he had cracked a joke.
