Bound as tightly to this gathering as a moth to a flame, I could not tear myself away from the clique. The earth beneath them seemed to anchor my feet, and the canopy of interlaced branches offered a shadow that promised protection. An officious lamp flickered, casting a single, fleeting beam upon an obscure, safe seat before vanishing into the dark. Now, let me briefly recount what I have silently gleaned from rumor regarding the origins and purposes of M. Emanuel's departure. The tale is brief and devoid of novelty; its beginning is Mammon, and its end is Interest.

To the devotees of Madame Walravens, if she appeared as hideous as a Hindu idol, she nonetheless possessed the consequence of one. Indeed, she had been wealthy—immensely so. Though currently without funds, following her husband's failure, it was believed that her vast estate in Basseterre, Guadeloupe, received as a dowry sixty years prior, would soon be cleared of claims. With a competent agent of integrity, it was expected to become highly productive within a few years.

Père Silas took a keen interest in this potential religious and ecclesiastical prosperity, for Magliore Walravens was a devout daughter of the Church. Madame Beck, a distant relative of the hunchback and aware that Walravens had no family, had long calculated contingencies with a mother's foresight. Harshly treated as she was by Madame Walravens, she never ceased to court her favor for financial gain. Thus, driven by monetary motives, both the priest and Madame Beck were equally and sincerely invested in nurturing the West Indian estate.

Yet the distance was vast, and the climate perilous. A competent and upright agent was required—one who must be devoted. Just such a man had Madame Walravens retained for twenty years, blighting his existence and then parasitically feeding upon him like an ancient fungus. Similarly, Père Silas had trained and bound this man through gratitude, habit, and faith. Madame Beck, knowing him and capable of influencing him to some degree, harbored her own secret reasons. "My pupil," Père Silas remarked, "if he remains in Europe, risks apostasy, for he has become entangled with a heretic." Madame Beck offered her private commentary, preferring in her own bosom her secret desire for his removal. Where she could not secure his departure herself, she desired that no other should win him; rather, she wished to destroy any hope he might harbor. As for Madame Walravens, she coveted her money and land, knowing that Paul, if inclined, could serve as the finest and most faithful steward. Consequently, these three self-seekers united to besiege the solitary unselfish one. They reasoned, appealed, and implored; casting themselves upon his mercy, they entrusted their interests to his hands. They asked but for two or three years of devotion, promising thereafter that he should live for himself; perhaps one of them secretly wished he might perish in the meantime.

Never before had anyone so humbly laid their advantage at M. Emanuel's feet, nor confided so trustingly in his hands, that he spurned the trust or repulsed the repository. What private pain or inward reluctance he felt regarding leaving Europe, what calculations he made for his future, remained unknown to all. To me, however, everything concerning these matters was a blank. I could only guess at his conferences with his confessor and conjecture the role duty and religion played in their persuasions. He was gone, having made no sign. My knowledge ended there.

With my head bowed, my forehead resting upon my hands, I sat amidst grouped tree-stems and branching brushwood. Whatever conversation passed among my neighbors, I might have heard if I chose; I was close enough. Yet for some time, there was scant motive to pay attention. They gossiped of dresses, music, illuminations, and the fine night. I listened, hoping to catch the words, *"It is calm weather for his voyage; the Antigua will sail prosperously."* No such remark fell; neither the *Antigua*, nor her course, nor her passenger were mentioned.

Perhaps the light chatter interested old Madame Walravens no more than it did me. She appeared restless, turning her head alternately to this side and that, peering through the trees and the crowd as if expectant of an arrival and impatient of delay. *"Où sont-ils? Pourquoi ne viennent-ils?"* I heard her mutter more than once. Finally, as if determined to receive an answer to her question—which hitherto none seemed to mind—she spoke aloud a phrase brief enough, simple enough, yet sending a shock through me: *"Messieurs et mesdames,"* she declared, *"où donc est Justine Marie?"*

"Justine Marie!" What was this? Justine Marie—the dead nun—where was she? Why, in her grave, Madame Walravens? What need did you have of her? You shall go to her, but she shall not come to you.

Thus I should have answered, had the response lain with me, but no one seemed of my mind. No one appeared surprised, startled, or at a loss. The quietest, most commonplace answer met the strange, dead-disturbing, Witch-of-Endor query of the hunchback.

"Justine Marie," said one, "is coming; she is in the kiosk; she will be here presently."

From this question and reply sprang a shift in the chat—it remained easy, desultory, familiar gossip—but hints, allusions, and comments circulated the circle. Everything was so broken, so dependent on references to unnamed persons or undefined circumstances that, listening as intently as I could—and I *did* listen now with a fated interest—I could discern no more than that some scheme was underway involving this ghostly Justine Marie, whether dead or alive. This family junta seemed to grasp at her for some reason; there seemed to be a question of a marriage, of a fortune—for whom I could not quite determine—perhaps for Victor Kint, perhaps for Josef Emanuel. Both were bachelors. Once, I thought the hints and jests rained upon a young, fair-haired foreigner of the party, whom they called Heinrich Mühler. Amidst all the badinage, Madame Walravens continued to intrude from time to time with hoarse, cross-grained speeches; her impatience being diverted only by an implacable surveillance of Désirée, who could not move without the old woman menacing her with her staff.

*"La voilà!"* suddenly cried one of the gentlemen, *"voilà Justine Marie qui arrive!"*

This moment was peculiar to me. I summoned to memory the pictured nun on the panel; present to my mind was the sad love-story. I saw in thought the vision of the garret, the apparition of the alley, the strange birth of the cradle. I underwent a presentiment of discovery, a strong conviction of coming disclosure. Ah! When imagination runs riot, where do we stop? What winter tree so bare and branchless, what wayside, hedge-munching animal so humble, that Fancy, a passing cloud, and a struggling moonbeam, will not clothe in spirituality and make of it a phantom?

With solemn force pressed upon my heart, the expectation of mystery breaking forth: hitherto I had seen this spectre only through a glass darkly; now was I to behold it face to face. I leaned forward; I looked.

"She comes!" cried Josef Emanuel.

The circle opened as if to admit a new and welcome member. At this instant, a torch chanced to be carried past; its blaze aided the pale moon in doing justice to the crisis, lighting the dénouement pressing on to perfection. Surely those near me must have felt some little of the anxiety I felt, in degree so unmeasured. Of that group, the coolest must have "held his breath for a time!" As for me, my life stood still.

It is over. The moment and the nun have come. The crisis and the revelation are passed by.

The flambeau glared still within a yard, held aloft in a park-keeper's hand; its long, eager tongue of flame almost licked the figure of the Expected—there, standing full in my sight. What was she like? What did she wear? How did she look? Who was she?

There were many masks in the park tonight, and as the hour wore late, a strange feeling of revelry and mystery began to spread abroad, so that scarce would you discredit me, reader, were I to say she was like the nun of the attic, wearing black skirts and white head-clothes, looking the resurrection of the flesh, a risen ghost.

All falsities! All figments! We will not deal in such gear. Let us be honest, and cut, as heretofore, from the homely web of truth.

"Homely," though, is an ill-chosen word. What I saw was not precisely homely. A girl of Villette stood there—a girl fresh from her pensionnat. She was very comely, possessing the beauty indigenous to this country. She looked well-nourished, fair, and plump of flesh. Her cheeks were round, her eyes good, her hair abundant. She was handsomely dressed. She was not alone; her escort consisted of three persons—two being elderly, whom she addressed as "Mon Oncle" and "Ma Tante."
