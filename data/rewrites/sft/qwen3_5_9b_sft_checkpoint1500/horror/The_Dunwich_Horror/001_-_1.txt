A traveler venturing north-central Massachusetts who mistakes the turn at the Aylesbury Pike, just past Dean's Corners, finds himself stranded in a landscape that defies nature's usual order. Here, the earth climbs steeply, forcing the stone walls—fringed with thorny briars—to squeeze tighter against the rutted, dust-choked path until the road itself seems hemmed in. The forests that break the terrain grow with a oppressive density, their trees swollen and unnatural, while the undergrowth of weeds and brambles thrives with a luxuriance alien to civilized lands. Yet amidst this wild vigor, the cultivated fields are shockingly sparse and barren, looking like scars on the skin of the land. The scattered dwellings, few and far between, share a disturbing uniformity: they are aged, squalid, and crumbling, standing as monuments to a forgotten decline. One hesitates, an instinctive dread seizing the throat, to approach the gnarled, solitary figures occasionally spotted on rotting doorsteps or wandering the rocky slopes. These people move with a silence so profound and a furtiveness so unsettling that they feel less like inhabitants and more like guardians of some forbidden, malignant secret, things best left undisturbed.

When the road finally ascends enough to reveal the mountains looming above the deep, suffocating woods, the chill of unease settles deeper into the bone. The summits are grotesquely symmetrical, lacking the rugged irregularity of true peaks, appearing instead as artificial, rounded domes. And atop these strange hills stand the stone pillars, tall and circular, rising like the teeth of some prehistoric beast against the sky. Deep gorges and ravines of impossible depth slice through the valley, crossed only by crude wooden bridges that look perpetually ready to collapse into the abyss below. When the road dips again, it plunges into stretches of marshland that strike the blood cold, especially at twilight. There, unseen whippoorwills chatter with a frantic, insect-like urgency, and fireflies bloom in an abnormal, sickly profusion, dancing to the raucous, grating rhythms of bullfrogs whose pipes sound like the grinding of stones.

The river, the Miskatonic, snakes through this nightmare, its thin, shimmering ribbon winding dangerously close to the feet of the domed hills, suggesting a serpentine malice rather than a life-giving flow. As the hills press closer, their dark, precipitous wooded sides loom like the flanks of sleeping giants, offering no escape route other than the treacherous path ahead. Crossing a covered bridge reveals a tiny, huddled village trapped between the streaming water and the sheer, vertical drop of Round Mountain. It is a cluster of rotting gambrel roofs, architectural ghosts from a century or two prior, starkly contrasting with the thriving towns nearby. A closer look confirms the despair: most houses are empty shells, decaying into ruin, and the broken steeple of the local church now shelters the sole, slovenly store of the hamlet. One cannot help but fear the shadows within the bridge's tunnel, yet there is no alternative. Emerging onto the village street, the air is thick with a faint, malign odor—the cloying scent of massed mold and centuries of slow decay. Relief comes swiftly when the road swings away from the place, circling the base of the hills and crossing the flat, open country beyond until the original Aylesbury Pike is reached again. Many travelers never know they have passed through Dunwich, for outsiders visit rarely, and since a season of unspeakable horror, every signpost pointing toward the town has been torn down.

Judged by any standard aesthetic, the scenery is breathtakingly beautiful, yet it draws no artists and no summer tourists. Two hundred years ago, when superstition ran high and tales of witch-blood and satanic worship were taken seriously, locals gave ample reasons for shunning the area. But in our sensible age, following the "Dunwich Horror" of 1928—which was quietly suppressed by those concerned with the town's and the world's welfare—people simply avoid it, unable to articulate why. Perhaps, though strangers remain ignorant, the truth lies in the natives' repulsive decadence. Isolated generations, cut off from outside influence, have walked a long, backward path of regression, forming a distinct and degenerate race marked by the physical and mental stigmata of severe inbreeding. Their collective intelligence is woefully low, and their history reeks of overt viciousness, hidden murders, incest, and acts of violence so perverse they defy naming. Even the old gentry, the remnants of two or three armigerous families who arrived from Salem in 1692, have barely maintained a foothold above the sordid populace. Many branches have sunk so deep into the sludge that only their names remain, serving as keys to an origin they utterly disgrace. Some of the Whateleys and Bishops still send their eldest sons to Harvard or Miskatonic University, only for those young men to rarely return to the moldering gambrel roofs where they and their ancestors were born.

No one, not even those privy to the facts of the recent tragedy, can precisely define what is wrong with Dunwich. However, old legends persist, whispering of unhallowed rites and Indian conclaves held amidst the hills, where forbidden shapes of shadow were summoned forth and wild, orgiastic prayers answered by loud cracklings and rumblings from beneath the earth. In 1747, Reverend Abijah Hoadley, newly installed at the Congregational Church, delivered a memorable sermon on the proximity of Satan and his demons. He declared:

"It must be allowed that these blasphemies of an infernal train of daemons are matters of too common knowledge to be denied; the cursed voices of Azazel and Buzrael, of Beelzebub and Belial, being heard from under ground by above a score of credible witnesses now living. I myself did not more than a fortnight ago catch a very plain discourse of evil powers in the hill behind my house; wherein there were a rattling and rolling, groaning, screeching, and hissing, such as no things of this earth could raise up, and which must needs have come from those caves that only black magic can discover, and only the devil unlock."

Reverend Hoadley vanished shortly after preaching these words, though the text, printed in Springfield, survives to this day. Reports of noises in the hills continued annually, baffling geologists and physiographers alike. Other traditions speak of foul odors wafting from the hill-crowning circles of stone pillars and of rushing, airy presences heard faintly from specific points at the bottom of the great ravines. There is also the Devil's Hop Yard, a bleak, blasted hillside where no tree, shrub, or blade of grass dares to take root. Furthermore, the natives hold a mortal terror of the numerous whippoorwills, which become vocal on warm nights. Folklore claims these birds are psychopomps, waiting to snatch the souls of the dying. They time their eerie cries in unison with the sufferer's gasping breath; if they catch the fleeing soul as it leaves the body, they instantly flutter away in demoniac laughter, but if they fail, they slowly subside into a disappointed silence.

Of course, these tales are obsolete and ridiculous, relics of a superstitious past. Yet Dunwich is ridiculously old, far older than any community within thirty miles. To the south of the village, one can still spy the cellar walls and chimney of the ancient Bishop House, constructed before 1700, while the ruins of the mill at the falls, built in 1806, constitute the most modern architecture visible. Industry never flourished here, and the nineteenth-century factory movement proved fleeting. The oldest structures of all are the great rings of rough-hewn stone columns crowning the hills, generally attributed to the indigenous peoples rather than the settlers. Within these circles and around the sizable table-like rock on Sentinel Hill, deposits of skulls and bones support the popular belief that these spots were once burial grounds for the Pocumtucks, even though many ethnologists, dismissing the absurd improbability of such a theory, insist the remains are Caucasian.
