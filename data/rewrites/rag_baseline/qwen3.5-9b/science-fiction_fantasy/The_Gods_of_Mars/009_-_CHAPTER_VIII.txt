To truly grasp the architecture of my deliverance, one must first confront the absolute terror of my imprisonment. For miles, the black pirate had maintained a hypnotic hold over me with his grotesque narrative, a distraction born of desperation. He sensed the approach of salvation—a battleship racing overhead—but for that single, fatal glance exchanged between us, the warship would have loomed directly above us moments ago. Had that happened, the boarding party, surely already swinging from the vessel's keel in their harness, would have swarmed our deck, extinguishing my fragile hope of escape in a sudden, total eclipse.

Yet, I was too seasoned a veteran of aerial warfare to panic. With surgical precision, I reversed the engines and plummeted the little vessel a sheer hundred feet into the abyss. Above me, the dangling forms of the boarding party bobbed like macabre toys as the battleship thundered past. Seizing the fleeting window, I surged upward at a sharp, violent angle, throwing my speed lever to its final notch.

Like a bolt fired from a crossbow, my splendid craft shot its steel prow straight at the whirring propellers of the leviathan above. If I could but make contact, the massive machine would be crippled for hours, granting us a sliver of freedom once more.

But at that critical instant, the sun breached the horizon, revealing a nightmare: a hundred grim, black faces peering over the stern of the battleship. A roar of pure rage erupted from a hundred throats. Commands screamed through the air, but it was too late to save the giant propellers. With a deafening crash, we drove our hull into the spinning blades.

Instantly, reacting to the shock of impact, I reversed my engine, but my prow was already wedged tight within the gaping wound I had torn in the battleship's stern. I hung suspended there for only a second before tearing free, but that brief interval was ample time for the black devils to swarm my deck.

There was no resistance. There was no space to fight; we were simply overwhelmed by numbers. Just as swords threatened my throat, a command from Xodar halted his fellows' violence.

"Secure them," he ordered calmly, "but do not injure them."

Several pirates had already released Xodar. Now, he personally oversaw my disarmament, ensuring I was properly bound. Or so he believed. Had I been a native Martian, the bindings might have held, but I had to suppress a grim smile at the puny strands confining my wrists. They were mere cotton strings; when the time came, I could snap them as easily as breaking dry twigs.

The girl was bound as well, and then they fastened us together. Meanwhile, they maneuvered our craft alongside the disabled battleship, and soon we were hoisted onto its deck. Fully a thousand black men manned the great engine of destruction. Their decks were packed with them, pressing forward until discipline forced them back, all eager to catch a glimpse of their captives.

The girl's beauty elicited a barrage of brutal comments and vulgar jests. It was painfully evident that these self-proclaimed supermen were infinitely inferior to the red men of Barsoom in both refinement and chivalry. My close-cropped black hair and thern complexion drew similar scrutiny. When Xodar recounted my fighting prowess and strange origins to his fellow nobles, they crowded around me, their curiosity insatiable.

The fact that I wore the harness and metal of a thern slain by a member of their party convinced them I was an enemy of their hereditary foes, placing me on surprisingly favorable terms in their estimation. Without exception, the blacks were handsome men, well-built and imposing. The officers stood out through the wondrous magnificence of their resplendent trappings. Many harnesses were so encrusted with gold, platinum, silver, and precious stones that they completely hid the leather beneath.

The commanding officer's harness was a solid mass of diamonds. Against the ebony backdrop of his skin, they blazed with a peculiarly accentuated effulgence. The whole scene was enchanting in its barbaric splendor: the handsome men; the opulent accoutrements; the polished skeel wood of the deck; the gloriously grained sorapus of the cabins, inlaid with priceless jewels and precious metals in intricate designs; the burnished gold of the handrails; the shining metal of the guns.

Phaidor and I were taken below decks, still fast-bound, and thrown into a small compartment containing a single porthole. As our escort departed, they barred the door behind them. We could hear the men working on the broken propellers, and through the porthole, we saw the vessel drifting lazily toward the south.

For some time, neither of us spoke. Each was lost in private contemplation. For my part, I wondered incessantly about the fate of Tars Tarkas and the girl, Thuvia. Even if they managed to evade pursuit, they must eventually fall into the hands of either the red men or the green ones. As fugitives from the Valley Dor, they could expect little else than a swift and terrible death.

How I wished I had accompanied them. It seemed certain that I could not fail to impress upon the intelligent red men of Barsoom the wicked deception that a cruel, senseless superstition had foisted upon them. Tardos Mors would believe me; of that, I was positive. And knowing his character, I was sure he would have the courage of his convictions. Dejah Thoris would believe me; not a doubt entered my head. Then there were a thousand of my red and green warrior friends who would gladly face eternal damnation for my sake. Like Tars Tarkas, wherever I led, they would follow.

My only danger lay in the possibility that, should I ever escape the black pirates, I might fall into the hands of unfriendly red or green men. Then it would mean short shrift for me. But there seemed little to worry about on that score, for the likelihood of my ever escaping the blacks was extremely remote.

The girl and I were linked by a rope that allowed us to move only about three or four feet apart. Upon entering the compartment, we had seated ourselves on a low bench beneath the porthole. The bench was the only furniture in the room, carved from sorapus wood. The floor, ceiling, and walls were constructed of carborundum aluminum, a light, impenetrable composition extensively used in the building of Martian fighting ships.

As I sat meditating on the future, my eyes were riveted to the porthole, which sat just level with them. Suddenly, I turned toward Phaidor. She regarded me with a strange expression I had never before seen on her face. She was incredibly beautiful in that moment.

Instantly, her white lids veiled her eyes, and I thought I detected a delicate flush tinging her cheeks. Evidently, she was embarrassed at being caught staring at a "lesser creature," I presumed.

"Do you find the study of the lower orders interesting?" I asked, laughing softly.

She looked up again with a nervous but relieved little laugh.

"Oh, very," she said, "especially when they possess such excellent profiles."

It was my turn to flush, but I did not. I felt she was poking fun at me, and I admired a brave heart capable of finding humor on the road to death. So, I laughed with her.

"Do you know where we are going?" she asked.

"To solve the mystery of the eternal hereafter, I imagine," I replied.

"I am going to a worse fate than that," she said, with a little shudder.

"What do you mean?"

"I can only guess," she replied, "since no thern damsel of all the millions stolen away by black pirates during the ages they have raided our domains has ever returned to narrate her experiences among them. That they never take a man prisoner lends strength to the belief that the fate of the girls they steal is worse than death."

"Is it not a just retribution?" I could not help but ask.

"What do you mean?"

"Do not the therns themselves do likewise with the poor creatures who take the voluntary pilgrimage down the River of Mystery? Was not Thuvia for fifteen years a plaything and a slave? Is it less than just that you should suffer as you have caused others to suffer?"

"You do not understand," she replied. "We therns are a holy race. It is an honor to a lesser creature to be a slave among us. Did we not occasionally save a few of the lower orders that stupidly float down an unknown river to an unknown end, lest they become the prey of the plant men and the apes?"

"But do you not, by every means, encourage the superstition among those of the outside world?" I argued. "That is the wickedest of your deeds. Can you tell me why you foster this cruel deception?"

"All life on Barsoom," she said, "is created solely for the support of the race of therns. How else could we live if the outer world did not furnish our labor and our food? Think you that a thern would demean himself by labor?"

"It is true then that you eat human flesh?" I asked in horror.

She looked at me with pitying commiseration for my ignorance.

"Truly we eat the flesh of the lower orders. Do not you also?"

"The flesh of beasts, yes," I replied, "but not the flesh of man."
