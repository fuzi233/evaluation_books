Catherine is rarely a blessing within a stranger's home; her influence is often a force of domination rather than harmony. Hardly had she crossed the threshold before the household began to shift under her invisible command: the utensils gleamed with renewed vigor, the hearth swept itself clean, the windows seemed to draw in extra sunlight, and the very spirit of the house appeared animated by a giant, energetic cricket. Yet, this cricket was a "Busy Body"—a formidable character lacking all discernment. It sought to correct everything and everyone indiscriminately. While many things improve when set aright, not everything does; indeed, nothing stands correction quite as poorly as living arrangements, save perhaps for the silence of the grave.

Consequently, Catherine rearranged every chair and table in Margaret's house, likely for the better, yet she felt compelled to disturb the living furniture as well. As Margaret's confinement approached, Catherine deceitfully summoned Denys and Martin to assist. When the simple-minded men earnestly inquired how they might be of service, she suggested they find utility by taking a short walk. She added, with a lack of tact, that extending this stroll into the following week would be even preferable. This was hardly an invitation designed to endear her to them.

The dynamic of the strong yielding to the whims of the weak might have appeased a she-bear with three cubs, one of whom was already sickly. Instead, Denys and Martin generally slipped out at dawn and returned like thieves at night. If caught inside, they moved with the caution of cats walking on walls lined with broken glass, wearing expressions of awe and a demeanor of total subjugation. Yet, this was insufficient. Their mere presence was ill-timed and grated on Catherine's nerves. Perhaps instinct whispered that a pair of depopulators had no place in a house destined to multiply twice over. No breastplate offers armor against a woman's tongue, and Catherine drove countless pins of sharp speech into them.

In summary, when Margaret descended the stairs, she discovered the kitchen stripped of its defenders. Martin, old and stiff, had retreated no further than the street, returning with the honors of war: he had commandeered a stool and sat upon it in mid-air. Margaret noted he was exposed to the sun but failed to realize he had become a fixture suspended in the luminary. Seeking Denys, she declared, "Good, kind Denys; he will be delighted to see me again."

Catherine, wiping a bowl with exaggerated vigor, informed her that Denys had departed for his friends in Burgundy. "High time!" Margaret observed, noting he hadn't visited in three years. "What, gone without bidding me farewell?" she asked, lifting two tender eyes like full-blown violets.

Catherine flushed. This new perspective pricked her conscience, yet she dismissed it with a toss of her head. "Oh, you were asleep at the time; I would not have woken you."

"Poor Denys," Margaret sighed, and visible dew gathered on her violet eyes. Watching from the corner of her eye, Catherine, without acknowledging the sentiment, turned her attention to the remaining soldier. Suddenly, she lavished hospitality and tenderness upon Martin, who was old and stiff in more ways than one.

"No, thank you, dame," Martin replied. "I have grown accustomed to being outdoors. I dislike changing and changing. I shall meddle with no one here, nor let anyone meddle with me."

"Oh, you nasty, cross old wretch!" Catherine shrieked, shifting instantly from syrup to vinegar, before flouncing back into the house.

On calm reflection, she allowed herself a small cry, then half-reconciled her actions by vowing that Margaret would never miss her "plagues of soldiers" again. Still feeling uneasy, she dispelled her regrets with a simple, sovereign act: she took the child and washed him. From head to foot, she bathed little Gerard in tepid water, and concerns regarding heroes and their grievances dissolved like dust in an ocean of soap and suds.

While this celestial ceremony continued, Margaret could not remain silent. Hovering around the fortunate washerwoman, she insisted on having an apparent, if not real, hand in the task. She dipped her finger into the water—presumably to pave the way for her son, though she surely knew Catherine would not permit her to judge the temperature. Kneeling opposite the infant, she chattered with remarkable fluency, careful not to speak as adults do, lest a cherub find their pronunciation ridiculous.

"I wish you could wash out *that*," she said, fixing her gaze on the baby's hand.

"What?"

"What? Have you not noticed? On his little finger."

Granny leaned in. There, indeed, was a tiny brown mole. "Eh, but this is wonderful!" she exclaimed. "Nature, my lass, you are strong—and meddling to boot. Have you noticed such a mark on anyone else? Tell the truth, girl!"

"On him? Nay, mother, not I."

"Well, then he has; and on the very spot. And you never noticed that much. But, dear heart, I forgot; you haven't known him from child to man as I have. I've had him hundreds of times on my knees, just as this, and washed him from top to toe in lukewarm water." Swelling with conscious superiority, she looked up at Margaret as a woman beyond comparison.

Catherine gazed down from her dizzying height of moral authority and mused. Unlike other busybodies, she occasionally reflected—not deeply, certainly, for she would ensure such thoughts remained unprinted. "It is strange," she remarked, "how things circle back. Life is but a whirligig. At least, for us poor women, our lives follow one pattern. Wasn't I, in my youth, trying to wash out my Gerard's mole? 'Oh, fie! Here's a foul blot!' I'd say, scrubbing until the poor wight cried, before deciding it was time to desist. And now you tell me, 'Mother,' 'try to wash out my Gerard's finger.' Think on it!"

"Wash it out?" Margaret cried. "I wouldn't for all the world! It is the sweetest part of his little body. I'll kiss it morning and night until the owner returns to us three. Oh, bless you, my jewel of gold and silver, for bearing a mark like your own daddy, to comfort me."

She kissed the mole, but could not stop there. Soon, she had little Gerard sprawled across her lap, kissing his back repeatedly, seeming to worry the lamb as a wolf might. Catherine watched, smiling. She had witnessed many such savage onslaughts in her day. This brief sketch indicated the tenor of Margaret's life for several months.

A few minor events occurred during this period that deserve mention, though I reserve them for another thread, as one string can hold many glass beads. However, while her boy's father endured those terrifying storms of the soul, culminating in the dead calm of the monastery, Margaret's existence could be summarized in one great, blissful word: Maternity.

To those who understand the weight of that word, expand this sketch slightly. See the young mother nursing, washing, dressing, and undressing; see her cooing and gamboling with her firstborn. Then, swift as lightning, dart your eye to Italy and behold the cold cloister, where monks pass like ghosts, heads bowed, hands meekly crossed over chests dead to earthly feeling. One of these cowled specters is the man whose return, filled with love, youth, and joy—the radiant young mother awaits.

Consider the traveler in the valley of Grindelwald. To one side rise the perpendicular Alps: rock, ice, and eternal snow, towering above the clouds and piercing the sky. To the other lie the modest, everyday slopes, green as emeralds, dotted with cows and charming cottages, teeming with life. Where those lofty neighbors stand leafless, lifeless, and inhumanly sublime, the lower valleys offer a gentle contrast.

Elsewhere, the commonplaces of nature often go unnoticed; but facing the grim Alps, they soothe and gently strike the mind through their juxtaposition with such tremendous opposites. Thus, in their own way, are the two halves of this story correctly viewed: on the Italian side, rugged adventure, strong passion, blasphemy, vice, penitence, pure ice, and holy snow soaring straight toward heaven. On the Dutch side, everything remains on a humble, feminine scale, yet evergreen. Just as a path divides the ice towers of Grindelwald, aspiring to the heavens, from its little sunny hillsides, so too here exists but a single page between "The Cloister and the Hearth."
