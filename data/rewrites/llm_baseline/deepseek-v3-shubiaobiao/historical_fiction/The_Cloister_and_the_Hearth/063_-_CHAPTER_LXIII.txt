A shadow fell upon a noble soul. Gerard’s love for Margaret had been his guiding light, pure and unwavering. Now that light was extinguished, and he drifted through a sea of despair, unmoored and lost. Worse still was the bitter sting of regret—had he stayed in Holland, had he not chased ambition across the continent, she might still be alive. The thought festered in him, poisoning his heart.

Driven by anguish, he flung himself into the whirl of vice. In Rome, where pleasure too often meant debauchery, he became its willing slave. Wine, women, dice—anything to drown the relentless ache within. The gold he had once set aside for Margaret now fueled his recklessness, and he soon led the very company he had once shunned. His spirit, once so tender, hardened. He abandoned his careful script for hasty portraiture, then abandoned even that when the lure of revelry called.

The descent was swift. He left his old lodgings, unable to bear the landlady’s gentle reproaches, and moved where none would remind him of what he had been. His new companions were the dissolute, the jaded, the cruel. Among them, Pietro Vanucci—a cynic, a libertine, a man who mocked virtue—became his closest shadow. Gerard, once so full of fire, now moved through life like a ghost, cold to friendship, deaf to conscience.

Then came the day of the buffalo race up the Tiber, a spectacle of folly devised by Vanucci. The revelers gathered, each with a beauty on his arm, laughing, drinking, daring one another to greater excess. Gerard arrived late, leading a figure so striking the others fell silent—tall, olive-skinned, with eyes like dark jewels. The women bristled; the men gaped. Only Vanucci, coiled in his seat like a serpent, watched with sly amusement.

The journey began, the boat drawn by snorting beasts, the party mocking their sluggish pace. A foolhardy gallant, goading one too many times, was tossed into the river, and the laughter rose again. But Gerard’s companion, sharp-tongued and fearless, turned on the women when their whispers grew too pointed.

*"Your beauty is bought in shops!"* she jeered. *"Paint on your cheeks, dye in your hair, padding in your bodices—false from head to heel!"*

The retort was swift. *"And you, who are so perfect—are you even a woman?"* A handful of almonds thrown into her lap betrayed her: the instinctive closing of the knees revealed a boy beneath the disguise. Laughter erupted, but the women, disarmed by his beauty, only fawned over him the more.

Yet Gerard, standing apart, felt no mirth. The sight of Princess Claelia passing in her galley had struck him like a blow. Once, he had turned away from her, faithful to Margaret. Now, what was he? A drunkard, a wastrel, a man who kept company with painted boys and jaded rakes. Shame curdled in him.

From that moment, his despair deepened. He abandoned even the hollow laughter of his companions, wandering alone through Rome’s ruins, brooding, drinking, staring into the dark waters of the Tiber. The thought that had once horrified him now whispered with terrible allure: *Here lies peace. Here lies an end.*

And all the while, unseen, a foe watched. Not a friend to pull him back, but an enemy, waiting for the final stumble.

The abyss yawned before him. And Gerard, blind to all but his pain, stepped closer to its edge.
