The following evening, Old Mr. Sillerton Jackson descended upon the Archers' household for dinner, a ritual as cherished by Mrs. Archer as any social gala. To Mrs. Archer, a woman of quiet temperament who nonetheless hungered for the currents of the city's tide, Mr. Jackson was the ultimate cartographer of the local landscape. With the patience of a fossil hunter and the taxonomic rigor of a naturalist, he cataloged the lives of his neighbors, piecing together a mosaic of the season from the fragments gathered by his sister, Miss Sophy Jackson. While Sophy, the perpetual guest of those who could not secure her formidable brother, supplied the minor gossip, Mr. Jackson reserved his visits for moments when specific knowledge was required. Thus, whenever Mrs. Archer desired to understand the shifting tides of society, she extended her invitation, honoring so few others that Mr. Jackson invariably arrived in person rather than dispatching his sister.

If Mr. Jackson could have engineered the universe, he would have orchestrated dinners where Newland was absent. Though the two men shared a genuine camaraderie at their club, the old anecdotist sensed a subtle, unspoken calculation in Newland's demeanor—a tendency to weigh his words with a gravity that the ladies of the family, in their innocent gossips, rarely displayed. Furthermore, he harbored a secret grievance regarding the quality of Mrs. Archer's cuisine. Yet, New York itself was divided into two distinct tribes: the Mingotts and Mansons, who venerated gastronomy, fashion, and fortune, and the Archer-Newland-van der Luyden clan, devotees of travel, horticulture, and high literature, who viewed the pleasures of the table as somewhat vulgar. One could not feast on canvas-backed ducks and vintage wine at the Lovell Mingotts' and simultaneously discuss Alpine vistas and *The Marble Faun* at Adeline Archer's, especially since the Archer Madeira had circled the Cape and returned transformed. Consequently, when summoned by Mrs. Archer, Mr. Jackson, a man of eclectic tastes, would sigh to his sister, "A trifle gouty since the Mingotts'—this diet at Adeline's shall do me a world of good."

Mrs. Archer, a widow long past, resided with her son and daughter on West Twenty-eighth Street. An entire floor was dedicated to Newland, leaving the mother and daughter to squeeze into the narrower quarters below. In this unclouded harmony, they cultivated ferns within Wardian cases, crafted macramé lace and wool embroideries, collected American Revolutionary glassware, subscribed to *Good Words*, and devoured Ouida's novels for the sheer atmospheric beauty of their Italian settings. They preferred tales of peasant life for the lush descriptions of nature and the wholesome sentiments, yet generally favored stories of society, finding the motives of the genteel more comprehensible. They spoke sharply of Dickens, whom they accused of failing to draw a gentleman, and regarded Thackeray as less worldly-wise than Bulwer-Lytton, who, however, was already beginning to be deemed old-fashioned. Both Mrs. and Miss Archer were ardent lovers of scenery, the primary pursuit of their occasional travels abroad, regarding architecture and painting as subjects reserved for men, or perhaps for scholars who read Ruskin.

Born a Newland, Mrs. Archer and her daughter Janey were, as the town whispered, "true Newlands." They stood tall and pale, slightly round-shouldered with long noses and sweet smiles, possessing a certain drooping distinction reminiscent of faded Reynolds portraits. Their physical resemblance would have been absolute were it not for the fact that an elderly *embonpoint* strained Mrs. Archer's black brocade, while Miss Archer's brown and purple poplins hung increasingly loosely upon her virginal frame. Mentally, however, the likeness was less complete than their identical mannerisms suggested. Years of living in a mutually dependent intimacy had bestowed upon them a shared vocabulary and a habit of prefacing opinions with "Mother thinks" or "Janey thinks," depending on who wished to advance the view. Yet, beneath the surface, while Mrs. Archer's serene unimaginativeness found comfort in the familiar and accepted, Janey was prone to sudden aberrations of fancy, wellsprings of suppressed romance bubbling up from depths unknown to her mother.

Mother and daughter adored each other and held their son and brother in reverential esteem. Archer, in return, loved them with a tenderness rendered compunctious and uncritical by the weight of their exaggerated admiration, bolstered by a secret satisfaction in their reliance. He believed it essential for a man to have his authority respected within his own home, even if his sense of humor occasionally led him to question the rigidity of his mandates.

That evening, Newland was certain Mr. Jackson would prefer dining elsewhere, yet he had his own reasons for staying. Of course, Old Jackson desired to speak of Ellen Olenska, and of course, Mrs. Archer and Janey craved his accounts of her. All three would feel a distinct embarrassment given Newland's prospective alliance with the Mingott clan, and the young man awaited the moment with amused curiosity to observe how they would navigate this delicate difficulty.

They began, obliquely, by discussing Mrs. Lemuel Struthers. "It is a pity the Beauforts asked her," Mrs. Archer observed gently. "But then Regina always does what he tells her; and *Beaufort*..."

"Certain *nuances* escape Beaufort," Mr. Jackson interjected cautiously, inspecting the broiled shad as if searching for evidence of a crime, and wondering for the thousandth time why Mrs. Archer's cook always reduced the roe to ash. (Newland, who had long shared this wonder, could always detect it in the older man's expression of melancholy disapproval.)

"Oh, necessarily," said Mrs. Archer. "Beaufort is a vulgar man. My grandfather Newland always used to say to my mother, 'Whatever you do, do not let that fellow Beaufort be introduced to the girls.' But at least he has had the advantage of associating with gentlemen; in England, too, they say. It is all very mysterious—" She glanced at Janey and paused. Mother and daughter knew every fold of the Beaufort mystery, but in public, Mrs. Archer maintained the decorous assumption that the subject was inappropriate for an unmarried woman.

"But this Mrs. Struthers," Mrs. Archer continued, "what did you say *she* was, Sillerton?"

"Out of a mine: or rather, out of the saloon at the head of the pit. Then with Living Wax-Works, touring New England. After the police broke *that* up, they say she lived—" Mr. Jackson, in his turn, glanced at Janey, whose eyes began to bulge from beneath her prominent lids. There remained hiatuses in her understanding of Mrs. Struthers's past.

"Then," Mr. Jackson continued—and Archer observed that he was wondering why no one had instructed the butler never to slice cucumbers with a steel knife—"then Lemuel Struthers came along. They say his advertiser used the girl's head for the shoe-polish posters; her hair is intensely black, you know—the Egyptian style. Anyhow, he... eventually... married her." There were volumes of innuendo in the spacing of the "eventually," and each syllable was given its due stress.

"Oh, well—at the pass we've come to nowadays, it doesn't matter," said Mrs. Archer indifferently. The ladies were not truly interested in Mrs. Struthers at that precise moment; the subject of Ellen Olenska was too fresh and too absorbing. Indeed, Mrs. Struthers's name had been introduced solely so that Mrs. Archer might presently ask: "And Newland's new cousin—the Countess Olenska? Was *she* at the ball too?"

A faint touch of sarcasm lingered in the reference to her son, and Archer knew it and had anticipated it. Even Mrs. Archer, who was seldom unduly pleased with human events, had been altogether glad of her son's engagement. ("Especially after that silly business with Mrs. Rushworth," as she had remarked to Janey, alluding to what had once seemed to Newland a tragedy bearing a scar upon his soul forever.)

There was no better match in New York than May Welland; consider the question from whichever angle you chose. Of course, such a union was exactly what Newland was entitled to. Yet, young men are so foolish and incalculable—and some women so ensnaring and unscrupulous—that it was nothing short of a miracle to see one's only son safely past the Siren Isle and into the haven of blameless domesticity.

All this Mrs. Archer felt, and her son knew she felt; but he also knew that she had been perturbed by the premature announcement of his engagement, or rather, by its cause. For that reason—because, on the whole, he was a tender and indulgent master—he had stayed home that evening. "It is not that I do not approve of the Mingotts' *esprit de corps*," he grumbled to Janey, the sole witness of her slight lapses from perfect sweetness. "But why Newland's engagement should be mixed up with that Olenska woman's comings and goings, I do not see."

She had behaved beautifully—and in beautiful behavior, she was unsurpassed—during the call on Mrs. Welland. Yet, Newland knew (and his betrothed doubtless guessed) that throughout the visit, she and Janey had been nervously on guard for Madame Olenska's possible intrusion.
