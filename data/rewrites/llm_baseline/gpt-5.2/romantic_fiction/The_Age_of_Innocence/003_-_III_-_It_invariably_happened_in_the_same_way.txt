It invariably happened in the same way: on the night of her annual ball, Mrs. Julius Beaufort never failed to appear at the Opera, as punctually as a course in a well-ordered dinner. She chose an Opera night on purpose, as one chose a costly truffle or a rare early peach—not for hunger, but for the pleasure of showing that hunger had nothing to do with it. The ball could be set in motion without her; it was proof, offered to all New York, that household cares were for other women, and that in her house an invisible brigade could whip, roast, ice, light, polish, and arrange the world while she sat serene behind her fan.

The Beauforts’ brown-stone was one of the few that possessed a ball-room—a chamber built for a single lavish use and then left, for three-hundred-and-sixty-four days of the year, to a shuttered darkness that smelled faintly of beeswax, closed velvet, and stored gilt chairs stacked like unused serving-platters in a corner. In an age when it was beginning to be called “provincial” to stretch a crash over the drawing-room floor and send the furniture upstairs like so many kitchen stools before a feast, the mere fact of a permanent ball-room—kept for no daily purpose, like a family silver tureen too sacred for soup—was felt to compensate for whatever might be regrettable in the Beaufort past.

Mrs. Archer, who liked to coin her social philosophy into axioms the way certain cooks reduced sauces to their sharp essence, had once said: “We all have our pet common people—” The phrase was a daring seasoning, but its truth was secretly savoured in many an exclusive bosom. The Beauforts, however, were not exactly common; some whispered they were worse. Mrs. Beaufort had been Regina Dallas, of one of America’s most honoured families—a penniless beauty, introduced to New York by her cousin Medora Manson, who was forever doing the wrong thing from the right motive, as if she could not help adding nutmeg where propriety required only salt.

To be related to the Mansons and the Rushworths was to possess, as Mr. Sillerton Jackson—who had frequented the Tuileries—called it, a “_droit de cité_” in New York society. But did one not forfeit it by marrying Julius Beaufort? That question, like the origin of a suspiciously excellent pâté, was never asked aloud at table, though everyone turned it over privately.

Who was Beaufort? He passed for an Englishman; he was agreeable, handsome, ill-tempered, hospitable, witty. He had come to America with letters from old Mrs. Manson Mingott’s English son-in-law, the banker, and had quickly made himself important in affairs; yet his habits were dissipated, his tongue bitter, his antecedents mysterious. When Medora Manson announced Regina’s engagement, it was received as another of Medora’s imprudences—an unwise marriage being, in that world, as irreparable as a ruined consommé.

But folly is as often justified of her children as wisdom. Two years after the marriage it was admitted—through pursed lips—that young Mrs. Beaufort had the most distinguished house in New York. No one could quite say how the miracle was accomplished. She was indolent, passive; the caustic called her dull. Yet she dressed like an idol hung with pearls, grew younger and blonder and more beautiful with each season, and sat throned in Mr. Beaufort’s heavy palace drawing all the world there without lifting her jewelled little finger, as if mere presence were enough to summon candles into flame and musicians into tune.

The knowing people said it was Beaufort himself who did the work—trained the servants to move like a drilled kitchen staff; taught the chef new dishes (and made sure they were served hot, not sacrificed to delay); told the gardeners what hot-house flowers to force for the dinner-table and drawing-rooms; selected the guests; brewed the after-dinner punch; even dictated the little notes his wife wrote. If he did, these labours were performed in private, in the service corridors where the real life of a house went on, where one heard the rustle of skirts, the clink of glass, the muffled orders—an acoustic ecology as carefully managed as the menu. To the world he presented himself as a careless, hospitable millionaire who strolled into his own rooms with the detachment of an invited guest and said, lightly: “My wife’s gloxinias are a marvel, aren’t they? I believe she gets them out from Kew.”

That, people agreed, was his secret: the way he carried things off. It was all very well to whisper that he had been “helped” to leave England by the international banking-house in which he had been employed; he carried off that rumour as easily as he carried off everything. New York’s business conscience was as sensitive as its moral standard; yet he swept all resistance before him and all New York into his drawing-rooms. For over twenty years now people had said they were “going to the Beauforts’” with the same tone of security as if they had said they were going to Mrs. Manson Mingott’s—only with the added satisfaction of knowing they would get hot canvas-back ducks and vintage wines instead of tepid Veuve Clicquot without a year and warmed-up croquettes from Philadelphia.

So Mrs. Beaufort, as usual, appeared in her box just before the Jewel Song; and when, again as usual, she rose at the end of the third act, drew her opera cloak about her lovely shoulders, and vanished, New York understood, as clearly as it understood the passing of a dish from hand to hand, that in half an hour the ball would begin.

The Beaufort house was one New Yorkers were proud to show to foreigners, especially on this night. The Beauforts had been among the first to own their own red velvet carpet and have it rolled down the steps by their own footmen under their own awning, instead of hiring it with the supper and the ball-room chairs. They had also inaugurated the custom of letting ladies take their cloaks off in the hall, instead of shuffling up to the hostess’s bedroom to recurl their hair by the gas-burner, as if preparing oneself were a backstage affair unfit for public. Beaufort was understood to have remarked that he supposed all his wife’s friends had maids to see they were properly _coiffées_ before leaving home. Even this was part of his performance: the quiet assertion that in his house the right things happened without fuss, like bread rising in a warm kitchen.

Then there was the bold plan of the house itself: instead of squeezing through a narrow passage to the ball-room (as at the Chiverses’), one marched solemnly down a vista of enfiladed drawing-rooms—the sea-green, the crimson, the bouton d’or—each one a different note in a carefully composed progression. From far off one saw many-candled lustres reflected in polished parquetry; beyond, the depths of a conservatory, where camellias and tree-ferns arched their costly foliage over seats of black and gold bamboo. In those planted depths, among damp earth and waxy leaves, the air carried its own language—green, faintly metallic, and sweet—while from the ball-room came the softened thunder of feet and music, moderated by doors and heavy hangings the way a good host moderated noise with architecture.

Newland Archer, as became a young man of his position, strolled in somewhat late. He left his overcoat with the silk-stockinged footmen—silk stockings being one of Beaufort’s few fatuities, an affectation like putting gold leaf on a dish that was already rich enough—and dawdled in the library hung with Spanish leather and furnished with Buhl and malachite, where a few men chatted and tugged on their dancing-gloves. Their voices had the dry, confident cadence of men who had never needed to listen for anything but approval. At last he joined the line of guests whom Mrs. Beaufort received on the threshold of the crimson drawing-room, with the faint air of someone who had stepped away from a supper-table merely to prove she could.

Archer was distinctly nervous. He had not gone back to his club after the Opera, as the young bloods usually did; instead, the night being fine, he had walked some distance up Fifth Avenue before turning back toward the Beauforts’. The city’s sounds had seemed unusually present—the far rattle of wheels, the occasional shouted call, the thin metallic ring of modernity creeping in at the edges of the old arrangements. He found himself thinking how a harsh note could spoil a whole evening, as one wrong spice could spoil a sauce. He was afraid the Mingotts might be going too far: that they might, in fact, have Granny Mingott’s orders to bring the Countess Olenska to the ball.

From the tone in the club box he had understood how grave a mistake that would be. And though he was more than ever determined to see the thing through—determined, that is, to manage Ellen’s return to the family circle without catastrophe—he felt less chivalrously eager to champion his betrothed’s cousin than before their brief talk at the Opera. The sound of Ellen’s name in other mouths had been like a foreign word pronounced too loudly: it did not belong to the accustomed lexicon of New York, and therefore it threatened the whole fragile grammar of their lives.

Wandering on to the bouton d’or drawing-room—where Beaufort had had the audacity to hang “Love Victorious,” Bouguereau’s much-discussed nude—Archer found Mrs. Welland and her daughter standing near the ball-room door. Beyond them couples were already gliding over the floor: the wax-candlelight fell on revolving tulle skirts, on girlish heads wreathed with modest blossoms, on the dashing aigrettes and ornaments of young married women’s coiffures, and on the glitter of highly glazed shirt-fronts and fresh _glacé_ gloves. The room breathed a mingled perfume—hot wax, crushed flowers, the faint citrus bite of punch, and, from somewhere farther back, the savoury promise of supper: roast skin tightening under heat, sauces held at just the right trembling point before separation.

Miss Welland, evidently about to join the dancers, hovered on the threshold with lilies-of-the-valley in her hand—she carried no other bouquet. Her face was a little pale, her eyes burning with a candid excitement. Around her was a small crowd of young men and girls; there was hand-clasping, laughter, and pleasantry, while Mrs. Welland stood slightly apart, shining on them the beam of a qualified approval. It was evident May was in the act of announcing her engagement, while her mother affected the parental reluctance considered suitable—like a hostess protesting she has done nothing, while the table groans under dishes.

Archer paused. It had been at his express wish that the announcement should be made, and yet this was not how he would have chosen to have his happiness known. To proclaim it in the heat and noise of a crowded ball-room was to rob it of the fine bloom of privacy which should belong to things nearest the heart—like cutting into a delicate dessert before the perfume has had time to rise. His joy was so deep that this blurring of the surface left its essence untouched; but he would have liked to keep the surface pure too.

It was some satisfaction to find that May shared his feeling. Her eyes fled to his beseechingly, and their look said: Remember, we’re doing this because it’s right. No appeal could have found a more immediate response in Archer’s breast; only he wished the necessity had been dictated by some ideal reason, and not simply by poor Ellen Olenska, whose very presence threatened to change the vocabulary of their world.

The group made way for him with significant smiles; after taking his share of congratulations, he drew his betrothed into the middle of the ball-room and put his arm about her waist.

“Now we shan’t have to talk,” he said, smiling into her candid eyes, as they floated away on the soft waves of the “Blue Danube.”

She made no answer. Her lips trembled into a smile, but her eyes remained distant and serious, as if fixed on some ineffable vision beyond the chandeliers and the turning skirts.

“Dear,” Archer whispered, pressing her closer; and it was borne in on him that the first hours of being engaged, even when spent in a ball-room, had something grave and sacramental in them. What a new life it was going to be, with this whiteness, this radiance, this goodness at one’s side—like beginning a family recipe that one’s children and their children would repeat without ever knowing the first hand that had measured and stirred.

When the dance was over, the two, as became an affianced couple, wandered into the conservatory. There, behind a tall screen of tree-ferns and camellias, with moisture beading on leaves like clear broth on the rim of a bowl, Newland pressed her gloved hand to his lips.

“You see I did as you asked me to,” she said.

“Yes. I couldn’t wait,” he answered, smiling.

After a moment he added: “Only I wish it hadn’t had to be at a ball.”

“Yes, I know.” She met his glance comprehendingly. “But after all—even here we’re alone together, aren’t we?”

“Oh, dearest—always!” Archer cried.

Evidently she was always going to understand; always going to say the right thing in the right idiom of their class. The discovery made his happiness overflow, and he went on gaily: “The worst of it is that I want to kiss you and I can’t.”

As he spoke he glanced about the conservatory, assured himself of their momentary privacy, and, catching her to him, laid a fugitive pressure on her lips. The act was as swift as stealing a taste from a sauce before it was served—more daring for being so slight. To counteract the audacity, he led her to a bamboo sofa in a less secluded part of the conservatory. Sitting down beside her, he broke a sprig of lily-of-the-valley from her bouquet, crushing between his fingers that cool, shy fragrance that seemed made for secrecy.

She sat silent, and for a moment the world lay like a sunlit valley at their feet: the candlelight, the music muffled by foliage, the subdued roar of society moving as one body, and beneath it all the steady, disciplined labour of servants and cooks, the unseen hands keeping heat in the dishes and order in the rooms.

“Did you tell my cousin Ellen?” she asked at last, as if speaking through a dream.

He roused himself and remembered that he had not. Some invincible repugnance to speak of such things to that strange foreign woman had checked the words on his lips, as if his tongue refused the unfamiliar name in the presence of his own happiness.

“No—I hadn’t the chance after all,” he said, fibbing hastily.

“Ah.” She looked disappointed, but gently resolved on gaining her point. “You must, then, for I didn’t either; and I shouldn’t like her to think—”

“Of course not. But aren’t you, after all, the person to do it?”

She pondered. “If I’d done it at the right time, yes: but now that there’s been a delay I think you must explain that I’d asked you to tell her at the Opera, before our speaking about it to everybody here. Otherwise she might think I had forgotten her. You see, she’s one of the family, and she’s been away so long that she’s rather—sensitive.”

Archer looked at her glowingly. “Dear and great angel! Of course I’ll tell her.” He glanced, a trifle apprehensively, toward the crowded ball-room, where the music surged and broke and surged again like a tide. “But I haven’t seen her yet. Has she come?”

“No; at the last minute she decided not to.”

“At the last minute?” he echoed, betraying his surprise that she should ever have considered the alternative possible.

“Yes. She’s awfully fond of dancing,” May answered simply. “But suddenly she made up her mind that her dress wasn’t smart enough for a ball, though we thought it so lovely; and so my aunt had to take her home.”

“Oh, well—” said Archer, with happy indifference.

Nothing about his betrothed pleased him more than her resolute determination to carry to its utmost limit that ritual of ignoring the unpleasant in which they had both been brought up. She knew as well as he did the real reason Ellen had stayed away; yet May’s gentle words offered a different story, a safer dialect for the family to speak in public—a language that preserved the soundscape of their world, smoothing discord into silence.

“She knows,” he thought, “the real reason; but I shall never let her see, by the least sign, that I am conscious of there being a shadow of a shade on poor Ellen Olenska’s reputation.”
