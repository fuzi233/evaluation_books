Midnight had barely claimed the Piazza della Signoria when the moon rose above the Old Palace, yet its silver light offered no comfort. Instead of casting the familiar play of shadow and breadth upon the cobblestones, the moonlight was swallowed by a living ocean of humanity. There was no pavement visible, save for the heads bobbing in a churning tide of eager, struggling bodies. Where usually the silence of the city held back the pattering of feet and the hum of nocturnal wanderers, here reigned a deafening roar. Shouts mingled with curses, boots trampled upon boots, and the accidental clash of weapons cut through the din, drowning out everything but the occasional darting shriek and the heavy, rhythmic tolling of a bell.

Inside the palace, the Council Chamber remained the epicenter of a storm that had raged through days of daylight and torchlit nights. The chief magistracies, the eighty senators, and the select citizens sat locked in debate over the fate of the prisoners: whether an appeal should be granted or if the sentence of death must be executed immediately to forestall the dangers of delay. Their arguments had grown so fierce, so akin to a brutal quarrel, that the clamor from within seeped out to meet the crowd below. For nearly the entire session, the vote had hung in a precarious balance, the Signoria deeply divided. Four magistrates stood immovable for the appeal, defying threats of sack and ruin, until Francesco Valori, in a brief, furious outburst, shattered the stalemate. With ominous clarity, Valori declared that if the Signoria refused to defend the liberties of Florence by executing the five "perfidious" citizens, others would seize the cause, endangering everyone who opposed them. In that moment, the Florentine Cato had triumphed. When the ballots were recounted, the four stubborn "white beans" vanished; nine fatal "black beans" remained, sentencing the five prisoners to immediate death—and, though unspoken, condemning Francesco Valori to a similar fate.

Now, as the Judicial Eight departed for the Bargello to prepare the machinery of justice, the five condemned men were led barefoot and shackled through the palace corridors. This humiliation, orchestrated by their friends, was a calculated gamble to stir the populace by associating such cruel ignominy with the two venerable elders, Bernardo del Nero and Niccolò Ridolfi, and the brilliant young leaders, Tornabuoni and Pucci. Yet the strategy failed to generate pity capable of rescue; instead, it merely fueled a tardy, vengeful fury.

Upstairs, amidst the weight of stone pillars in the courtyard, stood Romola. She waited for the arrival of her godfather, having secured permission through powerful intercession to remain with him until the council's verdict was sealed. Her heart, bent on clinging to the childless old man as her father once would, had silenced all objections. Beside her stood Giovan Battista Ridolfi, Niccolò's younger brother and a devoted follower of Savonarola, who himself walked toward death in bitterness to see his elder brother fall. They awaited the guards who would escort Bernardo to his doom.

Elsewhere in the palace, Tito lingered, unseen by Romola since the evening of the seventeenth. He understood, through the reports of the friar's neutrality, that her pleas had fallen on deaf ears. Surrounded by officials and foreign envoys, Tito maintained a grave, subdued silence, masking a painful internal strife between his public duty and private affection. When a whisper turned to mention his wife, he feigned indifference, claiming that her violent mental agitation and her deep-seated hostility toward the government—which he served—made any approach dangerous.

"The old Bardi blood!" scoffed Cennini with a shrug. "I shall not be surprised if this business shakes *her* loose from the Frate, as it has shaken others."

"It is excusable in a woman," smiled a young French envoy, bowing politely to Tito, "who is doubtless beautiful, to believe that her affections must override the welfare of the State. To think that no head falls unless it belongs to someone's cousin is a view not to be encouraged in men. It weakens Florentine polity."

"That is true," admitted Niccolò Machiavelli, his expression unreadable. "But where personal ties are strong, the hostilities they spawn must be reckoned with. Many of these halfway severities are merely hot-headed blunders. The only safe blows to strike at men and parties are those too heavy to be avenged."

"Nicolò," Cennini warned, "there is a clever wickedness in thy talk sometimes that makes me mistrust thy pleasant young face as if it were a mask of Satan."

"Not at all, my good Domenico," Machiavelli replied, laying a hand on the elder's shoulder. "Satan was a blunderer, an introducer of novelty who met with a stupendous failure. Had he succeeded, we would all be worshipping him, and his portrait would be far more flattering."

"Well, well," conceded Cennini. "I say not thy doctrine is not too clever for Satan; I only say it is wicked enough for him."

"I tell you," Machiavelli pressed, his voice taking on a prophetic edge, "my doctrine is the creed of all who seek an end slightly beyond their own noses. Ask our Frate, our prophet, how his universal renovation is to be achieved: first, by establishing a free and pure government. Since this cannot be accomplished by making all Florentines love one another, it must be done by cutting off every head that stands obstinately in the way. A man who incurs odium by sanctioning a severity that lacks the finality to be complete commits a blunder. And I suspect the Frate has committed exactly that blunder. On this occasion, he might have won great lustre by exerting himself to maintain the appeal; instead, he has lost lustre and gained no strength."

Before anyone could respond, the announcement rang out: the prisoners were leaving the chamber. The majority of the assembly surged toward the doors, jostling for the clearest path to the rear of the prison guard, drawn alike by deep passion and cold curiosity.

Tito remained behind. A native repugnance to sights of death and pain had once made him hope for exile rather than execution five days prior, a hope born of self-preservation. But now, the landscape of his fear had shifted. He felt that the instant decapitation of Bernardo del Nero was the ultimate guarantee of his own security. The new knowledge revealed to him by Romola on the day of his return had instilled a fresh dread of the power she wielded to render his position precarious. If any act of hers succeeded in making him an object of suspicion, he foresaw not only frustration but frustration under unpleasant circumstances. Her faith in Baldassarre had clearly hardened her wavering resolve against further submission; if her godfather lived, she would easily turn him to her side. Romola seemed more than ever an unmanageable fact in his destiny. Yet, if Bernardo were dead, the difficulties that would beset her in opposing her husband would likely become insurmountable to her shrinking pride. Thus, Tito felt a grim ease knowing the Eight had gone to the Bargello to erect the scaffold instantly. Four other men, his intimates and confederates, were to die alongside Bernardo. A man's own safety is often a god that makes grim demands. As he heard the shuffle of many feet and the clanking of iron on stairs echoing from the open doorway, Tito answered the French envoy's questions with a mask of sad resignation to the necessities of the State.

To Romola, those falling sounds were amplified, her senses heightened to a superhuman degree. She required no support; she shed no tears. An intensity of life, transcending both grief and joy, seized her—a state wherein the mind feels kin to the ancient forces that wrought existence before pleasure and pain entered the world. Since her godfather's fate was sealed, her previous inner struggle had collapsed into a total identification with him in these supreme moments. Inwardly, she asserted for him that if he suffered the punishment of treason, he did not deserve the name of traitor; he was the victim of a collision between two forms of faithfulness. It was not given him to die for the noblest cause, and yet he died precisely because of his nobility. He might have been a meaner man and found it easier to avoid this guilt. Romola felt the full force of that sympathy for the individual lot, constantly opposing the rigid formulas by which actions and parties are judged. She was treading the path with her second father to the scaffold, steeling herself to defy ignominy through the consciousness that it was undeserved.

The route to the execution site was fenced in by three hundred armed men, ordered by Francesco Valori. Among the contradictions of this event, none struck harder than the dual alarms: the genuine terror of the popular rage against the conspirators, and the equally real fear of a desperate rescue attempt by a hostile mob. Upon reaching the courtyard of the Bargello, Romola was permitted a fleeting moment to approach Bernardo with his confessor. Even in the crush of the agitated throng, many eyes were fixed on them. The aged man, forgetting that his hands were bound in iron, lifted them toward the bowed golden head, then checked the motion, leaning forward to kiss her. She seized the fettered hands hanging limp beside him and kissed them as if they were sacred relics.
