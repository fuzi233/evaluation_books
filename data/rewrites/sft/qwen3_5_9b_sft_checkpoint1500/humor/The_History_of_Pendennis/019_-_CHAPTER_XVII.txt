The Curate had departed on his usual errand to Fairoaks and was currently ensconced in Pen's study, ostensibly reading with his charge, when the afternoon of that same day brought a surprise. Mrs. Portman, having concluded her business with Mrs. Pybus, found the weather so gloriously fine that she decided to extend her stroll all the way to Fairoaks to pay a visit to her dear friend. During their conversation, the Rector's lady revealed a grand secret concerning the Curate, Mr. Smirke: namely, that he harbored a very old, deeply hidden attachment.

"And on whom has Mr. Smirke bestowed his heart?" inquired Mrs. Pendennis, feigning a superior air while harboring a distinct internal alarm.

"Why, my dear," replied the other lady, "when Mr. Smirke first arrived and dined at the Rectory, rumors flew that we coveted him for Myra, forcing us to withdraw our inquiries. Others claimed he was smitten elsewhere, but I always denied it, insisting that *you*..."

"That I!" cried Mrs. Pendennis, indignant. "People are dreadfully impertinent, I assure you. Mr. Smirke came here solely as Arthur's tutor, and I am shocked that anyone should dare speak so freely—"

"'Pon my soul, it is a trifle excessive," interjected the Major, lowering his newspaper and adjusting his double eye-glass.

"I've lost all patience with that Mrs. Pybus," Helen continued, her indignation rising.

"I told her there was no truth to it," Mrs. Portman explained. "I always maintained so, my dear. Now, however, it emerges that my demure gentleman has been secretly engaged to a young lady—Miss Thompson of Clapham Common—for quite some time. I am truly delighted, especially on Myra's account, for an unmarried curate can be a tiresome presence in one's household. Of course, the matter must remain strictly private, but I thought I ought to inform you, lest it cause further unpleasantness. However, mind you: not a single word of this story, if you please."

Mrs. Pendennis expressed her utmost delight at the news, hoping sincerely that the kind and amiable Mr. Smirke would soon secure a deserving wife. Once her visitor departed, Helen and her brother discussed the matter with immense satisfaction. The kindly lady chided herself for her recent haughty behavior toward Mr. Smirke, whom she had lately avoided, realizing now that she should have been grateful for his constant attention to Arthur.

"Gratitude to this sort of person is commendable," the Major remarked, "but familiarity is absolutely out of the question. This gentleman instructs and accepts payment like any other master. You are far too humble, my good soul. Distinctions between ranks must be observed. I warned you before that you were too kind to Mr. Smirke."

Yet Helen did not share her brother's skepticism. With Arthur preparing to depart, she recalled with fresh appreciation how polite Mr. Smirke had been, how he had run errands for her, delivered books, copied music, taught Laura, and gifted her with kindness. Her conscience stung her for her past ingratitude. Consequently, when Mr. Smirke descended from the study with Pen, looking eager for the hall prior to his leaving, she stepped forward, shaking his hand with a slightly flushing countenance, and invited him into her drawing-room—a sanctuary where they had rarely met. Given the prospect of a substantial dinner that day, she extended an invitation for him to partake, and it is safe to assume he was overjoyed to accept such a delightful summons.

Relieved of her former doubts and misgivings regarding the Curate, Helen proved exceedingly kind and gracious throughout the meal, perhaps doubling her attentions because the Major remained notably high-minded and reserved toward his nephew's tutor. When Pendennis requested wine, he addressed Smirke as if speaking to a sovereign addressing a lowly retainer, a manner so condescending that even Pen chuckled, though he himself was quite capable of being as conceited as most youths.

However, Smirke cared little for the Major's impertinence so long as his hostess treated him with such warmth. He spent a delightful time at her side, deploying all his conversational skills to please her. He discoursed with equal ease on the Fancy Bazaar, the Great Missionary Meeting, the latest novels, and the Bishop's excellent sermon on fashionable parties in London—an account he had gleaned from the newspapers. In short, he employed every art by which a college divine, possessing both sprightly and serious talents, a taste for the genteel, an irreproachable conduct, and a susceptible heart, endeavors to become agreeable to the object of his affection.

Not long after his sister and little Laura had retired from the apartment, the Major emerged from the dining-room yawning. "What an unsufferable bore that man is, and how he did talk!" he complained.

"He has been very good to Arthur, who is very fond of him," Mrs. Pendennis noted. "I wonder who the Miss Thompson is whom he intends to marry?"

"I always suspected the fellow was looking in another direction," said the Major.

"And in what?" asked Mrs. Pendennis, quite innocently. "Toward Myra Portman?"

"Toward Helen Pendennis, if you must know," answered her brother-in-law.

"Toward me! Impossible!" exclaimed Helen, who knew perfectly well that this had indeed been the case. "His marriage will undoubtedly be a very happy affair. I only hope Arthur does not imbibe too much wine."

Indeed, Arthur, flush with the pride of holding the cellar keys and anticipating several more dinners shared with his dear friend Smirke, had procured a liberal supply of claret. When the elders and little Laura departed, the two topplers began to pour the vintage freely. One bottle quickly yielded up the ghost; another shed more than half its blood within half an hour. Pen, with a hollow laugh and voice, drained a bumper to the falsehood of women, declaring sardonically that wine, at least, was a mistress who never deceived and was certain to offer a warm welcome.

Smirke gently countered that he knew of some women who embodied truth and tenderness. Casting his eyes upward toward the ceiling and heaving a sigh as if summoning some dear and unmentionable entity, he raised his glass and drained it. The rosy liquor began to suffuse his cheeks.

Pen recited some verses he had composed that morning, informing the listener that the woman who had slighted his passion was unworthy of winning it. He declared himself awakening from love's mad fever and proceeding to abandon a heartless deceiver. Though he would never again be the happy, careless boy of months past, nor possess the heart it had been before passion and grief nearly killed it, he hoped to demonstrate he was a man worthy of his race. Someday, he vowed, the false one would learn the magnitude of the treasure and nobility of the heart she had discarded.

Pen, a highly excitable individual, rolled out these verses in his rich, sweet voice, trembling with emotion. He possessed a habit of blushing in such states, and his large, honest grey eyes displayed a sensibility so genuine, hearty, and manly that Miss Costigan, if she had a heart, surely would have softened toward him. Very likely, she was altogether unworthy of the affection he lavished upon her.

The sentimental Smirke, caught by the emotion agitating his young friend, grasped Pen's hand over the dessert dishes and wine-glasses. "These verses are beautiful," he proclaimed. "You are a poet, a great poet, and by Heaven's permission, destined for a magnificent career." "Go on and prosper, dear Arthur," he cried. "The wounds you currently suffer are merely temporary; the very grief you endure will cleanse and strengthen your heart. I have always prophesied the greatest and brightest things of you, once you correct certain failings and weaknesses of character that presently afflict you. But you will overcome these, my boy; you will. And when you are famous and celebrated, as I know you will be, will you not remember your old tutor and the happy early days of your youth?"

Pen swore he would. With another vigorous handshake across the glasses and apricots, he declared, "I shall never forget how kind you have been to me, Smirke. I don't know what I should have done without you. You are my best friend."

"Am I, really, Arthur?" Smirke asked, peering through his spectacles, his heart beating so violently he feared Pen could hear it throbbing.

"My best friend, my friend forever," Pen repeated. "God bless you, old boy." He consumed the final glass of the second bottle of the famous wine, which his father had laid in, his uncle purchased, and Lord Levant imported. Like a slave indifferent to his fate, it now ministered pleasure to its present owner, granting delectation to its young master.
