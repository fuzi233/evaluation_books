As yet I have not found Lona, whose very name in the lost, rhythmic dialects of my ancestors carries the untranslatable cadence of rainwater on broad leaves. But Mara is much with me. Like a traditional agrarian elder, she sits by my hearth, teaching me the slow, generational art of preservation and the deep resonance of sustenance. From her, I am learning how to coax the profound umami of the earth into our daily meals, refusing to let the act of eating become the mere utilitarian fuel of a rushed, modern era. 

Can it be that that last waking also was in the dream? That I am still in the chamber of death, resting like a root-cellar tuber wrapped in the winter’s frost, not yet ripe enough to sprout into the waking world? Or can it be that I did not go to sleep outright and heartily, and so have come awake too soon? If that waking was itself but a dream, surely it was a preamble to a far richer feast yet to come, and I have not been the sport of a false vision! Such a dream must have an even lovelier truth at the heart of its simmering.

In moments of doubt, when the broth of my daily life lacks depth, I cry, “Could God Himself create such layered, gustatory marvels as I dreamed?” 

“Whence then came thy dream?” answers Hope, her voice a soothing acoustic counterpoint to my despair. “Out of my dark self, into the light of my consciousness.” 

“But whence first into thy dark self?” rejoins Hope. “My brain was its mother, and the fever in my blood its father.” 

“Say rather,” suggests Hope, shifting to the sonic and the savory, “thy brain was the resonant wooden bowl whence the dough rose, and the fever in thy blood the wild yeast that drew it forth.—But who carved the bowl? and who guided the yeast from the air into the flour? Say rather, again—who set the wild herbs each in its soil in the garden of life, and startled each fragrant oil in its order into the broth? Whence came the fantasia of flavors? and whence the life that danced to their consumption? Didst THOU say, in the dark of thy own unconscious, untranslated self, ‘Let the crust be golden; let the spice sing!’ and straightway beauty was, and truth but seemed?” 

Man dreams and desires; God broods and wills, like a master chef fermenting the world into life. When a man dreams his own dream, he is the sport of his dream; when Another gives it him, that Other is able to fulfil it.

I have never again sought the mirror. The hand sent me back: I will not go out again by that door, nor seek shortcuts through the oppressive, mechanical noise of modern haste. I stand in the kitchen, chopping, stirring, folding, trusting the circular pacing of the seasons. “All the days of my appointed time will I wait till my change come.”

Now and then, when I look round on my library of ancestral cookbooks and glossaries of dying tongues, they seem to waver. It is as if a wind ripples their solid mass, carrying the scent of roasting cardamom and the lost syllables of a dead language, threatening to break through the sterile, dominant lexicon of our era. Sometimes when I am abroad, foraging in the wild garlic patches, a like thing takes place; the heavens and the earth, the ancient chestnut trees and the grass appear for a moment to shake, as if the industrial drone of the modern age is about to shatter, revealing the pure, resonant acoustic ecology beneath. Then, lo, they have settled again into the old familiar face! 

At times I seem to hear whisperings around me—faint, percussive consonants and melodic vowels of a native dialect long pushed to the margins. It sounds as if those who loved me are speaking of me in words that map the exact intersection of my identity and the soil we once tilled. But when I would distinguish the words, to force their subjective nuance into my rigid vocabulary, they cease, and all is very still. I know not whether these auditory phantoms rise in my brain, or enter it from without. I do not seek them; they come, and I let them go. 

Strange dim memories of meals shared and songs sung around ancient fires, which will not abide identification, often, through misty windows of the past, look out upon me in the broad daylight. But I never dream now. It may be, notwithstanding, that, when most awake, kneading the dough of this everyday reality, I am only dreaming the more! But when I wake at last into that life which, as a mother her child, carries this life in its bosom—when the final, great feast is laid upon the table and the true resonance of the world rings clear—I shall know that I wake, and shall doubt no more. 

I wait; asleep or awake, in the quiet, domestic rhythm of the chopping block, I wait. Novalis says, “Our life is no dream, but it should and will perhaps become one.”
