“O gentlemen, the time of life is short;
To spend that shortness basely were too long,
If life did ride upon a dial’s point,
Still ending at the arrival of an hour.”

Two days after the sun-drenched archery meeting where Gwendolen Harleth’s sharp, bright laugh had outshone every other sound on the green, Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt lingered over his breakfast at his country estate, his long-time associate Mr. Lush sat across from him.
The morning was soft, golden and honey-thick: jasmine-scented summer air drifted through the wide open sash windows, where half a dozen pampered hounds wandered lazily in from the sun-warmed turf of the lawn beyond. The park rolled out purple-hued in the haze, fading into a thick wall of ancient oak wood at the horizon, and the breakfast room itself hummed with a quiet, old-world elegance that felt almost like a well-bred hush, far removed from the clatter and restlessness of common, newly rich households.
Whether the two men sharing the space were half as agreeable to each other as their surroundings was far less clear. Grandcourt had dragged his carved oak chair out of the table line to face the lawn, left leg propped carelessly over a second empty chair, right elbow braced on the linen tablecloth, smoking a fat, slow-burning cigar while Lush still picked at his poached eggs. The dogs drifted between them, half-playacting hunger, turning up their noses at the fancy table scraps they pretended to beg for — all except Fetch, the sleek liver-colored water spaniel, who sat planted firmly on her forepaws, big soft brown eyes fixed unblinkingly on Grandcourt, loyal to a fault no matter how little attention he paid her.
Perched on Grandcourt’s lap was a tiny Maltese dog with a silver bell on its collar, “Fluff,” whose warm little body he stroked whenever his hand was free of cigar or coffee cup. Fetch was clearly jealous, her drooping ears twitching every time Fluff’s bell tinkled, and when she could bear the neglect no longer she laid her heavy silky paw gently on her master’s knee.
Grandcourt stared at her, face entirely impassive, for a full half minute. Then he set down his cigar, lifted Fluff to nuzzle the tiny creature against his chin, and stroked her slow and deliberate, never taking his eyes off Fetch. The poor spaniel whimpered, trying to hold back the sound, until she finally rested her head against her own outstretched paw, looking up at him with a plea so piteous any dog-lover would have melted. Grandcourt, famous for his menagerie of well-cared-for hounds, understood exactly what she was asking for — but the second Fetch let out a loud, wounded howl of frustration, he pushed her paw off his leg without a word, dumped Fluff carelessly on the table (where the tiny dog’s black nose almost knocked over the salt cellar), and reached for his cigar, only to find it had gone out, and he blamed Fetch for the annoyance.
Fetch, once started wailing, couldn’t seem to stop. Her second howl was louder, the third even sharper.
“Turn that brute out, will you?” Grandcourt said to Lush, not raising his voice, not even glancing over at him — as if he knew full well his companion would jump to obey even the smallest unspoken order.
Lush stood at once, hoisted the heavy spaniel up even though he hated bending, and carried her out of the room, disposing of her somewhere out in the yard before he wandered back a couple minutes later. He lit his own cigar, angled his chair so he could watch Grandcourt’s face without turning his head, and spoke after a lazy drag.
“Ride or drive over to Quetcham today?”
“Not going to Quetcham.”
“You didn’t go yesterday, you know.”
Grandcourt smoked in silence for half a minute, the blue smoke curling slow around his head. “I assume you sent my card and my apologies round there?”
“I went myself at four yesterday,” Lush said. “Told them you were certain to show up shortly. They’ll assume some accident held you back. All the more reason to turn up today, really.”
Silence stretched for another two minutes. Then Grandcourt said, low and drawling, “Which people have you invited to stay here, the ones bringing their wives?”
Lush pulled a little leather notebook out of his pocket. “Captain and Mrs. Torrington arrive next week. Then Mr. Hollis and Lady Flora, the Cushats, and the Gogoffs.”
“Bit of a ragged lot,” Grandcourt said after a long pause. “Why on earth did you ask the Gogoffs? When you send out invites in my name, you will be so good as to show me the list first, instead of dropping a six-foot giantess of a woman on my doorstep unannounced. She makes the whole drawing room look ugly.”
“You invited the Gogoffs yourself, when we ran into them in Paris last spring,” Lush pointed out.
“What does running into them in Paris have to do with anything?” Grandcourt’s voice dropped, no longer the lazy, bored drawl he’d used all morning — now it was a quiet, sharp, unyielding tone, that Lush recognized after fifteen years as the sound of a will that would not be crossed. “I told you to show me the list before you sent any of them out.”
“Any other couples you’d like me to invite, then?” Lush asked, backtracking fast.
“Think of some decent people. Respectable. Bring a daughter or two with them, not just old bores. And get one of your damn musicians over here. None of the clowns who play comic songs, though.”
“I was thinking Klesmer might agree to come down once he leaves Quetcham,” Lush said, sounding casual, but his eyes never left Grandcourt’s face, watching for a reaction. “Miss Arrowpoint will only tolerate top-tier music, after all.”
Grandcourt turned his head to look at his companion for the first time all morning, slow, deliberate, taking two long, luxurious puffs of his cigar before he spoke, voice lower than ever, edged with clear contempt. “What in God’s name do I have to do with Miss Arrowpoint and her music?”
“Plenty,” Lush laughed, playful, but not joking. “You won’t have to put in much work, probably. But you do have to go through a few formalities if you want to marry a million pounds.”
“Likely enough. But I’m not marrying a million.”
“That’s a damn shame. You’d throw that perfect opportunity away, knock down all the plans we’ve been working on for years?”
*Your* plans, you mean,” Grandcourt corrected him, dry.
“You’ve got stacks of outstanding debts, you know that,” Lush pressed on. “Things could get very awkward for you very fast. The family heirship isn’t 100% guaranteed, no matter what everyone says.”
Grandcourt didn’t answer, so Lush kept going. “It’s such a perfect chance. The Arrowpoints would bend over backwards to welcome you, I saw that the second we met them. Their daughter isn’t a classic beauty, but she’s elegant, sharp, could carry any title you gave her perfectly, no one would ever whisper about her. And there’s no chance she’d turn down a match like yours.”
“Probably not,” Grandcourt said, noncommittal.
“Her father and mother would let you do pretty much anything you wanted once you’re married to her.”
“I don’t want to do anything with them at all,” Grandcourt said, flat.
Lush paused for a beat, then spoke, deep and serious, remonstrating. “Good God, Grandcourt! After everything you’ve done, after all this time you’re going to let some silly little whim ruin the comfortable, settled life you could have?”
“Spare me the speech. I know exactly what I’m doing.”
“What?” Lush set his cigar down, shoved both hands in his trouser pockets, like he was bracing for something that would try his patience, even if he wasn’t going to lose his temper.
“I’m going to marry the other girl.”
Lush’s voice dripped with scorn. “You’ve fallen in love, have you?”
“I’m going to marry her,” Grandcourt repeated, no elaboration.
“Did you already propose to her, then?”
“No.”
“That girl’s got a will of her own, if I’m not mistaken. Gwendolen Harleth? She’s the sort who’ll make a huge fuss if she doesn’t get what she wants. She knows her own mind.”
“She doesn’t like you,” Grandcourt said, the faintest flicker of a smile tugging at the corner of his mouth.
“True enough,” Lush said, sneer still thick in his tone. “But if the two of you are besotted with each other, that’ll be all that matters, I suppose.”
Grandcourt didn’t bother replying. He sipped his black coffee, pushed his chair back, and strolled out onto the lawn, all the remaining dogs trailing after him, bells tinkling and paws pattering on the grass.
Lush watched him go for a moment, then picked his cigar back up and relit it, smoking slow, running his fingers over his neatly trimmed beard, thinking hard, until he finally stroked it in a little smirk of conclusion, muttering soft under his breath. “Check, old boy.”
Lush had known Grandcourt for fifteen years, long enough to learn what sorts of tactics never worked on his stubborn, unreadable patron — though the right tactics to steer him where he needed to go were often very hard to pin down. Early in his career, he’d held a college fellowship, and had nearly taken holy orders to secure a quiet living, but the prospect bored him, so he’d taken a post as traveling companion to a marquess, then later to young Grandcourt, who’d lost his father very young. Grandcourt found Lush so convenient, he’d let him slowly take over as his right-hand man, handling all his messy personal affairs, for fifteen years running.
The habit of all that time had made Grandcourt more and more dependent on Lush’s willingness to fix every mess, and Lush more and more dependent on the lazy, luxury life he’d built for himself by Grandcourt’s side — no work worth mentioning, all the fine things he could ever want. Grandcourt had never respected Lush, not even on day one, and fifteen years hadn’t changed that, but it had cemented his certainty that he could kick Lush out any time he pleased — though of course he’d never stoop to kicking anyone himself. A gentleman got his dogs to do the messy work for him. He only said cruel, dismissive things that would have earned him a black eye if his “confidant” had been a man with any independent pride.
But what son of a poor vicar, who starved his own wife and daughters of new cotton dresses just to scrape together the money to send his son to Oxford, could keep his independent spirit intact once he’d tasted the luxury of fine dining, good thoroughbred horses, and the softest life imaginable, all without ever working a day? Lush had once been considered a brilliant scholar, and still remembered bits of his old learning when he bothered to dig it up, but all those little bachelor arts that soften a man’s manners were perfect preparation for a life of sinecure. His current comfortable situation was the best sinecure imaginable, all it required was a faint whiff of the old scholarship he’d left behind years ago.
He was perfectly aware Grandcourt looked at him as someone he could kick out at any minute, but he wrote that off as just one of his patron’s odd, unaccountable quirks — one whim from Grandcourt was as good as the next, when it came down to it. In his own mind, he’d never done a bad turn in his life, so why would he ever start, just to keep his easy life intact? Lush loved his soft comfort more than anything else, and this morning, even after the surprise of Grandcourt’s reckless new plan, he wandered off to his private sitting room later and played his violoncello for a full happy hour.
