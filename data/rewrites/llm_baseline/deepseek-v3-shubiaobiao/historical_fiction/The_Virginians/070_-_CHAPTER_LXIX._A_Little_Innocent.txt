George Warrington had noted in his recent correspondence that despite Lord Castlewood’s past dealings with Harry—transactions of a dubious nature—the ties of kinship remained unbroken between his lordship and George himself. Castlewood was ever the obliging relation, offering franks for George’s letters, securing introductions at court, or even procuring him a seat in the House of Lords should he wish to observe a debate. The viscount was a man of wit and charm, always ready to advance his cousin’s interests—so long as his own were not compromised.

Now, he went so far as to promise his influence in securing a position for George, who found himself increasingly reluctant to return to Virginia. The prospect of resuming life under his mother’s stern dominion held little appeal. London’s society and pursuits delighted him far more than the idle existence of a planter, which might have suited him had it come with independence. In Virginia, he was but the first—and, as he believed, the most ill-treated—of his mother’s subjects. The thought of subjecting his young bride to the tedium, the quarrels, and the stifling hierarchy of home filled him with dread. Better freedom and modest means in England, with its promise of intellectual companionship and future distinction, than the wearisome submission awaiting them across the Atlantic.

Thus, George eagerly embraced Castlewood’s offer of patronage, though the man’s own fortunes were far from secure, and his reputation even less so. But hope clings to what it desires, and George chose to believe in his kinsman’s goodwill.

When George’s engagement to Miss Theo Lambert was announced, the Warringtons might have sniffed at the match, but Lord Castlewood played the gracious relation to perfection. He paid his respects to the Lamberts, extolled George’s virtues to the parents, and Miss Theo’s charms to George, all with such polished ease that the good-natured family forgot his notorious past. In their company, he was the picture of amiability—witty, engaging, even virtuous. It was as though he shed his worldly vices with his cloak at the door, reclaiming them only when he stepped back into his chair.

“I do believe he is improved among us,” Mrs. Lambert murmured, her charitable heart softening further when Castlewood openly admired Theo—without, of course, any serious intent.

“It does not trouble my sleep,” he remarked with a smile, “nor shall I break my heart that Miss Lambert prefers another. Though I confess, had I met such innocence in my youth, it might have spared me much folly.”

“It is not too late for redemption, my lord,” Mrs. Lambert ventured gently.

He startled, misreading her meaning before laughing at his own assumption. “Too late for courtship, certainly—but repentance? Ah, madam, if only the heart could be remade as easily as one learns a language in the nursery. Some natures are cut for virtue, others—like mine—are shaped otherwise.”

She persisted, as women do, in her hopeful prayers for his reform, though he shook his head with a melancholy grace. “You do not know the whole of it,” he said. “Our lives were molded early—selfishness, then necessity, then habit. Fate drives the chariot, and we cannot leap from its path. Yet it grants us glimpses of better souls, like yours.”

Whether the lady’s faith or the gentleman’s cynicism proved truer remained to be seen. But while the debate lingered, an unexpected friendship blossomed between the Lamberts and Castlewood’s household. His ladies paid polite calls; Mrs. Lambert attended their card assemblies, where play was universal—even among bishops and peers—though none were compelled to join. The world, it seemed, was not so black as rumor painted it.

Among the visitors to the Lambert household was young Lydia Van den Bosch, the American granddaughter of George’s mother’s old neighbor. Madam Esmond’s letter had made her ambitions clear—hopes of a match between George and the girl—but with George pledged to Theo and Harry abroad, Mrs. Lambert’s thoughts turned to her own son, Jack.

“Why should he not have her?” she mused to her husband. “She is handsome, rich—”

“And ten times as clever as our Theo, no doubt,” George teased, earning a mock scowl from his betrothed.

“She may be all that,” Theo replied serenely, “but I am not afraid.”

Her steady gaze needed no further words.

Lydia, fresh from her Kensington schooling, returned to her grandfather’s care, and under his indulgent eye, her world expanded. From humble lodgings in the city, they moved to Bloomsbury, where society’s doors creaked open a little wider. The merchant’s granddaughter, once confined to tradesmen’s company, now caught the eye of loftier circles—particularly that of George Warrington, who resided nearby.

Old Mr. Van den Bosch was refreshingly candid about his past—his rise from Albany obscurity, his estranged son, his fortune built on land and trade. “They say I was hard on my boy,” he admitted, ruffling Lydia’s hair. “So now I spoil this one to make amends.”

Lydia, with her lively wit and fearless charm, needed no spoiling to shine. She met George’s gaze with laughing defiance when her grandfather jested that the young man had refused her.

“Nay, sir,” George protested, amused.

“Oh, he is right to deny it!” Lydia retorted, her eyes dancing.

The old man chuckled. “She’ll have more than a trifle when I’m gone—but she’d rather keep her Gappy awhile yet, eh?”

Lydia’s answering embrace was answer enough.

And George, watching, wondered if the girl’s artless affection was as guileless as it seemed—or if those bright eyes hid calculations as shrewd as her grandfather’s.
