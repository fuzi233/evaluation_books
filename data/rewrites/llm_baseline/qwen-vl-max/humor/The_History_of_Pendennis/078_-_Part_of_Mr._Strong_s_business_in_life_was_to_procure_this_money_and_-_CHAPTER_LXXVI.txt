A full month has passed since our last chapter, and the world has turned just enough to bring all our characters back together in the cozy little country town where we first met them—Clavering, to be precise. Time moves on, and so do people, especially when they’re involved in matters of love, money, and minor scandals.

Frederic Lightfoot, once maitre d’hôtel under Sir Francis Clavering at Clavering Park, has now taken over the Clavering Arms Hotel with all the dignity of a man who’s finally made it. His announcement reads like a royal decree:

> “The Clavering Arms, ancient and well-established, has been repaired and redecorated in the highest style of comfort. Gentlemen hunting with the Dumplingbeare hounds will find excellent stabling and loose-boxes for their horses. A commodious billiard-room has been added. The cellars are stocked with the finest wines and spirits, selected without regard to expense by C.L. Commercial gentlemen will find this hotel most agreeable. Charges have been regulated with due regard to economy.”

And indeed, the place is buzzing. The sign above the gate gleams with fresh paint. The coffee-room windows sparkle with Christmas holly. The magistrates have held court in the old Assembly card-room. The farmers’ ordinary continues as usual—only now with more customers than ever, drawn by Mrs. Lightfoot’s culinary genius. Her Indian curries and Mulligatawny soup have become local legends. Major Stokes, Captain Glanders (H.P.), and other pillars of society have publicly declared their approval, sampling her wares both privately and at the grand dinner celebrating the incorporation of the Clavering Institute’s reading room.

Sir Francis Clavering, Bart., presided over the festivities, supported by the esteemed Dr. Portman, while Barker, Esq.—the dynamic head of the ribbon factory and chief director of the Clavering and Chatteris Branch of the Great Western Railway—filled the vice-chair with great aplomb. The railway, though still under construction, is already causing excitement among engineers, laborers, and those who believe progress is inevitable—even if it means digging up half the county.

Meanwhile, the *Chatteris Champion, Clavering Agriculturist, and Baymouth Fisherman*—that noble journal of unshakable principles and unmatched loyalty to the British oak—reports that Arthur Pendennis, Esq., has abandoned his plans to run for Parliament. Rumor has it that should Sir Francis Clavering step down due to failing health, he’ll hand over his seat to a young gentleman of "colossal fortune" connected to the highest echelons of aristocracy—this same gentleman being about to marry an accomplished and lovely lady closely tied to the Clavering family.

Lady Clavering and Miss Amory have arrived at Clavering Park for the holidays. The gentry are expected. Festivities loom. And thus, the stage is set.

Now, dear reader, you may deduce what has transpired during our brief narrative pause. Though Lady Rockminster muttered slightly about Laura choosing Pendennis over Bluebeard, those who know Bluebeard’s secret understand why no other choice was possible. The kind old lady, guardian to Miss Bell, was not displeased. Marriage, after all, is the ultimate purpose of every young woman—and she saw it fulfilled. That very night, she told her maid: “Mr. Pendennis earns so much; the railroad will add to it. Miss Bell has her own fortune—perhaps more one day. For their station in life, they’ll manage handsomely.” Then, with a maternal flourish: “I shall speak to my nephew Pynsent—though I suspect he once had designs on her. But that’s long past. Not that you know anything about it, Beck. Of course not! But I’ll speak to George Pynsent, now chief secretary of the Tape and Sealing Wax Office, and see if Mr. Pendennis can’t get something decent. And you, Beck, carry my compliments to Major Pendennis—tell him I’ll call at one o’clock.”

“Of course,” muttered the old lady, “the Major must be reconciled. And he must leave his fortune to Laura’s children.”

At precisely one o’clock, Dowager Lady Rockminster arrived at Major Pendennis’s residence. The Major, though prepared for news of Pen’s broken engagement to Miss Amory, was still startled by her presence. Earlier, Pen had asked his new servant, Herr Frosch, after the Major’s health—then retreated to the coffee-room to write a note.

> “Dear Uncle,
> If there was any question between us, it’s over now. I went to Tunbridge Wells yesterday and found someone else had claimed the prize we were debating. Miss A., without a second thought for me, has given herself to Harry Foker, with his fifteen thousand a year. I walked in on their affair—found him in possession.
> And you’ll be glad to hear Tatham writes that three of my fields at Fairoaks have sold to the Railroad Company for a handsome sum. I’ll tell you more when we meet. Always your affectionate—A. P.”

When Lady Rockminster arrived, the Major greeted her with a bow fit for royalty. “I think I know what you’re about to say,” he said, smiling. “It was very kind of you to bring me the news. How well you look! How generous!”

“For the sake of his uncle,” replied the lady, with perfect politeness.

“Yes,” continued the Major, “Pen wrote me a nice note—yes, a very nice note. And I’ve learned he’s richer now. All things considered, I don’t regret the end of that business with Miss Amory. In fact, I’m rather glad.”

“We must console him,” said Lady Rockminster. “We must find him a wife.”

And then it dawned on the Major: *This* was why she’d come.

Needless to say, the conversation that followed was not recorded in full. But it ended with the marriage being approved—no objections from the Major, no resistance from Lady Rockminster, and only a few whispered intimations that carried immense weight. The Major, overwhelmed by her grace and kindness, eventually gave in. Especially when she declared, in a moment of rare passion, that Laura was far too good for Pendennis—“a paragon,” she said, “fit to be queen.”

And so, when Herr Frosch was sent upstairs to inform Miss Bell and Mr. Pendennis that the Major would receive them, Laura appeared, blushing and radiant, clinging to Pen’s arm. The Major shook their hands with genuine emotion, then bowed again to Laura—making her blush even deeper.

Happy blushes! Bright eyes shining with love! The storyteller pauses here, turns to his audience, and hopes one day they’ll all shine so.

With Pen safely out of the picture and Blanche happily betrothed to the wealthy and eager Harry Foker, joy burst forth in Lady Clavering’s household. The good Begum hadn’t felt this happy in years. She and Blanche became fast friends—almost sisters. Foker, meanwhile, was determined to shorten the mourning period that had kept him from claiming his bride. He pressed forward with wedding preparations, and Blanche, sweet and compliant, was only too willing to oblige.

Lady Clavering returned from Tunbridge. Milliners and jewellers were hired. Hymen’s chariot was being readied. Even Sir Francis benefited from the mood—so much so that reconciliation occurred between husband and wife. Sir Francis returned to London, sat at the head of his own table again, and resumed his former habits: gambling, drinking, and appearing flush with cash.

One evening, Major Pendennis and Arthur dined at Grosvenor Place. There, they encountered an old acquaintance—Mr. James Morgan—now serving as major-domo. The man, dressed in black and speaking with solemn dignity, offered them champagne with the precision of a diplomat. The Chevalier Strong was also present, in high spirits, regaling the company with tales of his adventures abroad.

“It was my Lady who invited me,” Strong whispered to Arthur. “Morgan looked like thunder when I walked in. He’s up to no good. I’ll wait for you at Hyde Park Gate.”

As Major Pendennis left, Morgan helped him with his coat and muttered something about accepting a temporary position with the Clavering family.

“I have a paper of yours, Mr. Morgan,” the Major said.

“You may show it to Sir Francis, sir, if you wish. I’m deeply grateful,” Morgan replied, eyes downcast. “If I can repay you for your kindness, I will.”

Arthur heard this—and saw the hatred in Morgan’s glance. He suddenly cried, “I forgot my handkerchief!” and dashed upstairs.

Foker was still lingering in the drawing-room. Pen gave him a meaningful look. When he returned, Blanche called out cheerfully, “Oh, Arthur—Mr. Pendennis! I want you to tell dear Laura something!”

She stepped to the door.

“What is it?” he asked, closing it.

“Have you told Harry? Do you know that villain Morgan knows everything?”

“I know,” she said.

“Have you told Harry?”

“No, no,” she said. “You won’t betray me?”

“Morgan will,” said Pen.

“No, he won’t,” said Blanche. “I promised him—n’importe. Wait until after our marriage—oh, until after our marriage—oh, how wretched I am.” The girl, who had been all smiles earlier, now seemed undone.

“Please, Blanche,” Arthur begged. “Tell him now. It’s not your fault. He’ll forgive you. Tell him tonight.”

“And give her this—Il est la—with my love, please. And if she can be at Madame Crinoline’s at half-past three, and if Lady Rockminster can spare her, I’d love to drive with her in the park.” With that, she kissed her fingers and sang back into the room, just as Morgan descended the stairs.

Pen heard Blanche’s piano play a bright tune as he rejoined his uncle.

“I told him what happened,” Arthur said.

“What was to be done?” asked the Major.

“What is to be done, begad?” the old man replied. “Leave it alone. Thank God we’re out of it. Don’t meddle, Arthur. Let them handle it.”

“I hope she tells him,” said Pen.

“She’ll take her own course,” said the Major. “Miss Amory’s a sharp one. And I’m doosid glad you’re out of it—doosid glad.”

Strong joined them later, puffing on a cigar. “I wanted to talk to you, Pendennis,” he said. “I’ve heard all the changes. Congratulations on your marriage—and on your escape. You acted like a man. You’re well out of it.”

“I went back to ask Blanche to tell Foker,” said Pen. “But I fear she won’t.”

“There’s only one policy,” said Strong.

“And lucky he who sticks to it,” said the Chevalier. “That rascal Morgan means mischief. He’s been snooping around our rooms for weeks. He found out Amory’s secret. He’s been asking questions—pumping Bolton, getting Costigan drunk. He bribed the inn porter. He got into Clavering’s service because of it. He’ll get paid handsomely—mark my words.”

“Where is Amory?” asked Pen.

“At Boulogne. I left him there. We had a terrible fight. I broke with him. He’s mad, but he’s got pluck. I had to thrash him within an inch of his life.”

“And did he challenge you?”

“Would I have shot him? No. He came to me later and said, ‘Strong, I beg your pardon. You whopped me—and you served me right.’ I shook hands. But I couldn’t live with him after that. I paid him what I owed—pawned everything. Then I played roulette with my last ten florins. If I’d lost, I’d have let him shoot me. I was tired of life. But I won. And now—I’m going into the wine business. My wife’s relatives are in Cadiz. I’ll import Spanish wine and hams. There’s a fortune in it—here’s my card.”

He handed Pen a fine card:

> **Strong & Co., Shepherd’s Inn**
> Sole agents for the celebrated Diamond Manzanilla of the Duke of Garbanzos, Grandee of Spain of the First Class
> And the famous Toboso hams, fed on acorns only in the land of Don Quixote
> Come and taste them at my chambers!

Pen laughed. “I don’t know if I’ll be allowed to go to bachelors’ parties. You know I’m going to——”

“But you must have sherry,” said Strong. “You must have sherry.”

“I’ll get it from you,” said Pen. “And I think you’re very well out of your other partnership. I hear Altamont and his daughter are corresponding.”

“Oh yes,” said Strong. “She wrote him long letters—sly little devil. He answered under cover to Mrs. Bonner. He wanted to kidnap her at first. Nothing would satisfy him but having his child back. But she didn’t want to come. He wasn’t keen either.”

The Chevalier chuckled. “Do you know why we fought? There was a widow at Baden—Madame la Baronne de la Cruche-cassee—not much better than he was. He wanted to marry her. I told her he was married. I don’t think she was much better. I saw her on the pier at Boulogne the day I returned.”

And so, we’ve reached the point the *Chatteris Champion* had already foretold.

Only a few days remained before the blissful day when Foker would claim Blanche as his own. The Clavering family had gathered to admire the most magnificent carriage in the world, standing proudly in the coach-house at the Clavering Arms. Mr. Foker’s head-coachman, ever ready to impress, showed it off in exchange for drink.

Madame Fribsby was busy making beautiful dresses for the tenants’ daughters, who would serve as bridesmaids’ chorus at the breakfast and ceremony. Festivities were planned at the Park—oxen would be slain, cups drained, bells pealed, and Sir Francis, with tears of sensibility in his eye, would bless the couple at the baronial porch.

“Ah, Mr. Huxter,” said Foker in the bar of the Clavering Arms, “a happy tenantry, the pride of the country, will gather in the hall. The ox shall be slain, the cup drained, the bells peal genteelly, and my father-in-law, with tears of emotion, shall bless us at the porch. That’s the plan, Mr. Huxter. And I hope to see you and your lovely bride beside me. What will you drink? Mrs. Lightfoot, madam, offer every refreshment to my excellent friend and body-surgeon, Mr. Samuel Huxter, M.R.C.S., and charge it to my account. And Mr. Lightfoot, sir, what will you take? Though you’ve had enough already, I think—yes, ha!”

Thus spoke Harry Foker, host and hero of the hour. He had rooms at the hotel and surrounded himself with friends. He bought drinks for everyone. He was hail-fellow-well-met. He danced around Madame Fribsby, Mrs. Lightfoot’s ally, as she sat pensive in the bar.

He consoled Mrs. Lightfoot, whose heart was heavy. For poor young Lightfoot, now in full command of the cellar, had lost control of his own desires. He drank from morning till night—tipsy, reeling through the yard and coffee-room, sharing his father’s best wines with farmers and tradesmen.

Mr. Morgan, the butler, often dropped by for a glass. He watched Lightfoot’s antics with cold disdain. Mrs. Lightfoot grew uneasy whenever Morgan was near. Just a few months married, and look at him now! Madame Fribsby sympathized. She’d known men worse. She’d suffered too. And so, perhaps, no one is truly happy. As Foker remarked, there’s bitterness in every man’s cup.

Yet none seemed to be in his. The honest young fellow was brimming with joy.

Morgan was always attentive to Foker. “Still,” said Foker candidly to Mrs. Lightfoot, “I don’t like him somehow. He looks at me like he’s measuring me for a coffin. Pa-in-law fears him. Ma-in-law’s a trump, Mrs. Lightfoot.”

“Indeed, my Lady was,” sighed Mrs. Lightfoot. “Perhaps it would have been better had I never left her.”

“No,” said Foker, “I do not like thee, Dr. Fell; the reason why I cannot tell. And he wants to be my head man. Blanche wants me to take him. Why does Miss Amory like him so?”

And so, our story ends—just before the final curtain falls.
