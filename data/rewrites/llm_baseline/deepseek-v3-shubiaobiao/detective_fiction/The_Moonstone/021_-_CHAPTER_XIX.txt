The word of Rosanna’s disappearance had spread like wildfire among the outdoor staff. They, too, had been searching, and soon enough, they cornered a wiry little garden hand known as Duffy—a boy who’d seen Rosanna not half an hour before, sprinting through the fir plantation toward the shore.

Sergeant Cuff’s eyes sharpened. “Does this boy know the coast?”

“Born and raised on it,” I answered.

“Duffy,” said the Sergeant, “fancy earning a shilling? Then come with me. Mr. Betteredge, keep the pony-chaise ready.”

And with that, he was off toward the Shivering Sand at a pace my old legs couldn’t hope to match. Duffy, wild with excitement, let out a whoop and dashed after him.

What followed was a blur. A restless, gnawing dread took hold of me. I moved about the house like a man half-asleep, doing things I scarcely remember now. It might have been minutes or hours before Duffy came tearing back, clutching a scrap of paper torn from the Sergeant’s pocket-book.

I sent the nearest maid to fetch it, then hurried after the boy myself—determined to see this mystery through before surrendering the boot. Some stubborn instinct to shield the girl, even now, flared up in me.

The storm rolled in as I neared the shore, rain slashing sideways, the sea roaring against the sandbank. I spotted Duffy crouched under the dunes, then, further on, the lone figure of Sergeant Cuff standing like a black sentinel against the yellow waste of the beach.

He waved me toward him. “This way! Down here!”

By the time I reached him, I was gasping, my heart hammering as if it meant to break free. His face stopped me cold—there was something in his eyes, something hollow and horrified. Without a word, he snatched the boot from me and pressed it into a fresh footprint in the sand.

A perfect fit.

The print led straight toward the jagged rocks of the South Spit, not yet washed away by the rain. Cuff followed the trail, testing each step with that dreadful patience of his, but no matter where he looked, the marks only went *toward* the sea. Never back.

At last, he straightened. His gaze locked onto mine, then drifted to the churning water, the tide swallowing the quicksand inch by inch. I didn’t need words to understand. A numb terror seized me, and my knees gave way.

“She returned to the hiding place,” Cuff muttered, more to himself than to me. “Something happened on those rocks.”

But I knew better. The way she’d looked at me earlier—empty, resigned—rose like a specter in my mind. This was no accident.

I tried to speak, to force the words out: *She went there to die.* But my throat closed around them. All I could see was Rosanna as she’d been that first morning, whispering how the Shivering Sand called to her, how she wondered if her grave waited there. The horror of it struck deep, twisting like a knife—my own daughter was her age. The thought was unbearable.

Cuff pulled me to my feet and turned me away from the water just as the others came running—the outdoor men, the fisherman Yolland—all shouting, asking if she’d been found.

The Sergeant showed them the footprints, his voice grim. “An accident. A fatal one.”

Then he turned to Yolland. “Could a boat have taken her from those rocks in this weather?”

Yolland shook his head, eyeing the monstrous waves. “No boat ever made could reach her there.”

Cuff studied the vanishing prints. “Then she didn’t leave by land.” He glanced at Yolland again. “Nor by sea.”

A pause. Then, calculating: “She was seen running here half an hour before I arrived. Call it an hour ago. How high was the tide then, on the south side?”

“Not enough to drown a kitten,” Yolland said.

Cuff’s gaze shifted north, toward the quicksand. “And here?”

“Less. Just awash.”

The Sergeant turned to me, but I found my voice before he could speak.

“No accident,” I said. “She came here to end her life.”

A ripple went through the men. Cuff stiffened. “How do you know?”

But before I could answer, Yolland stepped forward, his voice low. “There’s a ledge of rock four feet out from the Spit—waist-deep at most. If she’d slipped, she’d have struck it. She’d have been found.” He met my eyes. “She waded into the Deeps. On purpose.”

The Sergeant fell silent. So did we all.

As we trudged back toward the house, the under-groom came running, his face drawn. He pressed a note into my hand. “From Penelope, sir. Found in Rosanna’s room.”

Her last words.

*You’ve forgiven me often, Mr. Betteredge. When you see the Shivering Sand again, forgive me once more. I’ve found my grave where it waited for me. I lived and died grateful for your kindness.*

That was all. But it was enough.

The tears came then—great, heaving sobs I couldn’t control. Cuff moved toward me, perhaps to offer comfort, but I recoiled.

“It was the dread of you that drove her to it,” I choked out.

He shook his head. “You’re wrong. But we’ll speak of it later.”

With the groom’s help, I followed the others back through the lashing rain, toward whatever fresh nightmare awaited us at the house.
