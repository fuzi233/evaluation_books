I could not bring myself to distress Lady Verinder by revealing that I had already guessed the painful truth before she spoke. I waited in respectful silence, resolved to offer comfort when the moment permitted. Whatever duty lay before me—no matter how difficult—I was prepared to fulfill it.

“I have been gravely ill, Drusilla,” my aunt began, “and, strange to say, I did not realize it myself.”

My thoughts flew at once to the multitudes of souls who wandered lost in spiritual sickness, unaware of their peril. I feared my dear aunt might be among them.

“Yes, dear,” I murmured sorrowfully. “Yes.”

“I brought Rachel to London, as you know, for medical consultation,” she continued. “I thought it prudent to seek the opinion of two physicians.”

Two physicians! And yet (with Rachel’s soul in such peril!) not a single man of God among them!

“Yes, dear?” I pressed gently.

“One of the doctors was unknown to me,” she explained. “The other had been a dear friend of my late husband’s and had always shown me kindness for his sake. After examining Rachel, he asked to speak with me privately. I expected instructions for her care—but instead, he took my hand gravely and said, ‘Lady Verinder, I have observed you with both professional and personal concern. I fear you are in far greater need of medical attention than your daughter.’”

He questioned her closely, and though she had at first dismissed his concerns, she soon saw his distress deepen. The next day, accompanied by another physician, he returned when Rachel was absent. Their verdict, delivered with the utmost gentleness, was irrevocable: precious time had been lost. An insidious heart disease had weakened her over years without warning, and now her condition was beyond remedy. She might live months—or hours.

“Of course, my dear,” she confessed, “I have known despair since hearing this. But I am resigned now, and I labor to set my affairs in order. My greatest fear is that Rachel should learn the truth. If she did, she would blame herself for my decline—believing it caused by the Diamond’s loss—when the physicians agree my illness began long before. You will keep my secret, Drusilla? I see true sorrow for me in your face.”

Sorrow? Sympathy? Oh, how little she understood the divine fire that burned within me! As she spoke, my heart swelled with sacred gratitude. Here was a soul teetering on eternity’s brink, utterly unprepared—and Providence had guided her to *me*! The harvest was ripe, and laborers were plentiful. With holy fervor, I embraced her.

“Oh, the boundless concern I feel for you!” I cried. “Oh, the good I shall do you before we part!”

I offered her the choice of three devoted clergymen, each a beacon of mercy in her neighborhood, each eager to minister at my word. Alas, she recoiled, pleading weakness. I yielded—temporarily. Experience had taught me that books must prepare the soil. My library was well stocked for such emergencies.

“You will read, dear, won’t you?” I coaxed. “My own precious books, marked where you must pause and ask, ‘Does this apply to me?’”

Even this gentle plea seemed to startle her. “I will try, Drusilla,” she said faintly.

Time was short. The clock warned me to hasten home, select a dozen volumes, and return before the lawyer arrived for the will’s signing. Normally frugal, I indulged in a cab for my aunt’s sake. The driver, when paid, cursed—so I gifted him a tract. He fled as if I’d brandished a pistol. No matter; a second tract flew through his window as he sped away.

At the house, the footman informed me the doctor had just left, and Mr. Bruff waited in the library. The solicitor arched a brow at my carpet-bag.

“Here to stay, Miss Clack?”

I would not dignify his skepticism with the truth. “My aunt has asked me to witness her will.”

“Ah. Well, you’ll do. You’ve no stake in it.”

No stake! What blessed relief. Had she left me even a pittance, my motives might be questioned. Now, none could doubt my pure intent.

Mr. Bruff, restless under my silence, soon broached the scandal surrounding Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

“A vile falsehood!” I declared.

He smirked. “Appearances damn him. He was present when the Diamond vanished—and first to London after.”

I let him prattle on, relishing the moment before striking with Rachel’s testimony of Godfrey’s innocence. The lawyer staggered as if struck.

“If Rachel vouches for him,” he conceded, “I believe it.”

Yet his mind still writhed. “Then who *did* take the Moonstone?” he muttered.

Seizing my chance, I whispered, “Mr. Franklin Blake was also present that night.”

Mr. Bruff’s smile turned icy. “You overreach, Miss Clack. Why would Blake steal it? His debts are stalled, and Rachel would have married him.”

“The human heart is inscrutable,” I murmured.

He scoffed. “Then why did he lead the search? No, the case is a deadlock. Rachel is innocent, Ablewhite is innocent, Blake is innocent—yet the Diamond is in London, in Luker’s hands!”

Before I could mention Sergeant Cuff’s unshaken suspicions, we were summoned to Lady Verinder.

Thus ends my account—a necessary revelation of suspicion’s path, though it costs me the humiliation of admitting my own petty triumph over Mr. Bruff. Yet in that confession, I reclaim the higher ground. The balance is restored. Onward, dear friends.
