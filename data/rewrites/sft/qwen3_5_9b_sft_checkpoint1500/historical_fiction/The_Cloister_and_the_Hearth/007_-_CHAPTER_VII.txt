Upon a morning of unusual brilliance, the streets of Tergou shimmered under a canopy of velvet skies. Horses' hooves clattered rhythmically, and windows and balconies bristled with curious onlookers. The French Ambassador traversed the city en route to hunt in the adjacent woods, accompanied not merely by his own retinue but by several attendants loaned from the Duke of Burgundy to render him honor and service. Leading this procession was the Duke's jester, a figure whose grave, sedate majesty rendered his nobler companions to seem mere frivolities. Yet, whenever the atmosphere grew too thick with reverence, the jester would dismount with a clumsy, ignoble grace, provoking bursts of laughter from even the dignified Ambassador. He would then climb back onto his steed by the tail, playing his role with a provocative, comic flair. Bringing up the rear, however, drew far greater attention: the Duke's leopard.

Mounted upon a Flemish steed of colossal power, a huntsman carried a long box strapped securely to his waist. Upon this box sat a brilliant leopard, crouching low, chained firmly to the rider. The crowd marveled at her glossy coat and distinctive spots, pressing close until a few reached out to touch or pull at her tail. Then the huntsman's voice boomed, terrifying and sharp, "Beware! In Antwerp, a single handful of dust cast upon her caused the Duke to turn to dust!"

"Gramercy!" echoed a nearby spectator.

"I speak truth," the huntsman insisted. "The good Duke imprisoned a mocker in a subterranean cell where the rats stripped him clean of flesh within a single night. Justice served him right for disturbing the beast."

A ripple of fear swept through the Tergovians, shrinking them from even the thought of teasing the sovereign's leopard. Yet, their spirits lifted shortly thereafter with the arrival of the Duke's giant, a Hungarian towering seven feet and four inches. Like other giants of legend, he possessed a treble, flute-like voice lacking in power, yet he was utterly unaware of this flaw, consumed by vanity. As the pageant wound down, the giant spotted Giles perched atop a distant balcony. Stopping abruptly, he addressed the diminutive figure.

"Hallo! Brother!" he squeaked, his voice thin and airy. "I nearly passed without spotting thee."

"You are plain enough to see," Giles bellowed back, his bass tones resonating like a deep drum.

"Come on my shoulder, brother!" squeaked Titan, extending a massive arm as an invitation.

"If I do, I'll cuff your ears," roared the dwarf.

Recognizing the homunculus's irascibility, the giant pounced upon him, encouraged by the surrounding chorus of laughter. However, the spectators laughed not at the giant's wit, but at the absurd juxtaposition of the two voices—the gigantic body emitting a feeble whistle, and the tiny mound belching thunder. The incongruity created a singular duet that ended as uniquely as it began. Giles, losing all patience and self-command, seized the moment. Devoid of fear and driven to boot, he leaped from the balcony, dropping directly onto the giant's neck. With one hand, he gripped the giant's hair; with the other, he rained punches upon his head.

The giant's initial impulse to laugh vanished under the weight and speed of the blows. "He! He! Ah! Ha! Hallo! Oh! Oh! Holy Saints! Here! Help! Or I must throttle the imp! I can't! I'll split your skull against the—" he screamed, making a wild, stumbling run backward toward the balcony. Giles, sensing imminent disaster, grabbed the railing and whirled over it just as the giant's head collided with it with a stunning crack.

The populace roared with laughter and exultation at the little champion's acrobatics. Indignant, the giant seized two laughing bystanders, knocking them together like dumbbells before shaking them flat onto the cobblestones. Catherine shrieked, throwing her apron over Giles, before the enraged giant strode wrathfully away to join his master. Though no one present foresaw the long-term consequences, the immediate effect was agreeable: the Tergovians felt a surge of pride in Giles, listening with newfound affability to his pleas for parchment. Indeed, he conducted a regular trade with his brother Gerard, begging sheets freely while Gerard supplied copper coins.

Later that afternoon, Catherine and her daughter Kate sat chatting of their favorite topic: Gerard, his kindness, his benefice, and the brightened prospects of their entire household. Their fortune had arrived precisely as they wished. Beyond the material advantage of a clerical income—such as Countess Charolois would surely grant—they shared a profound feminine delight in having a holy man within their own circle. "He will marry Cornelis and Sybrandt," Kate declared enthusiastically, noting that the girls were now of age. "Gerard will tend to you and Giles when we depart."

"Yes, Mother," Kate agreed, adding, "And we may confess to him rather than a stranger."

"Aye, girl! And he may administer the sacred oil to your father and me, closing our eyes when our time comes."

"Oh, Mother!" Kate interjected softly, her expression shifting. "Not for many, many years, I pray Heaven. Pray speak not of that; it always saddens me. I hope to go before you, Mother dear. No, let us be gay today. I am free of all pain, Mother, quite out of it all; it seems so strange. I feel so bright and happy that..." She paused, lowering her voice. "Can you keep a secret?"

"Better than anyone, child. Why, you know I can."

"Then I will show you something so beautiful. You have never seen its like, I trow. Only Gerard must never know; surely he intends to surprise us with it. He hides it so carefully, and sometimes carries it away entirely."

Taking her crutches, Kate rose and moved slowly away, leaving her mother in a state of heightened curiosity. Soon she returned, bearing something wrapped in cloth. Unwrapping it, she revealed a lovely image of the Virgin Mary, adorned with all her insignias, her tiara resting above a cascade of magnificent, flowing hair. Catherine was immediately struck with awe.

"It is herself," she cried. "It is the Queen of Heaven. I have never beheld one like her before."

"And her eyes, Mother," Kate observed, pointing upward. "Lifted to the sky, as if they belong there, not to a mortal creature. And her beautiful hair, burning gold."

"To think I possess a son capable of making saints live again upon wood!" Catherine marveled.

"The reason is simple, Mother," Kate whispered reverently. "He is a young saint himself. He is too pure for this world; he is here to portray the blessed, and then he will depart to be with them forever."

Before they could finish their adoration, a strange voice intruded from the door. Driven by instinct, the women hastily concealed the picture. Moments later, Ghysbrecht Van Swieten entered, casting furtive glances around the room—a man who had not set foot in the house for ten years.

Startled, the women stared at him, exchanging a look. "The burgomaster!" they murmured. The tone compelled Ghysbrecht to acknowledge it.

"Yes! I admit the last time I visited was not on a friendly errand. Men love their own interests—Elias's and mine were opposed. Let this visit atone for the last. Today, I come solely for your business, not mine."

Catherine and Kate exchanged a swift glance of contemptuous incredulity. They knew the man far better than he imagined.

"It concerns your son, Gerard," Ghysbrecht stated.

"Ay! Ay! You wish him to labor for the town gratis. He told us."

"I come on no such errand," Ghysbrecht countered, his demeanor shifting. "I am here to warn you: he has fallen into bad hands."

"Now, Heaven and the saints forbid!" Catherine cried, clutching her hands. "Man, torture not a mother! Speak out, quickly! Speak before you have time to coin falsehood; we know you!"

Ghysbrecht turned pale at the affront, spite mingling with his motives. "Thus it stands," he ground out, speaking rapidly. "Your son Gerard is more likely to become a father of a family than a priest. He is constantly with Margaret, Peter Brandt's red-haired girl, and loves her like a cow her calf."

Both mother and daughter burst into laughter. Ghysbrecht stared, bewildered.

"What! Did you know it?"

"Carry this tale to those who do not know my son, Gerard," Kate declared. "Women are naught to him."

"Other women, perhaps. But this one is the apple of his eye, or soon will be, if you do not part them. Come, Dame, do not waste my time and friendly counsel. My servant has witnessed them together dozens of times, gazing into one another's eyes like... you know, Dame—you have been young, too."

"Girl, I am uneasy. Yes, I have been young and know how blind and foolish youth can be. My heart! He has turned me sick in a moment." Turning to Kate, she asked, "If it should be true?"
