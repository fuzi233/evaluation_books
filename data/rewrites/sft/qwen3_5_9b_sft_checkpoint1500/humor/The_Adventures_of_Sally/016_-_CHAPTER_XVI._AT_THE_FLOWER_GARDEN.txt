"And after all I've done for her!" Mr. Reginald Cracknell lamented, his voice trembling with a self-pity that was both melodramatic and deeply exhausting. "After all I've sacrificed, she throws me down!"

Sally offered no response. The orchestra at the Flower Garden played with a ferocious, rhythmic intensity that made vocal competition a foolish endeavor, and she was currently engaged in a desperate physical struggle to adjust her feet to Mr. Cracknell's erratic, jerky dance steps. They shuffled past the table where Miss Mabel Hobson, the club's newest hostess, sat observing the chaos with a distant, regal hauteur. Clad in old gold and black, Miss Hobson looked like a monarch presiding over a riot. As Mr. Cracknell stumbled beneath her gaze, a sorrowful gulp escaped his throat.

"If I told you," he moaned, leaning close to Sally's ear, "what... was that your ankle? Sorry! I have no idea what I'm doing tonight. If I told you the fortune I spent on that woman, you wouldn't believe it. And then she throws me down. Simply because I said I didn't like her hat. She hasn't spoken to me for a week, and she ignores me on the phone. And I was right, too! It was a rotten hat. It didn't suit her a bit. But that," he said morosely, "is a woman all over!"

Sally stifled an exclamation as his wandering foot descended upon hers. Mr. Cracknell, interpreting the noise as a protest against his cynical generalization, gallantly attempted to make amends.

"I don't mean *you* are like that," he assured her. "You're different. I saw that immediately. You have a sympathetic nature. That's why I'm confessing to you. You're sensible and broad-minded; you can understand. I've done everything for that woman. I secured her this job as hostess—do you realize what they pay her? I even starred her in a show once. Did you see those pearls? I bought them. And she won't speak to me. Just because of a hat! I wish you could have seen it. You'd agree with me, surely, because you're sensible and you understand hats. I don't know what to do. I come here every night."

Sally nodded, aware of his routine though this was the first time Lee Schoenstein, the gentlemanly master of ceremonies, had formally presented him to her.

"I come here every night," Mr. Cracknell continued, tears welling in his pale eyes, "and dance past her table, but she won't look at me. What would you do about it?"

"I don't know," Sally admitted frankly.

"Nor do I," Mr. Cracknell sighed. "I thought you wouldn't, because you're sensible... I mean, nor do I. I'm making one last try tonight, if you can keep a secret. You won't tell anyone, will you?" He pleaded urgently. "I know you won't because you're sensible... I'm bringing her a little present tonight. A little gift. That ought to soften her, don't you think?"

"A bigger one would probably do the trick better."

Mr. Cracknell kicked her shin in a dismayed sort of way.

"I never thought of that. Perhaps you're right. But it's too late now. Still, it might work. Or wouldn't it? Which do you think?"

"Yes," Sally said.

"I thought as much," Mr. Cracknell replied.

The orchestra stopped with a thunderous crash, leaving Mr. Cracknell clapping feebly in the center of the floor. Sally slipped back to her table. Her former partner, after casting an uncertain, searching glance around as if he had misplaced a vital limb, zigzagged off in search of his own seat. The roar of conversation, previously drowned by the music, erupted with renewed vigor. The air was hot, thick, and heavy with voices. Pressing her hands over her closed eyes, Sally was reminded once again that she suffered from a pounding headache.

It had been nearly a month since her return to Mr. Abrahams' employment. It had been a dull, leaden existence—a monotonous succession of lifeless days where reality felt like a bad dream. In some strange, nightmare fashion, she seemed severed from her kind. Weeks had passed without seeing a familiar face. Fillmore, perhaps plagued by conscience, had not sought her out, and Ginger was busy charting his destiny on the south shore of Long Island.

Lowering her hands, Sally opened her eyes and surveyed the room. It was packed, as always. The Flower Garden was one of many establishments that had ridden the wave of New York's dancing craze to popularity. Doubtless, because its proprietor claimed it was a "nice place" run "nice," it had survived, unlike many rivals, to enjoy unvarying prosperity. Its advertisements boasted of being a "supper-club for after-theatre dining and dancing," describing it as "sumptuously appointed," "one of the town's wonder-places," featuring an "incomparable dance-floor," "enchanting music," and "service de luxe." From which one could gather, even without Isadore Abrahams' personal endorsements, that he thought highly of the enterprise.

There had been a time when Sally liked it. In her first months of employment, she found it diverting and stimulating. But those days were long gone. Back then, she never had headaches, nor this dreadful, listless depression that weighed her down, turning her nightly labor into a burden.

"Miss Nicholas."

The orchestra, rarely silent for long, started again. Lee Schoenstein, the master of ceremonies, was presenting a new partner. Sally rose mechanically.

"This is the first time I've been in this place," the man said as they bumped over the crowded floor. He was big and clumsy, of course. Tonight, it seemed to Sally, the whole world was big and clumsy. "It's a swell place. I come from up-state. We got nothing like this where I'm from." He cleared a space before him, using Sally as a battering ram, and Sally, despite enjoying her recent excursion with Mr. Cracknell, began to look back at it with wistfulness. This man was undoubtedly the worst dancer in America.

"Give me li'l old New York," the man from up-state declared unpatriotically. "It's good enough for me. I've been to some swell shows since getting to town. Seen this year's 'Follies'?"

"No."

"You go," he urged earnestly. "You go! Take it from me, it's a swell show. Seen 'Myrtle Takes a Turkish Bath'?"

"I don't go to many theatres."

"You go! It's a scream. I've been to a show every night since I got here. Regular every night. Swell shows, all of 'em, except this last one. I certainly picked a lemon tonight, alright. I was taking a chance, y'see, because it was an opening. Thought it would be something to say when I got home that I'd been to a New York opening. Set me back two-seventy-five, including tax, and I wish I'd got it in my kick right now. 'The Wild Rose,' they called it," he said satirically, as if exposing a low subterfuge. "'The Wild Rose!' It sure made me wild, alright. Two dollars seventy-five tossed away, just like that."

Something stirred in Sally's memory. Why did that title feel so familiar? Then, with a shock, she remembered. It was Gerald's new play. For some time after returning to New York, she had been haunted by the fear that stepping out of her apartment might lead to a collision with Gerald. Then she had seen a newspaper paragraph relieving her of that anxiety. Gerald was out on the road with a new play, and "The Wild Rose," she was almost certain, was indeed its name.

"Is that Gerald Foster's play?" she asked quickly.

"I don't know who wrote it," her partner replied, "but let me tell you, he's one lucky guy to get away alive. There's fellows breaking stones on the Ossining Road that's done a lot less to deserve a sentence. Wild Rose! I'll tell the world it made me go good and wild." The man from up-state, an economical soul who despised waste, spread his humorous efforts thinly but effectively. "Why, before the second act was over, the people were beating it for the exits, and if it hadn't been for someone shouting 'Women and children first,' there'd have been a panic."

Sally found herself back at her table without knowing clearly how she had gotten there.

"Miss Nicholas."

She started to rise, and realized suddenly that this was not the voice of duty calling her through the gold teeth of Mr. Schoenstein. The man who had spoken her name had seated himself beside her, talking in precise, clipped accents that were oddly familiar. The mist cleared from her eyes, and she recognized Bruce Carmyle.

"I called at your place," Mr. Carmyle said. "The hall porter told me you were here, so I ventured to follow you. I hope you do not mind? May I smoke?"
