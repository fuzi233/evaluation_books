The second Rosa jolted back to full consciousness, the full weight of Jasper’s threat hung over her like a damp, smothering shroud. It had not left her even in her faint: it had chased her through unconsciousness, sharp and vivid as the sun-dial in the garden where he had laid his ravenous, twisted soul bare for her.

Frozen with fright, she had no clue what to do next—save one unshakable, screaming thought: she had to run from this terrible man.

But where? How? She had never breathed her fear of Jasper to anyone but Helena. If she went straight to the Landless girl now, laid out every vile word he had said, it would trigger the total ruin he swore to bring down on Helena’s brother Neville. The longer she turned Jasper’s snarls over in her mind, the more crippling her fear became: one wrong move, one slip, and his malevolence would destroy the innocent boy she had sworn to Helena she believed was innocent of killing Edwin.

For six months, a half-formed, unspoken suspicion had tossed and turned in Rosa’s mind, rising to the surface then sinking back out of sight, never quite solid enough to name. Jasper’s obsession with his nephew when he was alive, his endless, public crusade to prove Edwin had been murdered—everyone in Cloisterham ate it up. No one dared imagine the grieving, devoted uncle could be the hand that struck the blow. Rosa had asked herself a hundred times if she was just wicked, inventing a monstrosity no one else could see. She tried to logic it away: even if Jasper wanted her for himself, would he parade a six-month-long investigation for “vengeance” right in front of everyone? Would he lean on that sun-dial and threaten to “sweep even Edwin away” from her, if he had already done the deed?

But no matter how many times she picked the thought apart, she arrived at the same conclusion: this man was a monster no normal standard could measure. She had to run.

She could not turn to Helena. She could not risk Neville. The only safe port was her guardian, Mr. Grewgious.

The old, crawling feeling that the high walls of the Nuns’ House could not keep Jasper out, that he could follow her like a ghost, wrap her in a spell, was so strong she could not reason it away. When she glanced at the garden sun-dial through her window as she dressed, she went cold all over, as if he had infused the stone itself with his awful hunger.

She scribbled a hasty note to Miss Twinkleton, saying she had sudden urgent business with her guardian and begged the good woman not to worry, stuffed a handful of useless trinkets into a tiny palm-sized bag, left the note propped on her dressing table, and slipped out the gate. It was the first time she had ever stepped into Cloisterham High Street alone. She hurried straight for the omnibus stop, and the coach was just pulling away when she called out to the driver.

“Stop, please, Joe! I have to go to London right now.”

A minute later she was rumbling toward the railway station. Joe fussed over her, tucking her safely into a first class carriage, passing her tiny bag in after her as if it weighed a hundred pounds and might crush her if she so much as touched it. When he promised to deliver her message of safety to Miss Twinkleton, he added a quiet, unspoken thought to himself: he would not mind getting a bit of that sweetness for himself.

At first, the rush of running made Rosa feel brave. The sharp, indignant thought that Jasper’s declaration of love had soiled her, that only the honest, steady protection of her guardian could wipe that stain away, held her up. But as the evening darkened, and the sprawling, gritty mass of London loomed closer, doubts nipped at her: was this a wild, foolish move? What if Grewgious was not home? What would she do, alone, in that vast, strange city?

The train pulled into the London terminus as the gas lamps flickered on through the hot summer haze. Rosa rattled through the endless, shabby streets in a hired cab, the barrel organs and chapel bells echoing hollowly off brick walls, the shuffling of hundreds of feet on hot stone sounding like no comfort at all, until she pulled up at the locked, heavy gate of Staple Inn. The watchman let her and her tiny bag in, pointed her toward Grewgious’s staircase. At ten o’clock sharp, she tapped on his door.

Grewgious looked up from his seat by the open window, and his face went white. “Good Heaven!” he breathed, for a split second mistaking her for her long-dead mother. Rosa fell onto his neck sobbing.

“My child, my child! What on earth has happened? Who brought you here?”

“No one. I came alone. Poor, poor Eddy. His uncle has made love to me. I cannot bear it. I shudder at the very sight of him, and I have come to you to protect me, and all of us, from him, if you will.”

Grewgious exploded to his feet in a rush of uncharacteristic fire, thundering:
*“Damn him!
Confound his politics!
Frustrate his knavish tricks!
On Thee his hopes to fix?
Damn him again!”*
He paced wildly around the room, red-faced, half mad with loyal rage, before he stopped to mop his brow. “Forgive me, my dear—you’ll be glad to hear I feel much better now. Don’t tell me any more just yet, or I might burst out with worse. First, you need food, and rest.”

He got down on one knee to help her untie her hat, his awkward angular movements soft with true chivalry—the sort no one would ever have expected from the gruff old bachelor lawyer. He teased her gently about the tiny bag, which could barely hold a day’s seed for a canary, fussed that he would get her every meal under the sun, then ran hatless across to Furnival’s Inn to give his orders that the head chambermaid should spare no expense to get her everything she might have forgotten to pack.

Over tea, to calm her frayed nerves, he told her the silly, soft story of his clerk Bazzard: the discontented would-be playwright who had written a grand tragedy called *The Thorn of Anxiety* that no theatre would touch, who resented his master deeply because Grewgious could not write a play even if he were sentenced to hang for it. Rosa laughed, her first real smile in months, as Grewgious rambled about the circle of unpublished tragic poets who dedicated their unproduced work to each other in flowery, panegyric notes, while poor Bazzard’s boss had never once gotten a play dedicated to him.

When Rosa was fully calm, she laid out every last detail of her encounter with Jasper for him, lingering on every line that touched Helena and Neville Landless. Grewgious smoothed his balding head slowly, his face grave as he processed every word. “Well narrated,” he said at last, gesturing to the dark windows across the road, “that’s where the Landless siblings live, over yonder. We’ll sleep on the question of you visiting Helena tomorrow.”

He tucked her hand in his, carried her tiny useless bag on his arm, and walked her across the street to Furnival’s Inn, his stately awkward gait as if he were leading a minuet. He handed her over to the unlimited-expense head chambermaid, promised her he would be across the road at ten the next morning for breakfast, and smiled when Rosa told him she felt completely safe here.

“Safe from him?” he said, patting her hand. “This inn is locked behind iron bars, watched every hour of the night by the gatekeepers, and I live right over the way. If you need anyone to run to me at any hour after dark, tell the porter—he’ll get a whole crown for the trouble.”

Long after Rosa had gone up to her bright, comfortable room, Grewgious paced up and down outside the iron gates of Furnival’s Inn for nearly an hour, peering between the bars every few minutes, as if he had tucked a fragile dove high in its roost, and was standing guard to make sure no hawk slipped through to tear it apart.
