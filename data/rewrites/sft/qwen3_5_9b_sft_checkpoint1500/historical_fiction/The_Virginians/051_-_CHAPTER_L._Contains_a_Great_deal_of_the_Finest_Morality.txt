When we were first introduced to Sir Miles Warrington within the opulent drawing-room of St. James's Palace, I must confess that, regarding his jovial, round visage, his ample waistcoat, and his hearty, rural demeanor, I anticipated finding an ideal companion. I expected to form an intimacy with the quintessential "Good Old Country Gentleman," a figure lauded by song and men alike, positioned nearest the gods in earthly felicity. To be such a gentleman implies possessing a vast, unencumbered estate with rents paid punctually by adoring farmers, save perhaps for one recalcitrant tenant whose stubbornness allows the gentleman to display his sublime charity. It involves hunting thrice weekly, loving sport above all, enjoying robust health and a magnificent appetite, seated in church amidst a chorus of village blessings as the parish's chief benefactor, consciously accepting one's role as a "miserable sinner" solely for form's sake to set an example for others. With a healthy constitution, wealth, respect from the vicar, reverence from tenants, and a family that laughs at stories of grouse shot in the gunroom, this perfect being—despite the label of "sin"—could scarcely be heartily dissatisfied with his spiritual state. Were one a Royal Highness entering church in perfect health, attended by a waiting clergyman, would one truly believe oneself a "miserable sinner"? Only when racked with gout, alone, fearing death, and denied claret by a physician would humility arise. But in sunshine and spirits, the word "miserable" is merely a formality; one cannot genuinely feel pity for oneself. Consider Colin Ploughman, suffering from ague, supporting seven children, owing two pounds annually for his cottage, and surviving on eight shillings a week. By comparison, the misery of a healthy, wealthy, jolly country gentleman is supportable; if he is a sinner, few dare accuse him.

Yet, such a man may grow selfish, content with himself, and unmatched in the surrounding miles. His admirable wife serves him and the parish; his children bow; the vicar reveres him; he commands the sessions; poachers tremble; hats fall before him at market. Around his great coach, laden with spotless daughters and a sublime lady, tradesmen cringe bareheaded, and farmwomen drop countless curtseys. From their cushions, the ladies gaze down benevolently, smiling on the poor, buying yards of ribbon with affability, condescending to purchase salts or flowers, and deigning to bargain for geese. Their procession mimics a royal progress, with a happy populace pressing around to bless them, tradesmen bowing, farmers' wives bobbing, and town boys cheering the red-faced coachman driving the fat bays, shouting, "Sir Miles for ever! Throw us a halfpenny, my lady!"

However, let us entertain a darker hypothesis: what if a market-woman hides her fat goose from Sir Miles's coach, terrified lest his lady insist on a bargain? What if no copper coins ever emerge from the royal coach window? What if Sir Miles regales his tenants with notoriously small beer and his poor with thin broth? Such behaviors, it seems, characterize many "fine old English gentlemen" and ladies. They patronize the poor without relieving them, shout "Amen!" as loudly as the clerk, perform all the forms of piety, discharge every etiquette of gentlemanhood, and treat virtue as a bargain purchase, convinced they honor it. When poor Harry sought aid from his relations, his aunt dispatched a tract and a blessing; his uncle, occupied elsewhere, could not answer. How much of this conduct permeates respectable life? One might imagine Lord and Lady Macbeth concocting a murder with some awkwardness, but consider Lord and Lady Skinflint consulting in bed about refusing their luckless nephew a helping hand, then descending to family prayers to discourse virtuously before their children and domestics, remaining together afterward, nose to nose. What thoughts occupy them? How can they maintain these virtuous airs? How dare they look each other in the eye?

Do they believe they have done wrong? Does Skinflint torture himself with remorse over the distress calling in vain and the hunger sent away empty? Not at all. He is indignant with the Prodigal for being a fool, not ashamed of himself for being a curmudgeon. What? A young man squanders opportunities? Spends a fortune on gamblers and spendthrifts? "Horrible, horrible!" he cries, urging caution by the unfortunate youth's behavior and asserting the consequences of extravagance. According to the great, established Church of the Pharisees, here lies an admirable opportunity for moral discourse and the assertion of virtue. "To think of his deceiving us so!" cries Lady Warrington. "Very sad, very sad, my dear!" responds Sir Miles, wagging his head. "To think of so much extravagance in one so young!" exclaims Lady Warrington, listing cards, bets, tavern feasts, carriages, riding horses, and the company of profligate youths and iniquitous persons. "Hush, my Lady Warrington!" cries her husband, glancing toward the spotless Dora and Flora, who hold down their blushing heads at the mention of the last "naughty persons." "No wonder my poor children hide their faces!" the mother continues. "My dears, I wish even the existence of such creatures could be kept from you!" "They can't go to an opera, or the park, without seeing 'em, to be sure," says Sir Miles. "To think we should have introduced such a young serpent into the bosom of our family! And have left him in the company of that guileless darling!" she points to Master Miles. "Who's a serpent, mamma?" inquires the youth. "First you said cousin Harry was bad: then he was good: now he is bad again. Which is he, Sir Miles?" "He has faults, like all of us, Miley, my dear. Your cousin has been wild, and you must take warning by him." "Was not my elder brother, who died—my naughty brother—not he wild too? He was not kind to me when I was quite a little boy. He never gave me money, nor toys, nor rode with me, nor..." Why do you cry, mamma? Sure I remember how Hugh and you were always fighting—" "Silence, sir!" cry out papa and the girls in a breath. "Don't you know you are never to mention that name?" "I know I love Harry, and I didn't love Hugh," says the sturdy little rebel. "And if cousin Harry is in prison, I'll give him my half-guinea that my godpapa gave me, and anything I have—yes, anything, except... except my little horse—and my silver waistcoat—and—and Snowball and Sweetlips at home—and—and, yes, my custard after dinner." This was in reply to a hint from sister Dora. "But I'd give him some of it," continues Miles, after a pause. "Shut thy mouth with it, child, and then go about thy business," says papa, amused. Sir Miles Warrington possessed a considerable fund of easy humour. "Who would have thought he should ever be so wild?" mamma goes on. "Nay. Youth is the season for wild oats, my dear." "That we should be so misled in him!" sigh the girls. "That he should kiss us both!" cries papa. "Sir Miles Warrington, I have no patience with that sort of vulgarity!" says the majestic matron. "Which of you was the favourite yesterday, girls?" continues the father. "Favourite, indeed! I told him over and over again of my engagement to dear Tom—I did, Dora—why do you sneer, if you please?" says the handsome sister. "Nay, to do her justice, so did Dora too," said papa. "Because Flora seemed to wish to forget her engagement with dear Tom sometimes," remarks the sister. "I never, never, never wished to break with Tom! It's wicked of you to say so, Dora! It is you who were for ever sneering at him: it is you who are always envious because I happen—at least, because gentlemen imagine that I am not ill-looking, and prefer me to some folks, in spite of all their learning and wit!" cries Flora, tossing her head over her shoulder and looking at the glass. "Why are you always looking there, sister?" says the artless Miles junior. "Sure, you must know your face well enough!" "Some people look at it just as often, child, who haven't near such good reason," says papa, gallantly. "If you mean me, Sir Miles, I thank you," cries Dora. "My face is as Heaven made it, and my father and mother gave it me. 'Tis not my fault if I resemble my papa's family. If my head is homely, at least I have got some brains in it. I envy Flora, indeed, because she has found favour in the sight of poor Tom Claypool! I should as soon be proud of captivating a ploughboy!" "Pray, miss, was your Mr. Harry, of Virginia, much wiser than Tom Claypool? You would have had him for the asking!" exclaims Flora. "And so would you, miss, and have dropped Tom Claypool into the sea!" cries Dora. "I wouldn't." "You would."
