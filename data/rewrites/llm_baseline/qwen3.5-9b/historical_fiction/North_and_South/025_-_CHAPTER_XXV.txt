"Revenge may have her own; Roused discipline aloud proclaims their cause, And injured navies urge their broken laws." — Byron.

Margaret began to wonder if all proposals arrived with such startling suddenness, proving equally distressing the moment they occurred as the two she had recently endured. An involuntary comparison between Mr. Lennox and Mr. Thornton arose in her mind. She had regretted that an expression of anything other than friendship had been drawn from Henry Lennox by circumstance. That regret had dominated her feelings during his first approach. Yet, she had not felt so stunned, so utterly overwhelmed as she did now, while the echoes of Mr. Thornton's voice still lingered in the room. With Lennox, it seemed he had momentarily slipped across the boundary from friendship to love, only to retreat from it almost immediately, regretting the step nearly as much as she did, albeit for different reasons. With Mr. Thornton, however, there appeared to be no such transitional phase of friendship. Their interaction had been a continuous series of opposition. Their opinions clashed constantly, and indeed, she had never sensed that he valued her opinions as belonging to her as an individual. Wherever they challenged his rock-like character or his strength of passion, he seemed to cast them aside with contempt, until she grew weary of the futile exertion of making protests. And now, in this strange, wild, passionate manner, he had come to declare his love!

For, although it had initially struck her that his offer was forced from him by sharp compassion for the exposure she had made of herself—a sentiment he, like others, might misinterpret—yet even before he left the room, and certainly within five minutes thereafter, a clear conviction dawned upon her, shining brightly: that he loved her; that he had loved her; and that he would love her. She shrank and shuddered as if under the fascination of some great power repugnant to her entire previous life. She crept away, hiding from his idea. But it was in vain. Parodying a line from Fairfax's *Tasso*: "His strong idea wandered through her thought." She disliked him the more for having mastered her inner will. How dared he claim he would love her still, even if she shook him off with contempt? She wished she had spoken more strongly. Sharp, decisive words crowded into her mind now that it was too late to utter them. The deep impression of the interview was like a horror in a dream that refuses to leave the room once we awaken, rub our eyes, and force a stiff, rigid smile upon our lips. It remains there—cowering and gibbering with fixed, ghastly eyes in some corner of the chamber, waiting to hear if we dare acknowledge its presence to anyone. We dare not; poor cowards that we are! Thus, she shuddered away from the threat of his enduring love. What did he mean? Did she not possess the power to daunt him? She intended to see. It was more daring than became a man to threaten her so. Was it grounded upon the miserable events of yesterday? If need be, she would do the same tomorrow—not by a crippled beggar, willingly and gladly, but by him, just as bravely, in spite of his deductions and the cold slime of woman's impertinence. She acted because it was right, simple, and true to save where she could save; even to try to save. *"Fais ce que dois, advienne que pourra."*

Hitherto, she had not moved from where he had left her; no external circumstance had roused her from the trance of thought into which his final words and the look of his deep, intent, passionate eyes had plunged her, causing her own defenses to fall before his gaze. She went to the window and threw it open to dispel the oppression hanging around her. Then she opened the door with an impetuous wish to shake off the recollection of the past hour amidst the company of others or through active exertion. But the house was profoundly hushed in the noonday stillness of a household where an invalid catches the unrefreshing sleep denied to the night-hours. Margaret refused to be alone. What should she do?

*"Go and see Bessy Higgins, of course,"* she thought, as the memory of the message sent the night before flashed into her mind. Away she went.

When she arrived, she found Bessy lying on the settle, moved close to the fire despite the sultry, oppressive heat. She was laid down quite flat, as if resting languidly after some paroxysm of pain. Margaret felt certain Bessy needed the greater freedom of breathing afforded by a more upright posture. Without a word, she raised her and arranged the pillows so that Bessy was more at ease, though still very languid.

"I thought I should na' ha' seen yo' again," Bessy said finally, looking wistfully into Margaret's face.

"I'm afraid you're much worse. But I could not have come yesterday; my mother was so ill—for many reasons," Margaret replied, coloring slightly.

"You'd m'appen think I went beyond my place in sending Mary for yo'. But the wraglin' and the loud voices had just torn me to pieces. I thought when father left, oh! if I could just hear her voice reading me some words of peace and promise, I could die away into the silence and rest of God, just as a baby is hushed to sleep by its mother's lullaby."

"Shall I read you a chapter now?"

"Ay, do! M'appen I shan't listen to the sense at first; it will seem far away—but when ye come to words I like, to the comforting texts, it'll seem close in my ear, going through me as it were."

Margaret began. Bessy tossed to and fro. If, by an effort, she attended for one moment, it seemed as though she were convulsed into double restlessness the next. Finally, she burst out:

"Don't go on reading. It's no use. I'm blaspheming all the time in my mind, thinking angrily on what canna be helped. Ye'd hear of the riot, m'appen, yesterday at Marlborough Mills? Thornton's factory, ye know."

"Your father was not there, was he?" Margaret asked, coloring deeply.

"Not he. He'd ha' given his right hand if it had never come to pass. It's that that's fretting me. He's fairly knocked down in his mind by it. It's no use telling him; fools will always break out of bounds. Ye never saw a man so downhearted as he is."

"But why?" Margaret asked, confused.

"Why, ye see, he's a committee-man on this special strike. The Union appointed him because, though I say it as shouldn't say it, he's reckoned a deep chap and true to the backbone. He and the other committee-men laid their plans. They were to hold together through thick and thin; whatever the majority thought, the others were to think, whether they would or no. Above all, there was to be no going against the law of the land. Folk would follow them if they saw them striving and starving with dumb patience; but if there was once any noise of fighting and struggling—even with knobsticks—all was up, as they know by the experience of many times before. They would try to get speech of the knobsticks, coax 'em, reason with 'em, and maybe warn 'em off; but whatever happened, the Committee charged all members of the Union to lie down and die, if need be, without striking a blow. Then they reckoned they were sure of carrying the public with them. Besides all that, the Committee knew they were right in their demands and didn't want to have right mixed up with wrong, till folks couldn't separate it, no more than I can separate the physic-powder from the jelly ye gave me to mix. The jelly is much the bigger, but the powder tastes it all through.

"Well, I've told ye at length about this, but I am tired out. Ye just think for yorsel what it must be for father to have all his work undone by such a fool as Boucher, who must needs go right against the orders of the Committee and ruin the strike, just as bad as if he meant to be a Judas. Eh! but father gave it him last night! He went so far as to say he'd go and tell the police where they might find the ringleader of the riot; he'd give him up to the mill-owners to do what they would with him. He'd show the world that the real leaders of the strike were not such as Boucher, but steady, thoughtful men; good hands and good citizens, friendly to law and judgment, who would uphold order; who only wanted their rightful wage and wouldn't work, even though they starved, till they got it; but who would ne'er injure property or life. For," dropping her voice, "they do say that Boucher threw a stone at Thornton's sister that well-nigh killed her."

"That's not true," said Margaret. "It was not Boucher that threw the stone"—she went first red, then white.

"Ye'd be there then, were ye?" Bessie asked languidly, for indeed she had spoken with many pauses, as if speech was unusually difficult.

"Yes. Never mind. Go on. Only it was not Boucher that threw the stone. But what did he answer to your father?"
