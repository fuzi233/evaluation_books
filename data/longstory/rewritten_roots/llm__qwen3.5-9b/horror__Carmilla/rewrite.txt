Tacked to the manuscript that follows is a lengthy note penned by Dr. Hesselius. In it, he refers readers to his own treatise on the peculiar phenomenon illuminated by these pages—a subject he addresses therein with his customary erudition, sharp insight, and striking conciseness. That essay stands alone within the forthcoming collection of this extraordinary scholar's works. Since my purpose here is merely to engage the general public, I shall allow the intelligent woman who recounts this tale to speak without interruption. After much deliberation, I have resolved to omit any summary of the Doctor's complex reasoning or excerpts from his remarks on a matter he deems "to involve, not improbably, some of the most profound mysteries of our dual nature and its intermediate states."

When I first uncovered this document, I felt compelled to revive the correspondence begun decades ago between Dr. Hesselius and his remarkably astute and meticulous informant. Alas, to my deep regret, I discovered that she had passed away during the intervening years. Had she lived, she might have contributed little more than what she has already communicated in the following pages, rendered with such scrupulous attention to detail that, as far as I can judge, leaves nothing to be desired.

In the province of Styria, we dwell within a castle, or *schloss*, a structure that may lack magnificence yet commands a certain dignity. Here, in this remote corner of the world, a modest income stretches far beyond its nominal value; eight or nine hundred pounds annually performs miracles. What would suffice merely for survival among the wealthy classes in England would go entirely unspent here. My father is English, bearing an English name, though I have never set foot upon the soil of his birthplace. Yet, amidst this solitude and primitiveness, where labor and goods are marvelously cheap, I struggle to comprehend how additional wealth could materially enhance our comforts, let alone our luxuries.

My father served in the Austrian army, retiring upon a pension and his inheritance to purchase this feudal residence and its surrounding estate—a transaction secured as a true bargain. The setting is nothing short of picturesque and profoundly solitary. The castle perches upon a slight eminence deep within a vast forest. Before it lies a road, ancient and narrow, passing directly in front of the drawbridge, which has never been raised during my lifetime. Encircling the grounds is a moat, teeming with perch and navigated by numerous swans, their white forms drifting atop fleets of water lilies. Above this aquatic garden rises the *schloss* itself, displaying its many-windowed façade, towering spires, and a Gothic chapel. The forest parts to reveal an irregular, charming glade before the gate, while to the right, a steep Gothic bridge spans a stream that winds through the woods in perpetual deep shadow.

To emphasize the isolation: looking from the hall door toward the road, the forest envelops the castle, extending fifteen miles to the right and twelve to the left. The nearest inhabited village lies approximately seven English miles to the left. To the right, nearly twenty miles distant, stands the historic *schloss* of the late General Spielsdorf. When I speak of the "nearest inhabited village," I refer specifically to the living settlements; only three miles westward, in the direction of General Spielsdorf's domain, exists a ruined hamlet. Its quaint little church remains roofless, the aisle serving as a crypt for the moldering tombs of the proud Karnstein family, now extinct. They once owned the equally desolate chateau situated in the heart of the forest, overlooking the silent ruins of the town below. Legends abound regarding the cause of this desolation—tales I shall recount at another time—but for now, I must describe the tiny circle of souls who constitute the actual inhabitants of our castle. Excluding servants and dependents housed in the annexes, the count is startlingly low.

Listen, and perhaps you too will find yourself wondering. My father, the kindest soul imaginable, though aging; and I, at the time of writing this account, nineteen years of age. Eight years have elapsed since then. We two formed the entirety of the family residing in the *schloss*. My mother, a Styrian lady, passed away in my infancy. I recall having a benevolent governess who accompanied me from a time so early it borders on infancy. I cannot remember a moment when her fat, benignant face was absent from my memories. This was Madame Perrodon, a native of Berne, whose care and kindness partially compensated for the loss of my mother, whom I barely remember due to the premature nature of her death. She completed our trio at the dinner table.

A fourth member was Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, a "finishing governess" in your parlance. She commanded both French and German; Madame Perrodon spoke French and broken English, while my father and I contributed English. Driven partly by a desire to preserve the language lest it die out among us, and partly from patriotic sentiment, we committed ourselves to speaking English daily. The result was a linguistic babel that amused strangers, a cacophony I shall not attempt to replicate in this narrative. Occasionally, two or three young ladies of my own age visited for varying durations, and I sometimes reciprocated these visits. These were our regular social resources, supplemented by chance calls from neighbors residing mere five or six leagues away. Nevertheless, my life remained inherently solitary.

My governesses exercised a degree of control over me commensurate with their wisdom, managing a somewhat spoiled girl whose sole parent permitted her considerable freedom. However, the first incident of my existence to leave a terrifying, indelible mark upon my mind occurred among the very earliest events I can recall. Some might deem it trivial, unworthy of record, yet you shall soon understand why I bring it forth.

The nursery, though exclusively mine, was a spacious chamber located on the upper story of the castle, capped by a steep oak roof. I was no older than six when, one night, I awoke. Glancing around the room from my bed, I found the nursery maid missing. My nurse was nowhere to be seen either, leaving me convinced I was utterly alone. I was not frightened—I was one of those fortunate children shielded from ghost stories, fairy tales, and all lore that causes one to clutch one's head at the sound of a cracking door or the dancing shadow of a bedpost cast by a dying candle. Instead, feeling neglected and insulted, I began to whimper, preparing for a full-blown tantrum.

Then, to my astonishment, I perceived a solemn yet remarkably beautiful face gazing at me from the side of the bed. It belonged to a young lady kneeling there, her hands tucked beneath the coverlet. I regarded her with pleased wonder, ceasing my whimpers instantly. She caressed me gently, lay down beside me on the mattress, and drew me close, smiling. Immediately, I felt delightfully soothed and drifted back into sleep.

I was awakened moments later by a sensation akin to two sharp needles piercing my breast deeply and simultaneously. I screamed loudly. The lady recoiled, her eyes fixed upon me, before slipping down onto the floor and, as I believed, hiding beneath the bed. For the first time, genuine fear seized me, and I yelled with every ounce of strength I possessed.

Nurse, nursery maid, housekeeper—they all rushed in. Hearing my tale, they attempted to soothe me, yet even as children, I sensed an unusual pallor of anxiety upon their faces. I watched them peer under the bed, scour the room, lift the skirts of tables, and pry open cupboards. The housekeeper leaned close to the nurse and whispered, "Place your hand along that hollow in the bed; someone *did* lie there, surely as you did not; the place is still warm."

I recall the nursery maid stroking my hair while the three examined my chest, where I insisted I felt the punctures. They pronounced that no visible signs existed of any such injury. That night, the housekeeper and the two other servants charged with the nursery remained awake; henceforth, a servant was required to sit vigil in the nursery until I reached the age of fourteen.

For a prolonged period afterward, I lived in a state of nervous dread. A physician was summoned—a pallid, elderly man whose long, saturnine face bore the scars of smallpox, topped by a chestnut wig. For weeks, he visited every second day to administer medicine, which I detested.

The morning following the vision, I was consumed by terror, unable to endure being left alone, even in broad daylight. I remember my father ascending to the bedside, standing there cheerfully, questioning the nurse, laughing heartily at one of her replies, patting my shoulder, kissing me, and reassuring me that it was nothing but a dream incapable of harm. Yet, I found no comfort in his words. I knew the visitation of the strange woman was *not* a dream; I was awfully frightened.

I was slightly consoled when the nursery maid assured me that it was indeed she who had come, looked at me, and lain beside me, implying I must have been half-asleep to fail to recognize her face. Though supported by the nurse, this explanation did not fully satisfy me. Later that day, I recalled a venerable old man in a black cassock entering the room with the nurse and housekeeper. He spoke briefly to them and treated me with great kindness. His face was sweet and gentle. He informed us they intended to pray, joining my hands together and bidding me whisper softly while they offered supplication: *"Lord hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus' sake."* I am certain these were the exact words, for I repeated them to myself often, and my nurse made me utter them in prayer for years thereafter.

I retain a vivid memory of that thoughtful, sweet-faced, white-haired elder in his black cassock, standing in that rough, lofty, brown room filled with clumsy furniture dating back three centuries. Scanty light filtered through the small lattices, illuminating the shadowy atmosphere. He knelt, as did the three women with him, and prayed aloud with an earnest, quavering voice for what seemed like an eternity.

I forget all of my life prior to this event, and for some time afterward, my memories remain obscured. Yet, the scenes I have just described stand out with crystalline clarity, isolated pictures within a phantasmagoria surrounded by darkness.

I am about to recount a tale so singular that it demands every ounce of your faith in my honesty to accept it. Yet, it is undeniably true—a truth witnessed by my own eyes.

It was a sweet summer evening. As was his custom, my father invited me to accompany him on a short stroll along the magnificent forest vista bordering our estate. "General Spielsdorf cannot arrive sooner than I had hoped," he remarked as we walked. He was scheduled to visit for several weeks, with his arrival due the following day. He was bringing with him a young lady, his niece and ward, Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt. I had never met her, though I had heard her described as exceedingly charming, and I had dreamed of many happy days spent in her company. To be denied this introduction felt like a crushing disappointment, far worse than any young woman confined to a city or a bustling district could comprehend. This anticipated visit and the new friendship it promised had fueled my daydreams for weeks.

"And when does he come?" I asked.

"Not until autumn," he replied. "Perhaps not for two months."

"I am relieved now, dear, that you never met Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt."

"And why?" I asked, feeling both mortified and intensely curious.

"Because the poor young lady is dead," he answered simply.

"I completely forgot I hadn't told you, but you weren't in the room when I received the General's letter this evening."

I was deeply shaken. In his initial correspondence, sent six or seven weeks prior, the General had merely noted that she was not as well as he wished, offering no hint of imminent peril.

"Here is the General's letter," he said, handing it to me. "I fear he is in terrible distress; the writing seems almost frantic."

We sat upon a rough bench beneath a cluster of majestic lime trees. The sun was setting with all its melancholy brilliance behind the wooded horizon. The stream flowing past our home, passing under the steep old bridge I have previously mentioned, wound through noble groves at our feet, reflecting the fading crimson of the sky.

The General's letter was extraordinary, vehement, and in parts, self-contradictory. I read it twice, the second time aloud to my father, yet I could make sense of it only by assuming that grief had unhinged his mind.

*"I have lost my darling daughter, for as such I loved her,"* it began. *"During the final days of dear Bertha's illness, I was unable to write to you. Before then, I knew nothing of her danger. I have lost her, and now learn *everything*, too late. She died in the peace of innocence and the glorious hope of a blessed future. The fiend who betrayed our unsuspecting hospitality has done it all. I thought I was welcoming innocence, gaiety, a charming companion for my lost Bertha. Heaven! What a fool I have been! I thank God my child died without suspecting the cause of her suffering. She is gone without even conjecturing the nature of her illness or the accursed passion of the agent of all this misery. I dedicate my remaining days to tracking and extinguishing a monster. I am told I may hope to achieve my righteous and merciful purpose. For now, there is scarcely a gleam of light to guide me. I curse my conceited incredulity, my despicable pretension of superiority, my blindness, my obstinacy—all—too late. I cannot write or speak coherently now; I am distracted. As soon as I recover somewhat, I intend to devote myself to inquiry, which may lead me as far as Vienna. Sometime in the autumn, two months hence, or earlier if I survive, I will see you—that is, if you permit me; I will then tell you everything I scarce dare put to paper now. Farewell. Pray for me, dear friend."*

Thus ended this strange missive. Though I had never seen Bertha Rheinfeldt, tears filled my eyes at the sudden news; I was startled, profoundly disappointed. The sun had set, and twilight descended as I returned the letter to my father. It was a soft, clear evening. We lingered, speculating on the violent, incoherent meaning of the words we had just read. We still had nearly a mile to walk before reaching the road leading to the front of the castle, and by then, the moon was shining brilliantly.

At the drawbridge, we encountered Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, who had come out without their bonnets to enjoy the exquisite moonlight. We heard their voices chatting animatedly as we approached. Joining them at the drawbridge, we turned to admire the beautiful scene together. The glade through which we had just walked lay before us. To our left, the narrow road wound away under clusters of lordly trees, vanishing into the thickening forest. To the right, the road crossed the steep, picturesque bridge, near which stood a ruined tower that once guarded the pass. Beyond the bridge, an abrupt eminence rose, covered in trees and revealing grey, ivy-clad rocks in the shadows. A thin film of mist stole over the grassy lowlands like smoke, marking the distances with a transparent veil; here and there, the river flashed faintly in the moonlight. No softer, sweeter scene could be imagined. The news I had just heard cast a melancholy shadow over it, yet nothing could disturb its profound serenity, nor the enchanted glory and vagueness of the prospect.

My father, who relished the picturesque, and I, stood in silent contemplation over the expanse below. The two governesses, standing a little behind us, discoursed on the scenery, eloquently praising the moon. Madame Perrodon was fat, middle-aged, and romantic, talking and sighing poetically. Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, claiming descent from a German father, adopted a tone of psychological and metaphysical mysticism. She declared that when the moon shines with such intensity, it indicates special spiritual activity. The effect of a full moon in such brilliance was manifold: it influenced dreams, madness, and nervous constitutions, exerting marvelous physical forces connected to life itself.

Mademoiselle recounted that her cousin, a mate on a merchant vessel, had fallen asleep on deck during such a night, lying on his back with his face directly toward the lunar orb. He woke, after dreaming of an old woman clawing at his cheek, with his features horribly distorted to one side; his countenance had never fully regained its equilibrium.

"The moon, this night," she said, "is full of idyllic and magnetic influence—and see, when you look behind you at the front of the castle, how all its windows flash and twinkle with that silvery splendor, as if unseen hands had lit the rooms to welcome fairy guests."

There are indolent states of spirit in which, unwilling to speak ourselves, we find pleasure in the chatter of others. I gazed on, pleased by the tinkling of the ladies' conversation.

"I have fallen into one of my moping moods tonight," my father said after a silence, quoting Shakespeare, whom he often read aloud to maintain our English fluency. "'In truth I know not why I am so sad. It wearies me: you say it wearies you; But how I got it—came by it.' I forget the rest. But I feel as if some great misfortune hangs over us. I suppose the poor General's afflicted letter has contributed to it."

Just then, the unfamiliar sound of carriage wheels and many hooves on the road arrested our attention. They seemed to approach from the high ground overlooking the bridge. Soon, the equipage emerged from that point. Two horsemen crossed the bridge first, followed by a carriage drawn by four horses, with two men riding behind. It appeared to be the traveling carriage of someone of high rank, and we were immediately captivated by this unusual spectacle.

Within moments, the scene grew far more intriguing. Just as the carriage crested the steep bridge, one of the lead horses took fright, transmitting its panic to the others. After a few plunges, the entire team broke into a wild gallop. Dashing between the horsemen ahead, they thundered along the road toward us with hurricane-like speed. The excitement of the scene was rendered more painful by the clear, long-drawn screams of a female voice from the carriage window. We all advanced in curiosity and horror; I moved forward in silence, while the others uttered various ejaculations of terror.

Our suspense did not last long. Just before the castle drawbridge, on the route they were traversing, stands a magnificent lime tree on one side and an ancient stone cross on the other. At the sight of these, the horses, moving at a pace that was utterly terrifying, swerved violently, causing the carriage wheel to strike the projecting roots of the tree.

I knew what was coming. I covered my eyes, unable to bear the sight, and turned my head away. At that exact moment, I heard a cry from my lady friends, who had stepped forward slightly. Curiosity compelled me to open my eyes, and I witnessed a scene of utter chaos. Two horses lay on the ground; the carriage rested on its side with two wheels suspended in the air. The men were busy removing the harness, while a lady of commanding presence and figure had dismounted, standing with clasped hands, repeatedly lifting the handkerchief within them to her eyes.

Through the open carriage door, a young lady was being lifted out; she appeared lifeless. My dear old father was already beside the older lady, hat in hand, evidently offering aid and

We watched the *cortege* until it vanished into the mist-shrouded woods, the rhythmic clatter of hooves and wheels fading into the dead silence of the night. Nothing remained to prove the encounter wasn't merely a fleeting hallucination, save for the sudden opening of the young lady's eyes. Though her face was turned away, I saw her lift her head, scanning the darkness around her. A voice, sweet and trembling with distress, cut through the air: "Where is Mamma?"

Madame Perrodon responded with tenderness, offering soothing assurances. The girl's confusion deepened. "Where am I? What place is this?" she cried out. "I cannot see the carriage! And Matska... where is Matska?" Madame answered every question within her power. Slowly, the girl pieced together the chaos of the accident, relief washing over her to learn that no one inside or attending the carriage was injured. Yet, upon hearing that her mother had abandoned her there, to return only in three months' time, fresh tears welled in her eyes.

I moved to offer my own comfort, but Mademoiselle de Lafontaine gently restrained me. "Do not approach," she advised softly. "One person speaking to her at a time is all she can handle now; even a slight surge of excitement might overwhelm her fragile state."

Once settled comfortably in bed, I resolved to climb the stairs to check on her. Meanwhile, my father had dispatched a servant on horseback to fetch the physician, who resided two leagues distant, while a private chamber was prepared for our guest. The stranger rose, leaning heavily on Madame's arm, and walked slowly across the drawbridge and through the castle gates. Servants awaited in the hall, guiding her directly to her quarters.

Her room was spacious, serving as our drawing-room during the day. It featured four tall windows overlooking the moat, the drawbridge, and the forest I had just described. Furnished in antique carved oak, it boasted massive cabinets and chairs upholstered in crimson Utrecht velvet. The walls were draped in heavy tapestries framed in gold, depicting hunts, falconry, and festive gatherings in costumes of ancient times. While undeniably stately, the room retained a profound comfort. Here, we gathered for tea, for my father, ever the patriot, insisted that the national beverage accompany our coffee and chocolate.

That night, by candlelight, we recounted the evening's bizarre events. Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle de Lafontaine joined us. The young stranger had barely touched her pillow before sinking into a deep slumber, leaving the ladies to entrust her to a servant.

"How do you like our guest?" I asked as Madame entered. "Tell me everything about her."

"I adore her," Madame replied warmly. "She is, I believe, the prettiest creature I have ever encountered—of your age, so gentle and refined."

"She is absolutely beautiful," interjected Mademoiselle, who had peeked briefly into the stranger's room. "And what a sweet voice!"

"Did you notice a woman remaining in the carriage after it stopped?" Mademoiselle asked. "Someone who didn't exit but merely stared from the window?"

"No, we saw none," Madame confirmed.

Then she described a horrifying figure: a hideous black woman wearing a colored turban, staring relentlessly from the carriage window. She nodded and grinned with derision toward the ladies, her eyes gleaming unnaturally, revealing large, stark white sclerae, her teeth bared in a snarl of fury.

"And the servants?" Madame inquired. "Did you note their appearance?"

"Yes," my father answered, having just entered. "Ugly, hang-dog creatures I've never seen in my life. I pray they do not rob the poor lady in the forest. They are cunning rogues, however; they managed to restore order in moments."

"They must be exhausted from such a long journey," Madame mused. "Their faces were not only wicked-looking but strangely lean, dark, and sullen. I am quite curious, truly; but I trust the young lady will explain everything tomorrow, once she has recovered."

"I doubt she will," my father said, a mysterious smile playing on his lips accompanied by a subtle nod, suggesting he knew more than he cared to reveal.

This cryptic behavior only heightened our curiosity regarding his brief, earnest exchange with the lady in black velvet prior to her departure. We were scarcely alone when I implored him to speak. He required little urging.

"There is no reason I should withhold this," he began. "She expressed reluctance to burden us with the care of her daughter, citing delicate health and extreme nervousness. She volunteered, however, that she suffered from no seizures nor illusions—in fact, she claimed to be perfectly sane."

"How peculiar to assert such things!" I interjected. "So unnecessary."

"At least it *was* said," he chuckled. "Since you wish to know the full account, which was indeed sparse, I shall tell you. She stated, 'I am undertaking a journey of vital importance—she stressed the word—rapid and secret. I shall return for my child in three months; meanwhile, she will remain silent concerning who we are, whence we come, and whither we travel.' That was all. She spoke flawless French. When she uttered the word 'secret,' she paused for several seconds, fixing her gaze sternly upon me. I suspect she places immense weight on that concept. Observe how swiftly she departed. Do you suppose I acted foolishly in accepting responsibility for this young lady?"

As for me, I was delighted. Longing to see and converse with her, I simply awaited the physician's approval. Those living in cities cannot comprehend how monumental an event the arrival of a new friend becomes in such isolation. The doctor did not arrive until nearly one o'clock, yet I could not have retired to sleep sooner than I could have outrun the carriage carrying the princess in black velvet on foot.

When the physician descended to the drawing-room, his report was overwhelmingly favorable. His patient was sitting upright, her pulse regular, appearing entirely well. She had sustained no injuries, and the minor shock to her nerves had passed harmlessly. With his explicit permission, I immediately sent a servant to inquire if she would allow a brief visit. The servant returned promptly to confirm she desired nothing more. Naturally, I did not delay in exercising this privilege.

Our visitor lay in one of the finest chambers in the *schloss*. Perhaps slightly too grand, it featured a somber tapestry opposite the foot of the bed depicting Cleopatra with asp-serpents coiled at her bosom, alongside other faded classical scenes. Yet, the gold carvings and rich, varied colors of the other decorations more than compensated for the gloom of the ancient hangings. Candles burned brightly at the bedside. She sat propped up, her slender, lovely figure wrapped in a soft silk dressing-gown embroidered with flowers and lined with thick quilted silk, which her mother had draped over her legs as she lay upon the ground.

What struck me dumb, causing me to recoil a step or two as I approached the bedside, was unmistakable. I saw the very face that had haunted my childhood nightmares, fixed in my memory for years, the object of my terrified ruminations when no one suspected my thoughts. It was pretty, even beautiful, bearing initially the same melancholy expression. Yet, this instantly transformed into a strange, fixed smile of recognition.

A silence stretched for a full minute before she finally spoke; I remained unable to utter a word.

"How wonderful!" she exclaimed. "Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since."

"Wonderful indeed!" I repeated, struggling against the horror that momentarily paralyzed my speech. "Twelve years ago, in vision or reality, I certainly saw you. I could not forget your face. It has remained before my eyes ever since."

Her smile softened. Whatever seemed strange in it vanished, replaced by a delightful, intelligent beauty and dimpled cheeks. Feeling reassured, I resumed the tone dictated by hospitality, welcoming her and expressing how much joy her accidental arrival brought us all, particularly the happiness it meant to me. Taking her hand, I felt a mixture of shyness typical of lonely souls, yet the situation rendered me eloquent and bold. She pressed my hand, laying hers atop it, her eyes glowing as she looked hastily into mine, smiling again and blushing. She greeted my welcome with charming grace.

Sitting beside her, still bewildered, she said, "I must recount my vision of you; it is so strange that we should have shared such vivid dreams of one another, each seeing the other exactly as we appear now, when we were, of course, mere children. I was about six years old. I awoke from a confused, troubled dream to find myself in a room unlike my nursery, wainscoted clumsily in dark wood, with cupboards, bedsteads, and benches scattered about. The beds appeared empty, and the room contained no one but myself. After searching for some time, admiring particularly an iron candlestick with two branches—I would certainly recognize it again—I crept under one of the beds to reach the window. But as I emerged, I heard someone crying. Looking up while still on my knees, I saw you—most assuredly you—as I see you now: a beautiful young lady with golden hair, large blue eyes, and lips identical to yours here."

"My looks won me," she continued. "I climbed onto the bed and embraced you, and I believe we both fell asleep. I was awakened by a scream; you were sitting up

I have told you that I was charmed with her in most particulars. Yet, there were certain traits that did not please me so well. She stood above the average height for a woman. Let me describe her fully. She was slender and possessed a grace that was truly wonderful. Save for the extreme languor of her movements—a lethargy so profound it bordered on the unnatural—there was nothing in her appearance to suggest she was an invalid. Her complexion was rich and brilliant; her features were small and perfectly formed. Her eyes were large, dark, and lustrous. Her hair was, quite simply, magnificent. I had never seen hair so luxuriously thick and long when it cascaded down about her shoulders. I have often placed my hands beneath it, laughing in sheer astonishment at its weight. It was exquisitely fine and soft, a deep, rich brown interwoven with threads of gold. I loved to watch it fall, tumbling under its own gravity, as she reclined in her chair, speaking in her sweet, low voice. In those moments, I would fold and braid it, spreading it out and playing with the heavy strands. Oh, Heaven! If only I had known then what I would later discover!

I mentioned earlier that her confidence won me on the very first night I saw her. However, I soon realized that regarding herself, her mother, her history, and indeed everything connected to her life, plans, and acquaintances, she maintained an ever-wakeful reserve. I dare say I was unreasonable; perhaps I was wrong. I dare say I should have respected the solemn injunction laid upon my father by the stately lady in black velvet. But curiosity is a restless and unscrupulous passion, and no girl can endure, with patience, having her secrets baffled by another. What harm could it possibly do anyone to reveal what I so ardently desired to know? Did she lack trust in my good sense or my honor? Why would she not believe me when I assured her, with the utmost solemnity, that I would not divulge a single syllable of what she confided to any mortal breathing?

There was a coldness, it seemed to me, beyond her years, hidden within her smiling melancholy and her persistent refusal to grant me even a ray of light. I cannot say we quarreled over this point, for she refused to quarrel on any subject whatsoever. It was, of course, very unfair of me to press her, very ill-bred, but I really could not help it; and I might just as well have let it go. Whatever she chose to tell me amounted, in my unconscionable estimation, to nothing. It boiled down to three very vague disclosures: First, her name was Carmilla. Second, her family was ancient and noble. Third, her home lay somewhere to the west. She would not reveal the name of her family, nor their armorial bearings, nor the name of their estate, nor even the country in which they dwelt.

Do not suppose that I harried her incessantly on these subjects. I watched for opportunities and rather insinuated than urged my inquiries. Once or twice, indeed, I did attack her more directly. But regardless of my tactics, utter failure was invariably the result. Reproaches and caresses alike were lost upon her. Yet, I must add this: her evasion was conducted with such pretty melancholy and deprecation, accompanied by so many—even passionate—declarations of her affection for me, her trust in my honor, and so many promises that I should eventually know all, that I could not bring myself to remain offended for long.

She would often place her pretty arms around my neck, draw me close, and resting her cheek against mine, murmur with her lips near my ear: *"Dearest, your little heart is wounded; think me not cruel because I obey the irresistible law of my strength and weakness. If your dear heart is wounded, my wild heart bleeds with yours. In the rapture of my enormous humiliation, I live in your warm life, and you shall die—die, sweetly die—into mine. I cannot help it; as I draw near to you, you, in your turn, will draw near to others, and learn the rapture of that cruelty, which yet is love. So, for a while, seek to know no more of me and mine, but trust me with all your loving spirit."*

When she had spoken such a rhapsody, she would press me more tightly in her trembling embrace, her lips glowing softly with kisses upon my cheek. Her agitations and her language were unintelligible to me. From these foolish embraces—which were not of very frequent occurrence—I must admit, I often wished to extricate myself; yet my energies seemed to fail me. Her murmured words sounded like a lullaby in my ear, soothing my resistance into a trance from which I only seemed to awaken when she withdrew her arms. In these mysterious moods, I did not like her. I experienced a strange, tumultuous excitement that was pleasurable, ever and anon mingled with a vague sense of fear and disgust. I had no distinct thoughts about her while such scenes lasted, but I was conscious of a love growing into adoration, coupled simultaneously with abhorrence. This is a paradox, but I can make no other attempt to explain the feeling.

Now, writing this after an interval of more than ten years, my hand trembles, and my memory is confused and horrible regarding certain occurrences and situations through which I was unconsciously passing, though I retain a vivid and sharp remembrance of the main current of my story. Yet, I suspect that in all lives there are certain emotional scenes—those where our passions have been most wildly and terribly roused—that are of all others the most vaguely and dimly remembered.

Sometimes, after an hour of apathy, my strange and beautiful companion would take my hand and hold it with a fond pressure, renewing the grip again and again. Blushing softly, she would gaze in my face with languid, burning eyes, breathing so rapidly that her dress rose and fell with tumultuous respiration. It was like the ardor of a lover; it embarrassed me; it was hateful yet overpowering. With gloating eyes, she would draw me to her, her hot lips traveling along my cheek in kisses, whispering almost in sobs, *"You are mine, you *shall* be mine, you and I are one forever."* Then she would throw herself back in her chair, covering her small hands over her eyes, leaving me trembling.

*"Are we related?"* I would ask. *"What can you mean by all this? Do I remind you of someone you love? But you must not—I hate it; I don't know you—I don't know myself when you look so and talk so."*

She would sigh at my vehemence, then turn away and drop my hand. Regarding these very extraordinary manifestations, I strove in vain to form any satisfactory theory. I could not attribute them to affectation or trickery. It was unmistakably the momentary breaking out of suppressed instinct and emotion. Was she, despite her mother's volunteered denial, subject to brief visitations of insanity? Or was there here a disguise and a romance? I had read in old storybooks of such things. What if a boyish lover had found his way into the house, seeking to prosecute his suit in masquerade, aided by a clever old adventuress? But there were many things against this hypothesis, however highly interesting it was to my vanity. I could boast of no little attentions such as masculine gallantry delights to offer. Between these passionate moments, there were long intervals of commonplace behavior, of gaiety, of brooding melancholy, during which, save for the fact that I detected her eyes filled with melancholy fire, following me at times, I might have been nothing to her. Except in these brief periods of mysterious excitement, her ways were girlish; and there was always a languor about her, quite incompatible with a masculine system in a state of health.

In some respects, her habits were odd. Perhaps not so singular in the opinion of a town lady as they appeared to us rustic folk. She would come down very late, generally not until one o'clock. She would then take a cup of chocolate but eat nothing. We would then go out for a walk, which was merely a saunter, and she seemed, almost immediately, exhausted, returning to the castle or sitting on one of the benches placed here and there among the trees. This was a bodily languor in which her mind did not sympathize. She was always an animated talker and very intelligent. She would sometimes allude briefly to her own home, mention an adventure or situation, or recall an early memory indicating a people of strange manners and customs unknown to us. Gathering from these chance hints, I concluded that her native country was far more remote than I had first fancied.

As we sat one afternoon under the trees, a funeral procession passed us. It was that of a pretty young girl whom I had often seen, the daughter of one of the forest rangers. The poor man walked behind the coffin of his darling; she was his only child, and he looked quite heartbroken. Peasants walking two-by-two followed behind, singing a funeral hymn. I rose to mark my respect as they passed and joined in the hymn, which they were singing very sweetly. My companion gave me a slight, rough shake, and I turned, surprised.

*"Do you not perceive how discordant that is?"* she said brusquely.

*"I think it very sweet, on the contrary,"* I answered, vexed at the interruption and very uncomfortable,

That evening, a somber figure arrived from Gratz—a grave-faced man, the son of a picture restorer—pulling a horse-drawn cart heavily laden with two massive packing cases filled with canvases. The journey from our small capital had covered ten leagues, and whenever a messenger reached the Schloss from Gratz, the household would gather in the hall, breathless with anticipation, eager for the latest tidings. His arrival sent a ripple of excitement through our otherwise secluded quarters.

The cases were left standing in the hall while the messenger was ushered away by the servants to eat his supper. Soon, however, he returned, flanked by assistants and armed with hammers, chisels, and screwdrivers, ready to begin the restoration. We assembled once more in the hall to witness the unveiling of the old masterpieces. Carmilla sat apart, watching with a listless gaze as the aged portraits, mostly likenesses of ancestors, were carefully cleaned and brought back to life.

My mother belonged to an ancient Hungarian lineage, and most of these paintings had entered our possession through her. My father held a ledger, reading off numbers as the artist sorted through the crates. "These aren't particularly fine works," I noted mentally, "but they are undeniably ancient, and some are truly peculiar." For the most part, I was seeing them for the first time; decades of smoke and dust had nearly erased their features entirely.

"There is one portrait I haven't examined yet," my father announced. "In the upper corner, I can barely decipher the name: 'Marcia Karnstein,' dated 1698. I'm curious to see how it has survived."

I recalled it; it was a small canvas, roughly a foot and a half high and nearly square, lacking a frame. It was so blackened by time that its details were invisible to me. Now, the artist produced it with evident pride.

It was breathtaking. It was startling. It seemed to breathe. It was the effigy of Carmilla herself.

"Carmilla, dearest, this is an absolute miracle!" my father exclaimed, his voice trembling slightly. "Here you are, alive, smiling, ready to speak. Isn't it beautiful, Papa? Look, even the tiny mole on her throat is preserved."

My father chuckled softly. "Undoubtedly, it's a wonderful likeness," he said, though he quickly averted his eyes. To my astonishment, he appeared hardly moved by it, turning instead to converse with the picture cleaner—an accomplished artist himself—who spoke intelligently about the newly revealed portraits. While I grew increasingly entranced, staring at the image, my father continued his polite discourse.

"May I hang this picture in my room, Papa?" I asked timidly.

"Certainly, dear," he replied with a smile. "I'm delighted you find it so striking. If it pleases you, it must be even finer than I imagined."

The young lady beside me did not respond to his compliment. She leaned back in her chair, her dark eyes hidden beneath long lashes fixed on me in deep contemplation, a rapturous smile playing on her lips.

"And now you can clearly read the inscription in the corner," she observed, her voice soft. "It isn't Marcia; the letters appear to be in gold leaf. The name is *Mircalla*, Countess Karnstein, accompanied by a small coronet above and below the date, A.D. 1698. I am descended from the Karnsteins—that is, my mother was."

"Oh!" she murmured, her tone languid. "So am I, I suppose, of a very long and ancient line. Are there any Karnsteins living still?"

"I believe none remain bearing the name," my father answered. "The family was ruined during some civil wars centuries ago, though the ruins of their castle lie merely three miles from here."

"How fascinating!" she sighed. "But look at the beautiful moonlight!" She glanced toward the hall door, which stood ajar. "Shall we take a short stroll around the courtyard and look down at the road and the river?"

"It reminds me so much of the night you first came to us," I whispered.

She sighed, a sound of pure delight, and smiled. Rising gracefully, she took my arm, and together we stepped onto the pavement. In silence, we walked slowly toward the drawbridge, where the enchanting landscape unfolded before us.

"So you were thinking of the night I arrived?" she whispered, her voice barely audible. "Are you glad I came?"

"Delighted, dear Carmilla," I answered honestly.

"And you requested this portrait, believing it resembles you, to hang in your chamber," she murmured, drawing my arm tighter against hers until her head rested upon my shoulder. "How romantic you are, Carmilla. Whenever you tell me your story, it will surely consist mainly of some grand romance."

She kissed me silently.

"I am certain, Carmilla, that you have loved; that there is currently an affair of the heart underway."

"I have loved no one," she whispered, "and never shall... unless it might be with you."

How radiant she appeared in the moonlight! With shy, strange intensity, she buried her face in my neck and hair, emitting tumultuous sobs that seemed to shake her frame, clutching my hand with trembling fingers. Her soft cheek burned against mine.

"Darling, darling," she breathed, "I live within you; and you would die for me. I love you so."

Suddenly, I recoiled from her. She stared at me with eyes devoid of fire, stripped of all meaning, her face pale and utterly apathetic.

"Does the air feel chilly, dear?" she asked, her voice drowsy. "I almost shiver. Have I been dreaming? Let us go inside. Come, come, come in."

"You look unwell, Carmilla; rather faint. You certainly need some wine," I said, concern tightening my chest.

"Yes, yes. I will. I feel better already. I shall be completely well in moments. Do give me a little wine," Carmilla replied as we approached the door.

"Let us linger just a moment longer; it may be the last time I see the moonlight with you," she insisted.

"How do you feel now, dear Carmilla? Are you truly better?" I asked, alarm rising within me. I began to fear she had fallen victim to the strange epidemic rumored to be sweeping the countryside.

"Papa would be devastated," I added, "if he believed you were even slightly ill without immediate notification. We have a highly skilled physician nearby—the same one who attended to Papa today."

"He is excellent. I know how kind everyone is here," she assured me gently. "But, dear child, I am perfectly well again. Nothing is ever truly wrong with me, save for a slight weakness. People say I am languid; I am incapable of exertion. I can scarcely walk as far as a toddler. Every now and then, the little strength I possess falters, and I become exactly as you saw me just now. Yet, I recover so easily; in a moment, I am myself. Witness how I have regained my composure."

Indeed, she had transformed. We conversed animatedly for a while, and the remainder of the evening passed without any recurrence of those terrifying infatuations—the crazy talk and haunted glances that had previously frightened and bewildered me.

However, that very night, an event occurred which shifted my thoughts entirely and seemed to jolt Carmilla's usual lethargy into a sudden, startling energy.

When we finally entered the drawing-room and settled ourselves for coffee and chocolate—though Carmilla took none of either—she seemed instantly restored to her former composure. Madame and Mademoiselle de Lafontaine joined us shortly after, and we engaged in a modest card game. During the lull between rounds, my father entered to claim his "dish of tea." Once the cards were stacked away, he seated himself beside Carmilla on the sofa, his brow furrowed with a quiet anxiety.

"Have you received word from your mother since your arrival?" he asked gently.

She shook her head. "No."

He pressed further, seeking practicality. "Then where might a letter reach you at present?"

"I cannot say," she replied, her voice carrying a deliberate ambiguity. "But I have been considering leaving you. You have been far too hospitable, far too kind. I have caused you an infinity of trouble, and I believe it would be best if I departed tomorrow. I intend to hire a carriage and post myself in pursuit of her. I know exactly where I shall eventually find her, though I dare not reveal those details to you yet."

"But you must not dream of such a thing!" my father exclaimed, a wave of profound relief washing over me. "We cannot afford to lose you so easily. And I shall not consent to your departure unless it is under the direct care of your mother, who was so gracious as to agree to keep you company until her own return. I would be infinitely happier knowing you heard from her. Indeed, tonight the reports regarding the mysterious disease ravaging our neighborhood have grown even more terrifying. As your host, and unaided by counsel from your mother, I feel a heavy burden of responsibility. Yet, I promise to do my utmost. One thing is absolute: you must not consider leaving us without her explicit instruction to that effect. To part with you so abruptly would cause us unspeakable suffering."

"Thank you, sir, a thousand times for your generosity," she answered, bowing her head with a bashful smile. "You have all been excessively kind. I have rarely known such happiness in my entire life as I have within these walls of your beautiful château, under your protection, and in the company of your beloved daughter."

With a gallantry characteristic of his older generation, he kissed her hand, his face lighting up with pleasure at her words.

As was my custom, I escorted Carmilla to her chamber afterward, sitting with her while she prepared for bed.

"Do you truly believe," I ventured after a moment's silence, "that you will ever confide fully in me?"

She turned toward me, a radiant smile playing on her lips, yet offering no verbal response. She simply continued to gaze at me, her expression enigmatic.

"You offer no answer?" I persisted. "You cannot reply pleasantly; perhaps I should not have asked."

"You were entirely right to ask," she said softly, her tone shifting to something deeper, more intense. "Or to ask anything at all. Do you not realize how dear you are to me? If you did, you would understand that no confidence is too great to seek. However, I am bound by vows—vows more terrible than any taken by a nun—and I dare not recount my story to you, not even now. The time is drawing near when you shall know every truth. You may deem me cruel, perhaps even selfish; but love is inherently selfish. The more ardent the flame, the greater the self-absorption. How jealous I am, you cannot possibly comprehend. You must come with me, loving me, even unto death; or else hate me, and still accompany me. Even hating me, you must follow me through death and beyond. In my apathetic nature, there exists no such word as 'indifference.'"

"Now, Carmilla, you are merely speaking your wild nonsense again," I interrupted hastily, feeling a chill run down my spine.

"Not I, silly little fool that I am, brimming with whims and fancies," she countered, her voice dropping to a whisper. "For your sake, I shall speak like a sage. Have you ever attended a ball?"

"No," I admitted. "How does it happen? How charming must it be?"

"I almost forgot; it was years ago."

"You are not so old," I laughed nervously. "Your first ball can hardly be a distant memory yet."

"I remember every detail of that night—with an effort. I see it all clearly, as divers observe the world above them through a medium that is dense, rippling, yet transparent. That night, however, something occurred that blurred the image, fading its colors. On that occasion, I was nearly assassinated in my bed. Wounded here," she pressed a trembling finger against her breast, "I have never been the same since."

"Were you near dying?"

"Yes, very. A cruel love—a strange love—that sought to extinguish my life. Love demands its sacrifices. No sacrifice occurs without blood."

"Let us retire now," she murmured, her energy seemingly sapped. "I feel so incredibly lazy. How can I possibly rise and lock my door just now?"

She lay back, burying her tiny hands in her rich, wavy hair beneath her cheek, resting her small head upon the pillow. Her glittering eyes tracked my every movement across the room, fixed upon me with a shy, unreadable smile that defied interpretation.

I bade her goodnight and slipped quietly from the room, leaving behind an uncomfortable, prickling sensation. Later, I often wondered if our lovely guest ever said her prayers. I had never once witnessed her upon her knees. In the mornings, she descended only long after our family devotions concluded; at night, she never left the drawing-room to join our brief evening service in the hall. Had it not been for the casual revelation during one of our idle conversations that she had indeed been baptized, I might have suspected she was not a Christian at all. Religion was a subject she had never uttered a single word about. Had I possessed a broader knowledge of the world, this specific neglect—or perhaps active antipathy—would not have so greatly astonished me.

The precautions of the nervous are contagious. Persons of a similar temperament inevitably begin to mimic them after some time. I had adopted Carmilla's habit of locking my bedroom door, succumbing to her whimsical alarms about midnight invaders and prowling assassins. I had also taken to conducting a brief, ritualistic search of my own quarters to ensure no lurking thief or murderer was "ensconced" within.

Having taken these prudent measures, I retired to my bed and fell into a slumber.

A light burned steadily in my room. This was an ancient habit, established long ago, and nothing could have tempted me to abandon it. Fortified by this illumination, I believed I could rest in peace. But dreams penetrate stone walls; they illuminate dark chambers or shroud bright ones in shadow. Their inhabitants enter and exit at will, laughing at the efforts of locksmiths.

That night, I experienced a dream that marked the beginning of a very strange agony. I cannot classify it as a nightmare, for I remained fully conscious that I was asleep. Equally, I was acutely aware that I was in my room, lying in my bed, precisely as I physically was. I saw, or rather fancied I saw, the room and its furnishings exactly as I had observed them moments before, save for the fact that it was plunged in darkness. At the foot of the bed, something moved, indistinct at first. Soon, however, I discerned it: a soot-black creature resembling a monstrous cat. It appeared to be four or five feet in length, spanning the full width of the hearthrug as it traversed it. It paced back and forth with the lithe, sinister restlessness of a beast trapped in a cage.

I could not cry out, though terror seized me completely. Its pace quickened, and the room grew darker and darker until visibility vanished entirely, save for its eyes. Suddenly, I felt it spring lightly onto the bed. Two broad eyes approached my face, and instantaneously, I felt a searing pain—as if two large needles had darted, an inch or two apart, deep into my chest.

I woke with a scream.

The room was illuminated by the candle that had burned throughout the night. Standing at the foot of the bed, slightly to the right, was a female figure. She wore a dark, loose dress, and her hair hung down, covering her shoulders. She was as motionless as a block of stone. There was not the slightest stir of respiration.

As I stared, transfixed, the figure seemed to shift position, moving closer to the door. Then, right beside the threshold, the door swung open, and she stepped out into the corridor.

Relief washed over me, allowing me to breathe and move freely. My immediate thought was that Carmilla had played a prank upon me, forgetting to secure my door properly. I rushed to it and found it locked securely on the inside, just as usual. Fear gripped me anew; I was horrified. I threw myself back into my bed, pulling the covers over my head, lying there more dead than alive until the dawn broke.

It would be futile for me to attempt to convey the sheer magnitude of the horror that clings to my memory of that night, even now. It was not merely a fleeting terror, the kind a nightmare leaves behind to fade with the dawn. Instead, it seemed to deepen with the passage of time, seeping into the very walls and saturating the furniture that had witnessed the apparition. I found myself unable to bear solitude the following day; the mere silence of the room felt oppressive.

I hesitated to confide in my father. Paradoxically, two opposing fears held me back. On one hand, I dreaded his laughter, the possibility that my tale would be dismissed as a childish jest, a triviality I could not stomach. On the other, I feared he might suspect I had fallen victim to the mysterious affliction plaguing our neighborhood. Though I harbored no such doubts myself, his recent frailty made me reluctant to alarm him further.

Fortunately, I had solace in the company of my good-natured companions, Madame Perrodon and the vivacious Mademoiselle Lafontaine. They sensed my agitation and nervousness immediately. Eventually, burdened by the weight of my secret, I confessed to them. Mademoiselle laughed, but I noticed a genuine anxiety in Madame Perrodon's expression.

"By the way," Mademoiselle remarked, her mirth returning, "the long lime-tree walk behind Carmilla's bedroom window is haunted!"

"Nonsense!" Madame Perrodon exclaimed, likely deeming the topic inappropriate. "Who tells that story, my dear?"

"Martin claims he climbed up twice while the old yard gate was being repaired, before sunrise, and twice he saw the same female figure walking down the lime-tree avenue."

"So he well might, as long as there are cows to milk in the river fields," Madame retorted dismissively. "I daresay; but Martin chooses to be frightened. He is the most foolish creature I know."

"You must not mention this to Carmilla," I interjected sharply. "She can see down that walk from her own window, and she is, if anything, a greater coward than I."

Later that afternoon, Carmilla descended somewhat later than usual. As soon as we were together, she spoke of her own frights.

"I was terrified last night," she said, her voice trembling slightly. "I am certain I would have seen something dreadful had it not been for that charm I purchased from the poor little hunchback—whom I called many harsh names. I dreamed of something black circling my bed. I awoke in absolute horror, convinced for several seconds that a dark figure stood near the chimneypiece. But I felt beneath my pillow for my charm; the moment my fingers brushed it, the vision vanished. I knew with certainty that without it, something frightful would have emerged, perhaps throttling me just as it did those unfortunate souls we heard about."

"Well, listen to me," I began, recounting my own ordeal. Her face drained of color as she listened, horror dawning in her eyes.

"And did you keep the charm near you?" she asked, her gaze intense.

"No," I admitted. "I had dropped it into a china vase in the drawing room. However, I shall certainly carry it with me tonight, given your profound faith in it."

Looking back from where I stand now, I cannot explain—or even comprehend—how I managed to overcome my terror sufficiently to lie alone in my room that night. I clearly remember pinning the charm to my pillow. Sleep claimed me almost instantly, deeper and more restorative than usual. The following night passed equally well; my sleep was a blissful, dreamless void.

Yet, I awoke with a profound sense of lassitude and melancholy. While not debilitating, the feeling was strangely luxurious in its depth.

"Well, I told you so," Carmilla declared when I described my tranquil slumber. "I enjoyed such a delightful sleep myself last night. I pinned the charm to the breast of my nightdress; the previous night, it was too far away. I am convinced it was all fancy, save for the dreams. I used to believe evil spirits generated nightmares, but our doctor insists otherwise. He says a fever or some other malady often knocks at the door, finds entry denied, and moves on, leaving behind that lingering alarm."

"And what do you suppose the charm actually is?" I asked.

"It has been fumigated or soaked in some potent drug; it serves as an antidote against malaria," she replied confidently.

"Then it acts solely on the physical body?"

"Certainly. Do you imagine evil spirits are cowed by scraps of ribbon or the perfumes of a druggist's shop? No. These ailments wander through the air, beginning by testing the nerves and infecting the brain. But before they can seize you, the antidote repels them. That is undoubtedly what this charm accomplished for us. It is nothing magical; it is purely natural."

Had I been able to fully align with Carmilla's rationalization, I might have found peace. I tried my best, and the initial impression of her logic did lose some of its sting. For several nights, I slept deeply. Yet, every morning brought the same weariness, a languor that hung heavy over me throughout the day. I felt transformed into a stranger. A strange melancholy began to steal over me—a sadness I did not wish to interrupt. Vague thoughts of death surfaced, and the notion that I was slowly sinking took a gentle, almost welcome, hold on my mind. If the mood was sorrowful, the tone of acceptance was sweet. Whatever the nature of this shift, my soul acquiesced to it. I refused to admit I was ill, refusing to inform my father or summon a physician.

Carmilla, conversely, became more devoted than ever, her strange paroxysms of languid adoration growing more frequent. She would gaze upon me with increasing ardor as my strength and spirits waned. This behavior always shocked me, flashing across my mind like a momentary glare of insanity. Unknowingly, I had entered the advanced stages of the strangest illness a mortal could endure. There was an inexplicable fascination in its early symptoms that more than compensated for the incapacitating effects of the disease. This allure intensified for a time until it reached a critical threshold, after which a sense of the horrific began to mingle with it, deepening until it discolored and perverted the entirety of my existence.

The first change I experienced was, paradoxically, agreeable. It marked the turning point from which the descent of Avernus truly began. Certain vague, strange sensations visited me in my sleep. The predominant one was a pleasant, peculiar cold thrill, akin to the shiver one feels when moving against the current of a river during a swim. Soon, this was accompanied by dreams that seemed interminable. They were so nebulous that I could never recall their scenery, the figures within them, or any coherent sequence of action. Yet, they left an awful impression, a lingering sense of exhaustion as though I had endured a prolonged period of immense mental exertion and peril.

Following these dreams, upon waking, I retained a memory of being in a place of near-total darkness, speaking to invisible people. Most distinct among them was a single, clear female voice—deep and resonant—that spoke as if from a great distance, slowly, evoking a sensation of indescribable solemnity and dread.

Sometimes, I felt a hand drawn softly along my cheek and neck. Other times, it felt as though warm lips kissed me, lingering longer and more lovingly as they traced toward my throat, where the caress would fix itself. My heart would race, my breathing rising and falling rapidly and heavily. A sobbing would ensue, escalating into a sensation of strangulation, culminating in a dreadful convulsion that stripped my senses away, plunging me into unconsciousness.

Three weeks had passed since the onset of this unaccountable state. During the final week, my sufferings had begun to manifest visibly. I had grown pale, my eyes dilated and shadowed beneath, the languor I had long felt now etched onto my countenance. My father frequently asked if I were ill, yet with an obstinacy that now seems inexplicable, I persistently assured him I was perfectly well. In a sense, this was true. I felt no pain, nor could I identify any specific bodily derangement. My complaint appeared to reside entirely in the imagination or the nerves. Horrible as my sufferings were, I guarded them with a morbid reserve, keeping them almost entirely to myself.

It could not be the terrible *oupir* the peasants spoke of, for I had been suffering for three weeks, whereas they rarely endured the misery for more than three days before death ended their torment. Carmilla complained of dreams and feverish sensations, but nothing approaching the alarming intensity of mine. To say mine were terrifying is an understatement. Had I been capable of comprehending my condition, I would have begged for aid and advice on my knees. An unsuspected narcotic influence was acting upon me, numbing my perceptions.

I must now recount a dream that led directly to a bizarre discovery. One night, instead of the familiar voice echoing in the dark, I heard one that was sweet, tender, and simultaneously terrible. It whispered, *"Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin."* Simultaneously, a light flared unexpectedly. I saw Carmilla standing near the foot of my bed, clad in her white nightdress, bathed from her chin to her feet in a single, vast stain of blood.

The room stood before us, pristine and untouched save for the violence of our own intrusion. In the sudden quiet, our hearts slowed, and reason gradually reclaimed its throne. We dismissed the guards, their work done. Yet, a chill remained. Mademoiselle proposed a terrifying possibility: perhaps Carmilla's scream had roused her from sleep, sending her fleeing into a wardrobe or behind heavy drapes, hidden from view until the majordomo and his minions departed. If so, she could not emerge until the coast was clear.

We resumed our hunt, calling her name through the silent corridors. Silence answered us. Our confusion deepened into dread. We checked every window; they were bolted shut. I pleaded with her spirit, should she be hiding, to cease this cruel game, to reveal herself and end our torment. But the void remained. I was certain she was not here, nor in the adjoining dressing room, whose door still clicked locked from within. She could not have slipped past.

Puzzlement gnawed at me. Had Carmilla discovered one of the secret passages whispered about by the old housekeeper, paths whose locations had long been forgotten? Only daylight could unravel this knot. It was past four in the afternoon, and I resolved to spend the remaining hours of darkness in Madame's chamber, hoping dawn would bring answers.

Morning broke over the château, bringing no relief, only a feverish agitation. The entire household, led by my distraught father, scoured every corner. The grounds were trampled; the streams were nearly dragged dry. No trace of the missing lady existed. My father hovered on the brink of madness, terrified of the tale he would have to deliver to the grieving mother awaiting her daughter's return. I, too, teetered on the edge of despair, though my grief was born of a different terror.

By one o'clock, stillness returned, broken only by the absence of news. Driven by instinct, I rushed to Carmilla's room.

There she stood, illuminated by the morning light, before her dressing table.

I froze. My mind refused to process the image. She beckoned me with a delicate finger, saying nothing. Her face, usually radiant, wore an expression of profound, primal fear. An ecstasy of joy surged through me, blinding and overwhelming. I threw myself toward her, kissing and embracing her repeatedly, unable to speak. Then, I scrambled to the bell, ringing it furiously to summon everyone needed to ease my father's agony.

"Dear Carmilla!" I gasped, pulling back slightly. "What has become of you? We have suffered agonies! Where have you been? How did you return?"

"Last night was a night of wonders," she replied softly, her voice trembling. "Mercy's sake, explain whatever you can."

"It was past two," she continued, recounting her memory with lucid horror. "I retired as usual, my doors locked—the bedroom, the dressing room, and the gallery door. My sleep was uninterrupted, dreamless. Yet, I awoke just now on the sofa in the dressing room. I found the door between the rooms wide open, and the outer door forced. How could this happen without waking me? Such commotion must have been deafening. And how could I have been carried from my bed without stirring? I am startled by the faintest whisper."

Soon, Madame, Mademoiselle, my father, and a throng of servants filled the room. Carmilla was besieged by inquiries, congratulations, and frantic welcomes. She possessed only one story—a narrative of total bewilderment—and appeared the least capable of explaining the impossible.

My father paced the room, his brow furrowed in deep thought. I watched Carmilla's eye track him, a fleeting, dark glint passing beneath her lashes. Once the servants had been dismissed, and Mademoiselle had vanished to fetch valerian and salvolatile, the room held only us three. My father approached her gently, taking her hand and guiding her to the sofa. He sat beside her, his demeanor shifting from panic to calculated inquiry.

"Carmilla, will you forgive me if I risk a conjecture and ask a single question?"

"Who possesses a better right?" she asked, her gaze fixed on him. "Ask freely, and I shall tell you everything. Though my tale is merely one of confusion and shadow. I know nothing. Ask anything, but remember the limits Mother has set regarding certain subjects."

"I understand perfectly," he said smoothly. "We need not touch upon those forbidden topics. The marvel remains: you were removed from your bed and room without waking, while the windows were sealed and the doors locked from the inside. Here is my theory, followed by a question."

Carmilla leaned heavily on her hand, dejected. Madame and I listened, holding our breath.

"Have you ever been suspected of sleeping-walking?"

"Never, since I was a small child," she murmured.

"But you did sleep-walk as a child?"

"Yes," she admitted, recalling the stories of her old nurse.

My father smiled, a knowing nod replacing his earlier distress. "Then consider this. Last night, you rose in your sleep. You unlocked the door—not leaving the key in the lock as you usually do, but removing it entirely, securing it from the outside. You took the key with you, wandering into one of the twenty-five rooms on this floor, or perhaps ascending or descending stairs. This ancient house is a labyrinth of chambers, closets, and accumulated lumber; it would take a week to search it thoroughly. Do you see now?"

"I see part of it," she replied hesitantly. "But how do you account for my finding myself on the sofa in the dressing room, a place we searched so meticulously?"

"She arrived there *after* your search," my father deduced calmly. "Still asleep, she wandered in, finally awakening spontaneously. She was as surprised to find herself there as any of us." He laughed lightly, the tension breaking. "If only all mysteries could be solved with such innocent logic, Carmilla."

"We may then congratulate ourselves," he continued, his tone reassuring. "The most natural explanation requires no drugging, no tampering with locks, no burglars, poisoners, or witches—nothing to alarm Carmilla or anyone else concerning our safety."

Carmilla looked radiant, her beauty seemingly enhanced by a graceful languor that clung to her. I felt my father's gaze shift between us, comparing her vitality to my own pallor.

"I wish my poor Laura looked more like herself," he sighed, his smile fading.

Thus, our alarms dissolved. Carmilla was restored to her friends, the mystery wrapped in a convenient veil of somnambulism, leaving the true nature of the night unspoken.

IX. The Doctor

As Carmilla would not tolerate the idea of an attendant sleeping within her chamber, my father arranged for a servant to rest directly outside her door. This precaution was intended to ensure that she would not attempt another nocturnal excursion without being intercepted at her threshold. That night passed undisturbed.

The following morning, before dawn, the doctor arrived. My father had summoned him without informing me of his presence. Madame escorted me to the library, where the grave little physician awaited us. He was the same man I had previously described: distinguished by his white hair and spectacles. I recounted my story to him, and as I spoke, his expression grew increasingly somber. We stood face-to-face in the recess of a large window. Upon finishing my account, he leaned his shoulders against the stone wall, fixing his eyes upon me with an intensity that bordered on horror. After a moment of silent reflection, he requested permission to speak with my father.

My father was summoned accordingly. As he entered, smiling broadly, he remarked, "I dare say, doctor, you are going to tell me that I am an old fool for having brought you here; I hope I am." However, his smile quickly vanished into shadow as the doctor, maintaining a profoundly serious countenance, beckoned him closer. They conversed for some time within the same window recess where I had just spoken with the physician. Their discussion appeared earnest and argumentative. The library was vast, leaving Madame and me standing together at the far end, burning with curiosity yet unable to overhear a single word. They spoke in hushed tones, and the deep alcove of the window obscured the doctor entirely, while my father's foot, arm, and shoulder were barely visible. The thick walls and glass further dampened their voices, rendering them unintelligible.

Eventually, my father's face emerged into the main room. He looked pale, thoughtful, and, I fancied, deeply agitated. "Laura, dear, come here for a moment," he called out. "Madame, we shall not trouble you; the doctor requires my attention at present." Reluctantly, though feeling somewhat alarmed despite my general weakness, I approached. One often mistakenly believes that strength is a commodity available on demand, but I found myself lacking it. My father extended his hand as I drew near, yet his gaze remained fixed on the doctor. "It certainly is very odd," he murmured. "I do not fully comprehend it. Laura, come here, dear; listen carefully to Doctor Spielsberg and compose yourself."

"You mentioned a sensation akin to two needles piercing your skin, located somewhere around your neck, during the night of your first terrifying dream," the doctor asked. "Is there still any soreness?"

"None at all," I replied.

"Can you indicate with your finger approximately where you believe this occurred?"

"Very little below my throat—here," I answered, pointing to the area covered by my morning gown.

"Now you can verify it yourself," the doctor stated. "You will not mind your father lowering your dress just a fraction. It is necessary to detect a symptom of the condition under which you have been suffering." I acquiesced. The exposure revealed only an inch or two beneath the edge of my collar.

"Good heavens!" my father exclaimed, turning pale. "So it is!"

"You see it now with your own eyes," the doctor declared, his voice carrying a gloomy triumph.

"What is it?" I cried, fear beginning to grip me.

"Nothing, my dear young lady, but a small blue spot, roughly the size of the tip of your little finger," he explained, turning to my father. "The question now is, what course of action is best? Is there any danger?" I urged, trembling with trepidation.

"I trust not, my dear," the doctor answered calmly. "I see no reason why you should not recover. Indeed, I see no reason why you should not begin improving immediately. That is precisely where the sensation of strangulation originates, is it not?"

"Yes," I confirmed.

"And recall clearly—that same point served as the center of that thrill you described moments ago, like the current of a cold stream rushing against you?"

"It may have been; I think it was."

"Aha," he added, nodding toward my father. "Shall I mention this to Madame?"

"Certainly," my father agreed.

He summoned Madame and instructed her, "I find my young friend here far from well. While it likely will not prove of great consequence, certain steps must be taken, which I shall explain later. For the present, Madame, you will be so kind as not to allow Miss Laura to be alone for even a moment. That is the only directive I require at this juncture. It is indispensable."

"We may rely upon your kindness, Madame, I know," my father added.

Madame responded with eager assurance. Then, addressing me, he said, "And you, dear Laura, I know you will heed the doctor's instructions."

"The doctor has informed me that he intends to consult regarding another patient whose symptoms slightly resemble those of my daughter," my father continued. "Her case is much milder in degree, but I believe identical in nature. She is a young lady staying with us—a guest. Since you are scheduled to pass this way again this evening, you cannot do better than join us for supper here, allowing you to examine her. She does not descend until the afternoon."

"I thank you," the doctor replied.

"I shall meet you here at approximately seven this evening," he concluded.

They reiterated their final instructions to me and Madame. With these parting charges, my father departed, walking out alongside the doctor. I watched them pace together up and down the grassy platform between the road and the moat, clearly absorbed in earnest conversation. The doctor did not return to the house. Instead, I observed him mounting his horse, taking his leave, and riding eastward through the forest.

Nearly simultaneously, I saw a man arrive from Dranfield bearing letters. He dismounted and handed the bag to my father. Meanwhile, Madame and I remained lost in conjecture regarding the singular and urgent directives imposed by the doctor and my father. As Madame later confessed to me, she feared the doctor anticipated a sudden seizure, believing that without immediate assistance, I might either perish in a fit or suffer severe injury. Such an interpretation did not occur to me; indeed, I suspected, perhaps fortunately for my nerves, that the arrangement was prescribed merely to secure a companion who would prevent me from engaging in excessive exercise, consuming unripe fruit, or indulging in the myriad foolish habits attributed to youth.

About half an hour after my father returned, holding a letter in his hand, he announced, "This correspondence was delayed; it is from General Spielsdorf. He might have arrived yesterday, he may not come until tomorrow, or he may be here today." He placed the open letter into my hands, yet his demeanor offered no sign of pleasure. Unlike his usual warmth when welcoming a guest, particularly one so beloved as the General, he appeared distinctly displeased. There was plainly something weighing heavily on his mind that he chose not to disclose.

"Papa, darling, will you tell me this?" I implored, suddenly placing my hand on his arm and gazing imploringly into his face.

"Perhaps," he answered, smoothing my hair caressingly over my eyes. "Does the doctor consider me very ill?"

"No, dear," he replied, his tone somewhat dry. "He believes that, if the correct measures are taken, you will be completely well again, or at least on the high road to full recovery within a day or two."

"I wish our good friend, the General, had chosen any other time," he continued. "That is, I wish you had been perfectly well to receive him."

"But do tell me, Papa," I persisted, "what does he think is wrong with me?"

"Nothing," he snapped, displaying more irritation than I had ever witnessed before. Seeing that I looked wounded, he presumably realized his harshness and kissed me, adding, "You shall know everything in a day or two; that is, all that I know. In the meantime, do not trouble your head about it."

He turned to leave the room but returned before I could finish pondering the oddity of the situation. His purpose was merely to announce that he was departing for Karnstein and had ordered the carriage to be ready at noon. He intended for both Madame and me to accompany him. He was going to visit a priest residing near those picturesque grounds on business, and since Carmilla had never visited them, she could follow once she descended, accompanied by Mademoiselle, who would prepare provisions for what you might call a picnic, to be laid out in the ruined castle.

At noon, accordingly, I was prepared. Shortly thereafter, my father, Madame, and I set out on our projected drive. Crossing the drawbridge, we turned right and followed the road over the steep Gothic bridge, heading westward toward the deserted village and the ruined castle of Karnstein. No sylvan drive could be imagined more picturesque. The terrain broke into gentle hills and hollows, all draped in beautiful woodland, utterly devoid of the comparative formality imposed by artificial planting, cultivation, and pruning. The irregularities of the land frequently diverted the road from its straight course, causing it to wind beautifully around the flanks of broken hollows and the steeper slopes of the hills, traversing varieties of ground almost inexhaustible.

It had been roughly ten months since we last encountered him, yet that span of time had wrought an alteration akin to years upon his visage. He had wasted away; a heavy cloak of gloom and anxious foreboding had supplanted the cordial serenity that once defined his countenance. His dark blue eyes, perpetually sharp and penetrating, now burned with a sterner, harder light beneath his shaggy grey brows. This was no transformation born merely of sorrow; darker, angrier passions seemed to have carved these lines into his face.

We had not driven far before the General began to speak, employing his customary soldier's directness to discuss the "bereavement"—as he termed it—that he had suffered through the death of his beloved niece and ward. Suddenly, his composure shattered. In a tone of intense bitterness and fury, he launched into an invective against the "hellish arts" to which she had succumbed. With more exasperation than piety, he lamented how Heaven could possibly tolerate such a monstrous indulgence of the lusts and malignity of hell itself.

My father, perceiving immediately that something profoundly extraordinary had befallen the man, asked him, if the pain allowed, to detail the circumstances that warranted such vehement language.

"I should recount it all with pleasure," the General replied, "but you would not believe me."

"Why not?" my father pressed gently.

"Because," he answered testily, "you believe in nothing save what conforms to your own prejudices and illusions. I recall when I was like you, but I have learned better."

"Test me," my father said calmly. "I am no such dogmatist as you imagine. Furthermore, I know full well that you generally demand proof for your convictions; thus, I am strongly predisposed to respect your conclusions."

"You are correct in assuming I have not been led lightly into a belief in the marvelous—for what I have witnessed *is* marvelous—and I have been compelled by extraordinary evidence to credit that which runs diametrically opposed to every theory I hold. I have been made the dupe of a preternatural conspiracy."

Despite his confident assertions regarding the General's insight, I observed my father cast a glance toward him that, in my opinion, carried a marked suspicion of his sanity. Fortunately, the General failed to notice it. He stared gloomily and curiously into the glades and vistas of the woods unfolding before our carriage.

"You are heading to the Ruins of Karnstein?" he asked abruptly.

"Yes, it seems a fortunate coincidence," my father replied. "In fact, I was just about to request that you accompany me to inspect them. I have a specific objective in mind. Is there not a ruined chapel there, surrounded by numerous tombs belonging to that extinct family?"

"There are indeed—highly interesting sites," my father confirmed. "Do you intend to claim the title and estates?"

My father spoke this gaily, but the General appeared oblivious to the jest, failing to register the polite smile or chuckle that courtesy demands of a friend. Instead, he looked grave, bordering on fierce, lost in rumination over a matter that stirred both his rage and his horror.

"Something vastly different," he growled. "I intend to unearth some of those fine people. May God's blessing guide me, I aim to accomplish a pious sacrilege here, one that shall rid our earth of certain monsters and allow honest folk to sleep undisturbed in their beds, free from the threat of murderers. I possess strange tales to tell you, my dear friend, stories that I myself would have scoffed at as impossible mere months ago."

My father studied him again, but this time not with suspicion, but with an expression of keen intelligence and deep alarm.

"The House of Karnstein," my father stated firmly, "has been extinct for generations—a century at least. My late wife was maternally descended from the Karnsteins, yet the name and title have vanished long ago. The castle lies in ruins; the very village is deserted. It has been fifty years since a single wisp of smoke rose from its chimneys; not a roof remains standing."

"That is entirely accurate," the General conceded. "I have heard much about this since our last meeting; much that will astonish you. However, I believe it best to relate everything in the chronological order of events," he continued. "You saw my dear ward—my child, if I may call her. No creature could ever have been more beautiful, and only three months prior, none more blooming."

"Yes, poor soul!" my father exclaimed. "When I last saw her, she was certainly quite lovely. I was grieved and shocked beyond words, my dear friend; I knew precisely what a devastating blow this must have been to you."

He took the General's hand, and they exchanged a warm, sympathetic pressure. Tears welled in the old soldier's eyes, unashamed and unrestrained.

"We have been very old friends," the General whispered, his voice thick with emotion. "I knew you would feel for me, childless as I am. She had become an object of profound interest to me, and she repaid my care with an affection that brightened my home and made my life happy. All of that is gone. The years remaining to me on this earth may not be many; but by God's mercy, I hope to accomplish a service to mankind before I pass, and to subserve the vengeance of Heaven upon the fiends who murdered my poor child in the spring of her hopes and beauty!"

"You mentioned earlier that you intended to relate everything exactly as it occurred," my father reminded him softly. "Please, go on; I assure you, it is not mere curiosity that drives me."

By this time, we had arrived at the junction where the Drunstall road, by which the General had approached, diverged from the path leading toward Karnstein.

"How far is it to the ruins?" the General inquired, peering anxiously ahead.

"Approximately half a league," my father answered.

"Then pray, let us hear the story you were so gracious as to promise."

With all my heart," the General said, his voice strained with effort. After a brief pause to gather his thoughts, he began one of the strangest tales I have ever heard. "My dearest child was looking forward with immense joy to the visit you had so graciously arranged for her to meet your charming daughter." He offered me a gallant yet deeply melancholy bow. "Meanwhile, we received an invitation from my old friend, Count Carlsfeld, whose estate lies roughly six leagues across from Karnstein. We were to attend the series of festivities he hosted in honor of his illustrious guest, Grand Duke Charles."

"Yes; and I believe they were truly splendid," my father interjected. "Absolutely princely. Yet his hospitality knows no bounds; it is quite regal. He possesses something akin to Aladdin's lamp. That fateful night, from which my sorrow originates, was dedicated to a magnificent masquerade. The grounds were thrown open, the trees draped in colored lanterns. There was a display of fireworks that Paris itself had never witnessed. And the music... music, you understand, is my greatest weakness. Such ravishing melodies! Perhaps the finest instrumental ensemble in the world, alongside the most talented singers gathered from every great opera house in Europe. As you wandered through these fantastically illuminated grounds, the moonlight casting a rosy glow from the château's endless rows of windows, you would suddenly hear those enchanting voices drifting from the silence of some grove or rising from boats upon the lake. In that moment, looking and listening, I felt myself transported back into the romance and poetry of my early youth."

"When the fireworks concluded and the ball began, we returned to the noble suites of rooms prepared for the dancers. A masked ball is a beautiful sight in itself, but I had never before witnessed such a brilliantly spectacular affair. It was an assembly of the highest aristocracy. I was, practically, the only 'nobody' present.

"My dear child looked absolutely radiant. She wore no mask. Her excitement and delight added an indescribable charm to her features, which were already lovely. I noticed a young lady dressed magnificently, yet wearing a mask, who appeared to be observing my ward with extraordinary intensity. I had seen her earlier in the great hall, and again, for several minutes, walking near us beneath the castle windows, similarly engaged. This lady, also masked, was richly and gravely attired, possessing a stately air reminiscent of someone of high rank, accompanied by a chaperone. Had the young lady not concealed her face behind a mask, I could certainly have been far more certain regarding whether she was truly watching my poor darling. Now, however, I am fully convinced that she was.

"We had moved into one of the salons. My poor child had been dancing and was now resting in one of the chairs near the door; I stood close by. The two ladies I had mentioned approached; the younger one took the seat next to my ward, while her companion stood beside me, addressing herself to her charge in a low tone for a short time.

"Taking advantage of the anonymity granted by her mask, she turned to me, speaking in the tone of an old friend and using my own name, opening a conversation that greatly piqued my curiosity. She referenced numerous scenes where we had met—at Court, at distinguished households. She alluded to minor incidents I had long ceased to recall, yet which, I discovered, had merely lain dormant in my memory until her touch brought them vividly back to life.

"I grew increasingly desperate to identify her, moment by moment. She deflected my attempts to uncover her identity with remarkable skill and pleasantness. The depth of her knowledge concerning various passages of my life seemed almost inexplicable, and she appeared to derive a natural pleasure in frustrating my curiosity, watching me flounder from one conjecture to another in my eager perplexity.

"In the meantime, the young lady, whom her mother occasionally addressed by the peculiar name of Millarca, had, with equal ease and grace, engaged my ward in conversation. She introduced herself by stating that her mother was a very old acquaintance of mine. She spoke of the agreeable audacity that a mask permits; she conversed like a friend, admiring her dress and subtly praising her beauty. She amused her with witty critiques of the crowd filling the ballroom and laughed at my poor child's jests. When she chose, she was incredibly witty and lively. Eventually, they became fast friends, and the young stranger lowered her mask, revealing a remarkably beautiful face. Neither I nor my dear child had ever seen it before. Though entirely new to us, the features were so engaging and lovely that it was impossible not to feel their powerful attraction. My poor girl fell instantly and completely under its spell. I have rarely seen anyone so captivated at first sight, unless perhaps it was the stranger herself, who seemed to have lost her heart entirely.

"While this occurred, taking advantage of the liberties permitted by a masquerade, I directed several questions to the elder lady.

"'You have utterly puzzled me,' I said, laughing. 'Is that not enough? Will you not now consent to stand on equal footing and do me the kindness of removing your mask?'

'"Can any request be more unreasonable?' she replied. 'To ask a lady to surrender an advantage! Besides, how do you know you would recognize me? Years bring change.'

'"As you see,' I answered with a bow—and I suppose a somewhat melancholy little laugh.

'"As philosophers tell us,' she countered. 'And how do you know that seeing my face would assist you?'

'"I shall take my chances for that,' I responded. 'It is futile to try to pass yourself off as an old woman; your figure betrays you.'

'"Nevertheless, years have passed since I last saw you, and rather since you saw me, for that is what I am considering. Millarca there is my daughter; therefore, I cannot be considered young, even in the eyes of those whom time has taught to be indulgent. Moreover, I may not wish to be compared with what you remember me to be. You possess no mask to remove. You can offer me nothing in exchange.'

'"My petition appeals to your pity; remove it.'

'"And mine appeals to yours; let it remain where it is,' she retorted.

'"Very well, then, at least you will tell me if you are French or German; you speak both languages so perfectly.'

'"I do not think I shall reveal that, General; you seem to be planning a surprise, meditating a specific angle of attack.'

'"At all events, you cannot deny this,' I insisted, 'that being honored by your permission to converse, I ought to know how to address you. Shall I say Madame la Comtesse?'

"She laughed, and undoubtedly would have met me with another evasion—if, indeed, I can regard any occurrence in an interview, where every circumstance appears prearranged, as I now suspect, executed with profound cunning and liable only to modification by accident.

'"As to that,' she began, but was cut off almost immediately by a gentleman dressed in black. He looked particularly elegant and distinguished, save for one drawback: his face possessed the most deadly pallor I have ever witnessed, save perhaps in death. He wore no costume of disguise, only the plain evening attire of a gentleman. Without a smile, yet with a courtly and unusually low bow, he declared:

'"Will Madame la Comtesse permit me to say a very few words which may interest her?'

"The lady turned swiftly to him, touching her lip in a token of silence. Then she said to me, 'Keep my place for me, General; I shall return when I have spoken a few words.'

"With this playful injunction, she stepped aside with the gentleman in black, talking for several minutes with apparent earnestness. They then walked away slowly together into the throng, and I lost sight of them for a few moments.

"I spent the interval frantically trying to deduce the identity of the lady who remembered me so fondly. I considered turning around to join the conversation between my pretty ward and the Countess's daughter, hoping that by the time she returned, I might have her name, title, château, and estates memorized at my fingertips. But just then, she returned, accompanied by the pale man in black, who stated:

'"I shall return and inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage arrives at the door.'

"He withdrew with a bow."

"Then we are to lose Madame la Comtesse, though I trust only for a brief interval," I replied, bowing low.

"It may be merely a few hours, or it could stretch to several weeks," she answered. "His sudden address to me was most unfortunate. Do you recognize me?"

"I assure you, I do not," I stated firmly.

"You shall know me," she declared, "but not at this precise moment. We are far older and deeper friends than you likely suspect. I cannot reveal my identity yet. In three weeks' time, I shall pass your magnificent château, regarding which I have already made inquiries. Then I shall drop in for an hour or two to renew a friendship I cherish with countless fond memories. Just now, however, news has struck me like a thunderbolt. I must depart immediately, traveling a circuitous route of nearly a hundred miles with all possible speed. My difficulties multiply daily. Only the strict reserve I maintain concerning my name deters me from making a singular request of you. My poor child has not fully regained her strength. During a hunt she attended, her horse stumbled beneath her; her nerves have not yet recovered from the shock, and our physician insists she must not exert herself for some time. Consequently, we arrived here via very gentle stages—scarcely six leagues per day. Now, I must journey day and night on a mission of life and death—the gravity of which I shall explain to you upon our meeting, hopefully within a few weeks, without further concealment."

She proceeded to lay out her petition, delivered in the tone of someone granting a boon rather than asking for one. Yet, this was merely mannerism, seemingly unconscious. In substance, her plea was profoundly humble: she simply asked that I agree to take charge of her daughter during her absence.

Considering all circumstances, this was a strange, indeed audacious, request. She effectively disarmed my hesitation by acknowledging every argument against it and casting herself entirely upon my chivalry. Simultaneously, fate seemed to conspire; my own ward stepped to my side and, in a whisper, begged me to invite her new acquaintance, Millarca, to visit. Having just tested the waters, the girl believed that if her mother permitted it, she would delight greatly in the company.

Had I possessed a moment's respite, I would have advised patience, waiting at least until we knew their true identities. But there was no time to deliberate. Assailed simultaneously by both women—and compelled by the refined beauty of the young lady, whose face held an irresistible charm, coupled with the evident elegance and fiery spirit of high birth—I succumbed completely. Overwhelmed, I readily accepted the burden of caring for the young woman, whom her mother named Millarca.

The Countess signaled to her daughter, who listened with solemn attention as her mother recounted, in broad strokes, the sudden summons she had received and the arrangements made for her under my guardianship, emphasizing that I was one of her earliest and most cherished friends. Naturally, I offered the appropriate sentiments, only to realize upon reflection that I had placed myself in a position I did not wholly welcome.

The gentleman dressed in black returned, conducting the lady from the room with extreme ceremony. His demeanor impressed upon me the conviction that the Countess possessed an importance far exceeding her modest title suggested. Her final instruction to me was absolute: no attempt was to be made to uncover more about her than I had already surmised until her return. Our distinguished host, her current guest, understood her reasons.

"But here," she whispered, "neither I nor my daughter could safely remain longer than a single day. I removed my mask imprudently a moment ago, about an hour past, and, too late, I feared you saw me. Thus, I resolved to seize this opportunity to speak briefly with you. Had I discovered you had witnessed my face, I would have thrown myself upon your high sense of honor to guard my secret for weeks. Since you apparently did not see me, I am content; yet, should you now suspect, or later reflect and suspect, who I truly am, I entrust myself entirely to your honor. My daughter will adhere to the same secrecy, and I am certain you will, from time to time, remind her lest she thoughtlessly betray it."

She murmured a few words to her daughter, kissed her hurriedly twice, and departed, escorted by the pale gentleman in black, vanishing into the throng.

"In the next room," Millarca said softly, "there is a window overlooking the hall entrance. I wish to bid farewell to my mother and offer her a kiss."

We naturally acquiesced and accompanied her to the glass. Peering outward, we spotted a handsome, old-fashioned carriage surrounded by a retinue of couriers and footmen. There stood the slender figure of the pale gentleman in black, holding a thick velvet cloak. He draped it around her shoulders, pulling the hood over her head. She nodded to him, gently touching his hand with hers. He bowed deeply and repeatedly as the carriage doors closed and the vehicle began to roll away.

"She is gone," Millarca sighed.

"She is gone," I repeated to myself—for the first time, amidst the frantic minutes following my consent—regretting the foolishness of my decision.

"She did not look up," the young lady said, her voice plaintive.

"The Countess may have removed her mask and chosen not to display her face," I reasoned, "and she could not possibly know you were watching from the window."

She sighed again and gazed into my eyes. She was so radiant that my resolve crumbled. I felt remorse for my fleeting moment of coldness toward hospitality and determined to make amends for the unspoken rudeness of my reception.

Replacing her mask, the young lady joined my ward in persuading me to return to the grounds, where the concert was soon to resume. We complied, strolling along the terrace beneath the castle windows. Millarca grew increasingly intimate with us, amusing us with vivid descriptions and tales of many of the great figures we encountered on the terrace. I found her more delightful by the minute. Her gossip, devoid of malice, was exceedingly entertaining to me, who had been so long absent from the grand world. I imagined the vibrant life she would bring to our occasionally solitary evenings at home.

The ball did not conclude until the morning sun had nearly breached the horizon. The Grand Duke danced until then, and loyal subjects dared neither depart nor retire to bed.

As we emerged from a densely packed salon, my ward asked me where Millarca had vanished. I assumed she had remained beside her, and the girl presumed she was by my side. In truth, we had lost her.

Every effort I made to locate her proved futile. I feared she had mistaken others for her new companions in the confusion of a brief separation and, possibly, pursued them into the vast grounds opened to the guests, thereby losing her way.

With growing clarity, I recognized a fresh folly in undertaking the care of a young lady without even knowing her name. Bound by promises regarding the reasons for my acceptance—which I knew nothing of—I could not even direct my inquiries by identifying the missing girl as the daughter of the Countess who had departed mere hours prior.

Daybreak arrived. It was not until nearly two o'clock the following afternoon that we heard word of my missing charge.

About that time, a servant knocked at my niece's door to report that he had been urgently requested by a distressed young lady to ascertain where she might find General Baron Spielsdorf and his daughter, the young woman who had entrusted her care to them.

There could be no doubt, despite minor inaccuracies, that our young friend had reappeared; and so she had. Oh, that we had lost her forever!

She related a tale to explain her failure to locate us for so long. Very late, she claimed, she had reached the housekeeper's bedroom in despair of finding us, only to fall into a deep sleep. Though lengthy, this slumber had barely restored her strength after the exhausting festivities of the ball.

That day, Millarca returned with us. I was only too glad, ultimately, to have secured such a charming companion for my dear girl.

Soon, however, distinct drawbacks revealed themselves. First, Millarca suffered from profound lethargy—a lingering weakness from her recent illness—that kept her confined to her chamber until well into the afternoon. Furthermore, an unsettling discovery was made: despite locking her door from the inside and keeping the key hidden until the maid arrived to assist with her toilette, she was frequently found missing from her room. This occurred in the deepest hours of the morning and at other unannounced times throughout the day. Witnesses from the windows of the *schloss* reported seeing her, bathed in the pale grey light of dawn, wandering eastward through the trees with the vacant gaze of someone in a trance. It seemed clear she walked in her sleep. Yet, this explanation failed to resolve the mystery. How could she exit a room secured from within? How could she leave the house entirely without unlocking doors or raising windows?

Amidst these perplexities, a far more urgent dread seized me. My dear child's complexion faded, and her health deteriorated in a manner both mysterious and horrifying. Her terror escalated from appalling nightmares to visions of a specter—sometimes resembling Millarca herself, at others taking the form of a beast—pacing vaguely around the foot of her bed. Finally, physical sensations began to manifest. She described a peculiar, chilling flow against her breast, akin to an icy stream. Later, she felt sharp pains as if pierced by large needles just below her throat. Nights followed marked by a gradual, convulsive sensation of strangulation, culminating in total unconsciousness.

I listened intently to every word the kindly old General spoke, for we had driven onto the short grass flanking the road leading toward the roofless village, which had emitted no chimney smoke for over fifty years. Imagine my horror hearing my own symptoms so precisely mirrored in the sufferings of the young girl who, had fate spared her, would have been visiting my father's château at that very moment. Consider further my shock as he detailed habits and eerie peculiarities that belonged unmistakably to our beautiful guest, Carmilla!

Suddenly, a vista opened in the forest. We descended beneath the chimneys and gables of the ruined settlement, while the towers and battlements of the dismantled castle, crowned by gigantic trees, loomed above us from a slight eminence. In a state of frightened reverie, I alighted from the carriage. Silence fell between us, heavy with thought, as we climbed the ascent and entered the spacious chambers, winding staircases, and shadowed corridors of the castle.

"And this was once the palatial residence of the Karnsteins!" the old General remarked, gazing from a high window across the vast, undulating expanse of the forest. "It was a cursed lineage, and here their bloodstained history was written. It is a tragedy that, even in death, they must continue to plague humanity with their atrocious lusts. Down there lies the chapel of the Karnsteins." He gestured toward the grey walls of the Gothic structure, partially obscured by foliage halfway down the steep slope. "I hear the axe of a woodman busy among the surrounding trees. Perhaps he holds the information I seek, or can guide us to the grave of Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein. These rustics often preserve the local legends of great families whose stories vanish among the wealthy and titled as soon as the line dies out."

"We possess a portrait of Mircalla, the Countess Karnstein, at home. Would you care to see it?" my father asked.

"Time enough, dear friend," the General replied. "I suspect I have already seen the original. One motive that brought me to you sooner than planned was to explore the chapel we are now approaching."

"What! To see Countess Mircalla?" my father exclaimed. "She has been dead for more than a century!"

"Not so dead as you might imagine, I am told," the General answered.

"I confess, General, you utterly puzzle me," my father replied, fixing him with a look that briefly betrayed the suspicion I had sensed before. Yet, though anger and hatred flickered in the old man's demeanor, there was no madness in his eyes.

"There remains but one purpose that interests me during the few years left to me on this earth," he declared as we passed beneath the heavy archway of the Gothic church—"to exact upon her the vengeance which, thank God, a mortal arm can still accomplish."

"What vengeance do you mean?" my father asked, his voice rising in astonishment.

"I mean to decapitate the monster," he answered, his face flushing fiercely. He stamped his foot, sending a mournful echo through the hollow ruins, and raised his clenched fist as if gripping an axe handle, shaking it violently in the air.

"What?" my father gasped, bewildered beyond measure.

"To sever her head."

"Cut her head off!"

"Aye! With a hatchet, a spade, or any tool capable of cleaving her murderous throat. You shall hear," he trembled with rage. Hurrying forward, he commanded, "That beam will serve as a seat; your daughter is weary. Let her sit, and I shall conclude my dreadful tale in a few sentences."

The squared block of wood lying on the grass-choked pavement of the chapel served as a bench, and I gratefully sat down. Meanwhile, the General hailed the woodman, who had been clearing branches leaning against the ancient walls. Armed with his axe, the sturdy old man stood before us. He knew nothing of these specific monuments. However, he mentioned an elderly ranger currently staying with the priest, two miles away, who could identify every relic of the old Karnstein family. For a small fee, he promised to fetch the ranger and return within half an hour if we lent him one of our horses.

"How long have you worked in this forest?" my father asked the old man.

"I have been a woodman here," he replied in his rough dialect, "under the forester all my life, as did my father before me, and so on for as many generations as I can count. I could show you the very house in this village where my ancestors lived."

"Why was the village abandoned?" the General inquired.

"It was plagued by revenants, sir," the woodman explained. "Several were tracked to their graves, identified by the customary signs, and extinguished by the standard methods: decapitation, the stake, and fire. Yet, many villagers perished in the process."

"But after all these lawful proceedings," he continued, "after so many graves were opened and so many vampires stripped of their horrific animation, the village was not relieved. Then, a Moravian nobleman traveling this way heard of the trouble. Being skilled in such matters, as many in his homeland are, he offered to liberate the village from its tormentor. On a night of bright moonlight, shortly after sunset, he ascended the chapel towers, from where he could clearly survey the churchyard below. Watching from this vantage, he spotted the vampire emerging from its grave, placing the linen shroud in which it had been wrapped nearby, before gliding toward the village to haunt its inhabitants."

"The stranger observed everything," he recounted. "He descended, retrieved the vampire's linens, and carried them to the tower's summit, climbing back up. When the vampire returned from its prowling, confused and missing its clothes, it screamed furiously at the Moravian atop the tower. The nobleman beckoned it to ascend and claim them. Accepting the invitation, the vampire began to climb. As soon as it reached the battlements, the Moravian struck its skull in two with a single blow of his sword, hurling the corpse down into the churchyard. Descending the spiral stairs himself, the stranger followed, severed the head, and the next day delivered both head and body to the villagers, who duly impaled and burned them."

"This Moravian nobleman held authority from the head of the family to remove the tomb of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. He did so effectively, and within a short while, the site was completely forgotten."

"Can you indicate where it stood?" the General asked eagerly.

The forester shook his head and smiled. "No living soul could tell you that now," he said. "Besides, rumors say her body was removed, yet no one is certain of that either."

Having spoken, and pressed for time, he dropped his axe and departed, leaving us alone to listen to the remainder of the General's extraordinary narrative.

"My beloved child," he continued, his voice trembling with suppressed anguish, "was deteriorating with alarming speed. The physician attending her had achieved absolutely nothing; I had initially believed her ailment to be purely physical. Seeing my terror, he proposed a consultation. I summoned a far more distinguished colleague from Graz. Days passed before his arrival. He was a man of profound piety, immense learning, and genuine kindness. After examining my poor ward, the two doctors retired to my library to deliberate. From the adjoining room, where I waited for their summons, I heard their voices rising into something far sharper than a mere academic debate. I knocked and entered. I found the elder physician from Graz stubbornly clinging to his theory, while his rival dismantled it with undisguised mockery, punctuated by bursts of raucous laughter. This undignified spectacle ceased the moment I stepped inside.

"'Sir,' declared my first physician, 'my learned brother appears to believe you require a conjurer, not a doctor.'

"'Pardon me,' replied the old physician from Graz, his face clouded with displeasure. 'I shall reserve my full exposition of this case for another time. I mourn, Monsieur le General, that despite my skill and science, I am powerless to assist. Before I depart, allow me to offer a suggestion.'

"He appeared deep in thought, seated himself at a table, and began to write. Overwhelmed by disappointment, I bowed and prepared to withdraw. As I turned, the second doctor pointed over his shoulder toward his companion, who was scribbling furiously, then shrugged and tapped his temple with a significant gesture.

"This consultation left me exactly where I had begun: utterly helpless. I wandered into the gardens, nearly driven to distraction. Within ten or fifteen minutes, the physician from Graz overtook me. He apologized for following but insisted he could not leave without speaking further. He assured me he could not be mistaken; no natural disease presented such symptoms. Death was imminent, though perhaps a day—or maybe two—of life remained. If the fatal seizure could be halted immediately with extreme caution, her strength might yet return. But everything now hung precariously on the brink of the irreversible. A single more violent attack could snuff out the last flicker of vitality, which was fading with every passing moment.

"'What is the nature of this seizure?' I begged.

"'I have detailed everything fully in this note,' he said, pressing the paper into my hands. 'It rests on the strict condition that you summon the nearest clergyman and open this letter only in his presence. Do not read it alone; otherwise, you will dismiss it as nonsense, and it is a matter of life and death. Should the priest fail you, then—and only then—may you read it.'

"Before departing, he asked if I wished to meet a scholar uniquely versed in the very subject that would likely haunt my thoughts after reading his missive. He urged me earnestly to invite him to visit. Thus, he left.

"The priest was absent, so I read the letter in solitude. At another time, or in another circumstance, it might have provoked my scorn. Yet, into what depths of superstition will not desperate parents plunge when all conventional remedies fail and a beloved life hangs in the balance?

"You would surely say nothing could be more absurd than the learned man's epistle. It was monstrous enough to warrant confinement in an asylum. He claimed the patient was being visited by a vampire! The punctures she described near her throat, he insisted, were the work of those two long, thin, razor-sharp fangs peculiar to such creatures. Furthermore, he noted the unmistakable presence of the small, livid mark universally attributed to the demon's lips. Every symptom recounted by the sufferer matched the records of every documented vampiric visitation perfectly.

"Skeptical as I was regarding the existence of such portents, the good doctor's supernatural theory offered, in my mind, merely another example of brilliant intellect twisted by a singular delusion. Yet, consumed by misery, I chose to act upon his instructions rather than risk doing nothing.

"I hid myself in the dark dressing room opening onto the patient's chamber, where a candle burned dimly. I watched until she drifted into a deep sleep. Standing at the door, peering through a narrow crack, my sword laid upon the table as directed, I waited. Just past midnight, I saw a large, indistinct black shape crawl across the foot of the bed. It moved swiftly upward, swelling in an instant into a great, throbbing mass against the poor girl's throat.

"For several moments, I stood frozen in petrification. Then, I lunged forward, sword drawn. The black creature suddenly contracted, recoiling toward the foot of the bed. It glided over the mattress and landed on the floor, roughly a yard below the bed. With a gaze of skulking ferocity and horror fixed upon me, I recognized Millarca. Speculating wildly, I struck instantly. But she stood unharmed near the door. Horror-stricken, I pursued and struck again. She vanished, and my sword shattered against the doorframe into splinters.

"I cannot recount the entirety of that dreadful night. The entire household erupted into chaos. The spectral Millarca was gone. But her victim was dying rapidly, and before the dawn broke, she passed away."

The old General was visibly shaken. We dared not speak to him. My father walked a short distance away and began tracing the inscriptions on the tombstones. Absorbed in his task, he strolled into the door of a side chapel to pursue his studies. The General leaned against the wall, drying his eyes with a heavy sigh. Relief washed over me upon hearing the voices of Carmilla and Madame approaching. Their sounds faded away.

In this solitude, having just listened to such a bizarre tale—one connected to the great and titled dead whose monuments crumbled among dust and ivy around us, and where every detail resonated terrifyingly with my own mysterious affliction—a profound horror began to creep over me. My heart sank as I feared my friends would not soon enter to disrupt this triste and ominous scene. The old General's eyes were fixed on the ground as he leaned his hand against the base of a shattered monument.

Under a narrow, arched doorway, surmounted by one of those demonic grotesques favored by the cynical, ghastly imagination of old Gothic carvers, I very gladly beheld the beautiful face and figure of Carmilla entering the shadowy chapel. I was just about to rise and greet her with a nod and a smile, responding to her peculiarly engaging expression, when, with a sudden cry, the old man beside me seized a woodcutter's hatchet and surged forward. Upon seeing him, a brutish transformation seized her features. It was an instantaneous, horrific metamorphosis as she took a crouching step backward. Before I could even utter a scream, he struck her with all his force. But she ducked beneath the blow, unscathed, and seized his wrist in her tiny grasp. He struggled briefly to free his arm, but his fingers loosened; the axe clattered to the ground, and the girl was gone. He staggered against the wall. His grey hair stood erect on his head, and moisture shimmered over his face as if he stood on the verge of death. The frightful scene had unfolded in a heartbeat.

The first memory I retained afterward was of Madame standing before me, impatiently repeating the question, "Where is Mademoiselle Carmilla?"

"At length," I answered, "I do not know—I cannot tell—she went *there*," pointing to the door through which Madame had just entered, "only a minute or two ago."

"But I have been standing in the passage ever since Mademoiselle Carmilla entered, and she has not returned!" She then began calling "Carmilla!" through every door, corridor, and window, but received no answer.

"She called herself Carmilla?" asked the General, still agitated.

"Carmilla, yes," I replied.

"Aye," he said. "That is Millarca. That is the same person who long ago was known as Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Depart from this accursed ground, my poor child, as quickly as possible. Drive to the clergyman's house and remain there until we arrive. Begone! May you never behold Carmilla again; you will not find her here."

As I spoke, a figure emerged from the chapel doors—the same archway through which Carmilla had vanished. He was a strikingly peculiar man, towering yet narrow-chested, hunched under high, sloping shoulders. Clad entirely in black, his skin was a weathered brown, etched with deep, drying furrows. Upon his head sat an oddly shaped hat adorned with a broad leaf, while long, grizzled hair cascaded past his shoulders. Gold-rimmed spectacles perched on his nose, framing a face that wore a perpetual, enigmatic smile. He moved with a slow, shuffling gait, alternating between gazing upward at the sky and bowing low toward the earth. His long, thin arms swung wildly, clad in lanky black gloves far too large for his hands, gesturing in a state of utter abstraction.

"The very man!" cried the General, stepping forward with evident delight. "My dear Baron, what joy to see you! I dared not hope to meet you so soon."

He signaled to my father, who had just returned, and guided the eccentric elder—whom he addressed as the Baron—to join them. Formal introductions were exchanged, and immediately, serious discourse ensued. The stranger produced a rolled sheet of paper from his pocket, unspooling it across the worn surface of a nearby tombstone. Holding a pencil case, he traced imaginary lines across the document, his gaze darting frequently between the paper and specific points within the chapel structure. To me, this appeared to be a schematic plan of the sanctuary. Accompanying his lecture, he occasionally read aloud from a grimy, small book, its yellow pages densely inscribed.

They strolled together down the side aisle, directly opposite my position, conversing intently. Soon, they began pacing out distances, eventually halting before a section of the sidewall. There, they examined the masonry with minute scrutiny, stripping away clinging ivy and tapping the plaster with the ends of their sticks, scraping and knocking rhythmically. Finally, they confirmed the presence of a broad marble tablet bearing carved relief lettering. With the aid of the woodsman, who promptly returned, a monumental inscription and a carved coat of arms were revealed. They belonged to the long-lost monument of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein.

Though I suspected the General was not inclined toward prayer, he nonetheless raised his hands and eyes to the heavens in silent gratitude for several moments.

"Tomorrow," I overheard him declare, "the commissioner shall arrive, and the Inquisition will proceed according to law." Turning to the old man with the gold spectacles, the General grasped his hands warmly. "Baron, how can I repay your service? How can we all thank you? You have liberated this region from a plague that has ravaged its people for over a century. Thank God, the terrible enemy is finally tracked."

My father led the stranger aside, followed closely by the General. I knew they had withdrawn to speak privately regarding my condition, for I watched them exchange furtive glances in my direction as their discussion continued. When my father returned, he embraced me repeatedly, kissing me again and again before guiding me from the chapel.

"It is time to depart," he said gently. "But before we leave, we must summon the good priest, who resides nearby, and persuade him to accompany us to the castle."

Our efforts succeeded. Exhausted beyond words, I was relieved to reach home, yet my satisfaction curdled into dread upon realizing there was no news of Carmilla. No explanation was offered for the chaotic events within the ruined chapel; it was clear my father intended to withhold this secret from me for the present. The sinister silence surrounding Carmilla's absence rendered the memory of the scene even more horrifying.

That night, the arrangements were singular. Two servants and Madame were to remain awake in my chamber, while the clergyman and my father kept vigil in the adjacent dressing room. The priest had performed certain solemn rites earlier that evening, the meaning of which remained obscure to me, as did the rationale behind these extraordinary precautions for my safety during sleep. Only a few days later would the full significance become clear.

With Carmilla gone, my nightly torments ceased abruptly.

You are likely familiar with the appalling superstitions prevalent throughout Upper and Lower Styria, Moravia, Silesia, Turkish Serbia, Poland, and even Russia—the enduring belief in the Vampire. If human testimony, gathered with utmost care and solemnity before countless judicial commissions composed of numerous members selected for their integrity and intellect, holds any weight, then it is difficult to deny—or even doubt—the existence of such a phenomenon. For my part, having witnessed and experienced exactly what I describe, I possess no alternative theory to explain my ordeal save the ancient, well-attested beliefs of my homeland.

The following day, the formal proceedings commenced within the Chapel of Karnstein. The grave of the Countess Mircalla was opened. Both the General and my father recognized their treacherous and beautiful guest in the visage now exposed to the light. Though a century and a half had passed since her burial, her features retained the warmth of life. Her eyes were wide open; no cadaverous odor emanated from the coffin. Two physicians—one official, the other representing the prosecutor—attested to the marvel: a faint yet perceptible respiration and a corresponding heartbeat. Her limbs were perfectly supple, her flesh elastic, and the leaden coffin floated in a pool of blood, in which her body lay submerged to a depth of seven inches.

Here were all the admitted signs and proofs of vampirism. Consequently, in accordance with ancient custom, the body was raised, and a sharp stake was driven through the vampire's heart. She let out a piercing shriek at that precise moment—a sound indistinguishable from the final agony of any living soul. Immediately after, her head was severed, and a torrent of blood gushed forth from the wound. The body and head were then placed upon a pyre of wood, burned to ash, and cast into the river to be carried away. Since that day, the territory has never again suffered the visitations of a vampire.

My father possesses a copy of the Imperial Commission's report, complete with the signatures of all witnesses present, verifying this account. From this official document, I have summarized the details of this final, shocking event.

I attempt to write this account with a steady hand, yet the words tremble on the page. There is no composure in my soul; nothing but your repeated, earnest pleas could have dragged me back to this chair. To revisit these memories is to unstring my nerves anew, reopening wounds that, even years after my deliverance, kept my days and nights shrouded in dread. Solitude, once merely quiet, now feels insupportably terrifying.

Before I close this volume, I must speak of Baron Vordenburg. It was his obscure, antiquated learning that led us to the grave of Countess Mircalla. For years, he lived in Graz, surviving on a meager stipend—the last remnant of his once-princely estates in Upper Styria. His entire existence was consumed by the meticulous study of vampirism, a subject he treated with the gravity of sacred scripture. He knew every treatise by heart: *Magia Posthuma*, *Phlegon de Mirabilibus*, *Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis*, and John Christopher Herenberg's *Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris*. From a vast collection of judicial records, he distilled a system of laws governing the undead—a set of rules that applied either universally or intermittently depending on the creature's specific condition.

Let me correct a common misconception found in popular tales: the deadly pallor attributed to vampires is a theatrical fabrication. In the grave, and when they walk among the living, they possess the vibrant hue of health. Only when exposed to the harsh light within their coffins do they reveal the true signs of their cursed state, just as we witnessed with the late Countess Karnstein. Their ability to vanish from their tombs and return hours later, leaving not a single crack in the earth nor a shift in the linen wrappings, remains an inexplicable mystery. They sustain this amphibious existence through daily slumber in the dark, while their waking lives are fueled by a horrific hunger for fresh blood.

Furthermore, vampires are susceptible to a fascination akin to the intensity of romantic love. Driven by this obsession, they employ infinite patience and cunning to overcome obstacles, refusing to rest until they have sated their appetite and drained the very essence of their chosen victim. In such cases, they approach with the refinement of an epicure, prolonging their torment through the subtle arts of courtship, seemingly seeking a twisted form of consent. However, when faced with an unwilling prey, they revert to brute force, overpowering, strangling, and exhausting their victim in a single, brutal feast.

There are also peculiar limitations to their powers. As you know, Mircalla was bound by a strange constraint: she could only assume names that were perfect anagrams of her own—"Carmilla" or "Millarca"—with not a single letter added or omitted.

My father recounted to the Baron the tale of the Moravian nobleman and the vampire at the Karnstein churchyard. Curious as to how the Baron had located the hidden tomb, the old man's face crinkled into a cryptic smile. He gazed down at his spectacles, fumbling with the case before lifting his eyes again.

"I possess many journals," he began softly. "The most fascinating concerns the visit you mentioned. Tradition often distorts the truth. While he was called a Moravian nobleman due to his residence there, he was actually a native of Upper Styria. In his youth, he was a devoted lover of the beautiful Countess Mircalla. Her premature death cast him into an abyss of grief. Vampires, according to a ghastly law, multiply through a chain reaction: a suicide born of despair may become a vampire; this specter haunts sleepers, causing them to die and transform in the grave. This was the fate of Mircalla, haunted by a demon.

"My ancestor, the original Vordenburg, deduced that suspicion would inevitably fall upon her. Yet, he harbored a profound horror—not of her nature, but of her remains being profaned by a posthumous execution. He believed that expelling a vampire from its dual existence thrust it into a far more terrible state of being. Desperate to save his beloved, he devised a ruse: a fake exhumation followed by the total erasure of her monument.

"As age settled upon him, viewing the world from the vantage point of his declining years, he recoiled in horror at his own deception. He left behind detailed notes tracing the path to the true location, along with a confession of his fraud. Death cut short any further plans he might have had, but his work guided a distant descendant to the beast's lair, saving many souls too late."

Our conversation turned to another hallmark of the undead: the strength of the hand. The Baron recalled how Mircalla's slender fingers gripped the General's wrist like a vice of steel when he raised his axe, leaving a lingering numbness that never fully returned.

That spring, my father took me on a tour of Italy, a journey lasting over a year. Long ago, the immediate terror of our ordeal faded, though the image of Carmilla persists in my mind with shifting facets. Sometimes she appears as the playful, languid beauty I first met; at other times, she transforms into the writhing fiend I glimpsed in the ruined church. Often, I wake from a reverie convinced I hear the light, rhythmic tap of her footsteps against the drawing-room door.
