"Good heavens," I muttered, adjusting my spectacles as if they might somehow clarify the absurdity of the situation. "I suppose I shouldn't bore you with the minutiae of my domestic entanglements, but the circumstances regarding my friend Freddie Meadowes demand a recounting. I confess, my literary faculties are currently dormant; however, I shall engage a professional scribe to polish the manuscript upon completion. For now, suffice it to say that Freddie, a man of considerable charm in his prime, has recently descended into a state resembling a wilted flower left in the sun too long.

When I entered the club that morning, I found him sequestered in a shadowed alcove, staring vacantly at the wallpaper with the intensity of a man awaiting execution. Beside me sat Jimmy Pinkerton, a playwright of sharp intellect and equally sharp tongue. Between us, we interrogated the distraught gentleman until the truth emerged: a romantic catastrophe involving Angela West, his fiancée. The specifics of the dispute remained opaque, though the outcome was clear—she had severed ties with brutal efficiency. No contact, no conversation, not even a perusal of his correspondence.

"I sympathize," I admitted, recalling my own youthful misadventures with Elizabeth Shoolbred, a woman whose rejection of me remains a permanent stain on my autobiographical record. "Change of scenery is the prescription, Freddie. Come to Marvis Bay with me. I've secured a cottage, and Jimmy joins us on the twenty-fourth. We shall form a cozy trio."

"He speaks wisdom," Jimmy interjected. "A change of environment is essential. I recall a similar case: a suitor rejected, departed abroad, only to receive a wire two months later commanding his return. He attempted a reply but forgot the recipient's surname. Silence ensued, yet the damage was already repaired."

Freddie, however, remained immovable, his expression fixed in a mask of profound despondency. Reluctantly, he agreed to accompany me, conceding that Marvis Bay was preferable to remaining in the city.

Marvis Bay, located in Dorsetshire, offers a tranquility that borders on lethargy. Days are spent wading in the surf or gazing at the horizon; evenings involve strolling the shoreline while battling an onslaught of gnats. By nine o'clock, one applies soothing ointment to insect bites and retires to sleep.

Initially, this regimen suited Freddie. Under the moonlight, lulled by the rustling trees, he became a local fixture among the insects. Yet, by the end of the first week, my patience frayed. Without Jimmy's presence, Freddie proved an inadequate companion. His existence oscillated between scowling at the floorboards and attempting to play "The Rosary" on the piano with a single, trembling finger. The performance invariably collapsed after the third bar due to a blown fuse in his neural circuitry, forcing a restart.

One morning, as I returned from a swim, Freddie interrupted my reverie.

"Reggie," he croaked, lifting his gaze. "I've seen her."

"Miss West?" I asked.

"Yes. At the post office. She cut me!"

He resumed his pianoforte assault, missing the transition entirely.

"You brought me here for naught," he insisted. "I must depart."

"Depart?" I countered. "Nonsense. This is precisely where you will recover your composure."

"She ignored me!"

"Be a sport. Make another attempt."

"She looked right through me!"

"Then consider this strategy," I proposed, seizing the initiative. "You must place her under a debt of gratitude. Elicit a timid 'thank you' from her lips."

"But for what?"

"Save her from drowning," I suggested.

"I cannot swim," Freddie replied, highlighting his primary limitation.

He retreated to the piano, leaving me to execute the operation. Stepping onto the sands, I observed a blue dress near the water's edge. It was unmistakably Angela. Seated nearby was an elderly woman reading, and beside her, a stout child constructing a sandcastle. Deduction indicated the child was her cousin. While Freddie might have exploited this familial connection for sentimental leverage, I found the prospect of interacting with such a robust toddler unappealing.

Once the castle was complete, the child grew bored and wandered toward a sweet vendor. Angela followed. I continued my patrol.

Fellows often label me a simpleton, and I accept this designation without protest. However, occasionally, a surge of intellectual brilliance strikes me—a phenomenon rare enough to distinguish me from the average mind. Such a wave hit me as I walked back along the shore. The stout child was now absent, meditating on a jellyfish with a spade. Angela was nowhere to be seen.

In a flash of insight, the solution crystallized. If I abducted the child, creating a scenario of high anxiety for Angela, and then revealed Freddie rescuing the lost infant, her gratitude would inevitably bridge the gap between us. I seized the child and fled.

Upon returning to the cottage, I deposited the screaming toddler in the sitting room. Freddie, initially bewildered, quickly grasped the plan.

"You possess unexpected acumen, Reggie," he conceded. "This scheme holds merit."

He released the child to search the beach for Angela. My spirits soared; knowing Freddie would soon regain his vitality felt akin to inheriting a fortune. Yet, fate intervened. As I relaxed on the veranda, I spotted Freddie approaching, still burdened by the child. His demeanor suggested utter defeat.

"Success?" I asked.

"Yes," he replied, his laughter devoid of mirth. "I found her."

"And?"

Freddie collapsed into a chair, groaning. "This is not her cousin. He is a stranger she encountered on the beach. She had never laid eyes on him prior to today."

"What manner of child is this?"

"I do not know. Good heavens, the ordeal! Fortunately, you will likely spend the next several years in Dartmoor for kidnapping. That is my sole solace; I shall visit through the prison bars."

He recounted the tale, breaking frequently to hurl epithets at me. Angela had listened impassively, then hinted that a future encounter between Freddie and Dr. Cook would result in a legendary duel. Defeated, Freddie retreated with the child, licking his wounds.

"This falls to you," he declared. "If you wish to avoid incarceration, locate the parents and return the boy."

I traversed Marvis Bay like a bloodhound, yet no claimant emerged. The child seemed to exist in a vacuum of parental concern. Only by questioning the sweet vendor did I learn the boy's name was Medwin and his residence was Ocean Rest, Beach Road.

Rushing to the address, I found no answer. Voices echoed from within, but no one appeared. Just as I contemplated battering down the door, a voice hailed me from an upper window.

"Hi!"

I looked up to see a round, pink face framed by grey whiskers peering down.

"Who calls 'Hi'?" I demanded.

"You cannot enter," the face replied. "Hello, is that Tootles?"

"My name is not Tootles, nor do I seek entry," I stated firmly. "Are you Mr. Medwin? I have returned your son."

"I see him. Peep-bo, Tootles! Father sees!"

The face vanished abruptly. Moments later, it reappeared.

"Hi!"

I scrambled the gravel violently.

"Do you reside here?"

"I am staying temporarily."

"What is your name?"

"Pepper. Though—"

"Pepper? Related to Edward Pepper, the colliery magnate?"

"My uncle. But—"

"I knew him well. Poor old Edward. I wish I were with him now."

"I wish you were," I responded.

His countenance brightened. "Most fortunate. We are plagued by mumps. My daughter, Bootles, has contracted the disease. Tootles must remain isolated. We were at a loss regarding his care. Finding you is providential. As an uncle of Edward Pepper, I grant you implicit trust. Take Tootles to your quarters. It is an ideal arrangement. My brother in London arrives within a week to collect him. Until then, he stays with you. Excellent plan. Your wife will adore Tootles."

"I have no wife," I shouted.

The window slammed shut with the force of a germ containment unit sealing a breach.

I exhaled sharply, wiping sweat from my brow. The window reopened instantly.

"Hi!"

A package, weighing approximately a ton, impacted my skull with explosive force.

"Catch it?" the face queried. "Missed it. No matter. Retrieve it from the grocer. Request Bailey's Granulated Breakfast Chips. Tootles consumes them with milk. Ensure you obtain Bailey's."

My resolve shattered. Accepting the absurdity, I took Tootles by the hand and departed. Napoleon's retreat from Moscow paled in comparison to this humiliation.

On the road, we encountered Angela. The sight affected the child profoundly; he pointed and exclaimed, "Wah!"

She halted, smiling. I released the boy, who rushed to her.

"Well, baby?" she cooed, bending down. "Father found you, did he? Your little son and I befriended each other on the beach this morning," she added, addressing me.

This revelation, compounded by the previous encounter with the whiskered lunatic, completely derailed me. Before I could formulate a denial of paternity, she had nodded farewell and proceeded down the path.
