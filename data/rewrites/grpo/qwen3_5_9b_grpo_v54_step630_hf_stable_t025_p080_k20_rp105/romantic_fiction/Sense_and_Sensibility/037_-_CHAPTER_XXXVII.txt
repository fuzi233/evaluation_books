By the end of the fortnight, Mrs. Palmer had recovered sufficiently for her mother to cease her constant attendance, resuming her own domestic routine while visiting her daughter once or twice daily. Upon returning to Berkeley Street, Mrs. Jennings found the Dashwood sisters eager to resume their usual social engagements.

It was on the third or fourth morning after this resettlement that Mrs. Jennings entered the drawing-room, where Elinor sat alone. Her hurried demeanor suggested she possessed news of great import. Seizing the moment, she launched into her revelation.

"Lord! my dear Miss Dashwood, have you heard the news?" she exclaimed. "Something so strange! When I visited Mr. Palmer, I found Charlotte in a panic over the child—it was crying, fretting, covered in pimples. I assured her it was merely the red gum, and the nurse agreed. Yet Charlotte refused to be satisfied, so Mr. Donovan was summoned. Fortunately, he had just arrived from Harley Street. Upon seeing the infant, he confirmed it was nothing but the red gum, and Charlotte was relieved. However, as he departed, I impulsively asked if there were any other news. He immediately adopted a grave, smirking expression, as if guarding a secret. Finally, he whispered that, 'For fear any unpleasant report should reach the young ladies under your care regarding their sister's indisposition, I think it advisable to say there is no great reason for alarm; I hope Mrs. Dashwood will do very well.'"

"What! Is Fanny ill?" Elinor asked.

"That is precisely what I said," Mrs. Jennings replied. "Then the truth emerged. Mr. Edward Ferrars—the very young man I once joked with you about, though I am delighted there was nothing between us—has been secretly engaged to my cousin Lucy for over a year! Not a soul knew, save Nancy. It is no wonder they liked one another, but that such a union remained undetected is baffling. I never saw them together, yet I should have guessed. This secret was kept to avoid offending Mrs. Ferrars, leaving neither she, your brother, nor your sister aware until this morning. Poor Nancy, a well-meaning but simple creature, accidentally revealed it. Thinking, 'They are all so fond of Lucy, they will accept this easily,' she rushed to your sister, who was working alone on her carpet. Unbeknownst to her, your sister had only moments prior suggested matching Edward with a lord's daughter. The blow to her vanity was immediate. She fell into violent hysterics, screaming loudly enough to be heard by your brother, who was downstairs composing a letter to his steward. He rushed upstairs to find a terrible scene unfolding. Lucy had arrived, unaware of the turmoil, and was driven into a fainting fit by your sister's fury. Nancy knelt and wept bitterly, while your brother paced in despair. Mrs. Dashwood declared they must leave instantly, forcing your brother to kneel and plead for permission to pack their belongings. Only then did she relapse into hysterics, so terrified was he that he summoned Mr. Donovan. He found the house in uproar; the carriage waited, and Lucy, barely able to walk, was being helped in alongside a nearly hysterical Nancy. I confess, I have no patience with your sister. I hope, with all my heart, this becomes a match despite her. Imagine Mr. Edward's passion upon hearing this! They say he is deeply fond of her. Mr. Donovan agrees. He has already returned to Harley Street to be near when Mrs. Ferrars is informed, for your sister is certain she will also succumb to hysterics. I care not for her reaction. I pity neither of them. People make such a fuss over money and status! There is no earthly reason why Mr. Edward and Lucy should not marry. Mrs. Ferrars can easily provide for her son, and though Lucy has little, she knows how to stretch resources. With five hundred a-year, I am sure Mrs. Ferrars could maintain as grand an appearance as anyone else with eight. Imagine how snugly they might live in a cottage like yours, perhaps slightly larger, with two maids and two men. I believe I could even provide a housemaid, as my Betty has a sister seeking employment."

Mrs. Jennings paused, allowing Elinor time to compose herself. She responded with the expected observations, relieved that Mrs. Jennings no longer suspected her of romantic attachment to Edward. Above all, she was grateful for Marianne's absence, which allowed her to discuss the affair with impartiality.

Elinor struggled to determine her own expectations, though she earnestly suppressed the notion of any outcome other than the marriage of Edward and Lucy. She was anxious to hear Mrs. Ferrars's reaction and, more so, to observe Edward's conduct. She felt profound compassion for him, little for Lucy, and none for the rest of the party.

Realizing Mrs. Jennings could discuss no other topic, Elinor saw the necessity of preparing Marianne. There was no time to lose in revealing the truth and ensuring Marianne could hear such news from others without detecting her sister's distress or resentment toward Edward.

Elinor's task was painful. She intended to remove what she believed was Marianne's chief consolation, to detail facts that might ruin Edward forever in her sister's eyes, and to force Marianne to relive her own disappointment through a resemblance of their situations. Though unwelcome, it was necessary.

Elinor made no attempt to dwell on her own feelings or portray herself as suffering, relying instead on the self-command she had practiced since learning of Edward's engagement. Her narration was clear and simple. Though delivered with emotion, it lacked violent agitation or impetuous grief. That reaction belonged to the listener; Marianne listened with horror and wept excessively. Elinor offered herself as a comforter, providing assurances of her own composure and a earnest vindication of Edward from all charges but imprudence.

For some time, Marianne refused to accept either explanation. Edward seemed a second Willoughby. Acknowledging, as Elinor did, that she had loved him sincerely, how could she feel less? Regarding Lucy Steele, she deemed her so utterly unamiable and incapable of attracting a sensible man that she could not initially believe, nor later pardon, Edward's former affection for her. She refused to admit it was natural, leaving Elinor to prove it through a deeper knowledge of mankind.

Elinor's initial communication stated only the fact of the engagement and its duration. Marianne's emotions then overwhelmed her, halting further detail. For a while, the only goal was to soothe her distress, lessen her fears, and combat her resentment. The first question Marianne posed was, "How long has this been known to you, Elinor? Has he written to you?"

"I have known it for four months," Elinor replied. "When Lucy first came to Barton Park last November, she confided her engagement to me."

Marianne's eyes widened in astonishment, her lips unable to express the shock. After a pause of wonder, she exclaimed, "Four months! Have you known of this for four months?"

Elinor confirmed it.

"While attending me in all my misery, has this been on your heart? And I have reproached you for being happy!"

"It was not fit that you should then know how much I was the reverse!"

"Four months!" Marianne cried again. "So calm! So cheerful! How have you been supported?"

"By feeling that I was doing my duty. My promise to Lucy obliged me to remain silent. I owed it to her to avoid giving any hint of the truth, and to my family and friends, not to create a solicitude about me that I could not satisfy."

Marianne appeared deeply moved.

"I have often wished to undeceive you and my mother," Elinor added. "Once or twice I attempted it, but without betraying my trust, I could never have convinced you."

"Four months! And yet you loved him!"

"Yes. But I did not love only him; and while the comfort of others was dear to me, I was glad to spare them from knowing how much I felt. Now, I can think and speak of it with little emotion. I would not have you suffer on my account; for I assure you I no longer suffer materially myself. I have many things to support me. I am not conscious of having provoked this disappointment by any imprudence of my own. I have borne it as much as possible without spreading it farther. I acquit Edward of essential misconduct. I wish him very happy; and I am so sure of his always doing his duty that, though he may harbor some regret now, he must eventually overcome it. Lucy does not lack sense, and that is the foundation upon which everything good may be built. And after all, Marianne, after all that is bewitching in the idea of a single, constant attachment, and all that can be said of happiness depending entirely on one particular person, it is not meant—it is not fit—it is not possible that it should be so. Edward will marry Lucy. He will marry a woman superior in person and understanding to half her sex; and time and habit will teach him to forget that he ever thought another superior to her."

"If such is your way of thinking," Marianne said, "if the loss of what is most valued is so easily made up by something else, your resolution, your self-command, are perhaps less to be wondered at. They are brought more within my comprehension."

"I understand you. You do not suppose that I have ever felt much. For four months, Marianne, I have had all this hanging on my mind, without being at liberty to speak of it to a single creature. Knowing that it would make you and my mother most unhappy whenever explained, yet unable to prepare you for it in the least. It was forced upon me by the very person whose prior engagement ruined all my prospects, and told to me, as I thought, with triumph. Therefore, I had to oppose her suspicions by appearing indifferent where I was most deeply interested. I have had to listen to her hopes and exultation again and again. I have known myself to be divided from Edward forever without hearing one circumstance that could make me less desire the connection. Nothing has proved him unworthy; nor has anything declared him indifferent to me. I have had to contend against the unkindness of his sister and the insolence of his mother, suffering the punishment of an attachment without enjoying its advantages. And all this has occurred at a time when, as you know too well, it was not my only unhappiness. If you can think me capable of ever feeling, surely you may suppose that I have suffered now. The composure of mind with which I have brought myself to consider the matter, the consolation I have admitted, have been the effect of constant and painful exertion; they did not spring up spontaneously. No, Marianne. Then, if I had not been bound to silence, perhaps nothing could have kept me entirely—not even what I owed to my dearest friends—from openly showing that I was very unhappy."

Marianne was completely subdued.

"Oh! Elinor," she cried, "you have made me hate myself forever. How barbarous have I been to you! You, who have been my only comfort, who have borne with me in all my misery, who have seemed to be suffering only for me! Is this my gratitude? Is this the only return I can make you? Because your merit cries out upon myself, I have been trying to do it away."

Tenderest caresses followed this confession. In such a frame of mind, Elinor had no difficulty in obtaining whatever promise she required. At Marianne's request, she pledged never to speak of the affair to anyone with the slightest appearance of bitterness; to meet Lucy without betraying the smallest increase of dislike; and even to see Edward himself, if chance brought them together, without diminishing her usual cordiality. These were great concessions, but where Marianne felt she had injured, no reparation was too much.

She fulfilled her promise of discretion to admiration. She attended to everything Mrs. Jennings said with an unchanging countenance, dissented from her in nothing, and was heard three times to say, "Yes, ma'am." She listened to Mrs. Jennings's praise of Lucy with only shifting from one chair to another, and when Mrs. Jennings spoke of Edward's affection, it cost her only a spasm in her throat. Such advances toward heroism in her sister made Elinor feel equal to anything.

The next morning brought another trial in the form of a visit from their brother, who arrived with a solemn aspect to discuss the dreadful affair and bring news of his wife.

"You have heard, I suppose," he began gravely, "of the very shocking discovery that took place under our roof yesterday."

They all nodded in assent; it seemed too awful a moment for speech.

"Your sister," he continued, "has suffered dreadfully. Mrs. Ferrars too—in short, it has been a scene of such compounded distress—but I hope the storm may be weathered without us being quite overcome. Poor Fanny! She was in hysterics all yesterday. But I would not alarm you too much. Donovan says there is nothing materially to be apprehended; her constitution is good, and her resolution equal to anything. She has borne it all with the fortitude of an angel! She says she shall never think well of anybody again, and one cannot wonder at it after being so deceived! Meeting with such ingratitude where so much kindness and confidence had been shown! It was purely from the benevolence of her heart that she invited these young women to her house; merely because she thought they deserved attention, were harmless, well-behaved girls, and pleasant companions. Otherwise, we both wished very much to invite you and Marianne while your kind friend attended her daughter. And now to be rewarded thus! 'I wish, with all my heart,' says poor Fanny in her affectionate way, 'that we had asked your sisters instead of them.'"

He paused to receive thanks, then proceeded.
