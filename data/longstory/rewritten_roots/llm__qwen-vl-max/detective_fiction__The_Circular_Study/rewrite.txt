Detective Mr. Gryce was in a mood of quiet despondency. He had reached that stage in life when energy wanes and passion must be prodded into action. Lately, there had been no urgent cases, no thrilling pursuits—only routine, and with it, a creeping sense of obsolescence. He even considered stepping down from the force, retiring to his modest farm in Westchester, a retreat he’d long dreamed of but now found himself dreading. For Mr. Gryce, life without purpose was unbearable; his mind thrived on challenge, on unraveling mysteries, far more than any comfort or reward such work might bring. Yet, retirement loomed—a prospect as dull as the gray autumn afternoon stretching before him.

But fate, it seemed, had other plans.

Just as his thoughts turned inward, a telephone call came through to Headquarters—one that stirred something deep within him, awakening a spark he hadn’t felt in years. The message originated from Carter’s drugstore, a familiar landmark on the corner. A woman had sent a boy to deliver an urgent report: a strange crime had been committed in the old mansion across the street. The boy, trembling and reluctant, had accepted payment but refused to speak further unless compelled. He was being held at the store until police arrived.

“Strange crime,” Mr. Gryce repeated under his breath. That single word—*strange*—clung to him like a warning. It wasn’t just violence or theft; it hinted at something deeper, more unsettling. Around him, officers exchanged glances, their curiosity piqued by the mention of the house. It stood out—not for grandeur, but for its age and isolation. Once home to one of New York’s most distinguished families, it now endured amid modern shops and bustling restaurants, a relic stubbornly resisting time. And now, a crime had occurred inside?

The inspector in charge saw the fire returning to Gryce’s eyes and nodded. “You’re going.”

He didn’t hesitate. First stop: Carter’s.

There, the boy waited—small, wide-eyed, clutching a coin. Gryce took him gently by the arm and walked him toward the house, questioning him along the way. The story remained simple: a woman, elegant and commanding, had approached him while he stared at toys in a shop window. She handed him money and pulled him quickly to the drugstore. There, she showed him another coin, promising it if he delivered a message: *A strange crime has been committed in the old house on ---- Street.* The boy hesitated—he was afraid—but she shook him hard enough to snap him out of it. Then he ran, terrified, and called the police.

Gryce sensed truth in the boy’s voice. But when asked to describe the woman, all he could say was that she was “a stunner,” dressed in glittering clothes, adorned with jewels. No name, no face—just an impression of power and mystery.

Gryce let it go. His attention was already fixed on the house ahead.

It looked untouched by chaos. The door was closed, the stoop clean, the windows draped tightly with curtains, some shaded almost completely. The brick façade, trimmed in brownstone, stood dignified and still, contrasting sharply with the noisy neighbors crowding close on either side. Nothing suggested violence—or even disturbance.

“Probably a hoax,” Gryce muttered to himself.

Then, movement. From beneath the stoop, a patrolman emerged, brow furrowed. “Can’t get in,” he said. “Rang every bell. House seems empty… except I saw a flicker in one of the upper windows. Should I try the back? Break in through Knapp & Co.’s store next door?”

“Yes,” Gryce replied. “Take this boy. Lock him in one of their offices. Then find a way into the rear yard—and from there, into the house. It should be accessible.”

The officer nodded, led the boy away, and vanished into the adjacent building.

Gryce lingered in the area, soon surrounded by curious onlookers drawn by the commotion. But before they could press him for details, the front door opened from the inside. He slipped through, entering a basement hall leading to a kitchen—spotless, orderly, almost too neat for a household that hadn’t seen guests in months. No signs of struggle, no blood, no disarray. Just silence.

He followed the officer upstairs.

On the parlor floor, the officer whispered: “Mr. Raffner from next door says the man who lives here is odd—no one knows him. A recluse. Bookworm, they call him. Been here six months. Only ever seen him and an old servant—just as peculiar.”

“I know,” Gryce murmured.

Indeed, everyone knew about Mr. Adams—the reclusive millionaire whose wealth and charm were matched only by his aloofness. He lived alone, avoided society, spoke rarely. To enter his home was rare enough. To do so under suspicion of murder? That was a case worth waking the dead for.

The hallway was wide, antique, furnished sparsely in the old style. Two doors stood open. They stepped into the nearest, a bare room resembling a doctor’s waiting chamber—cold, sterile, unremarkable. But then—through a half-drawn portière at the far end—they glimpsed something rich, something ornate.

They pushed forward.

And stopped.

The room beyond was circular, dimly lit by a single, eerie beam of rose-colored light. Tapestries hung thickly on the walls, interspersed with priceless artifacts. Books lined the lower shelves in perfect order. A large painting dominated one wall—yet neither the art nor the literature drew the eye.

Instead, the light fell upon a man lying on a bearskin rug, a dagger piercing his chest. On his breast, a golden cross gleamed against the dark fabric of his robe—like a saint laid out for burial. But the face… the face told a different story.

Not fear. Not pain. Not surprise.

Only agony—unnameable, profound, as though he had known something terrible, something beyond death itself.

Gryce stepped closer, his breath shallow. This was not mere murder. This was a ritual. A warning. Or perhaps… a confession.

And somewhere, in the shadows, the clock ticked.

A moment of stillness—almost reverence—held Mr. Gryce motionless before he regained his composure. The dim glow, the heavy silence, the air of bygone romance that clung to the room, and the rigid form of the man who had once ruled this house now stretched out like a corpse awaiting burial—all combined to unsettle even the seasoned mind of the veteran detective. The sacred cross resting upon the dead man’s chest stood in stark defiance of the violent act that had ended his life. It was a contradiction so profound it stirred something deeper than mere professional curiosity. Yet it lasted only an instant.

Before the officer behind him could speak, Mr. Gryce stepped forward and took position beside the body. He knew at once that death had come swiftly and surely. But he checked anyway—pulse, eyes—each detail scrutinized with practiced precision. The gaze of the deceased, though fixed, carried an accusation so piercing it forced the detective to summon every ounce of his discipline. The body was still warm, but lifeless beyond doubt. He left the dagger embedded in the wound—no need to disturb it yet—but examined it closely. It was no common blade. A relic from some Eastern bazaar, ornate and exotic. That alone might suggest suicide. But the angle of the wound—the way the blade had entered—did not align with self-inflicted violence. Something else had happened here.

The room offered few clues. No signs of struggle. No shattered glass, no overturned furniture. Only scattered rose petals marked the floor, as if someone had dropped them in haste. The large central table, where the victim had stood when struck, remained untouched. At first glance, everything was in place: pipes, pens, portraits, ancient weights, Roman lamps, Venetian glass, rare porcelain, medals, rough metalwork, manuscripts, a scroll of music, a pot of blooming flowers—and then, oddly, a row of electric buttons. Mr. Gryce touched one. Instantly, the red light above flickered into a sickly green, casting eerie shadows across the walls. He pressed another. A soft white radiance replaced it.

“Childish tricks for a man of his age,” he muttered, but the thought lingered only briefly. Then he looked again at the face beneath his feet—sharp jaw, firm mouth, eyes wide open in silent reproach. This was no fool. Such features spoke of purpose, intelligence, resolve. To call such a man childish would be to misread human nature entirely. So the electric lights were not mere whimsy. They were part of something greater—perhaps a scientific pursuit hinted at by the books and instruments surrounding him.

He let the white light remain and turned to the walls, lined with tapestries and curiosities. Nothing obvious. Except—yes, there was one thing. A bent nail, torn from its place. The nail that had held the cross, now lying on the dead man’s chest. The cord still looped around it, its crimson threads mingling with the blood soaking through the victim’s shirt. Who had removed it? Not the victim. With a wound like that, such movement would have been impossible. And the nail was far from where he fell—across the room. Another hand had pulled it down. Placed it deliberately on his heart. A final gesture—of faith? Of guilt? Mr. Gryce couldn’t dwell on it. Time was precious, and he was alone.

Halfway up the wall, behind the table, hung a portrait. The only large painting in the room. A young woman—strikingly beautiful, her expression both tender and sorrowful. Her clothing and hairstyle placed her near the end of the Civil War. There was a haunting quality about her—intelligent, melancholic. And in her features, Mr. Gryce saw echoes of the man on the floor. Not identical—she was fair, he dark—but enough resemblance to suggest kinship. Below the portrait hung a sword, epaulettes, and a war medal. Mementos. Valuable ones. Perhaps useful.

Then, near the back wall, he noticed a narrow curtain—so dark, so patterned like the draperies around it, that it had escaped his notice until now. Not a window—those were high, nearly ceiling-level. This must conceal another room. He pushed aside the fabric and stepped into a small closet: a narrow cot, a dresser, a small table. The bed was neat, spare—a bachelor’s. The dresser, however, reflected refinement—precise, orderly. Except for one thing: a silver-backed comb, missing from its place, now lying on the floor. And near the door, a pearl-handled parasol. A woman had been here recently.

And then—on the threshold between the closet and the study—he saw it: a tiny black spangle, a sequin. He bent to examine it. And caught sight of another farther along. Then another. The trail led toward the center table. These shimmering fragments—tiny beads strung on thread—were meant to stay together. But if the thread broke, they’d fall one by one. Like breadcrumbs left by a woman whose dress was too fine for carelessness. Mr. Gryce smiled faintly. A delicate clue. Almost poetic. Fate turning vanity into evidence.

He searched for where the thread might have snapped. Nothing sharp in the doorway. The chairs were cushioned. But wait—what about the cross? Its base was gold filigree, intricate. Could it have snagged the edge of her dress as she tore it down? He hurried to the spot, scanning the floor. Nothing—until he reached the bearskin rug beneath the body. There, hidden in the long fur, lay one more spangle. She had knelt here. Before him.

Satisfied, he returned to the table. This time, he examined it thoroughly. His fingers brushed near the inkwell. He paused. A slight stain. He ran his thumb over it—then glanced at his finger with quiet satisfaction. He lifted the inkwell carefully, set it back down exactly as it had been. Was there something beneath it? He didn’t say. Detectives don’t reveal their thoughts.

Just then, a footstep echoed behind him. Styles, the officer, returned. “What is it?” Mr. Gryce asked sharply.

Styles pointed toward the antechamber. “Upstairs. I found him.”

Mr. Gryce hesitated, still absorbed in the room. Styles grew agitated. “Come! He’s in a room above. I’ve got him. But we must see him before he sees us.”

Mr. Gryce nodded. They retreated to the small closet, drew the curtain closed, leaving only a narrow slit. Steps approached—slow at first, then faster. Heavy breathing.

“Did he see you?” Mr. Gryce whispered.

Styles shook his head, pointing to the door. The intruder was coming.

Then—silence. The portière parted. A figure appeared in the doorway.

Mr. Gryce froze.

It was a thin, pale servant, holding a tray with a single glass of water. No weapon. No sign of menace. Just a man, ordinary in appearance, standing there.

But then—his eyes fell upon the body. A gasp escaped him. The glass slipped from his hand, shattering on the floor. A cry—then a wail.

“Look!” Styles urged, voice hushed. “Watch him now.”

And the man did something astonishing. He stepped forward, arms raised. One hand extended backward, stiffened. The other lunged—fingers closing as if grasping a knife. A swift, sideways thrust—into empty air. Then, slowly, he withdrew the imaginary blade. His body trembled. He staggered back. His face contorted—horror, grief, madness. He sank to his knees, then collapsed onto the floor, one arm still outstretched.

“It’s what I saw upstairs,” Styles breathed. “He’s gone mad. Driven insane by what he did.”

Mr. Gryce said nothing. He stared, transfixed. This was no ordinary crime. This was a mystery wrapped in madness, and he had never seen anything like it before.

The man who had just reenacted the scene of violence—his movements eerily precise, his expression contorted with grief—rose slowly from the floor. His face was blank now, eyes glazed, as though the intensity of his performance had drained him. He bent to pick up the glass he’d dropped, cradling it carefully in his hands as if it were fragile evidence. But before he could step away, Mr. Gryce’s voice cut through the silence.

“Wait,” he said sharply. “You’re not taking that glass anywhere. We need to know how your master ended up dead.”

It was a command meant to shake anyone out of their stupor. Yet this man didn’t flinch. He kept walking, moving past Styles, who lunged forward and grabbed his shoulder.

“Stop!” Styles snapped. “What do you think you’re doing? You heard him. Answer him!”

This time, the man froze. The glass slipped from his grasp, shattering on the hardwood with a sharp *crack*. At the same moment, a sound escaped him—not quite a scream, not quite a sob—but something between a moan and a gasp. He’d seen them now—the two men standing firm, their eyes locked on him—and his posture betrayed fear. His shoulders hunched, his breath came in ragged bursts.

He tried to bolt. Styles held fast, dragging him back toward the center of the room, where Mr. Gryce stood beside the bearskin rug, beneath which lay the still form of the deceased master.

And then, at the sight of the body, another transformation took place. A flicker of something dark lit his eyes—joy, cold and terrible. It twisted his lips into a grin, and from deep within his chest rose a low, chilling laugh. It echoed off the high walls, unnatural and grotesque.

Mr. Gryce recoiled slightly, though he remained motionless. Disgust warred with curiosity. This man wasn’t sane. Not fully. And yet, he was clearly connected to the crime. With an icy stare, Gryce fixed the servant with his gaze—eyes so rarely met by others, now unflinching.

“Now,” he said, voice hard. “Speak. Who killed him? You were here. You must know.”

The servant’s mouth opened. But no words came. Instead, guttural sounds tumbled out—strange, rhythmic syllables, like a language without meaning. Then, with one free hand (the other still gripped by Styles), he touched his ears, then his lips, and shook his head violently.

The truth hit them all at once: he was deaf and dumb.

Styles staggered back. The revelation overwhelmed him. He released the man, who stepped away slowly, returning to his corner. There, he began again—this time with even greater vigor—reenacting the murder and the mourning. He struck the air, fell to his knees, raised his arms in sorrow. It was a ritual, repeated with mechanical precision.

When he finished, he stood rigid, eyes darting around the room. But now, there was no pity in them—only a glint of something darker, something almost cruel. His presence felt like an intrusion, a blasphemy in the quiet horror of death.

Styles couldn’t bear it any longer. “Can’t you speak?” he roared. “Can’t you hear?”

The mute smiled—slowly, deliberately—his expression one of mockery, of satisfaction. It chilled Gryce to the bone.

“Take him away,” Gryce ordered, cutting through the tension. “Search him thoroughly. Look for blood, for traces. I’m going upstairs—to the room where you first saw him. He’s too close to this to be innocent. He must carry something with him.”

But the search yielded nothing. No bloodstains. No fingerprints. No sign of struggle. The rooms above revealed no clues linking the servant directly to the crime.

Gryce grew thoughtful. He returned to the study, scanning every inch of the space, every object, every shadow. Nothing new emerged. His mind wandered back to the lifeless face on the rug—mouth open, eyes wide, frozen in agony and resolve.

Then, from above—a cry.

Not human. But clear, piercing, deliberate.

“Remember Evelyn!”

It echoed down from the rafters, startling in its clarity. Who was Evelyn? And why did this voice—coming from nowhere—insist upon her name?

Gryce looked up. High near the top of one window, suspended in a cage, sat an English starling. Its neck stretched upward, its beak open wide. It shrieked again:

“Remember Evelyn!”

The bird seemed aware of the attention it commanded. It tilted its head, stared down, and cried once more.

It was the final surreal note in a night of impossible occurrences. Gryce leaned forward, drawn to the creature, when suddenly the light changed—white to green—without warning. He realized, with annoyance, that he’d pressed a button on the strange lamp overhead. These shifting colors were distracting, trivial. But they wouldn’t wait.

He stepped back from the table, preparing to leave. Then came Styles’s voice from the antechamber.

“Shall I let him go, Mr. Gryce? He seems agitated—not dangerous, but restless. Like he’s forgotten something. Or remembered a duty he hasn’t fulfilled.”

“Yes,” Gryce replied without hesitation. “Let him go. But watch him. Every move he makes matters. He might be giving us clues without knowing it.”

He moved toward the door, ready to observe.

At that moment, the starling called again—“Remember Evelyn!”—as Gryce passed the curtains.

He paused. Turned. Looked up.

To whom was this plea directed? To him? Or to the man lying dead below, whose ears would never hear again?

It could be meaningless. Or it could be the key to everything.

But there was no time to ponder. The old servant approached—returning from the front hall, where he’d vanished after being released. In one hand, he carried the master’s hat. In the other, his umbrella.

Gryce stopped. Watched.

The mute bowed slightly, handed over the items, and retreated silently to his seat in the antechamber. Without a word, he sank into himself, drifting once more into that vacant, dazed state.

Gryce replaced the hat and umbrella on the rack. Then he muttered under his breath:

“Humph. Mind gone. Memory unreliable. Testimony useless.”

He frowned. Was the man broken by what he’d witnessed—or had he already been broken long before? Had madness come first, or had it followed the crime?

He might have found the answer—if not for the sudden interruption.

The front door bell, ringing erratically, was silenced by a sharp, authoritative knock.

Someone important had arrived.

The coroner. Or worse.

The golden hour was over.

Mr. Gryce found himself more baffled than ever before in his long career as a detective. The case of Mr. Adams was unlike any other he had encountered—dark, isolated, and strangely sealed off from the world. The victim had lived alone, save for a deaf manservant who spoke not a word. His home stood in a part of the city where neighbors were nonexistent, and now it seemed that even friends had been few or none. Hours had passed since the news of the murder spread, yet no one had come to inquire, offer condolences, or share a clue. It was odd—especially since Adams had recently become something of a curiosity in the neighborhood. But then again, everything about him had been odd. A man who lived apart, avoided society, and kept his life wrapped in silence would hardly have drawn the usual circle of acquaintances. And so, rather than diminish the mystery, this isolation deepened it.

The absence of witnesses or confidants only heightened the challenge—and with it, the intrigue. As midnight approached, Mr. Gryce felt a quiet relief when the throng of reporters, doctors, and officials finally departed, leaving him once more in command of the scene. He still had two matters to settle before retiring. The first demanded immediate attention.

Passing through the hall, he paused near a chair where a small boy sat half-asleep. “Come on, Jake,” he said. “It’s time we moved. I need you to take me back to the exact spot where that woman stopped you earlier.”

Jake blinked awake, fumbling for his cap. “Can I see my mother first?” he asked, voice thick with sleep. “She must be worried. I’ve never stayed out this late.”

“She knows where you are,” Mr. Gryce replied. “I sent word hours ago. She said you’re a good lad—obedient, honest.”

“She’s a good mother,” Jake whispered, pulling on his hat. “I’d be just like my father if I didn’t treat her right.”

“Then let’s go,” said the detective.

They stepped into the night. The streets were quiet, but in one section, lights still flickered in shops and windows. They stopped before a dimmed storefront, where Jake pointed to two stuffed frogs locked in combat with tiny swords—the very sight he’d been staring at when the woman approached.

Mr. Gryce watched the boy closely, though Jake might have sworn his eyes never left the frogs. “Was she pretty?” the detective asked.

Jake scratched his head. “She made me scared. But her clothes—oh, they were splendid! Gorgeous!” Then he hesitated. “And she was young… and she had flowers… and looked upset?”

“No,” Mr. Gryce corrected gently. “Not young. No flowers. Not trembling.”

Jake shook his head slowly. “Well… maybe not crying. But she *was* troubled. When she grabbed my arm, it wasn’t her voice that showed it—it was her grip. She pulled me straight to the drugstore. If she hadn’t given me money first, I’d have wriggled free. But I like money, sir—I don’t get much.”

Mr. Gryce nodded, already thinking. “Not young,” he muttered. “Then perhaps an old flame. Old flames are often more dangerous than new ones.”

At the drugstore, he asked: “Where exactly did she stand while you went inside?”

Jake pointed to a spot on the sidewalk. “She was there,” he said. “But when I came out, she was gone.”

“Did you see her leave? Which way did she go?”

“I kept one eye on her,” Jake admitted. “Afraid she’d follow me in. My arm still hurts from that squeeze. So when she started moving, I stepped closer. I saw her reach the curb and lift her hand. But it wasn’t a streetcar—none came by for minutes.”

A faint smile touched Mr. Gryce’s lips. “So she hailed a cab or a hack. Were there any empty coaches nearby?”

Jake shrugged. “Didn’t notice. That’s all I saw.”

No more could be drawn from him. Mr. Gryce handed him over to an assistant and returned to the house.

“Anyone waiting?” he asked Styles at the door.

“Yes, sir—a young man named Hines. Says he’s an electrician.”

“That’s who I want. Where is he?”

“In the parlor, sir.”

“Good. Send no one else in. Anyone upstairs?”

“All gone, sir. Shall I stay or go up?”

“Go up. I’ll watch the door.”

Styles nodded and disappeared.

Mr. Gryce entered the parlor. A neatly dressed young man rose. “You sent for me?” he said.

The detective confirmed it, asked a few questions, and satisfied, led him into Adams’s study—the room where the body had been removed to another floor.

A candle burned softly on a side table, as ordered. But once inside, Mr. Gryce walked to the center table, placed his hand on a button, and said, “Would you kindly blow out the candle?”

The man complied. Darkness fell.

Then—suddenly—a wave of deep violet light flooded the room. The effect was dramatic, almost theatrical. Both men turned, startled.

“That’s just one setting,” Mr. Gryce said calmly. “Now press another button.”

The light shifted to pink. Then green. Then white. Then a sickly yellow. “This one isn’t flattering,” Mr. Gryce noted. “But it works well enough.”

He gestured toward the bookshelf. “Examine these books. Can someone with no formal training build such a system?”

Hines glanced at the titles. “With skill and patience, yes. These texts cover the basics. The aptitude is what matters.”

“So Adams had the aptitude,” Mr. Gryce said, tone flat with disappointment.

Men who liked mechanics often wasted their talents on childish gadgets. “Look at that birdcage,” he said. “High up, unreachable without a ladder. Yet it holds a lively bird. There must be a mechanism to lower it. Can you find it?”

Hines searched. After a moment, he pressed a hidden button in the tapestry. With a soft click, the cage descended.

“Let’s not send this poor bird back up,” Mr. Gryce said, lifting it. “An English starling isn’t common here. Listen—he’s about to speak.”

But the bird, sensing tension, fluttered once and settled into stillness—either asleep or pretending to be.

Mr. Gryce placed the cage on the floor and idly pressed another button he hadn’t used before.

Nothing changed. The yellow light remained.

“The wiring may be disturbed,” he said, shrugging. But then he froze.

The doorway leading to the antechamber began to close. Slowly. From a yard wide to a foot. Then an inch. Then—

“Ah,” laughed the electrician. “Clever. Seated here, he can shut the door without getting up. No shouting, no tripping over rugs. I approve—though…”

He trailed off, noticing the look on Mr. Gryce’s face.

“The slide… it’s not wood,” he said. “It’s steel.”

“Steel,” Mr. Gryce confirmed, voice low. “This is the strangest thing yet. Adams was obsessed with electricity.”

“And now we’re trapped,” the electrician added. “No lever, no button to open it.”

“There should be one,” Mr. Gryce said, though he clearly doubted it.

After five minutes of searching, they gave up.

“Let’s try a brighter light,” Mr. Gryce said. “Yellow doesn’t suit the mood.”

“Give us rose,” the other suggested. “Unless someone’s on the other side, we’ll be here until morning.”

“There’s a man upstairs—Styles. We might wake him. But what does this mean?” Mr. Gryce asked. “Every window in these rooms has been bricked up under the roof. This place feels like a tomb.”

“A very strange tomb,” Hines agreed. “But I need air. I can’t think in confinement.”

Mr. Gryce examined the steel plate. It fit perfectly into the curved wall—crafted with precision, likely at great cost. He ran his fingers along its edge.

“Adams took his mechanical hobbies to extremes,” he murmured. “This barrier is solid. It won’t yield easily.”

“Why not install a bell?” Hines asked.

“Because bells are ordinary,” Mr. Gryce replied. “And Adams preferred the unusual. Besides—his servant is deaf.”

“Then try a loud knock,” Hines suggested. “Or your whistle.”

Mr. Gryce whistled sharply. He struck the steel with his alpenstock. Again and again.

For an hour, nothing. Then—finally—Styles heard. But it took five more hours to chip through the reinforced wall and create a passage large enough for them to escape.

By then, the room had grown cold, and the mystery had deepened. The trap was not merely a device—it was a statement. And Mr. Gryce knew, with a chill, that the real puzzle had only just begun.

The events of the previous night had deepened Mr. Gryce’s resolve to unravel the mystery surrounding Mr. Adams’s death. His mind, already sharpened by intrigue, now burned with a new intensity. Arriving at Headquarters—where his prolonged absence had likely stirred concern among those unaware of its cause—his first question was urgent: Had Bartow, the mute butler, regained consciousness?

The answer was disheartening. Not only had there been no improvement, but the specialist in mental disorders consulted on the case had offered little hope for immediate recovery. Mr. Gryce’s expression darkened. Summoning the officer who had arrested Bartow, he asked how the man had reacted upon being detained.

“Surprised, sir,” came the reply. “Shook his head, made some odd gestures—then went through his usual pantomime. It’s quite a sight, sir. Poor fellow keeps holding his hand back like this.” The officer mimicked the motion.

Mr. Gryce noted it carefully. It was precisely the gesture Bartow had used when first realizing he was being watched. The meaning remained unclear, though the right hand—the one not known to be left-handed—was involved. He observed that Bartow repeated it as if hoping to draw attention to something crucial, perhaps a fact that could clear him of suspicion.

“Does he appear fearful? Angry?” Mr. Gryce pressed.

“It changes, sir. One moment he looks like he’s about to fall asleep; the next, he jerks awake, furious, pounding the walls. Not a pleasant sight. He’ll need constant supervision.”

“See that he is watched at all times,” Mr. Gryce said firmly. “His words may be useless, but every movement holds meaning. I’ll visit him tomorrow.”

As the officer departed, Mr. Gryce sat quietly, lost in thought. From his pocket, he withdrew a small package. Emptying its contents onto his desk, he revealed five tiny spangles—shiny, black sequins. He stared at them intently. He had long found comfort in focusing on insignificant objects while thinking—they helped center his mind. And indeed, after a few moments of quiet contemplation, he suddenly swept the spangles back into their envelope, glanced at his watch, and stepped briskly into the outer office.

There, he paused before a line of waiting men. One caught his eye—a young detective named Sweetwater, whose keen gaze had followed Mr. Gryce’s movements with unmistakable interest. With a nod, Mr. Gryce beckoned him forward and led him into his private room.

“You want a hand in this?” he asked, closing the door behind them.

“Oh, sir—” Sweetwater began, his face flushed with eagerness. “I don’t mean to presume—”

“Enough,” Mr. Gryce cut in. “You’ve been here six months without a chance to prove yourself. Now I’m giving you a real challenge. Are you up to it, Sweetwater? Do you know the city well enough to find a needle in a haystack?”

“I’d like to try,” Sweetwater replied, eyes alight. “If I succeed, it’ll be better than any promotion. I’ve been waiting for a chance like this. I’ll do it carefully—out of gratitude, even.”

“We’ll see,” Mr. Gryce said, studying him. “This task is nearly impossible. We’re looking for a woman we know almost nothing about—except that she wore a dress covered in black spangles and carried a parasol during her visit to Mr. Adams just before or at the time of the murder. I can show you the parasol later. She came from somewhere unknown, and vanished—your job is to find where. You won’t be alone, but the odds are against you. Unless she comes forward herself, we’re unlikely to succeed. But there’s one thing—something I’ll share only with you. As she disappeared from the one person watching her, she approached the curbstone in front of Hudson’s fruit store on 14th Street and raised her right hand like this.” He demonstrated the gesture.

“It’s slight,” he admitted, “but it’s all I have—along with these five spangles, which fell from her dress. And I believe she isn’t among the women usually seen by police—she’s someone respectable, out of the ordinary. But don’t let that limit your search. Convictions often blind us more than they help.”

Sweetwater, utterly captivated, smiled broadly. “I’ll do my best, sir—but please don’t show me the parasol. Just describe it. I don’t want the others making fun of me if I fail. I’d rather work quietly.”

“All right,” Mr. Gryce agreed. “It’s a delicate, frivolous thing—gray chiffon, pearl handle, pink ribbon bows. I doubt it was ever used before. Women value such things highly, so it would only have been dropped under extreme emotion or fear. No name was on it.”

“And the maker?” Sweetwater asked quickly.

“No,” Mr. Gryce answered. “That would have helped too much.”

“What time was she seen at the curbstone?”

“Half-past four—the exact moment the telephone message arrived.”

“Very good, sir. It’s the hardest task I’ve ever taken on, but that doesn’t stop me. When shall I report back?”

“When you have something to say. Though—I suspect her first name is Evelyn. But remember, it’s only a hunch.”

“Understood, sir,” Sweetwater said, stepping out with renewed confidence.

Mr. Gryce did not share his optimism. The fog around the case remained thick. But then, he wasn’t twenty-three, full of untested ambition. His next hope lay in the public reports spreading across the country. A man of Mr. Adams’s wealth and refinement must have had many connections—people who might come forward now that news of his death had reached them.

Yet the day passed without any claimants appearing. No friends emerged to shed light on the reclusive life of the dead man. Only his banker and lawyer visited—both confirmed his financial stability and lack of family. Even his landlord offered little. He’d first heard of Adams through a Philadelphia attorney, now deceased, who vouched for his respectability. Upon meeting him, the landlord had been struck by Adams’s ascetic appearance and reserved nature. The rent was always paid promptly, but Adams never encouraged conversation. He seemed absorbed in study and experiments, aided only by his deaf-mute servant.

Asked about the servant, the landlord admitted he knew little—only that he had met him twice, and had never sensed loyalty or hostility. He couldn’t say what the man’s true feelings were.

Later, the landlord was shown Adams’s study. He stood amazed at the massive steel plate sealing the doorway and the high windows, now blocked from floor to ceiling. “He requested this,” the landlord said. “Originally, the windows went down to the wainscoting.” He pointed out where lath and plaster had been added, and how the plate had been installed. But he could offer no explanation for it.

The lamp, too, puzzled him—its shifting lights a mystery. He’d never seen it during his visits, which had always been limited to the parlor. The starling had already been taken to headquarters, for Mr. Gryce’s inspection.

Other leads failed. The insignia found on the wall yielded no clues at pension offices or G.A.R. posts. The portrait painter was unknown in New York. No will or business papers were found in the study—no trace of identity. It was as if Adams had deliberately erased himself from the world.

By late afternoon, reports began trickling in from tradesmen. All spoke of Adams’s odd habits and the strange behavior of his servant. Both were described as hermits—unlike others in their seclusion, they indulged in fine luxuries but avoided society entirely.

Adams never shopped himself. His servant handled all purchases—with remarkable precision. He refused to be cheated, always bought the best, and rarely made mistakes. His demeanor, once calm and methodical, had shifted in recent weeks—more irritable, less patient. One clerk recalled a sudden flare of anger over a minor issue. Another said he seemed to carry a hidden grievance, held back only by force of will.

Mr. Kelly, a grocer, went further: “He looked like a man itching to lash out—but restraining himself.”

Had nothing tragic occurred, these signs might have been dismissed as nervousness. But now, with Adams dead and the servant implicated, their significance grew.

Still, no solid evidence emerged. Mr. Gryce was ready to call the day a loss—when Sweetwater returned.

His face lit with excitement. “What!” Mr. Gryce exclaimed. “You haven’t found her?”

“Not yet, sir,” Sweetwater said, smiling. “But I’d like to borrow the parasol for an hour. I’ll return it. I just need to make a test.”

“What test?” Mr. Gryce asked.

“Please excuse me, sir. I only have a short time before shops close. The test must be done in a respectable home.”

“Then you’ll have it,” Mr. Gryce said. “Wait here—I’ll get it.” He returned swiftly. “Bring it back soon. I don’t have the patience I once did.”

“An hour, sir. Give me an hour—and then—” Sweetwater’s words trailed off as he dashed out.

The hour felt endless to Mr. Gryce—until Sweetwater returned, breathless and agitated, clutching the parasol.

“My test failed,” he said, “but the parasol brought me luck. I’ve found her, sir—and—”

He took a deep breath. “And she’s exactly as I thought—respectable, in a respectable house.”

“Yes, sir,” Sweetwater continued. “More respectable than I expected. Quite a lady—not young, but—”

“Her name,” Mr. Gryce interrupted. “Is it… Evelyn?”

Sweetwater shook his head, his expression innocent. “I didn’t ask, sir. I didn’t dare. But she’s here—”

“Here!” Mr. Gryce stepped toward the door, then stopped himself. “I can wait. If she’s here, she won’t leave until I see her. Are you certain it’s her—the woman from Adams’s house? The one who sent the warning?”

“Let me tell you what happened, sir. It’ll take only a moment. Then you can judge.”

“Your story? It must be a good one. How did you find her so fast? Did you use my clue?”

Sweetwater hesitated. “I’m sorry, sir. I followed my own idea. I couldn’t spend days tracking hack drivers. And I couldn’t assume she used public transport. So I thought of another way.”

He paused. “You showed me those spangles. They were part of a rich trimming—new, expensive, worn mostly by women of means, sold only in upscale shops. I’ve seen such dresses—sewn on black net, in patterns: scrolls, wreaths. The spangles are so bright that any break in the pattern stands out—especially if the net is over a colored dress.”

“Now, I remembered: if she lost spangles on a crime scene, wouldn’t she try to replace them? She’d need extra net—probably wouldn’t have any. So she’d buy a small piece. A strip, maybe.”

“That was my clue. I went to leading stores on Broadway, 23rd Street, Sixth Avenue. I convinced clerks to watch for a woman buying a tiny amount of that bespangled net. Then I waited at Arnold’s emporium—why, I don’t know. Maybe luck.”

“Before mid-afternoon, a clerk gave me a meaningful look. I approached—he handed a small parcel to a young girl, who walked away. ‘For one of our top customers,’ he whispered. ‘I don’t think it’s the one you’re after.’ But I wouldn’t risk it.”

“I followed her—to a fine brownstone in a quiet, aristocratic neighborhood. I saw her enter the basement. I rang the bell above. Then—I bit my lip to keep from shouting.”

“The maid who answered was the same girl. She still held the parcel. I didn’t show interest. I asked, ‘Has your mistress lost a parasol? One has been found—’ I didn’t finish. Her face told me she had. I said, ‘I’ll bring it. If it’s hers, all right,’ and ran down.”

“I meant to report to you. But pride got the better of me. I wanted to be sure. So I asked to borrow the parasol—and went back.”

“I rang again. The same girl opened the door. I said, ‘Here’s the parasol.’ But before I finished, I saw her alarm. ‘That’s not the one my mistress lost,’ she said. ‘She only carries black.’ The door started to close—then a voice called from inside: ‘Let me see that parasol. Hold it up. There! At the foot of the stairs. Ah!’”

“Ah,” Sweetwater repeated. “I’ve never heard a word so full of meaning. I couldn’t see her—only her voice. She was leaning over the banister. I waited—then she spoke again: ‘You’re from Police Headquarters.’”

“I lowered the parasol. I didn’t need to confirm it.”

“She continued: ‘From a man named Gryce.’ I said, ‘Yes, sir. He wants to return your property. Is this yours?’”

“The reply was sharp: ‘Mr. Gryce has made a mistake. The parasol isn’t mine. But he deserves credit for using it so cleverly. I’d like to tell him so. Is he at his office? Would I be received?’”

“I said yes. Then—within minutes—a stately woman descended the stairs. Dressed for the street. Commanding. ‘Have you a cab?’ she asked.”

“No,” I said.

“Then get one.”

“I hesitated. Should I let her escape? Or trust her? She looked honest—very honest. So I went.”

“She was waiting on the stoop when I returned. She entered the cab without hesitation. I saw her just now—sitting in the outer office, waiting for you. Are you ready to say I’ve done well?”

Mr. Gryce studied him, then grasped his hand—his face a mixture of envy and admiration. Without a word, he turned and walked toward the outer office.

His curiosity was unbearable. He found the woman seated calmly, waiting.

He approached. Her presence surprised him—not because she recognized him, but because he recognized her. She was a woman he knew well—someone from a past he rarely revisited. Her name was Amelia Butterworth. Of Gramercy Park. One of the most respected ladies in the city.

And now, she sat before him—silent, composed, and utterly unexpected.

The look Miss Butterworth gave him was one that might have puzzled even the most seasoned observer—especially when directed at a man like Mr. Gryce, who prided himself on reading faces as clearly as he read documents. Yet she seemed to carry herself with a new gravity, a rare discomposure that unsettled her usual composure. Her eyes, usually so sharp and self-assured, now held a flicker of shame.

"Miss Butterworth!" he began, his voice low but edged with surprise. "What does this mean? Have you—"

"There!" she interrupted sharply, cutting off his question. "You’ve caught me in my old ways, and I’m ashamed. You’re triumphant. That gray parasol you sent to my house—it’s not mine. But I *was* in the room where you found it. And since you’ve already guessed it, and I can’t hide from your keenness, I’ve come here to confess."

Mr. Gryce leaned forward, interest flaring in his eyes. He pulled a chair close and sat down. "You were there," he repeated slowly. "When? And for what reason?"

She took a breath, then spoke with quiet resolve. "I must explain—though I dislike exposing my own weakness. But circumstances demand it. I know how surprising it is that I, a complete stranger to Mr. Adams and without any prior justification for meddling in police affairs, should find myself standing before you like this. This wasn’t a case that came to me—I went to it. I wish I’d gone to Jericho first. It might have cost more time and money—but it would have spared me this humiliation and possible exposure."

She paused, gathering herself. "Mr. Gryce, I never meant to get involved in another murder case. I’ve shown some talent for detection, and I’ve earned your approval—though I never let it go to my head. I truly believed I could stay out of such matters. I wanted to return to my own work, to live quietly, without being drawn into mysteries by any lure you might offer. But this time… the situation caught me unprepared. I didn’t resist. I was surprised into involvement. And now I’m ashamed—so deeply ashamed—that I tried to conceal everything. I thought I’d left no trace. Until that parasol appeared, thrust into my face, and I realized you knew I’d been in Mr. Adams’s room before you arrived."

She looked down, almost pleading. "Could you have seen—?"

"No," said Mr. Gryce gently. "But you dropped five small spangles from your dress. You wore a black-sequined gown, didn’t you? And you moved the inkstand. I saw those signs—along with the boy’s description of a well-dressed woman sending him to the drugstore with a message to the police—and I concluded, correctly, that it was this woman who had wielded the dagger. Not the deaf-and-dumb butler, whom everyone has blamed until now. So I set out to find you—never imagining the outcome would be this. Now I must apologize."

"Please don’t," she said quickly. "I had no business being there. Or at least, I shouldn’t have left those spangles behind. I was simply passing by. If I were still the woman I once was—the one who’d never stepped into a mystery—I’d have walked right past. But I stopped. And now I’m faced with the strange sensation of being seen as the cause—or motive—of a terrible crime, even though I’m innocent. Especially after studying crime and criminals for years. I don’t enjoy it. But I brought it on myself. If I hadn’t been so… compelled. Though it wasn’t curiosity. I refuse to admit I’m driven by mere curiosity. It was the look on the young man’s face. But I’m rambling. I should be telling you straight. Not my usual habit—you know that—but I’m not myself tonight. Honestly, I’m more disturbed by this discovery than I ever was by Mr. Trohm’s declaration in Lost Man’s Lane! Give me a moment, Mr. Gryce. In a few minutes, I’ll be coherent."

"I’m giving you time," he replied, bowing slightly. "I have half a dozen questions burning on my lips, but I sit here—patience personified."

She nodded, then suddenly broke in: "So you thought the killer was an outsider?"

"Most of the papers did," he admitted. "And there’s reason to believe it. The case isn’t simple, Miss Butterworth. It has layers. I recognized that immediately. But I won’t waste time on apologies. You’ve a right to share anything you discovered during your visit—especially if it hasn’t been reported elsewhere."

"And there is something," she said. "Something I haven’t heard mentioned. Mr. Adams had other visitors besides me—just before he died. A young man and woman. I saw them leave the house."

"Tell me plainly," he urged. "What first drew your attention to the house?"

She sighed. "That’s the second time you’ve reminded me to be direct. I’ll do better. I noticed the house because I always do. I pass it often, and I always think about its history. Yesterday afternoon, on my way to the art exhibition, I stopped in front of it. And just then—the inner door burst open. A young man came out, carrying a young woman in his arms. She was unconscious. He looked frantic. But before anyone could react, he set her down, took her arm, and pulled her down the steps into the crowd. They vanished quickly."

She paused. "I stood frozen. I watched where they went—and lingered long enough to peek inside. The front door had been left open. As I looked, I heard a cry from within. Muffled, distant, but unmistakable—fear, agony. And something else—an impulse I may regret—drove me up the stoop. I closed the door behind me, so no one else would follow."

"So far, instinct," she continued. "But once inside the dim hall, I hesitated. What was I doing? What excuse would I give if someone found me? Then—another cry, from the farthest room on the parlor floor. And I remembered the young man’s face—the terror in his eyes. That pushed me forward. Before I knew it, I was in the circular study, facing a man lying on the floor."

He was dead—or dying. His chest bore a cross, a dagger in his heart. His right hand trembled. When she knelt to lift his head, he shuddered—and then grew still. She, a stranger from the street, had witnessed his final breath—while the young man who’d fled earlier…

"Can you describe him?" Mr. Gryce asked urgently. "Did you see him closely enough to recognize him?"

"Yes," she said. "I think I could identify him. He wore a checked suit—very neat. Tall, strikingly handsome. But his expression… I’ve never seen anything like it. Not even on stage. A storm of emotion—passion, restraint, horror—all locked in one face. The girl—blonde hair, pale as marble—looked like a saint trapped by a demon. She’d seen death. He… I won’t interpret that face. It was gone from view almost instantly. Probably calmed in the crowd. I wouldn’t remember her."

"And the butler?" Mr. Gryce pressed. "Did you see him? Do you know whether he’s guilty or innocent?"

"I saw no sign of him in the room," she said. "He wasn’t there when I entered, nor did he appear while I was there. But it was *his* cry that led me to the scene—or do you think it was the bird?"

"It might have been," he allowed. "Its call is clear. What was it saying?"

"Something about Eva. 'Lovely Eva, maddening Eva! I love Eva! Eva! Eva!'"

"Eva?" he echoed. "Wasn’t it 'Evelyn'? Poor Evelyn?"

"No," she insisted. "It was Eva. I thought it referred to the girl I’d just seen carried out. Hearing that bird scream over the dead man… it was unsettling."

"You didn’t just stand there," he said. "You knelt. You looked into his face."

"I did," she admitted. "And caught my dress on the filigree of the cross. I couldn’t stand still with a man dying under my gaze."

"And you went to the table?"

"Yes."

"And moved the inkstand?"

"Yes—but carefully. Very carefully."

"Not carefully enough," he said. "The dust showed where it originally stood. Your glasses helped me see it."

"Ah," she murmured. "That’s because you wear yours. I didn’t. I tried to place it exactly where it was."

"Why did you move it?"

"For clues," she said. "I was searching. I moved several things. I was looking for the line of writing that should explain this murder."

"The line of writing?"

"Yes. I haven’t told you what the girl said as she slipped into the crowd."

"No," he said. "You haven’t mentioned any words. Do you have that clue?"

"I heard her," she said. "In a tone of fear: 'You’ve left that line of writing behind!' I didn’t think much of it then. But when I found the dying man, I recalled her words—and searched for that paper."

"And did you find it?"

"If I had," she said, "I would have reported it to the police. I did notify them of the crime."

"Of course," he said. "We’re grateful. But why keep silent about the two people leaving the house? Why not tell us?"

"I waited," she said. "I hoped the police would find them without my help. I wanted my name kept out of it. But now… I feel relief. I couldn’t rest after seeing so much. And I couldn’t stand idly by while false leads were followed and real clues ignored."

"This murder," she added, "is the most complex puzzle I’ve ever encountered. And I wonder—if I’ve been given a chance to redeem myself in your eyes."

He looked at her, curious. "You want to prove yourself?"

"I want to forget my failure in Lost Man’s Lane," she said softly. "It haunts me. Only a fresh success could erase it."

He nodded. "Then you have a theory. You believe the young man with the haunted face killed Mr. Adams."

"Possibly," she said. "I never believed the butler committed the crime. But I also saw something else."

"What?"

"Upstairs. After I found the body, I wanted to raise the alarm. No one answered below. So I went upstairs—and found the butler performing that pantomime described in the papers."

"Ah," he said. "That must have shaken you."

"It did. I fled. But I carried a mystery with me. How could I hear his cries in Mr. Adams’s study—and yet find him upstairs when I searched the house? He couldn’t have climbed the stairs in the time I spent in the hall."

"Perhaps your ears deceived you," he suggested. "Maybe the cries came from above."

"My ears are reliable," she said firmly. "There must be another explanation."

He frowned. "No back stairs. The study doesn’t connect to them."

"But I heard him clearly in the study."

He stared at her. "Then you’ve given me a puzzle."

"And I’ll give you another," she said. "If the butler committed the murder, why didn’t the young couple see him and accuse him? Or if he merely witnessed it—why didn’t the murderer notice him at the door?"

"He may be silent," Mr. Gryce said. "Or perhaps they were too stunned to notice."

"True," she conceded. "But consider the door’s position. If Adams fell where struck, the attacker had the doorway directly in front. He couldn’t miss someone standing there."

"Unless the attackers were too overwhelmed to care," he said. "They escaped. That’s all that mattered."

"Still," she said, "you’ll find them. A man who can track a woman through five spangles will surely find the owner of this parasol."

"Ah," he smiled. "But credit for that belongs to Sweetwater—the young man who brought you here. Not much to look at, but promising. But even he may fail. New York is vast. How did Sweetwater find you?"

"Through my habits," she said. "Had I been careless, torn my dress and not mended it—well, he might have searched forever. But I’m methodical. That helped him."

"And in the search for the parasol’s owner?" he asked.

"She’ll be found," she said confidently. "And through her, the young man. I have faith in your genius—and in Sweetwater’s potential."

"We’ll try," he said. "But I wonder—what else do you know?"

"I didn’t touch the buttons along the table," she said. "I avoid electrical devices. I’m intensely feminine. I don’t even have a telephone. Mechanisms frighten me."

He suppressed a smile. "A pity. Had you brought another light, you might have seen something vital."

"I have something more important to say," she insisted. "This mystery hinges on the line of writing the young people feared leaving behind."

"Ah," he said. "A good starting point. But we’ve found no such paper."

"We searched?" she asked.

"Thoroughly," he said. "We examined every corner, every drawer. Nothing. If there was a scrap in the desk or on his person—"

"His pockets?" she asked.

"Always searched," he said. "Every detail considered."

She looked satisfied—then paused, her eyes drifting to the miniature at her throat.

"Were you aware," she asked slowly, "that Mr. Adams’s face bore an extraordinary expression when I saw him? Not just pain or shock—but something deeper. A look of determination. As if he made a decision at the very moment of death. Can you read that face?"

"I noted it," he said. "It was unusual. But I cannot interpret it."

"Then perhaps Sweetwater can," she murmured. "But he hasn’t been allowed into the room. So I must speak."

She leaned forward. "Let me whisper."

And she breathed a single sentence into his ear.

Mr. Gryce froze. Then rose, his face alight with astonishment—then admiration.

"Madam!" he exclaimed. "Your idea—whether valid or not—is worthy of the mind that conceived it. We shall act on it—immediately."

With a deep bow, he turned and left the room.

Miss Butterworth had been raised in a household where decorum was law. She moved with precision, sat with stillness, and never allowed her body to betray the turmoil within. Fidgeting was an offense she could not comprehend—yet now, alone after her encounter in the house of death, she found herself trembling with suppressed agitation. Her hands clenched at her sides, her breath came too fast. For once, the discipline of years could not hold back the tide of unease rising inside her.

She was anxious—and she admitted it, plainly, without pretense. When Mr. Gryce returned, his presence like a quiet anchor in the storm, she exhaled almost imperceptibly. He greeted her with a smile that held a trace of amusement, though his eyes remained sharp.

“Well,” he said lightly, “that is seen to. All we need do now is wait for results. Madam, do you have any further thoughts? If so, I should be glad to hear them.”

Her composure snapped back into place. “You would?” she repeated, narrowing her gaze slightly. “I should like to know first whether your judgment values my earlier suggestion before I offer another. Let us discuss this together—compare what we’ve observed. For instance, why neither of us considers Bartow the murderer?”

“I thought we’d already settled that,” he replied. “Your suspicions were stirred by the young couple you saw fleeing. Mine… well, madam, I may confess something to you: there is something in the mute’s gestures—an odd rhythm, a certain detachment—that suggests he is reenacting what he *saw*, not what he *did*. And I have yet to see reason to doubt that.”

“Precisely,” she said. “I felt the same. Even if I hadn’t already suspected others, I would have sensed it. It’s more natural for a man to go mad from witnessing a crime than from committing one.”

“Yes—if he loved the victim.”

“And did Bartow love Mr. Adams?”

“He does not mourn him.”

“But he is no longer master of his faculties.”

“True. But if we take any of his actions as clues, we must take all. We believe his movements are a literal copy of what he witnessed. Then why ignore the sudden flash of dark joy that crosses his face when the act ends? It’s as if he *rejoices* in it—or finds deep satisfaction.”

“Perhaps it’s still part of the imitation,” she countered. “The killer might have laughed. But no—the young man I saw running from the scene carried no such expression. Only horror. Mr. Gryーce, we’re in deep water. I feel myself nearly overwhelmed.”

“Hold your head up, madam,” he said firmly. “Every flood has its ebb. If you surrender, what becomes of me?”

“You are being playful,” she observed, “which is a good sign. You only jest when you’re not truly baffled. Somewhere, you must see light.”

“Let us continue,” he said. “Insight often comes from the least expected quarter.”

“Very well,” she agreed. “Then let us set aside the old man’s laughter for now—it’s another mystery. Have you considered him as an accomplice?”

“I have. But the idea fails under scrutiny. One doesn’t go mad from anticipating horror. Shock shatters the mind. He wasn’t expecting his master’s death.”

“Exactly. So we can dismiss that. Bartow was an innocent witness. He saw the murder, and since he fears nothing, we may trust that his pantomime reflects the truth.”

“Agreed. Continue, madam. Your reasoning is always worth hearing.”

His tone was grave, his manner impeccable—but Miss Butterworth, ever alert for irony, stiffened just slightly. She did not show it, but the old detective had long since learned to read her.

“Let us reconstruct his movements carefully,” she began. “He must have entered the study from the doorway, seeing the attack from behind his master’s back—so directly in front of him. What was his first action?”

“At present,” she said, “he raises his right arm, stretches it backward, leans forward as if reaching for a dagger. What does that mean?”

“I cannot say,” Mr. Gryce replied. “But when I arrived, he was thrusting with his left hand across his own body—a forceful motion. That is significant.”

“It is,” she said. “Especially since the doctors agree: Mr. Adams was struck by a left-handed blow.”

“Do you see the problem now?”

“The problem?” he asked.

“If Bartow stood face-to-face with the attacker, imitating him in his confused state, he would raise the arm opposite to the one the attacker used. That means the assailant must have struck with his *right* hand.”

She paused, then added: “Try it yourself. Watch me lift my hand. See how you instinctively raise the one on the other side? It’s like looking in a mirror. Turn your head right, and the reflection turns left.”

Mr. Gryce laughed aloud—unintentionally, perhaps, but with genuine surprise. He rarely lost ground, but this woman had a way of catching him off guard. And rather than resent it, he found himself amused.

“Very well,” he conceded. “You’ve proven your point. But what follows? Does this mean the attacker wasn’t left-handed? Or that he wasn’t positioned where we assumed?”

“We cannot contradict the doctors,” she said. “They confirm a left-handed strike. But suppose—just suppose—we were wrong about Bartow’s position? Or that he’s not mirroring the attacker at all?”

She hesitated, then continued: “Mr. Gryce, I would give anything for an hour with you in Mr. Adams’s study—Bartow free to move, unobserved. I believe we’d learn more in ten minutes watching him than in hours of talk.”

It was said tentatively, almost shyly. The suggestion was bold, even reckless, given her usual reserve.

“I don’t wish to be intrusive,” she added, smiling faintly.

So far from being disagreeable, her words startled him. For once, Mr. Gryce reacted impulsively.

“I think it might be arranged,” he said. “After the funeral, at least. There are too many officials here now.”

“Of course,” she nodded. “I wouldn’t dream of imposing. But the case is so compelling—and my involvement so peculiar—that I sometimes lose control. Do you think…” She faltered, her voice tightening. “…do you think I’ve exposed myself to the coroner?”

He studied her, then her folded hands, with a look of quiet sympathy.

“The facts come slowly,” he said. “I doubt the inquest will be held for days. In the meantime, we may find those two young people ourselves. If we do, the coroner might *overlook* your role in bringing them to our attention.”

There was a subtle emphasis on the word *overlook*, and a glint in his eye that spoke of mischief. But Miss Butterworth didn’t flinch. She was preoccupied with something deeper.

“Ah,” she murmured. “If they had been the only ones I met. But they weren’t. Another person entered the house before I left. And I may be forced to speak of him.”

“Of him?” Mr. Gryce leaned forward. “Madam, you are a treasure trove of information.”

“Yes, sir,” she replied, meekly—though the tone was unexpected from her.

“I should have mentioned him sooner. But I dislike mixing matters. And this elderly gentleman…”

“Elderly gentleman?”

“Yes, sir. Very old, very dignified. He seemed unrelated to the crime—except for knowing the victim.”

“Ah,” said Mr. Gryce. “But that’s a significant connection. To know Mr. Adams—really, madam, patience has its limits. Please, speak.”

“Oh, I will,” she said. “The time has come. And this new subject is deeply intriguing.”

“Then begin,” he said dryly. “A full account, madam. Nothing left out.”

“I appreciate the sarcasm,” she replied. “But nothing has been omitted—except what I’m about to tell you. It happened just as I was leaving.”

“What?” he pressed. “I hate to ask, but justice demands clarity.”

“As I stepped out,” she said, “I encountered an elderly gentleman entering. His hand had just touched the bell pull. It was a disconcerting moment. His face—well preserved for his age—bore an air of cheerful expectation. He looked at me in surprise. My expression, however calm I usually am, must have shown distress. Neither of us spoke. Finally, he asked politely if he might enter. He mentioned an appointment in the study.”

“I stepped aside,” she continued. “I could not let a man nearing eighty—excuse me, not every man of eighty is as robust as you—enter such a scene alone. Besides, I hadn’t warned him of the condition of the only living occupant.”

“Discreet,” Mr. Gryce said. “Quite as expected. And you followed him down the hall?”

“Of course! What else could I do? He was in a strange house, no servant to guide him. He wanted directions. I showed him to the study.”

“Kind of you,” he said. “And it must have been… interesting.”

“Too interesting,” she confessed. “I’m not made entirely of steel, sir. The shock he received—finding a dead man instead of a live one—was contagious. Yet I stood firm.”

“Admirable,” he said. “I could not have done better. So this man—someone who had an appointment with Mr. Adams—was genuinely shocked?”

“Shocked, surprised, terrified—and something else. That ‘something’ troubles me. Was it morbid fascination? A realization of danger escaped? Or a dread yet to come? I cannot say. But I swear, there was more to his reaction than mere surprise.”

“He made no sound after the first cry,” she went on. “No movement. Just stared—at the body, then at the face. At first, curiosity. Then hatred. Then terror. And finally… something worse. A kind of hellish humor—almost laughter—had it not been for the bird.”

She paused.

“The bird in the high cage—until then unnoticed—woke suddenly, thrusting its beak through the bars and shrieking: *‘Remember Evelyn!’*”

“That startled him more than the corpse,” she said. “He turned, his fist rising as if to ward off an intruder. But then he saw the painting above the fireplace—and cringed, painfully, like a man cornered. He backed away until he reached the doorway. Paused. Looked again at the body. And I heard him whisper: *‘It is Amos’s son, not Amos. Is it fatality… or did he plan this meeting?’*”

Then—he saw me in the antechamber. Instantly, his demeanor changed. He bowed low, opened his mouth as if to speak—but stopped himself. He strode past me, silent, toward the front door.

Being an intruder myself, I did not stop him. But now I regret it. Just before stepping outside, he broke into a harsh, raucous laugh—one that chilled me more than the sight of the dead man. And when the door closed behind him, he wore the same easy, self-satisfied smile he had worn when he entered.”

Mr. Gryce sat back, silence hanging between them.

“Remarkable,” he said at last. “A secret history buried in that man. He must be found. He holds keys to Mr. Adams’s past. *Amos’s son*—he called him? Mr. Adams’s name was Felix. Felix, the son of Amos. A curious link. Not common. If we publish it, someone may recognize it. Perhaps it will lead us to a clue we’ve missed.”

“Your visit was productive,” he added. “Did you stay longer after he left?”

“No,” she said. “I had had enough. Ashamed of my curiosity, I sent a street boy to inform the police. I wished to escape all responsibility. But fate has other plans. A trace remains—evidence of my presence. Whether I like it or not, I am entangled.”

“Was it Providence?” she mused. “Perhaps. The future will decide.”

She rose. “Now, Mr. Gryce, since I’ve shared everything, and the hour is late, I’ll take my leave. If you hear from that scrap of paper—”

“If I do,” he said, “I’ll let you know. It would be the least I could do for a lady who has helped me so much.”

“You flatter me,” she said. “Proof that I’ve stayed a moment too long. Good evening, Mr. Gryce.”

“What?” he said. “You haven’t stayed too long? I have something else to ask.”

“Yes?” she said, pausing.

“Concerning a matter personal to you. May I inquire if you wore the same bonnet yesterday that you wear today?”

“No, sir,” she replied. “I know you have reason. Yesterday, I was in reception attire—my bonnet was black, with short strings.”

“With long strings tied under the chin?”

“No. Long strings are unfashionable now.”

“But you wore something that fell from your neck?”

“Yes—a feather boa.”

“How did you know?” she asked.

“You wrote it on the table,” he said. “Or so I dare to believe. You or the young woman—did she wear ribbons or streamers?”

“I cannot say. I only saw her face and the skirt of a dove-colored silk dress.”

“Then settle this for me,” he said. “If your boa bears the faintest trace of blood—any hint of having touched a dying man—then the streaks on the table were yours. But if it’s clean, or too short to reach the body… then it was the young woman who touched the table—not the one you saw collapse.”

She sighed. “I fear it will be my boa that tells the tale. Another proof of human fallibility—especially female.”

And with that humble admission—perhaps not entirely sincere—she departed.

Did Mr. Gryce feel guilt for extracting so much while revealing so little? Doubtful. His conscience, in certain places, was long since hardened.

The morning after the discovery of Mr. Adams’s body, a brief note arrived at Mr. Gryce’s office—almost simultaneously with one he had dispatched to Miss Butterworth. Hers read simply:
*You were quite correct. So far as I can determine, I was the only person who leaned over Mr. Adams’s study table after his death. I’ve had to trim the ends of my boa.*

His reply was equally concise:
*My compliments, madam! Mr. Adams’s jaws have been forced apart. A small piece of paper was found clenched between his teeth. It has been recovered and will be read at the inquest. Perhaps a few favored individuals may see it beforehand—yourself included.*

Of the two messages, Mr. Gryce’s stirred the greater excitement in its recipient. Miss Butterworth’s composure was unshakable—a quiet pride that could not be shared, and thus became the subject of whispered speculation among those around her. At Police Headquarters, meanwhile, detectives spent hours sifting through the flood of half-remembered tales from eager informants. The first day’s silence had given way to a deluge of claims: neighbors swore they’d seen figures entering or leaving the house on the afternoon of the murder. But when pressed for details, their accounts dissolved into vague impressions—“a gentleman in a long coat,” “a young woman with a gray feather,” “a pale face”—all useless, all unverifiable. One elderly woman claimed to have seen a lady open the door to a man in a greatcoat; another, breathless and stylishly dressed, described a young couple rushing past—him pale, her even paler, wearing a gray feather in her hat. None could identify them again. The stories confirmed nothing new. By nightfall, the investigation remained stalled.

The following day brought Mr. Adams’s funeral. With no known relatives or close friends appearing, his landlord oversaw the rites, and his banker served as chief mourner. As the coffin left the house, half a dozen detectives mingled with the crowd, scanning faces, but found no leads. At the cemetery, nothing unfolded. The case now rested entirely on what had already been gathered—and on intuition.

That evening, Mr. Gryce slipped away from Headquarters alone. He walked to the darkened house where the crime had occurred, entered with a key, and lit every room. In the study, he observed that the cross had been returned to the wall, the birdcage rehung from its ceiling hook, and everything restored to order—save for the broken casings flanking the doorway. The steel plate had been reinserted, but the blue glow visible through the hole in the antechamber wall remained jarringly out of place. With the precision of a housewife, Mr. Gryce pulled two rugs from the parlor and stretched them across the openings. Then, checking his watch, he noted it was nearly nine o’clock. He took position behind the parlor curtains.

Soon, a carriage stopped outside. A woman stepped down. Mr. Gryce hurried to the door.

“Madam, your punctuality matches mine,” he said.

“Only as far as the corner,” she replied, stepping inside. “My coachman will wait for your whistle.”

“Excellent. Are you afraid to remain alone while I watch for the others? At present, we are the only ones here.”

“If I were afraid, I wouldn’t be here at all. Besides, the house is well lit. I notice you’ve used a crimson light in the study.”

“Because a crimson light burned when Mr. Adams died.”

“Remember Evelyn!” called a voice from within.

“Oh, you’ve brought back the bird!” Miss Butterworth exclaimed. “But that’s not the cry it made before. It used to call ‘Eva! Lovely Eva!’ Do you suppose Eva and Evelyn are the same?”

“Madam, we have too many riddles to dwell on this one. I expect Mr. Adams’s valet shortly.”

“Sir, you relieve me of a great burden. I feared I might not be allowed to witness this test.”

“Miss Butterworth, you’ve earned your place. Bartow has been given a key—he’ll enter freely, as he always did. We must observe him without being seen.”

“In this room? I don’t think I’d like to face him directly.”

“You won’t need to. We don’t want to startle him. Even if he’s unbalanced, he must move naturally—without distraction. We’re looking for clues in his habits. I’ll place you where he’s least likely to see you—in this parlor. Evidence suggests it was rarely opened during Mr. Adams’s lifetime.”

“You’ll need to extinguish the gas. Unfamiliar light might draw his attention.”

“I’ll turn it off—and draw the portières tight. Just leave a small opening for your eye, and one for mine. A simple trick—but effective.”

Miss Butterworth gave a faint snort—a sound barely audible, yet betraying her mixed emotions. Had she been curious, she might have enjoyed the suspense. But she was there out of duty. She likely blushed behind the curtain—though the moment passed before she could reflect, for the front door opened once more.

A soft “Hush!” from Mr. Gryce confirmed her suspicion. The valet had arrived.

She strained to listen, watching the antechamber. Bartow appeared sooner than expected. He set his hat on a nearby table and approached the portière—his usual routine. But this time, he paused. He didn’t touch it. Was it memory? Or the unusual placement of the rugs? Neither, it seemed. He turned away with a blank expression, picked up his hat, and ascended quietly.

“I’ll follow him,” Mr. Gryce whispered. “Don’t worry, ma'am. My whistle will summon help instantly.”

“I’m not afraid. I’d be ashamed—”

But she couldn’t finish. Mr. Gryce was already gone.

He returned quickly, shaking his head. “It seems our experiment may fail. He’s gone to his room, preparing for bed.” He led her into the study and, perhaps absentmindedly, pressed a button on the table. Instantly, the room shifted under a violet glow.

Miss Butterworth marveled at the effect—until the sound of footsteps echoed below. They barely had time to retreat behind the portières when Bartow rushed in, moving with his usual briskness. He paused briefly at the threshold—then stepped inside, ignoring the rug laid over the spot where Mr. Adams had fallen. He retrieved a bottle of wine and a glass, wiped it clean, placed the dressing gown and slippers by the side, and entered the bedroom.

From their hiding place, they watched. Bartow bent over the comb Mr. Gryce had left on the floor. He lifted it, shook his head, and returned it to the dresser. Then he turned down the bed.

“Poor fool,” murmured Miss Butterworth. “He remembers nothing but routine. We’ll learn nothing from him.”

Then both froze. Bartow stood in the center of the study, staring intently at his master’s empty chair. His gaze shifted to the rug—where the body had lain. Suddenly, he seemed to awaken. He began mimicking the movements of the killer—repeating the exact sequence of actions witnessed earlier.

Then he crept silently upstairs.

Mr. Gryce and Miss Butterworth stepped into the light, exchanging glances of discouragement. But then Miss Butterworth brightened. “Try another color. I think we can bring him back.”

Mr. Gryce raised an eyebrow. “Do you believe he can see through the floor?”

But he pressed the button. The violet vanished, replaced by a rich blue.

Nothing happened.

Miss Butterworth frowned. “I trust your theories, sir—but how can he perceive signals from above, when there’s no connection between the rooms?”

She held up a finger. “Hark!”

Footsteps descended.

This time, Bartow moved slowly, haltingly. He entered the study, approached the table, and reached out as if expecting to find something. When he saw nothing, he looked up at the shelves, then back at the table—bewildered. He shook his head, collapsed, and fell asleep in the nearest chair.

Miss Butterworth drew a long breath. “Now do you see?”

“I still don’t understand. How can he know which light is burning?”

She smiled faintly. “When I came upstairs before—when I was here last—I noticed something small. You probably saw it too. It might explain why Bartow sees these lights.”

“I thought I searched thoroughly.”

“And the halls?”

“And the halls. Nothing escaped me.”

“Or perhaps you weren’t looking low enough.”

“On the floor?”

“The floor tells stories. It shows where someone walks most often—where they have business. You must have noticed the wear on the woodwork near the edge of the carpet on the little landing.”

“In the round of the staircase?”

“Yes.”

Mr. Gryce didn’t respond immediately. He climbed the stairs, pausing at the spot she indicated. “You see, it’s been worn here.”

“It’s been trodden heavily. But why? There’s no reason to walk back and forth in this corner. The stair is wide. No window—”

Mr. Gryce’s eyes scanned the wall. He reached past her shoulder, pulled aside one of the dozen pictures hanging along the stairway—and revealed a round opening. Through it streamed a ray of blue light, clearly from the study beneath.

“No window,” he repeated.

“No. But an opening into the study wall. Miss Butterworth, your observation is flawless. I wonder why you didn’t pull it aside yourself.”

“It wasn’t crooked then. And I was in a hurry. I’d just fled from that man. I saw the marks on the landing and the worn carpet on my way up. I had no time to examine them on the way down.”

Mr. Gryce nodded. “So he came here regularly—to check on his master.”

“Exactly. Ingenious of Mr. Adams to devise signals. He clearly relied on Bartow.”

“One mystery solved,” said Mr. Gryce. “And perhaps another. You’ve wondered why Bartow struck with his left hand—when he’s right-handed?”

“If he saw the attack from this vantage point, he would have seen it over the assailant’s shoulder. So he imitated the motion exactly—turning in the same direction, striking with the same hand.”

“Ah. Now we begin to unravel the threads.”

“Look—there’s the bird!” Miss Butterworth exclaimed. “Its cage must be almost under this hole.”

“A little to one side—but close enough to startle you. What did it say then?”

“‘Poor Eva!’ Sympathetic words.”

“Now look at Bartow. You can see him clearly from here.”

She peered through the opening—and let out a sharp grunt. For her, that meant surprise.

“He’s shaking his fist. He looks ready to kill the bird.”

“Perhaps that’s why the cage was hung so high. Mr. Adams had reasons for everything.”

“I don’t care to go back there,” she said. “He looks murderous.”

“And very weak in arm, madam. You’re safe under my protection. Mine is not feeble.”

At the base of the staircase, Mr. Gryce paused briefly, excused himself, and called in a few men he had left waiting outside. Together, they removed the valet’s body before escorting Miss Butterworth back into the study. Once silence returned and they could speak freely, Mr. Gryce said:
"One very important matter has been resolved by the experiment we just conducted. Bartow is cleared of any involvement in this crime."

"Then we may focus entirely on the young people," she replied. "Have you heard from them at all?"
"No."
"And the old man who laughed?"
"Not a word."

Miss Butterworth looked visibly disappointed. "I thought—well, it seemed almost certain—that the scrap of writing you found would reveal their identities. If it was significant enough for the dying man to try to swallow it, surely it must contain some clue to his attacker."

"Alas, it does not," Mr. Gryce answered. "It was an illegible scrawl, madam, something like this: 'I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!' And signed, 'Amos's son.'"

"Amos's son!" she exclaimed. "That must be Mr. Adams himself."

"We have every reason to believe so."

"Strange. Unfathomable. And yet that paper was clutched between his teeth! Was the handwriting recognized?"

"Yes. It matches his own, as far as we can judge from the samples of his signature on the fly-leaves of his books."

"Then the mystery deepens. The fact that he preserved this note with such desperate care—even in death’s throes—suggests it was hidden where no one could search for it. And the girl—her first words upon regaining consciousness were: 'You have left that line of writing behind.' Mr. Gryce, those few words, though cryptic, may hold the key to everything. Would you repeat them, please, sentence by sentence?"

"With pleasure, madam. I’ve repeated them often enough to myself. First: 'I return your daughter to you!'"

"So!" Miss Butterworth said. "Mr. Adams had custody of someone’s daughter—and now returns her. Whose daughter? Certainly not that young man’s, or she would be a child. And if these words were meant for his assailant, why go to such lengths to conceal them from him?"

"Exactly. I’ve asked myself the same question."

"But if not for the killer, then for whom? For the elderly gentleman who arrived later?"

"It’s possible. Since learning of him, I’ve considered it among the possibilities—especially since the next phrase reads: 'She is here.' The only woman present moments before his arrival was the fair-haired girl you saw carried out."

"True. But why assume the note was written just now? It might be an old fragment, referencing past sorrows or secrets."

"The evidence suggests otherwise," Mr. Gryce countered. "The paper was torn from a sheet lying on the desk that afternoon. And the pen—notice how it lay, displaced, on the far edge of the table."

"Certainly, sir. At the time, I didn’t grasp its significance. So these words are connected to the girl I saw. And she is not Evelyn—or he wouldn’t have repeated the bird’s catchword, 'Remember Evelyn!' I wonder—could she *be* Evelyn?" She pointed toward the large portrait on the wall.

"We may call her that for now. Such a sorrowful face might well belong to someone burdened by a family secret. But how do we explain the violent act of the young man—whom the note never mentions? It’s all a mystery, madam—a mystery we’re wasting time trying to solve."

"Yet I cannot bring myself to abandon it without effort. Those words—you haven’t repeated all of them."

"They came before 'Remember Evelyn!' They expressed a final resolve: 'Neither she nor you will ever see me again.'"

"Ah!" she said softly. "Those words carry weight, Mr. Gryce. Could he have struck himself? Might this have been suicide after all?"

"Madam, you have the right to question—but from Bartow’s gestures, you must have seen he mimics not self-inflicted harm, but a furious thrust from an enraged hand. Let us hold to our original conclusions. That said—and to be fair to all possibilities—the state of Mr. Adams’s affairs, and the absence of family documents or personal papers, suggest he had prepared for death. It may have come sooner than expected, in a different way—but clearly, he had anticipated it. And now, madam, I have a confession to make. I have not been entirely honest with my most trusted colleague. This study—this extraordinary room—holds a secret I have not shared with you. A peculiar one. It was revealed to me in rather startling fashion. This room could be sealed off completely from the rest of the house—turned into a death trap, or more accurately, a tomb. Perhaps this enigmatic man intended to die here. Look at this steel plate. It slides across the doorway via a hidden mechanism. The other night, I was behind that plate. These holes were drilled to let me escape."

"Ha!" she breathed. "Detectives truly have their trials. I would not have enjoyed that evening with you. But what an ancient tragedy we’re uncovering! I declare"—and she rubbed her eyes—"I feel transported back to medieval times. Who would think we’re in New York, within earshot of cable cars and telegraph wires?"

"Some men," Mr. Gryce replied, "can bring the medieval into Wall Street. I believe Mr. Adams was one such man. Romanticism colored every act of his life—even his death. And it followed him beyond. Witness the cross we found resting on his chest."

"That was placed there by another," she said. "A sign of superstition. It implies a priest—or a woman—was present when he died."

"Yet," Mr. Gryce continued, with a thoughtful expression, "he must have possessed some practical sense. All his devices functioned perfectly. He was a mechanical genius as well as a lover of secrets."

"A curious blend," she mused. "Strange that we don’t feel his spirit lingering in this room. I could almost wish we did. Then perhaps we’d find the key to this puzzle."

"That," Mr. Gryce said quietly, "can happen in only one way. You’ve already suggested it. We must locate the young couple who were present during his final struggle. If we fail, the case is lost."

"And so," she said, "we return to where we began—convinced we are utterly lost in the woods."

Miss Butterworth rose. She felt, at least, that she had reached the limit of her faculties. Mr. Gryce made no attempt to detain her. Indeed, he seemed eager to leave himself. Still, they paused long enough to take one last look around. As they did, Miss Butterworth slowly raised a finger.

"See?" she said. "From this side of the wall, you can barely detect the opening created by removing that picture on the landing. Doesn’t it seem hidden among those folds of dark tapestry up there?"

"Yes," he replied. "I’d already identified that spot. With the painting hung on the opposite side, it would be completely invisible."

"One must keep their eyes moving in a case like this," she observed. "That picture must have been pulled aside several times while we were here. Yet we missed it."

"Because we weren’t looking high enough," Mr. Gryce said. "High and low, madam. What happens at eye level is obvious to everyone."

He smiled as he acknowledged her parting remark and turned to escort her to the door. But the smile held less irony than sorrow. Was he beginning to realize that time leaves its mark—even on the sharpest minds? That neither high places nor low escapes notice forever?

“A blonde, you say, sir?”
“Yes, Sweetwater—not the usual type, but one of those delicate, ethereal creatures we find so hard to connect with crime. He, on the other hand—according to Miss Butterworth’s description—and hers is always reliable—is precisely the kind of gentlemanly athlete whose imposing stature and commanding presence draw attention wherever he goes. Together, they’d be unmistakable. But why should we believe they’ll be found together?”

“How were they dressed?”
“Like people of fashion and respectability. He wore a brown-checked suit, freshly tailored; she, a dove-gray dress with white trimmings. The parasol reveals the color of her hat and plumes. Both were young, and—again, by Miss Butterworth’s account—of sensitive temperament, unused to violence or wrongdoing. She was fainting when carried from the house; he, though under every pressure to remain calm, was overwhelmed by emotion. Had he not quickly set her down in the vestibule and vanished into the crowd with her, he would have been surrounded at once. Do you think you can trace them, Sweetwater?”

“Have you no clue to their identity beyond this parasol?”
“None, Sweetwater—unless it’s these few faded rose petals found on the floor of Mr. Adams’s study.”

“You’ve given me quite a puzzle, Mr. Gryce,” the young detective replied, eyeing the parasol offered to him while letting the rose leaves slip through his fingers. “I don’t feel the same confidence I did before. Now that I see how much depends on chance, I’m more aware of the difficulty. If Miss Butterworth hadn’t been so precise, I’d have failed earlier—as I likely will now. Still, I’ll try again to find the owner of this parasol, if only to learn from failure. And now, sir—where do you suppose I’ll go first? To a florist—with these rose petals. Because every other officer would start with the parasol, I’ll begin with the roses. I may be egotistical, but I can’t help it. The parasol means nothing to me.”

“And what hope do you have from the petals? How could a florist help you find this woman?”
“He might identify the kind of rose they came from. Once I know that, I might trace the bouquet back to the shop where it was sold—to the couple who stood out so sharply that day.”

“Possibly. I’m not one to discourage anyone’s method. Everyone has their way—until proven useless, I say nothing.”

Sweetwater, now full of energy and determination, bowed. He was content to proceed as he saw fit. “I may return in an hour—or not for a week,” he said as he left.

“Good luck,” was all Mr. Gryce replied.

The interview ended. A few minutes later, Sweetwater was gone.

An hour passed—no sign of him. Then a day. Another followed, marked only by brief notes exchanged between Mr. Gryce and Miss Butterworth.

Hers was short, crisp, and characteristic:
*I do not presume to dictate—or even suggest—to the New York police. But have you asked the postman in a certain district whether he recalls the postmark on any letters delivered to Mr. Adams?*

Mr. Gryce read it with a smile—perhaps because of its brevity, perhaps because of its precision.

His reply, however, was received with a frown by Miss Butterworth:
*Suggestions are welcome—even dictation, from you. But the postman handles too many letters to remember postmarks. Sorry to close another path.*

Meanwhile, both men grew anxious—Mr. Gryce especially so. His relief was palpable when, on the third morning, Sweetwater arrived at the office, his face lit with quiet triumph. He had waited to share his findings with his mentor alone. As soon as they were alone, he turned to Mr. Gryce, eyes bright.

“News, sir—good news! I’ve found them. Both of them. And by such a fortunate turn! It seemed like a blind trail—until the florist said those petals might have come from a bride rose. Well, sir, any woman can carry bride roses—but then I remembered: her companion’s clothes looked fresh from the tailor, and she wore gray and white. That gave me an idea. I began searching for this pair at the Bureau of Vital Statistics.”

“Brilliant!” Mr. Gryce exclaimed. “If it worked.”

“It did, sir—it did. I may have been born under a lucky star. But once I started, I went straight to the end. Shall I tell you how?”

He continued: “I searched through marriage records from the past two weeks. There was one name—Eva Poindexter. Eva—well known in the house on —— Street. I decided to follow up.”

“Wise choice. And how did you proceed?”

“I went directly to the clergyman who performed the ceremony. A kind man—very affable. No trouble talking to him.”

“And you described the bride?”

“No. I guided the conversation so *he* described her.”

“Good. What did he say? Delicate? Pale?”

“Sir, he hadn’t seen such a lovely bride in years. Very slight—almost fragile—but beautiful, with a soft bloom indicating better health than her figure suggested. It was a private wedding—in a hotel parlor. Her father was there.”

“Her father?” Mr. Gryce’s theory faltered.

Then he recalled—the old man laughing as he left Mr. Adams’s house—was not the father mentioned in the letter. Or this woman was not the one referred to.

“Is something wrong?” Sweetwater asked.

Mr. Gryce regained composure. “No. I didn’t expect him to attend. That’s all.”

“But he was there,” Sweetwater insisted. “The groom—”

“Yes, tell me about him.”

“The very man you described—tall, well-built, dignified, gentlemanly. His clothes matched exactly: brown-checked, latest cut. Oh, he’s the one!”

Mr. Gryce glanced down at his inkstand, recalling the final lines written by Mr. Adams before death:

How could this relate to a newly married woman? It sounded as if she were his own daughter—his bride.

There must be a mistake. Sweetwater had been misled by coincidence.

He dropped the inkstand lid with a clatter—making Sweetwater smile.

“I’m afraid it’s been a fool chase, Sweetwater. Your facts—especially the marriage—don’t align with what we’ve learned from other clues. They suggest this woman’s husband wouldn’t be Thomas Adams—but Felix. And the evidence I now have is direct and unassailable. You’ll have to start over.”

But Sweetwater remained calm, a faint smile playing on his lips.

“You haven’t asked where I’ve been since last we met. The investigations I mentioned took less than a day.”

“Very true. Where, then?”

“To Montgomery, sir—the small town in Pennsylvania where Mr. Poindexter and his daughter registered.”

“Ah, I see. And what did you learn? Something crucial?”

“I learned that John Poindexter—father of Eva—had a childhood friend named Amos Cadwalader.”

“Amos?” Mr. Gryce repeated, his gaze sharp.

“Yes. And Amos had a son—Felix.”

“Ah!”

“You see, sir—we’re on the right track. Coincidences don’t stretch across half a dozen names.”

“You’re right. I jumped to conclusions too fast. Tell me about Amos. You gathered his story?”

“All I could, sir. It’s tightly woven with Poindexter’s—and contains one detail that surprised me, though it may not surprise you. Though Felix’s father, his name wasn’t Adams. It was Cadwalader. He died six months ago. And Felix used that name in early life—alongside a sister whose name—”

“Well?”

“Was Evelyn.”

“Sweetwater, you’re admirable. So the mystery is ours.”

“The history, not the mystery. That still remains. Shall I tell you what I know of these families?”

“At once. I’m as eager as if I were twenty-three and in your shoes.”

“Very well, sir. John Poindexter and Amos Cadwalader were inseparable friends in youth. Both emigrated from Scotland and settled in Montgomery in the 1830s. They married there—Poindexter prospered early; Cadwalader struggled, nearly bankrupt when the Civil War broke out. He enlisted, while Poindexter stayed home, caring for his own family—and for Cadwalader’s two children, whom he took into his house. Poindexter had no children until 1880, when Eva was born. Cadwalader rose to captain—but was captured and imprisoned in Libby Prison, enduring horrors until released in 1865. Broken and aged, he returned home—only to arrive too late. His daughter, Evelyn, had died four years prior. He stepped off the train unaware, walking toward the green square where mourners gathered for her funeral. Pale and frail, few recognized him. One neighbor led him aside—and told him the truth.

“The shock didn’t weaken him—it strengthened him. He ran to Poindexter’s house just as the funeral procession emerged. Evelyn lay on an open bier, carried by six lads. When her father saw her, he fell to his knees—crying out in agony.

“At that moment, another cry rose—from behind the bier. John Poindexter reeled beside Felix Cadwalader, the youngest present, yet the only one who kept his composure.

“The grieving father tore at the black pall to see his daughter’s face—but was stopped not by Poindexter—who had vanished—but by Felix. With a pale, rigid hand, he placed it firmly on the cloth—denying access. When his father resisted, Felix held him back, guiding him to the spot where Poindexter had stood moments before. The bearers resumed their journey.

“But the drama wasn’t over. As they neared the churchyard, another procession emerged—carrying Evelyn’s lover, who had died of grief. He was laid beside her—but not in the same grave. All noticed. Most eyes were on Cadwalader, who had returned from hell to witness this double burial.

“Whether he understood the meaning then—or waited until he walked away with Felix—no one knows. From that moment until they left town next day, no one spoke to him except Poindexter and the lover’s mother. He appeared like a ghost—and vanished like one.

“Twenty years later, a boy claimed he saw a tall, long-bearded man standing by Evelyn’s grave at night, holding a child aloft as if to hurl him at the village. The boy described the man—matching Amos Cadwalader. The next morning, a child’s shoe was found on the grave. Many believed it was real. Others thought it imagination—fed by romance novels. Why, they asked, didn’t he visit Poindexter? The man who had sheltered his children? Poindexter himself doubted it—though he looked odd when he said so. Since Evelyn’s death, he had distanced himself from the Cadwaladers.

“Felix, meanwhile, was feared—even as a child—though never harsh. A year ago, word came of Amos Cadwalader’s death. No details of his family or burial. That’s all I’ve learned.”

Mr. Gryce sat silent.

“Do you suspect Evelyn’s death wasn’t natural?”

“No, sir. I spoke to the mother of the young man who killed himself upon hearing of Evelyn’s illness. If there’d been doubt, she couldn’t have hidden it. But there was none. Evelyn was always frail. Consumption took her quickly. Her lover—devastated—followed her. No mystery—except the rupture between two families once bound by friendship.”

“And yet—here they are again. Thomas Adams marries Eva Poindexter. But who is Thomas Adams?”

“Unless he’s the child held above Evelyn’s grave.”

“Humph. That’s speculative. What did you learn about him in Montgomery?”

“As well as any stranger can be known—when courting a young woman. He arrived a few months ago, from Paris, I believe. Charming, cultivated—he won Eva’s heart and her father’s approval. But not everyone in town approves. Some speak of his restless, uneasy eyes.”

“Naturally. He can’t marry all their daughters.”

“This history, Sweetwater—while tragic—is incomplete. A girl dead thirty years ago. A father vanished. A brother changed his name from Cadwalader to Adams. An Eva—whose name echoes that of the buried Evelyn—repeated in the house where Felix Adams lies dead, with those cryptic words clenched in his teeth. It’s a tangle—a perfect knot. We haven’t found the thread yet. But we will. I won’t be defeated in my old age by problems I’d have laughed at twenty years ago.”

“But how will we know the right thread among fifty?”

“First—find the couple. Didn’t you say you already had?”

“Yes. The postmaster in Montgomery told me a letter addressed to Mrs. Thomas Adams was sent to Belleville, Long Island.”

“Ah. I know that place.”

“I wanted confirmation. So I telegraphed the postmaster there. Here’s his reply: ‘Mr. Poindexter of Montgomery, Pa. Mr. Thomas Adams and Mrs. Adams of the same place have been at the Bedell House here for five days.’”

“Excellent. Be ready to leave for Belleville at one o’clock sharp. And mind, Sweetwater—keep your wits sharp, your tongue still. We’re feeling our way blindfolded. Stay that way until someone pulls the bandage off. Go. I have a letter to write—send a boy in five minutes.”

The letter was for Miss Butterworth. It caused a stir in Gramercy Park an hour later:

*Have you enough interest in the outcome of a certain matter to take a short trip into the country? I leave town at 1 P.M. for Belleville, Long Island. If you choose to join me, you’ll find me at the Bedell House by early afternoon. If you enjoy novels, bring one. Let me see you reading it on the hotel piazza at five o’clock. I may be reading too. If I put down my book and pick up a paper, devote one eye to your novel, the other to the passersby. If you leave your book open, I’ll understand you don’t recognize them. But if you close it—I’ll fold my newspaper. That will signal you’ve identified the young man and woman who left Mr. Adams’s house on the fatal afternoon of your first visit.*

Late in the afternoon, a well-dressed woman of indeterminate age sat alone in a quiet corner of the hotel piazza at Belleville. Her book lay open in her lap, but her eyes—sharp, expressive, and warmly intelligent—were fixed not on the page, but on the world unfolding before her. The sea stretched wide and blue, dotted with sails; the beach buzzed with brightly dressed youth; the village street hummed with life; and beyond, couples wandered across a sunlit lawn, rackets or clubs in hand. Children played nearby, but she barely glanced their way. She was clearly no mother, and perhaps no lover of children either. Yet when two figures emerged from the hotel’s grand entrance—tall, distinguished men—their appearance drew her full attention.

She snapped her book shut with a sharp motion, rose abruptly, and hurried to the edge of the lawn, where she stood with her back to them, pretending to comfort a small dog that seemed more terrified than reassured by her touch. Her hands trembled slightly as she stroked its fur, though her gaze remained fixed on the pair who now walked slowly toward the far end of the piazza.

At the other end, an elderly gentleman—neatly dressed, frail in frame but alert in manner—sat reading his newspaper. He had just closed his novel with a satisfied smile, as if the story had been both amusing and deeply engaging. But now, as the two men passed within view, he shifted his paper just enough to catch sight of them. One was older, dignified, of Scottish lineage, his bearing still upright and commanding despite his years. His eyes were cold, steady, unflinching. The other, younger but equally strong in build, moved with a quiet tension, avoiding eye contact with everyone around him—except for the old man reading the paper, whom he met with a brief, almost imperceptible nod.

“…I don’t think it does me any good,” the younger man murmured, voice low and strained. “I’m wretched when I’m near her, and worse when I try to forget her. I’d rather sit where she can see me than let her wonder what I’ve done. I’ll be careful—I promise—but you must understand how unbearable it is to keep silent about what’s destroying us all. I’m nearly as ill as she is.”

They paused briefly, then turned back toward the hotel. The elder spoke again, quieter this time, his tone measured, hopeful.

“Escape? South coast—she’ll stop watching you soon. A clinging nature—impetuous, yes, but foolishly loving—you know that. No danger. If found out—time. A cheerful home. Courage. Happiness—forgotten.”

The younger man gave a sharp gesture, as if dismissing the idea entirely.

Meanwhile, Miss Butterworth—yes, it was certainly Miss Butterworth—had not missed a word. Still bent over the trembling dog, she listened intently to two young women seated nearby, gossiping freely.

“She’s sick—badly,” one said. “I saw her yesterday. Pale cheeks, huge eyes. And him—he won’t speak to another woman. Just walks around that hot little room all day. Are they newly married?”

“Oh, I wouldn’t care about clothes,” the other replied. “But I saw her first day—she wore that pale blue dress. It looked like a dream. Then she got sick. Thank heaven. I felt like a dowdy beside her.”

“She’s not beautiful,” the first insisted. “No good nose, no striking eyes. But her mouth—oh, her mouth! And her walk—like floating. Until she fainted. Nobody did anything. Not even her husband. But I noticed—when she tried to get his attention three times and failed—she just collapsed. That woke him up. He hasn’t left her since.”

“I don’t think they look happy,” said the second. “For such a rich couple?”

“Maybe he’s dissipated,” the first mused. “Handsome men often are. But I wouldn’t care if…”

Then the dog yelped and bolted. Miss Butterworth had pinched it, hard—unconsciously, perhaps in outrage at the girls’ careless talk.

As the girls left, a young man approached her quietly.

“You recognize them?” he whispered. “No mistake?”

“None,” she replied. “The one heading toward the hotel—that’s the young man I saw leaving Mr. Adams’s house. The older one came in afterward.”

“Mr. Gryce says you should go home,” the young man said. “He’s going to arrest the young man.”

And Sweetwater walked away.

Miss Butterworth sat down heavily on a bench. Her heart pounded. She could picture the young wife—pale, frightened, trapped in silence—now facing the truth. She imagined the husband, stiff and distant, unable to offer comfort. The father-in-law, cold and remote, offered no solace either. His polished demeanor hid something harsh, something unyielding. Miss Butterworth felt a sudden, fierce need to be near the girl when the storm broke—to ease her own ache, perhaps, but also to stand guard against the cruelty of the world.

She rose to find Mr. Gryce—but he was gone. Searching the piazza, she saw the two men disappearing into a small smoking-room. Unable to follow, she went upstairs. A maid directed her to Mrs. Adams’s room at the end of a long corridor. At the far end, a window framed a sofa. She took her seat there, composed, waiting.

Through the transom above the door, a voice drifted out—soft, pleading.

“Where is Tom? Oh, where is Tom? Why does he leave me? I’m afraid of what he might do alone on those piazzas.”

“Mr. Poindexter is with him,” came a calm reply. “Mr. Adams was tired. Your father convinced him to go downstairs for a smoke.”

“I must get up. I must. Oh—the camphor—the—”

A sudden commotion. The young wife had fainted again.

Miss Butterworth looked helplessly at the door.

Inside the smoking-room, the air was thick with dread. The two men sat stiffly, cigars untouched, each pretending to enjoy the smoke while their nerves frayed. Then the door opened.

An old man entered—calm, deliberate, yet unmistakably a stranger in this place. He closed the door behind him.

“Gentlemen,” he said, voice firm, “I am Detective Gryce of the New York police. I am here—though I see one of you already knows why.”

One? Both.

The younger man crumpled into his chair. The older man remained rigid, but his jaw tightened. No denial. No protest.

Detective Gryce turned to the younger man.

“You are wanted in New York,” he said, voice softened by pity. “Coroner D---- is holding an inquest into the death of Mr. Felix Adams. You and your wife were seen leaving his house moments before he died. As such, you are considered key witnesses—whether his death was suicide or murder.”

The young man didn’t speak. His face flushed dark, then drained of color. Shame replaced terror. He stared at the floor.

Then—outside—a voice called through the hall: “Mr. Adams! Your wife has fainted again. Please come!”

The young man jerked upright, eyes wide with horror. He threw himself at the door, pressing his hand against it.

“She knows! She suspects!” he gasped. “Her nature is so sensitive!”

He sank to his knees before Gryce.

“Don’t disturb her! She’s an angel—pure. Let me take the blame. He was my brother. Let me go with you. Leave her ignorant—let her think nothing—please—”

Gryce placed a hand on his arm.

“Say nothing,” he said. “I remember everything. I’d rather hear nothing from you. And your wife—my warrant doesn’t include her. If you come quietly, I’ll allow you to say goodbye—in my presence.”

The young man rose slowly. He stood beside Mr. Poindexter, who had not moved.

“What have you against me?” he asked, voice suddenly clear, defiant. “What am I accused of? Tell me plainly. I cannot leave until I know.”

Poindexter stirred, glanced at him—warning, suspicious.

But the young man ignored him.

Gryce pulled out his warrant.

At once, the door shook.

“Lock it!” the young man shouted. “Don’t let anyone pass—no matter what.”

Gryce turned the key.

The young man unfolded the paper. His eyes scanned the lines—then stopped. He looked up.

Poindexter leaned forward, lips forming words without sound: *For murder? Him?*

Gryce bowed slightly. The young man exhaled—a long, shuddering breath.

“I am indebted to you,” he said. “Now I know. I am ready. I will go. If trial is needed, I will face it. You—” he turned to Poindexter, “you will watch over Eva. Not as a father—but as a mother. Be at her side when she wakes. Never leave her unless she sleeps. No newspapers. No gossips. Watch—watch—just as I would. And when I return—when I come back—will I?”

He looked at Gryce, pleading.

“I will come back,” he whispered. “My prayers will bless you—and her.”

A sob caught in his throat. He turned aside for a moment, then stepped forward calmly.

“I do not wish to say goodbye to my wife,” he said. “I cannot bear it. I would rather go now—without seeing her unconscious face. When I tell my story—when I speak to the first man who asks—I may find strength to write her. For now—take me away quickly. Let the world learn of my shame tomorrow.”

Gryce tapped the window. A young man appeared.

“There is a gentleman,” Gryce announced, “who must return to New York immediately. See that he reaches the train without delay—without fuss—without notice. I’ll follow with his luggage. Mr. Poindexter—you may now attend your daughter.”

With that, he turned the key. The door opened.

And one of the most painful moments in his long career ended.

Mr. Gryce was no stranger to subtlety. He had declared his intention of following Mr. Adams—and he did, but with such precision that he not only caught the same train, but secured a seat in the same carriage. His purpose was clear: to observe this young man closely, to study his demeanor under pressure. What intrigued him was not merely the crime, but the depth of emotion beneath it—a current that stirred something within himself, a quiet unease that made him feel the weight of his years more keenly than he ought, after solving so many complex cases.

From a distance, he watched as the young man’s face shifted—once twisted in agony, now composed, almost serene. The calmness was striking. How could someone who had just endured such torment sit so still, so unflinching, before strangers whose eyes might have once held judgment? Was it courage born of despair? Or was he so lost in sorrow that the world around him had faded into silence?

His brow was furrowed, his gaze distant—like a mind adrift in memory or regret. Where did such pain lead? Even a seasoned detective like Gryce, who had faced men of wealth and refinement, men who wore their sins like fine clothes, could not fathom the depths of this one’s guilt.

At the Brooklyn station, Gryce rejoined Adams, who barely glanced up as he approached. Within an hour, they were at Police Headquarters. The interrogation began without delay.

Adams did not resist. If anything, he seemed eager to speak. There was a burden on his soul, and he longed to release it—but not in the way the police expected. He did not confess to murder. Instead, he confessed to being the cause—unintentional, yes, but undeniable—of his brother’s death.

This is what he said:

"My name is Cadwalader, not Adams. My father was born in Scotland, naturalized in Pennsylvania, where he settled in Montgomery as a young married man. He had two children—my older brother, Felix, who died violently under the name Adams, and another child who died young. I am from a later marriage, born when my father lived in Harrisburg. But I left there before I turned three, so I remember nothing of that place. In fact, my earliest memories are of Europe, where I was sent after my mother’s death. I returned only this year, to find my father dying and my brother grown—wealthy, very wealthy. I had been told he would share his fortune with me. But after the funeral, Felix"—he spoke the name with visible effort—"Felix went to New York, leaving me alone, without prospects or means.

"I wandered until I came upon the town where my father once lived. Seeking out old friends, I met a young woman—the daughter of a man who had come over from Scotland with my father. Her name was Evelyn Poindexter." His voice trembled. "I fell in love with her quickly. But I had nothing—no money, no home, no future. Still, I couldn’t let go. So I wrote to my brother, Felix Cadwalader—though he’d taken the name Adams for family reasons—and told him I’d found a woman of good standing, with some wealth, and asked him to give me enough to marry her with dignity. His reply was the same vague promise he’d always given.

"But youth doesn’t wait. I decided to act. I married her—without telling either her or her father. They thought I was well-off. Mr. Poindexter is ambitious—he sees his daughter as worth a millionaire’s attention. If he’d known I had nothing but the clothes on my back and a few pennies, he would never have allowed it.

"Felix was informed of the marriage. He didn’t attend. He didn’t forbid it. It was done quietly, in a hotel room, with strangers as witnesses. Then I brought Evelyn to the house—hoping that even a man hardened by bachelorhood couldn’t remain untouched by her charm.

"But I miscalculated. Two days after our wedding, when we arrived, I saw a side of my brother I’d never known. Cold as steel, he dismissed her beauty, called love and marriage childish distractions—‘the pastime of idle minds.’ He turned away from us.

"I was furious. I shouted at him, angry beyond reason. And he—unprepared, startled—crouched as if struck. Then, losing control, he grabbed a dagger from the table and said, ‘You want my money? Take it!’ And with one desperate thrust, he drove it into his own heart."

He paused, breathing heavily. "I know you’ll think me mad. But that’s the truth. I did not kill him. Yet I caused it. I brought her here. I provoked him. And he died because of me."

The room was silent. Mr. Gryce leaned forward, his eyes sharp, not with suspicion, but with the slow dawning of understanding. This was not the confession of a murderer—but of a man broken by the consequences of love, pride, and a brother’s coldness.

Was it truly despair? Tragedies as sudden and uncalculated as this had surely unfolded throughout history—moments when human nature, in its rawest form, betrayed itself through impulsive acts of violence. Yet not a single man present, whether recalling Bartow’s silent pantomime or not—though that silent performance, as you may remember, bore no resemblance to the story now being told—could suppress the flicker of doubt, the unmistakable shadow of disbelief that fell over their faces as Thomas Adams poured out his account with relentless urgency.

The young man, gifted with an unusually sensitive soul, felt their skepticism like a physical weight. His head dipped low. “I shall add nothing,” he declared, voice firm but strained. “And I take nothing back. Make of it what you will.”

The inspector leading the inquiry glanced toward Mr. Gryce as those words left Adams’s lips. The detective merely nodded, his expression unreadable. Then he spoke: “We regret your decision, Mr. Adams. There are still many things unexplained. For instance—why, if your brother died by his own hand in such an unexpected way, did you leave the house so quickly, without raising an alarm, without even announcing your relationship to him?”

“You need not answer,” the inspector interjected, his tone gentle but firm. “No man is required to incriminate himself in this free country.”

A fleeting smile—pale, sorrowful, almost ghostly—touched the prisoner’s lips. Then, lifting his head with a sudden spark of defiance, he replied: “Incrimination is not my fear. From the way you all look at me, I know I am already lost. I have no means to prove my story.”

The statement hung in the air—a cry of innocence, perhaps, or the final surrender of a guilty mind. The inspector stared, momentarily stunned. “You forget,” he said slowly, “that you had your wife with you. She can confirm your words. She would be an invaluable witness in your defense.”

“My wife!” Adams choked, the words barely forming. “Must she be dragged into this? She is ill—so weak, so fragile. It would kill her, sir. She loves me—she—”

“Was she with you in Mr. Adams’s study? Did she see him raise the dagger to his chest?”

“No.” The denial was sharp, decisive. And with it, a new strength seemed to return to the young man. “She fainted moments before, during the height of the argument between my brother and myself. She did not see the end. And gentlemen—I might as well speak plainly now (I have nothing to gain by silence)—she finds it as hard to believe as you do that Mr. Adams killed himself. I’ve tried—tried everything—to convince her. But what hope have I, when even my own wife—the angel who loves me—cannot bring herself to believe me? She cannot be a witness for me. She is too honest, too true to her convictions. If I lose her—she will die…”

Mr. Gryce stepped forward, trying to calm him. But Adams would not be silenced.

“Spare her! Spare my wife! Write me down guilty, call me murderer, anything—just don’t force her to speak!”

The inspector cut in, his voice level but probing. “Have you read the newspapers lately?”

“I? Yes, yes, sir. How could I help it? Blood is blood. He was my brother. I left him dying—I knew I was in danger. Of course I read them.”

“And you know he was found with a large cross on his chest—one that once hung on the wall. How did it come to be there? Who placed it upon him?”

“I did, sir. I’m not Catholic—but Felix was. Seeing him die without absolution, without last rites, I thought of the holy cross. I tore it down—the only one I saw—and placed it in his arms.”

“A pious act,” the inspector murmured. “Did he recognize it?”

“I cannot say. I had my wife to tend to. She was unconscious—my thoughts were entirely on her.”

“I see. And you carried her out, so absorbed in her care that you didn’t notice Mr. Adams’s valet—”

“He’s innocent, sir. Whatever people think, he had nothing to do with this crime.”

“You did not observe him standing in the doorway, watching you?”

Now the inspector smiled inwardly. He knew Bartow had not been at the door—but above, at the loophole. This was a trap worth setting. And Adams, pale now with sweat beading across his forehead, faltered.

“But that man is insane—he’s deaf and dumb, as the papers say. Surely his testimony is worthless. You wouldn’t confront me with him?”

“We confront you with no one,” the inspector said calmly. “We only asked a question. You did not see the valet, then?”

“No, sir.”

“Nor understand the mystery of the colored lights?”

“No, sir.”

“Or the steel plate and other devices your brother used to entertain himself in solitude?”

“I don’t follow you, sir.”

But there was a shift in his voice—hesitation, unease.

“I see,” said the inspector. “The complications that have delayed our investigation—those intricate details—are not part of the story you’ve told. That’s possible. But there remains one small matter—important, nonetheless. Your wife mentioned it to you as soon as she regained consciousness. A few words—half a dozen or more—written by your brother just before he died. The paper has been found. Clearly, it played a role in your quarrel, since she regretted it was left behind. And he—Do you know where we found this paper?”

Adams looked up. His eyes were blank. The sight of the scrap of paper—held out by the inspector—seemed to drain every ounce of strength from him. He shook his head, reached out a trembling hand, failed to grasp it. Then, suddenly, he screamed:

“Enough! I can’t hold on any longer—without support, only lies! I killed my brother. For reasons as good as any man ever had. But I won’t tell them. I’d rather stand trial for murder. I’d rather hang.”

It was collapse—complete, devastating. A man of powerful build, noble features, reduced to ruin by the weight of accusation. A tragic end. Mr. Gryce, whose brow had deepened with furrowed concern, slowly rose and stood beside him, ready to catch him if he fell.

The inspector remained motionless, tapping a steady rhythm on the table with his fingers—as if counting time, or waiting for something beyond words.

Then, abruptly, another cry tore from Adams’s lips: “What is that?”

He turned, eyes wide, body rigid, fixed on the door.

“Nothing,” the inspector began—

But the door burst open.

A woman entered—pale as death, radiant with anguish, her presence electric with emotion. She broke free from the man who tried to hold her back and stood before them, trembling, breathless, her lips parted in a silent plea.

It was Adams’s wife—the invalid he had left hours earlier at Belleville. So frail, so delicately formed, she seemed a spirit from another world. Yet her face burned with human pain, her voice breaking as she spoke:

“What is he saying? Oh, I know what he’s saying! He says he killed his brother—that he held the dagger that freed the world from a monster whose evil none could fathom. But you must not listen to him! Not a word of it! He is innocent. I—his wife—have come twenty miles, from a sickbed, to tell you so. I—”

A hand gently, firmly, covered her mouth.

She looked up—into her husband’s eyes, filled with desperate entreaty. In return, she gave him a glance that pierced every heart in the room. Then, slowly, she removed his hand and placed her own upon his.

“It is too late, Tom,” she whispered. “I must speak. My father, my weakness, your command—they could not keep me at Belleville when I knew you were brought here. And shall I stop now, in front of these men who have heard his confession and may believe it? No. That would be cowardice—unworthy of our love, unworthy of the life we dream of together.”

She paused, drew a shallow breath.

“Sirs,” she said, her voice fading with each word, “I know my husband is innocent—because the hand that held the dagger was mine. I killed Felix Cadwalader.”

Silence.

Not a breath stirred. Not a muscle moved. The horror of that moment—unfathomable, unbearable—was written across every face. Slowly, a pallor crept over each cheek, a reflection of shock and awe at the purity of the speaker, the sweetness of her features, the sacredness of her suffering.

Only when someone cried from the doorway, “Catch her! She’s falling!” did the spell break.

Adams sprang forward—no longer broken, but reborn. With renewed strength, he caught her in his arms, carried her to a sofa, laid her down gently. As he knelt beside her, his face transformed—no longer haunted, but radiant with love and resolve.

Mr. Gryce watched, understanding dawning—this was the same change he had seen during the arrest, when the truth dawned: they had come for the wrong man. The real guilt lay elsewhere—in the heart of the one he adored.

Now, Adams stood before the assembled men, one hand resting tenderly on his wife’s unconscious head.

“Do not think,” he said, voice rising with passion, “that because this young woman has touched blood, she is wicked. There is no purer heart on earth than hers. None more worthy of a man’s worship. See—she killed my brother, son of my father, beloved by my mother. Yet I can kiss her hand, her forehead, her eyes, her feet—not because I hate him, but because I love her. The purest. The best.”

He stepped away, facing them all.

“Sirs,” he said, “I must ask you to listen to a strange, terrible tale.”

“It is like and unlike what I’ve already told you,” began young Adams, his voice low and strained. “In my earlier confession, I wove truth with falsehood. To make things clear—and to help you understand why my wife did what she did—I must begin again, as though I’d never spoken a word before.”

“Wait!” Mr. Gryce interrupted sharply, raising a hand. “We’ll hear you in a moment.” Then, leaning close to the inspector, he murmured a few words, after which he pulled out a pencil and scribbled several lines on a slip of paper. He handed it over to the inspector, who read it carefully, glancing back at it repeatedly as Adams—now properly known as Cadwalader—continued.

These notes, clearly meant as a guide for the unfolding tale, were written in a steady hand, each point precise and urgent. They read:

1. Why should a woman—calm enough to pause and fix her hair during the early moments of an interview—become so consumed by fury that she kills a man she had no known grudge against? (Recall the comb found on the floor of Mr. Adams’s bedroom.)

2. What meaning lies behind these words, written just before the confrontation: *“I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she shall ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!”*?

3. Why use the pronoun *“I”*? What was Mr. Felix Adams’s relationship to this girl—Evelyn—that allowed him to speak in such terms after her marriage to his own brother?

4. If he used those words, why, when attacked, did he try to swallow the paper? And why did he die with it clenched between his teeth?

5. If he died from rage—“as monsters do,” as she herself said—why did his face bear not hatred, but sorrow? Why did his expression show resolve, rather than the wild panic one might expect after being struck down by a sudden, unexpected attacker?

6. If he had strength enough to grab the paper and bring it to his mouth, why didn’t he use that same strength to turn on a light—one that could have summoned help instead of leaving the room bathed in crimson glow? According to our understanding of the signal system, red means: *“Nothing more required. Keep away.”*

7. What was the purpose of the massive steel plate hidden within the door frame? And why did it remain motionless—silent and still—during the most critical moment of his life?

8. How did old Poindexter arrive so quickly after the murder? His muttered words—*“It is Amos’s son, not Amos!”*—suggest confusion. Did he not know who he would find here? Was the sight of the dead man—bearing a cross upon his chest and pierced by a dagger—less shocking than the identity of the victim himself?

9. Recall Bartow’s silent performance. The fatal wound was delivered by a left-handed thrust. Watch closely for any sign that the woman is left-handed. Also, consider why she held her other arm rigidly stretched behind her for so long.

10. Why does the bird—whose cry is *“Remember Evelyn!”*—sometimes shift to *“Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?”* This variation suggests a deeper connection between Mr. Adams and his brother’s bride—one that spans years and secrets.

11. If Bartow is truly innocent—merely a witness—then why does he seem so joyful at the outcome? This may not be part of Thomas Adams’s confession, but it cannot be ignored. The deaf-and-dumb servant was driven mad by something that brought him pleasure. What could that be?

12. Study this chart, compiled through repeated tests with Bartow and the mysterious lamp in Mr. Adams’s study:
- White light: Water wanted.
- Green light: Overcoat and hat to be brought.
- Blue light: Return books to shelves.
- Violet light: Prepare the study for night.
- Yellow light: Await the next signal.
- Red light: Nothing needed; stay away.

The red light was lit during the final scene. Can this be explained by the account Mr. Adams is about to give?

With these questions in mind—each a thread in the tapestry of this crime—let us now listen to the full story. Not just of the murder, but of the events that led to it—events buried deep beneath silence, memory, and a name whispered by a bird: *Evelyn*.

Thomas Cadwalader—later known as Adams—never knew his mother. She died when he was still an infant. And though he lived under the same roof as his father, he never truly knew him either. Instead, he was raised in France by an elderly Scottish lawyer, a distant relative of his mother’s. This guardian spoke often of her, but never of the father—until Thomas turned fifteen.

Then, one day, the man handed him a stack of books with quiet gravity. “These are romances, Thomas,” he said. “Read them. But remember—none of them, no matter how thrilling, will ever match the story of your father’s life. A life wronged. A life suffering.”

“My father?” Thomas asked, startled. “Tell me about him. I’ve never heard a word.”

But the guardian said nothing more that day. He simply watched Thomas turn to the books, knowing full well that the seed had been planted.

And it took root.

From then on, Thomas could not stop thinking about his father. For years, he’d scarcely given him a thought. But now, framed as a victim of injustice, the man became a haunting presence in Thomas’s mind. He scoured his memory for any trace—a face, a gesture, a voice—but found only fragments: a stretch of darkness, two bars of orange light always burning, black lines like rooftops against them, a strange inner tremor, a sudden fear, and the rush of wind as if something vast were swooping down. These images flickered in and out, leaving behind unease and confusion.

He asked his guardian what they meant.

The old man shook his head. “I don’t know. But there is a duty waiting for you across the sea. A purpose. One that requires preparation—study, discipline, refinement. You must become someone who commands respect, whether in Europe or America.”

He didn’t explain further. But every hint he dropped—the talk of destiny, of return—was carefully timed. It was clear: this wasn’t mere advice. It was training for something greater.

Thomas began to live with a sense of mystery. The unknown nature of his future made every effort meaningful. His studies took on a romantic edge; he mastered music, fencing, riding, and academic excellence. By eighteen, he was a graduate of the Sorbonne and a skilled musician, admired by peers who called him a prodigy.

Yet he lacked one thing: grace. Not talent, but charm. That came to light when his guardian delivered a letter from his father.

In it, the father praised Thomas’s accomplishments—but criticized his manner. Too abrupt with men. Too timid with women. “You must learn to carry yourself with ease,” the letter read, “and to meet others without hesitation.”

How had the father seen him? Thomas had never met him. How could he judge?

His questions brought a revelation that stunned him.

“You’re not the only son,” the guardian said. “You have a brother. Older—by twenty years or more. He was here recently. In Paris. He saw you.”

It was a shock. A brother. A living, breathing link between Thomas and the father he had only dreamed of.

But why so much secrecy? Why the exile? What did the father want?

He was not told. But Thomas understood: this was no ordinary return. It was a mission.

So he changed. He softened his tone, studied manners, observed the polished youth around him. He learned the unspoken language of elegance—the way a man carried himself, how he smiled, how he spoke. Within a year, he transformed. He became a gentleman—not just in skill, but in bearing.

At the end of that year, his guardian visited him once more.

“You are ready,” he said. “Your father calls. It will come soon. Be careful. Do not form ties you cannot break. Once you go to America, you may never return. Your father has waited twenty years for you. You will bring him comfort. That is all I can say. The rest—you will hear from him.”

Thomas waited. With feverish hope. When the summons arrived, he left France immediately.

Six months before the tragedy in ---- Street, he stepped onto the New York wharf.

He expected his brother. Instead, a messenger met him—his face grim, his silence heavy with news.

Thomas’s father was ill. Possibly dying.

They gave him directions and sent him by train to a remote village in northern Pennsylvania.

Everything after that blurred into nightmare.

He traveled through the night, still reeling from the motion of the ship. Rain lashed the windows. Wind shrieked through the trees. At a station, a woman called to him, her voice trembling.

“Is that you, laddie? Come awa in. Dinna heed the rain. The maister’s been crying on you a’ day. I’m glad you’re no ower late.”

He dismounted, followed her voice, stumbled up steps, and entered a narrow door that slammed shut behind him.

Inside: bare floors, stained walls, a crude pine staircase. No grandeur. No warmth.

This was the home of the man who had demanded perfection? The man whose love he had spent years earning?

Disgust rose in him. Then, the old woman smiled—too wide, too eager—and whispered, “O the fine laddie! Wad your puir mither could see you the noo! Bonnie and clever! All mither, laddie, all mither!”

He pushed past her.

“Where is my father?” he asked, forcing calm.

“Dinna ye hear him? He’s crying on ye. Puir man, he’s wearying to see ye.”

He strained to listen. Over the storm, over the wind, over the rattling windows—there it was: a voice, faint but urgent.

“Thomas! Thomas!”

He moved toward the stairs.

“No! No!” the woman cried. “Come in first. Eat something.”

He shook his head.

“You will na? Then haste ye—haste ye. But it’s a peety you wadna ha’e eaten. Ye’ll need it, laddie. Ye’ll need it.”

“Thomas! Thomas!” the cry came again, louder now.

He ran up.

At the top, he looked back. The woman stood at the foot of the stairs, shielding the candle from the draft. Her hands shook. Her face was etched with fear. He hurried on.

He reached a window. Rain poured through the small panes. Chilled, he turned to the door beside it—faintly visible in the dimness—and grasped the old-fashioned latch.

It creaked open.

And there, in the darkened room, on a huge bed against the wall, two eyes burned with fever. They fixed on him—wild, intense, alive with a fire that was not merely illness, but something deeper.

Then arms rose. The moaning cry turned to a shout:

“Thomas! Thomas!”

Thomas fell to his knees. He seized the wasted hands stretched toward him.

The face was rugged, aged, far from the noble visage he had imagined. Yet it stirred something deep within him. This was his father. The man he had longed for. The man who had shaped his dreams.

But the old man did not embrace him. He pushed him back gently, commanding him to stand in the lamplight.

“Let me see you,” he gasped. “Look at me. Look at you.”

And he studied him—through pain, through exhaustion—as if measuring him for some terrible purpose.

Thomas felt the weight of it. Not love. Judgment.

He stood there, cold, as the wind howled outside—like the sound of his own heart breaking.

Finally, the father collapsed. But not before speaking.

“The grace of God be thanked,” he breathed. “Our efforts have not failed. Handsome, strong, noble in look and character—we could ask for nothing more. My revenge will succeed. John Poindexter will find that he has a heart—and that heart can be wrung. I do not need to live to see it. For me, it exists now. It exists here.” He struck his chest with trembling hands.

John Poindexter.

A name Thomas did not recognize.

He ventured to ask.

“You do not know who John Poindexter is?” the father whispered, his eyes narrowing. “What he has done to me and mine?”

He paused. Then, with a strength that seemed borrowed from beyond death, he continued.

“I was his friend. From boyhood we shared everything—bed, food, dreams. When he came to America, I went with him. He was sharp, cold. I was warm-hearted, but useless with money. In fifteen years, he was rich. I was poor. But Providence gave me two children—Felix and Evelyn. Then their mother died. I lost what little money I had. Life grew bleak.

John Poindexter, honest then—or so I hoped—offered to take one of my children. I refused… until the war broke out. Then I sent both. Felix and Evelyn. I joined the army.

For four years, I fought. I suffered. I was imprisoned. When I returned, I came home with gratitude, with love burning in my soul, ready to embrace my son and my daughter.

What did I find?

A funeral. Hers. Evelyn’s.

And Felix—your brother—walking beside her coffin, like a guard, while the man who had taken her in, the man I trusted—John Poindexter—stood silent, unmoved, as if she were nothing.

Betrayed!” The father screamed, rising on his pillow. “Her innocence! Her sweetness! And he—cold as stone—watched her die, without a tear, without a word of sorrow!”

“O God!” Thomas whispered.

“Ay, God,” the father repeated, shaking his head. “He allowed it. And I—But facts. Only facts. This is my story.

After her burial…”

A new voice cut through the air—smooth, firm, yet strangely calm.

“Cease,” it said. “Let me tell the rest. No man can tell it as I can.”

Thomas froze.

“Felix?” he breathed.

Below, the father whispered, “Felix.”

Thomas turned—then stopped. A hand pressed gently on his shoulder.

“Wait,” the voice said. “Listen. Think not of me. Think of her. Evelyn. She is the one you must avenge.”

Thomas obeyed.

Felix stepped forward.

“Evelyn had a voice like a bird,” he began. “She filled John Poindexter’s house with song. I, a bookish boy, thought she sang too much. But I remembered Edward Kissam—her lover—and stayed silent. He was good. Honest. Kind to me. She was eighteen, beautiful—every man looked at her, even Poindexter. But she was pure. I swear it. Until one day—my God, how I remember—her eyes faltered. Shrank from me. As if seeing something terrible.

I asked if she’d heard from father.

She moaned. ‘Our father! Pray God we never see him again. If you love him, if you love me—pray he dies in prison rather than return to see me as I am.’

I thought she was mad.

But then she laughed—trying to laugh away her fear—and sank to her knee, pressing her head against my side. ‘I am not well,’ she whispered. ‘I’ve had pains. Deep inside. Sometimes I think I won’t live long. All my songs, my laughter—it’s all a mask.’

‘Not live?’ I said. Impossible. But she was pale—so pale—her skin like ash.

I tried to rouse her. I mentioned Edward’s name.

She recoiled. ‘Not that! Never that! Do not speak his name. Let me never hear of him again. I am dead—do you understand?—dead to all the world. Except to you.’

And then—I understood.

She was sick. Secretly. Terribly. And she feared the truth would come out.

She forbade anyone to enter her room—not Mrs. Poindexter, not the servants, not even her guardian—least of all, her guardian.

I became her nurse. I sat by her door night after night, listening for the step she dreaded most.

For a week, I watched her fade. Like a flame burning low.

Then, as the shadow fell, I rushed to confront Poindexter—found him walking in his hall, pale, disturbed.

I told him: ‘My father is coming. I had a dream. I saw him turning toward this house. Are you ready to meet him? Do you have an answer when Amos Cadwalader asks what happened to his child?’”

A sigh escaped Amos Cadwalader—deep, shuddering, as if drawn from the very core of a man long buried beneath grief. It spoke louder than words: a wound still raw after thirty years, an ache that time had not healed. Felix, who had just spoken, echoed the breathless pause and pressed on, his voice low but steady.

"It was dusk when I told my story. From darkness to dawn, we sat facing one another—no sleep, no rest—our eyes locked in silent torment. Then we went to John Poindexter."

" Had he fled, we might have spared ourselves. But he met us openly, calmly, with the cold detachment of a man who cannot feel because he has never known feeling. His first words proved it: 'Spare yourselves, spare me—no more recriminations. The girl is dead. I cannot bring her back. Live your lives—eat, drink, spend, enjoy—there’s little time left for any of us. Why dwell on old sorrows?'"

Felix paused, then continued, his tone thick with bitterness. "That last word—'sorrows'—was a triumph. He meant to say, *When a man cares for nothing, no one, not even himself, there is no point in cursing him.*"

Ah, yes—that was the secret. That was what made him so powerful. He felt nothing. Not love. Not loss. Not guilt. And we knew it. We felt the blow—and we bowed under it. But before we left him, before we left the town, your father and I swore a vow: that we would make that heart bleed, however hard it was; that one day, by whatever means, we would force that soulless man to suffer as we had suffered—no matter how prosperous he seemed, how blessed his life appeared."

He stopped. Thomas stirred, but Amos silenced him with a gesture.

"Some men forget in a month. Others in a year. I have never forgotten. Neither has Felix. When you were born—after I married again, hoping joy might return—I felt something strange. I don’t know why, but I believed Evelyn’s avenger had come into the world."

"And when, a year or two later, we heard that God had either forgiven John Poindexter—or remembered his sins—and that he, too, had been given a child after eighteen years of marriage—I looked at your face, Thomas, and saw... I thought I saw... a way to fulfill the vengeance we had sworn to pursue."

Thomas grew. His spirit burned bright, his mind quick, his nature generous. When your mother died, leaving me a widower once more, I hesitated no longer. You were meant for this. You were born for it.

Do you remember the beginning of that journey—the one that took you far from me? How I carried you on my shoulder down a dusty road until we reached the edge of his land? There, among the tombs, I lifted you up and pointed to those distant gables silhouetted against the twilight gold. I said to you then, ‘This is the home of your enemy. This is where happiness will grow beneath a child’s smile—and you shall destroy it.’ Do you remember?"

Thomas nodded slowly. "I remember."

"Then now," Amos whispered, gripping his son's arm, "the hour has come. Fulfill that promise. Avenge our pain. Avenge your sister. _Poindexter’s daughter has grown to womanhood._"

At those words, Thomas recoiled as if struck. But his father misread his horror for hesitation.

"Woo her!" Amos urged, clutching his arm tighter. "Win her! They do not know you. To them, you are Thomas Adams—not Thomas Cadwalader. Take this young girl into your arms. Make her yours. She is only a country girl—you’ve lived in Paris. You can charm her, deceive her, break her. And when she suffers—when she cries out in despair—know this: he will feel it. He must love his child. Through her, we touch his heart. Make her suffer, and if, in doing so, you see him falter—then believe I am watching from beyond the grave. Believe I am smiling. For neither I nor you will have lived in vain."

He gasped, struggling for breath, but his will held firm. His eyes, though dimmed by death, still burned with purpose.

"I shall die tonight. I am dying now. I will never see your triumph. I will never witness John Poindexter’s eyes cloud over with agony—agony so deep it makes a man question whether God exists. But I will know it. Wherever I go, I will feel it. No mound of earth can hold my spirit down when he feels his doom. I will sense his pain—and I will rejoice."

He paused, trembling, then spoke again, his voice rising like a cry from the grave:

"Boy, boy—your life has been shaped for this moment. God gave you beauty. Man gave you strength. You have earned intelligence, grace, skill. All you need to do is show yourself to this girl—and she will fall into your hands. Once she does—remember _me_! Remember _Evelyn_!"

Never had Thomas seen such fire. It swept over him like a tide, pulling him forward despite every instinct. He raised his hand—meaning to speak—but could not form words.

His father misunderstood. "You are silent," he snapped. "You do not want this task. Perhaps you are virtuous—though you’ve lived alone in Paris, unbound by duty or law. Or perhaps you carry some noble instinct, some sense of honor, that blinds you to the wrongs that have screamed to heaven for thirty years. Thomas, Thomas—if you fail me now—"

"He will not fail you," came a calm voice from the side.

Felix. Always composed. Always certain.

"I have watched him. I know him. He will not fail."

Thomas flinched. He had nearly forgotten Felix. Now, turning, he saw the man clearly—for the first time.

Not large, not imposing in stature, but commanding. There was a quiet power in his gaze—gray, unwavering, utterly unshakable. Unlike his father’s frenzy, Felix’s presence was cold precision. Yet beneath the control, Thomas sensed a deeper force—one he could not resist.

And yet—Felix was elegant. Well-dressed, refined, everything a Parisian gentleman should be. Even the poverty that clung to Amos’s room did not touch him. Behind him, a dark-green curtain stretched from floor to ceiling, heavy and mysterious. It framed his face with eerie clarity, as if designed to draw attention.

For a moment, Thomas wondered if it had been placed there for that very reason.

But then Felix’s eyes returned to him—demanding, relentless.

Bowing his head, Thomas stammered, “I am at your command, Felix. At your command, father. I cannot say more. Only—I never saw Evelyn. She died before I was born. I—”

Felix cut in, gently but firmly. “Perhaps there is an obstacle we have not considered. Maybe you already love someone. If so, it need not matter.”

Thomas’s voice rose, sharp with indignation. “No. I am heart-whole. Save for a few fleeting dreams—dreams that fade each day.”

“Your heart will remain untouched,” Felix said. “I have seen the girl. I went there myself, a year ago. She is pale as a snowdrop. Lifeless. Dull. You won’t need to recall the laughter of Parisian women to resist her charm.”

Thomas shrugged. “She must taste the full sweetness of hope,” Felix continued, voice clear and cutting. “Only then can she know despair. She must experience every joy love brings—before we crush it. Can you play this role? One that requires patience, deception, courage, subtlety—and above all, the strength to endure her suffering without breaking?”

“I have the skill,” Thomas murmured. “But—”

“You lack the incentive,” Felix finished. “We understand. Our hatred has been fed by memories of a woman you never knew. Look, then. Look at your sister—how she was, how she remains in our hearts. See her, Thomas. See her as she was—despoiled, murdered, forgotten by Poindexter. Have you ever seen a face more beautiful? More tragic? Beauty and sorrow intertwined—can you not feel it?”

Thomas did not know what to expect. He braced himself—half-expecting to see a ghost.

But when he followed Felix’s hand, the curtain vanished—replaced by light.

And there, in its center, stood a woman.

Not a painting. Not a likeness.

She seemed alive. Tender. Gently sorrowful. Her eyes held a depth of pain that pierced through Thomas’s soul. She was Evelyn—more real than memory, more vivid than dream. He fell to his knees.

"Do not look at me so reproachfully, sweet Evelyn," he whispered. "I was born to avenge you. And I will. John Poindexter shall not rest in peace."

A soft sigh came from the bed.

"Swear it!" Amos cried, extending his arms in the shape of a cross.

"Yes, swear it!" Felix added, placing his hand upon the crossed arms.

Thomas stepped forward. Laid his hand beside Felix’s.

"I swear," he began, raising his voice above the storm raging outside. "I swear to win the affection of Eva Poindexter—and when her heart is mine, I will cast her back into anguish and scorn, into the arms of John Poindexter."

"Good," came a voice from the distance.

Darkness descended.

Thomas collapsed—unconscious—at his father’s feet.

* * * * *

Amos Cadwalader died that night.

But not before one final scene.

Around midnight, he stirred from the sleep that had followed the oath. His eyes opened—fixed on the door.

"Ah!" he breathed. "A visitor. John Poindexter. He comes to beg forgiveness before I leave this world."

The sarcasm in his voice, the mockery in his tone, sent chills through both sons.

They knew—he saw their enemy as clearly as he saw them.

"Does he fear meeting Evelyn? Does he wish to appease me before I join her shade? Let him not be disappointed."

He turned his head toward the door, voice rising with bitter triumph.

"I forgive you, John Poindexter. I forgive you the shame of my daughter. I forgive you my ruined life. I am dying—but I leave behind one who will not forgive. I have a son. An avenger of the dead. Who still lives—to—"

His voice broke.

He fell back.

And with those final words—sealed in blood and silence—Amos Cadwalader died.

Felix had not inherited his father’s inability to make money. Over the past twenty years, since Thomas had been abroad, he had built a fortune—wealth he refused to share with his father, though that same father was more than willing to see it used for their shared vengeance. That was why Felix’s parting words to his brother before leaving for Montgomery carried such weight:

“I have money; spend it. Spend whatever you need. When your task is done, there will still be enough left for your pleasure.”

Thomas bowed. “The laborer is worthy of his hire,” he thought. Then he asked, looking around the sparse, hollow walls of the house—now stripped of any reason to exist now that their father was gone—“And you? Will you stay here?”

Felix answered sharply but firmly. “No. I leave for New York tomorrow. I’ve rented a house there—one you may one day wish to share. The name under which I’ve leased it is Adams—Felix Adams. That is how you’ll address me. Cadwalader must never pass your lips in Montgomery. And remember: my face is known there. If we were not dependent on you, we might not have succeeded at all.” He smiled, fully aware he was the handsomer of the two. “Now, about those introductions you were supposed to bring from Paris?”

* * * * *

The next few weeks unfold best through the letters Thomas sent to Felix and the private diary he kept—each offering a different truth. Letters record facts. The diary captures feelings. Together, they reveal what neither alone could show.

* * * * *

I am here. I have seen her. She is, as you said, pale and blonde. Tomorrow I present myself to John Poindexter. From what I’ve already experienced, I expect a favorable reception.

Yours aff.,

I couldn’t write Felix the full truth of today. Why not? And why must I write it here? To distract my mind? Perhaps. I don’t understand my own emotions—or why I now dread this mission even as I rush forward to complete it.

I saw her. That much I told Felix. But I didn’t say where or how. How could I? Would he grasp how someone of Poindexter’s blood could perform an act of grace? Or how I, whose heart has grown dark since embracing this purpose, felt it swell—and my resolve falter—at the first sight of a face I swore would bring only pain?

I walked from the train straight to the cemetery. Before settling into town or securing lodging, I went to Evelyn’s grave—to renew my oath in the very place where, nineteen years ago, my father lifted me, a four-year-old child, and pointed toward John Poindexter’s home in threat.

I found the old, overgrown stone marking her resting place. As I bent in the fading light to read it, the rustle of a woman’s dress caught my attention. A young girl approached, bathed in a soft rose-gold glow that seemed to lift her from the earth, giving her delicate figure and luminous head an almost unearthly presence—no exaggeration, given her pure features and gentle bearing. In her arms, she carried a great bouquet of snow-white lilies. When I noticed her gaze wasn’t on me, but on the grave beside me, I stepped back into the shadows of nearby bushes and watched.

She knelt and scattered the flowers—symbols of innocence—over the grave I had just left.

What did it mean? Who was this girl honoring my long-dead sister?

As she rose, I could no longer contain my emotion or curiosity. Stepping forward, I greeted her with the respect her appearance demanded. Her face, when raised in speech, was even more radiant than when bent in quiet reverence. I asked, casually, who lay beneath the stone—and why a girl so young would scatter flowers upon a grave older than herself.

Her answer stunned me—just as much as any moment lately has.

“I come here,” she said, “because the girl buried here shares my birthday. I never knew her, but she died in my father’s house when she was no older than I am now. Since becoming a woman, I’ve understood what it means to die too soon. So I’ve made it a custom to give her my birthday flowers. They say she was like a lily—in looks and spirit. So I bring lilies.”

It was Eva Poindexter. The girl I—And she was placing flowers on Evelyn’s grave.

* * * * *

I’ve met John Poindexter. To win the daughter’s favor, I must first gain the father’s approval—or at least his notice. I believe I’ve done so.

Very truly,

I no longer feel like a man. John Poindexter appears cold, stern, unyielding. Yet he does not inspire the hatred I expected. He doesn’t stir the memory of my sister in me as I thought he would. Is it because he is Eva’s father? Has the beauty of the daughter cast a light around him? If so, Felix has every right to curse me—and my father—for—

* * * * *

The introductions you gave me have opened every door. This town is wealthy, filled with fine homes. I find myself in society perfectly suited to me—where she shines as its brightest star. Did I say he remains the most influential man here?

Yours truly,

She is beautiful. Dainty as a lily, flushed like a rose. But it isn’t her beauty that moves me—it’s the quiet strength in her nature, the sweetness that holds no weakness, only courage. Could Felix have imagined such a Poindexter? I cannot look at her without feeling shame for the sin that calls down vengeance on this house. I can’t even recall my past life. All that was dark, secret, vengeful slips away under her gaze. Instead, I dream of purity, truth, meaning—of something noble. And it’s not her words, but her smile that does this. Have I been given an angel to destroy? Or am I blind, seeing a saint where others—Felix, let’s say—would see only a charming girl?

* * * * *

Rides, dances, games—typical nonsense. My interest in her is beginning to be noticed. Only she seems unaware. Sometimes I fear our plan will fail because of her innocence. What if she never loves me? I’ve tried hard to please her—too hard. Today I rode twenty-five miles on horseback just to fetch her a trinket she admired.

Yours aff.,

This won’t go as smoothly as Felix thinks. Eva Poindexter may be country-bred, but she has standards. Grace and talent aren’t enough. Do I possess what she needs? Time will tell. I have one quality she doesn’t suspect. Will its shadow overwhelm everything else, making her pure soul recoil from me just as I think she’s mine? I don’t know. And the uncertainty torments me. Something deeper than revenge now drives me—the need to win her heart. I’ve forgotten my purpose. Forgotten everything except that she is the only woman I’ve ever loved, and I cannot rest until she belongs entirely to me. God! Have I become a slave when I meant to be master? Have I, Thomas Cadwalader, surrendered my soul to this innocent girl? I don’t ask. I only want her.

* * * * *

She may not love me, but she’s interested in no one else. John Poindexter confirms this—he seems eager to help me win her affection. Hard-won, tightly bound. If she ever comes to love me, it will be with the force of a strong nature. The pale blonde has a heart.

Yours aff.,

If this were merely passion, I might still control it. But it’s deeper. It compels me to see through her eyes, hear with her ears, feel with her heart. My soul, not my senses, is captivated. I want her not for myself—but to make her happy. To prove to her that goodness exists—me, who came here to corrupt and destroy; me, still bound by vows to do so. Ah, Felix, Felix—you should have chosen an older man. Or remembered that one who once felt family love has a heart too tender for such treachery.

* * * * *

Evelyn’s name is never spoken in this house. Sometimes I wonder if he’s forgotten her. That thought alone keeps my revenge alive. Forgotten her? Strange—that her child, born long after her death, remembers her, while he forgets. Yet the blow is meant for the daughter—if it falls at all. Should I pray it never does? That she hates rather than loves me? Distrust instead of trusting me? But who prays against himself? Eva Poindexter must love me—even if I destroy myself afterward, consumed by guilt once she gives her heart to me.

* * * * *

Send me a few exquisite things from Tiffany’s. Her father expects gifts. I think she’ll accept them. If she does, we can both rest easy about her feelings.

Very truly,

I can’t bear to spend a day away from her. If Felix were here, watching my devotion, he’d approve—coldly, rigidly—of my single-mindedness. He’d say, as he once wrote: “You are not disappointing us.” Us? As if our father still hovers near, sharing our goals. Alas—if he does, he sees deeper than Felix ever could. With each passing day—each day that brings progress—I draw farther from the end they planned. I am a traitor to my oath. For I now know I shall never betray Eva’s trust. I could not. I’d rather face my father’s accusing gaze beyond the grave, or Felix’s rage here, or my own contempt for the weakness that has led me to step back from the edge of this wicked revenge.

* * * * *

This morning, I passed beneath the window you described as Evelyn’s. I did it deliberately—to test my feelings. Enough.

Eva accepted the brooch. The simplest thing you sent.

Aff.,

I hate John Poindexter. Yes, I do. But I can never hate his daughter. Only Felix could confuse father and child so completely, punishing this gentle embodiment of grace, trust, and charm. But am I meant to hate her? Am I required to love her? I’ll ask Felix. No—I can’t. He wouldn’t understand her beauty, nor its power over me. He’d doubt, and come to Montgomery. Good God—am I so unfaithful to my blood that I can’t even imagine Felix gazing, secretly, upon the unsuspecting form of his enemy’s daughter?

* * * * *

A picnic in the mountains. I escorted Miss Poindexter down a treacherous slope. No words of love were spoken (she’s not ready), but I feel I’ve advanced in her regard.

Yours,

I touched her hand! Felt her body tremble against mine as we descended the mountain path! No one near. No eyes—moments when we were utterly alone in this vast, wooded paradise, as if the world held no other soul. Yet I dared not hold her hand, nor confess the madness of my heart. How I love her! How distant and dim seem the years of that dead crime my brother wants to punish in this sweet soul! Yes, and how far off that terrible hour when I knelt beneath my dying father’s hand and swore—Ah, that oath! That oath!

* * * * *

The dreaded moment has come. Felix has arrived in Montgomery. I received word today—a summons to meet him tonight, at Evelyn’s grave, at midnight. I don’t welcome it. I fear him. I suspect he plans some theatrical manipulation. But the time has passed for hesitation. I’ll show him I’m not swayed by place or hour—not even here, in this hotel room, where her little glove—may there be sin in stealing such a thing?—rests on the table before me. Evelyn is sacred. But the dead must not rule the living. Eva shall not be sacrificed to Evelyn’s ghost—not if John Poindexter lives in peace; not if Felix—well, I must stand firm. Felix is a dangerous foe to face alone, in a place steeped in bitter memories, at an hour when spirits—should they exist—haunt the tombs.

* * * * *

I wouldn’t have recognized Felix if I’d met him on the street. In the moonlight, he looked like a stranger. His voice changed. His manner hesitated—something I never thought possible. And his words weren’t what I feared. No accusations. No recriminations. Just a cold command: shorten your courtship. Hasten the end. Two weeks to bring matters to a climax.

Then he turned to Evelyn’s grave, bent low, and tried to read her name on the moss-covered stone. He lingered so long I leaned beside him and placed a hand on his shoulder. He was trembling. His body was as cold as the stone he leaned against. Was it grief for the sister buried there? If so, it was natural. He seemed to have loved her more than any other soul. I pulled back, moved by respect for such enduring sorrow—until he seized my hand and, with a violent grip that clashed with his measured tone, whispered: “Do not forget the end! Do not forget John Poindexter—his sin, his indifference to my father’s pain, the years of suffering that turned Amos Cadwalader into a hermit. I’ve seen the girl. She’s changed—women do at her age. Some men—though not you—might call her beautiful. But beauty must not blind you. Your goal is elsewhere. Ignore her—have you not already? Pale beauties don’t move one who has sat at the feet of Paris’s most dazzling women. Focus on John Poindexter. On the debt he owes. On the suffering we promised him. The sweeter she is, the greater the chance to reach his heart. The more worthy she is of love, the surer our grip on him—and the heavier his fall.”

The old spell returned. I could neither speak nor resist. He released my hand, stood up, and faced away from the village—never turning toward it since arriving. “I return to New York tomorrow. In two weeks, telegraph your readiness to join me. I have a home that will suit you—soon, it will be yours.”

He clutched his chest. Even in darkness, I saw a spasm cross his face. But it vanished before we left.

“You may hear from me again,” he said faintly, grasping my hand, then turning away. I hadn’t spoken a word.

* * * * *

I haven’t heard from you. Are you well? Did the journey affect you? I have no major news. John Poindexter seems genuinely interested in my courtship. He even offers advice. How does that strike you?

Aff.,

I’ll never understand Felix. He hasn’t left. He’s hiding here, watching me—no doubt checking whether my signs of weakening mean anything real. I know this. I’ve seen him several times this week, thinking himself invisible. Once, in Mr. Poindexter’s garden, as Eva and I talked by the window. Once, in church—dark corner, yes, but close enough to watch us in the Poindexters’ pew. He looked thin. The strain is wearing him down. Is it my duty to end it by confessing my betrayal? By declaring my intention to marry the girl who has undone my vow?

* * * * *

Miss Poindexter has confessed she cares for me. Are you satisfied now?

In haste,

She loves me. Oh, ecstasy of life! Eva Poindexter loves me. I forced it from her today—arms around her, her head on my shoulder. It came. Now let Felix do what he will! What is John Poindexter to me? Her father. What of Amos Cadwalader’s hatred? Of the wrong that called for revenge? Dead issues. Buried sorrows. God may remember them—but men must forget. Life with her! That is my future. That is the only vengeance I will take—the only justice Evelyn could demand, if she is the angel we believed her to be. I will write to Felix tomorrow.

I haven’t written him. I lacked the courage.

I dreamed. I saw my father meeting Evelyn’s white shade in the unseen realms beyond death. Horrors and glories met as their paths crossed. When they parted, a line of light followed her steps, a trail of gloom his. As they diverged, I heard my father cry:

“There is no stain on your garments, Evelyn. Can it be that earthly wrongs are forgotten here? That mortals remember what angels forget? That our revenge is too late for one so blessed?”

I woke. But the echo haunts me all day: “Is our revenge too late for one so blessed?”

I summoned courage. Felix returned. The truth was finally spoken. I had pressed Eva to name our wedding day. We stood together—John Poindexter, Eva, and I—in the bright drawing-room light—when I heard a faint groan, then a soft fall from the window overlooking the garden. It was Felix. He’d been watching. He’d seen my love, heard my talk of marriage. Now he was in the grounds—frenzied or satisfied, I couldn’t tell.

Determined to know, I excused myself and searched the paths he knew. At last, I found him near an old sundial—where I’d often seen Eva and me together. He was pale, almost deathly, in the starlight. Yet he greeted me calmly, as if he expected me.

“You’re playing your role well,” he said. “Too well. What was that I heard about marrying?”

The moment had come. I resolved to face it with courage. But it was hard. Felix is not easy to defy—even in small things. This is his life. His past, present, and future.

I don’t know who spoke first. There was stammering, broken words. Then I heard myself say, clearly, with a hardness born of restraint:

“I love her. I want to marry her. You must allow it. Then—”

I couldn’t continue. I felt the shock he received—as if it passed through me. Something beyond earth passed between us. I raised my hand as if to shield my face. Then, whether from branches shifting or my own vision clearing, I glimpsed his face—and saw a deep agony. At first, I thought it was my own heart breaking. But then I knew: it was his. Felix’s.

I stood stunned.

My weakness had shattered his one hope. Or so I thought. His silence made my heart ache—more than it ever had for him. I tried to explain. I was glad when darkness returned. Seeing his bowed head, his set jaw, was unbearable. Yet it made speaking easier. I spoke of her purity, her goodness—the qualities that had stolen my heart despite myself. “My heart!” The word felt strange between us—about a Poindexter. But it was the only one that fit.

Driven mad by his silence—so heavy, so moving—I cried out:

“You’ve never loved a woman, Felix. You don’t know what passion is when it seizes a man worn out by hollow pleasures. You can’t judge. You can’t forgive. You’re made of iron—”

In the silent chambers of a mind sealed off from compassion, a battle raged—one unseen, unheard, and unyielding. It was not fought with words or weapons, but in the dark corners of a heart that had long believed itself untouched by passion. I can only suggest its contours. Some tragedies are too deep for language, too private for witness. This one we glimpse only through fragments—letters torn and hidden, addressed to no one, yet betraying their author in the trembling hand that wrote them. They were discovered years later, beneath the floorboards of Bartow’s room, their origin lost to time, their purpose known only to the man who penned them.

* * *

"Madness!"

I have seen Eva Poindexter again—and since then, heaven and hell have warred within me. Eva! Eva! The girl I once considered merely a pawn in my design, the girl I gave to my brother. She is too radiant for him—too radiant for any man save one who has never known the touch of a woman’s voice, the glance of a face capable of stirring soul or blood. Is this love? Can I, Felix, whose life has been defined by a single purpose, whose heart has long been forged into iron, allow even a sliver of weakness—however secret—to be touched by a woman? Especially her? Never. I am either mad—or the target of some infernal jest.

Yet when I think of her—those eyes half-lifted in innocence, those lips parted without guile, that quiet strength wrapped in grace—I see angels. Not just beauty, but something divine: tenderness tempered with power, a presence that commands admiration and ignites devotion. I must flee. I cannot bear to see her again.

But Thomas is steady. His resolve is firm. He must never know that mine cracks like a statue struck by lightning.

Had Thomas grown up in Paris as I did, perhaps he too would have fallen. But I am the weak one. Curse my own damnation!

Did I say I would run? I cannot—not yet. One more look. Just to confirm whether this torment is born of my own frailty or of her true worth. Perhaps yesterday I mistook pallor for plainness, and found instead a brilliance I could not have imagined. If my pain comes from my own failing, then I must face it before it destroys me. I will stay. And Thomas shall not—shall not—know what I feel. He shall never learn how she haunts my thoughts, how her name echoes in my bones.

I told him tonight—under the guise of an old tale. Oh, the devils must laugh at us men. They have every reason. Sometimes I wonder if my father, now clear-eyed in death, joins their mockery.

Home. Back to my solitude, where nothing distracts from the ache that gnaws at me. I pace. I scream her name—Eva!—even beneath Evelyn’s portrait. There are moments when I consider writing to Thomas, forbidding him—demanding he stop. But no man shall ever know. Not Thomas. Not anyone.

Eva! Eva! Eva! Every fiber of my being cries it. But silence is my vow.

I wait for his letters—as a condemned man waits for the hangman. Are they truly in love? Or will her spirit, so refined, so full of depth, demand more than Thomas can give? A stronger man. A fiercer heart. A—

I saw my father rise from his grave in one black hour. He cursed me. Oh, let him curse on—if only I could burn away this fire.

But I spoke of secrecy. Yet Bartow knows. In his silence, in his deafness, he has sensed my wound. He watches me. He moves differently. He guards the birdcage with a glare that speaks of jealousy. If I were not drowning in torment, I might laugh. But instead, I want to scream.

A mute servant—picked up somewhere I’ve forgotten—jealous of my feelings for John Poindexter’s daughter? He never cared when I spoke of Evelyn. But now… now her name haunts these rooms. Her image—once my comfort—has become my shame. I used to speak her name often. I still do. But now it is Eva’s voice that echoes in the walls.

And today—today—the starling, trained to whisper Evelyn’s name, shrieked: “Eva! Eva!”

My first impulse was to kill it. Then—to cradle it close, as if it carried a piece of my soul. But I did neither. I am still human.

Bartow will kill it if I don’t act. This morning, I caught him hand on cage, eyes blazing with intent. He cannot hear the word. But he knows it is a name. He knows it matters. He wants it silenced.

Should I raise the cage out of reach? His deafness, his obedience—given only through colored signals I send—his uncanny loyalty rooted in an almost animal attachment to me—these things are vital now. But the bird—ah, there it goes again: “Eva! Eva!”

Is it hatred? Or love? Love would seek to protect. But I have no wish to save her. She loves Thomas. She has chosen him. And he—oh, it is no longer for Evelyn’s sake that I plot revenge. It is for myself.

These days of agony. The revelation of my own weakness. The forced consent to a union that brings them joy and me ruin. All of it demands retribution. They will not escape. Not Thomas. Not Eva.

I have laid my plans. Deep. Careful. Contingencies foreseen. Time spent. Money wasted. But the result is complete. If they step into my circular study, they must obey me—or die. And I will die with them.

They shall never live in peace. Thomas may dream otherwise. John Poindexter may hope. But I remember.

I remember Evelyn.

And I remember Felix.

Ah—again. Eva! Eva! Eva!

What follows is Thomas’s own account, as he confessed it to the detectives:

As I had promised, I brought my young wife to Felix’s house on the day and at the hour we had agreed upon. We walked there—no need for a carriage, since the hotel where we were staying was only a short distance away—and were met at the door by an old servant whose silence and deafness I had been warned about. The moment I saw him, and then the dim, grand hall stretching before us in quiet aristocratic gloom, Eva turned pale and looked at me with a question in her eyes. But I smiled reassuringly, though inside I was trembling with dread of what lay ahead. Taking her arm, I followed the servant down the hall, past the open drawing-room door—where I expected we might pause—into a plain room that made me frown until Bartow, as I later learned he was called, pulled aside a heavy portière and led us into my brother’s study.

At that moment, the room seemed like something out of a dream—fairyland, perhaps—had not Felix, who stood alone within, immediately drawn our attention with the sheer force of his presence, more powerful than ever before.

Eva, who knew little of this brother—nothing that would have prepared her for such a man—looked frightened, slightly awed. Yet she stepped forward bravely toward him, and when I introduced her, she smiled with such grace and warmth that I secretly hoped he might be disarmed.

And perhaps he was. His already pale face grew almost waxy under the strange yellow glow of the lantern above us. His hand, raised in a mechanical greeting, dropped heavily, and he could barely manage a few words of welcome. They sounded inadequate—too thin for the occasion—except for the way his eyes locked onto hers, revealing a depth of feeling she couldn’t possibly understand. For her very life—her happiness, which is life itself—hung in the balance beneath that iron will.

I, who knew—or thought I did—cast him a pleading glance. Then, saying I had explanations to make, I asked if Mrs. Adams might rest while we spoke privately.

He answered with a look so strange I couldn’t read it. Did he sense the rebellion in my tone? Did he understand then, as he would later, the nature of my compliance? I’ll never know. All I know is that he stopped fumbling with some small object on the table, bowed with a sarcastic elegance that made me feel suddenly inferior—even in stature—and led me to a small door I hadn’t noticed before.

"Your wife will find it more comfortable here," he said slowly, pausing between words as if struggling to contain a storm beneath his calm. He opened the door to reveal a small but elegant bedroom. "What we have to say won’t take long. Mrs. Adams won’t find the wait tedious."

"No," she replied, smiling with a natural laugh that, I hope, came from perfect joy. Still, she must have found the whole scene odd—my manner, his, the way I was leaving her with a stranger. It wasn’t the kind of introduction one expects from a groom bringing his bride to meet his only living relative.

"I’ll call you—" I began, but the image of her dimpled face above the bouquet of roses she carried made me forget the rest. The door closed behind me, and I was left face to face with Felix.

He breathed easier now that we were alone, and his manner seemed more natural. Yet he didn’t speak at once. Instead, he cast a curious glance around the room—the most unsettling space I’ve ever seen—and after seeming satisfied with what he saw, led me to the large table that had always held a strange pull over him.

"Come further back," he said. "I need air—space in this confined room. And so do you. Besides, why should she hear what we’re about to say? She’ll learn the truth soon enough. She seems a gentle woman."

"An angel!" I started to say, but he silenced me with a sharp gesture.

"We won’t discuss your wife—Mrs. Adams," he snapped. "Where is John Poindexter?"

"At the hotel," I replied. "Or maybe he’s already gone home. I no longer care about him. Felix, I will never leave my wife. I’d rather break the oath I swore—before I even knew how precious the woman was whose happiness I vowed to destroy. That’s what I’ve come to tell you. Make it easy, Felix. You’ve loved and suffered. Let us bury the past. Let us—"

Had I truly believed I could move him? Perhaps I still clung to childish hopes. But even as those words left my lips—before I’d fully spoken them—I saw his expression harden, as if carved from stone. I could falter; he could not. He stood firm, stronger than ever, beyond appeal.

But what did it matter? At worst, what did I have to lose? A struggle that might bring Eva pain, and me the loss of a fortune I had begun to think of as mine. Trivial concerns. Eva would learn the truth sooner or later—my real name, the story of her father’s guilt. Why not now? And if we had to begin life poor, at least we’d live together. To separate from her—

Felix broke the silence with words I never expected.

"I anticipated this. From the moment you begged me to allow you to marry her, I knew this would happen. I prepared for it. Weakness from her husband was predictable. So I’ve hardened myself. Your oath must be kept!"

The cold authority in his voice crushed me. It was the voice of power, against which any ordinary resistance would fail. I glanced around the room, imitating what I’d seen him do earlier. Nothing seemed threatening. Yet a new fear crept in—the first real distrust I’d felt—sparked by the way he’d scanned the bizarre, Eastern-style interior of the study. Was it his calm confidence? Or the eerie sensation of being surrounded by Oriental splendor, lit like night despite the daylight outside, all while the city’s noise raged just beyond the walls?

From that moment on, I felt a vague terror—not for myself, but for her. Her voice drifted through the wall, humming a cheerful tune in the next room. I forced myself to stay calm. I even smiled—though I felt the gaze of Evelyn’s painted face watching me. Evelyn, whose portrait I had seen the instant I entered, though I hadn’t looked directly at it. Now I stood with my back to it. Felix faced it, yet refused to raise his eyes. He waited for my reply. When I hesitated, he repeated:

"That oath must be kept."

This time, I found words.

"Impossible!" I shouted, throwing doubt, fear, hesitation—all I’d feared—into the wind. "I took that oath under pressure: family loyalty, our father’s dying breath, a thousand calculated influences designed to trap me. But I can’t keep it after four months of real life with a man thirty years removed from his crime, and with his innocent daughter. You can’t force me to abandon my wife because of a wicked vow made before I knew her. If you think your money—"

"Money?" he laughed, with a contempt that mocked both my sincerity and his own. "I’d forgotten I had any. No, Thomas, I wouldn’t trade money for the chance to live with a woman like your wife. But her life? That’s another matter. Carry out your threat—fail to pay John Poindexter the debt we owe—and things will become serious. You’re unprepared. A daughter dead in her honeymoon would break him as much as one dishonored. And either dead or disgraced, he’ll find her when he comes here searching for the child he can’t forget. Which shall it be? Speak!"

Was I dreaming? Was this really Felix? Was this really me? Were these words truly being spoken?

In a flash, I lunged to his side, where he stood near the table, and groaned, more than screamed:

"You wouldn’t kill her! You don’t mean to commit murder—here—on her—the innocent—the good—"

"No," he said quietly. "It will be you who kills her. You who won’t want to see her suffer—fade—go mad. Thomas, I am not the madman you imagine. I am a keeper of oaths. More than that—I have skill. This house proves it. This room—see? It has no exit except those high windows, barely visible, and that doorway, now covered by a curtain. But in an instant—without moving—I can seal this room so tightly that no man, unless armed with a crowbar and pickaxe, could enter, even if they knew we were trapped. And soon, the entire house will be sealed shut by Bartow, my servant, as he leaves."

"God protect us," I whispered, frozen with horror, my eyes darting from his face to the distant windows, then to the door—just steps away, yet infinitely far from the room where Eva waited.

Felix watched me calmly, explaining as if describing something mundane.

"You’re looking for the windows," he said. "They’re hidden behind the goblin faces on the tapestries overhead. As for the door—if you’d looked to your left when you entered—you’d have seen the edge of a massive steel plate flush with the frame. With a touch of my hand on this button, it slides across the opening instantly. Once moved, nothing—not even I—can push it back. I have power to send it forward, but not to return it. Doubt my mechanical skill? Or the electrical system I installed here? Don’t, Thomas. Nor doubt the will that needs only a second to act. Shall I press the button, brother?"

"No! No!" I cried, overcome by terror—not from his words, but from knowing the man I faced. "Let me think. Tell me what you demand. What will she suffer if I comply? What will I?"

His hand retreated—smooth, white, a hand I’d admired before. Then he smiled.

"I knew you wouldn’t be foolish," he said. "Life has its charms, even for hermits like me. And for a *beau garçon* like you—"

"Hush!" I interrupted, driven by rage. "I’m not buying my life. I’m saving hers—possibly yours too. You’ve planned to die with us. Isn’t that it?"

"Certainly," he replied, coldly. "Am I an assassin? Would you expect me to live, knowing you’re perishing?"

I stared, stunned. Such resolve—such sacrifice for an idea—was beyond comprehension.

"Why—what?" I stammered. "Why kill us? Why kill yourself?"

The answer struck me like thunder.

"Remember Evelyn!" shrieked a voice from above.

I froze, struck dumb by a horror I never thought possible. It wasn’t Felix. It wasn’t my conscience. Muffled, strange, echoing from the vaulted ceiling—lit by that golden glow that would forever mean death to me.

"What is that?" I asked. "Another of your machines?"

He smiled. I wished he’d frowned.

"Not exactly. A starling. My favorite bird. Alas, he repeats what he’s heard—echoes from the silence of these rooms. I thought I’d muffled him well enough. But he’s stubborn. This warns me to hurry. Thomas, this is what I require: John Poindexter—he doesn’t know where he is now, but I do—received a telegram moments ago. If he’s a man at all, he’ll arrive here in half an hour. Sent in your name. It says matters demand his immediate attention. That you’re coming to your brother’s—giving him this address—where, if you gain entry, you’ll wait in the study. But if you’re not home, he’s to wait for a message at the Plaza. The house’s appearance will tell him whether you’re here. That’s what I sent in your name."

"Bartow," he continued, "will leave in five minutes. He’s going on a journey. He expects me to follow. He’ll draw every shade, lock every door. After he leaves, the house becomes a tomb. In two weeks, John Poindexter will return—and discover… well, let’s not rush the future. But Thomas—shall Bartow go? Or shall I signal him, using signs he understands better than speech, to wait and open the door for the man we’ve decided to crush in his moment of pride? You need only write a line. I’ve copied the words you must use—so you don’t lose control. Leave it here on the table. You go. Eva stays. He’ll read it, and I’ll add what’s needed. Have you anything to say against this plan? Anything beyond what you’ve already said?"

I picked up the paper. It lay on the table, clearly written just before or during our arrival. I read the words. You know them. But now they carry a terrible weight:

"You want me to sign this—to write it in my own hand—so it’s mine? Mine!" I repeated.

"Yes. And leave it here. He won’t believe anything I say about your intentions."

Horror swept over me. Love, life, friendship, hope—everything—rushed through me in one overwhelming flood. I could never sign such a blasphemy against all that is sacred.

Tossing the paper at him, I yelled:

"You’ve gone too far! Better her death, better mine, better the end of us all—than dishonor the purest thing heaven ever made. I refuse, Felix—I refuse! May God have mercy on us all!"

The moment was unbearable. I saw his face change, his finger tremble over the fatal button. I imagined the steel plate sliding forward—felt the doom closing in—and reached for him, ready to fight, when a sound from another direction caught my ear.

Turning in terror, I saw Eva stepping out of the room where we’d left her.

A deep red light replaced the sickly yellow that had filled the room. But I barely noticed—only that it gave her cheeks a color the roses at her belt lacked.

Fearless, sweet as when she first told me she loved me, she approached us.

"What is this?" she demanded. "I’ve heard words like a nightmare. What does it mean, Thomas? What does it mean, Mr. ——"

"Cadwalader," Felix said, lowering his eyes but not changing his expression.

"Cadwalader?" she echoed. "I’ve heard that name. It was Evelyn’s name—the Evelyn who—"

"Whom you see painted above you," Felix finished. "My sister. Thomas’s sister. The girl your father—well, I spare you, though you’re the child of a man who spared nothing. From your husband, you may learn why a Cadwalader can never be happy with a Poindexter. Why, thirty years after that girl’s death, you—who weren’t born then—are given the choice between death and shame. I give you five minutes to listen. Then you’ll tell me your decision. But stay where you are. One step—either of you—and Thomas knows what follows."

Five minutes. To explain, to decide. I felt dizzy, speechless. But seeing her eyes—raised in silent trust—filled me with strength. I burst into words—excuses, explanations, everything a man might say in such a crisis.

She listened, understanding more from my face than my words. As the minutes passed, and Felix raised a warning hand, she turned to him and said:

"You’re serious? We must part in shame—or die in this prison with you?"

His answer was flat, mechanical, but firm:

"You’ve said it. You have one minute left, madam."

She shrank. Then, a sudden surge of courage—she became a blazing angel where a fragile woman had stood. She glanced at me, testing if I had the strength to strike my own blood. (Alas, I didn’t understand.) Then, grabbing an old Turkish dagger lying nearby, she plunged it sideways into his side, shouting:

"We cannot part—we cannot die—we are too young, too happy!"

It was swift. A single act of a woman shattered the most carefully laid plans of a mind that had mastered every contingency.

But I didn’t see that then. I didn’t think of our escape, or the cost Eva might pay. My world was Felix—whose near-death had awakened the full strength of his spirit.

Though wounded, he didn’t fall. It was as if his will—forged in thirty years of torment—held him upright so he could give Eva one last look—a look I’d never seen, and never will forget. Then, seeing her collapse, her delicate form weakening, he turned to me—and I saw—God help me—a tear rise from his stone-like eyes and fall slowly down his cheek, hollowing under death’s blow.

"Eva! Eva! I love Eva!" shrieked the voice from the vault above.

Felix heard. A faint smile touched his lips—a revelation to me. He loved Eva too.

When he saw I understood, his will failed. He sank to the floor, whispering:

"Take her away. I forgive. Save! Save! She didn’t know I loved her."

Eva, horrified, staring at her deed, didn’t move. But when she saw his head fall back, his limbs grow still, a shock ran through her—so intense I feared it would shatter her mind.

"I’ve killed him!" she murmured. "I’ve killed him!" Her eyes darted wildly, landing on the cross hanging on the wall behind us. It reminded her—perhaps—that Felix was Catholic. "Bring it!" she gasped. "Put it on his chest. It may bring him peace—hope."

As I rushed to obey, she collapsed.

"Save!" came again from lips we thought sealed forever.

I realized her danger—and the need to shield her from this horror. I placed the cross in his hands, lifted her from the floor, and ran out of the house.

But as I saw the busy street, the crowd rushing by, I woke to our peril. I set her down, revived her with my own strength, dragged her down the steps, merged into the throng, breathing for her, living in her until we reached a carriage and fled.

For the silence we’ve kept since, don’t blame Mrs. Adams. When she regained consciousness—days later—she wanted to confess, to take responsibility. But I wouldn’t allow it. I loved her too much to see her name in the papers. When her father learned the truth, he insisted on silence, sharing my duty to guard her.

But alas—his silence came from pride, not love. John Poindexter cares nothing for his daughter, no more than he did for his wife or the long-forgotten child whose grave sparked this tragedy. Had Felix won, he would never have broken that man’s heart. As he once said: when a man cares for nothing, not even himself, there’s no point in cursing him.

A heavy silence, thick with unspoken emotion, settled over the room after the final plea had been made. The listeners—each absorbed in the unfolding truth—exchanged hushed whispers, while Thomas Adams turned toward his wife, his face a blend of love and anguish. Then, Inspector Loring handed back to Mr. Gryce the memorandum he had received earlier. It now bore beside each point the word “Answered,” save one.

Here is what it read:

1. Why should a woman who remained calm enough to pause and fix her hair during the beginning of an interview become so consumed by fury that she killed a man—someone she had no known grudge against—with her own hands? (Recall the comb found on the floor of Mr. Adams’s bedroom.)

2. What was the meaning of the words written just before the murder: *"I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!"*

3. Why did the writer use the pronoun "I"? What relationship existed between Mr. Felix Adams and the young woman that would justify such language—especially after her marriage to his brother?

4. Having used such a claim, why did he attempt to swallow the paper when attacked, dying with it clenched between his teeth?

5. If he died in rage—as the woman herself said, “like a monster”—why did his face bear sorrow instead of hatred, and a quiet resolve far removed from the wild emotions expected upon receiving a fatal blow from an unexpected foe?

6. If he had strength to seize the paper and bring it to his mouth, why did he not use that same strength to turn on a light—something that might have summoned help—instead of leaving lit the crimson glow, which, according to the signal code now understood, meant: *"Nothing more required just now. Keep away?"*

7. What was the purpose of the massive steel plate discovered between the door casings—and why did it remain motionless at the moment of his death, when it could have been triggered?

8. How did old Poindexter arrive so swiftly after the crime? His words were overheard: *"It is Amos’ son, not Amos!"* Did he not know whom he was expecting? Was the sight of a man lying dead with a cross on his chest and a dagger in his heart less shocking than the identity of the victim?

9. Recall Bartow’s pantomime. The thrust that killed Mr. Adams was delivered with the left hand. Watch for confirmation that the woman is left-handed—and explain why she held her other arm rigidly behind her for so long.

10. Why did the bird, whose cry was usually *"Remember Evelyn!"*, sometimes shift to *"Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?"* This suggests that Mr. Adams knew his brother’s bride intimately, both in time and affection.

11. If Bartow is innocent—merely a witness—why does he express such unmistakable joy at the outcome? This may not be within Thomas Adams’s confession, but it cannot be ignored. The deaf-and-dumb servant was driven mad by the very fact that brought him pleasure. Why?
**Answered.** [Footnote: At the time, the police had not yet recovered the scraps of writing in Felix’s hand. Those notes revealed Bartow’s deep jealousy—a jealousy that transformed into triumph when he saw his master fall at the hands of the woman he both loved and loathed. How he recognized her—this woman married to another—as the one whose name often hovered on his master’s lips remains a mystery best left to psychologists. But likely, Bartow spent hours watching through the stairway loophole, studying every gesture, every expression, until he knew his master’s soul.]

12. Observe this chart, compiled after numerous tests with Bartow and the colored lights of the strange lamp in Mr. Adams’s study:
- White: Water wanted.
- Green: Overcoat and hat to be brought.
- Blue: Return books to shelves.
- Violet: Prepare the study for night.
- Yellow: Wait for next signal.
- Red: Nothing needed; stay away.

The red light was burning at the time of the murder. Can this be explained by Mr. Adams’s account?

Only two points remained unresolved. The most pressing was No. 9—the mystery of the left-handed thrust and the stiffened right arm in Bartow’s silent reenactment.

Mr. Gryce conferred briefly with the inspector, then approached Thomas Adams. “Do you have the strength,” he asked gently, “to demonstrate how you saw your wife strike?”

The young man stared, unable to speak. He knew Bartow had mimed the act—but not its details. That knowledge had been withheld by the authorities. So he hesitated, thinking deeply, before extending his left hand, seizing an object from the inspector’s desk, and lunging across his body toward an imagined target to his right.

He looked around anxiously, seeking approval—or understanding.

“You’re suggesting your wife was left-handed?” Mr. Gryce said quietly.

“I never saw her use her left hand before,” Adams replied. “But she struck with it—because her right was trapped in mine. I grabbed her instinctively as she lunged for the weapon. Our hands locked together—tight—until the horror of what she’d done overwhelmed her. She fainted, collapsing to the floor, whispering: *‘Tear down the cross and lay it on your brother’s breast. I want at least to see him die as a Christian.’*”

Mr. Gryce glanced at the inspector, relief softening his features. The puzzle of the extended arm—so puzzling in Bartow’s performance—was now clear. Taking up the blue pencil the inspector had laid aside, he smiled and wrote, decisively, **"Answered"** beside paragraph nine.

A few minutes later, Mr. Gryce stood in the outer room, his eyes scanning the gathered figures with quiet intensity. He was searching for answers—answers to a question that still lingered, unresolved, in the depths of his mind. And he found them not in grand declarations or dramatic confessions, but in the quiet corner where Miss Butterworth sat, composed and unflustered, as though she had merely stepped aside from a long-expected role.

He approached her with a subtle smile. “I suspect you were behind this entire arrangement,” he said, half-joking, half-serious.

She looked up, her expression firm. “I was simply present,” she replied, with a hint of indignation. “When the moment came—when that young woman learned her husband had vanished into New York with two strangers—it became clear that someone must be at her side who understood both her plight and the gravity of her situation. And I was that person.”

“You are always that person,” he said warmly. “If by ‘that person’ one means the woman who arrives precisely when needed, in perfect measure and timing. So you’ve already heard Mrs. Adams’s story?”

“Yes,” she answered, her voice softening. “Her words, spoken in anguish, revealed everything. She was the one who wielded the dagger that ended Mr. Adams’s life. But afterward, when reason returned, she explained what had driven her—what made such an act not only possible, but necessary. When I heard it, Mr. Gryce, I no longer doubted either her right to act or my duty to stand beside her. Do you think she will face trial? Will she be charged? Convicted?”

Mr. Gryce shook his head gently. “Madam, there are not twelve men in this city so lacking in judgment that they would label an act of self-defense as crime. No grand jury will return a true bill against young Mrs. Adams. You may rest assured.”

The shadow that had clouded Miss Butterworth’s features lifted instantly. “Then I may go home in peace,” she declared, almost breathlessly. “These past five hours have been harrowing. I feel thoroughly shaken. Would you do me the kindness of escorting me to my carriage?”

Though she spoke of being shaken, Mr. Gryce saw no sign of it. To him, she appeared more serene than ever—calm, collected, even radiant in her quiet strength. Yet she knew herself best, and once satisfied that Mrs. Adams was recovering under her husband’s care, she accepted his arm without hesitation.

As they walked, he asked about Mr. Poindexter. “Why isn’t Mrs. Adams’s father here? Did he permit his daughter to venture into such danger without offering to accompany her?”

Her reply was sharp, cutting. “Mr. Poindexter is a man devoid of feeling. He came with us to New York, but refused to follow us to Police Headquarters. Sir, you’ll find that three hearts consumed by passion—and a revenge more deeply held than any I’ve ever known—were all wasted on a man incapable of suffering. Do not speak of John Poindexter to me again. And now—if you would be so kind—tell the coachman to take me home to Gramercy Park. I have stirred the police pot for the final time, Mr. Gryce. For the very last time.”

She settled back into the carriage cushions, her expression stern, resolute—yet beneath it, a quiet satisfaction gleamed, unmistakable. It was the look of one who had done what was right, even if it defied convention, and who now felt no regret.

Mr. Gryce, suppressing a smile, bowed lower than usual—a gesture of respect, perhaps, or farewell. Under the shadow of that bow, the carriage rolled away.

Alone again, he turned slowly toward the house, a faint sigh escaping his lips. Was he wondering whether another case—another mystery—would bring them together again? Whether fate would once more place him beside the calm, courageous woman who never hesitated to step into the shadows?
