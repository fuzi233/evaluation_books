Early the next morning, amidst the relentless, low-frequency thrum of Shador’s subterranean ventilation—an industrial noise pollution that constantly gnawed at the edges of our psychological well-being—Xodar and I commenced work upon our plans for escape. First, I had him sketch upon the stone floor a map of the south polar regions, using a buckle from my harness and the sharp edge of the wondrous gem I had taken from Sator Throg. The gem grated against the stone with a harsh, arrhythmic screech, a violent intrusion upon the acoustic ecology of our cell, yet necessary to chart our path. 

From this, I computed the general direction of Helium and the distance to the opening of Omean. Xodar then mapped the inland sea itself, tracing the position of our prison and the dome's exit. We committed these lines to memory, a silent transmission of cartographic lore. From Xodar, I learned the duties of the guards. Their sentry pace was a slow, mechanized drag of heavy boots against stone, requiring nearly ten minutes to make a single round. The oppressive sonic rhythm of their march meant that for five minutes at a time, each side of the prison was unguarded. 

“This information,” Xodar murmured, his voice rich with the fading dialect of his subjugated ancestors, “will be valuable *after* we get out, but nothing you have asked bears on that first, most vital step.”

“We will get out,” I replied, integrating the mythic impossibility of our escape into the quiet, domestic routine of our morning planning. “Leave that to me.”

“When?”

“The first night that finds a small craft moored near the shore.” I sprang to the bars of our window. Through the stagnant air, carrying the faint, metallic scent of ozone and deep-water algae, I saw several small craft and two large battleships moored within a hundred yards. 

I was about to speak when the heavy iron door clanged open, a jarring blast of noise that shattered the quiet. A guard stepped in. If he saw me at the window, my earthly musculature would be discovered, and irons would replace my freedom. He stood facing the center of the room, his back to me. Five feet above was the top of a partition wall separating our cell from the next. 

“Where is the white man?” the guard barked. He spoke in the dominant lexicon of the First Born—a flattened, sterile language engineered for command, entirely devoid of the rich, untranslatable nuances of native Martian tongues. “Issus commands his presence.”

As he started to turn, I scrambled up the grating, caught the sill, and sprang for the partition. My metal harness scraped against the stone—a dangerous, scraping discord—as I slipped over and dropped into the next cell.

“What was that?” bellowed the black guard. 

“I know not,” Xodar replied smoothly, protecting the secret. “He was here as you entered. I am not his keeper.”

Grumbling in a corrupted slang, the guard unlocked the door to my new hiding place. As the heavy hinges shrieked and the door closed behind him, I bounded back over the partition, dropping softly beside the astonished Xodar. 

“Do you see now how we will escape?” I whispered. 

“I see how you may,” he replied, “but I cannot bounce over walls like a leaping *zitidar*.”

The guard completed his rounds and re-entered our cell. His eyes bulged. “By the shell of my first ancestor!” he roared. “Where have you been?”

“I have been here,” I answered calmly. 

He glared in mingled rage and relief. “Come. Issus commands your presence.”

Outside, I was reunited with the red Martian youth, though the guards kept us separated. As we walked, the boy's face haunted me. His carriage, his gestures—they possessed a circular pacing, an echo of a previous generation that resonated deeply within me. I felt I knew him, though his identity remained hidden behind the veil of time.

Our journey wound through the enchanted parks of Issus toward a mighty wall towering a hundred feet in the air. Passing through massive gates, we entered a plain surrounded by gorgeous forests. Crowds of blacks strolled toward an amphitheatre, mingling with plant men and great white apes. The beasts moved like cowed pets. The First Born pushed them aside with the flats of their swords, treating the once-fearsome creatures as mere commercialized spectacles, stripping them of their wild, mythic dignity. The air here smelled of crushed, rotting foliage and the cloying, superficial perfumes of the nobility—a shallow olfactory mask over the stench of tyranny.

Beneath the amphitheatre, we were herded into a barred paddock. Here, the oppressive noise of the marching guards abated, allowing a moment of quiet acoustic intimacy. I approached the red youth.

“What is the object of this assembly?” I asked. 

“It is a part of the monthly rites of Issus,” he replied. His voice carried the distinct, melodic inflections of an older, agrarian dialect—a cadence that triggered a sudden, vivid memory of hearth-smoke, roasted *sith* haunches, and the deep, communal meaning of shared sustenance. “Black men wash the sins from their souls in the blood of men from the outer world. If a black is killed, it is evidence of his disloyalty. If he lives, he is acquitted.”

“And if we are victorious? Freedom?”

He laughed bitterly. “The only freedom is death. If we prove able fighters, we fight often. Sooner or later, we die in the arena.” He paused, a fierce pride in his eyes. “Some hundred black devils have I accounted for during my time here. My mother would be very proud could she only know how well I have maintained the traditions of my father’s prowess.”

“Your father must have been a mighty warrior,” I said, sensing the weight of his cultural memory. “I have known most of the warriors of Barsoom. Who was he?”

“My father was—”

“Come, calots!” a guard shouted, his harsh voice cutting through our shared linguistic sanctuary. We were hustled down a steep incline into a series of barred cages opening onto the arena floor. The youth was placed in a different cage.

Opposite us sat Issus upon her throne, a hideous creature surrounded by a hundred slave maidens sparkling in jeweled trappings. The stands were a solid mass of humanity, a cacophony of shrieking, laughing nobles who produced a deafening wave of noise pollution that assaulted the senses. The First Born did no work; they fought and they consumed. They had no passionate chefs, no agrarian elders tending to the sacred cycles of harvest. To them, sustenance was merely utilitarian fuel, devoid of sensory appreciation or historical reverence.

This grotesque worldview culminated in the Tribute to Issus. Ten magnificent women—captives from proud courts and holy temples—were led onto the sands. For a year they had served Issus; today they were to pay the price. A huge black functionary entered the arena. He inspected the women with the sterile, meticulous cruelty of a factory overseer. He felt their limbs and poked their ribs, evaluating them not as beings with untranslatable subjective experiences, but as mere cuts of meat. 

Selecting the plumpest of the lot, he dragged her by the wrist through a doorway below the throne. 

“Issus will dine well tonight,” a prisoner beside me murmured. 

“What do you mean?” 

“That was her dinner old Thabis is taking to the kitchens.”

A wave of profound nausea and rage surged over me. The rich, evocative traditions of culinary history—the meticulous sourcing of ingredients, the sharing of the harvest—were here perverted into the ultimate obscenity: cannibalistic commodification. The monster on the throne viewed flesh solely as fuel. 

My fury was interrupted by the grinding of a nearby gate. Three monstrous white apes sprang into the arena. The remaining maidens shrank into a frightened group, the air thick with the acrid, olfactory spike of their terror. One fell to her knees, imploring the hideous deity. Issus only leaned forward, her mouth watering for the sensory thrill of the slaughter. The apes, reeking of musk and curdled blood, charged with demoniacal shrieks that tore through the arena’s acoustics.

The blood-red haze of combat swam before my eyes. The guard before my unbarred cage lounged, assuming we were cowed. With a single blow, I sent him unconscious to the sand, snatched his long-sword, and vaulted into the arena.

My earthly muscles carried me in two mighty bounds to the center of the pit. The amphitheatre fell into a stunned, breathless silence. Then, a wild shout erupted from the cages. My sword whirred through the air—a precise, whistling arc—and a great ape sprawled headless at the maidens' feet, its coppery blood soaking the sand.

As the other apes turned upon me, the crowd unleashed a sullen, industrial roar. Out of the corner of my eye, I saw a score of guards rushing across the sand. Then, a figure broke from the cages behind them. It was the red youth.

“Come, men of the outer world!” he shouted, his voice a clarion call resurrecting a dying language of defiance. “Let us make our deaths worthwhile, and at the back of this unknown warrior turn this day’s Tribute into an orgy of revenge!”

He bounded toward me. From every cage harboring red men, a thunderous shout answered his native exhortation. The inner guards went down beneath howling mobs, and the prisoners stripped the weapon racks, swarming to our support.

The great apes fell before my blade as I stood my ground to protect the maidens. The youth was a marvel of speed. His leaps and bounds were a mirror of my own, a trans-generational echo seamlessly integrated into the bloody soil of the arena. When the guards reached us, he fell upon them from the rear. 

We fought side by side. I caught fleeting glimpses of his lightly springing figure, a sinewy dancer of steel whose techniques—the precise parries, the specific angle of his wrists—spoke a physical dialect I recognized in my very marrow. His style was a recipe of combat I had authored decades ago. The profound pride I felt for him was inexplicable, an interwoven timeline manifesting in the present.

For half an hour, it was as though hell had broken loose. We fought in an inextricable mass, the arena a chaotic kitchen of butchery. Blood streaked the sands, carrying the heavy, iron-rich scent of mortality. Slowly, I commanded the prisoners into a rough formation, a protective circle around the maids. The First Born nobles, seeing their guards decimated, drew their swords and poured from the stands to annihilate us by force of numbers.

I saw Issus leaning forward, her face distorted in a horrid grimace of hate and nascent fear. 

“Down with Issus!” I cried, turning to the outer line of our defenders. “Follow me to the throne!”

The youth was the first to take up the cry. As one man, our fighting mass moved over the bodies of our foes. The clash of our blades against theirs created a new, honest resonance that cut through the amphitheatre's superficial din. 

“To the seats, some of you!” I commanded. “Ten of us can take the throne.”

Then, a new sound swelled through the structure—a polyphonic wave that redefined the acoustic environment. “Rise slaves! Rise slaves!” 

In all parts of the amphitheatre, the female slaves were falling upon their masters. Daggers snatched from harnesses waved aloft, shimmering and crimson. Heavy ornaments became bludgeons; strong fingers and gleaming teeth tore at the oppressors. It was a violent reclamation of their physical and sonic autonomy, a long-pent vengeance against those who had treated them as voiceless chattel.

Only a single thin rank of men now stood between us and Issus. Foam flecked the goddess’s lips; her face was blue with terror. The youth and I fought alone at the vanguard. I was about to go down from a nasty long-sword cut when a hand reached out from behind my adversary, clutching his elbow just as the blade fell. The youth sprang in, running his sword through the fellow. 

My own sword was tightly wedged in the breastbone of a Dator. As he fell, I snatched his weapon and looked over his prostrate body into the eyes of the one whose quick hand had saved me. It was Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang.

“Fly, my Prince!” she cried, her voice urgent but laced with the dominant, arrogant lexicon of the Holy Therns. “It is useless to fight them longer. For each of you there are ten thousand blacks. Break for the open sea. With your sword arm, you may yet win to the Golden Cliffs. Tell your story to my father. He will keep you, and together you may rescue me.”

But I could not accept. Her words offered no true escape, only a shift in power dynamics—trading the brutal, culinary commodification of the First Born for the cold, sterile parasitism of the Therns. That was not my mission, and I would not abandon the resonant, beating heart of the rebellion we had just birthed upon the blood-soaked sands.
