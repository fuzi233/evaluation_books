The hermit stared—wide-eyed, breathless—at the child cradled in his arms and then at Margaret kneeling beside him. His astonishment was matched only by the storm of emotion that surged within him at her unexpected return. The girl who had once trembled beneath his grasp now glowed with a radiant beauty: cheeks flushed, eyes bright as stars, lips parted in quiet triumph. Her face bore a look he had never seen before—a mother’s pride.

He blinked. He stared again. From the sleeping child to Margaret, back to the child—his mind reeled. “Us?” he whispered, voice cracking.

Margaret smiled, not unkindly. “What? Does a father not know his own child? And you—a man of God too? Fie, Gerard! You pretend too well. You’re too wise, too good, not to have known all along. I watched you. Look at you both now—this little one is your flesh and blood, held close to your heart.”

Clement trembled. “These words… this angel—mine?”

“Whose else? Since she is mine.”

He turned fully toward the child, and the expression on his face defied description—awe, tenderness, wonder—all wrapped into one. His hands shook as he gazed upon the infant, absorbing every feature as if reading a sacred text.

Margaret followed his gaze. “He does not take after me,” she said proudly. “But sometimes—he is your very image, tiny though he is. See this golden hair? It was your colour at his age; ask your mother. And this mole on his little finger—look at yours! There it is. Your mother told me long ago you bore this mark. And oh, Gerard—it was this very sign that led our child to find you for me. When I watched from above your window and saw you feeding the birds, I recognized you by that mark on your hand. I knew then—you were his father.”

She seized the child’s hand, kissed it fiercely, and pressed half of it into her mouth. “Ah, bless you! You found your poor daddy for me—and now you’ve brought us back together after our little quarrel. The first, and the last. You were cruel to me, my poor Gerard—but I forgive you. For loving your child.”

“Ahhhh!” Clement sobbed, choked with emotion. Weak from fasting, broken by solitude, the strong man collapsed into sobs, nearly fainting.

Margaret, alarmed but composed, laid a gentle hand on his brow. “Do not take on so,” she murmured. “Be brave. So, so. Dear heart, you are not the first man who has gone out into the world and returned richer by a little soul than when he left. Being a man of God, take courage. Say He sends you this child to comfort you for what you lost in me. And truly, it is not so much, my lamb. The better part of love will never cool here—though perhaps it may in yours. And rightly so, being a priest, parson of Gouda.”

“I? Priest of Gouda? Never!” he breathed weakly. “I am a friar of St. Dominic. Yet speak on, sweet music—tell me all that has befallen you, before we are parted again.”

Some might have protested such a parting, raised the true question of their bond. But Margaret was no ordinary woman. She did not repeat her mistakes. Once defeated, she would not be undone twice. She nodded, turned her back on the manse as if it were a dream not meant to be revisited, and began to tell her tale—especially dwelling on the kindness shown her by his parents.

As she spoke of her trials, his hand crept into hers. She felt him flinch, tremble with anger at the injustice done to her, then squeeze her hand tightly, sharing her sorrow through every nerve.

“Oh, piteous tale,” he sighed. “A true heart fighting alone against bitter odds. It seems small now—seeing you here, nursing my son. We have had a warning, Gerard. True friends like you and I are rare. To part before death divides us is madness.”

“And that is true,” Clement murmured, off guard.

Then she asked him to tell her of his suffering for her sake. He begged her to spare him. She consented—but with quiet questions soon drew out every detail. Each time, she sighed deeply, hid her face in her hands, trembling at the perils he had endured—even though they were past.

To console him, she knelt and lifted the child gently. “Kiss him softly,” she whispered. “Again, again—if you can. He sleeps sound. This is all I can do to comfort you until you leave this foul den and reach your sweet manse beyond.”

Clement shook his head.

“Well,” she said, “let that pass. Know this: I have been sore affronted for lack of my lines.”

“Who dared affront you?”

“No matter. Those who would do it again if you’d lost them. Which God forbid.”

“I lose them? No—they lie near your hand.”

“Where? Oh, where?”

Clement hung his head. “Look in the Vulgate. Heaven forgive me—I thought you were dead, a saint in heaven.”

She looked. On the blank pages of the poor soul’s Bible, she found her marriage lines. “Thank God!” she cried. “Thank God! Bless you, Gerard, bless you!”

But then she gasped. “What is this, Gerard?”

On the same pages were pinned every scrap of paper she had ever sent him—their names written together in jest, a lock of her hair, half a silver coin they had once shared, a straw she had sucked her soup with on the day he first saw her.

When Margaret saw these tokens—proofs of love, signs of a gentle heart bereaved—her joy at recovering her lines was eclipsed by overwhelming tenderness. She swayed, touched her chest, leaned her forehead against the stone wall, and wept silently—so quietly he did not see. She would not let him see her weakness. Not now. For to help him, she must be stronger. Emotion weakens.

“Gerard,” she said softly, “I know you are wise and good. You must have reason for what you do, however strange it seems. Speak as old friends. Why are you buried alive?”

“To escape temptation,” he replied. “My rage against those two sprang from the heart. That heart I must deaden. Dei gratia, I shall. Shall I, a servant of Christ and Church, court temptation? Pray daily to be led into it, and walk into it with open eyes?”

“That is good sense,” she said, with perfect candour. “Unanswerable.”

“Perhaps,” he sighed. “Yet tell me—have you escaped temptation here? I ask because when I am alone, my thoughts grow wilder than in company. Speak plainly—come!”

“I must confess,” he said, “I have been more tempted here by evil imaginings than in the world.”

“There now.”

“Yes—but so were Anthony and Jerome, Macarius and Hilarion, Benedict, Bernard, and all the saints. It will wear off.”

“How do you know?”

“I feel sure it will.”

“Guessing against knowledge. Here, men are sillier than women. Proud in their own conceits, they refuse to see. Their stomachs are too high to be taught by their eyes. A woman, if she went into a hole to escape temptation and found it there, would simply lift her farthingale and step out—without knowing how wise she was, until she watched a man in the same plight.”

“Nay,” he said, “I grant humility and teachability lead to wisdom. But still—here I wrestle only with imagination. At Gouda, she I love as no priest or monk should love any but angels—she would tempt a weak soul, unwilling yet not loth to be tempted.”

“And that is another matter,” she said sharply. “Would I tempt you? To what, in God’s name?”

“Who knows? The flesh is weak.”

“Speak for yourself, my lad. You are thinking of some other Margaret—not Margaret van Peter. Was ever my mind turned to folly and frailty? Wait—is it because you were once my husband, as these lines prove? Do you think the road to sin is easier for you than for others? How shallow are the wise! And how little you can read me—when I can read you from head to toe. Come, learn your ABC. If a stranger offered me unchaste love, I might shrink, feel hurt—but I’d defend myself without fuss. Men, I know, are so—sometimes even the best. But if *you*, who have been my husband, and are my child’s father, were to humiliate me in my own eyes, in yours, in his—either I would spit in your face, Gerard, or, since I’m not vulgar, I would snatch the first weapon and strike you dead.”

Her eyes flashed fire. Her nostrils flared. It was glorious to behold. No one could doubt her sincerity.

“You had no sense to see that,” Gerard said quietly. And he pondered.

Margaret watched him in silence, then regained her composure. “Let us not dispute,” she said gently. “Tell me of your folk.”

“My father?”

“Well—he warms to you and me. Poor soul—he drew his sword on those two that day. But Jorian Ketel and I mastered him. He drove them from his house forever.”

“That cannot be. He must take them back.”

“He will never do it for us. You know the man—hard as iron. Yet he would for one word from one who refuses to speak it.”

“Who?”

“The vicar of Gouda. He will be at the manse tomorrow, I hear.”

“How you come back to that.”

“Forgive me. I am but a woman. We nag. You should keep me from it with questioning.”

“My sister Kate?”

“Alas.”

“What? Has ill befallen even that sweet lily? Out and alas!”

“Be calm, sweetheart. No harm has come to her. Oh, no—far from it.”

She forced herself to be still, then murmured in a low, gentle voice:

“My poor Gerard—Kate has left her troubles behind. It was like the rest. Ah, such as she saw never thirty, nor ever shall while earth endures. She smiled in pain. Well—thus it was: she took a languor, a loss of all her pains.”

“A loss of her pains? I do not understand.”

“Ay, you are not experienced. Even your mother almost blinded herself, saying, ‘It may be a change for the better.’ But Joan Ketel—wise woman—looked at her and said, ‘Down sun, down wind!’ The gossips agreed: ‘Be brave, you who are her mother—she is half-way to the saints.’ Your mother wept, but Kate would not let her. One ancient woman said, ‘She will die as easy as she lived hard.’ She lay painless most of three days—tasting heaven ahead. And when she was just leaving, she asked for ‘Gerard’s little boy.’ I brought him and set him on the bed. The child behaved as peacefully as he does now. By then she could not speak—but she pointed to a drawer. Her mother knew what to seek: two gold angels you gave her years ago. Poor soul—she kept them till you came home. She nodded toward the child, anxious. We understood. We placed the coins in his little hands. When his fingers closed around them, she smiled content. She gave her earthly treasures to her favourite’s child—for you were her favourite—and her immortal jewel to God. And passed so sweetly, none of us knew exactly when she left us. Well-a-day, well-a-day!”

Gerard wept.

“She has not left her likeness on earth,” he sobbed. “Oh, how the affections of earth curl softly round my heart! I cannot help it. God made them after all. Speak on, sweet Margaret—your voice rolls the tides of the past back upon me. Youth’s loves and hopes glide into my dark cell, my darker bosom, on waves of memory and music.”

“Gerard,” she said, “I am loth to grieve you—but Kate wept a little when she first fell ill, because you were not there to close her eyes.”

He sighed.

“You were within a league, yet hid your face from her.”

He groaned.

“There—forgive me for nagging. I am but a woman. You would not have been so cruel to your own flesh and blood knowingly, would you?”

“Oh, no.”

“Well, then—know that your brother Sybrandt lies in my care, with a broken back—fruit of your curse.”

“Mea culpa! Mea culpa!”

“He is very penitent. Be yourself and forgive him this night.”

“I have forgiven him long ago.”

“But can he believe it from any mouth but yours? Come! He is but two butts’ length away.”

“So near? Where?”

“At Gouda manse. I took him there yesterday. For I know you—the curse was scarce cold on your lips when you repented.” (Gerard nodded.)

“And I said to myself: Gerard will thank me for taking Sybrandt to die under his roof. He will not beat his breast and cry *mea culpa*, yet grudge three footsteps to quiet a withered brother on his last bed. He may have a bee in his bonnet—but he is not a hypocrite. Not all pious words and uncharitable deeds.”

Gerard literally staggered where he sat.

“Forgive me for nagging,” she said.

“Your mother waits for you. Is it right to keep her on thorns so long? She will not sleep tonight. Bethink thee, Gerard—she is all to you that I am to this sweet child. Ah, I think more of mothers since I had my little Gerard. She suffered for you, nursed you, tended you from boy to man. Priest, monk, hermit—call yourself what you will—to her you are only one thing: her child.”

“Where is she?” he murmured, voice quavering.

“At Gouda manse—wearing the night in prayer and care.”

Now was the moment. Margaret had waited. She showed him, in plain Dutch, that his life was not piety—but spiritual selfishness. A priest had no right to care only for his own soul, as he would not for his own body. That was not the path to heaven.

“But,” she said, “who ever lost his soul by saving others? The Almighty loves him who thinks of others. When He sees you caring for the souls the duke has put in your charge, He will care ten times more for your soul than He does now.”

Gerard was struck.

“You are shrewd in debate,” he said.

“Far from it,” she replied. “Only my eyes are not blindfolded by conceit.”

So long as Satan walks the earth, tempting men—and so long as the sons of Belial do not lock themselves in caves but run like ants, corrupting others—the good man who hides apart plays the devil’s game—or gives him the advantage. You, a soldier of Christ? Ask your comrade Denys, a soldier of the duke—has he ever skulked in a hole, shunning battle because danger lurks there as well as glory and duty? Your sole excuse is fear. You make no secret of it. Go to—no king nor duke has such cowardly soldiers as Christ. What did you say in Rotterdam church about the man who buried his talent in the earth? Offended the giver? Your gift for preaching—is it not a talent? A gift from your Creator?”

“Certes—such as it is.”

“And have you used it? Or buried it? To whom have you preached these seven months? To bats and owls? Have you buried it in one hole with yourself and your once-good wits?”

“The Dominicans are preachers. They were founded for preaching. You are false to Dominic—and to His Master.”

“Remember, Gerard, when we walked young together—now old before our time—did you not see a sheep cast, three fields off? You left your sweetheart (by her good will) and ran to lift it for charity? Well—then, at Gouda is not one sheep in trouble—but a whole flock. Some cast, some strayed, some sick, some tainted, some devoured. All for want of a shepherd. Where is the shepherd? Lurking in a den like a wolf—in his own parish? Out fie! Out fie!”

“I scented you out, partly, by your kindness to the birds. Note this: you, Gerard Eliassoen, must love something. It is in your blood. Born to it. Shunning man, you only seek earthly affection—one peg lower.”

Gerard interrupted. “The birds are God’s creatures—His innocent ones. I do well to love them.”

“Are they creatures of the same God as we? As he who lies on your knee?”

“You know they are.”

“Then what excuse for shunning us and being kind to them? Since man is an animal, why pick him out to shun? Because he is the paragon? You court the young of birds—and abandon your own young? Birds need only food—and wings. They deserve no pity if they cannot fly. But this sweet dove on your knee needs not only bodily food—but spiritual. He is yours as well as mine. I have done my share. Soon he will be too much for me. I look to Gouda’s parson to teach him piety and lore. Is he not worth more than many sparrows?”

Gerard started, stammered an affirmation.

“You wonder,” she continued, “to hear me quote Scripture so fluently. I have studied it four years. Not because God wrote it—but because I saw it often in your hands before you left me. Heaven forgive me—I am but a woman. What do you think of this verse? ‘Let your light so shine before men that they may see your good works and glorify your Father which is in heaven.’ What is a saint in a sink better than a light under a bushel?”

Therefore—since the sheep entrusted to you bleat for you, cry, “Oh, desert us no longer—come to Gouda manse!”—since I, who know you ten times better than you know yourself, pledge my soul it is for your soul’s good to go to Gouda manse—since duty to your child, long abandoned, calls you there—since your sovereign, whom Scripture bids you honour, sends you there—since the Pope, whom the Church teaches you to revere, has absolved you of your vows and orders you to Gouda manse—

“Since your grey-haired mother watches for you in grief, turns the hourglass, sighs that you come so slowly to her at Gouda manse—

“Since your brother, broken by your curse, awaits your forgiveness and prayers for his soul, lingering in his body at Gouda manse—

Take in your arms the sweet bird with crest of gold who nestles to your bosom. Give me your hand—your sweetheart, your wife, your friend, the truest friend you ever had—and come with me to Gouda manse!”

“It is the voice of an angel!” cried Clement.

“Then heed it—and come forth to Gouda manse!”

The battle was won.

Margaret lingered, glanced swiftly around the room, selected the Vulgate and psaltery. The rest she sighed over and left. She picked up the breastplate and cilice of bristles, dashed them to the floor with feeble fury.

Seeing Gerard watch her in surprise, she blushed. “I am but a woman—‘little’ will still be ‘spiteful.’”

“Why carry those? They are safe.”

“Oh, she had a reason.”

And so they set out for Gouda parsonage. The moon and stars shone so brightly it seemed almost daylight.

Suddenly, Gerard stopped. “My poor little birds!”

“What of them?”

“They will miss their food. I feed them every day.”

“The child has a piece of bread in his cowl. Take it—feed them now, against the morn.”

“I will. Nay—I will not. He is as innocent, and nearer to me and to you.”

Margaret drew a long breath. “'Tis well. Had you taken it, I might have hated you. I am but a woman.”

After a quarter-mile, Gerard sighed. “Margaret—must I rest? He is too heavy.”

“Then give him to me. Take these.” She carried the child. “Alas, alas! I remember when you would have run with the child on one shoulder, and the mother on the other.”

“I trow,” said Gerard, looking down, “overmuch fasting is not good for a man.”

“A many die of it each year, winter time,” she replied.

Gerard pondered. Watched her carry the child with ease.

Near a mile, he said, surprised: “You said it was but two butts’ length.”

“Not I.”

“You said so.”

“That is another matter.”

She turned to him, face like a Madonna. “I lied,” she said sweetly. “And to save your soul and body, I’d tell a worse lie than that, if need be. I am but a woman. Ah, well—it is but two butts’ length from here, at any rate.”

“Without a lie?”

“Humph! Three, without a lie.”

And indeed, in moments they reached the manse. A candle burned in the vicar’s parlour.

“She is still awake,” whispered Margaret.

“Beautiful! Beautiful!” said Clement, stopping to gaze.

“What, in Heaven’s name?”

“That little candle—seen through the window at night. Look—does it not resemble some fair star of prodigious size? It delights the eye, warms the heart of those outside.”

“Come—let me show you something better,” said Margaret, leading him tiptoe to the window.

They looked in. Catherine knelt on the hassock, her “hours” open before her.

“Folk can pray out of a cave,” whispered Margaret.

“Ay—and hit heaven with their prayers. For it is for a sight of you she prays—and you are here.”

“Now, Gerard—be prepared. She is not the woman you knew. Her children’s troubles have broken the brisk, light-hearted soul. I see she has been weeping. She likely gave you up, being so late.”

“Let me reach her,” said Clement, trembling. “That door! I will wait here.”

When Gerard approached the door, Margaret, fearing sudden shock, tapped sharply at the window and called, “Mother!” in a loud, expressive voice.

Catherine clasped her hands, rose halfway—then the door burst open, and Clement threw himself wildly on his knees, arms outstretched.

She uttered a cry only a mother could—“Ah! My darling, my darling!”—and clung sobbing to his neck.

Truly, she saw neither hermit, priest, nor monk—but her child, lost, despaired of, and now in her arms.

After a while, Margaret entered, eyes wet, cheeks flushed, a holy calm settling over the troubled hearts.

They sat together—three—hand in hand, murmuring soft, loving words. The one in the middle drank in their affection, their humble wisdom. He was forced to eat a nourishing meal. At dawn, he was tucked into a snowy bed.

And by morning, he awoke—as from a hideous dream—no longer friar, no longer hermit, no longer Clement. But Gerard Eliassoen, parson of Gouda.
