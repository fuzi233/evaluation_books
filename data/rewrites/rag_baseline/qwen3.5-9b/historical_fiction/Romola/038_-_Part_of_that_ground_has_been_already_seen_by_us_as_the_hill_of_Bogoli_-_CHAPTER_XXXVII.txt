The tabernacle unlocked.

Romola was awakened by a sharp rap at the door. The room was already bathed in the cold, hard light of early morning. Maso had arrived for the traveling wallet. The old man started visibly when she opened the door, expecting the graceful silhouette he was accustomed to seeing, only to find instead the thick folds of a grey mantle, the crown of bright hair hidden beneath a dark cowl, and a pale face obscured by shadow.

"It is well, Maso," Romola said, forcing her voice into a calm register to reassure him. "The wallet is ready. Go on quietly; I shall not be far behind. Once you pass the gates, you may slow your pace, for I hope to join you before you reach Trespiano."

She closed the door behind him. Then, her hand went to the key resting in the casket—the original key of the little painted tabernacle. Tito had failed to drown it in the Arno; it had lodged, as such small things often do, in the corner of the embroidered *scarsella* he wore with his purple tunic. Long after their marriage, Romola had discovered it there and stored it away, unused, yet satisfied that it remained within reach.

The cabinet holding the tabernacle had been shifted to the side of the room, adjacent to a window where the pale morning light struck directly upon it, rendering the painted figures discernible to Romola, who knew them intimately. There was the triumphant Bacchus, adorned with grape clusters and clutching a vine-clad spear, embracing the crowned Ariadne; the Loves scattering roses, the wreathed vessel, the cunning-eyed dolphins, and the rippled sea, all enclosed by a flowery border resembling a paradise bower.

On this chill morning, however, Romola viewed these familiar images with renewed bitterness and repulsion. They appeared as a more pitiable mockery than ever, a lying screen rather than a tomb of sorrow. *Foolish Ariadne!* she thought, her mind sharp with disdain. With her gaze of love, as if that bright face, framed by hyacinthine curls like tendrils among the vines, held the deep secret of her life!

"Ariadne is wonderfully transformed," Romola murmured. "She would look strange among the vines and the roses now."

She picked up the mirror and examined her reflection once more. The sight was startling in the harsh morning light, causing her to lower it again with a sensation of shrinking nearly as acute as her earlier recoil from the joyful painting. The recognition of her own face, veiled by the cowl, triggered a fresh dread: the fear that she might eventually be drawn into fellowship with some wretched superstition, joining the company of howling fanatics and weeping nuns whom she had despised since childhood.

With hurried movements, she inserted the key into the tabernacle, opened it, and extracted the crucifix without looking at it. Trembling slightly, she threaded a cord through the small ring, hung the cross around her neck, and concealed it in the bosom of her mantle.

"For Dino's sake," she whispered to herself.

Still, the letters destined for Maso to carry back to Bologna required attention. They were brief. The first addressed Tito: *"Tito, my love for you is dead; and therefore, so far as I was yours, I too am dead. Do not attempt to enforce any laws to fetch me back; that would bring you no happiness. The Romola you married can never return. I need explain nothing further after the words I uttered to you during our last long conversation. If you believed them to be words of transient anger, you will now understand they marked an irreversible change."*

She continued: *"I think you will fulfill my wish regarding my bridal chest, sending it to my godfather, who gave it to me. It contains my wedding clothes and the portraits and other relics of my father and mother."* She folded the ring inside this letter and wrote Tito's name on the outside.

The second letter was for Bernardo del Nero: *"Dearest Godfather, if I could have been of any benefit to your life by remaining, I would not have departed to such a distance. But now I am gone. Do not ask the reason; and if you love my father, strive to prevent anyone from seeking me. I could not endure life in Florence. I cannot bear to tell anyone why. Help to cover my lot in silence. I have requested that my bridal chest be sent to you; when you open it, you will understand the cause. Please entrust all items belonging to my mother to my cousin Brigida, and ask her to forgive me for failing to offer her words of farewell."*

"Farewell, my second father. The finest possession I retain in life remains the memory of your goodness and my gratitude."

"Romola."

She placed the letters alongside the crucifix within the bosom of her mantle. Everything was done. She was prepared to depart.

The house was silent; she descended the stairs and stepped onto the quiet street almost as silently as a grey phantom. Her heart hammered violently, yet she derived a grim pleasure from the solidity of her tread on the broad flagstones—the swift movement felt like a chained resolution finally released. The urgent drive to execute her act and the terror of encountering obstacles pushed aside all sorrow. As she reached the Ponte Rubaconte, she sensed less the proximity of Santa Croce and more the widening yellow streak of morning light cutting through the grey. Unless she increased her speed, she risked meeting faces.

Her direct route led straight to the Borgo Pinti, then along the walls toward the Porta San Gallo, passing through the Piazza di Santa Croce. Yet she walked with steady, rapid purpose through the square, refusing to glance toward the church. The thought that any eyes might fix upon her with curiosity or recognition, prompting indifferent minds to speculate on her private sorrows, caused her to shrink physically as if from imagined torture. She felt degraded even by the act of her husband, from which she suffered helplessly. However, no signs indicated that eyes watched from windows to note this tall grey sister moving with a firm step and a proud, cowled posture.

Her path skirted the early traffic. Upon reaching the Porta San Gallo, she slipped through easily amidst a dispute over tolls for panniers of eggs and market goods entering the city.

Out!

Once past the houses of the Borgo, she was beyond Florence's outer fringe. The sky stretched broad above her; she had entered a new existence—one of loneliness and endurance, yet of absolute freedom. She possessed the strength to sever the bonds she had once accepted in blind faith. Whatever befell her, she would no longer feel the warm breath of soft, hated lips upon her cheek, nor the stifling pressure of an odious mind crushing her spirit. The bare wintry morning and the chilling air were welcome in their severity; the leafless trees and sombre hills were devoid of the gods of beauty and joy whose worship she had forever renounced.

Suddenly, the light burst forth with violent intensity, casting long shadows across the road. It seemed the sun intended to banish the greyness. Light is rarely felt more profoundly as a divine presence stirring the inarticulate sensibilities of our deepest lives than in these moments when it instantly awakens the shadows. A profound awe, inevitable in such a momentous act, became a conscious element in Romola's feelings as she found herself in the sudden presence of the impalpable golden glory and the long shadow of herself—a shadow that could not be escaped.

Hitherto, she had encountered only occasional peasants with mules, and the winding turns of the level road had prevented her from noticing that Maso was not far ahead. But as she passed Pietra and ascended the rising ground, she lifted the hanging roof of her cowl to look eagerly forward. Immediately, she lowered it again.

She had not seen Maso, but two monks approaching within a few yards. The edge of her cowl formed a penthouse over her brow, blocking anything above eye level; for the last few moments, she had gazed only at the brightness of the path and her own shadow, tall and shrouded like a dread spectre. Regret washed over her; she wished she had not looked up. Her disguise made encounters with monks particularly distasteful—they might demand pious passwords unknown to her. She proceeded with a careful appearance of unconsciousness until the black skirts of their mantles had passed her.

The encounter had made her heart beat unpleasantly. Romola harbored an underlying unease in her religious disguise, a shame at this studied concealment, heightened by a special effort to appear oblivious under actual scrutiny. Yet the black skirts would soon disappear as they descended the hill. Spotting a large flat stone against a cypress tree rising from a projecting green bank, she yielded to the impulse born of the slight shock and sat down to rest.

She turned her back on Florence, intending not to look at it until the monks were entirely out of sight. Raising the edge of her cowl once more as she settled, she discerned Maso and the mules at a distance where overtaking them was still feasible, assuming the old man would linger in expectation. For the present, she might pause. She was free and alone.
