The carriage halted, a sudden pause in the city's rhythm, attended by a powdered lackey and a bay horse that seemed to shimmer with the same artificial brilliance as the house they served. She swept the matting, trod the crimson stairs, performing her arduous function with the singular, hollow mandate simply *to be there*. Like Sirius rising over a silent, indifferent sea, she hid her heart behind a glittering facade, projecting a starlight that concealed the cold vacuum beneath.

Thus, the Grandcourts arrived at Grosvenor Square, timely enough to intercept a card for the musical gathering at Lady Mallinger's. Sir Hugo, driven by business imperatives, had anticipated the arrival of his ill-favored nephew, ensuring the household was primed for the intrusion. It was merely the third evening since their installation in this gilded cage, yet Gwendolen moved through the new ceilings and furniture with a distracted vagueness. Her mind was not on the decor, but on the impending confrontation with Deronda, and the anticipation of seeing Miss Lapidoth, a woman who had endured so much and possessed the terrifying capacity to submit to any duty. Gwendolen recalled nearly every syllable Deronda had spoken regarding Mirah, particularly a phrase she now repeated to herself with bitter acrimony. She harbored a vague, gnawing consciousness that her own submission bore no resemblance to duty. If anyone had articulated it then, she would have been forced to admit that what she submitted to could not possibly take the shape of obligation; it was a submission to a yoke forged by her own shameful actions, tightened by a selfish strength so absolute that it left no room for the weight of duty to rest upon it.

Before the Grandcourts entered, the drawing rooms of Park Lane, bathed in whites, golds, and pale crimsons, were agreeably furnished and sparsely populated. An hour of instrumental music had yielded to a languid interlude of chatter. Klesmer was present, accompanied by his wife, and in his generous patronage of Mirah, he had proposed accompanying her rendition of Leopardi's "O patria mia"—a piece he had previously recommended as uniquely suited to her spirit compared to the safer, more familiar repertoire. Already seated at the piano, Klesmer waited as Mirah stood conspicuously poised. Then, Gwendolen was ushered forward, magnificent in pale green velvet encrusted with poisoned diamonds, to a seat of honor commanding a clear view of the scene.

With her exceptional sight and iron self-command, Gwendolen possessed the rare ability to map a room instantly. As she turned her gaze toward Mirah, she did not neglect to exchange a bow with Klesmer, a gesture that sparked a lightning-flash of resentment within her. It recalled that morning when her ambition had been to stand where the "little Jewess" now stood—to survey a grand audience from the elevated platform of her own talent. Instead, she was merely part of the ordinary throng in silk and gems, whose highest achievement was to admire or find fault. *"He thinks I am in the right road now,"* whispered the lurking bitterness inside her.

In her transit, Gwendolen had failed to catch sight of Deronda. Seated now, engaging in polite chat with Sir Hugo, she scanned the room with careful ease, bowing to acquaintances, terrified lest an anxious, searching look for Deronda be interpreted by her husband as something "damnably vulgar." Yet, travel—even the slow, deliberate perusal of a room—carries the risk of unwanted encounters. Among the eyes meeting hers, forcing a slight nod, were those of Mr. Lush, the "amateur too fond of Meyerbeer," whom Sir Hugo deemed useful as a half-caste gentleman. Standing near her husband, Lush was ostensibly listening to Lord Pentreath, though Sir Hugo turned a shoulder toward him. Suddenly, a disagreeable sensation pierced Gwendolen: the realization that this man knew everything about her husband's life. Banished from her sight by her own will, Lush had sunk into the background of her thoughts, screened away by the agitating figures of her internal drama where he held no place. Now, he reappeared at her husband's elbow. Instantly, like a hallucination conjured in sleep, arose the sense that he was connected to the secrets that rendered her wretched. She felt the physical effort of turning her head away, feigning a wandering survey of the wall pictures as if nothing of consequence had occurred, until she finally located Deronda.

He was not looking toward her. She withdrew her eyes, offering no recognition, comforting herself with the delusion that he must have seen her entrance. In truth, he stood near the door with Hans Meyrick, whom he had carefully included in Lady Mallinger's guest list. Both men were slightly more anxious than comfortable, fearing Mirah might not be heard to advantage. Deronda felt himself on the brink of betrayal, his emotions stirred by Mirah's presence, which triggered a cascade of images—past and future—all converging on the brother he was soon to reveal to her. Having escaped the side of Lady Pentreath, who had played her violoncello voice to say, *"Well, your Jewess is pretty—there's no denying that. But where is her Jewish impudence? She looks as demure as a nun. I suppose she learned that on the stage,"* Deronda retreated inward.

He was beginning to feel, on Mirah's behalf, a kinship to the seraphic boyhood feelings he once held for himself, when Sir Hugo asked if he wished to become a great singer. This query provoked an indignant recoil; Deronda loathed the casual remarking upon her as if she were an imported commodity disdainfully purchased by the fashionable public. He winced, knowing that Mordecai would perceive the term "Jewess" as a brand mark, akin to the lettering on Chinese silk. In this vulnerable state, he witnessed the Grandcourts' entry and was immediately summoned by Hans regarding "that Vandyke duchess of a beauty." A transient renewal of his initial repulsion toward Gwendolen surged within him—as if her beauty and her failings were responsible for the undervaluation of Mirah as a woman—a sentiment bordering on class animosity, a reaction rarely escaped by those who cherish what remains unrecognized by others, whether in persons or poetry. To Hans, admiring Gwendolen with his customary hyperbole, Deronda replied with a sarcasm lacking warmth: *"I thought you could admire no style of woman but your Berenice."*

"That is the style I worship—not admire," Hans corrected. "Other styles of women I might make myself wicked for, but for Berenice I could make myself—well, pretty good, which is something much more difficult."

"Hush," Deronda murmured, feigning urgency as the singing was about to commence. He was not truly delighted by the answer, finding relief only in Hans's retreat to a more distant vantage point.

Deronda had never heard Mirah sing "O patria mia" before. He knew Leopardi's fine ode intimately—the image of Italy sitting like a disconsolate mother in chains, hiding her face on her knees and weeping—and the few selected words resonated for him with the grandeur of the whole, breathing an inspiration through the music. When Mirah sang, she made Mordecai a palpable presence beside her. Words excluded from the song nonetheless rang within Deronda as harmonies from the invisible: *"Non ti difende Nessun de' tuoi! L'armi, qua l'armi: io solo Combatterò, procomberò sol io"*—"Do none of thy children defend thee? Arms! bring me arms! alone I will fight, alone I will fall." These seemed the very voice of that heroic passion falsely claimed to be devoted in vain when it achieves the godlike end of manifesting unselfish love. That passion was present to Deronda now as the vivid image of a man dying helplessly, severed from the possibility of battle.

Mirah rose to his expectations. As the general applause swelled, Klesmer offered a private testament, audible only to her: *"Good, good—the crescendo better than before."* Yet her primary anxiety was to know she had satisfied Mr. Deronda; any failure tonight would pain her as a special injury to him. Naturally, all her prospects stemmed from his intervention, but this occasion of singing in the house that was his home imposed a peculiar demand. She looked toward him in the distance, and he saw her gaze; yet he remained stationary, watching the streams of emulous admirers close around her until they parted to make way for Gwendolen, who was escorted forward by Mrs. Klesmer.

Now easier regarding "the little Jewess," Daniel softened toward poor Gwendolen in her splendor. His memory returned, tinged with penitence for his momentary harshness, recalling the signs and confessions that she too required a rescue—one far more difficult than that of the wanderer by the river, a salvation for which he felt utterly powerless. The silent question, *"But is it not cowardly to make that a reason for turning away?"*, became the framework for his resolve to approach her on the first opportunity, honoring her past confidence despite Sir Hugo's unwelcome hints.

Having risen to greet Gwendolen as she approached, Klesmer lingered nearby, included in the opening conversation between the two young women seated on the red divan. His smile was more evident in his eyes than on his lips, observing the piquant contrast between the splendid one and the modest one. The solicitude appeared to flow entirely from the direction of the splendid one.
