The words spoken by Tatho did not induce sleep. Instead, they acted as a diagnostic tool, revealing the fracture between my governance of Yucatan and the currents flowing beneath the surface of Atlantis. For years, the maritime folk had maintained a disciplined silence, allowing only the filtered news the Empress deemed acceptable to leak across the borders. My immersion in colonial administration had rendered me blind to this stagnation, a blindness compounded by a principle I refused to abandon: the refusal to torture men bound by superior orders simply to satisfy my private curiosity. Yet, the iron discipline of the Priestly Clan offered no alternative path.

My removal from office was abrupt and total. Authority, documents, and command transferred instantly to my successor. While courtesy permitted me to witness one final sunrise from the pyramid that had recently housed my rank, the mandate was explicit: I was to vacate the territory immediately and hasten to Atlantis.

Before dawn, Tatho reappeared, ostensibly eager to facilitate my transition. He placed his resources at my disposal, though I possessed little to demand. "A ship to take me home," I stated. "I shall be your debtor."

His reaction was one of mild surprise. "You may certainly have it, if you wish. However, my vessels are fouled by the long passage and require a careen. To take them would ensure a slow voyage. Why not utilize your own navy? The ships are currently in harbor; I observed them upon our arrival. They are brave vessels."

"But not mine," I replied. "That navy belongs to Yucatan."

"Deucalion," Tatho countered, shrugging, "you are Yucatan—or rather, you were yesterday, and have been for twenty years."

The implication struck me with cold clarity. I responded stiffly that the fleet belonged to private merchants or the State, and I could not claim even a single ten-slave galley.

Tatho sighed. "You understand your policies best, though it appears risky for a man of our stature not to maintain a personal navy. One never knows when a recall arrives; without such precautions, a lifetime's earnings can vanish in a dozen hours."

"I have no fear for mine," I said, my tone devoid of warmth.

"Of course not, because you know me to be your friend. Had another man been appointed to this vice-royalty, you might have been severely stripped. Few can resist a snug hoard waiting in the very coffers they have filled."

"My Lord Tatho," I said, "we have diverged in taste. The wealth I accumulated here, few would covet. I wear the clothes you see—a simple robe—and possess a box of medicinal drugs. I hold three slaves: two scribes and one European savage who prepares my food and tends my bath. Throughout my service, I have extracted only a soldier's ration from the State. If any man has extorted a single ounce of bronze from a subject in my name, I request you hang him as a liar and a thief."

Tatho studied me with a mixture of admiration and pity. "Money is power. With it, nothing stops a man like you; without it, you are vulnerable to the slightest reversal."

"The Gods will choose my fate."

"Possibly. But I prefer to nourish my own destiny. I have not come to replicate your model of austerity. I shall govern Yucatan wisely, but I shall do so for the benefit of Tatho, the Viceroy. I brought eight ships and a personal guard. I brought my wife, her women, and her slaves. They must be provided for. Is it not the privilege of a governed people to pay handsomely for their prince?"

"We shall not agree," I said. "If I thought it useful, I would beg you to exercise lenience in taxing these people. They have grown dear to me."

"You have disgusted me, and I am grieved. Even to retain your good opinion—which I value above all—I cannot act as you did. It is impossible, even if I wished it. Do not judge all men by your standard: a Tatho is not a colossus like a Deucalion. Furthermore, I have a wife and children who must be sustained."

"Ah," I noted, "there lies my advantage. I have no wife to encumber me."

He seized the point eagerly. "You possess nothing that makes life worth living. No wife, children, riches, cooks, retinue, or finery. You are ignorant of essential matters. You do not know how to dine. On weary voyages, I promised myself that your future feasts would repay the abstinence of the sea. What awaited me instead? A country farmer eating delicacies daily. Your 'European savage' is lusty, perhaps, but a devil-possessed cook. I have lived better on campaign. This is a colony lacking refinement, but if the local resources are not utilized better, I shall see it my duty to fry the kitchen staff in grease to enforce competence. Have you forgotten your palate? Look at your clothes. You are garbed like a herdsman, devoid of ornament."

"I eat when hunger bids me," I said coldly, "and I wear this robe until it falls apart. The grossness of excessive banqueting and the effeminacy of many garments never appealed to me. We have discussed this long enough. Differences have crept into us imperceptibly with time. Let us part, remembering only our friendship of twenty years ago. That memory retains a pleasant flavor for me."

Tatho bowed. "So be it."

"And I charge myself upon your bounty for that ship. Dawn approaches; it is indecent for a dismissed ruler to walk the streets in daylight."

"So be it," Tatho agreed. "You shall have my navy. I wished you had asked for something greater."

"Not the navy, Tatho; one small ship. More is wasted."

"Now," Tatho declared, acting the tyrant, "I am Viceroy here. You may depart naked of possessions—that I cannot prevent—but you shall not depart unattended."

Thus, my transit occurred aboard the "Bear," Tatho's private vessel, escorted by the remainder of his fleet. Departure was not immediate. The ships lay moored against the stone quays, gutted of stores, their crews exhausted. To force them out instantly would have been suicide.

Instead, the courtesies were performed elsewhere. The craft carrying me hauled out into the entrance basin, anchoring in the swells of the fairway. She and her consorts then took on wood, water, cured meats, and fish, undergoing necessary repairs with remarkable speed.

For the first time in twenty years, I experienced a breathing space from labor. Officially, I had already left. Rules prohibited my inspection of the ship's workings, as sea matters were the exclusive domain of the Mariners' Guild, protected by royal patent.

My days became a series of observations. I watched the quays, the harbors, the palaces, and the pyramids of the splendid city I had helped build stone by stone. I roamed my eye over the pastures and grain lands beyond the walls, looking longingly at the dense forests behind, from which we had so tediously carved our territory.

Would Tatho continue the healthy work begun? I trusted so, despite his selfish rhetoric. Under the sun or the stars, I pursued the higher mysteries of the Priestly Clan, relying solely on mental training without books or temples.

The refitting proceeded with unprecedented velocity. Within a month of arriving, the fleet sailed eastward over the hills and dales of the ocean.

Modern navigation differed significantly from the past. Rowing slaves were no longer used for long passages due to logistical difficulties and the prohibition of forcing them to eat one another. Sails were secondary. Science had revealed how to extract force from the sun; when clouds parted, machinery drew seawater from the bow and ejected it forcefully from the stern, propelling the vessel even against adverse winds. Direction no longer relied solely on the stars. A balanced image with an outstretched arm pointed constantly toward the Southern Cross, allowing for correct steering angles. New instruments determined true position, reflecting a shift where the modern mariner trusted his own strength and wit more than the Gods.

Yet, the rituals persisted. The crew took their final farewell with theatrical fervor. They washed, donned their finest attire, and visited obscure temples to offer sacrifices with clamor and lavish expenditure. Returning to the ship, they were drunk, heavy, and ill-humored from gluttony.

This voyage differed profoundly from my previous sea-goings. We did not creep timidly along the coastlines. We stood straight across the open gulf toward home, navigating confidently through the Carib Islands as if they were signposts. We stopped only twice to replenish supplies, receiving wood, water, and fruit freely from the subdued savages. In neither instance was there even the semblance of conflict. It was a testament to the growing power of Atlantis and her colonies.

We crossed the vast ocean beyond without sacrificing to implore divine assistance. One might censure these rugged mariners for their impiety, yet one could not deny their lusty skill and confidence.

The perils of the desolate sea were distributed as the Gods will. We encountered storms, fought with stubborn endurance, and twice witnessed blazing stones hiss into the sea beside us without injury. Great sea beasts hunted us with their accustomed savagery. Only once did we suffer material loss: three large sea lizards attacked the "Bear" simultaneously.
