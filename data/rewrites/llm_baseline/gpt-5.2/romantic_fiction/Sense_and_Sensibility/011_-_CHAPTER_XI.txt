When the Dashwoods first crossed into Devonshire, Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters had imagined a quieter sort of life—hours long enough for needlework, reading, reflection; mornings not immediately portioned out by other people’s wishes. Yet the country, with its hedged lanes and talkative neighbours, took hold of them almost at once. Engagements sprang up like autumn mushrooms after rain. Invitations arrived with baskets of apples, with jars of preserved pears, with notes smudged by haste and sociable certainty. Visitors came so often that the very threshold seemed to remember footsteps.

As soon as Marianne’s health was restored, Sir John’s long-hoarded plans for their diversion poured out in a cheerful flood. The private balls at the Park commenced; and, as often as a showery October allowed, parties on the water were attempted and, with much laughter at the weather’s caprice, accomplished. The season’s damp had its own domestic music—the soft percussion of rain on leaves, the low swish of skirts brushed by wet grass, the creak of oars, the small, pleased roar of distant rivers swollen and busy. In that soundscape there was, to Elinor’s mind, something healing, a kind of honest accompaniment to life; it was the harsher noises of fashion—forced gaiety, the clatter of cards, the scrape of chairs in crowded rooms—that could turn the head and weary the heart.

In every meeting of the kind, Mr. Willoughby was included. He belonged to these parties as naturally as cider belonged to an October supper; and the ease and familiarity which attended them were exactly calculated to increase the intimacy of his acquaintance with the Dashwoods—to give him continual opportunity of witnessing Marianne’s excellencies, of marking his animated admiration of her, and of receiving, in her behaviour to himself, the most pointed assurance of her affection.

Elinor could not be surprised at their attachment. It had the inevitability of leaven at work: once introduced, it rose, warmed, and claimed the whole loaf. She only wished it were less openly shown; and once or twice, as gently as one might suggest lowering the fire rather than putting it out, she ventured to hint at the propriety of some self-command to Marianne.

But Marianne abhorred all concealment where no real disgrace could attend unreserve. To aim at the restraint of sentiments which were not in themselves illaudable appeared to her not merely an unnecessary effort, but a disgraceful subjection of reason to common-place and mistaken notions. Mr. Willoughby thought the same; and their behaviour at all times was an illustration of their opinions.

When he was present she had no eyes for any one else. Every thing he did was right; every thing he said was clever. If their evenings at the Park were concluded with cards, he cheated himself and all the rest of the party to get her a good hand, with a frank gaiety that made his injustice appear almost a sort of gallantry. If dancing formed the amusement of the night, they were partners for half the time; and when obliged to separate for a couple of dances, they were careful to stand together, and scarcely spoke a word to any body else. Even the room seemed to narrow around them, as if their attention drew the air into a smaller circle.

Such conduct made them, of course, most exceedingly laughed at; but ridicule could not shame them, and seemed hardly to provoke them. Mrs. Dashwood entered into all their feelings with a warmth which left her no inclination for checking this excessive display. To her it was but the natural consequence of a strong affection in a young and ardent mind; a bright blaze on the hearth, not to be scolded into embers for fear the neighbours should see the light through the curtains.

This was the season of happiness to Marianne. Her heart was devoted to Willoughby; and even her fond attachment to Norland, which she had brought with her from Sussex like a cherished family recipe—tenderly remembered, endlessly recited—was more likely to be softened than she had thought possible before, by the charms which his society bestowed on her present home.

Elinor’s happiness was not so great. Her heart was not so much at ease, nor her satisfaction in their amusements so pure. The engagements, for all their bustle, brought her no companion who could make amends for what she had left behind, nor any one who could teach her to think of Norland with less regret than ever. She found herself measuring voices the way one measures ingredients: the right tone, the right warmth, the right pauses that make conversation nourishing rather than merely filling. Here, most talk either came too loudly or with too little flavour.

Neither Lady Middleton nor Mrs. Jennings could supply the conversation she missed; although the latter was an everlasting talker, and from the first had regarded Elinor with a kindness which ensured her a large share of discourse. Mrs. Jennings told her history repeatedly, as a cook repeats instructions even to a careful listener—again the same details, again the same emphases, the same turns of phrase, as if repetition might preserve what time threatened to spoil. She had already related it to Elinor three or four times; and had Elinor’s memory been equal to Mrs. Jennings’s means of improvement, she might very early in their acquaintance have known all the particulars of Mr. Jennings’s last illness, and what he said to his wife a few minutes before he died.

Lady Middleton was more agreeable than her mother only in being more silent. Elinor needed little observation to perceive that her reserve was a mere calmness of manner with which sense had nothing to do. Towards her husband and mother she was the same as to others; intimacy was therefore neither to be looked for nor desired. She had nothing to say one day that she had not said the day before. Her insipidity was invariable; even her spirits never altered. Though she did not oppose the parties arranged by her husband—provided every thing were conducted in style and her two eldest children attended her—she never appeared to receive more enjoyment from them than she might have experienced in sitting at home. So little did her presence add to the pleasure of others by any share in their conversation, that they were sometimes only reminded of her being amongst them by her solicitude about her troublesome boys: sharp little cries and stamping feet that cut into the room’s softer sounds like ill-timed clanging in a church bell’s echo.

In Colonel Brandon alone, of all her new acquaintance, did Elinor find a person who could in any degree claim the respect of abilities, excite the interest of friendship, or give pleasure as a companion. Mr. Willoughby was out of the question. Marianne’s admiration and regard—even Elinor’s sisterly regard—was all his own; but he was a lover, and his attentions were wholly Marianne’s. A far less agreeable man might have been more generally pleasing, had he spared others the constant sense of being uninvited guests at an entertainment arranged for two.

Colonel Brandon, unfortunately for himself, had no such encouragement to think only of Marianne; and in conversing with Elinor he found the greatest consolation for the indifference of her sister. Elinor’s compassion for him increased, as she had reason to suspect that the misery of disappointed love had already been known to him.

This suspicion was given by some words which accidentally dropped from him one evening at the Park. The company were assembled in one of the larger rooms; the dancers moved in measured figures while the musicians—well-meaning, a little too eager—filled every corner with sound. Between the repeated strains, Elinor sometimes heard the quieter world outside: wind shifting in the trees, rain worrying at the panes, the brief hush before a new tune began. She and Colonel Brandon had seated themselves together by mutual consent, while the others were dancing.

His eyes, fixed on Marianne, followed her as she laughed and turned, her colour bright, her hair loosened slightly in the warmth of exertion. After a silence of some minutes—one of those silences that feels not empty, but laden, like a covered dish brought to table and not yet unveiled—he said, with a faint smile, “Your sister, I understand, does not approve of second attachments.”

“No,” replied Elinor, “her opinions are all romantic.”

“Or rather, as I believe, she considers them impossible to exist.”

“I believe she does. But how she contrives it without reflecting on the character of her own father, who had himself two wives, I know not. A few years, however, will settle her opinions on the reasonable basis of common sense and observation; and then they may be more easy to define and to justify than they now are, by any body but herself.”

“This will probably be the case,” he replied; “and yet there is something so amiable in the prejudices of a young mind, that one is sorry to see them give way to the reception of more general opinions.”

“I cannot agree with you there,” said Elinor. “There are inconveniences attending such feelings as Marianne’s, which all the charms of enthusiasm and ignorance of the world cannot atone for. Her systems have all the unfortunate tendency of setting propriety at nought; and a better acquaintance with the world is what I look forward to as her greatest possible advantage.”

After a short pause he resumed, as if trying to lay his thoughts more evenly, like a hand smoothing dough that will not quite come together. “Does your sister make no distinction in her objections against a second attachment? or is it equally criminal in every body? Are those who have been disappointed in their first choice—whether from the inconstancy of its object, or the perverseness of circumstances—to be equally indifferent during the rest of their lives?”

“Upon my word, I am not acquainted with the minutiae of her principles. I only know that I never yet heard her admit any instance of a second attachment’s being pardonable.”

“This,” said he, “cannot hold; but a change, a total change of sentiments—No, no, do not desire it; for when the romantic refinements of a young mind are obliged to give way, how frequently are they succeeded by such opinions as are but too common, and too dangerous! I speak from experience. I once knew a lady who in temper and mind greatly resembled your sister, who thought and judged like her, but who from an enforced change—from a series of unfortunate circumstances—”

Here he stopped suddenly. He appeared to think he had said too much; and by his countenance gave rise to conjectures which might not otherwise have entered Elinor’s head. The lady would probably have passed without suspicion, had he not convinced Miss Dashwood that what concerned her ought not to escape his lips. As it was, it required but a slight effort of fancy to connect his emotion with the tender recollection of past regard.

Elinor attempted no more. She would not press him, not with the music still sawing its way through the room, not with Marianne’s laughter rising and falling on the air like a refrain, not with Colonel Brandon’s voice—so usually composed—betraying, in those broken clauses, the existence of a story whose true language perhaps could never be properly translated into another’s understanding.

But Marianne, in her place, would not have done so little. The whole tale would have been speedily formed under her active imagination; every circumstance arranged, every sorrow seasoned to the richest poignancy; and every thing established in the most melancholy order of disastrous love.
