When Mr. Sapsea had exhausted, for the day, the pleasure of admiring Mr. Sapsea, he took to the Close as to a parlour that belonged to him by right of self-importance. The air was damp enough to lay a cold hand on the railings of the churchyard; the leaves along the path held that wet, herbal smell that rises when earth has been turned and then remembered by rain. He went swelling past the graves with the proprietary gait of a landlord inspecting tenancies, pausing—if chance offered—to imagine some stranger reading his inscription and retreating, appropriately abashed, in obedience to the stone.

His consequence had lately been spiced and puffed up by office: he was Mayor now. Cloisterham, he considered, was a kind of great household that could not possibly keep its accounts, its dignity, its very joints and bones, without a Mayor to bind it together like good gravy. A Mayor might even “go up” with an address; and if English Grammar suffered a few pellets of shot and shell in the discharge, why, Grammar must bear what it could for the commonweal. Rise, Sir Thomas Sapsea—so ran his private, nutritious fancy.

He had also improved his acquaintance with Mr. Jasper, the Cathedral’s Choir-Master. Mr. Sapsea enjoyed the gatehouse for its solid comfort—port, beef, salad, backgammon, and an epitaph or two like pepper thrown over all—and he enjoyed Jasper’s deference more. Jasper would sit down at the piano after dinner and sing until the very air seemed stroked and persuaded; not with the modern, foreign frippery that Mr. Sapsea suspected of treason, but with stout old songs that marched in time like drilled men, and made the room ring as if it had been built expressly to echo national virtue. Jasper’s voice, in those moments, was a kind of domestic hearth: it warmed; it soothed; it made Mr. Sapsea feel that Providence had taken pains with English throats.

This moist evening, as the lamplighter began to seed the Close with small, steady flames—climbing and dropping his little ladder at a practised hurry, that ladder which had been part of the town’s childhood and would have been defended like a family relic—Mr. Sapsea rounded a corner by the churchyard expecting perhaps his usual phantom stranger. Instead he encountered the Dean, talking with the Verger, Mr. Tope, and with Jasper.

Mr. Sapsea bowed at once into a pitch of ecclesiastical humility that would have done credit to an Archbishop, and the Dean—whose humour was as old as the stones but not as heavy—was saying to Jasper:

“You are evidently going to write a book about us, Mr. Jasper; to write a book about us. We are very ancient, and we ought to make a good book. We are not so richly endowed in possessions as in age; but perhaps you will put that in your book, among other things, and call attention to our wrongs.”

Mr. Tope, dutifully entertained, showed his teeth in the proper reverent measure.

“I really have no intention at all, sir,” Jasper answered, “of turning author or archaeologist. It is but a whim of mine. And even for my whim, Mr. Sapsea here is more accountable than I am.”

“How so, Mr. Mayor?” asked the Dean, with a nod that acknowledged Cloisterham’s Fetch. “How is that, Mr. Mayor?”

“I am not aware,” Mr. Sapsea began, looking about him as if the answer might be hanging somewhere on the weather, “to what the Very Reverend the Dean does me the honour of referring.” And then, as if to recover himself by studying authority, he fell to examining the Dean’s person with minute attention.

“Durdles,” hinted Mr. Tope.

“Ay! Durdles,” echoed the Dean, pleased to be reminded.

“The truth is, sir,” Jasper explained, “that my curiosity in the man was first really stimulated by Mr. Sapsea. Mr. Sapsea’s knowledge of mankind and power of drawing out whatever is recluse or odd around him, first led to my bestowing a second thought upon the man—though of course I had met him constantly about. You would not be surprised by this, Mr. Dean, if you had seen Mr. Sapsea deal with him in his own parlour, as I did.”

Mr. Sapsea accepted the compliment as a man accepts a dish he thinks has been prepared in his honour: slowly, with relish, and with a look around to see who notices his appetite.

“O! yes, yes. The Very Reverend the Dean refers to that? Yes. I happened to bring Durdles and Mr. Jasper together. I regard Durdles as a Character.”

“A character,” said Jasper, “that with a few skilful touches you turn inside out.”

“Nay, not quite that,” returned Mr. Sapsea, lumbering into modesty as into a too-tight coat. “I may have a little influence over him, perhaps; and a little insight into his character, perhaps. The Very Reverend the Dean will please to bear in mind that I have seen the world.” He edged a little behind the Dean, as if to inspect the Dean’s coat-buttons and so, by proxy, inspect history itself.

“Well!” said the Dean, glancing round for his verger as though he had mislaid him in his own joke, “I hope, Mr. Mayor, you will use your study and knowledge of Durdles to the good purpose of exhorting him not to break our worthy and respected Choir-Master’s neck; we cannot afford it; his head and voice are much too valuable to us.”

Mr. Tope, in respectful convulsion, murmured that surely any gentleman would deem it a pleasure and an honour to have his neck broken in return for such a compliment from such a source.

“I will take it upon myself, sir,” Mr. Sapsea announced, “to answer for Mr. Jasper’s neck. I will tell Durdles to be careful of it. He will mind what I say. How is it at present endangered?” He cast the question abroad with a magnificent patronage, as if necks in general were an item he supervised.

“Only by my making a moonlight expedition with Durdles among the tombs, vaults, towers, and ruins,” Jasper returned. “You remember suggesting, when you brought us together, that, as a lover of the picturesque, it might be worth my while?”

“I remember!” said Mr. Sapsea. And, in that moment, he truly believed he did.

“Profiting by your hint,” Jasper pursued, “I have had some day-rambles with the extraordinary old fellow, and we are to make a moonlight hole-and-corner exploration to-night.”

“And here he is,” said the Dean.

Durdles came slouching up with his dinner-bundle in hand, as if he carried not food but some private lump of the day. The smell of him was of grit and lime and old stone that has been cut and wet and left to dry again; the sort of smell that fills the throat in a mason’s shed and makes you aware of your teeth. Seeing the Dean, he took off his hat and was slouching away with it under his arm when Mr. Sapsea stopped him.

“Mind you take care of my friend,” Mr. Sapsea commanded.

“What friend o’ yourn is dead?” Durdles asked. “No orders has come in for any friend o’ yourn.”

“I mean my live friend there.”

“O! him?” Durdles said. “He can take care of himself, can Mister Jarsper.”

“But do you take care of him too,” said Sapsea.

Durdles, feeling the command in the tone rather than any logic in the words, gave the Mayor a sour, deliberate survey from head to foot.

“With submission to his Reverence the Dean, if you’ll mind what concerns you, Mr. Sapsea, Durdles he’ll mind what concerns him.”

“You’re out of temper,” said Mr. Sapsea, winking to the company to observe how suavely he would manage him. “My friend concerns me, and Mr. Jasper is my friend. And you are my friend.”

“Don’t you get into a bad habit of boasting,” Durdles retorted, with a grave nod as if delivering a mason’s measurement. “It’ll grow upon you.”

“You are out of temper,” Mr. Sapsea repeated, reddening, yet still winking as though the redness were part of his method.

“I own to it,” Durdles returned. “I don’t like liberties.”

Mr. Sapsea winked a third time, as who should say, I think you will agree I have settled his business, and stalked out of the controversy.

Durdles gave the Dean good evening and added, setting on his hat again, “You’ll find me at home, Mister Jarsper, as agreed, when you want me; I’m a-going home to clean myself,” and slouched away.

This “cleaning himself” was one of his bargains with propriety. He never appeared cleaner—his hat, his boots, his coat always wearing the same perpetual crust of dust, as if Cloisterham’s stones had claimed him for kin—but the phrase served him like a ritual phrase serves a churchman: it marked the hour and excused the act.

The Dean withdrew to dinner; Mr. Tope to tea; Jasper to his piano. There, with no light but the fire’s, he sat chanting low choir-music, voice flowing like dark wine from a careful bottle, for two or three hours; until the town had grown still, the last doors had muttered on their hinges, and the moon was about to rise.

Then he closed the piano softly. He changed his coat for a pea-jacket with a substantial wicker-cased bottle in its largest pocket, set on a low-crowned, flap-brimmed hat, and went out as quietly as if sound itself were a witness he did not wish to summon. No outward reason required such stealth; yet he moved as though some inward thing crouched and listened for his own footfall.

At Durdles’s unfinished house—more a hole in the city wall than a dwelling—he saw light. He picked his way among gravestones and broken stone-lumber in the yard, where moonlight already touched edges sidewise, making names look newly cut and newly accusatory. Two great saws had been left sticking in their blocks; in the shadow of the sentry-boxes the sawyers’ tools might have been the grin and teeth of two skeleton journeymen from the Dance of Death, ready to set to work on the next pair destined for Cloisterham’s ledger. Likely enough, the men who owned the saws were at supper and thought no more of mortality than of tomorrow’s wage.

“Ho! Durdles!”

The light moved; Durdles appeared at the door with it. His notion of “cleaning” had plainly involved bottle, jug, and tumbler rather than brush or water; no other instruments were visible in the bare brick room with rafters overhead and no plastered ceiling. A sour spirit-smell—spirits and stale smoke and the faint bitter tang of lime dust—lay in the place like a second atmosphere.

“Are you ready?”

“I am ready, Mister Jarsper. Let the old ’uns come out if they dare, when we go among their tombs. My spirit is ready for ’em.”

“Do you mean animal spirits, or ardent?”

“The one’s the t’other,” Durdles answered. “And I mean ’em both.”

He took down a lantern from a hook, pocketed a match or two in case he should need to light it, and went out with Jasper, dinner-bundle and all.

It was an unaccountable expedition. Durdles prowled among graves and ruins as naturally as other men walked to market; the stones were his livelihood and his habit, and the dead were, to him, a set of tenants whose leases he understood. But that Jasper—cathedral musician, domestic guest, man of cultivated ear—should choose to pace the same places for “moonlight effects” in such company, and at such an hour, was another matter. The town’s usual nocturnal soundscape—the measured far-off murmur of the High Street, a cart-wheel complaining, a late voice, a dog’s halfhearted bark—thinned here into hush. Even the lamplighter’s ladder had ceased its clink. The Close had the acoustic quality of old cloth laid over furniture: it muffled.

“’Ware that there mound by the yard-gate, Mister Jarsper,” said Durdles.

“I see it. What is it?”

“Lime.”

Mr. Jasper stopped and waited for him to come up; Durdles lagged, as if the very word had slowed him.

“What you call quick-lime?”

“Ay!” Durdles said. “Quick enough to eat your boots. With a little handy stirring, quick enough to eat your bones.”

They went on, passing the red windows of the Travellers’ Twopenny, where some late drinkers’ laughter leaked out thinly and then was shut back in by glass. Emerging into clearer moonlight, they crossed the Monks’ Vineyard and came to Minor Canon Corner, much of which lay in shadow until the moon should climb higher.

A closing house-door struck their ears: that clean, domestic clap that sounds, in quiet precincts, like a verdict. Two men came out—Mr. Crisparkle and Neville. Jasper’s face, in the dimness, took on a strange, sudden smile. He laid the palm of his hand upon Durdles’s breast and stopped him where he stood.

At that end of the Corner the shadow was profound; and there was a low dwarf wall—breast-high—the last boundary of a garden that had been, and was now a thoroughfare. Jasper and Durdles would have turned it in another moment; but, halted so abruptly, stood behind it.

“Those two are only sauntering,” Jasper whispered. “They will go out into the moonlight soon. Let us keep quiet here, or they will detain us, or want to join us, or what not.”

Durdles nodded and fell to munching fragments from his bundle. There was something pathetic in the homeliness of it: meat and bread and perhaps a bit of cheese carried into the precinct of the dead, as if all hours must be seasoned by eating. In that crunching, one heard life insist on itself. Jasper folded his arms upon the top of the wall and rested his chin on them. He did not watch the Minor Canon at all. He watched Neville—watched him as though his eye lay on the trigger of a loaded rifle and he had him covered and was waiting for the wind. Something in Jasper’s face expressed destructive power so plainly that Durdles paused with an unmunched piece lodged in his cheek, staring.

Meanwhile Mr. Crisparkle and Neville walked to and fro, quietly talking. Their words did not carry in full, only broken by echoes and swallowed by the Corner’s hush, but Jasper caught his own name more than once, like a note struck and repeated.

“This is the first day of the week,” Mr. Crisparkle could be distinctly heard to observe as they turned back; “and the last day of the week is Christmas Eve.”

“You may be certain of me, sir,” Neville replied.

The echoes favoured those phrases and then betrayed them. Jasper caught “confidence,” shattered and pieced together by the stone. As they drew nearer, a fragment came clear: “Not deserved yet, but shall be, sir.” When they turned away again, Jasper heard his own name joined with Mr. Crisparkle’s words: “Remember that I said I answered for you confidently.” The rest became confused; Neville made some earnest action; and then Mr. Crisparkle looked up at the sky and pointed before him. They passed out into the moonlight at the far end and slowly disappeared.

Jasper did not move until they were gone. Then, abruptly, he turned to Durdles and burst into laughter—laughter that did not belong to the Corner’s silence and yet seemed bred by it. Durdles, with that suspended morsel still in his cheek, saw nothing to laugh at and stared. Jasper laid his face down on his arms to have the laugh out. Durdles, as if resigning himself to indigestion and to mysteries beyond masonry, bolted the morsel.

After dark there was almost no stir in these nooks. There was little enough even at noon; but at night the Close seemed to hold its breath. The High Street ran near, and its traffic was the town’s natural channel; yet between it and these precincts the Cathedral rose like a barrier that stopped not only feet but sound. Ask a hundred Cloisterham citizens at midday whether they believed in ghosts, and ninety-nine would say no; ask them at night whether they would take this shorter route or the longer round by shop-lamps and familiar voices, and ninety-nine would choose the longer. It was not local superstition, though a mysterious lady with a child in her arms and a rope at her neck had been seen flitting by witnesses as intangible as herself. It was something more bodily than belief: dust with breath in it shrinking from dust where breath had passed away; and, too, that half-admitted thought that if the dead ever did show themselves, these would be the likeliest surroundings. Even the ear seemed to share the caution. In the Close, every footstep sounded too loud, as if it might wake something.

When Jasper and Durdles paused before a small side door by which the latter had a key, and glanced round, the moonlit expanse was utterly deserted. It was as if the tide of life were stemmed by Jasper’s own gatehouse. One could hear the murmur of it beyond, but no wave passed the archway; Jasper’s lamp burned red behind its curtain like a lighthouse warning ships off.

They entered, locked themselves in, went down rugged steps, and were in the Crypt. The lantern was not needed: moonlight struck through groined windows bare of glass; the broken frames cast patterned bars on the ground like pale latticework. Heavy pillars supported the roof and bred black masses of shade, but between them ran lanes of light. Up and down these lanes they walked, Durdles discoursing of “the old ’uns” he yet counted on disinterring, slapping a wall in which he believed “a whole family on ’em” were stoned and earthed up, just as if he were on familiar visiting terms with the household. His usual taciturnity had been loosened by Jasper’s wicker bottle, which circulated freely—meaning its contents entered freely into Durdles’s circulation. Jasper himself only rinsed his mouth once and cast forth the rinsing, as if he would taste without swallowing, taking the scent and promise rather than the warmth.

They were to ascend the great Tower. On the steps that rose towards the Cathedral level, Durdles paused to gather breath. The stairs were very dark, but out of the darkness they could see those lanes of moonlight below, like strips of pale cloth laid in order. Durdles sat on a step. Jasper sat on another. The smell of spirit soon thickened; the bottle—somehow now in Durdles’s keeping—had plainly been opened. They could not see each other; yet in speaking they turned their faces as though eyes and mouths might meet in the dark.

“This is good stuff, Mister Jarsper!”

“It is very good stuff, I hope.—I bought it on purpose.”

“They don’t show, you see, the old ’uns don’t, Mister Jarsper!”

“It would be a more confused world than it is, if they could.”

“Well, it would lead towards a mixing of things,” Durdles conceded, as if the chief objection to spectres was that they muddled housekeeping. He pondered, then asked with a mason’s stubborn curiosity, “But do you think there may be Ghosts of other things, though not of men and women?”

“What things? Flower-beds and watering-pots? Horses and harness?”

“No. Sounds.”

“What sounds?”

“Cries.”

“What cries do you mean? Chairs to mend?”

“No. I mean screeches. Now I’ll tell you, Mr. Jarsper. Wait a bit till I put the bottle right.” The cork was evidently fussed with—out and in again. “There! Now it’s right! This time last year, only a few days later, I happened to have been doing what was correct by the season, in the way of giving it the welcome it had a right to expect, when them town-boys set on me at their worst. At length I gave ’em the slip, and turned in here. And here I fell asleep. And what woke me? The ghost of a cry. The ghost of one terrific shriek, which shriek was followed by the ghost of the howl of a dog: a long, dismal, woeful howl, such as a dog gives when a person’s dead. That was my last Christmas Eve.”

“What do you mean?” Jasper retorted, abrupt—almost fierce, as if a wrong note had been struck in his hearing.

“I mean that I made inquiries everywhere about, and that no living ears but mine heard either that cry or that howl. So I say they was both ghosts; though why they came to me, I’ve never made out.”

“I thought you were another kind of man,” Jasper said, with scorn that seemed aimed not only at Durdles but at the idea that sound might outlast sense.

“So I thought myself,” Durdles returned, composed. “And yet I was picked out for it.”

Jasper had risen suddenly when he demanded what Durdles meant; now he said, “Come; we shall freeze here; lead the way.”

Durdles got up, not very steadily, used his key again, and they emerged on the Cathedral level in a passage beside the chancel. Here the moonlight was so bright that the nearest stained-glass window threw its colours upon their faces—purple and yellow splashes like bruises and jaundice. Durdles, holding the door open for Jasper to follow as if he were letting him out of a grave, looked ghastly with the colour laid across him; yet he bore Jasper’s close scrutiny without consciousness. Jasper’s hands were busy at his pockets, seeking a key confided to him that would open an iron gate and admit them to the staircase of the great tower.

“That and the bottle are enough for you to carry,” Jasper said, passing the bottle to Durdles. “Hand your bundle to me; I am younger and longer-winded than you.”

Durdles hesitated between bundle and bottle as between duty and delight; but the bottle was by far the better company, and he kept it, consigning the dry weight to Jasper.

Then they went up the winding staircase of the great tower—turning and turning, shoulders and heads lowered to avoid the stair above or the rough stone pivot around which they twisted. Durdles lit the lantern by striking from the cold hard wall a spark of that hidden fire that lives in all things; and by that small star they clambered through cobwebs and dust. They passed through strange places: low-arched galleries where they could look down into the moonlit nave, and where, when Durdles waved his lantern, the dim carved angels’ heads on the corbels seemed to move and watch. Then into narrower, steeper staircases; the night-air began to blow upon them; a startled jackdaw or frightened rook chirped in the confined space, wings beat heavily against stone, and dust and straw rained on their heads as the bird forced past. At last, leaving their light behind a stair because the wind blew fresh up here, they looked down on Cloisterham spread fair in the moonlight: ruins and sanctuaries of the dead at the tower’s base; moss-softened red-tiled roofs and red-brick houses of the living beyond; and the river winding down from the mist on the horizon as though it rose from that whiteness, already heaving with a restless knowledge that its course ran seaward.

Again, what an unaccountable expedition. Jasper—still moving softly, as if even stone could hear—contemplated the scene, especially that stillest part of it which the Cathedral overshadowed. But he contemplated Durdles quite as curiously. Durdles, by times, felt the watchful eyes and shifted, uneasy; only by times, because he was growing drowsy.

As balloon-men lighten their load when they wish to rise, so Durdles had lightened the wicker bottle in coming up. Sleep caught him in snatches, surprising him on his legs and stopping his speech in the middle like a tool dropped. A mild calenture took him: he imagined the ground far below level with the tower, and would as willingly have walked out into air as along the stone. In such a state they began to come down. And as balloon-men make themselves heavier when they wish to descend, so Durdles charged himself with more liquid from the bottle to come down the better.

They reached the iron gate and locked it—not before Durdles had tumbled twice and once cut an eyebrow open—and descended into the crypt again, meaning to issue by the way they had entered. But returning among the lanes of moonlight between the pillars, Durdles grew so uncertain in foot and speech that he half dropped, half threw himself down by one of the heavy columns—scarcely less heavy than himself—and, in a thick, indistinct appeal, begged his companion for forty winks of a second each.

“If you will have it so, or must have it so,” Jasper replied, “I’ll not leave you here. Take them, while I walk to and fro.”
