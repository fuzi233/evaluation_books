Thursday slipped away without a ripple, the silence unbroken until Friday dawn broke with two startling tidings. First, the baker's boy swore he saw Rosanna Spearman wandering toward Frizinghall the prior afternoon, veiled and cloaked, her silhouette unmistakable against the moor. Yet this testimony crumbled instantly under scrutiny; Rosanna had been confined to her sickbed all Thursday, utterly unable to traverse the path. The boy's vision was flawed, a classic case of mistaking the familiar for the absent.

Second, the postman delivered news of Worthy Mr. Candy's decline. The little doctor, who had previously boasted of his waterproof skin, had succumbed to the rain on the birthday night. Now fever-stricken and light-headed, he rambled incoherently, his mind clouded by delirium. While the household expressed genuine sorrow for his condition, Sir Joseph Franklin viewed the development with professional concern, fearing that Miss Rachel's fragile state required immediate and superior medical intervention if the suspense surrounding the Moonstone was not swiftly resolved.

Before breakfast concluded, a telegram from Mr. Blake, the elder, shattered the morning's gloom. It announced that, assisted by the Commissioner, he had secured a specialist to aid in the investigation. The name was Sergeant Cuff, arriving via the morning train from London. Upon reading the name, Sir Joseph gave a start, recalling peculiar anecdotes about the officer shared by a former London lawyer. "If half the tales I've heard are true," Sir Joseph murmured, "this man unravels mysteries faster than anyone in England!"

Excitement mounted as the anticipated hour approached. Superintendent Seegrave retreated to a chamber, pen poised, to draft his report, anticipating the arrival of this renowned figure. I considered fetching Sergeant Cuff personally, but Lady Verinder's carriage was unavailable; moreover, Mr. Godfrey Blake needed the pony-chaise later for a pressing charitable engagement. Though deeply regretful to abandon his aunt during such anxiety, Mr. Godfrey deferred his departure until the final train, eager to hear the London detective's insights before heading to town.

When the time for arrival fell, I descended to the gate. A fly pulled up, and out stepped a man who seemed carved from bone and shadow. He was gaunt, dressed in somber black, his face sharpened like a hatchet, the skin of his cheeks yellow and parchment-thin, resembling an autumn leaf. His eyes, a steely, light grey, possessed a disquieting habit of seeming to expect knowledge from you that you did not possess. His gait was soft, his voice melancholic, his fingers long and hooked. He might have been a clergyman or a sexton, yet he was neither. He stood in stark, jarring contrast to the boisterous Superintendent Seegrave; he was an unsettling presence for a distressed family.

"Is this Lady Verinder's?" he asked.
"Yes, sir."
"I am Sergeant Cuff."
"This way, sir."

As we traversed the grounds, I introduced myself to establish trust, yet the Sergeant offered no comment regarding the business. Instead, he admired the landscape, remarking on the bracing sea air. Privately, I wondered how a man of his apparent constitution had garnered such a formidable reputation. Upon reaching the house, we moved like two unfamiliar hounds tethered together for the first time. Seeking my lady, who was resting in a conservatory, we circled to the rear gardens. While awaiting her, Sergeant Cuff wandered into the rosery, displaying a sudden, intense curiosity. To the gardener's astonishment and my dismay, the celebrated policeman revealed himself to be a veritable scholar of horticulture.

"Ah, you've secured the right exposure here, south and south-west," the Sergeant observed, nodding his grizzled head. "This shape suits a rosery perfectly—not a circle set within a square, but open lines. And pray, let me advise you: gravel walks are too harsh for roses. Grass, Mr. Gardener, grass between the beds." He gently caressed a bed of white and blush musk roses, treating the blooms with the tenderness reserved for a child. "Pretty dear!" he cooed, stroking the petals with his claw-like fingers.

"You seem fond of roses, Sergeant?" I ventured.
"I have scarce time for fondness," he replied, his voice heavy with weariness. "Yet when I do indulge, roses receive my affection. I began my life tending them in my father's nursery, and I intend to end it there, if Providence permits. One day, perhaps, I shall retire from chasing thieves and devote myself to cultivating flowers. Then there shall be grass walks, Mr. Gardener, between my beds." He glanced sharply at the gravel paths, his expression sour. "It seems an odd pastime for a man of my trade," I suggested.
"If you cast your eye around, as few do," he countered, "you'll see that a man's tastes are often diametrically opposed to his profession. Exhibit to me two things more antithetical than a rose and a thief, and I shall amend my habits immediately. You find the Damask rose a sturdy stock, do you not? Ah! Here comes a lady. Is it Lady Verinder?"

He had spotted her before we could direct his gaze, though we knew precisely where she was bound. I noted a quickness of perception in him that belied his sluggish exterior. Whether his appearance or his mission unsettled my lady, she found herself momentarily at a loss for words, a rare occurrence. However, Sergeant Cuff soothed her unease almost instantly. He inquired if any other officers had been engaged prior to his summons. Learning that Superintendent Seegrave was present, he requested a private audience with him before further proceedings.

Lady Verinder led the way back. Before following, the Sergeant confided in the gardener, urging him to persuade his mistress to try grass walks instead of gravel, delivering his verdict with a lingering scowl.

Inside, I watched as Superintendent Seegrave appeared diminished, almost comically small, beside the towering figure of Sergeant Cuff. They withdrew to a private room, sealing themselves away for a considerable duration. When they emerged, the dynamic had shifted dramatically: the Superintendent was agitated, while the Sergeant yawned, seemingly indifferent.

"The Sergeant desires to inspect Miss Verinder's sitting-room," Mr. Seegrave announced with pomposity, ordering me to attend. "He may require questions."
While I was dispatched, I studied the great Cuff, who returned my gaze with that same quiet, expectant intensity. I suspected he was biding his time, waiting for his colleague to falter.

Guiding the way upstairs, the Sergeant surveyed the Indian cabinet and the "boudoir," questioning me incessantly, occasionally glancing at Mr. Seegrave, whom he clearly regarded as inferior. Eventually, his scrutiny landed on the decorative painting adorning the door. Laying a lean finger on a faint smudge beneath the lock—a mark the Superintendent had previously noted—he voiced a simple observation.
"That is a pity," Cuff murmured. "How did it come to pass?"
He directed the query at me. I explained that the maids had crowded into the room the previous morning, and their petticoats had likely caused the damage. "Superintendent Seegrave ordered them out, sir," I added, "before further injury could occur."
"Correct!" Mr. Seegrave affirmed in his military tone. "The petticoats did it, Sergeant—the petticoats!"
"But did you observe *which* petticoat?" Cuff pressed, maintaining his focus on me rather than the blustering officer.
"No, sir."
Cuff turned to the Superintendent. "_You_ noticed, I presume?"
Mr. Seegrave hesitated, feigning casual dismissal. "I cannot recall details, Sergeant. A mere trifling detail—a mere trifle."
Sergeant Cuff regarded the Superintendent with the same detached amusement he had shown the gravel paths. "Last week, I conducted a private inquiry concerning a murder and a mysterious spot of ink on a tablecloth that defied explanation," he stated calmly. "In all my years traversing the filthiest corners of this world, I have never encountered such a 'trifle.' Before we proceed, we must identify the offending garment and ascertain exactly when the paint was wet."
The Superintendent, sulking at the rebuke, asked if he should summon the women. Cuff sighed, shaking his head. "No," he decided. "Let us address the paint first. It is a binary question: yes or no. The matter of the women is lengthy. What hour was it when the servants entered the room? Eleven, was it not? Does anyone in this house know if the paint was wet or dry at eleven o'clock on Thursday morning?"
"My young master, Mr. Franklin Blake, knows," I reported.
Franklin arrived promptly. "Sir," he stated, "this door was painted by Miss Verinder under my supervision, using a varnish of my own composition. Whatever color is applied, it dries completely within twelve hours."
"Do you recall when the smudged section was completed?"
"Perfectly," Franklin answered. "It was the final touch, executed on Wednesday afternoon shortly after three o'clock."
"Today is Friday," Cuff interjected. "Let us calculate. Completed at three on Wednesday, dried by three on Thursday. The inquiry took place at eleven on Thursday. Subtract three from eleven... eight hours remain. That paint had been dry for *eight hours*, Mr. Superintendent, when you alleged the maids' petticoats smeared it."
