Among the inheritors of Art, as with the ancient division of the Promised Land, every share must be seized through fierce contention. Bestowal follows the pattern of prophecy; it grants a title to a realm not yet possessed. To carry a map of an untamed estate in one's pocket is merely a precarious form of tenancy, and in fantasy to cast a shoe across Eden offers no guarantee that a man will ever plant his boot on a single acre of his own soil.

The most stubborn delusions mortals harbor regarding themselves rest on no evidence beyond a constant, spontaneous throbbing of self-satisfaction—an invisible seed of madness, a blind faith that they can shift the world without understanding where they stand or what leverage to apply.

"Please go to church, Mamma," Gwendola said the following morning. "I prefer seeing Herr Klesmer alone." (He had replied to her note stating he would meet her at eleven.)

"That hardly seems correct, I think," Mrs. Davilow said, her brow furrowed with anxiety.

"Our affairs are too weighty for us to concern ourselves with such absurd rules," Gwendola retorted, her tone dripping with disdain. "They are insulting as well as ridiculous."

"You wouldn't mind Isabel accompanying you? She could read quietly in a corner."

"No, she couldn't. She'd gnaw at her nails and stare. It would be far too irritating. Trust my judgment, Mamma; I must be alone. Send them all to church."

Gwendola secured her wish, naturally. Only Miss Merry and two younger girls remained at home, sitting at the dining-room windows to lend the house an illusion of habitation.

It was a delectable Sunday morning. The melancholy, fading sunlight of autumn settled upon the half-scattered grass and filtered gently through the windows in oblique bands of brilliance, illuminating the antique furniture and the glass panel that mirrored it. Light fell upon the tapestried chairs adorned with faded floral wreaths, the dark, enigmatic paintings, and the superannuated organ where Gwendola had delighted in playing Saint Cecilia upon her first joyful arrival. Through the open doors of the antechamber, a multitude of pallid, dusty knick-knacks was visible—the backdrop where she had achieved the wearing of her Greek costume as Hermione. That specific memory was vividly active in her mind now. Had not Klesmer then been struck by admiration for her pose and expression? Whatever he had said, whatever she imagined him to have thought, was currently scrutinized with her keenest interest. Perhaps she had never in her life felt so inwardly dependent, so acutely aware of needing another person's opinion. A new fluttering of spirit vibrated within her, introducing a fresh element of deliberation into her self-assessment, which had previously been a blissful gift of pure intuition. Yet, the recurring burden of her internal soliloquy remained: Klesmer had seen but little of her, and any unfavorable conclusion he reached would surely rest on a foundation too narrow. She genuinely felt clever enough for any undertaking.

To occupy the time, she gathered her volumes and musical scores, laying them atop the piano, and set about classifying them. Catching the reflection of her movements in the glass panel, she was diverted to contemplating her own image and walked toward it. Dressed in black, devoid of ornament, with the warm whiteness of her skin set off between her light-brown crown of hair and her square-cut bodice, she might have tempted an artist to attempt again the Roman trick of a statue carved from black, white, and tawny marble. Seeing her image advance slowly, she thought, "I *am* beautiful"—not with exultation, but with grave decision. Being beautiful was, after all, the condition upon which she most desperately needed external testimony. If anyone objected to the turn of her nose or the form of her neck and chin, she lacked the sense that she could demonstrate her power of attainment in these branches of feminine perfection.

There was little time left to fill in this manner before the clatter of wheels, the loud ringing of a bell, and the opening of doors assured her that she would not, by any accident, be disappointed. This slight increase in her inner fluttering served to heighten her dread of Klesmer. Despite her self-confidence, she feared him as part of that unmanageable world which existed independently of her wishes—something vitriolic that would not cease to burn simply because one smiled or frowned at it. Poor creature! She was at a higher crisis point in her woman's fate than during her last experience with Grandcourt. The questioning then had been whether she should take a particular man as a husband. The inmost fold of her questioning now was whether she needed to take a husband at all—whether she could not achieve substantial independence for herself and know gratified ambition without bondage.

Klesmer made his most deferential bow in the wide doorway of the antechamber, displaying the deference of the finest gray kerseymere trousers and perfect gloves (for the "masters of those who know" are happily, altogether, human beings). Gwendola met him with unusual gravity, holding out her hand and saying, "It is most kind of you to come, Herr Klesmer. I hope you have not thought me presumptuous."

"I took your wish as a command that did me honor," Klesmer replied, answering gravity. He was indeed setting aside his own affairs to give his utmost attention to whatever Gwendola might have to say; yet his temperament was still in a state of excitation from the events of yesterday, likely enough to lend his expressions a more than usually biting edge.

Gwendola, for once, was under too great a strain of feeling to remember formalities. She stood near the piano, and Klesmer took his stand near the other end, his back to the light and his terribly omniscient eyes fixed upon her. No affectation was of use, and she began without delay.

"I wish to consult you, Herr Klesmer. We have lost all our fortune; we have nothing. I must earn my own bread, and I desire to provide for my mother, saving her from any hardship. The only way I can think of—and I should like it better than anything else—is to become an actress—to go on the stage. But, of course, I should like to take a high position, and I thought—if you thought I could"—here Gwendola became slightly more nervous—"it would be better for me to be a singer—to study singing as well."

Klesmer placed his hat upon the piano and folded his arms as if concentrating his thoughts.

"I know," Gwendola resumed, turning from pale to pink and back again—"I know that my method of singing is very defective; but I have been ill-taught. I could be better taught; I could study. And you will understand my wish: to sing and act, like Grisi, occupies a much higher position. Naturally, I should wish to take as high a rank as I can. And I can rely on your judgment. I am sure you will tell me the truth."

Gwendola somehow held the conviction that now, in making this serious appeal, the truth would prove favorable.

Yet Klesmer spoke not a word. He quickly removed his gloves, tossed them into his hat, rested his hands on his hips, and walked to the other end of the room. He was filled with compassion for this girl; he wanted to put a guard around his speech. When he turned again, he looked at her with a mild frown of inquiry and said, with gentle though rapid utterance, "You have never seen anything, I think, of artists and their lives?—I mean of musicians, actors, artists of that kind?"

"Oh, no," Gwendola said, unperturbed by a reference to this obvious fact in the history of a young lady who had hitherto been well-provided for.

"You are—pardon me," Klesmer said, pausing again near the piano—"in coming to a conclusion on such a matter as this, everything must be taken into consideration—you are perhaps twenty?"

"I am twenty-one," Gwendola said, a slight fear rising within her. "Do you think I am too old?"

Klesmer pouted his lower lip and shook his long fingers upward in a manner totally enigmatic.

"Many persons begin later than others," Gwendola said, betrayed by her habitual consciousness of possessing valuable information to bestow.

Klesmer paid no notice, but said with more studied gentleness than ever, "You have probably not thought of an artistic career until now: you did not entertain the notion, the longing—what shall I say?—you did not wish yourself an actress, or anything of that sort, until the present trouble?"

"Not exactly: but I was fond of acting. I have acted; you saw me, if you remember—you saw me here in charades, and as Hermione," Gwendola said, truly fearing that Klesmer had forgotten.

"Yes, yes," he answered quickly, "I remember—I remember perfectly," and again walked to the other end of the room. It was difficult for him to refrain from this kind of movement whenever he was engaged in any argument, whether audible or silent.

Gwendola felt that she was being weighed. The delay was unpleasant. But she did not yet conceive that the scales could tip on the wrong side, and it seemed to her only graceful to say, "I shall be very much obliged to you for taking the trouble to give me your advice, whatever it may be."
