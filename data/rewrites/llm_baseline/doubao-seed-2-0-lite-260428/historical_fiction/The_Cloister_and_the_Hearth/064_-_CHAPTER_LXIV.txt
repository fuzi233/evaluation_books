Beneath tapestries stitched with silver and gold thread, in a chamber draped with heavy crimson velvet, the polished stone floor was thickly strewn with striped wolf pelts and dappled leopard furs. A noble lady sat there, arms stretched out stiff before her, hands half clenched, every line of her face matching the restlessness of her posture: her cheeks flamed then blanched in sharp turns, one foot tapping a rapid, agitated rhythm against the soft fur at her feet.

At last the heavy velvet curtain over the doorway was drawn aside by a trembling domestic. The lady’s brow flushed hot at the sight. The maid leaned in, voice a whisper thick with awe: “Your Highness, the man you sent for has come.”

The lady bade her admit him, then snatched up a small black velvet mask and fixed it to her face. In a single heartbeat all colour drained from her cheeks, and the stark, uncanny contrast between the black mask and her marble-pale skin seemed ghostly, heavy with unspoken threat.

A man stepped inside, bowing and scraping so low his forehead nearly brushed the floor. He was the sort of unremarkable figure that vanished in any crowd: close-cropped hair, a round unmemorable head, plain but well-kept wool clothes, features neither handsome nor harsh enough to draw a second glance. Nothing about him stood out at all, save for a pair of singularly restless, sharp eyes that darted over every corner of the room in an instant. After a dozen more profuse bows, he planted himself opposite the lady and waited, silent, for her to speak.

“They have told you why you are summoned?” she said, low and tight.
“Yes, Signora.”
“And those who negotiated with you agreed on the full sum you are to receive?”
“Aye, Signora. The full price buys the greater vendetta, unless of your grace you choose to settle for the lesser.”
“I do not follow your meaning,” the lady said.
“Ah! This must be the Signora’s first time, then. The lesser vendetta, my lady, is the death of the body only. We track our mark as he leaves church, or catch him unawares in some innocent quiet hour, and strike clean. The greater vendetta? We lie in wait, seize him hot from some unconfessed, unrepented sin, and so slay his soul as well as his flesh. Though that kind is less in demand these days than it was a few years back.”

“Man,” she said, her voice sharpening, “silence his tongue for good. Let his treasonous, lying heart beat no more. His soul is no affair of mine, I have no grudge against it.”

“So it shall be, Signora. Those who sent word to me knew neither the man’s name, nor his face, nor where he lodges. From whom am I to learn these things?”
“From my own lips.”

At that, the bravo showed the first flicker of true anxiety he had yet betrayed, and entreated her to speak with great care and perfect exactness on this part of the business.
“Fear not for me,” she said. “Listen close. He is a young man, tall of stature, with auburn hair and dark blue eyes, and an honest face so guileless it would deceive a saint. He lodges in Via Claudia, at the corner house belonging to the glover. There are only three men lodging under that roof: him, a short, dark-visaged painter, and a slim young boy. The man who betrayed me is a stranger here, fair of face, and taller even than you are.”

The bravo drank down every word. “That is all I need,” he said.
“Stay,” she cut in. “Does he haunt any secret, out-of-the-way place where you might strike without being seen? My spies report he has taken to wandering the banks of the Tiber after dusk of late, no doubt to meet his light-o’-love, the girl who dares call me her rival. Slay him even there! Let that smooth, heartless, insolent traitor’s little paramour stumble across his corpse, and learn what it means to cross a princess.”

“Calm yourself, Signora. He will never betray another lady again.”
“I do not count on that,” she snapped. “He wears a sword, and he can wield it. He is young, and stubborn, and resolute.”
“Neither of those will save him.”
“You are so sure of your skill? What weapons do you carry for such work?”

The bravo held up a heavy steel gauntlet. “We strike with such force we must guard our own hand, lest we break our bones. That is our mallet, to stun our mark before he can cry out.” Then he undid the laces of his doublet, and showed her the glint of fine chain mail sewn into the lining beneath. Finally he laid a long, glittering stiletto down on the oak table with a flourish.

The lady shuddered at first, but then she reached out with her white soft hand, lifted the blade, and tested its sharp point against the tip of her finger.
“Beware, madam,” the bravo warned.
“What, is the edge poisoned?”
“Saints forbid! We do not steal lives like cowards. We take them with steel, not drugs or potions. But the blade is newly ground, and I feared for the softness of your Highness’s fair skin.”

“His skin is as white as mine,” she murmured, a sudden flicker of pity crossing her face. It died out as quickly as it had come. “But his heart is black as soot. Tell me, am I not right to rid the world of a traitor who slanders me, who spurns my favour for common trash?”
“The Signora may settle that account with her confessor,” the bravo said, with a small deferential shrug. “I am no more than a tool in noble hands, same as my stiletto there.”

The princess did not seem to hear him. “Oh, how I could have loved him! Loved him to the death, as fiercely as I hate him now. Fool! He will learn what it means to trifle with princes, to spurn their favour then fawn for it, to prefer the dregs of the town to one of royal blood, to make me a laughingstock for gossips.” She lifted her head sharply. “Why loiter here? Go! Avenge me at once.”

“By custom, Signora, half the sum is paid beforehand.”
“Ah, I forgot,” she said, pushing a heavy leather purse across the table at him. “Your revenge is bought. This is more than half. When the blow is struck, return for the rest.”
“I shall see you again very soon, Signora.” He bowed himself out, scraping and bobbing, until the curtain fell shut behind him.

Left alone, the princess burned with the heat of unspent jealousy, mortified pride, and the gnawing terror of public exposure—for no blemish had ever stained her reputation before she set eyes on Gerard. She sat exactly where the bravo had left her, mask still fixed to her face, arms held straight out before her, the nails of her clenched hands digging deep into the oak tabletop. So sat the fabled Sphinx of old, silent and terrible; so sits a tigress robbed of her cub.

Yet as soon as the assassin was gone, a cold chill crept over her. Moody, heavy misgivings swelled in her chest, the first faint precursors of a remorse that would come too late to do any good.

Gerard and Margaret were souls born far ahead of their time. The princess before us was the true unalloyed medieval noble: proud, amorous, vindictive, quick to give charity, quick to act the fool, sharp-witted when she wished, impulsive, unprincipled, and ignorant as the dirt beneath her shoes. Power is the curse of such a creature. If she had been forced to carry out her own crimes, the weakness of her frayed nerves would have balanced the violence of her passions, and her threats would have rung far louder than any blow she could ever have struck. But power puts the tools of violence into the hands of weak, furious women: male agents, trained for murder. The effect is as terrible as that combination is unnatural. And in this very moment, that combination was sharpening an assassin’s dagger for a poor, forsaken wretch who was even then standing on the riverbank, meditating his own suicide.
