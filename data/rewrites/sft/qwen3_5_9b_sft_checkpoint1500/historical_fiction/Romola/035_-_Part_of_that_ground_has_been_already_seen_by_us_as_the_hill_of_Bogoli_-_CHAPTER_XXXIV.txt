No Place for Repentance.

Messer Naldo returned sooner than anyone anticipated, arriving on the evening of the twenty-eighth of November, merely eleven days after his previous visit. This swift return indicated he had not ventured far beyond the mountains. A scene previously witnessed in the Via de' Bardi helps illuminate the impelling force that drew his steps toward the hill of San Giorgio.

When Tito first secured this dwelling for Tessa upon his return from Rome, more than eighteen months prior, he convinced himself he acted solely under the constraint of his innate kindness following the unfortunate incident that led poor Tessa to mistake him for her husband. While it was undeniable that his benevolence was directed toward a pretty, trusting creature whom one could not approach without an urge to caress, it was equally true that Tito harbored feelings of kindness toward her independent of any calculated gain. Indeed, charming as her beauty and chatter were in idle moments, he might have preferred freedom from her company; for he was not in love with Tessa. For the first time in his life, he was consumed by an entirely different woman, whose mere presence vibrated through his hours. Every spark of youthful ideal passion he possessed had been ignited by Romola. His fiber was too refined, his intellect too bright, to succumb to the habits of a coarse pleasure-seeker. Yet, he had spun a web around himself and Tessa that he felt powerless to break. In the initial moments following the mock marriage, he had been driven to leave her under an illusion by a distinct calculation of his future needs. However, since that critical juncture, the web appeared to spin itself autonomously, a growth over which he held no sway. The elements of kindness and self-indulgence were indistinguishable in a soft nature like Tito's. The annoyance he had felt regarding Tessa's pursuit on the day of his betrothal, coupled with his sincere intention to reveal the truth, served as sufficient proof that his ultimate decision to maintain her deception and provide her a home stemmed from overwhelming compassion. In these days of his first devoted attachment to Romola, he required a self-justifying narrative. He had learned to find solace in the fact that she was deceived about certain matters. Yet, every profound feeling constructs its own conscience, possesses its own piety; just as the bond between a son and mother may survive amidst the foulest stench of depravity, so Tito could not yet rest easy committing a secret offense against his wedded love.

Consequently, he exercised extreme caution in preserving the secrecy of this transgression. Monna Lisa, who, like many of her station, rarely left her confines except for specific shops or annual confession, knew nothing of his true identity or whereabouts. She merely knew that he compensated her handsomely to ensure her comfort and cared little for the details beyond her duties, save that she had grown fond of Tessa and derived satisfaction from the cares for which she was paid. An air of mystery clearly enveloped the situation, given that Tessa was a peasant girl (*contadina*) while Messer Naldo was a gentleman. To Monna Lisa, however, he might easily have been a real husband. Tito had thoroughly terrified Tessa into silence regarding the circumstances of their union, warning her that breaking that silence meant never seeing him again. Furthermore, Monna Lisa's deafness rendered it impossible to convey information without premeditation, thus saving Tessa from any accidental revelations, unlike her careless confidences to Baldassarre. For a long time, Tito's visits were so infrequent that it appeared he traveled extensively between them. These journeys were primarily motivated by a desire to verify that all was well with Tessa. Although he invariably found his visits more pleasant than the anticipation of them—always rediscovering the charm of that pretty, ignorant, loving trust—he had not yet developed a pressing need for them. Nevertheless, he was resolved, if possible, to preserve the simplicity upon which this charm depended. He intended to keep Tessa a genuine *contadina*, refusing to place the wildflower among conditions that would strip it of its grace. He would have been appalled to see her dressed in attire inappropriate to her rank; the zest of her speech would vanish if new relations entered her life, if her world expanded, and if her pleasures lost their childish innocence. Her squirrel-like enjoyment of nuts at leisure defined the standard of the luxuries he provided. Through this strategy, Tito preserved Tessa's charm from being sullied, and simultaneously, by a convenient coincidence, spared himself from exacerbating expenses that were already becoming burdensome to a man whose funds were wholly dedicated to his acknowledged lifestyle.

Briefly, this had been the history of Tito's relationship with Tessa until a very recent date. It is true that on occasion, prior to Bardo's death, the knowledge that Tessa resided up the hill, offering a chance for an agreeable hour, had induced him to escape the slight weariness of caring for the old man. Had there been no positive engagement, he might otherwise have endured the fatigue patiently and shared Romola's burden. However, the moment when he first felt a genuine hunger for Tessa's ignorant lovingness and blind faith in *him* had arrived only quite recently, distinctly marked by circumstances as memorable as the onset of a disease that permanently vitiated sight and hearing. It was the day he first encountered Baldassarre and purchased the armor. Returning across the bridge that night, clutching the coat of mail, he felt an unconquerable dread of an immediate encounter with Romola. Like her, she knew little of the actual world; like her, she trusted him. Yet, he possessed an uneasy consciousness that behind her frank eyes lay a nature capable of judgment, and that any misplaced trust of hers sprang not from a brutish inability to discern, but from a nobility that might prove an alarming touchstone. He desired a measure of ease, a repose from self-control, after the agitation and exertion of the day. He sought a place where he could adjust his mind to the morrow without concern for his current behavior. And there existed a sweet, adoring creature within reach whose presence was as safe and unconstraining as that of her own kids—one who would believe any fable and remain utterly unimpressed by public opinion. Thus, on that evening, when Romola waited and listened for him, he turned his steps up the hill.

No wonder, therefore, that the steps followed the same course on this evening, eleven days later, after he had recoiled under Romola's first outburst of scorn. He could not wish Tessa in his wife's place, nor refrain from wishing that his wife be thoroughly reconciled to him; for it was Romola, not Tessa, who belonged to the realm where the larger desires of an ambitious man with effective faculties must inevitably reside. Still, he sought refuge from a standard disagreeably rigorous, from which he could not liberate himself merely by dismissing it as folly; and Tessa's small soul was that inviting refuge.

It was scarcely past eight o'clock when he ascended the stone steps to the door of Tessa's chamber. Usually, she heard his entry and rushed to greet him, but not tonight. Upon opening the door, he discovered the cause. A single dim light burned above the dying fire, illuminating Tessa in a kneeling posture by the head of the bed where the infant lay. Her head had slumped onto the pillow, and her brown rosary, which usually hung above the pillow over the picture of the Madonna and the golden palm branches, lay loosely grasped in her right hand. She had fallen sound asleep over her beads. Tito stepped lightly across the small room and sat down close beside her. She had likely heard the opening of the door as part of her dream, for she had not been looking at him two moments before she opened her eyes. She opened them without a start, remaining quite motionless as she gazed at him, as if the awareness that he smiled at her suppressed any impulse to disturb that happy passivity. Yet, when he placed his hand beneath her chin and stooped to kiss her, she declared—

"I dreamed it, and then I said it was a dream—and then I awoke, and it became true."

"Little sinner!" said Tito, pinching her chin, "you have not recited half your prayers. I shall punish you by refusing to look at your baby; it is ugly."

Tessa disliked those words, even though Tito was smiling. A pouting distress marred her countenance as she said, bending anxiously over the child—

"Oh, it is not true! He is prettier than anything. You do not think he is ugly. You will look at him. He is even prettier than when you saw him before—only he is asleep, and you cannot see his eyes or his tongue, and I cannot show you his hair—and it grows—isn't that wonderful? Look at him! It is true his face is very much the same when he sleeps; there is not so much to see as when he is awake. If you kiss him very gently, he will not wake: you want to kiss him, is it not true?"

He soothed her by granting the small bundle a butterfly kiss, then placing his hand on her shoulder and turning her face toward him, he remarked, "You prefer looking at the baby to looking at your husband, you false one!"

She remained kneeling, resting her hands on his knee and gazing up at him like one of Fra Lippo Lippi's round-cheeked adoring angels.

"No," she said, shaking her head; "I love you always best, only I want you to look at the bambino and love him; I used only to want you to love me."

"And did you expect me to return so soon?"
