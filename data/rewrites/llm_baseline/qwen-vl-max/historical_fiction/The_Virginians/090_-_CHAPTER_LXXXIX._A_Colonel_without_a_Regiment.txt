When my visit to my brother had ended, and my wife and child had returned to our family home in Richmond, I made it my duty to travel to Williamsburg, where our Governor resided at his country estate, to discuss the mounting signs of war that were spreading across the colonies. Rumors of armed conflict reached us from every corner—Massachusetts stood on the brink of open hostilities with British troops; Maryland and Pennsylvania still seemed inclined toward loyalty; the Carolinas and Georgia remained firmly aligned with the Crown; and Virginia, proud of its heritage and ancient allegiance, was expected to stand firm. Though we had few regular troops, our gentry prided themselves on their descent from the Cavaliers, and around the Governor gathered a throng of zealous Loyalists eager to draw sword against what they called the rebellious rabble.

At these gatherings, I could not avoid hearing harsh words about my brother Harry. All agreed—perhaps justly—that his wife had influenced him toward extreme anti-British views. He was said to be blindly devoted to the gentleman of Mount Vernon, whose opinions Harry followed closely, and who daily deepened himself in the perilous path of resistance. “This is your friend,” the Governor’s circle would say, “the man you once trusted, who shared your table and your confidence!” Much of this was true, though what some called treason was really nothing more than a desperate desire to preserve loyalty to the Crown, to exhaust every avenue of reconciliation before embracing revolt.

Let traitors arm! Let villains raise the parricidal blade! We, at least, would remain faithful. England’s strength would prevail, and the wayward provinces would be punished and brought back into submission. After our banquets, we toasted His Majesty with fervor, swearing to die defending his rights. We dreamed of a prince from the royal house returning to rule over these ancient dominions.

In light of my own and my mother’s unwavering loyalty, perhaps my brother’s misguided course could yet be corrected. Was it too late to offer him a military command? Might I intercede on his behalf? I knew he respected me, and I believed I had influence over him.

Back in Williamsburg, our councils swayed between hope and despair, triumph and dread. We spoke of reinforcements from England, cursed the rebels, and trembled at the uncertain future. I promised to speak with Harry, and wrote to him—though with little expectation of success—repeating arguments I had used many times before. My mother also tried her hand, appealing to scripture and authority, warning of divine punishment for disobedience to earthly powers. She cited the example of Harry’s elder brother and hinted, rather cruelly, at his subjection to his wife—the worst possible argument in such a matter.

She did not show me her letter, but she did let me read his reply. To my surprise, it was composed with great clarity and elegance—so much so that I thought even his wife might have been incapable of writing it. It turned out Harry had enlisted the aid of Mr. Belman, the New Richmond clergyman, a staunch Whig who had preached and published sermons denouncing Hagan, one of our own faction, and who had clearly won the debate.

My entreaties, like my mother’s, were met with silence. When pressed further by the Governor’s allies, I rashly wrote that I would visit Harry at Fanny’s Mount at the end of the week. But upon arrival, I found only my sister, who welcomed me warmly but told me Harry had gone far into the countryside—toward the Blue Mountains—to inspect horses, and would be away for an unknown length of time.

Then I knew all hope was lost.

“Dear sister,” I said, “from the signs of the times, it seems inevitable that the fuse lit years ago will soon ignite. Harry is riding away—God knows where.”

“The Lord prosper the righteous cause, Sir George,” she replied.

“Amen,” I answered. “But you and he speak as Americans; I as an Englishman. Tell him this: if anything natural happens to our mother, I shall resign all my lands in Virginia to him. I have enough for myself and mine in England.”

“You don’t mean that, George?” she exclaimed, eyes brightening.

“Well, yes,” she said after a pause. “It’s only fair. Why should you, eldest by a mere hour, have everything—lands in England, plantations here, title, children—while poor Harry has nothing? Still, it’s generous of you. Handsome, proper—never expected it. And you don’t take after your mother in this, Sir George, nohow. Give my love to sister Theo!”

She offered me her cheek to kiss as I prepared to leave.

With such a woman guiding him, how could I ever hope to turn Harry?

Frustrated by my failure, I returned to the Governor. Together, we decided it was time to prepare seriously for war. The Court was agitated, furious in its denunciations of the Whigs, shouting “Old England forever!” with reckless zeal. They longed for the day when they could ride down the rebels in battle.

I left the Governor with thanks from his Excellency and a commission to raise a force in defense of the Crown. Thus began the Westmoreland Defenders—a regiment I was honored to command. News of my appointment spread quickly through the county, and officers stepped forward readily. But recruits were slow to come—and quicker to vanish.

Hagan, ever loyal, volunteered as chaplain. Madam Esmond presented our colors and traveled the region recruiting volunteers. But the most passionate recruiter was my old tutor, little Mr. Dempster, who had fought for the Jacobites in Scotland. He journeyed into the Carolinas, seeking descendants of exiled Highlanders who still wore the white cockade of Prince Charles and remained loyal to the Crown.

Meanwhile, Harry’s journey took him beyond the Blue Mountains, through Annapolis and Baltimore, and finally to Philadelphia, where a second Continental Congress was assembling. By then, history had already begun to unfold. Lexington had seen the first shots fired. The war for independence had begun.

We continued to profess loyalty to His Majesty, yet declared our resolve to die or be free. Twenty thousand loyalists gathered around Boston, armed and ready, having seized cannon from government stores. Arnold launched his bold campaign with the capture of two forts. Three generals from Bond Street advanced with reinforcements to aid General Gage.

Soon, armies clashed. At Breed’s Hill, the British made a costly blunder—fortifying instead of advancing—while Congress debated who should lead the colonial forces. The choice fell, famously, on a Virginian.

Among the regiments marching to join the new commander-in-chief was one led by Henry Esmond Warrington, formerly a captain in His Majesty’s service. Beside him rode his young wife, whose courage would later become legend. I was relieved she had left Virginia. Had she stayed, my mother might have challenged her to battle—though I am grateful that such a scene never occurred.

Farmers and country folk rushed northward to join the army. The selection of a Virginian for supreme command flattered our pride. The news of Lexington sparked fury across the colonies. Men raged against the “bloody British invader”—though the soldiers were merely doing their duty, facing armed men resisting their lawful actions. Yet anger blinds reason. The colonists had chosen sides, and with fiery spirit, they resolved to test their strength against the mighty mother country.

Breed’s Hill became a symbol—like a mountain crowned with Liberty, Victory, Glory. Farmers and ploughmen had held their ground. Civilians had outmaneuvered seasoned officers. If Britons could conquer the world, their children could now match them—even surpass them.

I do not know which side deserves greater credit—bravery or pride. We mock the French for their boastfulness, but who matches the unshakable self-confidence of the British—or their American offspring?

Most of those around us supported the people’s cause. As for me, my regiment began poorly manned and dwindled further after news of northern battles—and worse, after the conduct of our Governor.

He had vowed to raise the King’s standard and rally loyal gentlemen. I recall many names—gentlemen now serving in the U.S. government—who once pledged to die beneath the flag, or challenge the rebels to single combat. But history records that our Governor, a peer of Scotland and the Crown’s representative, was the first to flee.

He abandoned his post, slipping away at night to board a warship off York. He sent a message to the Assembly, begging them to continue their sessions, claiming he had fled only from popular fury.

What became of the sheep when the shepherd ran?

The Assembly pleaded with him to return, promising safety. But he refused. His ship became his seat of government. He demanded the House of Commons come to him.

This was raising the standard indeed.

Our Governor was gone. The Assembly ruled in his stead. A mob followed him to sea. Negroes deserted plantations to join his fleet. He landed in darkness, burning and destroying. He not only invited runaway slaves—he sent envoys to the Indians, urging them to join his banner.

When the people resisted, as at Norfolk and Hampton, he retreated to his ships again.

Even my mother, once so certain of victory, grew fearful. She saw the rebellion as both dangerous and cowardly—especially the arming of slaves. The loyal gentry suffered ruin, robbed of their property. Dozens fled to his fleet, some killed, others hanged for plundering under his command.

Yet despite the destruction, and the incompetence of the Crown’s chief officer, my mother never wavered. She became more fiercely loyal than ever. Her oddities were known throughout the province—her tales of the Marquis, her family’s grandeur—but so too were her kindnesses. Many rebels, as she called them, secretly respected the proud little Tory lady.

As for me, though my regiment shrank to nothing after the Governor’s disgrace, I escaped serious danger. After he burned the stores at Hampton and issued his proclamation calling slaves to arms, I went aboard his ship to remonstrate. I begged him to return to Williamsburg, where thousands would defend him. I spoke plainly, showing clear contempt for his actions.

He flew into a rage. “You’re a rebel like the rest!” he shouted, ordering me arrested.

As a militia officer—I often wore a red coat to show my allegiance—I demanded a court-martial. I asked two officers present to bear witness to our exchange. They may have sympathized with me. In the end, I was released unguarded.

Word spread among the Whigs. I had spoken boldly to the Governor—no Whig could have been more liberal in speech.

When riots broke out in Richmond, many Loyalists fled to the ships. But my mother’s house and property remained untouched. Our family was still hopeful of reconciliation.

“Aha!” a distinguished Whig once told me. “If all Tories were like you, we and the people at home might soon be united again.”

That was before the Fourth of July, before the Declaration of Independence made peace impossible. Later, when passions hardened, other motives were attributed to me. I was said to be a “liberal Tory” only because I was cunning, wishing to protect my estate no matter the outcome.

And this, I must admit, is the view held in high circles at home—where personal interest is supposed to explain all.

But there were only a few who knew the truth: I had resolved to cede my life-interest in our American lands to my brother—though I had not told my mother.

Why bother to correct the record? What worth has life if one must endure endless slander?

And I still do not fully understand how my mother, that notorious Loyalist, remained unmolested for years at Castlewood, even as Continental troops were occasionally quartered upon her.

I cannot say for certain. But I suspect it had something to do with Madam Fanny.

After a campaign before Boston, she returned to Fanny’s Mount, leaving her husband behind. My modest Hal, who would accept no rank higher than colonel, believed he served better leading a regiment than commanding a division.

Madam Fanny came back changed. Her bitterness toward my mother vanished. She showed affection for her and her estate. She was close to the new Governor and influential members of the Assembly. My mother, they said, was harmless—and for Harry’s sake, her errors deserved leniency.

So it was that for years, she remained safe—except for heavy requisitions, which she paid willingly. Only when the redcoats approached our house did real harm come.
