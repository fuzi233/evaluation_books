“I would speak with you,” Baldassarre said, his voice low and urgent, as Romola turned to him with quiet expectancy. It was clear he had followed her, waiting in silence until she paused. At last, the truth she had long suspected—yet dreaded—was about to be revealed.

“Yes,” she replied, her tone subdued, almost like one accepting a sentence of penance. “But you wish to go where no one can hear us?”

“Where _he_ will not come upon us,” Baldassarre murmured, glancing behind him with a flicker of fear. “Out—into the open air—away from the streets.”

“I sometimes go to San Miniato at this hour,” Romola said. “If you wish, I will go now. You may follow. It is far, but we shall be alone.”

He nodded, and she set off without hesitation. To another woman, such a journey might have seemed reckless—alone with a man whose strange demeanor hinted at madness, as Tito had claimed. But Romola did not tremble before danger. And though her heart was heavy, she welcomed the distance, the delay it offered before the next blow struck.

The afternoon sun dipped low, casting long shadows across the hills, when she stopped on a rough patch beneath cypress trees. She looked around for Baldassarre. He was near, but when he reached her, he sank down onto the stony ground, breathless. His once-strong frame had weakened; the vigor that marked him in the Duomo was gone. A shiver ran through him, and his eyes held a hollow vagueness, as if the world were slipping away.

“The hill is steep,” Romola said gently, seating herself beside him. “And I fear you are worn by hunger?”

He turned his head slowly, fixing his gaze on her. Words failed him—the moment had come, yet he could not shape them. She sat still, motionless, giving him space, afraid to show impatience. He looked like a broken man, forgotten by time and fortune. Yet Romola had learned to see beyond appearances, to feel the weight of lives buried beneath neglect.

At last, his eyes sharpened, and he spoke with sudden force:

“Ah! You would have been my daughter!”

A flush swept over Romola’s face—then vanished, leaving her pale, lips parted like stone carved in sorrow. In that instant, the truth burst upon her. The fragments fell into place: Tito’s lies, his coldness, the way he spoke of his father’s death. She saw it all—how he had stolen this man’s life, his memory, his love.

She did not move. But her mind raced, shaped by years of knowing Tito’s nature. Her face betrayed her shock, and that was enough to stir Baldassarre. For the first time, his words had touched something real.

“You are a woman of noble blood—is it not true?” he pressed on, gripping her arm. “You go to hear the preacher. You despise falsehood—especially when it wears a smile. You hate your husband?”

“Oh God,” Romola whispered, overwhelmed by memory. “Was he truly your son? Or was it as he said—did you take him when he was small?”

“Ah, you believe me!” Baldassarre exclaimed, his voice rising. “You know what he is!” He clutched her arm tighter, as if her touch gave him strength. “Will you help me?”

“Yes,” she said, not understanding the depth of his meaning. She placed her hand gently over his rough fingers, and tears welled up. “Oh, it is pitiful. Tell me—you were a great scholar. You taught him. How did it end?”

She hesitated. Tito had called him mad. Where were the signs of learning now? But she kept her hand still, listening.

“It is gone,” Baldassarre said, voice breaking. “All of it. They wouldn’t believe me. He lied—he said I was mad—and they dragged me to prison. I am old. My mind does not return. And the world turns its back.”

He paused, his shoulders slumping under a wave of despair. Then, lifting his head again, he said with fierce urgency:
“But _you_ are not against me. He made you love him—then broke your trust. You hate him. Yes, he made _me_ love him. He was beautiful, gentle—a child in need. I found him beaten. He slept in my arms. I watched him grow. I gave him everything—my knowledge, my books, my treasures. He had my gems—sold them. Left me in chains. Never came back. When I returned, poor and broken, he denied me. Said I was mad.”

“He told us his father drowned,” Romola said softly. “Surely he believed it then. Oh, he could not have been so cruel then!”

A vision rose—Tito as she once knew him: tender, radiant, untouched by darkness. The ache of that lost innocence brought a sob, and tears spilled freely.

“You are young,” Baldassarre said impatiently. “Tears are useless. They smother the fire within. It is the fire that moves things forward. Listen.”

Romola turned sharply. Doubt flickered—was this man merely mad? An assassin driven by delusion? But the truth she had glimpsed refused to fade. She chose compassion over fear.

“Tell me,” she said gently, “how did you lose your memory? Your learning?”

“I was ill,” he answered. “I don’t know how long—only darkness. Then, one day, I sat among stones, bathed in sunlight. Nothing else existed. Slowly, a longing grew—something I couldn’t name. On the ship, drifting over water, I understood: I longed for the Boy. To find my thoughts again. I was locked out. Still am. All I feel is a wall… and darkness.”

He fell silent, hands clasped around his head. Romola’s belief deepened. She thought of her own father—how he mourned Dino, the future he’d imagined. This grief was familiar.

“It came back once,” Baldassarre continued after a while. “I was master of all. I saw the world again—my books, my jewels. I thought I had him in my power. I went to expose him—where the lights were, where the trees stood. But he lied again. Called me mad. They dragged me away…”

His voice hardened. “Wickedness is strong. He wears armor.”

Again, he gripped her arm. “But you will help me. He has wronged you too. He has another wife—children. He makes her believe he is her husband. She is foolish, helpless. I will show you where she lives.”

Romola recoiled—not from fear, but from outrage. The violation of dignity, of truth—this was unbearable. Baldassarre sensed her anger, mistaking it for alliance.

“You hate him,” he said. “I know women can hate. You have pride. You despise deceit. And you can love revenge.”

She sat frozen, torn between horror and pity. She felt the pressure of his grip, but did not pull away.

“You shall plan it,” he whispered, eager. “I have learned that you are his rightful wife. A noble woman. You listen to the preacher of vengeance. You will help justice. But think for me. My mind falters—everything slips—but the fire remains. The fire is God. It is justice. It will not die. You believe that—do you not?”

“If they won’t hang him for stealing from me,” he went on, “you will strip him of his armor. I will stab him. I have a knife. My arm is still strong.”

He slipped his hand beneath his tunic and drew forth a blade, running a thumb along the edge as if to steady himself.

Romola felt the weight of every hour ahead growing heavier. She knew better than to plead or deny. Instead, she asked quietly:
“You say she is helpless—that other wife—believes him her husband. Perhaps he married her first. Before me.”

“I cannot remember,” Baldassarre said, pausing. “Only the place. You shall see her. I will take you—but not now.” He added hastily, “_He_ may be there. Night falls.”

“It is true,” Romola said, rising suddenly. The sky had darkened. “But you will come—when?”

“In the morning,” he said, already dreaming of her swift revenge. “Come to me—where you came today. In the church. I will wait at ten. If you do not come, I will return at mid-day. Can you remember?”

“Mid-day,” he repeated, as if needing to fix it in his mind. “Same place. Mid-day. And after…” He rose, gripping her arm again with his left hand, the knife still held tight in the other. “We will have our revenge. He shall feel the edge of justice. The world is against me—but you will help me.”

“I would help you in other ways,” Romola said, making a small effort to break his illusion. “I see you suffer. You must work hard and earn little. I could bring you comfort—make you feel someone cares.”

“Do not speak of that,” he snapped. “I want nothing but vengeance. One drop of justice before I die. I have only my knife. It is sharp. But there is a moment after the thrust—when men see the face of death. Let it be mine he sees.”

He released her and sank back, exhausted.

Romola felt powerless. There was nothing to do but wait.

“Mid-day, then,” she said clearly.

“Yes,” he replied, voice weak. “Go. I will rest here.”

She hurried away. Turning once, she saw him still seated, a solitary figure against the fading light.
