Mrs. Dashwood’s surprise at seeing Edward was brief—so natural did his arrival seem to her that wonder gave way swiftly to joy. Her warmth and genuine affection overwhelmed any trace of reserve in him; the moment he stepped into the house, his usual shyness began to fade, and by the time he sat down with them, it had vanished entirely under the charm of her kindness. Indeed, a man who loved either of her daughters could scarcely remain indifferent to their mother. Elinor watched with quiet satisfaction as Edward gradually returned to himself—his manner softened, his interest in their lives rekindled. He was attentive, polite, and thoughtful, complimenting their home and its view, yet there lingered a certain heaviness about him. He was not truly at ease.

The family noticed it plainly. Mrs. Dashwood, quick to interpret his mood, attributed it to his mother’s rigid expectations and sighed with indignation at the selfishness of parents who valued status above their children’s happiness. “What are your mother’s plans for you now, Edward?” she asked after dinner, when they gathered around the fire. “Still determined to make you a great orator against your will?”

“No,” he replied, smiling faintly. “I hope she’s finally convinced I have neither the talent nor the desire for public life.”

“But how will your reputation be made?” Mrs. Dashwood pressed. “Your family expects greatness from you, and without fortune, passion for strangers, or confidence, it may prove difficult.”

“I don’t seek distinction,” Edward said firmly. “I have no wish to stand out—and thank Heaven, I can’t be forced into genius or eloquence.”

“You’ve never been ambitious,” Mrs. Dashwood observed. “Your desires are modest.”

“As modest as anyone else’s,” he answered. “I want nothing more than what every person wants—perfect happiness—but only on my own terms. Grandeur won’t bring it.”

“Strange that it wouldn’t!” Marianne exclaimed. “What does wealth or rank have to do with true contentment?”

“Grandeur has little,” Elinor said gently, “but wealth has much.”

“Elinor, for shame!” Marianne protested. “Money can only buy happiness where nothing else offers it. Beyond basic comfort, it brings no real joy—unless one is already lacking in spirit.”

“Perhaps we’re closer in thought than you think,” Elinor said with a smile. “Your ‘competence’ and my ‘wealth’ likely mean much the same. Without it, we’d both agree that comfort would be impossible. Only your ideals are nobler than mine. So tell me—what is your competence?”

“About eighteen hundred or two thousand a year,” Marianne replied. “No more than that.”

Elinor laughed. “Two thousand! One is my wealth—I knew this was coming.”

“And yet two thousand is quite modest,” Marianne insisted. “A family cannot live on less. I’m not extravagant—just reasonable. Proper servants, a carriage, perhaps two, and hunters—none of that can be managed on a smaller income.”

Elinor smiled again, recognizing in her sister’s words an uncanny vision of their future at Combe Magna.

“Hunters?” Edward repeated, puzzled. “But why must you have hunters? Not everyone hunts.”

Marianne flushed slightly. “But most people do.”

Margaret, struck by inspiration, suddenly declared, “I wish someone would give us each a large fortune!”

“Oh, that they would!” Marianne breathed, her eyes bright and her cheeks glowing with imagined delight.

“We’re all agreed on that,” Elinor said dryly, “despite our current lack of means.”

“Oh dear!” Margaret cried. “How happy I’d be! I wonder what I’d do with it?”

Marianne looked as if she already knew.

“I’d be hard-pressed to spend such a sum myself,” Mrs. Dashwood mused, “if my children were all wealthy without my help.”

“You should start by improving this house,” Elinor suggested. “Then your troubles would vanish.”

“What magnificent orders would go to London,” Edward joked, “in such a case! What a day for booksellers, music shops, and print dealers! You, Miss Dashwood, would commission every new print of merit—while Marianne…” He paused, grinning. “I know her soul—there wouldn’t be enough music in London to satisfy her. And books—Thomson, Cowper, Scott—she’d buy them over and over. She’d buy up every copy, I suspect, just to keep them from falling into unworthy hands. And every book that teaches her how to admire a twisted old tree. Would you not, Marianne? Forgive me if I’m too bold—but I wanted to show you I hadn’t forgotten our old arguments.”

“I love hearing of the past,” Marianne said softly. “Whether joyful or sad, I cherish memory. You’ll never offend me by speaking of former days. And yes—you’re right about how I’d spend my money—at least some of it. My loose cash would certainly go toward expanding my music and book collections.”

“And the rest,” Edward teased, “would be spent on annuities for authors and their heirs.”

“No,” Marianne said, “I’d have other uses for it.”

“Perhaps,” he ventured, “you’d award it as a prize to whoever wrote the finest defense of your favorite belief—that no one can fall in love more than once in their lifetime. Your opinion hasn’t changed, I suppose?”

“Undoubtedly,” she replied. “At my age, opinions are fixed. Nothing I see or hear now would alter them.”

“Marianne remains steadfast,” Elinor remarked. “She hasn’t changed.”

“She’s simply grown a little more serious,” Edward added.

“Nay, Edward,” Marianne said, “you needn’t reproach me. You’re not very cheerful yourself.”

“Why should you think so?” he replied, sighing. “But gaiety has never been part of my nature.”

“Nor, I think, of Marianne’s,” Elinor said. “I wouldn’t call her lively—she’s earnest, passionate in everything she does—sometimes speaks much, always with animation—but she’s rarely truly merry.”

“I believe you’re right,” Edward admitted. “Yet I’ve always seen her as lively.”

“I often misjudge people,” Elinor confessed. “I imagine them far more cheerful or solemn, cleverer or duller than they really are. Sometimes I’m misled by what they say about themselves—or what others say. I don’t take time to judge properly.”

“But surely,” Marianne said, “it’s right to trust others’ opinions? I thought our judgment was meant to serve those of our neighbors. That’s always been your teaching, I’m sure.”

“No, Marianne,” Elinor corrected gently. “I’ve never sought to subdue reason. I’ve only ever urged better behavior. I’ve wished you’d treat acquaintances with more attention—but never advised you to adopt their views in matters of consequence.”

“You haven’t succeeded in changing your sister’s ways,” Edward said to Elinor. “Are you making progress?”

“Quite the opposite,” she replied, glancing meaningfully at Marianne.

“My judgment,” Edward said, “is on your side—but my practice leans more toward your sister’s. I never wish to offend, but I’m so awkward, so shy, that I often seem careless when I’m merely hesitant. I’ve often wondered if nature intended me for humble company—I feel so ill at ease among genteel strangers.”

“Marianne doesn’t have shyness to excuse her inattention,” Elinor said.

“She knows her worth,” Edward replied. “Shyness comes from feeling inferior. If I believed my manners were easy and graceful, I wouldn’t be shy.”

“But you’d still be reserved,” Marianne said. “And that’s worse.”

Edward started. “Reserved? Am I reserved, Marianne?”

“Yes, very.”

“I don’t understand,” he said, coloring. “Reserved—how? In what way? What am I supposed to do? What could you possibly mean?”

Elinor looked surprised at his sudden emotion. But trying to lighten the mood, she said, “Don’t you know my sister well enough to understand? She calls anyone reserved who doesn’t talk as fast or admire things as wildly as she does.”

Edward said nothing. His expression grew serious, thoughtful. The weight of his feelings settled back upon him, and he sat silent and distant, lost in his own thoughts.
