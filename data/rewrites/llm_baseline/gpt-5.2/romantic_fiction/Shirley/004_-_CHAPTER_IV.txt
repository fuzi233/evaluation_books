A Yorkshire gentleman he was, in the very marrow and mettle of the word: five-and-fifty by the register, but with hair gone wholly silver, as if the first frosts had taken a fancy to his head and would never leave it. His brow was broad rather than lofty; his cheeks had the wholesome ruddiness of a man used to weather and to his own will; the north showed itself in the set of his mouth and in the downright, rough-edged sound of his voice. There was no courtly curve in his countenance—nothing Norman, nothing softened for drawing-room approval; it was an English face as a crust is English: plain, browned, honest in substance, and likely to cut a delicate palate if you bit too rashly.

Fine folk might have called it vulgar. People of sense would have owned it expressive. Those who liked wit with teeth would have watched it with relish; for pith, sagacity, and a rude originality lay in every furrow, as plainly as the grain lies in seasoned oak. Yet it was an indocile face—scornful and sarcastic—the face of a man you might reason with for an hour and never once move by an inch, and whom to drive were as hopeful as trying to herd a hawk.

His figure matched it: rather tall, wiry, well-knit, and with a kind of grave self-possession in his bearing—no clown’s slouch anywhere. It was not easy to set down Mr. Yorke’s person; it was harder to set down his mind. If any one looks here for a pattern of benevolence, a soft philanthropist with hands for alms and words for everybody, they will be disappointed. He had spoken with some sound sense, even with some honest feeling, to Mr. Moore; but that was no pledge he always spoke justly, or kindly, or even consistently.

There was in him, first, a notable want of reverence—an emptiness where some men carry their kneeling-place. It did not make him an atheist; it made him intolerant. Kings and lords, priests and parliaments, dynasties and establishments—all that high scaffolding of the world—were to him, in the lump, rubbish fit for the midden. He could not taste the keen, clean pleasure other spirits find in admiring what is admirable; that fountain was dry in him, and with it went a hundred little refreshings. He believed in God and heaven; but his belief had neither the tremor of awe nor the hush of tenderness: the great words stood to him as facts stand to a hard, daylight mind, not as mysteries that move the heart.

Next, he had scant faculty for putting himself in another man’s place. He could talk, in broad doctrine, of toleration; then turn and speak with a fierce and unjust antipathy of “parsons,” and all that cleaved to parsons; of “lords,” and their appendages. He did not compare their faults with their temptations, their defects with their disadvantages; he did not imagine what he might have been, wrapped in their circumstances. Thus, while he cried for freedom and equality, there was pride at his core—pride that bowed to no one the world named superior, because he named no one so. Revolt was in his blood; he could no more endure control than a keen fire can endure water: his father had been the same; his grandfather before him; and his children after him, one might foretell, would not learn compliance.

And because his general benevolence was not abundant, he had no soft check on his sarcasm. He was impatient of dulness and weakness; when a thing grated on his strong sense, he struck at it as a man strikes a wasp. Sometimes he would wound, and wound again, not because he delighted in cruelty, but because he scarcely noticed pain when it was not his own.

As for what people call ideality—poetic imagination—he had little of it, and accounted the lack no misfortune. He could relish a good picture, and he had an ear for music that made him tolerant, even encouraging, towards painters and musicians, because their art came to him through the senses. But the quiet poet, however much fire burned under his calm, if he could not have made himself useful in a counting-house, or have shouted bids in a cloth-hall, might have lived and died despised in Hiram Yorke’s neighbourhood. Mr. Yorke did not suspect there are minds whose feast is spread in solitude, and whose bread is baked in ovens no mill-owner ever heated; that some men live, as it were, by a diet of wind and light, and are not starved by it. These were not his reflections. He dealt in what he could handle, and what he could taste.

Yet his faults were not the whole man—no more than the smoke is the whole hearth. In Yorkshire he was accounted one of the most capable and honourable; even those who disliked him were forced into respect. The poor loved him, for he was fatherly to them, and kindness sat on him without condescension. To his workpeople he was cordial and considerate; when he dismissed a man from one employment, he would strive to set him to another, or, failing that, help him remove—wife, children, and scant goods—to a place where bread might be earned. And if among his “hands” any began to show the first green spike of insubordination, Yorke—who hated being ruled, yet knew perfectly how to rule—had a rough husbandry of authority: he crushed rebellion in the germ, as he would trample a weed before it seeded.

Because his own affairs were so ordered, he took a certain fierce license in censuring those differently placed, laying their discomfort wholly to their own mismanagement; severing himself from the masters when it suited him, and speaking, with grim freedom, for the operatives.

His family was old in the district; he was not the richest, but among the most influential. His schooling had been good; in youth—before the French Revolution turned travel to a peril—he had been abroad. He had mastered French and Italian; he had spent two years in Italy and gathered paintings and rare, tasteful things which now gave his house an air of seasoned foreignness, as if sun had once lain long on its walls. When he chose, his manners were those of an old-school gentleman—finished, even courtly; his talk, when he cared to please, was singularly interesting, original, and full of sharp turns. But commonly he spoke the Yorkshire dialect, not from ignorance, but from preference: he clung to his native speech as a man clings to the bread he was raised on—coarse to a stranger, but nourishing, and sweet with memory. A Yorkshire burr, he maintained, was as much better than a cockney lisp as a bull’s bellow was than a rat’s squeak.

He knew everybody and was known for miles; yet his intimacies were few. Being thoroughly original, he had little relish for what was merely polished and ordinary. A racy, rough character—high or low—found welcome with him; a refined, vapid personage, however exalted, was his aversion. He would give an hour freely to a shrewd workman, or to some queer, sagacious old woman among his cottagers—one of those elders who keep a parish’s memory in their mouths, with old phrases salted down like winter meat—when he would grudge a moment to a commonplace fine gentleman or to the most elegant, fashionable lady if she were frivolous. In this he went too far, forgetting that amiable souls may live under smooth surfaces. Still, he made exceptions. There was a sort of plain, artless mind—unrefined, almost without intellectual ambition, incapable of valuing what was intellectual in him, yet not squeamish at his rudeness, not easily wounded by his sarcasm, not given to analyzing him—in whose company he was peculiarly at ease. With such people he was lord. They yielded to his influence without ever naming it, because they never reflected on it; and their easy insensibility was convenient to him—acceptable, in its way, as the chair he sat in or the floor he trod.

It had already been seen he was not quite uncordial with Mr. Moore. He had reasons for a faint partiality which might seem whimsical. First, Moore spoke English with a foreign accent, and French with perfect purity; and his dark, thin face, with its fine but worn lines, looked anti-British, anti-Yorkshire. The points were slight; yet they stirred old associations, like the first whiff of a spice you had not smelled since youth. They carried Yorke back to travel—to Italian streets and Parisian cafés, where voices had rung with that very cadence. He had been young then; and in listening to the alien tone, he seemed, for a moment, to taste youth again.

Secondly, he had known Moore’s father, and had had dealings with him—an old business tie not wholly agreeable, since Yorke’s firm had been in some measure implicated in that firm’s losses.

Thirdly, Robert himself he found sharp in trade. Yorke foresaw he would, by one means or another, make money; and he respected resolution and acuteness—perhaps even hardness. A fourth bond lay in circumstance: Yorke was one of the guardians of the minor on whose estate Hollow’s Mill stood; therefore Moore, in his alterations and improvements, had frequent occasion to consult him.

As to the other guest now present in Mr. Yorke’s parlour—Mr. Helstone—between host and clergyman there ran a double antipathy: of nature and of circumstance. The free-thinker hated the formalist; the lover of liberty detested the disciplinarian. Besides, it was said that in former years they had been rival suitors for the same lady.

Mr. Yorke, when young, had been known for liking sprightly, dashing women—showy carriage, lively wit, a ready tongue. He never offered himself to these brilliant belles whose company he sought; and then, all at once, he fell gravely and earnestly in love with a girl who was their opposite—a girl with the face of a Madonna; living marble; stillness made visible. It mattered not that when he spoke she answered in monosyllables; that his sighs seemed unheard; that his glances were unreturned; that she seldom smiled at his jests, and appeared to pay him no respect and no attention. For him Mary Cave was perfect, because—no doubt for a reason he could have defended with that stubborn logic of his—he loved her.

Mr. Helstone, at that time curate of Briarfield, loved Mary too—or fancied he did. Others admired her, for she was beautiful as a monumental angel; but the clergyman was preferred for his office’ sake—that office lending, perhaps, a consecrated glamour to the plain commission of matrimony, such as the young wool-staplers who courted her could not supply. Mr. Helstone neither had, nor professed to have, Yorke’s absorbing passion. He saw her more as she was than the rest did; therefore he was more master of himself, and in some measure of her. She accepted him at the first offer, and they were married.

Nature never meant Mr. Helstone to make a very good husband—least of all to a quiet wife. So long as a woman was silent, he assumed she was well and wanted nothing. If she did not complain of solitude, solitude, however prolonged, could not be irksome. If she did not talk, did not put herself forward with likes and dislikes, then she had none worth consulting. He made no pretence of comprehending women; to him they were a different order, probably inferior. A wife, in his creed, could not be a companion, much less a confidante, much less a stay. His wife, after a year or two, was of no great importance to him in any shape; and when she one day—as he thought, suddenly, for he had scarcely noticed her decline—took her leave of him and of life, leaving in the conjugal bed only a still, beautiful-featured mould of clay, cold and white, he felt his bereavement—who shall say how little? Yet perhaps more than he showed; for he was not a man whom grief could easily wring into tears.

His dry-eyed, sober mourning scandalized an old housekeeper and a woman who had waited on Mrs. Helstone in her sickness—one who had likely learned more of the dead lady’s capacity to feel and love than her husband ever guessed. They whispered together by the corpse, traded anecdotes, heightened the story of her lingering decline and its cause, until their pity turned to indignation against the austere little man in the adjoining room, sitting over papers, unconscious of the blame gathering like soot in a chimney.

Mrs. Helstone was scarcely under the sod before rumours ran through the neighbourhood that she had died of a broken heart. These swelled quickly into tales of hard usage, and at last into detailed charges of harsh treatment—grossly untrue, yet greedily received. Mr. Yorke heard them and partly believed. He had, of course, no friendly feeling for the rival who had won Mary Cave. Though now himself married, to a woman unlike Mary in every visible respect, he could not forget the great disappointment of his life; and when he heard that what would have been so precious to him had been neglected—perhaps abused—by another, he conceived against that other a rooted, bitter animosity.

Of the nature and strength of this animosity Mr. Helstone was but half aware. He did not know how much Yorke had loved Mary Cave; what he had suffered at losing her; nor was he conscious of the calumnies regarding his married life—familiar to every ear in the district but his own. He believed political and religious differences alone divided him from Mr. Yorke. Had he known the true case, no persuasion would likely have brought him across his former rival’s threshold.

In the parlour that evening, Mr. Yorke did not resume his lecture to Robert Moore. The talk, after a pause, began again in a more general strain, though still disputative. The country was unquiet; the late depredations on mill-property offered fuel enough, and the three men differed in nearly every view they held. Even the room’s quiet—its sheltered firelight, the thick walls that kept out the worst of the wind—could not wholly soften the harsh undertone of the time; through the pauses in speech, one could almost fancy the outer world listening: lanes that carried sound too far at night, distant wheels, the restless stir of a district where men lay awake with empty stomachs and full anger. It was as if the very air had learned a new, uneasy music—no birdsong, no brook’s patient talk, but the clang and grind of industry and want.

Mr. Helstone held the masters aggrieved and the workpeople unreasonable. He condemned, broadly and sternly, the spirit of disaffection against constituted authorities, and the growing indisposition to bear with patience evils he regarded as inevitable. His remedies were those of a man trained to discipline: vigorous government interference, strict magisterial vigilance; when needful, prompt military coercion.

Mr. Yorke demanded to know whether interference, vigilance, and coercion would feed the hungry and give work to those who wanted it and whom no man would hire. He scoffed at “inevitable evils.” Public patience, he said, was a camel whose back had already taken the last atom it could bear, and resistance was now a duty. The widespread disaffection against authorities he counted the most promising sign of the times. The masters, he allowed, were truly aggrieved; but their main grievances had been heaped on them by a “corrupt, base, and bloody” government. Madmen like Pitt, demons like Castlereagh, mischievous idiots like Perceval—these, in his phrase, were the tyrants and curses of the country, destroyers of trade. Their infatuated perseverance in an unjustifiable, hopeless, ruinous war had brought the nation to its pass. Their oppressive taxation, their infamous Orders in Council—whose authors, he swore, deserved impeachment and the scaffold—hung a millstone round England’s neck.

“But where’s t’ use o’ talkin’?” he broke out. “What chance is there o’ reason bein’ heard i’ a land that’s king-ridden, priest-ridden, peer-ridden?—where a lunatic’s t’ nominal monarch, an unprincipled debauchee t’ real ruler; where such an insult to common sense as hereditary legislators is tolerated; where such a humbug as a bench o’ bishops, such an arrogant abuse as a pampered, persecutin’ established church is endured and venerated; where a standin’ army’s maintained, and a host o’ lazy parsons and their pauper families is kept on t’ fat o’ t’ land?”

Mr. Helstone rose, and set on his shovel-hat with a composure that was itself a rebuke. He replied that in the course of his life he had met two or three men who maintained such sentiments bravely so long as health, strength, and worldly prosperity were their allies; but there came a time, he said, to all men “when the keepers of the house should tremble; when they should be afraid of that which is high, and fear should be in the way.” That time was the test of the advocate of anarchy and rebellion, the enemy of religion and order. He affirmed he had been called to read the church’s prayers for the sick at the miserable dying-bed of one of her most rancorous foes; he had seen such a one stricken with remorse, seeking a place for repentance and unable to find it, though he sought it carefully with tears. He warned Mr. Yorke that blasphemy against God and the king was a deadly sin, and that there was such a thing as judgment to come.

Mr. Yorke answered that he believed fully there was such a thing as judgment to come. If it were otherwise, it would be hard to conceive how all the scoundrels triumphant in this world—men who broke innocent hearts with impunity, abused unmerited privilege, were a scandal to honourable callings, took bread out of poor mouths, browbeat the humble, and truckled to the rich and proud—were to be properly paid in the coin they had earned. “But,” he added, “whenever I get low-spirited about such-like goin’s-on, an’ their seemin’ success i’ this mucky lump o’ a planet, I just reach down t’ owd book,”—and he pointed to a great Bible in the case—“open it like at a chance, and I’m sure to light o’ a verse blazin’ wi’ a blue brimstone low that sets all straight. I know where some folk’s bound for, just as weel as if an angel wi’ great white wings had come in ower t’ door-stone an’ told me.”

“Sir,” said Mr. Helstone, gathering all his dignity, “the great knowledge of man is to know himself, and the bourne whither his own steps tend.”

“Ay, ay. You’ll recollect, Mr. Helstone, that Ignorance was carried away from the very gates o’ heaven—borne through t’ air, an’ thrust in at a door i’ t’ side o’ t’ hill which led down to hell.”

“Nor have I forgotten, Mr. Yorke, that Vain-Confidence, not seeing the way before him, fell into a deep pit which was on purpose there made by the prince of the grounds, to catch vainglorious fools withal, and was dashed to pieces with his fall.”

Mr. Moore, who had sat silent through this hard encounter, amused rather than moved—his indifference alike to party politics and to neighbourhood tale making him an impartial, if somewhat cold, judge—leaned forward at last and interposed.

“Now,” said he, “you have both sufficiently blackballed each other, and proved how cordially you detest each other, and how wicked you think each other. For my part, my hate is still running in such a strong current against the fellows who have broken my frames that I have none to spare for my private acquaintance, and still less for such a vague thing as a sect or a government. But really, gentlemen, you both seem very bad by your own showing—worse than ever I suspected you to be. I dare not stay all night with a rebel and blasphemer like you, Yorke; and I hardly dare ride home with a cruel and tyrannical ecclesiastic like Mr. Helstone.”
