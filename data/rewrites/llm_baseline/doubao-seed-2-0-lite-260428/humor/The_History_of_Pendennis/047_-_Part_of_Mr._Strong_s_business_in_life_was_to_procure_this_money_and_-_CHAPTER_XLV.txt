Ever since those very pleasant free feasts at Grosvenor Place and Greenwich we watched Major Pendennis mooch off earlier, the old boy’s apparent fondness for the Clavering family ballooned like a spoiled dessert at their fancy dinner table. He popped by for calls so often the butler nearly mistook him for a permanent boarder, and his little attentions to Lady Clavering were so unrelenting you’d have thought he was running for a seat on her charity committee (spoiler: he was angling for free tickets to every posh party he could sneak into). Being a veteran London socialite who’d weaseled his way into half the fancy drawing-rooms in town, he informed her that of course a lady of her distinction simply *had* to be at the next big ball at Gaunt House, and wouldn’t she and her lovely daughter Blanche care to join him for the famous Watteau quadrille breakfast at Viscount Marrowfat’s Fulham estate? Every single person who mattered would be there—even a royal or two!—and Blanche in a frilly 18th century shepherdess costume would outshine every other girl there by a mile. The obsequious old gent even volunteered to chaperone them to every amusement under the sun, and offered to run any tiny little errand Sir Francis Clavering could possibly dream up.

The problem was, nobody in London seemed all that eager to hang around Sir Francis. The guy got blackballed from two separate clubs in a row, for heaven’s sake! In Parliament, he only ever chatted to the most disreputable backbenchers the place had to offer, with a real gift for seeking out the worst company imaginable as naturally as other people gravitate towards their own relatives. We won’t name *all* his dodgy MP mates, that would be rude, but a handful are too funny not to mention: there was Captain Raff, Epsom’s very own rep, who vanished for a “Levant diplomatic mission” the second he lost every penny he owned at the Goodwood races; there was Hustingson, the super “patriotic” Islington MP who hasn’t yelled about government corruption once since they gave him the cushy governor job of Coventry Island; there was Bob Freeny, from the famous Booterstown Freenys, who’s such a crack shot we’re not daring to say a bad word about him. The party whip Mr. Hotspur, whose job it was to round up every last one of these losers for votes, hated Sir Francis most of all, even though the man was the heir of an ancient family that had sat for their own family borough since the Norman conquest. “If that fool’s needed for a division,” Hotspur would snarl, “ten to one he’s holed up in a gambling hell. He got his education in the Fleet prison, and mark my words, he’ll finish his days on the hulks, blowing the Begum’s entire fortune at thimble-rig.” If even the stuck-up high-born Hotspur was willing to be civil to Clavering for work reasons, why on earth wouldn’t Major Pendennis have his own perfectly logical reasons for sticking around the guy’s house?

“The man’s got a cellar of claret worth drooling over and a cook that could make a saint steal a slice of roast beef,” the Major told his nephew Pen. “He’s quiet as a post most of the time, so he never gets in your way, and he barely ever opens his mouth. If he wants to lose all his money to card-sharps, what’s that to me? Don’t go snooping into every man’s business, Pen, my boy. Every last guy alive has a little locked cupboard in his house he’d die before letting you peek into. Why go digging when the rest of the place is wide open, and it’s a damned good house at that? And if the master of the house isn’t exactly a prize, the women are perfect. The Begum’s not the fanciest speaker alive, but she’s the kindest woman you’ll ever meet, and sharp as a tack too. Little Blanche? You know what I think of her, you rascal. I’m half convinced she’s sweet on you, you could have her for the asking if you bothered. Though you’re getting so big for your boots these days I suppose you’ll only settle for a duke’s daughter, eh? Go on, try and bag one, I dare you.”

Pen, who was still a little tipsy off the heady rush of being a minor literary celebrity around town, had to admit his uncle’s nonstop hints about Blanche had made him wonder if the little old flirtation they’d had by the sleepy Brawl river back in their country days might not be ready to pick up again. But he swore blind he was not getting married any time soon, copying his uncle’s worldly tone to sneer a little at the whole institution.

“You’re perfectly happy rattling around on your own, sir, and so am I,” he said. “If I had a wife trailing after me I’d lose my spot in every fun party in town. I’m not about to retire to a damp country cottage with some boring Mrs. Pendennis, or drag a wife into a tiny London lodging where a single overworked servant waits on you hand and foot. My silly little love illusions are all dead now. You cured me of that first schoolgirl crush of mine, remember? She would have married a total fool, and I’d have turned into a grumpy, resentful husband by Christmas. We young folks these days live fast! I’m only five and twenty and I feel as old as those stuffy old fogies loitering in the bay window at Bays’s club—sorry, didn’t mean to call you a fogey, sir! I just mean I’m completely blasé about all that love nonsense. I couldn’t work up a spark for Miss Amory now any more than I could fall in adoration with Lady Mirabel all over again. Though I do feel a little bad for old Mirabel, his whole obsession with his wife is the only halfway respectable thing he’s ever done in his life.”

“Sir Charles Mirabel was a *theater* man from way back,” the Major huffed, thoroughly offended that his nephew was being flippant about someone of Mirabel’s rank. “He acted at Carlton House when he was a page to the Prince, for heaven’s sake, of course he’s dramatic. And he’s rich enough to marry whoever he likes! Lady Mirabel is received everywhere—everywhere, mark you, the Duchess of Connaught has her over, Lady Rockminster invites her to every dinner. It doesn’t become cheeky young boys to run down people of that station. And those old fellows at Bays’s you’re calling fogies? They’re some of the finest gentlemen in England, you youngsters would do well to learn a little manners and modesty from them instead of strutting around like you invented the wheel.” For the next ten minutes he was fully convinced Pen was turning into an unbearably conceited little upstart who was getting far more praise than he deserved.

Pen thought his old uncle’s huff was hilarious. He never got tired of studying his weird little quirks, and found his worldly old mentor endlessly entertaining. “Oh, I’ve been hanging around grown-ups for fifteen years now, sir,” he said smoothly, smoothing over the old man’s ruffled feathers, “if you think *we* are disrespectful, you should meet the actual kids of today. That young protégé of yours, Master Clavering, came round to my rooms for breakfast the other day, just like you told me to invite him. We did the whole tourist London thing, dined at the club, went to the play. He said our club’s wine was worse than Ellis’s stuff in Richmond, smoked Warrington’s cheap cavendish tobacco right after breakfast, and when I handed him a sovereign as a little goodbye gift, he said he had pockets full of the things but he’d take it just to prove he wasn’t stuck up.”

“Did he now? You invited young Clavering?!” the Major was instantly grinning again, all anger forgotten. “Fine boy, a little wild but he’s got spunk! His parents absolutely adore that sort of attention, you couldn’t do anything better for our friends over at Grosvenor Place. You took him to the play and tipped him? That was excellent, sir, excellent!” And off he went, completely convinced the next generation wasn’t half bad, and that he’d still be able to turn Arthur Pendennis into a proper success story yet.

Young Frank Clavering, meanwhile, had grown big and strong enough that none of his family could control him any more. He didn’t obey his parents, his governess, he ruled the roost, basically. He ignored his dad as much as humanly possible, he screamed and threw full-on punch-ups with his mamma whenever she tried to stop him eating too many tarts or buying a new toy, and he kicked his governess’s shins so hard when she tried to make him do Latin grammar that she surrendered on the spot and never dared yell at him again. He tried the same trick on his half-sister Blanche once or twice, but she boxed his ears so hard his ears rang for a week, and after that he never dared mess with her, or the housemaid, or anyone else in the house who didn’t put up with his nonsense.

Eventually, when the family moved to London, Sir Francis announced the little beggar needed to be sent off to school. So off young Frank went to the Reverend Otto Rose’s fancy academy at Twickenham, the kind of place full of rich little noblemen’s sons being prepped for Eton or Harrow. We’re not going to follow him through his entire school career, because the path to learning was absurdly easy for those little rich brats back then. They got to “approach the temple of learning in a carriage and four,” so to speak, stop for snacks and naps whenever they wanted. Even at that tiny age they wore polished patent boots, the smallest size of lemon-coloured kid gloves Privat the glover ever manufactured, and cambric handkerchiefs. They dressed for dinner every night, had soft shawl dressing gowns, fires in their bedrooms, occasional rides on horses and in carriages, and special oil to make their hair shiny. The Reverend Rose had banned all corporal punishment entirely, swearing moral discipline was all you needed to raise boys right. The lads learned “advanced skills” at an alarming rate, too—like how to sneak their dads’ cigars and smoke them in the stables, and how to drink neat champagne, all before they were old enough to walk through the doors of a public school. Ten year old Frank Clavering could knock back a glass of bubbly as easily as any whiskered dragoon cornet half his dad’s age.

When he came home for holidays, Major Pendennis lavished the exact same over-the-top politeness on him as he did on the rest of the family, even though the little brat nicknamed the old man “Wigsby” behind his back, mimicked his little elaborate bows and smarmy smiles for Blanche and Lady Clavering, and drew rude caricatures exaggerating the size of the Major’s nose, the lopsided angle of his wig, and the ridiculous bow tie he wore. The Major was determined to make Pen spoil the little terror too, badgering Arthur to invite him round to his Temple chambers, treat him to a fancy club dinner, drag him to Madame Tussaud’s, the Tower, the theater, and slip him a little tip at the end of the day. Good-natured Pen agreed to do it one day: had Frank over for breakfast, where the kid sneered at his rickety old furniture, chipped crockery, and Warrington’s threadbare dressing gown, puffed away at a tiny cheap pipe, and told dramatic stories about a huge schoolyard fight between two lads called Tuffy and Long Biggings that entertained Pen and Warrington no end.

Just as the Major predicted, Lady Clavering was so grateful for Arthur’s kindness to her precious boy she could have kissed him. Frank didn’t even notice, he took all the free treats and cash as his birthright, and he probably had more sovereigns stuffed in his pockets than poor cash-strapped Pen did anyway.

Major Pendennis, with his sharp old man’s eyes and decades of experience snooping into other people’s business, had made it his mission to find out every last detail of the Clavering family finances, no matter how nosy it looked. As a former country neighbour who’d known their families for ages, he slowly but surely pried every last secret out of Lady Clavering. Turns out Blanche only got a tiny little slice of her mom’s fortune—almost all of the huge pile of cash the Begum had brought back from India was earmarked to go straight to young Frank. Sir Francis didn’t care about anyone or anything but gambling, Lady Clavering was dotingly obsessed with her little boy, and Blanche absolutely loathed her little half-brother. That was the full, simple picture. “Ah, my dear madam,” the Major would say, patting Frank’s head like he was the sweetest child ever born, “this boy could easily end up wearing a baron’s coronet at the next coronation, if only we all play our cards right and Sir Francis stops messing up.”

Lady Clavering heaved a huge, sad sigh. “Oh Major, I’m afraid that man plays far *too many* cards already, if you catch my meaning.” The Major pretended he already knew all about Sir Francis’s terrible gambling habit, sighed right back in pity, and said so many nice, sympathetic things that Lady Clavering decided he was the only person in the whole world she could confide in. She spilled her whole messy life story to him, tears of shame rolling down her face. She’d been sent back to India at 14 after two years of English school, full of silly romance novels, had married the awful first husband Amory who’d told her endless swashbuckling adventure stories. She’d never even met a man outside of her dad’s clerks and native servants before that, and she’d married him thinking he was a hero. He turned out to be a drunk, a gambler, and a terrible husband, made her life a complete hell. Her own dad wasn’t much better, a cruel miser who’d left her all his money but had never been kind to a soul in his life. “If Amory had died in prison before that stupid trial, not after, I’d never have been stuck with this horrid last name, and I probably never would have married Sir Francis either,” she blubbered. “Everyone thinks I’m so happy, with my diamonds and my carriages and all these fancy parties, but I’m not! My husband’s never around, my daughter Blanche sneers at me for not being posh enough and mispronouncing words like Apollo. I never had a chance to get a proper education! I was raised around native servants till I was twelve! If I’d had a good husband I could’ve been a good, happy woman, I swear.” She wiped her eyes quick, said she had to tidy up before Lady Rockminster arrived for their park drive, and ten minutes later she was the loud, cheerful, funniest lady in the room, not a trace of tears anywhere.

“Begad, she’s not half bad,” the Major thought to himself later, sipping a glass of that good Clavering claret. “Not exactly refined, sure, but she’s got a heart, and three full shares of India Stock to her name, all that money earmarked for that little brat Frank? Wouldn’t it be lovely if a good chunk of that cash found its way to Blanche instead, and even better, if one of those India Stock stars ended up in Arthur Pendennis’s name?”

He kept plotting away, using his old age and status as the family’s trusted friend to corner Blanche alone for a nice little chat one day. He popped in at luncheon so often no one even bothered to stop bickering in front of him half the time—Lady Clavering, loud and hot-tempered, fought with her sharp-witted daughter Blanche constantly, and Blanche’s mean little barbs almost always left her mom storming off red-faced and defeated. “I’m an old retired fellow,” the Major would say, “I’ve got nothing to do all day but mind everyone else’s business. I’ve fixed fights between husbands and wives, fathers and sons, moms and daughters for decades. You two are both wonderful people, I’m going to get you to make up, no fuss.”

One afternoon he walked in right as Lady Clavering stormed out of the drawing room, furious, muttering about that little wicked brat of hers as she ran up the stairs to her bedroom. He found Blanche pouting on the sofa, and immediately launched into his flattery routine. “Ah, my dear Miss Amory, I see what’s happened. You and your mom have had one of your little disagreements. Moms and daughters fight everywhere! I fixed a fight last week between Lady Clapperton and her daughter Lady Claudia, and Lady Lear and her eldest haven’t spoken for fourteen years! They’re both wonderful people, they just can’t stand to be in the same house as each other. The only solution for a brilliant, refined creature like you is your own establishment—you could run a better salon than any woman in London, you’d be the queen of your own circle.”

“I’m certainly not happy in *this* house,” Blanche sighed, flicking her fancy lacy sleeve. “My mother’s stupidity could try the patience of a saint.”

“Exactly! You two were never meant to live together!” the Major agreed. “Your mom made a huge mistake raising you to be the brilliant, intellectual woman you are, then sticking you in a house full of people who can’t even spell ‘Watteau’ half the time. You’re not meant to follow anyone—you’re meant to lead. You’re ambitious, you deserve to shine. You’ll never do that stuck here. I hope one day soon I get to see you as the mistress of your own perfect little home.”

Blanche shrugged her lily-white shoulders, looking perfectly dramatic. “Where’s the prince? Where’s the palace, Major Pendennis?” she said. “I’m ready! But there’s no romance left in the world, no real sincere affection anywhere.”

“Not a drop, sadly,” the Major said, putting on the most sad, sentimental face he could fake.

“Not that I know anything about it, of course,” Blanche said, casting her eyes all demurely down, “I’ve only ever read about real love in novels.”

“Of course you haven’t, dear girl! Novels are all lies, everyone knows that, there’s no romance left at all. Begad, I wish I was a young man like my nephew Arthur.”
