I had long lost all sense of direction, wandering raw and frayed with helpless impatience, when my feet slipped straight into the path of the leopardess, wading through the trickle that dripped from her paw. The flow surged against my ankles with the weight and momentum of a small fast brook, and I stumbled back fast, a cold, unformed dread pricking at the back of my neck: I did not want to linger long enough to wonder whose blood this might be. But I did not turn away. I followed the soft gurgle, wading up the gentle grassy slope the current carved, for that sound would lead me straight to Bulika.

It did not take me long to start questioning what I was seeing. No leopard, no elephant, no prehistoric titan that ever walked my home world could bleed that much. You would need every artery gaping wide, and the creature’s whole living system pumping straight from lakes and forests to replenish the flow as fast as it poured out. This could not be blood. I dipped a finger in, and the shakiness in my chest loosened. It was nothing but water: a softly murmuring rivulet, with no proper channel, spreading thin over the blades of grass. Sweet as its song sounded, I dared not take a sip. I kept moving, straining for the first glimmer of distant light, the familiar trickle a comfort I had not known in months, so far removed from the thick, scalding viscous streams I had navigated before. Even just the dampness on my feet refreshed me so thoroughly I walked on without fatigue, until the dark around me thinned, and I knew the sun was just about to crest the horizon.

Minutes later, against the pearly pale aurora, I spotted them: the spired wall-towers of a city, so weathered they looked older than the first stone ever carved by human hands. I glanced down, searching for the brook, and found it gone. I had noticed its sound fading little by little until I’d stopped paying attention entirely. I looked back: the grass all along its path lay bent flat where the current had pushed through, a few tiny glimmering puddles scattered here and there, but nothing else. No trace of it at all stretched toward the city. The fountain that fed it must have stopped flowing right where I stood.

Around the walls stretched acres of gardens, heavy with strange knobbly vegetables I barely recognized a single one of. No blooms, no birds, no bees, no hint of any living animal at all. The gardens pressed almost to the base of the walls, separated only by massive mounds of gravel and rotting refuse heaped over the battlements and dumped down the sides. I walked to the nearest gate: it hung half-ajar, no locks, no bars, no guards pacing the entrance. Its hinges were so rusted and fused they could not swing an inch further open, nor click shut any tighter. I stepped through, and stared down a long, ancient cobblestoned street. Dead silent. Not a wisp of smoke from the windows, no footfall, no hint that anyone alive resided here. Had I stumbled on a long-abandoned necropolis? I turned right back out, trudged over the dust heaps, and crossed three more roads each leading to a separate gate. I would not re-enter until someone, anyone, was up and moving.

What was I even doing here? What did I hope to find? I held no delusions. I did not crave Bulika’s company—she had woken something sharp and terrible and suspicious in me, and any thought of friendship, let alone anything softer, was absurd, impossible between us. But her presence warped reality around her, pulling at some hidden thread in my bones, and I had to see her one more time. I had to stand in the middle of that strange pull, resist it, and pick it apart. I wanted to crack the riddle of her, the things that made no sense, to peer into a kind of existence no human imagination could ever dream up. But I knew I was being reckless. No man should court temptation just to sate his hunger for knowledge. Worse still: I was the one who had pulled her back from the edge of oblivion. I owed it to the gentle child beings called the Little Ones, whom I had come to protect, to make sure she never harmed them. I already learned she was their enemy. I had come here chasing answers about their fate, and got a glimmer of light, but I needed more. I needed to learn how to keep them safe.

At last, I heard faint movement from within the walls. I walked through the next gate, down a narrow street lined with towering, fog-windowed houses, until I reached a small square, and sat on the base of a broken pillar topped with a hideous carved bat-like gargoyle. Soon, townsfolk started sauntering past. I spoke to the first one, and got a rude glare, a snappish insult, and the man kept walking. I got up, wandering from street to street as the roads slowly filled with loitering idlers. No children anywhere, just as I expected. Near one of the gates, I ran into a knot of young men, brutal, coarse, bearing a terrifying resemblance to the bad giants I had crossed paths with before. They swarmed around me, stared, started shoving, jostling, then hurling pebbles and rotten scraps at me. I took it, did not fight back— I did not want to make enemies when I needed to stay here long enough to find Bulika. I appealed to passersby I thought looked a little kinder, but not one would stop for even a second. I looked ragged, poor, and in Bulika that was a crime as far as the locals were concerned. Poverty, deformity, sickness—all were taxed, and no law their princess had ever passed was more beloved than the ones that forced the poor to serve the wealthy. At last I broke into a run, bolted for the gate, and none of them chased me past the walls. Only one lumbering lout, sitting by the gate gnawing a hunk of bread, lifted a stone to throw after me, but in his stupid eagerness, he flung the bread instead. I snatched it off the ground, and the man was too cowardly to follow past the walls to get it back. All of them turned to cowards the second they stepped outside the city gates.

I trundled a few hundred yards away, flopped down in the long grass, ate the bread, and dropped asleep fast, the warm midday sun seeping into my bones to refill my spent strength. I woke when it was dark. The moon hung high, bright and familiar, like an old friend who recognized me. It was the same moon, I thought, that had watched me through the terrors of my very first night in this uncanny world. A cold wind gusted out through the city gate, carrying a thick, foul stench, but it did not chill me— I was still brimming with the sun’s stored warmth. I slipped back inside the city. The few people still out in the open were huddled in doorways, shivering, trying to escape that cutting wind. I was walking slowly down the long narrow street when something huge, white, bounded across my path in a single flash of moonlight, vanishing before I could make sense of it. I turned down the next alley, narrow enough that I had to squeeze through the gaps between the houses, chasing it, until I emerged into a wider street. There, across the road, half-hidden in shadow, was the creature— I spotted it now, it was the white leopardess— trotting close at the heels of what looked like a man. Every few steps, the man glanced over his shoulder at the beast, but he never spoke, never tried to shoo it away. When he stepped across a patch of full moonlight, I froze. He cast no shadow. He was a shadow. Flat, two-dimensional, yet somehow opaque, so black he blotted out anything that sat behind him completely, made it vanish. In the deep shadow, he was blacker than the dark around him. In the moonlight, he looked like a man who had drawn his own shadow up tight around his skin, so no trace of it lingered at his feet. The leopardess glided right at his heels, so close she looked like a white negative of his blackness, her own dark shadow sliding smooth across the stone at her side. When they stepped together out of the shadow and into the moonlit patch, the man’s shadow-body deepened to pitch black, and the leopardess blazed bright, almost glowing white. I was walking level with them on the opposite side of the street, my bare feet soft on the flat cobblestones. The leopardess never turned her head, never twitched an ear. But the shadow-man twisted his head to look at me— for a split second, I lost his profile, saw nothing but a sharp, thin upright line of him. That instant, the cold wind cut straight through my body. I shuddered from scalp to toe, and my heart bounced hard against the inside of my ribs, like a pebble rattling around inside a child’s toy rattle.
