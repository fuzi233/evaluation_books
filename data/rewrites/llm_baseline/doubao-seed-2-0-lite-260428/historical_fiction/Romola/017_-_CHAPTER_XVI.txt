The pale gold edge of dawn was still gilding the slime-streaked cobbles of the narrow Ferravecchi thoroughfare when Tito stepped out of Bratti’s shop. The Genoese stranger had long since vanished around a corner with Tito’s heirloom onyx ring tucked safe in his pouch, and Tito’s own hand closed around the heavy bulge of fifty florins sewn into the inner lining of his doublet. A faint, fleeting twinge of regret pricked at him: if fortune somehow spared him the need to flee Florence after all, it would look odd to have lost the ring he’d always claimed he wore for the sake of cherished, irreplaceable memories. But he waved the thought away at once. It was a trivial thing, not worth dwelling on, not when his whole world had been tipped on its axis just a night before. The fevered, panicking rush of his first alarm had burned out, leaving nothing but a dull, heavy lassitude in its wake. He cared too deeply for the soft, easy respect and goodwill of his neighbours, and for the pleasures that goodwill bought him, to not now wish he had never gambled his good name by shrinking from the debts other men called sacred.

But deeds are like children born to us, he thought dully as he walked: they live and move apart from our own will. Even a child can be strangled, but a deed cannot. It carries an unkillable life of its own, inside and outside the mind that gave it birth, and that dreadful, unshakable vitality was pressing in on Tito for the very first time.

He was heading back to his lodgings near the Piazza di San Giovanni, but deliberately swerved away from the shortest route through the Mercato Vecchio. He did not want to stumble on Tessa just now. He had no taste for coaxing or small talk, no energy to spare for anything but waiting for the first sign of his shifting, uncertain fate.

The piazza was bathed in that soft, warm autumn morning sunlight that still lingers on leftover dew, that invites lassitude without the drag of tired bones. It was festival day, the Nativity of the Virgin, and the air hummed with the quiet invitation to loiter and stare. Stalls heaped with figs, pomegranates, and armfuls of white lilies were being set out around the octagonal Baptistery. Laden mules stood dozing, munching from their nose bags, their drivers slipped through the heavy sacred doors to kneel before the altar of the Madonna. On the wide, sun-warmed marble steps of the Duomo, groups had scattered: a white-haired old crone held a round-capped toddler up to test his tiny bare feet on the warm stone, while a scruffy street dog sat nearby snuffling at the pair suspiciously; two shaggy-headed boys leaned in to watch a pale, small cripple carving a crude little face into a cherry stone; and above them on the broad raised platform, knots of laughing men broke apart and reformed in desultory chat, or stood huddled in pairs gesturing fiercely over some point of local news.

But the largest, busiest crowd of loungers stood right in Tito’s path, clustered around Nello the barber’s shop. It was the peak hour for his trade, and in the soft warm weather, most men chose to be shaved out under his bright striped red-and-white awning, rather than crammed inside his narrow little shop walls. No man looks particularly dignified sitting lathered with his chin tilted back, having his nose tweaked like a handle, but a close shave was the mark of respectable Florentine manhood, and all the men gathered there carried themselves with perfect, unselfconscious gravity. It was also the hour when yesterday’s gossip was still at its freshest, and Nello’s tongue moved as fast and deftly as his razor, the two seemingly powered by the same hidden spring. Tito knew he could not slip past the crowd without being pulled in. He would have to smile, banter, play the part of the carefree, brilliant young Greek scholar to perfection. It was no worse, he told himself, than swallowing a bread-and-cheese pill. Only a weak man would let the mere fear of exposure choke him before the danger even arrived.

Then a hand closed firm on his shoulder. No amount of prior resolve could stop the sharp, jolting shock that shot through him. His face twisted before he could smooth his features, and the man who had touched him burst into a light, warm laugh. He was a young man near Tito’s own age, with sharp, keen features, a close-clipped head of hair, and a clean-shaven jaw that gave the impression of a mind as uncluttered by unnecessary softness as it was possible for a man to have. His eyes glowed with bright, hopeful friendliness, the same light that would later go out in the bitterness and disappointment that marked his later life; everyone in Florence at that moment still predicted great things for Niccolò Machiavelli, the promising young secretary who was expected to mend his ancient, once-great family’s broken fortunes.

“Why, Melema!” he said, grinning. “What evil nightmare did you have last night, that you took my friendly hand on your shoulder for the grip of a sbirro’s arm?”

“Ah, Messer Niccolò,” Tito said, laughing as he recovered his poise. “It must be that there’s a double measure of slowness in my veins this morning, that I flinched from the sharp edge of your wit. The truth is, I slept very badly.”

“That’s terrible luck,” Machiavelli replied. “You’re supposed to shine today in the Rucellai Gardens, no fog allowed to dim your light. I assume you’re coming?”

“Messer Bernardo was kind enough to invite me,” Tito said, “but I have another engagement I cannot get out of.”

“Ah, of course,” Machiavelli said, shrugging. “You’re in love. No other reason would give a man such inconveniently immovable plans. We’re supposed to feast on peacock and ortolans under the loggia, surrounded by Bernardo Rucellai’s rare fruit trees, with all the cleverest men in Florence and the best vintages the cellars can offer. Though since Piero de’ Medici will be there, all our fine wits are likely to be drowned out by the stupid game of capping impromptu verses. I hate that nonsense. It’s nothing but a trick to let small wits triumph, since they always get most inspired by the pettiest little occasions.”

“What’s that you’re saying about Piero de’ Medici and small wits, Messer Niccolò?” Nello called, bounding over to them as he skipped around the giant form of Niccolò Caparra. The famous master ironsmith, who Tito had last seen bared-armed in his leather apron at the Mercato Vecchio, was dressed in his holiday best that morning, sitting perfectly still and docile while Nello lathered his chin, tweaked his nose, and scraped his skin clean at impossible speed. He looked for all the world like a lion that had donned linen tunic and fine cuffs, preparing to make its first appearance in polite society.

“Any private secretary will never get ahead if he mixes great and small like that,” Nello continued, wiping the lather off his razor on a cloth. “When great men aren’t allowed to arrange their children’s marriages as they please, small men don’t get to arrange their words as they please. Did you hear the news Domenico Cennini just told us? Pagolantonio Soderini boxed Ser Piero da Bibbiena on the ear for goading Piero de’ Medici into interfering with young Tommaso Soderini’s marriage to Fiammetta Strozzi. And as punishment, they’re sending him off as ambassador to Venice.”

“I don’t know which I envy him more,” Machiavelli said, smiling. “The crime, or the punishment. The punch will make him the most popular man in all Florence, and the punishment gets him sent to the only city in all Italy that’s ever known how to run its own affairs.”

“Aye, and if Soderini stays in Venice long enough,” Cennini put in from the shaving bench, “he might pick up their ways, and bring them back home. The Soderini have always been loyal friends to the Medici, but this little incident will open Pagolantonio’s eyes to the value of that old Florentine trick of slipping into new harness when the old one starts chaffing. We haven’t forgotten that trick in the last fifty years, not entirely.”

“Of course we haven’t,” Caparra rumbled, now free to move his lips again. “Eat eggs in Lent, and the snow will melt. That’s what I tell the lads when they’re drunk and noisy at San Gallo, talking about starting a romor, an insurrection. I tell them, don’t ever plan a romor. That’s as stupid as trying to fill the Arno with buckets. When the water’s high enough, the Arno will fill itself, and that won’t happen until the torrent’s already ready.”

“Caparra, that oracular speech of yours is entirely my doing,” Nello cackled. “You never could have come up with that wisdom with all that dark rust of a beard on your chin. Ecco, Messer Domenico, I’m ready for you now. By the way, my learned Tito,” he called, spotting Tito edging towards the edge of the crowd, “old Maso was looking all over for you earlier. Your lodgings were empty. He looked sad and in a great hurry. I hope nothing’s gone wrong at the house in the Via de’ Bardi.”

“Of course Messer Tito hasn’t heard the news,” Cronaca said, pushing through the crowd just then. “Bardo’s son Dino is dead.”

Tito’s heart gave a violent leap. Had Dino died before Romola reached him?

“No, I had not heard,” he said, his face no more disturbed than was natural for a man hearing sad news about his betrothed’s brother. He turned and leaned against the doorpost, pretending he’d abandoned his plan to leave. “I knew his sister had gone to see him. Did he die before she arrived?”

“No,” Cronaca said. “I was at San Marco at the time. I saw her come out of the chapter house with Fra Girolamo, who told us the dying man’s breath had been preserved almost by miracle, just long enough for him to make a full disclosure to his sister.”

Tito’s fate was sealed. His mind raced back to every moment of his flight from Florence years before, and he hatched a new plan: he would get his money back from Cennini before that disclosure spread to the whole city. Once he had his gold in hand, he would not have to stay here enduring scorching looks and sharp, biting words for long. He stood perfectly still, hands tucked into his belt, eyes fixed absently on the cobblestones at his feet. Nello glanced over at him, and assumed the young scholar was overcome with grief and anxiety for Romola, looking like the very picture of unselfish sadness. He deliberately waggled his razor at Piero di Cosimo, the painter, who he had never forgiven for refusing to see any hint of flaw or hidden motive in Tito’s handsome, amiable face. Piero, leaning against the other doorpost, just shrugged. Nello’s repeated little challenges had long since worn down the painter’s neutrality, and now he found himself actively inclined to suspect the much-praised Greek of hiding some ill secret.

“So you’ve got your Fra Girolamo back, Cronaca?” Nello said, changing the subject. “I suppose he’ll be preaching again this coming Advent.”

“And not a moment too soon,” Cronaca said gravely. “We’ve had the best possible proof of his words since last Lent. Even the wicked now find wickedness a plague. The ripeness of sin has begun to rot, and even sinners can smell it. There’s not been a single change in Rome or Florence since last Lent that hasn’t put a new seal on his words: that the harvest of sin is fully ripe, and God will reap it with a sword.”

“I hope he’s had some new visions, then,” Francesco Cei sneered. “The old ones are getting a bit stale. Can’t your Fra Girolamo hire a poet to liven up his imagination for him?”

“He has no shortage of great poets around him,” Cronaca replied, quiet and unruffled, “but they are poets of real merit, not petty little rhymers. They are content to learn from him. They no more think the truth he speaks stale than they think the light of the moon stale. But perhaps those high prelates and princes who hate his denunciations will be pleased to hear that though Pico della Mirandola, Poliziano, Marsilio Ficino, and every other man of mark in Florence revere Fra Girolamo, Messer Francesco Cei despises him.”

“Poliziano?” Cei laughed, sharp and scornful. “Of course he believes in your new Jonah. Look at the fine orations he wrote for the Sienese envoys, telling Alexander VI that the world and the Church were never better off than since he took the papal throne!”

“Now, Francesco,” Machiavelli said, smiling. “A scholar of such wide reading must have room for many differing opinions. And as for Fra Girolamo, whatever we think of his sainthood, you judge his preaching too narrowly. The secret of great oratory is not saying new things. It is saying old things with a power that moves your audience. Without that, as old Filelfo once said, a man deserves not to be called an orator, but an arator, a ploughman. By that test, Fra Girolamo is a great orator.”

“That’s true, Niccolò,” Cennini said, from the shaving chair. “But part of that secret lies in his prophecies. Our people—no offence, Cronaca—will chase after any prophet, especially if he predicts terrors and disasters.”

“Say rather, Cennini,” Cronaca corrected him, “that the real secret lies in his pure life and unshakable faith, which mark him out as a true messenger of God.”

“I concede it, I concede it,” Cennini said, holding up his palms as he rose from the chair. “His life is spotless. No man has ever dared accuse him of a single vice.”

“He just gets his pleasure from the sweet sin of arrogance,” Cei burst out bitterly. “I can see it in that proud lip of his, that satisfied glint in his eye. He hears the whole city humming his name: Fra Girolamo Savonarola of Ferrara, the prophet, the saint, the mighty preacher, who scares Florentine children so bad they throw away their little wicked toys.”

“Come now, Francesco,” Nello said, stepping forward to smooth things over. “You’re just grumpy waiting for your shave. Let me stuff your mouth full of lather, that’ll shut you up. I won’t have my friend Cronaca getting angry: I’m very fond of his chin, and it hasn’t changed an inch since he turned Piagnone. For my part, I admit I got into the habit of slipping into the Duomo to listen to him last Advent. I might even have turned Piagnone myself, if two things hadn’t stopped me: first, the free, broad nature of my trade, and second, the length of those sermons, which drag on forever before they get to the moving part. But as Messer Niccolò says, the Frate holds people with a power far beyond his visions. Monks and nuns who claim to prophesy are not so rare. What did Luigi Pulci say? Dombruno’s famous scimitar was said to be enchanted, but Luigi said he rather thought it cut so sharp because it was made of perfectly tempered steel. Aye, paternosters might make you feel clean, but you still need a good sharp razor to shave properly.”

“Look, Nello!” Machiavelli laughed, pointing down the street. “What doctor is that riding up on his Bucephalus? I thought your piazza was clear of those furred, scarlet-robed lackeys of death. This man looks like he had the same night adventure as Boccaccio’s Maestro Simone, and got his bonnet and mantle pickled a little in the gutter. Though he himself is as sleek as a miller’s rat.”

“Ahhh,” Nello drawled, looking up at the approaching figure. It was a round, fat little man with a round head, perched on a half-grown, skittish raw horse that held its nose out stubbornly, constantly trying to back up and bolt off at a diagonal, with very advanced ideas about obeying authority that were far ahead of the century.

“I’ve got a few little pranks all ready for him,” Nello whispered to Machiavelli, “that’ll chase his prying nose right out of our part of the city. He’s a quack from Padua. They say he’s been in Prato for three months, and now he’s come to Florence to see what he can net. His favourite trick is roaming the countryside visiting the contadini. See those huge saddle bags he’s got slung over his horse? They’re for all the fat capons, eggs, and meal he extorts from silly country folk who don’t have much coin to pay for his cures. He sells his own secret medicines, so he never goes near the apothecaries, and for the last week he’s been turning up here in my piazza for two or three hours every day, drawing crowds of asthmatics and squalling sick babies to my shop steps. It makes my blood boil to see that toad-faced fraud counting out his greasy quattrini, or taking a fat pigeon in exchange for his worthless pills. But I’ll stick a few thorns in his saddle, or I’m no true Florentine. Laudamus! He’s coming over to get shaved, just like I was hoping. Messer Domenico, don’t leave yet. Stay, you’ll see a rare piece of fooling I dreamed up two days ago. Here, Sandro!”

Nello leaned in and whispered in his assistant Sandro’s ear. Sandro rolled his solemn eyes, nodded, broke into a slow, deliberate smile, and then took off running at a surprising speed.

“How are you today, Maestro Tacco?” Nello called, as the doctor finally wrestled his skittish horse around towards the barber’s shop. “That’s a fine young horse you’ve got there, but he’s a little raw in the mouth, isn’t he?”
