The first thing I did, once we were left alone, was to attempt rising from my seat upon the shifting sands. Mr. Franklin halted me.

"There is a silver lining to this wretched spot," he observed, "since it ensures privacy. Remain seated, Betteredge; I must speak with you."

As he spoke, I scrutinized him, searching for the ghost of the boy I once knew within the man standing before me. The resemblance was jarring. Where I expected rosy cheeks and a trim, youthful jacket, I found a pallor and a face obscured by a thick, curly brown beard and moustache. While he retained a certain lively, engaging rhythm to his movements, his former free-and-easy charm had vanished. Worse still, he had failed to live up to his boast of towering stature; he was neat and slim, yet fell far short of middle height. The years had stripped away nearly everything of his former self, save for those bright, straightforward eyes. It was there, in that gaze, that I finally located the familiar spirit of the boy, and decided to rest my inquiry there.

"Welcome back to the old haunts, Mr. Franklin," I said. "Especially so, since you arrived several hours ahead of schedule."

"I have reasons for my early arrival," Mr. Franklin replied. "I strongly suspect I have been followed and surveilled in London for the past few days. Consequently, I traveled on the morning train rather than the afternoon, hoping to evade a particular dark-complexioned stranger."

His words struck me with force, instantly summoning to my mind the trio of jugglers and Penelope's suspicion that they harbored malicious intent toward Mr. Franklin.

"Who is watching you, sir, and why?" I asked.

"First, tell me about the three 'Indians' who visited the house today," Mr. Franklin interrupted, ignoring my query. "It is conceivable, Betteredge, that your jugglers and this shadowy pursuer are pieces of the same puzzle."

"How do you know of the jugglers, sir?" I pressed, stacking question upon question—a breach of etiquette, I grant you—but human nature is rarely patient with protocol.

"I saw Penelope at the residence," Mr. Franklin explained. "She informed me. Your daughter pledged to remain a beauty, Betteredge, and she honored that vow. Penelope possesses a delicate ear and a petite foot. Did the late Mrs. Betteredge enjoy such charms?"

"The deceased Mrs. Betteredge possessed numerous flaws, sir," I retorted. "One, if you'll forgive the observation, was an inability to focus. She resembled a gnat more than a woman; she could not settle on a single subject."

"She would have suited me perfectly," Mr. Franklin admitted. "Nor do I have the habit of settling. Betteredge, your wit remains sharper than ever. Your daughter noted as much when I requested details regarding the jugglers: *'Father is a marvel for his age, and speaks with great eloquence.'* Penelope's own words, delivered with divine blushes. My respect for you did not prevent me from... never mind. I knew her as a child, and she is no worse for the wear. Let us be serious. What exactly did these jugglers do?"

A flicker of dissatisfaction crossed my heart—not for allowing Mr. Franklin to kiss Penelope, a courtesy I welcomed—but for being forced to recount her childish tale through a third party. Nevertheless, there was no recourse but to reveal the facts. As I spoke, Mr. Franklin's mirth evaporated. He knitted his brows and twisted his beard, his expression growing grave. When I finished, he recited two questions posed by the chief juggler to the boy, seemingly committing them to memory.

"'Is the journey to this house exclusive?' 'Does the English gentleman carry It with him?'" Mr. Franklin murmured, withdrawing a small, sealed parcel from his pocket. "I suspect 'It' refers to *this*. And 'this,' Betteredge, denotes my Uncle Herncastle's renowned Diamond."

"Good heavens, sir!" I exclaimed. "How do you find yourself in possession of the Wicked Colonel's Diamond?"

"My father's will designated the Diamond as a birthday gift for my cousin, Rachel," Mr. Franklin stated calmly. "Furthermore, as the Colonel's executor, my father entrusted me with the duty of transporting it here."

"If the ocean itself had drained away beneath the Shivering Sand, revealing dry earth before me, I doubt I could have been more astounded," I thought, though outwardly I feigned composure. "The Colonel's Diamond left to Miss Rachel!" I stammered aloud. "And your father, sir, the Colonel's executor! By all rights, I'd wager a fortune that your father would not have so much as touched the Colonel with tongs!"

"Strong words, Betteredge!" Mr. Franklin cried. "What grievance existed between the Colonel and your master? He belonged to your era, not mine. Inform me of what you know, and I shall explain how my father became his executor, along with other matters. I have made disturbing discoveries in London regarding Uncle Herncastle and his gem; they bear an ominous appearance. You just called him the 'Wicked Colonel.' Scrutinize your memories, old friend, and tell me why."

Seeing his seriousness, I complied.

Here, then, is the substance of my account, transcribed solely for your edification. Attend closely, lest you become lost when the narrative deepens. Dismiss thoughts of the children, the dinner, the new bonnets, or whatever trivialities occupy the mind. Attempt to banish politics, horse racing, City prices, and club grievances. I trust you will not resent this liberty; it is merely a method I employ to engage the gentle reader. Have I not often seen you engrossed in the works of the greatest authors? Am I not aware of how easily attention drifts when reading a tome that demands it, rather than conversing with a person?

I began by describing my lady's father, the ancient Lord with the short temper and the long tongue. He sired five children. Two sons preceded the daughters, born at intervals dictated by nature; the three young ladies followed swiftly thereafter, my mistress being the youngest and the favorite. Of the two sons, the elder, Arthur, inherited the title and estates. The younger, the Honorable John, received a substantial fortune from a relative and entered the army.

They say an ill bird fouls its own nest. Consider the noble line of the Herncastles as my nest, and I shall consider it a kindness if I am spared the necessity of detailing the Honorable John's conduct. I believe him to be one of the greatest scoundrels of his generation. He joined the Guards, but could not endure them for long—he was dismissed before reaching twenty-two. The army was too rigid for the Honorable John. He departed for India to test whether discipline held sway there as well, seeking active service. Regarding bravery, he was a blend of bulldog tenacity and game-cock spirit, laced with savagery. He fought at Seringapatam. Shortly thereafter, he transferred regiments twice, eventually attaining the rank of Lieutenant-Colonel. This promotion coincided with a severe sunstroke, leading to his return to England.

He returned with a reputation that barred his entry from every family circle. My lady, then newly wed, took the lead, declaring—with Sir John's tacit approval—that her brother was henceforth forbidden from crossing her threshold. Beyond various slurs that made acquaintances shy of him, the stain of the Diamond was sufficient evidence to require no further elaboration.

Rumors swirled that he had acquired his Indian jewel through illicit means, boldnesses he dared not confess. He never attempted to sell it, nor did he lack funds; indeed, he never sought profit. He gave it to no one, nor showed it to a living soul. Some whispered fear of military censure; others, ignorant of his true nature, claimed he feared losing his life if revealed. Perhaps a grain of truth lingered in the latter tale. It was untrue that he lived in fear, yet it was factual that his life had been twice threatened in India, and the Moonstone was widely suspected as the cause. Upon returning to England, ostracized by all, the Moonstone was blamed anew. The mystery of his life obstructed his path, effectively outlawing him among his own kin. Clubs refused him entry; women whom he courted rejected him; friends and relatives, blinded by prejudice, could not recognize him in the streets.

Most men in such a plight would seek redemption. Yet, to submit, even when unjustly maligned and opposed by all society, was not the way of the Honorable John. Having kept the Diamond in open defiance of assassination threats in India, he maintained it in defiance of public opinion in England. Thus stands his portrait: a character who braved everything, possessing a handsome visage that seemed haunted by the devil.

Gossip varied wildly regarding his habits. At times, he was rumored to smoke opium and collect antiquarian books; at others, experimenting with dangerous chemicals; occasionally, he was seen carousing amidst the lowest dregs of London's slums. Regardless, his existence was a solitary, vicious, subterranean life. On one singular occasion, roughly two years prior to the current date, and about eighteen months before his demise, I encountered him face-to-face.
