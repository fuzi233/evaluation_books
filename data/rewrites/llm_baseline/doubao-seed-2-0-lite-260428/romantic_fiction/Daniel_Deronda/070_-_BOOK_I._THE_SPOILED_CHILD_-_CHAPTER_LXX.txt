The seasons of human experience blur soft as they did in the oldest golden tales: fruit swells heavy on boughs still thick with blossom, the reaper swings his sickle even as another scatters seed, one tends unripe green clusters while another presses grapes into wine. All our lives, harvest and spring twine together, until that great unseen hand gathers us up to sow us new in fields no eye can trace. There is no sweeter gift in love than the quiet, glowing certainty that by weaving the one you love into your life, you can shield their joy, stitch soft comfort over old hardship, and pry open fountains of bright sweet joy over memories of hunger and grief.
Daniel Deronda’s love for Mirah hummed with that exact blessed protectiveness. Even as a small child, her feet had stumbled through brambles, and the very first time he saw her face, it had worn the raw, hollowed look of a girl who had all but given up on hope. Now she bloomed like a delicate flower with dark petal tips and soft ivory-hued skin, warmed through by the sunlight of quiet content. Any grief she could imagine no longer felt like something to fear—it was just a thread in the life she shared with Deronda, a life she could name nothing but entirely good. He watched her slow, happy movements, her soft still poses of rest, with a delight so deep he thought to himself that this was more than enough personal joy for him: to keep her safe from all pain.
Mirah knew nothing of Hans’s quiet, bitter struggle with unrequited love, nothing of the sharp lingering heartache Gwendolen carried for Deronda. Once she learned the secret love Deronda had hidden all this time was for her, she easily wrote off Gwendolen’s old frantic concern for him as the same grateful dependence she had felt, when he had pulled her back from the edge of despair. All Deronda’s few, distant references to Mrs. Grandcourt only reinforced that gentle view of their bond, and Mirah was fully ready to believe he had acted as a rescuing angel for dozens of lost souls just like her. The only miracle left, as far as she was concerned, was that out of all those people, she got the gift of being at his side for every day of the rest of their lives.
So when the bridal veil draped over Mirah’s head, it hid no shaky, doubtful tremors—only a soft, awed thrill, like she was holding a great sacred gift she had been tasked to use well. No velvet wedding canopy ever sheltered a more fair, kind-hearted bride and groom, or one their gathered loved ones could have wished more deeply full, beautiful lives for. No truer lips ever touched the sweet ceremonial marriage wine, no wedding blessing ever landed on a couple more certain to keep every word of their pledge, bound together by perfect, unbroken integrity.
Naturally, they were married under the traditional Jewish rite. No faith has ever demanded that wedding feasts only invite the highest, most noble acquaintances, and so no one should take offense that Deronda’s small, warm wedding feast counted the entire Cohen family among its guests—save for the youngest teething baby, who was fussed over at home by a neighbor. Mordecai would never have allowed the friends who had stood by him through his years of poverty to be barred from sharing his joy. Mrs. Meyrick had long since set aside her old quiet misgivings about the Jewish pawnbroker and his household, and arrived with her three daughters, all of them glowing with the warm knowledge that Mirah’s marriage to Deronda capped a romance they had all gotten to be a gentle, generous part of, a memory they would hold dear for the rest of their lives. Not one of the Meyricks, mother or daughters, had held back from giving Mirah their care and love when she needed it most. They all agreed it was a shame Hans could not be there, but Mab joked that men had no monopoly on silly, unruly hearts—if all four of the Meyrick women had fallen for Deronda, they would never have been so ridiculous as to ruin Mirah’s happy day.
Their willingness to set aside silly prejudice was rewarded when Mr. Cohen gave a speech unlike any standard, stuffy wedding toast, full of rough warm heart. Jacob Cohen ate enough cake for two boys his age, and let out a series of soft, proud snickers through his father’s speech, giddy with the knowledge his family was the center of a little bit of glory, while little Adelaide Rebekah, dressed in a spotless new Sabbath frock, sat through the whole feast with a grave, responsible air, as if she had been tasked to oversee every small part of the celebration. Mordecai’s brilliant eyes, sunken deep in their sockets, drifted over the whole scene with the gentle cherishing warmth of a spirit already half-lifted beyond the mortal world, freed from all selfish small needs but still clinging to every bit of soft, living sympathy. Again and again, his gaze would circle the room before settling back on Deronda, bright with a fresh flicker of that deep, trusting affection he had nurtured for years.
The feast was humble, but Mirah received no shortage of stunning, thoughtful wedding gifts. As soon as their betrothal had been announced, friends had rallied to find thoughtful, beautiful tokens of their joy. Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger had put great care into putting together a full, perfect set of supplies for their planned travels East, plus a precious carved locket inscribed: *To the bride of our dear Daniel Deronda, all blessings. H. and L. M.* The Klesmers sent a dainty, perfectly crafted engraved watch. But the gift far more precious than gold or jewels arrived for Deronda on the very morning of his wedding, sent from a home near Diplow. It was a short letter, in Gwendolen’s familiar slanted handwriting:
Do not grieve for me on your wedding day. I have remembered your words— that I may live long enough to become one of those good women who make everyone around them glad they were born. I do not see yet how that can come to pass, but you know better than I. If it ever does, it will be all because you helped me. I was selfish once, I only thought of my own pain, and I made you grieve. The thought of your grief still hurts me now. You do not need to grieve for me ever again. It will be better, it shall be better for me, because I knew you.
Preparations for all three of them to depart for the East began immediately. Deronda could not deny Ezra’s wish to set sail right away, so he could travel with them, rather than make them delay their journey to stay and watch over him. He held no illusions that Ezra’s weak body would survive the long voyage; the clear signs of his illness’s final stage had already set in. But Ezra had only smiled and said, “It does not matter where I die, so long as I am with you.”
He never made it to the ship. One early morning, he roused Deronda and Mirah and said softly, “Do not leave my side today. I will be gone before the sun sets.” He insisted they dress him and settle him in his favorite easy chair, Deronda on one side, Mirah on the other. For hours he was unusually quiet, not even making the effort to speak, only glancing at them every now and then with eyes full of a soft, restful meaning, as if to assure them that even when his breath came hard, he floated on an ocean of deep unshakable peace beneath him.
It was not until late afternoon, when the golden light was fading low through the windows, that he took one of each of their hands in his own, looked straight at Deronda, and said, “Death is coming to me like a divine kiss—one that is both a parting and a reunion, that takes me from your sight and lets me live fully in your soul. Wherever you go, Daniel, I shall go. Has it not already begun? Have I not breathed my whole soul into you? We shall live together, always.”
He paused, and Deronda waited, thinking there might be one last gentle word for him. Slow, with great effort, Ezra pressed their hands tight, raised himself a little in his chair, and spoke the ancient Hebrew confession of the Divine Unity that has rested on the lips of dying Israelites for hundreds of generations. He sank gently back into his chair, and never spoke again. It was several more hours before his breath stopped, all the while wrapped safe in the circle of Mirah and Deronda’s arms. There was nothing there for tears, nothing to wail or strike the breast over—no weakness, no blame, no dispraise. Only good, only fair, and all the quiet soft peace that fits such a noble, gentle death.
