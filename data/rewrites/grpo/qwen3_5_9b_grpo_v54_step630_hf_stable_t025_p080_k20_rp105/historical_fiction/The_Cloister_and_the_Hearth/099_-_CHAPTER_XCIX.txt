Jerome remained as stern and gloomy as his nature allowed, yet a thaw had begun in his heart toward Gerard. Throughout his time in England, he had missed his brother more than he realized, wondering constantly of his whereabouts. News from Gouda had only elevated his opinion of the younger monk, particularly the report that Gerard had stood firm against the Pope and the Minorites regarding "the infernal heresy of the immaculate conception." However, when a novice recounted the cause of Gerard's illness with tears streaming down his face, Jerome's disdain returned instantly. "Dying for a woman?" he muttered.

Driven to prevent such a scandal, Jerome visited Clement twice daily in his cell, deploying all his former influence and eloquence to urge the dying man to abandon this unspiritual despondency. He pleaded that the Church could not afford to lose Gerard's piety and gift of speech at such a critical juncture.

Gerard listened, acknowledging the logic, admiring Jerome's fortitude, and confessing his own frailty. Yet, he continued to fade toward the land of the faithful. One day, Jerome admitted he had heard Gerard's story and listened with pride. "But now," Jerome argued, "you ruin it all, Clement. This is the triumph of earthly passion. It would have been better to yield and repent while she lived, rather than resist her while she drew breath and succumb to her now, body and soul alike."

"Dear Jerome," Clement replied, his voice so sweet it stripped the rebuke of its sting, "I believe you misunderstand me. There is no passion here, only a profound affection for which I shall not blush in this place, nor in the next. Consider, Jerome: does the poor dog who dies of grief upon his master's grave commit a sin of passion? Neither do I. Passion would have saved my life but cost me my soul. She was my guardian angel; she upheld me in my duties and charity. Her face encouraged me in the pulpit; her lips soothed me under ingratitude. She became woven into every good thing in my life, and after leaning on her so long, I could not stand alone. Believe me, I am no rebel against Heaven. It is God's will to release me. When they threw the earth upon her poor coffin, something snapped within my chest that cannot be mended. I heard it, and I felt it. From that hour, Jerome, no food I ate had any flavor. With my eyes bandaged, I could no longer distinguish bread from flesh by taste."

"Holy saints!" Jerome gasped.

"And from that same moment, my deep depression vanished, and I smiled again. I often smile—why? I read it thus: He who holds the issues of life and death gave me that minute the great summons. Some cord of life snapped within me. He is merciful. I would have lived unhappy, but He said, 'No; enough is done, enough is suffered. Poor, feeble, loving servant, your shortcomings are forgiven, your sorrows have reached their end; come to your rest.' I come, Lord, I come!"

Jerome groaned. "The Church has always had her holy but feeble servants," he lamented. "Now I would give ten years of my life to save yours. But I see it cannot be. Die in peace."

Thus, within a few days, Gerard lay dying in a state of such holy happiness that more than one aged saint seemed present to gather his final words. In the evening, he had seen Giles, begging him not to let poor Jack starve, to ensure the trustees of little Gerard fulfilled their duties, to kiss his parents for him, and to send Denys to his friends in Burgundy, noting that the boy would feel strange without his comrade. Afterward, he had a private audience with Jerome alone. What transpired between them remains unclear, but it must have been extraordinary, for Jerome left the door with his hands crossed over his breast, his head bowed low, sighing heavily as he walked away.

The two monks who watched him until matins reported that throughout the night, Gerard broke into pious ejaculations, praises, and thanksgivings. Once, they said he wandered, believing he saw her walking in green meadows with other spirits clad in white, beckoning him; and they all smiled and beckoned back. Both monks claimed—though perhaps it was mere fancy—that just before dawn, three light taps struck the wall, one after another, very slowly. The dying man heard them and said, "I come, love, I come."

It is certain that Gerard uttered these words and prepared for his departure. He summoned all the monks keeping vigil. They gathered around him like gentle spirits, speaking holy words. Some prayed silently with faces touching the ground; others tenderly supported his head. When one mentioned his life of self-denial and charity, Gerard stopped him. Addressing them all, he declared, "My dear brethren, take note: he who dies so happy here holds not these new-fangled doctrines of man's merit. Oh, what a miserable hour it would have been for me if I did! Nay, I hold, with the Apostles and their pupils in the Church, the ancient fathers, that we are justified not by our own wisdom, or piety, or the works we have done in holiness of heart, but by faith."

Silence fell, and the monks exchanged significant glances.

"Please you sweep the floor," the dying Christian said, his voice restored with supernatural clarity and force.

They instantly obeyed, moved by awe and curiosity.

"Make me a great cross with wood ashes."

They strewed the ashes upon the floor in the shape of a great Cross.

"Now lay me down on it, for so will I die."

They gently lifted him from his bed and laid him upon the cross of wood ashes.

"Shall we spread out thine arms, dear brother?"

"Now God forbid! Am I worthy of that?"

He lay silent, eyes raised in ecstasy. Presently, speaking half to them, half to himself, he said with subdued but concentrated rapture, "Oh, I feel it buoyant. It lifts me floating in the sky whence my merits had sunk me like lead."

Day broke, displaying his face cast upward in silent rapture, his hands joined together, like Margaret's. Just about the hour she died, he spoke his last word in this world.

"Jesu!"

And with that word, he fell asleep.

They laid him out for his final resting place. Under his linen, they found a horse-hair shirt.

"Ah!" cried the young monks, "behold a saint!"

Beneath the haircloth, they discovered a long, thick tress of auburn hair.

They started, horrified, and a babel of voices arose, some condemning, some excusing.

In the midst of the uproar, Jerome entered. Hearing the dispute, he turned to an ardent young monk named Basil, who was crying out the loudest about scandal. "Basil," Jerome said, "is she alive or dead that owned this hair?"

"How may I know, father?"

"Then for all you know, it may be the relic of a saint?"

"Certes it may be," Basil replied skeptically.

"You have then broken our rule, which says, 'Put ill construction on no act done by a brother which can be construed innocently.' Who are you to judge such a man as this was? Go to your cell and stir not out for a week by way of penance."

Jerome then took the lock of hair. When the coffin was to be closed, he cleared the cell and placed the tress upon the dead man's bosom. "There, Clement," he said to the dead face. Setting himself a penance for the act, he nailed the coffin shut himself.

The next day, Gerard was buried in the Gouda churchyard. The monks followed him in procession from the convent. Jerome, evidently carrying out the deceased's wishes, read the service. The grave was deep, and at the bottom lay a lead coffin. Poor Gerard's body, light as a feather due to his wasting, was lowered and placed beside it.

After the service, Jerome addressed the crowd of parishioners who had come for a last look at their beloved friend. When he spoke of the departed's virtues, loud wailing and weeping burst forth, and tears fell upon the coffin like rain.

The monks returned home. Jerome gathered them in the refectory and spoke thus: "We have this day laid a saint in the earth. The convent will keep his trentals, but will feast, not fast; for our good brother is freed from the burden of the flesh; his labours are over, and he has entered into his joyful rest. I alone shall fast and do penance; for to my shame I say it, I was unjust to him, and knew not his worth till it was too late. And you, young monks, be not curious to inquire whether the lock he bore on his bosom was a token of pure affection or the relic of a saint; but remember the heart he wore beneath: most of all, fix your eyes upon his life and conversation, and follow them as ye may: for he was a holy man."

Thus, after life's fitful fever, these true lovers were at peace. The grave, kinder to them than the Church, united them forever. Now, a man of another age and nation, touched by their fate, has labored to build their tombstone and rescue them from long and unmerited oblivion.

He asks for your sympathy, but not your pity.

No, put this story to a wholesome use.

Fiction must often give false views of life and death. Here, as it happens, curbed by history, it gives you true ones. Let the barrier that kept these true lovers apart prepare you for this: here on earth, there will nearly always be some obstacle to your perfect happiness. To their early death, apply your Reason and your Faith, by way of exercise and preparation. For if you cannot bear to be told that these died young—who, had they lived a hundred years, would still be dead—how shall you bear to see the gentle, the loving, and the true glide from your own bosom to the grave, and fly from your house to heaven?

Yet this is in store for you. In every age, the Master of life and death, who is kinder as well as wiser than we are, has transplanted to heaven, young, earth's sweetest flowers.

I ask your sympathy, then, for their rare constancy and pure affection, and their cruel separation by a vile heresy within the bosom of the Church; but not your pity for their early but happy end.

"Beati sunt qui in Domino moriuntur."
