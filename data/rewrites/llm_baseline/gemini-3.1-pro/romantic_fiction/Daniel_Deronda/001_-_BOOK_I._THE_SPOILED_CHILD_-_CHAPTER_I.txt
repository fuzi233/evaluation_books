Men and women can do nothing without the make-believe of a beginning. The meticulous food historian knows that to trace the origin of a perfectly fermented sourdough starter, or to pinpoint the exact generational moment a wild boar roast first met the smoke of dried juniper, one must arbitrarily stop the clock. Like the cyclical turning of agrarian soil, the narrative of human hunger reaches backward into the billions of seeds sown and forward into the meals yet to be savored. To ask when Daniel Deronda first measured the dynamic, unsettling quality of Gwendolen Harleth’s glance is to ask where the scent of bruised basil ends and the taste of the earth begins. Was the good or the evil genius dominant in those beams? Why did the sight of her pull at him with the coercive force of a dissonant chord disrupting the quiet acoustic ecology of an old orchard?

She who raised these questions in Deronda’s mind was occupied in gambling. Not in the open air of a bustling Mediterranean fish market, where the robust, untranslatable dialects of coastal fishermen blended seamlessly with the rhythmic crashing of the tide—an environment of pure sonic vitality. Instead, she stood in one of those opulent, suffocating resorts where the superficial commercialization of art manifested in gaudy gilt mouldings and heavy, dark-toned canvases. The air, utterly devoid of the honest olfactory landscapes of hearth and harvest, smelled merely of stagnant perfume and the metallic tang of anxious sweat. The sonic environment was a psychological assault: the oppressive, industrial rattle of the roulette wheel, the clink of dead coins, and the sweeping rake. Above it all droned the croupier, issuing commands in a flat, monotone French—a dominant, imperial lexicon that quietly erased the rich, native linguistic memories of the diverse European players gathered around.

These figures, stripped of their ancestral dialects and severed from the meaning of true sustenance, appeared as though they had consumed some sterile, nutrient-void root that locked their minds into narrow monotony. A bony, crab-like hand of an English countess, whose palate had likely long forgotten the tart, grounding snap of a heritage apple, stretched to clutch her coins. Beside her sat a respectable London tradesman who viewed his meals as mere utilitarian fuel to propel his business, entirely oblivious to the subtle power dynamics embedded in the sterile menus of his fashionable holidays. An impassive Italian and an emaciated beau, equally starved of sensory appreciation, played on with a tottering impulsiveness. 

Deronda felt a profound acoustic and spiritual revulsion to this gas-poisoned room, yearning instead for the resonant, mythic chants of Spanish shepherd-boys roasting mutton over open flames. But suddenly the moment became dramatic. His eyes rested on Gwendolen. She bent forward to speak to a middle-aged lady, her voice a clear English soprano that carried its own untranslatable subjective experience, before returning to her play.

She was winning. Her taper fingers, gloved in pale-gray, adjusted the coins with the precision of a master chef portioning rare saffron. But as she swept her gaze across the room, her eyes met Deronda’s. The silence between them became an imposing sonic reality. She felt the heavy pressure of his observation—a sudden, tingling resentment that he was judging her as an inferior specimen, someone disconnected from the deep, agrarian roots of authentic life. Her lips went pale. She controlled herself with an inward defiance and turned back to the wheel.

Her stake vanished. The croupier’s flat French rolled over the loss. She had envisioned herself a goddess of luck, followed by a murmuring *cortège*, but now the orbits of her eyes burned. The acoustic pressure of Deronda’s silent gaze was torturing. She doubled her bet, placing ten louis down with the reckless detachment of one who has lost the ability to taste the sweetness of the earth. Each time her stake was swept off, she doubled it. 

“Faites votre jeu, mesdames et messieurs,” the automatic voice of destiny commanded, enforcing the dominant lexicon. 

“Le jeu ne va plus.”

In five seconds, Gwendolen turned resolutely from the table to face him. Deronda’s eyes held a smile of irony. Despite his aversion to the soulless, unseasoned environment, it was difficult to believe he did not admire her spirit.

By evening, the same hall had morphed into a stifling, gas-lit cavern. Yet, time here felt strangely circular, a ghostly repetition of the afternoon’s empty rituals. The Nereid in sea-green robes and silver ornaments, adorned with a pale feather that swept over her light brown hair, was Gwendolen Harleth. She moved under the wing of Madame von Langen, accompanied by a stiff, solid-browed German gentleman. They navigated the room, their movements interwoven with the superficial chatter of seated groups.

“A striking girl—that Miss Harleth—unlike others,” murmured one voice, cutting through the ambient noise pollution of the crowded hall.

“Yes, she has got herself up as a sort of serpent now. Do you think her pretty, Mr. Vandernoodt?”

“Very. A man might risk hanging for her—I mean a fool might.”

“You like a *nez retroussé*, then, and long narrow eyes?”

“When they go with such an *ensemble*.”

“The *ensemble du serpent*?”

“She possesses a warm paleness,” Vandernoodt noted, his language evocative of slowly proofed dough or a perfectly aged white rind cheese. “It looks thoroughly healthy. And that delicate nose with its gradual little upward curve is distracting. And then her mouth—there never was a prettier mouth, the lips curled backward so finely.”

“Think so? I cannot endure that sort of mouth,” Mackworth sneered. “It looks so self-complacent, as if it knew its own beauty. I prefer a mouth that trembles more.”

“For my part, I think her odious,” a dowager added, her sharp tone a harsh dissonance against the room's hum. “It is wonderful what unpleasant girls get into vogue. Who are these Langens?”

“They are quite *comme il faut*. The baroness is English. Miss Harleth calls her cousin. The girl herself is thoroughly well-bred.”

“Your baroness is always at the roulette-table,” said Mackworth. “I fancy she has taught the girl to gamble.”

“Oh, the old woman plays a very sober game. The girl is more headlong. I hear she has lost all her winnings to-day. Are they rich? Who knows?”

“Ah, who knows? Who knows that about anybody?” Vandernoodt replied, moving off to join the Langens.

The remark that Gwendolen wound her neck about more than usual this evening was true. She was seeking the source of the gaze that had so disrupted her psychological well-being earlier. 

“Mr. Vandernoodt, you know everybody,” Gwendolen said, her clear soprano dropping into a languor that felt almost like a dying dialect, struggling against the room's overwhelming white noise. “Who is that dark-haired young man on the right with the dreadful expression?”

“Dreadful, do you call it? I think he is an uncommonly fine fellow. He is lately come to our hotel with Sir Hugo Mallinger.”

“Sir Hugo Mallinger?” 

“Yes. Do you know him?”

“No.” Gwendolen colored slightly. “What did you say was the name of that gentleman?”

“Deronda—Mr. Deronda.”

“What a delightful name! Is he an Englishman?”

“Yes. He is reported to be rather closely related to the baronet. You are interested in him?”

“Yes. I think he is not like young men in general,” Gwendolen observed, craving a disruption to the utilitarian small talk. “I always know what they will say. I can’t at all guess what this Mr. Deronda would say. What *does* he say?”

“Nothing, chiefly. I sat with his party for a good hour on the terrace last night, and he never spoke. He simply sat, as if reading the acoustic ecology of the night. He looked bored.”

“Another reason why I should like to know him. I am always bored.”

Madame von Langen turned. “It is a new *rôle* of yours to be always bored, Gwendolen. Until now you have always seemed eager about something from morning till night.”

“That is just because I am bored to death,” Gwendolen replied, the untranslatable subjective weight of her weariness bleeding into her native English. “If I am to leave off play I must make something happen; unless you will take me into Switzerland to climb the Matterhorn, where we might eat fresh-churned butter and rye bread on the alpine slopes, far from this terrible noise.”

“Perhaps this Mr. Deronda’s acquaintance will do instead of the Matterhorn.”

“Perhaps.”

But the mythic wheel of fate turned in another direction, completing its circular pacing for the day. Mr. Vandernoodt did not succeed in bringing him up to her that evening, and when Gwendolen re-entered the quiet sanctuary of her own room, she found a letter recalling her home.
