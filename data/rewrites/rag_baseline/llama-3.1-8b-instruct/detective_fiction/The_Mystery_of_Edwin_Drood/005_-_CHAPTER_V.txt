The dim gas lamps outside cast flickering shadows on the walls of the Travellers' Twopenny. I walked beside Stony Durdles, his bundle slumped against his hip, as we navigated the narrow streets of Gas Works Garding. The night air carried the faint scent of smoke and damp earth. We walked in silence, the only sound the soft crunch of gravel beneath our feet. Every now and then, a stone would strike the cobblestones ahead, followed by the high-pitched whistle of a small boy. Durdles didn't flinch, his eyes fixed on some point in the distance. The boy's cries of "Mulled agin!" and "Widdy widdy wen!" hung in the air like a refrain, punctuated by the occasional thud of a stone hitting the ground.

As we turned a corner, the boy came into view. His eyes gleamed with a fierce intensity, and his ragged clothes seemed to be held together by sheer willpower. He was a small, wiry thing, with a jagged gap in his front teeth where half his teeth were missing. I felt a pang of unease as he began to chant again, his voice rising and falling in a strange, hypnotic rhythm.

"Widdy widdy wen!
I—ket—ches—Im—out—ar—ter—ten,
Widdy widdy wy!
Then—E—don’t—go—then—I—shy—
Widdy Widdy Wake-cock warning!"

The words seemed to hang in the air, a mixture of threat and warning. Durdles, however, merely nodded to himself, his expression unreadable. I felt a shiver run down my spine as the boy resumed his barrage of stones, each one landing with a soft thud on the cobblestones.

As we continued on our way, the boy's cries grew fainter, until we were alone once more in the quiet streets. Durdles seemed to be lost in thought, his eyes fixed on some distant point. I hesitated, unsure whether to speak or remain silent. Finally, I broke the stillness.

"Do you know this boy?" I asked, trying to keep my voice low.

Durdles nodded, his expression unchanged. "Deputy," he said, his voice barely above a whisper.

I repeated the name, trying to commit it to memory. "Is that his name?"
