"Pardon me, my Lord," Aspern interjected, "but I speak on behalf of Sigismund."

"For him?" Fronsberg replied instantly. "Oh, yes—for him I always keep a pardon safely banked. He will draw upon me sooner or later. What is his need? Has some mad project failed? Are his fine mechanical wings proving unable to fly? Is he in durance, assaulted by the watch, owing a bill for Epernay with not a crust to eat?"

"Oh, none of these, my Lord," Aspern assured him. "He has escaped Circe's herd and seeks to win the love of your fair ward, Cecilia. But he desires to win your consent first."

"You frown," Fronsberg observed sharply.

"Distinguish your words, my Lord," Aspern retorted. "I said I held a *pardon*, not *consent*."

Despite Daniel Deronda's pressing reasons to return to town—motives blended from his deep anxiety for Mirah and a burning curiosity regarding the enigmatic Mordecai—he did not reach Sir Hugo before the latter proceeded ahead of his family to prepare for the opening of Parliament on the sixth of February. Deronda took up his quarters in Park Lane, fully expecting to find his chambers occupied by Hans Meyrick. This much he anticipated; however, he discovered other details that defied his expectations.

Many recall Retzsch's drawing depicting Destiny as Mephistopheles playing chess with Man for his soul. In this game, the clever adversary feints with unintended moves, luring the mortal defender away from the true point of attack. The fiend mocks preparation above all else, persuading us against taking out waterproofs when the sky is destined to clear, foreseeing that our resulting prejudice will cloud our judgment of future weather signs. It is a peculiar test of a man's mettle when, after painfully adjusting oneself to what seems a wise provision, one finds all mental precautions slightly astray, and excellent intentions no better than miscalculated dovetails cut from a wrong starting point. When magnanimity prepares to meet misbehavior, it often finds a completely different call upon it. Something akin to this befell Deronda.

His first impression was one of pure pleasure and amusement. His sitting room had been transformed into an *atelier*, strewn with miscellaneous drawings and the contents of two chests brought from Rome. The lower half of the windows were darkened with baize, and the blonde Hans, in his weird youth, presided over the littered space as its genius. His hair was longer than before, his face more whimsically creased, and his high voice rose even higher under the excitement of rapid talk. The friendship between the two had been maintained warmly since their memorable days at Cambridge, sustained not only by correspondence but by little episodes of companionship abroad and in England. The original dynamic of confidence on one side and indulgence on the other had developed naturally, as is wont to occur where such spiritual borrowing and lending begins well.

"I knew you would like to see my casts and antiquities," Hans said after the initial hearty greetings and inquiries, "so I didn't scruple to unload my chests here. But I've found two rooms in Chelsea, not many hundred yards from my mother and sisters, and I shall soon be ready to hang out there once they scrape the walls and install new lights. That is all I'm waiting for. But you see, I don't wait to begin work: you cannot conceive what a great fellow I am going to be. The seed of immortality has sprouted within me."

"Only a fungoid growth, I dare say—a growing disease in the lungs," Deronda remarked, treating Hans in his customary brotherly fashion. He walked toward some drawings propped on the ledge of his bookcases: five rapidly sketched heads, different aspects of the same face. He stood at a convenient distance, offering no immediate comment. Hans remained silent for a minute, picked up his palette, and began touching the picture on his easel.

"What do you think of them?" he asked finally.

"The full face looks too massive; otherwise, the likenesses are good," Deronda said, more coldly than was usual with him.

"No, it is not too massive," Hans decided firmly. "I have noted that. There is always a little surprise when one passes from the profile to the full face. But I shall enlarge her scale for Berenice. I am making a Berenice series—look at the sketches along there—and now, thinking of it, you are just the model I want for the Agrippa."

Hans, still holding pencil and palette, had moved to Deronda's side while saying this, but he added hastily, as if conscious of a mistake, "No, no, I forgot; you don't like sitting for your portrait, confound you! However, I've picked up a capital Titus. There are to be five in the series. The first is Berenice clasping the knees of Gessius Florus, beseeching him to spare her people; I've got that on the easel. Then this one, where she stands on the Xystus with Agrippa, entreating the people not to injure themselves by resistance."

"Agrippa's legs will never do," Deronda objected.

"The legs are good realistically," Hans replied, his face creasing drolly. "Public men are often shaky about the legs—'Their legs, the emblem of their various thought,' as somebody says in the *Rehearsal*."

"But these are as impossible as the legs of Raphael's Alcibiades," Deronda countered.

"Then they are good ideally," Hans insisted. "Agrippa's legs were possibly bad; I idealize that and make them impossibly bad. Art, my Eugenius, must intensify. But never mind the legs now: the third sketch shows Berenice exulting in the prospect of becoming Empress of Rome, when news comes that Vespasian is declared Emperor and her lover Titus his successor."

"You must put a scroll in her mouth, else people will not understand that. You can't tell that in a picture."

"It will make them feel their ignorance then—an excellent aesthetic effect. The fourth depicts Titus sending Berenice away from Rome after she has shared his palace for ten years—both reluctant, both sad—*invitus invitam*, as Suetonius hath it. I've found a model for the Roman brute."

"Shall you make Berenice look fifty? She must have been that age."

"No, no; a few mature touches to show the lapse of time. Dark-eyed beauty wears well; hers particularly. But now, here is the fifth: Berenice seated lonely on the ruins of Jerusalem. That is pure imagination. That is what ought to have been—perhaps was. Now, see how I tell a pathetic negative. Nobody knows what became of her—that is finely indicated by the series coming to a close. There is no sixth picture."

Hans pretended to speak with a gasping sense of sublimity, drew back his head with a frown as if searching for a similar impression on Deronda, and continued, "I break off in the Homeric style. The story is chipped off, so to speak, and passes with a ragged edge into nothing—*le néant*; can anything be more sublime, especially in French? The vulgar would desire to see her corpse and burial—perhaps her will read and her linen distributed. But now, come and look at this on the easel. I have made some way there."

"That beseeching attitude is really good," Deronda said after a moment's contemplation. "You have been very industrious during the Christmas holidays; for I suppose you have taken up the subject since you came to London." Neither of them had yet mentioned Mirah.

"No," Hans said, applying touches to his picture, "I made up my mind to the subject beforehand. I take that lucky chance for an augury that I am going to burst on the world as a great painter. I saw a splendid woman in Trastevere—the grandest women there are half-Jewesses—and she set me hunting for a fine situation of a Jewess in Rome. Like other men of vast learning, I ended by taking what lay on the surface. I'll show you a sketch of the Trasteverina's head when I can lay my hands on it."

"I should think she would be a more suitable model for Berenice," Deronda said, struggling to express his discontent.

"Not a bit of it. The model ought to be the most beautiful Jewess in the world, and I have found her."

"Have you made yourself sure that she would like to figure in that character? I should think no woman would be more abhorrent to her. Does she quite know what you are doing?"

"Certainly. I got her to throw herself precisely into this attitude. Little Mother sat for Gessius Florus, and Mirah clasped her knees." Hans stepped away slightly to inspect the effect of his touches.
