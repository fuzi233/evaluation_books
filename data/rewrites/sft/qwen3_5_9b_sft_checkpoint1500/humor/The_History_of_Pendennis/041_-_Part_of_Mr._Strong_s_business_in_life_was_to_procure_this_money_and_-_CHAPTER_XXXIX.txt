On the appointed afternoon, Major Pendennis and Arthur Pendennis materialized at Sir Francis Clavering's residence with the same lack of premeditation that usually characterized their social engagements. They found the drawing-room occupied only by Sir Francis, his wife, and Captain Strong, whom Arthur received with genuine delight despite his Uncle's profound disdain. The Major, however, viewed Strong with the icy glare reserved for household stewards, muttering internally about the indignity of dining with Clavering's "d—— house-steward."

Tensions, however, dissolved with the arrival of Mr. Welbore Welbore, Clavering's dull but anciently wealthy neighbor. Though he contributed less to the conversation than the footman trailing behind his chair, his status as a seven-thousand-a-year country gentleman instantly calmed the Major's frayed nerves. The company swelled further with the Dowager Lady Rockminster, Lady Agnes Foker, and her son Harry, alongside the absent Mr. Pynsent. Finally, Miss Blanche Amory made her entrance, radiant in a white silk gown that showcased her pearly shoulders to perfection.

"Fairy!" whispered Foker to Pen, who returned the gaze with obvious admiration. Blanche treated Arthur with unusual warmth, extolling the virtues of Fairoaks and expressing a longing return to the country, appearing entirely simple and affectionate. For Harry Foker, unused to the company of women and typically tongue-tied, Blanche was a revelation. Where he had been mute before other ladies, he now spoke incessantly, becoming lively and talkative before the dinner bell even sounded.

While descending the stairs, Harry found himself separated from his object of desire, assigned instead to escort Mrs. Welbore Welbore due to his superior rank as an Earl's grandson. Yet, fate—or maneuvering—placed him back at Blanche's side at the dinner table, sandwiched between her and Pen. Both young men vied for her favor, though the conversation drifted quickly to the arts.

"Have you seen Taglioni in *La Sylphide*, Miss Amory?" Harry asked, ordering soup from the waiter and humming the famous ballet tune. "She clips in the air, doesn't she?"

"Miss Amory is a sylph herself," Pen interjected smoothly.

"What a delightful tenor voice you have, Mr. Foker," Blanche replied, turning her attention to the younger gentleman. "I'd love to sing with you."

Pen recalled similar sentiments from his own youth, wondering with a sneer how many partners she had since taken, but kept his thoughts private. "I should like to hear you again, Miss Blanche. I've never heard a voice I preferred so much."

"I thought you liked Laura's," she teased.

"Laura's is a contralto, often lost in the mix," Pen countered, adopting a tender tone. "I'm weary of London professionals; they sing too loud. One ages quickly here. I only care for the songs of my youth."

"My favorite is English music," Blanche declared. "Get me a saddle of mutton, Mr. Foker."

"I adore English ballads!" agreed Miss Amory. "Sing me one after dinner, will you?"

"Shall I sing you an English song?" she asked, turning to Foker with a look that nearly knocked him over. "If you promise to come up shortly."

"I'll be up fast enough," Foker assured her, accepting his share of claret with gusto. "I'm a domestic character, Miss Amory; simple habits. When I'm pleased, I'm in good humor, aren't I, Pen?" He paused, pointing at a jelly dish. "Pass the jelly with cherries inside. How do they manage those cherries?"

As the ladies retired, Blanche dropped her gloves near Foker and her handkerchief near Pen, distributing her attentions evenly, though perhaps with a slightly warmer encouragement toward the former. As she fled the room, her final glance swept over Strong's broad white waistcoat before locking onto Harry, leaving the charmer to sigh and swallow a bumper of wine.

Dinner concluded early, allowing the ladies to ascend to the drawing-room while the gentlemen lingered over wine. Through the open windows, passersby on the street gazed in at six men in white waistcoats surrounded by decanters and fruit. A policeman patrolled the sidewalk, while a man in the street, gorgeously attired in chains and a shiny boot, leaned against the railings. To the policeman, X, the man appeared dignified, perhaps a Member of Parliament, until he lurched and reeled under the influence of strong liquor.

Inside, Captain Strong was laughing heartily. As his laughter spilled through the window, the street man echoed it with a snigger, slapping his thigh and winking at Jeames, the footman standing guard at the portico. Jeames, initially mesmerized by the moon, was disturbed by the man's disheveled state and shiny attire. Realizing the stranger was not on duty, Jeames signaled Policeman X.

X approached the figure, demanding he move on. The stranger, however, ignored the command, nodding wildly at Strong and shouting, "Hullo! keep your hands off a gentleman!" He accused the house of hiding his friends, identifying Sir Francis, Strong, and a "young chap" involved in a recent row.

"It's that d—— Altamont!" cried Sir Francis from within, springing up with a guilty look. Strong followed suit, rushing out to seize the stranger's arm. A crowd gathered as Strong dragged the intoxicated man away, intending to soothe him in the private sitting room. However, Altamont, in a towering rage, refused entry, insisting, "That ain't the door—that's the dining-room door... I'll go and have some!"

The butler barred the path, but Sir Francis emerged, forcing a smile and welcoming the intruder. Colonel Altamont, revealed to be the Ambassador of the Nawab of Lucknow, reeled into the room, glaring triumphantly at Jeames before sinking into a chair. He alternated between frowning and smiling, hiccupping encomiums on the vintage.

"He's a singular man," Strong noted gravely. "Long resident in a native court; acquires singular habits."

Major Pendennis, dry-eyed, wondered what sort of company he had stumbled into. Harry Foker, meanwhile, whispered to Pen, "It's the man who sings the Malay song at the Back Kitchen." Trying to ingratiate himself, Foker offered a slice of pineapple to the Colonel.

"Pines? I've seen 'em feed pigs on pines," Altamont retorted.

"All the Nawab's pigs are fed on pines," Strong corrected quietly.

As the Colonel settled in, he locked eyes with Pen. "I know you, too, young fellow. Baymouth Ball, by Jingo. Wanted to fight the Frenchman." Laughing in the depths of his intoxication, he squatted his fists, recalling the memory. "What's his name?" he demanded. "Pendennis!"

"Pendennis! Pendennis be hanged!" Altamont roared, thumping the table.

"My name is also Pendennis, sir," the Major replied, his dignity wounded by the intrusion of a drunkard. "Please do not curse it so loudly."

Altamont turned, his eyes suddenly clearing as if sobriety struck him mid-sentence. He adjusted his black wig, staring fiercely at the Major, who met him with the steady gaze of a veteran. After a tense moment, the Colonel began buttoning his coat, stood abruptly, and tottered toward the door, shouting, "Captain Beak! Captain Beak, by jingo!" Strong hurried after him.

Within twenty minutes, the bizarre visitor vanished. Sir Francis appeared pale and agitated, asking the Major if he knew the man. Pendennis identified him as a deserter from the Horse Artillery who had ended up in Indian service. Relief washed over the baronet as the cab, driven by Strong, whisked the Colonel away.

Coffee was served, and the group ascended to the drawing-room. Foker confessed to Pen that the episode was the "rummest go" he had ever witnessed, a judgment Pen chuckled at. True to her promise, Miss Amory played the piano. Foker was enraptured, joining in whenever he recognized the tunes, while Pen feigned conversation with others before Blanche summoned him to the instrument. She sang verses of his own composition, setting them to music herself, or perhaps with the aid of Signor Twankidillo. Pen turned the pages assiduously, wishing he possessed her talent for verse.

Before the ladies departed, the curious incident belowstairs was omitted from conversation, though Arthur noticed a flicker of alarm in Sir Francis's face when Blanche requested Captain Strong. The evening proved dull overall; Welbore slept soundly, and the Major, contrary to his usual habit of endless anecdote, sat silently, watching Blanche perform.

At the conclusion of the evening, the Major expressed regret at the swift passage of time, offering Blanche a high compliment on her vocal prowess and pronunciation. "Your daughter, Lady Clavering, is a perfect nightingale..." he exclaimed, praising her linguistic abilities and beauty. Blanche, though astonished, accepted the praise gracefully.

Walking home with the young men, the Major eyed Foker's cigar smoke with distaste, wishing for the lad to depart. Foker, however, hung stubbornly on, finally releasing the pair at his home in Bury Street.
