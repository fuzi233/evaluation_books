Surely no man could lay fairer claim to universal sympathy than the young, brave, handsome, and ill-starred Major Washington. Madam Esmond, mistress of Castlewood, might have birthed a full score of sons, and still have had every right to dote on her dashing young protégé. His former chamber in the west wing was now more “Mr. Washington’s room” than ever, kept aired, furnished with his favourite books, and spoken of in every company where she found an audience. She held up his sterling, unpretentious virtues to her own two boys at every turn, contrasting his steady, honourable demeanour with Harry’s reckless devotion to pleasure (the wild, warm-hearted boy!) and George’s brooding, listless hours poring over dusty old tomes.

George cared not a whit for Mr. Washington’s merits, however, thanks to his mother’s ceaseless, overblown praises. He nursed the little jealous demon in his breast until it grew to be a constant, gnawing pest to himself and everyone around him, firing off sarcastic quips so layered and sharp that his simple, adoring mother could not parse their meaning. She would sit bewildered, staring at his sallow, brooding face, quite at a loss what to make of his dark, saturnine moods.

All the while, public events were unfolding that would twist the fortunes of this quiet Virginia household for good. The petty border skirmish between French and British colonists in North America had swelled from a provincial squabble to a full-blown national war. French reinforcements had already landed in Canada, and British regiments were sailing for Virginia’s shores.

“Alas, my dearest friend,” wrote Madame la Presidente de Mouchy from Quebec to her old young correspondent George Warrington, “how cruel is fate to us! I see you tear yourself from your adored mother’s arms to throw yourself into the embrace of Bellona. I see you carried wounded from the field of battle. I scarce dare wish victory to our lilies when I behold you ranged beneath the standard of the British Leopard. There are enmities the heart refuses to recognise, and ours, for certain, remain at peace amid all this tumult. Everyone here sends their greetings to you, and to Monsieur the Bear-Hunter, your brother— that cold Hippolyte who would always rather chase game than sit for soft, idle conversation with our ladies! Your friend and rival the Chevalier de la Jabotiere burns to meet you face to face on the field of Mars. M. Du Quesne spoke of you only last night at supper. My husband and he send their most affectionate remembrances to their young friend, joined ever by those of your sincere Presidente de Mouchy.”

That very “banner of the Leopard” the fair French lady wrote of was now unfurled to the winds, rallying thousands of the King’s soldiers beneath it. The crown had resolved to wrest every inch of French-occupied territory back under British rule. Two regiments were raised and funded by the King on American soil, while a fleet carrying two more fresh regiments sailed from home ports under a veteran commander.

In February of 1755, Commodore Keppel dropped anchor in Hampton Roads aboard the *Centurion*, the famous ship that had carried Anson on his legendary circumnavigation of the globe. His small fleet of warships carried General Braddock, his full staff, and the first half of his expeditionary force. Mr. Braddock had been personally appointed by the Duke of Cumberland, known across England simply as “The Duke” in that era—nearly the great military hero his partisans claimed, but far less of the cruel butcher his enemies would later paint him as. A fleet of transports followed hard on his heels, stuffed to the gunwales with stores, fresh troops, and piles of gold to fund the campaign.

The great general landed his men at Alexandria, on the banks of the Potomac, then travelled to Annapolis in Maryland to summon all thirteen colonial governors to a war council, ordering each provincial assembly to raise men, supplies, and wagons for the common cause.

The arrival of the general and his little army sent a shock of wild excitement rippling across every colony, and nowhere was the fever higher than at Castlewood. Harry was off the instant the news reached them, galloping for Alexandria to see the rows of white tents strung across the green fields. The rousing trill of fifes and rattle of drums thrilled him to his bones, and he made fast friends with the officers of both regiments within a week, a welcome guest at their mess table, begging every man he met for a spot on the coming expedition.

Madam Esmond was delighted that her boys had the chance to mix with polished, well-bred English gentlemen of quality. She was certain their company would be a vast improvement over the local horse-racing, cock-fighting Virginian squires Harry usually ran with, and the flock of fawning lawyers, pettifoggers, and toadies that hung around the lieutenant-governor’s table at Williamsburg. Madam Esmond had a sharp eye for flatterers in other people’s houses, and she mocked the little Williamsburg official set relentlessly for their absurd petty etiquettes and squabbles over who got to sit first at dinner.

If only the good lady knew half what Harry and George saw at the camp! If she could have heard the raucous, off-colour jokes the officers shouted over bowls of spiced punch, seen half of them carried dead drunk back to their lodgings after dinner, watched them swagger through the countryside terrorising peaceful farmwives and even insulting the wives of the native Indian allies who had travelled hundreds of miles to join the British fight against the French. The General raved and stormed at his troops for their disorder, and ranted at the provincial gentry for their “traitorous niggardliness” in holding back supplies, but the men treated the whole countryside like conquered territory, looking down on the colonists as crude hicks. Eventually Braddock was forced to ban all Indian women from his camp, and their humiliated, outraged husbands abandoned the expedition entirely—robbing him of the scouts and warriors he would desperately need just a few months later.

Madam Esmond would hear none of the camp gossip, though. Soldiers would be soldiers, everyone knew that, and the officers her sons invited to Castlewood were unfailingly polite, a fact that was quite true. The widow received them all with great graciousness, laying on the finest hunting feasts, venison, and wine the estate could provide.

Before long, the General himself sent a polite note to the mistress of Castlewood. His own father had served under the great Duke of Marlborough alongside her father, the legendary Colonel Esmond, whose name was still spoken with respect across England. With her permission, he wrote, General Braddock would do her the honour of calling at Castlewood to pay his respects to the daughter of so distinguished a veteran.

Had Madam Esmond known what lay behind Braddock’s sudden politeness, his compliments would not have charmed her half so much. The Commander-in-Chief was holding public levees at Alexandria, and among the provincial gentry who turned out to pay their respects were the two Warrington twins, mounted on their finest thoroughbreds, dressed in their latest London suits, with two liveried negro boys trailing behind them in the family’s smart new livery, riding in state to call on the great man. Braddock was sullen and furious at the provincial gentry by that point, and barely spared the young men a glance, casually asking his aide-de-camp at dinner later who those two “Squire Gawkeys” in blue-and-gold coats and scarlet waistcoats had been.

Mr. Dinwiddie, the Lieutenant-Governor of Virginia, who happened to be dining with his Excellency that night, had suffered a hundred petty pert snipes from Madam Esmond over the years, and jumped at the chance to tease. “Oh,” he laughed, “those are the sons of Princess Pocahontas.”

Braddock swore a tremendous oath and demanded, “Who the deuce is that?”

Dinwiddie proceeded to spin a long, disrespectful, absurd anecdote-filled account of Madam Esmond, making merry of her pomposity and grand pretensions until his Excellency fell fast asleep right at the dinner table.

When he woke, Dinwiddie was gone, but Mr. Franklin of Philadelphia still sat at the table, deep in conversation with the other officers. Braddock picked up the thread of their talk where he’d left off, speaking of Madam Esmond in short, rude, soldierly terms, demanding what the name of that “old fool” Dinwiddie had been rambling about was. He then launched into a tirade of contempt and wrath against all the provincial gentry and the whole American countryside in general.

Mr. Franklin repeated the widow’s full name, and painted a far different portrait of her character than Dinwiddie had. He knew a great deal about her, her father, and her vast estate—indeed he knew a great deal about every man and every subject that ever came up for discussion. He explained to the General that Madam Esmond held thousands of beeves, a stable full of fine horses, and stockpiles of stores large enough to provision half his expedition, and strongly recommended he conciliate her by every means possible. Braddock already thought Franklin the shrewdest, most intelligent man in the colonies, and promptly ordered an aide to invite the two young Warrington gentlemen to dinner the next day.

When the twins arrived, he was all charm and good humour, and his entire personal staff fawned over them. They behaved with the modesty and good breeding befitting their name, and rode home delighted with their reception— nor was their mother any less pleased that his Excellency had shown such civility to her boys. In reply to Braddock’s note, Madam Esmond penned a grand, flowery letter in her finest hand, acknowledging his politeness and begging his Excellency to name the day when he might come to Castlewood as her honoured guest.

Naturally, the coming war and the approaching expedition were the constant subject of conversation at Castlewood in the weeks that followed. To march on the campaign was the dearest wish of Harry’s whole heart. He dreamed of nothing but battles and glory, hung around the officers’ mess in Williamsburg every other day, polished and scoured every gun, sword, and pistol in the house until they shone, and revived his childhood game of drilling all the able-bodied negro men on the estate as if they were his private army. His mother, who had a stout, gallant spirit of her own, knew the time had come when one of her boys must leave her to serve the King. She scarce dared to think which of them it would be. She respected and admired her elder son deeply, but she knew she loved the wilder, warmer younger boy with all the passion of her heart.

Eager as Harry was to be a soldier, he scarcely dared to speak the desire that burned in his breast. Once or twice he ventured to bring it up to George, only to see his elder brother’s face darken into an ominous scowl. Harry felt a feudal, almost religious devotion to George, worshipped him with extravagant affection, and deferred to his every wish as the undisputed head of the family. To his infinite dismay, he soon saw that George too, in his quiet, grave way, was immersing himself in all things military. He dragged the old volumes of Marlborough and Eugene’s campaigns down from the shelves, pulled out all his grandfather’s military textbooks, and pored over the most warlike of Plutarch’s Lives. He and old Dempster, the mysterious Scottish steward, were practising fencing with foils every afternoon, the old man a secret adept at the art of war, though he was never willing to say where he had learned it.

Madam Esmond sent the two boys back to the General’s camp with her formal reply, along with such large, handsome gifts for Braddock’s entire staff and the officers of the two royal regiments, that the General thanked Mr. Franklin more than once for bringing such a welcome, wealthy ally over to his side.

“Would not one of you young gentlemen care to come along and see the campaign?” Braddock asked them casually. “A friend of yours who speaks of you often—Mr. Washington, who had such ill luck with the expedition last year—has already agreed to join me as aide-de-camp. I would be very glad to take another fine young Virginian gentleman onto my personal staff.”

Harry’s eyes blazed, and his face flushed bright red. “I would go with all my heart, sir!” he cried out.

George looked hard at his younger brother, and said gravely, “One of us would be proud to attend your Excellency. It will be the other’s duty, of course, to stay here and care for our mother at home.”

Harry let his elder brother speak, as he always did. His will was still completely obedient to George’s. Much as he ached to go off to war, he would not say a word until George had declared his own intentions. He wanted the campaign so badly the very desire made him timid, and he dared not bring the matter up as they rode the miles home in silence, both of them talking of trivial, unrelated subjects while they knew full well what was weighing on the other’s mind, both afraid to force the awful question to its final answer.

When they arrived home, they told their mother of General Braddock’s offer. “I knew this must happen,” she said, her voice trembling. “At such a crisis for our country, our family must step forward. Have you—have you settled yet which of you will leave me?” She looked anxiously from one son to the other, dreading to hear either of their names spoken.

“The youngest ought to go, mother! Of course I ought to go!” Harry cried, turning scarlet.

“Of course he ought!” put in Mrs. Mountain, who was sitting in the room with them. “There! You see Mountain says so! I told you!” Harry burst out, darting a sidelong look at George.

“The head of the family ought to go, mother,” George said sadly.

“No! No! You still have not fully recovered from that bad fever you had last spring! Ought he to go, Mountain?”

“You would make the better soldier, I know that, dearest Hal. You and George Washington are great friends, and you would travel well together. He does not care for me, nor I for him, however much everyone in this house admires him. But you see, it is the law of honour, my Harry.” He spoke to his brother then in a voice of extraordinary kindness and tenderness. “The greatest grief I have felt over this matter is that I must refuse you. I must go. If fate had given you that extra half-hour of life I hold over you, it would be your lot, and you would claim your right to go first, you know you would.”

“Yes, George,” poor Harry whispered, “I own I should.”

“You will stay home, and take care of Castlewood and our mother. If anything happens to me, you will be here to fill my place. I would give way to you, my dear, as I know you would lay down your life to serve me. But each of us must do his duty. What would our grandfather say, if he were here now?”

Their mother looked at her two boys, her face bright with proud tears. “My papa would say his boys were gentlemen,” she faltered, and turned and left the room, not willing to let them see how much emotion was flooding her heart.

It spread like wildfire through the household before nightfall that Mr. George was going on the campaign. Dinah, George’s old foster-mother, wailed at the top of her voice at the thought of losing him. Phillis, Harry’s childhood nurse, was just as loud complaining that Master George was being preferred over Master Harry, as he always was. Sady, George’s personal servant, packed his bags bragging nonstop of all the glorious deeds he would perform on campaign, while Gumbo, Harry’s boy, pretended to whimper that he was being left behind—though he was secretly overjoyed not to have to go anywhere near a battlefield.

Of all the people in the house, Mrs. Mountain was the most furious at George’s decision to march off. She would not hear a word of it. He had no idea what he was doing, leaving home like that. She begged, implored, stamped her foot, and insisted he change his mind, vowing that nothing but utter mischief would come of his departure.

George was surprised by how stubbornly she opposed him. “I know, Mountain,” he said, “that Harry would make the better soldier. But after all, it is my duty to go.”

“Your duty is to stay!” Mountain snapped. “Why didn’t your own mother say that, just now when we were talking of it?”

“Your mother!” Mrs. Mountain gave a dark, sardonic laugh. “Your mother, my poor foolish boy!”

“What is the meaning of that mournful face, Mountain?”

“It may be that your mother wishes you away, George!” She shook her head grimly. “It may be, my poor deluded child, that you will find yourself with a new step-father when you come home.”

“What in heaven’s name do you mean?” George cried, the blood rushing straight to his face.

“Don’t you think I have eyes to see what is going on under my own nose? I tell you, child, Colonel Washington wants a rich wife. The second you two boys are off to the war, he will ask your mother to marry him, and you will come back to find him master of Castlewood. That is why you ought not to leave, you poor, simple boy! Don’t you see how fond she is of him? How much she fusses over him? How she is always holding him up as a perfect example to you, to Harry, to every single guest that walks through this door?”

“But he is going on the campaign too!” George protested.

“He is going on the courting campaign, child!” Mountain insisted. “It is all an artifice to blind you! You will see, he will get himself ‘wounded’ within a week and come straight back home! I have proof of what I say, proof in his own handwriting—look here!” She pulled a half-sheet of crumpled paper out of her pocket, in Mr. Washington’s well-known bold, clerk-like script.

“Where did you get that paper?” George asked, his face turning ghastly pale.

“I—I found it in the Major’s chamber!” Mountain said, looking shamefaced.

“You read the private papers of a guest staying in our house?” George cried. “For shame! I will not look at it!” He snatched the paper from her hand and flung it straight onto the smouldering log fire on the hearth.
