In a fit of anger, Mr. Draper turned away from his morning meeting with Harry Warrington, convinced the young man had spoken behind him. Indeed, Harry had risen from his bed and begun to call after him—half an exclamation escaping his lips—but pride held him back. The lawyer, too, was offended, and chose not to pause. It stung Harry’s dignity to be forced into submission by mere want of money. “An hour later will do just as well,” he muttered, sinking back onto the mattress with a sullen sigh.

No, he did not care for Maria Esmond. Not truly. He was ashamed of how easily he had been drawn into that engagement—a trap laid by a woman far more cunning than he. She had played upon his youthful passion, taken unfair advantage, just as her brother had once outmaneuvered him at games. They were kin, blood of his blood—and yet they had used him. Together, they had made a fool of him, exploiting his trust for their own ends.

He thought now of how they had betrayed the sanctity of hospitality—how they had turned the guest who came in faith into a prisoner within their walls. His heart ached. His head fell against the pillow, and bitter tears traced paths through the grime on his face. “Had they come to Virginia,” he whispered, “I would have welcomed them differently.”

His thoughts were shattered by the sudden appearance of Gumbo, grinning broadly at the door. “A lady has come to see Master Harry,” he announced.

And then she entered—Maria Esmond, wrapped in her dark capuchin cloak, her face pale beneath its folds. Harry sat up abruptly, his eyes wide and hollow. Before he could speak, she rushed forward, arms outstretched, and clutched him around the neck with genuine emotion. Tears streamed down her cheeks as she pressed her face against his, kissed his cheek, and sobbed into his shoulder. “Oh, my Harry! Could I ever have dreamed I’d find you here?”

He recoiled slightly, startled by her presence, but she knelt beside the bed, seizing his feverish hand and pressing her forehead to his knee. There was real affection in her—real sorrow for the wretched place where she found him, the broken man before her. Her heart swelled with pity.

“I—I thought none of you would come,” Harry said, voice thick with grief.

She answered not with words, but with more tears, more kisses, more fervent embraces. Her hands clasped his tightly, as if afraid he might vanish. “My dear, my dear!” she wept. “I cannot bear to think of you suffering like this!”

Though hardened by life, her soul was not stone. In that moment, she seemed more mother than cousin. Her tenderness was deeper than any affection he had known. “Some of the debts,” she confessed, “were mine. My extravagance brought this upon you. You bought jewels and trinkets to please me—oh, how I hate them now! I never imagined I could feel so.” She pulled from her cloak brooches, rings, a gold watch, and a handful of guineas, pouring them all into his lap.

The sight overwhelmed him. A strange warmth surged through his chest, melting the cold despair that had gripped him. “Dearest, kindest cousin!” he choked out. Words failed him, but his eyes spoke volumes—gratitude, love, relief.

Soon enough, he smiled. He pushed aside some of the gifts—trinkets he had once given her—and told her how he’d fallen into ruin by selling goods bought on credit. He recounted how the lawyer had just insulted him over it. “I won’t take your money,” he insisted. “I’ve enough now. But these twenty guineas—your kindness—I’ll cherish them always. More than twenty thousand pounds, they are worth to me. I’ll never forget your love. I’ll tell my mother everything. May Heaven bless you for this!”

Their conversation continued warmly, filled with hope and affection, until dusk began to fall. Then Maria rose, sighed deeply, and bid farewell. As the door closed behind her, it opened again almost immediately—to admit Mr. Draper.

“Your humble servant, sir,” he said, bowing stiffly.

But his voice grated on Harry’s nerves. The lawyer’s presence felt like an intrusion. “I expected you hours ago,” Harry said curtly.

“A lawyer’s time is not always his own,” Draper replied, still reeking of port from his earlier meal at the Grecian. “Never mind. I’m at your service now. Presume it’s all settled, Mr. Warrington. Packed your trunk? Why, you’re still in your nightgown. Let me go down and arrange things while you summon your manservant to dress you. I’ve a coach waiting. We’ll be off and dine with the old lady.”

“Are you going to dine with the Baroness de Bernstein?” Harry asked.

“No such honour for me,” Draper scoffed. “I’ve already eaten. It’s *you* who’re dining with your aunt, I suppose?”

“Mr. Draper,” Harry said, standing tall in his brocade robe, “you suppose far more than you know.”

“What in God’s name do you mean?” Draper demanded.

“I mean,” Harry replied firmly, “that I’ve considered it. And having given my word to a faithful, honourable woman, I cannot break it.”

“Confound it!” Draper shrieked. “She’s lost the paper! There’s no binding contract! She’s old enough to be—”

“Enough!” Harry thundered, stamping his foot. “You seem to think you’re speaking to some petty clerk. I take it, Mr. Draper, you’re not used to dealing with men of honour.”

“Pettifogger, indeed!” Draper roared. “Men of honour? Let me tell you, Mr. Warrington, I’m every bit as honourable as you. I may not know many gamblers or jockeys. I haven’t squandered my inheritance, lived like a nobleman on two hundred a year, or bought watches on credit only to pawn them—touch me if you dare!”

With that, he lunged toward the door.

“That’s the way out,” Harry said calmly. “You can’t go through the window—it’s barred.”

“And the answer I give to my client is—NO!” Draper screamed, slamming the door behind him.

Harry stepped forward, fists clenched. “If you utter another word,” he said, “I’ll—”

The sentence died unspoken. The door shut swiftly. Draper stormed off to Madame de Bernstein, where, despite offering her the most polished version of events, he received even harsher rebuke than he had from Harry.

“What?” Harry murmured, pacing the room in his rustling robe. “Shall she trust me—and I abandon her? No. Dear, faithful, generous woman! If I must rot in prison for years, I’ll stay true to her.”

Meanwhile, at Castlewood, the desolate Baroness sat alone at the dinner table. The chair meant for her nephew stood empty. Silver gleamed, glasses sparkled, dishes were laid one after another—yet she touched nothing. The major-domo pressed her gently.

“It is eight o’clock,” he said. “You’ve eaten nothing all day. It would do you good.”

She shook her head. “I’ll have coffee. Send Case to fetch it.”

The servants cleared the table, leaving her seated before the vacant chair. Moments later, the old servant returned—without the coffee, his face ashen with terror.

“Mr. WARRINGTON!” he gasped.

The Baroness uttered a cry, rose from her chair, then collapsed back, trembling violently.

“So you’ve come, sir?” she whispered, voice trembling with joy. “Bring back the—Ah!”

Then she screamed. “Gracious God! Who is it?”

Her eyes widened in horror. Her painted face turned deathly white. She gripped the armrests as the figure approached.

It was a gentleman—tall, fair-haired, dressed in fine clothes—whose features mirrored Harry Warrington’s exactly. His voice, when he spoke, echoed Harry’s tone in uncanny fashion.

“You expected my brother, madam?” he said politely. “I am George Warrington. I’ve just arrived in London. I went to his house and met his servant at your door, carrying this letter. I thought I’d bring it to you before going to him.”

He placed the letter on the table.

“Are you…” the Baroness breathed, “are you my nephew—thought dead?”

“Was killed—and is alive,” the stranger said quietly. “I am George Warrington. And I ask you: what have you done with my brother?”

“Look, George!” she cried, bewildered. “I waited for him tonight. That chair was set for him. I’ve been waiting… till I’m faint. I don’t like being alone. Stay and sup with me!”

“Pardon me, madam,” George replied with grave courtesy. “Please God, my supper will be with Harry tonight.”

“Bring him back! Bring him back here—on any terms! It’s only five hundred pounds! Here—take it! Take it if you need it!”

“I have no need, madam,” George said softly. “I have money. And there is no better use for it than in my brother’s service.”

“And you will bring him to me? Say you will bring him to me!”

George bowed deeply—once, formally—and left the room, passing the stunned servants, calling over his shoulder, “Gumbo! Follow me!”

Had Harry received no letters from home? Had he not read the missive announcing his brother’s escape from French territory and return to Virginia?

No. No such letter had reached him. Otherwise, surely he would have told Gumbo.

“Quick, horses!” George commanded. “To Strand! To Temple Bar! This is the House of Captivity—and the Deliverer has come!”
