I rose to continue my journey, walking countless miles across desolate sands. My heart ached for a mountain, a towering rock from whose peak I might survey the bleak plain or the dried riverbeds, searching for a sliver of hope. Yet, what purpose could such foresight serve? The true determinant of fate lies not in what lies beyond our vision, but within the man himself; the operation performed upon him is the event. To foresee is not to understand, for if prophecy were merely latent in humanity, it would surface far more often.

The sun hung halfway to the horizon when a rugged ascent appeared before me. But before I could reach it, the urge to climb evaporated, replaced by a desperate need to lie down. By then, twilight had deepened, and the air grew heavy with approaching night. At my feet lay a carpet of the softest, greenest moss—a couch fit for a king. I threw myself upon it, and weariness began to recede. The moment my head touched the ground, for the third time, I heard below me the sound of many waters. They played broken airs and ethereal harmonies against the stones of their buried channels. It was the loveliest chaos of musical material the aquatic harp could send to my ears! What masterpieces a Handel might have crafted from that ever-recurring gurgle and bell-like drip, blending them into mutually destructive yet mesmerizing melodies!

As I lay listening, my gaze wandered up and down the rocky slope rising abruptly above me. I read upon its face the record of ages past, when a cataract rushed down here, filling the channels that had led me to its foot. My heart swelled at the memory of that splendid tumult, where waves danced in helpless fall, massing their music into one great organ-roar below. Soon, the hidden brooks lulled me to sleep, their lullabies weaving themselves into my dreams.

I woke before the sun, eager to climb and see what lay beyond. Alas, there was nothing but a desert of finest sand! Not a trace remained of the river that had plunged down the rocks. The powdery drift had filled its course to the level of the dreary expanse. Looking back, I saw the river had divided into two branches as it fell: the one whose bank I had now followed to the foot of the rocky scarp, and the one I had first crossed to enter the Evil Wood. I could see the wood between the two on the far horizon. Before me and to the left, the desert stretched beyond vision, but far to the right, a lift in the skyline offered hope of the forest my hostess had directed me toward.

I sat down and reached into my pocket for the half-loaf I had brought—only then understanding what my hostess had meant. Verily, the bread was not for the morrow; it had shrunk and hardened into stone. I threw it away and set out again.

About noon, I encountered a few tamarisk and juniper trees, then some stunted firs. As I proceeded, thicker thickets and larger firs met me, until finally I stood in a forest of pines and other trees just like that where the Little Ones found their babies. I believed I had returned to a farther portion of the same place. But what mattered *where* when *everywhere* was the same as *nowhere*? I had not yet, by doing something within it, made *anywhere* into a place. I was not yet alive; I was only dreaming that I lived. I was but a consciousness with an outlook! Truly, I had been nothing else in the world I had left, but now I knew the fact! I told myself that if in this forest I caught the faint gleam of the mirror, I would turn far aside lest it should entrap me unawares and give me back to my old existence. Here, I might learn to be something by doing something! I could not endure the thought of returning with so many beginnings and no end achieved. The Little Ones would meet their appointed fate; the awful witch I would never meet; the dead would ripen and arise without me. I would only wake to know that I had dreamed, and that all my going was nowhither. I would rather go on and on than come to such a close!

I went deeper into the wood; I was weary and intended to rest within it.

The trees were now large, standing in a regular, almost geometric fashion with roomy spaces between. There was little undergrowth, and I could see a long way in every direction. The forest was like a great church—solemn, silent, and empty, for I met nothing on two feet or four that day. Now and then, a swift thing or a slow thing would cross the space where my eye happened to settle, but always at a distance, enhancing only the sense of wideness and vacancy. I heard a few birds and saw plenty of butterflies, some of marvellously gorgeous coloring and combinations, others of a pure, dazzling whiteness.

Coming to a spot where the pines stood farther apart, allowing flowering shrubs to thrive, and hoping it signaled a nearby dwelling, I took the direction where yet more and more roses grew. I was hungry for the voice and face of my kind—for any living soul, human or otherwise, which I might in some measure understand. What a hell of horror, I thought, to wander alone, a bare existence never going out of itself, never widening its life in another life, but bound by the cords of its poor peculiarities, lying an eternal prisoner in the dungeon of its own being! I began to learn that it was impossible to live for oneself, save in the presence of others—and then, alas, fearfully possible! Evil was only through good; selfishness but a parasite on the tree of life! In my own world, I had the habit of solitary song; here, not a crooning murmur ever parted my lips! There, I sang without thinking; here, I thought without singing! There, I had never had a bosom-friend; here, the affection of an idiot would be divinely welcome. "If only I had a dog to love!" I sighed, regarding with wonder my past self, which preferred the company of book or pen to that of man or woman; which, if the author of a tale I was enjoying appeared, would wish him away so I might return to his story. I had chosen the dead rather than the living, the thing thought rather than the thing thinking. "Any man," I said now, "is more than the greatest of books!" I had not cared for my live brothers and sisters, and now I was left without even the dead to comfort me!

The wood thinned yet more, and the pines grew larger, sending up huge stems like columns eager to support the heavens. More trees of other kinds appeared; the forest was growing richer! The roses were now trees, their flowers of astonishing splendour.

Suddenly, I spied what seemed a great house or castle. But its forms were so strangely indistinct that I could not be certain it was more than a chance combination of tree-shapes. As I drew nearer, its lines held together, yet neither they nor the body of it grew more definite. When at length I stood in front of it, I remained as doubtful of its nature as before. Habitable house or castle, it certainly was not; it might be a ruin overgrown with ivy and roses! Yet of building hidden in the foliage, not the poorest wall-remnant could I discern. Again and again, I seemed to descry what must be building, but it always vanished before closer inspection. Could it be, I pondered, that the ivy had embraced a huge edifice and consumed it, its interlaced branches retaining the shapes of the walls it had assimilated? I could be sure of nothing concerning its appearance.

Before me was a rectangular vacancy—the ghost of a doorway without a door. I stepped through it and found myself in an open space like a great hall, its floor covered with grass and flowers, its walls and roof of ivy and vine, mingled with roses.

There could be no better place to pass the night! I gathered a quantity of withered leaves, laid them in a corner, and threw myself upon them. A red sunset filled the hall; the night was warm, and my couch restful. I lay gazing up at the living ceiling, with its tracery of branches and twigs, its clouds of foliage, and peeping patches of loftier roof. My eyes waded about as if tangled in it until the sun was down and the sky began to grow dark. Then the red roses turned black, and soon only the yellow and white were visible. When they vanished, the stars came instead, hanging in the leaves like live topazes, throbbing and sparkling and flashing many colors. I was canopied by a tree from Aladdin's cave!

Then I discovered that it was full of nests, from which tiny heads, nearly indistinguishable, kept popping out with a chirp or two and disappearing again. For a while there were rustlings and stirrings and little prayers; but as the darkness grew, the small heads became still. At last, every feathered mother had her brood quiet under her wings. The talk in the little beds was over, and God's bird-nursery was at rest beneath the waves of sleep. Once more, a few flutterings made me look up: an owl went sailing across. I had only a glimpse of him, but several times felt the cool wafture of his silent wings. The mother birds did not move again; they saw that he was looking for mice, not children.

About midnight, I came wide awake, roused by a revelry whose noises were yet not loud. Neither were they distant; they were close to me, but attenuated. My eyes were so dazzled, however, that for a while I could see nothing; at last, they came to themselves.

I was lying on my withered leaves in the corner of a splendid hall. Before me was a crowd of gorgeously dressed men and gracefully robed women, none of whom seemed to see me. In dance after dance, they vaguely embodied the story of life, its meetings, its passions, its partings. A student of Shakespeare, I had learned something of every dance alluded to in his plays, and hence partially understood several of those I now saw—the minuet, the pavane, the hey, the coranto, the lavolta. The dancers were attired in fashion as ancient as their dances.

A moon had risen while I slept and was shining through the countless-windowed roof; but her light was crossed by so many shadows that at first I could distinguish almost nothing of the faces of the multitude. I could not fail, however, to perceive that there was something odd about them. I sat up to see them better. Heavens! Could I call them faces? They were skull fronts—hard, gleaming bone, bare jaws, truncated noses, lipless teeth which could no more take part in any smile! Of these, some flashed set and white and murderous; others were clouded with decay, broken and gapped, colored of the earth in which they seemed so long to have lain. Fearful yet, the eye-sockets were not empty. In each was a lidless living eye! In those wrecks of faces, eyes of every color, shape, and expression glowed or flashed or sparkled. The beautiful, proud eye, dark and lustrous, condescending to whatever it rested upon, was the more terrible; the lovely, languishing eye, the more repulsive; while the dim, sad eyes, less at variance with their setting, were sad exceedingly, and drew the heart in spite of the horror out of which they gazed.

I rose and went among the apparitions, eager to understand something of their being and belongings. Were they souls, or were they and their rhythmic motions but phantasms of what had been? By look nor by gesture, not by the slightest break in the measure, did they show themselves aware of me. I was not present to them: how much were they in relation to each other? Surely they saw their companions as I saw them! Or was each only dreaming itself and the rest? Did they know each other as they appeared to the others—a death with living eyes? Had they used their faces, not for communication, not to utter thought and feeling, not to share existence with their neighbors, but to appear what they wished to appear and conceal what they were? And, having made their faces masks, were they therefore deprived of those masks, condemned to go without faces until they repented?

"How long must they flaunt their facelessness in faceless eyes?" I wondered. "How long will the frightful punishment endure? Have they at length begun to love and be wise? Have they yet yielded to the shame that has found them?"

I heard not a word, saw not a movement of one naked mouth. Were they because of lying bereft of speech? With their eyes, they spoke as if longing to be understood: was it truth or falsehood that spoke in their eyes? They seemed to know one another: did they see one skull beautiful, and another plain? Difference must be there, and they had had long study of skulls!

My body was to theirs no obstacle: was I a body, and were they but forms? Or was I but a form, and were they bodies? The moment one of the dancers came close against me, that moment he or she was on the other side of me, and I could tell, without seeing, which, whether man or woman, had passed through my house.

On many of the skulls, the hair held its place, and however dressed, or in itself however beautiful, to my eyes it looked frightful on the bones of the forehead and temples. In such cases, the outer ear often remained also, and at its tip, the jewel of the ear as Sidney calls it, would hang, glimmering, gleaming, or sparkling—pearl or opal or diamond—under the night of brown or of raven locks, the sunrise of golden ripples, or the moonshine of pale, interclouded, fluffy cirrus clouds—all lichenous on the ivory-white or damp-yellow naked bone. I looked down and saw the daintily domed instep; I looked up and saw the plump shoulders basing the spring of the round, full neck—which withered at half-height to the fluted shaft of a gibbous cranium.
