I touched upon my lady a moment ago. Now, logic dictates that the Diamond could never have vanished from our household if it had not first been gifted to my lady's daughter. And that daughter, in turn, would never have existed to receive such a gift without the labor and pain of my lady bringing her into the world. Therefore, if we trace our inquiry backward to my lady, we shall certainly begin far enough back to catch the thief. And let me assure you, when one undertakes a task as complex as this, finding a solid starting point is a genuine comfort.

Those familiar with the high society of the day will know the three renowned Miss Herncastles: Adelaide, Caroline, and Julia—the youngest and, in my humble opinion, the finest of the trio. I possessed ample opportunity to judge their character, as you shall soon discover. I entered the service of their father, the old Lord, at the tender age of fifteen, serving as a page-boy in attendance upon the three honorable young ladies. I remained in that capacity until Miss Julia wed the late Sir John Verinder. He was an excellent man, though one who required management; and, between ourselves, he found someone to manage him. He prospered under her care, grew stout and content, and lived happily until the day she gently relieved him of his final breath and closed his eyes forever.

I must omit to mention that I accompanied the bride to her husband's estate. "Sir John," she declared, "I cannot function without Gabriel Betteredge." To which Sir John replied, "My lady, neither can I." Thus was my fate sealed. It mattered little to me where I served, provided my mistress and I were united.

Noticing that my lady took a keen interest in agricultural affairs and the management of the farms, I naturally adopted the same passion, especially since I was the seventh son of a small farmer myself. She placed me under the supervision of the bailiff, I performed my duties to the best of my ability, satisfied everyone, and earned my promotion. Years later, on a Monday, my lady remarked, "Sir John, your current bailiff is a senile old fool. Pension him generously and appoint Gabriel Betteredge to his place." By Tuesday, Sir John had agreed: "My lady, the bailiff is pensioned; Gabriel Betteredge takes his post." You hear much of couples living in misery; here is a testament to the contrary. Let it serve as a warning to some and encouragement to others. Meanwhile, I shall proceed with my narrative.

Thus, I found myself in a position of trust and honor, residing in a modest cottage of my own. My mornings were filled with rounds across the estate, my afternoons with accounts, and my evenings with a pipe and a copy of *Robinson Crusoe*. What more could a man desire to ensure happiness? Recall what Adam sought when he was alone in the Garden of Eden; if you do not blame Adam, do not blame me.

The woman who caught my eye was the housekeeper at my cottage, Selina Goby. I align with the late William Cobbett regarding the selection of a wife: observe that she chews her food thoroughly and places her feet firmly upon the earth when walking, and you are safe. Selina Goby excelled in both regards, which was one reason for my proposal. I had another, purely practical motive. As a single woman, she charged me a weekly fee for her board and services. As my wife, she could not charge me, and would instead provide her labor gratis. This was my perspective: economy tempered with affection. I presented this argument to my mistress as a matter of duty.

"I have been contemplating Selina Goby," I stated, "and I believe, my lady, it would be more economical to marry her than to retain her services."

My lady erupted in laughter, declaring she could not decide whether to be more shocked by my language or my principles. Some jest tickled her fancy, a sort of wit reserved for those of quality. Understanding nothing of the joke, I simply proceeded to act on my conviction. And what did Selina say? Good heavens, how little you must know of women if you expect otherwise. Of course, she said yes.

As the wedding date approached, and discussions arose regarding a new coat for the ceremony, my confidence wavered. I have consulted with other men in similar predicaments; they all admitted that roughly a week prior to the event, they privately wished to escape their situation. I went even further; I literally rose up and attempted to withdraw. For nothing! A man of justice cannot expect to exit such a contract without compensation. Adhering to the law, and after careful deliberation, I offered Selina Goby a feather-bed and fifty shillings to dissolve the engagement. You will scarcely believe it, yet it is true—she was foolish enough to refuse.

After that, the matter was settled. I procured the cheapest coat available and endured the remainder of the process with equal frugality. We were neither a happy nor a miserable couple; we were six of one and half a dozen of the other. I cannot explain why, but we seemed constantly to obstruct one another's path. When I ascended the stairs, she descended; when she sought to go down, I came up. Such is married life, according to my experience.

Five years of such stairway misunderstandings passed before Providence, in its infinite wisdom, relieved us of each other by taking my wife. I was left with my daughter, Penelope, and no other child. Shortly thereafter, Sir John passed away, leaving my lady with her own daughter, Miss Rachel, and no other heir. I have written in vain if you need to be reminded that my little Penelope was cared for under my mistress's direct supervision, sent to school, educated, and eventually promoted to become Miss Rachel's personal maid.

As for myself, I continued my duties as bailiff year after year until Christmas 1847, when a change occurred in my life. On that day, my lady invited herself to tea in my cottage. She noted that, counting from the year I began as a page-boy under the old lord, I had served her for over fifty years. She then handed me a magnificent woolen waistcoat, woven by her own hands, to keep me warm against the biting winter chill.

I received this splendid gift at a loss for words to express my gratitude for the honor she bestowed. To my astonishment, however, the waistcoat was not merely an honor but a bribe. My lady had discerned that I was aging before I had realized it myself. She had come to my cottage to wheedle me—though perhaps "persuade" is a kinder term—into relinquishing my arduous outdoor work as bailiff and accepting a life of ease as her house steward. I fought this indignity as best I could. Yet, my mistress knew my weakness; she framed the offer as a favor to herself. The dispute concluded with me wiping my eyes, like a simpleton, with my new woolen garment, and promising to consider the matter.

The turmoil in my mind regarding this decision was truly dreadful once my lady departed. I applied the remedy I have never yet found to fail in times of doubt and emergency: I smoked a pipe and turned to *Robinson Crusoe*. Within five minutes of reading that extraordinary book, I encountered a comforting passage on page one hundred and fifty-eight: "Today we love, what tomorrow we hate." My path became clear instantly. Today, I favored remaining the farm-bailiff; tomorrow, citing *Robinson Crusoe*, I would be entirely opposed. By taking myself tomorrow while in tomorrow's mood, the matter was resolved. Having thus relieved my conscience, I slept that night as Lady Verinder's farm-bailiff and awoke the next morning as her house-steward. All quite comfortable, and all thanks to *Robinson Crusoe*!

My daughter Penelope has just glanced over my shoulder to see my progress. She remarks that my writing is beautiful and every word true. However, she points out a significant flaw. She states that what I have written so far bears no resemblance to what was requested. I am asked to recount the story of the Diamond, yet I have been narrating the chronicle of my own life. This is curious and beyond my comprehension to explain. I wonder if the gentlemen who make a living writing books ever find their own selves intruding upon their subjects as I have? If they do, I can empathize with them. For now, here is another false start and more wasted paper. What is to be done? Nothing, I suppose, but for you to maintain your temper and for me to begin this tale anew for the third time.
