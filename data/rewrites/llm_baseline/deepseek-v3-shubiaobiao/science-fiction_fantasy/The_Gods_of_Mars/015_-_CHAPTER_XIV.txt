The words struck me like a thunderbolt. *My son.* Carthoris stood before me, his face alight with hesitant hope. Now that I truly looked at him, I saw it—the sharp, noble lines of his mother’s beauty, tempered by the strength of my own. His gray eyes held the same fire, the same unyielding resolve.

He hesitated, torn between disbelief and longing.

"Tell me of your mother," I said, my voice rough with emotion. "Tell me of the years fate stole from us."

With a cry, he crossed the distance between us in an instant, his arms wrapping around me. For a breath, I held him tight, feeling the weight of lost time. The sting in my eyes shamed me not at all. A warrior may weep for love without sacrificing his strength.

"You move like her descriptions," Carthoris said when we parted. "Your bearing, your skill with the blade—but it was your first words that convinced me. No one else would speak of her so."

"She has never left my thoughts," I admitted. "Not for a moment. What of her, all these years?"

"Unchanged," he said softly. "Or so say those who knew her longest. Yet when she believes herself unseen, her face grows sorrowful. She mourns you, Father. All Helium mourns with her." His voice tightened with pride. "They honor your name. Every year, they celebrate the day you gave Barsoom life—yet there are tears in their gratitude."

"And your name?" I asked.

"Helium wished to name me after you. But Mother refused. She said you had chosen it together." He smiled. "Carthoris. A blend of her and you."

Xodar’s voice cut through the moment. "The ship falters," he called from the helm. "The bow’s wound has opened a forward tank."

The damage was worse than I feared. The bow sagged, forcing us into an unsustainable angle. Even at full speed, we were losing altitude. Carthoris and Xodar worked frantically to stem the leak, but the escaping rays drained our buoyancy.

By dawn, the vessel listed violently. The deck tilted, sending us scrambling for handholds. As the bow plunged earthward, I triggered the repulsion rays, slowing our fall. We landed with a jolt, the ship groaning like a wounded beast.

Northward, hills rose—a better refuge than the open waste. We reached their shelter by midday, finding solace among the flowering milk-shrubs. There, exhaustion claimed me.

I awoke to Thuvia’s hands clasping mine, her lips pressing fervent kisses to my skin. "My Prince!" she gasped. "I thought you dead!"

Behind her, Carthoris and Xodar stirred, the boy eyeing her with wary surprise. Thuvia seemed oblivious, her devotion unabashed.

"You forget yourself," I said gently, disentangling. "Dejah Thoris is my wife."

"I forget nothing," she replied, undeterred. "I ask only to serve you—as your slave, if you permit it."

The Martian custom was no stranger to me, yet it unsettled my Earth-born sensibilities. "If we reach Helium, you shall go as my equal," I said firmly. "Not a slave. You’ll find suitors enough there."

Her face fell, but she bowed. "As you command."

"Where is Tars Tarkas?" I asked, shifting the subject.

Her expression darkened. "The Warhoons took him. I saw them drag him into their city—wounded, perhaps dead."

"Then we go tonight," I said. "I’ll find him."

Carthoris and Xodar protested, but I silenced them. "This requires stealth, not force. I go alone."

As darkness fell, I slipped into the dead city. The nearer moon cast silver light over crumbling spires, guiding my path. The green horde clustered near the central plaza, their thoats grunting in courtyards. I moved like a shadow, hugging walls, pausing at every intersection.

Near the plaza, warriors streamed toward a great hall—likely their chieftain’s seat. I needed to reach it.

Darting through deserted buildings, I entered a courtyard teeming with restless thoats. The stench of moss and hide filled the air. Hugging the eastern wall, I scaled a balcony, peering into lit chambers where green men lounged.

A warrior’s voice carried through an open window: "Bring the Thark before Kab Kadja."

My pulse quickened. If they moved Tars Tarkas, I could follow.

I trailed them down spiral stairs into the pits—a maze of black corridors. Their torch flickered ahead, my only guide. At a junction, they vanished. Panic clawed at me until I heard chains clank down the middle passage.

Blindly, I pursued. Then—silence. The light vanished. Lost, I retraced my steps, only to find branching paths I hadn’t seen before.

Then came the sound.

A shuffle. A drag.

I turned.

Eyes burned in the dark.

Fiery, lidless, unwavering.

The same eyes that had haunted me in the Warhoon dungeons years ago—the eyes of the unseen feeders on the dead.

My sword slid free. I retreated, but the eyes advanced.

Then another pair appeared to my right.

Another to my left.

The hunt had reversed.

Now *I* was the prey.
