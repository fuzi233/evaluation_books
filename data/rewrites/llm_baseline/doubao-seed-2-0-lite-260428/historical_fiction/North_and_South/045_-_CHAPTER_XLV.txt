“Where are the sounds that swam along
The buoyant air when I was young;
The last vibration now is o’er,
And they who listened are no more;
Ah! let me close my eyes and dream.”

The memory of Helstone had risen unbidden to Mr. Bell’s waking mind mid-conversation with Mr. Lennox the day before, and it had run wild through every corner of his dreams all night long. He was young again, not a respected college Fellow but a lowly tutor, lingering through the long vacation as the guest of his newly married, giddy friend: the proud, overjoyed new Vicar of Helstone, Richard Hale. The two of them bounded across babbling little brooks in wild, impossible leaps, that hung suspended mid-air for what felt like whole sunlit days. Time and space melted away, even as every other detail felt sharp and true. No event followed the rules of real life, measured not by clock or calendar but by the soft swell of shared emotion, for none of it was real — and yet it all felt more vivid than his waking hours. The beech trees blazed in rich autumn crimson, the warm scents of wild herb and late blooming rose drifted thick and sweet on the breeze, and the young Mrs. Hale moved through the little parsonage with that exact familiar mixture of faint, playful irritation at their narrow household means, and unashamed pride in her handsome, adoring husband, that Bell had observed in real life a full quarter of a century prior.

The dream was so lifelike that when he jolted awake, his whole present life felt like the false, hollow fantasy. Where was he? Stuck in the close, over-furnished, stuffy rooms of a central London hotel! Where were the friends who had been laughing, speaking, brushing his sleeve not a heartbeat before? Dead. Buried. Lost forever, as far as any mortal “forever” could stretch. He was an old man now, his joints aching, even though only moments ago in the dream he had been brimming with the untested strength of youth. The utter, crushing loneliness of his life was too much to bear for a single second longer sitting still, so he stumbled out of bed and dressed at a rushed, clumsy pace, desperate to outrun the ghosts of the past and make it to the breakfast appointment in Harley Street.

He could barely focus on the fine details of Lennox’s legal presentation, even as he watched Margaret’s eyes grow wide and bright with tension, her lips blanching one by one, as every last scrap of evidence that might have exonerated Frederick Hale crumbled out from under them, one after another, as if fate itself had decreed no such proof could remain. Even Mr. Lennox, whose professional tone was always so measured and unshakable, softened into a gentle, tender cadence as he drew close to laying the last of their hopes to rest. It was not that Margaret had not known this outcome was likely all along: it was the relentless, sharp minuteness of every successive disappointment, laid bare one after another, that finally wore down her resolve, and she gave way to quiet, shaking tears.

Mr. Lennox set his papers down at once. “I ought not to go on with this,” he said, his voice thick with concern. “This was a foolish, ill-advised proposal of mine. Lieutenant Hale —” he used the naval title he had been so harshly stripped of, a small, soft gesture of respect that soothed Margaret more than any other comfort could — “is happy now. His fortune and his future across the sea are far more secure than anything he ever could have hoped for in the Royal Navy, and no doubt he has long since adopted his wife’s homeland as his own.”

“That is all true,” Margaret managed, trying to force a wobbly smile through her tears. “I feel so selfish for grieving as I do. But he is lost to me, and I am so very lonely.”

Mr. Lennox shuffled his papers into a neat stack, and silently wished with all his might that he was already as wealthy and successful as he was certain he would be in the years to come, so he could wave away every last one of her troubles. Mr. Bell blew his nose loudly into his handkerchief, and otherwise kept quiet, giving her space to collect herself. In a minute or two, Margaret had regained her usual quiet composure, and thanked Mr. Lennox with a courtesy and grace that was almost exaggerated, for she was all too aware that her tears might have led him to believe he had inflicted unnecessary pain. And yet, for all the sharp ache it carried, she would not have missed that full, honest reckoning with her loss for anything.

Mr. Bell shuffled over to her to say his goodbyes, his fumbling gout-ridden fingers tangling in his thick leather gloves. “Margaret,” he said, “I’m heading down to Helstone tomorrow, to go look over the old place. Would you like to come with me? Or would it be far too painful? Speak plain, don’t mince words on my account.”

“Oh, Mr. Bell —” Margaret could not get any more words past her tight throat. She only lifted his gnarled, swollen old hand and pressed a soft kiss to it.

“Come now, that’s quite enough of that,” he grumbled, his face flushing bright pink with awkward pleasure. “I’m sure your aunt Shaw will trust you in my care. We’ll leave first thing tomorrow morning, get there around two o’clock, I reckon. We’ll grab a light snack, order dinner at the little inn — the Lennard Arms, that’s what it used to be called — and then go wander the forest to work up an appetite. Can you bear it, Margaret? It’ll be a trial for both of us, I know, but it’ll be a pleasure to me, at the very least. We’ll dine there on roast venison, if the innkeeper still has access to the forest deer, and then I’ll take my usual afternoon nap while you go out and call on any remaining old friends from the parsonage days. I’ll bring you back safe and sound, barring railway accidents, and I’ll insure your life for a thousand pounds before we start out, that ought to be some comfort to your relations. I’ll have you back with Mrs. Shaw by lunchtime on Friday, no later. So if you say yes, I’ll go straight upstairs and put the plan to her.”

“There’s no way I can possibly tell you how much I want to go,” Margaret said, the tears still dripping down her cheeks.

“Alright then, prove your gratitude by drying those tear ducts up for the next two days,” Bell ordered gruffly. “If you don’t, I’ll get all wobbly lipped myself, and I hate that more than anything.”

“I won’t shed a single more drop,” Margaret promised, winking hard to shake the leftover tears off her eyelashes and forcing a bright, steady smile.

“That’s my good girl. Now let’s go upstairs and sort all of this out.”

Margaret was trembling with quiet, eager hope as Mr. Bell laid out his plan to Aunt Shaw, who went through every predictable stage of reaction: first startled, then doubtful, then hopelessly perplexed over whether the excursion was at all proper for a young unmarried woman. In the end she yielded far more to Mr. Bell’s gruff, earnest good sense than to her own uncertain misgivings, for right up until the day Margaret returned safe and sound, she could not make up her own mind if the trip was right or wrong, appropriate or unseemly. By the time the pair stepped back through the Harley Street door, the project had proved such a glowing success that Aunt Shaw declared without hesitation that it had been the kindest, most thoughtful idea Mr. Bell could possibly have had, and exactly the rest Margaret needed after all the long, anxious months of trouble she had weathered.
