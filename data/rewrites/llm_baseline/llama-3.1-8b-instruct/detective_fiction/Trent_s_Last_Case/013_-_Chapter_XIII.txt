In the two months that followed, Trent's encounters with Mrs. Manderson had left an indelible mark on his psyche. Each meeting had been a masterclass in calculated reserve, a delicate balance between polite acquaintance and the tentative steps towards familiarity. He recalled the opera, where he had chanced upon her in the company of Mrs. Wallace, a woman he had known since childhood. Mrs. Manderson's effortless ease in the midst of strangers, her effortless rapport with his acquaintances, had only served to heighten his bewilderment. 

As he reflected on their conversations, Trent couldn't shake off the feeling that he had been woefully out of his depth. His own words, once so fluent and confident, had deserted him, reduced to inane chatter as he found himself monopolizing the attention of Mrs. Wallace. And yet, when he turned to Mrs. Manderson, her expression remained serene, her manner unruffled. It was as if she had witnessed his awkwardness with the detachment of a scholar studying a specimen. He recalled the sensation of being trapped behind them in the box, mesmerized by the sweep of her dark hair, the curve of her shoulder, her hand resting on the velvet cushion. The memory still sent a shiver down his spine.

Subsequent meetings had seen him regain his composure, but the underlying tension remained. He sensed that she was aware of the truth – that she had deciphered the cryptic hints in his manuscript, that she had grasped the implications of his questions. And yet, she treated him with the same detached courtesy, as if he were just another of the countless men who had crossed her path. It was this dichotomy that had begun to unsettle him, that had led him to suspect that some injury had been inflicted, and that she had perceived it. 

Trent's introspection had yielded two resolutions. Firstly, he decided that he would retreat from the situation, abandoning his London lodgings to escape the unrelenting strain. He no longer yearned for clarity, no longer sought to validate his hunches about Manderson's death. Cupples' return to London had brought no new revelations, and Trent had come to accept that his words – spoken with such emphasis – had been prophetic: 'So long as she considered herself bound to him, no power on earth could have persuaded her.'
