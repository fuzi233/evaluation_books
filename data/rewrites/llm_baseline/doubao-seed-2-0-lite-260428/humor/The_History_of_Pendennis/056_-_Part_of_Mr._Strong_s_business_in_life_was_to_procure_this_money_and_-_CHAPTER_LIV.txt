CHAPTER LIV. Convalescence (or, The Fever That Cured Pen Of Falling For Porters’ Daughters)

Look, we’ve got a deeply embarrassing fact about our boy Arthur Pendennis to spill here, and no, we can’t skip it—you signed up for his unvarnished memoirs, you get the messy bits too. There he was, flat on his back with raging fever, half delirious and head over heels mooning over Fanny Bolton, the porter’s daughter. The doctors bleed him, blister him, shave half his pretty dandy curls off, pump him full of every vile tonic in the apothecary’s cupboard… and lo and behold, when the physical fever broke, the love fever vanished too. Pen woke up one morning, stared at the ceiling, and realized he was no more smitten with Fanny than you or I are smitten with the guy who sells us morning sausages.

He snickered into his pillow at that second unexpected cure, and immediately did his usual weird little self-autopsy, poking at his dead passion like a kid dissecting a road beetle. What on earth had possessed him to be so hot and bothered over her? It wasn’t her wit, she couldn’t tell a sonnet from a shopping list. It wasn’t her breeding, she’d grown up in a porter’s lodge by the Temple gate. It wasn’t even her looks—half the girls selling flowers on the Strand were prettier. The whole silly passion had grown out of Pen’s own overheated brain, not out of anything special about Fanny. She was exactly the same nice, plain little thing she’d always been. His fever-addled eyes had just seen a goddess where there was a perfectly ordinary girl, and now his eyes were back to normal. He felt nothing but gentle goodwill towards her, same as he’d feel towards a stray cat that wandered into his rooms, and he sure as hell didn’t want to see her again. The best part? He hadn’t done anything terribly stupid when he was out of his mind with that misplaced passion, so he didn’t even have to torture himself with guilt. It was like he’d wandered right up to the edge of a cliff when he was delirious, then woken up safe in bed, and he was half relieved, half mortified that he’d ever nearly fallen off.

But while Pen was busy patting himself on the back for escaping the silliest fling of his life, his mom Helen was at his bedside every second, radiating soft, tired love that made Pen feel as small and safe as he’d been when he was seven years old and scraped his knee at Fairoaks. She never left his side, ready to fetch him any silly little thing he asked for at 3 a.m., as if tending to his every whim was the only joy she needed in the whole world.

Pen had misty, half-forgotten snippets of memory of Fanny being there to nurse him through the worst of the delirium, but they were so blurry he couldn’t tell them apart from the weird fever dreams where he’d ridden a giant hedgehog to the moon. Since he’d never bothered to mention his little Fanny crush to his mom before, obviously he couldn’t sit her down now and explain “hey mom, that girl you saw tending me when I was sick? I was wild for her two weeks ago and now I don’t care, no big deal.” That tiny little lack of quick chat? It landed everyone involved in weeks of total unnecessary pain. Helen had walked in, seen Fanny tending her feverish boy, and immediately jumped to the absolute worst possible conclusion. The poor woman was convinced Pen had done something terrible and dishonorable to the girl, that his physical fever was nothing compared to the moral stain he’d come home with. She bit down on that fear every single day, forcing a bright smile while her heart ached, because she couldn’t bear to confront him about it when he was still too weak to sit up.

Over in Boulogne, Captain Shandon was reading the latest issue of the *Pall Mall Gazette* with his morning brandy, and snickered to his wife Mary that Jack Finucane’s goofy, soft-handed editor bits were gone. “That’s Warrington’s work now, mark my words. I know that man’s whip crack anywhere. Bludyer writes like a butcher with a cleaver, hacking the subject to pieces. Warrington? He slices a man neat, straight down the back, draws blood every single line he writes.” Mary Shandon swatted his arm and told him not to be so gruesome, that Warrington was a perfect gentleman who’d given their kids sugar lumps last time he was in town. Shandon shrugged, poured himself another drink, and said Warrington was kind to kids but a savage to anyone dumb enough to argue with him, and that they’d all be back slogging away at newspaper work soon enough, much to his annoyance.

Sure enough, Warrington had stepped in to cover every single one of Pen’s newspaper shifts, working like a man possessed. He wrote theatre reviews, literary takedowns, snarky little society notes, all with that signature sharp, brutal wit of his. But whenever he stopped by to check on Pen’s family, he wouldn’t shut up about how graceful and light and elegant Pen’s writing was, the sweetest over-praise any friend could ever hand out. “The country’s full of numbskulls who don’t know what good style is,” he told Helen, “I bred that boy’s writing myself, and I’m as proud of him as you are. Yeah, he’s a little vain, a little selfish, spends way too much on silk cravats, but he’s honest, he’s loyal, he wouldn’t hurt a fly.” Helen sighed deep, Laura’s chest felt tight with quiet affection, and both of them loved Warrington even more for loving their Pen.

Even Major Pendennis, who never gushed about anyone unless they were a member of the aristocracy, was singing Warrington’s praises at the top of his lungs. “Every inch a gentleman, my dear! Suffolk Warringtons, baronets back to Charles the First! His old dad was Sir Miles, friend of the Prince of Wales, couldn’t turn out anything less than perfect. That man’s got talent enough to climb straight to the top of everything, if he ever bothered to put his mind to it.”

Laura blushed bright pink when he said that, because she’d spent the last three days staring at Warrington’s dark, brooding eyes and decided on no evidence whatsoever that he’d been left at the altar by a cruel heiress and was now doomed to be a tragic lonely hero forever. She caught herself daydreaming about his sad secret backstory and immediately felt her cheeks heat up so hot she could have fried an egg on them.

Warrington rented cheap chambers right around the corner in Flag Court, so after he finished bashing out three newspaper articles before noon, he’d wander over to Pen’s place for the sunny autumn evenings, and more than once he offered Laura his arm for a stroll in Temple Gardens. When Laura asked Helen if that was all right, the Major practically bounced out of his chair. “Of course it’s all right! Everyone walks there! There’s beadles! No one will think twice about it!” Helen couldn’t argue after that, especially since Laura came back from the walks bright-cheeked and laughing, far happier than she’d been since they got the telegram about Pen’s fever.

You see, Helen and Laura had already had a proper cry together on the carriage ride up from Fairoaks. When they’d heard Pen was at death’s door, Laura had refused to stay behind, admitted through sobs to Helen that she’d been head over heels in love with Pen for years, that even if he’d done something so stupid they could never marry, she still deserved to be there to nurse him. The two of them cried so hard on the road they soaked three handkerchiefs each, and that shared grief made all their old little arguments melt away.

Poor Fanny Bolton, meanwhile, never stood a chance against those two ladies’ combined moral judgment. Women are unforgiving as hanging judges when they think another girl has messed around with a man who belongs to one of their own, right? The narrator isn’t even pretending to argue with that—we’re all fully on team “sew the bad girl up in a sack and toss her in the Bosphorus” over here. But come on, ladies, at least make 100% sure you’ve got the right girl in the sack before you heave it over the side, okay? We don’t want to execute an innocent porter’s daughter by accident.

The Major, for his part, was 100% behind Warrington and Laura hanging out as much as possible. If there was an art exhibition on, he insisted Warrington take her. If Warrington had suggested taking her all the way to Vauxhall Gardens for fireworks, the old man would have waved them off without a second thought. The two of them were perfect company for each other, anyway: Warrington had never spent time around a girl as sweet and unworldly as Laura, and Laura had never met a man as sharp, funny, and unpretentious as Warrington. His rough, no-fuss energy was such a nice change from Pen’s dandified, bored little sneer, that it made Pen’s usual spoiled, languid self look even sillier than usual. The women of Fairoaks had spoiled Pen rotten, after all, coddled him so much that he was bored of every little homage they paid him.

Wait a minute, though—if Pen was that much of a spoiled, lazy little hero-wannabe, why did Laura love him so much? Because what else was she supposed to do? She spent 12 years at Fairoaks with only Dr. Portman and that boring old army guy Captain Glanders for company, Pen was the only remotely charming man she’d ever seen, she’d been hearing his mom sing his praises since she was five years old, and it’s not like anyone falls in love with a portrait in the Royal Academy or a drawing in the Illustrated London News. You fall in love with the person you’re next to, the one who shares their jam tart with you, the one who climbs up a tree to save a baby bird instead of stealing its nest, the one who gives his last shilling to the old beggar woman on the common. You get attached to that image of him in your head, and you hold onto it tight for years, even when you meet far more interesting people later on.

Those quiet autumn evenings in Lamb Court were so cozy, none of them realized how fast the time was flying. The Major was later telling everyone at his club that September in empty London was “doosid pleasant, begad.” He wheezed up the three flights of black stone stairs every afternoon, bringing little fancy jellies and clear soups he’d bullied the famous chef at Bays’s coffee house into making for the invalid. When the doctor said Pen could have a little sherry, the Major showed up with a case of priceless Amontillado that the Marquis of Steyne had given him, a gift straight from King Ferdinand of Spain. Helen and Laura took one tiny sip and thought it tasted like bitter, nutty old dishwater, but Pen perked right up, Warrington declared it the best sherry he’d ever tasted, and they all made silly speeches toasting the Major and Lord Steyne and the entire British aristocracy, right as Doctor Goodenough walked in for his free evening visit and found them all cackling around Pen’s armchair.

Percy Sibwright, who lived in the chambers below, wrote them a flowery note saying they could use all his stuff whenever they wanted, his carpets, his bed, his piano, whatever. That piano was a wobbly little thing that Sibwright played very badly, and Laura sat down at it one evening, blushing bright pink, and started singing old country ballads from home in her lovely rich contralto. Warrington couldn’t tell “God Save The King” from a sea shanty, but he sat there completely rapt, staring at her like she was the greatest musician who’d ever lived.

If you’d looked up at their windows that evening, you’d have seen a pale little girl in a plain black bonnet standing under the lamp-post, Fanny Bolton, lingering there to listen to the music drifting out. No one noticed her. When Pen’s bedtime rolled around, the singing stopped, they led him off to his bedroom, and then the rest of them played cards or backgammon, while Laura stitched a pair of soft worsted slippers that could have been for any one of the three men in the room, all three of whom would have fought over who got them.

Just before ten, the chimes of St. Clement’s Church started pealing out their psalm tune, Martha the maid from Fairoaks got out her bed candles, the Major yelped “Good heavens, is it that late already?” and everyone said their goodnights. Martha locked the front door behind the two men, and bragged later that if any thief had tried to break in, she’d have grabbed that fancy old Damascus scimitar Sibwright brought back from his Levant travels, the one he wore to a fancy dress party at Gloucester Square, and hacked the intruder to bits. Good night, sweet Laura, sleep well. Nobody’s breaking in here, not when you’ve got half the Temple wrapped around your little finger.
