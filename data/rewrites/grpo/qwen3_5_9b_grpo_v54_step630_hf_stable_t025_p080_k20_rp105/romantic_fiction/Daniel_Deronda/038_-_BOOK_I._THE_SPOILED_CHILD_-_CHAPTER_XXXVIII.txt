There are those who believe the truest tragedy lies not after Prometheus secures the celestial fire, but before he finds the staff to carry it to mortals. It is the crushing solitude of despised ideas, forced by the brute weight of poverty and disease into a silence where many pass by yet none truly see.

"Second-sight" is often a banner flying over disputed territory. Yet, it is undeniable that some souls possess a vision where yearnings and conclusions manifest as images with a prophetic power. The act they intend to perform rises before them in full shape, a coercive blueprint; the event they crave or fear grows like a seed, fed by countless impressions. These visionaries are no less logical or sane than the common calculators of the market; indeed, their natures may hold manifold openings, like the hundred-gated Thebes, allowing a greater influx of experience than through a narrow, guarded portal. Of course, there are abject specimens of the visionary, just as there are tiny mammals one might trap in a glove. That small relative of the elephant causes no harm, but what great mental or social type is free from specimens whose insignificance is both ugly and noxious? One hesitates to consider the entirety of the "patriot" genus, or the elbowing that might occur at the final judgment for those who claimed authorship, carrying volumes in hand or on carts.

This defense of inevitable kinship serves to introduce the facts concerning Mordecai, whose figure had gnawed at Daniel Deronda's mind as a new question demanding an answer. Yet, for Deronda, the interest was merely a vague, expectant suspense. The consumptive-looking Jew, appearing to be a fervid student earning his crust through quiet handicraft, fit into none of Deronda's anticipations.

For Mordecai, however, the impact of their meeting was profound. For many winters, while his physical life ebbed and his spiritual loneliness widened, his passionate desire had concentrated on finding a young ear into which he could pour his mind as a testament. He sought a soul kindred enough to accept the spiritual product of his brief, painful life as a mission to be executed. Remarkably, the hopefulness often granted to consumptive patients was in Mordecai wholly diverted from the prospect of bodily recovery and channeled entirely into this yearning for transmission. The yearning, panting upward from overwhelming discouragement, had grown into a hope, and that hope into a confident belief. Instead of being checked by his clear conception of hastening decline, this belief took on the intensity of expectant faith in a prophecy destined for brief fulfillment.

Years had passed since he first began measuring men with a keen glance, searching for a possibility that became a distinct conception. Initially, this distinctness was reached through contrast: he sought a man who differed from himself. Tracing the reasons for his own rebuffs and hindrances, he imagined a man possessing all the elements necessary for sympathy, yet embodied differently. He needed a Jew, intellectually cultured and morally fervid—a nature ready to be filled from Mordecai's—but with a face and frame beautiful and strong. This man must be accustomed to the refinements of social life, his voice flowing with a full, easy current, his circumstances free from sordid need. He must glorify the possibilities of the Jew, not sit and wonder as Mordecai did, bearing the stamp of his people amid the signs of poverty and waning breath.

Sensitive to physical characteristics, Mordecai had looked at pictures as well as men, both abroad and in England. In vacant hours, he lingered in the National Gallery, searching for paintings that might feed his hopefulness with grave and noble types of the human form belonging to his own race. But he returned in disappointment. Instances are scattered thinly across the galleries of Europe where fortune or selection has gifted art with a face that is young, grand, and beautiful. Where melancholy exists, it is not feeble passivity but enters into the foreshadowed capability of heroism.

Observers may remember his emaciated figure, dark eyes deep in their sockets, standing before a picture that touched him to new or habitual meditation. He commonly wore a cloth cap with black fur, which no painter would have asked him to remove. Spectators likely viewed him as an odd-looking Jew, perhaps one who extracted money from pictures. Mordecai was perfectly aware of this impression. Experience had rendered him morbidly alive to how poverty and physical disadvantage cheapen ideas, unless one is a Peter the Hermit ringing a tocsin for the rabble. Yet, he was too sane and generous to attribute his spiritual banishment solely to the excusable prejudices of others; certain incapacities of his own had sealed the sentence of exclusion. Thus, his imagination constructed another man, something more ample than the "second soul" bestowed by Cabalistic notions to help an insufficient first. This would be a blooming human life, ready to incorporate all that was worthiest in an existence whose visible part was burning itself fast away. His inward need for this expanded, prolonged self was reflected as an outward necessity. The thoughts of his heart, that ancient phrase best shadows the truth, seemed too precious, too closely interwoven with the growth of things to lack a further destiny. As the more beautiful, stronger, more executive self took shape in his mind, he loved it beforehand with an affection half identifying, half contemplative and grateful.

Mordecai's mind wrought so constantly in images that his coherent trains of thought often resembled the significant dreams attributed to sleepers by inventors in their waking moments. Nay, they often resembled genuine dreams in their way of breaking off the passage from the known to the unknown. For a long while, he habitually thought of the Being answering to his need as one distantly approaching or turning his back, darkly painted against a golden sky. The reason for the golden sky lay in one of Mordecai's habits. He was keenly alive to the poetic aspects of London; a favorite resort was the bridges, especially at sunrise or sunset. Even when bending over watch-wheels or seated in a small upper room facing dingy bricks and cracked windows, his imagination spontaneously planted him on a spot with a far-stretching scene. His thoughts moved in wide spaces, and whenever possible, he sought the influences of a large sky. Leaning on the parapet of Blackfriars Bridge, gazing meditatively, the breadth and calm of the river, its long vista half hazy, half luminous, the grand dim masses of tall buildings signaling world-commerce, and the oncoming boats from the distance entered his mood. They blended indistinguishably with his thinking, as a fine symphony makes a medium that bears up our spiritual wings. Thus, the figure representative of Mordecai's longing was mentally seen darkened by the excess of light in the aerial background.

But in the inevitable progress of his imagination toward fuller detail, he ceased to see the figure with its back turned. It began to advance, and a face became discernible. The words youth, beauty, refinement, Jewish birth, and noble gravity turned into hardly individual but typical form and color, gathered from his memory of faces seen among the Jews of Holland and Bohemia, and from paintings that revived that memory. Reverently, it must be said of this mature spiritual need that it was akin to the boy's and girl's picturing of the future beloved. But the stirrings of such young desire are feeble compared with the passionate current of an ideal life straining to embody itself, made intense by resistance to imminent dissolution. The visionary form became a companion and auditor, keeping a place not only in the waking imagination but in those dreams of lighter slumber where it is truest to say, "I sleep, but my heart waketh"—when the disturbing trivial story of yesterday is charged with the impassioned purpose of years.

Of late, the urgency of irremediable time, measured by the gradual choking of life, had turned Mordecai's trust into an agitated watch for the fulfillment that must be at hand. Was the bell on the verge of tolling, the sentence about to be executed? The deliverer's footstep must be near—the one who would rescue Mordecai's spiritual travail from oblivion and give it an abiding-place in the best heritage of his people. An insane exaggeration of his own value, even if his ideas were as true and precious as those of Columbus or Newton, many would count this yearning as the sublimer part for a man to say, "If not I, then another," and hold cheap the meaning of his own life. But the fuller nature desires to be an agent, to create, and not merely to look on. Strong love hungers to bless, not merely to behold blessing. And while there is warmth enough in the sun to feed an energetic life, there will still be men to feel, "I am lord of this moment's change, and will charge it with my soul."

Yet, with that mingling of inconsequence that belongs to us all, saving us from many effects of mistake, Mordecai's confidence in the friend to come did not suffice to make him passive. He tried expedients, pathetically humble, within his reach, to communicate something of himself. It had been two years since he took up his abode under Ezra Cohen's roof, where he was regarded with much goodwill as a compound of workman, dominie, vessel of charity, inspired idiot, man of piety, and (if inquired into) dangerous heretic. During that time, little Jacob had advanced into knickerbockers and into that quickness of apprehension already manifest regarding hardware and exchange. Jacob had also advanced in attachment to Mordecai, regarding him as an inferior but liking him none the worse, taking his helpful cleverness as one might take the services of an enslaved Djinn.

As for Mordecai, he had given Jacob his first lessons, and his habitual tenderness easily turned into the teacher's fatherhood. Though fully conscious of the spiritual distance between himself and the parents, and never attempting communication from his peculiar world, the boy moved him with an idealizing affection that merged the qualities of the individual child in the glory of childhood and the possibilities of a long future. This feeling drew him on, first without premeditation and afterward with conscious purpose, to a sort of outpouring in the ear of the boy that might have seemed wild to any excellent man of business who overheard it. But none overheard when Jacob went up to Mordecai's room one day, for example, when there was little work to be done or when the work was ended. After a brief lesson in English reading or numeration, Jacob was induced to remain standing at his teacher's knees, or chose to jump astride them, often to the patient fatigue of the wasted limbs. The inducement was perhaps the mending of a toy or some mechanical device in which Mordecai's well-practiced fingertips had exceptional skill. With the boy thus tethered, Mordecai would begin to repeat a Hebrew poem of his own, into which years before he had poured his first youthful ardors for that conception of a blended past and future which was the mistress of his soul, telling Jacob to say the words after him.

"The boy will get them engraved within him," thought Mordecai; "it is a way of printing."

None were readier than Jacob at this fascinating game of imitating unintelligible words. If no opposing diversion occurred, he would sometimes carry on his share as long as the teacher's breath would last. For Mordecai threw into each repetition the fervor befitting a sacred occasion. In such instances, Jacob showed no other distraction than reaching out to survey the contents of his pockets, or drawing down the skin of his cheeks to make his eyes look awful, rolling his head to complete the effect, or alternately handling his own nose and Mordecai's as if to test the relation of their masses. Under all this, the fervid reciter would not pause, satisfied if the young organs of speech would submit themselves. But most commonly, a sudden impulse sent Jacob leaping away into some antic or active amusement. Instead of following the recitation, he would return upon the foregoing words most ready to his tongue, mouthing or gabbling, with a see-saw suited to the action of his limbs, a verse on which Mordecai had spent some of his too scanty heart's blood. Yet he waited with such patience as a prophet needs, and began his strange printing again undiscouraged on the morrow, saying inwardly, "My words may rule him some day. Their meaning may flash out on him. It is so with a nation—after many days."

Meanwhile, Jacob's sense of power was increased and his time enlivened by a store of magical articulation with which he made the baby crow, drove the large cat into a dark corner, or promised himself to frighten any incidental Christian of his own years. One week he had unfortunately seen a street mountebank, and this carried off his muscular imitativeness in sad divergence from New Hebrew poetry, after the model of Jehuda ha-Levi. Mordecai had arrived at a fresh passage in his poem; for as soon as Jacob had got well used to one portion, he was led on to another, and a fresh combination of sounds generally answered better in keeping him fast for a few minutes. The consumptive voice, generally a strong high baritone with variously mingling hoarseness, like a haze amidst illuminations, and its occasional incipient gasp, had more than the usual excitement while giving forth Hebrew verses with a meaning something like this:

"Away from me the garment of forgetfulness.
Withering the heart;
The oil and wine from presses of the Goyim,
Poisoned with scorn.
Solitude is on the sides of Mount Nebo,
In its heart a tomb:
There the buried ark and golden cherubim
Make hidden light:
There the solemn gaze unchanged,
The wings are spread unbroken:
Shut beneath in silent awful speech
The Law lies graven.
Solitude and darkness are my covering,
And my heart a tomb;
Smite and shatter it, O Gabriel!
Shatter it as the clay of the founder
Around the golden image."
