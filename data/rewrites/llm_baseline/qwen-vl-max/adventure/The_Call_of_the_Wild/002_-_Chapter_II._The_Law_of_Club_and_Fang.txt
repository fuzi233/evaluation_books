Buck’s first day on the Dyea beach was like a nightmare—each hour a fresh shock, each moment a new surprise. He had been ripped from the quiet comfort of civilization and thrown into the raw, untamed heart of the wild. There was no rest here, no peace, no safety. Only chaos, constant motion, and the ever-present threat of danger. To survive, he had to stay alert at all times. These dogs and men weren’t like those back home. They were wild, lawless creatures bound by no rules but the brutal code of club and fang.

He’d never seen dogs fight like this—savage, swift, and merciless. His first lesson came not from experience, but from watching. Curly, a gentle giant among the team, had approached a husky dog as large as a wolf, though half her size. There was no warning—just a flash of movement, a metallic snap of teeth, and a sudden leap away. In an instant, Curly’s face was torn open from eye to jaw. It was the way wolves fought: strike and vanish. But that wasn’t all. A circle of thirty or forty huskies formed around them, silent, intent, their mouths watering as they licked their chops. Buck didn’t understand their silence—or their hunger.

Curly charged again. Her opponent struck once more, leaping aside. Then, with a strange twist of his chest, he knocked her off balance. She fell—and never rose. That was the signal. The pack surged forward, snarling, yelping, burying her beneath a writhing mass of fur and teeth. She screamed in agony, then went still. It happened so fast, so violently, that Buck froze. He saw Spitz flick out his scarlet tongue—a gesture of mockery—and François rushing in with an axe, swinging it wildly to scatter the dogs. Three men with clubs joined in, driving the attackers back. Within two minutes, it was over. But Curly lay dead in the blood-soaked snow, her body nearly shredded, while the half-breed stood over her, cursing.

That scene haunted Buck in his dreams. So this was how it worked. No mercy. Once you fell, you were finished. He vowed he would never fall.

Spitz laughed again with that cruel tongue, and from that moment, Buck hated him—deeply, fiercely, without end.

Before he could recover from Curly’s death, another blow struck. François fastened a harness onto him—straps and buckles, like those used on horses back home. And just as horses pulled sleds, so now Buck was made to pull one. He hauled François to the forest edge and returned with firewood. Though pride stung him at being reduced to a beast of burden, he knew better than to resist. He focused, worked hard, and learned quickly. François was strict—demanding obedience, enforcing it with a whip. Dave, the experienced wheeler, nipped him when he slipped up. Spitz, the leader, growled sharp warnings or threw his weight in the traces to correct his path.

Buck adapted fast. By the time they returned to camp, he already understood “ho” for stop, “mush” for go, how to swing wide on curves, and how to keep clear of the wheeler when the sled rushed downhill. François praised him to Perrault. “T’ree vair’ good dogs,” he said. “Dat Buck—heem pool lak hell. I tich heem queek as anyt’ing.”

Later that afternoon, Perrault arrived with two more dogs—Billee and Joe. Brothers, both true huskies, yet opposite in every way. Billee was endlessly cheerful; Joe was sour, brooding, always snarling, eyes burning with hostility. Buck greeted them warmly. Dave ignored them. Spitz, however, attacked both. Billee tried to appease him, wagging his tail, but when that failed, he fled, crying softly as Spitz’s teeth tore his flank. Joe, though, met the challenge head-on—mane bristling, ears flat, lips curled in a snarl, jaws snapping like a trap. His eyes glowed with fury. So terrifying was he that even Spitz hesitated—and then turned on the harmless Billee, driving him to the edge of the camp.

That evening, Perrault brought in a fifth dog—an old husky named Sol-leks, meaning “The Angry One.” Lean, gaunt, with a scarred face and one glowing eye, he carried himself with quiet strength. Like Dave, he asked nothing, gave nothing, expected nothing. Even Spitz left him alone. But Buck soon discovered a weakness: Sol-leks disliked being approached from his blind side. Buck, unaware, stepped too close—and felt a searing pain as Sol-leks turned and slashed his shoulder, leaving a deep gash. From then on, Buck avoided that side—and never bothered him again.

That night, Buck faced a new problem: sleep. The tent, lit by candlelight, seemed warm and inviting. But when he entered, Perrault and François erupted in curses, hurling pots and pans at him. Startled, he fled into the cold. A biting wind lashed his wounded shoulder. He tried to lie down in the snow, but frost drove him shivering to his feet. Miserable, he wandered between tents, only to find everywhere was equally freezing. Some dogs lunged at him, but he bristled, snarled, and held his ground—learning fast. Finally, he decided to check on his teammates. But they were gone.

He circled the camp, confused, until he stumbled into a hole in the snow. Something moved beneath him. He leapt back, growling, afraid of the unknown. But then a soft yelp reached his ears—friendly, pleading. He crept closer. A warm breath rose from under the snow. There, curled tightly in a ball, was Billee. He whined, wriggled, and licked Buck’s face in peace.

Another lesson. This was how they survived. Buck chose a spot and dug his own hole. In seconds, his body heat warmed the space. He slept deeply, though his dreams were filled with growls and battles.

When he woke, he didn’t know where he was. Snow had fallen during the night, burying him completely. The walls pressed in. Panic surged through him—the primal fear of being trapped. Though he was a civilized dog, born in luxury, this instinct was ancient, buried deep in his blood. His muscles tensed, his fur rose, and with a fierce snarl, he burst upward, snow flying in a blinding cloud. As he landed, he saw the camp spread before him—and remembered everything: Manuel, the journey, the fight, the hole he’d dug.

François shouted. “Wot I say?” he called to Perrault. “Dat Buck for sure learn queek as anyt’ing.”

Perrault nodded. As a government courier, he needed strong dogs—and Buck was proving invaluable. Within hours, three more huskies joined the team, bringing the total to nine. Before long, they were harnessed and moving up the trail toward the Dyea Cañon.

Buck was glad to be gone. The work was hard, but he found he didn’t hate it. He was surprised by the team’s eagerness—it infected him. More astonishing was the change in Dave and Sol-leks. They were transformed by the harness. All passiveness vanished. They were alert, driven, furious at any delay. Pulling the sled was their purpose, their joy, their reason for living.

Dave was the wheeler, pulling ahead of him—Buck, then Sol-leks. The rest stretched out behind, led by Spitz. Buck was placed between Dave and Sol-leks to learn. And learn he did. They corrected him swiftly, with sharp teeth. Dave was fair—only nipping when necessary, never otherwise. With François’s whip backing them, Buck quickly learned to obey rather than fight.

Once, during a halt, Buck tangled in the traces and delayed the start. Both Dave and Sol-leks attacked, delivering a harsh beating. The mess was worse after, but Buck never made the same mistake again. By the end of the day, he was doing well. The whip cracked less. Even Perrault stopped to examine his paws—respectful approval.

It was a grueling run—up the cañon, through Sheep Camp, past the Scales, beyond the timberline, across glaciers and snowdrifts hundreds of feet deep, over the Chilcoot Divide, the great barrier between salt and fresh water, guarding the lonely North. They traveled fast through the chain of lakes formed in volcanic craters, and late that night reached the vast camp at Lake Bennett—thousands of gold seekers building boats for the spring thaw.

Buck dug his hole and slept deeply, exhausted. But dawn came too soon. He was dragged from sleep and harnessed again. That day, they covered forty miles—trail packed. But the next days were harder. They broke their own trail, working longer, going slower. Perrault often walked ahead, wearing webbed shoes to pack the snow, making it easier for the dogs. François guided the sled, sometimes switching places—but rarely. Perrault was in a hurry, and he knew ice better than anyone. Thin fall ice meant danger. Swift currents meant no ice at all.

Day after day, Buck labored in the traces. They left camp in darkness, hitting the trail at dawn. They pitched camp after dark, ate fish, and crawled into the snow to sleep. Hunger gnawed at Buck. His ration—pound and a half of sun-dried salmon—seemed to vanish instantly. He was never full. The others, lighter and born to the life, got only a pound and stayed strong. Buck lost his finicky ways. He watched as his mates finished first and stole what remained. He couldn’t defend it. While he fought two or three, the food disappeared.

So he ate faster. And when hunger drove him, he stole. He saw Pike, a clever thief, sneak a slice of bacon when Perrault turned. The next day, Buck copied him—taking the whole chunk. Chaos followed, but he was not suspected. Dub, clumsy and careless, took the blame.

This theft marked Buck as fit to survive. It showed his adaptability, his ability to change—not just physically, but mentally. His moral sense, once strong, began to fade. In the Southland, stealing was wrong. Here, under the law of club and fang, it was survival. To care about fairness was folly. Buck didn’t think it through. He simply did what was easiest. He didn’t steal for pleasure, but because his stomach demanded it. He didn’t rob openly—he stole quietly, carefully, out of respect for the law of the wild.

His transformation accelerated. His muscles hardened like iron. Pain became trivial. He could eat anything—rotten, bitter, foul—and his stomach extracted every ounce of nourishment. His blood carried it to every part of his body, building stronger flesh. His senses sharpened. He heard faintest sounds in sleep, knowing if they meant danger or calm. He bit ice free from his paws. When water holes were frozen over, he reared and smashed the ice with stiff legs.

But most remarkable was his ability to sense wind. No matter how still the air, when he dug his nest, the wind that came later always found him sheltered, leeward. And instincts long buried stirred awake. The generations of domestication fell away. Vaguely, he remembered the youth of his breed—the wild dogs who ran in packs, hunted in forests, killed as they chased. Fighting with slash and snap came naturally. It was how his ancestors fought. Their memories quickened within him, their tricks becoming his.

And on silent nights, when he raised his nose to the stars and howled long and low, it was not just Buck speaking. It was his ancestors—dead, dust, and forgotten—howling through him, across centuries. Their sorrow, their fear of cold and dark, their longing for freedom—all echoed in his voice.

Life, he now understood, was a puppet show. The ancient song surged through him, and he came into his own. He came because men had found gold in the North. And because Manuel, a gardener’s helper, could not feed his family on his wages.
