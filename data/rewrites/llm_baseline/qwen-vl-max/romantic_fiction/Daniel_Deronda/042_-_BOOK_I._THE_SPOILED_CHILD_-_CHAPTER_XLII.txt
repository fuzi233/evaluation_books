“If there are ranks in suffering, Israel stands at the top—if endurance and quiet strength elevate a people, then the Jews belong among the noblest of all nations—if literature is deemed rich for a few classic tragedies, what name can we give to a national tragedy lasting fifteen hundred years, written and lived by its own heroes?”

Daniel Deronda had recently read these words by Zunz, and they echoed in his mind as he walked toward the Cohen household—where sorrow, if it existed at all, wore no grand or solemn face. Ezra Cohen was not draped in the tragic dignity of martyrdom; instead, he thrived on commerce, his success in money-making a sharp contrast to the long history of Jewish hardship. This pawnbroker, this Jeshurun of trade, was no symbol of ancient sorrow. Yet could it not be said that within this ordinary home—a place of bustling prosperity and unreflective contentment—lived Mordecai, a fragile vessel carrying the weight of a nation’s memory, breathing with difficulty, yet still alive?

The Cohens greeted Deronda with open joy. Their faces shone with warmth when he arrived. Cohen himself remarked, half-jokingly, that while the diamond ring might have brought more profit if kept longer, he cared nothing for that now—not even a sixpence—compared to the pleasure it gave the women and children to see a young man whose first visit had been so delightful they’d “talked about it ever since.”

Young Mrs. Cohen apologized that the baby was asleep but was delighted that Adelaide was still awake. She urged Deronda to leave the shop and come into the parlor to meet “mother and the children.” He willingly followed, having brought small gifts: paper figures for Adelaide and an ivory cup-and-ball game for Jacob.

In the parlor, the grandmother sat with cards before her, making “plates” with the children—stacking them carefully until one fell apart. Just as Deronda entered, Jacob dashed forward.

“Stop!” he called. “Don’t step on my plate! Wait—let me throw it up again!”

Deronda paused, sharing a quiet smile with the grandmother as Jacob tossed the card stack high. It held together through several throws before finally breaking. Then, at last, the visitor was allowed to sit down.

He noticed that the door from which Mordecai had emerged during their earlier meeting was now closed. But Deronda wanted to show interest in the family before revealing his deeper concern for the man inside.

Only after settling Adelaide on his knee and setting the paper figures dancing on the table—while Jacob practiced catching the ball in the cup—did Deronda ask softly,

“Is Mordecai here?”

“Where is he, Addy?” Cohen called out, stepping in from a brief moment of business.

“In the workroom,” answered his wife, nodding toward the shut door.

“The truth is, sir,” Cohen said, “we don’t know what’s come over him lately. He’s always been a little… off, you understand”—he tapped his temple—“not quite as clear-headed as you or I. But usually he’s steady, hardworking, and takes real pride in the boy. But these past days, he’s been wandering like someone asleep, or sitting perfectly still, like a statue.”

“It’s the illness, poor dear,” murmured the grandmother gently. “I fear he won’t hold out much longer.”

“No,” said the younger Mrs. Cohen, “it’s something in his head. He’s been turning over papers constantly. When I speak to him, it takes ages for him to respond.”

“We may seem weak ourselves,” Cohen added, somewhat apologetically. “But my wife and mother wouldn’t let him go—even if he were worse than he is. We know how things stand, but it’s our principle. There are fools who lose money without knowing it—I’m not one of them.”

“Oh, Mordecai carries a blessing inside him,” the grandmother insisted.

“He has something wrong inside,” corrected Jacob, marching up with seriousness. “He told me he couldn’t talk to me, and he wouldn’t take any bread.”

“I don’t find your kindness surprising,” said Deronda. “In fact, I feel something of the same myself. I’ve spoken with him at Ram’s bookshop—and I promised to come here to fetch him. We’re going out together.”

“That’s it!” Cohen exclaimed, slapping his knee. “He’s been waiting for you. It’s taken hold of him. I suppose he talks about his learning to you. That’s very kind of you, sir. I doubt there’s much use in it—else he wouldn’t be where he is. But there’s the shop.” With that, he hurried back to his duties.

Jacob, who had been standing close to Deronda’s side, offered, “I’ll call Mordecai for you, if you like.”

“No, Jacob,” his mother said firmly. “Open the door quietly for the gentleman. Let him go in alone. Hush—don’t make a sound.”

Jacob obeyed, turning the handle with care. Deronda stepped behind him and paused at the threshold.

The room was dim—lit only by a dying fire and a single candle beneath a shade. On the board under the window lay scattered jewelry, and in the corner, a pile of books. Mordecai sat on a high chair, back to the door, hands folded on the board, a watch propped upright before him. He was in a state of painful expectation—like a prisoner listening for a delayed release.

Then Deronda spoke: “I’ve come for you. Are you ready?”

At once, Mordecai turned without a word. He grabbed his fur-lined cap and moved to join Deronda.

They returned to the sitting room just moments later. Jacob, noticing the change in Mordecai’s expression, seized his arm and shouted, “Look at my cup and ball!” He tossed the ball near Mordecai’s face—something cheerful to cheer a man recovering.

Mordecai smiled faintly. “Fine, fine.”

“You forgot your coat and blanket,” said young Mrs. Cohen. He returned to the workroom and retrieved them.

“He’s come back to life, see?” Cohen whispered, rejoining them. “I told you so. I’m mostly right.” Then, aloud: “Well, sir, we mustn’t keep you. But I hope this isn’t the last time we see you.”

“Will you come again?” Jacob asked, stepping forward. “See? I can catch the ball every time. I’ll bet I catch it without stopping—if you come back.”

“He has clever hands,” Deronda said, glancing at the grandmother.

“On my side,” Cohen replied promptly. “My wife’s family aren’t known for such things. But bless you—ours is a sort of cleverness like gutta percha—you can twist it any way. Some old gentlemen will do anything if you set them to it.” He winked at Jacob’s back.

But Jacob only let out a nasal whinny and began hopping around, singing, “Old gentlemen, old gentlemen,” in rhythm.

Deronda thought: *I’ll never truly know them until I ask Cohen plainly—did he lose a sister named Mirah when she was six?* But the moment felt too heavy to approach yet. Still, his initial discomfort at their plainness was softening. Though their speech and manner were rough, there was a quiet dignity in how they treated Mordecai—the sick, thoughtful man whom they saw not as a burden, but as a harmless dreamer, full of strange thoughts.

“The Cohens seem to care for you,” Deronda said as they stepped outside.

“And I for them,” Mordecai replied at once. “They carry the heart of Israel within them—even if they walk like horses and mules, blind to paths beyond their own.”

“I’m sorry I delayed,” said Deronda. “I meant to come yesterday, but couldn’t.”

“Yes—yes, I trusted you. But I’ve been uneasy. The spirit of my youth has stirred within me, and this body is too weak to bear it. I am like a man bound for years—suddenly freed, given voice, given motion. He weeps, he stumbles—joy threatens to break the house of flesh.”

“You shouldn’t speak so much in this evening air,” Deronda said, feeling each word like a thread binding him tighter. “Cover your mouth with the scarf. We’re going to the _Hand and Banner_, I suppose? Will it be private there?”

“No—that’s the trouble you didn’t come yesterday. Tonight is the club night. We may not have time alone until late, when others leave. Perhaps we should find another place. But I’m used to this one. In new places, the world presses in, and my inner vision narrows. And the people there know me.”

“I don’t mind the club,” Deronda said. “As long as I’m welcome. If we don’t have time, I’ll return. What kind of club is it?”

“It’s called ‘The Philosophers.’ Few of us—like the cedars of Lebanon—poor men who think. None poorer than I. Sometimes visitors of higher rank come. We may bring a friend interested in our talk. Each pays for a drink, and most smoke. I’ve gone when I could—there are others of my race who come, and sometimes I’ve spoken. I’ve found a faint likeness between these men and the ancient Masters—those who worked with their hands for scant bread, yet preserved the soul of Israel, passing down memory like a seed amid tombs. The heart finds comfort in such echoes.”

“I’d be glad to join you,” Deronda said, relieved at the prospect of delay before their private talk. “It sounds like a gathering I’d enjoy.”

Within minutes, they passed through the glazed door with its red curtain and entered a small parlor—barely fifteen feet square. Gaslight shimmered through a haze of smoke, illuminating a scene new and striking to Deronda. Half a dozen men of various ages, dressed simply, many with clay pipes in their mouths, sat in a tight semicircle around the fireplace. One man, broad-shouldered and blonde-haired, wearing pepper-and-salt clothes, had just finished quoting Shelley:

*“As thought by thought is piled, till some great truth
Is loosened, and the nations echo round.”*

The entrance of Deronda and Mordecai broke the silence. Seats shifted, glasses rearranged. This was a sober group—but sobriety did not mean abstention. Smoking and drink were essential tools for presence and conversation.

Mordecai was welcomed warmly, though with a quiet tone of sympathy. All eyes turned to Deronda.

“I’ve brought a friend,” Mordecai announced. “One who has traveled and studied much.”

“Is the gentleman anonymous? Is he a Great Unknown?” asked the broad man, with a grin.

“My name is Daniel Deronda. I am unknown—but not great.”

A smile lit his face, warm and modest. The room responded with a low murmur—almost a cheer. The broad man nodded.

“You recommend the name, sir. Welcome. Come, Mordecai—sit beside me,” he added, offering the coziest spot.

Deronda settled across from them, easily observing the group. Mordecai stood out—still, intense, marked by a deep melancholy. Among the others, several bore unmistakable signs of Jewish descent.

Indeed, pure English blood was rare. Miller, the bookseller, had German ancestors—or perhaps distant Jewish ones denied. Buchan, the saddler, was Scottish. Pash, the watchmaker, was a dark, lively Jew. Gideon, the instrument maker, was a red-haired Jew who looked entirely English. Croop, the shoemaker, seemed more Celtic than he knew. Only three—Goodwin, Marrables, and Lily—looked unmistakably English.

A select company of the poor, drawn together not by wealth or status, but by a hunger for thought—rare even among the privileged. Not the sort of place a gentleman would seek for scandal or comedy. Deronda, already somber from what lay ahead, found no reason to mock. Instead, he watched with quiet respect. He ordered whisky and water, offered cigars from his case—always carried, rarely used. He didn’t smoke, but liked to offer. Perhaps he feared seeming rigid, afraid of becoming a mere rule rather than a living person.

Yet he made an immediate impression. The men relaxed, eager to resume their talk.

“This is one of our touch-and-go nights,” Miller said, addressing Deronda. “Sometimes we stay focused. But tonight, Pash raised the law of progress—we went to statistics. Then Lily said we already knew things would repeat themselves without counting—just as quantities remain constant, so do qualities. Numbers reflect qualities—the number of drunkards is a quality of society. So numbers tell us nothing, only prompt us to study causes of difference between societies. From there, we turned to social change—and when you came in, I was speaking of ideas—the main force of transformation.”

“I disagree, Miller,” said Goodwin, the wood-inlayer. “You mean too many things by ‘ideas’—like saying light is a cause. Or you mean a narrow kind—then I reject it. Look: every action born of thought is an idea—sowing seeds, building boats, baking clay. These ideas grow with life, but they depend on material. Wood and stone yield to the knife—so the idea of shaping arises. With enough material, shaping continues. Ideas mixed directly with life are powerful. The slower the mix, the weaker the idea. As for social change—ideas are like a parliament, but outside lies a commonwealth, working change without knowing what the parliament does.”

“But if mixing is the test,” said Pash, “some impractical ideas spread fastest. They enter language without being understood.”

“They may act by changing gas distribution,” Marrables suggested. “With finer instruments, we might measure the spread of a theory through atmospheric shifts—and nerve changes.”

“Yes,” Pash said, his face brightening playfully, “the idea of nationality—perhaps wild asses are sensing it, growing more social.”

“You don’t share that idea?” Deronda asked, noting the irony in Pash’s tone against his strong features.

“Rather, he doesn’t share that spirit,” Mordecai said, turning a sorrowful gaze on Pash. “Unless nationality is a feeling, what power can it have as an idea?”

“Granted,” Pash replied, good-naturedly. “And since the feeling of nationality is fading, I see the idea as a ghost—already walking to announce death.”

“A sentiment may seem dead and yet revive,” said Deronda. “Nations have risen again. We may see a great awakening among the Arabs, inspired by new zeal.”

“Amen, amen,” Mordecai whispered, looking at Deronda with sudden light. His posture straightened, his face less worn.

“That may hold for backward peoples,” Pash said, “but in Europe, nationality is doomed. It will linger where oppression lasts—but nowhere else. Progress flows against it.”

“Ay,” Buchan said, his thin Scotch voice cutting in like cool air. “You’ve brought us to the point. You all agree societies change—not always, not everywhere—but generally, over time. Now, with respect, we must ask: Is all change progress? If not, how do we know which is progress? And third—how can we shape change to help it, or divert it when harmful?”

But Buchan’s order failed. Lily interrupted.

“Change and progress merge into development. Laws of development are being discovered. Changes following those laws are necessarily progressive. Any notion of progress opposed to them is mistaken.”

“I can’t see how calling change ‘development’ gives you certainty,” Deronda said. “We still face degrees of inevitability—our will, our actions. We still risk mistaking resistance for fate—which seems as dangerous a superstition as any.”

“That is true,” Mordecai said. “Woe to those who see no place for resistance! I believe in growth—a passage, a new unfolding. The seed holds greater potential, charged with divine form. A people’s life grows—it knits and expands, in joy and sorrow, thought and action. It absorbs other nations’ ideas, returns them transformed. It is a force in the body of humanity. But there may come a halt—memories silenced, love weakened. Or memories shrink into relics—the soul of a people may seem to die. But who can say, ‘Their spring is dried? They shall cease forever?’ Who dares say it? Not he who feels his people’s life stir within him. Shall he say, ‘Events move this way—I will not resist?’ His soul is resistance itself—a spark that may ignite millions, blaze a new path.”
