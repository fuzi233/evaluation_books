"So on those happy days of yore
Oft as I dare to dwell once more,
Still must I miss the friends so tried,
Whom Death has severed from my side.

But ever when true friendship binds,
Spirit it is that spirit finds;
In spirit then our bliss we found,
In spirit yet to them I'm bound."

Margaret rose before the appointed hour, granting herself a brief, private space to weep silently, to compose her countenance, and to summon a brightness that would not betray her grief. Her lingering anxiety concerned only the timeliness of their departure, lest they miss the train; yet relief washed over her as they arrived promptly, seated within the carriage opposite Mr. Bell. As the vehicle whirled past the familiar stations, the landscape unfolded beneath a warm, pure sun that rendered the tiles of the southern towns a deeper, ruddier hue than the slate roofs of the North. Flocks of pigeons circled the quaint, peaked gables, settling softly amidst the heat, fluffing their iridescent feathers as if welcoming the warmth. The platforms were devoid of the bustling crowds Margaret had encountered on her previous journeys to London; instead, solitary figures lounged with hands in pockets, absorbed in the static observation of trains arriving and departing, leaving behind only tracks, sheds, and distant fields. The air danced over a golden stillness, transforming farm after farm into scenes reminiscent of German idylls and medieval romances.

Suddenly, the dreamlike reverie shattered as the carriage halted at the junction for Helstone. Sharper emotions pierced her heart, a mixture of pain and pleasure she struggled to define. Every mile marked a return to a world of association, a journey she would not trade for anything, yet one that evoked an ineffable longing for "days that are no more." The last time she traversed this road, it had been a somber season, her spirits broken, accompanied by her parents. Now, she traveled alone, an orphan, as if the world itself had conspired to erase them from existence. The sight of the Helstone road bathed in sunlight, the trees standing in identical summer glory, struck her deeply; nature remained eternally young, indifferent to the passage of time.

Mr. Bell, attuned to her turmoil, wisely maintained a respectful silence. They approached the Lennard Arms, a establishment that stood slightly apart from the road, suggesting a reliance on local custom rather than the pursuit of travelers. Its façade faced the village green, dominated by an ancient lime tree encircled by benches, beneath whose foliage hung the faded heraldry of the Lennard family. Though the inn door stood open, there was no hurried reception. When the landlady eventually appeared, offering an apology for her delay due to the demands of hay-making, her demeanor shifted instantly upon recognizing Margaret. She rushed to the shade of the parlour, her eyes widening as the sunlight caught Margaret's face.

"It's Miss Hale!" the landlady cried, calling to her daughter before embracing Margaret with maternal affection. She inquired after the Vicar and Miss Dixon, expressing sorrow that the clergyman had departed. Margaret attempted to speak of her father's passing and her mother's demise, but the words lodged in her throat. She could only offer a single, heavy word: "Papa."

Mrs. Purkis, sensing the gravity of the situation, turned to Mr. Bell for confirmation. "Surely, sir, it's never so!" she exclaimed, recalling a gentleman who had visited weeks prior, speaking of Mrs. Hale's passing but failing to mention the Vicar's illness. Mr. Bell confirmed the tragedy gently, noting the suddenness of the death and describing the deceased as a man of profound calm. He introduced himself as a longtime friend of the Hale family and godfather to Margaret, explaining that they had traveled together specifically to honor the old place.

In Margaret's chamber, the casement window was framed by wild roses and vines. Pushing them aside, she glimpsed the chimney pots of the parsonage above the trees, tracing familiar lines through the leaves. Downstairs, Mrs. Purkis prepared for the guests' arrival, lamenting the changes in the village. The new Vicar, a teetotaler and magistrate with seven children, was constructing a nursery where the old arbour once stood. He and his wife, described as energetic and vocal, had installed new grates and plate-glass windows, sparking local debates over ginger-beer versus malt liquor and medicinal practices versus confectionery remedies. Mrs. Purkis departed briefly to tend to the inn, promising to return soon.

Upon descending, Mr. Bell presented a rustic luncheon of strawberries, cream, brown bread, milk, Stilton cheese, and port wine. Following this meal, they ventured forth for a walk, hesitating on the path as so many familiar landmarks beckoned. They bypassed the vicarage initially, choosing a route that allowed them to circle back later. As they walked, the landscape revealed subtle transformations: ancient trees felled the previous autumn, squatter cottages replaced by tidy, respectable dwellings, and gardens sprouting where the venerable, lightning-struck beech tree once stood. Margaret grieved for these losses, mourning the old man who inhabited the ruined cottage and the specific beauty of the vanished grove.

"I did not think I had been so old," Margaret murmured, turning away with a sigh.

"Yes!" Mr. Bell agreed. "It is the first changes among familiar things that create this mystery of time for the young. Later, we accept instability as a matter of course. For you, it remains new and oppressive."

Guided by a desire to connect with the past, Margaret led him toward the cottage where Susan's widowed mother resided. However, Susan was absent, having enrolled in the parochial school. Disappointment settled over Margaret, mirrored by the mother's apologetic silence. The mother recalled teaching Susan scripture nightly, lamenting the loss of a helpful girl who had surpassed her in learning. Mr. Bell expressed skepticism regarding formal schooling, preferring the simplicity of a home-based education rooted in faith and domestic duty.

Their conversation turned to the village gossip surrounding Betty Barnes, whom the mother accused of stealing her cat. Through further questioning, a harrowing narrative emerged. Induced by a gypsy fortune-teller to lend her husband's Sunday clothes, Betty had grown terrified when they failed to return. According to local superstition, the cries of a cat boiling or roasting alive compelled the powers of darkness to fulfill the wishes of an executioner. Consequently, the woman claimed she had resorted to roasting her cat to ensure Goodman Barnes recovered his missing garments. The account, delivered with unflinching sincerity, horrified both visitors. Margaret, unable to comprehend the lack of imagination or empathy required to justify such cruelty, walked away sickened.

"You are a good girl not to triumph over me," Mr. Bell remarked.

"Oh! I remember," Margaret replied, shifting the topic to Little Susan. "I must see her at the school."

Arriving at the schoolhouse, they were welcomed by the Vicar's wife, a brisk lady in black who moved with the confident authority of a hostess. She recognized Mr. Bell as the father of the deceased vicar and extended a polite greeting. Margaret, overwhelmed by the sentimentality of her visit, felt ill-equipped for inspection, yet the teacher's request for a grammar lesson brought her into contact with the eager faces of the students. Many were former parishioners baptized by her father, their features rapidly maturing beyond her memory. Seizing a fleeting moment, Margaret held Susan's hand privately before the lesson commenced.

During the instruction, the teacher struggled to explain the pronunciation of "a," prompting the children to correct her collectively by identifying it as an "adjective absolute." Abashed, Margaret sat in silence as the lesson concluded. Afterward, she lingered with a few favorites, observing how swiftly the children were growing into young women, just as she herself was fading from their collective recollection.

As the school session ended, the Vicar's wife proposed a tour of the parsonage to inspect the "alterations." Reluctantly, Margaret followed, expecting the modifications to jar against her cherished memories. Instead, the interior and exterior had transformed so thoroughly that the sting of familiarity was dulled. The meticulously trimmed garden, once a sanctuary of propriety, was strewn with toys and hoops, evidence of a lively household. The matted hall echoed with the chaotic energy of seven children. The former study, a place of green gloom conducive to meditation, now featured a new window opening onto the road, designed to monitor the Vicar's flock. The atmosphere was one of loud, brisk activity, lacking the delicate perception Margaret had valued in her father's home.

Walking back to the inn, Margaret chided Mr. Bell for his excessive praise of the new arrangements, fearing the Vicar's wife would interpret his admiration as mere politeness. Tired from the day's exertions, she declined another ramble, retreating to her chamber where she sat by the open window as twilight descended. The silence of the room amplified her inner turmoil.

"Mr. Bell, you remember what we were saying about Frederick last night?" she asked abruptly, her voice trembling.

Mr. Bell recalled the conversation. Margaret revealed a confession she had carried for months: a lie she had told to protect Frederick Hale following his mother's death. She recounted the terrifying encounter at the railway station, where a drunk man, Leonards, had fallen and died.
