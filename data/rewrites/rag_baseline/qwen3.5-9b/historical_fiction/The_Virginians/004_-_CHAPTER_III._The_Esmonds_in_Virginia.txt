The year ended with the dust of war settling over the realm. Henry Esmond, Esq., a colonel who had worn the uniform through the campaigns of Queen Anne, found himself stranded in the aftermath. The nation had chosen a new dynasty, preferring the House of Hanover to the restored Stuarts. Those who opposed the change, including Esmond, were advised to leave the three kingdoms. Though Esmond regretted his role in the conflict and the new prince proved a pacific ruler, the path forward was paved with exile. Friends managed to smooth the edges of his situation, securing a pardon, but the decision to depart remained.

Esmond belonged to the line of Castlewood in Hampshire. It was a known fact that King James II and his son had once offered him the title of Marquis, alongside the Irish peerage inherited by his family. He had refused to correct the informality required to claim it. Weary of European politics and personal turmoil, he chose Virginia instead. There, he took possession of a vast estate granted by King Charles I to his ancestor. It was in this American soil that his daughter and grandsons were born, and where his wife eventually died. She had been the widow of his kinsman, the Viscount Castlewood, slain in a duel by Lord Mohun near the end of William's reign.

He named his American home Castlewood, mirroring the ancestral seat left behind. The colony was modeled meticulously on English customs, a loyal outpost where the Virginians claimed Charles II reigned before he did in England. The English church and king were honored with rigid fidelity. The local gentry, allied with old English families, held their heads high above the Dutch traders of New York and the mercantile Roundheads of Pennsylvania. They were far from republican in spirit.

Life on these great estates followed a patriarchal rhythm. Each property commanded a multitude of hands—purchased and assigned servants—who obeyed the master's will. The land provided food, livestock, and game; the great rivers teemed with fish. Ships loaded tobacco from private wharves on the Potomac or James River, carrying it to London or Bristol in exchange for manufactured goods. Hospitality was boundless; no stranger was turned away. The gentry traveled between homes in a state nearly feudal. The institution of slavery existed, though the specific question of its morality was not yet born. To own black servants did not shock a Virginian gentleman, nor was the exercise of power over them generally savage. Food was plentiful, and the enslaved lived without want, though the whip and the corn-bag remained tools of control.

Madam Esmond accepted this order as naturally as letting horses roam loose. Her father, however, possessed a skeptical turn of mind. His doubts did not manifest as active rebellion, but rather as a quiet disaffection. Once, he had sought glory in active life, but later, a certain bankruptcy of the heart settled over him. He was not unhappy; he was kind, affectionate, and obsequious to the women of his family, rarely contradicting them. Yet, he submitted to life rather than enjoying it. He found his greatest spirits not in living, but in the prospect of laying it down.

With his wife gone, his daughter assumed management of the estate and her father's affairs. He acquiesced completely, caring only for his books and quiet. When visitors came, he entertained them handsomely with a pleasant, sarcastic wit, feeling no sorrow when they departed.

"My love," he told his daughter, "I shall not be sorry to go myself. And you, though the most affectionate of daughters, will console yourself after a while. Why should I, who am so old, be romantic? You may, who are still a young creature." He spoke this knowing she was a matter-of-fact person, lacking the capacity for such sentiment.

Fifteen years passed. Affairs prospered, and the worthy proprietor agreed to his daughter's plan for a mansion grander and more durable than the plain wooden edifice he had inhabited. She desired a habitation worthy of their noble name, perhaps fueled by ambition to take rank among neighbors who had already built handsome houses. Colonel Esmond cared little for quarters or quarterings, but his daughter held a high opinion of her lineage's merit and antiquity. In his serene decline, he humored her peculiarities, aiding her with his antiquarian learning and his proficiency in painting.

Heraldry had been part of the education of noble ladies and gentlemen a century prior. During her visit to Europe, Miss Esmond had studied family histories and pedigrees with grave diligence, returning to Virginia with documents she treated with implicit trust, alongside edifying French and English volumes on the noble science. These works convinced her that the Esmonds descended from Norman warriors who arrived with their victorious chief, and from native English of royal dignity. Two magnificent heraldic trees, painted by the Colonel's hand, depicted the family springing from Emperor Charlemagne, drawn in plate armor with imperial mantle and diadem, and from Queen Boadicea, portrayed in the light costume of an ancient British queen, wearing a prodigious gilded crown, a fur mantle, and a symmetrical figure tastefully tattooed with brilliant blue designs. From these illustrious stocks, the family tree rose until it united in the thirteenth century in the person of a fortunate Esmond claiming descent from both.

She wrote herself Esmond Warrington but was universally called Madam Esmond of Castlewood after her father's death. It is feared that quarrels over precedence occasionally disturbed her temper. Though her father had burned a marquis's patent from King James, she acted as if it existed and held full force. She considered the English Esmonds of inferior dignity to her own branch and asserted her superiority over the colonial aristocracy. Consequently, quarrels, angry words, and occasional scuffles arose at the Governor's assemblies in Jamestown. What purpose does recalling these squabbles serve? The participants are beyond reach now, and the republic has ended such social inequalities. Before independence, no country was more aristocratic than Virginia. Thus, the Virginians, whose history we narrate, were bred to respect home institutions fully. The rightful king had no more faithful subjects than the young twins of Castlewood.

When the boys' grandfather died, their mother, in great state, proclaimed her eldest son, George, the successor and heir. Harry, George's younger brother by half an hour, was always enjoined to respect his senior. The entire household, including the large and happy family of negroes and the assigned servants from Europe whose lot was made bearable under the Lady of Castlewood's government, was instructed to pay him honor.

There was scarcely a rebel in the family except Madam Mountain, the faithful friend and companion of Mrs. Esmond, and Harry's foster-mother, a faithful negro woman. She could never understand why her child should not be first, insisting he was handsomer, stronger, and cleverer than his brother, though in truth, there was little difference in their beauty, strength, or stature. In disposition, they were exceedingly unlike, yet in feature they resembled each other so closely that, aside from the color of their hair, it was difficult to distinguish them. In their beds, covered with vast ribboned nightcaps worn by great and small ancestors alike, it was impossible for anyone but a nurse or mother to tell one from the other.

Though alike in form, they differed in temper. The elder was peaceful, studious, and silent; the younger was warlike and noisy. Harry was quick to learn once begun but slow to start. No threat of the ferule would provoke him to study in an idle fit, nor prevent George from helping his brother. Harry had a strong military turn; he drilled the little negroes on the estate, caned them like a corporal, and engaged in many good boxing matches with them, never bearing malice if worsted. George, conversely, was sparing of blows and gentle with all around him.

As custom dictated, each boy had a special little servant. It was a known fact that George, finding his little wretch of a blackamoor asleep on his master's bed, sat beside it and brushed flies off the child with a feather fan. This act horrified old Gumbo, the child's father, who found his young master so engaged, and enraged Madam Esmond, who ordered the young negro off to the proper officer for a whipping. In vain George implored, entreated, burst into passionate tears, and besought a remission of the sentence. His mother was inflexible regarding the young rebel's punishment. The little negro went off beseeching his young master not to cry.

A fierce quarrel ensued between mother and son. Her son would not be pacified. He declared the punishment a shame—a shame—that he was the master of the boy and no one, not even his mother, had a right to touch him. He acknowledged she might order correction and that he would suffer the punishment as he and Harry often had, but no one should lay a hand on his boy. Trembling with passionate rebellion against what he conceived as injustice, he actually shrieked out an oath that shocked his fond mother and governor, who had never heard such language from the usually gentle child. He vowed that on the day he came of age, he would set young Gumbo free. He visited the child in the slaves' quarters and gave him one of his own toys.

The young black martyr was an impudent, lazy, saucy little personage who would be none the worse for a whipping, as the Colonel no doubt thought.
