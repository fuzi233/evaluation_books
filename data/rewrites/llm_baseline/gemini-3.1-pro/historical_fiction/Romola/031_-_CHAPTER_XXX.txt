It was the first time Baldassarre had ventured near the Piazza del Duomo since his escape, and he shrank from the very stones. The acoustic ecology of the square—the clamor of merchants, the reverberating bells—felt abrasive, but it was nothing compared to the auditory scar left by Tito’s voice. *“Some madman, surely.”* The smooth, perfectly modulated Florentine dialect had fallen from his adopted son’s lips like a blade, weaponizing the dominant lexicon against him. It was a violent disruption of Baldassarre’s internal equilibrium, an acoustic trauma that carried the viper sting of a terrifying truth: in the eyes of this superficial, mercantile world, Baldassarre lacked the language to prove his sanity. 

At the hospital, the brethren had offered him sanctuary from the industrial noise of the streets, alongside the profound dignity of true sustenance. They had clothed him in a coarse woollen tunic that smelled comfortingly of lanolin and damp stone, and they had fed him. It was not mere utilitarian fuel; it was a bowl of slow-simmered broth, thick with ancient grains, wild thyme, and root vegetables coaxed from the monastery’s agrarian soil. As the rich, earthy warmth had coated his palate, Baldassarre felt the care of the kitchen brothers—a communion of shared humanity in the grounding taste of roasted garlic and salted fat. The meal had restored his physical vitality, returning him to a body that remembered how to savor the world. 

With a few *danari* given to him by the monks, he paused at the first little town on the road to Arezzo. The scent of crushed lavender and sharp lye soap filled the barber’s alcove, where Baldassarre paid to have his circle of hair clipped and his face shaved. The metallic snip of the shears cut through the air, a clean sound amidst the morning’s quiet. When the barber handed him a small steel hand-mirror, a sudden, impetuous current of dread arrested Baldassarre’s breath. He looked at the landscape of his face. The skin was rough, deeply furrowed like an ancient olive grove, yellowed like a lifeless, macerated rind. But beneath the shaggy white beard, the architecture of his features was unchanged. The untranslatability of his current misery did not mask his identity. Tito *had* known him. The generational bond they shared, forged over years of domestic routine and shared meals in the sunlit courtyards of their past, had not been erased. The betrayal was absolute. 

Pushing the mirror away, he left the highroad and its jarring commercial traffic, seeking the restorative sonic environment of a quiet pool shadowed by alder bushes. The late November air smelled of decaying yellow leaves and damp earth. Here, accompanied only by the organic lap of water against the muddy bank, Baldassarre sat down to contemplate his reflection. He stared into the still, dark waters, confronting the erosion of his cultural memory. 

Where was the scholar? Where was the man whose mind had once been a vast library of Greek syntax, capable of parsing the subtlest flavors of ancient philosophy? That laboriously gathered store of knowledge had slipped away, leaving only the agonizing untranslatability of his subjective experience. He was an elder stripped of his native dialect, trapped in a silent void where the vocabulary of his identity had died. Once, he had fostered a helpless child. He had fed Tito the sweet honeycombs of Corinth, taught him to recognize the aroma of crushed coriander and the complex terroir of aged wine, weaving the mythic texts of their ancestors into the everyday domesticity of their meals. He had poured his lifeblood into the boy, hoping only that when he grew old, Tito might offer him a drop of water with a loving hand. Instead, Tito had served him ashes. 

*“There is no goodness but in hate,”* Baldassarre thought, staring at his dark-browed reflection until the face seemed to detach from his soul, becoming an instrument of pure vengeance. 

Yet, to enact this slow, deliberate justice within the living world, this aging body required nourishment. He refused to beg; he would labor, even if it meant carrying weights like a beast of burden in this clamorous city. As his fingers touched his bare neck, they brushed against a tiny parchment bag—a *breve* blackened with age. It was a relic of his childhood in Naples, smelling faintly in his memory of his mother’s hands, of wild fennel, sea salt, and olive wood smoke. He had worn it all his life as a quiet, domestic superstition. But the need to sustain himself, to fuel his sacred vendetta, transmuted piety into action. 

With nervous fingers, he bit and tore at the old parchment. Inside lay a mythic occurrence hidden within the mundane: an engraved sapphire, brilliant and blue as the Tyrrhenian Sea. It was worth gold ducats. 

The sapphire soon transformed into bread, clothing, and the means for retribution. Baldassarre purchased a dark, hearth-baked loaf, eating it slowly as he walked back toward Florence. He chewed with meticulous reverence, tasting the complex tang of the sourdough starter, the roasted malt of the crust, and the mineral sharpness of the salt. It was the flavor of endurance, a testament to agrarian traditions that outlasted the fleeting power dynamics of men. 

At dusk, he entered the shop of Bratti Ferravecchi. The space was a cacophony of commercialized art and salvaged goods, the abrasive clinking of old iron and broken glass forming a stark critique of Florence’s superficial mercantile obsession. The shop smelled of aged leather, rust, and the dust of discarded histories. High against a red scarf hung a bright dagger. Baldassarre’s eyes fixed upon it. 

As he bargained for the steel, Baldassarre skillfully employed the linguistic camouflage of a traveler from Genoa, mentioning an acquaintance who had bought a valuable ring. Bratti, eager to boast, unleashed a rambling account of his own commercial prowess. From the pedlar’s glib chatter, Baldassarre extracted the narrative of Tito’s life: the sale of the sacred family gems for profit, his quiet assimilation into Florence, his marriage, and his prosperous mastery of the city’s dominant lexicon. Tito had commodified their shared history, trading the mythic weight of his father’s love for social currency. 

Leaving the shop with the dagger at his side, Baldassarre felt the crushing weight of his fractured mind. To retain these new details, he had to repeat them endlessly, terrified that the fragile threads of memory would snap. The linguistic death within him was a constant, suffocating pressure. 

Returning to his lodging—an outhouse in the Oltrarno with a simple heap of straw—he passed the booksellers in the Via del Garbo. Desperate to reclaim his lost dialect, to resurrect the cultural memory that had defined his soul, he purchased the cheapest Greek book he could find. He carried it home and sat on the straw in the fading light. He opened the volume, inhaling the rich, olfactory history of aged vellum and oak-gall ink, a scent that should have heralded a feast of the mind. But as his strained eyes stared at the pages, the text remained silent. The characters were merely unintelligible black marks, stripped of their acoustic resonance. The lexicon of his past was dead, and the darkness of the evening closed over him, as absolute and untranslatable as his grief.
