One morning my godmother came into my room with the air she wore when she meant to be obeyed before one had even guessed there was to be an order.

“Open your drawers, Lucy; let me see your dresses.”

I drew them out and laid them before her—my scant, sober little store—each gown smelling faintly of lavender and the shut-wardrobe darkness in which it had lived. She turned them over with quick, decisive fingers, as if she were weighing flour by touch.

“That will do,” she said at last. “You must have a new one.”

She went, and almost before the air had settled back into quiet she returned with a dressmaker. I stood like a patient board while tape measured my shoulders, my waist, my length; the woman’s pins flashed and bit, and the chalk made pale, dusty kisses on the cloth.

“I mean,” said Mrs. Bretton, “to follow my own taste, and to have my own way in this little matter.”

Two days afterwards the box came home, and when the lid lifted there lay—pink.

“A pink dress!” I cried, the words breaking from me as if they were an alarm. “That is not for me.” I felt I might almost as soon have been asked to go abroad in some dazzling, ceremonial costume, stiff with embroidery, meant for a foreign court.

“We shall see whether it is for you or not,” returned my godmother; and her quiet was more absolute than anger. “Mark my words. You will wear it this very evening.”

I believed I never should. A pink dress and I had no acquaintance—no kinship. It knew not my habits; I knew not its audacity. Yet she decreed also that I was to go with her and Graham to a concert that night: a great affair in the grand hall of the principal musical society; pupils of the Conservatoire were to perform; afterwards a lottery “au bénéfice des pauvres;” and, to crown all, the King, Queen, and Prince of Labassecour were to be present. Graham’s tickets had come with strict injunctions—attention to costume, punctuality by seven.

At six I was summoned upstairs. There was no struggle, no persuasion—only that steady, domestic tide by which one is carried when another will is in the house and has chosen its course. The pink went on; black lace softened it into shadow; the pins pricked, the ribbons were drawn, the folds smoothed with hands that smelled of starch and cold metal thimbles. I was pronounced fit for “grande tenue,” and commanded to look in the glass.

I did, and fear shook me; I turned away, and fear followed. Then seven struck; Dr. Bretton had come; my godmother and I went down. She wore brown velvet, rich and grave as earth after rain. I walked in her shadow and envied her those folds of dark majesty, which asked no one’s pardon.

Graham stood in the drawing-room doorway.

My uneasy prayer was, I do hope he will not think I have decked myself out to draw attention.

“Here, Lucy, are some flowers,” said he, and put a bouquet into my hand.

The scent rose at once—green stems bruised to freshness, some sharp sweetness of blossom, and that faint, watery chill flowers keep when they have been cut and carried. He glanced at me with a kind smile and a satisfied nod, and in that simple approval my shame loosened. He made no compliment that might have scorched; he offered no ridicule that might have frozen. The dress itself, too, was plain—no flounce, no foolish frill—only the light fabric and the bright tint that startled me; and since Graham found in it nothing absurd, my own eye learned, against its will, to accept it.

I had been so little used to public gala that I scarce knew what I expected; yet I liked the drive. In the close carriage on a cold but clear night, wrapped and sheltered, I felt the comfort of warmth gathered close like broth in a covered bowl. Outside, the wheels made their steady, softened thunder on stone; now and then iron rang sharper at a rut, and once, passing under bare trees, the wind threaded their branches with a dry whisper. The city’s noise rose and fell in layers—far bells, near voices, the short crack of a whip, the distant roll of other carriages—sound making its own weather around us.

Dr. John and his mother were in their happiest contention; they fenced with words as easily as they might have carved at a table together, and both were frank and kind to me, as if I were indeed of their blood. That atmosphere of friendship had its own flavour; it warmed the mind as surely as good food warms the body.

We passed through some of the best streets of Villette—brightly lit, far livelier now than at noon. Shop-windows blazed; the tide of life flowed abundant along broad pavements. I saw baskets of oranges and heaps of chestnuts; I caught a whiff of roasting from some corner where a vendor had set his brazier; then a gust of perfume from a lady’s muff; then damp stone and lamplight. And suddenly, like the sour remembrance of thin fare in the mouth after a rich mouthful, came the Rue Fossette—its walled garden, its school-house, its dark vast “classes,” where at this very hour I might have been wandering alone, gazing at stars through high blindless windows, listening to the distant voice reading in the refectory, monotonously turning over a “lecture pieuse.” Thus must I soon again listen and wander; the future laid its shadow across the present, timely as sobriety is timely in the midst of wine.

We fell into a current of carriages all flowing one way, and soon a great illuminated building blazed before us. We alighted under a portico in bustle and crowd; the press of bodies made a low surf of sound—steps, murmurs, laughter, an occasional sharp call. I do not remember more distinctly till I found myself mounting a majestic staircase, wide and easy, deeply carpeted with crimson. The colour seemed to swallow the noise; feet fell muffled, as on thick moss. Great doors waited above, closed solemnly, their panels also clothed in crimson.

How those doors rolled back I hardly knew; Dr. John managed such points, and the world of ceremony opened as if it had been trained to obey him. Within lay a hall grand, wide, high—circular walls sweeping round, a hollow dome above, all stained to the look of dead gold, relieved with cornicing and fluting and garlands—now burnished bright, now alabaster-white, now white and gold mingled in wreaths of gilded leaves and spotless lilies. Everywhere, where drapery hung, where carpets spread, where cushions lay, the chosen colour was deep crimson: the place glowed warm as a heart’s chamber, though it glittered like a treasury.

From the dome hung a mass that dazzled me—a cataract of light, streaming with drops, sparkling with facets, scattered with stars, and tinted as if gems had been dissolved into dew, or fragments of rainbows had been shattered and caught there. It was only the chandelier; yet to my country ignorance it looked like a marvel wrought by eastern genii, and I half expected a huge, dark, cloudy hand—the Slave of the Lamp—to hover in that perfumed lustre and guard it.

We moved on—whither I scarcely knew. At a turn we met another party coming towards us. For one flashing instant I saw: a handsome middle-aged lady in dark velvet; a gentleman who might have been her son—best face, finest figure I thought I had ever seen; and a third person in a pink dress with black lace.

I took them all for strangers, and received—strangely—an impartial impression. Then it shattered: it was a great mirror between pillars, and the party was our own. Thus, for the first and perhaps only time in my life, I was given that harsh little gift—seeing myself as others see me. I will not dwell on the result. It jarred like a wrong note, it stung like a pin driven too deep. It was not flattering; and yet—one might be thankful. It might have been worse.

At last we were seated where we could view the vast hall—dazzling, yet warm and cheerful. It filled and filled: women not perhaps very beautiful, but their dresses were perfect; and foreigners, even those ungraceful in domestic privacy, have an art of seeming graceful in public. The peignoir and papillote belong to home; for gala use they keep in reserve a slide, a bend, a carriage of head and arms, a controlled mien of mouth and eyes, put on with the toilette as jewels are put on.

Here and there I saw fine forms of a style of beauty I think England seldom breeds: firm-set, sculptural, without angles; as flexible as a marble caryatid; as complete, in their still stateliness, as a Phidian goddess. Their features had the low-country classic regularity—round where England is sharp, straight where England is expressive—calm to the point of chill. For their depth of passionless peace, one might look to a polar snow-field. Such women need little ornament and seldom wear any; smooth hair braided close is enough against smooth cheek and brow; their dress cannot be too simple; rounded arm and perfect neck ask no chain, no bracelet.

A Juno of this order sat full in our view—cold, blonde, and beauteous as the white column beside her, capitalled with gilding. She was the mark of many eyes and knew it, yet seemed proof to gaze as stone is proof to sunlight.

Seeing Dr. John’s attention drawn thither, I bent to him and murmured, “For the love of heaven, shield well your heart. You need not fall in love with that lady. You might die at her feet, and she would not love you again.”

“Very well,” said he. “And how do you know the spectacle of her grand insensibility might not be my strongest stimulus to homage? The sting of desperation is a wonderful irritant to my emotions. But”—he shrugged—“you know nothing about these things; I’ll address myself to my mother. Mamma, I’m in a dangerous way.”

“As if that interested me!” said Mrs. Bretton.

“Alas! the cruelty of my lot!” returned her son. “Never man had a more unsentimental mother: she never seems to think such a calamity can befall her as a daughter-in-law.”

“If I don’t, it is not for want of having that same calamity held over my head: you have threatened me with it for the last ten years. ‘Mamma, I am going to be married soon!’ was the cry before you were well out of jackets.”

“But, mother, one of these days it will be realized. All of a sudden, when you think you are most secure, I shall go forth like Jacob or Esau, or any other patriarch, and take me a wife—perhaps of these which are of the daughters of the land.”

“At your peril, John Graham! that is all.”

“This mother of mine means me to be an old bachelor. What a jealous old lady it is! But now just look at that splendid creature in pale blue satin—hair of paler brown, with ‘reflets satinés’ like the robe. Would you not feel proud, mamma, if I were to bring that goddess home some day and introduce her as Mrs. Bretton, junior?”

“You will bring no goddess to La Terrasse: that little château will not contain two mistresses—especially if the second be of the height, bulk, and circumference of that mighty doll in wood and wax, and kid and satin.”

“Mamma, she would fill your blue chair so admirably!”

“Fill my chair? I defy the foreign usurper! A rueful chair should it be for her. But hush, John Graham! Hold your tongue, and use your eyes.”

While they thus skirmished, the hall went on receiving party after party until the semicircle before the platform showed one dense mass of heads, sloping from floor to ceiling. The platform itself—wider than any stage—had been deserted half an hour since; now it overflowed. Around two grand pianos placed near the centre, a white flock of young girls, pupils of the Conservatoire, had noiselessly poured—muslin and pale ribbons, hands folded, faces set with that strained look which comes of fear masked as decorum.

Two gentlemen officered this virgin troop. One I knew: an artistic-looking man, bearded, with long hair, noted as a pianiste and the first music-teacher in Villette, who twice a week attended at Madame Beck’s pensionnat to give lessons to those few pupils rich enough to afford his instructions. His name was M. Josef Emanuel, and he was half-brother to M. Paul. The other gentleman—visible now in person—was M. Paul himself.

M. Paul amused me; I smiled inwardly as I watched him in his element—standing conspicuous before a wide, grand assemblage, arranging, restraining, overawing about a hundred young ladies. He was perfectly in earnest—energetic, intent, absolute. And yet what business had he there? What had he to do with music, he who could hardly distinguish one note from another? It was his love of display and authority that had brought him—a love not offensive because so naïve. It soon became plain his brother Josef was as much under his control as the girls. Never was such a little hawk of a man as that M. Paul! When noted singers and musicians began to appear—when stars rose—this comet-like professor set. Insufferable to him were all notorieties and celebrities: where he could not outshine, he fled.

All now waited for one compartment to be filled: a space clothed in crimson like staircase and doors, furnished with stuffed benches on each side of two regal chairs beneath a canopy.

A signal was given; the doors rolled back; the assembly rose; the orchestra burst out; and to the welcome of a choral flourish entered the King, the Queen, and the Court of Labassecour.

I had never before seen a living king or queen, and strained my eyes with a hunger almost childish. Yet the first sight always brings a vague surprise, bordering on disappointment: one expects majesty to sit permanently on a throne, crowned and sceptred. Looking out for a king and queen and seeing only a middle-aged soldier and a rather young lady, I felt half cheated, half pleased—as if the extraordinary had consented to be human.

I recall the King well: about fifty, a little bowed, a little grey, and with a face unlike any in that assembly. I had read nothing of him; no one had told me his habits; at first the strong hieroglyphics on his brow, round his eyes, beside his mouth, puzzled me. Soon, if I did not know, I felt their meaning. There sat a silent sufferer—a nervous, melancholy man. Those eyes had looked on the visits of a certain ghost; had waited long for the comings and goings of Hypochondria. Perhaps he saw her even now upon that platform, over against him, amid all that splendour. Hypochondria has that wont: to rise in the midst of thousands—dark as Doom, pale as Malady, well-nigh strong as Death. Her victim thinks to be happy one moment—“Not so,” says she; “I come.” And she freezes the blood in his heart and beclouds the light in his eye.

Some might have blamed the foreign crown for the fold on his brow; others early bereavement. Something there might be of both; yet both are embittered by that darkest foe—constitutional melancholy.

The Queen, his wife, knew it. The reflection of his grief seemed a subduing shadow on her own benignant face. She was mild, thoughtful, graceful; not beautiful, not of the solid marble-feeling order. Her shape was slender; her features, though distinguished, bore the stamp of royal lines too strongly to give unqualified pleasure—one could not help remembering ignoble phases such profiles have worn elsewhere: feeble, sensual, cunning, as the case might be. But the Queen’s eye was her own; pity and goodness and sweet sympathy gave it a light that blessed. She moved not as sovereign but as lady—kind, loving, elegant.

Her little son, the Prince of Labassecour, and the young Duc de Dindonneau, accompanied her. The child leaned on his mother’s knee; and throughout that evening I saw her watching the monarch beside her, conscious of his clouded abstraction, seeking to rouse him by drawing his attention to their boy. Often she bent to listen to the child’s remarks, then smiled and repeated them to his sire. The moody King would start, listen, smile—and as soon as his good angel ceased speaking, relapse. It was mournful and significant; and what struck me most was that, in all that aristocratic and bourgeois crowd, the peculiarity seemed invisible. I could not perceive one soul either struck or touched.

With the royal pair entered their court—two or three foreign ambassadors—and with them the élite of foreigners resident in Villette. They took possession of the crimson benches; the ladies sat; most of the men stood behind, a sable rank making dark foil to the splendour in front. The middle distance filled with matrons in velvet and satin, in plumes and gems. The benches nearest the Queen’s right hand were devoted to young girls—the bud of Villette aristocracy. Here were no jewels, no head-dresses, no velvet pile—purity, simplicity, and an aërial grace reigned: young heads simply braided; fair forms robed in white, pale rose, placid blue, suggesting angels—though truth must say several of these “jeunes filles” of sixteen or seventeen boasted contours as robust as an Englishwoman of five-and-twenty.

I recognised a couple of those pale buds: two of Madame Beck’s late pupils—Mesdemoiselles Mathilde and Angélique—girls who during their last school year should have been in first class, but whose brains never carried them beyond the second division. In English they had been under my charge, and hard work it was to make them translate rationally a page of The Vicar of Wakefield. For three months one had sat opposite me at table; I remembered with a kind of domestic astonishment the quantity of household bread and butter and stewed fruit she habitually consumed at second déjeuner—only to be exceeded by the fact that she would calmly pocket slices she could not eat. Such seraphs have hearty appetites under their white muslin wings; there is more of the kitchen in human nature than gala language admits.

Another of these fair creatures I knew—prettiest, or at least least demure and hypocritical in look—seated beside the daughter of an English peer, an honest though haughty girl; both had entered with the British embassy. My acquaintance had a slight, pliant figure unlike the foreign damsels; her hair was not braided close like a shell of satin, but looked like hair—long, curled, flowing. She chatted volubly, full of a light-headed satisfaction with herself and her place. I did not look at Dr. Bretton; I knew without looking that he too saw Ginevra Fanshawe. He grew quiet; he answered his mother briefly; he more than once suppressed a sigh.

Why should he sigh? He had confessed a taste for pursuing love under difficulties, and here were difficulties enough. His lady-love shone from a sphere above his own; he could not come near her; he could not be certain of winning even a look. I watched whether she would favour him. Our seat was not far from the crimson benches; eyes so quick and roving as Miss Fanshawe’s must soon light on us. They did—at least on Dr. and Mrs. Bretton. I kept rather in shade, not wishing immediate recognition. She looked steadily at Dr. John, then raised a glass to examine his mother; a minute later she whispered and laughed to her neighbour; and when the performance began her restless attention flew to the platform.

Of the concert itself I need not speak at length; I was ignorant—ignorance crasse—and my impressions had more wonder than knowledge. The young ladies at the two grand pianos, much frightened, made a tremulous exhibition. M. Josef Emanuel stood by them while they played; but he had not the tact, the influence, the peremptory power of his kinsman, who under similar circumstances would have compelled pupils to demean themselves with heroism and self-possession. M. Paul would have placed the hysteric débutantes between two fires—terror of the audience and terror of himself—and would have inspired courage of desperation by making the latter terror the greater. M. Josef could not do this.

After the white-muslin pianistes came a fine, full-grown, sulky lady in white satin. She sang. Her singing affected me like conjuring: I wondered how she did it—how she made her voice run up and down and cut such marvellous capers; but a simple Scotch melody, scraped by a rough street minstrel, has often moved me more deeply than these sumptuous tricks. Perhaps I am made to be reached by what is plain—like bread well baked, not like sugar spun into towers.

Then stepped forth a gentleman who, bending his body much towards the King and Queen and frequently bringing his white-gloved hand to the region of his heart, vented a bitter outcry against some “fausse Isabelle.” He seemed to solicit the Queen’s sympathy; but her Majesty listened, as it appeared to me, with the calm of courtesy rather than the earnestness of interest. His state of mind was harrowing; I was glad when he wound up his musical exposition and let the air cease vibrating with complaint.

Some rousing choruses struck me as the best part of the evening. Deputies from provincial choral societies were present—genuine, barrel-shaped native Labassecouriens—men who threw their voices out as farmers throw seed, with no mincing and no waste of breath on delicacy. Their hearty exertions had at least this good result: the ear drank a satisfying sense of power, as one drinks deep from a cup that is honestly filled. In that strong, communal sound—so unlike the thin, trained flutterings of frightened girls—I seemed to hear something older than fashion, something that belonged to the country’s own mouth and chest; and for a moment, amid gold walls and crimson hangings, I felt how easily the true native voice may be drowned under show—how quickly a people’s tone becomes mere ornament when courts and crowds applaud what is foreign, and do not even notice the sorrow sitting, uncheered, upon their King.
