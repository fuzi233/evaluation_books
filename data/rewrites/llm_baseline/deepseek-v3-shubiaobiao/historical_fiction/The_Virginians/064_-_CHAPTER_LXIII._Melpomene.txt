George Warrington did not permit his legal studies to interfere unduly with his pleasures or his health. His mother, Madam Esmond, had written to remind him that though he might wear a student’s gown and dine in hall among the undistinguished, he bore a name of ancient honor, and ought to conduct himself as befitted a gentleman rather than a laborious drudge. George, ever obedient to her wishes, complied readily enough, treating the law as a voluntary pursuit rather than a grinding profession.

The law in those days, though less exacting than in our own, was still a demanding mistress. A man might rise to the ermine and the woolsack, as Murray had done, but George Warrington had other inclinations. The black-letter pages of Chaucer pleased him far more than the stern Gothic texts of Hale or Coke.

In those days, the classics still held sway. Gentlemen quoted Virgil in Parliament and cited Tacitus in the courts. But the age of the Olympians had waned. Who now cared for the Muses, or knew whether *Melpomene* had three syllables or four? The age of economists had dawned, and the Pantheon stood deserted.

George, in his youth, had courted the Tragic Muse during idle hours in Virginia and his captivity at Fort Duquesne. The fruit of that dalliance was a tragedy—now lost to Bell’s *Theatre*, though likely no worse than many that were printed. It is the way of young men to aim first at the highest, dreaming of epics and tragedies before contenting themselves with humbler verse.

Upon arriving in London, George became a frequent spectator at both playhouses, a patron of theatrical coffee-houses, and a familiar figure among wits and actors. He supped at the Bedford, exchanged jests at the Cecil, and found himself welcomed in these circles—partly for his wit, partly for the rumor of his wealth. To drink with Bobadil, to take snuff from Aristarchus himself—these were delights for a young man of poetic fancy. And if his mother frowned upon such dissipations, so much the sweeter the forbidden fruit.

He sought advice on his tragedy from his new acquaintance, Mr. Spencer of the Temple, who hosted a breakfast in Fig Tree Court where George read a portion of his play. The assembled critics—even the formidable Mr. Johnson—pronounced it promising, though it violated the unities. "But so have others," Johnson conceded. "There is something here of *Coriolanus*, something of *Othello*."

"And both good models, I hope," said George.

"The catastrophe is terrible—and, being partly true, all the more awful," remarked Mr. Spencer.

The plot was bloody enough. Drawing from his grandfather’s tales of the mercenary George Frundsberg, George had fashioned his hero, Colonel Carpezan, a Lutheran soldier storming a convent on the Rhine. The first act ended in flames and screams as the nuns fled before the besiegers.

Act II found Carpezan ennobled, married to the rescued Lady Sybil, and wretched. His wife taunted him for his low birth; his conscience gnawed at him for past crimes.

Act III brought them to Prague, where the young King of Bohemia’s attentions to Sybil grew unmistakable. By Act IV, Carpezan’s jealousy erupted in tragedy—he had her slain by his executioner, the deed done under a pale moon as the King strummed his fatal serenade outside.

Act V saw Carpezan, now a renegade in the Turkish camp, confronting the King at Mohacz. He struck him down, only to be rewarded with treachery—the Vizier, praising his valor, had him shot by archers. As Carpezan lay dying beside his old comrade Ulric, he gasped, *"Forgive me!"*—and the curtain fell.

When George finished reading, he turned to Johnson. "Well, sir? Is there hope for me?"

But Johnson, who had dozed through the latter acts, admitted he had missed the climax.

The assembled critics murmured their verdicts. Some quibbled over details—should the Vizier cry *"Loose!"* rather than *"Fire!"* when ordering the archers to shoot? Others wished Ulric had survived to wed some comic heroine.

"History forbids it," Johnson rumbled. "Mohacz was a slaughter. Let Ulric die with honor."

And so the verdict stood—flawed, perhaps, but promising. George Warrington’s tragedy might yet find its stage.
