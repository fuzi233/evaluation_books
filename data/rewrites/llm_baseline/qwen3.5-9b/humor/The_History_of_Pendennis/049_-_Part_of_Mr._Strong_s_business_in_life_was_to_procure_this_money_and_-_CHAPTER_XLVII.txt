The exertions of the previous night at Gaunt House had nearly broken Major Pendennis's constitution. As soon as he could move his weary frame without danger, he limped groaningly to Buxton, seeking solace in the region's famous healing waters. By the time Parliament adjourned, Sir Francis Clavering and his household had departed the city, leaving the affairs previously discussed largely untouched amidst the fleeting weeks that followed. Consequently, London itself seemed to empty out.

The season drew to a close. Pen's neighbors, the legal fraternity, had scattered onto the circuits; his fashionable circle had purchased passports for the Continent or fled to the Scottish Highlands for health and thrills. The bow-windows of the clubs were deserted, and the solitary pavement of Pall Mall echoed with silence. The scarlet coats of the Guards vanished before the Palace gates; the merchants of St. James's wandered abroad for leisure; the tailors had sported mustaches and migrated up the Rhine; the cobblers were found at Ems or Baden, flushing crimson when encountering their patrons among the resorts, or punting alongside their creditors at gambling tables. The clergy of St. James's preached to dwindling congregations devoid of notable sinners. The band in Kensington Gardens silenced their brass and silver trumpets. Only a handful of dilapidated carriages crawled along the Serpentine's banks. Even Clarence Bulbul, the Treasury clerk bound to the capital by duty, compared the emptiness of Rotten Row during his afternoon rides to the vastness of the Arabian Desert, casting himself as a lone Bedouin traversing dusty solitude. Meanwhile, Warrington stuffed his carpet bag with Cavendish tobacco and retreated to his brother's estate in Norfolk, as was his custom during vacations.

Left somewhat isolated in his chambers, Pen could not simply abandon the metropolis whenever he pleased; he was currently tethered by the demands of *The Pall Mall Gazette*. Acting as editor and *chargé d'affaires* in the temporary absence of the chief, Captain Shandon—who was enjoying the salutary waters of Boulogne-sur-Mer with his family—Pen found himself managing the paper's operations.

Though Pen had long professed himself a *blase* soul, thoroughly wearied of life, the reality was that he remained an exceptionally vigorous young man. He possessed a magnificent appetite, satisfying it with gusto daily, and a relentless craving for society that marked him as anything but a misanthrope. If a good dinner eluded him, he ate a mediocre one with perfect contentment; if witty, great, or beautiful company was unavailable, he accepted whatever presented itself. He felt entirely at home in a tavern parlour, aboard a Greenwich steamboat, on a jaunt to Hampstead with his colleague Mr. Finucane, at the summer theaters across the river, or at the Royal Gardens of Vauxhall. There, he maintained friendly terms with the great Simpson and shook hands with the lead comic singer of the equestrian troupe. While observing the grimaces and graces of the performers with a satirical humor undeniably tinged with sympathy, he also viewed the spectators with kindness: the boisterous youths chasing pleasure, the honest parents watching delighted children clap their hands, and the unfortunate outcasts whose laughter was less innocent but perhaps louder, bringing their shame and youth to dance until dawn, hoping to drown their cares. Arthur often boasted of this universal sympathy, claiming it as his favorite pursuit. Just as others ardently loved art, music, or science, Pen declared anthropology his passion, keeping his eyes wide open to its infinite varieties. Whether it was a coquettish dowager in a ballroom, a high-born beauty blushing in her prime, a hulking guard coaxing a maid in the Park, or an innocent child feeding ducks while a nurse watched, he contemplated them all with unfailing delight. A man with a relatively clean heart can indulge in such observation with ceaseless joy, perhaps even more keenly because it remains secret, carrying a touch of melancholy, for he feels both connected and apart, lonely yet not alone.

Indeed, Pen used to brashly tell Warrington, "In my youth, I was so fiercely in love that I've burned that flame out forever. If I ever marry, it will be a marriage of reason—a union with a well-bred, good-tempered, good-looking person with a little money to cushion our carriage through life. As for romance, it's all done; I've spent it, and I'm old before my time—I'm proud of it!"

"Stuff!" grunted Warrington. "You fancied you were going bald the other day and bragged about it just as you do everything else. But you started using bear's grease the moment the hairdresser mentioned it, and you're smelling like a barber shop ever since."

"You are Diogenes," Pen retorted, "expecting every man to live in a tub like yourself. Violets smell better than stale tobacco, you grizzly old cynic." Yet, as he spoke, Pen blushed, caring far more about his own appearance than a true philosopher ought. Considering his claimed indifference to the world, he adorned himself with considerable effort to remain agreeable, wearing tight boots and bright polish like a weary pilgrim determined to look his best.

It was on such a dull, shining Friday night in autumn that Mr. Pendennis, having penned a brilliant leading article at his newspaper office—one that Captain Shandon himself might have authored had he been in a working mood, which he rarely was unless compelled—reviewed his work with approval on the wet proof-sheets. Deciding to cross the water, he resolved to treat himself to the fireworks and amusements of Vauxhall. He tucked his special order—"Editor of *Pall Mall Gazette* and Friend"—into his pocket and paid the requisite toll to cross Waterloo Bridge. The walk toward the Gardens was pleasant, the stars twinkling overhead, gazing down upon the royal property before the rockets and Roman candles ascended to outshine them.

Before entering the enchanted grounds, where twenty thousand extra lamps burned nightly, visitors usually traverse a black, dreary passage guarded by wickets that conceal the splendor from the uninitiated. Illuminated holes in the walls served as checkpoints, where desk-bound officials accepted cash or admission orders. Pen approached the latter booth, only to find a gentleman and two ladies already engaged in conversation ahead of him.

The gentleman, sporting a hat tilted precariously and a short, shabby cloak worn with excessive flair, shouted in a voice Pen instantly recognized.

"Bedad, sir, if ye doubt me honor, will ye oblige me by stepping out of that box and—"

"Lor, Capting!" cried the elder lady.

"Don't bother me," the man in the booth snapped.

"And ask Mr. Hodgen himself, who's inside the gardens, to let these ladies pass. Don't be frightened, me dear madam; I'm not going to quarrel with this gentleman, especially before ladies. Will ye go, sir, and desire Mr. Hodgen (whom I keep in with, and he's me most intimate friend, and I know he's going to sing 'The Body Snatcher' tonight) to step out and let the ladies in—for myself, sir, I've seen Vauxhall, and I'd scarce allow any interference on my account: but for these ladies, one has never been there, and I'd hardly take advantage of my misfortune in losing the ticket to deprive her of her pleasure."

"It ain't no use, Captain. I can't go about your business," the ticket-checker replied peevishly. Whereupon the Captain swore an oath, and the elder lady exclaimed, "Lor, oh, provokin'!"

The younger girl looked up at the Captain and said, "Never mind, Captain Costigan, I'm sure I don't want to go at all. Come away, Mamma." Though she claimed no desire to enter, her emotions overwhelmed her, and she burst into tears.

"My poor child!" the Captain sighed. "Can ye see that, sir, and will ye not let this innocent creature in?"

"It ain't my business," the doorkeeper cried irritably. At that precise moment, Arthur stepped forward, recognizing Costigan immediately.

"Don't you know me, Captain? Pendennis!" he said, removing his hat and bowing to the ladies.

"Me dear boy! Me dear friend!" the Captain cried, extending a grasping hand. He quickly explained his "most unluckey contract." He held an order from Mr. Hodgen (who was currently inside singing favorites like *The Death of General Wolfe* and *The Banner of Blood*) admitting two persons. Believing it might accommodate three, he had brought his friends. However, on his way, Captain Costigan had lost the admission paper entirely. The ladies were forced to return, much to the dismay of the younger one, as Arthur observed.

Arthur possessed immense good nature and sympathy for all kinds of misfortune; how could he refuse in such a case? He had witnessed the innocent upward glance of the girl, the pleading expression, the pitiful quiver of her mouth, and the final eruption of tears. If it were his last guinea, he would have paid it to grant her pleasure. She turned her sad, imploring eyes away upon seeing a stranger and began wiping them with a handkerchief. Arthur looked handsome and kind, standing before the women with his hat off, blushing, bowing, generous, and truly gentlemanly. "Who are they?" he asked himself. He felt he had seen the elder lady before.
