Mr. Thornton departed the house before entering the dining chamber again. Being somewhat late, he hurried swiftly toward Mr. Hale's residence, mindful not to insult his newfound acquaintance through any display of disrespect regarding punctuality. As he stood at the threshold, waiting for Dixon's leisurely approach—a delay always compounded when she felt compelled to lower herself by answering the bell—he noted the church clock striking half-past seven.

Upon being admitted to the modest drawing-room, he was warmly received by Mr. Hale, who guided him to his wife. Mrs. Hale sat wrapped in a shawl, her pallor lending a silent justification to the cool, languid nature of her welcome. Margaret stood by the hearth, igniting the lamp as twilight descended. The flame cast a gentle illumination across the center of the dim room, an openness to the night skies and the elemental darkness outside that stood in stark contrast to the heavy, ponderous dining hall he had just vacated. That space, dominated by his mother's presence, offered no hint of female habitation save where she sat, designed solely for sustenance. This drawing-room, however, though lacking comfort, possessed a grace twentyfold greater. It held no mirrors, not even a fragment of glass to catch the light like water in a landscape; there was no gilding. Instead, it boasted a warm, sober breadth of color, softened by the aged Helstone chintz curtains and chair covers. An open davenport stood before the window, while nearby a stand held a tall white porcelain vase overflowing with English ivy, pale green birch, and copper-hued beech leaves. Baskets of needlework dotted the corners, and books, indifferent to their bindings, lay scattered on a table as if recently abandoned. Behind the door, another table was dressed for tea, draped in white linen and adorned with coconut cakes and baskets piled high with oranges and American apples resting on leaves.

To Mr. Thornton, these elegant attentions seemed a natural rhythm of the household, particularly aligned with Margaret's nature. She stood beside the tea table in a gown of light muslin tinged with pink. Though seemingly lost to the conversation, her sole focus was the arrangement of the cups, her round, ivory hands moving with a quiet, dainty precision. A bracelet encircled one slender arm, poised to slide down over her wrist. Mr. Thornton observed the removal of this accessory with a scrutiny far exceeding his attention to her father's words. It captivated him to watch her push the band up impatiently until it constricted her soft skin, only to witness its gradual loosening and eventual fall. He nearly cried out, "Again!" There remained little else to attend to once the preparations were complete, leaving him reluctant for the inevitable obligations of eating and drinking to interrupt his observation of Margaret.

She presented him with his cup of tea with the stately demeanor of a reluctant servant, yet her gaze betrayed anticipation the moment he signaled readiness for a refill. Almost, he longed to request she perform this service for him as he saw her compelled to do for her father, who held her little finger and thumb in his broad hand, guiding them like tongs. Mr. Thornton witnessed her beautiful eyes lift to her father, filled with a luminous mixture of half-laughter and half-love, as this silent pantomime unfolded between them, unnoticed by anyone but themselves. Margaret's head still ached, evidenced by her pallor and silence, yet she was resolved to bridge any long, awkward pauses rather than allow her father's friend, pupil, and guest to feel neglected.

Conversation flowed freely, and Margaret retreated to a corner near her mother with her embroidery, grateful for the liberty to let her thoughts wander without fear of interruption. Mr. Thornton and Mr. Hale remained deeply engrossed in the thread of thought begun at their previous meeting. Margaret was abruptly recalled to the present by a casual remark from her mother, and upon lifting her eyes from her work, she perceived a profound divergence in the outward bearing of her father and Mr. Thornton, reflecting distinctly opposing natures.

Her father, slight in build, appeared taller than he truly was unless contrasted with the towering, massive frame of the other. The lines of his face were soft and undulating, traversed by a frequent, tremulous movement that revealed every shifting emotion. His eyelids were large and arched, granting his eyes a peculiar, languid beauty bordering on the feminine. His brows were finely curved, raised high by the weight of those dreamy lids. In stark opposition, Mr. Thornton's straight brows fell low over clear, deep-set, earnest eyes. These eyes, though not sharply unpleasant, seemed intent enough to pierce the very core of whatever they surveyed. Few lines marred his face, yet they were firm, carved as if in marble, concentrated chiefly around the lips. They were slightly compressed over teeth so faultless and brilliant as to evoke a sudden burst of sunlight whenever a rare, bright smile transformed his severe, resolved visage into one of keen, honest enjoyment—a joy seldom displayed so fearlessly and instantly save by children. Margaret found this smile enchanting; it was the first quality she had admired in her father's new friend. The juxtaposition of their characters, evident in every detail, seemed to illuminate the magnetic attraction they clearly shared.

She rearranged her mother's woolwork and sank back into her reverie, effectively forgetting her surroundings. Mr. Thornton, meanwhile, was wholly absorbed in explaining to Mr. Hale the magnificent power yet delicate adjustment inherent in the steam-hammer, a subject that summoned memories of the genie stories in the *Arabian Nights*. One moment the machine stretched from earth to sky, spanning the entire horizon; the next, it was obediently compressed into a vessel small enough to be carried in a child's hand.

"And this realization of power, this practical embodiment of a colossal thought, emerged from the brain of a single man in our industrious town," Mr. Hale mused. "That same man possesses within him the capacity to ascend, step by step, from each wonder achieved to higher marvels still. And I am certain we harbor many among us who, were he absent, could spring into the breach and sustain the warfare compelling all material force to yield to science."

At her father's citation, Margaret looked up, her eyes alight with inquisitive wonder. How, in the world, had they drifted from cog-wheels to *Chevy Chase*?

"It is no boast on my part," Mr. Thornton replied, "but plain matter-of-fact. I need not deny that I take pride in belonging to a town—or perhaps better, a district—whose necessities birth such grandeur of conception. I would sooner be a man toiling, suffering—even failing and unsuccessful—here, than lead a dull, prosperous existence in the old, worn grooves of what you deem aristocratic society down in the South, with its slow days of careless ease. One may become clogged with honey, unable to rise and fly."

"You are mistaken," Margaret interjected, roused by the slight against her beloved South into a fervent defense that flushed her cheeks and brought angry tears to her eyes. "You know nothing of the South. If there is less adventure or progress—if I may not say less excitement—from the gambling spirit of trade necessary to forge these wonders, there is likewise less suffering. I see men here wandering the streets, worn down by some gnawing sorrow or care—not merely sufferers, but haters. In the South, yes, we have our poor, but there is not that terrible, sullen expression of injustice I perceive here. You do not know the South, Mr. Thornton," she concluded, collapsing into a determined silence, chastened by her own outburst.

"And may I suggest you do not know the North?" he asked, his tone imbued with an inexpressible gentleness as he sensed he had caused her pain. She remained resolutely silent, yearning for the lovely haunts she had left behind in Hampshire, with a passionate longing that rendered her voice unsteady if she dared to speak.

"At any rate, Mr. Thornton," said Mrs. Hale, "you must concede that Milton is a far more smoky, filthy town than you will ever encounter in the South."

"I'm afraid I must relinquish its cleanliness," Mr. Thornton smiled, the quick gleam returning to his eyes. "Yet, we are commanded by Parliament to burn our own smoke; thus, like good little children, we shall obey the bid, eventually."

"But I recall you telling me you altered your chimneys to consume the smoke, did you not?" Mr. Hale asked.

"My alterations were made by my own volition, prior to Parliament's meddling. It was an immediate expense, yet it repays me in the savings of coal. I am uncertain if I would have undertaken it had I waited for the Act. At least, I would have waited to be informed against and fined, expending all effort in legal compliance. However, all laws dependent for enforcement upon informers and fines grow inert from the odiousness of their machinery. I doubt if a single chimney in Milton has been reported in the last five years, despite some constantly emitting one-third of their coal as 'unparliamentary' smoke."

"I only know that keeping the muslin blinds clean above a week here is impossible, whereas at Helstone, ours have hung up for a month or more without appearing dirty. And regarding hands—Margaret, how many times did you say you washed them this morning before noon? Three, was it not?"
