Doctor Portman dispatched his stern missive to London, attempting to calm Mrs. Pendennis's nerves until a reply could surface—a response he optimistically believed would vindicate his son's morals. For now, Helen's plan to travel to London and confront Pen personally remained on hold. The local apothecary forbade her even a trip to Fairoaks for the first day, leaving her confined to her sofa, attended by the devoted, though taciturn, Laura.

Unfortunately for everyone involved, Pen never encountered the Doctor's homily until weeks later. Meanwhile, the widow waited day after day for an answer to the charges laid against her son, her own health deteriorating with each passing hour. For Laura, bearing this anxiety was a heavy burden; witnessing her dearest friend's suffering, compounded by the pain of Helen's estrangement and the unspoken grief between friends, was a trial she endured with saintly fortitude. Through her private, silent prayers, she supported them both, a virtue so noble it demands silence, much like a flower blooming quietly in a garden rather than strutting in a ballroom. Yet, alas, what tragedy befalls those pure spirits when grief or death strikes them down, wasting them in sickness or cutting them short in their prime? Surely, Heaven chastens those it loves most, refining their souls through repeated trials.

As for Pen, the letter was dutifully posted and delivered to his box in Lamb Court, eventually reaching his table amidst a pile of laundry brought by Mrs. Flanagan, who was currently draining a glass of gin in the room where a portrait of Pen standing outside his chamber hung. Those who have tracked Mr. Arthur's career thus far likely suspect his cardinal flaw: his greatest enemy is himself. Like many, Pen harbors a "rascal" within—a spoiled servant who grows insolent and rude at the slightest hint of coercion. While Laura, accustomed to self-denial, handles sacrifice with ease, Pen, unaccustomed to restraint, reacts with savage grumbling whenever asked to forego a pleasure.

Consequently, Pen resolved to see Fanny no more. He attempted to purge her image from his mind through relentless work, exercise, and dissipation. He overworked, over-walked, over-rode, and consumed excessive quantities of food, drink, and tobacco. Yet, despite the cigars and punch, the vision of Fanny haunted his fever-brightened brain. After a week of this harsh regimen, the young gentleman collapsed into bed with a high fever. Let anyone who has never suffered a fever in a garret pity the wretched soul forced to endure such solitude. One might imagine a series of Cruikshank engravings depicting the terrors of bachelor life: the lonely breakfast with a cold kettle in midsummer, the return from holiday to find the fire extinguished and the landlord's bill awaiting, the discovery of a shirt missing its buttons, and finally, the horror of falling ill alone. There is no kind hand to offer water, no gentle smoothing of the hot pillow, only the companion of sickening fancies and fevered delirium.

Such a fate befell Arthur Pendennis. One night he succumbed to illness, waking the next day in a terrible state. His sole visitors, aside from the laundress, were a printer's devil from *Pall Mall Gazette*, whom Pen struggled to entertain, and Mr. Finucane, the Irish editor. Finucane, rushing to assist, filled the blanks in the newspaper with magnificent tributes to the greatness of Ireland and the genius of its inhabitants, delighting the absent Chief Editor, Shandon, who was relaxing at Boulogne. Finucane, a warm-hearted partisan, even offered to skip his weekend holidays to nurse Pen, though Arthur, prideful and stubborn, insisted on facing his malady alone.

Later, Captain Costigan, alerted by Finucane, visited Pen on a Sunday afternoon. He found the laundress, Mrs. Flanagan, weeping in the sitting-room, having resorted to brandy to cope with the shock of her charge's condition. Her attentions became so intrusive to the invalid that he begged her to leave, prompting further tears and alcohol consumption. However, Pen was relieved to hear Costigan's voice. Lying in his fever-stricken bed, haggard and unshaven, he managed to summon a lively chatter about Vauxhall, the Back Kitchen, and Fanny. "How is little Fanny?" he asked.

Indeed, Fanny was far from well. Having witnessed Arthur lighting his lamp in his chambers after meeting Captain Costigan, she returned home in a deep melancholy. Restless through the night, she clutched 'Walter Lorraine' under her pillow, unable to read it aloud due to her father's strict rules. The following day, she visited the musician, Mr. Bows. When he mentioned seeing Pen, Fanny's defensiveness flared, fearing he would speak ill of her beloved. Bows, however, assured her that Pen was a good and honest young man, but explained that their paths were mismatched. He recounted Pen's past, unrequited romance with Lady Mirabel, noting that Pen, now a man of the world, realized he must marry someone of equal rank and fortune. A penniless girl, regardless of virtue, could not sustain the future Pen envisioned.

Fanny's emotions shifted from anger to dismay. She pleaded ignorance, insisting she meant no harm, but Bows gently guided her toward accepting reality. He advised her to forget Pen for her own reputation's sake. Though she promised to obey, her heart remained torn. Later, she confided in her mother, weeping over the "inequality" of their conditions. She idolized Pen, imagining him as a noble poet hiding ancient wealth, vowing to work and study to become worthy of him. Her mother, however, warned her against such thoughts, suggesting that if Pen did not pursue her, he was unworthy. Yet, as soon as her father, Mr. Bolton, entered the lodge for supper, the mother and daughter instantly masked their sorrow with feigned cheer, a testament to the remarkable hypocrisy with which women often conceal their hearts.

Ironically, fate seemed determined to fuel Fanny's passion. Every circumstance around her, including Bows's warnings, only heightened her longing. Pen, meanwhile, was not a seducer; he was simply trying to crush a foolish, imprudent passion born of his youth, terrified of the shame and scandal it would bring to his family. He viewed marriage to a girl of low degree as impossible, a sentiment solidified by his worldly experience. Thus, he waited, hoping the feeling would fade, while Fanny waited, praying for a sign.

Her vigil lasted a full week until Captain Costigan informed her of Arthur's critical illness. That same evening, after Costigan's visit, Arthur's uncle, the Major, arrived in town from Buxton. He sent his valet, Morgan, to inquire for his nephew and arrange a breakfast. Morgan returned with a pale face, reporting that Arthur was extremely ill, burning with fever, and that no doctor had been summoned. The Major, horrified, immediately despatched Morgan to fetch Dr. Goodenough. The physician arrived promptly, confirming the severity of the case. He bled the patient instantly and ordered the Major to send for Mrs. Pendennis, warning that the situation was precarious.

The Major, though plagued by his own ailments, felt compelled to act. He wrote a frantic letter to Helen and traveled to Lord Steyne's house, where he sat in gloomy silence, losing his money at whist and dreaming of his dying nephew. The next morning, a mysterious note arrived from Mr. Bows, confirming Arthur's fragile state. Just as the Major prepared to hunt, a shabby, grey-headed figure emerged from a nearby carriage—a fly driven up from the station. It was Mr. Bows himself, seeking the Major in urgent haste. Seeing the alarm on the old gentleman's face, Bows whispered gravely, "It's... it's my boy that's dead."

Before the shock could fully register, a britzka pulled up. Lord Steyne, impatient for the mail train, shouted for the Major to jump in. As they sped away, another carriage approached, carrying two ladies asking directions. The Major recognized the panel of the vehicle: the worn crest of the Eagle looking at the Sun, the motto "Nec tenui penna." It was his sister's old carriage. Helen and Laura were inside, racing to Pen's room.

The Major ran to meet them, kissing his sister's hand, and together they hastened down Temple Lane, ascending the gloomy stairs to the door marked with Arthur's name. They knocked softly, and the door swung open. Standing there, in the doorway, was Fanny Bolton.
