The Miss Dashwoods had now been rather more than two months in town, and Marianne’s impatience to be gone gathered upon her like a pot left too long at the boil—quiet at first, then fretting, then threatening to spill over at the slightest touch. Every day she longed more fiercely for the country air, for the unbought liberty of lanes and hedgerows, for the honest hush that falls at Barton when the wind drops and only a distant cartwheel speaks. London had its own incessant clatter—the iron-shod hooves, the calling of vendors, the thin music that was made to be heard through walls and coaches and crowds—and to Marianne it had become not life but noise, an assault that left her nerves raw, as if she were made to dine perpetually on nothing but smoke and stale bread.

Elinor was hardly less anxious to remove; only she carried her desire as she carried every other strong feeling—carefully, with an attention to practicalities as exact as measuring flour. She could not forget that a long journey was not a sigh made audible and then gone, but hours of jolting wheels, cold inns, late suppers, and a sister whose spirits could not be commanded by reason. Marianne would not be brought to acknowledge any difficulty; she spoke of returning to Barton as if it were as easy as stepping from one room to another, as if longing itself were a carriage and could bear them in a moment over leagues of road.

Yet Elinor had begun, in earnest, to turn her thoughts toward their departure, and had already hinted their wishes to their kind hostess. Mrs. Jennings resisted with all the eager eloquence of good-will—pressing them to stay as one presses a guest to take one more helping, convinced that affection must surely be proved by quantity. While this was in dispute, a plan was suggested which, though it detained them from home a few weeks longer, seemed to Elinor far more eligible than any other.

The Palmers were to remove to Cleveland about the end of March for the Easter holidays; and Mrs. Jennings, with both her friends, received a very warm invitation from Charlotte to go with them. In itself this would not have satisfied Miss Dashwood’s delicacy; but it was enforced with so much real politeness by Mr. Palmer himself—whose manners toward them had softened greatly since Marianne’s unhappiness had been known—that Elinor accepted with pleasure. It promised a safer, more orderly progress toward home: Cleveland lay within a few miles of Bristol; from there, Barton might be reached in a long day, and their mother’s servant could come to attend them down. They need not stay above a week at Cleveland; they might be home in little more than three weeks.

But when Elinor told Marianne what she had done, her sister’s first answer came like a knife set suddenly against the grain.

“Cleveland!” she cried, and the name itself seemed to strike her breath away. “No, I cannot go to Cleveland.”

“You forget,” said Elinor gently, “that its situation is not—that it is not in the neighbourhood of—”

“But it is in Somersetshire. I cannot go into Somersetshire.—There, where I looked forward to going…” The words broke upon themselves, as if she had bitten into some remembered bitterness. “No, Elinor, you cannot expect me to go there.”

Elinor would not reason her out of a pain that could not be argued down. She attempted instead to draw forth another feeling and set it against the first. She spoke of their mother—of that dear home Marianne so passionately wished to see—and represented this plan as the most comfortable, the most certain means of fixing the time of their return. The mind that cannot bear one association will sometimes be carried by another; and Marianne’s affection for her mother was sincere. It triumphed, after not much struggle, over the imaginary evils she had started; and in yielding, she snatched at the only consolation she would allow herself—certainty. She found relief in drawing up, with almost feverish minuteness, a statement of the hours that were yet to divide her from Barton, as if time could be portioned and tasted like a broth that must be endured until the richer dish arrives.

Mrs. Jennings, far from being weary of her guests, pressed them earnestly to return with her again from Cleveland. Elinor was grateful, but could not be moved from her design. Their mother’s concurrence was readily gained; everything relative to their return was arranged as far as it could be; and so the days in town, though still noisy, began to take on a shape—an end in view, like a meal planned in courses.

It was soon after their leaving Mrs. Jennings had been settled, that Colonel Brandon first called on her.

“Ah! Colonel, I do not know what you and I shall do without the Miss Dashwoods,” was Mrs. Jennings’s address to him; “for they are quite resolved upon going home from the Palmers;—and how forlorn we shall be, when I come back!—Lord! we shall sit and gape at one another as dull as two cats.”

Perhaps she hoped, by sketching their coming emptiness so vigorously, to provoke him into an offer that would save him from it—an offer which, in her imagination, could have but one form. If so, she was not long without what she took for confirmation. Elinor moved to the window, to take with quicker light the dimensions of a print she meant to copy for a friend; and the Colonel followed. He spoke to her there with a look of particular meaning, conversing for several minutes. Mrs. Jennings was too honourable to listen; she even changed her seat on purpose that she might not hear, drawing herself close by the pianoforte where Marianne was playing. Yet she could not keep herself from seeing.

Elinor changed colour. There was agitation in her manner. She was too intent on what he said to pursue her employment. A woman who lives much in other people’s prospects has an eye sharpened for the smallest signs; and Mrs. Jennings, watching as she would watch a sauce thicken, judged that something decisive was happening at the window.

In the interval of Marianne’s turning from one lesson to another, a few words of the Colonel’s inevitably reached her ear. He seemed to be apologising for the badness of his house.

This, to Mrs. Jennings, set the matter beyond a doubt. She wondered, indeed, why he should find it necessary to do so—since love, in her creed, ought to be able to dine happily off whatever was set before it—but supposed it to be proper etiquette. What Elinor replied she could not distinguish; but from the motion of her lips she judged that she did not think that any material objection. Mrs. Jennings commended her in her heart for being so honest.

They continued a few minutes longer without her catching another syllable; then, by another fortunate pause in Marianne’s performance, she heard the Colonel’s calm voice say, “I am afraid it cannot take place very soon.”

Astonished and shocked at so unlover-like a sentence, she was almost ready to cry out, Lord! what should hinder it?—but she checked herself and confined her indignation to silence. This is very strange!—sure he need not wait to be older.

Yet this delay did not seem to offend or mortify his fair companion in the least. When the conference soon after ended and they moved apart, Mrs. Jennings very plainly heard Elinor say, and with a voice which showed her to feel what she said, “I shall always think myself very much obliged to you.”

Mrs. Jennings was delighted with her gratitude, and only wondered that, after hearing such a sentence, the Colonel could take leave with the utmost sang-froid, and go away without making Elinor any reply. She had not thought her old friend could be so indifferent a suitor.

What had really passed between them was this.

“I have heard,” said he, with great compassion, “of the injustice your friend Mr. Ferrars has suffered from his family; for if I understand the matter right, he has been entirely cast off by them for persevering in his engagement with a very deserving young woman. Have I been rightly informed? Is it so?”

Elinor told him that it was.

“The cruelty, the impolitic cruelty,” he replied, with feeling, “of dividing, or attempting to divide, two young people long attached to each other, is terrible. Mrs. Ferrars does not know what she may be doing—what she may drive her son to. I have seen Mr. Ferrars two or three times in Harley Street, and am much pleased with him. He is not a young man with whom one can be intimately acquainted in a short time, but I have seen enough of him to wish him well for his own sake; and as a friend of yours, I wish it still more. I understand that he intends to take orders. Will you be so good as to tell him that the living of Delaford, now just vacant, as I am informed by this day’s post, is his, if he think it worth his acceptance; but that, perhaps, so unfortunately circumstanced as he is now, it may be nonsense to appear to doubt; I only wish it were more valuable. It is a rectory, but a small one; the late incumbent, I believe, did not make more than two hundred pounds per annum, and though it is certainly capable of improvement, I fear not to such an amount as to afford him a very comfortable income. Such as it is, however, my pleasure in presenting it to him will be very great. Pray assure him of it.”

Elinor’s astonishment at this commission could hardly have been greater had he truly offered her his hand. Two days before, any preferment for Edward had seemed hopeless, like a table spread in imagination only; and now, in the space of a morning, the very thing that could make his future possible was provided—and she, of all people in the world, was made the instrument to bestow it. The suddenness of such relief had the disorienting effect of a fragrance that brings back a whole season at once—one is transported, yet does not know whether to weep or to laugh.

Her emotion was what Mrs. Jennings had mistaken for romantic confusion; but whatever lesser feelings, less pure, less pleasing, might mingle with it, Elinor’s esteem for Colonel Brandon’s benevolence, and her gratitude for his friendship, were strong and sincere. She thanked him with all her heart; she spoke of Edward’s principles and disposition with the praise she knew them to deserve; and she promised to undertake the commission with pleasure, if it were truly his wish to put off so agreeable an office to another. Yet she could not help thinking no one could perform it so well as himself. It was an office from which—unwilling to give Edward the pain of receiving an obligation from her—she would have been very glad to be spared; but Colonel Brandon, on motives of equal delicacy, declined it likewise. He appeared so desirous that it should be given through her means that she would not make further opposition.

Edward, she believed, was still in town; and, fortunately, she had heard his address from Miss Steele. She could therefore inform him in the course of the day.

After this had been settled, Colonel Brandon spoke of his own advantage in securing so respectable and agreeable a neighbour; and then it was he mentioned, with regret, that the house was small and indifferent—an evil which Elinor made very light of, at least as far as it regarded size.

“The smallness of the house,” said she, “I cannot imagine any inconvenience to them, for it will be in proportion to their family and income.”

By this the Colonel was surprised to find that she considered Mr. Ferrars’s marriage as the certain consequence of the presentation; for he did not suppose it possible that the Delaford living could supply such an income as anybody in Edward’s style of life would venture to settle on, and he said so.

“This little rectory can do no more than make Mr. Ferrars comfortable as a bachelor; it cannot enable him to marry. I am sorry to say that my patronage ends with this; and my interest is hardly more extensive. If, however, by an unforeseen chance it should be in my power to serve him farther, I must think very differently of him from what I now do, if I am not as ready to be useful to him then as I sincerely wish I could be at present. What I am now doing indeed seems nothing at all, since it can advance him so little towards what must be his principal, his only object of happiness. His marriage must still be a distant good; at least, I am afraid it cannot take place very soon.”

Such was the sentence which, misunderstood, had so justly offended Mrs. Jennings’s delicate feelings. And thus the gratitude Elinor expressed at parting—“I shall always think myself very much obliged to you”—may appear not less reasonably excited, nor less properly worded than if it had arisen from an offer of marriage.
