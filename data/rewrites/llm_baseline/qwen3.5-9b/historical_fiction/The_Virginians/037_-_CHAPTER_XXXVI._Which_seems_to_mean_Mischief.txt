Though the Baroness Bernstein had clearly suffered the worst of the skirmish detailed in the previous chapter, she displayed no trace of rancour or anger upon her next encounter with her niece.

"Of course, my Lady Maria," she began smoothly, "you cannot expect me, as Harry Warrington's close relative, to be delighted by the prospect of him marrying a woman who is nearly his mother's age and possesses no fortune whatsoever. Yet, if he chooses to pursue such a folly, the matter is entirely outside my control. Indeed, I suspect I would hardly have taken the offer of five thousand pounds seriously had I not been speaking in the heat of the moment."

"So it was already settled at Castlewood?" the Baroness pressed gently. "Had I known how far things had progressed, my dear, I might have spared some unnecessary opposition. When a pitcher is broken, what railing can possibly mend it?"

"Madam!" interrupted Maria sharply. "Please pardon me—I mean nothing against your ladyship's honor or character, which, I am sure, remain perfectly intact. Harry confirms it, and you confirm it—what more could anyone desire?"

"You have spoken with Mr. Warrington, madam?"

"And he admitted making you a promise at Castlewood; furthermore, you possess it in his own handwriting."

"Certainly I do, madam!" declared Lady Maria.

"Ah!" The elder lady did not flinch at this admission. "I must confess, however, that at first I misinterpreted the tone of your letters to him. They seemed to implicate other members of the family..."

"Who have spoken most wickedly of me and endeavored to prejudice me against my dear Mr. Warrington in every possible way. Yes, madam, I admit I wrote against them to defend my reputation."

"But surely, you are pained to think that any wretch should seize upon stories damaging to our family name and turn them into public scandal. Hence your recent unease."

"Exactly so," replied Lady Maria. "From Mr. Warrington, I could conceal nothing henceforth, and I spoke freely to him. However, that is vastly different from wishing the entire world to know the domestic disputes of a noble household."

"Upon my word, Maria, I admire you, and I have done you a grave injustice. For these—say, twenty years."

"I am exceedingly glad, madam, that you conclude by granting me justice at all," said the niece.

"When I saw you last night, opening the ball with my nephew, can you guess what occupied my thoughts, my dear?"

"I truly have no idea what the Baroness de Bernstein was thinking," Lady Maria retorted haughtily.

"I merely recalled that you once performed to that very tune with the dancing-master at Kensington, my dear!"

"Madam, that was an infamous slander."

"Which resulted in the poor dancing-master receiving a severe beating for nothing!"

"It is cruel and unkind, madam, to revive that calumny—and I shall respectfully request to decline residing any longer with anyone who repeats it," Maria continued with remarkable spirit.

"You wish to return home? I imagine you will find Tunbridge disagreeable. It will be terribly hot for you if those letters are discovered."

"There was not a single word against you in them, madam; rest assured on that point."

"So Harry said, and you have done your ladyship justice. Well, my dear, we are both weary of one another and shall be better apart for a while."

"That is precisely my own opinion," said Lady Maria, curtsying briefly. "Mr. Sampson can escort you to Castlewood. You and your maid may take a postchaise."

"We can take a postchaise, and Mr. Sampson can escort me," echoed the younger lady. "You see, madam, I act like a dutiful niece."

"Do you know, my dear, I have a notion that Sampson has obtained the letters?" the Baroness said frankly.

"I confess that such a thought has crossed my own mind."

"And you intend to go home in the chaise and coax the letters from him! Delilah! Well, they can be of no use to me, and I trust you may succeed in retrieving them. When will you depart? The sooner the better, you say? We are women of the world, Maria. We only exchange names when we are in a passion. We do not require each other's company anymore, and we part on good terms. Shall we proceed to Lady Yarmouth's? It is her night. Nothing dispels unpleasant thoughts like a change of scene after one of those nervous attacks you have suffered, and card-playing drives away gloom better than any physician."

Lady Maria agreed to attend Lady Yarmouth's gathering. She was dressed and waiting in the drawing-room before her aunt arrived. As Madame Bernstein descended the stairs, she noted that Maria's door stood ajar.

"She has the letters upon her," the old lady thought.

The pair departed for their respective entertainments in separate carriages, displaying toward one another that charming cordiality and mutual respect which women can exhibit even after, and sometimes during, the bitterest quarrels.

That night, upon returning from the Countess's assembly, Mrs. Brett, the Baroness's maid, presented herself to answer Lady Maria's summons as the lady prepared to retire. When Lady Maria rang her handbell, Mrs. Brett hesitated. Betty, her companion, was not in a fit state to appear before her mistress. Betty had been carousing with Mr. Warrington's black attendant, Lord Bamborough's valet, and several other gentlemen and ladies of similar station. The liquor, Mrs. Brett confessed with shock, had proven too much for Mrs. Betty.

"Should I undress my lady?" Mrs. Brett asked timidly.

"My lady will undress without assistance," Lady Maria replied, giving Mrs. Brett leave to withdraw.

"She has the letters hidden in her stays," Madame Bernstein surmised.

Having bid each other an amicable goodnight on the stairs, the servants retreated. The following morning, Betty received a stern scolding from the closet adjoining Lady Maria's apartment, where she lay recovering. With contrition, she acknowledged her weakness for rum-punch—a delicacy brewed by Mr. Gumbo, whose skill in mixing it was renowned. She accepted her reprimand meekly, performed her usual duties regarding her mistress's person, and retired.

Betty was one of the Castlewood maids who had been utterly captivated by Gumbo. She was a striking, blue-eyed lass upon whom Mr. Case, the Baroness's confidential man, had also cast affectionate eyes. Consequently, jealousies and even quarrels had arisen between Messrs. Gumbo and Case. This tension had caused Gumbo, naturally a peaceful soul, to become shy around the Baroness's gentlemen, the majority of whom vowed to break his bones or end his life if he persisted in his attentions to Mrs. Betty.

However, on the night of the rum-punch, although Mr. Case spotted Gumbo and Mrs. Betty whispering in the doorway amidst the cool breeze, and Gumbo would have turned pale with terror had he been capable, no one was more gracious than Mr. Case. It was he who proposed the bowl of punch, which was brewed and consumed in Mrs. Betty's room, concocted by Gumbo with exquisite skill. He complimented Gumbo on his musical talents. Though usually sober, he insisted on drinking more and more until poor Mrs. Betty was reduced to the state that occasioned her ladyship's just censure.

As for Mr. Case himself, who remained out of the house, he was so violently ill from the punch that he confined himself to his bed the entire next day. He lacked the strength to appear and wait upon his ladies until supper-time, when his mistress, in a display of good nature, rebuked him lightly, noting that it was rare for him to sin in such a manner.

"Why, Case, I could have sworn it was you I saw on horseback this morning galloping along the London road," remarked Mr. Warrington, who was dining with his relatives.

"Me! Law bless you, sir! I was in bed, and I thought my head would roll off with the ache. I ate a bite at six and drank a fair amount of small beer, but I am almost my own man again now. But that Gumbo, saving your honor's presence, I shall not taste another drop of his punch."

Thus, the honest major-domo resumed his duties among the bottles and glasses.

After the meal, Madame Bernstein was surprisingly friendly. She prescribed strong fortifying drinks for Maria to prevent the recurrence of her fainting fits, which occurred not infrequently. She urged her to consult her London physician and instructed her to send an account of her case via Harry.

"By Harry?" the lady queried.

"Yes. Harry is departing for two days on an errand for his aunt to London."

"I do not care to inform you, my dear, that it is business which will prove beneficial to him. I wish Mr. Draper to include him in my will. Furthermore, as I am traveling on a round of visits when we part, I believe, for security's sake, I shall request that Mr. Warrington carry my trinket-box in his postchaise to London, for there have been robberies lately, and I have no inclination to be halted by highwaymen."
