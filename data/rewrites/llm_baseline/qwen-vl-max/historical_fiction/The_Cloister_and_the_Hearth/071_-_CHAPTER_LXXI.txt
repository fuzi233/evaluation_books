“As is the race of leaves, so is that of men.”

This year, in a quiet tailor’s house in Rotterdam, a child was born—unnoticed by the world, yet destined to leave an indelible mark upon it. At the same time, a great man fell from grace, not with a whisper, but with a thunderous crash. Philip, Duke of Burgundy, Earl of Holland, and many other titles too numerous to list, lay dying in Bruges.

In those days, sickness came swiftly to the poor and passed as quietly as it came. But for the rich, illness was a perilous trial. For every one who perished from disease, three more were lost to the remedies prescribed by doctors—whose cures often proved more deadly than the malady itself. The Duke’s illness, unnamed then, we now know as diphtheria: a wasting sickness, cruel and relentless. And though he was old, his strength had been formidable. Still, Dr. Remedy saw only one course: bloodletting.

The Duke grew cold. His limbs stiffened. The physicians stood in awe—this was no ordinary decline. Then Dr. Remedy turned to the deeper mysteries of science. “Ho!” he declared. “This is grave. Fetch me an ape at once, and place it against the Duke’s chest!”

At this command, officials of state scrambled through the streets, frantic for an ape—any ape—to counteract the human body’s supposed thirst for blood. But alas! No ape could be found. There were buffaloes, lizards, Turks, leopards—beasts enough to fill a menagerie—but none of them bore the resemblance needed.

“Why,” said one servant, “there used to be an ape here. I saw him myself standing on the gatepost.”

And indeed there had been—until the mastiff tore him apart for stealing his supper. Thus the beast fulfilled the human maxim: *Soyez de votre siècle!*—be of your age.

Despair gripped the court. Then, the seneschal cast his eyes about—and suddenly, hope flickered. “Here!” he whispered, pointing. “This may serve. It is altogether apelike—save for the doublet and hose.”

And what did he see? None other than our friend Giles, strutting proudly in cloth of gold, unaware of the chaos he was about to unleash.

“No!” snapped the chancellor, alarmed. “The Princess Marie would hang us. She favors this boy dearly.”

But Dr. Remedy, growing impatient, refused to wait. “Then flay a dog!” he ordered. “A dog is next best to an ape—only it must be of one color.”

So they seized a liver-colored hound, skinned it while still alive, and pressed its quivering flesh against the Duke’s breast. Yet it was too late. Philip the Good died—his life extinguished not by nature, but by the misguided art of medicine.

He left behind thirty-one children—only one of whom was officially legitimate. That son would inherit the throne. The good duke provided for nineteen of the others; the rest were left to fend for themselves.

According to Flemish chronicles, the deceased prince traced his lineage back to the kings of Troy, through Thierry of Aquitaine, Chilperic, Pharamond, and the ancient rulers of Franconia. But such claims were common among nobles of the age. In truth, nearly every prince of the time claimed descent from Troy. “Priam” had become, in medieval lore, a synonym for “Adam”—the first man, the root of all royal lines.

Philip’s body was carried to Burgundy and laid to rest in a grand mausoleum of black marble at Dijon. Throughout Holland, bells tolled and mourning echoed. Yet few knew—or cared—that in the same year, something far greater was born within the walls of a humble tailor’s home.

Now, centuries later, history has reversed its judgment. Men gaze upon that modest dwelling in Rotterdam, where the most significant birth of the fifteenth century took place. As for the house where the Duke died—no one knows it, nor does anyone care. Why? Because princes like Philip the Good come and go, leaving mankind no wiser, no better, no different than before.

But when, once in three hundred years, a child like Margaret’s son enters the world—then a torch is lit, kindled by fire from heaven. And across the earth, the cry rings out: *FIAT LUX*—Let there be light.
