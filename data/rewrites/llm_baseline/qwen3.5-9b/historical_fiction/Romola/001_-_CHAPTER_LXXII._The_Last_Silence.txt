More than three hundred and fifty years ago, in the midst of May, 1492, the angel of the dawn, traveling with his broad, slow wings from the Levant to the Pillars of Hercules, and from the snow-capped summits of the Caucasus across the Alpine ridges to the dark, bare islands of the West, beheld nearly the same outline of firm land and unstable sea. He saw the same great mountain shadows cast upon the same valleys; the same olive groves, pine forests, and broad plains verdant with young corn or refreshed by rain. He saw the domes and spires of cities rising by riversides or mingled with the sedge-like masts along the curved coast, in precisely the same spots where they rise today. As the faint light of his passage pierced into human dwellings, it fell, as it does now, upon the rosy warmth of nesting infants; upon the haggard awakening of sorrow and sickness; upon the hurried rising of the hard-handed laborer; and upon the late slumber of the night-student, who had been questioning the stars, the sages, or his own soul for that hidden knowledge capable of breaking through the barrier of man's brief life, revealing its dark path as an arc within an immeasurable circle of light and glory.

The great river courses that have shaped the lives of mankind have scarcely altered; nor have those other streams, the currents of life flowing within human hearts, ceased to pulse toward the same great needs, the same profound loves and terrors. As our thought follows closely in the slow wake of the dawn, we are struck by the broad sameness of the human condition, which remains unchanged in the main headings of its history: hunger and labor, seed-time and harvest, love and death. Even if, instead of tracking the dim daybreak, our imagination pauses at a specific historical point to await the fuller morning, we may behold a world-renowned city that has hardly altered its silhouette since the days of Columbus. It stands seemingly as an almost unviolated symbol amidst the flux of human affairs, reminding us that we resemble the men of the past more than we differ from them, just as the great mechanical principles governing the construction of those domes and towers ensure a likeness in human architecture that transcends all possible change.

Suppose, then, that the spirit of a Florentine citizen, whose eyes were closed forever while Columbus waited and argued for his three humble vessels at the port of Palos, were permitted to return from the shades and pause where our thoughts now pause. This Spirit, clothed in the habit of his life, wears his well-lined black silk *lucco* in grave, unbroken lines from neck to ankle. Upon his head rests a plain cloth cap with its *becchetto*, a long hanging strip of drapery serving as a scarf in need, surmounting a penetrating face—not perhaps very handsome, yet distinguished by a firm, well-cut mouth, kept distinctly human by a close-shaven lip and chin. It is a face charged with the memories of a keen and varied life spent on the banks of the gleaming river below. As he gazes upon the scene, the sense of familiarity overwhelms the perception of change, leading him to think it possible to descend once more into the streets and resume that busy existence where he left it.

For it is not merely the mountains and the westward-bending river that he recognizes; not only the dark flanks of Mount Morello opposite him, nor the long valley of the Arno stretching its grey, low-tufted luxuriance toward the distant ridges of Carrara. He sees the steep height of Fiesole, crowned with monastic walls and cypresses; and all the green and grey slopes sprinkled with villas, naming them as he looks. Yet, though he misses the seventy or more towers that once surmounted the city walls like a regal diadem, his eyes refuse to dwell on that blank. They are drawn irresistibly to the unique tower springing, like a tall flower-stem reaching for the sun, from the square, turreted mass of the Old Palace in the very heart of the city—the tower that looks no worse for the four centuries passed since he walked beneath it.

The great dome, the greatest in the world, which in his early boyhood had been nothing more than a daring thought in the mind of a small, quick-eyed man—there it raises its vast curves still, eclipsing the hills. The well-known bell-towers appear: Giotto's, with its distant hint of rich color; the gracefully spired Badia; and the rest, all viewed once from the shoulder of his nurse. "Surely," he thinks, "Florence can still ring her bells with the solemn hammer-sound that used to beat upon the hearts of her citizens and strike out the fire within them. And here, on the right, stands the long dark mass of Santa Croce, where we buried our famous dead, laying laurels on their cold brows and fanning them with the breath of praise and banners. But Santa Croce had no spire then; we Florentines were too consumed with great building projects to complete them all in stone and marble. We had our frescoes and shrines to fund, not to mention rapacious condottieri, bribed royalty, and purchased territories. Our façades and spires had to wait. But what architect could the Frati Minori (the Franciscans) have employed to build that spire for them? If it had been constructed in my day, Filippo Brunelleschi or Michelozzo would have devised something of another fashion—something worthy to crown the church of Arnolfo."

At this, the Spirit sighs and turns his gaze to the city walls, dwelling now on the changes there with wonder at these modern times. Why have five of the eleven convenient gates been closed? And why, above all, should the towers that were once a glory and defense have been leveled? Has the world become so peaceful, and do Florentines dwell in such harmony, that there are no longer conspiracies to bring ambitious exiles home with armed bands at their backs? These are difficult questions; it is easier and pleasanter to recognize the old than to account for the new.

And flows the Arno, with its bridges exactly where they used to be—the Ponte Vecchio, least like any other bridge in the world, laden with the same quaint shops where our Spirit remembers perhaps lingering to watch the progress of that great palace Messer Luca Pitti was setting afoot with huge stones from the Hill of Bogoli (now Boboli), or perhaps transacting business with the cloth-dressers in Oltrarno. The exorbitant line of the Pitti roof is hidden from San Miniato, but the yearning of the old Florentine is not to see Messer Luca's overly ambitious palace, built unto himself; it is to be down among those narrow streets and busy, humming piazzas where he inherited the eager life of his fathers. Is not the anxious voting with black and white beans still going on down there? Who are the Priori in these months, eating soberly regulated official dinners in the Palazzo Vecchio, with removals of tripe and boiled partridges, seasoned by practical jokes against the ill-fated butt among those potent signors? Are not the significant banners still hung from the windows—still distributed with decent pomp under Orcagna's Loggia every two months? Life had its zest for the old Florentine when he, too, trod the marble steps and shared in those dignities.

His politics possessed an area as wide as his trade, stretching from Syria to Britain, yet they retained the passionate intensity and detailed practical interest belonging only to a narrow scene of corporate action; only to the members of a community shut in close by hills and walls of six miles' circuit, where men knew one another as they passed in the street, set their eyes daily on the memorials of their commonwealth, and were conscious of having not simply the right to vote, but the chance of being voted for. He loved his honors and his gains—the business of his counting-house, his guild, his public council chamber. He loved his enmities too, fingering the white bean destined to keep a hated name out of the *borsa* with more complacency than if it had been a golden florin. He loved to strengthen his family through a good alliance, returning home with a triumphant light in his eyes after concluding a satisfactory marriage for his son or daughter under his favorite loggia in the evening cool. He loved his games of chess under that same loggia, his biting jests, and even his coarse jokes, considering them beneath no dignity of a man eligible for the highest magistracy.

He had gained insight into all sorts of affairs at home and abroad: he had been of the "Ten" managing the war department, of the "Eight" attending to home discipline, of the Priori or Signori heading the executive government; he had even risen to the supreme office of Gonfaloniere. He had served in embassies to the Pope and the Venetians, and as commissary to the Republic's hired army, directing the inglorious, bloodless battles in which no man died of brave breast wounds—*virtuosi colpi*—but only of casual falls and trampling. In this way, he learned to distrust men without bitterness, viewing life mainly as a game of skill, yet not dead to traditions of heroism and clean-handed honor. For the human soul is hospitable, entertaining conflicting sentiments and contradictory opinions with much impartiality.
