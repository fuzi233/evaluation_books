There is scarcely a day that passes beneath the sun wherein men and women of obscurity do not perform great deeds, utter profound words, and endure noble sorrows. Among these forgotten heroes, philosophers, and martyrs, the majority will remain unknown until that distant hour when the mighty shall shrink to insignificance, and the humble shall rise to greatness. Yet, regarding others, the world's memory remains asleep; their lives and characters lie buried in the archives of nations, hidden from view. To the general reader, these figures offer no warmth; they are presented so briefly and coldly that they feel less like breathing stories appealing to the heart, and more like tiny hailstones striking the chest and glancing harmlessly off. Nor can one truly understand them, for an epitome is not a narrative, just as a skeleton is not a living figure.

Consequently, records of fundamental truths often remain a dead letter to the common folk. Writers have left too much to the imagination—a gift that is exceedingly rare. Here, therefore, the author of fiction may serve the public as an interpreter.

Consider a musty chronicle, penned in intolerable Latin, containing a chapter where every sentence bears a heavy fact. Within it is recorded, with harsh brevity, the strange history of a pair who lived unheard and died unsung four hundred years ago. They now lie, unpitied, in that stern page like fossils trapped in rock. Whether living or dead, Fate remains unjust to them. For if I can reveal what lies beneath those dry chronicler's words, I trust you will correct the indifference of centuries and grant those two sore-tried souls a place in your heart—for at least a day.

It was past the midpoint of the fifteenth century. Louis XI ruled France; Edward IV sat, wrongly, upon the throne of England; and Philip "the Good," having seized power through force and cunning against his cousin Jacqueline and shattered her spirit, reigned undisturbed over Holland, where our tale commences.

In the little town of Tergou lived Elias and his wife, Catherine. Elias engaged in both wholesale and retail trade, dealing in cloth, silk, brown holland, and, most notably, curried leather. This material was highly prized by the middle classes for its durability, able to withstand twenty years of wear. In a century where men were so liberal with their steel—even carving neighbors at dinner over minor disagreements—a jerkin that could resist a blade was a significant virtue.

The couple were comfortably situated and would have been free from worldly cares but for their nine children. When these offspring arrived, one per year, they were greeted with rejoicing, and saints were thanked rather than questioned. While parents and children were young, the latter were viewed as lovely playthings invented by Heaven solely for the amusement, joy, and evening solace of busy adults.

However, as the olive branches sprouted upward and the parents aged, witnessing firsthand the fate of large families, misgivings and anxiety mingled with their love. They belonged to a remarkably wise and provident people; in Holland, reckless parents were as rare as disobedient children. Thus, when a massive loaf arrived on a gigantic trencher, resembling a fortress surrounded by a moat, and appeared to vanish after the tour of the table, Elias and Catherine would exchange a worried glance. "Who will find bread for them all when we are gone?" they would ask.

At this observation, the younger children required all their filial respect to maintain their little Dutch composure. In their minds, dinner and supper came by nature like sunrise and sunset. As long as the sun traveled around the earth and the brown loaf circled their family circle—setting in their stomachs only to rise again in the family oven—they believed they were secure. But this remark awakened the national thoughtfulness of the elder boys. Being often repeated, it caused several members of the family to ponder, generating both good and ill thoughts depending on the thinker's nature.

"Kate, the children are growing so large; this table will soon be too small."

"We cannot afford it, Eli," replied Catherine, answering not his words but his thought, in the manner of women.

Their anxiety for the future occasionally took a less dismal but more mortifying turn. Free burghers possessed pride equal to that of nobles, and these two could not bear the thought of any of their blood descending into servitude after their deaths. Through prudence and self-denial, they managed to clothe all the little bodies, feed all the great mouths, and yet save a small hoard for the future. As this fund grew, they experienced a pleasure the miser hoarding for himself knows not.

One day, the second-eldest son, aged nineteen, approached his mother. With that outward composure that has so often misled observers regarding the true nature of this people, he begged her to intercede with his father to send him to Amsterdam to apprentice with a merchant. "It is the path suited to my nature," he pleaded. "Merchants are wealthy; I am skilled with numbers. Pray, good mother, support me in this, and I shall forever remain, as I am now, your debtor."

Catherine threw up her hands in dismay and incredulity. "What! Leave Tergou!"

"What is one street to me more than another? If I can leave the people of Tergou, I can surely leave the stones."

"What! Quit your poor father while he is no longer young?"

"Mother, if I can leave you, I can leave..."

"What! Leave your poor brothers and sisters who love you so dearly?"

"There are enough in the house without me."

"What mean you, Richart? Who is more thought of than you? Stay, have I spoken sharply to you? Have I been unkind?"

"Never that I know of; and if you had, you should never hear of it from me. Mother," said Richart gravely, though a tear glistened in his eye, "it all comes down to a single word, and nothing can change my mind. There will be one mouth less for you to feed."

"There now, see what my tongue has done," said Catherine, and the next moment she began to weep. She saw her first young bird standing on the edge of the nest, testing his wings to fly into the world. Richart possessed a calm, strong will, and she knew he never wasted a word.

The conversation ended as nature decrees all such discourses shall: young Richart departed for Amsterdam with a face so long and sad as it had never been seen before, and a heart like granite.

That afternoon at supper, there was one mouth less. Catherine looked at Richart's empty chair and wept bitterly. Overhearing this, Elias shouted roughly and angrily at the remaining children, "Sit wider, can't ye? Sit wider!" He then turned his head away over the back of his seat for a while, falling into silence.

Richart was launched and never cost them another penny; however, fitting him out and placing him in the house of Vander Stegen, the merchant, consumed their entire little hoard minus one gold crown. They began anew. Two years later, Richart found a niche in commerce for his brother Jacob, who left Tergou directly after dinner—which was served at eleven in the forenoon. At that supper, remembering the previous departure, Elias whispered, "Sit wider, dears!" Until that moment, Catherine had refused to acknowledge the gap at the table, for her daughter Catherine had beseeched her not to grieve that night. "No, sweetheart, I promise I will not, since it vexes my children," she had replied. But when Elias whispered "Sit wider!", she answered, "Ay! The table will soon be too big for the children, and you thought it would be too small." Delivering this with forced calmness, she raised her apron the next moment and wept sorely.

"'Tis the best that leave us," she sobbed. "That is the cruel part."

"Nay! Nay!" said Elias. "Our children are good children, and all are dear to us alike. Heed her not! What God takes from us still seems better than what He spares to us; that is to say, men are by nature unthankful—and women silly."

"And I say Richart and Jacob were the flower of the flock," sobbed Catherine.

The little coffer was empty again, and to refill it, they gathered like ants. In those days, speculation was largely confined to the card-and-dice business. Elias knew no way to wealth but the slow and sure method. "A penny saved is a penny gained" was his humble creed. Everything not required for business and the necessities of life went into the little coffer, secured with steel bands and a florid key. They denied themselves, in turn, the humblest luxuries, and then, catching one another's looks, smiled—with perhaps greater joy than self-indulgence could bestow. Thus, in three more years, they had gleaned enough to establish their fourth son as a master tailor and their eldest daughter as a robe-maker in Tergou. Their own trade enabled them to throw work into the hands of this pair. But the coffer was drained to the dregs, and this time the shop itself bled slightly in goods, if not in coin.

Alas! Two remained unable to earn their bread, and two were unwilling. The unable ones were: first, Giles, a dwarf of the wrong sort, possessing half stupidity and half malice, all head, claws, and voice.
