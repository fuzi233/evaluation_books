The cyclical rhythms of Barton Cottage, governed by the ancestral turning of the seasons and the meticulous preservation of agrarian culinary traditions, absorbed Edward Ferrars’s arrival as naturally as the fertile earth drinks the autumn rain. Mrs. Dashwood was surprised only for a moment at seeing him; for his coming to their sanctuary was, in her opinion, of all things the most natural. Her joy and expression of regard long outlived her wonder. She welcomed him into the kitchen, where the tactile rhythm of kneading a sourdough starter and the rich, earthy olfactory landscape of roasting mutton—rubbed with foraged thyme and ancient rock salt—seemed to instantly thaw the frost of his shyness. 

They had begun to fail him before he crossed the threshold, his coldness and reserve quite overcome by the captivating, deeply grounded manners of Mrs. Dashwood. A man could not very well be in love with either of her daughters without extending the passion to her; and Elinor had the satisfaction of seeing him soon become more like himself, anchoring his presence in the sensory warmth of their home. His affections seemed to reanimate toward them all, yet an undercurrent of dissonance remained. He praised their house, breathed in the fragrant steam of the meal, and was attentive; but he was not in spirits. The whole family perceived this heavy timbre in his mood. Mrs. Dashwood, attributing it to some want of liberality in his mother, sat down to the table indignant against all selfish parents whose demands disrupted the natural harmony of their children’s lives.

“What are Mrs. Ferrars’s views for you at present, Edward?” she asked, as dinner concluded and they drew round the hearth, the rhythmic crackle of the fire weaving a protective acoustic barrier against the chill outside. “Are you still to be forced into the noisy, dominant lexicon of a public orator, in spite of yourself?”

“No. I hope my mother is now convinced that I have no more talents than inclination for a public life,” he replied, tracing the rim of a glass of cloudy, heritage cider pressed by the village’s elder tenants. “The hollow acoustics and relentless industrial clamor of London politics offer me no sustenance.”

“But how is your fame to be established?” Mrs. Dashwood pressed. “For famous you must be to satisfy all your family; and with no inclination for expense, no affection for the superficial chatter of strangers, no profession, and no assurance, you may find it a difficult matter to translate yourself into their world.”

“I shall not attempt it. I have no wish to be distinguished, and have every reason to hope I never shall. Thank Heaven! I cannot be forced into genius and eloquence.”

“You have no ambition, I well know. Your wishes are all moderate.”

“As moderate as those of the rest of the world, I believe. I wish as well as everybody else to be perfectly happy; but, like everybody else, it must be in my own native dialect. Greatness will not make me so.”

“Strange that it would!” cried Marianne. “What have wealth or grandeur to do with happiness?”

“Grandeur has but little,” said Elinor, “but wealth has much to do with it.”

“Elinor, for shame!” said Marianne. “Money can only give happiness where the sensory and emotional life is starved. Beyond a competence, it can afford no real satisfaction to the soul.”

“Perhaps,” said Elinor, smiling, “we may come to the same point. *Your* competence and *my* wealth are very much alike, I dare say; and without them, as the world goes now, we shall both agree that the physical sanctuary necessary to protect our well-being must be wanting. Your ideas are only more noble than mine. Come, what is your competence?”

“About eighteen hundred or two thousand a year; not more than *that*.”

Elinor laughed. “*Two* thousand a year! *One* is my wealth! I guessed how it would end.”

“And yet two thousand a year is a very moderate income,” said Marianne. “A family cannot well be maintained, nor their ancestral routines preserved, on a smaller. A proper establishment of servants, a carriage, perhaps two, and hunters, cannot be supported on less.” 

Elinor smiled again, recognizing the precise acoustic landscape of Combe Magna—the rhythmic thrum of hooves and the rustle of vast estates—embedded in her sister’s demands.

“Hunters!” repeated Edward. “But why must you have hunters? Everybody does not hunt.”

Marianne colored as she replied, “But most people do.”

“I wish,” said Margaret, striking out a novel thought, “that somebody would give us all a large fortune apiece!”

“Oh that they would!” cried Marianne, her eyes sparkling with animation at the thought of insulating her world from the erosion of want. 

“We are all unanimous in that wish, I suppose,” said Elinor, “in spite of the insufficiency of wealth.”

“I should be puzzled to spend so large a fortune myself,” said Mrs. Dashwood, scraping a spoonful of caramelized shallot and wild garlic from her plate, savoring the profound meaning found in such humble sustenance, “if my children were all to be rich without my help.”

“You must begin your improvements on this house,” observed Elinor, “and your difficulties will soon vanish.”

“What magnificent orders would travel from this family to London,” said Edward, teasingly critiquing the superficial commercialization of their era. “What a happy day for the merchants of polite society! You, Miss Dashwood, would give a general commission for every new print of merit. And as for Marianne, I know her greatness of soul—she would recognize that London’s commercial noise could never yield enough genuine music to content her. And books! Thomson, Cowper, Scott—she would buy them all over and over again. She would buy up every copy, I believe, to rescue them from the sterile grasp of unworthy hands, preserving their cultural memory. She would hoard every book that tells her how to admire an old twisted tree, guarding the mythic earth from industry. Should not you, Marianne? Forgive me, if I am very saucy. But I was willing to show you that I had not forgot our old disputes.”

“I love to be reminded of the past, Edward,” Marianne said softly. “Whether it be melancholy or gay, I love the circular echo of former times—and you will never offend me by recalling it. You are very right in supposing how my money would be spent; my loose cash would certainly be employed in improving my collection of music and books, keeping those fading native dialects alive.”

“And the bulk of your fortune would be laid out in annuities on the authors or their heirs.”

“No, Edward, I should have something else to do with it.”

“Perhaps, then, you would bestow it as a reward on that person who wrote the ablest defense of your favorite maxim, that no one can ever be in love more than once in their life—your opinion on the untranslatability of that singular experience is unchanged, I presume?”

“Undoubtedly. At my time of life opinions are tolerably fixed. It is not likely that I should now see or hear anything to change them.”

“Marianne is as steadfast as ever, you see,” said Elinor. “She is not at all altered.”

“She is only grown a little more grave than she was.”

“Nay, Edward,” said Marianne, “*you* need not reproach me. You are not very gay yourself.”

“Why should you think so!” replied he, with a sigh that seemed to merge with the settling ash of the hearth. “But gaiety never was a part of *my* character.”

“Nor do I think it a part of Marianne’s,” said Elinor. “I should hardly call her a lively girl—she is very earnest, very eager in all she does, firmly rejecting anything that lacks true sensory resonance—but she is not often really merry.”

“I believe you are right,” he replied, “and yet I have always set her down as a lively girl.”

“I have frequently detected myself in such kind of mistakes,” said Elinor, “in a total misapprehension of character—fancying people so much more gay or grave, or ingenious or stupid than they really are. The untranslatability of another's subjective experience is a vast gulf. Sometimes one is guided by what they say of themselves, and very frequently by the dominant lexicon of what other people say of them, without giving oneself time to deliberate.”

“But I thought it was right, Elinor,” said Marianne, her tone sharpening, “to be guided wholly by the opinion of other people. I thought our judgments were given us merely to be subservient to the superficial acoustics of our neighbors. This has always been your doctrine, I am sure.”

“No, Marianne, never. My doctrine has never aimed at the subjection of the understanding to a dominant power dynamic. All I have ever attempted to influence has been the behavior—the necessary translation of oneself into a civil dialect so that one might peacefully exist in society. I am guilty of wishing you to treat our acquaintance with greater attention, yes; but when have I advised you to adopt their hollow sentiments or to conform your inner truth to their judgment?”

“You have not been able to bring your sister over to your plan of general civility,” said Edward to Elinor. “Do you gain no ground?”

“Quite the contrary,” replied Elinor, looking expressively at Marianne.

“My judgment,” he returned, “is all on your side of the question; but I am afraid my practice is much more on your sister’s. I never wish to offend, but I am so foolishly shy, that I often seem negligent, when I am only kept back by my natural awkwardness. I have frequently thought that I must have been intended by nature to be fond of low company, grounded in the simpler rhythms of agrarian life, for I am so little at my ease among the polished, dissonant strangers of gentility!”

“Marianne has not shyness to excuse any inattention of hers,” said Elinor.

“She knows her own worth too well for false shame,” replied Edward. “Shyness is only the effect of a sense of inferiority in some way or other. If I could persuade myself that my manners were perfectly easy and graceful, I should not be shy.”

“But you would still be reserved,” said Marianne, “and that is worse.”

Edward started, the word striking a jarring, dissonant chord in the room’s acoustic warmth. “Reserved! Am I reserved, Marianne?”

“Yes, very.”

“I do not understand you,” replied he, coloring deeply, the sudden pressure of her accusation suffocating him. “Reserved!—how, in what manner? What am I to tell you? What can you suppose?” 

The sheer impossibility of translating his subjective reality—the secret, inescapable linguistic trap of his engagement—into a language they could comprehend overwhelmed him. Elinor looked surprised at his emotion, but trying to laugh off the subject, she sought to temper the tension. 

“Do not you know my sister well enough to understand what she means?” she said to him. “Do not you know she calls everyone reserved who does not talk as fast, and admire the sonic and physical landscapes she admires as rapturously as herself?”

Edward made no answer. The brief animation that the cottage’s sustaining food and fire had given him evaporated entirely. His gravity and thoughtfulness returned on him in their fullest extent, and he sat for some time silent and dull, his internal dissonance thoroughly drowning out the comforting rhythms of the hearth.
