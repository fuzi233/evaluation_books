From that week forward, my life no longer dragged in unbroken sameness. Madame Beck gave me full, ungrudging leave to go out whenever I pleased—she had always treated me with unwavering respect from the day I joined her establishment, and when she saw I was receiving regular invitations to the grand château outside town and the fashionable hotel where the de Bassompiere party stayed, that respect softened into deliberate, gracious favour.

She was never fawning, of course. For all her worldly pragmatism, Madame had no weakness for toadying, no clumsy eagerness that would have earned my contempt. She pursued her self-interest with measured sense, held tight to her gains with calm consideration, and made it clear without ever saying so outright that she was pleased one of her teachers moved in circles that elevated, rather than dragged down, the reputation of her pensionnat. She never praised my character or my friends to my face, only once, when she sat sunning herself in the garden with a cup of coffee at her elbow and the Gazette spread open on the bench, did she speak warmly when I asked for leave to go out for the evening:
“Yes, yes, my good friend, I give you permission with all my heart. Your work in my house has always been admirable, full of zeal and discretion, and you have earned every right to amusement. Go out as often as you like. As for your choice of acquaintances? I am perfectly content with it. It is sensible, dignified, laudable.”
She snapped her lips shut and returned to her paper.

It was around that time that my small, triple-wrapped packet of five letters went missing from my bureau drawer. My first instinct was blank dismay, but I steadied myself almost immediately.
“Patience,” I whispered to myself. “Say nothing, wait peacefully, and they will come back.”
And come back they did. They had only taken a short trip to Madame’s private chamber, passed her quiet inspection, and were returned to their proper place untouched the very next day. I found myself wondering what she had made of them: of Dr. John Bretton’s sharp, pithy thoughts, his sound, unpretentious opinions, his warm half-humorous tone that had always made my chest light up, the scattered, sparse kind words that felt rarer than diamonds in a mine. For a few days afterward I caught her gazing at me, steady and contemplative, a little puzzled but never malicious, and she offered only one stray comment before trotting off to attend to the pupils like the neat, determined little pony she resembled: “There is something very remarkable about English character. I cannot quite explain how, but you all have your own ideas about friendship, love, everything. And at the very least, you never need to be monitored.”
I breathed a quiet, private hope she would leave my letters alone after that.

But the thought crashed over me then, so sharp it blurred the sun, the schoolroom, the garden all out of focus: those letters were the last of their kind. I would never receive another one. That broad, bright river of Dr. John’s kind attention that I had lingered beside, sipping its few reviving drops, was bending to a new course, leaving my little lonely patch of ground dry and desolate, pouring all its rich, warm waters somewhere far away. The change was right, just, natural—there was not a single word I could say to argue it. But I had loved that river, my Rhine, my Nile, my almost-worshipped Ganges, and I grieved to see its grand tide roll away estranged, vanishing like a mirage. I was stoical, but not a stone: hot tears dripped fast onto my hands and my desk, a brief, heavy, sultry storm of sorrow, before I steadied myself again.
I told myself the hope I was mourning had brought me, and made me endure, far too much lingering pain. Its death had come at the right time. I closed the eyes of that dead hope gently, covered its face, composed its limbs with perfect calm. But mementos of a grief so private could not be left out where a stray glance could stab me fresh every hour. I had to put the letters somewhere completely, irrevocably out of reach.

When I went to fetch them one empty Thursday holiday afternoon to arrange their final rest, a hot spark of displeasure flared in my chest. The packet was still in my drawer, but the blue ribbon I had tied around it had been untied and retied. Someone had rummaged through my things again.
This was too much. I could almost forgive Madame Beck, sharp and discreet as she was, for reading my private notes—she would see them for what they were, innocent friendly letters, and judge them fairly. But the cold, dreadful thought struck me that she might have shared what she found with someone else, that she had laughed over my most sacred things with a confidant. I knew exactly who that confidant was: her sharp-tongued kinsman M. Paul Emanuel, who had dined with her the night before, and whose angry, lurid blue glance had struck me like a flash of lightning that morning in class, a look I had not understood until that moment. He had never judged me fairly, never treated me with tolerance. The thought of those gentle, harmless letters falling into his suspicious, unkind hands made my very soul jar.

I searched the whole pensionnat for a hiding place, and found none. The attic? No, its drawers were all mouldering, broken, full of rats and mice that would gnaw the paper to pulp, damp that would blur the ink beyond reading. There was no lock here strong enough to guard something that mattered. Then, sitting on the dormitory window seat, watching the pale winter sun set over the forbidden walk’s gnarled old pear tree—the nun’s pear tree, its bare branches standing like a grey, gaunt dryad skeleton—a strange, solitary thought struck me. I pulled on my bonnet, cloak, and furs, and walked out into the city.

I wandered to the oldest, quietest, most shadowed part of town I always sought when my heart was heavy, and found my way to a dusty old broker’s shop crammed full of ancient discarded things. I bought the thickest, sturdiest glass jar I could find, brought my letters home, wrapped them tight in oiled silk, bound them with twine, and paid the old frost-whiskered Jewish broker to stopper, seal, and solder the jar completely airtight. He glanced at me suspiciously the whole time, no doubt thinking I was hiding some dark secret, but for me it was not pleasure, it was a sad, lonely, solemn satisfaction, the same quiet compulsion that had once driven me to the confessional. I slipped back into the pensionnat at full dark just in time for dinner.

At seven the moon rose. By half past seven, all the pupils were at their evening study, Madame Beck was with her mother and children in the dining room, all the half-boarders had gone home, and the maid Rosine had left the vestibule empty. I wrapped a shawl tight around my shoulders, slipped the sealed jar under my arm, and stole out through the first classroom door, through the arbour, and into the forbidden walk.
The old pear tree stood at the far end, dim and grey, its thick ivy hiding a deep hollow near its roots that I had known about for months. I cleared away the ivy, pushed the jar deep inside the hollow, fetched a slate and a trowel of leftover mortar from the masons’ tool shed at the end of the garden, sealed the slate over the hole, covered it all with soft black garden mould, and tucked the ivy back into place to hide every trace. Then I leaned against the trunk, lingering like any mourner beside a fresh new grave.

The night air was perfectly still, veiled in a luminous mist that turned the moonlight to soft glowing haze. There was something electric in that mist, something that hummed in my bones, making me feel as strong as I had the night a year before in England, when I had been lost in lonely fields watching the aurora borealis stream across the sky. I was not happy, far from it, but I felt reinforced, steady, ready to fight my own battles alone, no one leaning on me and no one to lean on. I was already planning how I would leave this pensionnat when my resources ran out, ready to face whatever pitched battle fortune threw my way next.

Then the dim moon suddenly brightened. A sharp, distinct white ray gleamed before me, a dark shadow carved itself out of the haze, and I was standing no more than three yards away from a tall figure in a black sable robe, its face entirely covered by a white veil. Five minutes passed. I did not flee, I did not scream.
“Who are you?” I called out. “Why have you come to me?”
It stood silent. It had no features, no face beneath the veil, but its eyes were fixed straight on mine. I stepped forward, desperate enough to be brave, and stretched out my hand to touch it. It receded, fast and silent, slipping behind a thick mass of evergreen laurel and yew. When I pushed past the shrubs, it was gone. No amount of waiting or calling brought it back. This time, there was no Dr. John Bretton I could run to, no one in the world I dared whisper to that I had seen the nun again.

Paulina Mary de Bassompierre insisted I visit her at their house on Rue Crécy as often as possible. Back in the old Bretton household, she had never openly admitted she liked my company, but it had soon become an unconscious necessity for her—she would trot after me to my room, peep around the door, and order me in her sharp little tone to come back down and sit with her. Now she did everything short of dragging me through her front door.
“Leave Madame Beck’s pensionnat,” she begged. “Come live with us. Papa will pay you three times what she does to be my companion.” Mr. Home himself made the offer, generous and sincere, but I turned it down at once. Even if I had been poorer, with fewer prospects to my name, I would still have said no. I could teach, I could give lessons, but being someone’s paid shadow, a lady’s companion, was something I could never do. I would rather scrub floors for my independence, sew shirts for starvation wages, than be a pretty girl’s silent accessory.
I was no gem’s foil, no great person’s appendage. Madame Beck and I never tried to be friends, but we understood each other perfectly. She gave me total freedom, no strings tied to her or her interests, no petty demands. When she came back from a fortnight away tending her sick relative and found the school running perfectly, no signs of neglect, she handed out presents to all the other teachers, and came to my bedside at midnight to tell me she had no gift for me. “If I pay you more, we will misunderstand each other, and you will leave me. But the one thing I can give you that you want is your full liberty.” She kept that promise, and every small shackle she had ever laid on me vanished after that. I was happy to work twice as hard for her, no pressure needed.

My visits to Paulina soon made it clear my company would not be needed much longer. M. de Bassompierre, for all his sharp scientific mind, was still completely convinced his “little Polly” was still a child, totally blind to the way his daughter was growing up, completely unaware of the soft, tender little romance that was blooming between her and Graham Bretton, right under his nose.
With her father, Paulina was still playful, affectionate, the little girl he remembered. With me, she was serious, thoughtful, fully a woman. With Graham? She was painfully shy. His step would make her start, his entrance hush her, she could barely string a sentence together to answer him, and after he left she would fume at herself for being so awkward. Even her father noticed.
“Polly, you are far too shy,” he told her one day. “You treat Dr. Bretton like a total stranger. Don’t you remember how fond you were of him when you were small?”
“Rather fond, papa,” she echoed, in that soft, gentle little tone of hers.
“Then talk to him, get rid of that silly strangeness. He won’t bite you, I promise.”
“He barely talks to me,” she said. “Do you think he is afraid of me?”
“Any man would be afraid of such a quiet little lady!” he laughed.
The next day, I watched her make a brave, graceful effort to talk to him of ordinary, everyday things. The glow on Graham’s face was bright and soft, he spoke to her in his gentlest tones, like he was breathing around a wisp of gossamer happiness he dared not break with a sharp breath. It was the most exquisite, fairy-like thing I had ever seen. After he left, Paulina ran to her father.
“Did I do well, papa? Did I behave better?”
“My Polly behaved like a queen,” he said, grinning. “Soon we will have to polish up our own manners so we don’t get outshone.”

Since I refused to be her paid companion, Paulina talked me into taking weekly German lessons with her from the same tutor, so we would have a regular, consistent excuse to spend hours together every week. M. de Bassompierre was delighted, perfectly pleased that the quiet, discreet model governess Miss Snowe would spend her free time with his precious daughter.
I smiled to myself, thinking of how very differently everyone saw me. Madame Beck thought I was learned, a bluestocking. Miss Fanshawe thought I was sharp, cynical, ironic. Mr. Home thought I was the very model of sedate, proper governess correctness. And M. Paul Emanuel lost no chance to hint he thought I was fiery, rash, unruly, and audacious. None of them were entirely right. The only person who saw the real me, I thought, was small, quiet Paulina Mary.
