Upon returning to our shelter, I gestured for Infadoos to join us inside. Once the heavy door was secured, I laid out the gravity of our situation. "Infadoos," I began, "we must address a matter of urgent consequence." He nodded solemnly, inviting us to speak.

"It appears to us, Infadoos," I continued, "that King Twala is a ruler devoid of mercy."

"Alas!" the old warrior sighed, his face etched with deep sorrow. "The land itself groans under his tyranny. Tonight, you shall witness the Great Witch-Hunt. Dozens will be unmasked as sorcerers and put to death. Under Twala's rule, no life is secure. If he covets a man's livestock, his wife, or simply fears a potential rebellion, his agents—women trained by the vile witch Gagool—you'll see them sniff out the accused like bloodhounds on a trail. They will condemn the innocent without trial. Many must fall before the moon wanes. Even I may not survive tonight, though I have lingered only because of my martial skill and the soldiers' loyalty. Yet, the land is weary of Twala's crimson ways."

"If the people despise him so," I pressed, "why do they not topple him?"

"Nay, my lords," Infadoos explained, his voice trembling slightly. "He holds the throne, and if he falls, his son Scragga ascends. Scragga's heart is darker than his father's; his yoke would crush us further. Only if Imotu had survived, or if his son Ignosi lived, might hope remain. But both are dead."

A voice cut through the silence from behind us. "How do you know Ignosi is dead?"

We spun around, startled. Standing there was Umbopa, a young man whose presence felt oddly intrusive in our private council.

"What means this interjection, boy?" Infadoos snapped, his hand instinctively moving toward a weapon. "Who gave you leave to speak?"

"Listen, Infadoos," Umbopa insisted, his tone calm despite the accusation. "I shall tell you a tale. Years ago, King Imotu perished in this region, and his wife fled with their son, Ignosi. Is that true?"

"It is true."

"The rumor spread that mother and child perished on the mountains," Umbopa continued. "Is that so?"

"So it was believed."

"But they did not die," Umbopa declared, his eyes burning with intensity. "They crossed the mountains, guided by nomadic desert tribes across the sands, until they found water, grass, and trees once more. They traveled for many moons until they reached the lands of the Amazulu, a fierce branch of the Kukuana stock. There, they stayed for years until the mother passed. Then, the boy Ignosi became a wanderer again, venturing into a realm of wonders where white men dwell. For countless years, he absorbed their wisdom."

"A fanciful story," Infadoos muttered, skepticism clouding his brow.

"For years he served as a laborer and soldier among them," Umbopa pressed on, undeterred, "yet his heart remained anchored to his homeland, driven by a singular purpose: to return home before his dying breath. He waited, enduring long years of solitude. Finally, fate intervened. He encountered white travelers seeking a lost soul, a guide to the unknown lands. Together, they traversed the burning deserts and snow-capped peaks, eventually arriving at the Kukuana territory. And there, they found *you*, Uncle."

Infadoos stared, stunned. "You are mad to speak thus."

"You judge me rashly," Umbopa countered, stepping forward. "Behold!"

With a swift, fluid motion, Umbopa removed his leather girdle, revealing his bare torso. On his midsection, inked in vivid blue, sprawled a massive serpent tattoo. Its coils wound tightly around his waist, its tail vanishing seamlessly into its own gaping maw just above his thighs.

Infadoos gasped, his eyes widening until they seemed to pop from his sockets. He dropped to his knees, bowing his head. "Koom! Koom!" he stammered, overcome. "It is the son of my brother; it is the King!"

"I told you so, Uncle," Umbopa said gently, offering a hand. "Rise. I am not yet crowned, but with your aid—and with the support of these noble white men, my friends—I shall be. Yet, as the old witch Gagool prophesied, the land must first run with blood. Hers, if she possesses any, shall flow alongside theirs, for it was her venomous tongue that slew my father and banished my mother. Now, Infadoos, the choice is yours. Will you place your hand within mine and pledge yourself to me? Will you share the peril ahead and help me dismantle this tyrant's reign? Or will you refuse?"

The old man paused, his hand cupping his chin in contemplation. Rising slowly, he approached Umbopa. He knelt, grasping the young man's hand firmly. "Ignosi, rightful King of the Kukuanas," he proclaimed, his voice thick with emotion. "I bind my hand to yours, serving you unto death. When you were a babe, I rocked you on my knee; now, my aging arm shall strike for your freedom."

"It pleases me, Infadoos," Ignosi replied warmly. "If I triumph, you shall be the paramount lord beneath the crown. If I fail, you will likely die, and death draws near. Rise, my uncle."

Turning to us, Ignosi extended his inquiry. "And you, gentlemen of the East? What recompense do I offer? The White Stones! Were I victorious and able to locate them, you shall carry as many as you desire. Shall that suffice?"

I interpreted his words. "Tell him," Sir Henry suggested, "that he misunderstands an Englishman. Wealth is virtuous, and we shall take it freely if fortune smiles upon us; yet a gentleman does not barter his honor for gold. Nevertheless, regarding myself, I confess a fondness for Umbopa. I am willing to stand by him in this endeavor. It would be a distinct pleasure to settle accounts with that cruel devil, Twala."

"Well," chimed in Good, adopting the local flair for hyperbole, "in the grand dialect of these folks, you may assure him that a 'row' is delightful and warms the very core of one's being. As for me, I am his boy. My sole proviso is that he permits me to wear trousers."

I conveyed these sentiments. "It is settled," Ignosi affirmed. "And what sayest thou, Macumazahn? Art thou with me, old hunter, wiser than a wounded buffalo?"

I pondered the question, scratching my head. "Umbopa, or Ignosi," I finally said, "I harbor no love for revolutions. I am a man of peace, perhaps a trifle timid—" Ignosi offered a knowing smile—"but I stand firm by my friends. You have adhered to us and acted with courage, so I will stand by you. However, remember, I am a trader bound to earn a living. I accept your offer regarding the diamonds should circumstances permit. Furthermore, we ventured forth, as you know, to seek Sir Henry's missing brother. You must aid us in finding him."

"That I promise," Ignosi vowed. "But stay, Infadoos. By the mark of the serpent upon my skin, speak the truth. Has any white man, to your knowledge, set foot within this land?"

"No, O Ignosi."

"If any white traveler had been sighted or heard of, would you have known?"

"I would certainly have discerned them."

"Then thou hearest, Incubu," Ignosi addressed Sir Henry, "he hath not been here."

Sir Henry let out a long, melancholic sigh. "Well, well," he murmured. "So it stands. I suppose he never progressed so far. Poor fellow, poor fellow! All our efforts for naught. God's will be done."

"Now, to business," I interjected, eager to steer clear of the somber topic. "It is admirable to be a king by divine right, Ignosi, but how do you intend to become a king in fact?"

"Nay, I know not," Ignosi admitted. "Infadoos, hast thou a strategy?"

"Ignosi, Son of the Lightning," Infadoos responded gravely, "tonight is the Great Dance and Witch-Hunt. Many shall be exposed and perish, and within the hearts of many others, a storm of grief, rage, and fury against King Twala will brew. Once the dance concludes, I shall approach the great chiefs. If I can sway them, they will rally their regiments. I intend to speak softly at first, convincing them of your legitimacy. I believe that by tomorrow's dawn, you will command twenty thousand spears. I must now depart to think, listen, and prepare. After the dance, if I survive—and may Heaven grant us all survival—I shall return here to discuss further. War, alas, is inevitable."

Just as Infadoos finished, a shrill cry pierced the air. Messengers had arrived from the King. We hurried to the doorway and ushered them in. Three men entered, each carrying a resplendent shirt of chainmail armor and a magnificent battle-axe.
