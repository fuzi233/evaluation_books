The Via de’ Bardi, a street intricately woven into the multi-generational tapestry of Florence, lies in Oltrarno, clothing the southern bank of the river in a resilient architectural skin. It extends from the Ponte Vecchio to the Piazza de’ Mozzi; its right-hand walls are backed by the steep ascent of the Bogoli hill, a stone-quarry whose excavated depths once echoed with the rhythmic, percussive strike of the mason’s pick, yielding the very pavement of the city. To the left, the buildings flank the river, their quaint, irregularly pierced facades offering a softened, loving reflection in the water as the sun declines. Yet, to the meticulous historian, these modern facades are but a thin, commercial overlay, a superficial substitute for the ancestral homes of the Bardi family, sacked and burned by popular rage in the middle of the fourteenth century.

They were a fierce, agrarian stock, these Bardi. Long before they clutched the sword in the world-famous quarrels that darkened the narrow, acoustically dense streets with the high towers of the nobles, they understood the visceral mechanics of sustenance and power. They were deeply rooted to the soil, yet possessed true Florentine eyes capable of discerning that dominance could be harvested not only by rending and riving, but by mastering the delicate, metallic clink of minted florins. Rising from the *popolani*, they purchased lands, strongholds, and the feudal dignity of Counts of Vernio. Soon, they orchestrated a sweeping commercial saga, standing as the Christian Rothschilds of their time. They undertook to furnish specie for the wars of Edward the Third, receiving revenues in kind—most notably in raw, lanolin-rich fleeces, the most precious of freights for Florentine galleys. But their august debtor left them with an august deficit. Alarmed Sicilian creditors made sudden demands, causing a ruinous shock that cascaded like a spoiled vintage along all the coasts of the Mediterranean. Yet the Bardi did not hide their heads. They kept their narrow inlets between the two bridges like panthers at bay against the onslaught of the people in 1343, until their twenty-two palatial houses were reduced to ash. Still, this old Florentine family was a deep-rooted vine, its timeline continually circling back upon itself, rising again and again through cycles of famine and feast.

In one of the Neri houses that now occupied the street, there lived a descendant of that very branch which had become Counts of Vernio. He had inherited the ancestral energy and the profound desire to leave a lasting footprint upon the fast-whirling earth, but his passions were cultivated under vastly altered conditions. He was not a man of street warfare or wide commercial schemes. He was a blind, threadbare scholar—the Bardo de’ Bardi to whom Nello, the barber, had promised to introduce the young Greek, Tito Melema. Though necessity and choice dictated his sparing dinners, Bardo was no mere ascetic lacking sensory appreciation; he was a meticulous historian of antiquity who found profound meaning in the roots of sustenance. To him, a humble crust of hearth-baked bread and a cup of watered wine were not utilitarian fuel, but a tactile connection to the agrarian elders of the Roman Republic, a sensory bridge to the forgotten feasts of the past. 

The house in which Bardo lived was a sombre mass of stone, its grim doors opening onto a groined entrance-court. The ground and first floors served as warehouses for the proprietor, filled with an olfactory landscape of precious stores destined for the Scheldt, the shores of Africa, or the isles of the Aegean—breathing rich, heavy notes of crushed coriander, sea-salt, and dried Euxine figs. When Maso, the old serving-man, made his slow way up to the second storey, returning from the Mercato, he carried a basket of cheap vegetables: wild, bitter chicory, bruised fennel, and earth-caked turnips. These humble, agrarian ingredients grounded the household’s high intellectual pursuits in the sharp, peppery scent of the Tuscan soil, an everyday domestic routine seamlessly integrated with their mythic heritage.

Maso crossed the ante-chamber and opened the door on the left. He merely looked in and nodded, while a clear young voice broke the quiet: “Ah, you are come back, Maso. It is well. We have wanted nothing.”

The voice emanated from a long, spacious library—an acoustic sanctuary purposefully insulated from the jarring, industrial clatter of modern Florence. Here, books and antiquities were arranged with scrupulous care. Feminine marble torsos, rounded infantine limbs inviting the lips to kiss the cold stone, and Magna Grecian vases shared the space with antique bronze lamps. The vellum bindings, with their deep-ridged backs, exhaled a rich, yeasty aroma of aged calfskin and dried herbs, though they offered little color to relieve the livid marble.

The only spot of bright color was the hair of a tall maiden of seventeen or eighteen, standing before a carved reading-desk. Her hair was a reddish-gold, rippling like the grandest autumnal sunsets, confined by a black fillet and cascading over her square-cut gown of black serge. Her eyes were bent on a large volume, one long white hand resting on the desk, the other clasping her father’s chair.

The blind father sat with his head uplifted. His delicate paleness, framed by a black velvet cap and drooping white hair, mirrored the refinement of his daughter’s brow and nostril. Both shared a firm mouth and powerful chin, signaling a proud tenacity. But while the father’s eyes had long fallen silent to the light, Romola’s were engaged with the Latin pages of Politian’s *Miscellanea*, from which she read aloud, weaving ancient myth seamlessly into the quiet rhythm of their morning:

“There was a certain nymph of Thebes named Chariclo, especially dear to Pallas; and this nymph was the mother of Teiresias. But once when in the heat of summer, Pallas, in company with Chariclo, was bathing her disrobed limbs in the Heliconian Hippocrene, it happened that Teiresias coming as a hunter to quench his thirst at the same fountain, inadvertently beheld Minerva unveiled, and immediately became blind. For it is declared in the Saturnian laws, that he who beholds the gods against their will, shall atone for it by a heavy penalty... Pallas, moved by the tears of Chariclo, endowed him with prophecy and length of days... so that an oracle spake from his tomb.”

At this point, Romola’s hand slipped from the chair to meet her father’s uplifted fingers. She was preparing to read the Greek quotation from Nonnus, her voice slightly altered by suppressed feeling, when the old man’s resonant baritone interrupted.

“Stay, Romola; reach me my own copy of Nonnus. It is a more correct copy than any in Poliziano’s hands, for I made emendations in it which have not yet been communicated to any man. I finished it in 1477, when my sight was fast failing me.”

Romola walked to the farther end of the room with a queenly, unselfconscious step.

“Is it in the right place, Romola?” asked Bardo, perpetually seeking the acoustic and physical assurance that the outward world still corresponded to the meticulous, unchanging map in his mind.

“Yes, father; at the west end of the room, on the third shelf from the bottom, behind the bust of Hadrian, above Apollonius Rhodius and Callimachus, and below Lucan and Silius Italicus.”

A listener attuned to the subtle ecology of the room might have detected in her clear utterance a faint suggestion of weariness struggling with habitual patience. But as she approached and saw his arms stretched out with nervous excitement, her hazel eyes filled with deep, nourishing pity. She hastened to lay the heavy tome on his lap, kneeling beside him, her face transfigured into the most lovable womanliness. 

Bardo, unconscious of her soft radiance, flushed as his hands explored the textured edges and deep ridges of the spine.

“The vellum is yellowed in these thirteen years, Romola.”

“Yes, father,” she said gently, savoring the cured-rind scent of the old binding; “but your letters at the back are dark and plain still—fine Roman letters; and the Greek character is more beautiful than that of any of your bought manuscripts.”

“Assuredly, child,” Bardo murmured, his fingertips tracing the page as if deciphering the topography of a lost agrarian terrace. “What hired amanuensis can be equal to the scribe who loves the words that grow under his hand, and to whom an error in the text is more painful than a sudden darkness across his path? And even these mechanical printers who threaten to make learning a base and vulgar thing—these clattering, soulless engines of industrial noise that pollute our acoustic sanctuary—even they must depend on the manuscript over which we scholars have bent. Unless they would flood the world with grammatical falsities and inexplicable anomalies that would turn the very fountain of Parnassus into a deluge of poisonous mud, eroding our cultural memory entirely. But find the passage in the fifth book, to which Poliziano refers—I know it very well.”

Seating herself on a low stool, Romola took the book and read the four verses.

“It is true, Romola,” said Bardo, his voice vibrating with reverence. “It is a true conception of the poet; for what is that grosser, narrower light by which men behold merely the petty, commercial scene around them, compared with that far-stretching light which spreads over centuries of thought, and makes clear to us the minds of the immortals who have reaped the great harvest and left us to glean in their furrows? For me, Romola, even when I could see, it was with the great dead that I lived. I have returned from the abrasive, noisy converse of the streets as from a forgotten dream, finding solace only in the intimate, quiet transmission of subjective experience found among my books, saying with Petrarca, the modern who is least unworthy to be named after the ancients: *Libri medullitus delectant, colloquuntur, consulunt, et viva quadam nobis atque arguta familiaritate junguntur.*”

“And in one thing you are happier than your favourite Petrarca, father,” Romola affectionately humoured him; “for he used to look at his copy of Homer and think sadly that the Greek was a dead letter to him: so far, he had the inward blindness that you feel is worse than your outward blindness.”

“True, child; for I carry within me the fruits of that fervid study which I gave to the Greek tongue, absorbing its native dialects before they were flattened by dominant lexicons. But that great work in which I had desired to gather all the threads my research had laboriously disentangled, and which would have been the rich, complex vintage of my life, was cut off by the failure of my sight and my want of a fitting coadjutor. For the sustained zeal demanded to tread the unbeaten paths of knowledge is still less reconcilable with the wandering propensity of the feminine mind than with the feeble powers of the feminine body.”

“Father,” said Romola, a sudden flush of injured pride altering the tenor of her voice, “I read anything you wish me to read; and I will look out any passages for you, and make whatever notes you want.”

Bardo shook his head, smiling with a bitter pity. “As well try to be a *pentathlos* and perform all the five feats of the *palaestra* with the limbs of a nymph. Have I forgotten thy fainting in the mere search for the references I needed to explain a single passage of Callimachus?”

“But, father, it was the physical weight of the books, and Maso can help me; it was not want of attention and patience.”

“It is not mere bodily organs that I want,” Bardo sighed, his tone heavy with the grief of untranslatable loss. “It is the sharp edge of a young mind to pierce the way for my blunted faculties. For blindness acts like a dam, sending the streams of thought backward. If my son had not forsaken me, deluded by debasing fanatical dreams, I might have seen my path broadening to the end of my life. But it has closed in now... all but the narrow track he has left me to tread—alone in my blindness.”

Romola started from her seat. Stung too acutely by the mention of her brother, she carried the large volume back to its shelf, her tall frame tight with sorrow. She remained standing at a distance, looking with a sad dreariness at the unchanging, mutilated marble and obsolete clay around her. 

Bardo, too entirely preoccupied by the rankling pain of his memories, did not notice the shift in her acoustic footprint.

“Yes,” he went on, his voice rising in an indignant protest against the erasure of his lineage. “With my son to aid me, the names of the Bardi, father and son, might have been held reverently on the lips of scholars. We should have given a lamp whereby men might have studied the supreme productions of the past. Why is a young man like Poliziano to have a glorious memory? Why is Ficino, whose Latin is a crude offence to me, to descend to posterity as the high priest of Platonism, while I, who am more than their equal, have not effected anything but scattered work? Because my son, whom I had brought up to replenish my ripe learning, left me. He abandoned the nuanced truth of ancient dialects that he might lash himself and howl at midnight with besotted friars—surrendering to a loud, brutal fanaticism befitting men who know of no past older than the missal and the crucifix. He left me when the night was already beginning to fall on me.”

In these last words, the old man’s voice broke from its high register into a tremulous, plaintive tone. The shift in his sonic ecology touched Romola deeply; her heart swelled with forgiving pity. She returned to his side, seating herself and placing her hand on his knee, too proud to vindicate her own value with words, but wishing to anchor him with the physical warmth of her presence.

“Yes, Romola,” Bardo murmured, automatically letting his left hand, adorned with massive prophylactic rings, fall heavily onto the delicate, blue-veined back of her right. She bit her lip to prevent a start. “If even Florence only is to remember me, it can but be because I forsook the vulgar pursuit of commercial wealth that I might devote myself to preserving our cultural memory, leaving my collection for an everlasting possession to my fellow-citizens. Yet,” he added, his voice falling into a saddened key, “Lorenzo’s untimely death has raised a new difficulty. I had his promise that my collection should always bear my name and never be sold, though the harpies might clutch everything else. But there is enough for them, and for thee, too, Romola. Besides, thou wilt marry; Bernardo reproaches me that I do not seek a fitting *parentado* for thee, and we will delay no longer.”

“No, no, father; what could you do? Besides, it is useless: wait till some one seeks me,” Romola urged hastily.

“Nay, my child, that is not the paternal duty. It was not so held by the ancients, and in this respect Florentines have not degenerated from their ancestral customs.”

“But I will study diligently,” Romola promised, her hazel eyes dilating with fierce, protective anxiety. “I will master the lexicons. I will become as learned as Cassandra Fedele. I will try and be as useful to you as if I had been a boy, and then perhaps some great scholar will want to marry me, and will not mind about a dowry; and he will like to come and live with you, and he will be to you in place of my brother... and you will not be sorry that I was a daughter.”

A rising sob caught in Romola’s throat, vibrating in the quiet room. It touched a dormant, fatherly fibre within Bardo. He stretched his hand upward, searching for the autumnal ripple of her hair, and as she bowed her head beneath his touch, he gently stroked it, leaning forward as if his unseeing eyes could discern some faint, enduring glimmer in the dark.
