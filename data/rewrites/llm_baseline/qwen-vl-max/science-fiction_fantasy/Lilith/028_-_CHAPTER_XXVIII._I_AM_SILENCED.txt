I wiped the water from my eyes and saw the raven perched on the rim of a vast stone basin. Dawn’s pale light glinted off his sleek feathers as he gazed down at me with quiet composure. I lay half-submerged in the fountain’s cold embrace, propped up on my elbows just enough to keep my face above the surface. The fountain—my father’s grand creation—rose like a steel spine from the center of the lawn, hurling a torrent of water a hundred feet into the air before it burst into a crown of mist and foam.

“YOU TOLD ME NOTHING!” I snapped, bristling at the chill of his words.

“I told you to do nothing that anyone you distrusted asked of you,” he replied calmly.

“Tut! How was a mortal supposed to remember such a thing?”

“You’ll not forget the consequences of forgetting it,” said Mr. Raven, leaning forward over the edge. He extended a hand. I grasped it—and instantly found myself standing beside him on the dew-drenched grass, soaked through and shivering.

“You must change your clothes at once,” he said.

“A wetting doesn’t matter where you come from—though here, it brings its own troubles.” He turned and strode toward the house, now a raven again, moving with regal poise. The front door stood open, inviting.

“I don’t have much to change into,” I laughed, remembering I’d left my robe behind when climbing the tree.

“It’s been a long time since I shed a feather,” he remarked, without looking back.

Inside, silence reigned. No one stirred. I went to my room, pulled on a dressing gown, and made my way to the library. As I stepped in, the librarian emerged from the inner closet. I collapsed onto a couch. Mr. Raven drew a chair close and sat beside me. For a moment, neither of us spoke.

Then I broke the stillness. “What does it all mean?”

“A good question,” he said. “No one knows what anything *is*. A man can only learn what it *means*—and whether he does depends on how he uses it.”

“I’ve made no use of anything yet.”

“Not much. But you know that fact—and that’s something. Most people spend lifetimes learning they’ve learned nothing, and done less. At least you’ve never lacked the desire to be useful.”

“I wanted to help the children—the Little Ones,” I said.

“I know you did—and you began in the wrong direction.”

“I didn’t know the right way.”

“That’s true—but you’re to blame for not finding it.”

“I’m ready to believe whatever you say… once I understand it.”

“If you had accepted our invitation, you would have known the right path. When a man refuses to act where he stands, he must travel far to find his purpose.”

“I’ve traveled far—and found nothing. I left the children to learn how to serve them, and all I’ve learned is the danger they’re in.”

“When you were with them, you were where you could have helped. You abandoned your duty to search for it. It takes wisdom to know when to leave—fools learn to return at once.”

“Do you mean… I could have helped the Little Ones by staying?”

“Could you teach them anything by leaving them?”

“No. But how could I teach them? I didn’t even know where to start. Besides—they were far ahead of me.”

“True. But you weren’t a measuring stick for them. If they knew what you know—let alone what you might have known—they’d be beyond you, utterly out of sight. Yet you saw they weren’t growing—or growing so slowly they hadn’t even grasped the idea of growth. They feared it! And you had never seen children stay children.”

“But I had no power to make them grow!”

“You could have removed the obstacles to their growth.”

“What are they? I don’t know. I thought perhaps it was lack of water.”

“Of course it is. They have none to cry with.”

“I would have spared them that need.”

“Ah—just like every foolish philanthropist! But Mr. Vane, without tears, your world would never have become worth saving. You admit you suspected they needed water—why didn’t you dig them a well or two?”

“It never occurred to me.”

“Even when the sound of underground waters reached your ears?”

“I think it did once. But I feared the giants might harm them. That’s why I endured so much from the beasts myself.”

“And in doing so, you nearly taught those noble creatures to fear the Bags! While they fed you, comforted you, worshipped you—you submitted to brutish men! You gave them a coward as their hero! A greater injustice you could hardly commit. They gave you their hearts—you owed them your soul! By now, you might have made the Bags mere laborers for the Little Ones!”

“I fear you may be right, Mr. Raven. But I truly believed more knowledge might harm them—make them less innocent, less pure.”

“They gave you no reason to fear such a thing.”

“Isn’t a little knowledge dangerous?”

“One of your world’s favorite lies. Is man’s greatest knowledge more than a little? And if so, is it therefore dangerous? The belief that knowledge is inherently great makes any amount of it seem more perilous than ignorance. To know all things wouldn’t be greatness.”

“At least I served them out of love—not fear.”

“Granted. But you should have served the Little Ones, not the giants. You should have given them water—then they would soon have shown the giants their proper place. In the meantime, you could have forced the giants to cut down two-thirds of their coarse fruit trees to make space for the delicate ones. You lost your chance with the Lovers, Mr. Vane. You speculated about them instead of helping them.”
