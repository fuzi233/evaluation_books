“It is virtue, yea virtue, gentlemen, that maketh gentlemen…”

So ran the old sentence, oft mouthed in hall and chamber, and not the worse for being mouthed; for the age that delighted in such brave sounding words could, in the next breath, shoulder a pike, steer by dead-reckoning through a dirty night, or keep counsel as close as a cellar-door. If any man, reading what follows, should call it fantastical, let him remember that those who spoke thus of love and honor were the same who could count a tide, bargain for powder, and take a town. They could season their talk with roses and arrows, and yet smell treason on the wind as keenly as they smelt a fresh roast from the kitchen.

Amyas Leigh could not sail when he would. The south-west freshened and held, not as a kindly breath behind canvas, but as a hard palm set full against the bay; three parts of a gale, as the seamen said, dead into the mouth of it, with the bar moaning and the shingle hissing like frying fat. So the Mary Grenville was got down the river into Appledore pool, lying there ready as a dish laid for serving the instant the household hears the step at the door—only the step was the change of wind, and it tarried.

Amyas went quietly home; and when his mother, stern in sorrow and duty, started on pillion behind the old serving-man to ride to Clovelly—where Frank lay wounded—Amyas rode in with her as far as Bideford. The town was in its market stir: the lowing of cattle at one end, the clack of women’s tongues at the other, and between them the constant thud of hoof and wheel. Over it all rose the gulls’ sharp crying and, from a smithy, the ring of iron upon iron—good, honest sound that tells a man where he is, and steadies the mind better than any fine music played for money.

They had scarce entered the High Street when a little procession came down it, drawing eyes from doors and windows as a scent draws noses. Will Cary led—clad cap-a-pie in shining armor, sword on thigh, helmet at saddle-bow—so gallant that even the fishwives, with hands in brine and herring-scale, paused to grin and nudge. Behind him, on country ponies, rode four or five stout serving-men with lances and baggage and their own long-bows, swords, and bucklers; and behind all, in a horse-litter, was Frank Leigh himself, to Mrs. Leigh’s joy, looking pale and fine and very much alive.

He protested, with the airy obstinacy that made his mother angry and proud in one breath, that his wounds were but flesh-wounds, the dagger having turned against his ribs; that he must see the last of his brother; and that, with her leave, he would not go home to Burrough, but take up his lodging with Cary in the Ship Tavern by the Bridge-foot. And so he did, setting himself upon a couch like a young prince in exile, and holding his levee in state, mobbed by the gossips of the town and fed with their questions; to which he returned answers seasoned with white fibs—more pepper than salt—as to who had brought him into that sorry plight.

But in the meanwhile, he and Amyas, with that quick brotherly understanding which needs few words, laid a plot of their own, and set it afoot the next day—market-day—when the whole country-side came in as bees to a hive.

First, the innkeeper, under Amyas’s orders, began such a bustle of roasting, boiling, and frying as had not been heard in the annals of the Ship. The kitchen became a little world of heat and savor: spits creaked under their loads; fat fell hissing upon coals and sent up a sweet smoke that clung to rafters and hair and cloak; lids clattered; knives thudded on blocks; and the cook’s voice, sharp as vinegar, kept time with the scullions’ bare feet slapping the flags. Great joints were basted till they shone like varnished oak; capons browned and crackled; onions went down into pots with herbs from some hedgerow garden, and a handful of spice—dear, foreign, and used sparingly, as if each grain had a story. All through the house, the smell travelled, creeping under doors and along passages, making even sober men’s mouths water and their tempers soften, for hunger and ill-humor are near kin.

Next, Amyas went out into the market and invited, one by one and apart, as many of his old schoolfellows as Frank pointed out to him, to a merry supper and a “rowse” upon it. Each young gentleman came with his own hope tucked close, as a man carries a secret letter in his doublet; and each, entering, found himself—much to his disgust—set at table with six rivals, to none of whom he had spoken for half a year. Yet all were too well bred to let the Leighs see their teeth. Frank lay at the head on his couch, pale still, but bright-eyed; Amyas took the bottom; and, by filling every mouth with good things—meat that needed chewing, bread that had a crust worth cracking, and ale that made the throat grateful—they spared their guests the pain of speaking till the wine should loosen their tongues and warm their hearts.

Amyas and Frank, with provoking good-humor, talked and joked and told stories, as if silence were the merriest music in the world. Will Cary, who caught mirth as tinder catches flame, melted into one jest and then another; tried to make Mr. Coffin laugh and only got a bow; tried to make Mr. Fortescue laugh and only got a frown; and, unabashed, turned his light artillery upon the waiters till he drove them out, bursting with laughter, into the noisy haven of the kitchen, where the spit’s squeal and the pot’s bubble would cover their mirth better than any curtain.

So far so good. The cloth was drawn; sack and sugar came in—warm, sweet, and fragrant, like a cordial for chilled courage; and “Queen and Bible” was duly drunk with all honors, the cups clinking like small bells in a chapel. With the drink came the room’s new sound: chairs scraped, voices rose, the first hard edges of silence rounded off, as stones in a stream are rounded by continual water.

Frank watched his moment as a cook watches the instant when a sauce turns and must be taken from the fire.

“I have a toast, gentlemen—here it is. ‘The gentlemen of the Irish wars; and may Ireland never be without a St. Leger to stand by a Fortescue, a Fortescue to stand by a St. Leger, and a Chichester to stand by both.’”

That toast forced each of the three to drink and return thanks and pay compliments to the other houses, and in so doing to speak aloud to those whom they had lately scowled at in silence. The ice cracked. Young Fortescue, warmed in spite of himself, proposed “Amyas Leigh and all bold mariners;” and Amyas answered with blunt kindness that he wished to know no better fortune than to sail round the world again with the present company as fellow-adventurers, and so give the Spaniards another taste of the men of Devon.

Wine slid down sweetly; and the old saying proved itself true again, that it makes the lips of them that were asleep to speak. Now each man began talking like a rational Englishman to him at his elbow. The room filled with a better music than any hired fiddler—an honest weaving of voices, with the occasional burst of laughter that had in it the clean crack of breaking wood rather than the thin tinkle of courtly affectation.

“And now, gentlemen,” said Frank, seeing the moment ripe, “let me give you a health which none of you, I dare say, will refuse to drink with heart and soul as well as with lips;—the health of one whom beauty and virtue have so ennobled, that in their light the shadow of lowly birth is unseen…”

He went on, piling praise upon praise with that airy, dangerous grace of his, like a man scattering rose-leaves over hot embers. Each guest turned red, and pale, and red again, and looked at the others as if to say, “Lift your glass, and I will dash it out of your hand.” Then Frank, with sweet effrontery, drank:

“The health of the Rose of Torridge, and a double health to that worthy gentleman, whosoever he may be, whom she is fated to honor with her love!”

“Well done, cunning Frank Leigh!” cried Will Cary, clapping his hand upon the table till the cups jumped. “None of us dare quarrel with you now, however much we may sulk at each other. For there’s none of us, I’ll warrant, but thinks she likes him best; and so we are bound to believe you’ve drunk our healths all round.”

“And so I have,” said Frank; “and what better thing can you do, gentlemen, than drink each other’s healths all round likewise; and so show yourselves true gentlemen, true Christians, ay, and true lovers?”

Then he launched into one of those swift, fantastical harangues of his—Cupid, Psyche, Graces and Furies—turning jealousy into nettle and love into rose, and arguing, with a courtier’s tongue and a soldier’s nerve, that their common worship should bind them into friendship. The words ran on like a clever cook’s hand through a feast—now sharp, now sweet, now rich—till the listeners, taken unawares, had no leisure to kindle their tempers.

But when he paused, weak from his wounds, to breathe, a haughty murmur rose. More than one young gentleman took it for impertinent interference in his right to make a fool of himself. Mr. Coffin, who had sat bolt upright and stared at the opposite wall as if it were safer than any face, now rose, and with a look meant to be unconcerned walked toward the door. Another minute and the feast might have turned Lapithae indeed.

Frank’s heart and head never failed him.

“Mr. Coffin!” he cried, in a tone that compelled the other to turn; and so brought him under the power of a face too imploring, tender, and earnest for malice to dwell long upon. “My dear Mr. Coffin! If my earnestness has made me forget, even for a moment, the bounds of courtesy, I entreat you to forgive me. Do not add to my heavy griefs, heavy enough already, the grief of losing a friend. Only hear me patiently to the end; and then, if you are still incensed, I can but entreat your forgiveness again.”

Now Coffin, in truth, had never been to Court; and he was jealous of Frank’s Court talk and Court clothes and Court company. Being the eldest there, and scarce two years younger than Frank, he was nettled at being classed with lads of eighteen. Had Frank but hinted at superiority, all had been lost. Instead, he sued like a beggar for mercy; and Coffin, true-hearted enough, and having known him since either could walk, could do nothing but sit again, stiffly at first, and then with his anger melting as butter melts when brought too near the fire.

“Believe me,” Frank went on, more earnestly than ever, “I no more arrogate to myself a superiority over you than does the sailor hurled on shore by the surge fancy himself better than his comrade still battling with the foam. For I too, gentlemen,—let me confess it, that by confiding in you I may win you to confide in me,—have loved, ay and do love, where you love also.”

There was a stir; brows drew down; but he hurried on, turning their very frowns into fuel.

“Is it wonder that the sun which has dazzled you has dazzled me? I have learnt to love you for loving what I love, and to admire you for admiring that which I admire. Will you not try the same lesson? What breeds closer communion than allegiance to the same queen, duty to the same father, adoration of the same God? And shall not worship of the same beauty be likewise a bond? Let each see in his rival not an enemy but a fellow-sufferer…”

So he pleaded that they should make their rivalry an emulation in virtue: loyalty to queen, valor against foes, courtesy and mercy to the oppressed; and then, higher yet, that they should bind themselves in charity, vowing to abide by the lady’s choice and to serve him whom she should honor, as faithful worshippers of one divinity. And with that he ended:

“…as I do here promise to be the faithful friend, and, to my ability, the hearty servant, of him who shall be honored with the love of the Rose of Torridge.”

He ceased, and for a space there was no sound but the faint crackle of the hearth and, from without, the far-off cry of a hawker in the street—common noises, and yet in that hush each one seemed to hear his own blood.

At last young Fortescue spoke, half smiling.

“I may pay you a left-handed compliment, sir; but it seems to me that you are so likely, in that case, to become your own faithful friend and hearty servant—if you have not borne off the bell already while we have been asleep—that the bargain is hardly fair between such a gay Italianist and us country swains.”

“You undervalue yourself and your country,” said Frank quickly. “But set your mind at rest. I know no more of that lady’s mind than you do, nor shall I know. For the sake of my own peace, I have made a vow neither to see her, nor to hear—if possible—tidings of her, till three full years are past. Dixi?”

Mr. Coffin rose at that, as if a cord had been cut.

“Gentlemen, I may submit to be outdone by Mr. Leigh in eloquence, but not in generosity. If he leaves these parts for three years, I do so also.”

“And go in charity with all mankind,” cried Cary, leaping up. “Give us your hand, old fellow. If you are a Coffin, you were sawn out of no wishy-washy elm-board, but right heart-of-oak. I am going too—Amyas here can tell—to Ireland away, to cool my hot liver in a bog, like a jack-hare in March. Come, give us thy neif, and let us part in peace. I was minded to have fought thee this day—”

“I should have been most happy, sir,” said Coffin stiffly.

“—But now I am all love and charity,” Cary rattled on. “Can I have the pleasure of begging pardon of the world in general, and thee in particular? Does any man wish to pull my nose; send me an errand; make me lend him five pounds; ay, make me buy a horse of him, which will be as good as giving him ten? Come along! Join hands all round, and swear eternal friendship, as brothers of the sacred order of the—of what, Frank Leigh? Open thy mouth, Daniel, and christen us!”

“The Rose,” said Frank quietly, seeing his love-philtre work, and minded to strike while the iron was hot.

“The Rose!” cried Cary, catching Coffin’s hand with his right and Fortescue’s with his left. “Come, Mr. Coffin! Bend, sturdy oak! ‘Woe to the stiff-necked and stout-hearted!’ says Scripture.”

And somehow—whether it was Frank’s chivalrous pleading, or Cary’s broad fun, or Amyas’s good wine and the strong meat that had lined their stomachs and steadied their brains, or that native nobleness which lies in young men’s hearts if their elders will but trouble to call it out—the whole party came in to terms. They shook hands round the table and vowed, on the hilt of Amyas’s sword, to make fools of themselves no more—at least by jealousy—but to stand by each other and by their lady-love, and neither grudge nor grumble though she danced with, flirted with, or married whom she would. And because the honor of their peerless dame and of the brotherhood named after her must be spread through all lands, they would each go home and ask their fathers’ leave—easy enough in those brave times—to go abroad wheresoever there were “good wars,” and there emulate the courage and courtesy of Walter Manny and Gonzalo Fernandes, Bayard and Gaston de Foix. Sidney was a hero at five-and-twenty; why not they?

Frank watched and listened with one of those quiet smiles of his—his eyes smiling even when his lips were still—and only said:

“Gentlemen, be sure that you will never repent this day.”

“Repent?” cried Cary. “I feel already as angelical as thou lookest, Saint Silvertongue. What was it that sneezed?—the cat?”

“The lion, rather, by the roar of it,” said Amyas; and in a moment, as if the sound had pricked his seaman’s instinct for hidden things, he made a dash at the arras behind him. “Why, here is a doorway here! and—”

He rushed under the hanging, through an open door behind, and returned dragging out, by the head, Mr. John Brimblecombe.

And who was Mr. John Brimblecombe? If you have forgotten him, you have done almost what every one in that room had done. Yet you may recall a certain fat lad, son of the schoolmaster, whom Sir Richard, three years before, punished for tale-bearing by sending him—not to Coventry, but to Oxford. That was he. He was now one-and-twenty and a Bachelor, having learned such things as were then taught, with more or less success; and he was hanging about Bideford again, meaning after Christmas to return and read divinity, that he might become a parson and shepherd souls in his native land.

In person he was exceedingly like a pig—yet not like every pig; and here, if the savor of a feast has already made you attentive to flesh, fat, and the honest appetites of the body, you will understand the comparison the better. He was not like the lank Devon swine of that day, ill-shaped as hedgerow monsters; nor like the furry, grizzled little wild rooklers, game-flavored and quick-eyed, that still grunted in sounders about Swinley Down and Braunton woods, Clovelly glens and Bursdon moor. No: he was prophetic—face, figure, and complexion—of those great pigs that a later time would boast of as science’s triumph.

A Fisher Hobbs pig of twelve stone, set upon its hind-legs—that was Jack Brimblecombe, and nothing else: the plump mulberry skin with a few scattered black bristles; the sleek tightness as if he might burst with his own good keeping; the little toddling legs; the dapper bend in the small of the back; the cracked squeak of a voice; the low upright forehead and tiny eyes; the round, self-satisfied jowl; and, best of all, the sensitive cocked nose that was ever on the watch for a savory smell—yet, while sniffing for the best, contented with the worst. A pig of self-helpful and serene spirit; fatting fast while other pigs’ ribs stared through their skins.
