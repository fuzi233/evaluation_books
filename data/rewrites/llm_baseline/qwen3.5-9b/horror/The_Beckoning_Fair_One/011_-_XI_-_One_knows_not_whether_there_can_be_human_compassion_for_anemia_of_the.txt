One does not know if there remains any capacity for human compassion toward the anemia of the soul. When the pitch of life drops, and the spirit is so thoroughly overturned that only what was once sweet and worldly becomes truly horrible, the bond between humans dissolves. The sane mind recoils in terror, fearing not only for itself but for the very integrity of its own sanity. We are not gods; we cannot banish demons. Our only duty is to ensure they do not enter us, even if love floods our veins until we feel ourselves half-divine. In such states, words of honor and duty become hollow echoes. A letter slipped beneath a dark door lies ignored, or is glimpsed for a fleeting second between two abyssal gaps and then forgotten. Telegrams go undelivered; the whistling messenger, wiser than he realizes, walks away unaware that a blind has been nudged aside by a trembling finger and hastily restored. No—let the unfortunate one wrestle with his own shadows; let him, if he is truly mad, embrace and entangle the succubus, but let him do so in a house where no breath of heaven enters and where no ray of sunlight pierces the filthy twilight. The lost must remain lost. Humanity has other matters to attend to.

For Oleron, the handwriting of two letters he had spotted on the floor of his kitchen one June afternoon held no more significance than a distant, fading dream. Those letters, meant to clear his sitting-room of an armful of fetid, decaying flowers, seemed meaningless to him. When the telegraph boy knocked near his bed, Oleron gnashed his teeth and stuffed his ears. He imagined the lad standing just beyond his partition, surrounded by bundles of provisions and dead blooms. His landing outside was littered with such refuse. Terrified to open his door and admit them, he waited. A week later, the delivery boys reported a mistake and left nothing behind. Inside, in the red gloom, the old flowers turned brown, collapsed, and rotted where they lay.

Gradually, Oleron's power drained away. The Abomination fastened onto his vitality. The steady erosion often left him prostrate for hours, staring vacantly at his ceiling tinged with blood, passively allowing strange fancies to take root. Even his strongest memories held only a precarious grip. Sometimes a fleeting fragment—a novel he must write, a story vital to his existence—tantalized him before vanishing. Other times, entire novels, perfect and destined to endure, rose magically before his eyes. At other moments, the recollections were absurdly trivial: garrets he had once inhabited, lodgings that had sheltered him. Oleron knew well of such places in his youth, but all of that was gone. He had finally found a refuge he did not intend to leave until dragged out—a place some might call slightly green-sick, others too steeped in morbid history for a living man to inhabit, yet it was irresistible, commanding with an authority of its own, accompanied by a specific associate, offering delights once a man ceased struggling against its inexorable will.

"A novel?" someone ought to write about a place like that! There must be endless material if one could only uncover its depths! It had likely already been chronicled by a man named Madley who had lived there... but Oleron had never known Madley. He had a strong feeling he wouldn't have liked him; he wished Madley had lived elsewhere. He hated the fellow—in fact, he despised him. (Ah! That was a joke!). Oleron seriously doubted whether Madley had led the life he should have; sometimes he wondered if he shouldn't report the long-nosed guardian of public morals across the street. Probably the moralist knew and had prayed loudly for him—that was his specialty. Why, Oleron himself had once clashed with him over something... a girl... Elsie Bengough, he remembered.

Oleron felt deep unease regarding Elsie Bengough. Or rather, he wasn't uneasy about her specifically, but restless about the things she did. Her chief offense was persistently thrusting herself into his thoughts. Whenever he caught her appearing, he sent her packing immediately. The truth was, she was not merely a bore; she had always been that. Now, her mere presence in his imagination became inimical to the full enjoyment of certain experiences. She lacked tact; surely she ought to know that people are not available to everyone's thoughts at all times. Out of mere politeness, she should allow him seasons entirely to himself. She was monstrously ignorant if she didn't realize there were special hours when a man's veins ran with fire, daring, and power—hours when he had a reasonable right to treat folk as he had treated the prying Barrett: to shut them out completely.

But no—up she popped, the thought of her, ruining everything. Bright, towering fabrics, beside which even those perfect, magical novels he dreamed of seemed dull and gray, vanished utterly at her intrusion. It was as if a fog suddenly quenched a fair-beaming star, as if a pit gaped at the threshold of a golden portal prepared for Oleron, or as if a bat-like shadow turned growing dawn into mire and darkness. Therefore, Oleron strove to stifle even the nascent thought of her.

Nevertheless, there came an occasion when Elsie Bengough absolutely refused suppression. Oleron could not pinpoint exactly when it happened; he only knew by the glimmer of the streetlamp on his blind that it was late at night, and that for some time she had not presented herself. He received no warning, none. She simply arrived—she was there. Striving as he would, he could not shake the thought of her or the image of her face. She haunted him. But for her to arrive at that precise moment of all moments!... Truly, it was past belief! How she could endure it, Oleron could not conceive. Actually, to watch, as it were, the triumph of a rival.... Good God! It was monstrous! Tact—reticence—he had never credited her with an overwhelming amount of either; yet he had never attributed mere... oh, there was no word for it! Monstrous—monstrous! Did she intend henceforth... Good God! To look on!...

Oleron felt blood rush to the roots of his hair in anger against her. "Damnation take her!" he choked. But the next moment, his heat and resentment transformed into a cold sweat of cowering fear. Panic-stricken, he strove to comprehend what he had done. Though he knew not what, he knew he had done something fatal, irreparable, blasting. Anger he had felt, but not *this* blaze of ire that suddenly flooded the twilight of his consciousness with a white, infernal light. *That* appalling flash was not his—not his *that* open rift of bright, searing hell—not his, not his! His had been the hand of a child preparing a puny blow; but what was *this other* horrific hand drawn back to strike in the same place? Had *he* set that in motion? Had *he* provided the spark that ignited the whole accumulated power of that formidable, relentless place? He did not know. He only knew that that poor igniting particle within himself was blown out, that—Oh, impossible!--a clinging kiss (how else to express it?) had changed on his very lips to a gnashing and a removal, and that, out of pity for the awful odds, he must cry out to her against whom he had lately raged to guard herself... guard herself....

"Look out!" he shrieked aloud.

The revulsion was instant. As if a cold, slow billow had broken over him, he woke to find himself lying in his bed. The mist and horror that had long enwrapped him had departed. He was Paul Oleron, and he was sick, naked, helpless, and unutterably abandoned and alone. His faculties, though weak, eventually answered his calls. He knew it must have been a hideous nightmare leaving him sweating and shaking. Yes, he was himself, Paul Oleron, a tired novelist already past the summit of his best work, slipping downhill again empty-handed. He had struck short in his life's aim. He had tried too much, overestimated his strength, and was a failure, a failure.... It all crystallized in the single word, wrapped and complete; it required no sequential thought. He was a failure. He had missed.... And he had missed not one happiness, but two. He had missed the ease of this world, which men love, and he had also missed that other shining prize for which men forsake ease—the snatching, holding, and triumphant bearing aloft of which is the only justification of the mad adventurer who hazards the enterprise. And there was no second attempt. Fate has no tomorrow. Oleron's tomorrow must be to sit down to profitless, ill-done, unrequired work again, and so on the morrow after that, and the morrow after that, and as many tomorrows as there might be....

He lay there, weakly yet sanely considering it.... Since the whole attempt had failed, it was hardly worth while to salvage anything from the general wreck. No good would ever come of that half-finished novel. He had intended it to appear in autumn; he was under contract; no matter—it was better to pay forfeit to his publishers than to waste the days left. He was spent; age was not far off; and paths of wisdom and sadness were the properest for the remainder of the journey....
