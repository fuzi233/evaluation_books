The autumn light was fading fast over Florence that day, the echoes of the French king's grand entry procession still humming in the streets, as Tito Melema hurried across the Ponte Rubaconte, the weight of his newly purchased chain armour tucked hidden beneath his fur-lined mantle. A mile away, in the long shadow-draped old library atop Bardo de' Bardi's palazzo, Romola paced back and forth among the shelves of yellowed codices, her thoughts fixed only on her husband, her ears straining for the sound of his step on the stone stairs.

That day, nearly every window in the city had held a curious face leaning out to gawk at Cristianissimo and his retinue of French nobles. Romola's window was one of the very few that stayed empty. She had not joined a single public feast or celebration since her father's sudden death three months prior, when the blind old scholar had slumped mid-dictation in his oak chair, his pen still resting on the page, after waiting long past the hour Tito usually came to transcribe his commentary on the New Platonists. Even in the first raw burst of her grief, a thought she had been too ashamed to name had risen unbidden in her breast: perhaps now, unshackled from the endless, exacting demands of her father's monotonous days, her life with Tito might finally be the perfect, undivided union she had dreamed of as a girl.

The rainbow glow of her early wedded bliss had not faded, exactly, but it had grown threaded with tiny, unspoken thorns she had spent every month since marrying Tito trying to explain away. She told herself it was no flaw in his love that he had gradually wearied of the long quiet hours of transcribing for Bardo, that the novelty of the shared scholarly work had worn thin. No one but she, who had built her whole young life around tending to her father's needs, could possibly sustain that same unbroken patience year after year. Even she had felt small, secret surges of rebellious frustration, after all. It was only natural that a bright, quick-witted man like Tito, who was welcomed into every noble household, every cardinal's feast, every political gathering across Florence, would crave that vivid, buzzing society far more than the hush of a library full of dead languages. She told herself she would not lay unfair claims on him, that she would soften all her little sharp edges, bend her own will to his, for her love for him was the whole anchor of her world. To ever admit Tito could act unworthily of that love would be to let the ground crumble beneath her feet.

The one sacred duty she clung to, the small atonement she could make to Bardo's memory for that guilty quiet relief at his passing, was to fulfil his lifelong dearest wish: to turn his half-century of toil, his collection of rare manuscripts and ancient artefacts built up through decades of frugality, into a public library for Florence. The precious collection was protected from creditors thanks to her godfather Bernardo del Nero, who had given Romola a thousand florins out of his own modest fortune to settle their outstanding debts, taking only a lien on the manuscripts as security, saying cheerfully that the state would surely repay him once the cardinal found a building to house the library. But ten days prior, all hopes tied to the Medici had collapsed: the Medici family had been driven out of the city, their own famous library was at risk of being seized and sold off by the French agents who swarmed the streets, and the cash-strapped Florentine Republic, facing war to recover Pisa and forced to pay vast tributes to the French king, was far more eager to sell off manuscripts for gold than to honour Bardo's quiet scholarly dream. For Romola, these seismic political shifts had no meaning beyond their threat to her father's legacy. She had grown up in a world of books, far removed from the brawling streets and partisan rivalries of Florentine public life, and now, for the first time, she found herself curious to understand what power the fiery preacher Fra Girolamo Savonarola held over the whole city, that he might turn the tide of events.

That very morning, on a sudden impulse, she had walked from Bardo's tomb in Santa Croce straight to the Duomo to hear his Advent sermon. The memory of that last terrible meeting with her renegade brother Dino had faded into the background of her busy married days, and she had gone half-expecting to find nothing but the ranting of a narrow fanatical monk. But when Savonarola spoke of martyrdom, the whole crowd had dissolved into sobs, and Romola had wept with them, caught in a strange, unnameable thrill, like being held in the swell of actual music rather than just remembering its sound. The feeling had faded as soon as she left the cathedral, though, and all she could think of was waiting for Tito to come home.

It was more than two hours after Tito had crossed the bridge before Romola heard the great iron gate of the courtyard creak open. She flew to the top of the stone stairs, the gilded lamplight over the landing spilling over her flushed cheeks. Eighteen months of marriage had softened her once proud, unyielding face, turning its cold distant dignity into something tender and beseeching; she was far lovelier that night than the day Tito had first laid eyes on her.

"You are tired, my Tito," she said, reaching for him as he climbed the steps. "It has been such a long, fatiguing day, has it not?"

They did not speak of private things until they had shut the library door behind them, the fire crackling bright on the great iron andirons casting warm light over the rows of manuscripts. Tito kissed her perfunctorily when she took his mantle from him, then threw himself heavily into the high-backed chair by the hearth, shivering a little.

"Romola, I wish you would not spend all your time in this drafty old library," he said, not peevish, but worn thin, his usual bright charm fraying at the edges. "Our own private chambers are far cozier on a chill night like this."

Romola felt a small, sharp hurt prickle in her chest. He had never been so distant before, usually so full of quick, solicitous little attentions. "You forget," she said softly, leaning in to read the excuse for his coldness in his tired face, "I am making the new catalogue of the collection exactly as my father wished. You have no spare time to help me with it, so I must work here closely."

Tito closed his eyes, rubbing his hands over his face and hair. The terror of running into his escaped old master Baldassarre in the Duomo that day still gnawed at him, the secret fear that would alienate Romola from him forever if she ever learned the truth of his past, and he could not summon the usual soft affectionate words he had for her. "I am not well," he said. "Do not be surprised if I seem peevish."

"Oh, of course you are worn out after all that you have done today," Romola said, kneeling beside him, her arm resting light on his chest as she brushed the hair back from his face. Then she drew back sharply, her eyes wide with alarm. "What is this under your tunic, Tito? It is hard as iron."

"It is iron," he said calmly. "Chain armour. It is mine now. I will have to wear it constantly, for some time."

"Is there some sudden danger today?" Romola said, clinging to his arm, her face going pale.

"Anyone who is not a rabid enemy of the Medici is at risk right now," he said, smiling as he laid a hand on her neck, the familiar soft habit of his affection breaking through the cold distance of a few moments prior. "This armour will keep me safe from any hidden attacks."

"But my godfather Bernardo is a far more prominent Medici ally than you are," Romola protested. "He has no armour, no precautions. Why is he not in more danger?"

"Men of his standing are protected by their names and their vast family connections that stretch across every party in Florence," Tito said smoothly. "I am a foreign Greek, no one would avenge me if I were knifed in a dark alley. I have had specific threats, Romola. You must promise never to speak of this to Bernardo. It would be a breach of my confidence."

"I will not say a word, if you wish it to be a secret," Romola said, blushing. A small, ugly flicker of a thought that he was acting out of cowardice rose in her mind, but she pushed it away immediately. "But this heavy armour, this constant fear -- it will make you so wretched. It feels like some dark enchantment, as if a fiend has wrapped my bright, light-footed Tito in a cold hard shell."

"Would you rather I walk the streets unprotected, to be stabbed or shot?" Tito laughed. "If you do not mind me being harmed, why should I mind? I will throw the armour away right now, if you wish."

"No, no," Romola said hurriedly. "I am being fanciful. Do not listen to me. But are such violent attacks really common in Florence now? My father always said they were unheard of here."

"The bitter partisan hatreds that are growing by the day will make them common enough soon," Tito said, shrugging.

Romola forced herself to set the troubling subject aside. "Tell me of the procession today, then," she said, brightening. "Did all go well?"

"Perfectly well," he said, grinning. "The rain came just in time to cut short Luca Corsini's endless tedious oration that no one wanted to sit through, and someone stepped up quick to give the French king the shortest possible welcome in rough, stilted French -- no one is quite sure who it was, it happened so fast."

"It was you, I know it was you," Romola said, laughing and kissing his cheek, proud that he never bothered to claim credit for his clever little acts.

"After that there was nothing but parades, jewels, trappings, feasts, and endless flattery," Tito said. "I was at Tornabuoni's banquet later. I will tell you all the rest tomorrow, I am too tired now."

Romola hesitated a moment, then said cheerfully, "You will never guess where I went today. I went to the Duomo to hear Fra Girolamo's sermon."

Tito started sharply, immediately thinking of Baldassarre stumbling into the cathedral that day, but he relaxed when Romola went on. "I wanted to see for myself what power he has over all the people, what he says about this French invasion. He had such a strange hold over the whole crowd, I found myself sobbing along with everyone else, I cannot quite explain why. I even saw a wild old man there, an escaped prisoner with a rope around his neck, crying as he listened. Piero di Cosimo came and cut the rope off and led him out."

Tito paused for a beat before he answered. "I saw that man. I was outside the Duomo with Lorenzo Tornabuoni when he ran in, escaping from a French soldier." He rose slowly to his feet, the cold heavy secret of Baldassarre's freedom pressing down on his chest like a second sheet of armour, far heavier than the iron hidden under his tunic. "I need to rest, Romola," he said, and the quiet weight of dread hung heavy in the library firelight.
