A low ripple of delight ran through my companions and broke against me like warm broth against a cold spoon: ahead, scattered on the long meadow, the rest of the Little Ones had sighted us. The two who rode with me on Lona’s horse pressed on first, eager as children sent to fetch water at dusk. They reached their fellows and were met by a wavering shout—then, as if someone had shut a door on the very air, the cry snapped short.

We drew nearer, and what came to us was not shouting but the sound of sobbing—small voices breaking and reforming like tiny wavelets on a pebbled shore. When I stepped among them, I understood at once that this was no common grief. Terror had baked itself into their faces, leaving them drawn and gray, as if the life in their cheeks had been smoked out by some unseen fire. No loss alone could have worked such a change.

Several came slowly around me and lifted their arms to take my burden. I yielded her up. The smile with which they received Lona’s body—tender, hopeless, and careful as hands laying pastry into a hot oven without letting it collapse—swelled my heart with pity inside my own desolation.

They tried everything the heart knows when it cannot accept a silence. Their tears fell on her as if salt might wake her. They kissed her brow and stroked her hands, coaxing as they bore her, rocking her gently, pressing their cheeks to hers. In vain. She would not wake.

Two on either side took an arm, rubbing it with slow, soothing passes as if warmth could be kneaded back into muscle the way one works life into dough. As many as could reach slid their forearms under her body, spreading the weight so no one would hurt her. Those who could not support her crowded around the bearers, touching her hair, her sleeve, the hem of her garment—each contact a plea.

They found a place where the grass grew thicker and softer, like a bed made by patient hands. There they laid her down, and all the Little Ones gathered, sobbing, a tight ring of bent heads.

Outside that ring stood the elephants, immense and still; and I stood near them, looking at my Lona over the many small crowns between. The evening held a hush in the meadow, pierced by the thin, wet sounds of grief. Even the insects seemed to keep their music low, as if the whole place understood that certain noises—metallic, grinding, loud—would be an obscenity here. Their sorrow had its own acoustic weather: breath hitching, throats breaking, palms patting grass; a domestic soundscape of loss, intimate and unbought, nothing like the staged wailing of courts.

Those nearest me caught sight of the princess. They stared, shivering, as if the air around her had turned sour.

Odu was the first to speak. He leaned toward his neighbor and whispered, “I have seen that woman before!”

“It was she who fought the white leopardess,” he went on, “the night they woke us with their yelling!”

“Silly!” said the other, though his own voice trembled. “That was a wild beast, with spots!”

“Look at her eyes!” Odu insisted. “I know she is a bad giantess, but she is a wild beast all the same. I know she is the spotted one!”

The other took a step nearer. Odu caught his sleeve and jerked him back, sharp as a cook yanking a child away from a boiling pot.

“Don’t look at her!” he cried, shrinking—yet held by the hate-filled longing in the princess’s eyes, as if her gaze were a hook. “She would eat you up in a moment! It was HER shadow! She is the wicked princess!”

“That cannot be!” someone protested in the crowd. “They said she was beautiful!”

“Indeed it is the princess,” I said, stepping between their fear and her. “Wickedness has made her ugly.”

She heard. The look she gave me was a thing with teeth, like a bitter herb chewed too long until it poisons the mouth. Even without moving, she seemed to contaminate the air with her wanting.

“It was very wrong of me to run away!” Odu said then, suddenly thoughtful, as if he were confessing to an elder over a table where the bread is already cut.

“What made you run away?” I asked. “I expected to find you where I left you!”

He did not answer at once.

“I don’t know what made me run,” said another. “I was frightened!”

“It was a man that came down the hill from the palace,” said a third.

“How did he frighten you?”

“I don’t know.”

“He wasn’t a man,” said Odu; “he was a shadow; he had no thick to him!”

“Tell me more about him,” I said, for the word shadow in that place was never an empty word, and I felt the night itself lean closer to listen.

“He came down the hill very black,” Odu said, “walking like a bad giant, but spread flat. He was nothing but blackness. We were frightened the moment we saw him, but we did not run away; we stood and watched him. He came on as if he would walk over us. But before he reached us, he began to spread and spread, and grew bigger and bigger, till at last he was so big that he went out of our sight, and we saw him no more, and then he was upon us!”

“What do you mean by that?”

“He was all black through between us,” Odu answered, “and we could not see one another; and then he was inside us.”

Even in the telling, the meadow seemed to darken by a shade, as if the memory carried its own eclipse. I thought of how a sudden industrial roar can blot out a whole living world of sound—bird, leaf, stream—until you feel alone though you stand among friends. Their terror had been like that: a violence done to hearing and seeing alike, a smothering that made each child solitary inside his own skin.

“How did you know he was inside you?”

“He did me quite different,” said Odu. “I felt like bad. I was not Odu any more—not the Odu I knew. I wanted to tear Sozo to pieces—not really, but like!”

He turned and threw his arms around Sozo, clinging hard.

“It wasn’t me, Sozo,” he sobbed. “Really, deep down, it was Odu, loving you always! And Odu came up, and knocked Naughty away. I grew sick, and thought I must kill myself to get out of the black. Then came a horrible laugh that had heard my think, and it set the air trembling about me. And then I suppose I ran away, but I did not know I had run away until I found myself running, fast as could, and all the rest running too. I would have stopped, but I never thought of it until I was out of the gate among the grass. Then I knew that I had run away from a shadow that wanted to be me and wasn’t, and that I was the Odu that loved Sozo. It was the shadow that got into me, and hated him from inside me; it was not my own self me!

“And now I know that I ought not to have run away! But indeed I did not quite know what I was doing until it was done! My legs did it, I think: they grew frightened, and forgot me, and ran away! Naughty legs! There! and there!”

And with that he kicked each leg, as if scolding disobedient tools.

“What became of the shadow?” I asked.

“I do not know,” he said. “I suppose he went home into the night where there is no moon.”

His words left a taste like cold ash on my tongue. I sat there among grass and grief and the heavy breathing of elephants, wondering where Lona was gone—Lona whose presence had been a kind of language among them, a dialect of courage and gentleness they all understood without translation. Now her body lay like a closed book whose script the living could not read.

Dropping to the ground, I drew the dead thing into my lap and bent close, as if warmth and voice might still find a way in.

“Where are you, Lona?” I whispered into her ear. “I love you!”

No answer came. Her lips, not quite cold yet, held their silence. I kissed them—one last time tasting the fading human warmth—then laid her down again. I appointed a guard over her, and rose. There were practical needs to tend even in a myth: the night would come, and with it whatever moved in darkness, and the Little Ones must be kept.

Before the sun went down I set a watch over the princess outside the camp, and placed sentinels all around. I meant to walk the circle myself through the whole night, to keep the meadow’s fragile quiet from being shattered again by any invading wrong sound or unseen footstep. I told the rest of the little army to sleep. They flung themselves on the grass and were asleep at once, as children do after a day too heavy for their small bodies.

When the moon rose, its pale light lay on the field like flour sifted thinly over a table. In that washed silver I caught a glimpse of something white: the leopardess.

She swept silently around the sleeping camp, soundless as breath, and I saw her pass three times between the princess and the Little Ones, as if she were measuring, guarding, or choosing. After the third circuit, I made the watch lie down with the others. Then I stretched myself beside the body of Lona, keeping my own vigil where love had become as mute as death, and listening to the night—its honest grasses, its distant, unbought hush—against which every cruel laugh and every spreading shadow would always be an act of war.
