On the day Gwendolen Harleth became Mrs. Grandcourt, the morning air was crisp with a slight frost that curled the edges of the leaves, cutting the sunlight into sharp, bright shards. Half the village of Pennicote lined the path to the church, drawn by the spectacle of the bridal party, a procession that seemed to demand attention from every corner of the parish. The rector, acting as father to the bride, officiated alongside an old friend, lending a gravity to the proceedings that only heightened the sense of occasion. Yet, amidst the general effervescence, two faces stood out as islands of gloom: Mrs. Davilow's and Anna's. The mother's eyelids were swollen and pink, the physical toll of tears shed throughout the night, a testament to the agonizing severance from a daughter who was the very bloom of her existence. Anna's distress, however, puzzled the onlookers; she was being framed by a bridesmaid's gown of such splendor that it seemed to mock her melancholy.

The crowd reflected the brilliance of the day, especially the bride herself. Gwendolen possessed the figure and carriage of a titled lady, though one might argue a title demanded a rosier hue; yet, the groom, pale and possessing a complexion akin to the miller's wife's own husband, completed the union perfectly in the eyes of the spectators. They assumed he loved her dearly, hoping he would never hold against her the vicissitudes of her past—her years as a governess, her mother's humble cottage at Sawyer's. Even the miller's fourteen-year-old daughter, initially skeptical that high-born gentility treated its wives poorly, was corrected by her mother, who recounted tales of Squire Pelton whipping his wife in secret. "Men are men," the tailor interjected, noting how disputes begin with the tongue. "This Mr. Grandcourt has a wonderful little tongue," he added, observing the groom's demeanor. "Everything must be done dummy-like without his ordering." Mrs. Girdle nodded sagely, "Then he's the more whip, I doubt. She's got tongue enough." As the couple emerged, the tailor noted the long, dramatic corners of Gwendolen's eyes, remarking that she made people feel comical when she looked at them.

In truth, Gwendolen displayed an elasticity of bearing and a lustre in her brown glance born of intense, almost feverish excitement. It was not pain she felt, but a state akin to standing at a gambling table, watching her chips dwindle until the thrill of loss eclipsed the unease of a growing conscience. Ambition and a hunger for luxury, slowly poisoning her spirit, fueled a gratifying vanity that rendered her fears irrelevant. She did not repent her choice; rather, she surmounted the nagging doubts with an exulting defiance, daring the world to judge her as she stepped onto the board of life, eager to win or lose with éclat. She believed she was acquiring the official strength of marriage, an intoxication of youthful egoism returning stronger than before. Walking with an erect head and elastic step, she moved among illusions, unaware that beneath the surface, a subtle intoxication had already taken root.

"Thank God you bear it so well, my darling!" Mrs. Davilow cried, helping Gwendolen remove her white veil and slip into her traveling dress. The mother's trembling had been the only sign of weakness, urging Gwendolen to view the morning as a triumph. Gwendolen laughed tenderly, pressing her hands to her mother's cheeks. "Why, you might have said that if I were going to Mrs. Mompert's, you dear, sad, incorrigible mamma!" she teased, then spread her arms to display herself. "Here am I—Mrs. Grandcourt! What else would you have me?" She spoke of splendid houses, horses, diamonds, courtship, and titles, promising to love her mother better than anyone else. Mrs. Davilow accepted the declaration with a whispered plea for happiness, admitting she would bear the separation sorrowfully. Gwendolen, shaking her head with a bright smile, declared that sorrow was her mother's sauce, necessary for any joy. Clinging to each other, Gwendolen rained kisses on her mother's cheeks, wishing she could go with her, before handing her hand to Grandcourt to lead her to the carriage. The rector offered a final blessing, telling Mrs. Davilow that Gwendolen was in a position far beyond his hopes, chosen entirely for her own sake.

The journey to Ryelands covered fifty miles by rail, arriving as twilight veiled the sky. Driving through the park, Gwendolen watched the grand outlines of the estate emerge from the gloom: winding drives bordered by evergreens, vast grassy undulations dotted with dark clumps, and finally, the white house framed by a hanging wood and a rising terrace. During the ride, she was at her liveliest, chatting incessantly, treating Grandcourt's gentle grip on her hand as a playful contest to be won. She grew febrile with excitement, her heart palpating at the changing light and scenery. Was it the fulfillment of her girlish dream to be "somebody," walking corridors of lofty silence where her painted springs bloomed and zephyrs blew trumpets, surrounded by servants who revered her insolence? Or was it a dim, insistent dread mixing with the expectation of triumph? Exultation in her nature carried an inevitable infusion of dread, ready to curdle. As they approached the gates, she fell silent. When Grandcourt kissed her, she barely registered it, lost in the absorbing show of her own making. A numbness settled over her personality, replacing the half-willful excitement of the day.

Inside, the hall glowed with warmth, matting, carpets, and the imposing presence of portraits and statues. Servants were few, mostly from Diplow, plus a new maid assigned to Gwendolen. Led by Grandcourt down a subtly scented corridor, she entered an ante-room where a doorway spilled rich light and color. "These are our dens," Grandcourt said, pressing her hand to his lips before moving away, more in love than he anticipated. Gwendolen yielded her hat and mantle, throwing herself into a chair by the glowing hearth, admiring her reflection in the glass panels amidst her faint-green satin surroundings. The housekeeper lingered briefly before approaching with a packet, delivered strictly to Gwendolen's hands. "It was a present particularly ordered by Mr. Grandcourt," the housekeeper explained, bowing low. "He was not to know of its arrival till he saw you wear it."

Gwendolen placed the packet on her lap, assuming it contained the promised diamonds. Inside the sealed coverings lay a box, and within that, a jewel-case. As the gleaming stones revealed themselves, a letter lay atop them. Recognizing the handwriting instantly, she felt as if an adder had coiled upon the jewels. Her heart leaped, draining her strength; her hands trembled violently as she unfolded the thin paper. The words thrust themselves upon her: *These diamonds, once given with ardent love to Lydia Glasher, I pass on to you. You have broken your word... God is too just for that. The man you have married has a withered heart. His best young love was mine... It is dead: but I am the grave in which your chance of happiness is buried as well as mine.* The letter accused her of choosing to injure him and his children, declaring that she had taken him with her eyes open and that her willing wrong would be her curse.

At first, Gwendolen stared, spellbound by the horror of the words, reading them as a doom of penance. Suddenly, a spasm of terror seized her. She leaned forward, stretching the paper toward the fire, desperate to destroy the accusation before it could reach anyone's eyes. The note flew like a feather from her trembling fingers, consumed instantly by a great draft of flame. In her frantic movement, the casket toppled to the floor, scattering the diamonds. She paid no heed, collapsing back into her chair, utterly helpless. She could not see her own reflection anymore; she appeared to others as a woman petrified white, save for the visible tremor in her lips and hands. For a long while, she sat in a stupor, feeling ill, haunted by the repeating words of the burned letter. Truly, these were poisoned gems, and the venom had seeped into the veins of the poor young creature.

Finally, a tap at the door announced Grandcourt's entrance, dressed for dinner. The sight of him struck a new, violent nerve. Gwendolen screamed again and again, her hysteria erupting with terrifying force. Grandcourt, expecting a smiling bride ready to descend the stairs, found instead a pallid, shrieking woman with jewels scattered around her feet on the floor. Was it madness? Had the furies finally crossed his threshold?
