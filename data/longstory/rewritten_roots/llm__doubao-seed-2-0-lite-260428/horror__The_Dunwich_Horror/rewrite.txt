If you’re driving north through central Massachusetts, distracted by the hum of your tires and the stretch of Aylesbury pike ahead, you may not even notice the moment you take the wrong fork past Dean’s Corners—until the world softens into something that no longer fits the map.

The ground rises slow, almost imperceptibly, and brier-choked fieldstone walls creep closer and closer until they brush the rutted dust of the curving road, their lichen crusted thick as old dried bruise. The trees lining the endless woodlots are too big, trunks twisted like gnarled, knuckled fists, their leaves hanging so still no breeze from the settled counties behind you seems to reach them. Wild weeds, brambles, and thick unnameable grasses choke every empty patch of dirt, lush to a fevered, almost greedy degree you never see in farmed, lived-in parts of the state. The few tilled fields you pass are barren, stunted with yellowed unthrifty crops, and the scattered houses dotting the slopes all wear the same heavy, uniform cloak of age, squalor, and rot. You never think to stop and ask directions of the ragged, solitary figures you catch glimpses of on crumbling doorsteps or in the rock-strewn meadows below. They never wave, never call out, their heads tilted at a wrong, unhuman angle, their eyes tracking you all the way down the road without a single shift of their shoulders. To speak to them feels like reaching for something you know is forbidden, something that will cling to your skin long after you walk away.

When the road crests a small rise, the high hills loom over the treeline, and that faint, crawling unease curdles in your gut. Their summits are too rounded, too perfectly symmetrical, no craggy outcrops breaking their smooth curve, as if something massive pushed up through the earth eons ago and wore the peaks down with the weight of its own body. Silhouetted sharp against the pale sky, you can make out the queer, regular circles of tall rough stone pillars that crown almost every one of them, standing straight as sentinels at attention. Gorges of unguessable depth cut across the path, and the crude wooden bridges strung across their dark mouths creak and sway under your weight like they are breathing, not built to hold anything that does not belong here. When the road dips low again, you hit stretches of marshland that make your skin prickle, that you recoil from instinctively—worse at dusk, when unseen whippoorwills chatter in overlapping, unearthly tones, fireflies swarm so thick they blur the dark, and stridently piping bullfrogs croak in a harsh, steady rhythm that sounds like counting, slow and unrelenting. The thin, glinting upper reach of the Miskatonic winds coiled at the feet of those domed hills, glinting like the scaled back of some great sleeping serpent.

You do not look at the stone-crowned hilltops then. You stare instead at their dark, precipitous wooded sides, which loom higher and higher, pressing in on you until you ache to put distance between you and them—only to find there is no turnoff, no escape. You cross a rickety covered bridge, and spot the village huddled tight between the river and the sheer slope of Round Mountain, a clump of rotting gambrel roofs that look centuries older than any architecture for thirty miles. It is no comfort to see, as you draw nearer, that most of the houses are long abandoned, crumbling in on themselves, and the old church with its broken, half-collapsed steeple now houses the only slovenly general store in the whole hamlet. You dread driving into the pitch-black tunnel of the covered bridge, but there is no way past it. Once you emerge on the other side, you can not shake the faint, heavy malign odor clinging to the dirt street: the stench of centuries of mold, of rot that no sun has ever fully burned away. It is a physical relief to peel out of the village, follow the narrow road looping around the base of the hills, and hit open level country until you rejoin the familiar stretch of the Aylesbury pike. Later, a local at the next town’s gas station will blink at you and ask how the hell you ended up in Dunwich.

Outsiders visit Dunwich as rarely as possible. After the spate of unspeakable events of 1928, all the road signs pointing toward it were torn down and burned. By every ordinary standard, the rolling wooded hills and river valleys around it are stunningly beautiful, but no landscape artists or summer tourists ever venture that way. Two hundred years ago, folks did not laugh at talk of witch blood, Satan worship, and strange presences in the woods, so they had clear, sharp reasons to avoid the place. In our supposedly sensible modern age, the full facts of the 1928 horror were hushed up by everyone who claimed to have the town’s and the world’s best interests at heart—so now people shun it without being able to say exactly why.

One obvious reason, for anyone who has spent time nearby, is that the Dunwich natives are repellently decayed, generations deep down that path of inbred retrogression common to isolated New England backwaters. They have formed a distinct, unrecognizable race of their own, marked with clear, unmistakable stigmata of degeneracy: slack faces, sallow skin, jerky uncoordinated movements, an average intelligence woefully lower than any other community in the state. Their recorded history reeks of open viciousness, half-hidden murders, incest, and acts of unnamable violence and perversity no local paper will ever print. The old gentry, the two or three armigerous families that fled Salem in 1692, have stayed slightly above the general decay—though many of their branches have sunk so far into the sordid populace that only their old family names remain to mark the origin they disgrace. Some of the Whateleys and Bishops still send their eldest sons to Harvard and Miskatonic University, but those sons almost never return to the rotting gambrel roofs they were born under. Most die drunk, screaming in city bars about the things that live under the hills.

No one, not even the handful of people who hold the full facts of the 1928 horror, can ever fully explain what is fundamentally wrong with Dunwich. Old legends speak of unhallowed indigenous rites, where the Pocumtucks called forbidden shadow-shapes up out of the rounded hills, chanting wild orgiastic prayers that were answered with deep rumbling cracks and thunder from beneath the earth. In 1747, newly arrived Congregational minister Reverend Abijah Hoadley preached a sermon no one who heard it ever forgot, in which he thundered: “It must be allow'd that these Blasphemies of an infernall Train of Dæmons are Matters of too common Knowledge to be deny'd; the cursed Voices of Azazel and Buzrael, of Beelzebub and Belial, being heard from under Ground by above a Score of credible Witnesses now living. I myself did not more than a Fortnight ago catch a very plain Discourse of evill Powers in the Hill behind my House; wherein there were a Rattling and Rolling, Groaning, Screeching, and Hissing, such as no Things of this Earth cou'd raise up, and which must needs have come from those Caves that only black Magick can discover, and only the Divell unlock.” Hoadley vanished three days after that sermon. His printed sermon, published in Springfield, still survives. His body was never found.

Reports of unaccountable noises in the hills have been logged every single year since, a puzzle no conventional geologist or physiographer has ever been able to explain. Other local traditions tell of foul sulfurous odors clinging to the hilltop stone pillar circles, of rushing, unseen airy presences you can hear faintly at certain hours at the bottom of the deepest ravines. The most well-known local landmark is the Devil’s Hop Yard: a bleak, blasted hillside where no tree, shrub, or single blade of grass will ever grow, no matter how much fertilizer or seed you dump on its burnt black soil. The natives are mortally afraid of the swarms of whippoorwills that cry out on every warm night. They swear the birds are psychopomps, lying in wait for the souls of the dying, their eerie cries timed perfectly to match the heave and gasp of a dying person’s last breaths. If they snatch the fleeing soul the moment it leaves the body, they flutter away chittering in demoniac laughter. If they fail to catch it? They fall silent, disappointed, before they start to creep closer to the house, looking for another way in.

Folks write all these tales off as obsolete, ridiculous old superstitions. But Dunwich is not like any other old New England town. It is far older than any community within thirty miles. South of the village, you can still spot the crumbing cellar walls and chimney of the old Bishop house, built before 1700. The ruins of the 1806 mill at the falls are the most modern piece of architecture standing. The 19th century factory movement died there within two years, no one ever figured out why. Oldest of all are the great rings of rough-hewn stone columns on the hilltops, which most locals attribute to no human settler at all. Caches of skulls and bones found inside the circles and around the flat table-like boulder on Sentinel Hill are widely said to be Pocumtuck burial remains—though any ethnologist who gets close enough to look will admit, off the record, that those bones do not belong to any human race that was ever supposed to exist on this continent.

The hour was 5 a.m., February 2nd, 1913, in the backwoods township of Dunwich. The wind howled off the steep, unblessed hills, carrying the echo of unnameable rumblings that had rumbled through the dark for twelve straight hours, and the froth-flecked baying of every hound in the county that had not once fallen silent all night. The date was Candlemas, though no local dared speak that name out loud—they called it the Feast of Unlatched Gates, and none in the valley had ever forgotten what those open gates invited through.

It was in that half-rotted, half-empty farmhouse clinging to the slope four miles from the village, a mile and a half from the nearest neighbor, that Wilbur Whateley drew his first breath. No midwife attended. No doctor had been called. For minutes after he emerged, a shriek tore through the air loud enough to cut through the hills' groaning and the dogs' racket, a scream that would rattle distant farmhouse windows and linger in the memories of anyone who heard it for decades.

His mother was Lavinia Whateley: 35, a lopsided, unattractive albino, her pink eyes perpetually watering, her frame hunched, living alone with her half-insane 90-year-old father. Old Whateley had been the subject of the most horrific wizardry whispers in the valley for three generations, and his wife had been found hacked to pieces with an axe in the farm's root cellar when Lavinia was 12 years old—no killer ever identified. Lavinia had never gone to school, never had a known husband, and made no pretense of disavowing the dark, swarthy, goatish-looking infant that was nothing short of a stark, shocking contrast to her own pale, sickly skin and hair. She was not ashamed. She was strangely, unnaturally proud, muttering wild prophecies of unearthly power and a fate that would shake the hills as she rocked him on her knee. She was the kind of woman to mutter such things: a loner who wandered the moors barefoot even in the height of thunderstorms, poring over the two-hundred-year-old mouldering books the Whateley line had hoarded for centuries, pages softening with rot and wormholes, reeking of dried ichor and unknown incense. No standard of cleanliness or order had touched the farmhouse for decades, leaving her free to indulge in wild, grand daydreams of things the other Dunwich villagers only dared whisper about behind closed doors.

No one in the village knew the baby existed for a full week, until Old Whateley drove his sleigh through knee-deep snow down to Osborn's general store. The men loitering by the stove noticed at once that something had shifted in the old man: a new, furtive edge to his clouded mad eyes that turned him from a silly local oddity into something actively, fundamentally terrifying. He carried the same unholy pride his daughter did, and his rambling words about the baby's paternity were remembered word for word by every man who heard them, long after the horror of 1928 had burned its way across the valley:
"I dun't keer what folks think--ef Lavinny's boy looked like his pa, he wouldn't look like nothin' ye expeck. Ye needn't think the only folks is the folks hereabouts. Lavinny's read some, an' has seed some things the most o' ye only tell abaout. I calc'late her man is as good a husban' as ye kin find this side of Aylesbury; an' ef ye knowed as much abaout the hills as I dew, ye wouldn't ast no better church weddin' nor her'n. Let me tell ye suthin'--some day yew folks'll hear a child o' Lavinny's a-callin' its father's name on the top o' Sentinel Hill!"

Only two outsiders saw Wilbur in his first month of life: Zechariah Whateley, of the still-"respectable" branch of the family, who delivered a pair of Alderney cows Old Whateley had bought, and Mamie Bishop, Earl Sawyer's common-law wife, who tramped up the hill out of pure curiosity. Mamie came back down white as a sheet, and never repeated half of what she saw. The Whateleys began buying cattle by the head after that, hundreds of dollars worth of them every year, right up until the horror broke in 1928—yet their ramshackle barn never looked full. Curious locals would sneak up the steep pasture to count the herd grazing there, and never found more than ten or twelve thin, anemic, bloodless beasts. Weird, clean, incision-like slashes scored their hides, no explanation for the wounds. More than one caller spotted identical thin, oozing cuts on the throats of old Whateley and his slattern albino daughter, too.

Spring came, and Lavinia tramped the hills again, the heavy swarthy child cradled in her lopsided arms. No one bothered to remark on how impossibly fast he grew. At three months old, he was the size and muscular strength of a fully developed one-year-old, his movements slow, deliberate, totally free of the jerky flail of a normal human infant. He walked unassisted at seven months, no wobbly stumbles, no tumbles, and by eight months he moved across uneven ground as steadily as a grown man.

That Halloween, a searing, bonfire-sized blaze was spotted on the peak of Sentinel Hill, right where the ancient flat stone table jutted out of a tumulus piled high with unidentifiable human and animal bones. Silas Bishop, out rounding up a stray heifer, caught sight of mother and son darting up the slope an hour before the fire was seen, moving noiselessly through the underbrush without a stitch of clothing on, so fast he could barely track them. Later he would not swear to the boy's nakedness—he thought Wilbur might have worn a fringed leather belt, and a scrap of dark blue cloth around his hips. No one ever saw Wilbur in partial clothing after that. He was always fully, tightly buttoned from throat to ankle, and if anyone so much as brushed a hand against his collar to loosen it, he would snarl with unchildish rage and terror, as if any sliver of exposed skin would reveal the secret he hid under the cloth.

The next January, the gossips of Dunwich chattered over the fact that "Lavinny's black brat" had started talking at only eleven months old. No baby lisp marred his words. His accent was nothing like the thick rural drawl of the valley, the deep resonant tone seeming to rumble from somewhere far lower in his chest than a normal child's throat could reach. His face was preternaturally mature: no soft baby fat, a sharp, prematurely shaped nose, large dark near-Latin eyes that glinted with a sharp, unhuman intelligence. He was hideously ugly, despite that air of cleverness: thick full lips, coarse yellowed skin pocked with large pores, crinkly rough hair, and weirdly elongated, almost pointed ears that gave him a distinct, unsettlingly goatish cast. He was hated more deeply than his mother and grandfather had ever been, and every whisper about him circled back to old tales of Old Whateley standing in the stone circle on Sentinel Hill decades before, screaming the unnameable syllables of *Yog-Sothoth* at the top of his lungs, while the ground shook so hard every chimney in the valley cracked. Dogs hated him on sight, snapping and snarling and straining at their leashes until foam dripped from their jowls, and he was forced to carry a heavy ironwood club wherever he went to fend them off. No one in Dunwich knew then how right they were to fear him.

All the while, Old Wizard Whateley kept buying head after head of cattle, and not a single one ever turned up in his ragged, lowing herd. No hides hung on his fence line, no bones bleached in the pastures, no smatter of dried blood marked the grass where they might have been butchered—they simply vanished, swallowed whole by the dark that clung to his hillside farm like a shroud.

The old clapboard house, with its steep peaked roof and rear half buried deep in the unyielding rocky slope, creaked and hummed under his relentless labor. For decades, only three ground-floor rooms, half-rotted and strewn with trash, had ever been livable enough for him and his hunchbacked daughter Lavinia; now he hammered and sawed from dawn to long after the hollows went dark, repairing long-abandoned upper floors that no one had stepped foot in for two generations. The old man, half bent to the bone and gnarled with splinters and mildew, held reserves of strength no one could explain, babbling demented half-prayers to things in the hills as he worked, his carpentry sharp, precise, no wasted motion at all.

He had started the work the day Wilbur was born, first clearing and nailing tight a windowless toolshed at the edge of the property, slapping a heavy iron lock on its door that no local blacksmith could pick. Now, restoring the upper story, he boarded every single window shut from the inside, nailing planks an inch thick across every pane, and when the local gossips jeered that it was crazy to waste work on a part of the house no one had used in 50 years, he only stared back at them with milky, unblinking eyes.

Downstairs, he fitted out a new room for his grandson, lining every wall with heavy, tall shelves that no one but him and Wilbur were allowed to touch. He dragged out every tattered, rotting grimoire and partial book heaped in the corners of the house for decades—tomes bound in unknown leather, pages printed in black letter script that no local minister could identify—and sorted them with painstaking, reverent care, gluing torn pages back together with paste he stirred over his rusted kitchen stove. “I got some small use out of these,” he’d mumble to anyone who wandered in, fingers black with old ink and mold, “but that boy’s fitten to make far better use. He’ll need ’em all in perfect order, for they’re every scrap of learnin’ he’ll ever need.”

By September of 1914, when Wilbur was a year and seven months old, the whole of Dunwich began to whisper that something was deeply, unforgivably wrong with the child. He stood as tall and broad as a normal four-year-old, his speech fluid, sharp, and eerily old, as if the words were being pulled from a throat far older than his tiny frame. He roamed the hills with Lavinia at all hours, scrambling up cliff faces no other child could climb, chittering back at the crows that circled Sentinel Hill like he was trading secrets with them. At home, he pored over the queer diagrams and unnameable charts in his grandfather’s moldy books for hours on end, while Old Whateley quizzed him in low, rumbling tones through the hushed summer afternoons.

When the upper story renovations were done, the watchers on the village road spotted a thing they could not explain: one of the rear windows on the east gable, pressed right up against the hillside, had been cut out and replaced with a solid, cleated plank door, and a wide, heavy wooden runway ran straight up the bare rock from the ground to its threshold. Around that same time, the old locked toolshed Wilbur’s birth had left sealed was left gaping open, its iron lock hanging snapped off its hasp. When Earl Sawyer stopped by to sell Old Whateley a herd of calves a few days later, he stepped inside the shed out of curiosity, and stumbled back gagging, his throat burning. The stench, he swore later, was not rot, not sulfur, not any smell that belonged to this world—he had only ever caught a whiff of something close to it once, near the standing stone circles hidden deep in the highest hills. The rest of Dunwich folk merely shrugged; none of their homes or outbuildings ever smelled particularly sweet, anyway.

The months that followed brought no visible disasters, but every soul in the valley swore the low, thrumming rumble that had rumbled under the hills for decades was growing louder, steadier, making the mason jars rattle on pantry shelves in the dead of night. On May Eve 1915, the tremors were strong enough to shake windows 20 miles away in Aylesbury, and that Hallowe’en, pale, unearthly flames burst up from the summit of Sentinel Hill, synchronized with a deep underground roar that no earthquake could explain. The locals crossed their thresholds and spat over their shoulders, muttering that it was just those witch Whateleys, calling up their devils again.

Wilbur was growing at an uncanny, impossible pace. By the time he turned four, he looked like a boy of ten, his narrow, goatish face settling into a fixed, hungry look of unspoken evil that made strangers flinch when they met his gaze. He talked far less than he had as a toddler, turning taciturn and secretive, muttering snatches of guttural, unrecognizable jargon under his breath and chanting in bizarre, lurching rhythms that chilled the marrow of anyone who overheard. The local hounds hated and feared him with a ferocity no one could account for—they would snarl, cower, and lunge for his throat the second he came within sight, so he took to carrying a rusted revolver tucked in his belt. Every time he shot a dog that charged him, he sank even further into the black books of Dunwich’s farmers.

Visitors to the Whateley house almost always found Lavinia alone on the ground floor, while heavy, unhuman footsteps and echoing, inhuman cries boomed behind the nailed-shut second story walls. She would never explain what her father and her son were doing up there, not even when a jocular fish peddler tried to rattle the locked stairway door. The second his hand touched the knob, Lavinia went deathly pale, her whole body shaking with a terror so raw it made the peddler stop teasing immediately. Later, he told the loafers at the Dunwich general store that he’d heard something heavy as a team of draft horses stamping and snorting on the second floor, a floor that should never have held a weight half that great. The men on the store porch went quiet, thinking of that strange plank door set into the gable, that heavy runway built to bear impossible weight, all the cattle that had vanished without a trace. They shuddered, recalling old tales of Old Whateley’s youth, of the dark things you call up out of the earth when you slit a bullock’s throat at midnight on the right altar, for the old gods that don’t sleep under the hills. Before long, every dog in the county refused to step within a hundred yards of the Whateley farm, snarling and howling as if there was something worse than the boy himself waiting inside.

In 1917, the Great War pulled the draft boards into the remote valley, and Squire Sawyer Whateley, heading the local board, nearly wept trying to scrape together enough young Dunwich men even fit to send to a development camp, the whole region rotting with inbreeding and deprivation the government could not explain. Alarmed by the scale of the decay, Washington sent officers and medical experts out to survey the area, and the reports made headlines across New England. That publicity dragged city reporters out to Dunwich, and the *Boston Globe* and *Arkham Advertiser* ran flamboyant full-page Sunday spreads detailing young Wilbur’s impossible precociousness, Old Whateley’s rumored black magic, the shelves of unholy books, the sealed second floor of the Whateley farmhouse, and the strange, unearthly noises thrumming under the hills. Wilbur was four and a half years old then, and he looked like a lad of fifteen, coarse dark stubble fuzzing along his jaw and upper lip, his voice already cracking into a deep, guttural rumble that did not sound like any human child’s voice.

Earl Sawyer guided the reporters and photographers out to the farm, pointing out the strange, unearthly stench that now seeped out from under the nailed upper story windows, thick enough to taste on the back of your tongue. It was exactly the same smell, he told them, that he’d encountered in that abandoned tool shed years before, the same faint, alien tang that drifted off the stone circles hidden in the high hills at midnight. The local Dunwich folk grinned at the papers’ obvious, silly mistakes about their customs, but they went quiet when they read that the reporters had noticed Old Whateley always paid for every new head of cattle in ancient, tarnished gold coins so old no historian could name their origin. The Whateleys had greeted the city visitors with sullen, ill-concealed loathing, but they never dared raise a hand to drive them away—they knew drawing any more attention to the things growing behind their nailed-shut walls would be far, far worse than a few silly newspaper stories.

For ten long years, the Whateleys’ misdeeds bled indistinguishably into Dunwich’s rotting, unspoken folklore—this was a community long hardened to their queer habits, who no longer bothered to glance over their shoulders when the distant, guttering glow of May Eve and All-Hallow bonfires pricked the dark of Sentinel Hill. Twice a year, the flames would lick the hilltop sky, and the mountain would groan and rumble, each tremor worse than the last, shaking milk pails from kitchen tables and sending loose clapboard rattling off the sides of valley cabins. The rest of the year, strange, muffled thumps and slitherings echoed from the Whateleys’ sealed-off second story, even when every member of the family was spotted loitering by the well or trundling up the rutted lane. Locals muttered darkly about how long it took them to butcher a cow, how the screams of their livestock went on for hours too long, and there was even talk once of sending a complaint to the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals—though nothing ever came of it. Dunwich folk never willingly drew outside eyes to their cursed valley, not when there were secrets far worse than animal cruelty buried in the gorges.

By 1923, Wilbur Whateley was ten years old, and no child at all. His jaw was dusted with coarse, dark beard, his voice was the gravelly boom of a fully grown man, his frame broad enough to heft a full-grown steer over his shoulders without strain. That year, the old farmhouse thundered with carpentry work, all of it hidden behind the locked doors of the sealed upper floor. When splinters of discarded pine and rotten lath drifted down the lane, neighbors pieced together what the Whateleys had done: they’d ripped out every partition in the upstairs, torn the attic floor clean away, and left nothing but one vast, gaping, lightless hollow stretching from the first-floor ceiling all the way to the sharp peak of the farmhouse roof. They’d torn down the massive central chimney too, rigging the old rusted kitchen range to a flimsy tin pipe that jutted out the side of the wall, one that rattled and hummed at odd hours, as if something deep in that void overhead was breathing against it.

That next spring, Old Whateley started muttering about the whippoorwills. Hundreds of the small dark birds poured out of Cold Spring Glen after dark, swarming the farmhouse windowsills, their chirps lined up perfectly to match the wheeze in the old man’s chest. He treated it like the most sacred of omens, cackling around his plug tobacco as he loitered on the bench outside Osborn’s general store.
“They whistle jest in tune with my breathin’ now,” he told the other farmers, yellow crumbs of tobacco stuck in his cracked lips, “and I reckon they’re gittin’ ready to catch my soul. They know it’s slipping out, and they don’t mean to miss it. You boys will know if they got me, after I’m gone. If they do, they’ll sing and cackle right up till dawn. If they don’t, they’ll hush right down fast. Reckon them and the souls they hunt put up one hell of a fight sometimes.”

On Lammas Night, 1924, Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was wrenched out of his warm bed at two in the morning, summoned by Wilbur Whateley, who’d lashed his family’s last remaining workhorse so hard its sides streaked with blood, galloping three miles to the village to use Osborn’s only telephone. When he arrived at the Whateley farm, Old Whateley was at death’s door, his heart galloping erratically, his breath coming in gurgling, stertorous gasps. The shapeless albino Lavinia huddled by the bedside, and Wilbur, already looming well over six feet tall, stood at his grandfather’s other side. From that vast lightless void overhead, there came a soft, sickening rhythm: the lapping, surging shift of something huge and wet moving back and forth, the sound of waves dragging across a flat, sunless beach.
But what made the doctor’s skin crawl was the noise outside: a legion of whippoorwills, thousands strong, their cries perfectly, diabolically synced to every wheeze that tore out of the dying old man’s throat. There was nothing natural about it, nothing that belonged in the world of men.

Around one o’clock, Old Whateley’s eyes flew open. He grabbed Wilbur’s wrist with cold, crumbly fingers, choking out words no sane man could parse.
“More space, Willy, more space soon. You grow—but that thing grows faster. It’ll be ready to serve you soon, boy. Open the gates to Yog-Sothoth with the long chant you’ll find on page 751 of the complete edition—and then put a match to the prison. Fire from earth can’t burn it, nohow.”
He was stark mad. Outside, the flock of birds adjusted their cries to match the slower pace of his gasps, and the low, familiar rumble of Sentinel Hill drifted up from the dark.
“Feed it regular, Willy, mind the quantity—but don’t let it grow too fast for the walls. If it busts its quarters and gets out before you open the gates to Yog-Sothoth, it’s all wasted. Only the things from beyond can make it multiply, make it work. Only the old ones, the ones that want to come back.”
Then speech gave way to ragged gasps again, and Lavinia screamed as the whippoorwills shifted their rhythm once more to follow. An hour later, the old man’s throat rattled for the final time. Dr. Houghton drew his shrunken lids over the glazing gray of his eyes, and all at once, the tumult of ten thousand whippoorwills faded to total silence. Lavinia sobbed, but Wilbur only chuckled, a low, heavy bass sound, as faint hill noises rumbled in the distance.
“They didn’t get him,” he muttered.

By that point, Wilbur was a scholar of terrifying, lopsided erudition, corresponding in fluent Latin and Arabic with librarians at Miskatonic University and other far-off institutions that locked their most cursed forbidden books behind vault doors. More and more hikers and tramps vanished in the hills around his farm, no bodies ever found, and though every soul in Dunwich suspected him, no one dared press inquiries. He paid for a steady stream of stolen cattle with the same hoard of ancient, worn gold his grandfather had used, and the threat of his unnameable wrath was enough to silence anyone who might have talked. By 1925, when a horrified Miskatonic librarian visited his farm and fled back to Arkham pale and stammering, Wilbur stood six and three-quarters feet tall, and showed no sign of stopping his growth.

He treated his albino mother Lavinia with more and more unmasked contempt as the years went on, finally forbidding her from accompanying him to the hilltop sabbats on May Eve and Hallowmass. In 1926, the poor broken woman showed up at Mamie Bishop’s cabin, sobbing, and confessed she was terrified of her own son.
“There’s things about him I know that I can’t tell you, Mamie,” she whimpered, “and these days there’s things about him even I don’t know. I swear before God, I don’t know what he wants, what he’s trying to do.”
That Hallowe’en, the fires burned on Sentinel Hill as usual, the hill rumbles louder than anyone had ever heard them. But the whole valley’s attention was fixed on the whippoorwills: unnaturally late, swarming the Whateley farmhouse by the thousands, a full month past when they should have migrated south. After midnight, their chirps burst into a pandemoniac, unholy cackling that no bird could ever produce, a sound that curdled the blood of every farmer listening behind his locked door. Not until dawn did they fall silent, vanishing all at once, streaking south a full month overdue. No one in the valley seemed to have died that night—but twisted, half-sightless Lavinia Whateley was never seen again. No tracks, no scraps of her tattered wool shawl, no trace of her remained.

The summer of 1927, Wilbur repaired two rotting sheds in his farmyard, and dragged all his thousands of rare, mold-eaten books out of the main house to store them there. Not long after, Earl Sawyer told the loiterers at Osborn’s that more carpentry was echoing from the Whateley farmhouse. Wilbur had boarded up and locked every door and window on the ground floor, tearing out all the partitions down there just as he and his grandfather had torn out the upstairs four years prior. He lived in one of the sheds, pale and trembling, as if he feared the thing sealed inside the farmhouse would break through the walls at any moment. His height had already stretched past seven feet, and he was still growing. No local dared venture within half a mile of his property now, and the low, constant thudding that drifted from the Whateley farm after dark was louder than anything Dunwich had ever heard.

That bitter, bone-deep winter brought a spectacle no less grotesque than Wilbur Whateley’s very first step outside Dunwich’s gnarled, hill-bound borders. For months, he had barraged every major library on the continent—Harvard’s Widener, the Bibliothèque Nationale in Paris, the British Museum, the University of Buenos Aires, and closest of all, Miskatonic University’s own archives in Arkham—with smudged, claw-scrawled letters, begging for a loan of a book no librarian with half a wit would ever let leave their locked vaults. When all official channels failed, Wilbur set out in person: shaggy beard matted with pine sap, coat caked in Dunwich mud and the unnameable dark residue that clung to his farm’s walls, his thick Dunwich dialect rougher than the granite of the hills he’d walked his whole life. Nearly eight feet tall, stooped even at his full height to avoid brushing his head against low tree boughs, he carried a cheap new valise swiped off the shelf at Osborn’s general store, its canvas still stinking of factory starch. This dark, goatish gargoyle of a man stumbled into Arkham one gray, sleet-laden morning, single-mindedly hunting the volume chained under triple lock at Miskatonic’s special collections: the infamous *Necronomicon* of the mad Arab Alhazred, in Olaus Wormius’ 17th-century Spanish-printed Latin translation.

He had never laid eyes on a city before, but he did not pause to gawk at the steeples or the storefront windows. He moved straight for the university grounds, not even flinching when the great brindle mastiff chained to the library steps—an animal that never so much as growled at tramps or stray cats—erupted in a frenzy of unnatural, terrified fury. The beast ripped at its stout iron chain until its gums bled, froth flying from its jaws, screaming a high, guttural wail no dog’s throat should have been able to form, as if it could scent the thing beneath Wilbur’s skin that was not human.

Wilbur had brought his grandfather’s heirloom, a tattered, incomplete copy of Dr. Dee’s 16th-century English translation of the cursed text, its pages stiff with old, dark, unidentifiable viscous stain. When the curator, erudite Henry Armitage (A.M. Miskatonic, Ph.D. Princeton, Litt. D. Johns Hopkins), granted him access to the Latin volume, Wilbur fell immediately to collating the two, hunting a specific passage that had been torn clean from the 751st page of his own defective copy. He could not hide that much from Armitage, who had visited the Whateley farm years prior and knew full well the stench of unholiness that clung to the Whateley name. He admitted, when pressed, that he was chasing a formula, an incantation that bore the unspeakable name *Yog-Sothoth*—and the discrepancies, duplicated lines, and ambiguous scribbles between his broken copy and the complete Latin version were making it impossible to parse the exact words he needed.

As Wilbur scribbled furiously in a notebook bound in what looked like tanned goat hide, Armitage glanced involuntarily over his shoulder at the open text. The ink on the left-hand page seemed to squirm, black as stagnant marsh muck, as he translated its lines in his head, and a chill colder than any New England winter draft snaked down his spine:
> Nor is it to be thought that man is either the oldest or the last of earth's masters, or that the common bulk of life and substance walks alone. The Old Ones were, the Old Ones are, and the Old Ones shall be. Not in the spaces we know, but *between* them. They walk serene and primal, undimensioned and to us unseen. *Yog-Sothoth* knows the gate. *Yog-Sothoth* is the gate. *Yog-Sothoth* is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, future, all are one in *Yog-Sothoth*. He knows where the Old Ones broke through of old, and where They shall break through again. He knows where They have trod earth's fields, and where They still tread them, and why no one can behold Them as They tread. By Their smell can men sometimes know Them near, but of Their semblance can no man know, *saving only in the features of those They have begotten on mankind*; and of those are there many sorts, differing in likeness from man's truest eidolon to that shape without sight or substance which is *They*. They walk unseen and foul in lonely places where the Words have been spoken and the Rites howled through at their Seasons. The wind gibbers with Their voices, and the earth mutters with Their consciousness. They bend the forest and crush the city, yet may not forest or city behold the hand that smites. Kadath in the cold waste hath known Them, and what man knows Kadath? The ice desert of the South and the sunken isles of Ocean hold stones whereon Their seal is engraven, but who hath seen the deep frozen city or the sealed tower long garlanded with seaweed and barnacles? Great Cthulhu is Their cousin, yet can he spy Them only dimly. *Iä Shub-Niggurath!* As a foulness shall ye know Them. Their hand is at your throats, yet ye see Them not; and Their habitation is even one with your guarded threshold. *Yog-Sothoth* is the key to the gate, whereby the spheres meet. Man rules now where They ruled once; They shall soon rule where man rules now. After summer is winter, and after winter summer. They wait patient and potent, for here shall They reign again.

Armitage’s vision swam for a second. He had spent decades hearing the wild, whispered tales of Dunwich’s brooding hill presences, of Wilbur’s dubious birth and the quiet rumors that he had done away with his own mother months prior. The eight-foot giant hunched over the text before him no longer looked like a weird backwoods hick. He looked like spawn dragged up from a black gulf beyond time and space, half-human at best, tethered to horrors no mortal mind was meant to name.

Presently Wilbur lifted his head, and his voice rumbled out far too deep, thrumming with faint, unhuman bleats that did not belong to human speech. “Mr. Armitage,” he said, “I calc'late I've got to take that book home. They's things in it I've got to try under sarten conditions that I can't git here, an' it 'ud be a mortal sin to let red tape hold me up. Let me take it along, sir, an' I'll swear no one will never know the difference. I dun't need to tell ye I'll take good keer of it. It wa'n't me that put this Dee copy in the shape it is...."

He cut himself off when he saw the hard, unyielding refusal on Armitage’s face, and his goatish features twisted into something crafty, almost hungry. Armitage half-opened his mouth to offer permission to copy whatever passages Wilbur needed, before a cold, blinding surge of dread froze the words on his tongue. He could not hand a thing like Whateley the literal key to tear a hole between dimensions. That level of responsibility was more than any mortal could bear.

Whateley read the answer on his face, and shrugged, feigning lightness that did not reach his amber, goat-slitted eyes. “Wal, all right, ef ye feel that way abaout it. Maybe Harvard wun't be so fussy as yew be.” He did not wait for a reply, rising to his full height and stooping so low he nearly crawled through every doorway he passed on the way out.

Armitage heard the mastiff’s terrified wail cut off abruptly, and watched Wilbur’s gorilla-like lope carry him across the snow-dusted campus, his boot prints leaving strange, extra-toed indentations he would never mistake for a human’s. The old Dunwich tales flooded back: the *Advertiser*’s old Sunday scare stories, the half-mad rants from farmers he’d spoken to on his one trip to the valley. Unseen things not of earth—certainly not of the three-dimensional earth humans knew—rushed fetid and horrible through New England’s glens, brooding obscenely on the tops of the hills. Armitage had long suspected as much, but now he could feel that horror breathing right down his neck, advancing fast, feeding on the black rites Wilbur was performing in that rotting farmhouse on the edge of Sentinel Hill.

He locked the *Necronomicon* back in its lead-lined vault with a shudder of revulsion, but the stench Wilbur had left behind clung to the reading room for three full days: a reek of turned loam, brimstone, and something rotten that had never seen sunlight. “As a foulness shall ye know them,” he quoted to himself, and his skin crawled. It was the exact same stench that had made him sick to his stomach at the Whateley farm three years prior. He laughed bitterly, thinking of the dumb village rumors that Wilbur’s freakish form was just the product of generations of careless inbreeding. “Inbreeding?” he muttered half-aloud. “Great God, what simpletons! Show them Arthur Machen's *Great God Pan* and they'll think it a common Dunwich scandal! But what thing—what cursed shapeless influence on or off this three-dimensioned earth—was Wilbur Whateley's father? Born on Candlemas—nine months after May Eve of 1912, when the talk about the queer earth noises reached clear to Arkham—what walked on the mountains that May Night? What Roodmas horror fastened itself on the world in half-human flesh and blood?"

Over the weeks that followed, Armitage poured every waking hour into compiling data on Wilbur Whateley and the formless, roiling presences haunting Dunwich’s upper valleys. He corresponded with Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury, who had attended Old Whateley on his deathbed, and pored over the old man’s last raving words, full of talk of Old Ones rising and the gate opening soon. His visit to Dunwich village yielded little new—all the locals crossed themselves and shut their doors in his face rather than speak of the Whateleys—but his deep dive into the exact passages of the *Necronomicon* Wilbur had chased so desperately laid bare a terrible, unmistakeable blueprint for the end of the world. His consultations with other archaic lore scholars in Boston and across the world slowly curdled his faint unease into sharp, gnawing, acute spiritual terror. By the time summer crept over the Miskatonic valley, painting the edges of the rhododendron blooms a strange, bruised black, Armitage knew beyond any doubt that they could not wait. Something had to be done about the lurking terrors of the upper hills, and the monstrous half-human thing the world knew as Wilbur Whateley.

The Dunwich Horror proper unfurled between the Lammas harvest and the September equinox of 1928, and Dr. Henry Armitage counted himself among the first to witness its screaming, unholy prologue. By that point, he had already heard whispers of Wilbur Whateley’s lurching, grotesque trip down from his mountain hovel to Cambridge, of the half-bred hill thing’s frantic, rabid efforts to bully Widener Library staff into letting him borrow, or copy, the forbidden 17th-century Latin folio of the *Necronomicon*. Those efforts had been entirely in vain: Armitage had left explicit, fevered warnings for every librarian guarding that accursed volume, warnings written after he’d first glimpsed the depths of Wilbur’s unearthly curiosity, that no one would ever let the creature within three feet of pages that could tear the veil between our world and realms no human was meant to know.
Wilbur had jittered like a feral dog scented atrap the whole time he was in Cambridge: ragged coat hems clutched in gnarled, claw-tipped hands, half-crazed to get his hands on the book, but even more desperate to race back to Dunwich, as if an unseen, festering tether stretched straight from his chest back to that moldy Whateley farmhouse, ready to snap tight if he strayed too far from the thing he called his twin.

Early in August, that long-feared break finally came. In the small, pitch-black hours of August 3rd, Armitage was jolted straight out of sleep by the wild, tearing snarls of the campus mastiff — a dog so quiet he never so much as growled at wandering coyotes — his barks rising higher and sharper, laced with half-mad pain, broken by hideously long, dead silences that suggested something was halfway throttling him mid-bay.
Then a scream rang out from a throat that was no dog’s. It tore through Arkham’s quiet so sharp it slit the edge of every sleeping person’s dream, leaving them gasping upright in their beds, unable to place what sound could possibly twist living flesh that hard. Half the town would carry that echo behind their eyes for the rest of their lives, wake sweating at 2 a.m. for decades after, waiting to hear it scream again.

Armitage yanked on his wool dressing gown, shoved his feet into slippers, and raced across the dewy lawn to the library. Other townsfolk and students were already gathered there, the burglar alarm wailing so loud it made every tooth in your head rattle, and a second-story window gaped black and splintered wide in the moonlight. Whatever had crawled through that opening was already fully inside, and the mix of snarls and gurgling whimpers now fading to low growls was unmistakably coming from behind the brick walls.
Some primal instinct warned Armitage that no unsuspecting civilian should have their brain seared by the sight unfolding inside, so he waved the crowd back with sharp, unyielding authority as he unlocked the vestibule door. He spotted Professor Warren Rice and Dr. Francis Morgan — the two colleagues he’d already confided all his wild, unproven conjectures about Wilbur and the evil festering in Dunwich — and motioned for only them to follow him inside.
By then, the barks had softened to a low, watchful drone from the mastiff. Armitage’s blood turned to slush in his veins when he realized the whippoorwills nesting in the laurel bushes outside the library had started piping in perfect, unbroken rhythm: slow, counting off beats like a funeral march for a death they had been waiting to feast on for weeks. The heavy, cloying stench that hit him next he recognized all too well: that brimstone, sour reek of something that should not breathe the same air as earthly life, the same stench he had choked on during his only visit to the Whateley hovel.

The three men stumbled across the hall to the small genealogical reading room where the soft, doggy whine was coming from. For a long, taut second, none of them dared flip the light switch; then Armitage’s fumbling fingers found the plate, and the bulbs flared bright. One of the three (none of them later could say for certain who) shrieked aloud at what sprawled before them across overturned chairs and scattered papers. Rice swears he blacked out for a full, blank second, even though his feet never moved, his knees never buckled — his brain simply could not process what it was seeing fast enough to stay conscious.

The thing splayed half on its side in a puddle of sickly greenish-yellow ichor, thick and tarry, was nearly nine feet tall. The mastiff had torn every scrap of clothing off it, ripped great ragged chunks of skin away to get at the unearthly meat underneath. It was not quite dead, twitching in silent, spasming jolts, its chest heaving in monstrous, perfect sync with that mad, rhythmic piping of the waiting whippoorwills outside. Scraps of tattered canvas overalls, splintered shards of boot leather littered the floor; an empty rough burlap sack sat dumped just inside the window, evidently the thing Wilbur had brought to carry the *Necronomicon* away. A revolver lay discarded by the central desk, a dented, unfired round jammed in its cylinder — proof that whatever fumbling trigger finger the creature had could not even manage to pull the trigger before the mastiff hit it.

But the creature itself swallowed every other detail out of existence. It is not trite to say no earthly artist could ever render it accurately: no human whose idea of shape is bound strictly to three dimensions, whose idea of flesh is limited to life that evolved on this rock, could ever properly visualize its contours. It was partly human, beyond a doubt: large gnarled man-like hands, a narrow head, that gaunt, chinless, goatish face all the Whateleys carried in their blood. But the torso and lower half were teratologically unthinkable, the sort of fantasy made flesh that only yards and yards of loose, shapeless clothing could ever hide long enough to walk the roads of Dunwich unchallenged.
Above the waist it was semi-anthropomorphic, though the chest where the mastiff’s rending paws still rested bore the tough, reticulated, leathery hide of a crocodile. Its back was piebald, splotched yellow and black, scaled like a serpent’s, shifting subtly with every ragged breath. Below the waist, all traces of human resemblance vanished entirely. Thick coarse black fur matted with ichor covered its hips, and a score of long green-gray tentacles tipped with red, glistening sucking mouths protruded limply from its abdomen, arranged in a pattern that followed some cosmic geometry no mathematician on Earth had ever mapped. Deep in pink, ciliated, squirming sockets on each of its hips sat two half-formed, rudimentary eyes. Where a tail should have been, there hung a thick, trunk-like feeler ringed in purple, throbbing like an undeveloped throat gasping for air. Its legs, apart from the black fur covering them, roughly resembled the hind legs of prehistoric Earth’s giant saurians, ending in ridged, vein-padded feet that were neither hooves nor claws. When it breathed, its tail and tentacles shifted color, pulsing with the non-human blood of its cosmic ancestry: the tentacles darkened to a deep sick green, the tail blotted bright yellow between its purple rings, fading back to a dead, moldy gray-white. It bore no drop of true red human blood at all, only that fetid greenish-yellow ichor that trickled across the painted floor, leaving permanent, unscratchable stains no janitor could ever erase.

The men’s presence roused the dying thing. It did not turn its head, did not lift its gaze to look at them, only began to mumble, gurgling out syllables that did not belong to any language Armitage had studied in decades of archival work — no Latin, no Greek, no dead tribal tongue known to man. Toward the very last, the disjointed fragments cleared, ripped straight from the blasphemous pages of the *Necronomicon* it had died trying to steal: “_N’gai, n’gha’ghaa, bugg-shoggog, y’hah; Yog-Sothoth, Yog-Sothoth...._” The words trailed off into a wet rattle, and outside the whippoorwills shrieked in a rising, unholy crescendo, as if cheering the death they had smelled coming for miles.

The gasping stopped. The mastiff lifted his head and let out a long, lugubrious howl that shook the window panes. A hideous change came over the yellow goatish face of the prostrate thing, its great black eyes sinking in appallingly like rotting fruit. Outside, the whippoorwill piping cut off dead at once, and above the murmur of the gathering crowd, there came a vast roaring of thousands of wings. Against the moon, great roiling clouds of the feathery night watchers surged upward and fled for the hills, frantic as if they had just realized they had not been circling prey at all.
All at once the dog jolted to his feet, yelped in unmistakeable terror, and leaped nervously back out the open window he had entered by. A cry went up from the crowd, and Armitage shouted to the men outside that no one was to be admitted until the police or medical examiner arrived. Thankful the windows were set too high for any bystander to peer in, he drew every heavy dark curtain carefully down over the glass.
Two policemen arrived minutes later, and Dr. Morgan met them in the vestibule, urging them for their own sakes not to enter the stench-choked reading room until the examiner came and the sprawling thing on the floor could be draped out of sight.

By then, unnameable changes were already taking place on the painted boards. There is no need to describe the speed, the grotesque wrongness of the shrinkage and disintegration unfolding before Armitage and Rice’s eyes — suffice it to say that beyond the goaty face and gnarled, man-like hands, the truly human part of Wilbur Whateley must have been infinitesimally small. By the time the medical examiner pushed open the reading room door, nothing remained of the nine-foot horror but a sticky, whitish slime spread across the floor, and the monstrous alien stench had nearly dissipated. Wilbur Whateley had never had a proper skull, never a stable, bony skeleton, nothing to hold the shape of a man the way earthly flesh is meant to. He had taken far, far more after his unknown, unnameable father than anyone on this planet had ever dared to suspect.

All that odd, unsettling business after Wilbur Whateley’s violent death at Miskatonic University? It was nothing but the opening act.

Bewildered county officials dragged their feet through empty formalities, buried every too-strange detail from the press and public, and dispatched messengers to comb Dunwich and Aylesbury for any possible heirs to the late half-human hermit’s scattered property. The whole countryside was already fraying at the seams: low, thrumming rumblings rolled up out of the domed hills at all hours of day and night, and a thick, acrid stench clung to the air for miles around the Whateley farmhouse, its boarded-up shell groaning with slow, surging, lapping sounds no one could bear to investigate long. Earl Sawyer, the hired hand who tended Wilbur’s cattle during his trip to Arkham, had been reduced to a jittering wreck, jumping at every creak of the barn boards. The officials kept inventing flimsy excuses not to pry open the nailed-shut doors of the Whateley house, and were glad enough to limit their inspection of Wilbur’s living quarters—the newly patched, reeking sheds at the edge of the property—to a single hasty visit. They filed a bloated, vague report at the Aylesbury courthouse, and to this day endless litigation drags on between dozens of scattered, half-mad Whateley descendants spread across the upper Miskatonic valley. A massive ledger, filled end to end with indecipherable looping strange characters, was found on the rickety old bureau Wilbur used as a desk; officials labeled it a diary on account of its spaced entries and shifting ink and penmanship, and after a week of bickering, shipped it and Wilbur’s whole collection of unholy rare books up to Miskatonic University for study and translation. Even the school’s most seasoned linguists quickly realized they would never crack its secrets easily. No one has ever found a trace of the ancient, heavy gold Wilbur and old Whateley used to pay their debts, the coins no local bank could identify.

The real horror broke loose in the pitch dark of September ninth. The hill rumblings had risen to a fever pitch all evening, and every dog within ten miles barked so hard their throats went raw, nonstop through the small hours. Early risers on the tenth woke to a sharp, metallic stench hanging thick in the air. Around seven that morning, Luther Brown, the teenage hired boy at George Corey’s farm between Cold Spring Glen and the village, came sprinting back from driving the cows out to Ten-Acre Meadow, wild-eyed and nearly frothing at the mouth. He stumbled into the kitchen convulsed with fright, and the whole herd followed hard on his heels, lowing pitifully and tearing up the yard turf with their hooves, every last cow as panicked as he was. Between gasping sobs, he stammered his story to Mrs. Corey:
“Up thar past the glen, Mis’ Corey—there’s something been roaming the road! It smells like burnt thunder, an’ every bush and small tree is pushed flat back from the gravel like a whole house got dragged along the path. That ain’t the worst, neither. There are *prints*, Mis’ Corey—great round prints as big as barrel heads, sunk so deep in the mud they’d make an elephant look tiny, an’ there’s way more than four of ’em. I only looked at two or three before I ran, an’ every one of ’em’s covered in lines spreading out from the center, like giant palm-leaf fans twice or three times bigger than any you’ve ever seen got pounded straight down into the dirt. The smell was awful, just like the stench hanging around old Wizard Whateley’s place…”

He faltered and shivered so hard his teeth chattered, too spooked to get another word out. Mrs. Corey grabbed the party line telephone to alert all the neighbors, and that was how the first wave of panic rolled through Dunwich. When she reached Sally Sawyer, the housekeeper at Seth Bishop’s farm, the property closest to the Whateley homestead, she found Sally was already screaming for help down the wire. Sally’s son Chauncey, a light sleeper who’d been up half the night, had crept up the hill toward the Whateley place at dawn, only to come tearing back a minute later, frozen with terror.
“Yes, Mis’ Corey,” Sally’s voice shook over the line, “Chauncey just got back a-running, he can barely talk he’s that scared! He says old Whateley’s whole farmhouse is blown clean open, timbers scattered all over the yard like someone set dynamite off inside. The bottom floor didn’t fall through, but it’s all coated in a tarry black gunk that reeks to high heaven and drips off the broken planks onto the dirt below. There are terrible marks all over the yard, too—great round prints bigger than a hogshead, all sticky with that same black slime. They lead straight out into the meadows, where there’s a swath wider than a whole barn matted flat, and every old field stone wall in its path is toppled like it was made of dry twigs.
“He says he went looking for Seth’s cows, even scared as he was, and found them in the upper pasture near the Devil’s Hop Yard in the worst shape you ever saw. Half of them are gone, no trace left at all, and almost all the rest are sucked nearly dry of blood, covered in the same strange sores Whateley’s cattle had ever since Lavinia gave birth to that black little brat Wilbur. Seth’s out there right now checking on them, but I vow he won’t dare go anywhere near Whateley’s ruins! Chauncey didn’t wait to see where that big flattened swath went after it left the pasture, but he swears it points straight down the road toward the village, toward Cold Spring Glen.
“I tell ye, Mis’ Corey, there’s something loose out there that never should’ve gotten free. That half-human black Wilbur Whateley, he got the ugly end he deserved, but he and his grandpa summoned that thing in that nailed-up house, and it’s even less human than he was. Dunwich’s always had unseen things roaming around—living things that ain’t human, and ain’t good for folks. The ground was talking all last night, and toward dawn Chauncey heard whippoorwills screaming so loud in Cold Spring Glen he couldn’t sleep a wink. Then he heard another faint noise over toward Whateley’s place—some kind of ripping and tearing of wood, like a giant crate getting pried open a mile off. He never got back to sleep till sunup, and as soon as it was light he had to go check what was going on. He saw more than enough, I tell ye. This ain’t no good. We oughta round up every man in the village right now to go do something about it. Did your Luther see where those big tracks lead? No? Well, if they were on the glen road this side of the hollow and haven’t reached your place yet, they must’ve gone right down into the glen itself. That’s exactly what they’d do. I’ve always said Cold Spring Glen ain’t no healthy, decent place. The whippoorwills and fireflies down there never acted like God’s creatures, and folks say you can hear strange things rushing and whispering in the air between the rock falls and Bear’s Den, if you stand in the right spot.”

By noon that day, three-quarters of every man and boy in Dunwich were tramping between the smoldering Whateley ruins and Cold Spring Glen, staring in horrified awe at the vast, monstrous prints, the mangled Bishop cattle, the reeking tarry wreck of the Whateley house, and the flattened, bruised vegetation across every field and roadside. Whatever had broken free had definitely crawled down into the dark, forbidding ravine: every tree on the glen banks was bent and snapped clean in two, and a huge gaping avenue had been gouged straight through the almost-vertical cliffside underbrush, like an entire house had slid down the slope in an avalanche. No sound rose from the glen depths, only a thick, unplaceable fetor that burned the back of every throat. No one wanted to be the first to climb down into that dark hollow to face whatever Cyclopean horror was hiding there. The three farm dogs that had come with the crowd had barked furiously at first, but now they cowered low to the ground, whimpering and refusing to step a foot closer to the edge. Someone called the *Aylesbury Transcript* to tell the editor what was happening, but the man, used to silly tall tales from the backwoods of Dunwich, just wrote up a silly mocking paragraph about it, and the item got picked up for a tiny throwaway bit by the Associated Press, no one taking it seriously.

That night every family tramped home, and every house and barn was barricaded as tight as nails and heavy planks could make it. No one dared leave any cattle out in the open pastures. Around two in the morning, a suffocating stench seeped under the doors of Elmer Frye’s farm on the eastern edge of Cold Spring Glen, and the savage, panicked barking of the dogs woke the whole household. Everyone there heard a muffled swishing, lapping sound drifting in from the dark yard. Mrs. Frye reached for the telephone to call the neighbors, and Elmer was just about to agree, when a great crash of splintering wood cut them off. It came from the barn, followed by hideous, screaming stamping from the trapped cattle. The farm dogs slavered and crawled to huddle at the family’s feet, too terrified to make a sound. Elmer grabbed his lantern out of habit, but he knew it was a death sentence to step outside into that pitch-black yard. The children and women whimpered, and some old primal instinct of survival kept them from screaming, telling them the thing outside would hear if they made a sound. At last the cattle’s screams faded to faint, pitiful moans, and a great cracking, crashing racket echoed through the dark as the thing dragged itself away. The Fryes huddled in their sitting room, too scared to move, until the last echoes died away far down in Cold Spring Glen. Then, over the faint whimpering of the injured cattle in the stable and the demon piping of whippoorwills in the glen, Selina Frye staggered to the telephone to spread word of the horror’s second rampage.

The next day the whole countryside was in full panic. Groups of men wandered the farm roads, quiet and cowed, staring at the two new massive swaths of destruction that stretched straight from the glen to the Frye farmyard, the monstrous prints scattered across every bare patch of dirt, and the entire cave-in of one side of the old red barn. Of all the cattle in the barn, only a quarter could be found and identified: some were torn into strange unrecognizable fragments, and every animal that still lived was so mangled they had to be shot. Earl Sawyer suggested they send for help from Aylesbury or Arkham, but the rest of the men said it would do no good. Old Zebulon Whateley, from a branch of the family that was only half steeped in madness, spouted wild dark theories about old forgotten rites they ought to perform up on the hilltops. He came from a line where the old unholy traditions ran deep, and he still remembered the sound of chants echoing around the standing stone circles from his youth, rites that predated Wilbur and his grandfather’s.

Darkness fell over a stricken countryside that was too disorganized and exhausted to mount any real defense. A few groups of closely related families banded together to watch the dark from one shared house, but for the most part everyone just re-barricaded their doors like the night before, loaded their old muskets, and set pitchforks within easy reach, useless as they knew those small weapons would be. Nothing happened that night except the low rumbling of the hills, and when the sun came up half the villagers dared to hope the horror had vanished as fast as it had appeared. A few bold men suggested mounting an expedition down into the glen, but no one was willing to lead the way.

When night fell again, everyone barricaded their doors once more, though fewer families banded together for safety. At dawn, both the Frye and Seth Bishop households reported that their dogs had gone wild barking in the small hours, and that they’d smelled strange stenches drifting over from the glen. Early explorers found a fresh set of massive monstrous tracks running along the road skirting Sentinel Hill. Just like before, the sides of the road were bruised and flattened, marking the blasphemous, mountainous bulk of whatever had passed by, and the pattern of the tracks showed it had traveled the path twice: from the glen up to the hill, and back again. At the base of Sentinel Hill, a thirty-foot wide swath of crushed shrubbery and snapped saplings led straight up the steep slope, and the men gasped when they saw that the trail didn’t even deflect at the near-vertical sections of the cliff. The horror could scale a sheer, almost flat stone precipice like it was walking on level ground. When they climbed up to the hill’s summit by the safe, gradual paths, they saw the trail ended there, reversed at the top.

This was the spot the Whateleys used to build their unholy bonfires and chant their rites around the flat table-like standing stone on May Eve and Hallowmass. Now that very stone sat at the center of a vast circular space the mountainous horror had thrashed around in, its slightly concave top coated in a thick, fetid layer of that same tarry sticky gunk the men had found oozing from the floor of the ruined Whateley farmhouse. The men stared at each other, muttering, then looked back down the hill. The horror had slid back down the cliffside the same way it had climbed up. There was no point speculating. All reason, logic, and normal human ideas of what could be possible were completely shattered. Only old Zebulon, who wasn’t with the group that day, could have even tried to explain what they were seeing.

Thursday night started just like the others, but it ended far worse. The whippoorwills in the glen screamed so loud and nonstop that almost no one in Dunwich got any sleep, and around three in the morning every phone on the party line rang at once. The people who picked up the receivers heard a mad, screaming voice cry out, “Help, oh my Gawd!” then a great deafening crash, and then silence. No one dared to leave their houses in the pitch dark to go check. No one knew where the call came from until dawn, when the villagers called every household on the line and found no one answered at the Frye farm. An hour later, a hastily assembled group of armed men tramped out to the Frye property at the head of the glen. It was unspeakable, but no one was really surprised. There were more vast swaths of flattened vegetation, more monstrous prints, but no house. It had caved in like an empty eggshell, and there was nothing left in the ruins—no living thing, no dead body, no scraps of furniture, only the familiar thick stench and that black tarry stickiness coating every broken splinter. The whole Elmer Frye family had been erased completely from Dunwich.

The second, far quieter but infinitely more soul-churning phase of the nightmare was unspooling in the dark, sealed shelf-lined study at Miskatonic University in Arkham, where the walls reeked of mildewed vellum, old lamp oil, and a faint, unplaceable tang of brimstone no amount of airing could scrub away.

Wilbur Whateley’s strange manuscript-diary, left to the university for translation, had left every language expert on staff stumped and ill at ease. Its script, for all its superficial resemblance to the heavy, shadowed Mesopotamian Arabic scribes studied in the department, did not match any known writing system on Earth. The linguists finally concluded it was a purpose-built artificial alphabet, functioning as some kind of cipher—yet no standard cryptographic method turned up even a single crack in its defenses, no matter how many living and dead human tongues they used as a reference point. The grimoires seized from Whateley’s squalid farmhouse did nothing to help; one heavy, iron-clasped tome bore a second unrecognizable script, closer to Sanskrit than anything else, that made anyone who flipped its pages feel a cold prickle crawl up the back of their throat.

At last the whole of the Whateley diary was handed over to Dr. Henry Armitage, seventy-three years old, sharp of mind and steeped in decades of obscure mystical and linguistic scholarship, who had followed the grim trail of the Whateley family’s uncanny acts for years. Armitage suspected the alphabet traced back to esoteric forbidden cults, lineages of sorcerers who had inherited their most secret rites from the old Saracen wizards—but he cared little for the script’s origin, if his hunch held true: the bulk of the text was ciphered modern English. A document that long, he reasoned, would never have been written in anything but the author’s native tongue, save for scattered incantations and special formulae.

He spent all of late August sinking every spare hour into the accumulated lore of cryptography, poring night after night through the arcane pages of Trithemius’ *Poligraphia*, Giambattista Porta’s *De Furtivis Literarum Notis*, De Vigenere’s *Traité des Chiffres*, and every other ancient and modern cryptographic text he could lay his hands on. Bit by bit, he realized he was facing one of the subtlest, most ingenious ciphers ever devised: layered grids of corresponding letters, built around arbitrary key words only the initiated could know, clearly a system handed down through untold generations of occult practitioners, older than any modern code. He came close to cracking it a dozen times, only to hit an unforeseen, confounding wall every time—until the first days of September rolled around, and the fog of unknowing began to clear. Letters resolved sharp and unmistakeable, and he confirmed his hunch: the text was written in plain English, hidden beneath layers of code.

On the evening of September 2, the final barrier crumbled. Dr. Armitage read his first full, unbroken passage of Wilbur Whateley’s life’s record. It was exactly the diary they had all suspected, written in a strange, lurching style that mixed staggering, unholy erudition with the rough, uneducated phrasing of the remote Dunwich hills. The first long entry he deciphered, dated November 26, 1916, made his blood run cold. Wilbur had been three and a half years old when he wrote it, and already stood as tall and spoke with the grim weight of a boy of twelve or thirteen.
*Today learned the Aklo for the Sabaoth, which did not like, it being answerable from the hill and not from the air. That upstairs more ahead of me than I had thought it would be, and is not like to have much earth brain. Shot Elam Hutchins’s collie Jack when he went to bite me, and Elam says he would kill me if he dast. I guess he won’t. Grandfather kept me saying the Dho formula last night, and I think I saw the inner city at the 2 magnetic poles. I shall go to those poles when the earth is cleared off, if I can’t break through with the Dho-Hna formula when I commit it. They from the air told me at Sabbat that it will be years before I can clear off the earth, and I guess Grandfather will be dead then, so I shall have to learn all the angles of the planes and all the formulas between the Yr and the Nhhngr. They from outside will help, but they can not take body without human blood. That upstairs looks it will have the right cast. I can see it a little when I make the Yoorish sign or blow the power of Ibn Ghazi at it, and it is near like them at May Eve on the Hill. The other face may wear off some. I wonder how I shall look when the earth is cleared and there are no earth beings on it. He that came with the Aklo Sabaoth said I may be transfigured, there being much of outside to work on.*

Dawn found Armitage soaked through with cold terror sweat, too wired to sleep, his hands shaking as he turned page after page. He had telephoned his wife to say he would not come home, and when she brought him breakfast, he could barely choke down a single bite. He read straight through the day, pausing every so often to wrestle with the tangled key when a section of code slipped out of his grasp. Lunch and dinner arrived untouched, or nearly so. He drifted off in his armchair toward the middle of the next night, only to wake screaming from a tangle of nightmares every bit as hideous as the secrets he had unearthed, secrets that threatened the very existence of humanity.

On the morning of September 4, Professor Rice and Dr. Morgan forced their way into his study. They emerged an hour later, pale as ash, trembling so hard they could barely speak. Armitage went home to sleep that evening, but only in short, jittery bursts. By Wednesday he was back at his desk, scribbling page after page of notes, cross-referencing every line he had decoded. He napped a little in his office armchair in the small hours, and was back at the manuscript before the sun rose. His doctor, Hartwell, stopped by around noon to order him to stop working, but Armitage refused, saying the work was too vital to abandon, and promising a full explanation soon.

Twilight fell that evening just as he turned the final page of the diary. He sank back in his chair, completely drained, and when his wife brought him dinner he snapped a sharp, terrified warning to stop her when her eyes drifted to his scattered notes. He staggered to his feet, gathered every scrap of scribbled paper, sealed them all in a thick manila envelope, and stuffed it deep inside his coat pocket. He made it home under his own power, but was so clearly unwell that Hartwell was called immediately. As the doctor tucked him into bed, Armitage could only mumble the same line over and over: “But what, in God's name, can we do?”

He slept through the night, but wandered in and out of delirium the next day. He refused to explain himself to Hartwell, but in his lucid stretches he insisted Rice and Morgan be summoned for an urgent conference. His fevered outbursts were wild enough to curdle the blood: he screamed that the thing in the boarded-up Whateley farmhouse had to be destroyed, that an ancient, elder race from beyond our dimension planned to wipe every trace of human, animal, and plant life from the face of the Earth, to tear the whole planet out of the solar system and drag it back to some nameless, non-material plane it had been exiled from vigintillions of eons before. He shouted for the *Necronomicon* and Remigius’ *Dæmonolatreia*, begging for some formula powerful enough to stop the oncoming doom. “Stop them, stop them!” he would cry. “Those Whateleys meant to let them in, and the worst of all is left! Tell Rice and Morgan we must do something—it's a blind business, but I know how to make the powder.... It hasn't been fed since the second of August, when Wilbur came here to his death, and at that rate....”

His strong seventy-three-year-old constitution carried him through, and he slept off the delirium that night without developing a dangerous fever. He woke late Friday, his mind clear, but heavy with a cold, gnawing dread and a terrible, unshakable weight of responsibility. On Saturday afternoon he made his way back to the library, summoned Rice and Morgan, and the three of them debated and speculated long into the night. They hauled armfuls of obscure, forbidden grimoires from the locked vault stacks, copying page after page of diagrams and formulae with feverish, shaking hands. There was no trace of skepticism left between them. All three had seen Wilbur Whateley’s half-alien, gory corpse splayed out on the floor of that very library, and none of them could dismiss the diary as the raving of a madman.

They debated notifying the Massachusetts State Police, and ultimately voted against it. There was no way any man who had not seen a sliver of that unearthly horror could believe what they had to say, and the police would only lock them up as lunatics and get themselves killed for their trouble. The conference broke up late that night without a concrete plan, but Armitage spent all Sunday cross-referencing formulae and mixing strange, acrid compounds in the college chemistry lab. The more he thought about the thing Wilbur Whateley had left behind in the hills, the more he doubted any ordinary physical weapon could harm it—the earth-shaking, ravenous entity that they did not know would break free of the Whateley homestead within hours, to become the unspeakable Dunwich Horror.

Armitage spent Monday the same way, digging deeper into the diary’s notes, tweaking his plans as new details emerged. He knew there would be no perfect solution, no guarantee their work would work. By Tuesday he had a full course of action mapped out, and planned to leave for Dunwich within the week.

Then, on Wednesday, the great shock landed. Tucked in an obscure corner of the *Arkham Advertiser* was a facetious Associated Press blurb, joking that the moonshine brewed by Dunwich hillbillies was so strong it made locals hallucinate a giant, record-breaking monster that could step over the top of Sentinel Hill. Armitage went half-stunned, and immediately telephoned Rice and Morgan. They discussed the horror far into the night, and the next day was a whirlwind of frantic, desperate preparation. Armitage knew he was about to meddle with forces no human was meant to touch—but he saw clearly there was no other way to undo the deeper, far more malign meddling the Whateleys and their unearthly patrons had set in motion decades before.

The sun hung bleached and sickly over Dunwich at 1 p.m. that Friday, when Armitage, Rice, and Morgan pulled their motor car to a stop outside Osborn’s general store. The three Arkham academics had left their well-lit, book-lined offices at dawn, but the weight of the region’s ill omen settled over them before they’d even spotted the first of the hunchbacked, domed hills. Even in full midday light, the deep ravines gaped like unblinking shadowed mouths, and the jagged circles of standing stones perched on the highest crests jutted against the pale sky like fragments of some long-broken blasphemous altar.

The second they stepped into Osborn’s store, they could taste the hushed, wide-eyed terror hanging thick in the air. No one met their gaze directly, and the few muttered words they got told them the full of the horror: Elmer Frye, his wife, and all three of their children were gone. The afternoon blurred into a parade of mounting, cold dread as they drove the rutted back roads of the valley, questioning skittish natives and staring at the unholy wreckage left in the monster’s wake: the Frye home collapsed into splinters reeking of tarry, alien stickiness, barrel-sized prints sunk six inches into packed yard mud, Seth Bishop’s half-flayed cattle lowing in their pasture with wounds no earthly claw could tear, and wide swathes of forest where every pine and oak had been snapped clean in half like dry straw. Armitage traced the broad, crushed path leading up and down Sentinel Hill, then stood staring for long minutes at that flat, weathered altar-stone on its summit, as if he could see the ancient evil that had knelt there, waiting, for generations.

It was only then that they learned five State Police troopers had driven in from Aylesbury that morning, answering the first frantic calls about the Frye massacre. No one could find them. Their car sat empty by the Frye ruins, doors hanging open, half-smoked cigarettes still smoldering on the dash, no sign of a struggle. The natives’ confusion curdled into new, sharper terror when old Sam Hutchins went bone white, nudged Fred Farr hard in the ribs, and pointed a shaking finger at the dark, damp maw of Cold Spring Glen, so deep the midday sun never touched its floor. “Gawd,” he gasped, “I telled ’em not ter go daown there. I never thought nobody’d dew it, not with them tracks, that smell, the whippoorwills a-screechin’ daown in there even in full daylight.” A cold, windless shudder rippled through every person gathered there, and every head tilted back unconsciously, as if straining to hear the low, heavy breathing they swore was echoing up from the glen’s hidden depths.

Armitage’s hands shook as he realized the full weight of the responsibility resting on his shoulders. Night was coming fast, the hour the mountainous, unseen blasphemy lumbered out on its unholy path. *Negotium perambulans in tenebris*… he mouthed the old Necronomicon line he’d memorized months prior, clutching the crumpled sheet of paper with the alternative rites he’d not yet committed to memory, and fumbled to confirm his flashlight still worked. Rice hauled a heavy metal insecticide sprayer out of their valise, loaded with the strange, iridescent reagent they’d brewed back at Miskatonic, and Morgan unlatched the action of his big-game rifle, ignoring Armitage’s quiet warning that no earthly bullet could kill a thing that had no business existing in this dimension at all. Armitage had read Wilbur Whateley’s hideous diary cover to cover, he knew exactly what they were facing, but he said nothing to stoke the Dunwich villagers’ panic further. He hoped they could destroy the thing before the wider world ever learned the apocalyptic horror it had narrowly escaped.

As the sun dipped below the hills, the locals scattered for their cabins, slamming their doors and bolting them tight, even though every one of them had seen the creature crush a whole house as easily as a man crumples a paper bag. They shook their heads at the three academics’ plan to stand guard by the glen, none of them bothering to voice their certainty that the three would be torn to pieces before dawn.

That night, the hills rumbled like a living thing shifting under the ground, and the whippoorwills’ screams never once fell silent. Every few minutes, a gust of wind billowing out of Cold Spring Glen carried a thick, oily, unnameable stench, the exact fetor the three men had breathed when they stood over Wilbur Whateley’s half-uncloaked, dying body on the Miskatonic library floor. But the monster never emerged. Armitage told his companions venturing down into the glen in the dark was a death sentence, that they could never fight what they could not see in that pitch-black, unhallowed gully.

Dawn came wan and gray, drizzle drizzling down from a sky stacked heavy with roiling black clouds piling up over the northwest hills. The three men huddled for shelter under the only intact Frye outbuilding, debating their next move – wait the storm out, or march down into the glen to hunt their nameless quarry? Thunder boomed far off, then sheet lightning bleached the sky white, and a forking bolt stabbed straight down into the depths of Cold Spring Glen. The sky turned so dark it felt like twilight in mid-morning, and for a minute they thought the storm would pass quickly.

Then they heard it: a dozen villagers running screaming down the road, sobbing, half out of their minds, their clothes torn and caked in mud. The first man in the lead collapsed to his knees, gasping out the words that made Armitage and his friends go rigid: “Oh Gawd, it’s a-goin’ agin, an’ this time by day! It’s out, it’s movin’ right now, the Lord alone knows when it’ll tear through all of us.”

The tale poured out, disjointed and hysterical: Luther the hired hand had been chasing the cows out of the storm after the lightning strike, when he’d seen every single tree at the mouth of the glen bend sideways at once, smelled that same unholy tar stench, and heard a swishing, lapping sound no earthly wind or beast could make. He’d heard the planks of the Bishop’s Brook bridge creak, strain, and snap in two, and when he’d run back to check the mud, the rain hadn’t yet washed away the same barrel-sized prints he’d found on the road the Monday prior. Then the whole valley had been on the party line, listening when Seth Bishop’s housekeeper Sally called screaming: the trees by his road were bending for no reason, the dogs were howling so hard their throats bled, the out shed caved in like it was made of tissue paper, the picket fence crumpled into splinters, and something huge, invisible, kept slamming itself against the front walls of the Bishop house, over and over. Then there was a crash, a chorus of screams, and the line had gone dead. Not a sound after that. The villagers were sure it was divine judgment, no mortal thing could possibly stop it.

Armitage stepped forward, his voice steady and firm, reassuring the faltering crowd. “We can stop it,” he told them. “I’ve read Wilbur Whateley’s diary, I know the old rites the wizard used, rites that can banish this thing back to the spaces between the hills it crawled out of. That sprayer will make the invisible monster glow bright, just for long enough to finish the spell. This isn’t even half of what Wilbur would have unleashed on the whole world if he’d lived. We can’t hesitate.”

Earl Sawyer pointed out the shortest path to the Bishop ruins, cutting across the lower meadow, wading the brook, and climbing through the gnarled old timber lot at the foot of Sentinel Hill. The sun broke through the clouds halfway through their climb, but the ancient twisted trees around them loomed like groping hands, and the air grew thick with that familiar, unwholesome pressure. When they emerged on the far side of the treeline, the full ruin of the Bishop property lay before them: splintered rubble, no bodies, nothing but that clinging tarry stench, and a wide, clear path of crushed underbrush leading straight toward the rotting Whateley homestead, and up the slopes of Sentinel Hill.

Armitage handed his high-powered pocket telescope to Morgan, whose eyesight was sharper. After a second of staring, Morgan gasped, passing the glass to Earl Sawyer. It took the farmer a minute to focus the lenses, and when he did, his yell was raw, unhinged: “Gawd almighty! The grass and bushes is a-movin’! It’s crawlin’ slow up the hill, right now, heading straight for that altar stone on the summit. Heaven knows what it’s plannin’ to do up there.”

Panic pricked through the whole crowd of searchers. They had been chasing a story, a rumor – but now they could see the rippling path of the hundred-foot-tall, unseen monstrosity creeping ahead of them, huge, hungry, brimming with a malevolence no human had ever been meant to face. Every man there could feel it, that they were inches away from stepping over a line no sane person should ever cross, into the forbidden, unnameable spaces outside all human understanding.

The three men from Arkham moved up Sentinel Hill alone: white-bearded Dr. Armitage, his breath ragged in the thin high air, propped twice by his companions as the mud sucked greedily at his boots; stocky iron-gray Professor Rice, hefting the iron sprayer packed tight with the powdered Elder Sign compound they’d spent three months distilling; and lean, sharp-eyed Dr. Morgan, his knuckles white around the frayed leather spine of the forbidden grimoire. They left their field telescope with the jittery, white-faced crowd clustered on the rutted road below, walking them through every adjustment until they were confident the group could follow their progress.
The crowd tracked their every move, the lens passing from shaking hand to hand, as the great raw, flattened swath of uprooted shrubbery snaking up the slope heaved slow, deliberate, the unseen blasphemy that carved it moving at a snail’s pace, as if it knew they were coming and was toying with its prey. It was obvious the pursuers were gaining fast.
Curtis Whateley, from the untainted surviving branch of the Whateley bloodline, had the telescope pressed to his eye when the Arkham party veered hard away from the swath’s path. He called out to the rest that the three men were making for a narrow, jagged secondary peak that jutted out over the monster’s route, positioned to overlook the stretch of brush the thing would pass any second. He was right: the trio gained that small vantage barely a minute before the invisible entity slithered under the very spot, the air above it rippling like heat off an open furnace.
Wesley Corey snatched the lens next, and yelped that Rice was priming the sprayer, lining it up to fire the potent powder that was supposed to sear the horror’s invisibility right off it, strip away the concealing glamour and show it its true, hideous form. Half the crowd stirred uneasily, two or three clamping their eyes shut so tight their brows furrowed. Curtis wrenched the telescope back, straining his sight to the limit: the three men were perfectly positioned, directly behind and above the creature, with a clear, unobstructed shot.
The rest of the crowd without the glass only saw a single, fleeting flash of acrid gray mist, the size of a full barn, billowing near the mountain’s upper ridge. Curtis shrieked so raw his throat tore, the telescope slipping from his grasp to sink ankle-deep in the black stinking road mud. He reeled, and would have crumpled face-first into the muck if two local farmers hadn’t seized his arms to hold him up. All he could gurgle, half-sobbing, was “Oh, oh great Gawd... that... that...”
Pandemonium erupted around him. Only Henry Wheeler thought to dive for the fallen telescope, wiping the caked mud from its lens with his flannel shirt. Curtis was far past coherent for long stretches, hyperventilating so hard his chest heaved: “Bigger’n a whole barn... all made o’ squirmin’ thick ropes, all squished tight together... hull thing shaped like a hen’s egg bigger’n any structure ever stood, dozens o’ legs as thick as hogsheads, half-crumpling every time they step... nothin’ solid about it, all wobbly jelly, made o’ them writhing ropes pressed close... great bulging milky eyes every few inches all over its sides... ten or twenty trunks big as stovepipes stickin’ out, all tossin’ and openin’ and shuttin’ like they’re sniffin’ the air... all gray, streaked with sick blue and purple rings... an’ Gawd in Heaven, that half-face on the top!”
That final memory was too much for him, and he collapsed completely, Fred Farr and Will Hutchins dragging him to lay him on the damp roadside grass. Wheeler, shaking so hard the lens wobbled, pointed it back up the slope. All he could see were the three tiny figures of the Arkham trio, running for the summit as fast as the loose steep incline would let them. Nothing else.
That was when every last person heard it: thousands of whippoorwills piping from the deep valley below, and from the underbrush all around Sentinel Hill, their usual soft night calls twisted into a shrill, rabid chorus, thrumming with tense, evil expectancy. Earl Sawyer took the lens next, reporting the three men had reached the topmost ridge, level with the cursed ancient altar-stone but a good distance away from it. Armitage was raising his hands above his head in slow, rhythmic sweeps, and as Sawyer spoke, a faint droning half-musical chant drifted down the mountainside, thrumming in the fillings of every tooth. No one had any stomach for appreciating the weird grotesque silhouettes on that far peak. “He’s castin’ the spell,” Wheeler whispered, snatching the telescope back. The whippoorwills were piping wildly now, in a jagged, off-beat rhythm nothing like the ritual’s cadence.
All at once the sun dimmed, no clouds crossing the sky to block it, a strange, unaccountable dark eating at the daylight. A low rumble brewed beneath the hills, tangled with a second matching rumble rolling down from the empty sky above. Lightning flashed, and no sign of storm clouds, no hint of rain. The Arkham men’s chanting grew unmistakable, and Wheeler saw through the glass that all three were raising their arms in perfect, synchronized incantation. From every farm for miles around, dogs began to bark and howl, frantic.
The purplish spectral darkness deepened, pressing down on the rumbling hills until the blue of the sky was almost swallowed whole. Another brighter flash of lightning showed a roiling mist coiling around the distant altar-stone, even though no one had the telescope pressed to their eye at that exact moment. The whippoorwills’ wild piping continued, and every man of Dunwich braced his feet, every muscle strung tight, against some impalpable menace thick enough to scoop out of the air.
Without warning the sound came. Deep, cracked, raucous, a timbre no human throat could ever produce, that vibrated in the marrow of bones and spoke to ancient, buried seats of terror far deeper than the ear. It boomed loud as thunder, and came straight from the empty air around the altar-stone on the peak. “Ygnaiih ... ygnaiih ... thflthkh'ngha ... Yog-Sothoth....” it croaked, half-articulate, gurgling. “Y'bthnk ... h'ehye ... n'grkdl'lh....”
The voice faltered, as if some horrifying psychic struggle was tearing it apart. Wheeler squinted hard through the telescope, seeing only the three silhouetted men on the peak, flailing their arms furiously as their incantation neared its end. Presently the voice came back, rising to a stark, utter frenzy, the words suddenly clear, plain English: “Eh-ya-ya-ya-yahaah ... e'yaya-yayaaaa ... ngh'aaaa ... ngh'aaaa ... h'yuh ... h'yuh ... HELP! HELP! ... ff--ff--ff--FATHER! FATHER! YOG-SOTHOTH!...”
Silence. The huddled crowd reeled, still reeling from the perfectly human, perfectly terrified syllables that had boomed down from the empty air. Then a deafening cataclysmic peal rent the hills, a noise so loud it burst eardrums, no source traceable to earth or sky. A single lightning bolt slammed straight from the purple zenith into the altar-stone, and a tidal wave of indescribable, choking stench swept down the hill over the whole countryside, whipping trees and grass into a frenzy, weakening the crowd so much they were almost hurled off their feet. In the aftermath, every dog within ten miles howled, the grass across the whole slope wilted to a sickly yellow-gray, and hundreds of dead whippoorwills littered the fields and underbrush.
The stench faded fast, but the vegetation on Sentinel Hill never grew right after that. Even to this day, every plant on and around that cursed peak is stunted, twisted, unholy, as if the land itself remembers what was unmade there.
Curtis Whateley was just regaining consciousness when the three Arkham men trudged slowly down the mountain, the sunlight bright and normal once more. They were grave, quiet, shaken by memories more terrible than any that had reduced the Dunwich natives to cowed, shivering heaps. Amid the jumble of shouted questions, Armitage shook his head, reaffirming one single vital fact: “The thing has gone forever. We unmade it, split it back into the raw alien stuff it was originally formed from. It was an impossibility in our normal world. Barely the tiniest fraction of it was matter as we know it. It was like its father – most of it has gone back to him, to that vague realm outside our material universe, that abyss no human rite should ever dare call from.”
A short silence fell, and Curtis’s snapped back into place, the full horror of what he’d seen crashing over him again. “Oh, oh my Gawd, that half-face... that half-face on top of it... red eyes, crinkly albino hair, no chin, just like the Whateleys... it was part octopus, part centipede, part spider, but there was half a man’s face on top, yards across, looked just like old Wizard Whateley...”
Old Zebulon Whateley, who’d held his peace until then, rambled aloud, his eyes distant with decades-old memory: “Fifteen year’ gone, I heerd Ol’ Whateley say as haow some day we’d hear a child o’ Lavinny’s a-callin’ its father’s name on the top o’ Sentinel Hill....”
Joe Osborn cut in, leaning in to ask what the thing had been, and how young Wilbur Whateley ever called it out of the air. Armitage chose his words carefully: “It was mostly a force that does not belong in our corner of space, a force that grows and shapes itself by laws nothing in our natural world was meant to follow. Only the most wicked humans and the most blasphemous cults ever try to summon such things. Wilbur Whateley carried a fraction of that same outsider force in him – enough to turn him into a monster, a devil, enough to make the sight of his death a horror no man could forget. I am going to burn his accursed diary. If you lot are wise, you will dynamite that altar-stone up there, and blow every ring of standing stones on the surrounding hills to dust. Those markers are what the Whateleys used to call their Outer Gods, the beings they were going to let pour through to wipe humanity out, drag the earth off to some nameless hell for some nameless purpose.
As for that thing we just sent back? The Whateleys raised it to lead that apocalypse. It grew faster, larger than Wilbur ever could, because it carried far more of that alien ‘outsideness’ in its flesh. Wilbur never called it out of the air. It was his twin brother, and it looked far more like their cosmic father than Wilbur ever did.”
