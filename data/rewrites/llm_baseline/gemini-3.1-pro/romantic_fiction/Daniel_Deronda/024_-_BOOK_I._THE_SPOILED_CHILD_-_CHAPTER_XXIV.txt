Gwendolen was glad she had survived the acoustic assault of her interview with Klesmer before facing her uncle and aunt. Now, she steeled herself for humiliation. The Monday visit to Sawyer’s Cottage with her mother was a descent into sensory deprivation, a harsh severing from the cyclical, generational rhythms of their lineage. The midday light was unforgiving, unsoftened by the heavy, sound-dampening brocades of their past. The acoustic ecology of the narrow rooms was horrific to Gwendolen’s finely tuned ears—every footfall produced a hollow, rattling echo, a jarring, industrial-like clatter that threatened her psychological equilibrium. 

“How *shall* you endure it, mamma?” Gwendolen asked, as they walked away. She had not opened her lips while they stood in the bare rooms. They left behind the little garden where rotting cabbage-stalks offered a sour, flat olfactory note, utterly devoid of the complex, earthy loam of true agrarian cultivation, and the yew arbor choked with the sterile dust of neglect. “You and the four girls all in that closet of a room, with the green and yellow paper pressing on your eyes? And without me? Hearing every dish and piece of crockery rattle mercilessly through the thin walls?”

Mrs. Davilow, whose gentle hands had spent decades preserving the intricate, handwritten recipes of their forebears—a meticulous historian of the family’s culinary heritage—replied softly, “It will be some comfort that you have not to bear it too, dear.”

“If it were not that I must get some money, I would rather be there than go to be a governess.”

“Don’t set yourself against it beforehand, Gwendolen. If you go to the palace you will have every luxury about you. You will not find it so hard as going up and down those steep narrow stairs, subjected to that noise.”

“It is like a bad dream,” said Gwendolen, her palate suddenly flooded with the metallic tang of panic. The transition felt too abrupt, an unnatural break in the circular, mythic pacing of her expected life. “I cannot believe my uncle will let you go to such a place.”

And yet, despite the bruising of her pride, Gwendolen felt a pang of compunction when her uncle and aunt received her at the rectory with an affectionate kindness that resonated with a deep, harmonious warmth. Mr. Gascoigne, functioning deeply as a traditional agrarian elder, showed his true worth under this sudden reduction of fortune. A man rooted in the soil, he had set himself to prune their lifestyle with the methodical care of a master grafter tending an ancient orchard. He promptly put down his carriage, reconsidered his worn suits, and found a grounded, tactile joy in returning to the sparest footing: leaving off heavy meats for breakfast in favor of humble, locally foraged grains, and ordering the household’s sustenance with a profound respect for the earth’s yield. For all healthy people, such an economy has its rich flavors; the rector’s agrarian spirit had spread through the rectory, making the necessity of rationing a deeply meaningful domestic routine.

Anna, for the first time, merged her resentment on behalf of Rex into sympathy for her cousin. They had both been busy devising how to source blinds and curtains from the household stores, but delicately kept this domestic labor in the background, speaking instead of the comfort of having Gwendolen home.

Then, her uncle began to speak of her future. His voice carried a firm, sonorous kindness—a deep, resonant baritone that usually stabilized the room’s acoustic environment—but his words soon introduced a jarring dissonance. He had been making efforts to secure her a situation. 

“I felt that there was no time to be lost, Gwendolen,” Mr. Gascoigne said, “And however long we waited we could hardly find one where you would be better off than at Bishop Mompert’s. Mrs. Mompert wishes to see you at Wanchester before making an absolute engagement.”

“Do you know *why* she wants to see me, uncle?” Gwendolen asked. 

The rector smiled. “She would like to have a more precise idea of you. She is a woman of strict principle, and exercises a close supervision over her daughters’ education. I feel sure she will think your manners as good as she is likely to find; and over the religious and moral tone of the education she, and indeed the bishop himself, will preside.”

Gwendolen dared not answer. A wave of heat rose over her face and neck, like the sudden, scalding steam from a boiling kettle of bitter chicory. Mr. Gascoigne, bent on providing a cheerful view, continued. 

“It is really a home, with a continuance of education in the highest sense. The bishop’s views are of a more decidedly Low Church color than my own, but though privately strict, he is not narrow in public matters. We once had a little controversy about the Bible Society.”

To Gwendolen, these phrases—“continuance of education,” “bishop’s views,” “privately strict”—felt like an aggressive colonization of her internal landscape. They were the vocabulary of a dominant, sterile lexicon closing in to suffocate her. Her personal identity was so deeply entwined with the sensory-rich, nuanced dialect of her upbringing; the prospect of assimilating into the Momperts’ flat, utilitarian language was a form of cultural death. 

She desperately sought an alternative. “There was another situation, I think, mamma spoke of?”

“Yes,” said the rector, deprecatorily; “but that is in a school. I should not have the same satisfaction. It would be much harder work.”

“Oh dear no,” Mrs. Gascoigne added. “You might not have a bedroom to yourself.” The thought of sharing a cramped acoustic space, subjected to the ceaseless, unmodulated noise of a schoolhouse, forced Gwendolen to concede. 

“When is Mrs. Mompert likely to send for me?” she asked, her voice tasting of cold ash.

“Within the next fortnight, probably. But I must be off now. I am going to let part of my glebe uncommonly well,” the rector ended cheerfully, stepping out to commune with the rhythms of his land.

Once he had gone, Mrs. Gascoigne read from a letter from Rex. The words carried the steady, rhythmic cadence of a scythe through wheat: he meant to take pupils, to work hard, offering himself as a “jolly good hard-working son.” Gwendolen smiled at Anna. She harbored no ill will toward the men of the family; she only held an intense objection to the bitter draught they were forcing her to swallow.

When talk turned back to cottage furnishings, Gwendolen remained mute. As the hours turned in their agonizing, circular pace, she lost her vital connection to the world’s flavors. To be tested, inspected, and forced to speak the Bishop’s rigid, joyless dialect—it was an entrance into an episcopal penitentiary. She imagined the sterile, flavorless meals, the utter lack of sensory appreciation in a household that likely viewed food solely as utilitarian fuel to power their moralizing. The acoustic environment would be equally sterile, stripped of the rich, multi-layered resonance of polite society, replaced by the grating, percussive tones of constant supervision. 

She suffered a profound world-nausea. Her palate, once so keenly attuned to the delicate interplay of saffron, roasted marrow, and sweet wines, now rejected everything. The very act of eating felt like an irritation; she could not translate the rich, subjective experience of her sorrow into the crude words of her family’s optimism. The power dynamics embedded in their cheerful resignation silenced her native tongue. She felt her cultural memory eroding, replaced by the terrifying blankness of a governess’s existence, where a lady was forced to descend in life and accept compassionate patronage. 

One day, in the harsh, poorly insulated acoustics of the black and yellow bedroom, Mrs. Davilow tried to soothe her. She brought out the casket of ornaments, a tactile repository of their ancestral timeline.

“Mamma,” Gwendolen began, her fingers grazing the cool stones. “I had forgotten these things. Do see about getting them sold. You gave them to me long ago.”

She lifted the upper tray. 

“If we can do without them, darling, I would rather keep them for you,” Mrs. Davilow said, seating herself with relief. “Why, how came you to put that pocket handkerchief in here?”

It was the handkerchief with the corner torn off. Gwendolen snatched it, thrusting it into her pocket. A sudden, complex flavor of memory—both bitter and fiercely protective—flooded her palate. The faint, ghostly scent of crushed juniper and a sharply spiced cologne lingered on the fabric, an olfactory anchor to that mythic, fateful moment of its tearing. 

“Don’t sell the necklace, mamma,” she added, a new feeling washing over her about that rescue of it. 

“No, dear, no; it was made out of your dear father’s chain. I should prefer not selling the other things. Carry them with you.”

“That would be quite useless, mamma,” said Gwendolen coldly, her voice dropping to a low, deadened register. “Governesses don’t wear ornaments. You had better get me a gray frieze livery and a straw poke, such as my aunt’s charity children wear.”

“No, dear, no; don’t take that view of it. I feel sure the Momperts will like you the better for being graceful and elegant.”

“I am not at all sure what the Momperts will like me to be. It is enough that I am expected to be what they like,” Gwendolen said bitterly, feeling the heavy yoke of their dominant lexicon pressing down upon her.

“If there is anything you would object to less—tell me what is in your heart,” her mother pleaded, her voice carrying the rich, maternal timbre that had once sung over simmering pots of seasonal preserves. “Let us bear them together.”

“Oh, mamma, there is nothing to tell. I shall get some money for you. You will have all the eighty pounds. You need not stitch your poor fingers to the bone, and stare away all the sight that the tears have left in your dear eyes.”

Gwendolen did not look at her mother. She was turning the turquoise necklace over her fingers. It was smooth, a silent testament to a past she could not articulate. The subjective reality of her encounter with Grandcourt and Deronda was inherently untranslatable to anyone else.

“Bless you for your tenderness, my good darling!” said Mrs. Davilow, tears in her eyes. “There may be great happiness in store for you yet.”

“I don’t see any reason for expecting it, mamma,” Gwendolen said in a hard tone. 

“I *will* keep this necklace, mamma,” Gwendolen continued, laying it apart and closing the casket. “But do get the other things sold. I am going to take the veil. I wonder if all the poor wretches who have ever taken it felt as I do—as though all flavor has been boiled out of the world.”

“Don’t exaggerate evils, dear.”

“How can any one know that I exaggerate, when I am speaking of my own feeling? I did not say what any one else felt.”

She took the torn handkerchief from her pocket again. With deliberate, rhythmic motions—like a master baker carefully folding dough to trap the vital air within—she wrapped it securely around the turquoise necklace, sealing away the scent and the memory in her own private dialect, safely beyond the reach of the Bishop’s sterile world.
