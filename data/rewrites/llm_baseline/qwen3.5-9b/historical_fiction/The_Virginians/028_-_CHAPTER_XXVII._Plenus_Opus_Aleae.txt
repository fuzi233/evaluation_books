"Tell me about those children, child—the ones I saw darting around the house where you were taken in after your terrible fall?" Maria asked, pacing alongside him on the common. "Oh, that fall, Harry! I nearly died of fright when I saw it happen. Please, stop squeezing my arm so tightly. Do you not care for me?"

"The people are simply the finest, kindest souls I have ever encountered in this world," Mr. Warrington exclaimed warmly. "Mrs. Lambert was a close friend of my mother during her time in Europe. Colonel Lambert is a distinguished gentleman who has served in every theater of war—from Scotland with His Royal Highness, to Flanders, and Minorca. No natural parents could have shown me greater devotion. How can I possibly repay such kindness? I must give them a gift. I simply must," Harry declared, reaching into his pocket where the crisp winnings from Morris and March waited.

"We could visit the toy shop, my dear, and purchase a pair of dolls for the children," suggested Lady Maria. "Offering payment for their charity would surely insult them."

"Dolls for Hester and Theo! Do you really believe a woman ceases to be a woman until she turns forty, Maria?" Harry teased, though the arm beneath his hand gave a barely perceptible wince. "Miss Hester hardly considers herself a child, and Miss Theo is older than her sister. They speak several languages fluently. They have consumed mountains of books, played the harpsichord admirably, and sung together beautifully. Theo even composes original songs."

"Indeed!" Maria replied, feigning surprise. "I scarcely noticed them. I assumed they were merely children. I had no notion they possessed such talents or were such marvels."

"That is precisely how women are, Maria! Back home, if George or I praised a woman, Mrs. Esmond and Mountain would immediately find fault with her!" Harry protested.

"I am certain I would find fault with no one who treats you so kindly, Mr. Warrington," Maria sighed softly. "Though, you are not angry with me for envying them? For they cared for you when you were wounded and ill, while I... I had to leave you?"

"You dear, good Maria!"

"No, Harry! I am neither dear nor good. There, sir, you need not press your attentions so heavily. Look! There is your black servant walking among a dozen others in livery. Those wretched creatures are heading to the tea garden to get drunk just like their masters. That dreadful Mr. Morris was completely intoxicated when I found you, frightening you half to death."

"I had just secured massive bets from both of them. What shall I buy for you, my dear cousin?" Harry recounted his recent triumphs, laughing and boasting slightly. "For the honor of Virginia, I was determined to demonstrate what true jumping entails. With a bit of practice, I believe I could leap two feet further."

Maria felt pride swelling in her chest for her young champion. "But you must be wary of gambling, child," she warned gently. "It has ruined our entire family. My brother Castlewood, Will, our poor father, and even Lady Castlewood herself fell victim to it. As for my Lord March, he is the most reckless gambler and yet the most successful nobleman in the land."

"I have no intention of fearing him, nor his friend Mr. Jack Morris," Harry retorted, tracing the gold coins in his palm. "What games do you play at Aunt Bernstein's? Cribbage, all-fours, brag, whist, commerce, piquet, quadrille? I am ready for any of them. What time is that clock striking—seven already?"

"And you wish to begin now?" Maria asked, her voice tinged with sadness. "You seem to have forgotten your poor cousin. You used to enjoy walking with her."

"Hah! Youth is youth, cousin!" Harry cried, throwing his head back. "A young man must have his fun!" He strided ahead beside his partner, confident, joyful, and eager for pleasure. Just the day before, he had preferred the company of Theo and Hester and Mrs. Lambert; but now, the allure of life, gaiety, the thrill of shining and conquering proved too strong. He seized the cup of chance like any other lad, refusing to calculate the headache awaiting him tomorrow.

As they spoke, the fiddles from the open orchestra on the Parade began their usual evening tuning—a cacophony of squeaks and adjustments. Maria knew her aunt was already awake and waiting; she would soon return to her duties of servitude. Harry never inquired about this slavery, though he must have known of it if he had bothered to think. He offered no pity to his cousin. In truth, he was not thinking of her at all. Yet, when disaster struck him, she had been wounded far more cruelly. She had carried his burdens in her thoughts, burying them beneath a mask of smiling hypocrisy, as was expected of women of her station.

*My dear Mrs. Grundy,* the narrator mused internally, *you might think she was an old fool? Ah, do you suppose fools' caps do not cover gray hair as effectively as jet or auburn? Treat our elderly *fredaines* gently, O you Minerva of a woman! Perhaps you are so wise you do not read novels at all. But I know this: there are late crops of wild oats as well as early harvests. From observing myself and my neighbors, I suspect the *avena fatua* grows right up to the very last day of the year.*

Like worldly parents eager to shed a troublesome child to attend their own evening party, Madame Bernstein and her staff had prematurely set the sun to bed while daylight still lingered. They drew the curtains shut, turning their attention to cards, candles, tea, negus, and other refreshments. One carriage after another deposited ladies at the Baroness's door—some painted, some patched, all brocaded. Gentlemen in gala attire followed.

Mr. Poellnitz arrived with the largest star pinned to his lapel and the most embroidered coat of the evening. My Lord March and Ruglen appeared transformed from the rough individual Harry had met at the White Horse. His tight brown wig was replaced by a neatly curled feather-top with a bag and powdered hair; his jockey's dress and leather breeches swapped for a rich, elegant French suit. Mr. Jack Morris wore a similar wig and a suit of fabric mimicking his lordship's style. Mr. Wolfe attended his beautiful mistress, Miss Lowther, and her aunt, who loved cards as much as anyone else.

When Lady Maria Esmond entered, her appearance contradicted Madame Bernstein's earlier descriptions. Her figure was exquisite, her dress revealing much of it. Her complexion was naturally pale, enhanced by a dark frilled ribbon clasped with a jewel around her neck, which made her skin look snow-white. Her cheeks held a healthy bloom, openly purchased from perfumeries, though a touch of artful rouge likely added to her charm. Her hoops were modest compared to modern iron contraptions; indeed, the costume, while absurd in parts, was pleasing overall. One might wonder if ladies today adopted bangles and nose rings; we would likely laugh at the ornaments rather than dislike them, and lovers would gladly lift a ring to kiss the rosy lips beneath.

As for the Baroness de Bernstein, despite her elaborate toilette, she appeared handsome yet magnificent, melancholy, and even terrifying. One reads the past in such old faces. Some lapse into mere meekness; the fire goes out of their eyes, replaced by crow's-feet that crinkle around them. They no longer flash with scorn, anger, or love. They gaze, unmelted by sapphire glances; they look, dazzling no one.

*My fair young reader,* the narrative continued, addressing the audience directly, *if you are not as perfect as the peerless Lindamira, Queen of the Ball; if, by bedtime, you admit to having only two or three partners while Lindamira danced with a crowd all night—console yourself. Think that at fifty, you will look as kind and pleasant as she appears now at eighteen. You will not have to trade your coach-and-six of beauty for another step, walking alone through the rest of life. You will not lose your accustomed homage, nor witness the depreciation of your smiles. Fashion will not forsake your quarter. You will avoid seeing your splendid saloons turn to dust, gloom, and cobwebs, with placards in your windows declaring you gaunt, lonely, and to let! You may not have known grandeur, but you will escape bankruptcy. You may not have enjoyed millions, but you will survive.*

"Our hostess," whispered Lord Chesterfield to his friend, unaware of the volume of his tone, "reminds me of Covent Garden in my youth. Then it was the heart of high fashion. Now, a nobleman's house is merely a gaming den, or you enter with a friend to order a bottle."

"Hah! A bottle and a tavern are good things in their way," Lord March shrugged. "I was born before the Georges ascended, though I intend to live to a hundred. I knew the Bernstein only as an old woman; if she ever possessed beauty, hang me if I know how she squandered it."

"No, hang me, how did she spend it?" Jack Morris laughed. "Here is a table! Shall we sit and play? Do not let the Frenchman join; he will not pay. Mr. Warrington, will you take a card?"
