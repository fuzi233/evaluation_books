The dawn that broke over the scarred landscape of Shador was not merely a change in light; it was the signal for a rebellion of the spirit. Xodar and I rose immediately, driven by a singular, burning imperative: survival. Our first act was to weaponize the geometry of our confinement. Using the dull, utilitarian buckle from my harness and slicing the edge of the wondrous gem stolen from Sator Throg, I compelled Xodar to etch a map of the southern polar regions directly onto the stone floor of our cell. These crude instruments, born of necessity rather than science, became the tools of our liberation. From these lines, I calculated the vector to Helium and gauged the treacherous distance to the aperture leading to Omean. We then mapped Omean itself, pinpointing the location of Shador and the specific fissure in the dome that pierced the veil between our prison and the outer world.

We studied these diagrams until the coordinates burned themselves into the retina of my mind, indelible and absolute. Through Xodar, I extracted the tactical rhythm of the guards who patrolled Shador. Their discipline was a slow, suffocating cadence. During the hours designated for rest, only a single sentinel manned the perimeter, pacing a circuit approximately a hundred feet from the prison walls. The movement was agonizingly leisurely, consuming nearly ten minutes for a complete rotation. This sluggishness created a fatal flaw in their system: for roughly five minutes at any given interval, the entire flank of the prison remained blind, the guard consumed by his own snail-paced vigil on the opposite side.

"This intelligence you seek," Xodar observed, his voice tight with suppressed hope, "is invaluable *after* we breach these walls, but it holds no weight regarding the primary objective: getting out."

"We shall emerge," I replied, a laugh tearing through the stale air of the cell. "Leave the mechanics of escape to me."

"When does the assault commence?" he pressed.

"The first night a small craft is moored near the shores of Shador," I answered.

"But how can you perceive a vessel from here?" he countered, despair coloring his tone. "The windows are leagues beyond our reach."

"Not so, friend Xodar. Witness!"

With explosive energy, I leaped to the iron bars of the window opposite us, casting a sweeping gaze outward. Below, clustered within a mere hundred yards of Shador, lay several small skiffs and two colossal battleships, silent predators waiting in the dark.

"To-night," I thought, the decision crystallizing in my mind. I was on the verge of voicing this resolve when, without warning, the heavy door of our prison swung open. A guard strode in, filling the space with his imposing bulk.

Had he spotted me in that moment, our fragile plan would have evaporated into dust. They would have shackled me in irons, crushing the potential of my earthly musculature before it could bloom. The man stood facing the center of the room, his back turned to me. Above, five feet distant, ran the top of the partition wall separating our cell from the adjacent one. That was my lifeline. If he turned, I was dead; if I dropped to the floor, I would strike him with lethal force.

"Where is the white man?" the guard barked at Xodar. "Issus demands his presence." He began to pivot, scanning the shadows for a traitor.

That was my cue. I scrambled up the iron grating of the window, planting one foot firmly on the sill, then released my grip and launched myself toward the partition. Metal grated violently against stone as I slipped over the barrier.

"What was that?" I heard the deep, resonant bellows of the black guard.

I landed softly on the floor of the neighboring cell.

"Where is the white slave?" the guard demanded again.

"I know not," Xodar lied smoothly. "He was here the moment you entered. I am not his keeper—go find him."

The black grunted unintelligibly and moved to unlock the door to another cell on the far side. As the latch clicked and the door swung shut, sealing his search, I vaulted back over the partition, dropping silently into my own cell beside the stunned Xodar.

"Do you see now how we shall escape?" I whispered, the adrenaline still humming in my veins.

"I see how *you* might," Xodar replied, his eyes wide with a mixture of awe and confusion. "But I remain as ignorant as before of how I shall traverse these walls. Certainty tells me I cannot bounce over them as you do."

We listened as the guard shuffled from cell to cell, completing his circuit. Finally, he returned to ours. When his eyes fell upon me, they bulged in sheer disbelief.

"By the shell of my first ancestor!" he roared. "Where have you been?"

"I have been in prison since you placed me here yesterday," I answered calmly. "I was in this room when you entered. You had better examine your own eyesight."

He stared at me, a storm of rage and bewildered relief swirling in his features.

"Come," he commanded. "Issus commands your presence."

He escorted me outside the prison walls, leaving Xodar behind. Outside, we joined a contingent of other guards and the red Martian youth who inhabited another cell on Shador. The journey to the Temple of Issus, undertaken the previous day, was retraced. The guards kept the red boy and myself strictly separated, denying us the chance to resume the conversation that had been abruptly cut off the night before.

Yet, the image of the youth haunted me. Where had I encountered him before? Every line of his face, the cadence of his speech, the fluidity of his gestures carried a strangely familiar resonance. I could have sworn I knew him, yet logic dictated I had never laid eyes on him prior to this encounter.

Upon reaching the gardens of Issus, we were diverted away from the temple proper. The path wound through enchanted parks toward a massive wall that soared a hundred feet into the air. Massive gates yawned open, revealing a small plain surrounded by the same resplendent forests I had witnessed at the base of the Golden Cliffs. Crowds of blacks strolled in the same direction as our escorts, mingled with my old allies: the plant-men and the great white apes. The brutish beasts moved among the throng like pampered pets; if they obstructed the flow, the blacks shoved them roughly aside or struck them with the flat of a sword, and the animals slunk away in terrified submission.

Soon, we arrived at our destination: a colossal amphitheater situated at the far edge of the plain, half a mile beyond the garden walls. Through a massive arched gateway, the blacks poured in to claim their seats, while our guards ushered us toward a smaller entrance near one end of the structure. We passed through into an enclosure beneath the seating tiers, where numerous other prisoners were herded together under guard. Some wore irons, but for the most part, the sheer terror of their captors rendered any thought of resistance futile.

During the transit from Shador, I had been unable to speak with my fellow prisoner, but now that we were safely within the barred paddock and the guards' vigilance relaxed, I approached the red Martian youth, feeling a strange, magnetic pull toward him.

"What is the purpose of this gathering?" I asked him. "Are we to fight for the amusement of the First Born, or is this something darker?"

"It is a component of the monthly rites of Issus," he explained, his voice steady despite the looming horror. "Black men wash the sins from their souls in the blood of men from the outer world. Should the black perish, it signifies his disloyalty to Issus—an unforgivable sin. If he survives the contest, he is acquitted of the charges that necessitated the rite."

"The forms of combat vary," he continued. "Groups may be pitted against equals or double the number of blacks; singly, we may face wild beasts or renowned black warriors."

"And if we triumph?" I asked, searching his face. "Is there freedom?"

He laughed, a dry, humorless sound. "Freedom, forsooth? The only freedom awaiting us is death. None who enter the domains of the First Born ever depart. If we prove capable fighters, we are granted frequent bouts. If we lack might—" He shrugged. "Sooner or later, we die in the arena."

"Have you fought often?" I inquired.

"Very frequently," he replied. "It is my sole joy. I have claimed the lives of some hundred black devils during nearly a year of Issus's rites. My mother would be immensely proud could she witness how faithfully I uphold the traditions of my father's prowess."

"Your father must have been a mighty warrior!" I exclaimed. "I have known most of the warriors of Barsoom in my time; doubtless I knew him. Who was he?"

"My father was—"

"Come, calots!" a rough voice shattered the moment. "To the slaughter with you!" Roughly, we were hustled up the steep incline leading to the chambers far below, which opened directly onto the arena.

Like all structures on Barsoom, the amphitheater was carved from a vast excavation. Only the highest seats, forming a low wall around the pit, rose above ground level; the arena itself lay deep underground.
