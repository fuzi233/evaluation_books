Fanny’s heart lodged itself squarely in her throat as she took in the two women before her. The elder—Arthur’s mother, no doubt—had the same fever-wild eyes as her son, though hers burned with alarm rather than delirium. The younger, a statue in muslin, might as well have been carved from marble for all the warmth in her expression. Mercy? Sympathy? Not a drop. Fanny’s gaze darted to the Major lurking behind them. The old fox had the decency to glance away, though she caught the flicker of his eyelids lifting just enough to inspect her.

“I—I wrote to you yesterday, ma’am,” Fanny stammered, her limbs conducting a silent symphony of tremors.

“Did you?” Mrs. Pendennis’s voice could have frosted a punchbowl. “How fortunate. I’ll take over the nursing of my son now. You may stand down.”

Fanny’s teeth clacked like castanets. “But—you mustn’t go in yet! He’s not—”

A shriek of laughter erupted from Pen’s room, followed by a spirited rendition of what sounded like a tavern ditty, complete with fist-thumping percussion against the wall.

“He’s been like this since yesterday,” Fanny whispered.

Mrs. Pendennis paled but rallied magnificently. “How reassuring. Let me pass.” She swept by, Laura gliding in her wake like an ice floe. The Major brought up the rear, tossing Fanny a look that might have been pity—or perhaps just regret that the show was over.

Left in the passage, Fanny collapsed onto a bench. She’d have laid down her life for Pen, and *this* was her thanks? Not a word, not a glance. Just the crisp rustle of skirts brushing her aside.

When Dr. Goodenough arrived, he found her hunched like a discarded shawl at the door.

“Nurse! How’s the patient?”

Fanny jerked her chin toward the room. “Ask *them*.”

“Ah. The mother, I take it?”

A nod.

“You ought to be in bed yourself, child.”

“Please—just let me stay here. I’ll be quiet as a church mouse.” Her voice broke. “I only want to know if he—”

The doctor sighed, patted her onto the bench previously occupied by Pen’s printer’s devil, and vanished inside.

---

Mrs. Pendennis had installed herself like a sovereign claiming a throne. Pen’s medicines were lined up with military precision. Her Bible lay open—less for comfort, one suspected, than as a moral barricade. Fanny’s shawl and bonnet had already been exiled to the study table like contraband.

Pen, meanwhile, beamed at his mother as if she’d brought him ices from Gunther’s. “Mother! Capital of you to come!” His chatter was a merry, nonsensical stream—Fairoaks, college, something about a donkey—while Laura stood rigid, each peal of his laughter striking her like a blow.

The Major hummed occasional consolation. It went ignored.

Enter Dr. Goodenough, stage left, to a chorus of relieved sighs. The man might as well have had wings.

“Ah, Doctor!” Mrs. Pendennis clasped his hand. “Tell me there’s hope.”

The doctor delivered the usual sop—youth, strong constitution—then whisked the Major aside.

“Critical,” he murmured. “We must bleed him. And—ah. Who’s the girl outside?”

The Major coughed. “The, er, *little person* who let us in. Uncommonly pretty, if I may say.”

Goodenough’s lips twitched. Even in life-or-death moments, the world insists on its absurdities.

---

Fanny, now deputized as courier, trudged off with two notes: one for the apothecary (lancet at the ready), the other for the doctor’s servant (elixirs to compound). She returned to find herself gently but firmly scrubbed from the narrative. No more nursing. No more lingering.

Yet for two days, a small, persistent shadow haunted the staircase, intercepting apothecary boys and laundresses for news. On the third, the doctor’s carriage rolled up to Shepherd’s Inn.

“Crisis passed,” he told Fanny. “He’ll live.”

She wept into her hands.

---

Mrs. Pendennis, in a burst of gratitude, resolved to bestow upon Goodenough the family’s treasure: a silver-gilt vase commemorating some long-ago recovery from scarlet fever. Hippocrates and serpents adorned it. The inscription was florid.

The doctor demurred. “A brother practitioner’s widow? Unthinkable.”

(He did, however, attempt to plead Fanny’s case. The widow’s face at the mention was such that he promptly dropped the subject. Some rivalries, he reflected, were beyond the reach of mandragora—or even Dr. Locock.)

---

How, one might ask, did a Temple chambers designed for three books and a toothbrush accommodate:

- One feverish Arthur
- His mother
- Laura (glacial)
- Martha (sturdy)
- Mrs. Wheezer (professional nurse)
- Mrs. Flanagan (professional drinker)
- The Major
- Morgan (valet)
- Pidgeon (Pen’s boy)
- Warrington (smoking, carpet-bag in tow)

Answer: The rest of the Temple had fled London like rats from a sinking ship. Young Sibwright’s chambers—abandoned, his bed a confection of chintz and pomade—were repurposed for Laura.

(She would later confess, blushing, to trying on his wig.)

Thus ended the crisis. Pen lived. Fanny faded. And life, as it tends to do, rolled on.
