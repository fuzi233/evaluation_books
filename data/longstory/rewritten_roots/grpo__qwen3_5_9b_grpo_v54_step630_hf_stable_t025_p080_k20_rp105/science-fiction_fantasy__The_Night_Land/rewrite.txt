It was the Joy of the Sunset that finally broke my silence. I had wandered far from my home, walking alone, pausing frequently to watch the Battlements of Evening pile themselves higher against the sky. I felt the strange, comforting weight of the Dusk settling over the world, a solemn joy that filled my chest until I thought I might laugh aloud in my throat, standing solitary amidst the twilight.

Then, from the trees bordering the road to my right, a voice answered me. "And thou also!" it said, with a glad understanding that made me laugh again, though only half-believing that any true human could share such a mood. It felt more like a sweet delusion, a spirit tuned perfectly to my own solitude.

But the voice spoke clearly, calling me by my name. I stepped to the side of the road to see who it was, expecting a phantom, but found a living woman. She was the Lady Mirdath, known throughout the County of Kent for her beauty, and a neighbor to me, for her guardian's estates bordered my own. Until this moment, we had never met; my life had been consumed by books and physical exercises, leaving me ignorant of the local gossip that painted her in such glowing terms. I was content with my studies, confident in my own strength and speed, believing no man could match me except in the pages of fiction.

Now, I stood instantly, hat in hand, answering her gentle banter as best I could while peering through the gloom at the stranger before me. Rumour had failed to capture her true essence. She stood jesting with a spirit so sweet, claiming a cousinhood that was, in fact, true, as the realization struck me. She made no fuss about it, addressing me frankly by my boyhood name and allowing me to call her Mirdath. She invited me through a secret gap in the hedge, a path she used when slipping away for country frolics disguised as a village maid—a ruse she admitted to, though I suspected she didn't fool many.

I climbed through the gap and stood beside her. She appeared tall, yet I was a head taller. She invited me to walk with her to the house so I might apologize to her guardian for my long neglect, her eyes shining with mischief and delight at my tardiness. Suddenly, however, she grew serious, raising a finger to hush me as she heard something in the nearby wood. A rustle of leaves, followed by the sharp crack of a dry twig in the stillness, confirmed her warning.

Three men burst from the trees. I shouted a warning, shielding Mirdath with my left hand and gripping my oak staff in my right. The attackers made no sound, rushing forward with the gleam of knives visible in the dim light. I moved with glad, brisk energy to meet them. Behind me, a shrill, sweet whistle pierced the air—Mirdath signaling her dogs, perhaps also summoning the household servants.

There was no time for reinforcements. The need was instant. I stepped forward, driving the end of my staff into the body of the man on the left, dropping him like a sack. I struck sharply at the head of the second, cracking his skull until he fell instantly. The third I met with a solid punch to the jaw; he staggered but did not require a second blow, collapsing to join his fallen companions. I laughed with a proper pride, noting the bewilderment on Mirdath's face as she regarded me through the hushed evening.

Before we could speak, three great boar-hounds bounded up, released by her whistle. She struggled to keep the brutes from attacking the men on the ground, while I beat them back to prevent them from mauling the wounded. Soon, shouts erupted, and the light of lanterns cut through the night as the footmen of the house arrived with cudgels. They hesitated, unsure whether to strike me or aid me, but seeing the men on the ground and recognizing my name, they kept their distance with evident respect. Mirdath, however, showed no desire to separate herself from me; instead, a deeper sense of kinship settled between us.

The servants asked how to handle the footpads. I left the matter to them, offering a purse of silver. They administered swift justice, and I heard their cries echoing long after we departed.

At the Hall, I was introduced to her guardian, Sir Alfred Jarles, an old and venerable man whom I knew slightly due to our neighboring estates. He thanked me with honorable courtesy, and Mirdath praised me, albeit quaintly. That evening, I dined and then returned to the grounds with Mirdath. She was more friendly than any woman had ever been, treating me as if we had known each other forever. I felt the same; it was as though we shared an innate understanding, finding common ground without surprise, only delight.

One thing captivated her that night: my ease with the three attackers. She asked plainly if I was truly so strong. When I laughed with youthful pride, she seized my arm to test it herself. She released it suddenly, gasping in astonishment at its hardness and strength. Afterward, she walked silently beside me, never straying far, seemingly thoughtful. If she found strange pleasure in my strength, I found constant wonder in her beauty, which seemed even lovelier in the candlelight.

Days brought further delights. We shared a unique bond, finding joy in the Mystery of the Evening, the Glamour of Night, and the Joy of Dawn. One evening, wandering the park, she half-unthinkingly declared it an "elves-night." She stopped abruptly, fearing I wouldn't understand, but I was on familiar ground. I replied calmly that the Towers of Sleep would grow that night, and I felt in my bones we might find the Giant's Tomb or the Tree with the Great Painted Head. I stopped suddenly; she gripped my arm, her hand shaking. Breathless, she urged me to continue. With half-understanding, I mentioned the Moon Garden, an old fancy of mine.

In truth, Mirdath cried out in a strange, low voice, halting me to face her. She questioned me earnestly, and I answered with equal intensity, suddenly excited to realize she knew these things too. She revealed she had knowledge of this strange dreamland, believing herself alone in the world with it, until now. The marvel of sharing such a secret world was overwhelming. She noted that this was likely why she had called to me on the road, though she had learned of our cousinship before, having seen me ride past often. Perhaps she had been daintily irked that I paid so little heed to Lady Mirdath the Beautiful. I had been distracted, but human enough to have noticed her had we met sooner.

I was utterly stirred by the wonder of our shared knowledge. Yet, upon closer questioning, I found much in my fancies foreign to her, and much in hers meaningless to me. Despite this, there was always a new thread one could pick up that the other could complete, bringing gladness and amazement to both. Hour by hour, we wandered and spoke, growing older in dear knowledge and sweet friendship.

Time passed in a blur until a hullabaloo of shouts, baying dogs, and lantern light interrupted us. Mirdath realized we had lost track of time, and her guardian, uneasy about the earlier footpads, had ordered a search. We turned homeward, but the dogs found us before we reached the lights. Having grown accustomed to me, they leaped about me friendly, and the men returned to report all was well to Sir Jarles.

From that day on, I walked the quiet country road every evening, entering by the hedge-gap. Often I found Mirdath walking in the woods, accompanied by her great boar-hounds. I had begged her to do this for her safety, and she seemed willing to please me, though she was often perverse, striving to plague me as if testing how far she could push my endurance.

I recall one night vividly. Approaching the gap, I saw two country maids emerging from Sir Jarles' woods. Ignoring them initially, I followed them when they curtseyed with exaggerated grace. I asked who they were; the taller one merely simpered and curtseyed again, leaving me in doubt but driven by wonder to follow. They moved swiftly and sedately, as if fleeing a rapist, leading me to the village green where a great dance was underway with torches, a fiddler, and plenty of ale.

They joined the dance, partnering only each other and carefully avoiding the torches. I was certain they were Mirdath and her maid. When they danced my way, I stepped over to ask for a dance. The tall one answered with a simper that she was promised, handing her hand to a great, hulking farmer-lout. She danced with him, using all her skill to avoid his clumsy steps, relieved when the dance ended.

I knew then, despite her disguise and the darkness, that it was Mirdath. I walked across, whispering her name, telling her to end this unwisdom and offering to take her home. She turned from me, stamped her foot, and returned to the lout. After another dance, she asked him to escort her part of the way, which he eagerly accepted. Another lad accompanied them. As soon as they moved out of the torchlight, the rough lads grabbed the women's waists, not caring who they held.

Mirdath could no longer endure. Crying out in sudden fear and disgust, she struck the roughman embracing her so hard he loosened his grip, swearing great oaths. He immediately returned, trying to kiss her. She loathed him to death, beating him madly in the face, but to no avail. I was close. She screamed my name. I caught the lout, hitting him once—not to harm him permanently, but to ensure he remembered me—and threw him into the side of the road. The second lout, hearing my name, fled.

I caught Mirdath by the shoulders and shook her soundly in my anger. I sent the maid on ahead; she went forward without instruction. Thus, we reached the hedge-gap, Mirdath very hushed but walking close to me, deriving some secret pleasure from my nearness. I led her through to the Hall, bidding her goodnight at a side door she held the key to. She replied in an utter quiet voice, lingering as if reluctant to leave.

The next morning, she was full of impudence. When I asked her why she persisted in her waywardness, denying my need for companionship, she became instantly gentle and winsome, sensing my weariness. She brought out her harp and played dear old melodies from our childhood, deepening my love for her. She walked me to the gap that night, accompanied by her three boar-hounds. I followed her silently until she was safely inside the Hall, sending the dogs away quietly. She sang a love-song all the way home, unaware of my presence. Whether she loved me, I could not tell, though she certainly had a nice affection for me.

On the following evening, I found a cleverly dressed man of the Court standing in the gap with Mirdath. He eyed me insolently when I approached. I lifted him aside. Mirdath turned bitter upon me, calling me uncouth and brutal to a smaller man. I felt a pang of astonishment and pain, realizing she had no true love for me, or she would not have shamed me so before a stranger. There was a semblance of justice in her words, yet the man could have shown more spirit, and she had no right to shame her true friend and cousin. I bowed low to Mirdath, then apologized to the man, acknowledging my lack of courtesy. I turned and walked away, leaving them to their happiness.

I walked twenty miles that night, unable to rest. Deadly in love with Mirdath the Beautiful, my spirit and body ached with the sudden loss. For a week, I walked in other directions. Eventually, I returned to the old path. As I approached the gap, I saw Mirdath walking just outside the great wood, with the Courtier's arm around her. They were lovers; she had no brothers or youthful male kin.

When Mirdath saw me, she shamed instantly, pulling the lover's arm away and bowing, her face changed in color. I bowed low, a young man myself, and passed on, my heart dead within me. As I walked, I saw the lover return to her, wrapping his arm around her again. They looked after me as I went stiff and desperate, but I did not look back.

For a month, I avoided the gap, my love raging and my pride hurt. Yet, in that time, my love acted as a leaven, creating a sweetness and tenderness unknown to me before. Love and Pain shape the character of man. By the end of that time, I saw a little way into life with an understanding heart and resumed my walks past the gap. Mirdath never appeared, though one evening one of her boar-hounds emerged from the wood to nose against me friendly-wise. I waited, but she did not appear, and I passed on, my heart heavy but free of bitterness.

Two weary weeks passed, and I grew sick with longing. Finally, I resolved to enter the gap and approach the Hall. One evening, I did so, walking to the gardens. Lanterns and torches illuminated a great company dancing in quaint dress. A horrid dread seized me: could this be her marriage dance? No, I reasoned; I had heard of no marriage. She was turning twenty-one, ending her wardship, and this was likely a festival in her honor.

It was a bright spectacle, though I was heavy-hearted. The company was gay, the lights plentiful, and a great table spread with silver and crystal. Mirdath stepped out of the dance, lovely but pale. Since Mirdath, My Beautiful One, died and left me lonely in this world, I have suffered an anguish, and an utter and dreadful pain of longing, such as truly no words shall ever tell. For, in truth, I who had all the world through her sweet love and companionship, and knew all the joy and gladness of Life, have known such lonesome misery as doth stun me to think upon.

Yet am I to my pen again; for of late a wondrous hope has grown in me. In that I have, at night in my sleep, waked into the future of this world, and seen strange things and utter marvels, and known once more the gladness of life. I have learned the promise of the future, and have visited in my dreams those places where, in the womb of Time, she and I shall come together, and part, and again come together—breaking asunder most drearily in pain, and again reuniting after strange ages, in a glad and mighty wonder.

And this is the utter strange story of that which I have seen, and which, truly, I must set out, if the task be not too great; so that, in the setting out thereof, I may gain a little ease of the heart; and likewise, mayhap, give ease of hope to some other poor human, that doth suffer, even as I have suffered so dreadful with longing for Mine Own that is dead.

And some shall read and say that this thing was not, and some shall dispute with them; but to them all I say naught, save "Read!" And having read that which I set down, then shall one and all have looked towards Eternity with me—unto its very portals. And so to my telling:

To me, in this last time of my visions, of which I would tell, it was not as if I *dreamed*; but, as it were, that I *waked* there into the dark, *in the future of this world*. And the sun had died; and for me thus newly waked into that Future, to look back upon this, our Present Age, was to look back into dreams that my soul knew to be of reality; but which to those newly-seeing eyes of mine, appeared but as a far vision, strangely hallowed with peacefulness and light.

Always, it seemed to me when I awaked into the Future, into the Everlasting Night that lapped this world, that I saw near to me, and girdling me all about, a blurred greyness. And presently this, the greyness, would clear and fade from about me, even as a dusky cloud, and I would look out upon a world of darkness, lit here and there with strange sights. And with my waking into that Future, I waked not to ignorance; but to a full knowledge of those things which lit the Night Land; even as a man wakes from sleep each morning, and knows immediately he wakes, the names and knowledge of the Time which has bred him, and in which he lives. And the same while, a knowledge I had, as it were sub-conscious, of this Present—this early life, which now I live so utterly alone.

In my earliest knowledge of *that* place, I was a youth, seventeen years grown, and my memory tells me that when first I waked, or came, as it might be said, to myself, in that Future, I stood in one of the embrasures of the Last Redoubt—that great Pyramid of grey metal which held the last millions of this world from the Powers of the Slayers.

And so full am I of the knowledge of that Place, that scarce can I believe that none here know; and because I have such difficulty, it may be that I speak over familiarly of those things of which I know; and heed not to explain much that it is needful that I should explain to those who must read here, in this our present day. For there, as I stood and looked out, I was less the man of years of *this* age, than the youth of *that*, with the natural knowledge of *that* life which I had gathered by living all my seventeen years of life there; though, until that my first vision, I (of this Age) knew not of that other and Future Existence; yet woke to it so naturally as may a man wake here in his bed to the shining of the morning sun, and know it by name, and the meaning of aught else. And yet, as I stood there in the vast embrasure, I had also a knowledge, or memory, of this present life of ours, deep down within me; but touched with a halo of dreams, and yet with a conscious longing for One, known even there in a half memory as Mirdath.

As I have said, in my earliest memory, I mind that I stood in an embrasure, high up in the side of the Pyramid, and looked outwards through a queer spy-glass to the North-West. Aye, full of youth and with an adventurous and yet half-fearful heart.

And in my brain was, as I have told, the knowledge that had come to me in all the years of my life in the Redoubt; and yet until that moment, this *Man of this Present Time* had no knowledge of that future existence; and now I stood and had suddenly the knowledge of a life already spent in that strange land, and deeper within me the misty knowings of this our present Age, and, maybe, also of some others.

To the North-West I looked through the queer spy-glass, and saw a landscape that I had looked upon and pored upon through all the years of that life, so that I knew how to name this thing and that thing, and give the very distances of each and every one from the "Centre-Point" of the Pyramid, which was that which had neither length nor breadth, and was made of polished metal in the Room of Mathematics, where I went daily to my studies.

To the North-West I looked, and in the wide field of my glass, saw plain the bright glare of the fire from the Red Pit, shine upwards against the underside of the vast chin of the North-West Watcher—The Watching Thing of the North-West.... "That which hath Watched from the Beginning, and until the opening of the Gateway of Eternity" came into my thoughts, as I looked through the glass ... the words of Aesworpth, the *Ancient* Poet (though incredibly *future* to this our time). And suddenly they seemed at fault; for I looked deep down into my being, and saw, as dreams are seen, the sunlight and splendour of *this* our Present Age. And I was amazed.

And here I must make it clear to all that, even as I waked from *this* Age, suddenly into *that* life, so must I—*that* youth there in the embrasure—have awakened then to the knowledge of *this* far-back life of ours—seeming to him a vision of the very beginnings of eternity, in the dawn of the world. Oh! I do but dread I make it not sufficient clear that I and he were both *I*—the same soul. He of that far date seeing vaguely the life that *was* (that I do now live in this present Age); and I of this time beholding the life that I yet shall live. How utterly strange!

And yet, I do not know that I speak holy truth to say that I, in that future time, had *no* knowledge of *this* life and Age, before that awakening; for I woke to find that I was one who stood apart from the other youths, in that I had a dim knowledge—visionary, as it were, of the past, which confounded, whilst yet it angered, those who were the men of learning of that age; though of this matter, more anon. But this I do know, that from that time, onwards, my knowledge and assuredness of the Past was tenfold; for this my memory of that life told me.

And so to further my telling. Yet before I pass onwards, one other thing is there of which I shall speak—In the moment in which I waked out of that youthfulness, into the assured awaredness of *this* our Age, in that moment the hunger of this my love flew to me across the ages; so that what had been but a memory-dream, grew to the pain of *Reality*, and I knew suddenly that I *lacked*; and from that time onwards, I went, listening, as even now my life is spent.

And so it was that I (fresh-born in that future time) hungered strangely for My Beautiful One with all the strength of that new life, knowing that she had been mine, and might live again, even as I. And so, as I have said, I hungered, and found that I listened.

And now, to go back from my digression, it was, as I have said, I had amazement at perceiving, in memory, the unknowable sunshine and splendour of this age breaking so clear through my hitherto most vague and hazy visions; so that the ignorance of, Aesworpth was shouted to me by the things which now I *knew*.

And from that time, onward, for a little space, I was stunned with all that I knew and guessed and felt; and all of a long while the hunger grew for that one I had lost in the early days—she who had sung to me in those faery days of light, that *had been* in verity. And the especial thoughts of that age looked back with a keen, regretful wonder into the gulf of forgetfulness.

But, presently, I turned from the haze and pain of my dream-memories, once more to the inconceivable mystery of the Night Land, which I viewed through the great embrasure. For on none did it ever come with weariness to look out upon all the hideous mysteries; so that old and young watched, from early years to death, the black monstrosity of the Night Land, which this our last refuge of humanity held at bay.

To the right of the Red Pit there lay a long, sinuous glare, which I knew as the Vale of Red Fire, and beyond that for many dreary miles the blackness of the Night Land; across which came the coldness of the light from the Plain of Blue Fire.

And then, on the very borders of the Unknown Lands, there lay a range of low volcanoes, which lit up, far away in the outer darkness, the Black Hills, where shone the Seven Lights, which neither twinkled nor moved nor faltered through Eternity; and of which even the great spy-glass could make no understanding; nor had any adventurer from the Pyramid ever come back to tell us aught of them. And here let me say, that down in the Great Library of the Redoubt, were the histories of all those, with their discoveries, who had ventured out into the monstrousness of the Night Land, risking not the life only, but the spirit of life.

And surely it is all so strange and wonderful to set out, that I could almost despair with the contemplation of that which I must achieve; for there is so much to tell, and so few words given to man by which he may make clear that which lies beyond the sight and the present and general knowings of Peoples.

How shall you ever know, as I know in verity, of the greatness and reality and terror of the thing that I would tell plain to all; for we, with our puny span of recorded life must have great histories to tell, but the few bare details we know concerning years that are but a few thousands in all; and I must set out to you in the short pages of this my life there, a sufficiency of the life that had been, and the life that was, both within and without that mighty Pyramid, to make clear to those who may read, the truth of that which I would tell; and the histories of that great Redoubt dealt not with odd thousands of years; but with very millions; aye, away back into what they of that Age conceived to be the early days of the earth, when the sun, maybe, still gloomed dully in the night sky of the world. But of all that went before, nothing, save as myths, and matters to be taken most cautiously, and believed not by men of sanity and proved wisdom.

And I, ...how shall I make all this clear to you who may read? The thing cannot be; and yet I must tell my history; for to be silent before so much wonder would be to suffer of too full a heart; and I must even ease my spirit by this my struggle to tell to all how it was with me, and how it will be. Aye, even to the memories which were the possession of that far future youth, who was indeed I, of his childhood's days, when his nurse of that Age swung him, and crooned impossible lullabies of this mythical sun which, according to those future fairy-tales, had once passed across the blackness that now lay above the Pyramid.

Such is the monstrous futureness of this which I have seen through the body of that far-off youth.

And so back to my telling. To my right, which was to the North, there stood, very far away, the House of Silence, upon a low hill. And in that House were many lights, and no sound. And so had it been through an uncountable Eternity of Years. Always those steady lights, and no whisper of sound—not even such as our distance-microphones could have discovered. And the danger of this House was accounted the greatest danger of all those Lands.

And round by the House of Silence, wound the Road Where The Silent Ones Walk. And concerning this Road, which passed out of the Unknown Lands, nigh by the Place of the Ab-humans, where was always the green, luminous mist, nothing was known; save that it was held that, of all the works about the Mighty Pyramid, it was, alone, the one that was bred, long ages past, of healthy human toil and labour. And on this point alone, had a thousand books, and more, been writ; and all contrary, and so to no end, as is ever the way in such matters.

For years, the story had echoed through the Great Redoubt, vibrating through the stone of the thousandth floor and drifting down to the deepest tiers of the underground cities. It was a tale of a second sanctuary, a hidden refuge in the desolation of the Night Lands where the last millions of humanity had gathered to fight to the end. I had heard it everywhere during my travels, which began at my seventeenth year and lasted for three years and two hundred and twenty-five days. In those days, a child spent only one day in each city, a rigid training in the vast machinery of survival.

We traveled constantly, meeting countless souls whom I loved deeply, yet whom I could never see again, for life offered no space for lingering. Duty to the Redoubt demanded it. Yet, amidst the millions and the fleeting years, the story of the other Refuge remained constant. Libraries held volumes asserting its existence with confidence, records from ages past that seemed undeniable then but were now read with skepticism by scholars who doubted even as they studied.

I, however, possessed no such doubt. From the moment Master Monstruwacan spoke of it to me, I knew it was true. He and I shared a bond rare between mentor and student; though he was fully grown and I merely a youth, our friendship was absolute. At twenty-one, he appointed me to the Tower of Observation at the Pyramid's apex. This was the highest honor in the Redoubt, a post that satisfied the natural human curiosity for astronomy, even if the stars were forever hidden.

My appointment was justified by a singular gift: Night-Hearing. While a fanciful term, it described a rare ability possessed by none in the entire Pyramid except myself. I could hear the invisible vibrations of the aether, taking messages from the eternal darkness without instruments, often clearer than the machines themselves. To me, the voice of Mirdath the Beautiful, a love lost to an aeon ago, seemed to whisper from my memory-dreams, singing sweetly within my soul.

One day, at the fifteenth hour, the beginning of Sleep-Time, I stood pondering this ancient love. As a young man might, I imagined that lost beauty whispering into my ears. Suddenly, I thrilled as if struck. Out of the everlasting night came a whisper, thrumming against my subtle hearing.

Four long years had passed since that awakening when I was seventeen. Now, from the world-darkness of that lost life, the whisper arrived. Knowing it instantly, yet trained in wisdom, I did not answer with a name. Instead, I sent the Master-Word through the night, projecting it with my brain-elements. I knew that if the caller were truly Mirdath, she would possess the power to hear without instruments. If it were a false calling from the Evil Forces, the House of Silence, or cunning monsters, they would be unable to speak the Master-Word—a truth proven through the Everlasting.

Trembling, striving not to tense my spirit, I felt the throb of the Master-Word circle my essence. With all the sweetness in my heart, I called out mentally: "Mirdath! Mirdath! Mirdath!"

At that instant, Master Monstruwacan entered the observation chamber. Seeing my face, he stood utterly still. Though he lacked Night-Hearing, he was wise and respected my gift. He had just returned from the Receiving Instrument, expecting perhaps a faint signal too weak for the machine, and searched for me to listen.

I recounted my story, my thoughts, and that awakening. He listened with sympathy and a troubled heart, for in that age, sanity was defined differently; men could discuss matters now deemed madness, thanks to refined mental arts and strange experiments. As I spoke, I listened with my spirit. Save for a faint, happy laughter wrapping around me, I heard nothing else that day.

This connection sparked a lifelong debate with the learned men of the Redoubt. They doubted the veracity of the old stories—the Days of Light, the Sun—while my memories told tales that enchanted their hearts but angered their skeptical brains. Master Monstruwacan, however, always listened, often so absorbed in my stories drawn from Memory-Dreams that he forgot his instruments and duties. When he roused himself, he would chide gently, and the lesser Monstruwacans would flee guiltily to their posts, looking muddled and hungry for more.

Even those outside the Tower, the skeptics who hungered for truth, would listen from dawn to the fifteenth hour. A faction formed among the learned who upheld my tales as verity, and belief slowly grew. Master Monstruwacan believed from the start, a quality I cherished.

My stories traveled downward through a thousand cities. Eventually, even the ploughboys in the Underground Fields, a hundred miles deep, knew of my tales. These Fields were the mightiest work of the world, a tapering pyramid of country lands extending a hundred miles from base to top, sheathed in grey metal to protect against monsters. Lit by the Earth-Current, they were gardens of life, where millions walked and loved. Here, amidst the rustle of grain and the laughter of poppies, the memory of my dear one and that faint calling lingered.

A law in the Pyramid forbade males from adventuring into the Night Land before twenty-two, and females never. Those who ventured after receiving lectures on the horrors of the Dark Land wore a capsule implanted in their arm; if trapped, biting it would ensure their spirit's safety in death. I had considered this adventure, listening for the quiet calling, sending the Master-Word twice without certainty, but often whispering "Mirdath" into the darkness, sometimes sensing a weak, distant answer.

Then, on the thirteenth hour, the aether thrummed violently. I signaled silence, and the Tower fell still. The gentle thrilling broke into a clear, low calling in my brain: my own name, the old-earth name of this day. It struck me with the fright of fresh awakening memories. Immediately, I sent the Master-Word. A silence followed, then a beat afar off, and finally, the sure throbbing of the answer. Before this, I knew Mirdath had called; now, I had certainty.

Using the instruments, I said "Mirdath," and a swift, beautiful answer stole from the dark—an old love-name she had used only for me. I signaled the men to continue their records, for the communication was established. Master Monstruwacan stood quietly nearby, ready to note anything needed.

For a space of wonder, I spoke with the girl in the darkness. She knew my name and the old love-name, identifying herself as Mirdath. Yet, sorrow touched me as I questioned her further. Her name was not truly Mirdath; it was Naani. She had not known my name, but in the library of her refuge, there was a story of a man named by my name, called by that sweet love-name. When she first called, she had heard the cry of "Mirdath" returning to her, reminding her of the ancient tale. She had answered as the maid in the book might have.

The romance of my memory-love vanished, replaced by a strange trouble. Yet, I marveled that any book could hold a story so similar to mine, forgetting that the history of all love is written with one pen.

In that hour of quaint, foolish pain, a new thrill came. The girl wondered why my voice was not deeper. In the olden days of the Present Age, my voice had been very deep. I explained that the man in the book was said to have a deep tone, but she insisted otherwise. We argued over this triviality, yet it was a miracle: a human voice crossing the night to the Great Redoubt, perhaps breaking a million years of silence. News spread swiftly through the cities; the Hour-Slips were full of excitement.

Naani's voice verified our ancient records. Somewhere in the Outer Lands lay a second Redoubt, a small, three-sided pyramid built by weary humans escaping monster attacks. Its founder had once lived in the Great Redoubt but was exiled for irresponsibility. Tamed by fear, he planned this new refuge, aided by four million wanderers who found the Earth-Current in a great valley. They tapped the current, built the Pyramid, and maintained contact with the Great Redoubt for ages.

But the Earth-Current failed. Communication ceased, and a million years of silence followed. Populations dwindled, and for a hundred thousand years, no one was Sensitive enough to hear. Then, the Current renewed its power; aging slowed, happiness returned, and children were born anew.

Naani, the daughter of the Master Monstruwacan of the Lesser Redoubt, proved to be Sensitive. She perceived odd vibrations and urged her father to repair the ancient, rusted instruments. They built a new device, and Naani was given the right to call first. She called vaguely, and the Master-Word answered solemnly. She cried out that she was answered, and the people wept and prayed. She spoke the Master-Word, and "Mirdath! Mirdath!" rang back, stunning her into silence. When she tried to reply, the instrument failed.

For a long time, she heard the call of "Mirdath" and the beat of the Master-Word but could not answer. Stirred by the voice, she recalled an ancient book about a man and a maid named Mirdath. Filled with the awakening of those silent ages, she read the tale repeatedly, falling in love with its beauty. When the instrument was fixed, she called the man's name from the book, leading to the connection I experienced.

Often, I heard faint, sweet laughter and gentle stirrings of the aether. These came from Naani, using her brain-elements unwittingly, eager to answer my calls across the blackness.

After explaining the Lesser Refuge, Naani spoke of their plight. Food was scarce; though they had been unaware until the Earth-Current reawakened, they now tasted the lack of vitality in their food. We advised them that the soil had lost its life and taught them ways to restore it. They were eager to act, freshly alive after so long a time of half-life.

News of our reconnection spread rapidly through the Great Redoubt. Libraries filled with those seeking old records, and I was pestered with questions, nearly denied sleep. Yet, I was not spoiled; I had my work, and I was always busy listening and speaking through the darkness.

A fresh matter arose soon after. Tales of the Lesser Redoubt's dire need and calls for help came frequently. Yet, when I sent the Master-Word, there was no answering. I feared the Monsters and Forces of Evil knew. Sometimes, the Master-Word answered, confirming they had caught the beat, but then they contradicted previous messages cunningly. We realized the evil things were testing us, tempting us from safety. They knew of our speech but could not speak the Master-Word, leaving us a sure test of truth.

This awakening among the Monsters was extraordinary. Instruments recorded greater powers working in the darkness, and the Monstruwacans kept a strict watch. From the Country Whence Comes The Great Laughter, a heart-shaking thunder rolled from the unknown East. The Pit of the Red Smoke filled the Deep Valley, hiding the towers on the far side. Giants were seen around the Kilns to the East, belching great fires whose meaning we could not fathom.

From the Mountain Of The Voice, rising to the South-East, I heard the calling of the Voice for the first time. Shrill, peculiar, and horrible, it sounded like a giant-woman hungering strangely, shouting unknown words across the night. By all these signs, the Land was awakened.

Other tricks followed. Once, a call thrilled in the aether claiming humans had escaped the Lesser Redoubt, faint for food and seeking succor. But when we sent the Master-Word, the creatures without could not reply. This was a relief, proving it was a trap.

Constantly, I spoke with Naani, teaching her to send thoughts through the night with her brain-elements, warning her not to overuse the power lest it exhaust her body and mind. Despite this, her messages were weak unless she used the instrument, likely due to a lack of health force, though her Night-Hearing was keen, just less than mine.

Through many conversations and constant exchanges of thoughts and doings, we drew near in spirit, feeling a previous acquaintance in our hearts. This thrilled me strangely.

One night, toward the end of the sixteenth hour, as I prepared for sleep, the aether thrummed with a strange, electric vitality. Within that vibration, the voice of Naani rang clear and distinct, vibrating not just in the air but deep within my own soul.

Though I recognized her, I did not answer immediately. Instead, I projected the Master-Word into the darkness. Instantly, the night resonated with the steady, rhythmic beat of the response. I questioned her: Why speak by the Instrument during the watch, when all slept? The Monstruwacans in the Little Pyramid were entering their rest at the eleventh hour; it was now five hours into their sleep cycle. She should have been resting, confined to the Tower of Observation with her father. Yet, her voice was piercingly clear in my mind.

She offered no verbal reply. Instead, a sensation of profound trembling seized me as she spoke words that began:

"Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night--"

These words struck a chord of terrifying resonance. A memory-dream surged forth: I recalled speaking these exact lines to Mirdath the Beautiful in the long-gone Eternity, when she had died and left me alone in a world stripped of color. Weak with the tumult of this emotion, I called urgently to Naani, demanding an explanation for this utter turmoil in my heart.

Once more, she gave no direct answer. She repeated the words across the dark expanse of the world. Suddenly, the realization struck me: It was not Naani speaking. It was Mirdath the Beautiful, emerging from the everlasting night. I called out, "Mirdath! Mirdath!" into the void. Lo! the faint, distant voice answered again through the darkness of eternity, repeating those haunting lines. Though the voice belonged to Mirdath, it was also the voice of Naani. In my heart, I knew the truth: she had been reborn into this living time to mate once more with the Only One whose spirit and essence had been joined to mine through the ages. I called to Naani with all my strength, but there was no answer, no sign of hearing, though I called for hours.

Exhaustion claimed me, yet sleep refused to come. Eventually, I succumbed.

When I awoke, my first memory was of the wondrous event that had transpired in the sleep-time. None in this world could have known those words unless it were the spirit of Mirdath, my Beautiful One, looking down from above my shoulder in that lost time. The voice was Mirdath's; and Mirdath's voice was Naani's. What could anyone say to this but what lay in my heart?

Immediately, I called to Naani, once, then twice. Soon, the throbbing of the Master-Word filled the night, beating solemnly. I sent the word to offer assurance, and instantly, Naani's voice returned—a little weak, as it always was when she lacked the Instrument, transmitted instead through her brain-elements.

I answered her, questioning her eagerly about her words from the sleep-time. She disclaimed any knowledge, stating clearly that she had slept through the entire period and had merely dreamed a strange dream.

Confused and meditative, I soon realized that Naani's far voice was thrilling the aether around me, and she intended to recount the dream that had weighed heavily on her mind.

She described seeing a tall, dark man, built massive and dressed in unfamiliar clothing. He stood in a small room, sorrowful and lonesome. She had approached him. Presently, the man began to write to ease his sorrow. Naani could read the words, though the language was strange and unknown to her waking spirit. She could not recall what he wrote except for one short line: the word *Mirdath* appeared above it. She noted the strangeness of dreaming this name, suspecting I had implanted it during my initial calls.

With a tremor in my spirit, I asked Naani to recount what she remembered of the writing of that big, sorrowful stranger. In a moment, her far voice echoed around me:

"Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night--"

No more did she remember. Yet, this was sufficient. With a mad, strange triumph in my soul, I projected the remaining words of the line into her mind. My spirit felt them strike against Naani's consciousness with the violence of a blow. For a moment, she stumbled, dumbfounded by such newness and certainty. Then her spirit awoke fully, and she nearly wept from the fright and sudden wonder of this revelation.

Immediately, her voice thrilled all around me. It was the voice of Mirdath and the voice of Naani intertwined. I heard the tears of her spirit purify the bewildered, growing gladness of her far voice. She asked me, as one who had suddenly opened the Gates of Memory, if she might truly be Mirdath. I, utterly weak and shaken by this splendour of fulfilment, could make no instant answer. She asked again, using my old love-name, with a sureness in her far voice. Still, I was strangely dumb, the blood thudding peculiarly in my ears, before speech finally came swift.

Thus was the meeting of our spirits across the everlasting night.

Picture this: Naani stood in the world of that far eternity, her spirit speaking with mine, looking back through the part-opened gates of her memory into the past of our life and Age. She saw more than was given to me in that Age; she had memory and sight of other instances, other comings together, which held some confusion and half-meanings for me. Yet, of our present Age and life, we spoke as of some yesterday, very hallowed.

As may be conceived, the wonder of this surety stirred me fiercely to its completion. All my heart and spirit cried out to be with that one who was Mirdath, now speaking with the voice of Naani.

Yet, how could this be won? None among the learned men of the Mighty Pyramid knew the position of the Lesser Redoubt. The Records and Histories of the World offered no such knowledge, only a general thought among Students and Monstruwacans that it lay between the North-West and North-East. No man had surety; none could conceive of the distance from us to that Refuge.

Counting all this, there remained the incredible danger and peril of the Night Land, and the hunger and desolation of the Outer Lands, sometimes named the Unknown Lands.

I spoke much with Naani concerning their position. Neither she nor her father, the Master Monstruwacan of that Refuge, knew our location. They only knew that the Builder of the Lesser Redoubt had come from the Southward World in the Beginning, as their Records indicated.

Naani's father set an ancient Compass to bear. As he explained through the Instrument, such a power of the Earth-Current must belong to us that it perhaps affected the pointer from steadfastness. Indeed, the needle swung in an arc between North and South, within the Westward arc. This it had done ever for them, rendering it a helpless guide. Save that, perhaps, as we thought, the force of the Earth-Current accompanying us had some power to pull the needle toward us. If this were true, we reckoned the Lesser Redoubt lay to the North; they likewise placed us to the South. Yet, it was all built upon the sand of guess-work; nothing to adventure life and soul upon.

Curious, though a million times this had been done in past ages, we set the compass before us, taken from the Great Museum. But, as ever in that age, it spun if we but stirred the needle, stopping nowhere with surety. The flow of the Earth-Current from the "Crack" beneath the Pyramid had power to affect it away from the North, setting it wandering. This may seem strange to this present Age, yet to that, it was most true to the seeming nature of things. Harder to believe was that it ever pointed steadfastly to prove a guide of sureness and unfailing trust.

Be it known, we knew the positions of the Land by tradition, coming from that ancient time when, in the Half-Gloom, they had builded the Pyramid. They had known the use of that ancient compass and, with sight of the Sun, had named the Positions. Though we of that far future day had forgotten the very beginnings of those Names of Direction, we used them because our fathers had done so a million years and more. Likewise, we did the same with the names of day, night, weeks, months, and years. Though of the visible markings of these there was nothing but the everlasting night, it seemed very natural to that people.

Naani, heedful of my constant questions, craved with an utter keen hunger that I might come to her. Yet she forbade it, deeming it better to live and commune in the spirit than to risk my soul, and perhaps die, in the foolishness of trying to find her in all the darkness of the dead world. Yet, no heed had I taken of her commands had I but known the surety of the *direction* in which she might be discovered, and gained some knowledge of the space between. This might be named by thousands of miles, or but by hundreds; though a great distance it was surely.

Yet, one other thing had point here. When I sent my speech out into the night using my brain-elements, I came to know that, whether I had knowledge of the North or not at the moment, I turned often with a sure instinct to that Direction. The Master Monstruwacan took very great note of this. He had me experiment many times and ways, enclosing me with screens or bandages across my eyes so that I could have no external knowledge to point me the way. Yet, I would turn Northwards very frequently by a certain feeling, seeming unable of speech if forced otherwise.

When we asked Naani if she had an unusualness in this matter, she could discover none. We could but take note curiously of what affected my habits, truly setting it to the attracting of her spirit. I had mind that she was somewhere out that way in the darkness of the world; yet this was no more than supposing, as you perceive.

The Master Monstruwacan wrote a study of this matter of my Northwardness, setting it out in the Hour-Slips of the Tower of Observation. Thus it was copied by the Hour-Slips of the great cities, causing much comment and calling up to me through the home instruments. With this, and the speech going about concerning my powers to hear, I was much in talk, diversely pleased and oft angered by overmuch attention and importunity.

Now, whilst I pondered this matter in all my spirit and being—how I should some way come to Naani—a very terrible thing befell.

It was at the seventeenth hour, when all the millions of the Mighty Pyramid slept, that I was with the Master Monstruwacan in the Tower of Observation taking my due turn. Suddenly, I heard the thrilling of the aether all about me, and the voice of Naani in my soul, speaking. I sent the Master-Word into the darkness of the world, and presently, I heard the solemn answer beating steadfastly in the night. Immediately, I called to Naani with my brain-elements to know what troubled her in her sleep.

Her voice came into my spirit, weak and far and faint, so that scarce I could make out the words. Yet, in a while, I gathered that all the peoples of the Lesser Redoubt were in very deadly trouble. The Earth-Current had failed suddenly and mightily. They had called her from her sleep to listen whether we answered their callings by the Instrument; but indeed, no calling had come to us.

Those who had been of late so joyful were now grown old with sorrow in but an hour or two. They feared that the fresh coming of the Earth-Current had been but the final flicker and outburst before the end. Even in this short while of our speech, it seemed to me that the voice of Naani grew further off. I felt like to have broken my heart with the trouble of this thing.

Through all that remained of that sleep-time, I conversed with Naani as might two lovers who shall presently part forever. When the cities awoke, the news went throughout them, and all our millions were in sorrow and trouble.

Thus was it for, maybe, a little month. In that time, the voice of Naani had grown so weak and far-off that even I, who had the Night-Hearing, could scarce make real its meaning. Every word was a treasure and a touch upon my soul. My grief and trouble before this certain parting drove me that I could not eat, nor have rest. The Master Monstruwacan took upon him to chide and correct me, for if any were to help, how should it be done if I, who had the Night-Hearing and heard even now that the recording Instruments were dumb, came to ill-health?

Because of this, and such wisdom as was mine, I made to eat and order my life that I might have my full powers. Yet this was beyond all my strength. Presently, I knew that the people of the Lesser Pyramid were threatened by the monsters that beset them. Later, I had knowledge from faint, far words whispered in my brain that there had been a fight with an outside Force that had harmed many in their minds. In madness, they had opened the gate and run from the Lesser Pyramid out into the darkness of the Lands about them. There had their physical bodies fallen to the monsters of those Lands; but of their souls, who may know?

We set this assuredly to the failure of the Earth-Current, which had robbed them of all force and power. So, in those few weeks, all life and joy of living had left them. Neither hunger nor thirst had they much, nor any great desire to live; but yet a new and mighty fear of death. This doth seem very strange.

Into the Night Land

The destruction that had fallen upon the Ten-Thousand left no room for further aid, and the fresh terror of the Night Land settled heavily upon us. Those youths who now ventured onto the Road Where The Silent Ones Walk were far beyond our reach. We considered signaling them with the Home-Call, the great Voice emanating from the Machine atop the sealed base of the Mighty Pyramid, but we dared not. To do so would alert the Monsters of that Land that others were still abroad from the Pyramid. We could only hope, with trembling faith, that the Evil Forces remained ignorant of their presence, for no mortal could truly know the extent of the Powers' knowledge or ignorance.

Yet, a strange and quiet Influence lingered in the Land, recorded by our instruments but felt more deeply in the spirit. We believed it originated from the House of Silence, a grim structure on a low hill to the north of the Great Road. Many Monstruwacans feared it targeted the youths, but certainty was impossible; we could only wait and watch. Soon, the youths drew near the road's northern bend, approaching that horrid house. The Influence in the Land grew stronger, and I felt assured it came from the House, though proof remained elusive. I shared this feeling with the Master Monstruwacan, who trusted my judgment and shared my belief in the secret power radiating from the House of Silence.

Some urged sending the Home-Call to warn the youths and urge their return, but the Master refused. It was not meet to endanger their souls until we knew they were lost without intervention. The Home-Call was a voice of immense power, capable of echoing across the world, a relic of the olden days when great flying-ships traversed the sky. Its use would immediately reveal our location and the plight of the youths to the entire Land.

Day and night passed, and countless eyes peered into the Night Land, watching the youths hasten toward their fate. Talk was rampant—foolish, kind, yet devoid of courage to attempt a rescue. The Land itself seemed awake, unquiet, filled with a sense of impending doom and low, distant roars. I realized I had omitted earlier accounts of these sounds, a failure in my telling that I now acknowledge.

During this time, the youths slept not, ate but once, and pressed onward at a woeful speed toward the North along the Great Dismal Road. This confirmed our fears: they were under a spell from the House of Silence. Suddenly, a Monstruwacan reported a mighty Influence entering the Land. Simultaneously, through the Great Spy-Glass, I saw the youths break from the Road Where The Silent Ones Walk, running with desperate speed toward the House of Silence.

The Master Monstruwacan hesitated not. He sent the Home-Call across the world, pleading with the doomed youths to resist the force drawing them to destruction. He warned them that if they could not overcome the pull, they must slay themselves before entering the House. Immediately, a counter-force surged from the Pyramid, born of the prayers and soul-wishings of millions. A vast spiritual noise filled the night, stunning my spirit with its power. Tremblingly, I watched through the Spy-Glass as the youths stopped, gathered in a confused crowd, as if waking from a nightmare to find themselves in a strange place.

A great roar rose from the embrasures—five hundred thousand voices shouting in triumph. Yet, it was premature. The combined will of the millions broke against the Evil Force from the House, which drew the youths back to their running. The Pyramid fell into shaken silence, followed by lamentation. Then, a fresh wonder occurred: billows of mist, like pure white fire shining with a chilling light, appeared before the youths. This mist of cold fire halted their progress, a manifestation of the sweet Powers of Goodness striving to protect them. All the millions witnessed this, some with clarity, others doubtfully, yet all advanced in spiritual sight beyond the norm of their age.

Though none possessed the Night-Hearing to hear a soul's speech across the world, I did. A Monstruwacan reported that the Influence ceased working on the instruments, confirming that the Evil Force from the House was cut off and that a mighty Power fought for the youths' salvation. Hope and fear stirred the people, sensing a chance for return.

While the youths wavered, the Master Monstruwacan sent the Home-Call again, beseeching them to come home while the protective power shielded them. Some looked toward the Pyramid, reading the message of love from their mothers. But in a moment, they turned, obedient to a leader named Aschoff, a great athlete of the Nine-Hundredth-City. Unwittingly, Aschoff destroyed their souls; he leaped into the billows of bright, shining fire that formed the barrier. The fire sank into nothingness, and Aschoff, followed faithfully by all, resumed their run toward the House of Silence.

They reached the low hill and the great open doorway, which had been open since the Beginning, through which the cold, steadfast light and inscrutable silence of Evil had made a silence felt across the Land. Uncased windows emitted the utter silence of unholy desolation. Aschoff ran in, followed by the two hundred and fifty wholesome, innocent youths. They never emerged, unseen by any human thereafter.

Mothers and Fathers watched from the Redoubt, seeing the tragedy unfold. Though some hoped for their return, everyone knew in their hearts the young men had gone to Destruction. The night spoke horror to all souls, and a sudden, utter quiet fell upon the Land. To me, who possessed the Night-Hearing, came a great Fear—not of sound, but of the agony whispered into my spirit by the Quietness of the night. No sound reached my ears, for the youths had passed into a Silence the heart cannot conceive.

This strange Quiet, brooding within the night, was horrid beyond any roaring that had passed before. It offered my spirit no rest compared to the far-echoing thunder of the Great Laughter, the whining from the Silver-fire Holes, the Baying of the Hounds, or the Roaring of the Giants. These dreadful sounds could not offend me as this silence did.

No one thought they could succour the Peoples of the Lesser Redoubt, nor knew where it stood. Thus, they must suffer and come to their end alone, a sad and dreadful thought. The Great Pyramid had already suffered sorrow and calamity from previous attempts, bringing only failure and grief to mothers and wives. Now, this dread horror concerned the lost youths.

The sure knowledge that we could offer no aid weighed heavily on my heart. I had foolishly held vague hopes of secretly exploring the Night to discover the Lesser Pyramid and rescue the thousands. Above all, I dreamed of stepping out of the mystery and terror to put forth my arms to Naani, saying, "I am That One." Knowing she, who had been mine in a bygone Eternity, would surely recognize me instantly and come to me again. To think she might be suffering horror in the power of some foul Monster was like madness. Nearly, I could seize the Diskos and run unprepared into the evil and terror of the Night Land to find her or cast off my life in the attempt.

Often, I called to Naani, sending the Master-Word beating through the night to assure her it was indeed I speaking, and no foul thing spelling evil and lies. I instructed her never to be tempted from the shelter of the Redoubt by messages from the night, but to await the Master-Word, knowing no true friend would seek to entice her into the dark. I spoke with her via brain-elements, sending words silently into the dark. It was doleful and weariful to speak into the void without hearing the answering beat of the Master-Word or the sweet, faint voice whispering within my soul. Yet, once or twice, the aether thrilled weakly around me, and to my inward hearing, the Master-Word beat faintly in the night, offering a little comfort that the love-maid of my memory-dreams still lived.

Constantly putting forth my soul to harken, my health failed. I chided my being for lacking wiser control, fighting to remain sane. Day by day, my heart grew more weary and restless; life seemed a small matter against the great loss my heart felt. Often, a Voice spoke plainly out of the night, purporting to be Naani's. I always replied with the Master-Word, silencing it. I did not jeer at its failure to bewitch me but let it bide. The Voice would fall silent, then call again, but I never spoke with it, for therein lay danger to the soul. Instead, I shut it from my memory, thinking only of sweet and holy matters, often of Naani, which was both sweet and holy to my spirit.

Thus, Monsters without in the Night tormented me, perhaps intending to lure me to destruction or simply plaguing me with malice. Considering my trouble and giving my strength to Naani through the night worked upon me; I grew thin, plainly visible to those who loved me. The Master Monstruwacan, who loved me as a son, chided me gently with wise speech, making me love him the more, yet I gained no health. My heart destroyed me, as love held back and made always to weep does.

It may seem strange that my Mother and Father did not talk with me, but I had neither parent for many years—a fact I should have noted earlier so none would waste thought pondering it. Blame lies with my telling.

Concerning my love-trouble, a certain thing happened that gave me resolve. One night, I woke from a sorely troubled sleep, and it seemed Naani called my olden love name in a voice of utter anguish and beseeching. I sat up, sent the Master-Word into the Night with my brain-elements, and presently there was the solemn beat of the Master-Word answering, weak and faint, scarce audible. I called again to Naani, who was Mirdath, giving her assurance and urging her to tell me what was wrong and pitiful. Who shall be amazed that I was shaken with the eagerness of my spirit, for it had been so long since Naani spoke clearly within my soul?

Yet, though I called many times to the everlasting night, no more came the voice of Naani speaking strangely within my spirit; only at times a weak thrilling of the aether about me. At last, maddened with sorrow and the sense of harm about the maid, I stood upright, raised my hands, and gave word and honor to Naani through all the blackness of the night. I declared I would no longer abide within the Mighty Pyramid for my safety while she, who had been mine Own through Eternity, came to horror and destruction by the Beasts and Evil Powers of that Dark World. I gave the word with my brain-elements, bidding her be of heart, for until I died I would seek her. Out of the Darkness came naught but silence.

I clothed myself swiftly and went up quickly to the Tower of Observation to speak instantly with the Master Monstruwacan. My heart burned with intention to act speedily. I told him everything, declaring I meant no longer to suffer in quiet and to no end, but to make adventure into the Night Land to find Naani or perhaps find a swift peace from my long troubling.

When the Master Monstruwacan heard my words, it sat heavily upon him. He besought me long and many times to refrain, stating that none might achieve such a task and that I would be lost in my youth before many days passed. Yet to all his speech I said naught, save that this thing was laid upon me, and even as I had promised, so should I act. In the end, the Master perceived I was set to this thing and not to be moved. He pointed out my leanness due to troubling and advised wisdom to wait and regain full strength.

But even as I was, so would I go. I told him gently, showing how it was meet and helpful to the safety of my soul. My strength was still in me, yet I was sweeter in spirit because I stood lean and pure; much poor dross and littleness had been burned from me, so that fear was not in me. All I laid to the count of my love, which purifies and makes sweet and fearless the human heart. Because I was as I had said, I was less in trouble of the Forces of Evil, for long and sore had been my Preparation of Spirit. I knew none had ever gone forth into the Darkness so long withholden from that which weakens and taints the spirit.

Here, let me set down how the Three Days of Preparation, proper to those willing to go forth into the Night Land, had for their chief aim the cleansing of the spirit so that the Powers of Evil had less ability to harm. Also, as I have said, none should go forth in ignorance of the full dreadfulness of all that held the Night. During the Preparation, certain horrors were made known that were not told to the young—horrid mutilations and abasements of the soul that shook the heart with fear if whispered into the hearing. These things were not set down in any book easily come by but were warded and safely locked by the Master of the Preparation in the Room of Preparation.

Indeed, when I heard what I was soon to hear, I wondered in my heart that ever any went out into the Night Land, or that the Room of Preparation should have other than students who meant not to go forth but only to achieve knowledge of what had been done and might be done again. Yet, in verity, this is the way of the human heart; it has always been and will be so in all the years forever. For to adventure is the lust of Youth, and to leave Safety is the natural waywardness of the spirit. Who shall reprove or regret? It would be sorrowful if the Spirit of Man should cease. Yet, it must not be thought that I uphold fightings to the death or mutilation between man and man; rather, I sorrow upon this thought.

To those of the present age, this thought would seem quaint, perhaps impossible.

Eventually, I reached the Circle surrounding the Redoubt. To my surprise, it was not the massive structure I had reasoned it should be. Instead, it was a small, clear tube, barely two inches thick, yet it emitted a brilliant, intense light that seemed to expand when viewed from a distance. This tiny beacon had witnessed countless things; I remembered seeing beast-monsters peering over it, their faces emerging from the night. As a child, I had kept watch through the embrasures, hoping to be the first to spot a monster gazing inward at the Mighty Pyramid. We took pride in identifying the ugliest horrors, treating it as a game until a more fearsome brute appeared. Now, looking at that small tube, a half-familiar shudder ran through me, mingling with a childish joy in the safety that made such dangers seem manageable.

These memories surged forth, causing me to hesitate instinctively, fearing what might emerge from the Dark beyond. But soon, my heart cleared, and I gained the courage to bid farewell. I turned to gaze upon the wondrous Home of the Last Millions. The sight was breathtaking; a mountain of life rising measurelessly into the night, spreading across the earth to hold the world in its grasp.

On the nearer slopes stood three hundred thousand great embrasures, totaling a million and twenty thousand across the four sides of the Redoubt, as recorded in the schoolbooks and the quaintly named Atlas volumes. Half a mile upward began the lowest tier of these window-places, followed by countless others stretching into the distance. A great light poured from them, illuminating the darkness for miles. I looked up, separating each embrasure from its neighbor, until they faded into a constant, glimmering fire that shaped a shining peak against the black heavens. This was the Everlasting Monument.

I realized that the tiny shapes clustered against the embrasures were the countless millions of the Redoubt. In the lower tiers, they were visible like insects against the inner light, watching me through spy-glasses. I knew they looked out, and I returned my gaze upward along the grey metal slope, following it to the little star crowning the Wonder of the World—the Tower of Observation where I had spent my life. I knew Master Monstruwacan bent his Great Spy-Glass upon me, the lens through which I had so often spied. I raised the Diskos in salute and farewell, though I saw him not.

My heart swelled, yet my soul grew stronger. Then, a murmur rose from the night, dim and distant. I saw the little shapes in the lower embrasures moving constantly. Instantly, I understood that the Multitudes were taking my salute, crying out and waving farewells, or perhaps pleas to return.

Alone before that great mountain of metal and life, I knew the danger of lingering. I raised the Diskos, reversed it, offering a salute from one young man to all the Millions. I looked swiftly upward through eight great miles of night toward the Final Light, ensuring my friend knew I thought of him in this last moment. Perhaps the invisible millions high in the Upper Cities interpreted this too as a farewell; a faint, vague wind-like sound drifted down from the monstrous heights.

Lowering the Diskos, I turned away. I stepped across the Circle into the lonesomeness of the Night Land, breasting the Air Clog. I looked back no more; the sight of my Home weakened my heart, and I resolved not to glance behind for a great while.

Yet, as I walked, the world's aether surged around me, carrying the continual prayers, wishes, and watches of my people and kin. This gave me a sense of companionship, but it also brought a chilling realization: this disturbance might signal an Evil Force that I was abroad in the Land. How could I stop it? At first, I walked blindly, intent only on putting distance between myself and the ache in my heart. But soon, I slowed, applying reason to my path. I feared the over-watchfulness of the area trodden by the Youths, so I altered my course. Instead of heading directly North, I turned North-West, intending to circle around the back of the North-West Watcher, then proceed to the North of the Plain of Blue Fire, eventually finding a straight path North. This detour would take me clear of the House of Silence, which terrified me more than any other horror in the Land.

This made the journey longer, but survival demanded caution. Haste led to destruction; slowness offered a chance of success. My assurance in heading North came from an inward knowing and from studies of olden books within the Pyramid.

One such book, a strange, ancient metal volume forgotten in the Great Library for perhaps a million years, had recently caught my attention. While much of its content was dismissed as fairy tales by my contemporaries, I sensed ancient truths beneath the shell. It recounted a catastrophic event from a time long past: the world braked upward in a vast earthquake, rending the globe for a thousand miles. A mighty chasm opened, swallowing an ocean, shaking all cities, and blanketing the earth in mist and rain.

While the people of the Mighty Pyramid treated this as mere entertainment for nurseries, this specific book quoted witnesses with a gravity that demanded belief. Its final sections described a later age, perhaps a million years hence, when the Sun was dying. In that time, the Mighty Chasm had settled into a deep, wondrous Valley holding seas, forests, and lands of varying climates—some healthy, others filled with fire, steam, and poisonous sulfur clouds.

Here, in the warmth of the valley floor, Great Beasts bred anew, born from primal forces. Humanity, having grown soft through ages of ease, faced a cold, unfriendly world as the Sun waned. A hardy race emerged, fighting for survival. They discovered the Valley was a place of warmth and life and began the arduous climb down the wondrous height. Generations died climbing "The Road," unaware of the destination, until finally, they reached the bottom.

They fought monsters, built cities, and extended the Road along the Valley. But at the Great Corner where the Sun's light failed, they encountered a constant shadow. Driven by the horror of the Valley of Shadow and its evil things, the Road Makers retreated to the Red Light of the Westward Valley, living there for a hundred thousand years. There, in ignorance despite their wisdom, they experimented with harmful forces, opening gates to disaster.

When the twilight of the world deepened, madness spread among the city dwellers. Cities were attacked by monsters from the West, sparking a Pandemonium. An Age of Sorrows followed, breeding a determined generation and a Leader who united the sound Millions. He waged a mighty battle against foulness, scattering enemies up and down the Valley.

He warned his people of the growing Darkness and the dread powers abroad, urging them to build a Mighty Refuge. They acclaimed the idea, and a Great House was begun, but it proved insufficient. The Leader led the Peoples to wander, eventually reaching the Bight where the Great and Mighty Pyramid was finally constructed.

This was the sense of the book, discussed briefly with Master Monstruwacan before my sudden resolve to leave took hold. We agreed that the Great Road of the Silent Ones must be the same path made by those hardy peoples. If the Lesser Redoubt existed, it must lie within the mighty Valley. Whether the Road leading West to the Ab-humans or the Northward path would lead me there remained uncertain. I might wander a thousand miles astray, or fall into dreadful trouble before finding it.

There was little hope the Lesser Pyramid lay West or North beyond the House of Silence. Yet, I felt a strong certainty it lay to the North. The Valley was the only logical refuge; the upper world, a hundred miles up in the night, was too bleak and deadly for life, lacking the sweet, needful air found in the deep places of the earth.

Strange to think of the wondrous cliffs girding us, hidden in the dark, unknown except through the book's telling. We had always believed we lived in the bed of an ancient sea, sloping gradually away, not abruptly up into savage heights. The general populace held no deep conviction of such hidden worlds; they accepted the strange fires, the ever-abiding night, and the monsters as the natural order. Some fancied possibilities, but the majority were content with what they saw.

With this certainty, I made speed towards the North. I knew there were only two paths: the cold, dead lonesomeness of the hidden upper world, or the Valley below. The Valley held the answer. Though I had taken a circuitous route for sound reasons, I pressed on, driven by the hope that the Road Makers' legacy would guide me to safety.

As I pressed northward and westward, I navigated with extreme caution, steering clear of the Great Watcher looming in the northwest. My mind raced through the logistics of this monumental journey. First, I calculated my pace, concluding that moderation was essential; the destination was unknown, and the path ahead was vast. Next, I organized my routine: eating and drinking every six hours, with a block of sleep from the eighteenth to the twenty-fourth hour. This schedule allowed for three meals and six hours of rest daily. While this regimen seemed ideal, the Night Land was fraught with peril, and I knew I would often be forced to abandon sleep to watch for threats.

True to human nature, I broke this discipline immediately. For twenty-one hours, I walked without rest, hiding and creeping through areas likely to reveal me to the Watcher. Hunger gnawed at me, making the thought of food repulsive. Only after those grueling hours did exhaustion force me to seek shelter. In the distance, I spotted a small fire-hole, a familiar sight from my earlier travels. It offered warmth against the biting cold and a potential dry spot for slumber.

Approaching cautiously, I found it to be a cheerful enclave amidst the gloom. A dull, bubbling fire filled the hole, emitting a faint sulfur smoke. Sitting nearby, I placed my Diskos on the rock beside me. For a while, I remained motionless, too weary to eat or drink, my gaze fixed on the Mighty Pyramid. Though I had traveled far, it still appeared terrifyingly close, its immense scale shaking me with the weight of the task ahead. Yet, there was solace in its proximity; I knew millions watched over me from within. I made no signal, for a constant farewell was inappropriate; the command was simply to *GO*. It was strange to behave so distantly from humanity while standing so near my home, yet I accepted this solitude. I was happy to imagine that Master Monstruwacan, my dear friend, might be observing me through the Great Spy-Glass at that very moment.

Realizing my hesitation to eat was foolish, I opened my scrip and consumed three tablets of dense, strong food. They were not filling, so I shook dust from a special tube into a cup. The air acted upon the dust chemically, causing it to boil and fizz until it transformed into simple water. Thus, my small pack sustained me for a long time, a necessary comfort in this dark, hungry land.

I reviewed my gear, retrieving a small compass crafted by Master Monstruwacan. He had warned me that I might lose my way in the vast darkness, but if the ancient principle within the machine still functioned—perhaps oriented toward the Pyramid rather than the North—it could guide me home. I set it on the ground, but it spun wildly. I realized I was still high above the Underground Fields, too far from the "Crack" for the Earth-Current to stabilize the needle. I wondered if Monstruwacan could see my test through the Spy-Glass, though the shifting lights and rising mists of the Night Land made visual confirmation unreliable.

Eventually, I packed the compass and prepared for sleep. I recalled that, after many days of travel, I had indeed found the needle drawn northward—a comforting thought. Later, far from the Redoubt, I discovered a new power: the compass pointed westward, indicating a Great Power in the Darkness swaying it. This revelation brought a childlike wonder; it suggested the ancient Northward Force was active, a knowledge felt in the heart rather than just understood by the brain. I wondered if the Lesser Redoubt was influencing the needle, signaling my approach to the journey's end, but I trusted the North.

Wrapped in my cloak, I lay by the fire-hole, the Diskos nestled against my chest. As I drifted into a drowsy state, I felt the aether surge around me, stirred by the millions of watchers in the Redoubt who sympathized with my spirit. Exhausted, I slept deeply, my last conscious thought of Naani, the sweet maid I sought. In dreams, she spoke to me, surrounded by fairy-light, freeing me from life's sorrow.

Suddenly, a mighty howl shattered the silence—the Home-Call. Swiftly, I slid from my cloak, gripping the Diskos. I knew a Terror was approaching; the Night Land had been awakened. Peering toward the Pyramid, I scanned the upper blackness where the Tower of Observation's Final Light shone. Then, I saw them: quick, darting flashes of green fire. In the Set-Speech, they spelled a urgent warning: a Great Grey Man had scent of me and was crawling through the moss-bushes behind the fire-hole. The message was sharp: leap left and hide.

I vanished into the shadows of a moss-clump, my heart trembling with cold terror. From the bushes beyond the fire, the Grey Man emerged. It was a great, grey creature, laying its head to the ground to spy along the earth, avoiding the fire's glare. I stood back as it died, the Diskos spinning in my hand, sending forth fire as if alive, celebrating the slaying of the horrid monster. I moved to the far side of the fire-hole, holding the Diskos high and spinning to signal my victory to the Redoubt.

Master Monstruwacan spoke no further words, for it was not meet to disturb the aether unnecessarily. Calming my fear, I felt the gladness of the millions in the Redoubt vibrating through the night air, a wave of sympathetic fear and friendship. I gathered my wits, realizing I had slept through ten hours, violating my own rules. Resolving to obey reason in the future, I ate three more tablets and drank water, then circled the fire-hole before turning back toward the Pyramid. I made no salutation, wishing to avoid stirring the emotions of the Millions.

I moved swiftly and cautiously into the night, bearing the Diskos with a newfound love for the strange weapon that had befriended me. It seemed to live with the fire of the Earth-Current, a bond shared only by those of olden days who carried swords. The Redoubt knew that the Diskos belonged to its user; a stranger handling it would find it clumsy and dangerous. This affinity was a law of the land, rooted in the connection between man and his tool.

As I ventured deeper, the perspective shifted. Like a traveler ascending to the stars, the landscape rearranged itself. The fixed visions of my life within the Redoubt dissolved; the Night Land revealed new aspects with every step. Approaching the monstrous Watcher of the Northwest, I crept within a mile, only to be confounded by its appearance. Seen from the side, it resembled a vast cliff face hanging over the Red Pit, scored and weathered by the bloody shine rising from below. Lying prone, I stared into its monstrous bulk, a squat, rugged hill of blackness dotted with warts that glowed like lunar mountains under the red fire.

A profound unease settled over me. I felt the aether disturbed by the distress of the Millions, sensing their cunning awareness of my location. The thrilling vibration brought wisdom: I was foolish to linger. The Watcher might sense my trouble. Gathering courage, I retreated to a safe distance, feeling the quiet, steadfast life of the Monster surrounding me, a Great Evil Power gazing upon me. At the eighteenth hour, I paused to eat and drink, looking back at the Watching-Thing, a silent, solitary sentinel against the red glow. I continued for six more hours, reaching "The Place Where The Silent Ones Kill." It was a barren, rocky plain where no bushes grew, offering no hiding. A faint, grey, lichen-like light covered the desolation, creating shifting shadows that hinted at the presence of Silent Ones. Sliding quietly through the moss-bushes to the north of this plain, I searched for movement but saw none.

The plain stretched out before me, ending abruptly. I spotted a small, bare patch and decided to run across it to save time. As I rose from the bushes, I saw vague figures in the greyness: a line of lofty, shrouded figures standing motionless, watching me. My heart failed, but they did not advance. Finding courage, I backed slowly into the bushes and circled wide to avoid the Plain of Destruction.

After twenty-four weariful hours, I sought a fire-hole for warmth. Near one, I heard a large, husky voice speaking ordinary things in a monstrous, house-like tone. I fell into a hole among the moss-bushes behind me, choking on dust and ash. I lay still, refusing to breathe or cough. The search passed southward, saved by some unseen power.

Emerging shaken and weak, I lay in the bushes for ten hours, starving and freezing. Upon waking, I stamped my feet and warmed myself, then ate four tablets and drank double the usual water to replenish my emptiness. I resumed my journey, pausing to look at the Watcher's humped back, fueling my hatred for the monster. I gazed upward at the Great Redoubt, feeling doubly lost, but quickly turned away, gathering my courage.

Twelve hours later, I heard a strange, low humming noise from above, seemingly coming from a great distance yet close overhead. It was the Sound of the Doorways in the Night. History recorded that those who heard it usually met destruction by invisible evil forces. Panic seized me; I bared my arm to access the death capsule beneath my skin, ready to die instantly. I crept silently away, but the sound faded. I realized I had passed beneath the opening before it fully manifested. Shaken, I continued, hearing the sound again further ahead. I hid, then ran, only to realize I had passed it safely. The sound was a phantom of my fear.

By the eighteenth hour, I reached a safe hollow to sleep. Afterward, I walked through a bleak, desolate country, avoiding the Doorways. I passed nineteen great fire-holes and four small ones. At one, I saw a creature resembling a scorpion but squat and thick. I felt a restful spirit as I avoided monsters, growing bolder until I reached the North-West border of the Plain of Blue Fire.

This Plain was a terrifying void, a cold blue light rising from the earth that swallowed all vision. Hidden peoples of spirits wandered within it, calling to one another blindly. I kept two miles away, coasting along the edge for two days, sleeping under bushes and on rock ledges, bitter cold but unharmed. On the third day, emerging from the Plain, I saw the Mighty Pyramid again, a distant speck of light on my right. The aether stirred as news of my reappearance spread through the Redoubt. The Millions thought of me, their emotions rippling through the night, a sweet, homely sympathy that bolstered my spirit.

At the eighteenth hour, I found a boiling hot fountain shooting water northward. Following the steam, I found the water warm and bathed my tender feet. Suddenly, the bay of a Night-Hound echoed from the northwest. Horror struck me; the beast was tracking me. I leaped into the shallow stream, running down its middle. The Hound's bellow followed, closing the distance. I stopped, then dove into deeper water, lying on my belly, head above the surface. The Hound galloped past, tearing the earth, then bayed further up the stream. I stayed submerged, motionless, until the beast passed. I rose and followed the warm stream, keeping to the water to mask my scent, traveling twelve hours while the Hound searched endlessly.

The stream led me to the House of Silence. I was terrified, for my goal was to avoid it. The river breached the Road Where The Silent Ones Walk, leading directly to the House. I left the water, creeping through the moss-bushes westward, away from the House. Soon, the bushes thinned, revealing bare rock. I retreated, finding the Road bent dangerously close to the House. Above me, the monstrous, huge structure stood in the night, full of quiet lights, brooding over the land. The silence around it was an anguish of terror. I hid among the bushes, feeling the sheer dread of the place, remembering the youths who had entered it.

If there had been air and skill sufficient unto this purpose of flying, yet I had been wicked with foolishness that I should work to be hung upward in the night for all the Evil of the Night Land to behold. And though I had gone up some great way, the machine had surely made a great noise in the quietness of the eternal night, as you shall suppose.

Now indeed am I gone weary that I should need to tell so much concerning the air of that Time and Place; for surely I do seem to make this my story as that I did make a lecturing upon matters of chemistry. So I cast about that I may not bother to tell more upon this matter. Yet in truth, a little more of my thinkings and observings had I better set down here and be done with it. But you shall have patience with me, and know that had this my story been no more than an idle tale, I had been free to make no labour with such matters.

Now there doth a wonder come to me why the Road Makers, who were of that far-off Age before the Age of the Mighty Pyramid, did not *fly* downward from the upper world into the deep of the monstrous valley, but did instead build a road.

Yet it may be that the air of the upper world had grown thin a great age, so that they had truly forgot that once man did have power to fly. Moreover, the downward world that was the bottom of the Great Valley was full of monsters, as was told in the little metal book. Two hours later, I had descended from the Gorge and stood upon that alien country. Though my mind remained troubled and bewildered, a surge of joy welled up within me at the startling brilliance of this sudden land.

Before reaching the mouth of the Gorge, I had stood high within its throat, gazing out over the vast expanse. I counted twenty-seven great volcanoes, ignoring two monstrous ranges of fire-hills burning to my right, nor did I tally the hundred thousand lesser fires scattered about. Truly, it appeared a realm of fire and water. A small fire-hill stood isolated within a sea, perhaps a mile from where I stood, with a dozen or so more spreading behind it. Here, I must explain the seas: I counted three small ones at that time, and a mighty sea stretching endlessly into the red light of the fire-hills, vanishing utterly from sight with no visible end.

Islands rose from these waters, crowned with volcanoes; in other places, the fire-hills erupted straight from the sea. Over the near sea, a plentitude of steam hung, suggesting the ocean boiled at intervals in diverse places. Within the red atmosphere of the place, a low, constant muttering thunder shook the air, shifting from distance to nearness. I judged this to be the voices of the fire-hills, speaking through the fire that lived within them.

How utterly new this was to me! There existed in that country a constant Voice of the Energy of Life, restoring the World-Noise of our Age with keen and undeniable apparentness, perhaps even more intense than it is now.

Let me set down more closely what was ready to my gaze. First, a huge, blackened mountain rose to the left of the Gorge's mouth, ascending into the night for perhaps fifteen or twenty miles. From its side grew a mighty peaked volcano, rising five miles high on the far side. Above it, a second peak soared another nine or ten miles into the blackness. Furthermore, two other mighty fire-hills burned and glowed at an utter height upon the left crest of that black mountain, appearing to make strange, smouldering suns within the night. Below these upward fires rose vast mountains of ash and burned stuff, cast forth by the perched volcanoes and pouring downward through Eternity to build grey, sombre monuments to the dreadful glory of Time.

To my right lay endless sea and the red blazing of the fire-hills; to my left, mighty forests rose, with monstrous fire-hills appearing beyond the great woods. This was the first impression upon my brain and senses.

After descending from the Gorge, I paused, unsure which way led to my search. Upon returning to the Gorge, I saw there was but one way: to the right lay only the seas, but to the left, where the shores met the waters, a clear path seemed to extend for a space. Perhaps, having come so far, I would find a further way forward. Thus, I descended again into the Country of the Seas, that red-shining realm of water and fire.

By the time I returned from the Gorge's height, twenty-four hours had passed since my last sleep. I was forced to seek a nook to slumber. I found a neat place where three great trees surrounded a small, dry, warm rock basin. After eating three tablets and drinking some water—my belly yearning as ever for proper food—I made my bed in the rock basin and lay down, thinking briefly of Naani before falling asleep.

Suddenly, I awoke to find myself swimming in hot water. A mercy, I thought, that I had not drowned. I stood up; the basin was full of hot, steaming water, pungent to the taste. I realized the water poured in from a smoothed slit on the far side, gurgling and bubbling, driven upward by a deep, boiling well. Gladly, it did not boil as it emerged.

Now on dry land, I quickly surmised that the water poured forth into the basin seasonally for eternity, then receded through fissures in the basin's bottom, a cycle repeating slightly beyond the length of every hour. Indeed, the basin emptied slowly as I watched.

Much wetted, I stripped off my armor, having previously dipped my effects into the hot pool. I found a spot where the rock was hot and spread my garments. While they dried, I entered the pool for a pleasant bath, feeling no great fear of danger; I had surely left such threats behind in the Night Lands. Yet, I held the Diskos at the pool's edge, lacking proper assurance. As it proved, many monstrous beasts inhabited that country, but I never felt the nearness or horrid power of any Evil Force. These, I conceived, were gathered about the Mighty Pyramid, attracted by the great spiritual essence of so many humans crowded in one spot, much like sharks following a ship carrying cattle. Yet, how the Evil Powers gained entrance to this state of life, I knew not; my reasoning on the matter was surely vanity, for there is no certainty in my conclusions.

Presently, my clothing was dry. Before dressing, I emerged from the bath, which had nearly drained back into the earth. I donned my armor and felt a more lightsome state of mind, yet remained ready for sleep. I slept six more hours beside the pool, woken occasionally by the gurgling and bubbling as the pool filled again.

When the six hours ended, I awoke refreshed, ready to proceed through the red-lighted country. I ate and drank, then moved forward at a great pace. The nameless woods lay to my left, the shores of the seas to my right. Often, trees grew into the water, forcing me to walk among them. A wonderful mystery lived among these forest trees, a glad and wildness unknown to me in the Underground Fields, though those groves possessed solemn beauty. The scent of the woods was sweet to my spirit.

Throughout my journey, the shore remained to my right, while the great forests surrounded me to the left. Life thrived in those darksome woods; living eyes peered out at me occasionally before retreating into the dark. I did not know whether to fear or ignore the trouble, yet nothing came near to cause harm.

Three times that day I encountered little fire-hills burning redly, sending out fire and noise. I heard their trouble through the forest before reaching them. Around each was a deadness and desolation where the fire had killed the big trees, yet the quick life of little plants grew more nigh, born and living between the fire-bursts. In that single day, I passed thirty-seven boiling springs. Whether they truly boiled, I knew not, only that they sent out great steam often, and some made a strong roaring noise, sounding like wild beasts roaring strangely when heard afar off in the forests.

At the eighteenth hour, I sat down as I had at the sixth and twelfth, eating two tablets and drinking water that fizzed richly and quickly. Then I lay down to sleep, greatly wearied. I chose a place against a great rock so that no creature could approach from behind. I fell easily into sleep, but fixed in my mind that I would slumber only with my body, knowing from the shining eyes in the darksome woods that strange creatures abode in the mighty forests.

Before sleeping, I thought of Naani, as I had much that day, feeling her spirit hovering near, striving pitifully to speak with me. I set this out so you may know how it seemed to me in my thoughts. As I lay there, I placed a blessing upon her and determined to make a more desperate speed of my going, if possible, to reach the strange, unknown place in the dead world where the lesser Refuge stood. Then I was asleep in a moment.

Suddenly, I awoke. Lovely was the brightness of that country, showing me my danger instantly, unlike the grey darkness and strange shadows of the Night Land. On the instant I propped myself up, I saw certain things crouching within the borders of the trees, no more than a score of paces off. My spirit had given me knowledge and woken me. They watched me, their eyes shining like beast eyes, filling me with strange terror. Yet I had the Diskos and my armor. Though my heart shook a little, my spirit was assured to conquer.

I stood up, the Diskos ready in my hand. Behold! I could not see the Humped Men; they were gone from that place. Yet I never saw them leave, keeping my gaze steadfast on where they hid. I was near ready to believe there had been nothing within the wood's border, yet truly I knew the humped men had been there, as I had seen.

I looked presently and found I had slept five hours. I ate two tablets while standing watchful, then drank some water, ready to go forward. I was grown very anxious to leave that place, knowing with no certainty but that those strangely humped men were but a little way off among the trees, able to strike me in a moment, or further, to call an army of others to my destruction.

Ready with my gear secure, I set off at a great stride, holding the Diskos handy, looking this way and that while moving onward with speed. I felt so lean and hard that it seemed I had the power to outpace those men or anything of their kind.

All that day, for thirty great hours, I went forward at that stride, always watching. At every sixth hour, I ate two tablets, drank a little water, and continued. I hoped I was lost from those Humped Men. Yet my faith was not wise; twice and thrice it came to me that things went through the woods to my left all that day, keeping always to a level with my speed, yet always hidden. As you shall believe, this was a very shaking thing to my heart, making my hope of little account.

Lacking faith to accompany my hope, I found it difficult to sleep until I reached a proper, safe place. So, as I told, I went onward through thirty great hours, finding nowhere that seemed to fit my need.

Lo! About the end of the thirtieth hour, I perceived water ahead, besides the sea ever to my right. I thought perhaps the sea went inward there, but it was otherwise. When I reached that place, I found a river coming into the sea, flowing out from all the country to my left.

In the mouth of this river lay a small island. Surely I looked across to the island, thinking it a refuge from the Humped Men playing dog upon my going. Yet, truly, this was an idle thought. My need was to find a way to cross the river to go forward beside the great sea, which stretched onward seemingly forever before me on the far side.

I knew not how to cross; I had no power to swim, and if I had, there were surely monsters in that great, warm-flowing river.

I went up the river-bank to find where the river narrowed. I would have walked a mighty distance for this purpose, but I soon came to a second river entering the first, not a mile above the sea's shore. Thus, the sea was to one side, the second river to the other, and the first river before me. I was sorely perplexed, as anyone truly in such trouble would be.

Yet, the need to go forward and the danger of the Humped Men put wit into me. I looked about for a fallen tree. There were many, yet great, so that I was much wearied and strained of spirit before getting two little trees to the water.

When this was done, I made a rough pole from a young tree-plant. Afterward, I lashed the two trees together with my belts and straps, creating something of a raft.

Picture this: all the time I worked, I was very heedful lest the Humped Men come upon me before I was free upon the water. This constant heeding doubled the labor of my work, yet in the end, it was done, and I was ready to adventure over the river.

I pushed off with the pole, paddling perhaps half an hour, for indeed it was a clumsy contrivance, and I perhaps more so in my labors. Presently, I came so far over that I drew nigh to the island. By this time, it was thirty-three hours since my last slumber, so I was bitterly wearied.

I had a great, restful sleep. Truly, the island seemed a very safe place. In verity, I came to no harm, though I was as a dead man for nine great hours, so you shall perceive my weariness.

When I awoke properly, I ate two tablets, drank some water, finished my voyage, and took back my belts and straps from binding the trees, continuing forward on my way, no longer fearful of the Humped Men. Surely, I thought I had left them all on the far shore of the river, though afterward I minded they might grow on both sides. Yet, I was only discovered by those on one side, as you know.

All that day I went very swiftly, passing many strange matters and coasting upon wonders often. At the sixth and twelfth hours, I ate and drank as ever. Between the eighth and fourteenth hours, I passed two mighty fire-hills that made the whole country tremble with their noise. Four times monstrous creatures passed by me, but I was swift and hid, coming to no harm.

Often as I went, my thoughts were upon the dear Maid I journeyed to save from destruction. Yet, as you must see, my thinkings were always brought sharply to my going. Scarce was I set off to ponder upon Naani before some danger or wonder gave me heed to my way. Because of this, as you have learned, I was more put to plan free of the instant trouble and peril of my way through all that mighty journey than to have quiet chance for thoughts of love unto mine Own. Despair, vast and crushing, shattered in an instant, replaced by a swelling tide of joy and hope so potent it nearly blinded me. It seemed impossible that I could not reach my beloved within moments. I knew that pyramid must stand upon a hill in the heart of that dark country; only height could make it visible against the void. With renewed vigor, I sprinted downward into the land, driving toward that shape.

Minutes passed, and then—impact. I fell headlong, the ground's hardness promising a broken neck. Pain, sharp and absolute, seized me, stealing my ability to move. I lay there, helpless and moaning, vulnerable to any predator that might have stumbled upon me.

Slowly, the agony receded. I sat up, clutching my neck, then rose to my feet. But my haste was gone, replaced by a cautious, anxious stride. A terrible doubt gnawed at me: if this were the Lesser Refuge, why was the pyramid so utterly black? Was it a house of evil, a trick of some malevolent force designed to bewilder my senses? Yet, against the distant fires of the land, its form remained plain. It was, in truth, the Lesser Refuge.

In my initial rush, I had been blind, driven only by the length of my search. I had intended to summon Naani with the Master-Word, to announce my arrival. But now, prudence demanded I verify the nature of this darkness before calling out.

I descended further into the night, initially with care, then with a fierce, expectant eagerness that had returned after the shock of my fall. I had climbed the volcano's upper plain in thirteen hours; descending took ten, though my progress was hampered by the lingering tremors in my limbs.

By the end of the tenth hour, I reached the great plain of the land. The Refuge itself was lost to view, hidden upward in the deep night, but a massive bulk stood between me and the far-off shinings. This was the hill bearing the pyramid.

Four hours later, I crossed the plain, passing fire-holes that glowed red in the darkness. The ambient light prevented total blackness. As I began the ascent, excitement surged through me. I nearly shouted Naani's name, hoping for an answer. But as I climbed, caution returned, cold and heavy. My spirit sensed a dread my heart could not yet articulate.

Almost at the summit, taking three hours, the grim silhouette of the pyramid loomed above me, desolate and silent. An utter shaking fear seized me. I sat, weak and bewildered, my heart feeling dead within me. My spirit confirmed the evil residing in the dark pyramid; the Lesser Refuge had fallen, overrun by evil creatures and powers. If this were true, I was too late to save the Maid. Yet, in that despair, I found a twisted gladness: if I were to fight and die quickly, it would be preferable to living in a world devoid of hope.

After sitting awhile, a sudden thought struck me. How could I know if Naani lived? I remembered hearing the Master-Word faintly during my journey. If she answered, or if an Evil Power destroyed me, at least my heartache would end.

Instantly, the Master-Word broke through the mystery of the night, low and solemn. Within my brain, a far, small voice emerged, lone and faint, as if coming from the edge of the world. It was Naani's voice and Mirdath's voice, calling me by my old love-name.

Choking on the affright of joy, I shook with excitement. Despair vanished. Naani lived; she called to me across the dark world. I knew, however, that she was far away, and peril lurked before I could reach her side to fight for her life and my own joy.

She screamed and recoiled, trying to flee into the bushes. She didn't recognize me as human, fearing a monster. Immediately, I spoke the Master-Word aloud, granting her peace and help. I told her my name, declaring I was "That One." She knew instantly. In a broken voice, she cried out, ran to me, and thrust her small hands into my charge. She fell into great sobbing. I held her hands firmly, silent, for I wore no armored gloves.

She leaned against me, weak as a child. Soon, the sobbing ceased, replaced by ragged breathing. She said nothing. I realized she suffered from hunger. Her hands were thin and wasted. I lifted her gently onto a smooth rock, stripping off my cloak to wrap around her. Her clothes were torn rags; she shivered from cold and weakness, near starvation and grief.

I retrieved my scrip and pouch, breaking a tablet into my cup. Using water from a hot rock, I made broth swiftly. I fed it to her; her hands shook too violently to hold it otherwise. She drank, then fell back into quiet sobbing. I held her hands under the cloak, strong and firm, bringing peace and strength. The trembling stopped.

Her hands stirred within mine. I loosened my grip; she grasped them weakly and gently, refusing to look at me yet, gathering her strength. I was content, save for a new anxiety: what if a monster approached? My heart would break if harm came to her.

Suddenly, she tried to rise. I freed myself to help. She grabbed my hand, slipped to her knees, and kissed it, beginning to weep again. Abashed, I stood stupidly, letting her do it. Then, drawing free, I knelt before her, kissing her hands in humble return. She sobbed more, moved by my arrival through the night's desolation. I released her hands so she could weep freely, but she left them resting in my palms as she knelt, bowing her head, showing herself mine in essence.

I took her into my arms, gently, without caress. I stroked her hair, calling her Naani and Mirdath, saying things I barely recall now, which she understood in the after-time. She was quiet in my arms, content, yet sobbed for a long time. I coaxed her with vague comforts. She asked for nothing more than shelter. She had been alone in terror, grief, and dread for a great, horrid time.

Soon, she grew quiet. I tried to settle her comfortably against the rock to free my hands for more broth, but she nestled against me with a sweet wistfulness that warmed my heart. She began to speak odd words, obedient and resting against the rock while I prepared the food. Her gaze followed me constantly.

She drank the broth with her own hands. I ate three tablets and drank water, my first meal in a foolishly long time.

As the broth brightened her eyes, she began to talk, pausing often due to lack of strength. Twice she sobbed again. Her father, the Master Monstruwacan, was dead. The peoples of the Lesser Redoubt were slain and scattered.

An Evil Force had acted upon the Lesser Redoubt. The weak, failing from the Earth-Current's decline, had opened the Great Door and fled into the night. Horrid monsters entered the pyramid, chasing and slaughtering many. Some escaped.

Naani arrived after her father was killed by a shaggy, brutal man. Three maids had fled with her, but creatures stole two while they slept in the bushes. The third ran off with Naani; they never met again.

This catastrophe had happened a great while ago, though she could not measure the time. She had been lost for the entire duration of my journey. She had heard none of my calls. Her own cries had attracted beasts, which she sensed and evaded, hiding among rocks and bushes. Eventually, she stopped calling to avoid drawing monsters, except rarely.

Chased relentlessly, she had become nearly naked; bushes and rocks had torn her garments. She ate moss, strange berries, and drank from hot springs, often falling ill from sulfur or poison. Perhaps the first saved her from the second.

In that dreadful solitude, she heard a score of drear things: the slaying of a young maid by a brute, the running of people, the tread of giants. I understood the sounds I had heard crossing the land, and a new pity and horror rose within me. She recounted seeing others of her people hiding, who ran in panic, ignoring her pleas that they were human.

The bitter chill drove her to seek fire-holes, but these attracted the monstrous brutes. She often had to stay in the utter cold. Occasionally, desperate for warmth, she ventured out and was nearly slain in her sleep. Snakes, spider-crabs, and monstrous scorpions infested the area.

Even as she lay weak by the fire-hole, surrounded by crab-like creatures waiting for her to die, the Master-Word had beaten through the night, strong as spiritual thunder. She had thought it came from the far-off pyramid, bringing only despair. Then, her name was spoken clearly, different from the spirit-word. I emerged from the bush. She fell back in fear, expecting a monster, but saw a young man in grey armor. She knew instantly I was the olden one from her memory dreams, the voice across the dead world. I had come through the unknown desolation to succor her. She was safe, yet broken by weakness, joy, and sweet honor.

While she spoke of the spider-crabs, I looked around. They were not gone; a circle of silent, impudent horror watched us. Anger rose in me. I stood, went to the light's edge, and spurned, kicked, and drove away a dozen of them before they retreated. Their calm assurance was foolish; they lacked courage, though one wished to pinch my toe.

Returning to the maid, she laughed with weak gleefulness. I made her another cup of broth, which she drank easily. I ordered her sternly but playfully to sleep. She needed it sorely, her excitement fading, leaving her happy and fearless.

I made a smooth place for her, using the pouch and scrip as a pillow. I laid her quiet and sweet in the cloak, covering her feet. They were sore-cut and bare; her footgear was utterly worn out from her lonely journey and flight from brutes. I considered washing and binding them, but her exhaustion demanded sleep first. Her feet were very small and shapely.

Presently, she slept. I doubted she had slumbered peacefully in a great month; she never knew when evil might strike in her sleep.

While she slept, I stripped off my armor and the warm under-suit (the Armour-Suit), folding the latter beside her. She was nearly unclothed from the tearing of the bushes and rocks. I stood watch for ten long hours, walking back and forth, listening with ears and spirit. I was awakened to a new care and delight, coupled with a doubled fear of any Horrid Creature or Evil Force.

At the end of ten hours, she woke. I ran to her, joyful that she returned to consciousness. She sat up, looking at me with new light and movement. For a moment, she said nothing, looking keenly at me, causing me to wonder at her thoughts.

Suddenly, she asked how long it had been since I slept. Caught off guard, I said eighty-four hours—three days and a half. I knew this because I kept careful count, lest I lose track of time.

As I spoke, I felt strangely queer-headed. I had gone a wondrous while without slumber, doing bitter work.

Suddenly, she cried out, tossed the cloak aside, and pulled me into her arms, heedless of her nakedness. I knew not how I reacted so strangely, but I was near swooning from lack of rest. She held me steady, then helped me lie on the ground. She placed the scrip and pouch under my head. I lay calm and restful, my heart tired, the world growing quiet.

She noticed her lack of cover, retrieved the cloak, and wrapped it around herself. She sat beside me, rubbing my hands. I regained some composure; she grew happier, offering me food. She lifted my head, took the scrip, and fetched the tablets, flask, and cup. I had packed everything

The oppressive, suffocating gloom of the ancient seabed finally lifted, replaced by a light that seemed almost fragile after the endless night. I realized I had emerged onto land to the right of my entry point, a terrain dotted with numerous fire-holes. Their presence warmed my heart, yet prudence dictated caution; life thrived around these flames, and danger often lurked in their heat.

I looked down at the Maid, who gazed up at me. She drew closer, revealing a beauty that was both wondrous and heartbreaking. Her face was pale, etched with exhaustion, and I felt a surge of self-reproach for having pushed her so hard. In truth, I felt forged of iron, while she remained a delicate, tender creature. She silenced my guilt with a simple, beautiful gaze, and I wrapped my arms around her, kissing her forehead before scanning the landscape to chart our course.

Before me stretched the blue shimmer I had spotted at the mouth of the Upward Gorge. It was distant, but it served as my true north, preventing us from wandering in blind circles across the dark expanse. After a moment of calculation, I determined the Gorge lay to my left, though I must navigate carefully. To my left, far away, burned the red glow of the Giants' fire-pit, a place I must avoid entirely. Furthermore, I mistrusted the blue shimmer itself; it felt wrong, unnatural.

I placed my arm around the Maid, who stood quietly beside me, and pointed toward the left. "The Gorge lies that way," I told her, "far beyond sight, marked only by the landmarks I recall from my descent."

She had observed the surroundings intently during our walk and seemed to grasp the geography. "How do you intend to proceed?" she asked.

"Directly," I replied, "but we must skirt the blue shimmer and stay clear of the Giant's red pit."

She gestured for me to look ahead. There, faintly visible, were fire-holes surrounded by a sickly green luminescence. "That is the tract of poisonous gas," she explained, referencing the data from the Lesser Redoubt. "Where the gas flows, the fires glow green. It stretches far to the northwest, and the great blue shimmer burns across that entire sector."

I asked her what they called it. "They simply call it 'The Shine'," she answered.

My internal voice, Mine Own, urgently warned me against approaching The Shine. "The Fixed Giants dwell within its borders," she whispered, noting that they were usually hidden by the burning mist, revealed only when the vapor rolled. This realization chilled me; these Fixed Giants were akin to the Great Watchers guarding the Mighty Pyramid—forces of profound, terrible evil. I recalled the monstrous face I had glimpsed amidst the smoke of The Shine upon my first arrival.

"How far does the gas extend?" I asked.

She indicated the direction, and I realized a dilemma: to reach the Gorge while avoiding The Shine, I would have to cross the path of the gas. Yet, she reminded me that I had previously crossed the land by sheer luck, walking along the far side of the gas cloud to reach the seabed.

"Therefore," she proposed, "we descend back into the seabed. We will walk beneath the shore for hours, heading southwest until we are safely beyond the gas zone. Then, we ascend and proceed with extreme caution, keeping the Giants' fire-pit in our rear."

It was a sound plan, one I would have devised instantly had she not already spoken it. Her eagerness and quick thinking were a sweet relief; I cherished the sound of her voice, the clarity of her mind, and the fact that she was always my partner in thought and action.

We adopted her strategy. However, it had been nearly seventeen hours since our last rest, and the Maid was visibly drained. I urged her to find a fire-hole nearby to warm ourselves before continuing. We selected one to the north, slightly to our right.

The journey to the fire-hole took longer than anticipated—an hour of trudging through the dark. When we arrived, it was a massive, red-glowing fissure venting heat into the night sky. I signaled for silence, retrieved the Diskos from my hip, and knelt, beckoning Mine Own to follow suit.

We crept to the edge of the hollow and peered down. A fierce fire burned within, yet no monsters immediately surrounded it, offering a fleeting sense of peace. Still, I knew better than to linger near the fires, for they attracted all living things. The Maid, however, was shivering violently from the cold. I recovered quickly and ran again. The Pyramid's light was dimming, a cold realization settling in my heart: the Earth-Current had been drained to save us, leaving the Millions vulnerable. If it did not replenish soon, they would all perish. I pushed forward, ignoring the warning that death was close.

As I ran, I sensed a new peril. I looked up, expecting the Master Monstruwacan to signal danger via the Set Speech, but the instruments were failing; the Pyramid's machinery was grinding to a halt. Instead, a pale circle of holiness hovered above us, a shield against the dread power approaching. I trusted in this force and pressed on.

Within four hundred paces of the Circle, three beast-men rose from the earth, growling. I struck the first with the haft of the Diskos, then swung wildly at the others, my mind cold and focused solely on survival. They fell like mice. A shout of welcome erupted from within the Circle, but I saw men in grey armor holding back, terrified by the monstrous Black Mounds swaying outside the perimeter. These mounds were the visible signs of evil forces; to step beyond the Circle was to be destroyed in spirit.

"Come!" they shouted. "Haste!"

I ran faster, but a herd of squat, brutish men with pig-like tusks burst from the shadows. They grabbed at me and the girl. I kicked free, turning to face them. The Diskos spun, casting a strange light on their tusks. I raged through the herd, taking a thousand blows to my armor while shielding the girl above their reach. They seemed endless, but I drove forward, fueled by desperation.

Fifty paces from the Circle, I was near collapse, dazed and bleeding, having carried the girl through endless days and nights without sleep. Suddenly, the Hundred Thousand hurled their Diskos into the herd, thinning the line. I gathered my strength, charging through the gap, and broke into the Circle.

A thousand hands reached out, but recoiled in awe at my state. I stood there, the Diskos dripping blood, gasping for air. I tried to speak, to ask for doctors for the girl dying in my arms. Voices cried out warnings of giants and the fading holy light. The Master of the Diskos approached, saluting me, but I insisted on finding a doctor immediately.

Two large men brought a small man on a sling—a Master of the Doctors. He gently helped me lay the girl down. The Hundred Thousand fell silent, a fearful quiet settling over the Pyramid. The Doctor looked up at me with pity, and I knew in that instant: she was dead. He covered her face and signaled for men to support me and carry her to the Gateway.

I fought to breathe, gesturing that no one should touch me or the girl. The Doctor understood my strength was gone, only my will to die remaining. I stooped, lifting the girl for one last journey.

I walked down the silent lane of the Hundred Thousand, who made a mute salute with their reversed Diskos. I carried my dead love into the Great Lift, accompanied by the Masters, including Monstruwacan, who looked at me with the sorrow of a father. The Pyramid's lights began to glow again as the Earth-Current restored itself, allowing the Great Gate to open.

Inside, the air was thick with grief. The weeping of women drifted down from the upper cities. Monstruwacan and the Doctor stood silently by my side as we ascended. I realized then that I was bleeding heavily from a hundred wounds, but the Doctor did not tend to me; he knew that my heart was already broken beyond repair. Whirlings came to my head, and someone tried to take the girl from my arms, but I held her dumbly. Monstruwacan's face seemed human in the dim light, and he held me up as darkness closed in.

Three days passed in a half-dream, during which I was tended by the best medical knowledge of the Humans. On the third day, I regained my senses, the pain in my breast sharp and real. I refused a loose garment, insisting on my broken armor, a symbol of my battle. As they dressed me, I heard the sorrow of the Millions, knowing they were descending to the Country of Silence.

Just as I was being fitted, the memory struck me again: I had never woken to find the girl kissing me in my sleep. The pain was so acute it nearly killed me, but the Doctor administered a drug to dull my senses. I was carried to the lift again, destined not for the surface, but for the Underground Fields, the Country of Silence, a hundred miles deep where the dead rest.

The Stress Masters arranged the descent, and we dropped through the miles. At the bottom, in the vast quiet, I stood on my feet, refusing the sling, demanding to walk to The Last Road. The Doctor obeyed, walking behind me. The country was filled with the Peoples of the World, their sorrow creating a low, rolling thunder in the air.

Below me lay the Last Rest, where a figure covered in a white robe glimmered with the embroidery of women's love. This was the place for me, the head of the mound reserved for Love, which holds dominion over Faithfulness and Honour. Two maids in white knelt by the grave, symbols of Faithfulness.

I took my courage and stepped to the head of the mound. The girl lay beneath a white garment worked with yellow Flowers of Weeping, a testament to her death in love. No hand but a maiden's had touched her. From far away, the Millions began to sing the Calling Song, a low, anguished chant calling to a lost beloved. It passed over us, a wave of collective grief.

Then, a strange wind-like sound arose, growing into the Song of Weeping, sung by millions across the deep earth. Monstruwacan looked at me, and I knew the moment of parting had arrived. I placed the Diskos beside her. The maids drew back the garment to reveal her face. She slept there, sweet and quiet as a child.

The pain was sufficient. I knew I was dying as I looked. I set my soul about her, fighting to stay upright, but the maids covered her face again. Monstruwacan commended her to Eternity, raising his reversed Diskos in honor. The Road began to move upward toward the Dome, carrying her away.

A low moaning filled the air, the true weeping of multitudes. I stood still, watching the small form recede into the luminous vapour of the Earth-Current at the base of the Dome. The vapour clung to her, making her seem unreal, shifting and uncertain.

Suddenly, a hoarse noise erupted from the nearby Millions, growing into a mighty roar. I saw the girl move on the Road, but it was only the shifting vapour playing tricks on my crazed, longing mind. Then, truly, I saw her move. She was alive.

Life surged back into me, my heart stilled for a moment before pounding anew. Monstruwacan signaled the Road to stop and reverse, but I was already upon it, running as a madman, shouting her name. The Millions held back to prevent a crush, and a signal was sent for calm. I staggered upward, the Roof ringing with their shouts.

Others ran behind me, but I was first. I reached her quickly. She lay on her back, having pushed the garment from her face, her eyes open with a look of gentle wonder. She saw me, and her eyes smiled, weak but glad.

I fell beside her, kneeling on my hands and knees, my heart shaking my lips into dry whispers. She looked steadfastly at me, and I tried to speak, but my mouth refused. Understanding dawned in her eyes like a light; she knew she was in the Pyramid, that I had brought her home. She woke fully, trembling, reaching out from the garment.

I saw the blood flowing constantly from my wounds. She perceived it, waking from her death-swoon with renewed clarity. I spoke simply, "I loved her." She was in a haze, but I knew she had come to her knees, placing my head on her breast. The air shook with a great sound, a mighty spiritual stirring.

The voices of Monstruwacan and the Doctor were dull in my ears, meaningless words. All I knew was that she lived. I resolved not to die, to fight for life, and even as I made this vow, I sank into an utter blackness.

When consciousness returned to me, I found myself ascending in the Lift, resting upon that same bed where I had once believed I would never lie again, nor ever need to rise from the silent country below. A vague, strange sensation washed over me as the deep, thunderous resonance of the Underground Organs rose from the world's mighty depths. It sounded like a distant music beyond death itself—a rolling chant sung by multitudes across far mountains, sometimes a low-blowing wind from the Deep, and again, the clear, ancient melody of the Song of Honour. I knew, in a dreamlike state, that the millions in that subterranean realm were celebrating this wonder of joy. Yet, everything remained faint and half-hidden; my eyes felt powerless to open, and I seemed to float eternally upon strange waters of unreality.

Then, sweet and lovely odours drifted toward me—scents of reality emanating from the great Fields where flowers perpetually bloomed along the Lift's passages, as the elevator climbed through its vast miles. Perhaps I stirred slightly, for the voice of the Master Doctor came low and gentle, bidding me rest and assuring me that the Maid was well. Soon after, I drifted into a haze, living half-days and half-sleeps, wondering without trouble whether I was truly dead.

Then came days of profound quietude, devoid of thought. The Master Doctor often leaned over me, scrutinizing my face with keen intensity. Finally, after strange intervals, another figure bent down—the dear, lovely face of Mine Own. Her eyes spoke love directly into my soul, though she remained calm and hushed. I began to live within my body again, perhaps making a small, fumbling movement with my hands. She took them, holding them gently, and life flowed from her to me. She was ever wordless and tender; contentment grew within me, leading to a natural slumber.

One day, I was allowed to rise. Those who tended me carried me to one of the Quiet Gardens of the Pyramid, setting me down and seemingly leaving me alone. Then, one emerged from behind a bush, looking at me for a moment with a half-shy expression. Yet the love shining in her eyes rendered that shyness insignificant. Truly, I knew it was Mine Own Maid, though I had never seen Naani dressed so prettily as a maid. Looking at her, I realized she appeared more dainty than I could have imagined. Suddenly, I tried to rise to approach her, but she ran quickly to stop my natural foolishness. She sat beside me, taking my head against her breast, refusing to deny me her lips. In that single moment, she was both maid and mother to me.

Afterward, she kept me very still, and we sat in utter, dumb happiness until the attendants returned. The Master of the Doctors was among them, and I saw a trace of satisfaction on his face. From that day forward, I saw Mine Own Maid every day, recovering health with wondrous speed, for Love healed me. Soon, I was permitted to descend to the Fields, though by private paths, for the Multitudes might otherwise follow me constantly, and I needed silence.

The Maid accompanied me. The Master Monstruwacan and the Master of the Doctors agreed to this arrangement, appointing an Officer of Marriage to wed us quietly and simply. I was still too weak for a public ceremony, which would come later when the Millions erected a Guard of Honour eight miles high, from the top to the bottom of the Mighty Pyramid. That grand ceremonial was reserved for the People, who could not be denied the honor they bestowed upon me.

Surely, the Maid stayed with me always, becoming my wife and my strength, allowing Mine Own to regain perfect health. We entered the Love Days, the most beautiful of all if Love is true. We wandered through the mighty Fields at our will, walking the Love Paths near the villages. We hid our names to avoid being beset by those driven by natural curiosity and kindness, needing to remain utterly together and quiet.

We chose sleeping spots where the beauty of flowers was most wondrous, carrying some food, though we also ate when we reached the villages scattered throughout the Fields—places truly as vast as countries. Mine Own kept her promise a hundred times, preparing me a great, hearty meal. She teased me relentlessly as I ate, calling me a glutton, and kissed me to ensure I never had a chance to defend myself. Truly, she was everything my heart and spirit desired; she companioned me with Love and brought Joy into my spirit.

Once, we descended to the Country of Silence, though we did not stay long, for my memories began to return. In later times, we wandered there often, accompanied by Memory, the holiness of great thoughts, and a Love that holds all. As we left that country, I told Mine Own how, when she had been suspended by the Horrid Force of the House, I had suffered dreadful pain, fearing I would never wake to discover her kissing me while I slept. She blushed most lovely, unaware I had been conscious of her sweet naughtiness. She had spent all her thoughts on my agony during her death, before the Earth-Force's vapour freed her spirit from Silence. She came to me with dear understanding.

She explained that the Doctors said she had been stunned and frozen in spirit, her entire being and life suspended. The great life-force of the Earth-Current had woken her spirit, restoring her body and blood. The Doctors had searched the old Records of their Work, finding mention of one such occurrence in ancient times, but nothing like it had happened through a mighty age of years.

While wandering and resting in the Fields, I often told Mine Own of various matters. I knew she had learned some odd things before I recovered, though not much; she, too, had been utterly wretched, as you might imagine. She had risen from her bed while I lay still, ordered by the Master Doctor who feared I would truly die without something to awaken my spirit. In verity, you must consider the depth of my Love as I know she held my hands so bravely and gently, having scarce power in her own feet. I offered a little holy praise for Mine Own.

Thus, I come to this ending, with one final thing to tell. This happened a while later, after we had undergone the second marriage, the Public Marriage. One day, My Wife, Mine Own, took me with sweet cunning to the Hall of Honour. When I arrived, I saw many of the People standing in silence within that great Hall, waiting for something. My Wife led me to the center of the Hall. Suddenly, I understood why she had brought me there. Standing in the Place of Honour was a statue of a man in broken armour, carrying a maid forever.

I stood dumb. How could this Age know the honour this meant? It was an honour given only to the Great Dead. I was but a young man, far from greatness, save that I loved with all my heart and spirit, making death a small thing before Love. You must understand how Love makes the heart sweet and brave, granting humility, wonder, and natural pride that anyone would think to honour me. Mine Own wept with joy and honest pride beside me. An utter silence of dear sympathy filled the great Hall. Those present let me pass in quietness with Mine Own, a lovely thing of understanding.

I walked lovingly and thoughtfully with my Wife, who stayed very near. I had gained Honour, yet I learned that Honour is but the ash of Life unless you possess Love. I had Love. To have Love is to have all, for truly Love mothers Honour and Faithfulness; and these three build the House of Joy.
