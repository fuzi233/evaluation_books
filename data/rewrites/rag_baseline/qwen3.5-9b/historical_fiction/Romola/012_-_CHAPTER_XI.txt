"Monk is a Florentine," Tito muttered to himself as he turned from the church of Santa Felicita, the weight of the decision settling heavily upon him. "If he stays in Florence, everything must be disclosed."

A new crisis had seized him, yet he moved with a calculated composure, suppressing the agitation that threatened to betray his inner turmoil. He proceeded along the Via de' Bardi toward the house of Bardo, intending to apologize for his absence. His talent for concealment was rapidly evolving from a passive trait into a weaponized skill. While it remained theoretically possible—and perhaps inevitable—that he should openly acknowledge the altered circumstances and confess Baldassarre's existence, such an admission would cast a damning retroactive light on his previous silence. It would frame his earlier reticence not as prudence, but as studied equivocation designed to evade a recognized claim, compounded by the fact that he had already settled his own affairs and invested his florins before his benefactor's fate was confirmed.

For now, the tactically superior move was to feign ignorance. He would suspend decisive thought until the darkness of the night allowed for private meditation, ensuring no one could pinpoint the exact hour he received the incriminating letter. Entering the second-story apartment where Romola and her father sat amidst parchment and marble, aloof from the street's chaos, Tito masked his anxiety with a brightness that barely concealed his regret at arriving late—a regret born of sacrificing the spectacle of the Corso to prioritize this meeting. He projected extra animation onto the evening conversation, though his mind operated like a complex engine, depositing thoughts distinct from the flow of polite discourse. By the time he descended the stone stairs and emerged into the starlight, his psyche had crystallized into a new strategic phase.

The following day, freed from his professorial duties, Tito turned up the Via del Cocomero toward the convent of San Marco. His purpose was now fully formed: he intended to interrogate Fra Luca regarding the extent of his suspicions and the evidence supporting them, alongside the duration of the friar's stay. Armed with this intelligence, he planned to construct a statement that would absolve him of the necessity of fleeing Florence.

Tito had never before fabricated an intricate deception, yet the occasion demanded it. Circumstance invariably breeds opportunity for tacit falsity, and his ingenuity was primed. He had convinced himself that he was under no obligation to hunt for Baldassarre. He had previously stated that a fair assurance of his father's location would compel him to pursue him, but he now questioned the absolute imperative. Why was he bound to go? Ultimately, the goal of life was to extract the maximum sum of pleasure. Was not his own blooming vitality a promise of incomparable joy—not only for himself but for others—far surpassing the withered, rigid existence of a man past the prime of enjoyment? These ideas, sown in the fertile soil of his young mind, were vibrant germs aligned with the natural order, which treated maturity merely as a nursery for youth.

Baldassarre had completed his cycle; he had drawn his draft of life. It was Tito's turn. The prospect was vague—"I think they are taking me to Antioch"—yet it represented a vast horizon. After a long voyage, he could spend months or years searching for a prize with no guarantee of success, abandoning a life of distinction and love behind him. Even if he found something, he risked returning to the old, exacting companionship he knew by rote. Technically, the gems and the florins belonged to Baldassarre in the narrow legal sense of possession. However, in the broader, radical view of nature where the world belongs to youth and strength, they rightfully belonged to whoever could derive the greatest pleasure from them.

Tito was acutely aware that societal norms expected him to immediately apply those funds to his benefactor's rescue. But what was the sentiment of society? A tangled web of anomalous traditions and opinions, useful only as a guide for personal comfort. He did not cherish the florins solely for their own sake; he would relinquish them readily for Romola. What he craved was the joy hovering close to his lips, the sweetness of existence he felt no moral compulsion to deny himself. Maxims demanding the rejection of necessary goods for the sake of abstract duty were merely the lining of human selfishness turned outward, crafted by those wishing others to sacrifice for their benefit.

Ideally, he wished Baldassarre to suffer no harm; he disliked pain in anyone. Yet, could any philosophy prove he was obligated to value another's suffering more than his own? Only if he loved Baldassarre devotedly. He did not. Was that his fault? Gratitude, scrutinized closely, held no valid claim. Baldassarre's life would have been dreary without Tito; were humans truly indebted for the pleasures others generate for themselves?

Once initiated, Tito's reasoning acted as a virulent acid, dissolving the tissues of sentiment. His mind lacked the dread often dismissed as mere animal self-preservation—the awe of Divine Nemesis felt by religious pagans or Christians alike. That terror of the unseen, which serves as a guardian of the soul, forcing wisdom upon men even under the full sunshine, was absent. Without it, the hard scrutiny of imperfect thought could probe obligations lacking sanctity. As he entered the outer cloister of San Marco and inquired for Fra Luca, there was no shadowy presentiment in his mind; he considered himself too cultured and skeptical for such superstition, nurtured in contempt for the impudent lives of priests and erudite familiarity with disputes on the Chief Good, which he deemed a matter of mere taste.

Yet, fear remained a potent element in his nature: specifically, the fear of losing pleasure. He harbored a definite dread that Fra Luca might be the instrument of his expulsion from Florence.

"Fra Luca?" a *fra converso* answered, his accent marking him as a raw peasant (*contadino*), his dull gaze offering no curiosity. "Ah, he is gone to Fiesole—to the Dominican monastery there. He was taken on a litter in the cool of the morning. The poor Brother is very ill. Could you leave a message for him?"

"Thanks; my business can wait."

Tito turned away with a profound sense of relief. "This friar is unlikely to survive," he reasoned internally. "I saw he was worn to a shadow. At Fiesole, there will be nothing to recall me to his mind. Besides, if he returns, my current explanation will suffice. Still, I wonder what it was about his face that struck me."
