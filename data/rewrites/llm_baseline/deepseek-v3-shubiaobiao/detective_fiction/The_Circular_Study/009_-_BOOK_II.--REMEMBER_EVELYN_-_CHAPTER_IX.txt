At the foot of the stairs, Mr. Gryce paused, excusing himself briefly before summoning the men stationed outside. With quiet efficiency, he directed them to remove the valet, ensuring the scene was undisturbed before leading Miss Butterworth back into the study.

When silence settled once more, Mr. Gryce turned to her, his expression contemplative. "One thing, at least, is now clear—Bartow had no hand in the crime."

"Then we may focus entirely on the young pair," Miss Butterworth replied. "I assume you’ve uncovered nothing further?"

"Nothing."

"And the old man who laughed—no trace of him either?"

"None."

Miss Butterworth’s lips tightened in frustration. "I had hoped that scrap of writing you found would offer some direction. If it was worth swallowing in his final moments, surely it held significance."

"Unfortunately, it’s as cryptic as the rest. A hurried scrawl, reading: *‘I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!’* Signed, *‘Amos’s son.’*"

"Amos’s son—that would be Mr. Adams himself."

"So we believe."

"Strange. And yet the paper was clenched between his teeth as though his life depended on it. Was the handwriting verified?"

"Yes, it matches his signature in his books."

"The mystery deepens. To go to such lengths to hide those words—even in death—suggests they held a truth too dangerous to leave exposed. And the girl—her first words upon waking: *‘You have left that line of writing behind.’*" Miss Butterworth leaned forward. "Mr. Gryce, those words are the key. Repeat them again, line by line."

"Very well. First: *‘I return your daughter to you.’*"

"So—Mr. Adams had someone’s daughter in his keeping. But whose? Not the young man’s, unless she were a child. And if the message wasn’t meant for his assailant, why conceal it so desperately?"

"A valid point. I’ve puzzled over the same."

"Then perhaps it was intended for the old gentleman who entered later?"

"Possible. Especially given the next line: *‘She is here.’* The only woman present before the old man’s arrival was the fair-haired girl carried out."

"True. But why assume the message was freshly written? It could be an old fragment, tied to some past secret."

"Unlikely. The paper was torn from a sheet on the desk, the pen left askew—you noted its placement, I’m sure."

"I did, though I hadn’t grasped its significance. So the words were written that day, connected to the girl I saw. And she is not Evelyn, or he wouldn’t have repeated the phrase *‘Remember Evelyn!’*" Miss Butterworth’s gaze drifted to the large portrait on the wall. "Shall we call her Evelyn for now? Such sorrow in that face suggests a family tragedy. But how does the young man fit into this? His violence is absent from these lines."

"A mystery indeed, madam. One that may defy our efforts."

"Yet I cannot let it go. The final words—*‘Neither she nor you will ever see me again.’* Followed by *‘Remember Evelyn!’* Could it be—was it a suicide?"

"You have the right to ask. But Bartow’s pantomime showed an attack, not self-infliction. Still, the state of Mr. Adams’s affairs—the missing documents, the lack of personal papers—suggests he anticipated death. Perhaps not like this, but he was prepared."

Miss Butterworth exhaled sharply. "There’s more you haven’t told me."

"A confession, then. This study—it conceals a secret. One I discovered in a rather startling manner. The room can be sealed off entirely, turned into a tomb. This steel plate"—he gestured to the wall—"slides across the doorway. I was trapped behind it myself; these holes were drilled to free me."

"Good heavens! A medieval death trap in modern New York?"

"Some men carry the past with them. Mr. Adams was such a man—a romantic, a mechanist, a lover of enigma. Even his death bore the mark of it. And the cross on his breast—placed by another’s hand, surely. A priest or a woman stood by as he died."

"Yet he was no dreamer when it came to practicality. His devices worked flawlessly."

"A contradiction, like the rest of him." Miss Butterworth rose, restless. "I wish his spirit *would* haunt this room. Perhaps then we’d grasp the truth."

"There’s only one path forward," Mr. Gryce said. "We must find the young couple. Without them, the case is hopeless."

"Then we’ve come full circle," she murmured. "Lost in the woods."

As they prepared to leave, Miss Butterworth’s gaze lingered on the walls. "Look there—the gap where the stair landing’s painting was removed. From this angle, it blends into the tapestry. Nearly invisible."

"Yes. With the picture restored, no one would suspect."

"One must keep their eyes moving in a case like this. That painting was shifted more than once while we were here, yet we never noticed."

"Because we didn’t look high enough," Mr. Gryce mused. "High and low, Miss Butterworth. What happens at eye level is plain to all."

His smile, as he escorted her out, held less irony than melancholy. Was it age that dulled his once-keen sight? Once, he would have missed nothing—neither the heights nor the depths. Now, even the keenest mind could falter.
