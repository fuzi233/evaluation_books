I held my tongue, not daring to hint at the truth I had long suspected—out of respect for poor Lady Verinder. She had not spoken yet, and so I waited in silence, prepared to offer comfort when the time came. I had already made up my mind to say a few gentle words at the first opportunity, no matter how painful the duty might be.

“I have been seriously ill, Drusilla,” she began, her voice soft but steady. “For some time now. And strangely enough—I didn’t even know it.”

At that moment, my thoughts turned to all the countless people who suffer silently, unaware of their own inner decay. I feared—deeply feared—that my aunt might be one of them.

“Yes, dear,” I said, sorrowfully. “Yes.”

She continued. “I brought Rachel to London, as you know, for medical advice. I thought it right to consult two doctors.”

Two doctors! And not a single clergyman—especially with Rachel in such a state!

“Yes, dear?” I asked again. “Yes?”

“One of the doctors was a stranger to me,” she went on. “The other had been an old friend of my husband’s and had always shown genuine concern for me out of loyalty to him. After treating Rachel, he asked to speak with me privately. I expected instructions about her care. Instead, he took my hand gravely and said: ‘Lady Verinder, I’ve been watching you—not just as a patient, but as someone I care about. You’re far more in need of medical attention than your daughter.’”

He asked me questions I at first dismissed lightly—until I saw the distress in his eyes. That led to an appointment the next day, when he would come with another physician, at a time when Rachel wouldn’t be home.

The outcome—delivered with kindness and gentleness—was clear: precious time had been lost, and the damage could not be undone. Both doctors agreed my condition was beyond their skill.

For over two years, I’ve suffered from a hidden heart disease—one that left no warning signs, yet slowly destroyed me. I may live for months—or I may die before tomorrow. The doctors cannot say more than that.

I won’t pretend I haven’t had moments of despair since learning the truth. But I’m growing more resigned. I’m trying to settle my affairs. My only worry is that Rachel must never learn this. If she did, she’d blame herself—think my illness stemmed from anxiety over the Diamond. And that would break her heart, though it has nothing to do with her.

Both doctors believe the illness began two or three years ago. I trust you’ll keep this secret, Drusilla. I can see compassion in your face.”

Compassion? Oh, what a strange thing to expect from a Christian woman grounded in faith! Yet, as she spoke, I felt a wave of gratitude wash over me—a deep, sacred joy. Here was a chance to serve God through service to a dying relative. A beloved soul, on the brink of death, unprepared—and by divine providence, confiding in *me*!

How many clerical friends I had at my disposal—dozens, not just a few! I embraced my aunt, unable to contain my emotion.

“Oh!” I whispered fervently. “What a privilege you are to me! What good I will do for you before we part!”

After a few more words of caution, I offered her a choice among three devout men—neighbors who spent their days ministering to others, full of wisdom and mercy, ready to help at my request.

But her response was discouraging. She looked confused, frightened. Her only objection was that she wasn’t strong enough to meet strangers.

I yielded—for now. But I knew what to do. From my experience—reading and visiting under fourteen different clergymen—I understood this required preparation through books. I had a small library of spiritual works—perfect for her condition, designed to awaken, instruct, strengthen, and guide.

“You’ll read, dear?” I asked sweetly. “If I bring you my own books? They’re marked—turned down at key passages. And noted in pencil where you should pause and ask yourself, ‘Does this apply to me?’”

Even that simple suggestion seemed to unsettle her. She replied, “I’ll do what I can, Drusilla, to please you,” with a look of bewilderment that both taught me and chilled me.

No time to lose. The clock told me I had just enough to rush home, select a dozen readings, and return in time to witness Lady Verinder’s will.

I promised to return by five and left. Normally, I take the omnibus—but today, I hired a cab. This was no ordinary errand. I drove home, chose and annotated my first batch of books, then returned to Montagu Square with a carpet bag full of spiritual treasures—such a collection, I believed, unmatched in any European nation.

I paid the driver exactly what he asked. He responded with a curse. Instantly, I handed him a tract. His reaction was shocking—he jumped onto the box, swearing wildly, and drove off furiously.

Useless, I thought. I had sown the seed anyway—throwing a second tract into the cab window as it sped away.

At the door, the footman (not the one with the ribbons, thank heaven) informed me the doctor had just left, and Mr. Bruff, the lawyer, had arrived and was waiting in the library.

I was shown in. Mr. Bruff looked surprised to see me. We’d met before under Lady Verinder’s roof. He was a man worn by years of worldly service—once sharp, now grizzled. In business, he preached law and money; in leisure, he read novels and tore up tracts.

“Have you come to stay, Miss Clack?” he asked, eyeing my bag.

To reveal its contents to such a man would invite profanity. I lowered myself to his level and said simply, “My aunt told me she’s signing her will. She asked me to be a witness.”

“Aye? Aye? Well, Miss Clack, you’ll do. You’re over twenty-one. And you have no financial interest in the will.”

No pecuniary interest. Oh, how grateful I was to hear that! Had my aunt remembered me with even a small legacy—say five pounds—my enemies might have doubted my motives. Why carry such books? Why spend so much on a cab? But now—no doubt remained. Pure devotion. No motive but love.

I was lost in these thoughts when Mr. Bruff broke in.

“Well, Miss Clack, what’s the latest in charitable circles? How’s your friend Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite after the beating he took in Northumberland Street? Egad! They’re telling quite a tale about him at my club!”

I had ignored his earlier remarks about my age and lack of interest. But this—this insult to a dear friend—was too much.

Feeling bound, after what I’d heard that afternoon, to defend Mr. Ablewhite whenever his name was questioned—I also felt compelled to humiliate Mr. Bruff.

“I live outside the world,” I said. “And I don’t belong to a club. But I know the story—and I know it’s the vilest lie ever told.”

“Yes, yes,” he replied. “You believe in your friend. Natural enough. But Mr. Ablewhite won’t find the world so easy to convince as a committee of ladies. Appearances are against him. He was in the house when the Diamond vanished. And he was the first to go to London afterward. Ugly circumstances, ma’am.”

I should have corrected him then. But the temptation to draw him into his own trap was too strong.

“What do you mean by ‘later events’?” I asked, feigning innocence.

“Later events,” he said, growing more confident, “are those involving the Indians. As soon as they were released from Frizinghall prison, they went straight to London and targeted Mr. Luker. He, alarmed for a ‘valuable of great price,’ hid it in his bank’s safe. Clever move. But the Indians suspected it was being moved. So they devised a bold plan—to search not just Mr. Luker—but Mr. Ablewhite too.”

“Why?” I asked.

“Mr. Ablewhite says they acted on blind suspicion after seeing him talk to Mr. Luker. Absurd! Half a dozen others spoke to him that morning. Why weren’t they searched too? No—the real reason is, both men had a stake in the diamond. The Indians couldn’t tell which one had it, so they searched both.”

“Public opinion agrees,” he added, looking wise. “And public opinion isn’t easily challenged.”

I let him go on—just a little longer.

“I don’t argue with clever lawyers,” I said. “But is it fair to ignore Sergeant Cuff’s opinion? He investigated the case. Not a shadow of suspicion fell on anyone except Miss Verinder.”

“Do you agree with the sergeant?” he asked sharply.

“I judge no one,” I said. “I offer no opinion.”

“And I do both,” he said. “I think the sergeant was wrong. And I believe, if he knew Rachel as I do, he’d suspect everyone but her. She has flaws—secretive, headstrong, odd. But she’s true as steel, generous to a fault. If evidence pointed one way, and only her word pointed the other—I’d take her word, even as a lawyer.”

“Suppose,” I said, “you found Miss Verinder deeply interested in Mr. Ablewhite’s situation? Asking strange questions, agitated when she learned the scandal was spreading?”

“Suppose anything,” he replied. “It wouldn’t shake my belief in Rachel.”

“So she’s utterly trustworthy?”

“Utterly.”

“Then let me tell you,” I said, “that Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite was here less than two hours ago—and Miss Verinder herself declared his innocence in the strongest terms I’ve ever heard from a young lady.”

He leapt to his feet, stunned. I sat still, and recounted the scene.

“And now,” I asked gently, “what do you say about Mr. Ablewhite?”

“If Rachel vouches for him,” he said, “then I believe him as firmly as you do. I’ve been misled by appearances. I’ll publicly correct the slander wherever I encounter it. And I congratulate you—on your brilliant strategy. You’d have made a fine lawyer, if you’d been a man.”

He turned away, pacing irritably. I noticed his muttered words—phrases that hinted at his earlier suspicions. He had assumed Mr. Ablewhite stole the diamond, and that Rachel lied to protect him. Now, with Rachel’s testimony, that theory collapsed.

He stopped at the window, drumming on the glass.

“It defies explanation,” he muttered. “It’s beyond conjecture.”

I should have stayed silent. But I couldn’t.

“Pardon me,” I said, “but surely there’s a conjecture we haven’t considered.”

“Maybe,” he said. “I don’t know what it is.”

“Before I convinced you of Mr. Ablewhite’s innocence,” I said, “you mentioned he was in the house when the Diamond disappeared. But so was Mr. Franklin Blake.”

He turned sharply, took a seat opposite me, and smiled coldly.

“You’re not as good a lawyer as I thought,” he said. “You don’t know when to stop.”

“I don’t follow,” I said modestly.

“It won’t do,” he snapped. “Franklin Blake is a favorite of mine. But that doesn’t matter. I’ll accept your view. Yes—let’s suspect both. It’s in his character to steal the Moonstone. The question is—did he have a reason?”

“Mr. Blake’s debts are well known,” I said.

“And Mr. Ablewhite’s aren’t as advanced. True. But two obstacles stand in your way. First: I manage Blake’s affairs. Most of his creditors, knowing his father is wealthy, are content to wait and charge interest. That’s tough. The second is tougher. Lady Verinder told me her daughter was ready to marry Franklin Blake before the Diamond disappeared. She teased him, delayed him—but confessed her love. And her mother trusted him with the secret. So there he was—creditors willing to wait, and a certain future as an heir. Why steal the diamond?”

“The human heart is unfathomable,” I said gently. “Who can understand it?”

“In other words,” he replied, “he had no reason—but maybe did it out of depravity. Very well. Say he did. Then why make himself the most prominent person searching for it? To distract suspicion? But no one suspected him. He steals the diamond without cause, then plays a role that only draws attention—and offends the girl he meant to marry. That’s the absurdity you’re forced into if you accuse Blake.”

“No,” he said firmly. “After today, the case is locked. Rachel’s innocence is beyond doubt. Ablewhite’s is equally certain—if Rachel says so. And Blake’s innocence stands unchallenged. Yet—we know the diamond is in London. We know Mr. Luker or his banker holds it. What use is experience in such a case? It baffles me. It baffles you. It baffles everyone.”

Not everyone, I thought. It hadn’t baffled Sergeant Cuff.

I was about to mention that—gently, carefully—when the servant entered.

“The doctor has gone,” he said. “Lady Verinder is waiting.”

We rose. Mr. Bruff gathered his papers, looking weary. I picked up my bag, feeling I could have talked for hours.

We walked in silence to Lady Verinder’s room.

Let me add this: I’ve recorded this conversation not just to recount the facts, but because I am ordered to reveal the course of suspicion—and the names involved—when the Diamond was believed to be in London. This exchange answers that purpose. And it carries a moral benefit: it forces me to admit my own weakness. I confess—my fallen nature overcame me. But in confessing, I reclaim my strength. The balance is restored. The air feels clear again.

Dear friends—we may continue.
