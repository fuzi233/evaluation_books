Spring, 1849. I was wandering the East, having recently abandoned the itinerary I had meticulously planned and communicated to my lawyer and banker in London. This sudden shift necessitated sending a servant to retrieve my letters and funds from the English Consul in a city I was no longer visiting. He was to rejoin me at a designated time and place. Fate, however, intervened; an accident beyond his control delayed him. For a week, my party and I camped on the desert's edge, waiting. Finally, he appeared at my tent's entrance, bearing the money and letters.

"I fear I bring bad news, sir," he said, pointing to a letter marked with a mourning border and addressed in Mr. Bruff's handwriting.

Suspense is a torture I know well. That letter with the black border was the first I tore open. It announced my father's death and declared me heir to his vast fortune. With this wealth came heavy responsibilities, and Mr. Bruff urged me immediately to return to England.

By dawn, I was on my way home.

My old friend Betteredge's account of my departure paints a picture slightly exaggerated, in his own quaint fashion. He took my mistress's satirical jibes about my foreign education too seriously, convincing himself that I possessed genuine French, German, and Italian facets of character. My lively cousin merely teased about these traits, which never truly existed outside Betteredge's imagination. Yet, he was right about the core truth: I was heartbroken by Rachel's treatment and fled England in the raw agony of life's bitterest disappointment.

I went abroad, resolved that change and distance would aid my healing. I believed, and still believe, that separation does help a man; it forces the mind away from the exclusive contemplation of sorrow. I did not forget her, but the sting of memory dulled gradually as time, miles, and novelty interposed themselves between us.

Yet, as I turned homeward, the remedy that had worked so steadily began to falter. The closer I drew to the land she inhabited, the more irresistibly her influence reclaimed me. Upon leaving England, she was the last name I would utter; upon returning, she was the first I inquired about when I met Mr. Bruff again.

Of course, I was informed of everything that had transpired in my absence—the very events Betteredge had chronicled—with one crucial exception. Mr. Bruff felt no liberty to reveal the private motives that had driven Rachel and Godfrey Ablewhite to recall their mutual marriage promise. I spared him the embarrassment of questioning this delicate matter. It was sufficient relief, after the jealous pain of learning she had considered marrying Godfrey, to know that reflection had convinced her of her rashness and secured her release from the engagement.

Having heard the past, my inquiries naturally turned to the present: Who cared for her now? Where did she live?

She resided under the guardianship of the late Sir John Verinder's widow, Mrs. Merridew, appointed by her mother's executors. Reports stated they were getting along admirably, currently residing in Mrs. Merridew's house in Portland Place.

Within half an hour of receiving this news, I was on my way to Portland Place, lacking the courage to confess my haste to Mr. Bruff. The man who answered the door hesitated, unsure if Miss Verinder was home. I sent him upstairs with my card to settle the matter quickly. He returned with an impenetrable expression, informing me she was out.

I might have suspected others of deliberately denying me access, but it was impossible to suspect Rachel. I left word to call again at six.

At six, the servant repeated that Miss Verinder was not at home. Had she left a message? None. Had she even received my card? The servant apologized, insisting she had. The inference was undeniable: Rachel refused to see me.

Refused to be treated thus without cause, I demanded to know why. I sent my name to Mrs. Merridew, requesting an interview at her convenience. She agreed immediately, showing me into a comfortable sitting-room where I found a pleasant, elderly lady. She expressed great regret and surprise on my behalf but admitted she could offer no explanation or pressure Rachel on a matter of private feeling. Her polite patience was endless, yet it yielded nothing.

My final recourse was writing. My servant delivered a letter the next day with strict instructions to await a reply. The answer arrived in a single, stinging sentence: "Miss Verinder begs to decline entering into any correspondence with Mr. Franklin Blake."

Though fond of her, I felt the insult acutely. Before I could compose myself, Mr. Bruff entered on business matters. I dismissed his concerns instantly and laid the entire case before him. He proved as incapable of enlightening me as Mrs. Merridew. I asked if slander had been spoken against me. He knew of none. Had she mentioned me while under his roof? Never. Had she not asked, during my long absence, if I lived or died? No such question had ever passed her lips.

I produced from my pocket-book the letter Lady Verinder had written to me from Frizinghall the day I departed Yorkshire. I pointed out two specific sentences: *"The valuable assistance which you rendered to the inquiry after the lost jewel is still an unpardoned offence, in the present dreadful state of Rachel's mind. Moving blindfold in this matter, you have added to the burden of anxiety which she has had to bear, by innocently threatening her secret with discovery through your exertions."*

"Is it possible," I asked, "that the bitterness described there remains unchanged?"

Mr. Bruff looked deeply distressed. "If you insist on an answer," he said, "I can place no other interpretation on her conduct than that."

I rang the bell, ordering my servant to pack my portmanteau and fetch a railway guide. Mr. Bruff, astonished, asked what I intended.

"I am going to Yorkshire," I answered, "by the next train."

"For what purpose?" he asked.

"Mr. Bruff, the assistance I innocently gave regarding the Diamond was an unpardoned offence in Rachel's mind nearly a year ago, and it remains so. I will not accept that position! I am determined to uncover the secret of her silence toward her mother and her enmity toward *me*. If time, effort, and money can achieve it, I will lay my hand on the thief who stole the Moonstone!"

The worthy old gentleman attempted to remonstrate, to urge reason, to remind me of my duty. I was deaf to everything. No earthly consideration could shake my resolve.

"I shall resume the inquiry where I left off," I continued. "I will follow it step by step until I reach the present. There are missing links in the evidence as I left it, which Gabriel Betteredge can supply. To Gabriel Betteredge, I go!"

That evening, as the sun set, I stood once more on the well-remembered terrace, gazing at the peaceful old country house. The gardener was the first I saw in the deserted grounds. He had left Betteredge an hour prior, sunning himself in the customary corner of the back yard. I knew the routine well; I would seek him out.

Walking round the familiar paths, I peered through the open gate of the yard.

There he was—the dear old friend of the happy days that would never return. He sat in the old corner on the beehive chair, pipe in mouth, *Robinson Crusoe* on his lap, flanked by his two dog companions dozing on either side. From my vantage point, the last slanting rays of the sun cast my shadow forward. Either the dogs saw it or their keen scent detected my approach; they sprang up with a growl. Starting in his turn, the old man quieted them with a word, then shaded his failing eyes with his hand and looked inquiringly at the figure at the gate.

My own eyes filled with tears. I was forced to wait a moment before I could trust myself to speak.
