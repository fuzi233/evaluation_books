She held the spindle as she sat, Errina with the thick-coiled mat
Of raven hair and deepest agate eyes,
Gazing with a sad surprise
At surging visions of her destiny—
To spin the byssus drearily
In insect-labor, while the throng
Of gods and men wrought deeds that poets wrought in song.

When Deronda arrived at his mother’s apartment in the *Italia*, a familiar warmth stirred within him—the echo of childhood, tinged with early longing. The two servants in the antechamber glanced at him with quiet curiosity, surprised that the doctor their lady had summoned was this striking young man, whose presence lent even the strict lines of evening dress an air of grace. But Deronda barely noticed them. Only when the second door opened did he find himself face to face with the woman who had shaped his dreams from afar.

She stood at the far end of the spacious room, draped in black lace that fell loosely from her silvery hair to the long train trailing behind her. Her arms, bare to the elbow and adorned only with rich bracelets, were folded across her chest. The noble tilt of her head gave her features a dignity beyond mere beauty. Yet before Deronda could fully take her in, he was already before her, holding the hand she extended, then lifting it gently to his lips.

She kept her hand in his, studying him with a gaze both piercing and unreadable. He felt no time to think—only the awareness that her eyes shifted like living shadows, her expression changing with each breath, as if she were not just a woman but a force shaping the moment. He stood still, uncertain how to respond, his cheeks warming despite himself. He was shaken—not by joy, nor sorrow, but by something deeper: the realization that every imagined reunion had been more vivid than this one. He couldn’t even guess what language she would speak.

Then, suddenly, she released his hand and placed both palms on his shoulders. A flash of admiration lit her face—every line softened, every trace of age erased for a fleeting instant, as though youth had returned in defiance of time.

“You are a beautiful creature,” she said, her voice low and melodic, with a rhythm that hinted at foreign lands yet carried a charm all its own. “I knew you would be.” Then she kissed him once on each cheek, and he returned the gesture. It was not love, but recognition—like the meeting of two sovereigns.

She paused, letting the lines return to her face, and then spoke again, colder now. “I am your mother. But you can have no love for me.”

“I have thought of you more than any other being,” Deronda replied, his voice trembling slightly. “More than anyone.”

“I am not what you imagined,” she said firmly, withdrawing her hands and folding her arms once more, her eyes fixed on him as if inviting him to judge her. He had always pictured her face as resembling his own—he saw echoes now, yes, but they were drowned in differences too profound to ignore. She was extraordinary. What made him feel such distance? Her beauty was worn, yes, but also alien—as though she belonged not to this world, but to some half-remembered myth, a Melusina caught between realms.

“I used to think you might be suffering,” Deronda said, careful not to wound her. “I wished I could bring you comfort.”

“I _am_ suffering,” she answered, her voice harder now. “But not the kind you can ease.” She moved toward a sofa where cushions had been arranged with care. “Sit down.” She gestured to a chair beside her, then softened, noticing the distress in his face. “I am not suffering now. I am at peace. I can talk.”

Deronda obeyed, sitting quietly, waiting. He felt as though he stood before fate itself, not the mother he had dreamed of. A strange distance had opened between them—one born not of absence, but of revelation.

“No,” she began. “I did not call you here to be comforted. I didn’t know how you’d feel about me. I don’t know now. I don’t expect you to love me simply because I am your mother—when you’ve never known me. But I believed I chose better for you than keeping you with me. I didn’t believe I took anything worth having from you.”

“You cannot mean to tell me your affection wouldn’t have been worth having,” Deronda said, sensing she expected a reply.

“I’m not speaking ill of myself,” she said sharply, pride flaring. “But I had little affection to give. I didn’t want it. I was smothered by it. I wanted to live the life inside me, unburdened by others’ lives. You wonder who I was. I wasn’t a princess then.”

She rose abruptly, standing tall as before. Deronda rose with her, breathless.

“No princess in this quiet life I lead now. I was a great singer. I acted as well as I sang. All others paled beside me. Men followed me across borders. I lived a thousand lives in one. I did not want a child.”

There was fire in her voice—defensive, raw. Precedent meant nothing to her. She had cast it aside. There was no excuse, only truth, spoken with desperate intensity. She seemed to hurl those words into the air, as if warding off some unseen judgment. And Deronda stood there, clutching his coat collar, feeling as though he watched a sacred ritual—something holy and terrible, where crime wore a veil of reverence.

What else would she reveal?

She continued, her face pale yet luminous with inner light.

“I did not want to marry. I was forced into marrying your father—forced by my father’s demands. And even then, it was my way to gain freedom. I could rule my husband. Not my father. I had a right to be free. I had a right to break free from a bondage I despised.”

She sat again, her eyes narrowing slightly, lips pressed tight—as though she held back more words. Deronda remained standing, watching.

“And the bondage I hated for myself, I wanted to spare you,” she said, softer now, almost pleading. “What more could any mother do? I freed you from the burden of being born a Jew.”

“Then I *am* a Jew?” Deronda burst out, voice deep and urgent, making her recoil slightly against her cushions. “My father was a Jew… and you are a Jewess?”

“Yes,” she said, watching him closely, her expression shifting. “Your father was my cousin.”

“I am glad,” Deronda said, impulsively, his voice low but fierce. He hadn’t expected to say it. He hadn’t allowed himself to admit it before. Yet here it was—spilled forth in opposition to her, born of anger and confusion. He was shaken—by rage, yes, but also by grief. This mother had borne him unwillingly, abandoned him willingly, and now revealed herself reluctantly. Was that why she spoke so harshly?

The mother, too, trembled—but differently. Her body shook visibly, her eyes wide with pallid excitement.

“Why are you glad?” she demanded, voice rising. “You are an English gentleman. I ensured that.”

“You didn’t know what you ensured,” Deronda replied, throwing himself sideways into his chair, leaning his arm over the back. “How could you choose my birthright for me?”

He was aflame with a sense of injustice—foreign, yet undeniable. But he fought to silence himself. A horror crept in: that he might say something irreversible in this moment of transformation.

Silence stretched between them.

Then she spoke again, her voice firmer now, layered with resistance:

“I chose for you what I would have chosen for myself. How could I know you’d carry your father’s spirit? How could I know you’d love what I despised—if you truly love being a Jew?” The last words cut through the air with bitterness. Any listener might have thought hatred had taken root between them.

But Deronda had regained his composure. He recalled the life she had lived—the years lost, the pain etched in her frame. He remembered his own tendency to shamefully accept things as final, as if they were solely his burden. Now, looking at her, he found a new clarity. His face softened, regaining its calm depth. Yet it unsettled her—her eyes locked onto his, fascinated, yet untouched by maternal joy.

“Forgive me if I speak hastily,” he said, voice grave and humble. “Why have you chosen now to reveal this—to me, after keeping it hidden? Why—since you seem angry that I am glad?”

“Oh, the reasons for our actions!” she said, a bitter edge in her tone. “When you’re as old as I am, you’ll see it’s not so simple to ask, ‘Why did you do this?’ People speak of motives as if they were neatly tied. Every woman is supposed to feel the same—otherwise she’s a monster. I am not a monster. But I did not feel what others claim to feel—or pretend to, so they won’t be seen as different. When you blame me for sending you away, you mean I should have said I loved you as mothers say they love their children. I did not. I was relieved to be rid of you. But I did what was best for you. I gave you your father’s fortune. Do I now seem to be undoing everything? No—there are reasons. I feel things I cannot explain. A fatal illness has grown in me for a year. I may not live another. I will not deny what I’ve done. I will not feign love where none exists. But shadows gather. Sickness brings them. If I wronged the dead—I have little time left to make amends.”

Her speech flowed with a mastery of tone and emotion—like a performance by the finest actress. Yet it was real. She was not merely acting; she *was* her emotions. For her, feeling became drama instantly. Pain, joy, regret—they all transformed into spectacle. In lesser people, this might be common; in her, it was artistry perfected. She felt deeply, yes—but through a lens of self-awareness, where every ache bore a halo of spiritual intoxication, both elevating and numbing.

Deronda did not reflect on this. He listened only to the meaning—her confession, her suffering, the weight of her past. He longed to understand the strange conflict that had brought him into the world. Compassion swelled within him, drawn to her pain, her honesty. He dared not question further when she fell silent, brow furrowed, head turned slightly away, eyes fixed on something invisible.

She broke the silence abruptly, turning to him with sudden intensity.

“Sir Hugo has written much about you. He says you have a remarkable mind—you comprehend everything—you are wiser than he, at sixty. You say you’re glad to be born a Jew. I won’t pretend I’ve changed my mind about that. Your feelings oppose mine. You don’t thank me. Will you understand your mother—or only condemn her?”

“There is not a fiber in me that does not wish to understand her,” Deronda replied, meeting her gaze steadily. “It is a cruel reversal of my desire to blame. For fifteen years, I’ve tried to understand those who differ from me.”

“Then you are unlike your grandfather in that,” she said. “Though you resemble him in face. He never understood me. Or if he did, he only sought to bind me to obedience. I was to be the ‘Jewish woman’ under threat of his curse. I was to feel what I didn’t feel, believe what I didn’t believe. I was to revere the parchment in the *mezuza*; dread butter touching meat; admire men binding *tephillin* while women were forbidden. I was to adore laws that seemed absurd to me. To love the prayers in the ugly synagogue, the chanting, the fasting, the endless feasts, my father’s endless lectures on our people—words like thunder without meaning. I was to care forever about Israel’s past. I cared for nothing of it. I cared for the wide world, for all I could become in it. I hated living under my father’s rigid shadow. Teaching, teaching—‘this you must be,’ ‘that you must not’—it pressed on me like a cage tightening with every year. I wanted to live freely, to move with the current, not be bound to care. Ah!”—her voice sharpened—“you’re glad to be born a Jew. You say so. That’s because you weren’t raised as one. That separation seems sweet to you because I saved you from it.”

“When you decided that, you meant I should never know my origins?” Deronda asked, unable to contain himself. “You’ve changed in your feelings about that.”

“Yes—that was my intent. That is what I upheld. It’s not true I’ve changed. Things have changed around me. I remain Leonora”—she touched her chest—“here, the same desire, the same will, the same choice. But”—she spread her hands, palms up, lips compressed—“events come upon us like curses. Thoughts, feelings, visions in the dark—these are events, are they not? I do not consent. We only consent to what we love. I obey something tyrannical”—she gestured again—“I am forced to wither, to suffer, to die slowly. Do I love that? Well, I’ve been forced to obey my dead father. I’ve been forced to tell you you are a Jew, to deliver what he commanded.”

“I beg you—tell me what drove you, when you were young, to take this path,” Deronda said, seeking refuge in the past, away from the agony of her present suffering. “I understand your struggle. Though my experience is different, I feel your pain. I can imagine the cruelty of enforced renunciation.”

“No,” she said, shaking her head, arms folded decisively. “You are not a woman. You may try—but you cannot imagine what it is to possess a man’s genius, yet endure the slavery of being a girl. To have a mold imposed—‘this is the Jewish woman; this is what you must be.’ A woman’s heart must be small—no larger, or it must be crushed like Chinese feet. Her happiness is made like cakes, by fixed recipe. That was what my father wanted. He wished I had been a son. He saw me as a substitute. His heart was set on Judaism. He hated the idea that Jewish women would be seen as fit for public singing, for acting. As if we weren’t more enviable for that! That’s a chance to escape bondage.”

“Was my grandfather learned?” Deronda asked, eager for details.

She waved her hand impatiently. “Oh, yes—clever, a physician, good. I don’t deny he was admirable. A man worthy of a play—grand, with iron will. Like Foscari before he pardons. But such men turn wives and daughters into slaves. They’d rule the world if they could; failing that, they crush women’s souls beneath their will. But nature sometimes resists. My father had no other child but me—and I was like him.”

She folded her arms again, facing whatever came next.

“Your father was different. Gentle, loving. I knew I could rule him. Before marriage, I made him promise secretly that he would never hinder my art. My father lay dying when we wed. From the start, he intended me to marry my cousin Ephraim. And when a woman’s will matches a man’s, half her strength lies in concealment. I meant to have my way—but only by pretending obedience. I feared my father—always. I hated that fear. I wished I could defy him openly—but I never could. I couldn’t imagine it. I couldn’t act it out in my mind: defying him and succeeding. And I would never risk failure.”

She paused, as if the words unlocked a flood of memories.

Deronda listened, emotions swirling—repulsion at her coldness, indignation at her choices, but now also a growing awe. The initial clash of expectations had faded. He saw not just a mother, but a woman of immense power, whose mistakes walked on high paths. If she had not been his mother, he might have admired her as a stranger. Still, he could not be neutral. He feared what she might say next. He dreaded the coercion she seemed to endure in revealing herself. He almost wished to say, “Only tell me what’s necessary.” Yet he was captivated—drawn to her words, her face, her soul.

He tried to pull her back to facts.

“Where was my grandfather’s home?”

“In Genoa—where I married. His family lived here for generations. But my father traveled widely.”

“You must have lived in England?”

“My mother was English—a Jewess of Portuguese descent. My father married her in England. Certain circumstances of that marriage changed everything. Through it, my father thwarted his own plans. My mother’s sister was a singer—later she married the English partner of a merchant house here. They lived here eleven years. My mother died when I was eight. My father let me stay with Aunt Leonora, taught under her eye—as if he didn’t mind the danger of her encouraging my dream to sing, as she had. But this was it—I saw it again and again: he didn’t guard against consequences, because he believed he could stop them whenever he liked. Before my aunt left Genoa, I had enough training to awaken the singer and actress within me. My father didn’t know everything—but he knew I was learning music, that I loved it. That meant nothing to him. He expected obedience. And he insisted I marry my cousin Ephraim—the only surviving member of his family he knew. I didn longer to marry. I planned ways to resist. But finally, I realized I could control him. So I agreed. My father died three weeks after our wedding—and then I had my way!”

She said it with a surge of triumph—but then her face hardened.

“It hasn’t lasted. My father is winning now.”

She looked at her son again, more contemplatively.

“You are like him—but milder. There’s something of your father in you. He devoted his life to me. He gave up banking, money-changing—lived only to serve me. He went against his conscience for me. As I loved my art, so he loved me. Let me see your hand again—the one with the ring.”

Deronda drew closer and offered his hand. We know the hand—strong, noble. Hers, smaller, was of the same type. As she touched it, as he saw her face—his own, aged not by time but by passion—he felt a surge of reverence.

“Mother,” he said, voice fervent, “take us all into your heart—the living and the dead. Forgive everything that wounds you. Accept my love.”

She looked at him—admiring, not loving. Then kissed him gently on the brow.

“I reject nothing,” she whispered, “but I have nothing to give.”

And with that, she released his hand and sank back into her cushions.

Deronda turned pale—the pain of rejected tenderness, a sensation deeper than emotion. She noticed, and said, softly, melodiously,

“You are strong. But I am weak. And weakness cannot answer strength.”
