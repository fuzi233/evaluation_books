The key to Gerard's prison lay heavy in Ghysbrecht Van Swieten's pouch, a cold weight against his hip. He waited until the clock struck ten, calculating the silence of the morning. "A little hunger sometimes does well; it breaks 'em," he muttered to the empty air, his voice rasping like dry leaves. At the appointed hour, he crept up the stairs, a loaf of bread and a pitcher of water tucked under his arm, trailed by his servant, Dierich Brower, who moved with the silent, armed readiness of a wolf guarding its kill. Ghysbrecht paused at the door, listening to the stillness within. A grim smile twisted his features. "By this time, he will be as down-hearted as Albert Koestein was," he thought, a cruel satisfaction warming his chest. He turned the lock and pushed the door open.

There was no sign of Gerard.

Ghysbrecht stood frozen, the blood draining from his face until he looked as though he might faint. Though his visage remained hidden in shadow, his entire body seemed to lose its vitality, stiffening into a statue of shock. Moments later, he collapsed, trembling violently against the wall. Behind him, Dierich sensed the shift in his master's demeanor and stepped forward, peering over the old man's shoulder. The sight of the empty cell, the discarded rope, and the iron bar hanging loose sent a cry of wonder through the servant's throat. His astonishment doubled when he saw his master disregard everything else, throwing himself to his knees before the empty chest. With quivering hands, Ghysbrecht frantically patted the wood, as if he feared the truth might vanish if he stopped touching it.

Dierich stared at him in utter bewilderment. "Why, master, what is the matter?"

Ghysbrecht's pale lips moved, forming words that never escaped his throat. His hands fell limp at his sides, and he fixed his gaze on the hollow space where the records should have been. "Why, master, what avails glaring into that empty box? The lad is not there. See here! Note the cunning of the young rogue; he hath taken out the bar—"

"GONE! GONE! GONE!" Ghysbrecht shrieked, the sound tearing from his throat.

"Gone! What is gone, Holy saints! Is he planet-struck?" Dierich stammered, panic rising in his voice.

"STOP THIEF!" Ghysbrecht roared, suddenly turning on his servant with the fury of a cornered beast. He grabbed Dierich by the collar, shaking him with a strength that belied his age and infirmity. "Do you stand there, knave, and watch your master robbed? Run! Fly! A hundred crowns to him who finds them for me again. No, no! 'Tis in vain. Oh, fool! Foolish fool to leave that in the same room with him! But none ever found the secret spring before. None ever would but he. It was to be. It is to be. Lost! Lost!" The frenzy burned bright and brief, only to be extinguished instantly by the crushing weight of his years. He sank back onto the chest, muttering the word over and over, "Lost! Lost!"

"What is lost, master?" Dierich asked, his voice softening with kindness.

"House and lands and good name," Ghysbrecht groaned, wringing his hands until they were red and feeble.

"WHAT?" Dierich cried, the word exploding in the quiet room.

That emphatic tone, laced with eager curiosity, struck a chord in Ghysbrecht's mind and revived his natural cunning. "I have lost the town records," he stammered, looking askance at the servant like a fox caught near a hen-roost.

"Oh, is that all?"

"Is't not enough? What will the burghers say to me? What will the burghs do?" Ghysbrecht's composure shattered again. "A hundred crowns to him who shall recover them; all, mind, all that were in this box. If one be missing, I give nothing."

"'Tis a bargain, master: the hundred crowns are in my pouch. See you not that where Gerard Eliassoen is, there are the pieces of sheepskin you rate so highly?"

"That is true; that is true, good Dierich: good faithful Dierich. All, mind, all that were in the chest."

"Master, I will take the constables to Gerard's house, and seize him for the theft."

"The theft? Ay! Good; very good. It is theft. I forgot that. So, as he is a thief now, we will put him in the dungeons below, where the toads are and the rats. Dierich, that man must never see daylight again. 'Tis his own fault; he must be prying. Quick, quick! Ere he has time to talk, you know, time to talk."

Within half an hour, Dierich Brower and four constables burst into the hosier's house, demanding young Gerard from the panic-stricken Catherine.

"Alas! What has he done now?" she cried, her voice trembling. "That boy will break my heart."

"Nay, dame, but a trick of youth," Dierich replied smoothly, masking his inner turmoil. "He hath but made off with certain skins of parchment, in a frolic doubtless. But the burgomaster is answerable to the burgh for their safekeeping, so he is in care about them; as for the youth, he will doubtless be quit for a reprimand."

This polished speech completely deceived Catherine, but her daughter, Kate, remained suspicious. Her doubts were sharpened by the disproportionate anger and disappointment Dierich displayed the moment he learned Gerard was not at home, nor had he been seen that night.

"Come away then," Dierich said roughly, dismissing the woman. "We are wasting time." He added vehemently, "I'll find him if he is above ground."

Affiction sharpens the wits, and often it has made an innocent person more than a match for the wily. As Dierich prepared to leave, Kate made a signal for him to speak with her privately. He bade his men wait outside the door and joined her.

"Hush!" she whispered. "My mother knows not. Gerard has left Tergou."

"How?"

"I saw him last night."

"Ay! Where?" Dierich cried, his interest piqued despite his frustration.

"At the foot of the haunted tower."

"How did he get the rope?"

"I know not; but this I know: my brother Gerard bade me there farewell, and he is many leagues from Tergou ere this. The town, you know, was always unworthy of him, and when it imprisoned him, he vowed never to set foot in it again. Let the burgomaster be content, then. He has imprisoned him, and he has driven him from his birthplace and from his native land. What need now to rob him and us of our good name?"

At another moment, this might have struck Dierich as sound logic; but he was too mortified by Gerard's escape and the potential loss of a hundred crowns to listen to reason.

"What need had he to steal?" he retorted bitterly.

"Gerard stole not the trash; he but took it to spite the burgomaster, who stole his liberty; but he shall answer to the Duke for it, he shall. As for these skins of parchment you keep such a coil about, look in the nearest brook or stye, and 'tis odds but you find them."

"Think ye so, mistress?—think ye so?" Dierich's eyes flashed with sudden suspicion. "Mayhap you know 'tis so."

"This I know, that Gerard is too good to steal, and too wise to load himself with rubbish, going a journey."

"Give you good day, then," Dierich said sharply. "The sheepskin you scorn, I value it more than the skin of any in Tergou."

With that, he hurried away on a false scent, leaving the house to its own devices.

Kate returned inside and drew Giles aside. "Giles, my heart misgives me; breathe not to a soul what I say to you. I have told Dirk Brower that Gerard is out of Holland, but much I doubt he is not a league from Tergou."

"Why, where is he, then?"

"Where should he be, but with her he loves? But if so, he must not loiter. These be deep and dark and wicked men that seek him. Giles, I see that in Dirk Brower's eye makes me tremble. Oh, why cannot I fly to Sevenbergen and bid him away? Why am I not lusty and active like other girls? God forgive me for fretting at His will; but I never felt till now what it is to be lame and weak and useless. But you are strong, dear Giles," she added coaxingly, her eyes pleading. "You are very strong."

"Yes, I am strong," thundered Perpusillus, then, catching sight of her meaning, "but I hate to go on foot," he added sulkily.

"Alas! Alas! Who will help me if you will not? Dear Giles, do you not love Gerard?"

"Yes, I like him best of the lot. I'll go to Sevenbergen on Peter Buyskens his mule. Ask you him, for he won't lend her me."

Kate remonstrated. The whole town would follow him. It would be known whither he was gone, and Gerard would be in worse danger than before.

Giles parried this by promising to ride out of the town the opposite way, and not turn the mule's head towards Sevenbergen until he had rid himself of the curious. Kate then assented and borrowed the mule. She charged Giles with a short but meaning message, and made him repeat it after her over and over, until he could say it word for word.

Giles mounted the mule, and little Kate retired, doing the last thing now in her power for her beloved brother—praying on her knees long and earnestly for his safety.
