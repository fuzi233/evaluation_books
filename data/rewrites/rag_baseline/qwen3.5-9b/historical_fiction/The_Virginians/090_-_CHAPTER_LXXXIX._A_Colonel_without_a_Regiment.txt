The silence in the library was not empty; it was heavy with the weight of things unsaid. I sat at the mahogany desk, the inkwell a dark pool beside me, tasked with the mechanical arrangement of lies into a coherent narrative of war. Outside, the air hummed with a low-frequency vibration—the collective anxiety of a continent holding its breath. In Virginia, the gentry spoke of loyalty with the fervor of religious zealots, their voices rising in toast after toast to the King, promising to die rather than submit. Yet, beneath the surface of this performative patriotism, a fissure was widening, a crack running through the foundation of the colony itself.

I had written to my brother, Hal, urging him to reconsider his path, to see the folly of the resistance. My mother had joined the campaign, wielding scripture like a weapon, threatening divine retribution for his disobedience. Her letters were fiery, her logic rigid, but Hal's response was a masterpiece of evasion. Written with a hand that seemed borrowed from a stranger, it was a composition of such neat, temperate elegance that I suspected Mr. Belman, the Whig clergyman, had penned it. Hal had not answered my plea; he had simply walked away, disappearing into the countryside under the guise of inspecting horses, a ruse that masked his retreat toward the mountains and eventually the enemy lines.

With my efforts fruitless, I returned to the Governor's estate, where the atmosphere was thick with the scent of gunpowder and arrogance. The officials there were agitated, their faces flushed with the anticipation of conflict. They spoke of "Old England" with a savagery that bordered on madness, eager to ride down the "wicked Whigs" once the signal was given. They had appointed me Colonel of the Westmoreland Defenders, a corps raised in the vain hope that my name alone would fill its ranks. The officers were chosen quickly, but the recruits vanished as fast as they appeared, fleeing the shadow of the coming storm. Even my old tutor, Mr. Dempster, had ridden south to the Carolinas, seeking out the descendants of Jacobites to bolster the royalist cause, but the tide was turning faster than any sermon could arrest.

News from the north arrived in fragments, carried by riders who looked as though they had seen hell. Lexington. Shots fired. The war had begun. The almanacs recorded dates that would later be etched in stone, but here, in Williamsburg, the reality was a disorienting blur. We claimed loyalty, yet we watched as twenty thousand armed petitioners gathered around Boston, demanding freedom. Benedict Arnold, a name whispered with a mixture of awe and dread, had already begun his career of theft and treason, capturing forts with the audacity of a burglar. The British generals, marching from London with reinforcements, prepared to crush the rebellion, only to stumble at Breed's Hill. It was a blunder of epic proportions, a defeat that transformed a hill into a symbol of liberty, a beacon that burned brighter than any torch the Crown could wield.

And then, the final blow to our delusion. The Virginian regiment, selected to march under the new General-in-Chief, included Henry Esmond Warrington. My brother. By his side rode his wife, Fanny, a figure of small stature and immense courage. Their departure meant that the terrifying specter of civil war would not consume our immediate family, sparing us the sight of mothers fighting daughters, brothers against brothers. But the cost elsewhere was catastrophic. The people, inflamed by the news of Lexington, rallied behind the rebels. Farmers and ploughmen, doctors and lawyers, threw aside their tools for muskets, convinced that they could match the bravest sons of Britain.

Our Governor, a peer of Scotland and the Sovereign's representative, had been the loudest voice calling for unity. He had invited all loyal subjects to rally to the King's standard. But when the moment of truth arrived, he did not stand firm. Instead, he fled. Under the cover of night, he boarded a man-of-war, escaping to the sea, leaving his office and his people to face the consequences of his cowardice. The Assembly, stripped of its protector, was forced to rule in his absence. The mob, emboldened by his flight, descended upon the plantations. Runaway slaves, seeking refuge from the harsh discipline of the masters, joined the fugitive governor, landing ashore to burn and destroy. Where the shepherd had abandoned the flock, the wolves entered. The Governor's ship became a floating fortress of chaos, sending ambassadors to Indian tribes and inviting destruction upon the very soil he had sworn to defend. As the smoke rose over Norfolk and Hampton, the illusion of the "unconquerable power of England" shattered, revealing a leadership that had run before the first shot was truly fired.
