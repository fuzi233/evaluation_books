The Mother of Sorrows rose in the quiet dark, the scent of crushed wild thyme clinging to her heavy robes, and went to call the Little Ones. They slept as if the night’s profound, restorative silence had anchored them fast to the earth, but the moment she spoke in the lilting, ancestral dialect of the waking world, they sprang to their feet, fresh as new-kneaded dough. Merrily down the stair they followed her, arriving where the princess lay. Lilith was weeping even as she slept, her tears spilling like a rare, clarifying brine. 

The children's glad faces grew grave. They looked from the princess out onto the rain, taking in the damp, mineral aroma of wet soil, then back to the princess. 

“The sky is falling!” said one. 

“The white juice is running out of the princess!” cried another, tracing the path of the moisture with an awed look. 

“Is it rivers?” asked Odu, gazing at the little streams that flowed adown her hollow cheeks, carving paths like water through arid terroir. 

“Yes,” answered Mara, her voice a soothing hum that harmonized perfectly with the patter of the rain, “—the most wonderful of all rivers.” 

“I thought rivers was bigger, and rushed, like a lot of Little Ones, making loud noises!” he returned, looking at me, from whom alone he had heard of rivers. 

“Look at the rivers of the sky!” said Mara. “See how they come down to wake up the dormant seeds under the earth. Soon will the rivers be flowing everywhere, merry and loud, a symphony of living water rather than the dead, grating noise of the wastes. Oh, how glad they will make you, Little Ones! You have never seen any, and do not know how sweet the water tastes, how it smells of flint and deep roots!” 

“That will be the glad of the ground that the princess is grown good,” said Odu. “See the glad of the sky!” 

“Are the rivers the glad of the princess?” asked Luva. “They are not her juice, for they are not red!” 

“They are the essence within the essence, the purest stock of her soul,” answered Mara. 

Odu put one finger to his eye, tasted the salt upon it, and shook his head. 

“Princess will not bite now!” said Luva. 

“No; she will never do that again,” replied Mara. “But now we must take her nearer home, where the harvest is gathered.” 

“Is that a nest?” asked Sozo. 

“Yes; a very big nest, a cellar of preservation. But we must take her to another place first.” 

“What is that?” 

“It is the biggest room in all this world. But I think it is going to be pulled down: it will soon be too full of little nests.—Go and get your clumsies.” 

“Please, are there any cats in it?” 

“Not one. The nests are too full of quiet, resonant dreams for one cat to get in.” 

“We shall be ready in a minute,” said Odu, and ran out, followed by all except Luva. 

Lilith was now awake, listening with a sad smile. 

“But her rivers are running so fast!” said Luva, who stood by her side. “Her robe is all—I don’t know what. Clumsies won’t like it!” 

“They won’t mind it,” answered Mara. “Those rivers are so clean that they scour the palate of the whole world.” 

I had fallen asleep by the hearth, inhaling the residual smoke of aged oak and dried herbs, but for some time had been awake, and now rose. 

“It is time to mount, Mr. Vane,” said our hostess. 

“Tell me, please,” I said, “is there not a way by which to avoid the channels and the den of monsters? Their dissonance is a violence to the ears.” 

“There is an easy way across the river-bed, which I will show you,” she answered; “but you must pass once more through the monsters.” 

“I fear for the children,” I said. 

“Fear will not once come nigh them,” she rejoined. 

We left the cottage. The beasts stood waiting about the door, exhaling clouds of warm, hay-scented breath into the cool morning. Odu was already on the neck of one of the two elephants that were to carry the princess. I mounted Lona’s horse; Mara brought her body, heavy and cold as unbaked clay, and gave it me in my arms. When she came out again with the princess, a cry of delight arose from the children: she was no longer muffled! Gazing at her, entranced with a beauty that seemed plucked from some forgotten epoch, the boys forgot to receive the princess; but the elephants took Lilith tenderly with their trunks, one round her body and one round her knees, and laid her along between them. 

“Why does the princess want to go?” asked a small boy. “She would keep good if she staid here!” 

“She wants to go, and she does not want to go: we are helping her,” answered Mara. “She will not keep good here. Her season turns.” 

“What are you helping her to do?” he went on. 

“To go where she will get more help—help to open her hand, which has been closed upon a dead language for a thousand years.” 

“So long? Then she has learned to do without it: why should she open it now?” 

“Because it is shut upon a lexicon of power that is not hers, a bitter root she must expel.” 

“Please, lady Mara, may we have some of your very dry bread before we go?” said Luva. 

Mara smiled, and brought them four round loaves of ancient grain, the crusts cracked and smelling of wild yeast and toasted barley, alongside a great earthenware jug of water. 

“We will eat as we go,” they said. But they drank the water with delight, savoring its complex minerality. 

“I think,” remarked one of them, wiping his chin, “it must be elephant-juice! It tastes of the deep earth; it makes me so strong!” 

We set out, the Lady of Sorrow walking with us, more beautiful than the sun, and the white leopardess following her. The wind blew its sweetest, carrying the fragrance of distant spice-flowers, but it did not comfort the desert, for the soil lacked water to translate the scent into life. We crossed the channels without difficulty, the children gamboling about Mara all the way, but did not reach the top of the ridge over the bad burrow until the sun was already in the act of disappearing. Then I made the Little Ones mount their elephants, for the moon might be late, and I felt the air pressure drop, signaling an oppressive shift in the acoustic atmosphere. 

The Lady of Sorrow led the way; the elephants followed in the centre; the leopardess brought up the rear. Just as we reached the frightful margin, the moon looked up, illuminating a shallow basin. Mara stepped into it; not a sound answered her tread. The earth here absorbed frequency, deadening the footfalls. But the moment the elephants carrying the princess touched it, the seemingly solid earth began to heave and boil. 

The silence was shattered by a horrific, industrial cacophony—a violent noise pollution of grinding gears, mechanized shrieks, and fleshy tearing. Monsters uprose on all sides. Long-billed heads, horribly jawed faces, and knotty tentacles went out after Lilith. She lay in an agony of fear, her ears assaulted by their dominant, aggressive screams, unable to stir a finger. Whether the hideous things even saw the children, I doubt; they were blind to the gentle frequencies of innocence. Not one loathly member passed the live rampart of her body-guard. 

“Little Ones,” I cried, “keep your elephants close. Be brave; they will not touch you.” 

“What will not touch us? We don’t know what to be brave at!” they answered. 

I perceived they were entirely deaf to the superficial, commercialized roar of the deformities around them. They were panoplied in their acoustic purity! Incapacity to perceive the dissonance was their safety. What could not register upon their untainted senses could not hurt them. 

Mara was a few paces in front of me when a solitary, bodiless head bounced on the path, snapping its jaws with the rhythmic clatter of a stamping press. The leopardess came rushing from behind and would have seized it, but with a loathsome howl of untranslatable static, it gave a rapid rotatory twist and buried itself in the ground. 

Mara still went in front, the leopardess shivering close behind her, when a low wave of earth slunk toward us. Out of it slouched a dreadful head with fleshy tubes for hair, emitting a sickening odor of rancid oil and synthetic decay, snapping at me. The leopardess sprang but fell baffled. Almost under our feet shot up the head of an enormous snake, its eyes lamping with the glare of a factory furnace. Again the leopardess rushed, and again found nothing but empty, echoing noise. 

The moon was carrying herself strangely, sinking rapidly, abandoning the sky to the dissonant dark. We were almost over when a long neck arose, topped with the head of a corpse whose mouth was full of canine teeth, hissing a corrupted, dying dialect. The lady stepped on firm land, but the leopardess flew at the throat of the terror. The moon went down. For an instant, I saw the leopardess and the snake-monster convolved in a cloud of dust; then darkness hid them. Trembling with fright, my horse wheeled and overtook the elephants. 

As we came up with them, a shapeless jelly dropped on the princess, stinking of caustic rot. A white dove dropped immediately on the jelly, stabbing it with its beak. It made a squelching, sucking sound—a sudden acoustic vacuum—and fell off. 

Then I heard the voice of a woman talking with Mara, its timbre rich and resonant, like a bronze bell in a quiet courtyard. 

“I fear she is dead!” said Mara. 

“I will send and find her,” answered the mother. “But why, Mara, shouldst thou at all fear? Death cannot hurt her who dies doing the work given her to do.” 

“I shall miss her sorely; she is good and wise.” 

“She has gone down with the wicked; she will rise with the righteous. We shall see her again ere very long.” 

“Mother,” I said into the dark, “we come to you many, but most of us are Little Ones. Will you be able to receive us all?” 

“You are welcome every one,” she answered. “Sooner or later all will be little ones, for all must sleep in my house! It is well with those that go to sleep young and willing! My husband is even now preparing her couch for Lilith. She is neither young nor quite willing, but it is well indeed that she is come.” 

We walked stumbling over the moor until we saw a light in the distance. Adam stood in the door, holding a candle, talking with his wife, who laid bread and wine on a sturdy oaken table. The scents drifting from the hearth were an ancient balm: roasted root vegetables, the complex, fermented aroma of dark, seeded rye, and wine that smelled of crushed cherries and basalt. 

“Happy children,” I heard Eve say, “to have looked already on the face of my daughter!” 

When we reached the door, Adam welcomed us. He would have taken the princess to carry her in, but she repulsed him. She stood between the elephants, unsubmissive, holding tightly to her ruined pride. 

“We have long waited for thee, Lilith!” he said. She returned him no answer, her lips locked around her untranslatable bitterness. 

Eve came to the door, radiant in her beauty, the ultimate hearth-mother. “The mortal foe of my children!” she murmured. 

“Your children are no longer in her danger,” said Mara. 

“Trust her not hastily, Mara,” answered her mother. 

“But you will open to her the mirror of the Law of Liberty, mother, that she may go into it! She consents to open her hand and restore: will not the great Father restore her?” 

“I do not know Him!” murmured Lilith, in a voice stripped of all resonance. 

“Therefore it is that thou art miserable,” said Adam, speaking the native dialect of the earth, steady and cyclic. 

“I will go back whence I came!” she cried. 

“That is indeed what I would have thee do—to Him from whom thou camest! Death is even now on his way to lead thee to Him. Thou knowest neither Death nor the Life that dwells in Death! Both befriend thee. I am dead, and would see thee dead, for I live and love thee. Wilt thou not be restored and BE?” 

She stood silent. 

“Father,” said Mara, “take her in thine arms, and carry her to her couch. There she will open her hand, and die into life.” 

“I will walk,” said the princess. 

Adam led the way. The princess walked feebly after him. Eve reached up her arms, took Lona from my bosom, and carried her in. I dismounted, and the children also. The horse and the elephants stood shivering; Mara stroked them, and they lay down to sleep. 

Inside the cottage, Eve offered the Little Ones the dense bread and the rich wine. Adam and Lilith stood silent. Eve came from the chamber of death and offered the bread and wine to the princess, holding out the sustenance of centuries, a meal crafted to heal the deepest starvation of the soul. 

“Thy beauty slays me! It is death I would have, not food!” said Lilith, turning her face away, her palate too corrupted by power to bear the taste of genuine communion. 

“This food will help thee to die,” answered Eve. But Lilith would not taste of it. 

“If thou wilt nor eat nor drink, Lilith,” said Adam, “come and see the place where thou shalt lie in peace.” 

He led the way through the door of death. When her foot crossed the threshold, a wild blast fell roaring on the roof, dying away in a moan. She stood ghastly with terror. 

“It is he!” said her voiceless lips. 

“Who, princess?” I whispered. 

“The great Shadow,” she murmured. 

“Here he cannot enter,” said Adam, his voice carrying the acoustic authority of the stone walls. “Here he can hurt no one. Over him also is power given me.” 

“Are the children in the house?” asked Lilith. 

“He never dared touch a child,” Eve said. “And now Death shall be the atonemaker; you shall sleep together.” 

As soon as Adam went back to fetch the children, the princess knelt to Eve. “Beautiful Eve, persuade your husband to kill me! Indeed I would but cannot open my hand.” 

“You cannot die without opening it,” answered Eve. “To kill you would not serve you. No one can kill you but the Shadow.” 

“Show me then to my grave; I am so weary I can live no longer. I must go to the Shadow—yet I would not!” 

She struggled to rise, but fell. The Mother lifted and carried her inward. We followed into the vast chamber of death. It was not a crypt, but a magnificent root-cellar of the soul, a curing chamber where the dead were laid down to ferment and age into their true forms. The air was cool, still, and fragrant with dried lavender, curing salt, and sweet earth. It was a sanctuary of perfect acoustic ecology; the profoundest sleep filled the wide place, a restorative hush that healed the auditory wounds of the surface world. 

The dim light in Adam’s hand glimmered on the sleeping faces. The darkness, the cold, the silence, and the still air made the hearts of the children beat softly, and their little tongues spoke in low, hushed voices, instinctively respecting the sanctity of the resting space. 

“What a curious place to sleep in!” said one. “I would rather be in my nest!” 

“It is SO cold!” said another. 

“Yes, it is cold,” answered our host; “but you will not be cold in your sleep.” 

“Where are our nests?” asked more than one, looking round the vast culinary archive of humanity. 

“Find places, and sleep where you choose,” replied Adam. 

Instantly they scattered, their gentle voices blending into the domestic rhythm of the chamber. One little fellow crept under the sheet beside a beautiful lady. Another lay down beside a woman, pressing close to warm her. Luva stood on tiptoe by a couch. 

“My own mother wouldn’t have me,” she said softly. Eve lifted her, and she got under the snowy covering. The orphans adopted mothers, seamlessly integrating into the agrarian cycles of rest. 

On the middle of three unoccupied couches lay Lona. Eve set Lilith down beside it. Adam pointed to the vacant couch on Lona’s right. “There, Lilith, is the bed I have prepared for you!” 

She glanced at her daughter, who lay like a statue carved in semi-transparent alabaster, preserved in perfect stillness, and shuddered. “How cold it is!” 

“You will soon begin to find comfort in the cold,” answered Adam. 

“I fear that child,” she said. “She will rise and terrify me!” 

“She is dreaming love to you.” 

“But the Shadow! He will be wroth with me!” 

“He at sight of whom the horses of heaven start and rear, dares not disturb one dream in this quiet chamber!” 

The princess lay down, drew the sheet over her, and lay still with open eyes. Mara drew near. 

“Lilith,” said Mara, “you will not sleep until you have opened your hand, and yielded that which is not yours to give or to withhold.” 

“I cannot,” she answered. 

“The shadows of death will gather, but they cannot infold you while yet your hand remains unopened. Open your hand, and make an end.” 

A tinge of colour arose in the parchment-like face; the contorted hand trembled with agonised effort. It was clenched tightly around the ghost of a dominant lexicon, a physical manifestation of oppressive power she could not translate into surrender. Mara sought to aid her. 

“Hold, Mara!” cried her father. “There is danger!” 

The princess turned her eyes upon Eve. “There was a sword I once saw in your husband’s hands. I heard him say it would divide whatever was not one and indivisible!” 

“I have the sword,” said Adam. 

“Bring it, Adam,” pleaded Lilith, “and cut me off this hand that I may sleep.” 

He gave the candle to Eve, and went. When he returned, he held an ancient weapon. The scabbard looked like vellum grown dark with years, like a centuries-old ledger of lost languages, but the hilt shone like untarnished gold. He drew out the blade. It flashed like a clean butcher’s knife, precise and unyielding, and its light made the princess open her eyes. She held out her hand. 

Adam took it. The sword gleamed once, performing its swift culinary severance—there was one little gush of blood, rich as the wine she had refused, and he laid the severed hand in Mara’s lap. Lilith had given one moan, and was already fast asleep, her features relaxing into their native dialect of peace. 

Mara covered the arm with the sheet. 

“Will you not dress the wound?” I said. 

“A wound from that sword,” answered Adam, “needs no dressing. It is healing and not hurt. It prunes the dead branch to save the root.” 

“Where the dead deformity clung,” replied Mara, “the true, lovely hand is already growing.” 

We heard a childish voice behind us. The candle shone on the sleeping face of Lilith, and three Little Ones grouped on the other side of her couch. 

“How beautiful she is grown!” said one. 

“Poor princess!” said another; “I will sleep with her. She will not bite any more!” He climbed into her bed and was immediately fast asleep. 

“I will go on her other side,” said the third. “She shall have two to kiss her when she wakes!” 

“And I am left alone!” said the first mournfully. 

“I will put you to bed,” said Eve, leading the child away. 

We turned to go back to the cottage. Mara by my side carried the severed hand of Lilith in the lap of her robe, bearing away the bitter ingredient of her pride. 

“Ah, you have found her!” we heard Eve say as we stepped into the cottage. The door stood open; two elephant-trunks came through it out of the night. “I sent them with the lantern to look for Mara’s leopardess: they have brought her.” 

I followed Adam, and between us we took the white creature from the elephants. We carried her to the perfectly tuned chamber we had just left, the women preceding us. There we laid the beauty across the feet of the princess, her fore-paws outstretched, and her head couching between them in the resonant, restorative silence of the earth.
