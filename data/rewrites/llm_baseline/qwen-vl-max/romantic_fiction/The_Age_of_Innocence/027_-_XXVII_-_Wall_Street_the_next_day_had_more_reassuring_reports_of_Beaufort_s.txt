XXVII. The next day, Wall Street offered more comforting news about Beaufort’s situation. Though the reports were still uncertain, they carried a glimmer of hope. It was widely believed that he had access to powerful allies in times of crisis—and that he’d already called upon them with some success. That evening, when Mrs. Beaufort appeared at the Opera wearing her usual radiant smile and a dazzling new emerald necklace, society collectively exhaled in relief. New York had always been unforgiving when it came to moral or financial transgressions. There had never been a single exception to its unspoken rule: those who betrayed trust must face the consequences. Even Beaufort and his wife were expected to be sacrificed without hesitation to this principle. But the thought of sacrificing them stirred not just sorrow, but discomfort. Their absence would leave a noticeable gap in their close-knit circle. For some, indifferent to the deeper implications, the real tragedy was the loss of what many considered the finest ballroom in the city.

Archer had made up his mind to go to Washington. He only waited for the start of the patent lawsuit he’d mentioned to May, so that his trip could coincide with the court date. But on Tuesday, Mr. Letterblair informed him the case might be delayed for weeks. Still, Archer resolved to leave the very next evening. He didn’t expect May—so detached from his professional life, so untouched by its intricacies—to notice the delay, nor to remember the names involved if they came up. And besides, he could no longer postpone seeing Madame Olenska. There were things he needed to say—things that couldn’t wait.

On Wednesday morning, as Archer arrived at his office, Mr. Letterblair met him with a grim expression. Beaufort, it seemed, hadn’t managed to weather the storm after all. Yet by spreading word that he had, he had calmed his depositors. A flood of withdrawals had poured into the bank until the previous evening, when troubling rumors resurfaced. A run had begun, and the bank was likely to shut its doors before the day ended. The whispers around town were cruel—calling Beaufort’s actions cowardly, deceitful. His downfall was shaping up to be one of the most disgraceful in Wall Street’s history. The weight of it left Mr. Letterblair pale and shaken.

“I’ve seen hard times,” he said, “but nothing like this. Everyone we know will feel the ripple. And what about Mrs. Beaufort? What can possibly be done for her?” He paused, then added, “I feel for Mrs. Manson Mingott as much as anyone. At her age, who knows how this might affect her? She believed in Beaufort—she made him a friend! And there’s the Dallas connection: poor Mrs. Beaufort is kin to all of you. Her only hope would be to leave him—but how can anyone tell her that? Her duty is beside him. Luckily, she’s always been blind to his flaws.”

A knock interrupted them. Mr. Letterblair turned sharply. “What is it? I can’t be disturbed.”
A clerk entered, handed Archer a letter, and withdrew.
Recognizing May’s handwriting, Archer opened it at once:

*Won’t you please come up town as early as you can? Granny had a slight stroke last night. Somehow, she learned about the bank before anyone else. Uncle Lovell is away hunting, and the idea of the disgrace has made Papa so nervous he’s running a fever and can’t leave his room. Mamma needs you terribly. I hope you can get here right away and go straight to Granny’s.*

Archer passed the note to Mr. Letterblair and soon found himself squeezing into a crowded horse-car, switching later to one of the swaying omnibuses along Fifth Avenue. It was past noon when he finally reached old Catherine’s house.

The sitting-room window, where she usually sat like a queen, was now occupied by her daughter, Mrs. Welland, who gave a weary greeting as she saw Archer. At the door, May greeted him with a pale but steady smile. The hall looked unnaturally chaotic—coats and furs piled on chairs, a doctor’s bag and coat on the table, letters and cards piling up unread. Dr. Bencomb had just arrived for the second time, and though the outlook was still uncertain, he offered a hopeful note. Mrs. Mingott’s fierce will to live was already lifting the spirits of those around her.

May led Archer into the sitting-room, where the sliding doors to the bedroom were closed, draped with heavy yellow damask curtains. Mrs. Welland whispered the details of the night’s events. The previous evening, just after dinner, when Mrs. Mingott had finished her game of solitaire, the bell rang. A woman, heavily veiled, asked to see her. The butler, recognizing her voice, opened the door and announced: “Mrs. Julius Beaufort”—then closed it again behind them. They stayed together for nearly an hour. When Mrs. Mingott rang for help, Mrs. Beaufort had already slipped away unnoticed. The old lady, pale and immense, sat alone in her chair, silently signaling the butler to assist her to bed. At first, she seemed fully aware and composed.

But at three in the morning, the bell rang again. The servants rushed in to find her propped against her pillows, a strange crooked smile on her lips, one hand limp at her side. The stroke had been minor—she could speak, express herself, and gradually regain control of her face. But the shock had been terrible.

And worse still—the reason for her distress. From fragments of speech, it became clear that Regina Beaufort had come to beg her—unimaginably bold—to support her husband, to help them through, to prevent the family from “deserting” them. In fact, to make the entire family condone their shame.

“I told her,” Mrs. Mingott had gasped, her voice thick with paralysis, “‘Honour’s always been honour, and honesty honesty, in the Manson Mingott house—and will be till I’m carried out feet first.’ And when she said, ‘But my name, Auntie—my name’s Regina Dallas,’ I replied, ‘It was Beaufort when he covered you with jewels, and it’s got to stay Beaufort now that he’s covered you with shame.’”

Mrs. Welland spoke these words in horrified tones, trembling at having to confront such a scandal. “If only I could keep it from your father-in-law—he always says, ‘Augusta, for pity’s sake, don’t destroy my last illusions.’ How can I shield him from this?”

“After all,” May gently suggested, “he won’t have _seen_ it.”

Mrs. Welland sighed. “Ah, yes—thank heaven he’s safe in bed. And Dr. Bencomb promises to keep him there until Mama improves—and until Regina is sent away.”

Archer sat near the window, staring blankly at the quiet street outside. He understood now why he’d been summoned—not for practical help, but for presence, for strength. Messages had been sent to other family members; Mr. Lovell Mingott had been telegraphed. But for now, there was little to do except talk in hushed voices about the fallout.

Mrs. Lovell Mingott returned from another room, joining the conversation. In their day, the elder women agreed, a man’s disgrace meant his wife should vanish—disappear with him. “There was the case of poor Grandmamma Spicer,” Mrs. Welland recalled. “Your great-grandmother, May. Of course, your great-grandfather’s troubles were private—cards, or signing a note for someone—I never knew exactly, because Mamma wouldn’t talk about it. But her mother had to leave New York after whatever it was. They lived alone up the Hudson, winter and summer, until Mamma was sixteen. Never once did Grandmamma Spicer think of asking the family to ‘countenance’ her, as Regina calls it. Though a private failure is nothing compared to ruining hundreds of innocent people.”

“Yes,” Mrs. Lovell Mingott nodded, “it would be far more fitting for Regina to hide her own face than to demand others cover theirs. I heard the emerald necklace she wore at the Opera Friday was sent on approval from Ball and Black’s that afternoon. I wonder if they’ll ever get it back?”

Archer listened quietly. The idea of absolute integrity was deeply rooted in him—no sentiment could soften it. Men like Lemuel Struthers could amass wealth through dubious means, but in old New York, honor was the duty of the privileged. And Mrs. Beaufort’s fate stirred little sympathy in him. He felt sorry for her, perhaps more than her relatives did—but he believed that marriage, even if fragile in good times, should be unbreakable in ruin. As Mr. Letterblair had said, a wife’s place was beside her husband in trouble. But society’s place was not beside him—and Mrs. Beaufort’s assumption that it was made her seem almost complicit. To ask family to protect a husband’s financial disgrace was unthinkable—it was something the Family, as a whole, could never do.

The mulatto maid called Mrs. Lovell Mingott into the hall. She returned with a troubled look. “She wants me to telegraph Ellen Olenska. I’d already written to Ellen and Medora, but now it seems that’s not enough. I’m to send a telegram immediately—and tell her to come alone.”

Silence fell.

Mrs. Welland sighed. May rose and gathered scattered papers from the floor.
“It must be done,” Mrs. Lovell Mingott said, as if hoping someone would object.

May turned back. “Of course it must be done. Granny knows what she wants, and we must obey her wishes. Shall I write the telegram for you, Auntie? If it goes right away, Ellen can catch tomorrow’s train.”

She pronounced the name with unusual clarity—like two silver bells chiming.

“Well, it can’t go right away,” Mrs. Lovell Mingott said. “Jasper and the pantry-boy are both out with messages.”

May turned to Archer with a gentle smile. “But here’s Newland, ready to help. Will you take the telegram, Newland? There’s just time before lunch.”

Archer stood, murmuring agreement. May sat at her grandmother’s rosewood writing desk and wrote the message in her large, careful script. When finished, she blotted it neatly and handed it to him.

“What a pity,” she said softly, “that you and Ellen will cross paths on the way—Newland,” she added, turning to her mother and aunt, “you’re going to Washington for a patent case before the Supreme Court. I suppose Uncle Lovell will be back by tomorrow night, and since Granny is improving, it doesn’t seem fair to ask you to give up such an important engagement—does it?”

She paused, waiting for confirmation. Mrs. Welland quickly responded: “Oh, of course not, darling. Your Granny would never want that.”

As Archer left the room with the telegram, he heard his mother-in-law whisper, “But why on earth should she want you to telegraph for Ellen Olenska—”

And May’s clear voice replied: “Perhaps it’s to remind her—once again—that her duty lies with her husband.”

The outer door clicked shut behind him. Archer walked quickly toward the telegraph office, the weight of the moment pressing down—of choices, of loyalties, of love and obligation, all converging in the silence of a city caught between scandal and silence.
