The Sergeant stood silent, his brow furrowed in deep contemplation, as we navigated through the grove of firs leading toward the dangerous quicksand. Once we had crossed the threshold of the plantation, however, his demeanor shifted abruptly. He straightened his spine, as if a final decision had crystallized within him, and addressed me once more.

"Mr. Betteredge," he declared, "since you have graciously agreed to row in my boat, and considering you may still prove useful before the sun sets, I see no point in maintaining this veil of mystery between us. I propose to model my approach on absolute candour. You are resolute in withholding any information that implicates Rosanna Spearman. Your motives are clear: she has treated you with kindness, and you harbour genuine pity for her plight. These humanitarian sentiments reflect well upon your character, yet in this specific instance, they serve you poorly. Rosanna Spearman faces no peril whatsoever—not so long as I pin her to the crime of the Diamond's disappearance with evidence as undeniable as the nose upon your face!"

"Do you mean that Lady Verinder will not press charges?" I ventured.

"I mean that your lady *cannot* prosecute," corrected the Sergeant. "Rosanna is merely an instrument manipulated by another individual. Consequently, Rosanna will be held harmless for the sake of that mastermind."

He spoke with an intensity that brooked no contradiction. Yet, a discordant note vibrated within me, a sense that something essential was being withheld. "Will you not reveal the identity of this other person?" I pressed.

"And you, Mr. Betteredge?" countered Cuff.

"No."

The Sergeant halted, surveying me with a gaze of melancholic curiosity. "It is always a delight to me to extend compassion toward human frailty," he mused. "I feel acutely tender toward you, Mr. Betteredge, at this precise moment. And you, driven by the same noble impulse, feel a profound tenderness for Rosanna Spearman, do you not? Have you recently observed whether she has acquired a fresh supply of linens?"

His abrupt insertion of this peculiar inquiry left me bewildered. However, since confessing the truth posed no threat to Rosanna, I admitted that the girl had arrived at the estate somewhat scantily equipped, noting that Lady Verinder, in reward for her exemplary conduct—which I emphasized heavily—had presented her with a new wardrobe of linens just two weeks prior.

"A wretched world," sighed the Sergeant. "Life, Mr. Betteredge, resembles a target; misfortune is perpetually aiming and striking the bullseye. Had it not been for that outfit, we would undoubtedly have uncovered a discarded nightgown or petticoat among Rosanna's effects, securing her conviction instantly. Do you not follow my thread? You, having inspected the household staff personally, know precisely what two servants discovered outside Rosanna's chamber. Are you unaware of her activities yesterday afternoon, once she fell ill? You cannot guess? Alas, it is as transparent as that sliver of moonlight piercing through the tree line at the beach's end."

He continued rapidly, weaving a narrative that demanded immediate comprehension. "At eleven o'clock on Thursday, Superintendent Seegrave—a veritable mountain of human weakness—directed all female servants to inspect the smudge on Rosanna's door. Suspecting foul play regarding her own belongings, Rosanna seized her first available opportunity to retreat to her room. There, she located the paint stain on her nightgown, petticoat, or similar garment. Feigning illness, she slipped away to town, procured materials for a replacement garment, and constructed it secretly within her chambers during the night. She kindled a fire—not to incinerate the evidence, for two colleagues were spying through keyholes, aware that smoke would betray her—and instead used the heat to dry and iron the substituted dress after wringing it out. The stained original remains concealed, likely worn beneath her clothes, while she currently occupies herself disposing of it somewhere along that desolate stretch of beach ahead. I have tracked her this evening to your fishing hamlet and to a specific cottage, a location we may need to visit before returning home. She lingered there for a duration and emerged carrying something wrapped beneath her cloak. A cloak, draped over a woman's shoulders, symbolizes charity; it conceals a multitude of sins. Observing her departure, I noted she headed northward along the coastline. Does the seaside here, Mr. Betteredge, constitute a magnificent example of maritime scenery?"

I replied, "Indeed," with brevity.

"Tastes vary greatly," observed Sergeant Cuff. "From my perspective, however, I have rarely encountered a coastal vista I admire less. Should you be pursuing someone along this shore, and if that individual happens to glance around, there exists not a single patch of cover wherein they might conceal themselves. I faced a dilemma: arresting Rosanna on suspicion versus allowing her to continue her gambit unobserved. For reasons I shall not burden you with, I chose to make any sacrifice rather than alert a certain individual—who shall remain unnamed between us—to the situation this evening. I returned to the residence to request that you escort me to the northern extremity of the beach via an alternate route. Sand, regarding its capacity to preserve footprints, ranks as one of the finest detectives I know. If we fail to intercept Rosanna Spearman by approaching her from this angle, the sand may yet reveal her secrets, provided the daylight persists. Behold, the sand. Would you excuse my suggestion? Perhaps you should hold your tongue and permit me to lead the way?"

A malady akin to a "detective fever"—if such an ailment existed at the apothecary's—had now firmly seized me. The Sergeant advanced between the dunes of sand, descending toward the beach. I trailed behind, my heart hammering against my ribs, pausing at a respectful distance to await the unfolding drama.

As fate would have it, I found myself standing nearly where Rosanna and I had conversed previously, the moment Mr. Franklin first materialized before us upon his arrival from London. While my eyes fixed upon the Sergeant, my mind drifted involuntarily back to those past interactions between Rosanna and myself. I felt a phantom sensation of her slender hand slipping back into mine, offering a grateful, gentle squeeze of thanks for my kind words. I fancied I heard her voice again, whispering of the Shivering Sand drawing her irresistibly toward it whenever she ventured outdoors—I imagined her countenance brightening, just as it had when she first beheld Mr. Franklin emerging briskly from the dunes. My spirits plummeted with these reveries, and the bleak panorama of the solitary bay, which I scanned to regain composure, only deepened my unease.

The twilight was ebbing away, casting a still and ominous pall over the desolate locale. The swelling of the vast ocean upon the massive sandbar offshore was a motion devoid of sound. The inner sea lay shrouded in gloom and obscurity, undisturbed by a breath of wind. Patches of repulsive ooze drifted, pale yellow-white, across the lifeless surface of the water. Scum and slime glimmered faintly in certain spots, illuminated by the dying light reflecting off two great rocky spits jutting north and south into the sea. Now stood the turn of the tide; even as I awaited, the broad, brown visage of the quicksand began to ripple and tremble—the sole animate element in that horrific setting.

I perceived the Sergeant start as the shudder of the sand caught his eye. After gazing at it for a minute, he turned and returned to me.

"A treacherous spot, Mr. Betteredge," he remarked. "And no sign of Rosanna Spearman anywhere upon the beach, search where you may."

He guided me lower onto the shore, revealing that his footsteps and mine were the only imprints registered in the sand.

"How fares the fishing village, standing where we now reside?" asked Sergeant Cuff.

"Cobb's Hole," I responded—that being the name of the place—"lies approximately due south."

"I spotted the girl this evening, strolling northward along the shore from Cobb's Hole," the Sergeant noted. "Therefore, she must have been heading toward this location. Is Cobb's Hole situated on the other side of that promontory there? And can we reach it now, at low water, via the beach?"

I affirmed both queries.

"If you'll pardon my suggestion, we shall proceed briskly," urged the Sergeant. "I wish to locate the point where she abandoned the shore before darkness fully claims it."

We traversed perhaps two hundred yards toward Cobb's Hole when Sergeant Cuff suddenly knelt upon the beach, appearing seized by a sudden fervor for prayer.

"There is merit in your marine landscape here, after all," observed the Sergeant. "Behold, Mr. Betteredge, a pair of feminine footsteps!
