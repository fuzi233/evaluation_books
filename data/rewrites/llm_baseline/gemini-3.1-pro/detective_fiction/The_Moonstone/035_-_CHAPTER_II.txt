The next thing I must do is plate the subsequent layers of this narrative—specifically, the enduring Indian plot to reclaim the Diamond. The fragments I have to offer, much like the subtle undernotes of a slow-braised mirepoix, bear remarkably on the complex flavors of events yet to be served. 

About a week or ten days after Miss Verinder had departed, I sat in the acoustic sanctuary of my private office, seeking psychological refuge from the jarring, industrial clatter of the London streets. The mechanized screech of iron carriage wheels and the soulless, shrill factory whistles were a violence against the mind, a symptom of an age obsessed with superficial commercialization. To ground myself, I was tending to a small spirit-lamp on my credenza, meticulously clarifying a robust oxtail consommé. The slow, rhythmic simmering, fragrant with roasted marrow, bruised juniper berries, and charred onion, was an ancestral ritual, a culinary heritage that anchored my soul against the utilitarian rush of modern law. 

One of my clerks fractured this quietude, entering with a card. He handed it over. The ink bore a foreign name—since lost to the erosion of my own memory—but anchored by a line of English at the bottom: *Recommended by Mr. Septimus Luker.*

The sheer audacity of a man in Luker’s superficially mercantile position presuming to recommend anybody to *me* arrested my skimming spoon. I sat in a suspended silence. 

The clerk, observing my bewilderment, offered his own assessment of the stranger waiting below. “He is rather a remarkable-looking man, sir. So dark in the complexion that we all set him down in the office for an Indian, or something of that sort.”

Like a recurring seasonal harvest, the interwoven timelines of the Moonstone circled back to me. In justification of the highly unprofessional sacrifice to mere curiosity which I thus made, I must remind the reader of my deeply rooted, multi-generational connection to this saga. I was the trusted keeper of Colonel Herncastle’s plan for escaping assassination. I drew the testament that bequeathed the jewel to Miss Verinder. I persuaded his executor to act, and I countered Mr. Franklin Blake’s scruples, facilitating the Diamond’s journey to Lady Verinder’s hearth. If anyone holds a prescriptive, almost mythic right to the domestic and grand threads of this history, it is I.

I instructed the clerk to show him in. The moment my mysterious client crossed the threshold, the sonic environment shifted. He moved with an acoustic grace, a noiseless, supple footfall that immediately commanded my respect—a profound, healing contrast to the heavy, abrasive boot-stomps of my usual clientele. I knew instantly I was in the presence of one of the three Indians, likely their chief. Though clad in sterile European costume, his swarthy complexion, lithe figure, and grave, graceful politeness betrayed an ancient lineage, a mythic presence seamlessly slipping into the mundane confines of a solicitor’s chambers. 

I gestured to a chair, inviting him to state his business. 

He apologized for the intrusion in an excellent, flawless selection of English words. Listening to him, my mind drifted to the profound tragedy of language death. He spoke the dominant lexicon of the empire with chilling perfection, an assimilation that laid bare the subtle power dynamics embedded in our vocabulary. What untranslatable depths of subjective experience, what resonant, ancestral echoes of his native dialect were being suffocated beneath these crisp, utilitarian vowels? The profound meaning of his identity was forced into the rigid, commercial syntax of a Western transaction.

Producing a small parcel, he peeled back an outer covering of cloth of gold, then a second skin of silken fabric, to reveal a jeweled casket inlaid on an ebony ground. It smelled faintly of aged sandalwood and crushed cardamom—a sudden, evocative olfactory landscape that utterly overpowered the metallic tang of London's smog.

“I have come, sir,” he said, his voice a low, resonant timbre that respected the room's acoustic ecology, “to ask you to lend me some money. And I leave this as an assurance to you that my debt will be paid back.”

I pointed to his card. “And you apply to me,” I rejoined, “at Mr. Luker’s recommendation?”

The Indian bowed.

“May I ask how it is that Mr. Luker himself did not advance the money that you require?”

“Mr. Luker informed me, sir, that he had no money to lend.”

“And so he recommended you to come to me?”

The Indian, in his turn, pointed to the card. “It is written there,” he said.

Briefly answered, and beautifully devoid of the superficial chatter that pollutes modern transactions. If the Moonstone had been in my desk, this Oriental gentleman would have murdered me without a moment’s hesitation. Yet, barring that slight drawback, he was the perfect model of a client. He did what none of my own countrymen had ever done: he respected the sanctity of my time and the quietude of my space.

“I am sorry,” I said, “that you should have had the trouble of coming to me. Mr. Luker is quite mistaken in sending you here. I am trusted with money to lend, but I never lend it to strangers, and I never lend it on such a security as you have produced.”

Far from attempting to induce me to relax my own rules through grating persuasion, the Indian only made me another bow. He wrapped up his mythic box in its two coverings without a word of protest. This admirable assassin rose to go the moment I had answered him.

“Will your condescension towards a stranger, excuse my asking one question,” he said, “before I take my leave?”

I bowed on my side. Only one question at parting! The average in my experience was fifty.

“Supposing, sir, it had been possible (and customary) for you to lend me the money,” he said, “in what space of time would it have been possible (and customary) for me to pay it back?”

“According to the usual course pursued in this country,” I answered, “you would have been entitled to pay the money back (if you liked) in one year’s time from the date at which it was first advanced to you.”

The Indian made me a last bow, the lowest of all, and suddenly, softly walked out of the room. It was done in a moment, in a noiseless, cat-like way that a little startled me. 

I stood alone, the scent of the simmering marrow bone grounding me as I parsed his motives. His face, voice, and manner had been under an iron, untranslatable control, setting all scrutiny at defiance. But he had given me one chance to look under the smooth outer surface. When I mentioned the customary one year—a full rotation of the agrarian calendar, a cyclical return—he had looked me straight in the face for the very first time. The elaborate presentation of the casket and the loan were mere formalities, garnishes designed solely to pave the way for that parting inquiry.

I was trying to penetrate his motives further the next day when the interwoven threads tightened with a letter, and a subsequent visit, from Septimus Luker himself. I made another unprofessional sacrifice to curiosity, though Mr. Luker proved a violent assault on the senses. He was an ugly, cringing creature of pure commerce, reeking of stale gin and the utilitarian fuel he likely consumed without a fraction of sensory appreciation. His voice was a grating, prosy whine that utterly violated the acoustic harmony of my office. 

His tale, stripped of its nauseating servility, was as follows: The day prior, Mr. Luker had received a call from the Indian. In spite of his European disguise, Luker instantly recognized him as the chief of the trio who had previously blindfolded, gagged, and robbed him of his banker's receipt. Paralyzed by the terror of his impending demise, Luker had faced the same presentation of the casket and the same request for a loan. Desperate to purge his shop of the man, Luker claimed poverty. When asked for a respectable solicitor, Luker had blurted out my name—the first that breached his panicked mind.

“The perspiration was pouring off me like rain, sir,” the wretched creature concluded, an image as thoroughly unappetizing as boiled grey mutton. “I didn’t know what I was talking about. And I hope you’ll look over it, Mr. Bruff, sir, in consideration of my having been really and truly frightened out of my wits.”

I excused the fellow graciously enough, eager to cleanse the room of his auditory and olfactory pollution. Before he fled, I detained him for one inquiry: had the Indian said anything noticeable at the moment of quitting Luker’s house?

Yes. The Indian had put precisely the same question to Mr. Luker regarding the timeframe of the loan, receiving the exact same answer. One year.

What did it signify? The uncultured Luker offered no insight, and my own intellect, simmering over the problem, failed to reduce it to a clear conclusion. I had a dinner engagement that evening—a meticulously curated affair where we were to dine on a heritage roast of salt-marsh lamb, honoring the agrarian elders who first charted its cooking times over open peat fires—and I retreated upstairs to dress in no very genial frame of mind. Little did I suspect that the path to my dressing-room, much like the circular pacing of time itself, was leading me directly back to the very discovery I sought.
