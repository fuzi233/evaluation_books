Amyas slept as a man sleeps when the body is spent and the mind refuses the bargain. His limbs lay heavy, as if the long road and the sea-air had salted them into stiffness; but behind his shut eyes the day kept moving—faces, voices, a hand lifted in warning—till his mother and Frank, bending now and then over the pillow, saw by the quick twitching of his brows that he was chased by thoughts he could not master.

It was the name of John Oxenham that had taken hold of him, like a burr in wool. All the evening, in the bay-windowed room where that man had once sat living, Amyas had found himself calling back every turn of the lost adventurer’s head, each half-smile and sudden stillness, as though memory were a dish he could not leave until it was scraped clean—though scraping only brought up more taste of it, more craving. He wondered at himself, and the wondering made it worse; and when he lay down, the fancy did not die, but rose up hotter in his sleep.

He was, he knew not how, sailing westward ever, up the wake of the sinking sun, chasing a tiny sail that was Oxenham’s. A painful certainty pressed on him—that unless he overtook her in time, some horror would be done and could not be undone. Yet his ship would not sail. The bows were clogged with sargasso weed, those long, slimy coils that looked like a sea-serpent’s hair, dragging and clinging, dead weight and living menace together. He strained, he willed, he shouted without sound, and still the vessel only wallowed as the sun went down and the sea turned to ink.

Then the moon rose, and with it came Oxenham’s ship close aboard—too close, as if it had been behind him all along. Her sails were torn and snapping like old linen in a gale; pitch streamed down her sides in black tears; the bulwarks looked soft with rot. And along the mainyard there hung a line of dark shapes, swaying gently. A line of hanged men.

He tried to cry out, and could not. From the yard-arm, above him, John Oxenham’s corpse looked down with grave-bright eyes and beckoned—beckoned and pointed, striving to speak, and failing, his lips moving like a fish’s in air. And always the finger pointed, not onward into that glittering west, but backward—back along their course.

Amyas turned his gaze the way the dead man commanded; and behold, behind him the Andes shone in the moon, sharp as knife-edges under snow. He knew then, with the helpless knowledge that comes in dreams like a blow, that he was in the South Seas again, and all America lay between him and home. Still the corpse pointed back and back, yearning in its agony to tell some secret that could not be said; till Amyas sprang up with a shout that tore him awake.

He lay in his little coved chamber at Burrough, the gray of autumn morning stealing in as if through wet wool. His heart hammered like a smith at iron. He tried to sleep again and could not; after an hour’s tossing he rose, dressed, and went down for a bathe on the beloved pebble ridge, as a man goes to cold water to drive out fever.

Passing his mother’s door, he could not help but look in. In the dim light he saw the bed; the pillow had not been pressed. His mother, in her long white night-gear, knelt at the far end by her prie-dieu, so intent that even the dawn seemed a thing she would not acknowledge till her soul had finished its work.

He slipped in without a word and knelt beside her. She turned, smiled, and put her arm around him—quiet, steady, as if he were a child again brought in from wind and fright—and then went on with her prayers. Why not? He knew they were for him, and so he prayed too; for her, and for poor lost John Oxenham and all the vanished men who had followed him as one follows a bell into fog.

At last she rose and stood above him, parted the yellow hair from his brow, and looked long into his face. There was no need for speech; between these two there was nothing to hide, and nothing that could not be known without words. When at last the gaze broke, she stooped and kissed him on the brow. As she turned away, a tear fell and splashed warm on his skin.

Her bare feet showed beneath the hem. He bent and kissed them again and again; then looked up, half laughing at himself, half pleading—

“You have such pretty feet, mother!”

In a woman’s instant instinct she hid them. She had been a beauty once; her hair was gray now and the roses long faded, but there was a beauty left that belonged to bone and bearing and the clear courage of her eyes.

“Your dear father used to say so thirty years ago.”

“And I say so still. You always were beautiful; you are beautiful now.”

“What is that to you, silly boy? Will you play the lover with an old mother? Go take your walk, and think of younger ladies, if you can find any worthy of you.”

So the son went out, and the mother returned to her prayers, as to bread-making—quiet, daily, necessary, and done for love.

He walked down to the pebble ridge where the bay’s surges, after centuries of beating, had built a rampart of gray boulders—two miles long, cunningly curved and smoothed and fitted, as if some giant mason had laid each stone by hand. Inside it lay a sheet of rich turf, alluvial and level, protected from the spring and autumn tides. Amyas drew in the salt air with the greedy delight of a young sea-dog; it seemed to scrape his lungs clean. He stripped, plunged into the breakers, and dived and rolled, letting the cold bite and the foam buffet him till his blood ran bright and his dream began to lose its claws.

Then he heard himself hailed from the shore. Looking up, he saw on the top of the stony rampart the tall figure of his cousin Eustace.

Amyas was half-disappointed; for, love-lorn rascal, he had been walking down dreaming of Rose Salterne, and had no wish for a companion to interrupt such sweet folly on the way back. Still, it was three years since he had seen Eustace. He scrambled out and dressed, while his cousin paced the turf inside the ridge.

Eustace Leigh was the son of a younger brother of Leigh of Burrough, one who had more or less cut himself off from his kin and country by remaining a Papist. Born so, he had turned Protestant under Edward, Papist again under Mary, and—honor to him—had stopped there, too honest to turn once more with the wind when Elizabeth came in, as so many did. He lived withdrawn in a great rambling dark house on the Atlantic cliffs in Moorwinstow parish, still called Chapel, not far from Sir Richard Grenville’s Stow.

The penal laws troubled him little. They seldom troubled any man who did not make conspiracy part of his creed; and even for those, the council, for England’s peace, kept a tighter hand upon spiritual zealots than the zealots liked. Jesuits and seminary priests came and went at Chapel House as quietly as owls. They found sport in secrecy, like schoolboys in a forbidden pantry: little dens in a lonely turret, trap-doors, a hidden chamber under the roof where mass might be said. When Moorwinstow’s zealous parson, having discovered what all knew already, complained to Sir Richard and demanded the act of the fourteenth of Elizabeth be enforced, Sir Richard rated him like a fantastical Puritan and bade him mind his own business unless he wished the place made too hot for himself. The parson, who found Mr. Thomas Leigh paid tithes well enough, learned to bow his head in the house of Rimmon like Naaman—eating Mr. Leigh’s dinners when invited, and ignoring old Father Francis opposite him, dressed as a layman and calling himself the young gentleman’s pedagogue.

Yet those same priests held a lien on Mr. Thomas Leigh’s conscience—lands once belonging to Hartland Abbey. The rents burned in his purse. He half believed he would be lost for holding them; but not wholly, and therefore he did not give them up. Like many, he compromised one wrong with another: petted foreign priests, listened—or pretended not to listen—to their practisings, and gave up a son as a sin-offering, to be carried overseas to Douay or Rheims or Rome, to be trained as a seminary priest. One such scapegoat was Eustace, sent out to be made—so the harsh phrase went—a liar at Rheims.

A fair liar he had become; not a bad fellow at the core, but shaped by those who had chosen him for what they called a “vocation”—a certain trembling fear of the unseen that can be cultivated like fungus in a cellar, and with it that sly bend toward deceit which too often grows in superstitious men. He was tall and handsome, light of complexion, with a great upright forehead and a very small mouth. His face wore a dry set look, as if holding itself in by force; it was always trying to get free into proper dimples and smiles, because one ought to have Christian love, and if one loved one ought to be cheerful, and cheerful people smiled; therefore he would smile, and tried. But his charity prepense looked as little winning as malice prepense; and had he not been handsome, many a woman who raved about his sweetness might have likened his frankness to a skeleton’s in fetters, and his smiles to the grin thereof.

He had come back to England a month before, under the proclamation that those with children and wards beyond seas should register their names and call them home within four months. He stayed now with his father at Chapel, yet with private matters to transact for the society that had bred him; one such matter had brought him to Bideford the night before.

Now he sat beside Amyas on the pebbles and looked him over from the corners of his eyes very gently, as if afraid to hurt him—or even the flies on his back. Amyas faced him full, laughing, and held out a lion’s paw. Eustace took it rapturously, and a great shaking of hands followed: Amyas gripping with a round fist and a quiet quiver that said, I am glad to see you; Eustace pinching hard with straight white fingers and sawing the air up and down as if to cry, Don’t you see how glad I am?

“Hold hard, old lad,” said Amyas. “Before you break my elbow. And where do you come from?”

“From going to and fro in the earth, and from walking up and down in it,” said Eustace, with a small smile and a nod of mysterious self-importance.

“Like the devil, eh? Well, every man has his pattern. How is my uncle?”

If there was one man Eustace dreaded above another, it was Amyas. Not only because Amyas could have killed him with one blow; but because there are natures that do not rejoice in strength outside themselves—strength makes them irritable, then fearful, then spiteful, as if expecting the strong to do what they know they would do were they in the strong man’s place. And further, Eustace could not help feeling that Amyas despised him. Three years had not changed that memory: Amyas had never argued with him, because Amyas treated his haunted questions of saints and spirits and the unseen as “wind and moonshine,” and his cousin as half-baked. Eustace knew it, knew too that he had been wronged by it; and a secret voice in him whispered, Let us see if he finds me a match now—at which thought he fell at once into inward contrition for self-seeking, as if the very wish to be thought wise were a sin to be scourged.

Yet he spoke smoothly enough.

“Ah, my dearest cousin! How disappointed I was this morning to find I had come a day too late to witness your triumph. But I hastened to your home as soon as I could, and learning from your mother that I should find you here, hurried down to bid you welcome again to Devon.”

“Well, old lad, it does look natural to see you.” Amyas pulled on his jerkin and jerked the wet hair from his eyes. “I often used to think of you walking the deck o’ nights. Uncle and the girls are all right, then? Is the old pony dead yet? And how’s Dick the smith, and Nancy? Grown a fine maid by now, I warrant. ’Slid, it seems half a life since I was away.”

“And you really thought of your poor cousin? Be sure he too thought of you, and offered up nightly his weak prayers for your safety—to those saints to whom would that you—”

“Halt there, coz. If they’re half as good fellows as you and I take them for, they’ll help me without asking.”

“They have helped you, Amyas.”

“Maybe. I’d have done as much for them, I’m sure, if I’d been in their place.”

“And do you not feel, then, that you owe a debt of gratitude to them; and above all to her, whose intercessions have, I doubt not, availed for your preservation? Her—the star of the sea—the all-compassionate guide of the mariner?”

“Humph!” said Amyas. “Here’s Frank. Let him answer.”

As he spoke, up came Frank, and after greetings sat down beside them on the ridge, the tide hissing and sucking below as if it were frying something in a giant pan, and the gulls crying overhead with that wild, clean, knife-edge music that makes a man’s thoughts feel aired out and honest.

“I say, brother,” Amyas began at once, “here’s Eustace trying already to convert me, and telling me I owe all my luck to the Blessed Virgin’s prayers.”

“It may be so,” said Frank. “At least you owe it to the prayers of that most pure and peerless virgin by whose commands you sailed; the sweet incense of whose orisons has gone up for you daily, and for whose sake you were preserved from flood and foe, that you might spread the fame and advance the power of the spotless championess of truth, and right, and freedom—Elizabeth, your queen.”

Amyas answered the rhapsody with a loyal chuckle. Eustace smiled meekly; but his answer had a thin edge.

“I at least am certain I speak truth when I call my patroness a virgin undefiled.”

Both brothers’ brows clouded at once. Amyas, lying back on the pebbles, spoke quietly up toward the gulls as if to them rather than either cousin—

“I wonder what the Frenchman whose head I cut off at the Azores thinks by now about all that.”

“Cut off a Frenchman’s head?” said Frank.

“Yes, faith; and so fleshed my maiden sword. I’ll tell you. ’Twas in some tavern. George Drake and I had gone in, and there sat this Frenchman with his sword on the table, ready for a quarrel—I found after he was a noted bully. He begins loud enough about this and that; but after a while, by instigation of the devil, out he vents a dozen slanders against her majesty’s honor, one atop of another. I was ashamed to hear them, and I should be more ashamed to repeat them.”

“I have heard enough of such,” said Frank. “They come mostly through lewd rascals about the French ambassador, bred—God help them—among the filthy vices of that Medicean court where the Queen of Scots had her schooling, and who can only perceive in virtuous freedom a cloak for licentiousness like their own. Let the curs bark. Honi soit qui mal y pense is our motto, and shall be for ever.”

“But I didn’t let the cur bark. I took him by the ears to show him into the street. Whereon he got to his sword and I to mine; and a near chance I had of never bathing on the pebble ridge more. For the fellow did not fight with edge and buckler like a Christian, but had some newfangled French devil’s device of scryming and foining with his point—ha’ing and stamping and tracing at me—till I thought I should be full of eyelet holes ere I could close.”

“Thank God you are safe,” said Frank. “I know that play well enough. Dangerous enough it is.”

“Of course you know it; but I didn’t—more’s the pity.”

“Well, I’ll teach it thee, lad, as well as Rowland Yorke himself—

‘Thy fincture, carricade, and sly passata,
Thy stramazon, and resolute stoccata,
Wiping maudritta, closing embrocata,
And all the cant of the honorable fencing mystery.’”

“Rowland Yorke? Who’s he?”

“A roystering rascal in London, turning profit by teaching that art; and like enough to have his mortal thread clipped in a tavern brawl, as thy Frenchman. But how did you escape his pinking iron?”

“How? Had it through my left arm before I could look round. And then I got mad, and leapt on him, caught him by the wrist, and had a fair side-blow; and fortune willed it, off tumbled his head upon the table, and there was an end of his slanders.”

“So perish all her enemies!” said Frank.

Eustace, who had been trying not to listen, rose.

“I trust you do not number me among them?”

“As you speak, I do, coz,” said Frank. “But for your own sake, put faith in the true report of those who have daily experience of their mistress’s excellent virtue—as they have of the sun’s shining and the earth’s bringing forth fruit—and not in the tattle of a few cowardly back-stair rogues who would curry favor with the Guises. Come; we will say no more. Walk round with us by Appledore, and then home to breakfast.”

But Eustace declined. He had immediate business, he said, in Northam town, and then in Bideford; and so left them. The brothers lounged a while longer on the beach, letting the sea’s steady roar—old, unbought music, not some hired fiddling for a lord’s supper—wash the sharpness out of their talk; and then walked across the smooth turf toward the little white fishing village two miles above the bar, where Taw and Torridge meet and gossip together in brackish swirls.

Now it came to pass that Eustace had told them he was going to Northam; but he did not tell them that his true point was the same as theirs, namely Appledore. Therefore, after satisfying his conscience by going as far as the nearest house in Northam village, he struck away sharp to the left across the fields, repeating Heaven knows what to the Blessed Virgin as he went. By that crookedness—such as crooked spirits are prone to, Jesuits especially—he went miles out of his road, and only outwitted himself.

For Amyas and Frank, going straight like honest men across the turf, came into Appledore opposite the little Mariner’s Rest just in time to see what Eustace had taken such pains to hide: four of Mr. Thomas Leigh’s horses at the door, held by the groom with saddles and mail-bags on, and on three of them mounted Eustace and two strange gentlemen.

“There’s one lie already this morning,” growled Amyas. “He told us he was going to Northam.”

“And we do not know that he has not been there,” Frank suggested, with that bland fairness which could, when it chose, be sharper than any insult.

“Why, you’re as bad a Jesuit as he, to help him out with such a fetch.”

“He may have changed his mind.”

“Bless your pure imagination, my sweet boy,” said Amyas, laying his great hand on Frank’s head and mimicking their mother’s tone. “Come, dear Frank, let’s step into this shop and buy a pennyworth of whipcord.”

“What do you want with whipcord, man?”

“To spin my top, to be sure.”

“Top? How long hast had a top?”

“I’ll buy one then, and save my conscience. But the upshot of this sport I must see. Why mayn’t I have an excuse ready-made as well as Master Eustace?”

So saying, he pulled Frank into the little shop, unobserved by the party at the inn-door. The place smelled of hemp and tar and smoked fish—cordage, hooks, and sea-gear hanging like dry ribs from beams; a faint sweetness of apples stored somewhere in back; and beneath it all that briny, living odor that clings to any room near a working tide.

“What strange cattle has he been importing now?” Amyas murmured, peering through the window-crack. “Look at that three-legged fellow, trying to get aloft on the wrong side. How he claws at his horse’s ribs, like a cat scratching an elder stem!”

The three-legged man was tall and meek-looking, bedizened in gorgeous garments with a great feather and a sword so long and broad it differed little in size from the thin stiff shanks between which it wandered unhappily.

“Young David in Saul’s weapons,” said Frank. “He had better not go in them, for he certainly has not proved them.”

“Look, if his third leg is not turned into a tail! Why does not someone in charity haul in half-a-yard of his belt for him?”
