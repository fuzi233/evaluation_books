The Curate—Mr. Smirke, a man whose soul was as delicate as a wet handkerchief and whose heart beat like a clockwork mouse—had just finished his daily pilgrimage to Fairoaks, where he was currently pretending to teach Arthur Pendennis Latin while secretly composing sonnets about someone who wasn’t even in the room. It was during this noble act of scholarly pretense that Mrs. Portman, having completed her business with Mrs. Pybus (a woman whose gossip could curdle milk at fifty paces), decided that the weather was so gloriously fine it would be a crime not to visit her dear friend at Fairoaks.

She arrived just in time to drop a bombshell—or rather, a perfectly timed secret—into the middle of tea.

“Now,” said Mrs. Portman, sipping her tea with the air of one who knew exactly what she was doing, “I’ve been meaning to tell you something about Mr. Smirke.”

“Oh?” said Mrs. Pendennis, raising an eyebrow like a finely tuned instrument. “And what is it? Has he finally confessed to stealing my silver?”

“No, no,” Mrs. Portman replied, “much more serious. He’s engaged. To a lady. Miss Thompson of Clapham Common. Been engaged for years, poor dear.”

“Engaged?” cried Mrs. Pendennis, nearly spilling her tea. “But he’s only just arrived! And I thought—well, I thought he was looking elsewhere!”

“Ah,” said Mrs. Portman, leaning forward conspiratorially, “that’s precisely why I’m telling you. Because people have been saying he’s smitten with *you*, Helen.”

“Me?” shrieked Mrs. Pendennis, turning as red as a beetroot. “Why, that’s absurd! He’s my son’s tutor! A gentleman of modest means and even more modest manners! Why, I wouldn’t marry him if he were made of gold and wore a crown!”

“Well,” said Mrs. Portman, “that’s good. Because now we know he’s not marrying you. So all’s well.”

Major Pendennis, who had been reading the newspaper with the intensity of a man trying to decipher ancient runes, put it down slowly. “Hmph,” he said. “This is getting out of hand. That fellow has been here long enough to learn how to behave like a gentleman—but never quite enough to become one.”

“I always thought he was too kind,” said Helen, feeling a sudden pang of guilt. “Too polite. Too helpful. He brought books. He copied music. He once gave Laura a toy horse made entirely of wood and hope. And yet I treated him like a servant. A servant with glasses.”

“Exactly,” said the Major. “You mustn’t be too kind to tutors. They begin to think they’re family. Then they start asking questions. Then they start making eyes at your sister-in-law. Then—”

“Then nothing!” snapped Helen. “He’s going to marry Miss Thompson. That’s settled.”

“Is it?” asked the Major, raising an eyebrow. “Because I’ve seen him look at you like a man who’s lost his way in a forest and found a map written in tears.”

Helen blushed furiously. She did not deny it. But she also didn’t say anything. Instead, when Mr. Smirke came downstairs from the study (looking slightly dazed, as if he’d just read a particularly emotional sermon), she marched up to him with surprising grace and shook his hand.

“Mr. Smirke,” she said, “I’ve been unkind. I’ve been rude. I’ve been… well, I’ve been me. Please come into the drawing-room. We rarely see you there.”

Smirke, whose heart was already fluttering like a trapped bird, nearly dropped his hat.

“And dinner,” continued Helen, “will be served soon. You’re welcome to join us.”

Smirke, who had spent the past two years imagining dinner with her as a dream sequence involving candlelight and angels, accepted with trembling hands.

At dinner, things began to unravel—mostly because of the wine.

Pen, who had recently been granted the sacred keys to the cellar (a privilege usually reserved for those who hadn’t yet discovered the meaning of moderation), had brought out a bottle of claret so old it had probably witnessed the fall of Rome. Or at least a very dramatic argument between two butlers.

As the elders left the table, Pen and Smirke were left alone—with a second bottle, a third, and a growing sense of destiny.

Pen, now flushed and poetic, began reciting verses he had composed earlier that morning—poems about love, betrayal, and the crushing weight of unrequited affection. The verses were melodramatic, heartfelt, and utterly ridiculous.

> “The woman who rejected my passion,” he declaimed, “cannot be worthy to win it.
> I am waking from love’s mad fever—yes, I am—
> And though my heart may never be whole again,
> Though death itself seems sweet compared to life,
> Yet I shall rise—like a phoenix—like a poet—
> Like a man who will one day be famous—*and* respected!”

Smirke, who had been quietly sipping his wine, suddenly leaned forward, eyes wide.

“You are a poet,” he said, voice cracking. “A great poet. One day, you’ll be celebrated. And when you are, will you remember your old tutor? Your friend?”

Pen grabbed his hand across the apricot tarts. “Of course I will! You’ve been my best friend. My only true friend. God bless you, old boy.”

Smirke, now sweating like a man who’d just run a marathon through a thunderstorm, whispered, “Am I… really?”

“Yes,” said Pen. “You’re my best friend. My only true friend. Now, let’s drink to your fiancée.”

Smirke froze.

“To whom?” he asked.

“To Miss Thompson,” said Pen, laughing. “Or is it someone else? Someone here? Not old Pybus? Not Miss Rolt? She’s only fourteen!”

“It’s not Miss Portman?” Pen added, grinning. “She’s got a *devilish* large waist. But I wish you joy anyway.”

Smirke groaned. “It’s not Miss Portman,” he said, voice breaking. “It’s someone older than me. Someone… here.”

Pen burst out laughing. “Madame Fribsby? By Jove, it’s Madame Fribsby! The immortal Madame Frib!”

Smirke could take no more. He threw his head back and cried, “No! Not Madame Fribsby! I’ve loved someone else for two years—someone pure, someone angelic—someone whose beauty transcends mere human form!”

Pen stopped laughing. “Who?”

“The most perfect woman in the world,” said Smirke, trembling. “Your mother.”

Pen jumped up. “My mother? You’re mad! She’s seven or eight years older than you!”

“Did you find that objectionable?” Smirke asked, voice rising.

Pen blushed. “Well… no. But still. It’s not right. You can’t propose to a lady of such rank! It’s a breach of confidence! A liberty!”

Smirke clutched his hands together. “But I love her! I’ve loved her since the moment I saw her—her kindness, her grace, her ability to make tea without burning the kettle!”

Pen stared. “You mean… you’re in love with my mother?”

“Yes,” said Smirke, sobbing. “And I wanted to ask her to marry me. But I couldn’t. I didn’t know how. I was afraid.”

Pen looked at him, then at the empty bottles, then at the ceiling. “So you’ve been mooning over my mother—while I’ve been mooning over Miss Costigan, who’s probably married by now and living in a cottage with six cats?”

He paused. “And you think I should care about this?”

“No,” said Smirke, “but I hoped you’d understand.”

Pen sighed. “Understand? Of course I understand. But I can’t let you marry my mother. It’s not proper. It’s not right. It’s not—well, it’s just not.”

He rang the bell. “Bring coffee. And get Mr. Smirke’s horse ready. He’s leaving.”

Smirke, heartbroken, nodded silently. He put on his coat, bowed to Helen (who was still walking the lawn like a queen contemplating a tragic fate), and walked out to the stable.

Pen followed, still half-drunk, half-awake, half-horrified. As Smirke mounted his pony, Pen held out his hand.

“Forgive me,” he said. “If I said anything harsh. You’ve been very kind.”

Smirke nodded. “I’ll always remember you, Arthur.”

Pen watched him ride away, disappearing down the road like a ghost fleeing from its own heartbreak.

When he returned to his mother, he grinned. “Smirke’s unwell,” he said.

Helen smiled. “Oh, dear. Poor man.”

They walked together, arm in arm, under the setting sun. Laura tapped on the window, laughing. The Major called from inside: “Your coffee’s cold!”

That night, Pen told his mother everything—how Smirke had confessed, how he’d been drunk, how he’d almost proposed to her.

Helen laughed—then blushed—then laughed again.

“Confound the man’s impudence,” said the Major later. “What next? Will he try to marry the parson’s cat?”

But deep down, he was amused. And so was everyone else.

Meanwhile, Captain Costigan, having paid off all his debts (with money from Mr. Dolphin), was given a farewell banquet by the Buccaneers—a club of gentlemen who believed in three things: punch, loyalty, and drinking until they forgot their names.

The toast was raised. The speech was given. Captain Costigan, moved beyond words, declared Chatteris the most beautiful city in England—“though I must confess,” he said, “the ladies of London are somewhat taller.”

And so, with a final cheer, he left—his daughter’s future secured, his honor restored, and his liver forever grateful.

And thus ends the first part of this history—where love, wine, and miscommunication reign supreme.
