I owe a debt of gratitude to my late parents, whose memory rests in peace, for instilling in me at a tender age a rigorous habit of order and regularity. In those happier days, I was taught to maintain my hair in perfect tidiness at all hours, to fold every garment with meticulous care, and to arrange them in a specific sequence upon a designated chair at the foot of my bed before retiring. This ritual was invariably preceded by an entry in my small diary detailing the day's events, followed by the recitation of the "Evening Hymn" in bed, which in turn ushered in the sweet, uninterrupted slumber of childhood.

Alas, in later life, the Evening Hymn has been replaced by somber and bitter reflections, and that peaceful sleep has yielded only to the fractured restlessness of anxiety. Yet, I have preserved the habits of folding my clothes and keeping my diary. The former connects me to my pre-troublesome youth, before my father's ruin; the latter, originally intended to discipline the fallen nature inherited from Adam, has unexpectedly become vital to my modest pursuits. It has allowed me to serve the whims of a wealthy relative, specifically Mr. Franklin Blake, into whom my late uncle married.

Cut off from news of my kin by marriage for some time, I reside in a small town in Brittany, surrounded by a select circle of serious English friends and enjoying the benefits of a Protestant clergyman and affordable provisions. Amidst this retreat—a spiritual Patmos amidst the roaring waves of Catholicism—a letter from England finally reached me. My insignificant existence had been recalled by Mr. Franklin Blake. My affluent kinsman wrote bluntly, revealing his desire for assistance. He wished to revive the scandalous history of the Moonstone, requesting that I recount my observations during my recent visit to Aunt Verinder's household in London. A fee was offered, characterized by the typical lack of sentimentality found in the wealthy. I was asked to reopen wounds that time had scarcely healed and to dredge up excruciating memories, all for the compensation of a fresh wound in the form of Mr. Blake's cheque. My nature is frail; it required a strenuous internal battle before Christian humility subdued sinful pride, allowing me to accept the payment.

Without my diary, I fear I could not have honestly earned this sum. With it, the humble laborer (who forgives Mr. Blake for his insults) deserves her wage. Nothing escaped my notice during my stay with dear Aunt Verinder. Every detail was recorded daily, thanks to my early training, and everything, down to the minutest fragment, shall be recounted herein. My reverence for truth stands far higher than my respect for individuals. It will be easy for Mr. Blake to omit anything that might appear unfavorable to the central figure of this narrative. He has purchased my time, but no amount of his wealth can buy my conscience.

*Note added by Franklin Blake:* Miss Clack may rest assured regarding this point. Nothing will be added, altered, or removed from her manuscript or any others I receive. Regardless of the opinions expressed or the stylistic peculiarities that may mar the literary quality of these collected narratives, not a single line will be tampered with. Sent to me as genuine documents, they shall be preserved as such, endorsed by witness attestations. Furthermore, the person primarily concerned in Miss Clack's account is currently content enough to endure the sharpest scrutiny of her pen and recognizes its undeniable value in exposing her unique character.

According to my diary, I was accidentally passing Aunt Verinder's house in Montagu Square on Monday, July 3rd, 1848. Seeing the shutters open and blinds raised, I deemed it polite to inquire. The servant who answered informed me that my aunt and her daughter (whom I hesitate to call my cousin) had arrived from the countryside a week prior and intended to remain in London. I sent up a message declining to disturb them but asking if I could be of service. The servant delivered my message in insolent silence and left me standing in the hall. She is the daughter of an elderly pagan named Betteredge, who has been tolerated in my aunt's family far too long. Sitting in the dirty hall on a hard chair, I selected a tract from my bag that seemed providentially suited for her. Despite the discomfort, the joy of returning good for evil elevated me above such trivial concerns. The tract, titled "A Word With You On Your Cap-Ribbons," was part of a series addressing young women on the sinfulness of fashion.

"My lady is much obliged and requests you come to lunch tomorrow at two," she replied.

I ignored the insolence of her delivery and her daring gaze. Thanking her, I adopted a tone of Christian concern: "Will you accept a tract?" She glanced at the title, scoffed, "Is it written by a man or a woman, Miss? If by a woman, I'd rather not read it. If by a man, I assure him he knows nothing about it," and handed it back before opening the door. Determined to sow the good seed, I waited until she closed the door, slipped the tract into the letterbox, and dropped another through the area railings, feeling slightly relieved of my burden toward humanity.

That evening, the Select Committee of the Mothers'-Small-Clothes-Conversion-Society met. Our noble charity aims to redeem unredeemed fathers' trousers from pawnbrokers and prevent their reuse by the irreclaimable parent by altering them to fit the innocent son. I was a member of the committee at the time, mentioning this association because my esteemed friend, Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, was involved in our work of moral utility. I expected to see him in the boardroom that Monday evening to share news of Aunt Verinder's arrival. To my dismay, he was absent. Upon expressing my surprise, the committee members, busy with pressing business involving trousers, looked up in astonishment and asked if I hadn't heard the news. Admitting my ignorance, I learned for the first time of an event serving as the catalyst for this narrative. On the preceding Friday, two gentlemen occupying vastly different social strata had become victims of an outrage that shocked London. One was Mr. Septimus Luker of Lambeth; the other, Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

Living in my current isolation, I lack access to newspaper accounts of the crime. Deprived also of hearing Mr. Ablewhite relate the events with his characteristic fervor, I can only state the facts as they were told to me that Monday evening, adhering strictly to the method of folding clothes taught to me from infancy: everything neat, everything in its place. These lines are penned by a poor, weak woman. From such a source, what cruelty might one expect?

The date, precise thanks to my diligent upbringing, was Friday, June 30th, 1848.

Early that fateful day, our talented Mr. Godfrey went to cash a cheque at a bank in Lombard Street. The name of the firm is unfortunately smudged in my diary, and my commitment to truth prevents me from guessing. Fortunately, the bank's identity is irrelevant. What matters is a minor incident occurring afterward. As Mr. Godfrey exited, he encountered a stranger leaving simultaneously. A brief contest of politeness arose regarding who should pass first. The stranger insisted Mr. Godfrey go ahead; they bowed and parted.

Superficial observers might dismiss this as a trivial anecdote told with excessive detail. Oh, my young friends and fellow-sinners, beware of relying solely on your carnal reason! Be morally tidy. Let your faith be as your stockings, and your stockings as your faith—ever spotless and ready for immediate wear!

Forgive me; I have slipped into my Sunday-school persona, wholly inappropriate for this record. Let me attempt to be worldly: trifles often lead to terrible consequences. Premising that the polite stranger was indeed Mr. Luker of Lambeth, we shall follow Mr. Godfrey home to Kilburn.

Waiting in the hall was a poorly dressed yet delicate and intriguing little boy. He handed Mr. Godfrey a letter, noting he had been entrusted with it by an unknown old lady with no instructions to await a reply. Such occurrences were common in Mr. Godfrey's extensive experience promoting public charities. He dismissed the boy and opened the letter.

The handwriting was unfamiliar. It requested his presence within an hour at a house in Northumberland Street, Strand, a location he had never visited. The purpose was to provide details concerning the Mothers'-Small-Clothes-Conversion-Society to an elderly lady wishing to augment the charity's funds. She identified herself and noted her brief stay in London precluded giving earlier notice to the eminent philanthropist she addressed.

While ordinary people might hesitate to alter their plans for a stranger, the Christian Hero never pauses when good is to be done. Mr. Godfrey immediately turned back and proceeded to Northumberland Street. A most respectable, albeit somewhat corpulent, man answered the door and, recognizing Mr. Godfrey, guided him to an empty apartment on the drawing-room floor. Two unusual things struck him upon entry: a faint scent of musk and camphor, and an ancient Oriental manuscript, richly illuminated with Indian designs, lying open on a table.

Absorbed in examining the book, with his back turned toward the closed folding doors leading to the front room, he was suddenly seized around the neck from behind. Just in time to notice the arm was naked and tawny-brown, his eyes were bandaged, his mouth gagged, and he was thrown helpless onto the floor by what he judged to be two men. A third rifled his pockets and, if I may use such phrasing as a lady, searched him thoroughly.
