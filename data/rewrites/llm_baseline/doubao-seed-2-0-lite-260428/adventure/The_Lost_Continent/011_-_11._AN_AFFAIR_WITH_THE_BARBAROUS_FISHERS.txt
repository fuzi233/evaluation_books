The all-powerful Empress Phorenice, consumed with petty jealousy over a mere female prisoner? The thought barely had time to land before my soldier’s training kicked in, sorting the only logical path through the chaos in a heartbeat. My orders from the High Council of Priests required me to sacrifice myself for Atlantis, if need be—and if it came to it, I would let Nais seem to fall to Phorenice’s wrath, while a quiet scheme I’d just hatched would slip her to safety.

I bowed low before the throne of the woman who had just declared her grudge against the captive, and spoke clearly. “In all things where a mortal hand can work, I will see your wishes done, Phorenice.” She patted my arm then, those luminous, otherworldly eyes softening so sharply I almost forgot the danger we stood in. The gods had gifted her face, her voice, that impossible magnetism for a reason—she won half her wars before a single blade ever left its sheath.

By then, merchants and waterfront dwellers clustered thick around the royal quay we’d washed up on after the mammoth’s wild ride, pressing us with offers of dry clothes and shelter. Phorenice played the gracious queen perfectly, and while I cared little for damp wool, the stretch of harbour we’d been thrown into reeked of rotting fish and bilge, so I followed her lead. I did not glance at Nais again, did not even spare her a parting look, but that small concession was not enough for Phorenice. Before she turned away, she issued the port captain strict, unforgiving orders to lock the prisoner under round-the-clock guard.

At the merchant’s house they bathed me, and I relieved my host of the plainest tunic in his stores—he was overjoyed to get off so cheaply, little knowing his misfortune was just beginning. I spent an hour loitering on the pavement, listening to the distant rumble of city bombardment, before Phorenice stepped out to join us, and I bit back a grin at the merchant’s shell-shocked face. His entire stock of gem-encrusted finery, bought in shared partnership with a dozen other traders, had caught her eye, and she had draped every last piece of it over her person, leaving him effectively ruined.

“Are they not lovely?” she purred, turning to show me the jewels flashing bright in the sun. “I am almost glad that mammoth threw us into the bay. This is far better than any royal gift. Tell the man that if he earns more trinkets soon, I will grace his shop again. Ah—our litters are here. Send one away. You will ride with me, Deucalion. We play at being lovers today, even if the real thing will come far more certain tomorrow. No extra bearers, my own slaves are strong enough for both of us. I am glad you are not some gross, overfed courtier. I am small, and I do not want a husband who will loom over me like a giant.”

“Back to the royal pyramid?” I asked.

“Not to the walls, not to the throne. I do not want to fight, or to rule, today. I want a few hours to be just Phorenice, the maiden. If you would woo me now, the way common maidens are wooed, I would treasure it. Tell the slaves to follow the curve of the harbour. And tell those gawking crowds that if they dare follow, I will rain fire down on them the same way I did on the sacrifice horse.”

I had heard rumours of the city’s unruliness ever since the welcoming banquet at the pyramid, but if anyone knew the full scope of the chaos, it was Phorenice, so I did not press her. I dismissed the royal guard, snapped sharp words at the gawking throng of onlookers to send them scattering, and slipped into the litter at her side as the bearers hefted the poles.

They swung along the wide promenade that ran between the merchants’ houses, the mariners’ huts, and the glinting harbour water, and I felt a sharp, sad pang at the sight of how empty the great port had grown. Once, ships berthed so close that hundreds had to wait out in the estuary for a spot, and the old kings had ordered the harbour doubled in size just to keep up with the glut of trade. Back then, a voyage to Egypt and back took a full year, and one in three ships never made it home, devoured by sea monsters or torn apart by storms. But trade was alive, roaring. Merchants were wealthy, generous to the temples. Now? Everyone wanted to be a courtier. Trade was vulgar. Any man of means threw down his cargo manifests, curled his beard in the latest fashion, and wasted days ordering tailors to decorate his tunics instead of counting bales. Smaller merchants, crushed under endless taxes and war levies, were nearly starved out of existence. A month before I returned, a full cargo of slaves dragged from the wilds of Europe had sailed into the port—and not a single man in all Atlantis would take even one as a gift. They’d been set loose on the far side of the estuary, left to starve or fend for themselves in the wild marshes.

I said none of this to Phorenice. I had learned her moods well enough to know that complaining of Atlantis’s decay would only sour the rare light, playful mood she was in that day. We traveled on until we reached the far western tip of the harbour wall, where the bearers set us down, panting. She led me to a small isolated stone house perched on a rocky promontory where the tide ran fast, and after half a dozen heavy iron bolts were drawn back, she showed me her secret sea gold collectors.

All of us who studied at the sacred mountain’s colleges knew gold was dissolved in trace amounts in seawater—but no priest had ever devised a way to pull it out. This “country-bred upstart” Empress had stumbled on the trick like it had fallen right into her lap from the sky.

She laughed and patted my arm as I stared, transfixed. “I tell every other soul in Atlantis that the gods gave this secret to me, that I am half divine. But I am no goddess to you, Deucalion, am I? Truth be told, I cannot even explain how I thought of it. I must have dreamed it. Look—the seawater flows in through that culvert, washes over these rough grooved metal plates in the floor, and flows right back out to the bay. See the yellow gold crusted in the ridges? That is ours. My workers melt it down into bars, and carry them to my smiths in the city. At first the men tried to cheat me. I towed two of them behind a galley until the cannibal fish tore them to pieces. The rest have believed in my power ever since.”

“Believed you are a goddess,” I said.

“Precisely.” She grinned. “But I show you how it works. Do the math, see how much gold pours out of the tides every month. With your clever priest’s brain, you could make them worship you as a god too, if you just use your wits.”

I could not tell if she was jesting or in earnest. One minute she would brag that all her triumphs came from nothing but her own sharp wits, the next she would spin some impossible tale that made it seem she could not possibly be mortal. That small, supple, quicksilver woman mystified me more every minute I spent at her side. I despaired more and more that I could ever restore Atlantis to peace and the old gods, even if I carried out the Council’s orders and married her. The only human solution that ever crossed my mind was the one I tried to shove away: one quick dagger thrust through her heart, and the chaos would run its course until the country healed on its own. But the thought sickened me. If death was the right path for her, the High Gods would have given me clear orders to carry it out.

But Phorenice’s hold on me was stronger than I realized. Minutes later, when we stumbled straight into mortal peril, I fought for her harder than I had ever fought for any battle in my life. Some of that was the old, familiar battle-lust that thrills even the coldest soldier when blades start singing. But most of it was the sharp, hot urge to protect her, the utter revulsion at the thought of those crude, uncouth fishers tearing her down.

The trouble started where the merchant quarters ended, at the cluster of mud-and-grass huts that were the fishing district, a stretch of waterfront famous for riots even in the old king’s peaceful days. Fishermen lived the hardest lives of all Atlantis’s toilers: storms, firestones rained down from the sky, great sea beasts hunted them across the waves, and scarcely a week went by that a boat did not fail to come home. They fished with tamed giant sea birds, raised from chicks, with bronze rings around their necks to stop them swallowing the fish they dove to catch. If a fisher lost both his hunting birds, the guild cast him out entirely, claiming the sea gods had turned against him, and left him to starve. These were desperate, broken men, who would spend a month’s earnings in a single night of drinking because they knew they might be eaten by a sea monster the next dawn. They feared no consequences.

I spotted two clumsy hide-covered fishing boats paddling hard toward us across the ripples, and at the same time, savage-faced men were pouring out of the narrow lanes behind us, swarming after the litter.

“Let me step out and scatter this rabble,” I said.

“They always crowd around me,” Phorenice said, laughing. “Ugly souls, they love to stare at my face. But go on, chase them off. I am on holiday today, remember? You are supposed to be wooing me.”

I stepped onto the pavement, and the shouting grew louder. The fishers in the boats yelled to the mob on the shore to make sure we did not escape, and their numbers swelled by the second. I marched straight for the largest group clustered behind the huts, and barked at them to disperse. “Go clean the fish offal out of your lanes, fix your rotting roofs. I will come inspect tomorrow, and set this whole quarter to rights. Today, the Empress desires privacy. Be gone.”

“Give us money!” A shrill voice piped from the crowd.

“I will bring a torch and rig a gallows for every last one of you if you do not clear off right now,” I snapped.

For half a heartbeat they hesitated. Men in the sun slipped for the shadows, many scurried back into their huts. But the men in the boats paddled right onto the beach, leaped out of their crafts, and yelled at the mob to stand their ground. I ordered the litter bearers to move fast down the path. I could hold the crowd off, but if Phorenice stayed as their glittering, jewel-stuffed target, things would turn far uglier fast.

But Phorenice had no intention of running. She ordered the bearers to halt, stepped out of the litter, strapped a sword to her hip, and slung a dented gold-plated target shield over her left forearm. She walked right over to me, grinning. “You cannot hog all the fun, Deucalion. If you act this greedy now, what are you going to do when I am your little meek wife? Do these fools truly mean to fight us?”

The crowd closed in, the stench of their unwashed bodies thick enough to make us cough. One grubby hand lunged straight for the jewels glinting on Phorenice’s breast. I lopped it clean off at the elbow, and it dropped at her feet. The fight exploded.

“Back to back with me, comrade!” Phorenice laughed, and she laid about her with her blade so fast and sharp I could scarcely believe my eyes. It was slaughter, at first. The mob was so frantic to grab loot that none of them had room to swing their own clumsy weapons. We carved a perfect circle of empty space around us with nothing but whirling steel. And every single one of Phorenice’s thrusts landed true. She did not waste a single move. The throne of Atlantis had not been won by her pretty face and silver tongue alone. I could not have admired her more. The fishers were ferocious, unkillable in their greed, and I would have been dragged down under their sheer numbers if I had not snatched a shield from one overconfident attacker who got too close.

Above the roar of the crowd, Phorenice’s battle cry cut clear, ringing out my name, again and again. I knew she was a master of flattery, but hearing my name screamed in the middle of a blood fight, at her side? The flattery wrapped around me, warm and sharp. In that moment, the memory of Nais, her danger, the High Council’s orders, all vanished completely. For that second I cared for nothing but the fight, and the woman at my back.

The attackers broke. They shrank back out of sword reach, leaving a perfect ring of twisted, still bodies on the ground between us and them. It was the perfect moment to roar a command, send them scrambling back to their huts, and walk away unharmed. But Phorenice would not have it. She laughed, gloating over how well our blades had worked, and then she taunted them. She told them the quarter would be burned, the skin flayed from every last one of them, sawn in half, tortured, for killing her litter bearers.

“You are scared now, aren’t you?” She jeered. “Why not prove I am no goddess? If you finish this fight well, I swear by my own face I will be lenient with the ones who live.”

The mob roared, and charged for us again.
