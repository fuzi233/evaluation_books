Gerard opened his eyes to find Denys gazing upon him with a mixture of relief and lingering worry. "It is you still asleep!" Denys exclaimed, glancing toward the window where the midday sun beat fiercely against the glass. "'Tis already high noon."

"It was a benediction," Gerard replied softly, pressing a hand to his brow. "Methinks Heaven itself bestowed this slumber upon me. It has drawn a curtain, thick and white, between me and that dreadful nightmare. To sit here, breathing freely, you and I, safe and sound—it feels like a miracle. The vision I saw... it was so terrible."

"Ay, lad, that is the proper way to view such terrors once they have passed," Denys advised gently, pouring water from a pitcher. "They are but shadows, fleeting phantoms. Eat thy breakfast, and soon enough, thou shalt speak of naught else. Furthermore, I promised to escort thee into the town before the hour strikes, to present thyself to His Worship."

Gerard dipped a slice of coarse rye bread into a bowl of deep red wine, consuming it with a grateful fervor to banish the last dregs of his hunger. Then, under Denys's guidance, they traversed the battlefield where the struggle had unfolded. As they walked, Gerard shivered, his gaze darting nervously through the hedgerows, imagining ambushes where there were none, until they finally reached the outskirts of the little town.

Denys steered him into an inn known as "The White Hart."

"No need to fear the knife-wielders here," Denys assured him, leaning against the doorframe. "I have known this host for many a year. He is a burgess, likely the bailiff himself. I intended to seek shelter here last night, but time slipped away, and darkness overtook us before I could secure a room—and..."

"And yet, a woman stood at the threshold," Gerard interrupted, his voice rising with emotion. "She proved far wiser than any Jeanneton; she told us the danger was a lie."

"Why, what other salvation did we have if not her?" Denys pressed, his eyes wide. "Yes, she risked her own life to save ours."

"That is truth, Denys," Gerard affirmed, clutching his heart. "Though I hold women in little regard usually, I long to thank this poor soul, to offer her a reward, nay, I would give all I possess, every penny in my purse, just to see her face again. Dost thou not feel the same?"

"By God's bones, yes," Denys muttered.

"But where can we find her?"

"Perhaps the alderman knows," Denys suggested. "We must pay him a visit first."

The alderman received them with a countenance so peculiar and unreadable that it seemed almost theatrical. Yet, after a brief pause, his features softened into a grim, knowing smile, and he began to question Gerard in detail, committing every answer to memory. Once the interrogation concluded, he declared that they must remain within the town walls until the thieves were brought to trial, warning them that failure to appear would incur heavy fines and imprisonment.

Both men stared at him, bewildered by the severity of the order.

"However," the alderman continued, "time shall be short. The culprits were caught red-handed." He added casually, "And let me remind thee, in any event, thou couldst not depart this place this week."

Denys gazed at the old man with a furrowed brow, while Gerard smiled inwardly, amused by what he perceived as the antiquated gentleman's naive belief that a provincial town in Burgundy possessed any charm capable of detaining a pilgrim bound for Rome and destined for Margaret.

Now, driven by a singular impulse, Gerard turned his attention to the matter nearest both their hearts.

"Your Worship," he said earnestly, "we cannot locate our benefactress within these streets."

"Nay, but who is thy benefactress?" the alderman asked, his tone shifting from official to curious.

"The good maiden who approached thee by night," Gerard answered, his voice trembling slightly with gratitude. "She saved our lives at the peril of her own. Sir, our hearts burn within us to thank and bless her. Where might we find her?"
