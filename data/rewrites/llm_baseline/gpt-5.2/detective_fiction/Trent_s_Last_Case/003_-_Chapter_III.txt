Mr. Nathaniel Burton Cupples stood on the hotel veranda at Marlstone a little before eight, letting the morning arrange itself in his mind the way a well-set table does—each thing in its proper place, no more and no less. He had been awake an hour, and the hour had done what honest sea air always does: it had sharpened him into hunger.

He reviewed yesterday with the same conscientious exactitude with which he might have reviewed a pantry. The shock and commotion after the finding of the dead man had disordered his appetite, and therefore his intake. That deficiency must be made good. This morning, then: three slices of toast, not two; an extra egg, the rest as usual. Luncheon could bear the remainder of the burden when it came.

The waiter brought what was ordered, and Cupples ate with the quiet gratitude of a man who notices what others swallow unthinking: the crackle of toast where the butter had melted into the pores; the faint, clean sulphur of the egg as the shell yielded; tea that smelt of leaf and hot china. Beneath it all lay the shore’s own seasoning—brine, warmed kelp, and the resinous breath of the pines above the cliff-path—set to the steady acoustic of the place: gulls turning their cries over the water, the hush-and-draw of the tide in the shingle, the thin click of flag halyards against a mast somewhere down in the gap of the village. It was an honest soundscape, not yet torn to pieces by the hysterical grinding of machinery; even the hotel, with its clatter of plates, seemed to accept the sea’s rhythm rather than impose its own.

When he had eaten, and before he called for anything further, he gave himself a few minutes of landscape, for he delighted in it with the same respectful connoisseurship he gave to a well-made meal. The great pierced rock stood out of a glassy sea like an emblem—an accident of geology that looked, in this light, almost intentional; and beyond it the sloping levels of pasture and tilled land rose toward the moor in ordered bands, hedges and copses making patterns as old as the place. It was a country where memory sat in the very contours.

Cupples himself, spare and nearly sixty, had the active wiriness of a man whose mind never let his body lapse into negligence. His sparse beard and moustache could not hide the kindliness of his mouth; his keen pleasant eyes took in detail without fuss. The sharp nose and narrow jaw, the dark clothes and soft black hat, gave him a priestly air, though his creed was of another sort: Positivist, bookish, museum-haunting, retired from banking into the quiet half-lit world of scholarship and gentle research. He was orderly, industrious, conscientious, with little imagination; yet his heart was inexhaustibly kind, and his gaiety—innocent, unhumorous—could surface like sunlight on water. His favourite author was Montaigne, and he quoted him as other men quoted Scripture.

He had only just finished when a large motor-car swung into the drive with a noise that jarred the morning—an arrogant, metallic intrusion into the sea’s soft governance. The engine idled with a growl that seemed to flatten the gull-cries and make the whole veranda unconsciously tense. Cupples turned, mildly displeased, and asked the waiter, “Who is this?”

“Id is der manager,” said the young man, listless. “He have been to meed a gendleman by der train.”

The car drew up. The porter ran out; the manager’s voice sounded, brisk and important. Then, out of the machine, long-limbed and loose-built, much younger than Cupples, came a man whose presence altered the air at once, as a strong spice changes a broth. His tweeds were rough, his hair and short moustache tolerably untidy, and his high-boned, quixotic face wore a smile that looked both pleasant and alert, as if it was listening for something.

“Cupples, by all that’s miraculous!” he cried, and pounced upon him before Cupples could fully rise, catching his outstretched hand in a hard grip. “My luck is serving me today. This is the second slice within an hour. How are you, my best of friends? And why are you here? Why sit’st thou by that ruined breakfast? Dost thou its former pride recall, or ponder how it passed away? I am glad to see you!”

“I was half expecting you, Trent,” Cupples said, beaming. “You look splendid, my dear fellow. But you cannot have had your breakfast yet. Will you have it at my table?”

“Rather! An enormous great breakfast too—with refined conversation and tears of recognition never dry. Will you get young Siegfried to lay a place for me while I go and wash? I shan’t be three minutes.”

He vanished into the hotel with the energy of a man who could not waste motion. Cupples, after a moment’s thought, went into the porter’s office and used the telephone.

When he returned Trent was already seated, pouring tea as if he had always belonged there, and studying the food with genuine interest—the sort of interest that begins in appetite but travels quickly into observation.

“I expect this to be a hard day for me,” Trent said in his curious jerky way. “I shan’t eat again till the evening, very likely. You guess why I’m here, don’t you?”

“Undoubtedly,” Cupples replied. “You have come down to write about the murder.”

“That is rather a colourless way of stating it,” Trent said, and began upon a sole with the swift neatness of a man whose hands had been trained to accuracy, whether in cutting fish or cutting facts. “I should prefer to put it that I have come down in the character of avenger of blood, to hunt down the guilty, and vindicate the honour of society. That is my line of business. Families waited on at their private residences. I say, Cupples, I have made a good beginning already. Wait a bit, and I’ll tell you.”

He ate quickly, abstractedly, as if breakfast were fuel and pleasure together, but also an interval to think in. Cupples watched him with content, as if the sight of a friend eating well were itself restorative.

“Your manager here,” Trent said at last, “is a fellow of remarkable judgement. He is an admirer of mine. He knows more about my best cases than I do myself. The Record wired last night to say I was coming, and when I got out of the train at seven this morning, there he was waiting with a motor-car the size of a haystack. He is beside himself with joy at having me here. It is fame.”

He drank tea and went on. “Almost his first words were to ask me if I would like to see the body of the murdered man—if so, he thought he could manage it for me. Keen as a razor. The body’s in Dr. Stock’s surgery down in the village, exactly as it was when found. It’s to be post-mortem’d this morning, so I was only just in time. He ran me down to the doctor’s, giving me full particulars all the way. I was pretty well au fait by the time we arrived. The manager has some pull with the doctor, I suppose. Anyhow, no difficulties—nor the constable on duty either, though he was careful to insist I mustn’t give him away in the paper.”

“I saw the body before it was removed,” Cupples said. “I should not have called anything remarkable, except that the shot in the eye had scarcely disfigured the face at all, and there was scarcely any effusion of blood, apparently. The wrists were scratched and bruised. I expect that, with your trained faculties, you remarked other details.”

“Other details, yes; whether they suggest anything, I don’t know. They’re odd.” Trent paused long enough to consider his next mouthful. “Take the wrists. How was it you could see bruises and scratches on them? I dare say you saw something of Manderson before the murder.”

“Certainly.”

“Well, did you ever see his wrists?”

Cupples searched his memory. “No. Now you raise the point, I am reminded that when I interviewed Manderson here he wore stiff cuffs, coming well down over his hands.”

“He always did,” Trent said. “My friend the manager says so. I pointed out to him something you didn’t observe: no cuffs visible. They’d been dragged up inside the coat-sleeves—like yours would be if you hurried into a coat without pulling your cuffs down. That’s why you saw his wrists.”

“Well, I call that suggestive,” Cupples said mildly. “You might infer, perhaps, that when he got up he hurried over his dressing.”

“Yes—did he?” Trent’s voice sharpened with that sudden turning of attention which made him so effective. “The manager drew that inference too. ‘He was always a bit of a swell in his dress,’ he told me; therefore, when he got up in that mysterious way before the house was stirring and went out into the grounds, he was in a great hurry. ‘Look at his shoes,’ he said. ‘Mr. Manderson was always specially neat about his footwear. Those laces were tied in a hurry.’ I agreed. ‘And he left his false teeth in his room,’ says the manager. ‘Doesn’t that prove he was flustered?’ I allowed it looked like it. But I said: if he was pressed, why did he part his hair so carefully? That parting is a work of art. Why did he put on so much? A complete outfit of underclothing, studs in the shirt, sock-suspenders, a watch and chain, money and keys in his pockets. That’s what I said. The manager couldn’t explain. Can you?”

Cupples considered, honestly trying to fit the facts together as one fits ingredients to a recipe. “Those facts might suggest he was hurried only at the end of his dressing,” he said. “Coat and shoes come last.”

“But not false teeth. Ask anybody who wears them. And besides, I’m told he hadn’t washed at all on getting up—which in a neat man looks like violent hurry from the beginning. And here’s another: one waistcoat pocket was lined with wash-leather for his gold watch. But he put the watch in the pocket on the other side. Anyone with settled habits can see how odd that is.” Trent’s knife tapped lightly against his plate. “There are signs of agitation and haste, and signs of exactly the opposite. For the present I’m not guessing. I must reconnoitre the ground first—if I can get the right side of the people in the house.”

Cupples smiled. “That,” he said, “is precisely the point on which I can assist you.”

Trent looked up sharply. “You can?”

“I told you I half expected you,” Cupples went on. “I will explain. Mrs. Manderson, who is my niece—”

“What!” Trent dropped knife and fork with a clatter. “Cupples, you are jesting.”

“I am perfectly serious,” Cupples said earnestly. “Her father, John Peter Domecq, was my wife’s brother. I never mentioned my niece or her marriage to you. To tell the truth, it has always been painful to me, and I have avoided discussing it with anyone.”

He indicated, as he spoke, a red roof among poplars some three hundred yards away—the only building in sight standing apart from the tiny village in the gap below. “Last night, when I was over at the house—you passed it in the car—Mr. Bunner, one of Manderson’s two secretaries, expressed a hope that the Record would send you down; the police seemed quite at a loss. He mentioned your successes, and Mabel—my niece—was interested when I told her afterwards. She has a great horror of the newspaper side of this sad business, and she had entreated me to do anything I could to keep journalists away—I’m sure you can understand that; it isn’t a reflection on your profession. But she said you appeared to have powers as a detective, and she would not stand in the way of anything that might clear up the crime. Then I told her you were a personal friend of mine, and I gave you your character for tact and consideration; and it ended in her saying that if you came she would like you to be helped in every way.”

Trent leaned forward and took Cupples’s hand, and for once said nothing. Cupples, warmed by this and pleased at the good turn of things, continued:

“I spoke to my niece on the telephone only just now, and she is glad you are here. She asks me to say you may make any enquiries you like; she puts the house and grounds at your disposal. She had rather not see you herself. She is keeping to her own sitting-room. She has already been interviewed by a detective officer who is there, and she feels unequal to any more. She adds she does not believe she could say anything useful. The two secretaries and Martin, the butler—who is a most intelligent man—could tell you all you want to know, she thinks.”

Trent finished eating with a thoughtful brow, as if the last mouthfuls had become suddenly tasteless under the pressure of new knowledge. He filled a pipe slowly and sat on the veranda rail, the motor’s earlier din forgotten now in the clean persistence of sea and birds.

“Cupples,” he said quietly, “is there anything about this business that you know and would rather not tell me?”

Cupples started slightly and stared at him. “What do you mean?” he asked.

“I mean about the Mandersons. Look here. Something strikes me at the very beginning. Here’s a man suddenly and violently killed, and nobody’s heart seems to be broken about it, to say the least. The manager spoke of him as coolly as if he’d never set eyes on him, though they’ve been neighbours every summer for years. Then you talk about it in the coldest blood. And Mrs. Manderson—well, I’ve heard of women being more cut up about their husbands being murdered than she seems to be. Is there something in this, Cupples, or is it my fancy? Was there something queer about Manderson? I travelled on the same boat with him once, but never spoke. I only know his public character, which was repulsive enough. This may bear on the case. That’s the only reason I ask.”

Cupples took time. He fingered his sparse beard and looked out over the sea, where the tide’s soft breathing seemed to urge caution—say only what is necessary, and say it cleanly, because words, once loosed, cannot be recalled. At last he turned.

“I see no reason,” he said, “why I shouldn’t tell you as between ourselves, my dear fellow. I need not say it must not be referred to, however distantly. The truth is that nobody really liked Manderson; and I think those who were nearest to him liked him least.”

“Why?” Trent cut in.

“Most people found it difficult to explain.” Cupples spoke carefully, as a food-historian might speak of some lost dish that cannot be perfectly reconstructed because the old dialect of the kitchen has died out. “In trying to account for my own sensations I could only put it that one felt in him a complete absence of the sympathetic faculty. There was nothing outwardly repellent. He was not ill-mannered, or vicious, or dull—indeed, he could be remarkably interesting. But I received the impression there could be no human creature whom he would not sacrifice in pursuit of his schemes, in his task of imposing himself and his will upon the world. Perhaps that is fanciful; I think not altogether so.”

He paused, then went on with a quiet sorrow that sat oddly with the bright morning. “The point is that Mabel—my niece—was very unhappy. I am nearly twice your age, my dear boy, though you always try to make me feel we are contemporaries. I am getting to be an old man, and many people have confided their matrimonial troubles to me; but I never knew another case like my niece’s and her husband’s. I have known her since she was a baby, Trent, and I know—understand, I do not employ that word lightly—I know she is as amiable and honourable a woman, besides her other gifts, as any man could wish. But Manderson, for some time past, had made her miserable.”

“What did he do?” Trent asked when Cupples stopped.

“When I put that question to Mabel, her words were that he seemed to nurse a perpetual grievance. He maintained a distance between them, and he would say nothing. I don’t know how it began or what lay behind it. All she would tell me on that point was that he had no cause in the world for his attitude. I think she knew what was in his mind, whatever it was; but she is full of pride.” Cupples’s voice tightened a little. “This went on for months. At last, a week ago, she wrote to me. I am the only near relative she has. Her mother died when she was a child; after John Peter died I was something like a father to her until she married—five years ago. She asked me to come and help her, and I came at once. That is why I am here.”

He took a sip of tea, as if to moisten words that did not want to come. Trent smoked, staring out at the hot June fields that rose toward the moor—the kind of land where families stayed rooted for generations, where speech and habit were handed down like seed-corn, and where a marriage was not merely two people but two histories tied together.

“I would not go to White Gables,” Cupples resumed. “You know my views upon the economic constitution of society and the relationship of capitalist to employee; and you know, no doubt, what use Manderson made of his vast industrial power on several notorious occasions—especially the trouble in the Pennsylvania coal-fields three years ago. I regarded him, apart from any personal dislike, as a criminal and a disgrace to society. I came to this hotel, and I saw my niece here. She told me what I have told you. She said the worry and humiliation, and the strain of keeping up appearances, were telling upon her, and she asked my advice. I said she should face him and demand an explanation. But she would not. She had always taken the line of affecting not to notice the change, and nothing would persuade her to admit to him that she was injured once pride had led her into that course. Life is quite full,” Cupples sighed, “of these obstinate silences and cultivated misunderstandings.”

“Did she love him?” Trent asked abruptly; then corrected himself. “Had she any love left for him?”

Cupples played with his teaspoon. “I am bound to say,” he answered slowly, “that I think not. But you must not misunderstand the woman. No power on earth would have persuaded her to admit that to anyone—even to herself, perhaps—so long as she considered herself bound to him. And I gather that, apart from this mysterious sulking of late, he had always been considerate and generous.”

“You were saying she refused to have it out with him.”

“She did,” Cupples said. “And I knew by experience it was useless to attempt to move a Domecq where the sense of dignity was involved. So I thought it over carefully, and next day I watched my opportunity and met Manderson as he passed by this hotel. I asked him to favour me with a few minutes’ conversation, and he stepped inside the gate. We had held no communication since my niece’s marriage, but he remembered me. I put the matter to him at once. I told him what Mabel had confided. I said I would neither approve nor condemn her bringing me into it, but she was suffering, and I considered it my right to ask how he justified placing her in such a position.”

“And how did he take that?” Trent asked, with a private smile at the notion of gentle Cupples confronting so formidable a man.

“Not very well,” Cupples said sadly. “Far from well. I can tell you almost exactly what he said—it wasn’t much. He said: ‘See here, Cupples, you don’t want to butt in. My wife can look after herself. I’ve found that out, along with other things.’ He was perfectly quiet—he was said never to lose control—though there was a light in his eyes that would have frightened a man who was in the wrong, I dare say.”

Cupples’s hand closed on the teaspoon as if it were the only solid thing in reach. “But I had been thoroughly roused by that last remark, and the tone of it, which I cannot reproduce. You see—I love my niece. She is the only child there has been in our—my—house. Moreover, my wife brought her up, and any reflection on Mabel I could not help feeling, in the heat of the moment, as an indirect reflection upon one who is gone.”

“You turned upon him,” Trent said softly. “You asked him to explain.”

“That is precisely what I did.” Cupples looked, for a second, as if he could see again the man’s face. “For a moment he only stared, and I could see a vein on his forehead swelling—an unpleasant sight. Then he said quite quietly: ‘This thing has gone far enough, I guess,’ and turned to go.”

“Did he mean your interview?” Trent asked.

“From the words alone, yes.” Cupples’s eyes drifted again toward the red roof among poplars. “But the way he uttered them gave me a strange and apprehensive feeling. I received the impression he had formed some sinister resolve.”

He drew a breath. “I regret to say I had lost the power of dispassionate thought. I fell into a great rage”—his apology was in his mildness, not in his words—“and said a number of foolish things. I reminded him the law allowed a measure of freedom to wives who received intolerable treatment. I made irrelevant references to his public record, and expressed the view that such men as he were unfit to live. I said these things and others as ill-considered under the eyes—and possibly within earshot—of half a dozen persons sitting on this veranda. I noticed them, in spite of my agitation, looking at me as I walked up to the hotel again after relieving my mind—for it did relieve it,” he added with a sigh.

“And Manderson? He said no more?”

“Not a word. He listened with his eyes on my face, quiet as before. When I stopped he smiled very slightly, and turned away and strolled through the gate toward White Gables.”

“And this happened—?”

“On the Sunday morning.”

“Then you never saw him alive again?”

“No,” Cupples said. Then corrected himself with the exactness that was his habit. “Or rather yes—once. Later in the day, on the golf-course. But I did not speak to him. And next morning he was found dead.”

They sat in silence, the sea’s breathing underneath it. A party of guests, back from bathing, came up the steps chattering; their laughter, striking the veranda boards, sounded oddly loud after what had been said. The waiter approached with the practised tread of service. Cupples rose, and, taking Trent’s arm, led him away along the side of the hotel to the long tennis-lawn, where the sound softened again into wind in grass and distant surf.

“I have a reason for telling you all this,” Cupples began as they paced slowly up and down.

“Trust you for that,” Trent returned, refilling his pipe with the careful economy of a man who dislikes waste. He lit it, drew a few puffs, then said, “I’ll try and guess your reason, if you like.”

Cupples’s solemnity relaxed into the faintest smile. He said nothing.

“You thought it possible,” Trent said meditatively—“may I say you thought it practically certain?—that I should find out for myself there’d been something deeper than a mere conjugal tiff. You thought my unwholesome imagination would begin at once to play with the idea of Mrs. Manderson having something to do with the crime. Rather than let me lose myself in barren speculations, you decided to tell me exactly how matters stood—and incidentally to impress upon me, since I know how excellent your judgement is, your opinion of your niece. Is that about right?”
