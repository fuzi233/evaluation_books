From this date, my life did not want variety; I went out a good deal, with the entire consent of Madame Beck, who perfectly approved the grade of my acquaintance. That worthy directress had never from the first treated me otherwise than with respect; and when she found that I was liable to frequent invitations from a château and a great hotel, respect improved into distinction. 

Not that she was fulsome about it: Madame, in all things worldly, was in nothing weak. There was measure and sense in her hottest pursuit of self-interest, a calm considerateness in her closest clutch of gain. Without laying herself open to my contempt as a time-server, she marked with tact that she was pleased her staff should frequent associates who might cultivate and elevate, rather than those who might deteriorate. She never praised either me or my friends. Only once, when she was sitting in the sun in the garden, enveloped in the rich, earthy aroma of dark-roasted coffee and bruised chicory that steeped in the porcelain dripper at her elbow, the *Gazette* in her hand, did she address the matter. The ambient acoustics of the afternoon—the distant, rhythmic scrape of a wooden spoon against a copper preserving pan in the kitchen, the wind rustling the espaliered fruit trees—formed a tranquil ecology, far removed from the abrasive, metallic grind of the modern industrial boulevards beyond the pensionnat walls. I came up to ask leave of absence for the evening, and she delivered herself in the dominant lexicon of the house, a language she wielded with quiet administrative supremacy:

“Oui, oui, ma bonne amie: je vous donne la permission de cœur et de gré. Votre travail dans ma maison a toujours été admirable, rempli de zèle et de discrétion: vous avez bien le droit de vous amuser. Sortez donc tant que vous voudrez. Quant à votre choix de connaissances, j’en suis contente; c’est sage, digne, laudable.”

She closed her lips, the sharp, clipped phonetics of her French fading back into the garden’s acoustic harmony, and resumed the *Gazette*. 

The reader will not too gravely regard the little circumstance that about this time the triply-enclosed packet of five letters temporarily disappeared from my bureau. Blank dismay was naturally my first sensation; but in a moment, I took heart of grace.

“Patience!” whispered I to myself, letting the syllables dissolve into the quiet of my room. “Let me say nothing, but wait peaceably; they will come back again.”

And they did. They had only been on a short visit to Madame’s chamber; having passed their examination, they returned duly and truly. I found them all right the next day, though the ribbon smelled faintly of Madame’s pressed lavender rather than the crisp, clean scent of English paper I so cherished. 

I wonder what she thought of my correspondence? What estimate did she form of Dr. John Bretton’s epistolary powers? In what light did those pithy thoughts, grounded in an easily-flowing style, appear to her? The subjective experience of his humor, native to our shared English dialect, was surely untranslatable, its nuanced warmth lost when filtered through her pragmatic, Francophone sensibilities. Oh, Madame Beck! How seemed these foreign linguistic artifacts to you?

I think in her eyes the five letters found a certain favor. One day after she had *borrowed* them of me, I caught her examining me with a steady, contemplative gaze. It was during that brief space between lessons, when the pupils turned out into the court, and the sudden rush of their voices left the first classe in a resonant, echoing silence. 

“Il y a,” said she, “quelquechose de bien remarquable dans le caractère Anglais.”

“How, Madame?”

She gave a little laugh, repeating the word “how” in English—a momentary yielding of her dominant tongue. 

“Je ne saurais vous dire ‘how;’ mais, enfin, les Anglais ont des idées à eux, en amitié, en amour, en tout. Mais au moins il n’est pas besoin de les surveiller,” she added, getting up and trotting away.

“Then I hope,” murmured I to myself, “you will graciously let alone my letters for the future.”

Alas! Something came rushing into my eyes, dimming the sight of the schoolroom and the bright winter sun, as I remembered that never more would letters such as she had read come to me. The rich harvest had passed. That goodly river, whose waters had been as nourishing to my spirit as a slow-simmered, marrow-rich broth to a starving frame, was bending to another course. It was leaving my little field sand-dry. I loved my Rhine, my Nile; I had almost worshipped my Ganges. I grieved that the grand tide should vanish. Drops streamed fast on my hands, on my desk: I wept one sultry shower, heavy and brief, mourning the sensory deprivation that was to follow.

But soon I said to myself, “The Hope I am bemoaning suffered much: it did not die till it was full time. Following an agony so lingering, death ought to be welcome.”

Welcome I endeavored to make it. In the end, I closed the eyes of my dead, covered its face, and composed its limbs with great calm, like an agrarian elder preparing the earth for a long, fallow winter.

The letters, however, must be put away out of sight, preserved and sealed against the rotting influence of time and prying eyes. 

One vacant Thursday afternoon, going to my treasure to consider its final disposal, I perceived that it had been tampered with again. The ribbon had been untied and retied. My drawer had been visited.

Madame Beck was the soul of discretion, and her knowledge of my casket might be borne. But the idea that she had communicated this information to others shocked me cruelly. I even guessed her confidant. Her kinsman, M. Paul Emanuel, had spent yesterday evening with her. This very morning, in class, that gentleman had favored me with a glance he seemed to have borrowed from Vashti, the actress. The sudden, lurid flash of his angry blue eye had cut through the classroom’s acoustic hum like a harsh dissonance. He was not apt to judge me with tolerance. The thought that these letters had fallen into his hands jarred my very soul.

Where could a key be a safeguard? In the grenier? No. The boxes there were mouldering. Mice made nests amongst the litter, their tiny claws scratching a relentless rhythm against the decayed wood. My dear letters might be consumed by vermin, the ink obliterated by the damp, their physical memory eroded completely.

While pondering this, I sat in the dormitory window-seat. The winter sun gleamed pale on the tops of the garden-shrubs in the *allée défendue*. One great old pear-tree—the nun’s pear-tree, a multi-generational bearer of sweet, heavy fruit—stood up a tall dryad skeleton, grey and gaunt. A thought struck me. I put on my bonnet, cloak, and furs, and went out into the city.

Bending my steps to the old historical quarter, whose hoar and overshadowed precincts I instinctively sought to escape the clattering, commercialized noise of the modern thoroughfares, I wandered until I found a broker’s shop. It was an ancient place, steeped in the olfactory richness of oxidized brass, beeswax, and centuries of undisturbed dust. I purchased a thick glass jar with a hermetic seal, a vessel traditionally used for preserving the autumn harvest through the bitter frosts.

I made a little roll of my letters, wrapped them in oiled silk to repel the damp, bound them with twine, and had the old Jewish broker stopper and seal the jar. He glanced at me suspiciously from under his frost-white eyelashes, an elder custodian of his own fading cultural memory. The transaction brought me a sad, lonely satisfaction, akin to the visceral grounding one feels when pressing seeds into dark soil. With quick walking, I regained the pensionnat just at dark, in time for the evening meal.

At seven o'clock the moon rose. At half-past seven, the house fell into a profound acoustic stillness. I shawled myself, took the sealed jar, and stole out into the *allée défendue*. 

Methusaleh, the ancient pear-tree, stood at the further end of the walk. Though very old, he was of sound timber, with a deep hollow near his root hidden by thick ivy. Here I meditated hiding my treasure. I was not only hiding a treasure; I was burying a grief, interring it in the earth's quiet cellar. 

I cleared away the ivy, the crushed leaves releasing a sharp, green, tannic scent into the chill air. I thrust the jar deep into the hollow. From a nearby tool-shed, I fetched a slate and some mortar left by masons. I covered the hollow, secured the slate with cement, packed it thick with rich, black mould that smelled of generations of fallen leaves, and replaced the ivy. I rested against the tree, lingering like a mourner.

The night air was still, dim with a peculiar luminous haze. The sonic landscape was pristine—only the faintest whisper of the dormant branches. In this mist, there was an electrical quality that acted upon me just as the sweeping aurora borealis had done a year ago in England. I felt strong. 

I was pondering how to break up my winter-quarters—how to leave an encampment where sustenance and sensory forage had failed—when the moon shone out brighter. A shadow became distinct in the obscure alley. I stood about three yards from a tall, sable-robed, snowy-veiled woman. 

Five minutes passed in the breathing silence of the garden. The mythic occurrence did not fracture the domestic rhythm of the night; it was seamlessly integrated into the ancient shadows of the pear tree. I neither fled nor shrieked. I spoke, my voice a quiet intrusion in the mist.

“Who are you? and why do you come to me?”

She stood mute. She had no face, only a white cloth masking her features, but she had eyes, and they viewed me. Desperation filling the post of courage, I advanced one step. I stretched out my hand to touch the fabric of her habit, to feel the physical reality of her presence. She receded. I drew nearer, the rustle of my skirts mingling with the wind, but her silent recession became swift. She vanished behind a mass of full-leaved evergreens, laurel, and dense yew.

“If you have any errand to men, come back and deliver it,” I said. Nothing reappeared. This time there was no Dr. John to whom I dared whisper, “I have again seen the nun.”

Paulina Mary sought my frequent presence in the Rue Crécy. In the old Bretton days, my society had become to her a sort of unconscious necessary. She would urge me now, “Leave the Rue Fossette, and come and live with us. Papa would give you far more than Madame Beck gives you.”

Mr. Home offered me thrice my present salary to be companion to his daughter. I declined. Rather than fill such a post in any great house—reduced to a decorative shadow, stripped of physical purpose and sensory engagement—I would deliberately have taken a housemaid’s place. I would have bought strong gloves, swept wooden stairs, polished iron stoves with black lead, and found profound meaning in the rhythmic, utilitarian labor of maintaining a home. Rather than be a sterile companion, I would have made shirts and starved in peace and independence.

I was no bright lady’s shadow. My qualifications were not convertible; they could not be made the foil of any gem. Madame Beck and I understood each other well. She left me free. Once, returning from a fortnight’s absence and finding no glaring neglect in the house’s routine, she presented gifts to the other teachers but came to my bedside at midnight empty-handed. “One thing, however, I *can* do to please you—leave you alone with your liberty: c’est-ce que je ferai,” she had said. She kept her word, removing every slight shackle. Thus, I found gratification in devoting double pains to the pupils she committed to my charge.

M. de Bassompierre seemed impervious to the fitful beginnings of his daughter’s maturation. He was much taken up with scientific interests, but trustful in ordinary affairs, regarding his “daughterling” as still a child. “Polly” would take his honored, iron-grey head between her little hands, kissing his locks, though she had different moods for different people. With Graham, she was shy, her answers failing of fluency, the natural rhythm of her speech disrupted by his presence.

“My little Polly,” Mr. Home said once, “you really make quite a stranger of Dr. Bretton. Don’t you remember that, as a little girl, you used to be rather partial to him?”

“*Rather*, papa,” echoed she, with her slightly dry, gentle tone.

“Talk away when he is here, and have no fear of him.”

“*He* does not talk much. Is he afraid of me, do you think, papa?”

“Oh, to be sure, what man would not be afraid of such a little silent lady?”

The next day, she tried to keep her word, conversing affably with Dr. John. The attention called a pleasurable glow to his face, though he replied in his softest tones, fearful of disturbing the gossamer happiness hanging in the room’s acoustic space. 

When the Doctor was gone, she approached her father. “Did I keep my word, papa? Did I behave better?”

“My Polly behaved like a queen. Still, Polly, there is a little flutter, a little tendency to stammer now and then, and even to lisp as you lisped when you were six years old.”

“No, papa,” she interrupted indignantly.

“I appeal to Miss Lucy. Did she not, in answering Dr. Bretton’s question, say, ‘yeth,’ she had been there ‘theveral’ times?”

“Papa, you are satirical, you are méchant! But tell me this—do you like him yourself?”

“To be sure: he is a kind-hearted fellow and clever in his profession: yes, the callant is well enough.”

“*Callant*! Ah, Scotchman! Papa, is it the Edinburgh or the Aberdeen accent you have?”

“Both, my pet, both: and doubtless the Glaswegian into the bargain. It is that which enables me to speak French so well: a gude Scots tongue always succeeds well at the French.”

“*The* French! Scotch again: incorrigible papa. You, too, need schooling.”

“Well, Polly, you must persuade Miss Snowe to undertake both you and me; to make you steady and womanly, and me refined and classical.”

The light in which M. de Bassompierre regarded me occasioned much inward edification. What contradictory attributes we find ascribed to us! Madame Beck esteemed me learned; Mr. Home, the pink and pattern of governess-correctness; whilst Professor Paul Emanuel never lost an opportunity of intimating that mine was a fiery, indocile nature. I smiled at them all. 

As I would not be Paulina’s paid companion, she persuaded me to join her in studying the German language. We agreed to take our lessons in the Rue Crécy with the same mistress. It was an arrangement that pleased M. de Bassompierre perfectly, and for me, it offered a new linguistic landscape—a deep, guttural tongue full of untranslatable textures, preserving the complex memory of its people, far removed from the sterile lexicons I sought to escape.
