The final day descended upon Helstone with the weight of a closing parenthesis, sealing the narrative arc of the Hales' residence. The house, once a sanctuary of domestic order, was now a chaotic tableau of departure. Packing cases, stacked like fortifications against the inevitable, lined the halls, while carts rattled incessantly at the front door, hauling the physical remnants of their lives toward the nearest railway station. Even the verdant lawn, usually a testament to the family's stewardship, had been ravaged by the wind; straw drifted through the gaping apertures of open doors and windows, scattering untidiness across the grass. Inside, the atmosphere had shifted perceptibly. The rooms possessed a strange, hollow resonance, as if the air itself had been evacuated of warmth. Light poured through the uncurtained windows with a harsh, unforgiving intensity, casting shadows that seemed unfamiliar, rendering the space alien despite its recent familiarity.

In Mrs. Hale's dressing-room, the final act of preservation played out. Here, amidst the dust motes dancing in the shafts of light, Mrs. Hale and Dixon worked in a rhythm of reluctant intimacy. They packed clothes with mechanical efficiency, yet their movements were frequently interrupted by shared exclamations over forgotten treasures—relics of childhood, small tokens of the children's youth preserved in fabric and bone. They handled these items with a fond, reverent care, turning them over as if reading sacred texts, though their progress was agonizingly slow, weighed down by the gravity of separation.

Downstairs, Margaret stood as an immovable pillar of composure. While the men assisted the cook and Charlotte, she remained a silent observer, ready to counsel or direct whenever needed. The two servants, however, viewed her endurance with a mixture of awe and confusion. Between sobs, they speculated on the young lady's capacity to maintain such equanimity on her last day, concluding that her prolonged absence from Helstone, coupled with her immersion in London society, must have dulled her attachment to the village. They failed to perceive the storm raging beneath her surface. Margaret stood pale and still, her large, grave eyes scanning every corner of the room with hyper-vigilance. She observed the smallest details, cataloging the present circumstances with a precision that bordered on clinical. To anyone else, her heart was visibly breaking, a heavy pressure crushing her chest that no sigh could alleviate. Constant mental exertion was the only shield keeping her from screaming in agony. Yet, she held her ground. Who else could act? Her father was buried in the vestry, surrounded by clerks and ledgers, sorting through papers and registers that required his unique touch to pack correctly. Furthermore, Margaret refused to yield to strangers, let alone to household friends like the cook and Charlotte who were already crumbling.

Eventually, the four packers retreated to the kitchen for tea, leaving Margaret alone in the hallway. She moved stiffly, her steps heavy, drifting from the echo-filled corridor into the drawing-room, emerging into the twilight of an early November evening. A film of soft, dull mist obscured the world outside, lending everything a spectral lilac hue, though nothing was truly hidden. The sun had not yet surrendered completely. Somewhere nearby, a robin sang—a bird her father had often claimed as his winter companion, for whom he had crafted a rustic shelter beside his study window. The garden foliage burned with a final, desperate brilliance; the first frost was imminent, poised to lay the vibrant colors low. Already, amber and golden leaves floated downward, caught in the low, slanting rays of the dying sun.

Margaret wandered along the walk beneath the pear-tree wall, a path she had last traversed at Henry Lennox's side. Here, at this specific bed of thyme, he had once spoken of the things she must not dwell upon. Her gaze lingered on a late-blooming rose as she struggled to formulate a response, her mind snagged on the vivid image of carrot leaves he had described moments before. Only a fortnight ago! How drastically the world had shifted. Where was he now? Likely in London, navigating the same circular routine, dining with the elite of Harley Street or mingling with his youthful peers. Even as she walked through the damp, dreary garden, surrounded by decay and fading light, he might be safely indoors, dismissing his lawbooks after a day of productive toil. He would probably be refreshing himself with a run in the Temple Gardens, immersing himself in the grand, inarticulate roar of thousands of busy men, unseen yet omnipresent, while catching fleeting glimpses of the city lights rising from the river depths. He had often spoken of these hurried walks, snatched between study and supper, describing them during his best moods. Those descriptions had once captivated her imagination, painting a picture of intellectual vitality that now felt like a distant dream.

Here, there was no sound. The robin had vanished into the vast, swallowing stillness of night. Occasionally, a cottage door in the distance clicked shut, admitting a weary laborer to his home, but the sound seemed miles away. Beyond the garden, a stealthy, crunching noise emerged from the crisp fallen leaves of the forest. It was close, almost intrusive. Margaret recognized it instantly: a poacher. Earlier that autumn, sitting in her bedroom with the candle extinguished, she had watched the silent leaps of these intruders over the garden fence, their quick tramps across the dew-laden lawn, their disappearance into the black shadows. Their wild, adventurous freedom had fascinated her; she had wished them success and harbored no fear. But tonight, an irrational terror gripped her. She did not know why.

She heard Charlotte securing the windows, fastening them for the night, unaware that someone had ventured into the garden. Suddenly, a small branch—rotten or snapped by force—crashed heavily down in the nearest part of the woods. Instinct took over. Swift as Camilla, Margaret sprinted to the window and tapped frantically, her tremulous knocking startling Charlotte inside.

"Let me in! Let me in! It is only me, Charlotte!"

Her heart hammered against her ribs until she was safely back in the drawing-room, the windows bolted, the familiar walls enclosing her like a protective shell. She collapsed onto a packing case. The room was cheerless and chilling, stripped of fire and proper lighting, illuminated only by Charlotte's long, unsnuffed candle. Charlotte stared at her with genuine surprise. Margaret felt the woman's gaze more than she saw it, rising slowly from the floor.

"I was afraid you were shutting me out altogether, Charlotte," she said, a faint, half-smile touching her lips. "Then you would never have heard me in the kitchen, and the doors to the lane and churchyard have been locked for hours."

"Oh, miss, I should have been sure to have missed you soon. The men would have wanted you to tell them how to proceed. And I've put tea in Master's study; it's the most comfortable room, so to speak."

"Thank you, Charlotte. You are a kind girl. I shall be sorry to leave you. Try to write to me if I can ever offer you any help or advice. I shall always be glad to receive a letter from Helstone, you know. I will certainly send you my address once I know it."

The study was prepared for tea, a beacon of warmth in the gloom. A roaring fire crackled in the grate, and unlit candles waited on the table. Margaret sank onto the rug, partly to warm herself against the dampness clinging to her dress and the chill born of exhaustion. She balanced herself by clasping her hands tightly around her knees, her head dipping slightly toward her chest. Her posture spoke of despondency, regardless of her internal state.

But when she heard her father's footsteps on the gravel outside, she sprang up. Hastily shaking her heavy black hair back and wiping away tears that had gathered on her cheeks without her conscious permission, she went to open the door.

Mr. Hale displayed a depression far deeper than hers. She struggled to engage him in conversation, forcing herself to speak of topics that might interest him, each attempt costing her a renewed surge of effort she feared would be her last.

"Have you been on a very long walk today?" she asked, noting his refusal to touch any food.

"As far as Fordham Beeches. I went to see Widow Maltby; she is deeply grieved at not having wished you goodbye. She says little Susan has kept watch down the lane for days past."

"Nay, Margaret, what is the matter, dear?"

The thought of the little child waiting endlessly, continually disappointed—not from forgetfulness on her part, but from her sheer inability to leave home—was the final drop in Margaret's cup. She broke down, sobbing as if her heart would literally shatter.

Mr. Hale was distressingly perplexed. He rose and paced nervously up and down the room. Margaret tried to restrain herself, refusing to speak until she could find the strength to do so firmly. She heard him muttering to himself.

"I cannot bear it. I cannot bear to witness the suffering of others. I think I could endure my own with patience. Oh, is there no going back?"

"No, father," Margaret replied, meeting his gaze directly, her voice low and steady. "It is bad to believe you in error. It would be infinitely worse to have known you a hypocrite."

She lowered her voice at the end, as if the mere suggestion of hypocrisy regarding her father tasted of irreverence.
