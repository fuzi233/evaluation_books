We dwell within the walls of a *Schloss* in Styria, a fortress that speaks less of magnificence and more of isolation. In this land, a modest fortune stretches far further than it ever would in England; eight or nine hundred pounds annually secure a life of considerable ease. My father, an Englishman of retirement, purchased this feudal seat and its surrounding estate—a bargain, I am told—upon the strength of a pension and ancestral lands. Here, amidst a landscape where costs are vanishingly low, wealth seems almost superfluous to comfort.

The setting is nothing short of gothic solitude. Our castle sits atop a slight eminence, hemmed in by a dense forest. Before us lies a road, ancient and narrow, crossing a drawbridge that has never seen the need to rise in my lifetime. Below, a moat teems with perch, traversed by swans and carpeted with fleets of white water-lilies. Above the water, the *Schloss* unfolds: a façade of many windows, flanked by towers and crowned by a Gothic chapel.

Before the gate, the woods break into an irregular, moonlit clearing. To the right, a steep stone bridge arches over a stream that cuts a dark, serpentine path through the timber. This is a desolation of the highest order. From the hall door, the forest sprawls fifteen miles to the east and twelve to the west. The nearest living village lies seven miles to the north-west; the closest *Schloss* with any historical weight belongs to the late General Spielsdorf, twenty miles to the south-east.

Yet, I must speak of a ruin closer at hand. Only three miles westward, nestled between the directions of Spielsdorf and our own gate, lies a deserted village. Its quaint church stands roofless, the aisles echoing with the dust of moldering tombs belonging to the Karnsteins—a proud family now extinguished, like their equally forsaken château that looms silently above the silent ruins of the town. Legends whisper of the curse that drove them away, stories I save for another time.

For now, consider the inhabitants of our domain. If we exclude servants and dependents who live in the annexed wings, the count is chillingly small. My father, kind but aging, and I, at nineteen, were the sole family. Eight years have passed since I wrote this account. My mother, a Styrian, died when I was an infant, a void filled only by Madame Perrodon, a Swiss governess from Bern whose benevolent presence became the anchor of my childhood. She formed a trinity with my father and myself at dinner. A fourth figure, Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, a "finishing" governess fluent in both German and French, completed the circle. We spoke a chaotic Babel of tongues—broken English, fractured French, stilted German—which amused travelers but bound us tightly. Occasionally, young ladies of my age visited for seasons, returning the courtesy, but ultimately, my life was a tapestry woven from solitude.

Even with the influence of my governesses, I was a girl accustomed to indulgence, the spoiling of a daughter whose only parent doted upon her. Yet, the shadows of my youth hold a specific, terrifying memory.

It occurred when I was barely six. The nursery, a vast chamber beneath a steep oak ceiling, belonged entirely to me. One night, I awoke to find the room empty. The maid was gone; the nurse was absent. Alone, I felt not fear—for I was sheltered from the terrors of ghosts and fairies—but a sting of neglect. Whimpering rose to a roar, ready to tear through the silence, when I caught sight of a face.

She knelt by the foot of my bed, hands hidden beneath the heavy covers. Her features were solemn yet strangely beautiful. I stared, bewildered, and the noise died in my throat. She reached out, stroking my hair, and laid herself down beside me, drawing me close with a smile. Instantly, the cold knot in my stomach unraveled, and sleep claimed me again.

Then, the sensation struck. Two sharp pricks, like hot needles driven deep into my breast, sent a scream tearing from my lips.

The lady recoiled, her eyes wide and fixed, then slid backward until she vanished beneath the bedframe.

For the first time, true terror seized me. I screamed until my voice broke. The rush of footsteps echoed—the nurse, the maid, the housekeeper. They gathered around, soothing me, dismissing it as a trick of the light. But their faces betrayed them. Pale, drawn, their expressions held a frantic, unnatural anxiety. They peered under the bed, behind the curtains, opened cupboards. The housekeeper leaned close to the nurse, her voice a hushed whisper: *"Lay your hand along that hollow in the bed... Someone *did* lie there, so sure as you did not; the place is still warm."*

They examined my chest, searching for wounds, finding none. Yet, they sat vigil through the night, and from that hour, a servant never slept in the nursery until I turned fourteen.

That night shattered my innocence. A physician arrived, a pale, elderly man with a saturnine visage scarred by smallpox and topped with a chestnut wig. For weeks, he administered bitter medicines I detested.

The following morning, daylight offered no solace. I could not endure being alone, not even for a heartbeat. My father climbed the stairs, standing by my bed with a cheerful, forced brightness. He asked the nurse questions, laughed at her answers, patted my shoulder, and kissed me, insisting it was merely a dream, harmless and fleeting.

*I knew*, however, with a clarity that terrified me, that it was *not* a dream. The chill of her touch, the warmth of the mattress, the pain in my chest—it was real. I was awfully, deeply frightened.

A sliver of comfort came when the nursery maid insisted it was she, that I must have been half-asleep, failing to recognize her. The nurse backed her up, but the doubt lingered, a seed of unease. Later, I recalled a venerable old man in a black cassock, his face sweet and gentle, joining us in prayer. He took my hands, guiding me to whisper, *"Lord, hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus' sake."* I repeated those words for years, a mantra taught by my nurse.

I remember the old man's thoughtful expression, the stark contrast of his white hair against the dim, shadow-laden room, the clumsy furniture frozen in a fashion three centuries past. Light filtered weakly through the small lattices, casting long, dancing shapes across the floor. He knelt with the three women, and his voice, earnest and quavering, rang out for what seemed an eternity.

I cannot recall a single moment prior to that evening, nor the days that followed immediately after. Those scenes stand out vividly, isolated like paintings in a phantasmagoria surrounded by absolute darkness. That night marked the beginning of something else, something that would soon turn the quiet solitude of the *Schloss* into a nightmare.
