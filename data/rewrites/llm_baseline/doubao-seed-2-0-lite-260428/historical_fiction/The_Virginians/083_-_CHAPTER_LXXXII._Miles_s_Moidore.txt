Our little Miles drew his first breath scarce a handful of days after the royal Prince George, who would one day hold command over the regiment the boy would one day join. Across London, the birth of the royal heir was marked by blazing street illuminations and thundering cannon volleys, while crowds of gawkers thronged St James’s Palace to peek at the princely infant laid behind gilded railings, dandled under the watch of high-born court nurses.

No crowds, no bowing courtiers lined the threshold of our small Lambeth lodgings to greet our own little prince. The only faithful retainers who doted on him were our devoted servant Gumbo and sharp, warm-hearted Molly, who loved and admired the tiny heir to our straitened household with a loyalty no court flatterer could equal.

We might have named him George, for the new royal paragon and for his own father, as was custom. But we chose instead Miles, for a small, scapegrace member of our Warrington line: the single distant cousin of my father’s blood who had shown me true kindness at a time when every other kinsman had turned their back. Theo and I had agreed the moment we knew we were expecting that our son would bear that name, in tribute to that long-forgotten act of gentle generosity. We sent word of the birth to my family across the Atlantic, and boldly printed his notice in the *Daily Advertiser*, marking our small home in Church Street plain for every London gossip to see.

Aunt Bernstein’s reply came swift, sharp-edged as ever but warm under its briskness: “My dear boy, whatever possessed you to broadcast to all the world that you are tucked away in such a hovel, where you have chosen to bury yourself? I kiss the little new mother, and send a keepsake for the child.” That keepsake was a fine silk coverlet edged with lace so delicate it might have graced a royal cradle. The gold spent on that lace would have fed our household for a full month, but Theo and Molly clapped their hands over the gift, and our son’s cradle was draped as fine as any nobleman’s babe in Mayfair.

Good Dr. Heberden called often to check on Theo, and recommended a skilled local surgeon in the neighbourhood to tend her lying-in, though she needed very little extra care beyond the attention our landlady and Molly lavished on her. Soon after, the gilded, gleaming chariot of Lady Castlewood rolled up our rickety lane, drawing stares from every passing street vendor. She brought a pot of jellied sweetmeat, she said, certain Theo would crave it — no doubt leftover from one of her own grand banquets, served to dozens of fine guests the week before. She chattered nonstop about court ceremonies, the feasts and masquerades thrown to celebrate the royal heir’s birth, all the glittering splendour that surrounded that other little prince across the city.

Mr. Samuel Johnson happened to pay me a call that very same hour, just as Lady Castlewood was stepping down from her chariot. He bowed to her with that heavy, lumbering courtesy of his, far more earnest than graceful. She greeted me as “cousin” with great affability, and even helped spoon the jelly out of her polished silver serving dish into our chipped earthen pan. The good Doctor took a full spoonful, smacked his lips, and pronounced it excellent. “The great of the world are fortunate in all things,” he rumbled. “They can hire the most skilful cooks, just as they gather the wittiest men around their tables. If this sweetmeat, as its well-worn dish suggests, has already done service at my lord’s table, it has kept fine company. It has trembled under the gaze of celebrated beauties, been sipped by ruby lips, divided the attention of a whole room of distinguished guests alongside tarts, creams, and fruits that were no doubt as delicious as itself.” He proceeded to eat a good half of the pot before anyone could stop him. I am bound to add, though, that Theo took one small taste later and pushed the dish away, declaring it not at all to her liking, and Molly tossed her head and announced plainly that the jelly had turned mouldy sitting unused on a nobleman’s shelf for three days.

Our little Miles had the rare honour of an earl’s daughter for his godmother: kind Lady Maria, who had long lost her own solitary chance at motherhood. Her attention to Theo and the new infant was beyond all praise; she yearned over our small boy so tenderly that it brought tears to even Gumbo’s eyes, and many of the fine little linen garments he wore in his earliest days had been stitched and laid aside for the child she would never hold. Years later, when Captain Miles rode with the Prince’s Hussars in a uniform blazing with gold braid, as fine a fashion plate as any in the regiment, I hope he remembers that quiet, gentle kindness that surrounded his cradle.

Sampson Hagan christened him in the very same Southwark chapel where Theo and I had been wed. No man ever read the words of the Prayer Book more beautifully or more movingly than he that day — until he reached the final blessing, and his voice cracked, and every last one of us in that tiny congregation was dabbing at our eyes. “Mr. Garrick himself could not have read those words half so nobly,” Hagan said afterwards. “No innocent babe ever entered the world carried on more tender, sincere wishes than our little Miles.”

I have not yet told how Miles came by his name, when we had fully intended, two days before the christening, to name him George for his father. We heard the patter of tiny pony hooves clattering up our cobbled lane, our gate bell jangled, and there was young Cousin Miles, dusty from the long ride, who I suspect had snuck off from his parents’ house that day in open disobedience. “You know Cousin Harry gave me my little pony,” he panted, red-cheeked and breathless, “and I can’t help but love you — you look so much like Harry, and they are always saying unkind things about you at home, and it’s not fair. I brought my whistle and coral that my godmother Lady Suckling gave me, for your little boy. And if you are really as poor as they say, Cousin George, here is my gold moidore. It is worth a great deal, and it’s no use to me anyway, since I am never allowed to spend it.”

We carried the boy up to Theo’s bedchamber, his scuffed little riding boots (of which he was inordinately proud) thumping on every stair. Theo kissed his grubby cheek and thanked him, and that same gold moidore has rested in her purse from that day to this.

My mother Madam Esmond wrote to me soon after, full of royal surprise and displeasure that I had dared christen my son Miles — a name unheard of in the proud, ancient Esmond line. I did not explain the reason at the time, but when, in after years, I told her the story of that small boy galloping all the way to Lambeth on his pony to give our babe his most precious treasure, I saw a tear roll down her wrinkled old cheek. I heard afterwards that she sent for Gumbo half a dozen times, to ask every last detail about the little cousin who gave his name to our Miles — our Miles Gloriosus, the future toast of Pall Mall, Valenciennes, Almack’s, and Brighton.
