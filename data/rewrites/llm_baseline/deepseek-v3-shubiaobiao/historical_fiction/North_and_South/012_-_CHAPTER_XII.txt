Mr. Thornton had spent considerable effort persuading his mother to extend civility to the Hales. Mrs. Thornton was not one for casual visits, and when she did call upon others, she did so with solemnity, as though performing a duty. Her son had gifted her a carriage, but she refused to keep horses for it, preferring to hire them only for formal occasions. She had exhausted her social obligations not a fortnight prior, having paid visits to every acquaintance in Milton—leaving them now to reciprocate at their own expense.

Yet Crampton was too distant to walk, and she had repeatedly questioned whether her son’s insistence on calling upon the Hales justified the cost of a cab. Privately, she hoped it did not. "I see no sense in befriending every instructor and tradesman in Milton," she had remarked. "Next, you’ll expect me to call on Fanny’s dancing-master’s wife!"

"And so I would," John replied, unyielding, "if they were as friendless and displaced as the Hales."

"Oh, there’s no need for haste. I shall go tomorrow. I only wished you to understand the matter fully."

"If you go tomorrow, I shall order the horses."

"John, don’t be absurd. One would think you had money to burn."

"Not yet. But I will not have you jolted about in a cab again. The last time left you with a headache."

"I never complained."

"No, you never do," he said, pride softening his tone. "Which is why I must watch over you. Fanny, on the other hand, could do with a little hardship."

"She is not made of your stern material, John. She could not bear it."

Mrs. Thornton fell silent. The remark touched upon an old wound—Fanny’s fragility, so unlike the resilience of her mother and brother. Mrs. Thornton was not given to introspection, but she knew, instinctively, that Fanny would never endure adversity with fortitude. The knowledge pained her, yet it bred a gentler manner toward her daughter—the sort reserved for the sickly or weak.

A stranger might have mistaken her indulgence for greater affection toward Fanny than John. But such an observer would have been profoundly wrong. The blunt honesty between mother and son revealed a mutual trust, a bedrock of understanding. Her tenderness toward Fanny, laced with unease, betrayed no such security. She never called John by endearments—those were reserved for Fanny. Yet her pride in him was boundless, her gratitude silent but constant.

---

"Fanny, dear," Mrs. Thornton announced, "we shall take the carriage today to call on the Hales. You might visit Nurse while I am with Mrs. Hale—it is in the same direction."

"Oh, Mamma, it’s such a long way, and I am dreadfully tired."

"From what?" Mrs. Thornton’s brow furrowed.

"The weather, I think. It’s so enervating. Couldn’t Nurse come here instead? The carriage could fetch her."

Mrs. Thornton set down her sewing, considering. "It is a long walk back for her in the evening."

"I’ll send her home in a cab," Fanny said airily.

At that moment, Mr. Thornton entered, pausing before departing for the mill.

"Mother," he said, "if there is anything that might comfort Mrs. Hale in her illness, you will offer it, I trust?"

"If I can discern what she needs. I’ve little experience with invalids."

"Fanny might advise—she is seldom without some complaint. Isn’t that so, Fan?"

"I am not *always* ill," Fanny retorted. "And I shan’t be going. I have a headache."

John’s expression darkened. His mother remained bent over her stitching.

"Fanny," he said, in a tone that brooked no argument, "you will go. It will do you good. Consider it a favor to me."

He left before she could protest further. Had he stayed, she would have dissolved into tears at his severity. As it was, she muttered, "John always speaks as though I invent my ailments. Who *are* these Hales, that he makes such a fuss over them?"

"Fanny, do not speak so of your brother. He has his reasons. Now ready yourself."

Yet the exchange did little to endear the Hales to Mrs. Thornton. A quiet resentment simmered—*Who are they, that we must pay them such attention?* The question lingered long after Fanny had forgotten it, preoccupied with the effect of her new bonnet in the mirror.

---

Mrs. Thornton was not at ease in unfamiliar company. Society, to her, was a matter of dinners given and judged—not idle calls upon strangers. As she entered the Hales’ modest drawing room, her bearing was more rigid than usual, her expression forbidding.

Margaret sat embroidering cambric for Edith’s unborn child—work Mrs. Thornton privately deemed frivolous. The room was cluttered with trinkets that surely demanded excessive dusting. Time, to a woman of limited means, was money.

Her conversation with Mrs. Hale was stilted, a recitation of pleasantries. Yet when Mrs. Hale admired the antique lace at Mrs. Thornton’s collar—a rare heirloom—the visit warmed slightly.

Meanwhile, Margaret struggled to converse with Fanny.

"You have no piano," Fanny observed. "I suppose you are not musical."

"I enjoy listening, but I play poorly. My parents care little for music, so we sold ours when we moved."

"How can you bear it? A piano is nearly a necessity."

*Fifteen shillings a week, and three saved,* Margaret thought wryly. Aloud, she said, "We have concerts here, I hear."

"Oh, yes! Though too crowded. One hears the newest music, at least. I always order the latest pieces afterward."

"Do you like new music simply because it is new?"

"If it is fashionable in London, it must be worth hearing. You’ve lived there, I suppose?"

"Yes."

"How I long to see London! And the Alhambra!"

"The Alhambra?"

"From *Tales of the Alhambra*. You’ve not read it?"

Margaret admitted she had not.

Fanny sighed. "Mamma has never been to London herself. She adores Milton, smoky and grim as it is."

Margaret replied evenly, "If it has long been her home, it is natural she should love it."

Mrs. Thornton’s voice cut in. "What are you saying of me, Miss Hale?"

Caught off guard, Margaret hesitated. Fanny answered instead: "Only wondering why you are so fond of Milton, Mamma."

"It requires no explanation," Mrs. Thornton said coolly. "One does not apologize for affection toward one’s birthplace."

Margaret flushed, vexed. The exchange had been misconstrued, yet Mrs. Thornton’s pride was needlessly sharp.

"You have seen little of Milton’s industry, I gather?" Mrs. Thornton pressed.

"No," Margaret admitted. Then, truthfully: "I have no great interest in factories."

Mrs. Hale interjected with a memory of a ruined lilac gown during a candle-making tour.

Mrs. Thornton’s lips thinned. "I merely thought, as newcomers, you might wish to understand what elevates Milton. My son’s mill, for instance, showcases the finest machinery in the kingdom. Should you ever condescend to visit, I would arrange it."

As they departed, Fanny whispered, "I’m glad you dislike mills. They are so dreary."

Margaret answered quietly, "I think I should like to understand them, if I were you."

In the carriage, Mrs. Thornton spoke firmly. "We shall be civil to the Hales, Fanny, but do not grow attached to the daughter. She will do you no good. The mother is ill and seems harmless enough."

Fanny pouted. "I’ve no desire to befriend her. I only tried to amuse her."

"At least John will be satisfied," Mrs. Thornton murmured, though her thoughts remained uneasy.
