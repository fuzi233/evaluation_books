"We must be vigilant," he said, his voice tight with a dread that vibrated through the quiet room. "Or she will outwit us once more. She would deceive even the chosen!"

"And how do we keep guard?" I asked, my mind racing to find a logical defense.

"Every conceivable way," he answered, pacing slowly. "She fears, and thus she hates her own creation. She is here, in this house, on a mission to destroy what she made. To her, the birth of children is the death of the parents; every new generation is an enemy to the last. Her daughter appears to her as a gaping chasm through which her immortality—something she believes is inherent to her own soul—is rapidly draining away. To fill that void, she has pursued her offspring with absolute enmity since birth. Yet, the result of her schemes is a colony of children in her claimed region, led by that daughter, who serves as their heart, their head, and their shelter. My Eve longed for the child, wishing to be a mother to her firstborn, but we were unfit to raise her then. She was carried into the wilderness, and for ages, we knew nothing of her fate. But she was divinely fostered, playing with young angels, untouched by care until she found a baby in the woods. Then, the mother within her awoke. Since then, she has found many children, one by one, and that heart is not yet full. Her family is her consuming charge; she has never mothered children more devotedly. Her authority over them is absolute, though unknown to herself, surfacing only in watchful service. She has forgotten the time before them, believing she emerged from the wood as the first of the family."

"You have saved the life of her and their enemy," he continued, his gaze locking onto mine. "Therefore, your life now belongs to them. The princess was on her way to destroy them, but as she crossed that stream, vengeance overtook her. She would have died had you not intervened. You did. Had you not, she would now be raging among the Little Ones, daring to cross the stream again. But there remained a path to the blessed colony through the world of three dimensions. By slaying her former body, she had excluded herself from it; she could not re-enter except through personal contact with someone belonging to that realm. You provided the opportunity. Never in her long years had she had one like it. Her hand, with the lightest touch, rested on one or both of your muffled feet with every step you climbed. In that small chamber, she watches now, waiting for the slightest chance to leave."

"She cannot know anything about the door!" I protested, though my heart trembled beneath my words. "She cannot at least know how to open it!"

"Hush!" the librarian whispered, raising a hand to silence me. "She can hear through anything. You must go at once to my wife's cottage. I will remain to guard her."

"Let me go to the Little Ones!" I cried, desperation rising in my throat.

"Beware, Mr. Vane. Go to my wife, and do as she commands."

His advice offered no comfort. Why hasten to meet measureless delay? If not to protect the children, why go at all? Even now, I believed him only enough to ask questions, not to obey.

"Tell me first, Mr. Raven," I demanded, "why, of all places, you have shut her up there! The night I fled your house, I ran straight into that closet!"

"The closet is no nearer our cottage, nor farther, than any other place."

"But," I countered, struggling against the illogical nature of his words, "how is it that, when you choose, you take from that same door a whole book where I saw and felt only a part? The other part, you just told me, protrudes into your library. When you return it to the shelf, will it not again stick through into that space? Must not the two places, where parts of the same volume exist simultaneously, lie close together? Or can one part of a book be in space, somewhere, while the other is out of space, nowhere?"

"I am sorry I cannot explain it to you," he answered gently. "There is no provision in you for understanding it. Not merely is the phenomenon inexplicable to you, but its very nature is inapprehensible. Indeed, I only partially grasp it myself. Yet, you constantly experience things you do not, and cannot, understand. You think you understand them, but your comprehension is merely habit; you are not surprised because you are used to them. You accept them not because you understand, but because they are there, bound to you by unavoidable relations. The truth is, no man truly understands anything. When he knows he does not understand, that is his first stumbling step—not toward understanding, but toward the capacity to eventually understand. To such mysteries, you are not accustomed, so you do not pretend to understand. Neither I nor any man can help you comprehend them here, but I may perhaps help you believe."

He moved to the closet door, gave a low whistle, and stood listening. A moment later, I heard—or seemed to hear—a soft whir of wings. Looking up, I saw a white dove perch for an instant atop the shelves above the portrait, then drop to Mr. Raven's shoulder, resting its head against his cheek. Only the motion of their heads revealed they were conversing; I heard nothing. I did not move my eyes from them when suddenly the bird was gone, and Mr. Raven returned to his seat.

"Why did you whistle?" I asked. "Sound here is not sound there!"

"You are right," he replied. "I whistled so you would know I called her. Not the whistle itself, but what the whistle meant reached her. There is not a minute to lose. You must go!"

"I will at once!" I replied, moving for the door.

"You will sleep tonight at my hostelry!" he said, not as a question, but with mild authority.

"My heart is with the children," I replied. "But if you insist—"

"I do insist. You can achieve nothing else. I will accompany you as far as the mirror and see you off."

He rose. Suddenly, a shock rippled through the closet. Apparently, the leopardess had thrown herself against the heavy door. I looked at my companion.

"Come; come!" he urged.

Before we reached the library door, a howling yell followed us, mingled with the screech of claws scoring the hard oak. I hesitated, turning halfway.

"To think of her lying there alone," I murmured, "with that terrible wound!"

"Nothing will ever close that wound," he answered, sighing deeply. "It must eat into her heart! Annihilation itself is no death to evil. Only good where evil was can kill evil. An evil thing must live with its evil until it chooses to be good. That alone is the slaying of evil."

I held my peace until a sound I did not understand overtook us.

"If she breaks loose!" I cried.

"Make haste!" he rejoined. "I shall hurry down the moment you are gone. I have already disarranged the mirrors."

We ran, reaching the wooden chamber breathless. Mr. Raven seized the chains and adjusted the hood. Then he set the mirrors in their proper relation and stepped beside me in front of the standing one. Already, I saw the mountain range emerging from the mist.

Between us, wedging us apart, darted the huge bulk of the spotted leopardess, yelping like a demon. She leaped through the mirror as through an open window and settled instantly into a low, swift gallop.

I cast a look of dismay at my companion and sprang through to follow her. He trailed behind me leisurely.

"You need not run," he called out. "You cannot overtake her. This is our way."

As he spoke, he turned in the opposite direction.

"She has more magic at her fingertips than I care to know!" he added quietly.

"We must do what we can!" I said, running on, but stopping as I saw her dwindle in the distance. I turned back to him.

"Doubtless we must," he answered. "But my wife has warned Mara, and she will do her part. You must sleep first: you have given me your word!"

"Nor do I mean to break it. But surely sleep is not the first thing! Surely, action takes precedence over repose!"

"A man can do nothing he is not fit to do. See! Did I not tell you Mara would do her part?"

I looked where he pointed and saw a white spot moving at an acute angle to the line taken by the leopardess.

"There she is!" he cried. "The spotted leopardess is strong, but the white is stronger!"

"I have seen them fight; the combat did not appear decisive regarding strength."

"How should such eyes tell which have never slept? The princess did not confess herself beaten—that she never does—but she fled! When she confesses her last hope gone, when it is indeed hard to kick against the goad, then will her day begin to dawn! Come; come! He who cannot act must make haste to sleep!"
