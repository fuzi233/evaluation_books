When the first shock of meeting had spent itself, the little maiden fell upon him at once, as a kitchen-wench might fall upon a careless scullion who has let the cream boil over and spoiled the whole day’s making.

“So you are come at last to ask after Theo,” cried Miss Hetty, her voice sharp as vinegar upon hot iron, “and you feel sorry your neglect has made her so ill? For six weeks she has been unwell, and you have never asked a word about her! Very kind of you, Mr. George—mighty kind!”

“Kind!” Warrington gasped, as if the word had been thrust into his ribs. “Do you call it kind to be with her every day and all day for a year, and then to be turned out without so much as a syllable?”

“My dear, you know my promise to your father,” I put in, for I had been the messenger between these fires, and felt them burning through my coat.

“Promise!” Miss Hetty cried, with a shrug that made her little shoulders rise like a cat’s when it scents a dog. “A fine promise indeed—to make my darling ill, and then, one fine day, to say ‘Good-bye, Theo,’ and walk away for ever. I suppose gentlemen make promises because they wish to keep ’em. I wouldn’t trifle with a poor child’s heart and leave her afterwards, if I were a man. Pray, sir, by what right do you take her away from all of us, and then desert her, because an old woman in America don’t approve of her? She was happy with us before you came. She loved her sister—there never was such a sister—until she saw you. And now, because your mamma thinks her young gentleman might do better, you must leave her forsooth!”

“Great powers, child!” I cried, for her reasoning flew about the room like sparks in a draught, catching wherever there was tinder. “Was it I that drew back? Am I not forbidden your house? Did not your father require, upon my honour, that I should not see her?”

“Honour!” Hetty flung the word as if it were a bone with no meat upon it. “And you are the men who pretend to be our superiors; and it is we who are to respect you and admire you! I declare, George Warrington, you ought to go back to your schoolroom in Virginia again; have your black nurse to tuck you up in bed, and ask leave from your mamma when you might walk out. Oh, George! I little thought my sister was giving her heart away to a man who hadn’t the spirit to stand by her; but, at the first difficulty, left her!”

She ran on, in that way where anger makes a child older than her years, and grief makes her younger again in the same breath.

“When Doctor Heberden said he was attending you, I determined to come and see you, and you do look very ill—which I am glad to see; and I suppose it’s your mother you are frightened of. But I shan’t tell Theo you are unwell. She hasn’t left off caring for you. She can’t walk out of a room, break her solemn engagements, and go into the world the next day as if nothing had happened! That is left for men—our superiors in courage and wisdom; and to desert an angel—yes, an angel ten thousand times too good for you; an angel who used to love me till she saw you, and who was the blessing of life and of all of us—is what you call honour? Don’t tell me, sir! I despise you all! You are our betters, are you? We are to worship and wait on you, I suppose? I don’t care about your wit, and your tragedies, and your verses; and I think they are often very stupid. I won’t set up of nights copying your manuscripts, nor watch hour after hour at a window wasting my time and neglecting everybody because I want to see your worship walk down the street with your hat cocked!”

At that, I confess, I felt each thrust. For we men take services as naturally as we take bread—break it, dip it, swallow it, and seldom ask who kneaded it, who kept the coals alive, who carried the water that made the dough.

“If you are going away—and welcome—give me back my sister, I say!” she cried. “Give me back my darling of old days, who loved every one of us till she saw you. And you leave her because your mamma thinks she can find somebody richer for you! Oh, you brave gentleman! Go and marry the person your mother chooses, and let my dear die here deserted!”

“Great heavens, Hetty!” I cried. “Is it I who wish to leave your sister? Did I not offer to keep my promise, and was it not your father who refused me, and made me promise never to try and see her again? What have I but my word and my honour?”

“Honour indeed! You keep your word to him, and you break it to her! Pretty honour! If I were a man I would soon let you know what I thought of your honour! Only I forgot—you are bound to keep the peace and mustn’t—Oh, George, George! Don’t you see the grief I am in? I am distracted, and scarce know what I say. You must not leave my darling. They don’t know it at home. They don’t think so; but I know her best of all, and she will die if you leave her. Say you won’t! Have pity upon me, Mr. Warrington, and give me my dearest back!”

Thus she ran from scorn to entreaty, from a lash to tears—like a cook, poor soul, who cannot choose whether to beat the cream harder or to cover the pan and weep, because the guest she labours for will not eat.

Was my little doctor right in what he had hinted of his patient? Was there truly no remedy but the one Hetty cried for? Have not others endured that cruel pain of being cut from a love, suffered fever after it, lain as if death were the only broth left in the pot—and yet recovered, and afterwards hobbled through life upon a changed limb? Love is selfish; it hears its own pulse louder than any other sound. And ours, being so fierce and particular to ourselves, seemed a thing no other heart had ever borne.

Yet here, in Hetty’s very pleadings, was proof of a different suffering—one which the lovers, in their triumph and tender confidences, had been blind to. Theo and I, comfortable by our own hearth of happiness, had often spoken of Hetty’s attachment as of a storm seen safely from indoors; a ship labouring at sea while we walked by a calm river-bank. We drew, perhaps, a secret security from another’s mischance. Be the truth as it may: at the time I was eager enough to share Hetty’s terrors. I chose to believe, willingly, that the life dearest to me would be sacrificed if sundered from mine. Was I wrong? Of myself I may doubt now—indeed I know my own frailty well—but of her, never.

I would have poured all this out to our doctor; but the good gentleman clapped a figurative hand upon my mouth, as if he feared the very syllables might be overheard by Fate.

“Hush,” says he, with a comical look of fright. “I must hear none of this. If two people who happen to know each other chance to meet and talk in my patient’s room, I cannot help myself; but as for match-making and love-making, I am your humble servant! What will the General do when he comes back to town? He will have me behind Montagu House as sure as I am a live doctor; and alive I wish to remain, my good sir!”

And with that, he skipped into his carriage and was gone, leaving his wheels to rattle away down the stones like dice flung upon a table, whilst I stood in the aftermath of Hetty’s storm and felt the air strangely thin.

“And you and Miss Hetty must have no meetings here again, mind you that,” he had said previously.

Oh no. Certainly not. We were gentlemen of honour: our word was our word. Besides, had I not been granted an inestimable boon—one drop of water to a man in a desert? I was so refreshed by the sight of Hetty’s honest passion, by her very reproaches, that I thought I could endure for ever so long now upon that single draught.

I walked away with her towards Soho, and never once thought—no, not once—of contriving another meeting.

But the little emissary had contrivance in her bones, as housewives have it when they must feed a family upon scant flour and still keep every mouth believing the loaf is hearty.

She asked me if I went to the Museum now to read.

“Oh yes, sometimes, my dear,” I said; “but I am too wretched for reading now; I cannot see what is on the paper. I do not care about my books. Even Pocahontas is wearisome to me. I—”

“Nonsense!” she cried, stamping her little foot, so that her shoe gave a crisp report on the pavement, a tiny gunshot in the city’s continual cannonade of carts and cries. “Why, I declare, George, you are more stupid than Harry!”

“How do you mean, my dear child?” I asked, for I was indeed stupid—love makes a man deaf to everything but its own miserable humming.

“When do you go? You go away at three o’clock. You strike across on the road to Tottenham Court. You walk through the village, and return by the Green Lane that leads back towards the new hospital. You know you do! If you walk for a week there, it can’t do you any harm. Good morning, sir! You’ll please not follow me any farther.”

And she dropped me a curtsey and walked away, her veil over her face, as if she were hiding not from me but from the noise and notice of the world.

That Green Lane—built all over with houses now—was in my time, when good old George II still sat king, a shabby rural outlet of London, dangerous enough that City folk returning from their Hampstead villas would come in parties at night, with waiters bearing lanthorns, for fear of footpads that haunted the outskirts. The very sound of the place was a kind of warning: the wind worrying the hedge, the clop of a solitary horse suddenly checked, an owl’s complaint from a tree, and then—too often—quick feet upon the soft verge and a voice that did not ask but ordered.

Hampstead and Highgate churches, each crowning its hill, filled up the background when you turned your back on London; and for one, two, three days, Mr. George Warrington had the pleasure of looking upon that landscape and walking back in the direction of the new hospital, as punctually as if Hetty’s words were a recipe and he dared not alter an ounce.

Along the lane were small houses of entertainment. At one place, where they sold cakes and beer, at the sign of the Protestant Hero, a decent woman smiled at me on the third or fourth day and curtseyed in her clean apron.

“It appears the lady don’t come, sir!” she said, with that practical pity which feeds you instead of consoling you. “Your honour had best step in, and take a can of my cool beer.”

Even in my wretchedness I noted the smell: yeasty froth, damp wood, and those warm, carraway-scented cakes that poor folk buy for a holiday, the spice doing its best to make coarse flour feel grand.

At length, as I was coming back through Tottenham Road, on the five-and-twentieth of May—O day to be marked with the whitest stone!—a little way beyond Mr. Whitefield’s Tabernacle, I saw a landau before me; and on the box-seat by the driver sat my young friend Charley, who waved his hat and called out, “George! George!”

I ran up to the carriage, my knees knocking together so that I thought I should fall under the wheel; and inside I saw Hetty, and by her—propped with a pillow—my dearest Theo.

How thin the little hand had grown since last it lay in mine! The cheeks were flushed and wasted; the eyes strangely bright; and the thrill of her voice, when she spoke a word or two, smote me with a pang in which grief and joy were so kneaded together that I could not separate them—like salt stirred into honey until neither is itself.

“I am taking her an airing to Hampstead,” said Hetty, demure as a nun who has contrived a feast beneath her cloak. “The doctor says the air will do her good.”

“I have been ill,” Theo whispered, “but I am better now, George.”

At that moment there came a great burst of music from the people in the chapel hard by. The psalm poured out as if the doors had opened on a sea of voices—men’s basses steady as a mill-wheel, women’s tones bright as larks, children piping where they could, the whole hymn riding above the street’s harsh clatter and turning it, for an instant, into accompaniment instead of assault. In a city where every trade advertises itself by hammer and bell, by hawker’s cry and coachman’s oath, that swell of sacred sound was like a clean current of water running through a guttered lane.

I held her hand in mine. Her eyes were looking into mine once more. It seemed as if we had never been parted.

I can never forget the tune of that psalm. I have heard it all through my life. My wife has touched it on her harpsichord, and her little ones have warbled it. Now you understand, young people, why I love it so: because ’twas the music played at our amoris redintegratio—our love made whole again. Because it sang hope to me at the most miserable period of my existence. Yes, the most miserable; for even that dreary confinement of Duquesne had its tendernesses and kindly associations—poor Biche, my tipsy jailor, the reveille of forest birds, and the military music of my prison—sounds that belonged to their place as naturally as bread belongs to the oven. But this misery was soundless save for my own blood in my ears—until that hymn broke upon it.

Master Charley looked down from his box-seat upon his sister and me, caught in our beatific contemplation, and upon Hetty too, listening to the music with a face suddenly softened.

“I think I should like to go and hear it,” Hetty said. “And that famous Mr. Whitfield—perhaps he is going to preach this very day! Come in with me, Charley—and George can drive for half an hour with dear Theo towards Hampstead and back.”

Charley showed no strong desire for devotional exercises and proposed that George Warrington should take Hetty in; but Hetty was not to be denied.

“I will never help you in another exercise as long as you live, sir,” cried Miss Hetty, “if you don’t come on,”—and the youth clambered down, and they entered the temple together, swallowed up by that tide of singing.

Can any moralist, bearing my previous promises in mind, excuse me for jumping into the carriage and sitting down once more by my dearest Theo?

Suppose I did break them—will he blame me much? Reverend sir, you are welcome. I broke my promise; and if you would not do as much, good friend, you are welcome to your virtue. Not that I for a moment suspect my own children will ever be so bold as to think of having hearts of their own, and bestowing them according to their liking. No, my young people: you will let papa choose for you; be hungry when he tells you; be thirsty when he orders; and settle your children’s marriages afterwards.

And now, of course, you are anxious to hear what took place when papa jumped into the landau by the side of poor little mamma, propped up by her pillows.

“I am come to your part of the story, my dear,” say I, looking over to my wife, as she plies her needles—each small prick and pull as steady as a metronome, a domestic music answering the remembered psalm.

“To what, pray?” says my lady. “You should skip all that part, and come to the grand battles, and your heroic defence of—”

“Of Fort Fiddlededee in the year 1778, when I pulled off Mr. Washington’s epaulet, gouged General Gates’s eye, cut off Charles Lee’s head, and pasted it on again!”

“Let us hear all about the fighting,” cry the boys. Even the Captain condescends to own he will listen to any military details, though only from a militia officer.

“Fair and softly, young people! Everything in its turn. I am not yet arrived at the war. I am only a young gentleman, just stepping into a landau, by the side of a young lady whom I promised to avoid. I am taking her hand—which, after a little ado, she leaves in mine. Do you remember how hot it was, the little thing; how it trembled; how it throbbed and jumped a hundred and twenty in a minute? And as we trot on towards Hampstead, I address Miss Lambert in the following terms—”

“Ah, ah, ah!” cry the girls in a chorus with mademoiselle, their French governess, who adds, “Nous écoutons maintenant. La parole est à vous, Monsieur le Chevalier!”

So there they are, all in a circle: mamma at her side of the fire, papa at his; Mademoiselle Éléonore, at whom the Captain looks rather sweetly (eyes off, Captain!); the two girls listening like—like nymphas discentes to Apollo, let us say; and John and Tummas, with their obtuse ears, bringing in the tea-trays and urns, the steam rising with that homely fragrance of boiled water meeting leaf—comfort in a scent, memory in a breath.

“Very good,” says the Squire, pulling out the manuscript and waving it before him. “We are going to tell your mother’s secrets and mine.”

“I am sure you may, papa,” cries the house-matron. “There’s nothing to be ashamed of.” And a blush rises over her kind face.

“But before I begin, young folks, permit me two or three questions.”

“Allons, toujours des questions!” says mademoiselle, with a shrug of her pretty shoulders. (Florac has recommended her to us; and I suspect the little Chevalier has himself an eye upon this pretty Mademoiselle de Blois.)

To the questions, then.
