The air in the van der Luydens' dining room was thick with the scent of roasted meat and the heavy, unspoken weight of social obligation. Black walnut tables held plates that gleamed under electric light, reflecting faces that performed the rituals of propriety with mechanical precision. Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden had descended from Skuytercliff, fleeing the scandal of Beaufort's failure, only to find themselves trapped in the very city they sought to avoid. Their presence was a duty owed to society, a performance of stability in a crumbling theater.

"You must stand in the breach, Louisa," Mrs. van der Luyden instructed, her voice smooth as polished mahogany. "People like Mrs. Struthers cannot be allowed to step into Regina's shoes. Newcomers always push in when the foundation cracks."

They ate in silence, the clink of cutlery the only rhythm breaking the monotony. Four guests sat around the table: the hosts, Mrs. Archer, Newland, and his wife, May. The menu was rigid, the timing exact, designed to mask the anxiety beneath the surface. Dinner concluded precisely at seven, allowing the gentlemen to retreat to their cigars while the women engaged in the delicate art of conversation.

The topic of the evening was inevitable. The failure of the Beauforts hung over the room like a storm cloud, dissected by Mrs. van der Luyden with the clinical detachment of a surgeon. Her gaze shifted, finally resting on May Archer.

"Is it true, dear?" she asked, avoiding the offending lady's name. "That Madame Olenska's carriage was seen at Mrs. Beaufort's door?"

May's color flushed, a brief betrayal of composure. Mrs. Archer interjected quickly, defending her daughter-in-law's character, but the damage was done. The implication lingered: a foreigner, untethered from American norms, visiting a defaulter.

"The standard at the Tuileries was excessively lax," Mr. Sillerton Jackson offered, seizing the opening with a reminiscent smile. "One wonders where Morny found his funds, or who bailed out the court beauties..."

Mrs. Archer shot him a sharp look. "Are you suggesting we adopt such standards?"

"I never suggest," Jackson replied, impervious to the tension. "But perhaps Madame Olenska's upbringing makes her less particular."

The sighs that followed were audible, a collective exhalation of judgment. Yet, amidst the polite condemnation, a strange current ran through the room. Newland Archer watched his wife. Across the table, illuminated by the harsh overhead lights, she appeared pale and distant, yet her eyes burned with an intensity that unsettled him. She wore white—a bridal gown of blue-satin and old lace, a garment reserved for the first year of marriage, now draped on a woman who had already begun to fade into the background of her own life.

He realized then how rarely she had worn it since their return from Europe. The sight triggered a memory, a ghost of a future that had evaporated two years prior. On that night, watching her sit between Mrs. Lovell Mingott and her cousin, he had felt a blissful anticipation. Now, the outline of her figure was slightly heavier, her carriage athletic but weary. The innocence that once defined her now carried a tragic weight, a quiet resignation that moved him more profoundly than any drama on stage.

Outside, the opera continued. In the grand box, Marguerite sang of love and loss, a familiar tragedy played out before thousands. Archer watched the scene unfold, his eyes drifting to the empty seat where Old Mrs. Mingott usually sat. The absence was a void, a hole in the fabric of the evening.

Suddenly, the music swelled. "_M'ama, non m'ama..._"

Archer moved. He ignored the unwritten laws of the club box, ignoring the stares of the van der Luydens and Jackson. He slipped through the crowd, pushing past the barriers of his own restraint, and entered the box.

"M'ama!" the singer cried triumphantly.

The occupants looked up in surprise. Archer leaned over his wife, his voice low, urgent. "I have a headache. Do not tell anyone. Come home."

May understood instantly. She exchanged a glance with her mother, who nodded silently, before rising to join him. As they prepared to leave, the older ladies shared a knowing smile, a silent acknowledgment of the shift in power dynamics.

In the carriage, the city blurred past, a stream of gaslights and shadows. May touched his hand, apologetic. "You seem unwell. Perhaps the office has been too demanding?"

"No," he said, opening the window to let in the cold night air. "Something else."

He stared out at the passing buildings, feeling the weight of the carriage pressing against him. His wife sat beside him, a silent observer, her presence a constant, interrogative pressure. When they reached their door, she stumbled, catching herself against him.

"My dress," she exclaimed, pointing to a tear stained with mud. "See?"

Inside the library, the gaslight flickered to life. The warmth of the room felt like a trap, a familiar cage he had lived in for years. He lit the lamps, the flame casting long, dancing shadows against the walls.

"Shall I bring you brandy?" he asked, noticing her pallor.

"No," she said, removing her cloak. "Go to bed."

He placed a cigarette in his mouth, then crushed it in his palm. "No. My head is fine. There is something I must tell you. Something important."

She sat in an armchair, raising her head. "Yes, dear?" Her tone lacked the shock he anticipated.

"May—" he started, the word hanging in the air. "There is something I have to tell you... about myself."

She listened, her face a mask of tranquil indifference. He searched for the words, the conventional excuses, but found none.

"Madame Olenska," he said.

His wife raised a hand, silencing him. "Oh, why discuss Ellen tonight?"

"Because I should have spoken sooner."

"Is it worth it?" she asked, her voice steady. "We have all been unfair. You have always been kind to her. But what matters now that it is finished?"

"Finished?" he stammered.

"Since she is returning to Europe," she explained, her eyes clear and direct. "Since Granny approves and has arranged for her independence. I suppose you were detained at the office regarding the business arrangements?"

He turned away, covering his face with his hands. The noise in his ears grew louder, a drumming chaos that drowned out the ticking clock. Five minutes passed in agonizing silence.

"It is impossible," he finally shouted.

"Impossible?"

"How do you know what you just told me?"

"I saw Ellen yesterday. I told you I saw her at Granny's."

"Not then?"

"No. I received a note this afternoon."

He could not speak. She left the room and returned moments later, holding a single sheet of paper.

"I thought you knew," she said simply.

She placed the letter on the table. Archer picked it up, reading the sparse lines.

*"May dear, I have at last made Granny understand that my visit to her could be no more than a visit... She sees now that if I return to Europe I must live by myself... I am hurrying back to Washington to pack up, and we sail next week. You must be very good to Granny when I'm gone... Ellen."*

He laughed, a sudden, jagged sound that startled them both. It reminded him of a childhood memory, of a time when laughter could dissolve fear.

"Why did she write this?" he asked, struggling to control the emotion.

May answered with unshakeable candor. "I suppose because we talked yesterday. I told her I feared I had not been fair to her—that I hadn't understood how difficult it was for her, alone among strangers who felt entitled to judge. I knew you were the one friend she could rely on. I wanted her to know that you and I were the same—in all our feelings."

She paused, waiting for his reaction. "She understood my wish to tell her this. I think she understands everything."

She stepped forward, taking his cold hand and pressing it against her cheek. The gesture was simple, final, and devastatingly complete.
