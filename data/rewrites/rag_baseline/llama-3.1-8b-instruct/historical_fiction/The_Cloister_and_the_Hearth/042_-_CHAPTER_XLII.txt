As I recall the days when the church wall became a canvas for divine graffiti, the flames of fire danced upon its surface, casting an otherworldly glow over the crowd. The letters, a plea from above, questioned the absence of a mass for the duke amidst the turmoil. The spectacle captivated many, their faces frozen in rapt attention as the flames faded, leaving behind a sea of gaping onlookers. The corporation, not one to be outdone by celestial authorities, responded by investing in masses, and their cure, an old acquaintance of ours, reaped a handsome reward in gold. Though this development would normally be of little consequence, it serves as a reminder of the intricate web of relationships that bind us.

In the midst of this trivial yet telling occurrence, Denys found himself entangled in a far more complex dance. His friend, the bastard, begged for his arms, pleading not to be shamed and heartbroken. Denys, faced with the prospect of abandoning his comrade, made a solemn vow on the cross of his sword to remain by his side until the uprising was quashed. The terms were agreed upon, and Denys reclaimed his arms and freedom.

The troops paused at "The Three Fish," where Marion's warm smile greeted Denys like a ray of sunshine. Her jests, though coarse, couldn't mask the genuine concern etched on her face as she inquired about his troubles. Denys shared his tale, and Marion offered a reassuring perspective – that Gerard's good looks and charming demeanor would ultimately shield him from harm. The women, too, would rally behind him, and if fortune smiled, he might even join a noble retinue bound for Rome. If fate turned sour, however, he would return home, possibly even before his friend. Her words, laced with optimism, buoyed Denys's spirits, and he was heartened by her straightforward honesty. As she pressed upon him his gold pieces, Denys was struck by her integrity, and his perception of the world began to shift.

Meanwhile, scattered groups of foot soldiers, numbering from two to three, meandered along the roads, their pockets often empty but their bellies full, thanks to the bailiffs' orders for sustenance and lodging at the local inns. Anthony of Burgundy encountered these stragglers and gathered them beneath his banner, swelling his ranks to six hundred men.
