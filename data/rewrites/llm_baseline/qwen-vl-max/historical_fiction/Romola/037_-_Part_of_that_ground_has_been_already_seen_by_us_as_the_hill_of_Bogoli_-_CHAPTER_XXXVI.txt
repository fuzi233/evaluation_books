More than three weeks passed before the last of the library’s contents were packed and carried away. Romola did not shut her eyes or ears to it. Instead, she stood witness to every step—watching, listening, as if the very act of seeing could force the truth into permanence. The exhaustion that follows a violent emotional upheaval often brings with it a dreamlike disbelief in what has happened. Yet each evening, when the workmen had gone, Romola would take her hand-lamp and walk slowly through the chaos of straw and wooden crates, pausing at each empty pedestal, each once-prized object now laid low. She lingered, not for comfort, but to confirm—to assure herself, again and again—that her love was truly gone, and that the world had turned barren beneath her feet.

And still she returned, night after night—not to prove anything anymore, but because the ache of remembering her father, of mourning his memory in this ruined space, was all that remained alive in her heart. On December 23rd, she knew the final load was leaving. She ran to the loggia at the top of the house, so she wouldn’t miss the last sight—the slow wheels crossing the bridge, carrying away what had been her father’s life’s work, his dreams, his hopes.

It was a cloudy day, dusk falling fast. The Arno flowed dark and restless; the hills stood mournful. Florence, wrapped in its stone towers, looked silent, tomb-like from above, cloaked in shadow. Santa Croce, where her father lay, was swallowed by darkness. And over the bridge, crawling slowly into the narrow street beyond, came the white bundle of books and manuscripts—like some cruel, deliberate fate dragging her father’s legacy into an unmarked grave.

Romola felt less like she was watching it happen than that her father himself was aware of it, helpless under the weight of stone, unable to hear her voice say he was not alone.

She stood motionless even after the cart vanished, oblivious to the cold, soothed by the gloom that draped around her like a shroud, shielding her from the noise and laughter of a city that had no room for grief.

Then—suddenly—the great bell in the palace tower rang out, not in alarm, but in wild triumph. A peal of joy, sharp and insistent, echoed across the sky. One by one, every other bell in every tower answered, joining in a chorus that swelled until the air itself seemed to vibrate with sound. Flames flickered between the turrets—tiny, dancing lights—as if the sound had caught fire.

That sudden clang, that leaping light, struck Romola like wounds. It was the victory of demons, celebrating the success of her husband’s betrayal and the ruin of her life. Less than three weeks ago, she had stood enchanted by these same bells, hearing in their song a promise of her own happiness. Now, the city’s joy felt like cruelty. She stood apart, severed from the life below—Florence shouting its exultation into the wind, deafening sorrow with noise.

She could never join in such gladness again. Only those whom joy forgets would remain near her. In her bitterness, all celebration seemed mockery. Men shouted songs with hearts full of sorrow, then glanced at one another’s faces, searching for proof that joy was real.

Romola had lost faith in the happiness she once longed for. It was now a thing hateful—a smiling, soft-handed creature with a selfish, narrow heart.

She ran down from the loggia, hands pressed hard against her ears, hurrying across the antechamber—only to stop short, startled, as she met her husband coming toward her.

His step was light, his face lit with satisfaction, a glow uncommon in him.

“What? Was the noise too much for you?” he asked, noticing how tightly she clutched her ears. He took her wrist gently, drew her arm into his, leading her into the saloon adorned with dancing nymphs and fauns.

“Florence is mad with joy,” he said, “over the new Great Council—it will end all evils, especially the sin of merriment.” He smiled. “You look stunned, my Romola. And you’re cold. You shouldn’t stay so late on that drafty loggia without wraps. I came to tell you I’m being called to Rome on business for Bernardo Rucellai. I leave immediately—I’ll meet my party at San Gaggio tonight, so we can depart early tomorrow. No trouble for you—I’ve already packed my things. I’ll be back soon.”

He expected nothing more from her than quiet acceptance. He didn’t even dare kiss her brow tonight—only pressed her hand to his lips and left.

Tito sensed now that Romola was colder, harder than he’d imagined. Her love wasn’t the sweet, clinging devotion he’d once believed in—the kind that transcends judgment, that makes marriage a true bond. But this frozen silence was better than rage. At least she didn’t resist. Her pride, her ability to see when struggle was useless—these made her easier to bear.

But as soon as the door closed behind him, Romola shed the mask of stillness. The icy calm melted. Inside, she was far from passive. These past weeks had been spent in quiet preparation—planning, gathering strength—for a decision she had made long before.

The first thing she did was summon old Maso.

“Maso,” she said, voice firm, “we leave tomorrow morning. We can catch the first convoy of cloth—they’re waiting at San Piero. See to the two mules tonight. Be ready to set off at dawn, and wait for me at Trespiano.”

She meant to travel with Maso as far as Bologna, then send him back with letters to her godfather and to Tito—telling them she had gone, and would never return. She had planned everything so carefully—so that her departure would be secret, her broken heart unseen by any vulgar eye. Bernardo del Nero had been away at his villa, escaping political suspicion, content to tend his land. Romola had paid him the debt without meeting him. He didn’t know the library had been sold—he’d simply assume Tito had found sudden fortune.

Maso knew only that her journey was to be kept secret. And obeying her commands exactly—that was all he cared for in his old age.

Romola had no intention of sleeping that night. Once the door was locked, she lit her taper and went to the carved chest where her wedding clothes lay—white silk, gold embroidery, the long veil, the circlet of pearls. A sob rose in her throat. They looked like a shroud for a dead joy.

In a tiny gold loop of the circlet, a pink sugar-plum still clung—a relic from the shower of sweets at her wedding. Tito had noticed it first and said it should stay there forever.

At moments like this, memory swept over her—pulling her back into a time of perfect trust. She saw again the man whose love had made the world fresh and wondrous, as though she were a child among sunlit flowers. She heard his gentle voice, saw his honest eyes, breathed again the freedom of soul that comes from believing someone near you is greater than yourself.

And always, at such moments, tears came—because the woman who loved still felt something akin to the mother who bends over a child’s cold body, feeling warmth where there is none.

But there was another thing in the chest—something dark and coarse, tightly rolled. She turned from the white and gold to it, and as her fingers touched the rough serge, her tears stopped. This harshness pulled her back to reality—the world where love and delight had fled.

She untied the thick cord and spread the bundle on the table. It was the grey serge dress of a sister from the Third Order of Saint Francis—someone who lived in the world but devoted herself to piety. The Florentines called such women *Pinzochere*. Romola would wear this as disguise. She decided to put it on now—so if she needed sleep, she would wake ready to go.

She removed her black gown and slipped into the coarse sleeves of the serge mantle. The rope girdle bit into her fingers as she tied it. She welcomed the discomfort—each sensation reminded her of her new scorn for pleasure, for ease, for the way men avoided suffering while others bore burdens too heavy to carry. This garment was a rejection of everything her husband represented.

She gathered her long hair, pulled it tight from her face, bound it into a hard knot at the back. Then she tied a square of black silk across her head and under her chin, and drew the cowl over it. She held up the candle to the mirror.

Yes—her disguise was complete. To anyone who hadn’t known her closely, she might pass. To herself, she looked strangely like her brother Dino—the oval of her cheek needing only to grow thin, the eyes already sad, just needing to deepen in shadow. Was she becoming more like him in other ways? Only in this: she now understood how men could flee earthly delights, how they could choose sorrow over beauty, how they could fix their minds on pain rather than joy.

But she did not linger. She began collecting the relics of her parents—every object too large for her small wallet. Portraits, trinkets, keepsakes—all would go into the chest with her wedding clothes. When she was safely gone, she would leave the chest with her godfather.

She paused. There was one more thing to cast off—something belonging to the past she was turning away from forever.

She touched the betrothal ring on her finger. Her thumb and forefinger rested there—still. She had come so far toward this act—breaking free from a husband who had shattered her trust, severing a bond that no longer meant love. Yet the power of symbols—the way outward signs bind us to our identities—gave the simple act of removing the ring a strange weight. It felt like tearing her life in two.

Was it possible that this impulse, so clear and strong, was actually blindness? That there was something deeper in human bonds than mere illusion?

If the Tito who gave her the ring was not the same man she no longer loved, why return it? Why not keep it as a memorial?

This thought shook her. The act, which should have been a clean break, instead stirred a hidden fear—a sense that she was betraying something sacred.

But then a stronger voice rose within her.

“It cannot be! I cannot be ruled by him. He is false. I recoil from him. I despise him!”

She snatched the ring from her finger and placed it on the table beside the pen she meant to use for her letter.

Again, she felt the truth: there was no law for her but the law of her affections. Love had shaped her life—her patience, her courage, her capacity for sacrifice. But now, that strength had transformed into repulsion. Her hatred of Tito grew in direct proportion to the depth of the love he had betrayed, the devotion to her father he had violated.

All motive had faded—except indignation, scorn, the need to tear herself from him.

She was not following rules or traditions. The stoic philosophy her father taught her—its grand severity—was familiar, even inspiring at times. But she had never used it. She had endured not because of reason, but because she loved. Maxims about detachment, about not clinging, had been powerless against her tenderness—just as they had been against her father’s longing for justice.

She had no theories. She had only felt strong in love. And now, without that strength, life became a problem she had never faced.

Her solution was simple. She would follow only the instinct to leave the man she no longer loved.

Yet even as her resolve burned bright, anguish shifted beneath it—constantly changing shape. Now, with her preparations nearly done, she hesitated. She delayed writing the final words of farewell.

The emotions of the past weeks surged back—cruel, urgent. She reached for the pen—and her hand dropped. Tears came, bitter now, not from remembrance, but from shame. Shame for the words she had spoken to Tito—“Have you robbed someone else who is _not_ dead?” To have said such things—to her husband—felt like a degradation of her whole life.

Hard words between lovers are monstrous in memory—like seeing greatness fall into rags.

The contrast between past and present pressed upon her so fiercely that it became almost physical. She felt numb, hollow, trembling inside. She needed something solid—something harsh.

She gripped the knotted cord at her waist. Then she seized the rough lid of the chest—nothing else to go in? No. She closed it, pressing her palm against the carved wood, and locked it.

Now she remembered: she still needed to finish her disguise. The leather purse, the *scarsella*, must hang at her side—filled with small coins. Her florins and jewels, gifts from her godfather and cousin Brigida, were safely tucked inside her mantle. On the other side, the rosary.

She hung it there.

It never occurred to her that more than clothing was needed to pass as a *Pinzochera*—that her expression, her manner, her very presence, were unlike those of a woman whose eyes bowed in prayer, whose lips moved in silent repetition. Her inexperience shielded her from imagining the dangers ahead. And her pride helped her ignore them.

She didn’t know any Florentine woman had ever done what she was doing. Wives fled to friends or monasteries—but those paths were closed to her. She had forged her own: to go to Venice, to Cassandra Fedele, the most learned woman in the world, and ask how a woman of knowledge could live alone, sustain herself, and find purpose.

She was not afraid of hardship or uncertainty. Her life could never be happy again—but it must not be ignoble.

And in a tender mix of romance and realism, her future vision included not just survival, but heroism—stoic, proud, enduring. She imagined herself writing, someday, something that would rescue her father’s name from oblivion.

After all, she was only a young girl—this poor Romola, who had reached the end of her joys.

There were still tasks to do. A small key sat in a casket on the table—but now, her taper flickered and died. She had forgotten another light.

In moments, the room was dark.

She felt her way to a chair and sat, waiting for morning.

The key had brought back memories—memories that had haunted her since the revelation that split her from Tito. The last meeting with Dino had never left her mind. His dying words echoed in her thoughts:

“The man whose face was blank loosed thy hand and departed. As he went, I saw his face—it was the face of the great Tempter… And thou, Romola, didst wring thy hands and seek water, and there was none… and the plain was bare and stony again, and thou wast alone…”

She couldn’t stop hearing it. The wasted face, the strained gaze at the crucifix, the awe that made her kneel, the broken words, the silence after—everything replayed in her mind like a vision from another world.

But her mind resisted. Just as a strong body fights fumes when they threaten to choke, a strong soul resists phantasms when they threaten to rule.

What did those words have to do with her real grief? They were chance—mere echoes of Dino’s beliefs. He feared pagan learning. He valued celibacy. He remembered their home, the library. From these threads, the vision had been woven.

What reason had she to believe such a thing? None. True as the warning had proved, Romola knew with certainty that acting on it would have been cowardice. Her trust had failed—but she would choose again to have trusted, rather than be led by ghosts.

Yet the memory persisted, linking itself to her present. It gave her a glimpse into lives she had once dismissed—lives shaped by visions, voices, inner revelations.

“If only they were stronger in me,” she whispered, “I might lose sight of what that vision really was—and take it for prophecy. I might become a seer myself, like Suora Maddalena, Camilla Rucellai…”

She shuddered. All her education, all her upbringing, had taught her to scorn such superstition—the weakness of those who sit in darkness, reading fate in flickering flames.

And yet—she felt something deeper than coincidence. The moment with Dino, the connection with his dying mind—it lived anew in her. If such experiences were common, she would want to understand them. Even learn the thoughts of those who wept before images of martyrdom.

There seemed to be more than madness in that communion with suffering. Her springs were dried up. She wondered what other waters existed—what strength men found in the desert.

And the moments in the Duomo, when she had sobbed with a strange blend of rapture and pain as Fra Girolamo offered himself for the people—those came back to her, like a taste of a distant spring.

But again, she recoiled. She refused to let herself be drawn into visions, into fears that twisted natural love, as Dino had done.

She sat in darkness, waiting for dawn—her disguise complete, her heart torn, her path chosen.
