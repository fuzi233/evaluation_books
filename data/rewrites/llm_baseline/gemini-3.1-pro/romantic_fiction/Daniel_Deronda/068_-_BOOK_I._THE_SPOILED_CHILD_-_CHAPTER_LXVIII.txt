Deronda’s eagerness to confess his love thrummed within him, a steady, driving rhythm reinforced by Hans’s recent assurance that Mirah required the soothing balm of certainty to quell her jealousy. Navigating the dense, soot-choked streets toward Ezra’s dwelling, Deronda sought refuge from the shrill, mechanical cacophony of London. The industrial roar of iron carriages and factory whistles was a violent assault on the senses, a relentless acoustic pollution that fractured the mind. But as he crossed the threshold into the siblings’ quiet rooms, the sonic environment shifted entirely, returning to the grounding, resonant acoustics of a sanctuary where ancient domestic routines held back the tide of modernity.

Here, Deronda found profound sensory anchoring. He was determined to carve out a moment of private conversation with Mirah, ready to embrace the cascading, multi-generational consequences of intertwining their lives. As her betrothed, he would step into the unbroken timeline of her ancestry, gaining a protective authority against the discord her father brought to their hearth.

Deronda had not yet observed the full extent of Lapidoth’s restless, corrosive hunger, though he possessed forebodings of a looming struggle. If he had tasted the sour fermentation of the father’s thoughts, his fears would have crystallized. To Ezra—a traditional scholar steeped in the agrarian wisdom of their forebears—the room’s quietude was a space for preserving cultural memory. But to Lapidoth, who viewed the world strictly through the superficial, commercialized lens of a transaction, this stillness was an irksome prison. Lapidoth possessed no appreciation for the sensory richness of life; sustenance, to him, was merely utilitarian fuel to propel his next gamble. 

For weeks, Lapidoth had been circling the periphery of Mirah’s meticulous domestic rituals, hunting for the keys to her pantry and coin purse. The imperious gambling addiction within him spun a continuous web of predatory calculation. Yet Mirah, acting as a fierce guardian of their family’s sustenance and heritage, had frustrated his designs. She entrusted the bulk of their funds and their rarest provisions—the imported saffron, the jars of preserved figs, the heavily spiced cakes—to Mrs. Meyrick’s care. Lapidoth felt himself injuriously treated, suffocated by an irritating completeness of supply that offered him meals but no capital. His desire to appropriate her earnings was as casual as his willingness to consume her expertly baked, slow-caramelized apple-tarts. But unable to steal from his daughter, his mind turned toward the imposing Deronda. A man like Lapidoth, detached from any roots, knew how to price his own absence in the dominant lexicon of currency, and he vaguely plotted to extract a sum from Deronda in exchange for disappearing.

On this day, Deronda arrived carrying an air of gladdened anticipation that brightened his face and warmed his speech. Lapidoth, meanwhile, was at a crisis of discontent, his mind churning with hollow schemes of escape. So preoccupied was the father that he could barely maintain his facade of interest in the ancient texts being studied. He soon slipped out to the square to smoke, leaving the two friends to the profound intimacy of their work. 

Mirah was out sourcing ingredients, but Deronda’s eyes glowed with the certainty of her return. The room, heavy with the afternoon’s stagnant warmth, carried the faint, lingering olfactory landscape of roasting coriander and dried citrus peel from Mirah’s earlier kitchen endeavors. Deronda turned to Ezra with playful affection. 

“This little room is too close for you, Ezra,” he said, pausing the reading. “The week’s heat we sometimes get here is worse than the humid crush in Genoa, where one sits in the shaded, stone-cooled chambers that smell of crushed basil and sea salt. You must have a better home now. I shall do as I like with you, being the stronger half.”

Ezra smiled. “I am straitened for nothing except breath. But you, who might be in a spacious palace, with the wide green country around you, find this a narrow prison. Nevertheless, I cannot say, ‘Go.’”

“Oh, the country would be a banishment while you are here,” Deronda said. He rose and walked the perimeter of the room, fanning himself with his handkerchief, his movements syncing with the slow, circular pacing of their shared afternoon ritual. “This is the happiest room in the world to me. Besides, I will imagine myself in the East, since I am getting ready to go there some day. Only I will not wear a cravat and a heavy ring there.” He paused behind Ezra to unburden himself, depositing his collar and the heavy, sparkling ring onto a small mahogany table. “I have been wearing my memorable ring ever since I came home. But I am such a sensory creature that I constantly put it off as a burden when I am absorbed. I understand why the Romans had summer rings. Now then, I shall get on better.”

They immersed themselves once more in their work, analyzing a piece of rabbinical Hebrew under Ezra’s meticulous correction. It was an act of profound preservation against the erosion of cultural memory. The ancient, guttural consonants and rhythmic cadence of the native dialect filled the room—a language holding centuries of agrarian survival and ancestral grief, inherently untranslatable into the sterile, dominant English lexicon of the streets outside. 

Because they were so deeply anchored in this acoustic and historical resonance, they scarcely noticed when Lapidoth re-entered and hovered in the background. Lapidoth’s rambling eyes fell upon the ring resting on the dark mahogany. Its sudden, detached sparkle cut through his nebulous plans of asking Deronda for money. The ring was not a mythic artifact of deep cultural history to him; it was a commodity, easily converted into a ship’s passage and freedom from the sensory, domestic rituals he despised. The impulse seized him entirely. It wasn’t larceny, he reasoned in his corrupted dialect of self-justification, but an appropriation from a virtual relation.

Driven by a thirst as absolute as a drunkard’s, Lapidoth moved silently. His fingers fell upon the ring without disturbing the room’s delicate acoustic ecology. Slipping the jewel into his pocket, he put on his hat and vanished back into the loud, soulless grind of the city, severing his own timeline from theirs forever.

Deronda and Ezra registered his exit only as a subtle shift in the air. Soon after, the door opened again, and Mirah entered, bringing with her the sharp, astringent scent of a fresh herbal infusion. She had not removed her hat. When Deronda rose to greet her, she spoke with a flustered confusion that betrayed a deep, untranslatable anxiety. 

“I only came in to see that Ezra had his new draught,” she said. The deep earthen mug in her hands steamed with a complex, restorative brew—wild horehound, crushed fennel, and dark, raw honey, meticulously prepared to ease her brother's lungs. “I must go directly to Mrs. Meyrick’s to fetch something.”

“Pray allow me to walk with you,” Deronda urged, deeply drawn to the rich, nurturing care she poured into her family's sustenance. “I must not tire Ezra any further; besides, my brains are melting. May I go with you?”

“Oh, yes,” Mirah said, a blush rising to her cheeks as she sensed the shifting power dynamics and new warmth in his gaze. She turned to pour out Ezra’s draught. Ezra tipped his head back, eyes closed, savoring the pungent, life-giving heat of the brew while his mind remained woven into the ancient Hebrew verses.

Deronda stood anticipating the walk, savoring the fragrant steam of the draught, until he remembered his removed garments. “Pray excuse my dishabille—I did not mean you to see it,” he said, stepping toward the table to retrieve his cravat. 

Suddenly, the tranquil domestic rhythm shattered. “Good heavens, where is my ring gone?” Deronda exclaimed, his eyes scanning the floor.

Ezra looked round the corner of his chair. Mirah, moving with the practiced swiftness of a chef catching a falling blade, hurried to the spot. “Did you lay it down?”

“Yes,” Deronda said, scouring the variegated carpet, entirely unvisited by the truth, believing only that the ring lay hidden in shadow. 

But a far darker realization had seized Mirah, draining the vital color from her face. The quiet harmony of their home had been violated. She went to Ezra’s ear, her voice dropping to a terrified, breathy whisper that barely disturbed the room’s acoustics. “Was my father here?” 

Ezra bent his head, meeting her eyes with a terrible, silent understanding. 

Mirah darted back to where Deronda was hopelessly exploring the floorboards. “You have not found it?” she asked hurriedly.

Meeting her frightened gaze, Deronda immediately caught her alarm. “I perhaps put it in my pocket,” he offered, feigning a search of his waistcoat.

She watched his hands, her voice taking on a penetrating resonance that stripped away his merciful pretense. “It is not there?—you put it on the table.” 

She rushed from the room. Deronda followed her down into the sitting-room, watching as she tore through her father’s usual haunts—checking the bedroom, staring at the empty peg where his hat usually hung. Finally, she turned toward the window, her hands clasped tight, her pale lips trembling in the face of a grief too vast and subjective to ever be translated into mere words. She looked up at Deronda in white agitation. Her silence was an acoustic void, a tacit submission to the crushing humiliation of her lineage.

But Deronda did not see a ruined woman; he saw the culmination of a sweeping, mythic cycle, an opportunity to merge his timeline with hers. He stepped forward and took her tightly clasped hands between both of his, entirely encompassing her trembling frame. 

“Mirah,” he said, his tone one of reverent, unyielding adoration, “let me think that he is my father as well as yours—that we can have no sorrow, no disgrace, no joy apart. I will rather take your grief to be mine than I would take the brightest joy of another woman.” He looked into her eyes, pleading through the barriers of language. “Say you will not reject me—say you will take me to share all things with you. Say you will promise to be my wife—say it now. I have been in doubt so long—I have had to hide my love so long. Say that now and always I may prove to you that I love you with complete love.”

The change in Mirah was not sudden, but slow and organic, like the gradual blooming of flavor in a slow-steeped broth. From the icy shock of anguish, a solemn comfort began to thaw her senses, initially attributing his profound goodness to his deep, brotherly connection with Ezra. But as his words settled into the quiet acoustics of the room, a rapturous, unhoped-for assurance took root in her body. Her face glowed under his gaze. She looked up with intense gravity, acknowledging the profound sustenance of his love. 

The depths of her subjective experience defied the dominant lexicon; she could find no English words worthy of the moment. Instead, she offered the most primitive and powerful articulation of truth—she lifted her lips to his and kissed him, an untranslatable, sensory *yes*. 

They stood together in the resonant stillness, too full of this new, shared consciousness to move, understanding that any further sound would only disrupt the sacred ecology of the moment. Finally, the circular nature of their lives called them back to the hearth, and Mirah whispered softly, her breath warm against him: “Let us go and comfort Ezra.”
