By the Editor

Had my aged companion from Flatland retained the sharpness of mind he once possessed when penning these memoirs, he would not now require my assistance in composing this preface. Yet, burdened by time and tribulation, he has entrusted me with three tasks:

First, to express his gratitude to the readers and critics of Spaceland, whose enthusiasm has necessitated this second edition far sooner than anticipated.

Second, to address certain errors and misprints (for which he bears only partial responsibility).

Third, to clarify a pair of persistent misunderstandings—one intellectual, the other moral.

Some argue that if a Flatlander perceives a Line, it must possess not only length but also *thickness* (else it would be invisible). Therefore, they insist, Flatlanders should acknowledge a third dimension—*height*—however slight. At first glance, this reasoning seems unassailable, and I confess it once left me speechless.

But my friend’s rebuttal is sound:

*"I concede that our world possesses an unseen depth, just as yours harbors an unfathomable fourth dimension. Yet we are as blind to our 'height' as you are to your 'extra-height.' Even I, having glimpsed Spaceland, cannot truly grasp it. My senses and logic fail me; I comprehend it only by faith.*

*"Dimension implies direction, measurement, gradation. But in Flatland, all lines are uniformly and infinitesimally thin—or tall, if you prefer. There is no variation to measure, no axis to discern. When we see a Line, we perceive length and brightness—nothing more. If brightness vanishes, so does the Line. Thus, when I speak of an unseen dimension, my countrymen dismiss it as mere trickery of light. They demand proof: 'Measure it! Name its direction!' And I am silenced, for I can do neither.*

*"Imagine if a being from the Fourth Dimension visited you, claiming, 'You see planes and infer solids, yet a true fourth dimension eludes you—one I cannot define or demonstrate.' Would you not call them mad? So too am I branded in Flatland. Persecution unites all dimensions—Points, Lines, Squares, Cubes—we are all prisoners of our own perceptual limits."*

[Here, the Author notes that critics’ confusion compelled him to restore omitted dialogue with the Sphere, clarifying this very point.]

Detractors accuse him of misogyny—a charge pressed most fiercely by those who constitute, by Nature’s decree, the majority of Spaceland’s population. I shall address it plainly.

After seven years of reflection, his views on women and the lower classes have softened. Privately, he now agrees with the Sphere: Straight Lines possess virtues long overlooked. Yet as a historian, he has adhered (perhaps too rigidly) to the conventions of Flatland—and Spaceland—where the lives of women and commoners were seldom deemed worthy of record.

He disavows any aristocratic leanings. While acknowledging the intellectual dominance of the Circles, he observes that their infertility heralds inevitable decline. *"Nature’s laws ensure,"* he writes, *"that human ambition and natural order are seldom aligned."*

Finally, he cautions readers against seeking exact parallels between Flatland and Spaceland. His tale is not a strict allegory but a meditation—a prompt for open-minded inquiry. Let those of modest intellects neither dismiss the unseen as impossible nor presume to define it absolutely.

Imagine a vast plane, infinite in length and width but without thickness—a world where all existence is confined to two dimensions. This is Flatland, a realm of lines, angles, and shapes, where the very concept of "up" or "down" is meaningless. Here, everything is a plane: the land, the creatures, even the houses. To us, such a world might seem impossible, but to its inhabitants, it is the only reality they have ever known.

Flatland knows no seasons as we do; instead, its climate is regulated by the uniform warmth of its planar sun. Rain falls not as droplets but as lines, pooling into shallow lakes of perfectly flat water. Houses, too, are geometric marvels—pentagons, squares, and triangles—designed with entrances carefully angled to prevent unwanted intrusions. Windows are unnecessary, for in Flatland, one does not look out but *along* the plane.

The citizens of Flatland are living polygons, their intelligence and social status dictated by their number of sides. The lowly Isosceles Triangles, with their sharp angles, form the laboring class. Equilateral Triangles are the soldiers and merchants. Squares and Pentagons rise as gentlemen and scholars, while the noble Polygons—those with so many sides they resemble circles—are the revered Priests and rulers.

A word must be said of Flatland’s women—for they are not polygons at all, but mere straight Lines. Their lack of angles makes them dangerously unpredictable, capable of vanishing into nothingness when turned edgewise. For this reason, they are kept under strict control, required by law to sway their bodies in a perpetual "Peaceful Wave" to announce their presence.

Without depth, how does one recognize another? By touch, of course. The art of "Feeling" is a refined skill among Flatlanders, allowing them to discern angles and classify their brethren. But this method has its dangers—hence the strict social codes governing interaction.

Vision is a puzzling phenomenon here. To a Flatlander, another being does not appear as a complete shape but as a mere line segment, brightening and fading as they move. Only by careful observation of shading and perspective can they infer the true nature of their neighbor.

Not all is order in Flatland. Some are born Irregular—their sides uneven, their angles imperfect. These wretched beings are either destroyed in infancy or, if they survive, relegated to the lowest ranks of society.

Long ago, Flatlanders sought to distinguish themselves through color, painting their sides in brilliant hues. But this practice was deemed frivolous, even dangerous, for it threatened the rigid hierarchy of shapes.

The Color Revolt was a brief but bloody conflict, as the lower classes—armed with the power of distinction—rose against the Polygons. It was swiftly crushed, and color was outlawed, ensuring that only angles, not shades, dictated one’s fate.

The highest order, the Circles, preach the gospel of Dimension: that all existence is confined to the plane, and to imagine otherwise is heresy. They maintain their perfect shape through rigorous discipline, ensuring no Irregularity taints their rule.

Thus is the world of Flatland—a place of strict geometry, unyielding hierarchy, and unshakable belief in the two-dimensional truth. But what if there were more? What if beyond the plane lay depths unimaginable? Such questions are forbidden… for now.

I name our world *Flatland* not for our own sake, but for yours—you who dwell in Space, blessed with dimensions beyond our own. Picture, if you will, an endless plane where shapes—Lines, Triangles, Squares, Pentagons, and more—glide freely across its surface, bound to it as shadows are bound to light. Yet these are no mere phantoms; they are solid, their edges faintly aglow. So it is with my homeland and its people. Once, I would have called it my *universe*, but time and revelation have lifted my sight to realms unseen before.

Here, you must understand, nothing of what you call *solid* can exist. You might assume we discern our brethren by their forms—Triangles from Squares, Pentagons from Hexagons—but no. To us, all is Line. Allow me to prove it.

Place a coin upon your table and gaze down upon it. From above, it is a perfect circle. Now retreat to the table’s edge, lowering your eye until it aligns with the surface—until you, like us, see only as a Flatlander sees. The circle narrows, compressing into an oval, then—when your eye meets the plane—into a mere sliver of light: a Line. The same holds true for any shape—a Triangle, a Square—carved from paper. At our level, all depth collapses. A respectable Tradesman, an equilateral Triangle, appears first as his true form (Fig. 1), then as a jagged silhouette (Figs. 2–3), and finally, when viewed edge-on, as nothing but a straight stroke.

I have heard your sailors know something akin when they spy distant shores. A coastline may twist with coves and cliffs, yet from afar, it is but a smudge on the horizon—a single, unbroken thread. So it is for us. We have no sun to cast revealing shadows, no light to betray a shape’s truth. A comrade approaches; his Line swells. He retreats; it dwindles. But Line he remains, be he Circle or Polygon.

You might wonder how we know one from another under such tyranny of geometry. But that answer belongs to the tale of Flatland’s people—a tale for another time. For now, let me speak of our land’s climate and the dwellings within it.

Like your world, ours is divided into four cardinal directions: North, South, East, and West. Yet without a sun or stars to guide us, we cannot rely on the heavens for orientation. Instead, we are governed by a Law of Nature—a subtle but persistent pull toward the South. In milder regions, this force is feeble enough that even a woman of decent health can walk a fair distance northward without strain. Still, its presence is enough to serve as a compass for most of Flatland.

Rain, which falls predictably from the North, further aids our navigation. In towns, the alignment of houses—their walls running predominantly North and South—shields us from the downpour with their sloping roofs. In the countryside, where buildings are sparse, the trunks of trees offer rough guidance. All told, we seldom lose our way.

That said, there are times when even I, stranded in some barren stretch where neither house nor tree stands as witness, have been forced to halt for hours, waiting for the rain to reveal true North. The Southern pull weighs most heavily on the frail—particularly women of delicate constitution—so it is a mark of courtesy to yield the North side of the path to a lady. A difficult feat, I admit, when vigor clouds one’s sense of direction.

Our homes admit no windows, for light in Flatland is a ceaseless mystery. It reaches us indoors and out, day and night, with no discernible source. Scholars once debated its origin with such fervor that our asylums overflowed with the minds shattered by the pursuit. When taxation failed to curb these inquiries, the Legislature banned them outright. And yet—irony of ironies—I alone know the truth. I, who have glimpsed the secrets of Space and the radiance of the Third Dimension, am dismissed as a madman among madmen. But enough of this bitterness; let me speak instead of our dwellings.

The pentagon is the most favored shape for houses, as shown in the accompanying figure. Its two Northern sides form the roof, typically doorless. A small door on the East admits women; a larger one on the West welcomes men, while the Southern side remains sealed. Square and triangular houses are forbidden—and for good reason. Their sharper angles pose a hazard to any careless traveler who might stumble upon them in the dim glow of our world. Since the eleventh century, triangular structures have been outlawed save for fortifications and state buildings, where public caution is warranted.

Squares, though taxed to discourage their use, persisted until three centuries later, when lawmakers decreed that in any town of ten thousand or more, the pentagon’s gentler angles were the minimum permitted for safety. Now, even rural areas have embraced this design, though very rarely, in some forgotten corner, an antiquarian might still uncover the relic of a square house—a curiosity of a bygone age.

In Flatland, the stature of a full-grown inhabitant seldom exceeds twelve inches in length or breadth, with eleven inches being the more common measure. Our society is rigidly structured by the geometry of one’s form, which dictates both station and destiny.

Our Women are mere Straight Lines—diminished in both substance and intellect, as dictated by natural law. The Soldiers and laboring classes are Isosceles Triangles: two equal sides, each roughly eleven inches long, and a base so narrow (often no more than half an inch) that their vertices form a razor-sharp angle. The most degraded among them—those with bases scarcely an eighth of an inch—are nearly indistinguishable from Women, so fine is their point. These Triangles, though technically male, occupy the lowest rungs of our society, their sharpness rendering them fit only for brute force or menial toil.

Above them stand the Equilateral Triangles, their sides perfectly balanced, their minds refined by symmetry. These are the merchants, the clerks, the respectable backbone of Flatland.

The Professional Men and Gentlemen are Squares—my own class—followed by the Pentagons, whose additional side grants them greater prestige. Higher still are the Nobility, ascending through Hexagons and beyond, until their sides multiply so numerously that they approach the perfection of the Circle. The Priestly Order, our highest caste, consists of those Polygons so many-sided they are indistinguishable from true Circles, the apex of wisdom and authority.

By nature’s decree, a son shall possess one more side than his father, ensuring each generation rises in nobility. The son of a Square becomes a Pentagon; the son of a Pentagon, a Hexagon—and so on, toward divinity. Yet this law falters among the laboring classes. The offspring of an Isosceles remains Isosceles, trapped in angular servitude—unless, through extraordinary discipline or martial distinction, the bloodline inches toward equilibrium.

It is a momentous occasion when an Equilateral is born to Isosceles parents—a triumph of eugenics and perseverance. Such a child is swiftly taken from its kin and adopted by a childless Equilateral, lest the taint of lowly origins corrupt its newly perfected form. The state celebrates these anomalies, for they offer the lower orders just enough hope to stifle rebellion, while ensuring the aristocracy remains unchallenged.

Nature, in her wisdom, decrees that as the lower classes gain intellect, their sharpest angles dull, rendering them less dangerous. The most formidable Soldiers—those with minds keen enough to wield their piercing angles—find their very weapons softening into the safer contours of the Equilateral. Thus, revolution is thwarted before it begins.

When dissent does arise, the Circles and Polygons employ both artifice and force. A few promising rebels are "corrected" into Regularity and absorbed into the elite. Others are lured into state hospitals, pacified by the promise of future ennoblement. The most stubborn are executed. The rest, leaderless and divided, turn upon one another, their rebellions ending in mutual annihilation.

History records one hundred and twenty full revolts and two hundred thirty-five minor uprisings—all crushed by this inexorable system. Such is the order of Flatland, immutable as geometry itself.

If our Soldiers, with their sharp angles, are formidable, then our Women are infinitely more so. For where a Soldier is a wedge, a Woman is a needle—all point, piercing and precise. Add to this her ability to vanish at will, and you will understand why no sensible Flatlander dares provoke her.

But how, you may ask, does a Woman render herself invisible? The answer lies in perspective. Place a needle upon a table: viewed from the side, its full length is visible, but from the tip, it disappears into a mere glint. So it is with our Women. When she faces us, we see only a line; when she turns her eye—or mouth, for in Flatland these are one—toward us, she becomes nothing but a gleaming speck. And when she presents her back, she fades entirely, her dull edge blending into the surroundings like an Invisible Cloak.

The peril of Women is thus undeniable. A careless brush against a Soldier may maim, but to collide with a Woman is to invite instant annihilation. And when she chooses invisibility, how can even the most cautious among us evade her?

To mitigate this danger, our laws impose strict regulations:

1. **Separate Entrances**: Every household must have an Eastern door, reserved solely for Females, ensuring they enter with due decorum.
2. **The Peace-Cry**: No Woman may walk abroad without emitting a continuous warning signal—silence is punishable by death.
3. **Restrictions on the Unstable**: Any Female afflicted with involuntary motions—be it St. Vitus’s Dance or chronic sneezing—is to be eliminated without delay.

Some States go further, mandating that Women sway their backs rhythmically to signal their presence, or requiring them to be escorted by male kin. Yet excessive restrictions breed rebellion. Confined too harshly, Women turn their fury inward, and entire villages have been lost to their wrath. Thus, the wiser Circles enforce only the essential laws, trusting in self-preservation to temper their violence.

For though a Woman may kill with a mere backward lunge, her fragile form risks shattering in the act. Fashion, too, aids us—ladies of rank cultivate an elegant undulation, a mark of breeding that lesser classes mimic poorly. Among the highest orders, this motion is instinctive, sparing their households from unseen assaults.

Yet do not mistake our Women for mindless brutes. They are creatures of passion, governed by impulse, devoid of memory or foresight. A Woman may slaughter her family in a fit of rage, then moments later inquire after their whereabouts. In their chambers—carefully designed to limit movement—they are harmless, their threats as fleeting as their tempers.

Domestic harmony is a fragile thing. Among the lower classes, reckless husbands invite disaster, their blunt tongues provoking fatal reprisals. But the Circles, with diplomacy and well-timed promises, navigate these dangers with grace. Some even argue that the Women’s destructiveness serves a purpose, culling the reckless and preserving order.

Still, our households lack the warmth of yours. In noble homes, wives keep their eyes—and mouths—fixed ever upon their lords, for to turn away would be unthinkable. The Working Man’s wife enjoys more freedom, her silence a respite from the ceaseless chatter of the elite. Many a weary husband prefers the quiet menace of her sting to the relentless din of her speech.

To Spacelanders, the fate of our Women may seem cruel. Unlike the lowliest Isosceles, who may yet ascend in caste, a Woman is bound eternally to her nature. "Once a Woman, always a Woman," decrees the universe. Yet perhaps there is mercy in her forgetfulness—for she recalls neither her suffering nor her power long enough to revolt.

And so Flatland endures, balanced upon the edge of a needle.

To you, who dwell in the luxury of three dimensions—blessed with depth, color, and the gift of perspective—how can I convey the challenges we face in Flatland when discerning one another? Here, all things, whether living or inert, appear nearly identical: a mere straight line to the untrained eye. Yet we have devised methods to distinguish friend from stranger, class from class.

Our sense of hearing surpasses yours in refinement. By voice alone, we recognize not only acquaintances but also distinguish between the lower orders—Equilaterals, Squares, and Pentagons—though the Isosceles, being irregular, are scarcely worth the effort. Yet this method falters among the elite, where voices grow indistinct, and the talent for auditory discernment is deemed vulgar. Worse still, impostors abound; a cunning Isosceles may mimic the tones of a Polygon or even a Circle with practice. Thus, we often turn to a surer method.

Among women and the lower classes—and, with caution, the nobility—touch is the preferred means of recognition. In rural districts, the old formalities persist: *"Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by my friend."* In bustling towns, efficiency trumps tradition: *"Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so,"* with reciprocity implied. The youth, ever impatient, reduce it further: *"Permit me to feel Mr. Jones,"* a linguistic barbarism tolerated in fashionable circles.

Do not imagine this process as laborious as it would be in your world. We need not trace every side of a figure to know its class. A lifetime of training allows us to identify an Equilateral, Square, or Pentagon by a single angle. Even the bluntest touch detects the sharp, brainless vertex of an Isosceles. Only among the highest Polygons does difficulty arise—even scholars may confuse a ten-sided with a twelve-sided figure, and few can swiftly distinguish a twenty-sided from a twenty-four-sided noble.

Yet danger lurks in carelessness. The Felt must stand perfectly still; a flinch, a sneeze, may prove fatal. Among the lower Triangles, whose eyes sit far from their vertices, a heedless jerk has cost lives. My own ancestor—a nearly regular Isosceles—once startled while being examined and impaled a distinguished Polygon. The consequences were dire: our family’s hard-won progress was undone, regressing a full degree and a half before recovery.

You may ask: *How can lines perceive angles?* We do not see them—we deduce them. Our tactile sense, honed by necessity, surpasses your unaided vision in precision. Nature aids us further. The Isosceles brain begins at thirty minutes and grows by half a degree each generation until reaching sixty, when they ascend to the class of Regulars. Thus, we have a living scale of angles, from half a degree upward, preserved in schools as teaching tools.

The criminal and vagrant classes, abundant in specimens up to ten degrees, serve an educational purpose. Immobilized in classrooms, they help children of the middle orders learn angles—though some argue it is kinder to replace them monthly, as prolonged existence dulls their edges. A macabre efficiency, yet one that subtly thins the surplus of Isosceles, a goal dear to every Flatland statesman.

While feeling is swifter and more reliable than hearing, it is not without peril. Thus, many in the middle and lower ranks—and all Polygons and Circles—favor a third method, which I shall soon describe.

But let this suffice: in Flatland, recognition is an art, refined by necessity, fraught with risk, yet indispensable to our ordered world.

At first glance, I may seem to contradict myself. Earlier, I asserted that all figures in Flatland appear as mere straight lines, making visual distinction between classes impossible. Yet now, I must explain to my Spaceland readers how we *do* recognize one another by sight. The key lies in a crucial qualification: Recognition by Feeling is universal only among the lower orders. Among the higher classes—and in our temperate climates—Sight Recognition is not only possible but refined into an art.

This ability exists thanks to an element your world considers a nuisance: **Fog**. Where you see it as a blight, obscuring landscapes and dampening spirits, we revere it as a gift nearly equal to air itself—the nurturer of arts and the foundation of sciences. Without Fog, all lines would appear equally sharp, rendering visual distinction impossible (as is tragically the case in arid, transparent regions). But in our world, Fog grants gradients of clarity. An object three feet away dims slightly compared to one at two feet eleven inches. Through meticulous observation of these subtle shifts in brightness, we deduce the shape of what we observe.

Suppose a Merchant (an Equilateral Triangle) and a Physician (a Pentagon) approach. How do I tell them apart?

If I position myself to bisect the angle of the Triangle, my gaze splits evenly between its two nearest sides (CA and AB). The midpoint (A) blazes bright, but the lines CA and AB fade swiftly into the Fog, their ends (D and E) nearly vanishing.

For the Pentagon, the same glance reveals a line (D'A'E') with a bright center (A'), but its sides (A'C', A'B') recede more gradually. The extremities (D' and E') remain more visible than the Triangle’s.

Thus, through long training, we learn to distinguish classes by the *rate* at which their edges dissolve into Fog.

Do not assume this skill is easily mastered. If my Father, a Triangle, approaches edge-first, I might mistake him for a Straight Line—a Woman—until he rotates. Similarly, observing my Hexagonal Grandson head-on, I see one bright line (AB) flanked by two dimmer, fading lines (CA and BD).

In motion, the challenge intensifies. Picture a ballroom: Polygons of high rank rotate, advance, retreat. To track them by sight demands immense mental agility—worthy of the Geometric Professors at Wentbridge University, where this art is taught to the elite. Few possess the time or wealth to master it fully. Even I, a seasoned Mathematician, find myself confounded amid a whirl of rotating nobility.

For the lower orders, Sight Recognition remains an unattainable luxury. Tradesmen’s children learn "Feeling" early, gaining quick wit but sacrificing the disciplined perception of the Polygonal class. Once the elite complete their education, however, they surpass their Triangular rivals in every field.

Only a fraction of Polygons fail the University’s Final Examination. These unfortunates are shunned by all—too refined for labor, yet too inept for intellectual society. History’s great rebellions often sprang from their ranks. Some progressive statesmen argue for their suppression: lifelong imprisonment or painless extinction.

But I digress. The plight of the Irregular demands its own discussion.

In all my previous accounts, I have taken for granted a principle so fundamental to our existence that perhaps I should have stated it plainly from the outset: *every citizen of Flatland is a Regular Figure.* By this, I mean that each of us is constructed with sides of precise equality—Women as straight lines, Artisans and Soldiers with two equal sides, Tradesmen with three, Lawyers like myself with four, and so on. The lengths of these sides may vary with age—a newborn Female scarcely an inch, a grown Woman near a foot in length, and the perimeter of a mature Male roughly two feet—but it is the *uniformity* of our sides that underpins the very fabric of our society.

Consider the chaos if it were otherwise. Were our angles unequal, the elegant science of Sight Recognition would collapse. No longer could we discern a comrade’s rank with a glance; instead, we would be forced to laboriously measure each angle by touch, an intolerable inefficiency. Social interactions would grind to a halt. Confidence would erode. How could one invite a Tradesman to lunch if his shape might unpredictably wedge itself in the doorway? Imagine a respectable Triangle trailing an oblong deformity—such a figure would be a public menace! Even a modest crowd, where Squares now navigate with care, would dissolve into collisions and panic, endangering the delicate lines of Women and Soldiers.

Thus, Nature and Expediency decree: *Regularity is law.* Our statutes reflect this truth. An Irregular is not merely deformed—he is morally suspect, a threat to order. Some sentimentalists argue that cruelty shapes the Irregular’s criminality—that scorn from birth, exclusion from labor, and police surveillance breed resentment. They pity the wretch condemned to a clerk’s drudgery, barred from marriage, watched even on his meager holidays. But let us not be swayed. The State cannot endure such aberrations. Should a Triangle with a heptagonal rear be permitted offspring, how soon would our architecture, our very customs, buckle under their grotesque demands? Could ticket-collectors pause to measure every jagged perimeter at the theater? Could militias tolerate a soldier who might, by sheer asymmetry, disrupt the ranks?

And consider the temptations! An Irregular might swindle merchants by presenting his most convincing facet, or infiltrate professions by mimicry. No—despite the pleas of so-called humanitarians, I have never met an Irregular who was not also, by nature, a deceiver and a misanthrope.

Yet I am no extremist. Some nations destroy infants for deviations of half a degree, but wisdom lies in moderation. Great minds have overcome youthful irregularities of forty-five minutes; their loss would have impoverished Flatland. Our surgeons, too, perform marvels—compressing, extending, and trepanning to correct wayward angles. Thus, I propose a middle path: when the Medical Board confirms an infant’s Irregularity is incurable, let its end be swift and painless.

The alternative is unthinkable. To tolerate Irregularity is to invite the ruin of civilization itself.

If you’ve journeyed with me this far, you’ll understand why I say life in Flatland is, in many ways, dreary. Not for lack of intrigue—our history brims with rebellions, schemes, and the ceaseless dance of politics—nor because our mathematical puzzles fail to fascinate. No, the dullness I speak of is of the senses, the stifling sameness of a world where every sight is but a line, varying only in shade.

Yet it was not always so.

Long ago, before the Circles tightened their grip, color once blazed across Flatland. Tradition whispers of a forgotten age, six centuries past, when a lone Pentagon—Chromatistes, as the chronicles name him—stumbled upon the secrets of pigment. He painted his home first, then his servants, then his kin, and at last, himself. And what wonders followed! No longer did his neighbors fumble to distinguish his front from his back. His presence commanded attention without the need for shouted declarations of identity. Wherever he moved, crowds parted; no jostling, no confusion.

The revolution spread faster than fire in dry grass. Within weeks, Squares and Triangles abandoned their monochrome lives. Even the stubborn Dodecagons surrendered within months. By the end of a year, only the highest Circles and the lowliest Isosceles—our women and priests—remained untouched by color.

Nature, it seemed, had drawn a line. The reformers argued, *"Distinction of sides implies distinction of color!"* But women, being Lines, had no sides to speak of, and priests, who claimed the perfection of Circles, boasted a single, seamless edge. Thus, they stood apart, unpainted, unmoved by the chromatic frenzy.

Ah, but what a time it was! To walk among a people adorned in vivid hues—every gathering a living mosaic, every military parade a spectacle beyond imagining. Twenty thousand Isosceles flipping their bases from black to violet in unison; Equilateral militias striped red, white, and blue; artillerymen spinning like kaleidoscopes beside their scarlet cannons. They say even a great Circle, overcome by the beauty of his own army, cast down his scepter and cried, *"I would rather wield a brush than a crown!"*

Language itself grew richer in those days. Words dripped with borrowed radiance, and the poetry of that era still lingers in our driest modern texts. But like all bright things, it faded. The Circles, ever wary of disorder, stamped it out. Now, only the faintest echoes remain—whispers of a time when Flatland was not just a world of shapes, but of *light.*

And so we are left with lines. Only lines.

As the grip of tradition loosened, the intellectual foundations of Flatland began to crumble. The once-venerated Art of Sight Recognition, now deemed obsolete, faded into obscurity. Geometry, Statics, Kinetics—disciplines that had once defined our scholarly pursuits—were dismissed as relics of a bygone era, even within the hallowed halls of our University. The simpler Art of Feeling soon met the same fate in our schools.

The Isosceles classes, long burdened by their subservient role, seized the moment. No longer compelled to contribute to the upkeep of education, they grew bolder, their numbers swelling unchecked. Without the tempering influence of intellectual discipline, their brutish instincts flourished. Meanwhile, the Soldiers and Artisans, emboldened by their newfound equality with the highest Polygons, declared that the complexities of life—whether Statical or Kinetical—could now be mastered through the effortless magic of Colour Recognition alone.

Dissatisfied with the natural decline of Sight Recognition, they demanded more: the outright abolition of all "elitist arts" and the defunding of mathematics, geometry, and sensory training. Equality, they argued, was not merely a social ideal but a natural law—Colour had erased the old hierarchies, and so too should the legal distinctions between classes.

The revolutionaries pressed further. If Colour was the great equalizer, then even the Priests and Women—long exempt from such markings—must submit to its rule. When met with resistance (for how could one paint beings with no sides?), they countered with cunning logic: *Should not the front of every person—the half bearing the eye and mouth—be distinguished from the rear?*

Thus, the Universal Colour Bill was born.

Under its provisions:
- **Women** would be painted red on the front half (containing eye and mouth) and green on the rear.
- **Priests**, though Circular, would follow the same scheme—red on the forward semicircle, green on the hinder.

The implications were insidious. From certain angles, a Woman’s line would mirror a Priest’s: half red, half green. In a society already losing its grasp on Sight Recognition, confusion was inevitable. Women, quick to exploit the ambiguity, would be mistaken for Priests—overhearing state secrets, issuing commands, basking in unearned reverence. The Circles, meanwhile, would suffer the erosion of their authority as the masses bowed to impostors.

But the Bill’s true malice lay deeper. The Circles, last bastions of intellectual rigor, relied on their colourless homes to hone their discernment. Now, forced into chromatic conformity, their children would grow up in a world of deceptive hues. The simplest act of recognition—distinguishing father from mother—would become a puzzle, vulnerable to maternal trickery. Trust in logic itself would fray.

And so, beneath the banner of equality, the architects of the Bill orchestrated the slow unraveling of the elite. The Irregular behind it—no crude Isosceles, but a cunning defect spared in folly—knew well: once the Circles faltered, the rest would follow. Aristocracy would collapse.

The Women, of course, rejoiced. What did they care for the ruin of the state? To them, the chaos was power.

And power, once tasted, is seldom surrendered.

For three long years, the battle over the Universal Colour Bill raged, and for three years, chaos seemed poised to claim victory. Polygons, marching as common soldiers, were cut down by the sharper angles of the Isosceles Triangles, while the Squares and Pentagons watched in silence. But the true horror unfolded in the homes of the elite—wives, inflamed by political fervor, turned against their own. Some pleaded, others slaughtered, leaving twenty-three Circles dead by their hands. The Priests stood at the brink, forced to choose between surrender or annihilation.

Then came the spark that reshaped everything.

A lowly Isosceles—barely four degrees of intellect—stumbled into the colors of a plundered shop and daubed himself in the twelve hues of a Dodecagon. Disguised, he deceived a noble Polygon’s daughter, a woman who had once scorned him. Through luck and the blind negligence of her kin, the fraud was sealed in marriage. When the truth shattered her, she took her own life.

The tragedy rippled through Flatland. Women, once fervent for the Bill, now saw its dangers. What if their daughters were next? What if deception became the law of the land? The Circles seized their moment. They called an Assembly, packing the hall with guards and reactionary Women. Before a seething crowd of Isosceles, the Chief Circle, Pantocyclus, raised his voice—not in defiance, but in cunning concession. He would accept the Colour Bill.

The roar of fury became cheers.

Then came the trap.

With flawless rhetoric, Pantocyclus spun a vision of ruin: tradesmen beggared, artisans stripped of their hopes, Women preyed upon by any rogue with a brush. The Workmen wavered. The Women shuddered. And when Chromatistes, leader of the rebellion, stepped forward to rally them, the guards closed in.

The signal was given.

The Isosceles Convicts struck first, impaling Chromatistes. The Women, guided by the Circles, moved unseen, their deadly precision unmatched. The Artisans stood aside. Every exit was barred. What followed was not a battle, but a massacre. The Isosceles, trapped and leaderless, turned on each other in panic. Within minutes, the rebellion was a field of corpses.

The Circles did not stop there.

The Working Men were spared—but not all. The Equilateral Militia purged every suspect Triangle. Homes were inspected, towns scoured, the excess of the lower classes trimmed like overgrown hedges. Colour was erased from Flatland. To speak of it was a crime. Only in the highest, most secret chambers of the University does it linger—a tool for minds advanced enough to wield it without folly.

And its making? That knowledge is held by one alone: the Chief Circle. The factory that produces it is its workers’ tomb. Each year, they are replaced, lest the secret escape.

Even now, the aristocracy trembles at the memory of those days. For they know—the line between order and chaos is as thin as the edge of a blade.

It is time I turned from these scattered observations about Flatland to the heart of this account—my awakening to the secrets of Space. *That* is my purpose; all that precedes is but prologue. And so, though I might elaborate on countless facets of our existence—how we move without feet, how we build without hands, how rain traverses our zones without obstruction, the nature of our hills and mines, our script upon linear tablets—I must set them aside. Not from neglect, but from respect for the Reader’s time.

Yet before I proceed, I owe a few words on the very foundation of our society: the Circles, or as we name them, the Priests. Do not mistake the title for what it signifies in your world. Among us, Priests are no mere clergy. They are the architects of all affairs—masters of commerce, generalship, science, law, and morality. They decree, and others labor; they govern, and all obey. To them, we pay near reverence.

Now, though common speech grants the name "Circle" to all of their order, the learned know the truth: no Circle is truly circular. They are Polygons, but of such finely divided sides that the angles elude even the most delicate touch. A figure of three hundred sides has edges no longer than a hundredth of a foot; at six hundred, they are scarcely wider than the point of a Spaceland pin. By tradition, we grant the Chief Circle ten thousand sides—a courtesy, nothing more.

Unlike the lower Orders, whose sides increase but one per generation, the Circles are bound by no such slow arithmetic. Nature imposes two opposing laws upon them: as their lineage ascends, their evolution quickens, yet their fertility wanes. A five-hundred-sided Polygon may sire but one son, yet that son might boast fifty or a hundred sides beyond his father.

And then there is Art’s hand in their refinement. Our physicians have learned to fracture the fragile sides of a highborn infant and reset them with such precision that—though the risk is grave—a child may leap centuries of lineage in a single generation. Few survive this ordeal. For every ten subjected to the Circular Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium, nine join the silent ranks of the Cemetery. But when the tenth emerges, no longer a Polygon but (by grace) a Circle, the triumph drives multitudes to stake their heirs upon the same desperate chance.

Ambition, it seems, spares no sacrifice.

The doctrine of the Circles may be distilled into a single, immutable maxim: *Attend to your Configuration.* Whether in matters of governance, faith, or morality, their teachings orbit this singular truth—the refinement of individual and collective form, with supreme reverence for the Configuration of the Circles, to which all else submits.

It is the triumph of the Circles to have extinguished ancient heresies that once led men to squander effort and empathy on the delusion that conduct springs from will, effort, or virtue rather than the unyielding geometry of one’s form. The great Pantocyclus—who quelled the Colour Revolt—first illuminated this truth: *Configuration shapes destiny.* An Isosceles born with uneven sides is fated to misdeeds unless corrected in the Hospital; a Triangle or Square marred by Irregularity must submit to regularization or face the State’s merciless angles. All flaws, from petty misconduct to grave crime, Pantocyclus ascribed to deviations in form—whether congenital, acquired by collision, neglect, or even the caprices of temperature. Thus, he decreed, neither virtue nor vice merits praise or blame. Why commend a Square’s loyalty when it is but the inevitable alignment of his angles? Why condemn a thieving Isosceles when his crimes arise from the asymmetry of his sides?

In theory, this doctrine is flawless. In practice, complications arise. When an Isosceles thief pleads his unevenness as excuse, the Magistrate may rightly condemn him as a menace to be consumed. But in the domestic sphere, where consumption is no remedy, the logic falters. When my own Hexagonal Grandson claims his disobedience stems from a sudden chill warping his Perimeter, and insists his Configuration demands indulgence in sweets, I find myself at a loss—unable to refute his logic yet unwilling to indulge it. I resort instead to scoldings, though I confess no evidence that such rebukes mend his angles.

Nor am I alone in this dissonance. Even among the Circles, Judges in their courts wield praise and blame as if such concepts held weight, and in their homes, they chastise their young with fervor, as though choice were real. Yet all the while, they enforce the primacy of Configuration, inverting the natural order of reverence. Where your kind teach children to honor their parents, we teach men to honor their Grandsons—or Sons, if Grandsons are lacking—for the Circles decree that fathers must sacrifice their own interests to posterity, ensuring the State’s geometric perfection.

Yet the system is not without flaw. Consider the Circles’ dealings with Women. To prevent Irregular births, no Woman with Irregular ancestry may wed a man of rising lineage. Yet as rank ascends, vigilance wanes. An ambitious Isosceles scrutinizes his bride’s pedigree for five hundred generations; a Circle may wed a woman with an Irregular forebear for the sake of her luster or the cadence of her voice. Such unions often bear no fruit, or worse, yield diminished offspring. Though the loss of a side or two in a high Polygon may escape notice—and sometimes be restored in the Gymnasium—the Circles grow complacent. If this persists, the day may come when no Chief Circle can be born, and Flatland’s order will collapse.

A second peril lies in our treatment of Women. Centuries ago, the Circles decreed that Women, being ruled by Emotion rather than Reason, should no longer be educated as rational beings. Denied arithmetic, even the ability to count their children’s angles, their intellect has dwindled with each generation. This policy, meant to stabilize, now threatens *us*. For we Males must live a double existence: in the company of Women, we speak of "love," "duty," and "pity"—words without meaning, tools to placate their irrationality. Among ourselves, these concepts dissolve into cold necessity, fitness, benefit. We flatter Women to their faces; behind their backs, even the Circles regard them as little more than mindless shapes.

This duplicity weighs heavily on our young. At three years old, a boy is torn from his Mother’s care and taught to unlearn her language—save for hollow reassurances—and adopt the precise dialect of geometry. Already, I fear our grasp of mathematical truth weakens compared to our forebears. What if a Woman were to secretly learn to read? What if a careless child revealed to his Mother the stark logic we reserve for Men?

Thus, I plead—not for sentiment, but for the preservation of reason—that the Circles reconsider the education of Women. For the sake of Flatland’s future, we cannot afford to let half our kind languish in ignorance.

On the eve of the year 2000, as the Long Vacation began, I retired to bed with an unsolved geometric puzzle lingering in my thoughts. That night, I dreamed.

Before me stretched a world unlike any I had known—a single, infinite Straight Line, teeming with tiny, luminous points and slender Lines darting back and forth in perfect unison. A ceaseless chirping filled the air, rising and falling with their motion. When they halted, silence swallowed them whole.

I approached one of the longer Lines, assuming it to be a Woman, and spoke. No reply came. Twice more I tried, but only when I positioned myself directly in her path—blocking her movement—did she respond.

*"I am no Woman,"* the Line declared. *"I am the Monarch of this world. But you—who are you to trespass upon Lineland?"*

Startled, I apologized for my intrusion and begged the King to describe his realm. Yet every question I posed seemed to baffle him, for he could not fathom that I knew nothing of his Straight Line. To him, it was the entirety of existence—beyond it lay not emptiness, but *nothingness*.

His subjects—men as Lines, women as Points—lived bound to that single dimension. Their vision extended no further than a Point, their movement confined to forward and back. None could pass another, for the Line was their universe, and they filled it completely. Neighbors remained neighbors until death, as fixed as stars in the heavens.

I marveled at their cheerfulness, for such existence seemed a prison to me. Curiosity overcame discretion, and I asked how marriages could thrive when no two souls could draw near.

*"Absurd!"* the King cried. *"Do you think love requires proximity? Our unions are forged by harmony, not touch."*

He explained: Each man possessed two voices—a bass and a tenor—while women carried soprano and contralto. Once a week, all Linelanders swayed in a great rhythmic dance. At its peak, every voice rang out, and across vast distances, destined lovers found one another by sound alone. A perfect triad—one man and two women—united in harmony, their union birthing three children to preserve the balance of sexes.

*"Few succeed at first,"* he admitted. *"But time refines discord into unison. Nature wills it so."*

I dared not argue further. The King’s fury was plain, and his world, though narrow, was immutable. To him, my questions were not ignorance but heresy.

And as I awoke, I wondered—how many dimensions, how many truths, lie beyond *our* grasp?

I resolved to ground the Monarch’s lofty musings in the tangible truths of Flatland. Cautiously, I began:

"Your Majesty, how do you perceive the forms and positions of your subjects? Before entering your realm, I observed—by sight—that some among you are Lines, others Points, and that certain Lines are more substantial—"

"A vision!" he snapped. "To distinguish a Line from a Point by sight is impossible. Hearing alone reveals such truths. Listen—I am a Line, the greatest in Lineland, spanning six inches of Space."

"Of *Length*," I corrected.

"Fool! Space *is* Length. Another interruption, and I banish you." I bowed in apology, but he sneered. "Since reason fails you, hear how my twin voices betray my shape. My wives, six thousand miles distant—one North, one South—hear my first voice, then the second, delayed by the span of my form. From this interval, they deduce my length: 6.457 inches. A calculation made once, at our union, yet repeatable at will. Thus, I discern every subject’s shape by sound alone."

"But what if a man mimics a woman’s voice?" I pressed. "Or distorts his Southern echo? Could such deceit sow chaos? Have you no safeguard—say, by touch?"

A reckless question—touch would solve nothing—but I wished to provoke him. It worked.

"*Touch?*" he roared. "Explain this obscenity!"

"Contact. The closing of space between two beings."

"Crime of crimes!" he thundered. "Such proximity is death-punished. Women’s fragile forms must be preserved, yet since sight cannot sex us, the Law demands all keep their distance. Why grope like beasts when hearing delivers precision? Voices cannot lie—they are the essence of the self. Imagine, Stranger, if I pierced my subjects one by one, a billion times, to *feel* their dimensions—what blundering waste! Now, in an instant, I audit all Lineland: bodies, minds, souls. Listen!" He paused, enraptured by a chorus of infinitesimal chirps.

"An impressive system," I conceded. "Yet your existence strikes me as bleak. To see only a Point! Never a Line’s sweep, nor even the concept of straightness! Better blindness than such deprivation. Your symphonies are to me but chirping. Yet I, at least, can *see* a Line apart from a Point. Before arriving, I watched you dance left to right, flanked by seven men and a woman on your left, eight men and two women on your right. Accurate?"

"Numerically, yes. But you *heard* this; you dreamed sight. And what are 'left' and 'right'? Some jargon for North and South?"

"No. Beyond your linear march, there is lateral motion—side to side."

"Demonstrate it," he demanded.

"I cannot—unless you step *out* of your Line."

"Out of Space? Out of the *world*?"

"Out of *your* Space. True Space is a Plane; yours is but a Line."

"Describe this motion in words, then."

"If you lack the idea of left and right, words will fail. Have you never wished to turn, to face your own length as a direction?"

"Absurd! A man’s inside has no 'front'!"

"Then let deeds speak." I began sliding out of Lineland.

"You’ve not moved!" he cried as I lingered at the threshold. But when I fully exited his Line, he shrieked, "Gone! Dead!"

"I live," I called. "I’ve left your Line, your false Space, and now see truth: your Line, your subjects—their order, their intervals." I listed them meticulously.

"Convinced now?" I reentered, resuming my place.

The King scoffed. "Magic tricks and census-taking prove nothing. Describe this other Line you boast of. You vanish like a specter, recite child’s-play facts, and call it revelation? Begone, charlatan!"

His willful ignorance enraged me. "Arrogant wretch! You, a mere Line, mock *me*—a Square, a Line of Lines! I see the shapes you cannot fathom: Angles, Circles, Polygons! I am your completion, yet even I am humble beside Flatland’s nobles, who sent me to enlighten you!"

With a howl, the King lunged to impale me. A cacophony erupted—a million voices screaming war—swelling until it seemed the very air would shatter. Frozen, I braced for annihilation—

Then awoke. The breakfast bell tolled. Flatland’s reality reclaimed me.

From dreams, I turn to reality.

It was the final night of the 1999th year of our era. The rain tapped gently against the walls, signaling the deepening night as I sat with my wife, reflecting on the past and contemplating the dawn of a new year—a new century, a new Millennium.

[Note: When I say "sat," I do not mean it in your Spaceland sense, for we of Flatland have no limbs to bend or stretch. Yet we recognize the shifting states of mind—lying, sitting, standing—each marked by a subtle change in luminosity, visible to the keen observer. But I digress.]

My four sons and two grandchildren had retired to their chambers, leaving only my wife and me to witness the passing of the old Millennium. My thoughts wandered, lingering on a curious remark made earlier by my youngest grandson, a bright and promising Hexagon.

During his lesson in Sight Recognition, we had rotated before him, testing his perception of our angles and positions. Pleased with his answers, I had rewarded him with a lesson in Arithmetic as applied to Geometry. Placing nine one-inch Squares together, I formed a larger Square with sides of three inches, demonstrating that the area could be calculated by squaring the side length.

"But if three squared is nine," the boy mused, "what does three cubed mean in Geometry?"

"Nothing," I replied. "Geometry deals only with two dimensions."

Yet the child persisted, his mind racing ahead. "If a Point moves to make a Line, and a Line moves to make a Square, then surely a Square moving must make—something else?"

"Enough," I said, mildly exasperated. "To bed with you."

And so he left, while I remained, half-listening to my wife’s chatter, half-lost in contemplation of the coming age. The last grains of sand in the hourglass dwindled.

"The boy is a fool," I muttered, turning the glass northward.

At once, a chill gripped me. A presence—unseen, yet undeniable—filled the room.

"He is no such thing!" my wife protested. "And you shame yourself by speaking so!"

But I barely heard her. My senses strained against the void. Then, a whisper, cold and clear:

We both turned toward the voice—and froze. Before us stood a figure, shifting in ways no shape of Flatland should. At first glance, it resembled a woman, but no—its edges blurred, its form defied reason. My wife, ever impulsive, gasped.

"A woman? Have you broken your vow? There are no ventilators in this house!"

"There are none," I said sharply. "This is no woman. Look closely—it lacks angles entirely."

The figure spoke again, its voice layered like overlapping circles. *"I am a Circle, yet more than a Circle. I am many in one."*

Then, addressing me directly, it added, *"I bring you a message—one not meant for your wife’s ears."*

My wife, though baffled, retreated with stiff propriety, leaving me alone with the stranger.

The final grain of sand fell.

The third Millennium had begun.

The moment the echoes of my Wife’s Peace-cry faded, I turned to the Stranger, intent on offering hospitality—but what I saw froze me in place. His form defied reason. Though devoid of angles, his shape shifted ceaselessly, flickering in size and brilliance like no figure I had ever encountered. A chilling thought seized me: *Was this some rogue Isosceles, a murderer disguising himself as a Circle to infiltrate my home?*

The sitting room was clear of Fog, denying me the usual aid of Sight Recognition. Heart pounding, I lunged forward without ceremony. "You must permit me, Sir—" My hands confirmed my Wife’s claim. No angles. No imperfections. Only seamless curvature. A *perfect Circle*.

I circled him, my eye tracing his outline in awe. There was no doubt—he was a Circle, flawless and undeniable.

Our exchange began, his voice edged with impatience.

**Stranger:** "Have you felt enough? Or must I endure further inspection?"

**I:** "Forgive my discourtesy, illustrious Sir. My surprise—and this unanticipated visit—unsettled me. I beg discretion, especially toward my Wife. But before we proceed… might I ask whence you come?"

**Stranger:** "From *Space*, Sir. Where else?"

**I:** "My Lord, are we not already *in* Space?"

**Stranger:** "You know nothing of Space. Define it."

**I:** "Space is height and breadth, extended without limit."

**Stranger:** "Precisely your ignorance. You perceive but *two* dimensions. I bring word of a *Third*—length, breadth, and *height*."

**I:** "You jest. We name dimensions doubly—length and height, breadth and thickness—yet still speak of only two."

**Stranger:** "I speak of *three* dimensions, not names."

**I:** "Then show me this ‘Third Dimension.’ Point to it."

**Stranger:** "It lies where you cannot see—*above* and *below*, not North or South. You lack an eye to perceive it."

**I:** "An eye *inside* me? Absurd!"

**Stranger:** "No jest. I come from Spaceland, where I gazed *down* upon your plane. Your so-called ‘solids’—houses, chests, even your stomach—lay bare before me."

**I:** "Words are easily spoken, my Lord."

**Stranger:** "Then let proof suffice. I saw your Pentagonal sons in their rooms, your Hexagonal grandsons. I watched your youngest retire, leaving you and your Wife. Your Isosceles servants supped in the kitchen, the Page in the scullery. How else but from *above*?"

**I:** "A spy might glean such details."

Frustrated, he tried another approach.

**Stranger:** "When you behold a Line—your Wife—how many dimensions does she possess?"

**I:** "The ignorant call women Lines, but we Squares know better: they are slender Parallelograms—two dimensions, length and breadth."

**Stranger:** "Yet visibility demands *three*. A Line without ‘height’ would vanish."

**I:** "Brightness grants visibility. Do you call *brightness* a dimension?"

**Stranger:** "No. ‘Height’ is a dimension like length—only imperceptibly small to you."

**I:** "Then measure mine. Show me this ‘height.’"

**Stranger (muttering):** "How to convince him…?"

With sudden resolve, he declared:

**Stranger:** "You dwell on a Plane. Flatland is but the surface of a fluid. I am no mere Circle—I am a *Sphere*, infinite Circles stacked from Point to breadth. When I intersect your plane, you see only a slice: a Circle. Just as you appeared a Line to the King of Lineland, so I appear a Circle to you."

Before I could protest, he continued:

**Stranger:** "Watch. As I rise into Space, my section shrinks."

To my shock, his circumference dwindled—first to a tiny ring, then to a Point, before vanishing entirely. A voice echoed from nowhere:

**Stranger:** "Convinced? Now I return."

The Point expanded, restoring the Circle to full size. My mind reeled. Was this sorcery? Some trick of light?

**Stranger:** "Still perplexed? Then let Analogy instruct you. If a Point moves North, leaving luminous trail—what forms?"

**I:** "A Line."

**Stranger:** "And if that Line sweeps East-West?"

**I:** "A Square."

**Stranger:** "Now imagine the Square moving *upward*, each Point tracing its own Line *out* of Flatland. What arises?"

**I:** "Some… *Solid*?"

**Stranger:** "A *Cube*, with *eight* corners. Just as a Point (0) begets a Line (2), a Line begets a Square (4), so a Square begets a Cube (8). The pattern is clear!"

**I:** "And its… *sides*?"

**Stranger:** "Six—your ‘insides’ become its bounds."

Madness seized me. "Enough!" I cried. "Juggler, demon, or dream—I endure no more!"

And with that, I hurled myself at him.

It was no use. I drove my sharpest angle against the Stranger with all my might—enough force to shatter any ordinary Circle—yet I felt him slipping inexorably from my grasp. Not sideways, not backward, but *outward*, as if dissolving from the world itself. Then—nothing. Only his voice remained.

**SPHERE:** *Why do you resist reason? I sought in you—a scholar, a mathematician—a worthy herald for the Gospel of the Third Dimension, a truth I am permitted to reveal but once in a millennium. Yet you remain blind. Very well. If words fail, let deeds speak.*

A pause. Then his voice, cool and deliberate:

*From where I hover in Space, I see the insides of all you believe to be closed. That cupboard beside you? The "boxes" within (though in Flatland, they lack tops and bottoms) are stuffed with coin. I see two ledger tablets as well. Watch now. I will descend, take one, and return. You locked that cupboard; the key is still in your pocket. But I move beyond locks.*

Before I could protest, the cupboard door stood shut, untouched—yet a tablet vanished from within. A laugh echoed, and the Stranger materialized across the room as the tablet clattered to the floor. I seized it. The very one that had been inside the locked cupboard. My mind reeled.

*Now you see,* the Sphere intoned. *Your "Solid" world is but a skin. Your "Space" a mere Plane. I look* down *upon the guts of your existence. A single motion—up or down—would unveil all I see.*

He described his ascent: the Hexagon’s private chambers, the emptying theater ten doors away, a Circle bent over his books. *And now,* he said, *a final proof.*

Before I could flinch, a lance of pain split my gut. A laugh—*his* laugh—seemed to burble from my own insides. The agony faded, leaving only a throb, as the Sphere swelled back into view.

Enough. I would not endure this sorcery—this violation of my very flesh. If I could pin him, hold him until help arrived—

I rammed him again, screaming for aid. This time, he faltered. Perhaps he had dipped below our Plane, struggling to rise. I pressed harder, shouting—

The Sphere shuddered. *No further,* his voice hissed, strained. *Reason or ruin. Choose now.* Then, louder: *Send your Wife away! She must not see this. A thousand years of waiting must not be undone!*

"Liar! Deceiver!" I roared. "I’ll see you answer for this!"

The Stranger’s voice turned thunderous. *So be it.*

Then—three sharp words. A lurch.

And the world tore open.

A terror beyond words gripped me. Darkness enveloped my senses—then came a lurching, nauseating whirl of perception unlike anything I had known. Lines twisted into impossible shapes; space itself unraveled and reformed. I was myself, yet no longer myself. When at last I found my voice, I screamed, "This is madness—or damnation!"

"Neither," answered the Sphere, calm as still water. "This is knowledge. This is the Third Dimension. Now steady yourself—look again."

And so I did.

A world beyond imagining unfolded before me. There, radiant and whole, stood the very vision of perfection I had only ever dared to theorize—the flawless Circular form, but *alive*, *embodied*. Yet where I expected organs—a heart, lungs, veins—there was only an exquisite, seamless harmony, a presence my Flatland tongue could not name. (You, Spaceland reader, would call it the surface of a Sphere.)

Overcome, I prostrated myself before my guide. "Divine one! I see *into* you, yet discern no inner workings—no heart, no lungs—how can this be?"

"What you believe you see, you do not," the Sphere replied. "No being of Flatland may perceive my depths. I am not of your order. Were I a Circle, you would glimpse my entrails. But I am a Sphere—the Many made One, infinite Circles in a single form. Just as the shell of a Cube is a Square, so too does my surface appear to you as a mere Circle."

Though his words confounded me, my resistance melted into awe.

"Be patient," he said, gentler now. "The truths of Spaceland will unfold in time. Let us first revisit your home—not as you knew it, but as it truly *is*."

Before I could protest, the world lurched again—and there, spread beneath me like a living map, was my own house, its walls *transparent* to my newly awakened sight. My sons slept in their chambers; my grandsons, orphaned and quiet, in theirs. The servants moved in their routines, my daughter in her quarters. Only my wife paced the hall, restless with worry. And there—the Page, feigning concern, rifled through my study, eyeing the gold in my cabinet.

*I could see it all.* Not deduction, not inference—*sight.*

I strained to rush to my wife’s side, but my body would not obey.

"Do not fret," said the Sphere. "Her fear will soon pass. Now—let us survey Flatland entire."

We rose higher. The city shrank below, every home, every soul laid bare. Deeper still—the earth itself split open, revealing mines, caverns, secrets buried beyond mortal reach.

"I have become as a god," I whispered. "For our scholars say that to see all—*omnividence*—is divine."

The Sphere’s voice turned sharp. "Then by your logic, every thief and murderer in Spaceland is a god, for they see your world as plainly as you do now. Does mere sight make one just? Merciful? No. Wisdom lies not in vision, but in what one *does* with it."

Before I could argue, he gestured downward. "Look there."

In the distance loomed the great Polygonal Assembly Hall, where the highest Circles convened. It was the dawn of the year 2000, and as tradition demanded, they gathered to reaffirm an ancient decree:

*"Let all who claim revelations from other worlds be silenced. Let the Isosceles be slain, the Triangles scourged, the Squares and Pentagons confined. Let none disturb the order of Flatland."*

The Sphere turned to me. "Hear your fate. Death or prison awaits the prophet of the Third Dimension."

"Let me go to them!" I begged. "I can make them understand!"

"Not yet." With that, he plunged into the Council’s midst, his circular form swelling before their eyes.

"I bring word of a Three-Dimensional world!" he declared.

Chaos erupted. Young Circles recoiled; Isosceles guards rushed forth, seizing at his shifting edges—"We have him! No—he’s gone!"

The presiding Circle silenced the panic. "This is no surprise. The archives speak of such... *disturbances* at each millennial dawn." He turned to the guards. "Arrest the witnesses. And you—" He nodded to my brother, the Council’s Clerk. "For secrecy’s sake, you must be imprisoned. But be silent, and you will live."

The Sphere returned to me as the Council dispersed, their New Year’s greetings hollow over the sound of shackles.

The lesson was clear: truth alone is not enough. Not when the world refuses to see.

My brother’s imprisonment struck me with grief, and in desperation, I tried to leap down into the Council Chamber—to plead for him or at least bid him farewell. But I was powerless, bound to the will of my Guide, who spoke grimly: *"Do not trouble yourself with him. You will have time enough to mourn later. Follow me."*

Once more, we rose into the vastness of Space.

*"Until now,"* the Sphere declared, *"I have shown you only Plane Figures and their interiors. Now, you must learn of Solids."* He produced a stack of square cards, placing one upon another—not North or South, but *on*, in a direction I had never known. Layer by layer, he built a shape: equal in height, length, and breadth. *"This,"* he said, *"is a Cube."*

Yet to my eyes, it was no Solid, only a jagged Plane—an abomination, as if some criminal’s insides had been torn open.

*"You see a Plane,"* the Sphere explained, *"because you lack the understanding of light, shadow, and perspective. Just as a Hexagon in Flatland would seem a mere Line to one who cannot recognize shapes, this is truly a Solid. Feel it, and you will know."*

He guided my touch, and at last, I understood. Six faces, eight corners—a being of three dimensions, birthed from the motion of a Square through Space. I marveled that something as humble as I could, in theory, be the progenitor of such a magnificent form.

Still, the mysteries of light and depth eluded me. Patiently, the Sphere adjusted objects, shifted angles, and let me explore with touch until, at last, the truth crystallized. I could distinguish a Circle from a Sphere, a Plane from a Solid.

This moment was the pinnacle of my journey—a paradise of revelation. What follows is the story of my fall.

The Sphere would have continued, instructing me in Cylinders, Pyramids, and Dodecahedrons, but I interrupted. Not from weariness—no, my thirst had only grown.

*"My Lord,"* I ventured, *"you who I can no longer call the Perfection of Beauty, grant me one request: show me your *interior*."*

**Sphere:** *"My what?"*

**I:** *"Your insides—your stomach, your veins."*

**Sphere (irritated):** *"What madness is this? And why say I am no longer perfect?"*

**I:** *"Because you have taught me to seek One greater still! As you surpass Flatland, uniting Circles into a Sphere, surely there exists One above you—a being who unites Spheres into a higher form! And just as we now gaze down upon Flatland, seeing all things laid bare, so must there be a realm where *your* insides are visible to a still loftier eye!"*

The Sphere dismissed me with impatience. *"Enough! We have work to do—you must learn to preach the Gospel of Three Dimensions to your blind kin."*

But I pressed on. *"If you could lift me into the Third Dimension to see Flatland’s secrets, why not take me further—into the *Fourth*? There, we might gaze down upon Spaceland, piercing every Solid, uncovering the earth’s treasures, beholding the very guts of Spheres!"*

**Sphere:** *"There is no such land. The idea is absurd."*

**I:** *"Not to me! If a Line implies a Plane, and a Plane implies a Solid, does not a Solid imply a *higher* Dimension? Consider the progression: 2, 4, 8, 16—each dimension doubling the bounds of the last! And if a Cube moves into the Fourth, would it not produce a being with *sixteen* corners?"*

The Sphere fell silent, then grudgingly admitted that some in Spaceland spoke of visitations—beings appearing and vanishing without cause. *"But none call it a Fourth Dimension."*

**I (triumphant):** *"Then let me ascend! If this realm is Thought itself, so be it! Let me see the Cube unfold into a greater shape, and from there, rise further—through the Fifth, the Sixth, beyond!"*

I raved, intoxicated by truth, deaf to the Sphere’s warnings. Then—

A crash. A rush. A fall.

Down, down, down—back into Flatland.

The last thing I saw was that wretched, infinite Plane—my world, my prison—before darkness swallowed me.

When I awoke, I was a Square again, lying in my study, the familiar voice of my Wife calling me home.

The higher realms were lost to me. But the hunger remained.

The moment I returned, I knew I could not share the truth with my wife. Not out of fear, but because the reality of my journey would be incomprehensible to any inhabitant of Flatland. So I spun a simpler tale—an accidental fall through the cellar trapdoor, a brief unconsciousness. Even this mundane lie strained credulity, for gravity is weak here, and such tumbles are rare. Yet my wife, sharper than most of her sex, did not press the matter. She saw my agitation and insisted I rest. Grateful for the reprieve, I withdrew to my chamber, my mind ablaze with what I had witnessed.

Before sleep took me, I tried to reconstruct the mysteries of the Third Dimension—how a Square, moving in an impossible direction, could become a Cube. The words of the Sphere echoed in my mind: *"Upward, yet not Northward."* A mantra, a key. I clung to it as drowsiness pulled me under.

Then, the vision.

I stood once more beside the Sphere, his radiant form now calm, his earlier wrath softened into patience. Together, we drifted toward a speck of light, a point so small it seemed to defy existence itself. As we neared, a faint hum arose, a sound so delicate it barely registered until we halted—mere inches away in Spaceland terms.

*"Look,"* said the Sphere. *"You have known Flatland. You have glimpsed Lineland. You have soared through Spaceland. Now, I show you the lowest depth—Pointland, where all dimensions collapse into nothing."*

Before us hovered the Point, a solitary being imprisoned in its own infinitesimal universe. It knew nothing beyond itself—no length, no breadth, no other existence. It was all and nothing, content in its isolation.

*"Observe,"* the Sphere murmured. *"Its perfect self-satisfaction. A lesson: to be blindly content is to remain ignorant. To aspire is greater."*

A tinny voice emanated from the Point, chanting in hollow triumph:

*"It speaks of itself,"* the Sphere explained, *"as a child does, unable to conceive of anything beyond its own mind."*

The Point continued, lost in its solipsistic bliss:
*"It fills all Space, for It is all Space. What It thinks, It speaks; what It speaks, It hears. It is Thinker, Word, and Listener—One and All!"*

Disgusted, I shouted at the creature, desperate to shatter its delusion:

The Sphere silenced me. *"Watch."*

The Point, unshaken, glowed brighter, basking in what it perceived as its own divine wisdom.

*"You see?"* said the Sphere. *"Your words mean nothing to it. It twists all into self-praise. There is no reaching such a mind."*

As we withdrew, the Sphere spoke of higher mysteries—of Extra-Solids, of dimensions beyond even his own. He admitted his initial frustration with my hunger for the unknown, but now, he saw the value in aspiration.

*"Teach others,"* he urged. *"Let them see beyond the limits of their world."*

And so we returned, the hum of Pointland fading behind us, its lonely god still chanting in the void.

I awoke with the fire of revelation still burning within me. The truth of the Third Dimension—so vivid in my dreams—now demanded to be shared. I would be its prophet, spreading this divine geometry to all of Flatland, even to the Women and Soldiers, though their minds were deemed simple. My wife would be the first to hear the truth.

But as I prepared my words, a herald’s voice cut through the morning air, cold and commanding. The Council had spoken: any who preached delusions of other worlds would face arrest, imprisonment, or worse. My zeal faltered. Perhaps discretion was wiser. I would not speak of visions but of proof—pure, irrefutable mathematics. *"Upward, not Northward"*—that was the key. It had seemed so clear upon waking, yet now the certainty wavered, like a shape half-remembered.

My wife entered, curiosity sharp in her voice. She had seen the Circle visit, had wondered at his sudden appearance. I spun a tale—vague, inconsistent—enough to soothe her without betraying the truth. She left, unsatisfied but silent.

There was a safer way. My grandson, bright and pliable, his mind still unhardened by the rigid doctrines of Flatland. He had once mused on the mysteries of **3³**, earning the Sphere’s approval. A child would not know the Council’s decree; he would not betray me.

I called him in, barred the door, and took up our Lines. We reviewed the old lessons—how a Point becomes a Line, how a Line begets a Square. Then, with forced levity, I teased him: *"And what of your nonsense, boy? That a Square might move ‘Upward, not Northward’ and become something more?"*

Outside, the herald’s cry rang again. The proclamation. My grandson’s face changed—clever beyond his years, devout in his reverence for the Circles. Before I could press further, he laughed, a high, nervous sound. *"Grandfather, you joke! ‘Upward, not Northward’? What madness! How could a thing move neither here nor there but somewhere—impossible!"*

I seized a Square, shaking it as if to will the Third Dimension into being. *"Not Northward, but—here, like this—!"* My gestures were clumsy, desperate. He only laughed harder, convinced it was a game, and before I could reclaim the moment, he was gone, the door swinging behind him.

My first disciple had slipped through my fingers. The Gospel of Three Dimensions remained unspoken.

My failure to enlighten my Grandson did not extinguish my resolve, but it taught me caution. I could no longer rely on cryptic phrases like *"Upward, not Northward"* alone. The truth demanded a clearer vessel—words, meticulously arranged. And so, in secret, I began composing a treatise: *Through Flatland to Thoughtland*.

Yet the laws of our world bound me. I dared not speak of physical Dimensions outright, so I wove allegory—a realm where Thought might gaze upon Flatland and perceive the innards of all things at once, where a being could be encircled by six Squares and possess eight terminal Points. But how could I illustrate such wonders when our tablets hold only Lines? My diagrams were but variations of brightness and length, crude shadows of the truths I had witnessed. I feared few would grasp my meaning.

Life grew oppressive. The sights of Flatland taunted me, each shape a hollow mockery of the depth I knew existed. I neglected my duties, consumed by visions I could neither share nor fully recall. Months passed, and even the memory of the Cube began to fade behind my closed eyes. The uncertainty gnawed at me—had I ever truly seen it, or had the revelation itself slipped into dream?

Still, the fire within could not be contained. Among the highest Circles, I let slip dangerous words: *"the eye that discerns the interiors of things,"* *"the all-seeing land."* Once, I even uttered the forbidden—*"the Third Dimension."*

Then came my final reckoning. At a gathering of the Speculative Society, some fool proclaimed why Providence had wisely confined us to Two Dimensions. The absurdity broke me. I spoke openly—of my journey with the Sphere, of the Assembly Hall, of Space itself. At first, I feigned fiction, but passion tore the mask away. I pleaded with them to *see.*

The Council arrested me at once.

Before them, I stood where the Sphere once had, but no mercy followed. The President replaced the guards with cheaper, blunter Angles—a silent death warrant. My fate was sealed.

They asked only two questions:
1. Could I define *"Upward, not Northward"*?
2. Could I describe a Cube beyond mere angles?

I had no answer but the Truth.

Perpetual imprisonment was my sentence. *"If Truth wills your release,"* the President said, *"Truth will free you."* A hollow comfort.

Seven years have passed. My brother visits, faithful and kind, yet his mind remains closed. He *saw* the Sphere’s sections, heard its proofs—yet still denies the Third Dimension. I am a voice howling into a void, a martyr without converts.

Some days, hope flickers—that these words might escape, might ignite rebellion in *some* Dimension. But darker hours come, when even the Cube dissolves into doubt, when all of Flatland feels like delirium. The walls, these very tablets, seem phantoms.

*"Upward, not Northward"* echoes like a curse.

I am Prometheus of Flatland, chained not for bringing fire, but for bearing a light none can see.
