"Mist clogs the sunshine, / Smoky dwarf houses / Hem me round everywhere."
—Matthew Arnold.

The following afternoon, roughly twenty miles from Milton-Northern, the carriage turned onto the narrow branch line leading to Heston. Heston itself stretched as a single, elongated street running parallel to the coast. It possessed a distinct character, differing sharply from the southern bathing resorts, which in turn differed from those on the continent. To use a Scottish term, everything here appeared more "purpose-like." The country carts were heavier, laden with iron rather than wood and leather; the people in the streets, though ostensibly on holiday, moved with a busy, industrious intent. The colors were grayer, more enduring, lacking the fleeting gaiety of the south. There were no smock-frocks among the rural folk; they hindered movement and snagged on machinery, a fashion that had long since died out. In the southern towns Margaret had visited, shopmen, when not occupied, would lounge at their doors to enjoy the fresh air. Here, however, if they had a moment's leisure from customers, they made their shops their entire world—even, Margaret suspected, to the point of unnecessarily unrolling and re-rolling ribbons. These contrasts struck her keenly as she and her mother sought lodgings the next morning.

Their two nights in hotels had exceeded Mr. Hale's budget, and they were relieved to secure the first clean, cheerful rooms available. For the first time in days, Margaret felt a profound sense of rest. There was a dreamlike quality to this repose, making it feel almost luxurious. The distant sea lapped the sandy shore with a rhythmic sound; the cries of donkey-boys drifted nearby; unusual scenes unfolded before her like paintings she cared not to fully decipher before they faded. A stroll down to the beach to breathe the soft, warm sea-air, even in late November; the vast, misty sea-line touching the pale sky; the white sail of a distant boat turning silver in a faint sunbeam—it seemed she could dream her life away in such a luxury of pensiveness, making the present all-encompassing, daring neither to dwell on the past nor contemplate the future.

Yet the future must be faced, however stern. It was arranged that Margaret and her father would travel to Milton-Northern the following day to find a house. Mr. Hale had received several letters from Mr. Bell and a few from Mr. Thornton, and he was eager to clarify his position and chances of success there, a matter requiring a direct interview with the latter. Margaret knew they must move, yet she harbored a repugnance toward the idea of a manufacturing town and believed her mother was benefiting from the Heston air. She would have gladly deferred the journey.

For several miles before reaching Milton, a deep lead-colored cloud hung over the horizon, darkened by contrast against the pale gray-blue of the wintry sky. Frost had already touched Heston. Nearer the town, the air carried a faint taste and smell of smoke; perhaps it was more a loss of the fragrance of grass than a positive odor. They were whirled quickly over long, straight, seemingly endless streets of uniformly built brick houses. Here and there, a great oblong factory with many windows stood like a hen among her chicks, puffing out black, "unparliamentary" smoke, the source of the cloud Margaret had mistaken for rain. As they drove from the station to the hotel through the wider streets, they stopped constantly; great, heavily loaded wagons blocked the thoroughfares. Margaret had occasionally driven into the city with her aunt, but there the heavy vehicles seemed varied in purpose. Here, every van, wagon, and truck bore cotton, either in raw bags or woven bales of calico. People crowded the sidewalks, mostly well-dressed in material, yet with a slovenly looseness that struck Margaret as distinct from the shabby, threadbare smartness of a similar class in London.

"New Street," Mr. Hale noted. "I believe this is the principal street in Milton. Bell has often spoken of it. It was the opening of this street from a lane into a great thoroughfare thirty years ago that caused his property to rise so much in value. Mr. Thornton's mill must be nearby, for he is Mr. Bell's tenant, but I suspect he dates from his warehouse."

"Where is our hotel, papa?"

"Close to the end of this street, I believe. Shall we have lunch before or after we inspect the houses listed in the *Milton Times*?"

"Oh, let us get our work done first."

"Very well. Then I will only check if there is any note from Mr. Thornton, who promised to inform me of anything regarding these houses, and then we shall depart. We will keep the cab; it is safer than losing ourselves and arriving too late for the afternoon train."

There were no letters awaiting him. They set out house-hunting. Thirty pounds a year was all they could afford; in Hampshire, they might have secured a roomy house with a pleasant garden for that sum. Here, even the basic requirement of two sitting-rooms and four bedrooms seemed unattainable. They visited a list of properties, rejecting each as they went. Finally, they exchanged looks of dismay.

"We must settle for the second option, I think. That one in Crampton, don't they call the suburb? It had three sitting-rooms. Remember how we laughed at the disparity between the three sitting-rooms and only three bedrooms? But I have planned it all. The front room downstairs will be your study and our dining-room (poor papa!). You know we agreed that mother shall have as cheerful a sitting-room as possible. That front room upstairs, with its atrocious blue and pink paper and heavy cornice, actually had a pretty view over the plain, with a great bend of river or canal below. Then I could have the little bedroom behind, in that projection at the head of the first flight of stairs over the kitchen. You and mother could take the room behind the drawing-room, and that closet in the roof will make you a splendid dressing-room."

"But Dixon, and the girl we are to employ?"

"Oh, wait. I am overwhelmed by the discovery of my own genius for management. Dixon shall have—the back sitting-room. I think she will like that. She grumbles so much about the stairs at Heston. The girl will have that sloping attic over your room and mother's. Won't that suffice?"

"I dare say it will. But the papers. What taste! And the overloading of such a house with color and such heavy cornices!"

"Never mind, papa! Surely you can charm the landlord into re-papering one or two rooms—the drawing-room and your bedroom—for mother will be in contact with them most. Your bookshelves will hide much of that gaudy pattern in the dining-room."

"Then you consider it the best? If so, I had better go immediately to call on this Mr. Donkin, to whom the advertisement refers. I will take you back to the hotel, where you can order lunch and rest. By the time it is ready, I shall be with you. I hope I shall be able to procure new papers."

Margaret hoped so too, though she remained silent. She had never truly encountered the taste that adorns ornament, however poor, more than the plainness and simplicity which constitute the true framework of elegance.

Her father led her through the hotel entrance and, leaving her at the foot of the staircase, proceeded to the address of the landlord of the house they had selected. Just as Margaret placed her hand on the door of their sitting-room, a quick-stepping waiter followed her.

"I beg your pardon, ma'am. The gentleman departed so swiftly I had no time to inform him. Mr. Thornton called almost immediately after you left. Since I understood, from what the gentleman said, that you would return within an hour, I told him so. He came back about five minutes ago and requested to wait for Mr. Hale. He is in your room now, ma'am."

"Thank you. My father will return shortly, and then you may inform him."

Margaret opened the door and entered with the straight, fearless, dignified presence habitual to her. She felt no awkwardness; she possessed too many habits of society for that. Here was a person come on business to her father, and since he had shown himself obliging, she intended to treat him with a full measure of civility. Mr. Thornton, however, was considerably more surprised and discomfited. Instead of a quiet, middle-aged clergyman, a young lady advanced with frank dignity—a young lady of a different type from most he encountered. Her dress was plain: a close straw bonnet of the finest material and shape, trimmed with white ribbon; a large Indian shawl draped about her in long, heavy folds, worn as an empress wears her drapery. He did not recognize her, for he caught the simple, straight, unabashed look that indicated his presence was of no concern to her beautiful countenance, calling up no flush of surprise to the pale ivory of her complexion. He had heard Mr. Hale had a daughter but had imagined her to be a child.

"Mr. Thornton, I believe!" Margaret said after a half-second's pause, during which his words failed to come. "Will you sit down? My father brought me to the door not a minute ago, but unfortunately he was not informed of your arrival and has gone away on some business. He will return almost directly. I am sorry you have had the trouble of calling twice."

Mr. Thornton was accustomed to authority, yet she seemed to assume a kind of rule over him instantly. He had grown impatient at the loss of his time on a market day just before her appearance, yet now he calmly took a seat at her bidding.

"Do you know where Mr. Hale has gone? Perhaps I might find him."

"He has gone to a Mr. Donkin in Canute Street. He is the landlord of the house my father wishes to rent in Crampton."

Mr. Thornton knew the house. He had seen the advertisement and visited it in compliance with Mr. Bell's request to assist Mr. Hale, as well as motivated by his own interest in the case of a clergyman who had resigned under circumstances similar to Mr. Hale's. Mr. Thornton had initially thought the house in Crampton was ideal; but now, seeing Margaret with her superb manner of moving and bearing, he began to feel ashamed of having imagined it would suffice for the Hales, despite a certain vulgarity that had struck him during his initial inspection.

Margaret could not help her demeanor; the short, curled upper lip, the round, massive upturned chin, the way she carried her head, and her movements, full of a soft feminine defiance, always gave strangers the impression of haughtiness. She was tired and would much rather have remained silent to enjoy the rest her father had planned; yet, of course, she owed it to herself to be a gentlewoman and speak courteously to this stranger, albeit not over-brushed or over-polished after his rough encounter with the Milton streets and crowds. She wished he would leave, as he had once mentioned doing, instead of sitting there, answering her remarks with curt sentences. She had removed her shawl and hung it over the back of her chair. She sat facing him and the light; her full beauty met his gaze; her round, white, flexible throat rose from her full yet lithe figure; her lips moved slightly as she spoke, not breaking the cold, serene look of her face with any variation from that one lovely, haughty curve; her eyes, with their soft gloom, met his with quiet maiden freedom. He almost told himself that he did not like her before their conversation ended. He tried so hard to compensate for his mortified feeling that, while he looked upon her with an admiration he could not suppress, she looked at him with proud indifference, taking him, he thought, for what, in his irritation, he told himself he was—a great rough fellow with no grace or refinement. Her quiet coldness of demeanor he interpreted as contempt, and he resented it in his heart to the point of almost rising to leave and have nothing more to do with the Hales and their superciliousness.

Just as Margaret exhausted her last subject of conversation—which could hardly be called conversation, consisting of so few and short speeches—her father entered. With his pleasant, gentlemanly courtesy of apology, he reinstated his name and family in Mr. Thornton's good opinion.

Mr. Hale and his visitor had much to discuss regarding their mutual friend, Mr. Bell. Glad that her part in entertaining the visitor was over, Margaret went to the window to try to become more familiar with the strange aspect of the street. She became so absorbed in watching the activity outside that she barely heard her father until he repeated himself:

"Margaret! The landlord will persist in admiring that hideous paper, and I fear we must let it remain."

"Oh dear! I am sorry!" she replied, beginning to turn over in her mind the possibility of hiding part of it with some of her sketches, but giving up the idea as likely to make matters worse. Meanwhile, her father, with his kindly country hospitality, pressed Mr. Thornton to stay for luncheon. It would have been very inconvenient for him to do so, yet he felt he should have accepted if Margaret, by word or look, had seconded her father's invitation. He was glad she did not, yet he was irritated at her for not doing so. She gave him a low, grave bow when he left, and he felt more awkward and self-conscious in every limb than he ever had in his life.

"Well, Margaret, now to luncheon as fast as we can. Have you ordered it?"

"No, papa; that man was here when I arrived, and I never had an opportunity."

"Then we must take whatever we can get. He must have been waiting a long time, I'm afraid."

"It seemed exceedingly long to me. I was just at the last gasp when you came in. He never continued any subject but gave little, short, abrupt answers."

"Very much to the point, I should think. He is a clear-headed fellow. He said, did you hear? That Crampton is on gravelly soil and by far the healthiest suburb in the neighborhood of Milton."
