Across the way, if the gracious reader will deign to follow, he will find our young gentlemen at Lord Wrotham's residence, a property lent to the General for the duration of his stay. The little family party, familiar to us from Oakhurst and Tunbridge Wells, is assembled within. James Wolfe has pledged his attendance for dinner; yet James is currently attending upon Miss Lowther with such devoted fervor that he would trade the finest culinary creations of Lord Wrotham's kitchen, or the promised desserts, for a single glance from her eyes. You shall occupy the sixth seat; you may confidently assume Mr. Wolfe's place, for his absence is assured. As for me, I shall stand sentinel by the sideboard to chronicle the conversation.

First, observe the radiant countenances of the women. Recall when Harry Warrington was seized by the bailiffs; I had intended to recount how Mrs. Lambert, upon hearing of her son's plight, rushed to her husband. She implored, insisted, and beseeched that her Martin intervene. "Forget his recent rebellion," she argued, "forget his anger regarding the returned presents—anyone would be angry, especially a spirited lad like Harry! Disregard our poverty and the need for funds for the boys at college; there must be a way to extricate him. Did you not rescue Charles Watkins two years ago? He repaid every halfpenny, and your benevolence made a whole family happy. Blessed be God, Mrs. Watkins prays for you daily, and our fortunes have prospered since. Surely, this act will secure your promotion to Major-General!"

Martin Lambert, requiring little persuasion to perform a kindness, yielded readily. Having resolved to seek James Wolfe and post bail for Harry, he donned his hat, squeezed Theo's hand—who seemed to divine his purpose—or perhaps the sensible mother had inadvertently revealed it—and kissed little Hetty's flushed cheek before departing. Yet, the mother followed him to the doorway.

Once alone with him, the enthusiastic matron could no longer restrain her emotions. She threw her arms around her husband's neck, kissing him a hundred and twenty-five times in rapid succession, calling upon God to bless him, and weeping profusely upon his shoulder. In this display of conjugal sentimentality, she was discovered by Old Mrs. Quiggett, the lord's housekeeper, who bustled about the house and presumably found the scene astonishing.

"We have had a tiff and are making it up! Do not spread tales out of school, Mrs. Quiggett!" the gentleman declared, walking away.

"Well, I never!" Mrs. Quiggett exclaimed, emitting a shrill, strident laugh reminiscent of a venerable old cockatoo—a white, hook-nosed, long-lived bird she strongly resembled. "Well, I never!" she laughed again, shaking her old sides until her keys and, one might imagine, her ribs clattered and jingled.

"Oh, Quiggett!" sobbed Mrs. Lambert, "what a man that is!"

"You've been quarrelling, mum, and now you're making it up? That is right."

"Quarrel with him? He never told a greater story. My General is an angel, Quiggett. I should like to worship him. I should like to fall down at his boots and kiss them! There has never been a man so good as my General. What have I done to deserve such a husband? How dare I possess such a good man?"

"My dear, I think there is a pair of you," the old cockatoo replied. "And what would you like for your supper?"

When Lambert returned very late to the meal, announcing that Harry was free and that his brother had come to his senses to rescue him, one can imagine the commotion that ensued among those present! If Mrs. Lambert's General was an angel before, what office of humble devotion would she prefer in the evening? Little Hetty nestled silently against her father, drinking a drop from his glass. Theo and her mother's faces beamed with happiness, like two moons of brightness. After supper, the four of them fell to their knees at a certain signal, paying glad homage in awful mirth-rejoicing, with such pure joy as angels experience for the sinner who repents. Then came a great knocking at the door. My lord was miles away in the country; it was past midnight, so late had they talked. Mrs. Lambert likely guessed the visitor.

"This is George," a young gentleman announced, ushering another in. "We have been to Aunt Bernstein's. We could not go to bed without coming to thank you too. You dear, dear, good..." Speech ceased as Aunt Lambert kissed Harry, Theo snatched up Hetty—who was as pale as death—and hugged her back to life. George Warrington stood with his hat removed, and upon the conclusion of Harry's transaction, approached to kiss Mrs. Lambert's hand; the General passed his hand across his eyes. They were all in a tender, happy state. Generous hearts often feel this way when wrong is forgiven, peace restored, and love returns thought lost.

"We came from Aunt Bernstein's; we saw lights here and could not sleep without saying good-night," Harry explained. "Could we not, George?"

"'Tis certainly a famous nightcap you have brought us, boys," the General remarked. "When are you to dine with us? Tomorrow?" No, they must visit Madame Bernstein's tomorrow.

Then the next day? Yes, they would come the following day—the very day we are writing about, and the very dinner at which my gracious reader has just been invited to sit.

Why sit? Not to a mere Barmecide feast, but to hear the statement of Mr. George Esmond Warrington, which he was about to deliver. Here they all sat—not in the grand dining-room, but in the snug study or parlour before. The cloth was withdrawn, the General had toasted the King, the servants had departed, and the guests sat *conticentes*. After a moment of hemming and blushing, Mr. George proceeded:

"I recall sitting at the General's table when a little Philadelphia agent, whose wit and shrewdness we had noted at home, voiced objections to the conduct of the campaign, the disastrous outcome proving his justice. 'Of course,' he said, 'your Excellency's troops, once before Fort Duquesne, will never be able to resist such a general, such an army, such artillery. But do you calculate on the difficulty of reaching the place? Your march will be through woods almost untrodden, over roads you must make yourself, with a line four miles long. This slender column, forced through the forest, will face endless attacks in front, rear, and flank from enemies unseen, practiced in the dexterous laying of ambuscades.'

"'Psha, sir!' the General retorted. 'The savages may frighten your raw American militia—but the Indians will never make any impression on his Majesty's regular troops.'

"'I heartily hope not, sir,' said Mr. Franklin, sighing. Of course, the gentlemen of the General's family sneered at the postmaster as a pert civilian offering opinions beyond his comprehension.

"We despised the Indians on our own side, and our commander made light of them and their service. Our officers disgusted the allied chiefs with outrageous behavior toward their women. Only seven or eight remained with our force. Had we had a couple of hundred in our front on that fatal ninth of July, the event must have been vastly different. They would have repelled the French Indians, preventing the surprise and panic that ensued. It is known now that the French were ready to surrender, never dreaming of defense, and that the French Indians themselves remonstrated against the audacity of attacking such an overwhelming force.

"I was with the General and the main body when firing began. Aide-de-camp after another was sent forward. Initially, the enemy's attack was answered briskly; our men huzzahed with good heart. But soon our fire slackened, while from behind every tree and bush came single shots, laying man after man low. We marched in orderly line, skirmishers ahead, colors and two small guns in the center, baggage bringing up the rear, moving over open ground for a mile or two, flanked by thick, tangled brushwood. After brief frontal firing, the ambush opened from both sides of the surrounding wood. Men dropped rapidly, officers falling in greater numbers than the rank and file.

"At first, cheering and answering fire, our guns even opened on the wood, seemingly silencing the French ambush. But the hidden rifle-fire resumed. Men halted, huddled together, ignoring shouts and orders to advance, firing wildly into the brushwood with no effect. Those in front ran back to the main body, frightened and wounded, reporting five thousand Frenchmen and a legion of yelling Indian devils, scalping our people as they fell. We heard their cries as our men dropped. No one could be induced to move forward. One aide-de-camp after another was sent and never returned. Finally, it was my turn. I was sent with a message to Captain Fraser of Halkett's in front, which he never received, nor did I deliver.

"I had not gone thirty yards when a rifle-ball struck my leg, and I fell straightway. I recall a rush of Indians and Frenchmen, the former crying fiendish war-cries, the latter as fierce as their savage allies. I was amazed and mortified to see so few whitecoats; not above a score passed me, indeed fewer than fifty in the accursed action where two of the bravest British regiments were routed.

"One of them, half Indian, half Frenchman, wearing mocassins and a white uniform coat with a cockade, saw me prostrate. Turning back, he ran toward me, his musket clubbed to dash my brains out and plunder me. I had my little fusil, given to me by Harry before the campaign, fallen nearby. Seizing it, I felled the Frenchman dead at six yards. Saved for the moment, I bled heavily and fainted while trying to load, the weapon dropping from my lifeless hand.

"Scarcely in my senses, yells and shots ringing dimly, I saw an Indian busy over the Frenchman's body, glancing toward me as I lay bleeding. He rifled the corpse, tore open the coat, felt in the pockets, then scalped him. With his bleeding knife in his mouth, he advanced. I saw him coming through a film, in a dream, powerless to move or resist.

"He put his knee upon my chest. With one bloody hand, he seized my long hair, lifting my head. As he raised it, I saw a French officer rapidly advancing behind him.

'Good God! It was young Florac, my second in the duel at Quebec,' I cried. 'A moi, Florac! C'est Georges! aide moi!'

"He started, ran up at my cry, laid his hand on the Indian's shoulder, and commanded him to hold. But the savage did not understand French, or chose not to. Clutching my hair firmer, he waved his dripping knife, motioning the French lad to leave me to my prey. I could only cry out pitifully, 'A moi!'

''Ah, canaille, tu veux du sang? Prends!' said Florac, cursing. The next moment, with an ugh, the Indian fell over my chest dead, pierced by Florac's sword.

"My friend looked around. 'Eh! la belle affaire! Where art thou wounded? In the leg?' He bound my leg tightly with his sash. 'The others will kill thee if they find thee here. Ah, tiens! Put me on this coat, and this hat with the white cockade. Call out in French if any of our people pass. They will take thee for one of us. Thou art Brunet of the Quebec Volunteers. God guard thee, Brunet! I must go forward. 'Tis a general debacle, and the whole of your redcoats are on the run, my poor boy.' Ah, what a rout! What a day of disgrace for England!

"Florac's rough application stopped the bleeding. The kind creature helped me rest against a tree and load my fusil, placing it within reach to protect me from other marauders. He gave me the gourd of the unlucky French soldier I had slain, and the drink refreshed and invigorated me. Taking a mark of the tree and noting the bearings, the young lad hastened to the front. 'Thou seest how much I love thee, George,' he said, 'that I stay behind in a moment like this.' I forget whether I told thee, Harry, that Florac owed me money. I had won it from him at cards in Quebec, playing at his entreaty, and there was a difficulty in payment. I remitted his debt to me, and lighted my pipe with his note-of-hand. You see, sir, you are not the only gambler in the family.

"At evening, when the dismal pursuit ended, the faithful fellow returned with two Indians, each bearing reeking scalps. He informed them I was a Frenchman, his brother, wounded early in the day, to be carried back to the fort. They laid me in a blanket and carried me, groaning, with trusty Florac by my side. Had he left me, they would surely have plundered me and added my hair to the spoils hanging at their girdles. He promised brandy at the fort if they brought me safely there. I have but a dim recollection of the journey; the anguish of my wound was extreme, and I fainted more than once.

"We reached the fort's end. I was taken to the officer's log-house and laid upon Florac's own bed. Happy for me was my insensibility. I was brought in as a wounded French soldier of the garrison. I learned later that during my delirium, the few prisoners made on the day of our disaster had been brought under the walls of Duquesne by their savage captors, there horribly burned, tortured, and butchered by the Indians under the garrison's eyes.
