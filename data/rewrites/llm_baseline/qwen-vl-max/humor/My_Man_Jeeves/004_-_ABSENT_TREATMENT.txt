Let me tell you about Bobbie Cardew. It’s a tale so rich in moral instruction that even the most hardened skeptic might find himself nodding sagely, like a man who’s just realized he left his hat on the bus and can’t remember where he was going. I won’t pretend to write like a novelist—no flowery prose or poetic flourishes here. The story stands on its own merits, and those merits are entirely due to the lesson it teaches: if you’re a man, read this and shudder; if you’re a woman, read it and laugh—because it’s all about how one lady managed to make a gentleman feel profoundly, deeply, and rather permanently embarrassed.

Now, if you’ve only recently met Bobbie, you might be surprised to learn that once upon a time, he was known less for his charm and more for his memory—or lack thereof. In fact, it wasn’t uncommon for people to say, “Oh, poor Bobbie! He forgets everything!” And they weren’t exaggerating. I mean, I’ve been called a scatterbrain myself, but compared to Bobbie? I’m a walking encyclopedia of half-remembered facts. He was the human equivalent of a faulty alarm clock: set it, forget it, and hope someone else remembers to wake you up.

When I first knew him, Bobbie was the undisputed champion of absent-mindedness within a five-mile radius. If I wanted him to dinner, I’d send a letter Monday morning, a telegram Tuesday night, a phone call Wednesday afternoon, and finally—just thirty minutes before the appointed hour—a messenger in a taxi with strict instructions to stand at the door until Bobbie arrived, preferably still wearing his slippers and holding a sandwich.

And yet—he wasn’t *entirely* an idiot. Deep down, beneath layers of woolly thinking and vague intentions, there was a kernel of sense. But you had to blow it out with dynamite. Or so I thought.

Then came marriage—the real dynamite, the soul-shaking kind. That’s what changed Bobbie. You know how a puppy chases a bee? It sees it, thinks it’s fun, doesn’t understand the sting until it’s too late. That’s how Bobbie fell into love. One moment he was chasing a pretty nurse, the next he was married—on impulse, with a whoop, as though tying the knot was just another game of polo.

Her name was Mary Anthony. Five feet six inches tall, with hair like spun gold and a chin that could cut glass. She worked as a hospital nurse, which, to a man who’d never lifted a finger for work in his life, seemed oddly romantic—like a knight rescuing a damsel who also happened to be a qualified medical professional.

Bobbie broke his leg playing polo (a classic move), and Mary was assigned to look after him. Within a week, they were off to the registry office, arm-in-arm, like two kids skipping school. A true romance—except maybe not quite the kind you’d expect.

I met them both at the club one evening. Bobbie announced the news proudly. The next day, he introduced me to Mary. I liked her instantly. I’ve never worked a day in my life—my name’s Reggie Pepper, by the way. My uncle Edward ran Pepper, Wells & Co., coal merchants, and left me a tidy sum. So I don’t need to earn a living, but I do admire anyone who does—especially women who’ve had to fight for every penny. Mary had been orphaned early, raised herself, and clawed her way through life with grit and grace. I respected her.

We got along famously at first. Not anymore, but we’ll get to that. Back then, she looked at Bobbie like he was made of moonlight and magic. He returned the favor with equal enthusiasm. I figured, if only Bobbie remembered the wedding date, they might actually survive.

Fast forward a year. They’d moved into a flat. I dropped by often. Everything seemed perfect—until the quiet dinner incident.

I ran into Bobbie in Piccadilly. He invited me to dinner at home. Being the fool I am, I accepted. What was I thinking?

When we arrived, Mary was waiting. And let me tell you—she looked like a queen who’d just discovered she was about to be deposed. Her hair was piled high with diamonds, her dress was something from heaven, and her smile was pure ice. I thought, *If this is how she dresses for quiet dinners, no wonder Bobbie loves coming home.*

“Here’s old Reggie,” said Bobbie cheerfully. “I’ve brought him to share a bite. I’ll phone the kitchen—what?”

She stared. Then turned red. Then white. Then gave a tiny, strained laugh. I wanted to vanish. She recovered quickly.

“I’m so glad you could come, Mr. Pepper,” she said, smiling at me like a diplomat negotiating a peace treaty.

Dinner went smoothly. She joked, played ragtime, laughed. But I saw the strain. I saw the effort. I knew she was holding back a scream that would have shaken the foundations of the building. I left as soon as I could.

The next day, I found Bobbie at the club looking like a lost sock in a blizzard.

“Do you know how long I’ve been married?” he asked.

“About a year?”

“No,” he said, voice cracking. “Exactly a year—yesterday.”

Lightbulb moment.

“Yesterday was……?”

“Our anniversary. I’d planned to take Mary to the Savoy, then to Covent Garden. She really wanted to hear Caruso. I had the ticket in my pocket. All through dinner, I kept feeling like I’d forgotten something—but couldn’t place it.”

“Until your wife mentioned it?”

He nodded slowly.

“She mentioned it,” he said, as if the phrase held the weight of the world.

I didn’t ask for details. Women like Mary—strong, determined, with hair like fire and chins like armor—can be angels. But when they lose their patience, they don’t half-hearted about it.

“To be perfectly honest,” Bobbie said, voice breaking, “my stock’s pretty low at home.”

I lit a cigarette and said nothing. He didn’t want talk. He walked out. I watched him go. He stopped, hesitated, then entered a jeweler’s. That, I thought, was the first sign of intelligence in years.

From that point on, I became genuinely invested in Bobbie’s marital crisis. Most marriages are mildly interesting. This one was a full-blown disaster waiting to happen. Bobbie was a gentle soul, yes, but fundamentally a dunce. Mary was determined to fix him. Nature, however, was on Bobbie’s side. When Nature creates a dunce, she protects it with a special gift: short-term memory loss. It keeps the dunce forever a dunce. Take me, for instance. I’ve forgotten more things than I’ve learned. If I’d remembered half the advice I’ve been given, I’d be wearing a size nine hat. As it is, I wear a ten—and it’s still too big.

For a week, Bobbie was energized by shame. He remembered things. Elephants, I’ve read, have excellent memories. But they were no match for Bobbie during that week. Yet the shock was too small. It dented his mental armor, but didn’t break it. Soon, he was back to normal.

It was heartbreaking. Mary loved him. But she was afraid. She knew that if a man forgets his wedding anniversary after one year, he’ll forget he’s married at all by year four. She had to act now.

I tried to help. One afternoon, he came to me, distressed.

“It’s such a little thing,” he said, “but she made a fuss. I forgot to bring home that book she asked for.”

“She knows you forget things,” I said.

“Of course. But it’s not fair. I can’t remember anything. Never could.”

He rambled on. Then pulled out two sovereigns.

“Oh, by the way,” he said. “I owe you.”

“For the bet on Tuesday?”

“Yes. Murray and Brown playing hundred-up. I gave you two-to-one on Brown. Murray beat him by twenty.”

“So you remember some things?”

He bristled. “Of course I do! Don’t think I’m the sort who forgets to pay debts!”

“Subside, laddie,” I said.

Then I spoke like a father.

“What you need to do, my dear old friend,” I said, “is pull yourself together. You’re heading for trouble. You’ve shown you *can* remember things—like bets. So why not birthdays? Anniversaries? Make it part of your list. It may hurt your brain, but you can’t avoid it.”

“I suppose you’re right,” he said. “But why does she care so much about dates? Does it matter if I forget when we got married or when she was born or when the cat had the measles? She knows I love her.”

“That’s not enough for a woman,” I said. “They want to *see* it. Remember that. Forget it, and there’ll be trouble.”

He chewed his cane.

“Women are frightfully strange,” he said.

“You should have thought of that before marrying one,” I replied.

I did what I could. I laid it bare. But Bobbie didn’t listen. He went back to his old ways.

Weeks passed. Then, one spring morning, I was smoking by the window when Bobbie burst in, eyes bulging, face pale as a ghost, waving a piece of paper.

“Reggie!” he cried. “She’s gone!”

“Who?”

“Mary! Left me! Gone!”

“Where?”

Silly question? Maybe. But Bobbie nearly foamed at the mouth.

“How should I know? Read this!”

I took the letter. It was brief, sharp, devastating:

> MY DEAR BOBBIE,—I am going away. When you care enough about me to remember to wish me many happy returns on my birthday, I will come back. My address will be Box 341, _London Morning News_.

I read it twice.

“Well,” I said, “why don’t you?”

“Why don’t I what?”

“Why don’t you wish her many happy returns? Seems simple.”

“But she says ‘on her birthday.’”

“And when is that?”

“Can’t you understand?” he snapped. “I’ve forgotten.”

“Forgotten?”

“Yes.”

“How?” I asked. “Just the date? Or completely?”

“I know it was somewhere between January 1st and December 31st. That’s how close I get.”

“Think.”

“Think? What’s the use? I’ve been trying since I opened the letter. I’ve knocked sparks out of my skull.”

I rang for drinks.

“Well,” I said, “this is a tough case. Suppose someone came to Sherlock Holmes and said, ‘Mr. Holmes, when is my wife’s birthday?’ Even Sherlock would blink. But I know enough to know we need clues. Can you remember the last birthday? What was the weather?”

“Ordinary.”

“Warm?”

“Warmish.”

“Cold?”

“Fairly cold, maybe. I can’t recall.”

I ordered more drinks. “You’re a great help, Bobbie. Truly invaluable.”

Then—miracle of miracles—he had an idea.

“I’ve got it!” he said. “I gave her a present last year. We just go to the shop, find the receipt, and—done!”

“What did you give her?”

He slumped. “I can’t remember.”

But then—another idea! Like golf, sometimes inspiration comes in bursts. He suggested buying twelve little books called *When Were You Born?* Each month has its own volume, telling you your character, talents, weaknesses—all for fourpence halfpenny. We’d buy all twelve and find which one matched Mary.

Clever plan. We bought them. Half each. We sat down.

But every month described Mary perfectly. December: “Keeps secrets, travels widely.” Mary had kept her secret and traveled far. October: “Original ideas, loves movement.” Exactly her style. February: “Wonderful memory.” Yes, indeed.

Bobbie favored May: “Capricious—barrier to happiness.” I preferred February: “Determined, earnest, expects full return.” He agreed.

Eventually, he tore the books up, stamped on them, burned them, and went home.

But something had changed. Bobbie was thinking. Really thinking. For the first time in his life, he was using his brain. It was painful to watch, but I knew it was good. He was improving. Maybe not becoming brilliant, but becoming… less of a disaster.

He came to me often. I gave him sympathy. And always, when he was weak, I delivered the Moral Lesson.

One day, he arrived with a glimmer of hope.

“Reggie,” he said, “I’ve got it. I remember something vital.”

“What?”

“We went to the Coliseum on her birthday. That helps, doesn’t it?”

“It’s a start,” I said. “But how?”

“They change the program weekly. I remember one act—Professor Somebody’s Terpsichorean Cats.”

“Ah! Now we’re talking.”

So we went to the Coliseum. They dug through archives and found the cats performed in mid-May.

“I told you it was May,” Bobbie said triumphantly.

“If you’ve any sense,” I said, “there won restrained.”

He agreed.

That night, my phone rang. Bobbie.

“Reggie,” he said, “I’ve got it. We saw the cats at a matinee.”

“Ah. So it must have been Wednesday the 7th or Saturday the 10th.”

“Unless they had daily matinees,” I said.

I heard a groan.

Then I remembered: “You had lunch at the Ritz that day. You wrote a cheque.”

“For ten pounds. To the hotel. Check your book.”

He gasped. “Reggie, you’re a genius!”

He called back.

“It was the 8th. Reggie, old man, I——”

“Topping,” I said. “Good night.”

I rang the hotel near the Strand.

“Put me through to Mrs. Cardew.”

“It’s late,” said the operator.

“And getting later,” I said. “Hurry up.”

She answered.

“What is it?”

“My feet are cold,” I said. “But I’m calling to tell you—Bobbie’s remembered.”

She screamed.

I smiled. I’d done my job.

And that, my dear reader, is the story of how one man learned the hard way that love isn’t just feeling—it’s remembering.
