For a decade, the annals of the Whateleys sank indistinguishably into the morbid sediment of Dunwich, a community hardened by their own peculiarities and accustomed to the annual orgies of May Eve and All-Hallows. Twice yearly, fires blazed atop Sentinel Hill, triggering mountain rumblings that grew in violent crescendo; meanwhile, strange portents haunted the lonely farmhouse at all seasons. Callers claimed to hear sounds emanating from the sealed upper story even when the entire family remained below, speculating on the duration of sacrifices involving cows or oxen. Talk arose of reporting to the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, yet nothing came of it, for Dunwich folk possess no desire to invite the scrutiny of the outside world.

Around 1923, when Wilbur was a boy of ten whose mind, voice, stature, and prematurely bearded face projected an aura of maturity, a second great siege of carpentry commenced within the old house. Confined to the sealed upper section, discarded lumber suggested that the youth and his grandfather had demolished all internal partitions and stripped away the attic floor, leaving a single, vast open void spanning the distance between the ground story and the peaked roof. They had also razed the great central chimney, fitting the rusty range with a flimsy exterior tin stove-pipe. In the spring following this renovation, Old Whateley noted an increasing influx of whippoorwills emerging from Cold Spring Glen to chirp beneath his window at night. He regarded this phenomenon with grave significance, telling the loungers at Osborn's that his time had nearly arrived. "They whistle jest in tune with my breathin' naow," he rasped. "An' I guess they're gittin' ready to ketch my soul. They know it's a-goin' aout, an' dun't calc'late to miss it. Yew'll know, boys, arter I'm gone, whether they git me er not. Ef they dew, they'll keep up a-singin' an' laffin' till break o' day. Ef they dun't, they'll kinder quiet daown like. I expeck them an' the souls they hunts fer hev some pretty tough tussles sometimes."

On Lammas Night, 1924, Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was hastily summoned by Wilbur Whateley, who had lashed his solitary horse through the darkness and telephoned from Osborn's in the village. He found Old Whateley in a critical state, his cardiac action and stertorous breathing signaling an imminent end. By the bedside stood the shapeless albino daughter and the oddly bearded grandson, while from the vacant abyss overhead came a disquieting suggestion of rhythmical surging, akin to waves lapping upon a level beach. Yet, Dr. Houghton was chiefly disturbed by the chattering night birds outside—a seemingly limitless legion of whippoorwills crying an endless message in repetitions timed diabolically to the dying man's wheezing gasps. It was uncanny, unnatural, excessive, thought the doctor, mirroring the reluctant region he had entered.

Toward one o'clock, Old Whateley regained consciousness, interrupting his wheeze to choke out instructions for his grandson. "More space, Willy, more space soon. Yew grows--an' _that_ grows faster. It'll be ready to sarve ye soon, boy. Open up the gates to Yog-Sothoth with the long chant that ye'll find on page 751 _of the complete edition_, an' _then_ put a match to the prison. Fire from airth can't burn it nohaow!" He spoke with obvious madness. After a pause, during which the flock of whippoorwills outside adjusted their cries to the altered tempo while distant hill noises stirred, he added another fragment. "Feed it reg'lar, Willy, an' mind the quantity; but dun't let it grow too fast fer the place, fer ef it busts quarters or gits aout afore ye opens to Yog-Sothoth, it's all over an' no use. Only them from beyont kin make it multiply an' work.... Only them, the old uns as wants to come back...." Speech gave way once more to gasps, prompting Lavinia to scream as the whippoorwills seemed to follow the shift in rhythm. This cycle persisted for over an hour until the final throaty rattle ceased. Dr. Houghton drew shrunken lids over the glazing gray eyes as the bird tumult faded imperceptibly into silence. Lavinia sobbed, but Wilbur only chuckled, the hill noises rumbling faintly behind him. "They didn't git him," he muttered in his heavy bass voice.

By this time, Wilbur was a scholar of tremendous, one-sided erudition, quietly known by correspondence to librarians guarding rare and forbidden texts in distant repositories. He was increasingly hated and dreaded around Dunwich due to vague suspicions regarding certain youthful disappearances, yet he silenced all inquiry through fear or by deploying the fund of old-time gold that flowed regularly—and increasingly—for cattle-buying, just as in his grandfather's era. Now tremendously mature in aspect, his height, having reached the normal adult limit, seemed inclined to wax beyond it. In 1925, when a scholarly correspondent from Miskatonic University visited and departed pale and puzzled, Wilbur stood fully six and three-quarters feet tall. Throughout the years, he had treated his half-deformed albino mother with growing contempt, eventually forbidding her to accompany him to the hills on May Eve and Hallowmass. By 1926, the poor creature complained to Mamie Bishop of her terror of him. "They's more abaout him as I knows than I kin tell ye, Mamie," she whispered. "An' naowadays they's more nor what I know myself. I vaow afur Gawd, I dun't know what he wants nor what he's a-tryin' to dew."

That Hallowe'en, the hill noises roared louder than ever, and fire burned on Sentinel Hill as custom dictated, but villagers paid more attention to the rhythmical screaming of vast flocks of unnaturally belated whippoorwills assembled near the unlighted Whateley farmhouse. After midnight, their shrill notes erupted into a pandemoniac cachinnation filling the countryside, quieting only at dawn. Then they vanished, hurrying southward, a month overdue. No one could be certain of the meaning until later; none of the countryfolk seemed to have perished—but poor Lavinia Whateley, the twisted albino, was never seen again.

In the summer of 1927, Wilbur repaired two sheds in the farmyard and began moving his books and effects outward. Shortly thereafter, Earl Sawyer reported to the loungers at Osborn's that further carpentry was underway in the Whateley farmhouse. Wilbur was sealing all doors and windows on the ground floor, appearing to remove partitions as he and his grandfather had done upstairs four years prior. Living in one of the sheds, he seemed unusually worried and tremulous. People generally suspected him of knowledge concerning his mother's disappearance, and very few approached his neighborhood now. His height had increased to more than seven feet, showing no signs of ceasing its relentless development.
