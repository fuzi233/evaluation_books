The air inside the theater was thick with a sudden, collective exhale. The lethargy that had draped the company like a wet woolen coat evaporated instantly. To the members of the Primrose Way, the news that Fillmore Nicholas had injected twenty thousand dollars into the abyssal depths of their production was indistinguishable from the arrival of the Millennium. The assistant stage manager, once a creature of shadows, now stood elevated to the dizzying altitude of proprietor. This transformation resonated with their theatrical instincts; they had performed similar ascents for persecuted heroines around eleven o'clock, and the scenario felt structurally sound. With Miss Hobson removed, the blight was gone. Optimism bloomed, fueled by rumors from a bowler-hatted Teddy via a lift-boy and a cigar-stand girl at the Pontchartrain regarding imminent lifts on bans and openings in Toledo. Fate, it seemed, had abandoned its campaign to keep good men down.

Fillmore Nicholas, restored to his former self, expressed his elation through the acquisition of a new waistcoat, a hundred half-dollar cigars, and a fussiness over lunch that suggested a sudden expansion of his girth. Where the serf managing paper-knives had been almost slim, the manager now possessed soft, billowy curves. Standing on the sidewalk before the theater, he blocked the path of the populace, forcing them to detour around the legend emblazoned on the new posters: *FILLMORE NICHOLAS PRESENTS*.

Yet, amidst this bubbling joy, Sally, the fairy godmother whose magic had conjured the miracle, remained strangely unmoved. A faint shadow lingered at the back of her mind, refusing to emerge into the light. Enlightenment arrived only later, during a drive on Belle Isle with Gerald. Since Miss Hobson's departure, Gerald had also succumbed to the sunshine of prosperity; his moodiness vanished, replaced by expansive plans. "If this play gets over—and it's going to—I'll show 'em!" he declared, his jaw squared, eyes glowing with the promise of a future where everyone would be after him.

Sally stopped listening. The afternoon warmth had already surrendered to the evening chill, the wind rising from the river carrying a sharp edge. Suddenly, the obstruction in her mind crystallized. She perceived the fundamental divergence between male and female outlooks. Men worshipped success as a deity, straining every nerve to win the race while ignoring the scenery and the companions left by the wayside. Fillmore, Gerald—they viewed life solely as a competition. Women, conversely, were relegated to the status of an afterthought, a spare-time accessory.

To Gerald, success was the summit; to Sally, he himself was the destination. Without the trappings of triumph, he was sufficient. But she was not sufficient for him. A spasm of futile jealousy seized her, followed by a shiver.

"Cold?" Gerald asked, oblivious. "I'll tell the man to drive back... I don't see any reason why this play shouldn't run a year in New York. Everybody says it's good..."

Sally stared out at a world that had lost its color. The sky was a leaden grey, and the wind from the river blew with a dismal, final chill.
