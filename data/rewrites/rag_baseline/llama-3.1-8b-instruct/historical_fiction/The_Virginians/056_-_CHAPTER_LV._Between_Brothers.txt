The room was dimly lit, the only sound the creaking of old wooden floorboards beneath his feet. Harry and George sat across from each other, their conversation meandering through memories of home. Harry spoke of his dogs, of little Dempster, and good old Nathan, their stories unfolding like the threads of a worn tapestry. He asked after Mountain, and Fanny's growth into a pretty young woman, and the engagement between Parson Broadbent's daughter and Tom Barker of Savannah. George listened intently, his expression a mixture of amusement and wistfulness.

As they spoke, George couldn't help but think of his own interactions with Colonel G. Washington's wife, a woman of good fortune and pleasant disposition. He recalled the visit to Richmond, the ceremonial politeness exchanged between the two women, and his mother's good-natured teasing about the Colonel's tall stature. "Never mind, George, my dear!" she had said, "The Colonel has taken another wife, but I feel certain that at one time two young gentlemen I know of ran a very near chance of having a tall stepfather six feet two in his boots." George smiled at the memory, his mind drifting back to the countless games of cards and tea-drinking sessions with his family.

But beneath the surface of their conversation, a current of tension flowed. George felt the weight of his birthright, the knowledge that he was the younger son, destined for a life of secondary importance. He thought of his mother's haughty demeanor towards the Indian woman, Biche, and the way she had dismissed the rumors of a possible romance between the Colonel and the servant. "I should as soon fancy Mr. Esmond stealing the spoons, or marrying a negro woman out of the kitchen," she had said, her words a barb that still lingered in George's mind.

As the evening wore on, George's thoughts turned to his own life, his struggles to find his place in the world. He felt like a ghost hovering on the periphery of his family's affairs, a presence without substance or purpose.
