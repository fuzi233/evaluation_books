“Moses, trotz seiner Befeindung der Kunst, dennoch selber ein großer Künstler war und den wahren Künstlergeist besaß. Nur war dieser Künstlergeist bei ihm, wie bei seinen ägyptischen Landsleuten, nur auf das Kolossale und Unverwüstliche gerichtet. Aber nicht wie die Ägypter formierte er seine Kunstwerke aus Backstein und Granit, sondern er baute Menschenpyramiden, er meisselte Menschenobelisken, er nahm einen armen Hirtenstamm und schuf daraus ein Volk, das ebenfalls den Jahrhunderten trotzen sollte . . . er schuf Israel!”—HEINE: *Geständnisse*.

Imagine the gulf between Daniel Deronda who sailed for Genoa, unmoored and adrift, and the man who stepped back onto English soil. He had left not knowing if the truths he was about to uncover would pull him far from the quiet, half-cherished paths his heart had begun to wander down—paths that led straight back to Mirah Lapidoth, ever since that rainy evening in Chelsea when her tearful farewell look had wrapped itself around his soul, settling there as soft and permanent as ivy on old stone. He returned now not just with answers, but a charter: proof of the heritage he had never known he ached for, a duty he had been unknowingly groomed for all his life, sweeter than any empty freedom ever could have been. Now he dared finally name the secret love he had hidden even from himself: that quiet, reverent devotion to Mirah that had made every other woman’s charm feel thin and hollow by comparison, even when Gwendolen’s desperate, clinging dependence on him had stirred only self-sacrificing pity, no romantic fire.

He had fought this feeling for months, telling himself it was wrong to woo her, when the circumstances that had brought them together made any advance feel like an unfair imposition—until his own mother had snapped at him, mid-confrontation, that he was in love with a Jewess, and he had realized that to lie about it, to evade the truth, would be an unforgivable betrayal. That moment of decisive admission had unlocked a flood of energy he had never known he held: no more self-suppression, no more second-guessing every impulse. The closer he drew to England, the more every remaining mile felt like an unbearable delay. It was as if finding his ancestry had gifted him a second soul: no longer was he adrift in the vague, impartial sympathy of a man with no roots, no people, no purpose. Now he had a path, a community, a mission he could throw his whole heart into—and he ached to share it first with Ezra, Mirah’s brother, the man who had lit that flame of purpose in him long before he knew the truth of his own birth. He had no real fear Hans Meyrick would win Mirah’s heart, but he knew how shocking it would be for a woman who had only ever seen him as a steady, reliable protector to suddenly find he wanted to be her lover. He meant to build that transition gently, step by soft step.

No wonder he went straight from the London railway terminus to their tiny Brompton square lodgings, without stopping first to drop his bags, even though the sun had already set, and he had promised Lady Mallinger he would visit her at the Abbey the very next day. He was desperate to deposit his precious family chest with Ezra, to let his old friend pore over its ancient manuscripts at his side, and nothing could have felt more right than to walk through their door unannounced, his chest thrumming with a joy he could barely contain. He hardly even noticed his own ordinary summer drab suit, though the faint flush in his cheeks, the bright, unguarded glow in his eyes, would have made even the plainest clothing look like the regalia of the long-awaited heir Ezra had spent years waiting for. He slipped into the house as quietly as a shadow.

It was the very same evening Mirah had returned shattered from her brutal, devastating meeting with her father. Ezra, shaken to his core by her sobs and the cruel memories the encounter had dragged to the surface, had stopped sorting through his papers in the first moments of his distress; pages lay scattered across the floor, neither of them had bothered to tidy them. They had sat perfectly still for what felt like hours, hand in hand, listening to the mantel clock tick while the light faded to dusk. Mirah had thrown off her travel cloak and sunk down beside him, too sick at heart to think of eating, watching his face fall slack, his breathing grow shallow, looking so pale and still she was certain he would slip away from her at any moment. Her grief was doubled now: she had lost her mother, she had thought she was free of her father, and now he was back to haunt her, and she feared she had broken her one remaining brother with the violence of her tears. Her oval cheeks were pale, her dark eyes glistening with the faint leftover sheen of unshed young tears, her curls tangled like a child’s just woken from a nightmare, and the future stretched out before her nothing but a repeat of all the old miseries she had thought she was finally escaping.

Then the front door creaked open, and a familiar, soft voice called out: “Daniel Deronda—may he come in?”

“Come! Come at once!” Ezra cried, leaping to his feet, his face lighting up as if he had seen Deronda that very morning and expected this visit all day long, no trace of surprise in his shining eyes. Mirah started up, her face flooding with hot color, her heart thrumming with a confused, half-alarmed, half-delighted flutter.

But when Deronda stepped over the threshold, the sight of him felt like bright, clear sunlight breaking through a storm. He reached out to take Mirah’s hand in his right, rested his left light and warm on Ezra’s shoulder, and stood that way for a long quiet moment, holding both of them close, reading the traces of grief on their faces, before he leaned forward, voice soft with anxiety. “Has anything happened? Any trouble?”

“Speak no words of trouble now,” Ezra cut in, saving Mirah the need to find an answer. “There is joy written all over your face—let that joy be ours first.”

Mirah thought to herself that he must carry some secret too big to share, too wonderful to put into ordinary words. They all sat down, Deronda pulling his chair right up close in front of Ezra.

“You are right,” he said, his voice thick with feeling. “I carry a joy that will hold for us even through the worst of trouble. I did not tell you why I traveled abroad, Ezra, because there was no use in speaking of possibilities until I knew the truth. I went to learn my parentage. And you were right, all along. I am a Jew.”

The two men clasped hands, the jolt of the moment so vivid it shot through Mirah like an electric shock. Deronda did not pause, speaking as much from Ezra’s heart as his own. “We are of the same people. Our souls hold the same calling. Nothing on earth, not life nor death, will ever separate us now.”

Ezra answered in a quiet, reverent whisper, the ancient Hebrew liturgical words that had bound generations of their people to their faith: “Our God and the God of our fathers.”

The weight of the moment was too heavy for the usual flow of Ezra’s passionate, quick words, and he fell silent for a long moment, breath catching.

Mirah sank to her knees by her brother’s side, staring up at his suddenly luminous face, which had looked so deathly pale not ten minutes before. The motion was entirely instinctive, the only possible outlet for the sudden tidal wave of gladness that washed all her despair away, as solemn and sacred as if she was witnessing a holy rite. For that moment, she thought only of what this news meant for her brother’s long, suffering life, for the dream he had carried for decades that was finally coming true.

“It is not only that I am a Jew,” Deronda went on, smiling that rare, bright smile that broke through his usual quiet gravity like sun over a lake. “I come from a long line of Sephardic Jews, the Charisi family, who spent generations devoting themselves to our people’s scholarship and good works. My grandfather, Daniel Charisi, guarded a chest full of ancient manuscripts, family records stretching back centuries, hoarding them for the grandson he hoped would come one day to carry on his work. His wish has come true, after all the years of people trying to hide my heritage from me. That chest is downstairs right now, in the house with us. I want to leave it here with you, Ezra, so we can pore over every page together. I can read the Spanish and Italian manuscripts easily enough, and we will work through the Hebrew and the Arabic ones side by side, just as you always dreamed.”

When his bright, happy glance drifted from Ezra to Mirah, it felt like too much warm sunshine on her skin, and she shifted, flushing bright, rising from her knees to sit back in her usual chair, hands folded tight in her lap, trying to still her pounding heart. Deronda, sensitive as ever, thought he had let too much of his hidden love show in his gaze, that he had startled her, that he had revealed a feeling she did not return. He was terrified that any clumsy advance would ruin the perfect peace of this moment, turn their easy, gentle bond into something awkward, something that would make Mirah wretched in all the inevitable days they would spend together, caring for Ezra. If she did not love him back, he would never force that weight on her.

While the two of them sat wrapped in their own quiet, flustered thoughts, Ezra was already speaking, his old eloquence fully restored, bright with exultation. “Daniel, I told you from the very first that we do not know all the paths our lives walk. Look how they wove together, as if one single soul was drawing all the scattered pieces to itself, feeding on every small kind act, every quiet choice, to bring us exactly here. The misguided, unloving choices of other people tried to keep you from your birthright, but your own good, loving will overcame them—you cared for my sister, you sought me out when I was lost, and that was the path that made your soul ready to receive this news, to hear the call of all your waiting brethren.”

“It is true you and Mirah were my teachers,” Deronda said gently. “If I had learned this truth before I met you two, I think I would have rebelled against it. I would have thought, given the choice, I would never have chosen to be a Jew. But now? My whole being consents to it, completely. It was the slow, steady accord between our hearts, the trust we built little by little, that brought me to that full, willing acceptance.”

He thought vividly of that first afternoon in the old bookshop, when he had stood back, distant and doubtful of Ezra’s wild, prophetic confidence, and he leaned forward, voice warm with new fervor. “It was your inspiration that showed me what my life’s work could be. All my life, I have carried a vague, inherited longing for a purpose, a mission that would make me feel like the heart and brain of a community—no prize I had to fight for for myself, but a duty that found me. You have shown me that work: to bind our people back together, beyond all the divides of doctrine, to remind them they were one nation long before they split into quarreling sects. I am going to do that work, in your spirit. Failure will not be shameful, but it would be a thousand times more shameful if I did not even try.”

“Even as my own brother that fed at my mother’s breast,” Ezra murmured, sinking back in his chair, his face soft with the repose of a man who has seen his life’s greatest hope come to pass.

For Mirah, the moment felt like dawn breaking over a dark land she had wandered in for years, revealing paths she had never dared imagine could exist. But after a moment of quiet, Ezra started to ask about Deronda’s parents, then caught himself, and said he would not press for any secrets Deronda did not choose to share.

“Gradually, in time, you will know all of it,” Deronda said. “But now tell me of your days, while I was gone. I can see there has been trouble. Mirah has been so distressed about something.”

Mirah turned quickly to Ezra, begging him with her eyes not to speak of her father, not to taint this perfect, holy new joy with the old disgrace that clung to her family, that would now cling to Deronda too, by his own choice. She rose to get her hat and cloak, to retreat to her room and leave the two of them to speak freely, but Ezra spoke first.

“Today we faced a great grief. A duty we thought long buried returned to find us, bringing dread instead of gladness. But for now, we are free of its yoke. Let us speak no more of it tonight. This evening is the start of our festival—we offer our first fruits of joy here, and we will mix no mourning with them.”

Deronda respected the quiet boundary, rising to his feet as he saw Mirah stand. “I must leave very soon, to help Mrs. Adam bring the chest up from the carriage, and give you the key, Ezra. May I call you Ezra now? I have heard Mirah call you that for so long, it feels like the right name for the man who is my brother.”

“Please call him Ezra,” Mirah said, her voice faint and soft, her skin flushing under his gaze. Was he truly different, or was all the new warmth she felt just in her own heart? She was so worn out, faint from no food and hours of tears, that Deronda saw her hands tremble, and his whole ached to reach for her, to hold her steady, but he dared not. She held out her hand to him with a small, wobbly smile, and he opened the door for her to step out. That was all.

A man of refined pride might shrink from confessing love to a woman of far higher rank or greater wealth, for fear his motives would be questioned—but Deronda’s difficulty was far softer, far more tender. He knew Mirah felt she owed him a debt of gratitude larger than she could ever repay, and he feared that if he told her he loved her, she would think his affection was a demand that she pay him back by becoming his, that she would say yes out of obligation, not desire. The thought of forcing that weight on her, of making their quiet, loving care of Ezra feel like a burden, was unbearable to him. He would never speak of his love until he was certain, beyond any doubt, that her heart had already chosen him first.

Even a man who has grown glib and cynical from years of casual courtship can fall prey to the lover’s awe eventually, trembling and stammering in the face of a love that shakes every part of him—so how could Deronda, whose whole life had kept his heart tender, his capacity for passion fresh as new leaves by a mountain spring, not shake a little at the very thought of telling Mirah Lapidoth what she meant to him?
