At the Wells, Madame de Bernstein found ample distraction in her card games and quiet scoldings of her niece, content enough with the social rhythm of the place. Meanwhile, young Harry Warrington, still in the bloom of twenty, found himself perfectly at ease among the idle pleasures of Tunbridge. For a man of his years, few things mattered more than good company, fresh air, and a steady stream of sport and amusement. At that age, even the simplest delight feels like a triumph—every moment anticipated with eager hope, every day counted down until pleasure arrives. But as life draws on, we grow cautious, distant, and our memories turn nostalgic, recalling the joy of youth with a soft, wistful glance.

Do you not remember your first ball, Madam? How the music swelled, the lights danced, and the world seemed full of possibility? And now, sitting quietly by the fire while your daughters twirl through their dances, does not your mind drift back to those golden hours? I, for one, can recall walking three miles across the countryside just to dine with old Captain Jones—a man whose half-pay port came from the village wine merchant and cost little more than a shilling. Yet it tasted richer then, not because of the wine, but because of the warmth of the welcome, the glow of friendship, and the unburdened spirit of youth. Viximus nuper—we have lived recently, indeed.

I do not judge Harry’s idleness too harshly, as Colonel Lambert might. For there is something noble in leisure when it is free from guilt or purposelessness. Oh, blessed idleness! Divine, languid nymph! Bring me a novel as I lie wrapped in my dressing gown at three o’clock in the afternoon; mix me a sherry cobbler, and light a cigar. You may call me lazy, accuse me of sloth, name you a thousand sins—but you cannot deny that you are the most charming companion in all the world.

Lord March had departed northward, and Lord Chesterfield, disappointed by the waters’ failure to cure his deafness, returned to his solitude at Blackheath. But others remained—gentlemen who sought only recreation and relaxation—and Harry found himself well-liked among them. At the White Horse ordinary, he quickly learned to order a French dinner as deftly as any man from St. James’s, conversed fluently with Monsieur Barbeau in French, and developed a discerning eye for fine wines—able to distinguish between Clos Vougeot and Romande with remarkable accuracy.

He was the young king of the Wells, where the regulars were men of the world—easygoing, worldly, and indifferent to the rumors of gallantry and extravagance that clung to Harry’s name. Such tales had frightened Mr. Wolfe, but they meant little to these seasoned revelers. Though Harry moved freely among them, swimming in the same waters as the most carefree, he possessed a natural honesty and sharp wit that kept him clear of traps laid for the unwary.

He rarely made foolish bets with the idle fellows around him, and even the most experienced gamblers found it hard to outwit him. He played games—indoors and out—not from recklessness, but from genuine skill and love of competition. He never gambled with strangers, always settled debts promptly, and insisted on fairness above all. He would have fared poorly in a university exam, yet he carried within him prudence, loyalty, keen insight, generosity, and courage that no book could teach.

And he had moments to prove it.

When Cattarina, the opera dancer who had once caused him so much trouble, pressed her attentions beyond what Harry considered proper, he withdrew with calm dignity, leaving her to seek softer prey. Her mother soon arrived, weeping and dramatic, claiming a cruel bailiff had seized their goods, and that her husband languished in a London prison. Harry listened politely, then firmly declared that his acquaintance with Cattarina did not make him responsible for her family’s debts. He refused to act as surety for her father or settle her accounts at Tunbridge.

The woman called him a monster, then sneered, asking why he didn’t accept payment for his “services.” At first, Harry was puzzled—until she clarified. Then, with horror, he rose, declaring that in his homeland, even the fiercest savages would recoil from such a trade. He bowed her out with formal courtesy, ordered Gumbo to bar her from his lodgings forever, and let her go—no doubt vowing vengeance against the “Iroquois,” as she called him. She and her daughter fled to London as soon as their landlord allowed.

Harry faced other dangers too—those of gaming, not just of the heart. A man who plays at bowls must expect to lose some rubbers. After dinner at the ordinary, having declined another game of piquet with Captain Batts, Harry was roughly questioned. He replied honestly: he only played with gentlemen who paid, as he did. He added that he’d be happy to settle their account once it was cleared. That satisfied Captain Batts—so much so that he left without paying Harry or anyone else. The debt remained unsettled, but Harry’s integrity stood firm.

There was also the evening when a rude chairman insulted Miss Whiffler as she walked to the assembly. Harry stepped forward, challenged the man, and delivered a sound beating. Later, when Mr. Hector Buckler—famed for his sharp tongue—repeated the false tale of Harry’s affair with Cattarina in the pump-room, Harry confronted him publicly. Standing before a gathering of water-drinkers, he declared the story a lie and warned any who repeated it would answer to him.

So though Harry lived among wolves, he proved himself a worthy one. He did not merely follow the customs of the place—he upheld honor within them. And when Colonel Wolfe learned the truth, he likely hastened to correct the accusations he had once believed. There was great joy in the Lambert household when the news arrived.

“Hadst thou brought me a regiment, James,” said Mr. Lambert, “I should not have been more pleased.”

Mrs. Lambert summoned her daughters from the garden, kissed them both, and shouted the news. Hetty leapt with delight, and Theo played an extraordinary suite on the harpsichord that night. When Dr. Boyle came for his usual game of backgammon, he found the house alight with laughter and warmth. Only then did he understand—when the three ladies sang together, “We were wrong! We were wrong!”—that the sermon he had preached about mercy had come true.

“What shall we do, my dear?” asked the Colonel, leaning toward his wife. “The hay is in, the corn won’t be cut for weeks, and the horses are idle. Suppose we…”

And here, he whispered into her ear.

“My dearest Martin!” she exclaimed, taking his hand. “The very thing!”

“What’s the very thing, mother?” asked young Charley, home for his holidays.

“The very thing,” she said, “is to go to supper. Come, Doctor! Let us open a bottle of wine and drink to repentance—for all who judged wrongly.”

“Amen,” said the Doctor, “with all my heart.”

And so the family gathered, joyful and reconciled, to share a meal under the glow of forgiveness.
