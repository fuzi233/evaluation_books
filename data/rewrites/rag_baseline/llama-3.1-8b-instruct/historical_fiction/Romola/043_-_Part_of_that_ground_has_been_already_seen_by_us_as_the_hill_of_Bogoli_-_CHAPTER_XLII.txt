Romola in her Place.

The autumn sun cast a warm glow over the terracotta rooftops of Florence on the thirtieth of October 1496. Though the morning air was crisp, the gentle breeze carried the sweet scent of ripening olives from the nearby groves. Yet, the Florentines' thoughts were far removed from the serene landscape. They worried about the tempests raging at sea, which seemed to be conspiring against the city with the rest of the world to contradict the Frate's conviction that Heaven watched over Florence.

Those fierce storms had forced the ships from Marseilles to seek shelter from the coast of Leghorn, carrying soldiers and grain, which were sorely needed in Florence. The city teetered on the brink of disaster: food was scarce, and its borders were threatened on all sides. The once-mighty walls of Florence now seemed fragile against the encroaching forces of famine and war.

King Charles VIII of France, the self-proclaimed new Charlemagne, had vanished from Italy fifteen months prior, leaving behind a power vacuum. His intentions to return and restore order in the region were beginning to wane, consumed by his grief over the loss of a newborn son. Meanwhile, a Holy League had formed against him, led by the cunning Pope Alexander VI, to drive out the French from Naples. This pact, however, seemed less a noble endeavor and more a wolfish agreement to oust one predator and divide the spoils among themselves. Florence, unlike other major Italian states, had chosen to stand alone, adhering to its alliance with France.

This decision had not gone unnoticed. Pisa, still fighting for its freedom, was being supported by powerful forces from Venice and Milan, as well as the German Emperor Maximilian, who had joined the fray to claim Leghorn and block Florence's access to the sea. If Leghorn fell, Florence would be cut off from the outside world, trapped between the Papal States and the jealousy of smaller city-states.

In response to these perils, the Florentine government displayed unwavering resolve, mobilizing its resources to meet the challenges ahead. Despite the scarcity of food and the increasing desperation of the people, they chose not to close their gates to the hungry peasants and beggars fleeing from neighboring cities. Instead, they continued to send conciliatory embassies, seeking to negotiate with their adversaries and secure aid from abroad.
