“Three lords sat drinking late yestreen,
And ere they paid the lawing,
They set a combat them between,
To fight it in the dawing.”—Scotch Ballad.

All who have seen Bideford know its bridge—the very heart of the town, its soul and center. As Edinburgh is defined by its castle, Rome by its Capitol, and Egypt by its pyramids, so Bideford lives and breathes through its ancient stone span. Yet few know the deeper secrets that have shaped this marvel for five centuries: not merely a structure of stone and arch, but a living thing—inspired, spiritual, charitable, learned, sentient, and, above all, generous to the point of feasting. It was said that when the bridge first began to rise half a mile upstream, unseen hands carried each stone downriver every night until Sir Richard Gurney, the parish parson, saw an angel in his dreams commanding him to build where the stones had been brought. There alone, he was told, could the foundation hold against the shifting sands beneath.

Bishop Grandison of Exeter once declared indulgences, blessings, and eternal spiritual benefits to all who aided in bridging the perilous ford—so that men might serve both God and country, and make the best of both worlds. And though many legends surround it—how it trembles at a horse’s step despite its woolen foundation, or how William Alford once bore four bushels of salt on his back across all twenty-three arches—it is most famed for being a noble esquire in its own right. The bridge bears arms—a ship and bridge upon a plain field—and owns lands and tenements across several parishes. From these, it has founded schools, waged lawsuits, and, most notably, hosted annual banquets, maintaining the finest wine cellar in Devon, stocked with vintages from far corners of Christendom.

In the year 1583, one such feast was held, and among those invited were the leading citizens of Bideford, including Mr. St. Leger of Annery, brother to the marshal of Munster and kin to Lady Grenville. A man of great generosity, he gave away his wealth freely during his life, so that his children might never know the temptations of riches. At this gathering, a debate arose between him and one of the bridge’s trustees: whether a salmon caught below the bridge was superior to one taken above. Since no theory could settle it, Mr. St. Leger offered a wager of five pounds—he would catch a fish as firm and flaky as the Appledore salmon they had just eaten from the pool beneath Annery. In good humor, he then invited the entire company to dine with him at Annery three days later, each bringing a wife or daughter. Don Guzman, who happened to be present, was included.

The feast at Annery was grand beyond compare—such a banquet as had not been seen since Judge Hankford entertained King Edward IV. The hall echoed with laughter, music, and clinking goblets. Amidst the revelry, Rose Salterne and Don Guzman sat apart, pretending not to notice each other, yet watching intently. Rose, especially, was cautious—her father sat nearby, and opposite her were William Cary and Arthur St. Leger, lieutenants in Her Majesty’s Irish army, recently returned on leave.

Six months had passed since Rose and the Spaniard had spoken. He avoided her only within her father’s house; elsewhere, he remained deliberately distant, offering nothing more than a formal salute. Yet wherever she was, he made himself radiant—witty, eloquent, romantic—more so than ever before. His silence was not indifference, but strategy. Having failed in his first attempt to win her with open affection, he now sought to awaken desire through absence. For what greater punishment could he inflict than withdrawing his attention? And what better way to stir longing than to appear indifferent?

He knew women well—how their pride often masks vulnerability, how their coyness hides dependence. The more a man seems unattainable, the more they value him. So he let her believe he could live without her. And slowly, Rose began to wonder: *Why does he not care?* What secret strength did he possess? Was there ambition, mystery, hidden power? She found herself drawn to the idea of knowing him—of being the one who unlocked his depths. Could she not be the treasure he guarded? And if so, why had she refused him?

She felt ashamed, confused, and strangely stirred. That day, sitting at table, she could no longer wear her mask of composure. Her eyes dropped, her cheeks flushed. Don Guzman noticed. He smiled inwardly—his plan was working. He glanced at her again, not boldly, but with quiet confidence. She looked up, startled, as if caught in thought. Then, meeting his gaze, she faltered, blushed, and lowered her eyes once more. But after a moment, she raised them again, searching for his—perhaps hoping for forgiveness.

And then, silently, he asked: *Do you forgive me?*

She could not speak. But her face answered. Her lips parted, her neck bent slightly, and in that moment, she whispered with her whole being: *And do you forgive me?*

At once, Don Guzman came alive—jovial, animated, the center of the room. Stories flew, jests sparkled, and he became the most charming knight among them. Rose, meanwhile, felt a pang of guilt. She had caused this joy, and yet she longed to flee. To hide. To dream of love she dared not name.

Outside, the terrace shimmered in midsummer light. Women in rich silks strolled beneath the sunlit trees, admiring one another's gowns, whispering about husbands and servants. Only Rose stood apart, wanting to laugh or cry, unsure which.

“Dear Rose looks sad,” said Lady Grenville, approaching. “Come, child—sing for us.”

Rose obeyed, though her voice trembled. She took the lute and sat beneath the house while others gathered round.

“What shall I sing?” she asked.

“Your old song—‘Earl Haldan’s Daughter.’”

She hesitated. It was a bold, lively ballad—too loud for her mood. Frank had loved it once. Now it seemed ominous. But to refuse would invite suspicion. So she began—

“It was Earl Haldan’s daughter,
She look’d across the sea;
She look’d across the water,
And long and loud laugh’d she:
‘The locks of six princesses
Must be my marriage-fee!
So hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat!
Who comes a wooing me?’”

“It was Earl Haldan’s daughter,
She walk’d along the sand;
When she was aware of a knight so fair,
Come sailing to the land.
His sails were all of velvet,
His mast of beaten gold,
And ‘hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat,
Who saileth here so bold?’”

“‘The locks of five princesses
I won beyond the sea;
I shore their golden tresses
To fringe a cloak for thee.
One handful yet is wanting,
But one of all the tale;
So hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat!
Furl up thy velvet sail!’”

“He leapt into the water,
That rover young and bold;
He gript Earl Haldan’s daughter,
He shore her locks of gold;
‘Go weep, go weep, proud maiden,
The tale is full to-day.
Now hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat!
Sail Westward-ho, and away!’”

As the final note faded, a voice cut through the silence—low, foreign, resonant.

“In the East, they say the nightingale sings to the rose. But Devon, more blessed, has both in one.”

Lady Grenville laughed. “We have no nightingales, Don Guzman—but our thrushes sing sweetly enough. Why are you leaving the gentlemen so soon?”

“These letters,” he replied, holding up a sealed missive, “have just arrived. They summon me home to Spain—and thence to the Indies. My ransom has come, and with it a promise of office. I am to be Governor of La Guayra in Caracas. Let me ask your congratulations.”

A hush fell. Then voices rose: “To Spain? And then to the New World?”

“Yes,” he said, smiling. “And I shall need a wife there to complete my paradise.”

Rose’s heart stopped. The news was sudden, cruel. Was this his farewell? Had he never truly cared? Her face burned. She could not look away.

“And where is La Guayra?” she asked, her voice trembling.

“Half around the world, on the Spanish Main. A land of palms and mountains eight thousand feet high. The governor’s house is like a palace in Eden—only lacking a queen.”

“I doubt not some noble lady of Seville will accompany you,” said Lady Grenville.

“Ah, but since I’ve known English ladies,” he replied, “I’ve begun to think none compare. You are the only ones worth wooing.”

“You flatter us kindly,” she said. “But surely none of our free English maidens would submit to a duenna—especially one with a horn on her forehead!”

Rose turned crimson. Lady Grenville understood at once. But before she could speak, another woman murmured something about Spanish jealousy, and another giggled about West Indian masters and their slaves.

Don Guzman grew serious. “Ladies,” he said, “these are lies born of ignorance. We may love deeply—yes, fiercely—but that is because we value honor. If some abroad fall short, it is because they lack a helpmate—like the amethyst, which keeps its wearer pure. I could tell you tales of Spanish husbands’ loyalty in the Indies—stranger than any romance.”

“Then tell us one,” challenged Lady Grenville.

“It would be long,” he said.

“The longer, the better,” she insisted. “While the gentlemen finish their wine.”

Storytelling was a beloved pastime in those times, when books were rare and memory cherished. Knowing the Spaniard’s gift, the ladies gathered close. Servants brought chairs. Don Guzman took his seat, modestly proud, at Lady Grenville’s feet, and began—

“Forty-five years ago, Sebastian Cabota sailed under Emperor Charles the Fifth to find the golden lands of Tarshish, Ophir, and Cipango. Needing supplies, he paused at the mouth of a mighty river—now called Rio de la Plata—and ventured up its course, discovering Paraguay. There, at the mouth of the Rio Terceiro, he built a fort—Cabot’s Tower—where he left a garrison of a hundred and twenty men, commanded by Nuno de Lara, Ruiz Moschera, and Sebastian da Hurtado—old comrades of my grandfather, Don Ferdinando da Soto.

With them went Lucia Miranda, wife of Hurtado—a woman of unmatched beauty, wisdom, and grace. Once a court favorite, she had renounced royal life to marry a poor adventurer and share his perils. Mangora, cacique of the Timbuez Indians, saw her and desired her. He plotted to take her by force, knowing he could never win her by right.

She sensed his intent—wise enough to know when she was loved—and kept from him. Mangora summoned Hurtado to visit, asking him to bring his wife. Hurtado, suspecting a trap, declined politely. The savage, furious, began planning his murder.

Weeks passed. Food ran low. Hurtado and Moschera led fifty men on a foraging mission. Mangora saw his chance.

The tower stands on a knoll where two rivers meet, surrounded on land by marshland—perfect for ambush. He hid four thousand warriors in the reeds, then approached boldly with thirty men bearing grain, fruit, and game.

Smiling, he expressed sorrow for the Spaniards’ hunger, offered gifts, and was welcomed in. The feast lasted into the night, songs and wine flowing freely. Finally, he asked for Miranda.

She had locked herself away, claiming illness.

But when the Spaniards slept, Mangora and his men struck. Fire erupted. The magazine exploded. The hidden warriors surged from the marsh.

Within minutes, the garrison was slaughtered. Only Miranda and four women with infants survived—bound and led into the forest.

Stunned, Miranda traveled all night, arriving at dawn before the Indian king—to find he was not Mangora.

“A ray of hope flashed,” said Don Guzman. “She asked where he was.”

“Slain last night,” said the king. “I, his brother Siripa, am now cacique.”

It was true. Lara, drunk and wounded, had fought wildly, killed Mangora, then fallen pierced by a hundred blows.

Miranda’s hope died. She faced a new tyrant.

“‘You cannot be his queen,’ said the new king. ‘You must be mine.’”

She defied him, calling him a traitor, accusing him of cruelty. She demanded death—hoping to provoke it.

But he smiled. He ordered her freed, gave her a hut, adorned her with jewels, and treated her with courtesy—as if she were a Castilian noblewoman.

For three months, she endured. Then, one day, drawing water by the river, she saw a white man on the opposite bank—clothed in tattered Spanish garb, sword drawn. He shrieked her name, plunged into the current, swam across, and clasped her.

It was Don Sebastian himself—returned with Moschera to find only ruins.

By instinct, he guessed her fate, and vanished into the forest alone. Guided by love, he found her—unharmed, still his.

Their reunion was ecstasy and terror. The Indian women fled. For ten minutes, they kissed—life itself a single embrace.

But escape was impossible. He tried to swim her across, but his strength failed. They sat together, praying.

Then the warriors returned. Don Sebastian, desperate, would have slain them both—but could not raise his hand against her.

He was seized, disarmed, carried off in triumph.

And if you cannot feel for him in that hour,” said Don Guzman, pausing, “then I, a prisoner myself, can.”

He looked up—not at the ladies, but at Rose Salterne. And she, her eyes wide, met his.
