Mrs. Jennings could not stop singing Edward Ferrars’ praises for his honorable choice, but only Elinor and Marianne truly understood the full weight of his virtue. No one else knew how little reward there had ever been for choosing to disobey his family, how no comfort awaited him beyond the quiet, unshakable knowledge that he had done right, after he was cut off from all his fortune and cast out of his mother’s good graces. Elinor’s chest swelled with quiet pride at his unbreakable integrity; Marianne, soft with pity for the hardship he had borne, forgave every past slight he had seemed to inflict on her sister, at once.

Yet even as their full trust in each other was restored by the public reveal of Edward’s secret engagement, neither sister cared to dwell on the subject when they were alone. Elinor avoided it on principle: Marianne’s too-warm, too-certain insistence that Edward still carried a torch for her only rooted that forbidden hope deeper in her mind, when she was trying with all her might to let it fade. Marianne, for her part, soon lost the courage to speak of it at all. Every comparison it forced between Elinor’s steady, selfless conduct and her own wild, impulsive recklessness over Willoughby left her writhing in self-reproach. She felt every sting of that contrast, but not to spur her to better behavior now, as her sister had hoped: she only sank deeper into the torture of unending penitence, bitter that she had never exerted self-control before, with no hope of amending her ways now. Her spirit was so worn thin that present effort felt impossible, leaving her only more dispirited than before.

For two full days, no new news reached them of the goings-on in Harley Street or the Steeles’ lodgings on Bartlett’s Buildings. Though Mrs. Jennings already had more than enough juicy gossip to spread far and wide without seeking out more, she had resolved from the first moment of the reveal to pay her cousins a comforting, curious visit the second she could, and only the unexpected rush of extra callers had kept her away. The third day after they learned the full particulars was a bright, glorious Sunday in early March, warm enough to draw crowds out to Kensington Gardens even in the second week of March. Mrs. Jennings and Elinor joined the throng, but Marianne, who knew the Willoughbys had returned to town and lived in constant dread of encountering them, chose to stay safely home rather than brave such a public space.

An old close friend of Mrs. Jennings joined them almost as soon as they stepped through the garden gates, and Elinor was grateful for the distraction: the woman monopolized all of Mrs. Jennings’ attention, leaving Elinor free to wander and sink into quiet reflection. She spotted no sign of the Willoughbys, no sign of Edward, and for a long stretch no one at all who could spark her interest, grave or light. Until, to her quiet surprise, she was approached by Miss Anne Steele, who looked a little flustered but lit up at the sight of them. She gushed with unfeigned delight to meet them, and after Mrs. Jennings greeted her with especial warmth, Anne left her own party to linger with them for a little while.

Mrs. Jennings leaned in to whisper to Elinor at once: “Fish all the news out of her, my dear. She’ll tell you anything you ask, you see I can’t tear myself away from Mrs. Clarke right now.”
Luckily for both their curiosities, Anne would have spilled every secret unprompted anyway.
“I’m so glad I ran into you!” she chirped, looping her arm familiarly through Elinor’s. “You’re the very person I’ve been dying to see.” She dropped her voice to a stage whisper. “I suppose Mrs. Jennings has heard all about our little drama? Is she angry with us?”
“Not at all, I am sure,” Elinor assured her.
“Oh that’s such a relief! And Lady Middleton? She isn’t cross either?”
“I cannot imagine she would be.”
“Thank goodness, I’ve had such a dreadful time of it these past few days! I’ve never seen Lucy in such a fury. She swore at first she’d never sew me a new bonnet, never do a single thing for me for as long as she lived—but now she’s quite over it, and we’re thick as thieves again. Look, she made this little pink bow for my hat, and stuck the feather in it last night. I know you’re going to laugh at me for wearing pink ribbons, but why shouldn’t I? Everyone teases me that it’s the doctor’s favorite color, but I never would have known he liked it best if he hadn’t said it out loud! My cousins plague me about it nonstop, I declare sometimes I don’t know where to look.”
She wandered off that tangent for a minute, then quickly circled back to the news everyone was desperate to hear.
“Well, but Miss Dashwood, people can say whatever they like about Mr. Ferrars deciding he’d never marry Lucy, but that’s all nonsense, I can tell you! It’s a downright shame those ill-natured rumors are flying around. Whatever Lucy might have feared, no one else had any right to say for certain he’d back out.”
“I never heard any such rumor hinted at before, I promise you,” Elinor said.
“Oh no? Well it was being spread all over town, more than once! Miss Godby told Miss Sparks that no sensible person would ever expect Mr. Ferrars to give up a girl like Miss Morton with thirty thousand pounds, for Lucy Steele who has nothing at all. And my cousin Richard swore to me that when push came to shove, he was sure Mr. Ferrars would run. Then Edward didn’t come near our rooms for three whole days, I didn’t know what to think! Lucy was all ready to give up hope entirely—we left your brother’s house on Wednesday, and never saw hide nor hair of him Thursday, Friday, Saturday. She almost wrote to him, but then her pride wouldn’t let her. But this very morning, he turned up just as we got home from church, and he told us everything. His mother summoned him to Harley Street on Wednesday, yelled at him for hours, the whole family threatened to disown him, and he stood right there and swore he loved no one but Lucy, and he’d marry no one but her. He was so upset by the whole row he jumped on his horse right after he left his mother’s house and rode off into the country, hid out at some random inn all Thursday and Friday just to calm his head. He thought it all over, you see, and realized he had nothing but two thousand pounds to his name, no other income, and if he took orders like he’s been planning, the only post he’d get would be a tiny curacy that barely paid enough to feed one person. He thought it would be cruel to trap Lucy in a life of poverty, so he begged her, if she had the slightest wish to be free of the engagement, to end it right then and there, no hard feelings. But he never once said he was tired of her, or wanted to marry Miss Morton, or anything like that! I’d swear to it on my life. Of course Lucy wouldn’t hear a word of breaking it off. She told him straight, all soft and sweet, that she didn’t care about being poor, she’d happily live on nothing if she got to be his wife. Then they were so happy, they started planning everything: he’ll go get ordained as soon as he can, and they’ll wait to marry until he can secure a proper church living.”
Anne paused, and Elinor blinked. “Wait—you were in the room with them the whole time to hear all this?”
Anne snickered, as if Elinor had said something terribly silly. “La! Miss Dashwood, you don’t think people make love with other folks standing right there? For shame, you know better than that! No, no, they were shut all alone in the drawing room together. I just stood outside the door and listened to what I could hear.”
Elinor froze, horrified. “You listened at the door? I wish you’d told me that before, I never would have let you tell me details of a conversation you had no right to overhear. How could you be so unfair to your own sister?”
Anne waved the protest away. “Oh that’s nothing! Lucy would do the exact same to me. A year or two back, when Martha Sharpe and I had all sorts of secrets, she’d hide in closets or behind chimney boards all the time just to listen to what we were saying.”
Elinor tried to steer the conversation to another topic, but Anne could not stay away from the subject preying on her mind for more than a couple of minutes.
“Edward says he’s going up to Oxford soon,” she babbled. “Right now he’s lodging on Pall Mall. His mother is such a horrid ill-tempered woman, isn’t she? And your brother and sister-in-law weren’t very kind to them either! But I won’t say a word against them to you, and I will say they did send us home in their own carriage, which was more than I expected. I was half terrified they’d ask for those little sewing kits they gave us a few days before, but no one said a word, and I made sure to hide mine where no one would see it. My cousins are going to tease me something dreadful when they hear he’s going to be ordained, you mark my words. They’ll tell me I should write to the doctor to get Edward the curacy of his new living. But I’d never do that for all the world! I’ll just say ‘La! How could you ever think I’d bother the doctor with such a thing?’”
Before Elinor could reply, Anne’s friends the Richardsons came into view. “Oh, there they are! I had loads more to tell you, but I can’t stay away any longer. They’re such genteel people, he makes a fortune and they keep their own private coach. Tell Mrs. Jennings please that I’m so glad she isn’t angry with us, and Lady Middleton isn’t either! And if you and your sister ever leave town, and Mrs. Jennings is lonely, us Steeles would be more than happy to come stay with her as long as she likes. I don’t think Lady Middleton will invite us back again any time soon, though. Goodbye! I’m so sad Miss Marianne missed seeing me. Give her my love. Oh, you aren’t wearing your spotted muslin today! I’d have been scared it would tear walking all over the grass.”
She rushed off to greet the Richardsons, leaving Elinor to her thoughts. Almost nothing Anne had told her was new; every detail lined up exactly with what she had already guessed would happen. Edward and Lucy’s engagement was as firm as ever, their wedding date as uncertain as could be, hanging entirely on the off chance Edward would ever be gifted a proper church living that could support a wife.
When they climbed back into their carriage to head home, Mrs. Jennings begged for every scrap of news, but Elinor chose only to share the simple, public details Lucy would want spread, leaving out the unfortunate eavesdropping anecdote. She told her that the engagement was still fully on, and they were making plans for Edward to take orders as soon as possible.
“Wait for a living, indeed!” Mrs. Jennings huffed, shaking her head. “We all know how that goes! They’ll wait a year, no living will turn up, and they’ll end up living on fifty pounds a year from that tiny curacy, plus the interest on his two thousand pounds, plus whatever pennies Mr. Steele and Mr. Pratt can scrape together for Lucy. Then they’ll have a baby every year, and Lord help them, they’ll be so poor! I’m going to see what I can scrape together to help furnish their little cottage, that’s for sure. Two maids and two footmen, like I was joking about the other day? No no, they’ll be lucky to afford one hardworking maid-of-all-work.”
The next morning, a twopenny post letter arrived for Elinor, postmarked from Bartlett’s Buildings, in Lucy’s own neat handwriting:
> Bartlett’s Buildings, March
> My dearest Miss Dashwood,
> I hope you will excuse the liberty I take in writing to you, but I know your kindness to me will make you happy to hear such good news of me and my dear Edward, after all the dreadful troubles we have suffered these past weeks. Thank God, though we bore so much persecution, we are both well now, and as happy as two people deeply in love can be. We owe so much gratitude to our friends, none more than you, whose great kindness I will never forget, nor Edward, whom I have told every bit of it.
> I am sure you and dear Mrs. Jennings will be glad to hear that I spent two perfect happy hours with him yesterday afternoon. He would not hear a word of us parting, even when I begged him, as I thought was my duty for the sake of prudence, to set me free. I would have ended our engagement that very second if he had asked, but he swore it would never happen. He does not care a fig for his mother’s anger, as long as he has my love. Our prospects are not bright, it is true, but we must wait, and hope for the best. He will be ordained very shortly, and if it ever falls in your power to recommend him to anyone who has a living to bestow, I know you will not forget us. I trust dear Mrs. Jennings will speak a good word for us to Sir John, or Mr. Palmer, or any friend who might be able to help us.
> Poor Anne was very much to blame for blabbing our secret, but she meant well, so I say nothing of it. I hope Mrs. Jennings will not think it too much trouble to call on us any morning she is passing this way, it would be such a great kindness, and my cousins would be so proud to receive her.
> My paper is almost full, so I will end here, begging you to pass my most grateful, respectful regards to Mrs. Jennings, Sir John, Lady Middleton, and all the dear children the next time you see them, and give my love to Miss Marianne.
> I am, your most obliged and affectionate friend,
> Lucy Steele.

Elinor handed the letter straight to Mrs. Jennings, who read it aloud, gushing over every line.
“How prettily she writes! That bit where she offered to let him break the engagement? That’s just like Lucy, sweet sensible girl. Poor soul, I wish I could get him a good living tomorrow, with all my heart! She calls me ‘dear Mrs. Jennings’, did you see that? She’s the sweetest good-hearted girl that ever lived. I will absolutely go call on her, no question. So thoughtful of her to remember everyone! This is the prettiest letter I’ve ever read, it does her heart and head all the credit in the world.”
