You, Captain Miles Warrington, have the honour of winning the favour of a lady—or perhaps many ladies—let us say, the Duchess of Devonshire, Mrs. Crew, Mrs. Fitzherbert, the Queen of Prussia, the Goddess Venus herself, or Mademoiselle Hillisberg of the Opera. Never mind whom, in truth. But if you do win a lady’s good graces, do you always rush to the mess and boast of it?

“Not such a fool, Squire!” replies the Captain, adjusting his side curl in the mirror. “Have you, Miss Theo, told your mother every word you whispered to Mr. Joe Blake, junior, in the shrubbery this morning?”

“Joe Blake, indeed!” cries Theo junior, scandalized.

“And you, mademoiselle?” he continues, turning to her with a twinkle. “That scented letter which arrived under Sir Thomas’s frank—have you revealed all its contents? Look at her blush! As red as the curtain, I swear! No, mademoiselle—we all keep our secrets,” says the Squire, bowing with exaggerated grace. “No, Theo, there was nothing in the shrubbery—only nuts, my dear child! No, Miles, my son, we don’t tell all—even to the most indulgent of fathers—and if I ever reveal what passed between us in a landau on the Hampstead Road on the 25th of May, 1760, may the Chevalier Ruspini pull out every tooth in my head!”

“Pray tell, papa!” cries mamma. “Or, since Jobson, who drove us, is now in your service, perhaps you’ll summon him from the stables! I insist upon knowing!”

“What is this mystery?” asks mademoiselle, in her delicate French accent, turning to my wife.

“Eh, ma fille!” whispers the lady. “Thou wouldst ask me what I said? I said ‘Yes!’—and that is all.”

So it is my wife who has betrayed me, not I; and that was the sum of our conversation as the carriage—far too swiftly, as I thought—galloped toward Hampstead and returned.

Theo had not consented to defy her parents—not at all. But she would marry no other man, not even if she lived to be as old as Methuselah, not even the Prince of Wales himself. Her heart had been given away—with her father’s approval, nay, his command—it was no longer hers to reclaim. A kind father must relent one day; and if George kept his promise—now, in twenty years, or in another world—she knew she would never break hers.

Hetty’s face lit up with joy when, after my brief meeting with Theo, she saw her sister’s serene expression. All the doctor’s medicines had done her no such good, the devoted sister declared. The girls returned home after their act of quiet disobedience. I gave up the seat I had occupied during my brief happiness beside my dear invalid. Hetty skipped back into her place, and Charley climbed onto his box. He later confessed that he had heard a very dull sermon. The little fellow was too devout to appreciate dissenting preachers.

Hetty was not alone in noticing her sister’s changed countenance and renewed spirits. On their return, their mother embraced both girls—especially the invalid—with greater warmth than usual. “There’s nothing like a country ride,” Aunt Lambert remarked, “to restore dear Theo’s health. She went to Hampstead, did she? Then she must go again tomorrow. Heaven bless us, Lord Wrotham’s horses are at our disposal three or four times a week—why, the sweet child might enjoy them daily!”

As for the idea that Mr. Warrington might have met the girls on their drive, Aunt Lambert never once entertained it—at least, never mentioned it. Let those interested guess whether she could possibly have imagined her daughter and her lover would ever meet again.

Do women help each other in matters of love? Do they scheme, whisper, devise little plans, tell small lies, arrange secret meetings, hang up rope ladders, coax, wheedle, mislead guardians or servants, and turn their backs while Strephon and Chloe embrace in twilight, or flee in a postchaise to Gretna Green?

My dear young friends, yes—there are some who do. And some gentle souls, who once loved deeply and truly, continue to show kindness and tenderness toward younger lovers playing the same tender game. Miss Prim does not. If she hears of two young people in love, she either mocks them as fools or imagines the worst about them. Because she bears a burden herself, she seeks to wound others. I suspect she’d throw a stone at a pair of turtles cooing in a wood rather than watch them. But I speak of your grandmother, Aunt Lambert—a woman whose heart was a great syllabub of human kindness. And as for the matter at hand, how can I possibly know whether she suspected anything?

All she said to Theo upon her return was: “My child, the country air has done you immense good. I hope you’ll take another ride tomorrow—and another, and another.”

“Don’t you think, papa,” Aunt Lambert asked the General when he came in for supper, “that the ride has done the child wonderful good? Shouldn’t she be encouraged to go out in the fresh air?”

“Yes, indeed,” said Lambert. “If a coach-and-six will benefit my little Theo, she shall have it. Or I’ll drag the landau up Hampstead Hill myself, if no horses are available.” He would have spent his guineas, his breath, his very blood, to give his child pleasure. He was enchanted by his daughter’s improved appearance—she ate a bit of chicken with appetite, drank a little negus he made for her. It seemed better than any medicine the kind doctor had prescribed, which, God knows, had done little good.

Mamma was radiant. Hetty was bright and cheerful. It felt like an evening at Oakhurst. Not in months—certainly not since that terrible, cruel day no one spoke of—had they enjoyed such a night.

Yet though the other women might coax and flatter, Theo herself was too honest to sustain the illusion for long. When the General returned to the topic of her recovery—“What has done it? The country air? The Jesuit’s bark? The new medicine?”—she placed a trembling hand on his, eyes wide and clear.

“Can’t you guess, dear?” she asked softly. “It’s because I’ve seen him again, papa.”

The two women turned pale. Theo’s own heart began to race, her cheeks grew white as she stared into her father’s stricken face.

“It wasn’t wrong to see him,” she continued quickly. “It would have been wrong not to tell you.”

“Great God!” groaned the father, pulling his hand back, his face twisted with anguish. Hetty rushed to her sister, clutching her tightly. “Theo knew nothing of it, sir! It was all my doing—I planned it!”

Theo collapsed onto her sister’s shoulder, kissing her neck over and over.

“Women, women!” cried the father, breaking into a fierce cry. “Are you mocking my honour?”

Aunt Lambert sobbed wildly. “Martin! Martin! Don’t say a word to her!”

But Hetty staggered backward, fainting against the wall, for Theo had fainted into her arms.

I was taking breakfast the next morning when my door opened, and my faithful servant announced, “General Lambert.”

At once I saw the storm in his face—he knew everything.

“Your accomplices did not confess,” he said as soon as we were alone. “They sided with you against their father—a proof of how desirable clandestine meetings are. I learned it from Theo herself—she admitted she had seen you.”

“Accomplices, sir?” I replied, perhaps eager to divert attention. “You know how deeply and dutifully your children regard you. If they oppose you now, it is because justice is on their side. A man like you cannot expect *sic volo sic jubeo* to be the only law in his household.”

“Psha, George!” he exclaimed. “Though we are parted, God forbid I should wish our friendship ended. I had your promise not to seek her.”

“I did not go to her, sir,” I said, feeling my face grow warm. For though it was true, it was also untrue.

“You mean she came to you?” he asked, agitated. “Is it behind Hester’s petticoat you hide? What a fine defence for a gentleman!”

“Well, I won’t shelter behind the poor child,” I replied. “To deny it was an attempt to evade the truth—one I am unaccustomed to making. I did not break the letter of my agreement—but I broke its spirit. From this moment, I annul it entirely.”

“You break your word to me!” he shouted.

“I recall a hasty promise made in a moment of extreme emotion and distress. No man should be bound forever by words spoken under such circumstances. And more—no man of honour or humanity, Mr. Lambert, would try to bind another so.”

“Dishonour to me, sir!” he thundered.

“If the word is to be tossed between us,” I retorted hotly, “then let it be. There can be no question of love, age, or difference when that word is used. Were you my own father—and I love you more than a father, Uncle Lambert—I would not endure it! What have I done? I have seen the woman I consider my wife before God and man. If she calls me, I will come. If she comes to me, here is my home and half of what I possess. You have no right—having given me the gift—to reclaim it. Because my mother unjustly taunts you, are you to punish this tender, innocent girl? You claim to love her, yet you cannot bear a wounded pride for her sake. Better she perish in misery than an old woman in Virginia accuse you of scheming to marry one of your daughters. Say that to satisfy what you call honour—and I call selfishness—we part, we break our hearts, we rally, we try to forget—can any man be to me what she is? Can any woman be to me what she is? You may marry her to the Prince of Wales tomorrow—it would be cowardice and treason. How can we undo the promises we made before Heaven? You may separate us—but she will die as surely as Jephthah’s daughter. Have you sworn to Heaven to murder her? Kill her if you believe your promise binds you. But this I swear: I am glad you came, so I might formally cancel a hasty pledge, and declare that whenever she calls, I will come to her!”

No doubt I spoke with the heat and passion of youth, convinced death awaited us both if we were torn apart. Who does not believe his first love eternal?

Now, having watched the world, seen passions rise and fade—alas, I must say it—I smile at my own youthful fervours. Yet if it is a superstition, I prefer to hold it. I would rather believe neither of us could live with another, that Heaven itself ordained these two souls to be joined.

“We must come to what I had hoped to avoid,” the General said, in reply. “An unfriendly separation. When I meet you, Mr. Warrington, I must know you no more. I shall order—my family and children will obey—never to acknowledge you. Since you refuse to show me the respect due to my age, the courtesy of gentlemen, I must treat you as an enemy. An enemy to my home’s peace, my children’s honour! And so I shall deal with you when you molest me.”

He waved his hand, put on his hat, and left abruptly.

I stood stunned. War, I thought, had begun. The fleeting joy of yesterday was gone, replaced by deeper sorrow—the agony of parting now mingled with the bitterness of quarrel. I was not only alone but friendless.

In a year of close companionship, I had come to revere and love Lambert as I had never loved any man except Harry. To see his face turned from me in anger was like losing the sun. Darkness surrounded me.

Yet I knew I had acted rightly in retracting my promise. My loyalty to Theo—hers to me—was above all duty or obedience. Ceremony or none, I was hers, first and foremost. Promises made before Heaven could not be undone by any parent. Even all the priests in Christendom could only confirm what we had already sealed in silence.

I met Jack Lambert by chance that day, walking toward the library of the new Museum. Overcome by grief, I pulled him aside, led him through the gardens, and poured out my story.

I didn’t care much for Jack—truthfully, he was somewhat pompous, fond of Latin quotes, and wearisome—but in my sorrow, I clung to him. Having suffered himself in his affair with the American girl, he understood pain. He listened sympathetically.

I told him of my meeting with Theo, my confrontation with her father, my resolve never to part from her.

When I saw he leaned toward my side—quoting Greek and Latin authors—I thought him wise, clung to him fiercely, and showered him with affection he rarely received from me.

We walked to his father’s lodgings in Dean Street. I watched him enter, gazed at the house with longing, hoping to glimpse Theo within. At the coffee-house where I waited, I ordered a bottle of wine—Jack’s Coffee-House, as it was called—and then another.

I called him Brother when he left. I clung to him like a condemned man to a jailer or parson.

I drank a whole bottle, then another. I feared Jack would never return.

He finally appeared, looking startled. He poured himself several bumpers from my second bottle and began his tale—most interesting to me.

My poor Theo, he said, was confined to her room, deeply agitated. He returned just in time for dinner. Afterward, his father spoke freely, relieved to have his eldest son present and Theodosia absent.

The General recounted our conversation. He silenced Hester, who sat dumb as a mouse. He chided Aunt Lambert for her handkerchief drama and cursed the women—“pimps,” he called them—for aiding each other.

Then he turned to Jack: “What is your view?”

To everyone’s surprise, Jack defended me.

Citing ancient authorities (which I didn’t catch), he argued the matter was beyond parental control. Once consent was given, the elders had stepped outside the realm. Though he didn’t agree with the sacramental views of marriage held by many divines, he believed it even more sacred—binding before Heaven, regardless of ceremony.

“It is not merely,” Jack said, turning to his father, “those whom I, John Lambert, Priest, have joined, let no man put asunder. It is those whom God has joined—let no man separate.”

(Here he removed his hat, as he told me.)

“My views are clear,” he continued. “And surely these young people were joined—or permitted to plight themselves—by your consent. My views are clear. I shall lay them out in a series of discourses, no doubt satisfying you.”

On hearing this, the General said, “I am satisfied already, my dear boy.”

And Hetty whispered, “Jack, mother and I will make you a dozen shirts, as sure as eggs is eggs.”

“While we talked,” the General resumed, “Theodosia appeared—pale, agitated. She kissed me, sat beside me, and took a sippet of toast, dipping it in my negus.”

“You should have been here to hear Jack’s sermon!” said Hester.

“Has he?” asked Theodosia, too weak to care.

“He has preached for three quarters of an hour by Shrewsbury clock,” said the General—though Jack insisted it was far shorter.

“And his discourse has been you, my dear,” said papa, playing with her hand.

“Me, papa?”

“You and—Mr. Warrington—and—George, my love.”

At this, Theodosia leaned closer, laid her head on his shoulder, and wept.

“This is different, sir,” said Jack, “from a passage I remember in Pausanias.”

“In Pausanias? Indeed!” said the General. “Who was he?”

I smiled at my father’s ignorance before his children.

“When Ulysses took Penelope from her father,” I explained, “the king pursued them, begging his daughter to return. Ulysses offered her a choice—return or go with him. She covered her face with her veil. For lack of a veil, my sister has taken refuge in your waistcoat, sir.”

We all laughed—though my mother vowed she would have returned home with her father if such a choice had been offered.

“But I am not a girl of spirit, dear mother!” said Theodosia, still resting in her father’s lap.

I don’t recall such affection being common in my youth, Jack added.

After more talk, he remembered me and left his parents pleading with Theodosia to return to bed. The events had weakened her greatly.

“I’ve experienced,” he said, “how full of anxiety and fear this passion is. It racks the soul. I have no doubt it harms health when carried too far—especially among women who lack philosophy.”

He bowed and said, “My service to you, brother.”

From grief to hope—how swift the change! Joy flooded my soul. Landlord, more port! I would have drunk a cask if Jack had asked. His sympathy was vast, generous all day. I refused to tally the bottles. Let the waiters’ calculations stand.

Jack was my dearest, best brother. I swore my friendship would last forever. If I could serve him—were it a bishopric—by George, he should have it.

I awoke the next morning with a splitting headache, yet joyfully, rapturously. I did not yet grasp the full extent of my happiness—or the transformation of my noble enemy from the previous day.

It must have been painful for an older man to yield to arguments so undutiful. But the true Christian gentleman, wounded by my harshness and moved by his daughter’s suffering, withdrew thoughtfully. Later, he went to church—his private sanctuary—and knelt in prayer.

There, submitting his case to the divine guidance he often sought, it seemed to him that his daughter was right—fidelity to me was just—and he was wrong to demand blind submission.

Thus Jack’s cause was won before he began. The brave, gentle heart—unable to bear pain, unwilling to assert authority—was only too eager to return to love and affection.
