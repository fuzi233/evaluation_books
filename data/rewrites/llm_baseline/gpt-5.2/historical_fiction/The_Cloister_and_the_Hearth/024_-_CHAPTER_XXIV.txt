Not far along the road Gerard overtook a little knot of people—two men in sober, workmanlike suits, each leaning with lazy confidence against a horse’s flank, their heads together in talk. The rider, by contrast, was dressed as if he had meant to be seen: a silk doublet, and bright green jerkin and hose of English cloth, smooth and shining as a mole’s fur. Yet this splendid creature lay full length on his belly in the afternoon sun, sprawled in the dust like a great lizard. His velvet cloak—flaming yellow—had been taken from him with care and spread across the horse’s loins, as though the beast were the more delicate body.

“Is aught amiss?” Gerard asked, for the sight jarred with any sober sense of order.

“Not that I wot of,” said one servant.

“But your master lies like a corpse. Are ye not ashamed to let him grovel so?”

“Go to,” the other answered, untroubled. “The bare ground is the best cure for his disorder. If you get sober in bed, it gives you a headache; but you leap up from the hard earth like a lark in spring. Eh, Ulric?”

“He speaks sooth,” said Ulric, warming to it.

“What—then the gentleman is drunk?”

Both servants broke into a hoarse laugh at the plainness of the question; and then Ulric checked himself, scanned Gerard from head to heel, and turned grave.

“Who are you, and where born, that know not the Count is ever drunk at this hour?”

Gerard felt suspicion snap about him like a cold cord. “I am a stranger,” he said steadily, “but a true man; and one that loves knowledge. Therefore I ask questions, not for love of prying.”

“If you be a true man,” Ulric returned, shrewd as a toll-gatherer, “then give us trinkgeld for the knowledge we have given you.”

Gerard’s purse was lean enough to show through his courage. Yet he put a good face on it. “Trinkgeld you shall have, such as I can spare—an if you will tell me why ye have taken his cloak from him and laid it on the beast.”

At the sweet prospect of coin they both became philosophers at once, and offered him two answers as swiftly as two cooks crying their own sauce the best. One swore it was prudence: should the Count wake (and seasoned topers do come to themselves in a breath) and find his horse sweating in the cold while his cloak lay idle, he would curse, and mayhap lay on. The other gave a grander reason, as if a stable were a school: a horse, drinking nought but water, must be cockered and warmed without; but a master, being ever filled with good beer, carries his heat inside to the skin, and a cloak is but a shred of vanity.

Each fell enamoured of his own doctrine; and to tell truth, each had taken a hair or two of the dog that bit his master. Their voices rose; their breath, strong with yesterday’s ale, steamed in the mild air; and as they wrangled the green lord began to growl instead of snore. In their heat they marked it not.

Soon the argument took the turn that, in that age, sooner or later enlivened most debates. Hans, holding the bridle in his right hand, cuffed Ulric soundly with his left. Ulric returned it with interest, his right being free. There they went—ding-dong—over the horse’s mane, pummelling each other and jagging the poor beast, until it backed and set an iron heel upon a tender promontory of the sprawled Count.

He started up with a howl, one hand clapped to his hurt, the other tugging at his hilt; and in an instant his naked sword was out and his tongue outstripped it in curses. The servants, frightened out of their courage, let go the horse. Off it went whinnying; off they ran after it crying; and after them plunged the green noble, sword in hand, rebounding from hedge to hedge in a headlong, zigzag fury down the narrow lane.

Gerard, in the midst of this hurtling folly, turned his back on them all and went calmly south—glad to have saved the four tin farthings he had actually made ready for trinkgeld, and far too heavy at heart to smile at the spectacle.

The sun drew low. For some time he had been hoping, and hoping in vain, to see an inn by the way; and now uneasiness crept up his wet sleeves. Worse followed: black clouds gathered and thickened, and the light that had gilded the road became a thin, frightened thing.

He quickened to almost a run; but it was in vain. The rain came down in torrents, drenching him to the skin, and it seemed to douse the very sun, whose rays, already failing, could not contend with this new assault.

He trudged on—dark, wet, and in an unknown region. “Fool! to leave Margaret,” he said aloud, as the water ran from his hair into his eyes.

Then the darkness thickened again. He was entering a great wood. Huge branches shot across the narrow road; the way became an inky passage, a cave with a rugged floor, and he stumbled and stumbled, hands out, feeling for the safer patches, his stomach hollow, his limbs shivering.

On, and on, and on, until the wolves rose from their lairs and bayed all round the wood. The sound was not a single cry but a ring of voices—near, far, high, low—crossing each other like notes in a cruel choir. There was no wind; each small noise carried: light feet pattered at times over the newly fallen leaves; low branches rustled with creatures gliding swiftly past. To Gerard’s strained ear the forest became an instrument played by unseen hands, every snap and scuff a message without words, and therefore the more dreadful for being untranslatable.

His hair bristled; he gripped his cudgel and made ready to sell his life dear.

Then, in the sea of ink, he saw a great fiery star close to the ground. He hailed it as he would his patron saint. “CANDLE! a CANDLE!” he cried, and tried to run; but the dark, broken way tripped him back to walking. The light was farther than he had thought, as hope often is when one is hungry and wet. Yet at last, in the very heart of the forest, he came upon a house. Candles shone in its windows; loud voices rolled out from within, thick as steam.

He looked up for a signboard. There was none. “Not an inn after all,” he said sadly. “No matter. What Christian would turn a dog out into this wood to-night?” And he went to the door that led to the voices.

He opened it slowly and put his head in, timidly. He drew it out at once, as if slapped in the face, and recoiled into the rain and darkness.

Within was a large but low room, and in the middle stood a huge round stove, a clay oven that rose nearly to the ceiling. About it wet clothes were drying—some on lines, and some more compendiously on rustics themselves. Those garments, soaked with the day’s wet and the dirt of a life, and lined with what a foot-traveller in these parts would call rammish clowns, breathed out rank vapours in steaming clouds. It was not one smell but a parliament of smells, each insisting on dominion: sour wool, old sweat, damp leather, wet mud scraped with knives, and beneath it the persistent animal note that clung to men who lived near beasts and seldom near clean water.

In one corner huddled a travelling family, and from it flowed the peculiar sickly smell of neglected brats—warm milk turned stale, unwashed skin, and bedding that had swallowed too many nights. Garlic filled the interstices of the air, as if someone had tried to plaster the room with it, clove by clove. All this with the windows shut, the furnace at full heat, and the breath of at least forty persons thickening the same small space. The room had a sound too: not music, but the constant chew of voices—laughs that broke like sticks, coughing, the scrape of boots, and over all a deep, communal exhalation, as if the house itself breathed.

They had just supped.

Now Gerard, like most artists, had sensitive organs; and these potent effluvia struck dismay into him. Yet outside the rain lashed him, and inside the light and fire tempted him like warm bread to a fasting man.

He could not force his way through the palpable perfumes at once; he returned to the threshold again and again like a moth singed yet still drawn. At last he discovered that the smells did not entirely mix—no fiend was there to stir them round—so that each held its own little empire. The family’s sourness predominated in two corners; stewed rustic reigned in the centre; garlic ruled near the window. By hasty analysis he found, too, that garlic described the smallest aerial orbit, and the reeking rustic darted farthest—a flavour as if ancient goats, or the fathers of all foxes, had been dragged through a river and were here dried by Nebuchadnezzar.

So he crept into a corner close to the door. Even there the heat and vapours circulated and made the walls drip; and the home-nurtured novice felt a cold snake-wind about his legs while his head turned heavy as lead. Next came the strange mingling of choking, sweet slumber, and a sense of dying—three things in one cup.

He was within an ace of swooning; but he recovered into a deep disgust and a deeper discouragement, and resolved to go back to Holland at peep of day. The resolve gave him a thin comfort: it set an end, at least, to this road. Being faint with hunger, he asked one of the garlic men whether this was not an inn after all.

“Whence come you,” was the answer, “who know not ‘The Star of the Forest’?”

“I am a stranger; and in my country inns have aye a sign.”

“Droll country yours! What need of a sign to a public-house—a place every soul knows?”

Gerard was too tired and empty for argument. He asked where he could find the landlord.

At this new display of ignorance the man’s contempt rose beyond words. He pointed across the room to a middle-aged woman seated on the other side of the oven, and then told his mates what an outlandish animal had strayed among them. Loud voices stopped one by one as the news travelled. Eyes turned on pivots and followed Gerard and his every movement—silently, zoologically—as if they could read his origin in the cut of his wet sleeves.

The landlady sat on a chair an inch or two higher than the rest, between two bundles. From the first, a huge heap of feathers and wings, she plucked downy plumes and stripped the others from their quills, stuffing bundle two. The floor about her was littered ankle-deep; the feathers contributed a stuffy little malaria which in a sweet room might have been noticed, but here went for nothing.

Gerard asked if he could have something to eat.

She opened her eyes wide. “Supper is over this hour and more.”

“But I had none of it, good dame.”

“Is that my fault? You were welcome to your share for me.”

“But I was benighted, and a stranger; and belated sore against my will.”

“What have I to do with that? All the world knows ‘The Star of the Forest’ sups from six till eight. Come before six, ye sup well; come before eight, ye sup as pleases Heaven; come after eight, ye get a clean bed, and a stirrup cup, or a horn of kine’s milk, at the dawning.”

Gerard stared. “May I go to bed then, dame?” he said, sulky with hunger. “For it is ill sitting up wet and fasting, and the byword saith, ‘He sups who sleeps.’”

“The beds are not come yet,” she replied.

“The beds are not come?” Gerard echoed inwardly, but did not dare ask what she meant; every word he spoke seemed to astonish the room, and the eyes were on him—he could feel them as one feels a draught at the nape. He leaned against the wall and sighed audibly.

A titter ran round the watchful company.

“So this is Germany,” he thought bitterly; “and Germany is a great country by Holland. Small nations for me.”

He told himself it would be his first night in the land and his last. He was in that thin place between wakefulness and collapse when an elbow drove into his ribs. He turned sharply. The offender only pointed across the room. In a corner a woman beckoned him. He went toward her gingerly, surprised and irresolute, so that to a spectator her curling finger might have been pulling him across the floor by gut-line. When he reached her, she said in a fine hearty voice, “Hold the child.”

Before he could refuse, she plumped the bairn into his arms.

He stood transfixed, the child a sudden weight of living warmth in his hands, and a horror on his face long and plain as a confession.

At that rueful countenance the lynx-eyed conclave laughed loud and long.

“Never heed them,” the woman said cheerfully. “They know no better; how should they, bred an’ born in a wood?” She rummaged among her clothes with two busy hands—one of which Gerard had freed by shifting the child. Presently she fished out a small tin plate and a dried pudding; then, taking her child back under one arm, held plate and pudding out to Gerard with the other, keeping a thumb on the pudding to prevent it slipping.

“Put it in the stove,” she said. “You are too young to lie down fasting.”

Gratitude surged in him—sharp and clean as the first draught of fresh water after brine. He thanked her warmly. Yet as he went toward the stove, he caught the landlady’s eye and asked, almost pleading, “May I, dame?”

“Why not?” she said, as if he had asked whether he might breathe. Even that question surprised them, though less than the rest.

At the stove he found the oven door blocked by the rammish clowns. They did not budge. He hesitated, and the landlady, seeing, calmly put down her feathers and came. Without a word of temper she pulled a hircine man or two aside and pushed another hircine man or two this way and that, her impassive face the face of a housewife shifting furniture.

“Turn about is fair play,” she said. “Ye have been dry this ten minutes and better.”

Her eye was not deceived: Gorgonii had done stewing and begun baking. Debarred the stove they trundled away, all but one whom she had set like a table where she wished him, and there he stood like a table. Gerard slid the pudding into the oven, and, drawing near the heat, felt his own soaked clothes begin to steam. The smell of warm grain and fat rose faintly from the pudding as it revived; it was not a feast, but it spoke a language his body could understand without translation.

The door opened and in flew a bundle of straw, hurled by a hind with a pitchfork. Another and another followed until the room looked like a clean farmyard. Then the straw was spread in rings round the stove in layered ranks, like seats in an arena, and in a moment the whole company was down on its back.

The beds had come.

Gerard drew out his pudding. It was delicious—salt, brown, and honest; the heat had softened it, and hunger did the rest. While he relished it, the woman who had saved him from fasting, now abed, beckoned him again.

He went to her bundle-side. “She is waiting for you,” she whispered.

He hurried back to the stove, gobbled the rest, casting uneasy glances at the landlady, who sat silent as fate amid the prostrate multitude. When the last bite was swallowed, he went to her.

“Thank you kindly, dame, for waiting for me.”

“You are welcome,” she said calmly, making neither much nor little of it, and began again to gather feathers.

He stopped her. “Nay, that is my task,” he said, and went down on his knees to collect them with ardour, scooping the soft drift into order. The feathers clung to his wet fingers and to his sleeves; the room’s stale warmth made them stick as if the air itself were a paste.

She watched him demurely. “I wot not whence ye come,” she said, with the last relic of distrust; then, more cordially, “but ye have been well brought up. Y’ have had a good mother, I’ll go bail.”

At the door she committed the whole company to Heaven in a formula, and disappeared.

Gerard went to his straw in the very corner—for the guests lay round the sacred stove by seniority, that is, by priority of arrival. This punishment was a boon to him, for he lay upon the shore of odour and stifling heat instead of mid-ocean. He turned his face away from the steaming centre and began to drift.

He was just dropping off when he woke to a noise: the hind with the pitchfork was remorselessly shaking guest after guest to ask whether it was he who had picked up the mistress’s feathers.

“It was I,” Gerard cried.

“Oh, it was you, was it?” The man strode over the intermediate sleepers. “She bade me say, ‘One good turn deserves another,’ and so here’s your nightcap.” He thrust a great oaken mug beneath Gerard’s nose.

“I thank her, and bless her; here goes—ugh!” Gratitude ended in a wry face: the beer was muddy and carried a strange medicinal twang, new and wrong to a Hollander’s tongue, as if herbs had been boiled in it for a sick cow.

“Trinke aus!” shouted the hind reproachfully.

“Enow is as good as a feast,” Gerard murmured, Jesuitically, and stopped.

The hind looked at him with pity, as at a man lacking a sense. “Ich brings euch,” he said, took the mug, and drained it to the bottom.

Then Gerard turned to the wall, pulled up two handfuls of clean straw, and bored into them with his finger until he made a little scabbard; he sheathed his nose in it like a weapon he wished to keep from harm. Soon they were all asleep—men, maids, wives, and children—higgledy-piggledy and snoring in a dozen keys like an orchestra slowly tuning. The room’s sounds knitted into a thick blanket of breath and grunt; and in that coarse music Gerard’s body lay on straw in Germany, while his spirit went away to Sevenbergen, where the air was different, and a familiar voice spoke in a familiar tongue.

When he woke, morning had cleared the night’s foulness as much as it might. Nearly all his fellow-passengers were gone; one or two waited for dinner at nine o’clock—though it was but six. He paid the landlady her demand—two pfenning, about an English halfpenny. The hind demanded trinkgeld; getting a trifle more than usual, he saw Gerard eyeing a foaming milk-pail newly brought from the cow, and hoisted it bodily to his lips.

“Drink your fill, man,” he said.

The milk was cold and sweet and thick with the pasture’s own green breath; it rinsed away, for a moment, the night’s garlic and steam. Gerard offered to pay, but the hind told him in broad patois that a man might swallow a skinful of milk, or a breakfast of air, without putting hand to pouch.

At the door Gerard found his benefactress of the night, and with her a huge-chested artisan, her husband. Gerard thanked her, and in the spirit of the age offered her a creutzer for the pudding.

She repulsed his hand quietly, colouring faintly. “For what do you take me? We are travellers and strangers the same as you, and bound to feel for those in like plight.”

Gerard blushed and stammered excuses. The hulking husband grinned, superior to them both.

“Give the vixen a kiss for her pudding, and cry quits,” he said, with an air at once impartial, judge-like, and Jove-like.

Gerard obeyed the lofty behest and kissed the wife’s cheek—warm, flour-scented, and real as kindness. “A blessing go with you both, good people,” he said.

“And God speed you, young man!” they answered; and so they parted, and never met again in this world.

The sun had just risen. Rain-drops on leaves glittered like diamonds; the wood’s small birds tried their morning notes in the rinsed air, and each sound, clean and separate, was like a balm after last night’s choking chorus. Gerard steered south, and did not even remember his overnight resolve to turn back.

Eight leagues he walked that day. In the afternoon he came upon a huge building with an enormous arched gateway and a postern by its side.

“A monastery!” he cried, joyfully. “I go no further lest I fare worse.”

He knocked at the postern. On stating whence he came and whither bound, he was admitted at once and directed to the guest-chamber, a large lofty room where travellers were fed and lodged gratis by the charity of the monastic orders. Soon the bell tinkled for vespers, and Gerard entered the church. From his place he heard the service sung so exquisitely it seemed the choir of heaven. Yet one thing was wanting: Margaret was not there to hear it with him, and that absence pricked him so sharply he sighed bitterly in the midst of rapture, as if a sweet dish had been set before him without the one spice his tongue craved.

At supper they set before him and his fellows plain but wholesome, abundant food, and good beer brewed in the convent—clearer, kinder, and without the forest inn’s bitter herbs. At an early hour they were ushered into a large dormitory; the number being moderate, each had a truckle bed, and for covering sheepskins dressed with the fleece on.

Before this, a monk, struck by Gerard’s youth and beauty, questioned him, and soon drew out his projects and his heart. When he was found to be convent-bred and going alone to Rome, he became a personage. In the morning they showed him over the convent and made him stay to dine in the refectory. They pricked him a route on a slip of parchment; and the prior gave him a silver guilden to help him on the road, and advised him to join the first honest company he should fall in with, “and not face alone the manifold perils of the way.”

“Perils?” Gerard said to himself, the word echoing in him like a note struck on wood.

That evening he came to a small straggling town where there was one inn. It had no sign; but now, better versed in the country’s customs, he detected it at once by the coats of arms painted on its walls—tokens left by distinguished visitors who had slept there at different epochs since its foundation. At present it looked more like a mausoleum than a house of cheer. Nothing moved nor sounded in it or about it; the silence was so complete it seemed a policy, as if sound itself were taxed.

Gerard hammered on the great oak door. No answer. He hallooed. No reply. After a while he hallooed louder; and at last a little round window—rather a hole in the wall—opened. A man’s head protruded cautiously, like a tortoise from its shell, and eyed Gerard stolidly without a syllable.

“Is this an inn?” Gerard asked, with a covert sneer.

The head appeared to sink into a brown study; at length it nodded, lazily.

“Can I have entertainment here?”

Again the head pondered and ended by nodding, sullenly, like a skull overburdened with catch-penny questions.

“How am I to get within, an’t please you?”

At that the head popped in as if shot; a hand popped out, pointed round the corner of the building, and slammed the window.

Gerard followed, and after some search found the fortification’s one vulnerable point: a small low door on the flank. As for the main entrance, it was used to keep out thieves and customers, except once or twice a year, when they entered together—some duke or count arriving in pomp with his train of gaudy ruffians.

Once within the outer fort, Gerard found his way to the stove—the public room, so called from its principal article—and sat near the oven, where only a few live embers glowed, giving off a mild grateful heat.

He waited long. At last a grim old fellow with a long white beard stalked solemnly in, turned the hour-glass, and stalked out again. Gerard asked him when supper would be.

The grisly Ganymede counted the guests on his fingers. “When I see thrice as many here as now.”

Gerard groaned, the sound wrung from his belly more than his throat.

The old tyrant resented even that. “Inns are not built for one,” he said. “If you can’t wait for the rest, look out for another lodging.”

Gerard sighed and held his tongue, learning that here, too, a man’s needs must be translated into the inn’s rude grammar or not spoken at all.

By degrees company trickled in, until full eighty persons of various conditions crowded the room. To the novice it became a chamber of horrors—not by ghosts, but by what living bodies do when no one expects refinement. Mothers gathered and compared ringworms as openly as if they spoke of ribbons. Men scraped mud off shoes with their knives and left it on the floor, then combed their long hair out—guests and inmates alike—and made their toilet, which was generally a dry rub. Water came in ewers; Gerard pounced on one, but at sight of the liquid lost his temper.

“Wash you first your water,” he said to the waiter, “and then a man may wash his hands withal.”

“An’ it likes you not,” came the answer, “seek another inn!”

Gerard said nothing. Quietly and courteously he asked an old traveller how far it was to the next inn.

“About four leagues.”

Then Gerard understood the grim pleasantry: the inn’s rudeness was guarded by distance, and patience was forced on the legs.

The white-beard returned with an armful of wood, and, counting the travellers, put on a log for every six—so that the hotter the room grew, the more heat he added, by an obstinate arithmetic. Gerard noticed the flaw but carefully suppressed every symptom of intelligence, lest his feet be made to carry his brains four leagues farther that night.

When perspiration and suffocation were well advanced they brought in the table-cloths—brown, dirty, and coarse as sacks worn out in agriculture and come down to this, or as shreds from the mainsail of some worn-out ship. Gerard, who had never seen such linen even in nightmare, uttered a faint cry.

“What is to do?” asked a traveller.

Gerard pointed ruefully to the dirty sackcloth. The other stared with lack-lustre eye and comprehended nought.

A Burgundian soldier, an arbalest on his back, peeped over Gerard’s shoulder. Seeing what ailed him he laughed so loud the room rang, then slapped Gerard on the back.

“Courage! le diable est mort,” he cried.

Gerard stared; he doubted the good tidings and their relevance. Yet the tones were hearty, and the arbalestrier’s face, despite a formidable beard, was so gay and genial that Gerard smiled. After a pause he said drily, “Il a bien faite avec l’eau et linge du pays; on allait le noircir a ne se reconnaitre plus.”

“Tiens, tiens!” the soldier cried. “V’la qui parle le Francais peu s’en faut.” He sat by Gerard and at once began to talk volubly of war, women, and pillage, seasoning his speech with curious oaths at which Gerard drew away from him by degrees, as from a dish over-peppered—tasty to some, but burning to others.

Presently in came the grisly servant again, counted them all on his fingers superciliously like Abraham telling sheep, went out, and returned with a deal trencher and a deal spoon for each.
