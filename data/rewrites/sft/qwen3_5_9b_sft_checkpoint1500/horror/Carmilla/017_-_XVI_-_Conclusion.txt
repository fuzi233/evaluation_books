I commit these words to paper with a facade of composure, though the truth is, I am a trembling ruin. It takes nothing short of your earnest, repeated pleas to force me to sit and recount a nightmare that has left my nerves frayed for months and resurrected the unspeakable terrors of my past. Even now, delivered from the immediate threat, those shadows make my days and nights a hollow dread, turning solitude into a place where the walls seem to breathe.

Before I close this account, let me speak briefly of Baron Vordenburg, the keeper of that strange and terrible lore. To our fortune—and perhaps to the Countess Mircalla's undoing—it was his knowledge that revealed the location of her grave. He had retreated to Graz, surviving on a meager stipend, the last dregs of a once-princely fortune in Upper Styria. There, he dedicated himself to the minute, laborious excavation of every authenticating record regarding vampirism. He knew the classics by heart—"Magia Posthuma," "Phlegon de Mirabilibus," "Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis"—and John Christopher Herenberg's treatise, *Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris*. He possessed a voluminous digest of judicial cases, from which he had distilled a system of principles governing the creature's condition.

Vordenburg taught me a vital lesson: the deadly pallor often attributed to these revenants is merely theatrical fiction. In the grave, and when they emerge to haunt the living, they wear the mask of healthy vitality. When exposed to the light within their coffins, however, the rot reveals itself, just as it did with the long-dead Countess Karnstein.

They possess a terrifying physiology. They slip out of their tombs and return within set hours, disturbing neither the earth nor the linens, their movement defying natural law. Their existence is amphibious, sustained by a daily, renewed slumber in the dark soil, fed by a horrific thirst for living blood. This hunger grants them vigor in the waking world, fueling a fascination that rivals the intensity of earthly love. For specific victims, they display an inexhaustible patience and cunning, employing stratagems to bypass obstacles, courting their prey with the refinement of an epicure to savor the moment of consumption. They crave a semblance of consent, yearning to drain the life from those they have ensnared. Yet, in ordinary instances, they abandon subtlety, striking with brute violence, strangling, and exhausting their victim in a single, brutal feast.

There appears to be a unique constraint on the vampire, particularly in the case of Mircalla. She seemed bound to a name that, if not her true identity, must anagrammatically reconstruct the letters composing it. *Carmilla*. *Millarca*. These were her guises, shifting forms of the same fatal essence.

After Carmilla's expulsion, the Baron stayed with us for some weeks. My father recounted to him the tale of the Moravian nobleman and the tragedy at the Karnstein churchyard. Curiously, the Baron asked how he had ever pinpointed the exact coordinates of the concealed tomb. His features, usually sharp and severe, puckered into a mysterious, knowing smile. Gazing down at a worn spectacles case, he fumbled with it before looking up again.

"I hold many journals and papers penned by that remarkable man," he began, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "The most curious of all treats of the visit you mentioned to Karnstein. Tradition, of course, distorts the truth, coloring it with myth. He might have passed as a Moravian nobleman due to his residence there, yet he was, in truth, a native of Upper Styria. In his earliest youth, he was a passionate, favored lover of the beautiful Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Her premature death plunged him into an inconsolable grief."

He paused, his eyes distant. "It is the nature of vampires to multiply, but they follow an ascended, ghostly law. Assume a territory free from such pestilence. How does the plague begin? A person, driven to wickedness or despair, puts an end to themselves. Under certain conditions, a suicide becomes a vampire. This specter haunts the sleeping, causing them to die, and almost invariably, those who pass into the grave transform into vampires. This was the fate of Mircalla, haunted by a demon. My ancestor, Vordenburg—whose title I still bear—soon discerned the truth. Through his studies, he uncovered the laws of this wretched existence."

"The suspicion inevitably fell upon the dead Countess, the woman who had been his idol," the Baron continued. "Yet, a profound horror seized him at the thought of her remains being profaned by a posthumous execution. He believed that upon expulsion from the amphibious state of the grave, the vampire would project into a far more horrible existence. Driven by love and a twisted mercy, he devised a stratagem. He staged a journey to retrieve her remains, only to execute a real obliteraton of her monument. When age finally claimed him, looking back across the valleys he had left behind, a fresh horror possessed him. He realized the magnitude of his deception. With trembling hands, he traced the errors of his plan, creating notes that would guide future seekers to the beast's lair. He drafted a confession of his own treachery. Had he lived longer, he might have acted further, but death intervened. And now, the hand of a remote descendant directs this pursuit, too late for many, to the final resting place of the monster."

We spoke for some time afterward. Among the chilling details he shared was this: "One sure sign of the vampire is the power of the hand. The slender fingers of Mircalla closed upon the General's wrist like a vice of cold steel when he raised the hatchet. But the grip is not merely crushing; it leaves a lingering numbness in the seized limb, a paralysis that recovers only slowly, if it ever does."

That spring, my father took me on a tour of Italy. We were gone for more than a year, seeking distance from the trauma. Long before the terror of recent events fully subsided, the image of Carmilla returned to my mind with ambiguous, cruel alternations. Sometimes she appeared as the playful, languid beauty; other times, the writhing fiend I had witnessed in the ruined church. Often, awakening from a reverie, I found myself startled, convinced I had heard the light, impossible step of Carmilla pausing just outside the drawing-room door.
