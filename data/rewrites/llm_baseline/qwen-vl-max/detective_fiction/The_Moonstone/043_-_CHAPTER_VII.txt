The moment I appeared in the doorway, Rachel rose from the piano.

I closed the door behind me. We stood facing each other across the length of the room, silent and still. The motion of rising seemed to be all she could manage—her body rigid, her mind suspended, as if every faculty had collapsed into the single act of staring at me.

A pang of doubt struck me—had I come too abruptly? I stepped forward a few paces. “Rachel,” I said softly.

The sound of my voice stirred something deep within her. Colour returned to her cheeks, warmth to her limbs. She moved toward me, though without speaking. Slowly, almost as if guided by forces beyond her control, she drew nearer. Her eyes brightened with returning awareness; a flush crept over her face. I forgot everything—the purpose that brought me here, the stain on my name, the weight of past and future alike. I saw only the woman I loved, coming closer, trembling, uncertain. I could not hold back. I pulled her into my arms and pressed kisses upon her face.

For a fleeting second, it seemed as though she might return them—might have forgotten. But before the thought could take root, she recoiled. With a cry like horror, and strength I would not have resisted, she pushed me away. In her eyes, I saw cold fury; on her lips, contempt. She looked at me as one might look at a stranger who had dishonored them.

“You coward!” she said. “You vile, heartless, selfish coward!”

Those were her first words—words no woman should ever speak to a man she once loved.

“I remember,” I said, “a time when you could have told me I’d wronged you in a way more worthy than this. I beg your pardon.”

There may have been bitterness in my voice. At the sound of my reply, her gaze, which had turned away moments before, flickered back—reluctantly. She answered in a low tone, her manner subdued, sullen, unlike anything I had known in her.

“Perhaps there’s an excuse,” she said. “After what you’ve done—how can you come to me like this? Is it manly to creep in unannounced, to test my weakness? To surprise me into letting you kiss me? That’s only a woman’s judgment. I should have known better. I should have controlled myself. I should have said nothing.”

Her apology was worse than the insult. Even the most broken man would feel shame beneath such words.

“If my honor weren’t in your hands,” I said, “I’d leave now—and never see you again. You asked about what I’ve done. What have I done?”

“What have you done?” she echoed, stunned. “_You_ ask _me_?”

“I do.”

“You’ve kept my secret,” she replied. “And you’ve suffered for it. Do you think I deserve to be insulted by being asked what you’ve done? Has all sense of gratitude died in you? You were once a gentleman. Once dear to my mother—dearer still to me—”

Her voice broke. She sank into a chair, turned her back, and buried her face in her hands.

I waited, holding myself together. In that silence, I felt two things equally: the sting of her scorn, and the pride that barred me from sharing her pain.

“If you won:” I said, “I must. I’ve come here with something serious to say. Will you do me the justice of listening?”

She did not move. I made no appeal. I did not approach. With quiet dignity, I told her of my discovery at the Shivering Sand—of Rosanna Spearman, of the nightgown, of everything that led to it. It took time. From beginning to end, she never turned to look at me. She said nothing.

I held my temper. My entire future hung on not losing control. The moment had come to test Mr. Bruff’s theory. In the tense silence, I stepped to face her directly.

“I have a question,” I said. “It brings us back to a painful subject. Did Rosanna Spearman show you the nightgown? Yes or no?”

She leapt up, walked straight to me. Her eyes searched mine as if seeking something new—something hidden.

“Are you mad?” she asked.

I remained calm. “Rachel,” I said quietly, “will you answer?”

She ignored me. “Do you have some ulterior motive? Some fear about the future? They say your father’s death made you rich. Have you come to compensate me for the Diamond? And do you feel shame for it? Is that why you pretend innocence? Why you tell this story about Rosanna? Is there guilt beneath all this falsehood?”

I stopped her. I could not hold back any longer.

“You’ve done me an unspeakable wrong!” I burst out. “You suspect me of stealing your Diamond. I have a right to know—why!”

“Suspect you?” she cried, her anger rising. “_You villain! I saw you take it with my own eyes!_”

The revelation hit me like a blow. The entire foundation of Mr. Bruff’s theory crumbled. I stood there, innocent, yet looking like a guilty man caught in his crime. Before her eyes, I was defeated.

She stepped back from the sight of my humiliation. The sudden silence frightened her. “I spared you then,” she said. “I would have spared you now—if you hadn’t forced me to speak.” She turned to leave—but hesitated at the door. “Why did you come here to humiliate yourself? Why to humiliate me?” She took a few steps, paused. “For God’s sake, say something! If you have any mercy left, don’t let me degrade myself like this! Say something—and drive me out!”

I moved toward her, barely aware of my actions. Perhaps I meant to keep her until she revealed more. But from the moment I realized that the evidence condemning me in her eyes was her own vision, nothing was clear—not even my own innocence. I took her hand. I tried to speak firmly. All I could say was, “Rachel, you once loved me.”

She shuddered, looked away. Her hand trembled in mine. “Let go,” she whispered.

My touch had the same effect as my voice had earlier. After calling me a coward, after accusing me of theft—yet still, while her hand lay in mine, I was her master.

I gently pulled her back to the center of the room. I sat beside her. “Rachel,” I said, “I can’t explain the contradiction in what I’m about to tell you. I can only speak the truth—as you have spoken it. You saw me—with your own eyes—you saw me take the Diamond. Before God, I swear I now know I took it for the first time. Still doubt me?”

She didn’t hear. “Let go of my hand,” she repeated faintly. That was all. Her head fell onto my shoulder. Her fingers curled around mine, even as she asked me to release her.

I did not press further. But I would not give up. My chance to regain my standing among honest men depended on making her reveal everything. Maybe she had missed something—a detail, perhaps—small enough to seem insignificant, but which, under scrutiny, might prove my innocence. I held her hand. I spoke with the sympathy and trust of the past.

“I want to ask you something,” I said. “Tell me everything—from when we said goodnight, to when you saw me take the Diamond.”

She lifted her head, tried to pull away. “Oh, why go back to it? Why go back?”

“I’ll tell you why, Rachel. You’re a victim. So am I. We’re both trapped by a monstrous lie that’s passed for truth. If we examine the night of your birthday together, maybe we’ll understand each other.”

Her head dropped again. Tears gathered, slid down her cheeks. “Oh,” she said, “have I never hoped? Have I not tried to see it as you do now?”

“You’ve tried alone,” I said. “You’ve not tried with me.”

Those words stirred something in her. She began to answer—not just obey, but truly engage.

“Let’s begin,” I said. “After we said goodnight—did you go to bed?”

“I went to bed.”

“What time?”

“About midnight.”

“Did you sleep?”

“No. I couldn’t.”

“You were restless?”

“I was thinking of you.”

The words nearly undid me. The tone—more than the words—cut through me. I paused before continuing.

“Did you have light in your room?”

“None—until I got up and lit a candle.”

“How long after?”

“About an hour. One o’clock.”

“Did you leave your bedroom?”

“I was going to. I had on my dressing-gown. I was going to get a book from my sitting-room.”

“Had you opened the door?”

“I had just opened it.”

“But you didn’t enter?”

“No. I was stopped.”

“What stopped you?”

“I saw a light under the door. Footsteps approaching.”

“Were you afraid?”

“Not then. I thought it was my mother. She sleeps poorly. She’d begged me to let her guard the Diamond. I thought she was coming to check on me, to talk about it again.”

“What did you do?”

“I blew out the candle—so she’d think I was asleep. I was stubborn—I wanted to keep the Diamond where I chose.”

“After that, did you go back to bed?”

“No. At that moment, the sitting-room door opened—and I saw——”

“You saw?”

“You.”

“Dressed normally?”

“No.”

“In your nightgown?”

“In your nightgown—with a candle in your hand.”

“Alone?”

“Alone.”

“Could you see my face?”

“Yes.”

“Clearly?”

“Quite. The candle lit it.”

“Were your eyes open?”

“Yes.”

“Did they seem strange? Fixed? Vacant?”

“No. Bright—brighter than usual. You looked around, as if you knew you shouldn’t be there. As if you feared being seen.”

“Did you notice how I walked?”

“You walked as you always do. You came halfway into the room—then stopped, looked around.”

“What did you do when you saw me?”

“I froze. I couldn’t speak. Couldn’t call. Couldn’t move.”

“Could I see you?”

“You could have. But you didn’t look my way. I’m certain you didn’t see me.”

“How are you sure?”

“Would you have taken the Diamond? Would you have acted as you did? Would you be here now—if you’d known I was awake?”

Don’t make me talk about that part,” she said. “I want to stay calm. Go on.”

She was right. I continued.

“What did I do after stopping?”

“You turned and went to the corner near the window—where the Indian cabinet stands.”

“When I faced the cabinet, my back was to you. How did you see what I did?”

“When you moved, I moved.”

“To see what I was doing?”

“There are three mirrors in the room. I saw you reflected in one.”

“What did you see?”

“You placed the candle on the cabinet. Opened and closed drawers—until you reached the one with the Diamond. You stared at it. Then you reached in and took it.”

“How do you know it was the Diamond?”

“I saw your hand go in. Then the gleam between your fingers when you pulled it out.”

“Did your hand return to the drawer?”

“No. You held the Diamond in your right hand. You picked up the candle with your left.”

“Did you look around again?”

“No.”

“Did you leave immediately?”

“No. You stood still—long enough for me to see your face in the mirror. You looked like someone thinking—unsatisfied.”

“What happened next?”

“You suddenly stirred. You walked straight out.”

“Did you close the door?”

“No. You passed quickly into the hall, leaving it open.”

“And then?”

“The light vanished. Your footsteps faded. I was alone in the dark.”

“Was there anything else—between then and when the house found out the Diamond was gone?”

“Nothing.”

“Are you sure? Might you have slept?”

“I never slept. Never went back to bed. Nothing happened until Penelope came in the morning.”

I released her hand, stood, paced the room. Every question answered. Every detail laid bare. I’d even asked about sleepwalking, intoxication—both dismissed by her own testimony. Yet the theft remained—solid, undeniable—while all else swirled in darkness. No light. Not even after uncovering Rosanna’s secret. Not even now, after hearing it from Rachel herself.

She broke the silence.

“Well?” she said. “You asked. I answered. You made me hope—because you hoped. What do you say now?”

Her tone warned me: my influence was gone.

“We were to look at the night together,” she said. “And understand. Did we?”

She waited—pitilessly. In answering, I made a fatal mistake. I let frustration override reason.

“If you had spoken when you should have,” I began, “if you had done me the justice to explain—”

She interrupted with a cry of rage. My words ignited her fury.

“Explain myself?” she repeated. “Oh! Is there another man like this? I spare him—my heart breaking. I protect him—my reputation at stake. And he—of all people—turns on me and says I should have explained! After believing in you? After loving you? After thinking of you day and night—he wonders I didn’t accuse him the moment we met: ‘My darling, you’re a thief!’ ‘My hero, you stole my Diamond!’ That’s what I should have said! You villain! You mean, mean, mean villain! I’d lose fifty diamonds rather than see your face lying to me as it does now!”

I picked up my hat. For her sake—yes, I can say it honestly—for her sake, I turned silently and opened the door.

She lunged forward, seized the door, slammed it shut, pointed back to my seat.

“No!” she said. “Not yet! It seems I owe you an explanation. You will stay. Or you will stoop to the lowest infamy and force your way out.”

It tore at my heart to see her—to hear her. I gave a sign of submission. That was all I could do.

The flush of anger faded from her face. I returned to my seat in silence. She waited, composed herself. When she spoke again, only one sign of emotion remained: she did not look at me. Her hands were clasped tightly in her lap. Her eyes fixed on the floor.

“I ought to have done you the justice to explain,” she said, echoing my words. “You shall see if I tried. I told you I never slept, never returned to bed. I refrained from alerting the house. Despite what I saw, I loved you enough to believe any impossibility—rather than admit you were a deliberate thief. I thought and thought—and finally wrote you a letter.”

“I never received it.”

“I know. Wait. My letter would not have ruined you. It would have said—only what you could not miss—that I knew you were in debt. That I knew you were careless, reckless, about money. You’d remember the French lawyer. You’d know what I meant. Then I offered—privately, no word spoken aloud—money. As much as I could raise. I would have pledged the Diamond myself, if need be! That’s what I wrote. And I did more. I arranged with Penelope to give you the letter when no one was near. I planned to lock myself in my room, leave the sitting-room empty. I hoped—oh, I hoped with all my soul—that you’d take the chance and return the Diamond secretly.”

I tried to speak. She raised a hand, silenced me. Her temper surged again. She rose, approached me.

“I know what you’ll say,” she continued. “You’ll remind me I never got the letter. I’ll tell you why. I tore it up.”

“For what reason?”

“For the best reason. I preferred tearing it up to giving it to a man like you! What news reached me in the morning? Just as my plan was complete, I heard—_you_, you!!!—were the first to summon the police. You led the search. You worked harder than anyone to recover the jewel! You even had the audacity to ask to speak to _me_ about the missing Diamond—the very one you’d stolen, the one you still held! After that proof of your deceit, I tore up the letter. But even then—when I was being questioned by the policeman _you_ sent in—even then, a foolish hope remained. I told myself: ‘He played his farce before everyone. Let me see if he can play it before me.’ Someone told me you were on the terrace. I went down. I forced myself to look at you. To speak to you. Do you remember what I said?”

I might have said I remembered every word. But what purpose would it serve?

How could I tell her that her words had startled me, distressed me, suggested she was in danger of nervous collapse, even sparked a fleeting doubt—yet never hinted at the truth? Without proof, how could I convince her I knew nothing of her inner thoughts that day?

The silence stretched. The truth remained buried. And I stood helpless—between love, suspicion, and the unbearable weight of a mystery that refused to yield.
