The farmhouse perched a mile above the village, nestled in a rugged expanse of moorland where the heather crept aggressively over the garden walls and the bridle path terminated abruptly at the threshold. On three sides, an amphitheater of granite hills cradled the house like a ship in a harbor. These hills were bare of trees, their surfaces a mosaic of moss and heather, yet they possessed a comforting, protective presence. Durrance often felt they donned their shifting coats of emerald, purple, and russet brown with deliberate intent, merely to please the girl they sheltered. The house faced the long, sloping country leading down to the inlet of the Lough. From its windows, the view swept over sparse thickets, a few tilled fields, and whitewashed cottages until it caught a glimpse of the tall woods lining the bank and the bright water where gulls poised and dipped.

Durrance rode up the track one afternoon and recognized the house instantly. As he approached, the melody of a violin drifted from the windows like a warm welcome. His hand hesitated on the reins, and a specific, potent hope—one he had allowed his imagination to nurture—rose within him, suspending his breath.

He tethered his horse and stepped through the gate. Outside lay the formless silhouette of a barrack; inside, however, was a sanctuary of comfort. The room shown to him, adorned with brass fittings and gleaming oak, offered a wide prospect that made it as bright as the afternoon itself. Durrance found himself imagining it later, with blinds drawn against a winter night, a fire burning red on the hearth, and the wind skirling around the hills and tapping against the glass.

Ethne greeted him without a trace of surprise.

"I thought you would come," she said, a smile lighting her face.

Durrance laughed suddenly as they shook hands, leaving Ethne wondering at the suddenness of his mirth. Her gaze followed his eyes to the violin resting on a table beside her. It was pale in color, bearing a small patch near the bridge where worm-eaten wood had been carefully replaced.

"It is yours," she said. "You were in Egypt. I could not bear to send it back to you there."

"I have hoped lately, since I learned of it," Durrance replied, "that you might accept it."

"You see I have," Ethne said, meeting his gaze directly. "I accepted it some time ago. There was a time when I needed assurance that I had sure friends. Something tangible helped. I was very glad to have it."

Durrance lifted the instrument from the table, handling it with the delicacy due to a sacred vessel.

"You have played upon it? Perhaps the Musoline overture?" he asked.

"Do you remember that?" she returned, laughing. "Yes, I have played it, but only recently. For a long time I kept it away. It spoke to me too intimately of many things I wished to forget," and she spoke these words without hesitation or a lowering of her eyes.

Durrance collected his luggage from Rathmullen the following day and remained at the farm for a week. Yet, until the final hour of his visit, neither Ethne nor Durrance mentioned Harry Feversham, despite spending much time together. Dermod was even more broken than Mrs. Adair's descriptions had suggested. His speech had shrunk to monosyllables; his frame had wasted away, his clothes hanging loosely on his limbs, his stature diminished. Even the anger had vanished from his eyes. He had become a recline, dozing most of the day by a fire, even in the heat of July. His longest excursion was to the small grey church standing naked on a mound a quarter-mile away, a journey that taxed his remaining strength. He was an old man fallen into decrepitude, barely recognizable, so that his gestures and the rare tones of his voice struck Durrance as painfully artificial, like the mimicry of a dead man. His collie dog seemed to age in his company; seeing them side by side, one might have said they shared a mutual sympathy.

Consequently, Durrance and Ethne were thrown much together. During the day, whether the weather was wet or fine, they tramped the hills. She, her face glowing with color and her eyes burning with jealous eagerness, showed him her country and demanded his admiration. In the evenings, she would take her violin and, sitting as of old with an averted face, bid the strings speak of heights and depths. Durrance sat watching the sweep of her arm and the absorption of her face, silently calculating his chances. He had not arrived at Glenalla with Lieutenant Sutch's expectation that he would succeed. The shadow of Harry Feversham might well stand between them. Furthermore, Durrance knew that poverty would weigh more heavily on him than on most women. He had proof of this: though the Lennon House was ruined and its lands gone, Ethne remained among her own people. They still awaited her visits; she was still the princess of that countryside.

On the other hand, she took a frank pleasure in his company and led him to speak of his three years of service in the East. No detail was too insignificant for her inquiries. As he spoke, her eyes constantly scanned him, and the smile on her lips continually approved. Durrance did not understand what she sought. Possibly, no one could have understood unless they knew what had passed between Harry Feversham and Ethne. Durrance wore the likeness of a man, and she was anxious to ensure that the spirit of a man informed it. He was a dark lantern to her; there might be a flame burning within, or there might be only vacancy and darkness. She was pushing back the slide to be sure.

She brought up Egypt on the last day of his visit. They sat on the hillside at the edge of a stream that leaped from ledge to ledge down a miniature gorge of rock, flowing over deep pools between the ledges as a torrent of clear black water.

"I traveled once for four days amongst the mirages," he said. "Lagoons, still as a mirror and fringed with misty trees. You could almost walk your camel up to its knees in them before the lagoon receded and the sand glared at you. One cannot imagine that glare. Every stone within view dances and shakes like a heliograph; you can see—yes, actually see—the heat flow breast-high across the desert swift as this stream here, only pellucid. So until the sun sets ahead of you, level with your eyes! Imagine the nights that follow—nights of infinite silence, with a cool, friendly wind blowing from horizon to horizon—and your bed spread for you under the great dome of stars. Oh," he cried, drawing a deep breath, "but that country grows on you. It's like the Southern Cross—four overrated stars when first you see them, but in a week you begin to look for them, and you miss them when you travel north again." He raised himself on his elbow and turned suddenly toward her. "Do you know—I can only speak for myself—but I never feel alone in those empty spaces. On the contrary, I always feel very close to the things I care about, and to the few people I care about too."

Her eyes shone brightly upon him, her lips parted in a smile. He moved nearer to her on the grass, sitting with his feet gathered under him on one side and leaning on his arm.

"I used to imagine you out there," he said. "You would have loved it—from the start before daybreak in the dark to the campfire at night. You would have been at home. I used to think so as I lay awake wondering how the world went with my friends."

"And you go back there?" she asked.

Durrance did not answer immediately. The roar of the torrent throbbed around them. When he finally spoke, all enthusiasm had drained from his voice. He gazed into the stream.

"To Wadi Halfa. For two years. I suppose so."

Ethne knelt on the grass at his side.

"I shall miss you," she said.

She knelt just behind him as he sat on the ground, and again a silence fell between them.

"Of what are you thinking?"

"That you need not miss me," he said, aware that she drew back and sank down on her heels. "My appointment at Halfa—I might shorten its term. I might perhaps avoid it altogether. I have still half my furlough."

She did not answer, nor did she change her posture. She remained very still, and Durrance was alarmed. All his hopes sank. He knew that a stillness of attitude was with her as definite an expression of distress as a cry of pain with another woman. He turned toward her. Her head was bent, but she raised it as he turned, and though her lips smiled, there was a look of great trouble in her eyes. Durrance was a man like another. His first thought was whether there was not some obstacle hindering her compliance, even though she herself were willing.

"There is your father," he said.

"Yes," she answered. "There is my father too. I could not leave him."

"Nor need you," he said quickly. "That difficulty can be surmounted. To tell the truth, I was not thinking of your father at the moment."

"Nor was I," she said.

Durrance turned away and sat for a while staring down the rocks into a wrinkled pool of water just beneath. It was, after all, the shadow of Feversham that stretched between himself and her.

"I know, of course," he said, "that you would never feel trouble, as so many do, with half your heart. You would neither easily care nor lightly forget."

"I remember enough," she returned in a low voice, "to make your words rather a pain to me. Some day perhaps I may bring myself to tell everything that happened at that ball three years ago, and then you will be better able to understand why I am a little distressed. All that I can tell you now is this: I have a great fear that I was to some degree the cause of another man's ruin. I do not mean that I was to blame for it. But if I had not been known to him, his career might perhaps never have come to such an abrupt end. I am not sure, but I am afraid. I asked whether it was so, and I was told 'no,' but I think very likely that generosity dictated that answer. And the fear stays. I am much distressed by it. I lie awake with it at night. And then you come, whom I greatly value, and you say quietly, 'Will you please spoil my career too?'" She struck one hand sharply into the other and cried, "But that I will not do."

And again he answered:

"There is no need that you should. Wadi Halfa is not the only place where a soldier can find work to his hand."

His voice had taken on a new hopefulness. For he had listened intently to her words and construed them by the dictionary of his desires. She had not said that friendship bounded all her thoughts of him. Therefore, he need not believe it. Women were given to a hinting modesty of speech, at all events the best of them. A man might read a little more emphasis into their tones, underline their words, and still be short of their meaning, as he argued. A subtle delicacy graced them in nature. Durrance was near to Benedick's mood. "One whom I value"; "I shall miss you"; there might be a double meaning in the phrases. When she said that she needed to be assured that she had sure friends, did she not mean that she needed their companionship? But the argument, had he been acute enough to see it, proved how deep he was sunk in error. For what this girl spoke, she habitually meant, and she habitually meant no more. Moreover, on this occasion, she had particularly weighed her words.

"No doubt," she said, "a soldier can. But can this soldier find work so suitable? Listen, please, till I have done. I was so very glad to hear all that you have told me about your work and your journeys. I was still more glad because of the satisfaction with which you told it. For it seemed to me, as I listened and as I watched, that you had found the one true straight channel along which your life could run swift and smoothly and unharassed. And so few do that—so very few!" She wrung her hands and cried, "And now you spoil it all."

Durrance suddenly faced her. He ceased arguing; he cried in a voice of passion:

"I am for you, Ethne! There's the true straight channel, and upon my word I believe you are for me. I thought—I admit it—at one time I would spend my life out there in the East, and the thought contented me. But I had schooled myself into contentment, for I believed you married." Ethne ever so slightly flinched, and he himself recognized that he had spoken in a voice overloud, so that it had something almost of brutality.

"Do I hurt you?" he continued. "I am sorry. But let me speak the whole truth out; I cannot afford reticence. I want you to know the first and last of it. I say now that I love you. Yes, but I could have said it with equal truth five years ago. It is five years since your father arrested me at the ferry down there on Lough Swilly, because I wished to press on to Letterkenny and not delay a night by stopping with a stranger. Five years since I first saw you, first heard the language of your violin. I remember how you sat with your back towards me. The light shone on your hair; I could just see your eyelashes and the color of your cheeks. I remember the sweep of your arm.... My dear, you are for me; I am for you."
