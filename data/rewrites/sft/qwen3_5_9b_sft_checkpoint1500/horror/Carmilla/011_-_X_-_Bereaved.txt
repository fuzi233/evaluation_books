Bereaved

A decade had passed since we last laid eyes on him, yet the interval felt like a lifetime of decay. Time, it seemed, had not merely aged him; it had hollowed him out. Where once cordial serenity had softened his features, now a jagged edge of gloom and gnawing anxiety carved deep lines into his face. His hair, previously a rich brown, had turned a matted, shaggy grey, framing dark blue eyes that no longer held their usual penetrating warmth. Instead, they burned with a sterner, colder light, gleaming with a ferocity that suggested angers far older and deeper than simple sorrow usually breeds.

As soon as our carriage rolled back onto the gravel path, the General launched into his tale. With the blunt, unvarnished directness of a man unaccustomed to delicacy, he spoke of his "bereavement"—the loss of his beloved niece and ward. But as he spoke, his voice curdled into a venomous bitterness, a fury that bordered on madness. He inveighed against "hellish arts," words that tasted foul on his tongue, and cursed the heavens for tolerating such monstrous indulgences in sin and malignity. To him, her death was not a tragedy of nature, but a war crime committed by invisible enemies.

"My father," who was quick to read the air around him, sensed immediately that something profoundly unnatural had transpired. He leaned forward, his voice gentle but probing, asking if it would be too painful for the General to detail the circumstances that warranted such vitriolic speech.

"I would gladly tell you all," the General replied, his jaw tight, "if you possessed the capacity to believe me."

"Why not?" my father pressed gently. "What evidence would stop you?"

"Because!" The General snapped, his composure cracking momentarily. "You believe only in what suits your own narrow prejudices and comforting illusions. I remember when I stood where you stand now, blind to the truth, but I have learned the hard way."

"Test me," my father countered, his expression open and earnest. "I am no dogmatist, sir. And I know you demand proof for every conviction. Therefore, I am predisposed to respect whatever conclusion you have drawn."

"You speak the truth," the General murmured, staring ahead through the misty windows as if seeing ghosts in the passing trees. "I have not been led lightly into believing in the marvellous. What I have witnessed defies all reason, tearing apart the theories I once held sacred. I have been made the dupe of a preternatural conspiracy."

Even as he spoke these words with absolute conviction, I watched my father exchange a fleeting, sharp look with the General—a glance heavy with suspicion, perhaps bordering on doubt regarding the soldier's sanity. Fortunately, the General missed it entirely. He remained lost in a gloomy, curious scrutiny of the glades rushing past, his mind fixed on a destination that chilled the blood.

"We are heading toward the Ruins of Karnstein?" he asked suddenly, his gaze snapping back to reality. "Fortunate coincidence, indeed. I was just about to request permission to inspect those grounds myself. I have a specific purpose there. A ruined chapel, yes? And beneath it, the tombs of that extinct line?"

"There are," my father confirmed, though his tone lacked its usual gaiety. "Highly significant, I trust. Are you considering reclaiming the title and estates?"

The question hung in the air, but the General did not catch the faint, polite smile that might have accompanied it. Nor did he register the customary courtesy required when a friend jokes. Instead, his face grew grim, fierce, and terrifyingly serious, consumed by a rage that stirred within him like a storm.

"Not a single thought of inheritance," he growled, his voice rough with emotion. "I intend to dig up the bones of those fiends. By God's grace, I plan to commit a pious sacrilege here, liberating this earth from monsters and allowing honest men to sleep soundly again without fear of murder in their beds. I have stories to tell you, my dear friend, tales that I would have laughed off as impossible fiction a few short months ago."

My father studied him, and this time his gaze was not one of suspicion, but of acute intelligence and profound alarm.

"The House of Karnstein," my father noted softly, "has been silent for over a century. My late wife was maternally descended from them, but the name, the title, the castle itself—all are dust. The village lies abandoned, untouched by smoke for fifty years. Not a roof remains standing."

"That is precisely why I must go," the General interrupted, his voice dropping to a dangerous whisper. "I have heard much since I last saw you, facts that will stagger your imagination. But first, I must recount the events in their proper order. You saw my ward... my child. No creature ever walked the earth more beautiful, and only three months prior, she was in the full bloom of youth."

"Yes, poor soul," my father whispered, his voice thick with sympathy. "She was lovely, and I was heartbroken to hear of her passing. I know how deeply this wound has cut you."

He reached across, taking the General's hand, and they clasped it in a moment of shared, heavy grief. Tears welled in the old soldier's eyes, unashamed and raw.

"We have been friends for generations," the General choked out, his grip tightening painfully. "And because I am childless, she became a daughter to me. She repaid my care with a love that warmed my lonely home and gave meaning to my days. All of that is extinguished. Perhaps the years remaining to me on this wretched earth are few, but by God's mercy, I swear to accomplish a service to mankind before I breathe my last. I shall bring vengeance down upon the fiends who slaughtered my innocent child in the prime of her beauty and hope!"

"You mentioned earlier," my father interjected, pulling his hand away slowly, "that you wished to relate everything as it unfolded. Pray, proceed. I assure you, it is not idle curiosity that drives me, but a need to understand."

At that precise moment, our carriage jolted as it reached the fork where the Drunstall road branched off from our path toward Karnstein. The wheels clattered over the stones, a rhythmic, mocking beat.

"How far still to the ruins?" the General demanded, his head craning anxiously forward, scanning the darkening treeline.

"A half-league," my father replied, his voice steady despite the chill in the air. "Now, please, let us hear the story you promised."
