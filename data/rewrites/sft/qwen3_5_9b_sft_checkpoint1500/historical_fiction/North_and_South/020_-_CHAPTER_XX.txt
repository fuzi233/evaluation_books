"Old and young, boy, let 'em all eat, I have it;
Let 'em have ten tire of teeth a-piece, I care not."

Margaret returned home with a mind so burdened by the sights and sorrows she had witnessed that she struggled merely to summon the energy required for her household duties. Her mother, confined to the house and accustomed to receiving news from Margaret's briefest walks, expected a cheerful report.

"And can your factory friend come on Thursday to see you dressed?"

"She was so ill I never thought of asking her," Margaret murmured, her voice heavy with pity.

"My dear!" cried Mrs. Hale, a flicker of jealous concern crossing her features at the thought of another suffering invalid. "But it must be terribly sad to be ill in those little back streets. It is hard enough here already. What aid could you offer her, Margaret? Mr. Thornton has sent me some of his vintage port since you left. Do you think a bottle would help her?"

"No, Mother!" Margaret protested gently. "They do not appear to be destitute; they do not speak of poverty. Moreover, Bessy suffers from consumption—wine would not serve her. Perhaps I might bring her a jar of preserves made from our beloved Helstone fruits."

"But there is another family," Margaret continued, her composure breaking as she recounted the grim realities of Higgins's cottage, describing the despair and the stark conditions she had observed. "Oh, Mamma, Mamma! How am I to adorn myself in finery and attend these fashionable gatherings after witnessing such profound misery today?"

Her distress proved too much for Mrs. Hale. The sight of her daughter's agitation, coupled with the knowledge of the suffering outside, stirred a restless irritation within her. She immediately dispatched Margaret to pack a basket in the drawing-room, instructing it to be sent to the afflicted families instantly. She was nearly incensed when Margaret remarked that it mattered little if the gift arrived until morning, for Mrs. Hale knew Higgins had already secured immediate provisions and had left funds with Bessy. To suggest such indifference was, in her view, unforgivable. She hovered over the task until the basket was safely delivered, then sat down, reflecting with troubled conscience.

"After all," she mused aloud, "we may have erred. It was only on Mr. Thornton's last visit that he remarked those who prolong the struggle by aiding the strikers are not true friends. And this Boucher was a striker, was he not?"

Upon Mr. Hale's ascent from a lesson with his son, the matter was brought to his attention. Margaret, in her agitated state, did not dwell on the broader implications of charity versus principle regarding the strike itself. Mr. Hale, however, approached the dilemma with the deliberation of a judge. He recalled the stark arguments Mr. Thornton had voiced moments prior, yet found himself making a reluctant compromise. While acknowledging the validity of Thornton's stance—that a prolonged strike might necessitate hiring distant labor or lead to mechanization, rendering local aid counterproductive—he admitted that in this specific instance, refusing assistance to Boucher seemed morally untenable. He resolved to visit the man personally the following morning to ascertain his needs.

True to his word, Mr. Hale visited the next day. Though Boucher was absent, he held a lengthy and compassionate conversation with his wife. He promised to secure an Infirmary order for her and, observing the ample provisions Mrs. Hale had generously supplied—which the children, acting as masters in their father's absence, had consumed somewhat freely—returned with a far more reassuring account than Margaret had dared to anticipate. Indeed, Margaret's fearful description the previous night had primed her father's imagination to envision a catastrophe; consequently, his reaction painted a picture of comfort that exceeded the grim reality.

"I shall return soon to see the man himself," Mr. Hale declared. "I am still learning to measure these homes against the cottages of Helstone. Here, I see furnishings and foods that our laborers could never dream of acquiring, yet with their wages halted, the pawnshop remains their sole recourse. One must cultivate a new standard of judgment when living in Milton."

Meanwhile, Besser showed slight improvement, though her weakness rendered her oblivious to Margaret's earlier feverish longing to see her dressed. As Margaret prepared for the evening, she could not help but contrast this somber occasion with the joyous, frivolous preparations of the past year, when she and Edith had delighted in dressing for mere amusement. Now, the act of adorning herself offered her only solace in the knowledge that her mother would derive pleasure from viewing her. When Dixon opened the door to the drawing-room, inviting admiration, Margaret blushed.

"Miss Hale looks well, ma'am," Dixon observed approvingly. "Mrs. Shaw's coral could not have been placed more successfully. It provides the perfect accent of color. Without it, Miss Margaret, you would be far too pale."

Margaret's hair, too dense for plaits, was wound in intricate twists and massive coils, forming a crown-like circle around her head before gathering into a large spiral knot at the nape. Two substantial coral pins, resembling arrows, anchored the mass, while matching strings looped her white silk sleeves. Heavy coral beads rested upon her neck, framing the curve of her throat.

"Oh, Margaret!" her mother exclaimed, seizing the moment for maternal vanity. "How I long to accompany you to one of the old Barrington assemblies, taking you as Lady Beresford once took me!"

Margaret kissed her mother, suppressing a smile despite her low spirits. "I would rather remain at home with you, Mother."

"Nonsense, darling! Do observe the dinner carefully. I am eager to learn how they manage affairs in Milton, particularly the second course. Note what they serve instead of game."

Had Mrs. Hale witnessed the table, she might have been astonished by its sumptuousness. Yet to Margaret, with her refined London sensibilities, the array of delicacies seemed excessive, the presentation lacking the tight, elegant restraint she preferred. This abundance, however, reflected Mrs. Thornton's rigid code of hospitality: every guest must have access to every dainty, regardless of inclination. Careful in her daily habits, Mrs. Thornton prided herself on setting feasts before those who cared for them. Her son, John, shared this sentiment. Though he denied himself the trivial expense of unnecessary items and regretted sending invitations, he nonetheless welcomed the opportunity to witness the old magnificence of preparation.

Margaret and her father arrived punctually. The drawing-room was empty save for Mrs. Thornton and Fanny. The space blazed with yellow silk damask and flowered carpets, adorned with ornaments until the eye grew weary, presenting a jarring contrast to the bare, utilitarian exterior overlooking the vast mill yard, where wide gates stood open for carriages. High on the left, the mill loomed, casting a shadow that darkened the summer evening prematurely.

"My son was detained by business until the last moment," Mrs. Thornton explained to Mr. Hale. "He will be here directly. Will you take a seat?"

Standing by a window, Mr. Hale turned away. "Do you not find proximity to the mill somewhat disagreeable at times?"

Mrs. Thornton drew herself erect. "Never. I have not grown so refined as to wish to forget the source of my son's wealth and power. Furthermore, there is no other factory in Milton. A single room measures two hundred and twenty square yards."

"I meant the smoke and noise—the constant flow of workers entering and leaving might prove irritating."

"I concur, Mr. Hale!" Fanny interjected. "There is a perpetual odor of steam and oil, and the noise is utterly deafening."

"I have heard noises termed 'music' far louder," Mr. Hale countered calmly. "The engine-house is at the street end; we hear it only in summer when windows are open. As for the hum of the workers, it troubles me no more than the buzzing of a bee-hive. If I reflect upon it, I associate it solely with my son, feeling how everything flows through him, directed by his head. Currently, there are no sounds from the mill; the hands have proven ungrateful enough to strike, as you may have heard. However, the business I discussed upon your arrival concerns the steps he intends to take to teach them their proper station." Her usually stern countenance deepened into dark anger as she spoke. The expression did not fade when Mr. Thornton entered. She perceived instantly the heavy burden of care and anxiety weighing upon him, a load he could not shed, even as he greeted his guests with a facade of cheer. He extended his hand to Margaret. They had never touched hands before, yet she remained unaware. He inquired after Mrs. Hale and heard Mr. Hale's optimistic report. Glancing at Margaret to gauge her agreement with her father, he saw no dissenting shadow upon her face. In that glance, struck anew by her great beauty, he noted how becoming her elegance was to her noble figure and serene countenance. It seemed she ought to wear such attire always.

While Margaret conversed with Fanny, he observed his sister's restless movements, constantly adjusting her gown, her wandering eyes scanning the room without focus. He contrasted this with Margaret's large, soft eyes, fixed steadily on a single point, radiating a gentle influence of repose. Her lips, curved in attentive listening, framed by the sweep of her glossy raven hair down to her smooth shoulders, and her round, white arms resting motionless in a poised attitude, captivated him. Mr. Thornton sighed as he absorbed this vision, then turned his back
