Days passed, and the child showed little inclination to warm to anyone in the house. She was neither disobedient nor unruly, yet there was something unsettling about her presence—a quiet melancholy that clung to her like a shadow. No adult could have embodied despondency more thoroughly; her small face, usually so youthful, bore the unmistakable marks of longing, as if she carried the weight of an exile’s sorrow.

I, Lucy Snowe, was not one to indulge in fanciful notions, yet whenever I found her curled alone in a corner, her chin propped on tiny hands, the room felt less occupied than haunted. And on moonlit nights, when I awoke to see her kneeling at the bedside, whispering fervent prayers like some devout novice, I could not help but wonder at the intensity of her solitude. The words I caught were always the same—*Papa, my dear papa!*—a refrain that revealed the singular fixation of her heart.

It might have continued thus, this slow erosion of childish spirit, had fortune not intervened. One afternoon, Mrs. Bretton coaxed her from her corner to the window-seat, bidding her watch the passersby below. She obeyed listlessly, her gaze dull—until, in an instant, her whole being transformed. Her eyes, once clouded, flashed with sudden fire; her posture straightened, and before anyone could react, she was gone—a streak of black frock and tiny braided apron darting down the street.

From the window, I watched as a gentleman—evidently familiar with her—swept her up into his arms, shielding her with his cloak before returning her to the house. He lingered, and soon enough, ascended the stairs. Mrs. Bretton’s flustered reception betrayed recognition, though her tone held a note of reproach.

*"I could not leave without seeing her settled,"* he explained.

*"You will unsettle her,"* she countered.

But the child—Paulina—had eyes only for him. When he spoke to her, her reply was simple, yet weighted with emotion: *"How is Polly’s papa?"*

There was no outburst, no childish clamor—only a quiet intensity that made the scene all the more affecting. Mr. Home, for his part, was a man of stern features, his northern bearing evident in his sharp cheekbones and measured speech. Yet his hand, resting on her head, betrayed a tenderness at odds with his rugged exterior.

*"Kiss Polly,"* she demanded softly.

He obliged, and she seemed, in that moment, utterly content.

She was his in spirit, if not in likeness—her mind shaped by his, as a cup is filled from its flagon. And though she fussed over him with a comical earnestness at tea—spilling sugar, struggling with cream pitchers—he watched her with undisguised fondness.

*"She is my comfort,"* he confessed to Mrs. Bretton.

That evening, Graham Bretton arrived, his entrance scattering the quiet like a gust of wind. He was sixteen, fair-haired, and possessed of a roguish charm that masked his sharper edges. His eyes soon fell upon Paulina, perched primly on her stool, laboring over a handkerchief with exaggerated diligence.

*"Mother,"* he declared, *"I see a young lady here to whom I have not been introduced."*

His teasing was met with maternal admonishment, but he persisted, bowing with mock gravity. Paulina, ever solemn, descended from her seat and offered a precise curtsy.

*"Tor-rer-ably well,"* she replied, her diction precise despite her youth.

Their exchange was a dance of playful barbs—his flippancy met with her blunt honesty.

*"Do you like me?"* he prodded.

He laughed, undeterred, and when she made to leave, he lifted her effortlessly into the air—an act that earned him a scolding.

*"For shame, Mr. Graham!"* she protested, indignant. *"I wonder what you would think of me if I were to treat you so!"*

And with that, she departed, leaving him grinning in her wake.
