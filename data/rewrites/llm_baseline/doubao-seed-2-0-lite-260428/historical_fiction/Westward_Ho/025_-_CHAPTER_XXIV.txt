“Let us alone. What pleasure can we have To war with evil? Is there any peace In always climbing up the climbing wave? All things have rest, and ripen toward the grave In silence; ripen, fall, and cease: Give us long rest or death, dark death, or dreamful ease.”

The great traveller Humboldt once penned a curious reflection, staring at a ragged band of Indians huddled stupidly around their fires, caked in grease and ochre paint, gnawing at ants and clay. He noted that were it not for the fashionable science of his day that framed all humanity as slowly climbing upward from brute origins, he would have been forced to conclude these wretched beings were not the raw seed of humankind, but the last tattered remnants of a once-great race, fallen far from their old grace. That instinct of his was truer than he knew, aligned straight with the old Biblical truth our English voyagers held as unshakable fact: man left to his own devices in the wilds was not ascending, but fallen, a soul trapped in darkness, “captivated by the devil at his will”, as gruff old seaman Yeo would put it. They wasted no breath debating whether their new hosts had sprung from apes, but looked on them as brothers in Christ, lost deep in the Amazon forests, as worthy of rescue as any soul back home in Devon.

The village they landed at that bright, steamy morning was far cleaner and neater than the squalid camps of the savage Guahibas they had met further downriver. Palm-leaf sheds dotted the sun-dappled clearing, hammocks strung between tall palm trunks, and small plots of cassava and indigo spread through gaps in the jungle, tended with obvious care. But the first sound that hit their ears as their keel scraped the shore was no peaceful welcome: four or five strapping warriors lined the landing, beating tall wooden drums and blaring on long earthen trumpets swollen with hollow chambers, the din a cacophony of howls and rumbles loud enough to make the teeth rattle in their heads.

“They’re calling on their devil, sir,” grunted Yeo, squinting at the painted figures.

Cary laughed beside him, clapping pious chaplain John Brimblecombe on the back. “Small wonder none but the foul fiend Sir Urian would deign to turn up to listen to such a racket. You can have that blessing job, Jack.”

Amyas, his mouth full of smoke from a fresh Cuban cigar, waved off their unease. “Nonsense. We could cut every last one of them down in half an hour, and they know it well enough.”

A great pageant was clearly afoot. The villagers had arrayed themselves in two long lines down the clearing, men at the front, women behind, all slathered in bright red arnotto and deep blue indigo, crowned with plumes of wild parrot feathers. Then with a shriek that split the air, a figure leaped into the open space: a man dressed head to toe in a jaguar pelt, complete with long bushy tail, grinning carved teeth, curved horns, a plume of black and yellow feathers, and a huge carved rattle in his fist, made up to look like the very devil himself.

“Here’s the Piache, their conjuring rascal,” Amyas muttered.

“Ay, tricked out in Satan’s own livery,” Yeo growled. “You may be sure his works match the costume.”

The Piache had clearly meant to strike the strangers dead with awe, but at the sight of the Englishmen snickering behind their hands, he stumbled in his performance for a moment. A practiced charlatan, he recovered fast, whacking one of the drummers over the head with his rattle to silence the racket, and launched into a long, gesticulating harangue, the words rapid and guttural.

The young interpreter boy translated for Amyas: “He asks if you have seen Amalivaca, the great civilizer of their people, on the far shore of the great ocean? The one who carved all the strange statues on the inland cliffs before sailing home again.”

Amyas had heard this myth a dozen times from different Amazon tribes. He answered, as he always did, singing the praises of their own great monarch, Queen Elizabeth of England.

The Piache nodded solemnly, and replied that she must surely be one of Amalivaca’s seven daughters — the ones he had taken back across the sea with him, after breaking the legs of the rest to stop them running away, leaving them to people the forests.

Amyas laughed and assured him his queen had no broken legs, was the most graceful dancer in all her realms, and far more importantly, asked if the tribe would give them cassava bread, and let them rest peacefully on the small offshore island they had fortified, before marching inland to fight the “clothed men”, the hated Spaniards, on the other side of the mountains.

The Piache responded with a wild capering dance, rolling head over heels across the dirt before waving them to follow. The Indians were all unarmed, grinning broadly, so the English party went with him, their hands resting light on the hilts of their swords. The conjuror crawled on all fours to the door of a tight-shut, thatched hut, and began whining at the dark interior like a begging dog.

Amyas turned to his interpreter: “Ask the old cacique what fool game this is.”

The old chief, who was trailing after them, answered that the Piache was consulting the Daughter of the Sun, the tribe’s sacred oracle.

“This is the mare’s nest we’ve all been chasing, lads,” Cary whispered, as the Piache’s whines rose to screams, then violent convulsions that left him foaming at the mouth, his eyes rolling back in his head, before he collapsed as if dead.

“Better than any play on the London stage,” Cary murmured.

“The devil’s played his part,” Brimblecombe said, frowning. “Now by all the old morality play rules, Vice ought to come out next.”

“A very fair Vice it will be, a right sweet Iniquity,” Cary said. “Listen.”

From the dark of the hut rose a low, rich, clear song — not the shrill guttural whine of an Indian voice, but the rounded, full tone of a European woman, soaring high and soft, weaving through notes so sweet even the rough old English seamen, who knew every glee and catch and psalm tune ever sung in Devon, were struck silent with wonder. The Indians bowed their heads, reverent, as the song rose and fell like a nightingale’s call, then burst into one final triumphant, ringing note that made every ear hum, before the clearing fell dead still.

The Piache jumped upright as if he had never been unconscious, and began jabbering again at Amyas. The interpreter translated that the Daughter of the Sun had accepted them as friends: her people were their people, her foes their foes. The crowd of Indians cheered loud enough to rouse the parrots in the treetops.

“Then tell her to bring us cassava,” Amyas said, rolling another tobacco leaf in plantain, and lighting his new cigar.

The hut door swung open. Every Indian prostrated themselves face down in the dirt. Stepping out into the sunlight was the same fair, pale-skinned woman they had glimpsed once on a smaller river island, now draped in a robe of iridescent macaw feathers, plumes of every bright hue woven into her dark hair. She walked slow and stately, as one used to command, glancing over her prostrate followers, then gestured gracefully at the gardens, the huts, the forest around them — all of it, her sign said, was theirs for the taking. She lifted Amyas’s calloused hand gently to her forehead, in a gesture of total submission.

The crowd roared with rapture, and as the mysterious girl retreated back to her hut, the Indians swarmed around the Englishmen, caressing their armor, gaping at their swords, pressing fruit and flowers and calabashes of fermented palm drink into their hands. Brimblecombe and the pious old Yeo were so scandalized by the wild, gyrating dances the young villagers started, that they convinced Amyas to retreat back to their fortified island before the men got too drunk. They paddled back at dusk, all of them turning over the same puzzle in their heads: who on earth was this strange white “Daughter of the Sun”?

Amyas was certain she was a last survivor of the lost Inca royal line, perhaps the descendant of that fair wife of the Inca Manco, whom the tyrant Pizarro had stripped, scourged, and shot to death with arrows forty years before, just to torment the fugitive king who had escaped his clutches.

That night, as they did nearly every night after leaving England, they gathered for evening prayer, singing psalms then old drinking catches until the moon hung high over the roar of the nearby cataract. Twice they thought they heard an echo of their song drifting across the river from the dark jungle, but they paid it no mind, until Cary slipped away, came back, and tugged Amyas aside by the arm.

“Our sweet Daughter of the Sun is mimicking us, lad,” he whispered.

They crept to the river bank, and over the roar of the falling water, heard that same clear, rich voice floating out of the black treeline, repeating every snatch of the old English airs they had just sung, perfectly in tune. They stood for an hour staring out over the moonlit eddies, but saw nothing but the impenetrable wall of the jungle, until the song faded away.

They returned to the village every day for the next week, but the girl rarely appeared, and when she did, kept her distance, haughty as a crowned queen. When Amyas’s Spanish was good enough to question the old cacique about her, the old man shook his head mysteriously, refused to speak her name aloud, and only said she was a Child of the Sun, and nothing more needed saying.

Cary joked that they ought to tell the Indians they were the Sun’s children by his first wife, come to check how they had treated their step-sister, and threaten to blight all their cassava if they didn’t confess the truth. Amyas chided him for lying, but the threat was enough. The old cacique bundled them into his canoe and paddled a full mile downriver, far from the village, afraid the oracle might overhear, and told them the whole story in a low, chanting voice.

Many years before, he said, his Omagua people had lived far in the mountains, until the Spaniards had hunted them out, and they had fled north. On the lower slopes of Cotopaxi’s smoking cone, they had found this little white girl wandering alone in the forest, no older than seven, her skin pale as the moon, too tender to have survived the jaguars and the snakes on her own. The tribe worshipped her as a divine gift, certain she was a surviving Inca royal, one of the race the Sun himself had fathered, who the old legends said would one day return to drive the Spaniards out of their lands. They raised her with every royal honor, she grew wiser than all their elders, braver in war, swifter in the chase, and her strange songs held all the secrets of the forest, the birds, the rivers, and the flowers, given to her by the Sun himself. She was named Ayacanora, and no Indian warrior dared to ask for her hand, for the Piache had declared it sacrilege for mortal men to touch a Child of the Sun.

Amyas pondered the tale long and late. The lost Inca remnants he had dreamed of finding, the road to the golden city of Manoa, could be just a little further south. He made plans to send Cary back downriver with half a dozen men to fetch the sick crewmen they had left stranded on the Orinoco, a dangerous journey through the lands of the brutal Guahibas. Ayacanora, hearing of his need for porters, stepped up before the whole tribe, sang a song that (per the Piache’s terrified translation) threatened the whole village with the wrath of a nameless evil spirit if they did not volunteer to go, and the men signed on instantly.

Conflict soon brewed, though. The pious Jack Brimblecombe began preaching to the villagers, drawing huge crowds with the English evening hymns, and the Piache saw his power slipping away. He plotted to kill Brimblecombe with a poisoned arrow, and thought to win Ayacanora to his side — only to receive a ringing box on the ear, and a lashing torrent of abuse. Ayacanora ran straight to Amyas, told him the whole plot, and begged him to throw the traitor conjuror to the alligators in the river, and install Brimblecombe in his place.

Jack forgave the Piache magnanimously, and preached all the harder — but the conjuror still had one last trick. He owned a sacred magic trumpet, hidden in a cave in the hills, that the tribe believed had to be blown every night at the proper season, or the palm trees would bear no fruit, the manioc would rot in the ground, and disaster would fall on every household. He announced one day that the village’s new fondness for the white strangers’ foreign God had angered the spirits, and he would take the magic trumpet and leave, abandoning the Omaguas to blight.

The whole village erupted in wails and fury. The villagers had loved Brimblecombe’s songs, but none of them would risk the crops failing over the new white man’s Gospel. The mob turned on the English, the men grabbing bows and blowguns dipped in deadly curare poison. There were less than a dozen of Amyas’s men at the camp that day, facing nearly a hundred enraged Indians. The air hung thick with the threat of blood.

“It is God’s own quarrel, sirs,” Brimblecombe said, steady as a rock. “Let Him defend the right.”

At that very moment, Ayacanora’s magic song rose up from her hut, soaring high above the shouts and the fury, weaving through the green treetops like a messenger straight from the Sun.
