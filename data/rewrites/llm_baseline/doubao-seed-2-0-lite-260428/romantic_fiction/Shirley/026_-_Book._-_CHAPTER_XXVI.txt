By the time the Fieldhead house party trundled back to Briarfield through soft gray autumn drizzle, Caroline Helstone had almost recovered her strength after the weeks of wasting sickness that had nearly stolen her away. Shirley Keeldar, who had pored over every line of the postmark convalescence updates from her friend, barely shed her travelling cloak in her own hall before she had her bonnet tied tight again, determined not to let a single hour slip past before she called at the rectory.

The rain fell gentle but steady, pattering over the last of the late roses and the russet-edged bramble hedges, when the garden wicket creaked open and Caroline spotted Shirley’s familiar bright figure bobbing toward the window. When she stepped inside, the weight of her recent fear for Caroline came out not in giddy chatter, but in that quiet, intense way only Shirley had when her heart was wrung. She did not ramble or exclaim. She simply pulled Caroline straight into her arms, held her there for one long, searching look, pressed a single soft kiss to her forehead, and breathed, “You are better.”

A minute later, her voice still thick with suppressed feeling, she added, “I see you are safe now. But take care. God grant your health never has to bear another shock like that.”

She launched into bright, vivacious stories of their travels after that, but her eyes kept darting back to Caroline unbidden, every glance heavy with unspoken solicitude, quiet unease, and faint, lingering awe at how close her friend had come to the edge of the grave. “She may be mending,” that look said, “but how thin and weak she still is! What peril she walked through to get back to us.”

Suddenly her gaze snapped over to Mrs. Pryor, sharp and unwavering, piercing right through the quiet governess’s usual soft reserve. “When will my governess come home to me?” she asked.

Caroline glanced at her mother, hesitated, then asked permission to tell Shirley the whole truth. A quiet nod from Mrs. Pryor was all the sign she needed, and soon Shirley was fully apprised of the secret that had hung over the rectory for months: that Mrs. Pryor was no stranger’s widow, but Caroline’s long lost biological mother.

“Very good,” Shirley said, cool as a summer breeze, “very good. But this is no news to me.”

“What? You knew?” Caroline gasped.

“I guessed the whole truth ages ago,” Shirley told her. “I never heard it from Mrs. Pryor, of course, but I’ve picked up bits of her history from other folks. That afternoon I spent talking to Miss Mann? She told me every last detail of James Helstone’s life and character. Mrs. Yorke trots him out as one of her blood-red warning tales, too—one of the men she points to to scare young girls off of hasty marriage. I’d have thought both of them were just dramatizing the dark side of life, too, if I hadn’t asked Mr. Yorke straight out. He said to me, ‘Shirley, my lass, if you want to know about that James Helstone, all I can say is he was a man-tiger. Handsome, dissolute, soft-spoken, treacherous, courteous, cruel—’” She broke off, stroking Caroline’s hair gently. “Don’t cry, Cary. We’ll speak of him no more.”

“I’m not crying,” Caroline mumbled, wiping her cheeks, “or if I am, it’s nothing. Go on. You’re no friend if you hold back the full truth from me. I hate that cowardly habit of twisting and cutting the truth to spare people’s feelings.”

“Luckily I’ve nearly nothing left to tell,” Shirley said. “Your own uncle confirmed every word Mr. Yorke said, and he’s a man who scorns lies, never peddles those shabby little conventional evasions that are worse than outright falsehoods.”

“But papa’s dead now,” Caroline whispered. “They should leave him in peace.”

“They will. We will,” Shirley assured her, pulling her close. “Cry all you want, Cary. It’s wrong to hold back natural tears. And besides—” her voice softened, glancing over at Mrs. Pryor, whose eyes were glistening as she watched her long lost daughter weep “—every tear you cry blots out a little of that old sin. They’ve got the cleansing power the rivers of Damascus lacked. They’re like the Jordan, washing clean a leprous memory.”

She turned then to address Mrs. Pryor directly, a faint, amused smile tugging at her lips. “Did you think I could spend every day seeing you two together, marking how eerily similar you are in a hundred small ways, noticing the way you cannot hide how much you love her, whether she is in the room or not, and never put the pieces together? I did, and every last one of my guesses was correct. I do believe I’m far more sharp-witted than I give myself credit for.”

“And you never said a word?” Caroline asked, once she had steadied her breathing again.

“Not one,” Shirley said. “I had no right to. It was none of my business, so I did not make it my business.”

“You guessed such a deep secret, and you never even hinted you knew?” Caroline pressed.

“Is that so strange?”

“It’s not like you,” Caroline said, frowning. “You’re never reserved. You’re always so frank and open.”

“I can be frank and open, and still know where to stop,” Shirley laughed. “When I show off my treasures, I can still hold back a gem or two—some curious, unpolished graven stone, an amulet whose mystic glitter I rarely let even myself catch a glimpse of. Now, good day for now. I’ll come fetch you tomorrow, for a drive.”

It was a new side of Shirley Caroline had never seen before: quiet, secretive, far more careful with hidden truths than she had ever guessed. And soon, that new side of her would reveal more, far more.

As soon as Caroline was strong enough to handle a change of scene and the gentle excitement of new company, Shirley begged her to come stay at Fieldhead for as long as she liked. Whether Shirley had grown bored of her honoured relatives from the south no one could say, but she clung to Caroline’s company with an eagerness that made it plain an extra warm, sympathetic presence in that stiff, proper household was a gift she was grateful for.

The Sympsons were devout church people, of course, so the rector’s niece was welcomed with perfect, cold courtesy. Mr. Sympson was a man of spotless respectability, a fussy, worrying temper, strict pious principles, and very little patience for anything that did not fit his narrow view of the world. His wife was a good, gentle woman, patient and well-bred, but raised on a diet of small prejudices, starved of all warmth and joy, her excellent good sense trapped under a thick, indigestible crust of bigotry. She was far too submissive to ever complain about that meagre, bitter diet, or ask for a single crumb more.

Their two daughters were held up as perfect examples of young womanhood: tall, each with a stately Roman nose, educated to absolute faultlessness, their minds stuffed full of history and solid improving books, their opinions so perfectly polished there was not a single flaw to find in them. No one could have found two more regulated lives, feelings, manners, or habits anywhere. They knew by heart every tiny petty rule of the young ladies’ schoolroom code, never deviated from its rules, and regarded any small departure from it in other people with quiet, shuddering horror. To them, the great unforgivable sin, the Abomination of Desolation, was what other people called originality. Any spark of it—whether in a person’s glance, their words, their actions, even the vivid lively prose of a novel—made them recoil, as if they had stumbled on something unspeakably dangerous. If they could not understand it, it had to be bad. It had to be denounced, locked away, stamped out.

Their only son, Henry, was fifteen years old: small, lame, pale, with big eyes that were usually soft and dim, but could flare bright and blazing when his quiet emotions were roused. A flush could rise to his pale cheeks, a new sharp decision to his crippled movements, when something moved him deeply. His mother doted on him, convinced his peculiarities were a sign he was specially elected by God, a little new Samuel born to be a clergyman. Mr. Sympson and his two daughters never quite understood him, so they mostly left him alone. But Shirley made him her special pet, and he worshipped her as his dearest playmate.

Hovering at the edge of the Sympson family circle, connected but always apart, was the tutor: Louis Moore, satellite of the household, ever present, ever distant. Every member of that proper family treated him with perfectly correct, distant dignity. Mr. Sympson was austerely polite, sometimes sharp when his temper frayed; his wife was kind but formal, attentive to his needs but never treating him as an equal; the two daughters treated him as a walking abstraction, not a real man. The most vivid, witty sketch he ever drew might as well have been blank paper to them. The most clever, original thing he said fell on completely unhearing ears. Their behaviour was immaculate, and completely cold.

But nothing Caroline saw surprised her more than this: Shirley, who was always so quick to champion the underdog, did not treat Louis Moore any better. She was not haughty to him, exactly—but she barely acknowledged he existed. He came and went, spoke or fell silent, and she hardly ever so much as nodded at him. She let him keep to his quiet, isolated life under her roof as if he were no more than a shadow at the edge of the room.

As for Louis himself, he moved through the days like a man long used to this cold, distant treatment, who had long since made up his mind to bear it for as long as he must. All his sharp, brilliant faculties seemed walled up inside him, murmuring no complaint against their captivity. He never laughed, rarely even smiled, never spoke a word of grievance. He fulfilled all his tutoring duties scrupulously. Little Henry adored him, and he asked nothing more of the rest of the world than basic civility. He seemed determined to accept nothing more, here at least; for when Caroline tried to make gentle overtures of friendship, to reach out to her cousin, he avoided her instead of encouraging her. Only two living things in the whole house he showed softness to: his little crippled pupil Henry, and the great rough, ill-tempered mastiff Tartar, who was sullen and unfriendly to every other soul under the roof, but had conceived a fierce, loyal partiality for the quiet tutor. So strong was that loyalty, that once when Louis walked into the dining room for supper unacknowledged, Tartar stood up straight from his usual spot at Shirley’s feet, trotted over, and settled down right at Louis’s chair. Shirley had reached out her white hand, calling him softly to come back, but Tartar only slavered and sighed, then ignored her invitation completely, curling up at Louis’s feet. Louis lifted the dog’s great scarred, chewed black muzzle onto his knee, patted him, and smiled one tiny, private little smile, all to himself. An hour later, when Tartar had wandered back to Shirley’s feet at her footstool, one quiet word and small gesture from Louis made the dog prick up his ears, leap up, and trot back to him again for another caress, that quiet secret smile rippling across his face once more.

One day, when Caroline and Shirley were sitting alone in the little ivy-covered summer house at the bottom of the garden, Caroline turned to her and asked, “Shirley, did you know my cousin Louis was the Sympsons’ son’s tutor long before they moved down to Fieldhead?”

Shirley did not answer right away, slower than her usual quick, snappy responses. At last she said, “Yes. Of course. I knew that perfectly well.”

“I thought you must have,” Caroline said. “It puzzles me, though. How did you never mention it to me? You talk so freely, about everything—you never hold back little details like that.”

“Because it never came up,” Shirley laughed a little, a strange, breathless laugh.

“You are the most singular being,” Caroline said, shaking her head. “I thought I knew you inside out, but I keep finding new sides I never guessed at. You were silent as the grave about Mrs. Pryor’s secret, and now here is another one. I just cannot make out why you made a secret of this.”

“I never made a secret of it,” Shirley protested. “I had no reason to. If you had asked me who Henry’s tutor was, I would have told you straight away. Besides, I thought you already knew.”

“There are so many things I don’t understand about it,” Caroline said, frowning. “You don’t like poor Louis, do you? Why? Are you angry at what you see as his servile, lowly position? Do you wish Robert Moore’s brother was more highly placed, respected as he deserves?”

“Robert’s brother, indeed!” Shirley burst out, her tone sharp with sudden, unmistakeable scorn. She snatched a wild pink rose off the vine peeking through the summer-house lattice, her fingers crushing the soft petals in her sudden proud impatience.

“Yes,” Caroline said, steady and gentle, refusing to be put off. “Robert’s brother. He is closely related to Gérard Moore of Hollow’s Mill, true—nature did not give him Robert’s handsome face or his bold, noble bearing, but his blood is just as good. He is every bit a gentleman, if he only had the chance to be free.”

“Wise, humble, pious Caroline!” Shirley exclaimed, her tone thick with irony. “Men and angels, listen to her! We must never despise plain features, or honest, hard work, must we? Look at the object of your panegyric now,” she said, pointing through a gap in the ivy creepers, where Louis Moore was walking slowly down the garden path.

“He is not ugly, Shirley,” Caroline pleaded. “He is not ignoble. He is sad, his mind sealed up by all the years of silence and slights. But I know he is brilliant. And you can be certain, if he did not have something very good in his character, Mr. Hall would never seek out his company the way he does.”

Shirley laughed, once, then again, each laugh sharp with faint sarcasm. “Very well, very well,” she said. “If you say he is Cyril Hall’s friend and Robert Moore’s brother, we will tolerate his existence, won’t we, Cary? You think he’s intelligent, do you? Not quite a complete idiot? Some redeeming qualities in his character—meaning he’s not an utter ruffian? Good. Your good opinion counts for a lot with me. To prove it, if he comes this way, I will speak to him.”

Louis reached the summer house a moment later. Unaware anyone was inside, he sat down on the low stone step, Tartar settling across his feet as usual.

“Old boy,” he said, tugging gently at the dog’s torn, tattered ear, chewed to rags in a hundred old fights, “the autumn sun shines just as soft on us as it does on the richest, fairest people in the county. This garden is not ours, but we get to enjoy the green grass and the sweet flowers, don’t we?”

He fell silent, stroking the dog, while little sparrows twittered in the trees around them, hopping out onto the grass at a shy distance, as if waiting for something. “The little brown elves remember I fed them biscuit crumbs the other day,” Louis said to himself, quiet. “They want more. But I forgot to save a crumb for them today. Eager little things, I’ve got nothing to give you.”

He reached into his pocket, pulled it out empty.

“Your providence just showed up,” Shirley whispered to Caroline, quiet. She pulled a crumb of sweet cake from her reticule—she always kept the little bag stuffed full of treats for sparrows, stray kittens, hungry lambs—and crumbled it up, leaning over Louis’s shoulder to drop the crumbs right into his open palm.

“There,” she said. “There’s a little provision for the improvident.”

“This September afternoon is very pleasant,” Louis said, not even flustered or surprised, as he scattered the crumbs over the grass for the waiting birds.

“Even for you?” Shirley asked.

“Just as pleasant for me as it is for any king in the world,” he said.

“You take some harsh, solitary triumph in pulling joy out of the sun, the sky, the animals, the things no one else can take from you,” Shirley said.

“Solitary, but not harsh,” he corrected her. “With animals, I feel like the heir of Adam, the man who was given dominion over every living thing that moves on the earth. Your dog follows me. When I walk through the farmyard, the pigeons from your dovecot flutter at my feet. Your mare in the stable knows me better than she knows you, and obeys me more quickly.”

“And my roses smell sweet to you, and my trees give you shade,” Shirley said.

“And none of those things can be taken away by anyone’s silly whims,” Louis said. “They are mine.”

He walked off, Tartar trotting loyally at his heels, and Shirley stayed standing on the summer house step. Caroline saw her face then, pale as snow, as if her proud heart was bleeding somewhere deep inside.

“You see,” Caroline said, trying to explain, “people hurt his feelings so often it makes him morose and withdrawn.”

“You see,” Shirley snapped back, sharp with unmistakeable irritation, “he is a subject that you and I will quarrel over if we keep discussing it. So let us drop the subject. For ever.”

Caroline nodded, but her head was full of questions. She thought to herself: He must have snubbed her a dozen times over, that is why she is so distant with him. But Shirley is never usually so unforgiving, never so quick to be irritable and unfair.

Her good opinion of Louis only grew stronger, when two of her friends spoke up to praise him.
