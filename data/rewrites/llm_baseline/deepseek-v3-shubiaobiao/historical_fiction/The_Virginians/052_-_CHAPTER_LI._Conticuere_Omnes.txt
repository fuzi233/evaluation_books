The reader is kindly invited to cross the way with us to Lord Wrotham’s house, presently lent to General Lambert, where our young gentlemen find themselves gathered among familiar faces—those very friends encountered at Oakhurst and Tunbridge Wells. James Wolfe, though expected for dinner, is conspicuously absent, no doubt lingering in the company of Miss Lowther, for whom a single glance holds more allure than the finest dishes Lord Wrotham’s kitchens might prepare. You, dear reader, shall take his place as the sixth at table—though we may be certain the enamored lieutenant-colonel will not appear. As for myself, I shall stand aside, a silent observer, ready to recount the evening’s discourse.

First, mark the radiant contentment upon the ladies’ faces! When Harry Warrington fell into the clutches of bailiffs, I had meant to describe how the tender-hearted Mrs. Lambert, upon hearing of his plight, rushed to her husband, imploring, insisting—nay, commanding—that he aid the boy.

“Never mind his rebellious words the other day,” she pleaded. “Never mind his pride over those returned gifts—what young man of spirit would not have taken offense? And though we’ve little to spare, with our own boys at college, surely there must be some way to free him! Did you not once rescue Charles Watkins from debt? And did he not repay every penny? Think of the good you did then—how that family still blesses your name! I dare say it was such kindness that brought you favor—why, I’ve no doubt it helped make you a major-general!”

Now, Martin Lambert, ever a man swayed by compassion, needed little persuasion. Resolving to seek out his friend James Wolfe and stand bail for Harry, he took his leave, pressing Theo’s hand (who, perhaps through maternal indiscretion, seemed to divine his purpose) and kissing Hetty’s flushed cheek before departing. His wife, unable to contain herself, followed him into the hall.

Once alone, her restraint dissolved. She flung her arms about his neck, showering him with kisses, calling down blessings upon him, weeping freely upon his shoulder—a scene abruptly interrupted by the housekeeper, Mrs. Quiggett, whose astonished face appeared round the door.

“A mere marital squabble, now mended!” Lambert declared with a laugh, striding away.

“Well, I never!” cackled the old woman, her laughter like the screech of some ancient parrot.

“Oh, Quiggett!” sighed Mrs. Lambert, dabbing her eyes. “What a man he is!”

“A pair of angels, if you ask me,” Quiggett retorted. “Now, what’s for supper?”

When Lambert returned late that night, bearing news of Harry’s release—and the miraculous resurrection of his brother—the household erupted in joy. If Mrs. Lambert had adored her husband before, she now worshipped him outright. Hetty nestled silently against her father, sipping from his glass; Theo and her mother glowed like twin moons. After supper, the four knelt together in gratitude, their hearts lifted in the purest exultation—until a sudden knocking at the door startled them.

Past midnight, long after all expected visitors, who could it be?

“George,” came a voice, as the door opened to reveal Harry, leading his brother forward. “We’ve been to Aunt Bernstein’s, but we couldn’t sleep without thanking you all.”

No more words were needed. Mrs. Lambert embraced Harry; Theo, pale with emotion, clutched Hetty; George bowed deeply and kissed Mrs. Lambert’s hand; the General wiped his eyes. It was a moment of perfect grace—when wrongs were forgiven, peace restored, and love, once thought lost, returned.

“We saw your lights,” Harry explained. “We leave for Aunt Bernstein’s tomorrow, but the day after—?”

“Dinner!” declared the General. “And you shall tell us everything.”

And so here we are, gathered not in the grand dining hall but in the snug parlor, the cloth drawn, the servants dismissed, the guests leaning forward as Mr. George Esmond Warrington prepares his tale.

“At General Braddock’s table in Virginia,” George began, “I once heard a shrewd Philadelphia agent—none other than Mr. Franklin—warn against the perils of our march. ‘Your Excellency,’ said he, ‘may easily take the fort once you reach it. But the wilderness itself is your foe. Your line will stretch for miles through untamed forest, exposed to ambush at every turn.’

“‘Pshaw!’ replied Braddock. ‘Savages may terrify your militia, but they are no match for His Majesty’s troops.’

“How fatally mistaken he was.

“On that ill-fated July day, our orderly advance dissolved into chaos. The French and their Indian allies struck from the trees, their shots picking us off unseen. Our men faltered, then broke, officers falling faster than the ranks. I was sent forward with a message—only to fall wounded, my leg shattered by a musket-ball.

“As I lay helpless, a French-Indian hybrid, his musket raised, rushed to finish me. My fusil—Harry’s gift—saved me. But before I could reload, another figure loomed: an Indian, scalping-knife in teeth. I was too weak to resist.

“Then—a miracle. A French officer dashed forward. ‘*À moi, Florac!*’ I cried.

“It was my old dueling-second from Quebec! With a curse, he ran the Indian through. ‘*Eh, la belle affaire!*’ he muttered, binding my leg. ‘Play dead, or the rest will scalp you.’ He pressed a dead man’s coat and cockade upon me, then vanished into the fray.

“Florac’s ruse spared me. When the massacre ended, he returned with Indians—scalp-trophies at their belts—and bade them carry his ‘brother’ to the fort. Had he abandoned me, they’d have stripped and killed me without a thought.

“For weeks I languished in fever at Duquesne, nursed by a half-breed woman. My possessions—watch, money—were stolen by the fort’s commandant, a rogue named Museau. When I begged to send word to Virginia, he dictated a ransom letter, threatening to cast me to the Indians if I refused.

“Months crawled by. Spring came, then summer. No reply arrived—the messenger had been shot. Museau, grown brutal, kept me close, though his sergeant’s Indian mistress showed me kindness.

“I feigned lameness long after my leg healed, biding my time. And then—” George paused, glancing at his audience. “But that is a tale for another evening.”

The room sat in stunned silence. Theo clasped Hetty’s hand; Harry’s fists clenched.

“By Heaven,” the Colonel muttered, “such devils must be hunted down.”

George nodded gravely. “In Pennsylvania, bounties are offered—a hundred and thirty dollars per scalp.”

A shiver passed through them all. The candles flickered. Outside, the night lay still—but within, the echoes of war lingered.
