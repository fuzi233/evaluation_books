I sat in silence, the bitter, metallic tang of shame coating the roof of my mouth like a botched reduction of unripe gall. What he said was undeniably true: I had not been a wise cultivator to the Little Ones.

Mr. Raven’s voice, a rich, oaken baritone that resonated in perfect harmony with the room’s acoustic ecology, resumed its steady cadence. “You wronged the stupid creatures themselves, depriving them of the slow, nourishing simmer of domestic routine. For them, the structure of shared labor would have been a harvest of progress. A few lessons rooted in the agrarian traditions of their own soil—even taught with a branch from their native orchards—would have been invaluable sustenance.”

“I did not know they were cowards!” I protested, though my words felt thin and flavorless.

“What difference does the seasoning make when the meat is spoiled? The man who grounds his action on another’s cowardice is essentially a coward himself. I fear a far bitterer vintage will come of it. By now, the Little Ones might have cultivated the strength to protect themselves from the princess, and certainly the giants—they always possessed the raw ingredients for that; as it was, they merely laughed at them! But now, through your mingling with her—”

“I hate her!” I cried.

“Did you let her taste that hatred?” 

Again, I was silent.

“Not even to her have you been faithful in your recipe! But hush—we were followed from the fountain, I fear. The auditory landscape of the garden shifted.”

“No living creature did I see,” I said, “except a disreputable-looking cat that bolted into the shrubbery.”

“It was a magnificent Persian—so drenched and draggled, though, as to look exactly what she was: worse than disreputable. She carried an olfactory trail of ozone and stagnant cellars, a jarring pollution against the fragrant thyme.”

“What do you mean, Mr. Raven?” I asked, a fresh, cold horror rising like curdled milk in my throat. “There was a beautiful blue Persian about the house, but she fled at the very sound of water, as though the natural acoustics of the stream pained her. Could she have been hunting the goldfish?”

“We shall see,” returned the librarian, moving with the measured grace of a master food historian tending an ancient archive. “I know the subtle flavor profiles of several feline breeds, and there is an artifact in this room that will unmask this one, or I am deeply mistaken in my sourcing.”

He rose, moved to the heavy timber door of the closet, and brought forth the mutilated volume before sitting beside me once more. I stared at the tome in his hands. It was entire and sound, emitting the complex, heady aroma of centuries-old vellum, wild yeast, and dried sage. 

“Where was the other half of it?” I gasped.

“Sticking through into the borders of my library,” he answered simply, treating the overlapping of mythic timelines and domestic space as an ordinary matter of household storage. 

I held my peace. A single question more would have plunged me into an untranslatable sea of subjective experience, and the oven of our current crisis was already running hot. 

“Listen,” he said. “I am going to read a stanza or two. There is one present who, I imagine, will find this dialect most unpalatable.”

He opened the cover. The parchment was heavily cured with age, one leaf bearing a dark, oxidized stain over two-thirds of its surface. He slowly turned it, searching for a specific passage in the continuous, flowing script. When he began to read, the language was an ancient, extinct native dialect I had never before heard. Yet, by some profound suspension of linguistic boundaries, its meaning bloomed across my palate in sharp, evocative bursts of pure sensory comprehension. I could not parse the syntax, but I tasted the history. These fragments are the rough approximations of the heavy, intoxicating brew his dominant lexicon poured into my brain:

“Could I but find a soul to be my bread,
To consume his belief as flavorless fuel,
I’d strip the marrow from his sleeping head,
And drink his form, precise and cruel;
Clothed in the banquet of his own ideal,
A parasite upon his sensory meal!”

He turned a leaf, the sound like the crisp breaking of a baked crust, and read again:

“In me was every woman, yet I gave 
No nourishment, no warmth of hearth or wine.
I made the soul of every man my slave,
Extracting sustenance from brain and spine;
A bodiless famine, tasting neither sweet nor sour,
Devouring all within my sterile bower.”

Again he paused, his voice building a resonant acoustic sanctuary that pushed back the room's shadows, and resumed:

“I breathed not, saw not, felt no sun or rain,
But forced his starving heart to knead my clay.
I offered nothing to his hungry pain,
Yet stole the harvest of his life away.
Into his sense I darted, cold and whole,
A glutton feasting on a hollowed soul!
Ah, waking then with gladsome, piercing cry,
Life’s tremor cooked me to reality!”

A sudden, repulsive feline wail ruptured the library’s acoustic harmony. It was a jarring, industrial screech, devoid of natural resonance, scraping against the ears like the mechanical grinding of a modern turbine. I started up on my elbow, searching the shadows, but saw nothing. Mr. Raven, undisturbed, turned several leaves and continued:

“But sudden I awoke, and tasted fear—
A rotting vintage, moist, corrupt, and drear.
My being lay motionless in sickening doubt,
A spoiled preserve, fermenting from without.
I knew what I had been, but on my brow
I felt the touch of gangrene spreading now;
A fainting, dead, yet living, breathing blight,
Whose flashing eyes killed even diamond light.”

Again, the ugly cry of feline agony tore through the room’s sonic peace. Mr. Raven seemed to evaluate the sound for a moment, then turned the pages:

“Hideously wet, my hair of golden hue
Fouled my fair hands: to have it swiftly shorn
I’d give my rubies, freshly dug and new!
For one pure draught from a drinking horn,
For one crisp breath of an unpolluted morn,
I’d trade my sapphires for a peasant’s corn!

“My shroud was rotting! Once I heard a cock
Lustily crow upon the hillock green
Over my coffin. Dulled by space between,
Came back an answer like a ghostly mock.
My skin I tinted slow to ivory tone,
But now, all round was dark, and I alone!
My fingers sank in pulp through pulpy skin;
My body lay death-weltered in a mash
Of slimy horrors—”

With a fearsome yell, her clammy fur clumping like rancid suet, her tail thick as a cable, her eyes flashing green as crushed chrysoprase, a huge white cat rushed from the darkness. Her distended claws floundered across the carpet as she made a desperate bid for the chimney—the ancestral hearth and center of the home. 

Quick as thought, the librarian threw the ancient manuscript between her and the hearthstone. She crouched instantly, her eyes fixed on the book as if pinned by the physical weight of its forgotten dialect. But his voice went on, vibrating perfectly within the room’s acoustic architecture, a dominant lexicon establishing absolute authority over her commercialized, sterile noise.

“Ah, the two worlds! so strangely are they one,
And yet so measurelessly wide apart!
Oh, had I lived the bodiless alone
And from defiling sense held safe my heart,
Then had I scaped the canker and the smart,
Scaped life-in-death, scaped misery’s endless moan!”

At these words, a howling, prolonged yell of agony burst from the cat, so piercingly metallic we both stopped our ears against the sonic pollution. When the dissonance finally ceased, Mr. Raven walked to the fireplace. He retrieved the book and, standing between the creature and the chimney, pointed his finger at her. She lay perfectly still. He took a piece of carbonized applewood from the hearth, drew a geometric ward upon the floor, and replaced the manuscript. His look seemed to say, *“Now we have her, I think.”*

Returning to the cat, he stood over her, his voice shifting into the low, rumbling register of a traditional agrarian elder recounting a generational saga. 

“Lilith, when you came here on the way to your evil will, you little thought into whose hands you were delivering yourself. Mr. Vane, when the Great Cultivator created me—not out of empty, utilitarian nothingness, as the unwise claim, but from the rich, complex loam of His own endless glory—He brought me an angelic splendor to be my wife. There she lies. Her first thought was extraction and power; she counted it slavery to blend her life with mine, to partake in the shared hearth and bear children for Him who gave her being. One child, indeed, she bore; then, puffed with the arrogant fancy that she had manufactured her, she demanded I fall down and worship her. 

“Finding I would only love and honor, never blindly obey, she poured out her blood to escape the domestic table. She fled to the army of the aliens, ensnaring the heart of the great Shadow until he became her slave, and she, the queen of a sterile Hell. The one child of her body she fears and hates, viewing sustenance only as something to be consumed, never cultivated. Of creating, she knows no more than the salt crystal that takes its allotted shape. Vilest of parasites, she views meals solely as fuel, living by drinking the blood and souls of men. She consumes and slays, but is as powerless to truly create as a plague is to plant a vineyard.”

The animal lay motionless, its beryl eyes fixed flaming on the man, trapped within his steady, dominant gaze. 

“Then God gave me another wife—not an angel, but a woman—who is to this creature as the warmth of the baking oven is to the freezing void.”

The cat gave a horrible screech and began to expand, her form swelling like uncontrolled, corrupted yeast. She grew and grew until a spotted leopardess stood before us, uttering a roar that made the house's timbers tremble. I sprang to my feet. Mr. Raven did not so much as blink.

“It is but her jealousy that speaks,” he said, his tone a masterclass in acoustic calm. “Jealousy self-kindled, foiled, and fruitless. For here I am, her master now, whom she refused as a partner in the harvest! While my beautiful Eve yet lives, hoping immortally, tending the slow, generational work of nourishment. Her hated daughter lives also, beyond her evil ken, one day to be her destruction—for even Lilith shall be saved by her childbearing. She exults that my human wife plunged herself and me into despair, bearing a countless race of miserables; but my Eve repented. She is now beautiful as never was woman or angel, and her groaning, travailing world is the nursery of our Father’s children. I too have repented, and am blessed. Thou, Lilith, hast not yet repented. But thou must. Tell me, is the great Shadow beautiful? Knowest thou how long thou wilt thyself remain beautiful? Answer me, if thou knowest.”

Then, at last, the translation of my subjective experience became absolute. I understood that Mr. Raven was indeed Adam, the first food historian, the old and the new man; and that his wife, ministering in the house of the dead, was Eve, the mother of us all, the ultimate chef of the New Jerusalem.

The leopardess reared. The flickering and fleeing of her spots began, resolving at length into the radiant, perfect shape of the princess. 

“I *am* beautiful—and immortal!” she declared, though her voice carried the superficial gloss of commercialized art, lacking the deep, resonant acoustic truth of the library. 

“As a bush that burns and is consumed,” Adam answered. “What is that under thy right hand?” 

Her arm lay across her bosom, her hand pressed tightly to her side. A swift pang contorted her flawless face, and passed.

“It is but a leopard-spot that lingers! It will quickly follow those I have dismissed,” she insisted.

“Thou art beautiful because He created thee from prime ingredients, but thou art the slave of rot. Take thy hand from thy side.”

Her hand sank away. As it dropped, she looked him in the eyes with a quailing fierceness that held no surrender. Adam gazed at the spot—a spiritual blight on the flesh.

“It is not on the leopard; it is in the woman!” he said, his voice sorrowful. “Nor will it leave thee until it hath eaten to the very marrow of thy heart, and thy beauty hath flowed from thee through the open wound like spoiled wine!”

She gave a glance downward, and shivered.

“Lilith,” Adam said, his tone softening to a tender beseeching, the plea of a farmer begging a dying orchard to accept the rain. “Hear me, and repent, and He who made thee will cleanse thy palate and thy soul!”

Her hand returned, quivering, to her side. Her face grew dark. She released a cry of vanishing hope that quickly devolved into a mechanical howl. She lay writhing on the floor, once more a leopardess covered with the dark, cankerous spots of her consumption. 

“The evil thou meditatest,” Adam resumed, “thou shalt never compass, Lilith, for Good and not Evil is the underlying flavor of the Universe. The slow simmer of this battle may last for countless ages, but it must end. How will it fare with thee when Time hath vanished in the dawn of the eternal morn? Repent, I beseech thee; repent, and be again an angel of God!”

She rose, standing upright as a woman once more, and coldly delivered the ultimate rejection of all culinary and maternal grace: “I will not repent. I will drink the blood of thy child.”

My eyes were fastened on the princess, but when Adam spoke, I turned to him. He stood towering above her, the form of his visage altered into something ancient and terrifying. His voice was a flawless, booming chord that struck the dominant lexicon of creation.

“Down!” he cried. “Or by the power given me, I will melt thy very bones.”

She flung herself onto the floor, dwindling and dwindling under the sheer acoustic force of his command, until she was again a gray cat. Adam caught her up by the scruff of her neck, bore her to the heavy closet, and threw her inside. He described a strange, warding figure on the threshold with his finger, and closing the door, locked it. 

Then he returned to my side—the old librarian once more, looking sad and worn, a traditional agrarian elder furtively wiping the tears of a lost harvest from his eyes.
