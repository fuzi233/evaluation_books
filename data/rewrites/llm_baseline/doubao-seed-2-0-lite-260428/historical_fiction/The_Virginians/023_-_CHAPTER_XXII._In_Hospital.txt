Lulled by soft, lingering, familiar strains of hymnody drifting through his sickroom, young Harry Warrington sank into a honeyed, unbroken slumber free of fevered delirium, and woke only when golden summer sunlight streamed across his bed curtains at dawn. His kind hosts, Colonel and Mrs. Lambert, were already standing beside his bed, smiling down at him. He was ravenous, and the Colonel—who had tended his wound without fee, and proved a far more skilled surgeon than any of the apothecaries peddling their trade in the nearby market town—gave him immediate leave to tuck into a bowl of tender chicken and rice. Mrs. Lambert told him the meal had been prepared entirely by the hands of one of her two daughters.

One of her daughters? A faint, warm, fuzzy memory stirred in Harry’s groggy mind: two red-cheeked girls with dark wavy locks hovering at his bedside the moment he first regained consciousness, slipping away before he could mumble a word of thanks. Then the mortifying recollection hit him, unbidden: the woman he had foolishly doted on only weeks prior, the one whose “pearls” were nothing but paste and whose gilded “golden locks” were mere pinchbeck, and he writhed so sharply Mrs. Lambert leaned over in alarm. He lied smoothly, saying it was only a small twinge in his bandaged shoulder, and stammered out his gratitude for their kindness.

“We are no strangers at all, Mr. Warrington,” Mrs. Lambert laughed. “My husband knew your father long years ago, and I and your own dear mamma were schoolgirls together at Kensington. Your aunt the Baroness Bernstein and your cousin had no need to beg us to take you in when your accident happened. They rode on to Tunbridge Wells last night, and sent a courier all the way from Reigate the day before just to bring us news of how you fared.”

Harry glanced down at his bandaged arm, and the Colonel chuckled, swearing he had worked a fine cure on the gash already, and that now it was the women of the house’s turn to nurse him back to full strength. Mrs. Lambert teased that it was their eldest daughter Theo who’d made the broth, stitched his coat sleeve to fit comfortably around his wounded shoulder, and cut the whole vase of fresh roses sitting on his toilet table that Harry exclaimed over a moment later. The Colonel, amused his wife kept singing Theo’s praises unprompted, nudged her under the side of the bed to prompt a little more discretion, but Harry had already put the pieces together when he asked if the beautiful evening hymn he had half-dreamt he heard the night before had been sung by the girl. The Colonel laughed and teased that their kitchen maids swore Harry’s black valet Gumbo had been singing that same hymn so loud the whole house shook, as if he was a full church organ all by himself. Harry’s voice softened and dropped as he said that all his family at home in Virginia sang that same hymn, that his grandfather had been a close friend of Bishop Ken who had written it, and that his late twin brother had loved it more than any other tune. Mrs. Lambert’s heart ached so much she nearly leaned down to kiss the poor boy’s cheek.

For six weeks straight, Harry had been trapped in the giddy, selfish, corrupt social whirl of London and the Castlewood household, and now, breathing the pure, kind, unpretentious air of the Lamberts’ quiet home, his hard, guarded young heart softened and opened to all this gentle goodwill. He ate his fill, his mood light and brimming with gratitude, and soon the Colonel settled in a chair beside his bed to chat. Harry was usually taciturn, only ever opening up when he spoke of horses, hounds, and the practical work of running a Virginia plantation, but the honest plain-spoken old veteran, who wore a simple fustian coat and a waistcoat with not a scrap of lace on it, put him completely at ease. Harry soon learned that the Colonel’s own father had fought alongside his grandfather, Colonel Esmond, in Queen Anne’s famous wars, that the whole household were longstanding trusted friends of his family.

The Colonel explained that both his sons were away from home: one was studying to enter the church, and the other was in Italy, working as a travel companion and tutor to Lord Wrotham’s son. That meant the only company Harry would have to amuse him while he recovered was Mrs. Lambert and their two daughters, Theo and Hester. He laughed and said he knew his wife overpraised Theo, but the girl was a bright, cheerful, hardworking sixteen, the best little housekeeper he could ever ask for. Then he apologized for unthinkingly bringing up Harry’s dead brother, and said Gumbo had been chattering nonstop to the household staff, boasting that half of Virginia belonged to his master. Harry laughed out loud, and said Gumbo was a terrible liar who thought making his masters sound ten times richer than they were was a point of honour: his family was well-off by colonial standards, but no grand lords, and the man was such a coward he would have run a mile at the first sight of an Indian on the Braddock expedition, not rescued Harry from a whole war party as he had claimed.

The two men soon fell to discussing battles and loyalties, and Harry’s fierce Tory sympathies spilled out unprompted: he swore that if Bonnie Prince Charlie had not turned back at Derby, the king on the throne now would be James the Third, the rightful Stuart heir. The Colonel laughed and advised him, as an old soldier, to keep those opinions quiet, especially around strangers. Even his own wealthy relative Lord Castlewood, and his aunt the Baroness Bernstein, had abandoned their old Stuart loyalties to side with the reigning Hanoverian court, he warned, and servants’ gossip spread far faster than any gentleman realized—Gumbo had already blabbed the whole embarrassing story about Harry buying that broken-kneed horse from his cousin. Harry turned bright red, and swore he would give Gumbo a sound thrashing if he ever dared gossip about his personal affairs ever again.

When Harry was fully dressed, his wounded shoulder supported by a silk ribboned sling, he made his way down the broad oak staircase with the Colonel. The walls of the entrance hall were lined with ancient arms: pikes from Cromwell’s wars, a tattered French guidon taken at the Battle of Malplaquet, a pair of Highland broadswords abandoned on the field of Culloden, and portraits of stern old Parliamentary generals, one of whom was a distant kinsman of the Lamberts. The Colonel joked that his family had been radical Whigs back in those days, riding out to greet William of Orange at Exeter, but that they were all good Church of England folk now, and every last woman in the house was a staunch Tory at heart, as every good woman worth her salt was.

He flung open the parlour door to reveal the two girls waiting by the window. “Here is Miss Hester,” he said, “and this is Miss Theo, the soup-maker, the seamstress, the harpsichord-player, the songstress that lulled you to sleep last night, and the girl who picked those roses you admired so much in your bedroom. Curtsy to our guest, lassies!” Both girls dropped into deep, stately old-fashioned curtseys, the sort the locals called “cheeses”, and Theo blushed so bright the roses in her cheeks outshone the cut flowers in Harry’s vase. She was no simpering fashionable beauty: she had freckled ruddy cheeks, kind soft eyes, dark brown curling hair falling over a round white neck, sturdy red arms bare from the elbow as was the custom for young ladies of the time, no absurd tiny wasp waist, and feet big enough to be seen without a spyglass. But Harry’s heart softened immediately to her sweet, unfeigned shyness.

The Colonel teased his family about their favourite books, naming Rabelais, Fielding, Swift, and Tom Brown, and when Harry admitted he had never heard of any of them, that his book learning had been neglected back on the Virginia plantation where he’d spent all his time with horses and hounds, the Colonel quickly reassured him that honesty and a good heart were far better than any fancy book learning. When Harry said he was dying to go off to fight in the European wars, to see the Prussian king and ride out on campaign, Mrs. Lambert’s face fell soft and sad, and Theo darted a quick, curious, wistful glance at her mother.

It is a foolish fancy of mine, I think, that no matter how quiet and sheltered a young girl is, tucked away in a small country town like Oakhurst, there is a little fairy tale instinct in her heart, waiting for the knight on the white charger to ride up to the castle gate. More often than not, the rider knocking at the door turns out to be nothing more exciting than a postman with a double letter from Northamptonshire, or a costermonger selling panniers of cabbages. First crushes are as common and unremembered as the day you were christened, and no man is ever the very first person his sweetheart ever fancied, no matter how much she swears she is. But there is no mistaking that soft, hopeful little flutter in the air, the very second that young Harry Warrington stepped across the threshold of that quiet parlour on that bright summer morning.
