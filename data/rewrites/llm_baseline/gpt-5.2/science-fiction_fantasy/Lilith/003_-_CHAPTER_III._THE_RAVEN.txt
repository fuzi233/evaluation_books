I turned and looked behind me. Everything wavered in a half-light that would not settle into named things: not fog and not field, not cloud and not hillside—only a vast uncertainty that refused the comfort of edges. One fact alone held: I saw nothing I knew.

As if sight had betrayed me and touch might be the honest sense, I stretched out both hands and walked a little way this way and that, fingers combing the air like a cook testing steam for heat. My palms found nothing—no wall, no trunk, no latch, no familiar grain of wood. Only the ground underfoot, and the faint stir of low growth brushing my boots with the dry whisper of leaves.

Instinct, desperate for any living witness, pulled me toward the raven.

He stood a little off, glossy as spilled ink, watching me with an expression that was at once respectful and mocking—like an old servant who has seen too many masters panic at the first crack in their certainties. The very notion of asking counsel from a raven struck me as absurd; I turned away again, the bewilderment thickening, fear threaded through it like a bitter herb.

Had I walked into a region where the relations of mind and matter no longer kept their bargain? Could a man step, by chance, beyond order—beyond the grammar that binds cause to effect—and become a toy for the lawless?

Yet there was the raven. There was the firm press of earth beneath my soles. And there was sound: a wind-sound in the small plants around me, not loud, but present—an acoustic proof that I was not entirely unmade. It was the kind of sound that, in my own world, would have meant hedges and garden-beds and the safe hush around a house at dusk: a soundscape that steadies the mind because it belongs to living things, not to engines.

“How did I get here?” I said—apparently aloud, for my question was answered at once.

“You came through the door,” replied an odd voice, rather harsh, like a throat made for croaking forced into human consonants.

I looked behind me, then all about, but saw no human shape. The thought that madness had come—quiet-footed, without announcement—caught me hard. Must I now distrust every sense, and even the witness of my own awareness?

In the same instant I knew it was the raven that had spoken, for he stood looking up at me as if waiting to see whether I would accept the plainness of his reply. The sun was not shining, yet the bird seemed to cast a shadow, and the shadow seemed not something he made but something he wore—part of his body, like a second pelt.

I beg you—who read—to lend me your patience, and more, your imagination, for I must try to speak of a place where words do not sit properly on things. I was in a world—call it a state, an economy of conditions—so unlike our customary one that every phrase I choose is only a sketch, a smudged charcoal outline of a form that refuses to be held. I begin to suspect the impossibility of what I attempt: to tell what I cannot truly tell, because no speech I command is shaped to these impressions. When I try to refine what I write, the reality slips away like a scent you almost place—smoke, resin, rain on stone—until it changes and changes, and at last is no longer recognizable even to memory.

Still, a bird who can speak to a man deserves a civil answer. Perhaps, being a bird, he deserved more than a man. His voice carried the roughness of a croak pressed through language, but it was not disagreeable; and though what he said lit no lantern for me, it did not sound rude.

“I did not come through any door,” I said.

“I saw you come through it—saw you with my own ancient eyes!” the raven returned, positive without insolence.

“I never saw any door!”

“Of course not!” said he. “All the doors you have seen—and you haven’t seen many—were doors in. Here you came upon a door out. The strange thing to you will be that the more doors you go out of, the farther you get in.”

“Oblige me,” I said, clinging to manners as to a rail over a drop, “by telling me where I am.”

“That is impossible. You know nothing about whereness. The only way to come to know where you are is to begin to make yourself at home.”

“How am I to begin,” I demanded, “when everything is so strange?”

“By doing something.”

“What?”

“Anything; and the sooner you begin the better. Until you are at home, you will find it as difficult to get out as it is to get in.”

“I have, unfortunately, found it too easy to get in. Once out I shall not try again.”

“You have stumbled in,” he replied, “and may, possibly, stumble out again. Whether you have got in unfortunately remains to be seen.”

“Do you never go out, sir?” I asked, for his tone had the air of someone who visited my world from choice.

“When I please I do, but not often, or for long. Your world is such a half-baked sort of place—at once so childish and so self-satisfied. In fact, it is not sufficiently developed for an old raven—at your service!”

“Am I wrong, then,” I said, “in presuming that a man is superior to a bird?”

“That is as it may be. We do not waste our intellects in generalising, but take man or bird as we find him. I think it is now my turn to ask you a question!”

“You have the best of rights,” I returned, “in the fact that you can do so.”

“Well answered!” he said. “Tell me, then, who you are—if you happen to know.”

“How should I help knowing? I am myself, and must know!”

“If you know you are yourself,” said he, “you know you are not somebody else. But do you know that you are yourself? Are you sure you are not your own father?—or, excuse me, your own fool?—Who are you, pray?”

At that, a blankness opened in me. I perceived, with sudden shame, that I could not give him even the outline of an answer. Indeed—who was I? It would not help to say I was who. I understood that I did not know myself: did not know what I was; had no ground firm enough to set my heel on and say, This is me and not another. My name from my own world—what I was called, the sound by which others summoned me—was gone from me as if it had been a word in a dying dialect, fading because nothing here required it. And oddly, I did not even care to recover it; it meant nothing in this place, and it seemed of no consequence. I had nearly forgotten that, where I came from, it was customary for everybody to carry a name like a label tied to a jar.

So I held my peace—and it was wiser than any speech I might have forced. What could I say to a creature like this raven, who looked through accident into essence?

“Look at me,” he said, “and tell me who I am.”

As he spoke, he turned his back to me—and instantly I knew him.

He was no longer a raven, but a man: above middle height, stooping, very thin, wearing a long black tail-coat. Then he turned again, and I saw a raven once more, black and bright, balanced on his feet as if the ground were his familiar shelf.

“I have seen you before, sir,” I said, feeling more foolish than astonished.

“How can you say so from seeing me behind?” he retorted. “Did you ever see yourself behind? You have never seen yourself at all. Tell me now, then—who am I?”

“I humbly beg your pardon,” I answered. “I believe you were once the librarian of our house; but more who I do not know.”

“Why do you beg my pardon?”

“Because I took you for a raven,” I said—though he stood before me as plainly raven as raven could be.

“You did me no wrong,” he replied. “Calling me a raven, or thinking me one, you allowed me existence, which is the sum of what one can demand of his fellow-beings. Therefore, in return, I will give you a lesson: No one can say he is himself until first he knows that he is, and then what himself is. In fact, nobody is himself, and himself is nobody. There is more in it than you can see now, but not more than you need to see.

“You have, I fear, got into this region too soon; but none the less you must get to be at home in it. For home—whether you know it or not—is the only place where you can go out and in. There are places you can go into, and places you can go out of; but the one place, if you do but find it, where you may go out and in both, is home.”

He turned to walk away, and again I saw the librarian. He did not appear to have changed; rather, it seemed as if he had taken up his shadow and put it on. I know how that sounds; I can only tell what I saw, and even in the telling it comes out crooked.

I watched him until I could see him no more; whether distance hid him, or whether he vanished among the heather, I cannot say.

Could it be, I thought, that I was dead and did not know it? Was this what we used to call the world beyond the grave, and must I wander seeking my place in it, as one seeks a house whose address has been rubbed off the envelope?

How was I to find myself at home? The raven had said I must do something. But what could I do here? And would doing make me somebody?—for now, alas, I was nobody.

I set myself to follow the way Mr. Raven had gone, and went slowly after him. Presently I saw a wood of tall, slender pines and turned toward it. Its odour met me on the way: resin and dry needles, a clean sharpness like a knife on a cutting-board, and underneath it a sweetness like sap warming in the sun. The smell pulled me as bread-smell pulls a hungry child; I hastened, eager to bury myself in that familiar twilight, where scent at least had a language I understood.

Plunged at length into its dimness, I spied ahead something that shone between two stems. It had no colour. It was like the translucent trembling of hot air rising from sun-baked ground at noon, vibrant like the struck chord of a musical instrument—visible the way sound is sometimes visible, if you stare long enough at a humming wire. As I went nearer, it grew no plainer; and when I came close up, I ceased to see it at all. Only the forms and colours of the trees beyond seemed suddenly unsure, as if their outlines had forgotten the words that held them.

I would have passed between the stems.

A slight shock met me—no blow, rather a refusal—and I stumbled and fell as if I had walked into a pane of invisible glass. When I rose, I stood before the wooden wall of the garret chamber.

I turned. There was the mirror, and on its top the black eagle seemed to have perched but that moment, as if it had never moved since I first beheld it.

Terror seized me. I fled.

Outside the chamber, the wide garret spaces had an uncanny look. They seemed to have long been waiting for something; it had come, and now they waited again. The air there had its own silence—not the living hush of pine-woods, filled with fine rustlings, but the deadened pause of a room where sound has been swallowed and refused a natural echo. My mind recoiled from it. A shudder went through me on the winding stair: the house itself had grown strange to me, and I felt as if something might leap upon me from behind—not merely a body, but a thought with claws.

I darted down the spiral, struck against the wall and fell, rose and ran. On the next floor I lost my way, and went through several passages twice before I found the head of the stair. At the top of the great stair I had come a little to myself, and in a few moments I sat in the library, recovering breath.

Nothing should ever again make me go up that last terrible stair. The garret at the top of it seemed to pervade the whole house. It sat upon it, threatening to crush me out of it. The brooding brain of the building, it felt full of mysterious dwellers, one or other of whom might at any moment appear in the library where I sat. I was nowhere safe. I would let the place; I would sell the dreadful place, in which an aerial portal stood ever open to creatures whose life was other than human. I would purchase a crag in Switzerland and build upon it a one-storey wooden nest with never a garret above it—guarded by some grand old peak that would send down nothing worse than a few tons of whelming rock.

All the while I knew my thinking was foolish. I heard, beneath it, an undertone of contemptuous humour at my own expense, as if another part of me were listening and smiling at the panic. But suddenly even that humour was checked, and I seemed again to hear the raven’s croak, like a phrase in a tongue I half remembered and could not translate without losing its taste.

If I know nothing of my own garret, I thought, what secures me against my own brain? Can I tell what it is generating—even now—what thought it may present me the next moment, the next month, or a year away? What is at the heart of my brain? What is behind my think? Am I there at all? Who—what—am I?

I could no more answer now than when the raven put it to me in—at—where? “Where in? where at?” I said aloud, and gave myself up as knowing anything of myself or the universe.

Then, as if surrender itself were an action—something at last done—I started to my feet and hurried across the room to the masked door. The mutilated volume, sticking out from the flat of soulless, bodiless, non-existent books, seemed to beckon me like the handle of a tool half-hidden in a drawer.

I went down on my knees and opened it as far as its position would permit, but I could see nothing. I rose, lit a taper, and peeped in as into the reluctant jaws of some patient beast. The manuscript was verse.

Further I could not carry discovery. Beginnings of lines showed on the left-hand page, ends of lines on the other; but I could not reach the beginning and end of a single line. What I could read was too broken to yield a sense.

Yet the mere words—those torn fragments—woke feelings in me that I cannot properly describe, because they were unlike any I had known. There are dreams, poems, musical phrases, pictures, that rouse sensations new in colour and shape—spiritual, yet as unmistakable as flavour. So here: some phrases, some senseless half-lines, some even single words, affected me with something like the aroma of an idea—like catching, for an instant, the scent of a dish you ate in childhood in a house that no longer exists, and feeling hunger not for food but for the vanished life around it. They stirred in me a great longing to know what the poem—or poems—might hold, even in their mutilation.

I copied out a few of the larger shreds I could reach, and tried hard to complete some of the lines, but without the least success. The only thing I gained was weariness—so much that, when I went to bed, I fell asleep at once and slept soundly.

In the morning, all that horror of the empty garret spaces had left me.
