The drawing-room was empty when Archer descended. The silence was not the absence of life, but a curated vacuum, a deliberate suspension of the household's usual rhythm. Dinner was to be a solitary affair for him and May, the family engagements suspended in the wake of Mrs. Manson Mingott's illness. Yet, as Archer entered, the air felt thick with unspoken motion. He knew May was present; he had heard the soft, domestic percussion of her movements in her room while he dressed. Her delay was a variable he could not solve, a knot in the fabric of the evening that demanded unraveling.

This habit of interrogating the gaps in reality had become his anchor. He often wondered if his father-in-law, Mr. Welland, had once possessed similar escapes, similar visions of a world beyond the domestic sphere, and if he had constructed this entire fortress of routine merely to keep the chaos at bay.

May appeared then. She carried the fatigue of the day, draped in the low-necked, tightly laced dress that the Mingott ceremonial demanded even for informality. Her hair, coiled into its accustomed architecture, seemed to hold the weight of the house. Her face, however, was wan, almost faded, though her eyes retained the blue dazzle of the previous day. She approached him with a tenderness that was habitual, a reflexive warmth that masked the cooling of her spirit.

"What became of you, dear?" she asked. "I was waiting at Granny's. Ellen came alone and said she had dropped you on the way because you had to rush off on business. Is there nothing wrong?"

"Only some letters I'd forgotten," he replied, surprised by the intensity of her concern. "I wanted to get them off before dinner."

"Ah—" she said, and paused. "I'm sorry you didn't come to Granny's—unless the letters were urgent."

"They were," he rejoined, noting her insistence. "Besides, I don't see why I should have gone to your grandmother's. I didn't know you were there."

She turned toward the mirror above the mantelpiece. As she lifted her long arm to secure a puff that had slipped from its place in her intricate arrangement, Archer observed a quality in her posture that was languid and inelastic. It was the physical manifestation of a life lived in circles, a monotony that pressed down with invisible weight. He recalled her words from the morning, her promise to meet him at her grandmother's so they might drive home together. He had called back a cheerful "Yes!" and then, lost in other visions, had allowed the promise to dissolve. Now, a sharp pang of guilt pierced him, mingled with irritation that such a trivial omission should be stored away against him after nearly two years of marriage. He was weary of this perpetual tepid honeymoon, devoid of the heat of passion yet burdened by all its demands. If May had voiced her grievances—he suspected she harbored many—he might have dismissed them with laughter; but she was trained to conceal imaginary wounds beneath a Spartan smile.

To mask his own unease, he asked after her grandmother. Mrs. Mingott was improving, she said, though she had been disturbed by recent news regarding the Beauforts.

"What news?"

"It seems they're staying in New York. I believe he's entering an insurance business, or something similar. They're looking for a small house."

The preposterousness of the situation was undeniable, and they proceeded to dinner. Their conversation flowed within its usual narrow channels, yet Archer noted the conspicuous absence of any mention of Madame Olenska, or of old Catherine's reception of her. He felt a vague sense of omen in this silence.

Later, in the library, Archer lit a cigar and selected a volume of Michelet. He had turned to history in the evenings since May had begun to request that he read aloud whenever she saw him with poetry. It was not that he disliked the sound of his own voice, but he could always anticipate her commentary. In the days of their engagement, she had simply echoed his words; but since he had ceased providing opinions, she had begun to offer her own, with results that destroyed his enjoyment of the texts.

Seeing that he had chosen history, she fetched her workbasket, drew up an armchair to the green-shaded student lamp, and uncovered a cushion she was embroidering for his sofa. She was not a skilled needlewoman; her large, capable hands were made for riding, rowing, and open-air pursuits. Yet, since other wives embroidered cushions for their husbands, she refused to omit this final link in her devotion.

She positioned herself so that Archer, by merely raising his eyes, could see her bent over the frame. Her ruffled elbow-sleeves slipped back from her firm, round arms. The betrothal sapphire shone on her left hand above her broad gold wedding ring, while her right hand moved slowly, laboriously, stabbing the canvas. Under the lamplight, full upon her clear brow, he felt a secret dismay rise within him. He realized that he would always know the thoughts behind her actions; she would never surprise him with a new mood, a fresh idea, a sudden weakness, a cruelty, or an unexpected emotion. She had spent her poetry and romance during their brief courtship; the function was exhausted because the need was past. Now, she was simply ripening into a copy of her mother, and, mysteriously, through this very process, attempting to transform him into a Mr. Welland.

He laid down his book and stood up impatiently. Immediately, she raised her head.

"What's the matter?"

"The room is stifling," he said. "I want a little air."

He had insisted that the library curtains slide back and forth on a rod, allowing them to close in the evening, rather than remaining nailed to a gilt cornice and immovably looped up over layers of lace as in the drawing-room. He pulled the cords, pushing the sash up and leaning out into the icy night. The mere act of not looking at May, seated beside his table under his lamp, of seeing other houses, roofs, and chimneys, of grasping the sense of other lives existing outside his own, of perceiving other cities beyond New York and a whole world beyond his immediate sphere, cleared his brain and made it easier to breathe.

After leaning out into the darkness for a few moments, he heard her voice: "Newland! Do shut the window. You'll catch your death."

He pulled the sash down and turned back. "Catch my death!" he echoed. He felt compelled to add: "But I've caught it already. I _am_ dead—I've been dead for months and months."

Suddenly, the play of the word sparked a wild suggestion. What if it were _she_ who was dead? If she were dying—if she were going to die soon—and leave him free! The sensation of standing there, in that warm, familiar room, looking at her, and wishing her dead, was so strange, so fascinating, and overmastering that its enormity did not immediately strike him. He simply felt that chance had offered him a new possibility to which his sick soul might cling. Yes, May might die. People did: young people, healthy people like herself. She might die, and set him suddenly free.

She glanced up, and he saw by the widening of her eyes that there must be something strange in his expression.

"Newland! Are you ill?"

He shook his head and turned toward his armchair. She bent over her workframe, and as he passed, he laid his hand on her hair. "Poor May!" he said.

"Poor? Why poor?" she echoed with a strained laugh.

"Because I shall never be able to open a window without worrying you," he rejoined, laughing also.

For a moment she was silent; then, her head bowed over her work, she said very softly: "I shall never worry if you're happy."

"Ah, my dear; and I shall never be happy unless I can open the windows!"

"In _this_ weather?" she remonstrated. With a sigh, he buried his head in his book.

Six or seven days passed. Archer heard nothing from Madame Olenska, and became aware that her name would not be mentioned in his presence by any member of the family. He did not attempt to see her; to do so while she was at old Catherine's guarded bedside would have been almost impossible. In the uncertainty of the situation, he let himself drift, conscious, somewhere below the surface of his thoughts, of a resolve that had come to him when he had leaned out from his library window into the icy night. The strength of that resolve made it easy to wait and make no sign.

Then one day, May told him that Mrs. Manson Mingott had asked to see him. There was nothing surprising in the request, for the old lady was steadily recovering, and she had always openly declared that she preferred Archer to any of her other grandsons-in-law. May delivered the message with evident pleasure; she was proud of old Catherine's appreciation of her husband.

There was a moment's pause, and then Archer felt it incumbent on him to say: "All right. Shall we go together this afternoon?"

His wife's face brightened, but she instantly answered: "Oh, you'd much better go alone. It bores Granny to see the same people too often."

Archer's heart beat violently as he rang old Mrs. Mingott's bell. He had wanted above all things to go alone, for he felt certain the visit would afford him the chance to say a word in private to the Countess Olenska.
