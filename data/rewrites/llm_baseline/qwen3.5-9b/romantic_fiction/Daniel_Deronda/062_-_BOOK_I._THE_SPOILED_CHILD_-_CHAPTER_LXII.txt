Something that Mirah had recently watched for as the fulfillment of a threat now appeared as the persistent visit of that familiar sorrow which had returned laden with heavy baggage. Having emerged from Knightsbridge after singing at a charitable morning concert held in a wealthy household—where she had been introduced by Klesmer, and where crowds had gathered outside to bid farewell to the departing guests—she soon felt herself pursued by footsteps keeping a steady, unnerving pace beside her own. Her concert dress was simple black, covered by a dust-cloak; it offered no pretext for unpleasant scrutiny nor rendered walking imprudent. Yet, these rational considerations failed to reach Mirah's mind. A different alarm dominated her thoughts.

She immediately thought of her father. She could no longer glance around as if she were being stalked by a ghost. To turn and confront him would be to voluntarily invite the rush of emotions she had previously deemed unbearable. If it were indeed her father, he must intend to demand recognition, forcing her to face him. She decided to wait for that compulsion. Walking on, she did not quicken her step—for what purpose?—but she pictured the impending encounter with absolute certainty, assuming the man trailing her was her father. Alongside this visualization arose a regret: she had promised Mrs. Meyrick not to conceal anything regarding him. This regret eventually spurred her to attempt preventing a sudden betrayal that might shock her brother unnecessarily. Driven by this motive, she resolved to turn before reaching her own door, choosing to actively summon the encounter rather than passively submit to it.

She had already arrived at the entrance of the small square housing her home and prepared to turn when she felt her embodied presentiment closing in, slipping to her side, grasping her wrist, and saying, with a persuasive curl of accent, "Mirah!"

She paused instantly, without a start. It was the voice she expected, and she met the anticipated gaze. Her face remained as grave as if she were facing her executioner, while his expression was adjusted to soothe and appease her. Once handsome, with bright coloring, his face was now sallow and deeply lined, bearing that peculiar stamp of impudent smoothness that arises from courting favor while accepting disrespect. Lightly built and active, he possessed a semblance of youth that made the signs of age appear like a disguise; in truth, he was barely fifty-seven. His attire was shabby, as she remembered seeing it before.

The presence of this irreverent father struck Mirah anew with a mixture of anguish, shame, grief, repulsion, and pity—feelings intensified now that her own world had transformed into one devoid of comradeship capable of shielding him from scorn and contempt.

Slowly, with a sad, tremulous voice, she said, "It is you, father."

"Why did you run away from me, child?" he began, speaking rapidly in a tone intended as tender remonstrance, accompanied by various quick gestures akin to an abbreviated finger-language. "What were you afraid of? You knew I never forced you to act against your will. It was for your sake I broke up your engagement in the Vorstadt, for I saw it did not suit you, and you repaid me by leaving me to the difficult times that ensued. I had arranged an easier engagement for you at the Vorstadt Theater in Dresden; I withheld this news to surprise you. And you left me stranded there, compelled to vanish because I had breached a contract. That was hard lines for me, after sacrificing everything to secure an education for you, which was to become your fortune. What father devoted himself more to his daughter than I did to you? You know how I bore that disappointment in your silence, making the best of it. When I had no one but you, and was breaking down—as men must who fight their way with their intellect alone—you chose that moment to abandon me. Who else did you owe everything to if not to me? Where was your gratitude in return? For what my daughter cared, I might have died in a ditch."

Lapidoth halted abruptly, not from a lack of invention, but because he had reached a pathetic climax, emitting a sudden sob like a woman's. He hastily produced an old yellow silk handkerchief. He genuinely felt that his daughter had treated him poorly—a sensitivity naturally strong in unscrupulous individuals who demand what is theirs without acknowledging any counter-obligations.

Despite his sob, Mirah possessed enough energy not to allow him to believe he had deceived her. She answered more firmly, though it was the first time she had ever used accusatory words toward him. "You know why I left you, father; and I had reason to distrust you, for I was certain you had deceived my mother. Had I trusted you, I would have stayed with you and worked for you."

"I never meant to deceive your mother, Mirah," Lapidoth replied, tucking away his handkerchief, his voice struggling against further weeping. "I intended to take you back to her, but circumstances hindered me precisely at that time, and then news of her death arrived. It was better for you that I remain where I was, allowing your brother to care for himself. No one had any claim upon me but you. I received word of your mother's death from a particular friend entrusted with managing my affairs, and I sent him money to cover expenses. There is one possibility to consider—"

Lapidoth quickly conceived he must guard against an unlikely yet plausible scenario. "He may have written me falsehoods solely to extract money from me."

Mirah offered no response. She could not bear to utter the only truthful reply: "I do not believe a single word of what you say." Instead, she simply indicated a desire to continue walking, sensing that standing still might attract unwanted attention. As they proceeded, their companionship was sufficient to make a passerby turn back to stare. The figure of Mirah, her beauty enhanced by the quiet, careful dress of an English lady, formed a strange contrast to this shabby, foreign-looking, eager, and gesticulating man. Yet, he retained an indelible jauntness of manner, perhaps due to the bushy curls of his grizzled hair, the smallness of his hands and feet, and his light stride.

"You seem to have done well for yourself, Mirah?" the father asked, examining her with emphatic scrutiny. "You are in no want, I see."

"Good friends who found me in distress have helped me secure work," Mirah replied, hardly aware of her actual words, consumed by what she needed to say later. "I give lessons. I have sung in private houses. I have just performed at a private concert." She paused, then added significantly, "I have very good friends who know all about me."

"And you would be ashamed if they saw your father in this plight? No wonder. I came to England with no prospects, save the chance of finding you. It was a mad quest; but a father's heart is superstitious—it feels a loadstone drawing it somewhere. I might have prospered staying abroad; when I had no one to care for, I could roll or settle as easily as a ball. But it is hard to be lonely in the world when one's spirit begins to break. I thought my little Mirah would repent leaving her father when she looked back. I've had a sharp pinch working my way; I don't know what I'll descend to next. Talents like mine are useless in this country. When a man is reduced to rags, no one believes in him. I couldn't obtain decent employment with my appearance. I've been forced to sink quite low for a shilling already."

Mirah's anxiety was swift enough to imagine her father sinking into further degradation, a fate she felt bound to prevent if possible. Before she could respond to his string of inventive sentences, delivered with the glibness of someone reciting a memorized script, he prompted, "Where do you live, Mirah?"

"Here, in this square. We are not far from the house."

"In lodgings?"

"Yes."

"Anyone to take care of you?"

"Yes," Mirah repeated, fixing her gaze on the keen face turned toward hers— "my brother."

The father's eyelids fluttered as if lightning had flashed across them, accompanied by a slight shrug of the shoulders. After a brief, perceptible pause, he asked, "Ezra? How did you know—how did you find him?"

"That would take long to explain. Here we are at the door. My brother would not wish me to shut it on you."

Mirah was already on the doorstep, yet her face remained turned toward her father, who stood below on the pavement. Her heart began to beat faster at the prospect of introducing him to Ezra. Already, in this posture of granting permission to the father she had been accustomed to obey—seeing him shrink beneath her, indirectly asking for admission—she felt a pang of peculiar, sympathetic humiliation and shame, the stabbed heart of reverence belonging to a nature intensely filial.

"Stay a minute, *Liebchen*," Lapidoth said in a lowered tone. "What sort of man has Ezra turned out?"
