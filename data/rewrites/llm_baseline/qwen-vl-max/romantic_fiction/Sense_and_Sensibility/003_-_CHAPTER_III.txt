Mrs. Dashwood stayed at Norland for several months—not because she longed to linger among the familiar scenes that once stirred her heart with sorrow, but because she had not yet found a home worthy of her family’s comfort and dignity. Though grief gradually gave way to a quieter peace, she was eager to leave the place where every corner whispered memories of loss. Yet the thought of moving far from Norland was unbearable; it was too deeply tied to her past, too sacred to abandon entirely. So she searched diligently for a house nearby—something modest enough for their means, yet comfortable and peaceful. But nothing seemed quite right. Her eldest daughter, Elinor, insisted on prudence, rejecting homes that were too large or costly, while Mrs. Dashwood’s heart yearned for something more spacious and elegant. They could not agree, and so they remained.

She still remembered her husband’s final words—the solemn promise made by their son John, that he would care for them in their time of need. She trusted this vow completely, just as her husband had done. It brought her quiet comfort, especially when thinking of her daughters’ futures. For herself, she believed that even half the promised sum would be more than enough to live well. And for her son’s sake, she felt genuine joy—he had proven himself kind, attentive, and generous in ways she had once doubted. His kindness toward her and her girls had softened her heart, and she now saw him not as cold or selfish, but as a man whose love for his family ran deep.

Her feelings toward his wife, however, had only grown colder. From the beginning, Mrs. Dashwood had found Fanny Dashwood unlikable; now, after six months under the same roof, she despised her aloofness, her pride, and her lack of warmth. The two women might have clashed bitterly if not for one unexpected development—one that made staying at Norland seem not only bearable, but preferable.

That development was Edward Ferrars, brother to Mrs. John Dashwood, who had recently come to visit his sister and had since become a frequent guest at Norland. He was a gentleman of quiet manners and gentle bearing—neither striking nor flamboyant, but possessing a calm charm that drew people in without effort. He was introduced soon after Fanny settled into the household, and though he had been present for weeks before Mrs. Dashwood truly noticed him, his presence began to grow more meaningful as time passed.

At first, she merely appreciated his silence. In her sorrow, she welcomed anyone who did not intrude upon her thoughts. He spoke little, never pressed conversation, and never disturbed her melancholy. But then, one day, Elinor remarked on the contrast between Edward and his sister—how different he was in spirit, in nature, in everything that mattered. That simple observation opened Mrs. Dashwood’s eyes.

“It is enough,” she said, “to say that he is unlike Fanny. That alone implies all that is good and noble. I already love him.”

“I think you will like him,” Elinor replied gently.
“Like him?” Mrs. Dashwood smiled. “I feel no sentiment short of love.”
“You may esteem him.”
“I have never known such a thing as separating esteem from love.”

From that moment, she sought to know him better. Her manner, warm and sincere, quickly broke through his shyness. She discovered his thoughtful mind, his steady character, and his deep affection for Elinor. Though he lacked the boldness or brilliance others might expect in a young man, she came to see that his quietness masked a tender heart. When he read aloud, she sensed his sincerity, even if his voice lacked passion. And when he listened to music or admired Elinor’s drawings, she saw not mere courtesy, but genuine regard—even if it wasn’t always expressed with flair.

As soon as she perceived the depth of his feelings for Elinor, she accepted their bond as certain. Their marriage, she believed, was only a matter of time. “In a few months,” she told Marianne, “Elinor will likely be settled for life. We shall miss her—but she will be happy.”

“Oh, Mama!” Marianne exclaimed. “How can we live without her?”

“My dear, it won’t be a true separation. We’ll be within miles of each other, meeting often. You’ll gain a brother—a real, kind-hearted brother. I have great faith in Edward’s goodness.”

But Marianne frowned. “Perhaps I do not fully approve,” she said softly. “Edward is amiable—I love him dearly. Yet… he is not the kind of man I imagined for my sister. He lacks fire. His figure is plain, his manner subdued. His eyes hold no spark, no light that speaks of soul or intelligence. And worse—his taste is dull. Music moves him little, and though he praises Elinor’s art, it is not the praise of someone who truly understands. He admires her as a lover, not as a judge. To me, both must go together. A man must share my passions, my feelings. We must find beauty in the same books, the same melodies. Last night, when he read Cowper aloud—oh, Mama! How flat, how lifeless it sounded! I suffered for my sister. She sat calmly, as if nothing stirred her. But I could barely stay in my seat. Those lines—so full of feeling, so alive—spoken with such indifference! It was agony.”

“You are right,” Mrs. Dashwood admitted. “He would have done better with prose. But you insisted on Cowper.”

“Yes,” Marianne sighed. “And if he cannot be moved by Cowper… what hope is there? Still, we must allow for differences. Elinor does not feel as I do. That is why she can be content. But I—had I loved him, I would have died of disappointment. Mama, the more I see of the world, the less I believe I will ever meet a man I could truly love. He must have all of Edward’s virtues—and yet be radiant with charm, grace, and spirit. I require so much.”

“And remember,” Mrs. Dashwood said softly, “you are not seventeen yet. It is too early to give up hope. Why should you not be as fortunate as your mother? There is only one thing—perhaps—where your fate may differ from mine.”
