There could be no doubting the speed with which Empress Phorenice's will took root in the city of Atlantis. To her, the nation and everything within it existed solely for her benefit, and no price was too steep to pay for her desires. Having issued her decree regarding the entombment of Nais, though she had ordered me to construct the stone throne personally, the unspoken truth was that the labor would be performed by others. Heralds sounded the alarm in every district, summoning masons, stonemasons, sculptors, engineers, and architects from their current tasks. These artisans gathered at the rendezvous, selecting a chief director. Lesser architects and engineers ensured his orders were executed with ruthless efficiency. Any material sealed within the city limits was seized immediately, without compensation, forcing the demolition of numerous structures to clear paths for the colossal blocks required.

As I had noted before, there is a perpetual frenzy among nations to erect new palaces and pyramids. Even as a rebel army battered the city walls with war engines, the building guilds worked tirelessly. Their skill, aided by Phorenice's marvelous inventions, grew with every hour. While they lacked the ability to shift the massive stones placed by the early Gods for the sacred circle of the Sun Lord, they possessed rams, trucks, and cranes capable of moving immense burdens.

The throne was destined for the open square before the Royal Pyramid. Its foundation consisted of seven tiers of stone, each knee-high and cut with three steps in the front. The topmost layer featured a cavity designed to hold Nais's body, surmounted by a vast block forming the seat itself. Throughout the night, illuminated by torches, endless relays of stonecutters, masons, and sweating laborers toiled to dress the stone and lay it in perfect alignment. Engineers constructed lifting machines, and architects verified every placement. Whips cracked, men fainted from exhaustion, but the moment anyone fell, another rushed forward. There was no tolerance for delay when Phorenice had spoken.

By morning, as the square filled with onlookers, the debris and scaffolding were cleared, revealing the bodies of nearly thirty workers who had perished from accidents or overexertion. Standing there, resplendent in its carvings, was the throne, ready for completion. Its base stood two man-heights above the ground, composed of courses weighing twenty men apiece, while the upper section doubled that weight, carved with a royal snake whose hooded head overshadowed the seat. Currently, however, the upper portion hung suspended high by lifting rams, held aloft for a purpose everyone understood.

Summoned from the Royal Pyramid by the chamberlains, I emerged into the cool of the next morning. Every dignitary arriving before me was heralded by banner-bearers and trumpeters; the middle classes displayed their finest apparel, and even the poorest, starving souls wrapped colorful wisps around their heads to mark the day's "gaiety." Trumpets announced my arrival, and the crowd shouted welcomes. Accompanied by the gorgeous chamberlains walking backward ahead of me, I crossed to a scarlet awning and took my seat upon soft cushions.

Then arrived Phorenice, my bride for that day, fresh from sleep and radiant in her splendor. She was borne from the pyramid in an open litter of gold and ivory by fantastical savages from Europe, her refined features heightened by the contrast of their brutish ugliness. The people drew a collective breath of delight at her sight; it was impossible for any man in that throng—not even Ylga, who might be veiled among them—to imagine I was not overjoyed at winning such a lovely wife.

Summoning the iron discipline of my training, I guarded the expression of my face. This was a ceremonial affair, ghastly in its nature, yet still ceremonial. I intended to present a grand manner to Phorenice, leaving her no room for complaint. After enduring so much, I resolved not to shatter the scheme by letting agony show in a shaking hand or twitching cheek. When the time came, I would lay the living body of my love in the hollow beneath the stone with the same calm detachment as a priest burying a dead stranger. And she, I knew, would not betray our secret. With her, as with me, it was truly "before all Atlantis."

To my relief, the proceedings lacked mockery or flippancy. Everything was solemn and impressive. Though a profound sombreness, biting deep into my breast, remained hidden from the vulgar crowd, the outward shape of the double sacrifice they witnessed would surely not be forgotten, even if its inner meaning eluded them. When it suited her mood, the many-mooded Empress was as strict as any ancient ritualist, and today she commanded rigid exactness and pomp.

She was lifted by her European attendants to the scarlet awning, and I handed her down. Seating herself on the cushions, she beckoned me to her side, entwining our fingers as was custom between Atlantean rulers and their consorts. Before us, a body of soldiery marched forward, spreading out to reveal Nais in their midst. She wore a metal collar around her neck, from which chains depended, firmly held by a pair of guards to prevent her from running into the spears of the escort—a common method for condemning prisoners to lingering punishment.

Yet, strangely, she still wore her clothes. Custom dictated that the garments of the condemned belong to the soldiers guarding them, but since Nais was not stripped, I inferred that someone had bribed the guards with money. I believed I saw Ylga's hand in this act, and I felt a quiet gratitude toward her.

The soldiers brought her to the edge of the pavilion's shade. She was bidden to prostrate herself before the Empress, a wise action that spared her rough handling. Her face was pale, yet devoid of fear or defiance; her eyes were calm and natural. She remembered what was due to Atlantis, and I was thrilled with love and pride watching her. Outwardly, however, I remained impassive as stone. Knowing Phorenice's gaze was fixed upon me, I allowed nothing on my face—nothing she would not have wanted to see.

"Nais," the Empress said, "you have eaten from my platter when you were a fan-girl, and drunk from my cup, and what was yours I gave you. You should have had more than gratitude; you should have known that the arm of the Empress is long and her hand heavily weighted. But it seems you possess neither. Moreover, you attempted to seize a matter set apart for the Empress. You were offered pardon on terms and rejected it. You were foolish. Yet, today, I am inclined toward clemency. Presently, seated on that carved throne of granite which Deucalion has built me yonder, I shall take my Lord Deucalion to husband. Give me a plain word that you are sorry, girl, and name a man you would choose, and I will remember the brightness of this occasion. You shall be pardoned and wed before we rise from these cushions."

"I will not wed," she said quietly.

"Think for the last time, Nais, of the other choice," Phorenice continued. "You will be taken, warm and quick, and beautiful as you stand, and laid in the hollow beneath the throne-stone. Deucalion, who is to be my husband, will lay you in that awful bed as a symbol that so shall perish all Phorenice's enemies. Then he will release the rams and lower the upper stone into place, and the world shall see your face no more. Look at the bright sky, Nais, fill your chest with the sweet, warm air, and then consider what this death means. Believe me, girl, I do not wish to make you an example unless you force me."

"I will not wed," the prisoner repeated quietly.

Phorenice loosened her fingers from my arm and lay back against the cushions. "If the girl presumes on our old familiarity, or thinks I jest, show her now, Deucalion, that I do not."

"The Empress is far from jesting," I replied. "I will do this thing because it is the wish of the Empress, and because it is her command that a symbol remain forever as an example for others. Lead your prisoner to the place."

The soldiers wheeled, and the two guards holding Nais's collared neck prepared to use force to drag her up the steps. Instead, she walked with them willingly, her color unchanged. I rose from my seat, made obeisance to the Empress, and followed them.

Before the ten thousand watching eyes, we made no display of emotion. Not only for Atlantis' sake, but because both Nais and I possessed a certain nicety and pride in our natures; we were not like Phorenice to flaunt endearments before others. Yet, when I bid the guards unhasp the collar and clasped my arms around her, showing all the roughness of one determined to prevent escape or undue struggle, a thrill surged through me so potent I nearly fainted. It required a supreme strain of will to maintain composure. I, who had never embraced a woman save with the arm of roughness, now held pressed to me one I loved with infinite tenderness. The revelation of how love links with love was almost my undoing. Outwardly, Nais made no sign, lying half-strangled in my arms as any woman does when borne away by a spoiler.
