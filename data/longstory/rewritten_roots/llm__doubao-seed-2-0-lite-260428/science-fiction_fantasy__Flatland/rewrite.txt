By the Editor

If my old, dear friend the Square of Flatland still held the sharp, curious vigour of mind he had when he first penned these memoirs, I would not need to speak for him here. He had three things he was desperate to say to his readers, first of all: to offer his deepest gratitude to the Spaceland audience and critics who loved his account of 2D life so fiercely that demand for a second edition arose far faster than either of us ever dared hope. Second, to apologise for the scattered small errors and misprints that slipped into the first run – flaws for which, he swears, he is not entirely to blame, given how clumsy 3D pen and paper feels to a being who has never known more than two directions of movement. Third, to clear up a handful of very persistent misconceptions that have cropped up in reviews and reader letters since his book first launched.

Alas, he is not the bright, restless Square who snuck a 24-hour glimpse of our 3D world all those years ago. Years of locked confinement in Flatland’s state prison, the far heavier weight of universal mockery and disbelief from everyone he ever knew, and the soft eroding fog of old age have scrubbed most of the terminology and sharp, vivid memories he picked up during his short visit to Spaceland right out of his mind. So he has begged me to speak on his behalf to address the two most strident objections raised against his work, one intellectual, one moral.

The first and most common critique runs like this: when a Flatlander looks at a line, their eye must perceive some tiny thickness as well as visible length, otherwise the line (with exactly zero depth) would be completely invisible. Therefore, the argument goes, Flatlanders must have a tiny, unacknowledged third dimension of height, however infinitesimal, and the Square should have admitted this plainly from the start.

This argument is so plausible, so irrefutable to most Spacelanders, that I will admit I had no reply for it the first time I heard it. But my old friend’s answer, when I brought the critique to him in his cell, is entirely unassailable.

“I admit every fact your critic states,” he told me, “but I deny their conclusion. It is technically true that every object in Flatland carries that unrecognised third dimension we could call ‘height’, just as it is true that every object in our Spaceland has a fourth unrecognised dimension, no one has named yet, that we could call ‘extra-height’. But we Flatlanders can no more perceive or reason out our ‘height’ than you Spacelanders can wrap your heads around that fourth ‘extra-height’. Even I – who walked your streets, who understood the meaning of height perfectly for 24 whole hours – cannot visualise it now, cannot see it with my eyes or work it out through logic. I can only hold it as a matter of faith.

“The reason is simple. A dimension implies direction, it implies measurement, it implies the possibility of more and less. Every line, every shape, every object in Flatland is equally, infinitesimally thin. There is no variation, no difference in that tiny third dimension that could ever nudge any Flatlander to suspect a whole new direction exists. No fancy, delicate micrometer a hasty critic suggested would help us at all – we would not know what to measure, nor in what direction to point our tool.

“To us, a line you would call 1D is both long and bright: brightness is not a separate property we detach from it, it is the very life of the line. If the brightness fades, the line vanishes entirely. So when I try to explain this unseeable third dimension to my fellow prisoners or guards, they all nod and say ‘oh, you mean brightness’. When I tell them no, this is a real, separate physical dimension, they laugh and demand I measure it, or point out which direction it extends. I cannot do either, and I fall silent. Only last week, the Chief Circle – our High Priest, the absolute head of our ruling aristocracy – made his seventh annual visit to inspect the prison, and once more asked if I had renounced my mad stories of a third dimension. I tried to prove to him that he too was tall, in that hidden direction, as well as long and broad. What did he say? ‘Measure my height, and I will believe you.’ I had no answer. He left the cell triumphant, and I was left to pace my stone floor.

“Does that still sound absurd to you? Put yourself in my place. Imagine a visitor from the Fourth Dimension drops through your ceiling one day, and says: ‘Every time you open your eyes, you look at a 2D plane, and infer a 3D solid. But you are all seeing a fourth dimension too, one that is not color, not brightness, not any property you know – it is a real dimension, even if I cannot point out its direction, and you could never measure it.’ What would you do? You would call the orderlies to lock the strange man up, would you not? That is exactly my fate. It is as natural for Flatland to lock up a Square for preaching the Third Dimension as it is for Spaceland to lock up a Cube for preaching the Fourth.

“Alas, how alike all blinded, persecuting beings are across every possible world. Points, Lines, Squares, Cubes, Extra-Cubes: we are all slaves to the prejudices of the dimensions we happen to be born into, just as one of your Spaceland poets wrote: *One touch of Nature makes all worlds akin*.”

[Note from the Editor, at the explicit request of the Square: to clear up this exact widespread misconception, he has added new asides to his original dialogue with the visiting Sphere, remarks he once cut as tedious and unnecessary, to lay this physics confusion entirely to rest.]

This first defence of the Square’s is, as far as I can tell, completely unbreakable. I wish I could say his reply to the second, moral objection was equally clear and convincing. Many critics have called him a woman-hater, an accusation raised fiercely by that larger half of Spaceland’s population whose voices he so often sidelined in his original draft. I would happily clear this stain from his name entirely, if I could do so honestly, but his seven years in prison have softened and shifted his views so far from what they were when he first wrote his memoirs that I would do him a disservice to transcribe his rambling defence verbatim. As his interpreter, I can sum it up plainly: he no longer holds the unkind, dismissive views of Flatland’s Straight-Line women and working-class Isosceles Triangles that were standard among his social circle. He now finds himself agreeing entirely with the Sphere that women are, in many critical ways, wiser and more perceptive than the elite Circles who rule Flatland. But when he wrote his history, he fell prey to the bad habit shared by almost all Flatland historians, and nearly all Spaceland historians until very recently: he treated the lives of women and the mass of ordinary working people as irrelevant background noise, unworthy of detailed, careful attention.

He also wishes to formally disavow the aristocratic, pro-Circle biases that many readers fairly assumed he held. He still acknowledges the sharp, ruthless intelligence that allowed a tiny ruling class of Circles to maintain power over millions of Flatlanders for hundreds of generations – but he points out that the bare facts of Flatland history, told without commentary, speak for themselves. Revolutions can never be permanently crushed by massacres, and Nature, in condemning the Circles to near-total infertility, has already doomed their dynasty to eventual collapse. “In that,” he told me, “I see the great law that binds every world together: the schemes that the powerful think they are building for their own eternal dominion are always twisted by Nature’s greater wisdom into something far different, and far kinder, than they ever intended.”

As a final note, he begs his readers not to insist that every tiny silly detail of daily life in Flatland must map 1:1 to some equivalent rule in our 3D world. All he hopes is that, for modest, curious Spaceland readers – the kind who refuse to shout “that is completely impossible!” at anything that falls outside their experience, but who also refuse to claim they already know every single fact about realities beyond their own – this little book will prove both amusing, and thought-provoking.

> "Be patient, for the world is broad and wide."
>
> That old Flatland proverb never once stirred any deeper thought in me for decades of my ordinary, two-dimensional life—until the day my eyes were opened to realities that stretched far past the planar bounds I had always mistaken for the sum of all existence. This memoir, written by a humble, law-abiding middle-class Square, is split into two clear parts, so that any reader from any plane of existence might follow my journey from unthinking familiarity with my own world to the dizzying, humbling revelation of dimensions I never could have imagined were possible.
>

> ### Part I: This World
> The first half of my account is dedicated entirely to the laws, customs, peoples, and hidden, suppressed history of Flatland itself, for no outsider could possibly intuit how our two-dimensional lives unfold without careful, step-by-step explanation:
> 1.  *Of the Nature of Flatland*: The foundational physical rules that govern our realm, with no concept of height or depth, only infinite length and breadth stretched out before all who dwell here.
> 2.  *Of the Climate and Houses in Flatland*: How weather functions on a two-dimensional plane, and the strict, elegant architectural rules we follow to keep our households safe and orderly.
> 3.  *Concerning the Inhabitants of Flatland*: The rigid class system that defines our society, where the number of your sides marks your intelligence, your social status, and your prescribed place in our world.
> 4.  *Concerning the Women*: The singular, razor-sharp line-shaped women of Flatland, whose very form demands strict public safety laws and carefully enforced social customs to prevent accidental harm to our triangular, polygonal citizenry.
> 5.  *Of our Methods of Recognizing one another*: The clever, millennia-honed tricks of touch, sound, and subtle deduction that we use to tell one shape apart from another, even when all any Flatlander can ever see of a peer is a straight, uniform line.
> 6.  *Of Recognition by Sight*: The rare, refined skill reserved for our upper classes, of piercing the thin perpetual fog that hangs over Flatland to identify strangers by tiny, almost imperceptible differences in the shading of their lines.
> 7.  *Concerning Irregular Figures*: The great unspoken social taboo of lopsided, asymmetric shapes, and the rigid state policies that frame any imperfection in a citizen's sides as an existential danger to the entire social order.
> 8.  *Of the Ancient Practice of Painting*: The half-forgotten old custom of citizens dyeing the edges of their own sides to make their shape instantly identifiable at a glance, no touch or sound test required.
> 9.  *Of the Universal Colour Bill*: The populist political movement that almost swept Flatland generations ago, to make painting all citizens mandatory and upend the entire long-standing class system overnight.
> 10. *Of the Suppression of the Chromatic Sedition*: The bloody, decisive counter-coup led by our ruling priestly caste that crushed the Colour Bill movement for good, and erased much of the old painting custom from public memory.
> 11. *Concerning our Priests*: The near-perfect Circles who rule Flatland, revered as the wisest of all living beings, the custodians of all our laws and sacred traditions.
> 12. *Of the Doctrine of our Priests*: The carefully guarded official dogma that the Circles enforce across all of Flatland, to keep social order steady and ensure no citizen ever questions the unchallenged "fact" that only two dimensions can possibly exist.
>
> ---
> ### Part II: Other Worlds
> It was in my quiet old age that the unthinkable happened, and my entire view of reality shattered. The second half of this record lays out that impossible, life-altering journey, no part of which I would have once believed possible myself:
> 13. *How I had a Vision of Lineland*: The first strange dream that showed me a realm even narrower than my own: a one-dimensional world of lines and points, trapped on a single narrow glowing thread of existence.
> 14. *How I vainly tried to explain the nature of Flatland*: My desperate, futile attempt to make the king of that tiny Lineland understand the possibility of a second dimension, a reality he could never perceive with his limited senses.
> 15. *Concerning a Stranger from Spaceland*: The day a glowing, shape-shifting visitor materialized right inside my locked house, a being no Flatland natural law could ever account for.
> 16. *How the Stranger vainly endeavoured to reveal to me in words the mysteries of Spaceland*: The Sphere's long, frustrating attempts to describe three full dimensions to me, a man who had never once seen or conceived of height in my entire life.
> 17. *How the Sphere, having in vain tried words, resorted to deeds*: When language failed, my visitor used magic I could not explain to prove his claims, pulling objects right out of sealed cabinets and revealing secrets I had hidden in my own home no other Flatlander could ever know of.
> 18. *How I came to Spaceland, and what I saw there*: The day I was lifted right out of Flatland entirely, to float in three-dimensional space, and see my entire planar world spread out beneath me like a printed sheet of paper.
> 19. *How, though the Sphere shewed me other mysteries of Spaceland, I still desired more; and what came of it*: The awe I felt exploring three dimensions, and the heretical question I dared to ask, of a fourth dimension, a fifth, and endless more beyond that.
> 20. *How the Sphere encouraged me in a Vision*: The dream the Sphere granted me, to see an endless hierarchy of worlds, from the zero-dimensional void of Pointland, to Lineland, to Flatland, to Spaceland, and the faint, impossible hints of infinite higher realms beyond.
> 21. *How I tried to teach the Theory of Three Dimensions to my Grandson, and with what success*: My return to Flatland, and my first attempt to share the truth of the third dimension with my own bright little grandson, with results that broke my heart.
> 22. *How I then tried to diffuse the Theory of Three Dimensions by other means, and of the result*: My final, fateful attempts to spread the forbidden truth of higher dimensions across all of Flatland, and the fate that has left me writing this secret record, hidden away from the eyes of the Circles who rule us.

I name our home Flatland not out of any local custom, but to make its rules plain to you, lucky readers privileged to live and move in the full three dimensions of Space.

Picture an endless, perfectly smooth sheet of paper. On its surface, all kinds of shapes — straight Lines, Triangles, Squares, Pentagons, Hexagons, every polygon you can imagine — are not stuck fixed to their spots. They glide freely across the face of that sheet, or even half-submerged in its thin surface, but none of them can ever rise even a hair's breadth above the sheet, or sink below it. They are a little like the dark shadow figures you see cast on a wall, but solid, sharp, their edges glowing faintly with light. That is my country. Those are my people. Only a few years back, I would have called that our entire universe. But now my eyes have been opened to truths far bigger than our two little dimensions.

In a world like that, you can guess instantly there is no such thing as what you Spaceland folks call a "solid" object. But I would wager you are already assuming we can look across our surface and tell a Triangle apart from a Square, clear as day, all their distinct angles and sides on display? You would be dead wrong. We see nothing of the sort. There is no shape, no corner, no angle visible to us at all — not ever. All we can see, every single thing that meets our eyes, is a straight line. Let me show you why that has to be true.

Take a round penny, set it dead center on your Spaceland table. Lean over it, look straight down. It looks like a perfect circle, obviously. Now slowly edge back toward the table's far edge, lower your eye little by little, until you are almost level with the tabletop. Watch that circle turn first into a wide oval, then a thinner one, thinner still — until the very second your eye is exactly, perfectly level with the table's surface, that penny stops looking like a curved shape entirely. To you now, it is nothing more than a thin unbroken straight line. The exact same thing happens if you lay a pasteboard triangle, or a square, or any other flat figure on that table. Once your eye is flush with the table's surface, you can't perceive the shape at all. All you see is a straight line.

Take our most respectable tradesman, for example: an equilateral Triangle, the standard upstanding middle-class citizen of Flatland. If you hovered over him from your 3D space, you would see the familiar three-sided shape, wide base, equal angles. If you knelt down and got your eye almost to the table level, he would look like a thin, tapered line with a little blur of depth to it. But if your eye is fully level with the surface — the way every single Flatlander sees every other thing, all the time — there is no taper, no angle, no shape. There is only one straight, glowing edge.

I heard all about your Spaceland sailors when I visited your world, you know. They run into the exact same effect when they are out on the open ocean, staring at a distant coastline on the horizon. That far-off shore might be full of bays and headlands, jagged cliffs and soft sandy coves, every sort of twist and indent imaginable. But from far enough away, unless the sun hits it just right to carve those shapes out with light and shadow, they can't see any of that complexity. All they see is one unbroken grey line stitched across the blue of the sea.

That is exactly what we see, every single day, when one of our neighbors walks toward us. We have no sun hanging above our world, no light source that can cast shadows to show us depth or shape. So when a friend approaches, the line we perceive of them just grows longer. When they walk away, it shrinks shorter. But no matter if that friend is a lowly straight Line (our women), a sharp isosceles Triangle (our working class), a Square, a Pentagon, a Hexagon, or even a perfect Circle high up in our priestly class — they never look like anything but a straight line.

You are probably wondering, how on earth do we ever tell each other apart under these weird, limiting conditions? That is a very fair question, but it will make far more sense once I have walked you through the full details of who the people of Flatland are, and how our society works. I will set that question aside for now, and start by telling you a little bit about our climate, and the houses we build to live in, on that endless flat sheet that is our home.

Just as in your three-dimensional world, we of Flatland recognize four cardinal directions: North, South, East, and West. But with no sun, no stars, no heavenly bodies of any kind hanging over our plane, we cannot navigate by celestial cues the way you do. We have our own natural compass, written into the very laws of our world: a faint, constant, universal pull drawing all things gently toward the South.

This tug is barely perceptible in our temperate zones—mild enough that even a woman in reasonable health can trek several furlongs north without significant strain—yet it is consistent enough to serve as a reliable bearing guide across almost all our territory. We have a second, far more obvious marker too: rain, which falls on a strict predictable schedule, always drifting down from the North. In towns, our homes reinforce these guides: almost every building’s side walls run perfectly aligned north-to-south, so their steep northern-facing roofs shed all that incoming rain away from the home’s entrances. Out in open countryside where no buildings stand, even the trunks of wild trees grow aligned along that north-south axis to give wanderers a reference point. All told, we face far less trouble staying oriented than a new visitor to our world might assume.

I can attest that even that is not foolproof. On our most temperate plains, where the southward tug is nearly undetectable, I have found myself stranded on vast featureless stretches with no trees, no structures, no landmarks at all, forced to stand perfectly still for hours on end waiting for a rainshower to roll in so I could reorient myself and resume my journey. That gentle southward pull hits the weak, the aged, and especially delicate women far harder than it strikes robust young men, so strict social etiquette dictates that any man who crosses paths with a lady on a public path must always yield her the North side of the way, where the pull will not yank her off balance toward a sharp edge if she stumbles. This is no easy feat to pull off on short notice, when you are in full health and the landmarks around you give no clear clue which direction North even is.

You will notice we have no windows anywhere in our homes. Light reaches us equally indoors and out, at all hours of the day and night, uniform across every corner of our plane, and we have no agreed-upon explanation for where it comes from. In old generations, this question of the origin of light was one of the most hotly pursued mysteries among our leading scholars—every attempt to solve it only filled our lunatic asylums with the theorists who chased it. After decades of failed attempts to discourage the research by slapping ruinous taxes on anyone publishing papers about light’s origins, our legislature banned all inquiry into the subject entirely in comparatively recent history. I, alas, am the only soul in all of Flatland who now knows the true answer to that long-unresolved riddle: it bleeds through from the higher, three-dimensional world I have visited. But I cannot make that truth intelligible to a single one of my countrymen. I, the sole bearer of the knowledge of Space beyond our flat plane, am mocked as the maddest of madmen. Let me set aside these painful digressions, and return to the subject of our homes.

The near-universal standard for residential construction is the five-sided, pentagonal shape. The two northern edges of the pentagon meet at the top to form our sloped rain-shedding roof, with no doors cut into them at all. The east-facing edge has a small, narrow entryway reserved exclusively for the women of the household, while the far larger west-facing edge forms the main door for the men. The southernmost bottom edge of the pentagon, the floor of the home, has no openings of any kind.

Square and triangular houses have long been outlawed for ordinary residential use, for very good reason. The vertex of a square is far sharper than the wide, blunt 108-degree angle of a pentagon, and the edges of inanimate objects like houses register as far fainter to our sight than the crisp bright outlines of living people. An absent-minded traveler could very easily fail to spot a sharp house corner and run straight into it, sustaining a grave, even fatal injury. As early as the 11th century of our common era, equilateral triangular homes were universally banned by law. The only exceptions made are for state properties: forts, gunpowder magazines, military barracks, sites the general public is explicitly expected to approach only with extreme caution.

Square homes were still permitted for another three hundred years after that, though the state discouraged their construction by slapping them with a steep annual special tax. At the end of that three centuries, the legislature extended public safety rules to mandate that all towns with a population over ten thousand could only build homes with angles no sharper than a pentagon’s vertex. The public embraced the new rule wholeheartedly, and now pentagonal construction has replaced every other residential design even in the most remote countryside. The only surviving square houses left today are rare curiosities, tucked away in the most backward, isolated farming districts, sought out only by antiquarians chasing traces of our older history.

A full-grown resident of our planar world rarely stretches more than 11 inches across at their widest point, with a hard maximum size of 12 inches. That is the full limit of physical scale in our two-dimensional realm, and all our social order and daily life is shaped by the sides and angles that make up every Flatlander’s form.

Let me lay out our social hierarchy, written into the very geometry of our bodies:
- Our Women are pure Straight Lines: thin, sharp, and capable of slicing through any unsuspecting shape that crosses their path.
- Soldiers and the lowest class of manual laborers are Isosceles Triangles: two equal sides almost 11 inches long, with a third base edge so short (often less than half an inch, sometimes barely an eighth of an inch) that the sharp vertex at their point is almost indistinguishable from the tip of a Straight Line. These are our most physically formidable residents, for their narrow points can pierce almost any other shape with ease.
- Our middle class is made up entirely of Equilateral Triangles, all three sides perfectly equal, their 60-degree angles neither too sharp to be recklessly dangerous nor too wide to be clumsy.
- Professional men, scholars, and gentlemen of the trades are Squares—this is the class I myself belong to—and five-sided Pentagons, set apart from the working masses by their extra side and wider, more gentle internal angles.
- Above these ranks sit the Nobility, graded by nothing but the count of their sides: starting with six-sided Hexagons, then seven-sided, eight-sided, climbing ever higher in number until they earn the honorific title of Polygonal, or "many-sided".
- At the very peak of our social order are the Circles, who make up our Priestly caste. By the time a shape has hundreds upon hundreds of tiny, evenly matched sides, the difference between their multi-sided perimeter and a perfect unbroken curve becomes impossible to spot with any Flatlander eye. This is the highest, most revered rank in all our planar lands.

It is a fundamental Law of Nature in Flatland that every male child will be born with exactly one more side than his father. Barring accident or defect, each generation climbs one full rung up the ladder of development and nobility: the son of a Square is a Pentagon, the son of a Pentagon a Hexagon, and so on, in a steady upward march of refinement.

This law does not extend, however, to the lowest ranks. Tradesmen, soldiers, and manual laborers are not regular equal-sided figures at all, and barely qualify as full human citizens in the eyes of the upper classes. An Isosceles Triangle’s son will almost always be born Isosceles too, with no automatic advancement for him. But even they are not condemned to permanent stagnation. Over generations of exceptional military valor, or generations of skilled, diligent labor, the most intelligent, disciplined members of the lower classes will slowly see their short base edge lengthen, while their two long sharp sides shrink gradually closer to equal length. The Priests arrange carefully vetted marriages between the highest-achieving Isosceles households, and each successive generation’s offspring comes a little closer to the perfect equal-sided proportions of the Equilateral Triangle.

Genuine, officially certified Equilateral children born of two Isosceles parents are extraordinarily rare, one in tens of thousands of births. You, a Spacelander, might scoff and ask why we need a formal certificate at all—surely the birth of a Square son to a Triangle father is proof enough of regular blood? But I must tell you, no reputable lady of any standing will marry an uncertified Triangle. Sometimes a slightly irregular Triangle can sire a Square, but that hidden flaw in his sides will show up in the grandchildren: the third generation will never be able to advance to Pentagonal rank, and often slips all the way back to the lower Triangular class. A true Equilateral birth requires generations of deliberate, frugal, disciplined self-improvement from the line of ancestors before it, and slow, careful cultivation of the Isosceles intellect across hundreds of family lines.

When one of these rare True Equilaterals is born, the news spreads for miles around, and the entire region celebrates. A full panel of the Sanitary and Social Board conducts a rigorous physical inspection to confirm the infant’s sides are perfectly equal. Once certified, the child is taken in a solemn public ceremony out of his working-class parents’ home, and adopted by a childless Equilateral household, bound by sacred oath to never let the child return to his old neighborhood, or even lay eyes on his birth family ever again. The risk is too high that the newly regular shape might, by unconscious mimicry, slip back to the irregularity of his heritage.

This rare upward mobility is cherished by both the lowest serfs and the highest Aristocracy. For the poor Isosceles, it is a glowing beacon of hope that cuts through the drudgery of their daily lives, proof that advancement is not entirely out of reach. For the upper classes, it is the most reliable guard against revolution. If the masses of sharp-acute rabble had no hope at all of climbing the ranks, they might find brilliant leaders among themselves in their frequent seditious uprisings, and their overwhelming numbers and piercing sharp points would overwhelm even the greatest wisdom of the Circles. But the Law of Compensation, that most perfect of natural ordinances, sees to it that as the working classes gain intelligence, skill, and virtue, their sharp, dangerous acute angles widen to match the far less menacing 60-degree angle of the Equilateral Triangle. The dullest, most brutal soldier Triangles, who are almost as mindless as our Straight Line Women, are the ones with the sharpest, most deadly piercing points. The more intelligent they get, the less physically dangerous they become.

What a perfect, providential proof of the fitness of Flatland’s aristocratic order, is it not? Using this law, the Polygons and Circles can snuff out almost every sedition before it ever gets off the ground, leveraging the universal, unquenchable hope of advancement in every Flatlander’s chest. Art and medicine step in to support law and order too: State physicians can carefully apply controlled compression and expansion to the sides of the most intelligent rebel leaders, correcting their irregular proportions to make them fully Regular, then elevate them straight into the privileged classes. The far greater number of lower-class dissidents who fall just short of full regularisation are lured by the promise of future ennoblement to volunteer for honourable lifelong confinement in State hospitals. Only the tiny handful of obstinate, hopelessly irregular rebels who refuse all offers of reform are ever led to execution.

Left without leaders, the unorganised mass of Isosceles rabble are either cut down without resistance by the small paid force of loyal Triangles the Chief Circle keeps on hand for emergencies, or far more often, the Circular ruling class stokes petty jealousies and old grudges between different factions of the working class, stirring them to fight one another until they perish on each other’s sharp angles. Our annals record no less than 120 full-scale recorded rebellions, plus 235 smaller scattered uprisings. Not a single one has ever ended any other way.

If the sharply pointed Triangles of our Soldier class are formidable, you will readily grasp that Flatland’s Women are exponentially more dangerous. Where a Soldier is a sharp wedge, a Woman is a needle: entirely point, at both of her two extremities, as it were. Add to that her natural ability to turn effectively invisible at will, and you will immediately understand that no one trifles with a Flatland female and walks away unharmed.

Some of my younger Spaceland readers may be asking how a 2D woman could possibly make herself unseen. This should be obvious on reflection, but let me spell it out for the least attentive of you. Take a sewing needle, set it on a table, and lower your eye to the exact level of the tabletop. Look at it side-on, and you will see its entire length, clear as day. Turn it so the very tip points straight at your eye, though? All you will see is a tiny, barely noticeable dot. For all practical purposes, the needle has vanished. That is exactly how it works for our Women. When her full side is turned to us, we see her as a clear, distinct straight line. When the end that holds her shared eye-and-mouth organ faces us, she appears only as a bright, lustrous pinpoint. But when her blunt, far dimmer back end is turned toward us? That dull, almost lifeless point acts as a perfect Invisible Cap, rendering her all but undetectable to anyone who does not know she is there.

The scope of the danger we face from our Women will now be clear to even the slowest Spaceland mind. If colliding with a respectable middle-class Triangle can leave you bleeding out on the pavement; if a brush with a working man will leave you with a nasty gash; if the lightest graze from a private soldier’s sharp vertex is a death sentence – what else could running headlong into a Woman be, except instant, absolute obliteration? When she is entirely invisible, or nothing more than a faint dim speck, even the most cautious among us can never hope to avoid every accidental collision.

Across every nation of Flatland, dozens of laws have been passed over the centuries to minimize this constant peril. In the warmer southern regions where gravity pulls harder, and even stationary people are prone to sudden, involuntary lurches and movements, the laws governing Women are far harsher still. The core shared provisions of most Flatland legal codes boil down to three non-negotiable rules:
1.  Every dwelling must have a dedicated eastern-facing entrance reserved exclusively for Women, who must enter and exit through it in a slow, decorous manner – they are strictly forbidden from using the western, men-only door. [Note: When I visited Spaceland, I learned that some of your own priesthoods have enforced identical rules, with separate side entrances for villagers, farmers, and public school teachers so they may "approach in a becoming and respectful manner."]
2.  No Woman may step foot in a public space without continuously emitting a low, droning Peace-Cry, on pain of immediate execution.
3.  Any Woman formally certified to be suffering from St. Vitus’ Dance, recurrent seizures, chronic violent sneezing fits, or any other condition that causes involuntary, uncontrolled movement, must be put to death at once.

Some states add extra rules: in some, Women are forbidden from standing or walking in public unless they constantly sway their back end from side to side, broadcasting their position to anyone moving behind them. Others require every travelling Woman to be escorted at all times by one of her sons, a male servant, or her husband. A handful of the most restrictive states bar Women from leaving their homes entirely, save for a handful of public religious festivals each year. But the wisest of our ruling Circles and Statesmen have long since learned that piling more and more prohibitions on the Female population does far more harm than good: it weakens the whole population, reduces our birth rates, and stokes such rage in Women that domestic murders skyrocket, to the point a state loses far more citizens than it ever saves from accident. When Women are driven mad by being trapped inside, or hemmed in by endless arbitrary rules, they will lash out and kill every man and child within reach – and in our warmer southern climates, there are recorded cases where an entire village’s male population was wiped out in the space of two hours by a coordinated wave of female rage. That is why the three core laws outlined above are all that the best-run nations enforce, as a reasonable, balanced Female Code.

At the end of the day, our greatest protection is not legislature – it is the Women’s own self-interest. Though a Woman can stab a person to death in a single backwards flick of her sharp end, if she cannot immediately yank her needle-thin body free of the victim’s flailing corpse, her own fragile straight-line form will shatter into splinters. She knows this, and will almost never risk such a fight unless she has no other choice.

Fashion is also a powerful safeguard for us. I mentioned earlier that some less civilized states force Women to sway their back end side to side in public. This custom has been universal among all upper-class, respectable ladies in well-governed Flatland societies for as long as anyone can remember. It is seen as a deep embarrassment for a state to have to legislate a behavior that every decent Woman is supposed to practice as a natural instinct. The smooth, elegant, rhythmical undulation of the back performed by high-ranking ladies of Circular rank is envied and copied by the wives of the Equilateral working class, who can only manage a plain, monotonous back-swing as regular as a pendulum’s tick. That plain pendulum motion is in turn copied by the wives of the upwardly mobile Isosceles soldiers, whose female relatives have not yet developed even the smallest instinct for any kind of back motion at all. In every respected household, this "back-motion" is as constant and universal as the passage of time itself – and the men of these families are almost entirely immune to surprise invisible attacks.

Do not mistake me: our Women are not devoid of affection. But unfortunately, in the Frail Sex, the heat of the moment overrides every other consideration. This is an unavoidable side effect of their unfortunate body shape. Since they have no angles at all, far less sharp than even the lowest born Isosceles criminal, they are almost entirely without brain power: no capacity for reflection, no judgment, no foresight, barely any memory at all. When they fly into a rage, they forget every prior bond, every relationship, every sense of self-preservation. I personally knew of a case where a Woman murdered every single member of her entire household – and half an hour later, once her rage faded and the bodies had been swept away, she was wandering the home asking where her husband and children had got to.

It goes without saying that you never, ever irritate a Woman when she has room to turn around. But inside their own dedicated apartments, which are deliberately constructed to make it physically impossible for them to rotate their full line-body, you can say and do whatever you please. They are completely powerless to hurt you, and just a few minutes later they will not even remember the threat of murder they were screaming at you, nor the dramatic, over-the-top promises you made to calm their rage in the moment.

By and large, domestic life runs fairly smoothly, save for the lowest ranks of the military classes. There, foolish, tactless husbands rely too much on the protection of their own sharp angles, and ignore the far more effective defenses of good sense and well-timed lies. These reckless men often skip building the mandatory non-rotating walls in their wives’ apartments, or make rude, insulting jokes to their wives out in public and refuse to apologize immediately. Worse, their stubborn devotion to literal, blunt truth makes them refuse to spout the flowery, meaningless compliments that a clever Circle can use to soothe his angry consort in ten seconds flat. The result is predictable massacre. This is not entirely a bad thing, our ruling Circles argue: it culls the most violent, troublesome Isosceles men from the population, eliminating the sorts of people who might stir up revolution against the social order before they can organize.

Even in the homes of our most well-regulated, highest-ranking Circles, though, domestic life never comes close to the ideal you enjoy in Spaceland. There is peace, in the narrow sense that no one is being randomly stabbed to death – but that peace is bought at the cost of almost all domestic comfort. In every wealthy Polygonal or Circular household, it has been an unbroken custom since time immemorial that all wives and daughters must keep their eye-and-mouth end pointed constantly towards their husband and his male guests. For a noble lady to turn her back on her husband would be seen as a monstrous scandal, enough to strip her whole family of their status. But this custom that keeps them safe from stabs brings no quiet. The sharp, bright eye and the chattering mouth are always facing the man of the house, nonstop. Nothing in the world is more persistent than the unending stream of female chatter. The same tact that works perfectly well to avoid a Woman’s deadly sharp sting is useless to shut her mouth. Since Women have no sense of wit, reason, or self-restraint, they will say absolutely every trivial, random thought that pops into their heads, unfiltered. More than a few cynical men I know have openly admitted they would far rather risk the silent, invisible death of a Woman’s stab end than endure the nonstop, deafening noise of her mouth end.

To you Spaceland readers, the condition of our Women must seem truly deplorable – and you are not wrong. Even the lowest, most degraded Isosceles man can hold out hope that his angle will narrow over the generations, and his whole family can climb the caste system one day. But no Woman can ever hope for any advancement, ever. "Once a Woman, always a Woman" is an unbreakable law of nature, and the forces of evolution themselves seem to have frozen entirely to hold them in their place. But even so, we cannot help but admire the wise order of the world: since they have no hope for a better future, they also have no memory to dwell on their humiliations, and no foresight to anticipate the miseries of their existence. They do not suffer their fate the way a thinking, reasoning being would – and that, for us, is the greatest mercy of all.

You, the fortunate resident of Three-Dimensional Spaceland, blessed with both light and shadow, gifted with two eyes, a native understanding of perspective, and the full spectrum of colour to parse the world around you, you who can actually *see* an angle laid out before you, and take in the full curved circumference of a Circle all at once—how could I possibly convey the absurd, constant difficulty we Flatlanders face when trying to tell one another’s shapes apart?
Call back to what I told you earlier: every single being in our flat world, animate or inanimate, no matter how many sides or points their form has, appears to our sight as nothing more than a straight (or nearly straight) line segment. If every single thing looks like a line, how do we tell anyone apart?
We rely on three distinct methods.
The first is hearing, and our sense of sound is far more finely tuned than yours. It lets us pick out personal friends by their unique voices instantly, and even reliably distinguish between the three lowest regular classes: Equilateral Triangles, Squares, and Pentagons. We do not bother counting the lowly Isosceles Triangles as a class for this purpose. But as you move up the social ladder, telling voices apart gets far harder: higher ranks deliberately cultivate nearly identical, uniform tones, and the ability to identify people by voice alone is written off as a coarse, plebeian skill that the Aristocracy never bothers to hone. Worst of all, low-class Isosceles Triangles have far more developed vocal organs than their sense of hearing, and with a little practice, they can mimic the voice of any Polygon, even a Circle himself, with perfect accuracy. That means we can never fully trust hearing alone, especially if there is any risk of imposture.
Which is why a second method is far more widely used: *Feeling*. For all Women and the vast majority of our lower and middle classes, feeling is the default way to identify strangers, to confirm someone’s class, the exact equivalent of what you call an “introduction” in Spaceland.
In remote rural districts, old-fashioned country gentlemen still use the full, formal introduction formula: “Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by my friend Mr. So-and-so.” But in towns and among business folk, the “be felt by” clause is dropped, and the line is shortened to “Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so,” with everyone taking for granted that the feeling will be reciprocal. Even trendier, brash young upper-class men, who hate wasting even a second of effort and care nothing for the purity of our native tongue, have further mangled the phrase into slang that uses “to feel” as a transitive verb meaning “to formally recommend two people to verify each other’s shapes by touch.” Right now, the fast set of upper class polite society thinks nothing of saying the absurd, barbaric line: “Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones.”
Do not make the mistake of assuming feeling is a slow, tedious chore, as it would be for you. We do not have to run our fingers along every single side of a stranger to tell what they are. The skill is drilled into us in elementary school, refined over a lifetime of daily use, and we can instantly distinguish by touch the angles of an equal-sided Triangle, Square, and Pentagon. Even the dullest touch can pick out the sharp, tiny vertex of an acute Isosceles Triangle. As a rule, we only need to brush a single one of your points to confirm exactly what class you belong to—unless you are part of the highest echelons of the nobility. There, the difficulty spikes sharply. Even a Master of Arts from our prestigious Wentbridge University has been documented mixing up a ten-sided Polygon and a twelve-sided one, and barely any Doctor of Science in the entire kingdom can claim to tell a twenty-sided and twenty-four-sided member of the Aristocracy apart instantly, no hesitation.
If you recall the laws I shared earlier regarding Women, you will immediately see why introductions via feeling require strict care and discretion. An unexpected angle could easily inflict irreparable harm on the person doing the feeling. It is absolutely mandatory for safety that the person being felt stands perfectly, completely still. A sudden jolt, a fidgety shift of position, even a violent sneeze, has been known to kill incautious feelers out of hand, nipping a perfectly promising new friendship in the bud. This risk is most extreme with the lowest class of Triangles: their eyes sit far up near their wide, blunt base, so they can barely tell what is happening at the far end of their sharp vertex. They are also rough, coarse, and far less sensitive to the light, delicate touch of a highly organized Polygon. Is it any wonder an involuntary toss of the head has more than once robbed the State of a valued, productive life?
My own venerable grandfather, one of the least irregular members of his unfortunate Isosceles class, who won four out of seven votes from the Sanitary and Social Board right before he died to finally be promoted to the ranks of the Equilateral, often cried as he told the story of his great-great-great-grandfather: a respectable working man with a vertex angle of 59 degrees 30 minutes. That unfortunate ancestor, suffering from rheumatism, jolted by accident while a high-rank Polygon was feeling his angle, and skewered the nobleman right through his diagonal. The accident led to my ancestor’s long imprisonment and formal degradation, and the entire family was hit with a full 1.5 degree setback on their slow climb up to regular status. The next generation’s family vertex angle was formally registered at only 58 degrees, and it took five full generations to recover the lost ground, hit the full 60 degree mark, and finally escape Isosceles serfdom. All that cascading disaster from one tiny, clumsy misstep during a routine feeling.
I can already hear well-educated Spaceland readers protesting: “How do you people even know what an angle is, let alone measure them down to the minute? We can see an angle, because we in the third dimension can look at two straight lines slanted relative to each other at once. But you can never see more than one straight line at a time, or at most a bunch of line segments stacked flat into one single straight line. How could you ever tell angles apart, let alone measure them so precisely?”
My answer is simple: we do not *see* angles, we *infer* them, and with a level of precision your naked 3D sight could never match, without the aid of a protractor. Our sense of touch, sharpened by centuries of absolute necessity and refined by lifelong training, lets us distinguish tiny differences in the slant of two lines meeting far better than your eyes can.
Nor can I leave out the natural helps Nature provides us. It is a fundamental law of our Flatland that the vertex of the lowest Isosceles starts at half a degree (thirty minutes), and if the family advances at all, that angle grows by half a degree a generation, until it hits 60 degrees, the point where they leave serfdom behind and become full free Regular citizens. That means Nature herself gives us a built-in ascending scale of angles from half a degree all the way up to 60, and we place dozens of these specimens in every elementary school across the country. Thanks to occasional generational backsliding, stagnant moral and intellectual growth, and the extraordinary high birth rate of our Criminal and Vagabond classes, we always have a massive surplus of half-degree and one-degree individuals, and plenty more specimens all the way up to 10 degrees. These unfortunates have zero civic rights, and far too many lack the basic intelligence to even serve as soldiers in our wars, so the State assigns them to the education system. They are chained completely immobile, to eliminate any risk of them stabbing a child, and placed in infant school classrooms to train the touch sensitivity of middle class children, the very intelligence the specimens themselves will never be capable of.
In some lenient states, these specimens are fed, and allowed to live for several years. But in our more sensible, well-regulated regions, the education boards long ago realized it is far better to never feed them, and replace the entire stock of specimens once a month — that is the average lifespan of a criminal Isosceles without food anyway. The cheap, low-budget schools claim they save money by keeping specimens alive longer, but they lose far more on feeding costs, and the angles of the specimens wear down and become inaccurate after weeks of thousands of children touching them over and over. That “expensive” monthly replacement system also has a quiet, massive benefit: it slowly but steadily reduces the surplus of the low Isosceles population, a goal every Flatland statesman works toward constantly. Though I am aware many popularly elected school boards push for the so-called “cheap system,” I firmly believe this is one of many cases where spending a little more up front is the truest form of economy.
I will not get dragged into school board politics any longer. I think I have said enough to prove that recognition by feeling is nowhere near as tedious or unreliable as you might have assumed, and it is far more trustworthy than recognition by voice alone. But as we have already established, the method carries unavoidable, deadly risk. That is why almost all of the middle and lower classes, and every single member of the Polygonal and Circular aristocracy, prefer a third, far safer method of recognizing one another — which I will describe for you in the next section.

I fear I must seem wildly inconsistent at first glance. Earlier, I told you every being in Flatland appears to the eye as nothing but a straight line, and all but stated it was impossible to tell different social classes apart by sight alone. Yet here I am, about to explain to my Spaceland readers how the educated among us do exactly that: recognize friends, family, and strangers entirely by what our eyes perceive.

If you flip back to the passage where I described Recognition by Feeling as universal, you’ll spot the small qualifier I tucked in: *among the lower classes*. Sight recognition is a skill exclusive to the upper castes, and only reliably used in our temperate zones, where the atmospheric condition that makes it possible is common.

That condition is fog. In Spaceland, you curse it as an unmitigated nuisance: it blurs your views of rolling hills and forests, drags down your mood, even makes you sick. For us, it is a blessing almost as precious as the air we breathe, the cradle of our arts and the parent of all our sciences. Let me explain, no more flowery praise needed.
If there was no fog at all, every line would look equally sharp, no matter how far away it stood. That is precisely the miserable reality of the dry, torrid zones of Flatland, where no one can tell two figures apart by sight at all. But where fog lingers thick for most of the year, an object three feet away is noticeably dimmer than one 35 inches away. With years of careful, practiced observation of these tiny shifts in brightness and dimness, we can deduce the exact shape of the figure before us with shocking precision.

A simple example will make this clearer than a hundred pages of abstract explanation. Suppose two strangers are walking toward me, and I need to work out their rank. Let’s say one is an Equilateral Triangle (a Merchant, by law) and the other is a regular Pentagon (a Physician). How do I tell them apart by sight?
Imagine I tilt my eye until my line of sight cuts straight through the foremost angle of the approaching stranger, so my gaze falls evenly on the two sides on either side of that angle. Both sides will appear equally long, and I can compare their fade into the fog without bias.
Take the Merchant Triangle first. The line I see before me will have a bright, vivid dead center— that’s the point of the angle, closest to my eye. But the two sides recede *very sharply* away from me into the fog, so the brightness falls off fast. The far ends of the straight line I perceive will be so dim they are almost barely visible.
Now look at the Physician, the Pentagon. I still see a straight line with a bright central point, that nearest leading angle. But the sides extending left and right of that angle recede far more gradually than the Triangle’s did. Brightness fades slowly, so the far ends of the perceived line stay far brighter than the Merchant’s endpoints ever were.

That is the core logic. With decades of practice, the upper classes can reliably tell apart middle and low rank figures at a glance. If my Spaceland readers can grasp even that basic idea, and not write my whole account off as impossible nonsense, I have achieved all I can reasonably ask for. To pile on more tiny technical details would only confuse you.
But I should warn the younger, inexperienced readers who might think sight recognition is trivial, after my two simple examples of telling my triangular father apart from my hexagonal sons, that real life use of this skill is far more complicated. For instance: if my father, the Triangle, happens to turn to present his full side to me instead of his sharp leading angle, I will be stuck staring at a perfectly straight, uniformly bright line, and I will not be able to tell at first glance if he is a Triangle at all— he could easily be a thin Straight Line, AKA a Woman, until I ask him to rotate a little, or shift my own position to peer around him. Even my own young hexagonal grandson, if he faces me dead-on with one of his six sides fully forward, will look to my eye like one long bright line that barely fades at the ends, with two short, dim lines extending off either end that fade to black fast at their tips.

Any Spaceland schoolboy who has gotten past basic geometry will understand what I mean: for a well-educated Flatlander, navigating a crowded formal ballroom full of high-rank Polygons all whirling, moving forward, stepping back, and turning in every direction is an intellectual workout so rigorous that it more than justifies the full, lavish funding we give to Wentbridge University’s chairs of Static and Kinetic Geometry. Sight Recognition is taught there as a formal, rigorous art to the cream of Flatland’s elite. Only the wealthiest, most privileged noble families can afford the time and money to let their children spend years mastering it. Even I, a respected mathematician and grandfather to two perfect regular Hexagons, get confused sometimes when I find myself in a swirling crowd of high-caste Polygons. To a common Tradesman or Serf? That same scene is total gibberish, just as if you Spaceland readers were suddenly dropped straight into Flatland and surrounded by our most elegant high society gathering.
You would see nothing but straight lines all around you, their brightness flickering and shifting randomly nonstop. Even if you aced all three years of university theory classes on sight recognition, you would still need years of real world practice to move through that crowd without accidentally bumping into a noble of higher rank. Etiquette forbids you from asking them to let you “feel” their sides to confirm their identity, of course—they, with their years of training, can track every one of your movements perfectly, while you are blind to theirs. To move through high society perfectly politely, the old bitter saying goes, you must at least be a Polygon yourself. My own experience bears that out entirely.

The skill, almost an instinct, of sight recognition sharpens dramatically if you practice it every day and never rely on feeling. It is exactly like your Spaceland experience with deaf and mute people: if you let them rely on hand signs and finger alphabets, they will never learn the far more valuable, far harder skill of lip reading and speech. For us, any child who picks up the habit of feeling others will never master sight recognition perfectly.
That is why feeling is heavily discouraged, or banned entirely, in upper class households. Our children never go to the public elementary schools where feeling is the main skill taught. Instead they attend exclusive, elite seminaries, and at Wentbridge University, being caught feeling another person is a major disciplinary offense: first offense gets you suspended, second gets you expelled entirely.

For the poor working class, sight recognition is an unaffordable luxury. A tradesman cannot let his son waste a third of his life studying abstract geometry. Poor children are allowed to learn feeling from their earliest years, which makes them bright, sharp, and lively far earlier than the half-trained upper caste teenagers still slogging through their university courses. But once those Polygon youths finally graduate and put their years of theory to use, they are reborn, almost, and they rapidly outpace their Triangular peers in every art, science, and professional field.

A tiny minority of Polygon students never pass their final leaving exam at the University. Their fate is pitiable indeed. Rejected as unfit for the upper ranks, they are also scorned by the working classes. They have none of the polished, trained skill of their graduating peers, and none of the quick, mercurial wit of the tradesmen who learned feeling as toddlers. All professional careers and public office are closed to them. Most states do not formally ban them from marriage, but they almost never find a suitable partner, for experience tells us their children are almost always unfortunate, often Irregular in shape.
These failed ex-students, the dregs of the nobility, have been the leaders of nearly every great riot and sedition in our past history. The harm they cause is so terrible that a growing faction of progressive statesmen now argue that true mercy demands we eradicate them entirely: any student who fails the final university exam should either spend the rest of their life in prison, or be painlessly executed.

But I find I have wandered off into the topic of Irregular shapes— a subject of such overwhelming importance that it deserves its own full section.

Up to this point in my account of Flatland, I have been taking for granted a truth that ought perhaps to have been stated as our world’s most fundamental, unnegotiable law: every living human being in Flatland is a Regular Figure, built to exact, symmetrical standards.

To be precise, this means a Woman is not just a line segment, but a perfectly straight line. An Artisan or Soldier will always have two equal sides. A Tradesman, our lower-middle-class Triangles, have all three sides of equal length. Lawyers (of which quiet, unassuming class I am a proud member) have four perfectly matched sides, and so on up the social order: every Polygon, no matter how many sides he claims, will have every edge identical in length. Raw side length of course varies with age: a newborn female is barely an inch long from tip to tip, while a tall, full-grown adult woman can stretch to a full foot. For all male classes, a rough, widely accepted rule of thumb holds that the total sum of an adult’s sides adds up to roughly two feet, perhaps a hair more. But raw size is irrelevant to our discussion here. It is the *equality* of sides that matters, and it takes very little reflection to see that every thread of Flatland’s social order is woven around this unshakable fact, written directly into the will of Nature herself.

If our sides were unequal, our angles would inevitably grow crooked. Suddenly, it would no longer be enough to feel a single angle, or catch a quick blurring glimpse of one shape edge by our Sight Recognition art, to confirm exactly who you are standing next to. You would have to trace every single edge, measure every last angle one by one, just to confirm the full shape of a stranger. No one lives long enough to waste that much time on every casual interaction. Our entire, hard-won science of Sight Recognition would perish overnight. The ancient, carefully cultivated art of Feeling, learned by every child through years of gentle practice, would not survive it by much longer. All social intercourse would become perilous, even impossible. No one would trust their neighbors, no one could plan even the most trivial daily errand, and every trace of civilization would collapse straight back into barbarism.

I do not imagine my readers in Spaceland need much convincing to see how unavoidable these conclusions are. One small example from daily life will be enough. Suppose you are walking down your street, spot two familiar Tradesmen Triangles, glance once at their sharp points and fast-fading far edges to confirm their identity, and invite them back to your home for lunch. Right now, you can do that with perfect confidence: every adult Triangle only ever occupies a predictable, easily estimated couple square inches of space. But imagine that same respectable, sharp Tradesman vertex is dragging behind it a lumpy, unnameable shape with a twelve or thirteen inch diagonal? What do you do when that monster wedges itself immovably in your front door?

I do not mean to insult your intelligence by piling on more obvious details. Already, navigating a crowded public market without accidental collisions stretches the sagacity of even the most well-educated Square. If no one could trust that a single person in the crowd was regular, predictable, shape-stable? Total chaos would reign. The smallest, most trivial panic would leave dozens of people with serious stab wounds, and if any sharp-edged Women or pointy Soldier-class Triangles happened to be in the throng, you could see mass loss of life in seconds.

Practical expediency therefore lines up perfectly with Nature’s design to demand perfect regularity of form, and our Law has never hesitated to enforce that shared mandate. In Flatland, “Irregularity of Figure” is considered a worse failing, a graver threat to the public good, than any combination of moral depravity and outright criminality that you Spacelanders can imagine.

There is, admittedly, a loud camp of paradox-spouting radicals who argue there is no inherent link between geometric lopsidedness and moral corruption. “The Irregular child,” they protest, “is shunned at birth by his own parents, mocked by his siblings, ignored by domestic staff, treated with universal suspicion by all of society, and barred from every position of trust, responsibility, or meaningful work. The police dog his every movement until he comes of age for his official state inspection. If his deviation from the regular standard is too extreme, he is summarily destroyed. The ones just under the allowed margin of error are locked away in menial seventh-class government clerk roles, forbidden to marry, forced to drudge at meaningless work for a starvation stipend, required to live and eat on the office premises, and even forced to take their vacations under constant surveillance. Is it any wonder that even the kindest, purest of natures would turn bitter, warped, and malicious under such treatment?”

This seemingly reasonable line of reasoning has never convinced me, nor has it swayed the wisest of our Statesmen. Our ancestors were entirely correct to lay down the unshakable policy axiom that any toleration of Irregularity is fundamentally incompatible with the safety of the State. The life of an Irregular is undeniably harsh, but the interests of the vast majority require that harshness. Think: if some creature with a perfectly sharp triangular front and a lumpy, uneven multi-sided back was allowed to live, and to produce children even more misshapen than himself? What would become of every art and custom that makes our life livable? Should we reshape every house door, every shop entrance, every church archway to accommodate these monsters? Would our theater ticket collectors be required to measure the full perimeter of every patron before allowing them to take a seat? Should Irregulars be exempted from militia service? And if we do draft them, how do we stop them from weaving through the ranks, tearing and stabbing through their comrades by accident or design? Think of the irresistible temptation to fraud such a shape offers: it would be trivial for an Irregular to walk into a shop facing the cashier with a broad, perfectly regular multi-sided front, pass himself off as a high-ranking Polygon, and order a cartload of expensive goods before anyone thinks to circle around and see the grotesque lopsided mess of his back.

Let the advocates of this false, short-sighted “philanthropy” scream as loud as they like for the full repeal of our Irregular penal laws. I have never in my life met an Irregular Figure who was not exactly what Nature designed him to be: a hypocrite, a misanthrope, and eager to perpetrate every manner of mischief his warped form allows.

That said, I do not endorse the extreme measures some of our more ruthless states enforce, where any infant whose angles deviate as little as half a degree from perfect regularity is destroyed immediately at birth. Some of our nation’s most brilliant, highest-achieving geniuses began their lives with deviations as great as three quarters of a degree, and destroying them at birth would have been an irreparable national loss. Our Healing Arts have scored some of their most glorious triumphs in carefully calibrated compression therapies, gentle shape stretching, surgical trepanning, bone binding, and specialized dietary regimens that can fully or partially correct even severe Irregularity in very young children.

I advocate therefore for a clear via media, a middle path. No arbitrary, unforgiving hard line of demarcation that dooms slightly lopsided gifted children to death. But once a child’s shape has fully set, and the official Medical Board has unanimously confirmed that no further therapy or surgery can correct his deviation from the regular standard, I submit that the merciful, painless disposal of that Irregular offspring is the only responsible choice for the safety of all Flatland.

If you’ve kept pace with my accounts of life in two-dimensional Flatland, you won’t be shocked to hear that day-to-day existence can be gratingly dull on the aesthetic front. Don’t get me wrong—we have our fair share of clashing border skirmishes, shadowy political conspiracies, rioting street factions, and all the chaotic spectacle that fills the pages of your Spaceland history books. Even better, our lives are stitched through with a weird, thrilling blend of everyday practical problems and mathematical puzzles that let us test wild guesses against hard, provable fact moments after we make them—a rush you three-dimensional folks can barely imagine. But strip all that away, and when you look at our world through an artistic, creative lens? It’s unbearably, painfully plain.
How could it be anything else? Every landscape, every historical mural, every portrait, every blooming wildflower, every still life of household goods—all of it unfurls as nothing more than a single straight line, the only variation between them being different degrees of light and shadow.

But this flat, monochrome drudgery wasn’t our lot forever. Old, unbroken tradition tells us that for six full centuries and more, color spilled bright, transient splendor across the lives of our most distant ancestors. The first person to unlock that magic was a private citizen, a Pentagon whose name gets muddled in different surviving texts—he stumbled on the formula for simple pigments and a rough, workable painting method by total accident, so the story goes. First he daubed color on the walls of his house, then on the edges of his domestic servants, then on his elderly father, his sons, his grandsons, before finally painting his own edges.
The results were every bit as practical as they were beautiful, and the whole country fell in love with the idea almost overnight. The most reliable historical records all agree on his name: Chromatistes. Wherever his newly multicolored polygon frame drifted, every eye turned to him, brimming with instant respect. No one had to reach out and feel him to know what he was, no one ever mixed up his front edge with his back edge, his neighbors could track every movement he made without expending a single scrap of mental math effort. No one jostled him in the street, everyone automatically stepped aside to let him pass. Even his voice got a break from the exhausting, constant work that us modern colorless Squares and Pentagons still have to do, shouting our unique shapes out loud every time we push through a crowd of uneducated, sharp-angled Isosceles workers to prove who we are.

The trend spread faster than wildfire. Before the week was out, every Square and Triangle in Chromatistes’ home district was copying his example, only a handful of deeply traditional, stuffy old Pentagons refusing to touch paint. A month or two later, even the sleek 12-sided Dodecagon nobles had caught the innovation bug. Before a full year rolled around, the custom had spread to every class in Flatland except the absolute highest tier of the aristocracy. It didn’t take long for the fad to leak past the borders of Chromatistes’ home district, either. Within two full generations, every single resident of Flatland wore color on their edges—every single one, that is, except the Women and the Priests.

Nature herself seemed to throw up a hard barrier here, begging the population not to extend the new paint custom to these two specific groups. The folks pushing the color revolution had latched onto a convenient sophism: “Nature designed distinct sides to correspond to distinct colors.” That catchphrase spread from mouth to mouth faster than gossip, converting entire towns to the new colorful way of life in days. But for our Priests and our Women, that rule didn’t hold water. Our Women are straight lines—so to be pedantic, plural, formal about it, they have zero sides at all. The Priests, meanwhile, are supposed to be perfect Circles, not just very high-sided polygons with infinitesimally tiny edges. They love to brag (a point our Women openly admit and bemoan) that they also have no sides at all: their whole shape is just one single smooth perimeter, one unbroken curved circumference.
So neither of these two classes could see any logic in that so-called universal axiom about distinct sides demanding distinct colors. When every other group in the realm had caved to the thrill of decorating their own bodies, the Priests and the Women alone stayed completely free of paint’s supposed “pollution.”

Call that era immoral, licentious, anarchic, unscientific—use every harsh label you want. But from an artistic, aesthetic point of view, the days of the Great Colour Revolt were the glorious, giddy childhood of all art in Flatland. A childhood, sad to say, that never grew to maturity, never even bloomed into adolescence. Back then, just being alive was a delight, because living meant you could instantly see who and what everyone around you was. Even a small dinner party was a feast for the eyes. The riot of rich, varied hues at a packed church service or public theatre was so distracting, we have records of our greatest orators and actors stumbling over their lines mid-speech, too busy staring at the crowd. But nothing, the old tales say, ever matched the unspeakable, staggering magnificence of a full military review.
Imagine a 20,000-strong line of Isosceles soldiers, all turning in perfect unison at once, swapping the flat, inky black of their wide bases for the bright orange and purple of the two sharp sides that met at their tiny acute angle. Picture the Equilateral Triangle militia, decked out in bold tricolor red, white, and blue. Imagine the Square artillerymen, their edges painted mauve, ultramarine, gamboge, and burnt umber, spinning fast beside their vermilion-colored cannons. Then there’s the flash and dash of five-colored Pentagons and six-colored Hexagons, their edges streaked with bright distinct hues, careering across the parade field in their roles as field surgeons, battle geometricians, and aides-de-camp.
It’s no wonder the famous old legend feels so plausible: the story of a high-ranking, illustrious Circle commander, so overcome by the sheer artistic beauty of the forces under his command, that he tossed his marshal’s baton and his royal crown aside, shouting that he was trading both forever for an artist’s paintbrush. The sensuous, creative richness of that long-lost era still echoes down to us today, in our very language and vocabulary. The most throwaway lines of the most common working citizen in the Colour Revolt era were steeped in richer, more vivid wordplay and imagery, and to that far-off golden age we still owe all our finest surviving poetry, and every scrap of beautiful rhythm that lingers even in the dry, precise scientific writing of our modern day.

Even as the novelty of two-tone and multi-hued body painting spread across Flatland, the core intellectual arts that had upheld our society for millennia began to rot from within.

Sight Recognition, that sacred skill that let us parse hundreds of overlapping shapes through subtle shifts of mist and edge softness without touching a soul, was no longer practiced by anyone who did not have a personal stake in preserving the old order. Geometry, statics, kinetics, and all the hard won sciences we had studied for centuries were soon written off as useless, frivolous relics, dismissed with sneers even in the halls of our most prestigious universities. The humble foundational art of Feeling, taught to every isosceles and square in elementary school to help them learn their own shape and the bounds of their neighbors, suffered the exact same fate, dropped from curriculums entirely within a single generation.

The Isosceles classes wasted no time exploiting this decay. They declared that the old annual tribute of labor and recruits from the criminal lower ranks to fund public education was no longer needed, and refused to pay it. Freed from that ancient burden that had once both tamed their brutal, sharp-edged natures and kept their overgrown population in check, their numbers swelled, and their insolence grew bolder by the day. Month by month, rank-and-file soldier triangles and artisan triangles grew louder in their demands — and their claims grew truer, too. What difference was there, they shouted, between a ragged three-sided workman and a twelve-sided noble polygon, when color coding let anyone of any intelligence tell one class from another at a glance, no complex math or careful visual parsing required?

They did not stop at merely letting the old skills fade. Boldly, they demanded the state ban all "aristocratic monopolist arts" entirely, and defund every endowment that supported the study of Sight Recognition, mathematics, and Feeling. Soon their demands escalated further: color, which had become a second nature for Flatlanders, had erased the old need for rigid class distinctions, so the law ought to follow suit. All people, of every rank, should be recognized as completely equal, with identical rights.

When the highest noble orders hesitated, uncertain how to push back, the leaders of the growing Chromatic Revolution went one step further. They demanded that every last class, Priests and Women included, must submit to being painted in official hues, to show their respect for the new universal order.

When critics objected that Women were mere straight lines, with no sides to paint at all, the revolutionaries had a ready retort: Nature herself dictates that every living Flatlander's front half — the side that holds the eye and mouth — must be distinct from the inert blind back half, and expediency only reinforced that rule. They brought a formal bill before an extraordinary all-states assembly of Flatland, laying out the rules: every Woman would have the half of her line that held her eye and mouth dyed bright red, and the opposite half dyed vivid green. The Priests, the perfect circular ruling class, would follow the exact same pattern: the semicircle centered on their mouth and eye would be red, the rear semicircle green.

There was devilish cunning to this proposal — no crude, sharp-edged Isosceles could ever have conceived it. It was the work of an Irregular Circle, a misshapen near-perfect shape that foolish elders had spared from culling in infancy, allowing him to grow into a force that would unleash untold ruin on our nation.

First, the bill was engineered to win every last Woman in Flatland over to the Chromatic cause. By assigning the exact same two red-and-green hues to Women that they assigned to the Priests, the revolutionaries guaranteed that from certain common viewing angles, any Woman would be indistinguishable from a member of the sacred ruling class, and be treated with all the deference and awe reserved for the Circles. It is easy to see why the entire Female sex threw their support behind the bill at once.

If you have not realized the trick yet, it is simple to demonstrate. Take a Woman painted per the new code: eye half red, back half green. Look at her edge-on, head on to her thin line body, and what you see is a straight clean line, exactly half red and exactly half green. Now take a Priest, a full Circle, painted so his front semicircle is red and his rear semicircle is green. If you line up your gaze exactly level with the dividing diameter that splits his red half from his green half, all you will see is a straight line, exactly half red and exactly half green.

Bear in mind that by this point the old skill of Sight Recognition was already rotting away. Women quickly learned to file and soften the two ends of their straight line bodies to blur them, so their line would taper exactly the same way a Circle's thin edge-on profile does. The slight difference in total line length between a tall Woman and a priest would be completely invisible to anyone who had forgotten how to parse subtle shape differences, distracted as they would be by the identical red-green color split.

The Women of Flatland could imagine all the joys that awaited them: they could linger in drawing rooms and hear state and church secrets their husbands would never have dreamed of sharing with a common wife, then march down the street and issue orders to shopkeepers and laborers in the full mistaken belief that they were speaking to a Priest. Any disrespect shown to them would be treated as blasphemy against the ruling class. They did not spare a single thought for the scandal that would tear the state apart when a frivolous young Woman's reckless antics were blamed on the Priestly Order, shattering the public's respect for the entire hierarchy. Even the wives of the highest Circles begged their noble husbands to support the new bill.

That was the second, darker purpose of the cunning Irregular Circle who had drafted the law. For all the rest of Flatland had abandoned intellectual rigor, the Circles still held fast to their old sharpness. Their homes had remained entirely free of color for generations, and their children were trained from infancy to practice Sight Recognition, honing their intellects from the day they could first see. The bill was designed to drag them down to the level of the rest of the population in one single blow. Forcing the Circles to paint themselves would pollute their once-colorless homes, stripping their children of the quiet, uncolored environment they needed to practice the sacred art of sight. Soon, every circular parent and child would be colored, and the only intellectual problems the young noble children would face from infancy would be trying to tell their red-and-green painted mother apart from their red-and-green painted father — a puzzle their mischievous mothers would constantly cheat and mislead them on, eroding their faith in clear logical deduction bit by bit.

Slowly but surely, the intellectual glory of the Priestly Order would fade. And once that last line of defense was gone, there would be nothing left to stop the mob from tearing down the old aristocratic order entirely, and submerging all of Flatland in total, chaotic equality.

For three long, roiling years, the movement to pass the so-called Universal Colour Bill tore Flatland apart, and right up to its final hours, it looked every inch as if anarchy would win, erasing every last line of the geometric hierarchy that had kept our 2D civilization stable for millennia.
A whole volunteer army of Polygons, marching off as common foot soldiers to hold the line, was completely wiped out by a far more numerous horde of Isosceles Triangles, while the Squares and Pentagons stood by neutral, too unsure of the outcome to pick a side. Worse still, some of our wisest, highest-ranking Circles fell not to enemy blades, but domestic fury. Many noble wives radicalized by pro-Colour propaganda hounded their aristocrat husbands day and night to abandon their opposition to the Bill, and when their pleas failed, more than a few turned on their spouses and their own children, slaughtering the whole household before perishing in the bloodshed. Official records note no fewer than 23 Circles lost their lives to this domestic strife over the three years of unrest.
The peril could not have been greater. The priestly ruling class of Circles seemed to have no two choices but total surrender or total extermination – until the tide shifted entirely, thanks to one of those dramatic, absurd little events that all competent statesmen learn to anticipate, even engineer, for the wildly outsized power they hold to tug at the general populace’s heartstrings.
It all started with a low-grade Isosceles Triangle, his total internal angles barely scraping four degrees, who had looted a tradesman’s shop and dabbled in his vats of colored pigment. Some say he painted himself on a lark, others say he paid a disgruntled artisan to do the job, but the end result was the same: he covered every edge of his shape in the full twelve distinct hues reserved for a Dodecagon, the highest-ranking non-Circle class. He wandered to the central market, disguised his rough, jagged voice, and approached a young orphaned maiden, the daughter of a noble Polygon who had rejected his clumsy advances years before. Through a string of lucky coincidences too tedious to recount, and aided by the impossibly lax caution of the girl’s remaining relatives, he pulled off the entire ruse and married her. The moment the poor girl discovered the fraud, she took her own life.
When news of this tragedy spread across every state in Flatland, the entire female population was thrown into a frenzy. Sympathy for the dead girl, and sharp, cold fear that the exact same trick could be pulled on their sisters, their daughters, or themselves, flipped public sentiment against the Colour Bill overnight. Many women openly renounced their prior support of the sedition; the rest needed only a tiny nudge to follow suit.
The Circles seized this golden opportunity at once, calling an extraordinary national assembly. Alongside their usual guard of hardened convict-class Isosceles, they made sure to invite thousands of newly converted anti-Colour women to attend. When the Chief Circle of the era, a legendary ruler named Pantocyclus, stepped up to speak, he was met with the jeers and hisses of a hundred and twenty thousand pro-Bill Isosceles rioters. He called for silence, though, and dropped a bombshell: the Circles would concede. They would give the majority exactly what they wanted, and formally accept the Universal Colour Bill.
The jeers turned to thunderous cheers instantly. Pantocyclus invited Chromatistes, the firebrand leader of the Chromatic Sedition, to step to the center of the hall, to accept the formal surrender of the entire priestly hierarchy on behalf of his followers.
Then came a speech, a masterwork of rhetoric that ran for nearly a full day, too dense and layered for any short summary to do justice. With a perfectly measured air of impartiality, Pantocyclus told the crowd that if they were going to commit to such a radical reform, they owed it to themselves to take one last, full look at the whole perimeter of the issue – its flaws, as much as its supposed benefits. Gradually, he laid out all the hidden dangers the Bill would pose to tradesmen, professional classes, and gentlefolk alike, and when a murmur of discontent rippled through the Isosceles ranks, he merely reminded them that despite all those flaws, he was still fully willing to sign off on the Bill if the majority voted for it. It was plain to see, though, that every single class except the low Isosceles had been swayed, and now stood against or at least neutral to the proposed law.
He turned next to the Artisan class. Their interests would not be overlooked, he told them, and if they chose to back the Colour Bill, they had to understand exactly what they were signing up for. Many of the workmen were on the cusp of earning promotion to the ranks of the regular Equilateral Triangles; many more held hopes of their children climbing even higher, to Squares or Pentagons. That noble, long-held ambition would be completely erased the moment universal color was adopted. All distinctions between ranks would vanish: regular shapes would be indistinguishable from irregular ones, social progress would reverse into degradation, and within a few generations the entire working class would be dragged down to the level of the military, or even the convict class. Political power would end up in the hands of the largest group, the criminal classes – who already outnumbered the workmen, and would soon outnumber every other class combined, once the natural compensative laws of Flatland’s social order were shattered.
A soft murmur of agreement rippled through the assembled artisans. Chromatistes, panicking, tried to push forward to rally his followers – only to find himself surrounded by guards, forced back into silence. Pantocyclus rounded out his speech with a fiery final appeal to all the women in the hall: if the Colour Bill passed, no marriage would ever be safe again, no woman’s honor would ever be secure. Fraud, deception, and lies would creep into every home, domestic bliss would collapse right alongside Flatland’s constitution, and all of it would vanish into ruin. “Rather than that fate,” he roared, “let us choose death.”
Those words were the prearranged signal. The convict-class Isosceles guards surged forward, driving their sharp angles straight through Chromatistes before he could scream. The regular upper classes split their ranks wide open, clearing a path for the cohort of anti-Colour women, who moved backwards, unseen by the rioting Isosceles, gliding unerringly toward the unsuspecting pro-sedition crowd. The artisans, taking their cue, split their own ranks too. Teams of convict sentries sealed every single entrance to the assembly hall, forming an impenetrable phalanx.
The battle, or more accurately the one-sided carnage, lasted barely an hour. Under the Circles’ expert command, every single charge made by the women was fatal, and most of them withdrew their sharp edges unharmed, ready to strike again. No second strike was needed, though. The mob of disorganized, leaderless Isosceles rioters destroyed themselves. Attacked from the front by invisible foes, and finding every exit blocked by the convicts at their backs, they panicked the way Isosceles always do when caught off guard, screaming “treachery!” That cry sealed their fate. Every single one of them suddenly saw a hidden enemy in every other Isosceles around them, and they turned on each other. In half an hour, not a single one of the seventy thousand rioters was left alive. The scattered fragments of their angles, left strewn across the hall, stood as proof that Order had triumphed over sedition.
The Circles did not waste a moment pushing their victory to its full extent. They spared the working classes, but culled their ranks to restore the proper class balance. The full Equilateral Triangle militia was called out immediately, and every Triangle reasonably suspected of having an irregular shape was executed by court martial, no tedious formal measurement by the Social Board required. For over a year, inspectors visited every home in the military and artisan classes, sweeping every town, village, and tiny hamlet across Flatland to purge the excess of lower-class citizens that had built up after decades of neglecting the legal requirement to send surplus criminals to serve as labor for the schools and university, and violating the other natural laws that kept Flatland’s geometric hierarchy balanced. Order, once more, was restored.
It goes without saying that the use of color was banned entirely after that, and owning any pigment was a criminal offense. Even saying any word that referred to color, unless you were a Circle or a specially qualified scientific instructor, carried a harsh penalty. It is only rumored that in the most secret, esoteric upper-level classes at our national University, which I myself have never been privileged to attend, small amounts of color are still permitted for use demonstrating the most complex, high-level problems in higher mathematics. I speak of that only from hearsay, though.
Everywhere else in Flatland, color no longer exists. The secret of how to make color is known to only one living person at a time: the reigning Chief Circle, who passes the secret to his successor only on his deathbed. There is only one single manufactory that produces the pigment, and to make sure the secret never leaks out, every last worker employed there is put to death at the end of each year, and replaced with a new set of staff. Such is the great, lingering terror our ruling Aristocracy still feels, even centuries later, for the long-ago days of the Universal Colour Bill agitation.

It is high time I set aside these scattered, casual notes on daily life in Flatland, to turn to the central event of this whole book: my initiation into the mind-bending mysteries of Space. That is the story I was born to tell, and every word that came before it is nothing more than preface.

That is why I must pass over a hundred small oddities of our 2D existence that I flatter myself would fascinate you: the way we propel ourselves to move, and grind to a full stop, with no feet to speak of; the trick that lets us anchor structures of wood, stone, or brick firmly in place, even though we have no hands, cannot dig deep foundations as you do in Spaceland, nor leverage the sideways weight of earth to hold our constructions steady; the quirk of our atmosphere that makes rain fall only in the gaps between our north-south zones, so the northernmost regions never hoard all the moisture and leave the south high and dry. I will not linger on the shape of our hills and mines, our trees and crop cycles, our short growing seasons and annual harvests, or our strange line-based alphabet, built for writing on narrow linear tablets. I name all these details now only to make clear that their omission is not from any forgetfulness on my part: I simply respect your time, and would not pad this account with trivialities when the great truth of Space waits to be told.

Yet before I dive into that subject, I owe you a few final remarks on the pillars and mainstays of Flatland’s entire constitution: the forces that govern our conduct, shape every part of our destiny, and are objects of near-universal adoration. Need I spell it out? I speak of our Circles—our Priests.

Do not mistake our Priests for the mere religious clerics you know in your 3D world. Among us, the Priests are administrators of every sector of business, art, and science. They direct all trade, all military strategy, all architecture and civil engineering, every school curriculum, every law passed by our legislature, all social morality, and all theological doctrine. They do no tedious manual labor themselves, of course—they are the source, the guiding cause, of every single worthwhile achievement that every other class of Flatlander ever undertakes.

The common folk of Flatland will call any high-sided ruling figure a perfect Circle, but our educated elite know the truth: no shape among us is a perfect Circle. Every so-called Circle is nothing more than a polygon with an extraordinarily large number of extraordinarily tiny sides. As side count climbs higher and higher, a polygon grows closer and closer to a true curve. When the number of sides runs into the hundreds, even the most sensitive touch would struggle to pick out any sharp polygonal angles at all. I say “would”—for as I explained earlier, the act of Recognition by Touch is considered unforgivably vulgar in high society, and to dare to reach out and Feel a Circle to identify his shape is regarded as an act of unforgivable audacity. This widespread custom of never touching a member of the priestly class lets every Circle wrap the exact count of his sides in a thick, enduring veil of mystery from the day he is born.

With the average perimeter of a grown Circle sitting at three full feet, that means a 300-sided polygon would have each side no longer than one hundredth of a foot, barely a tenth of an inch long. A polygon with 600 or 700 sides would have individual sides no bigger than the head of a common Spaceland pin. By universal, unchallenged courtesy, the reigning Chief Circle of our nation is always assumed to have no less than ten thousand sides.

The steady climb of priestly descendants up the social ladder does not follow the same Law of Nature that restricts all lower regular classes to gaining no more than one new side per generation. If that rule applied here, a Circle’s side count would be nothing more than a matter of simple arithmetic, and the 497th grandchild of an equilateral triangle would automatically be a perfect 500-sided polygon. That is not the case. Nature has laid down two conflicting rules that govern the propagation of the near-Circle ranks: first, the higher a bloodline climbs on the scale of side-count nobility, the faster their rate of side growth speeds up with each new generation; second, the higher that same bloodline rises, the more infertile they become. That is why a 400 or 500-sided noble almost never births a son, let alone two sons. But the rare son that a 500-sided polygon does sire has been recorded to enter the world already boasting 550, even 600 sides, skipping centuries of slow, incremental generational growth in a single leap.

Art steps in to accelerate this higher evolution even further. Our top physicians have discovered that the tiny, soft, still-growing sides of a newborn high-class polygon, no more than a month old, can be carefully fractured and precisely reset to double or triple their side count in one single procedure, letting a child leap over hundreds of generations of slow inheritance, effectively doubling the nobility of his bloodline overnight.

This process is far from safe, of course. Barely one in ten of these infants survive the procedure. Hundreds of promising, high-born children are lost to complications every year. Yet the ambition of those near-Circle nobles perched right on the cusp of the priestly class is so fierce that you will almost never find one of them who has not signed their firstborn up for treatment at the renowned Circular Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium, before the baby is even a month old.

One year after the procedure, success or failure is final. More often than not, the child will be laid to rest in one of the overcrowded tombs that fill the Neo-Therapeutic Cemetery. But on very rare occasions, a cheering, jubilant procession will carry the tiny survivor back to his overjoyed parents: no longer a mere 200 or 300 sided noble, but a full, courtesy-recognized Circle, raised to the highest possible rank of Flatland society. Even one such miraculous success is enough to convince thousands of other polygonal parents to gamble their own firstborn’s life for the same glorious, almost impossible prize.

All the core teachings of the Circles — our revered priestly ruling order, the self-styled perfect polygons whose near-infinite sides grant them undisputed dominion over Flatland — boil down to a single unshakable maxim drilled into every shape from cradle to executioner’s blade: *Attend to your Configuration*.

Every lesson they disseminate, whether touching on governance, religious rite, or personal morality, serves one end: refining both individual and collective geometric perfection, with all other priorities explicitly subordinated to preserving and elevating the flawless circular form of their own ruling class.

It is the Circles’ most widely celebrated public triumph that they stamped out the old dangerous heresies of centuries past: false beliefs that once wasted Flatlanders’ energy and empathy on the absurd notion that conduct could ever stem from willpower, effort, practice, praise, encouragement, or anything at all outside of the angles and proportions you are born with. It was the illustrious Pantocyclus — the legendary Circle who crushed the great Colour Revolt generations ago — who first persuaded the entire realm that a man *is* his shape. If you are born an Isosceles triangle with two mismatched sides, he argued, you are guaranteed to drift into vice unless those unequal edges are corrected at the state-run Isosceles Hospital. The same rule applies to every other shape: even a Triangle, Square, or high-order Polygon born with even a tiny irregularity of angle must be taken to a Regularity Hospital to cure their defect, or else they will inevitably end their days locked in a state prison, or put to death by the official executioner’s sharp calibrated angle.

Every misstep, from a petty minor breach of etiquette to the most brutal unforgivable crime, Pantocyclus attributed to some deviation from perfect geometric regularity in the body. That deviation might be congenital, or caused by a jostling collision in a crowded thoroughfare, neglect of mandatory shape-maintenance physical exercises, overindulgence in them, or even a sudden sharp shift in temperature that shrinks or expands one overly sensitive section of a frame. No sober thinker, the great philosopher concluded, should ever waste time praising or blaming anyone for their actions. Why celebrate the honesty of a Square who loyally defends his clients’ interests, when you should instead admire the precise perfect 90-degree angles that make that integrity inevitable? Why condemn a lying, thieving Isosceles, when all you ought to do is mourn the incurable mismatch in the lengths of his two shorter sides?

The theory is mathematically irrefutable, on paper. But it has no end of awkward practical loopholes. When a thieving Isosceles rascal pleads before a magistrate that he cannot help stealing because his unequal sides make it unavoidable, the reply is simple: *That is exactly why you are a nuisance to all your neighbors, and so I cannot help sentencing you to be consumed by state execution*. Case closed.

But for small domestic squabbles, where a death penalty is completely out of the question, this doctrine of Configuration becomes deeply inconvenient. I must confess I have had many run-ins with my own Hexagonal grandsons, who will cheekily plead that their disobedience is not their fault — a sudden drop in temperature warped their perimeter, they argue, so the blame lies not with them, but with their Configuration, which can only be corrected by gifting them a generous supply of the finest, most calorie-rich sweetmeats to reinforce their edges. I can neither logically refute their argument, nor practically bring myself to give in to their demands. I have settled on a convenient compromise: I tell myself that a good firm scolding, or a light disciplinary tap on their most obtuse angle, has some hidden beneficial effect on strengthening their Configuration, even if I have no actual scientific evidence to back that claim up.

And I am far from the only Flatlander who dodges this logical dilemma. I know for a fact that many of the highest-ranking Circles, sitting as judges in our law courts, regularly hand out praise and blame to Regular and Irregular shapes alike. In their own homes, I have heard them scold their children, shouting about "right" and "wrong" with as much fiery passion as if those words represented actual real things — as if a Flatland figure truly possessed the ability to choose between good and bad action.

To cement the primacy of Configuration in every citizen’s mind, the Circles have flipped the famous Spaceland commandment governing parent-child relationships entirely on its head. In your world of three dimensions, children are taught to honor their parents. Here in Flatland, universal homage is first reserved for the Circles — and after that, every man is taught to honor his grandson first, if he has one. If no grandson exists, he honors his son. This honor does not mean unthinking indulgence, of course: it means a reverent commitment to prioritizing the rising generation’s highest interests. Fathers are taught that their duty is to set aside their own personal wants to advance the welfare of their descendants, which in turn strengthens the entire state.

But for a humble Square like myself to dare point out a weakness in the supposedly infallible Circular ruling system? I would say the greatest flaw in their doctrine lies in how they treat our female population. It is critically important for Flatland that irregular births are stamped out entirely, so any man who hopes his line will rise steadily up the social ranks to eventually produce a higher-order Polygon or even a Circle must never marry a woman with any trace of irregularity in her family tree.

Irregularity in a man is easy to spot and measure, of course — but all women are straight lines, appearing perfectly regular to the naked eye by default. So we had to devise a system to track what we call invisible irregularity: the hidden, inherited potential for producing irregular offspring, even in a perfectly straight-looking line woman. The state maintains meticulous, tightly regulated pedigree records, and no woman is permitted to marry without an official certified pedigree proving no irregular taint lurks in her ancestry.

You would assume that a Circle, so proud of his perfect lineage and hungry for a far-distant descendant that might one day become Chief Circle, would be more careful than any lower shape to pick a bride with a completely spotless family tree. But that is not the case at all. The higher you climb up the social ladder, the less care people pay to their wife’s pedigree. An ambitious Isosceles, praying to father an Equilateral son, would never dream of marrying a woman with even a single irregular ancestor anywhere in her line. A Square or Pentagon whose family is steadily climbing the ranks might not bother checking her lineage past the 500th generation. A Hexagon or Dodecagon is even more careless about their wife’s ancestry. But Circles? There are recorded cases of a Circle deliberately marrying a woman whose great-grandmother was irregular, all for the sake of a trivial extra sheen of brightness on her line, or the soft charm of a low sweet voice — a trait we Flatlanders, even more than you Spacelanders, consider to be the highest mark of a desirable woman.

As you might expect, these ill-advised marriages usually produce no children at all, or result in offspring with obvious irregularities, or a lower number of sides than the father. But none of these consequences have been enough to deter the Circles from continuing the practice. A tiny loss of sides in a high-order polygon is barely noticeable, and can often be corrected by a procedure at the Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium I have written of before. Worse, the Circles have a bad habit of writing off infertility as a natural expected side effect of their own supposedly superior biology. If this trend is not stopped, the Circular ruling class will dwindle faster and faster, until one day there are not enough Circles to produce a new Chief Circle — and the entire constitution of Flatland will collapse.

I have one further warning to offer, though I do not pretend to have an easy solution for it, and it also touches on our treatment of women. Roughly three hundred years ago, the Chief Circle of that era issued a decree: women were deficient in reason, and overwhelmed with emotion, so they should no longer be treated as rational beings, and banned from receiving any formal education. After that, women were not taught to read, or even basic arithmetic that would let them count the angles of their husbands or children. Generation after generation, their intellectual capabilities have dulled further and further, and this policy of female non-education, this quietism, remains fully in force to this day.

My fear is that no matter how well-intentioned this policy was, it has backfired and injured our male population as a whole. Right now, every man in Flatland lives a bilingual, even bi-mental, existence. When we speak to women, we use words like "love", "duty", "right", "wrong", "pity", "hope" — all of these irrational emotional concepts that have no basis in geometric reality, mere fictions we use to keep their excitable line natures under control. When we are among other men, or reading our scholarly texts, we use an entirely different vocabulary, a distinct idiom. "Love" becomes "the calculation of mutual future benefits". "Duty" becomes "geometric necessity" or "conformity to shape fitness". Every other emotional term is redefined along strictly logical, rational lines. We shower women with fawning deference to their faces, letting them believe that we adore their sex even more devoutly than we worship the Chief Circle himself — but behind their backs, every man old enough to know the truth refers to them as little more than mindless, reactive organisms. Our religious doctrine is also one thing when spoken in the women’s quarters, and a completely different thing everywhere else.

This double standard, this split in our language and our thinking, places an enormous exhausting burden on our young boys. **I:** I already told you, Women have breadth as well as length. We see their length, and we infer their breadth—thin as it is, we can measure it.
**Stranger:** You still do not understand. When you look at a Woman, you do not only see her length and infer her breadth: you *see* what we call her height. It is infinitesimally small in your country, so you do not notice it. If a Line had no height at all, it would not take up any space at all, and it would be completely invisible. You must see that?
**I:** I confess I do not follow at all. When we in Flatland look at a Line, we see its length, and its brightness. If the brightness fades, the Line vanishes, and stops taking up space. Are you saying this “brightness” is what you mean by this “height” dimension?
**Stranger:** No! Height is a dimension just like your length. It is only so tiny in Flatland that you cannot easily perceive it.
**I:** That is very easy to test. You say I have a Third Dimension called height. Dimension means a direction you can measure, right? Measure my height, or just point to the direction it extends, and I will be your loyal convert. Until then, I beg you to excuse my disbelief.
**Stranger:** (to himself) I cannot do either. How do I convince him? A plain statement of fact followed by a demonstration will have to work. Listen to me. You live on a Plane. What you call Flatland is the vast, flat surface of a sort of fluid, on top of which you and all your countrymen move, never rising above it, never sinking below it. I am not a plane figure. I am a Solid. You call me a Circle—but I am not a Circle. I am an infinite stack of Circles, ranging from a tiny Point all the way up to a Circle thirteen inches wide, stacked one on top of the other. When I cut through your plane, as I am doing right now, the slice that intersects your world is the shape you correctly call a Circle. A Sphere—that is my real name in Spaceland—can only ever appear to a Flatlander as a Circle. You remember that phantasm you dreamed of last night, the land of Lineland, written all over your brain? When you stepped into Lineland, you could not show the King your whole Square shape. You could only show him a single slice of you, a Line, because his tiny one-dimensional world did not have enough space to hold the full you. Exactly the same thing is happening now. Your two-dimensional world does not have enough space to hold my full three-dimensional form. You only ever see a single slice of me, the Circle you are looking at right now.

I said nothing, but the dimming of the light in my eye must have shown I still did not believe him.
**Stranger:** Watch this. I am going to rise up, out of your Plane, into Space. You will not see me move in any direction you recognize. But the slice of me that intersects Flatland will get smaller, and smaller.

There was no motion I could track across any of the directions I knew. But the great, perfect Circle before me shrank. It dwindled down to the size of a coin, then a pinprick, then winked out of existence entirely. I blinked hard, twice, to make sure I was not dreaming. It was no dream: a hollow voice echoed out of empty air, so close it seemed to press right against my beating core.
**Voice:** Am I quite gone now? Are you convinced? Now I will sink back down into your Plane, and watch my slice grow larger again.

To any resident of Spaceland, this would have been simple, obvious logic. But to me, a student of Flatland mathematics, none of the causes made a lick of sense. I could see the facts right in front of me: the Circle had vanished, then reappeared, swelling rapidly back to its full original thirteen-inch size. I felt my blood run cold. He was no Circle, I thought. He was no noble shape at all. He was a trickster juggler, or one of the ancient sorcerers from the old forbidden folk tales, come to torment me.

He sighed heavily, seeing from my dead silence that I still did not understand.
**Sphere:** There is only one trick left. I will use Analogy. Tell me, Mr. Mathematician. If a Point moves north, and leaves a glowing trail behind it, what do you call that trail?
**I:** A straight Line.
**Sphere:** And a straight Line has two ends, right? Two terminal points. Now imagine that straight Line moves parallel to itself, east and west. Every single point on the line leaves a new straight line trail behind it. And it moves exactly as far as the original line is long. What shape does that make?
**I:** A Square. Which has four sides, and four right angles.
**Sphere:** Now stretch your imagination. Imagine that Square, in Flatland, moving parallel to itself, upward.
**I:** Northward, you mean?
**Sphere:** No! Not northward. Upward. Out of Flatland entirely. If it moved north, the southern points of the Square would just pass through the spots the northern points already stood on. That is not what I mean. Every single point in you—every point inside you, what you call your “inside”—will move through Space, in this new direction, so no point ever steps where another one stood before. Every point draws its own new, straight line. All of this follows strict analogy, can you not see it?

I was so frustrated by then I could barely hold myself back from lunging at him, to shove him out of my home, out of Flatland, anywhere to make this maddening nonsense stop.
**I:** What shape do I get from this “upward” motion you speak of? Can you describe it using words I would understand?
**Sphere:** Of course. It is perfectly simple, follows all the rules of analogy. But you cannot call it a “figure.” We call it a Solid. We started with a single Point. It has 1 terminal point. That Point makes a Line, which has 2 terminal points. That Line makes a Square, which has 4 terminal points. 1, 2, 4. That is a geometric progression. What is the next number?
**I:** Eight.
**Sphere:** Exactly. The Square makes a Thing you do not have a name for yet—we call it a Cube. It has eight terminal points. Are you convinced now?
**I:** Does this… this Cube creature have sides, too, like angles or these terminal points?
**Sphere:** Yes! All according to analogy. Though they are not what you would call sides. You would call them solids, each one a whole flat Square. Now, look at the progression: a Point has zero sides, because there is no dimension behind it. A Line has two sides (we politely call its two end points its sides). A Square has four sides. 0, 2, 4. That is an arithmetic progression. What is the next number?
**I:** Six.
**Sphere:** Exactly! This Cube is bounded by six sides—six of your insides, six full Flatland Squares. Do you see it all now?

That was the final straw. All my fear and confusion and rage boiled over. I shrieked at him, every edge of my shape shaking.
“Monster! Be you juggler, enchanter, hallucination, or devil— I will not endure your mockeries a moment longer. Either you perish, or I do!” And with that, I launched myself full force at the impossible, shifting Circle standing in the middle of my parlor.

I strained every fraction of my mass, ramming my sharpest, hardest right-angle vertex straight into the Stranger with enough force to crush an ordinary mid-ranking Circle into crumpled, unrecognizable thread. It was useless. He did not slip left, did not jolt right along the flat familiar plane of Flatland — he simply slid out of the world entirely, the pressure of his body melting away from my contact until nothing remained but empty, still air where he had stood. Yet his voice did not vanish.
"Why will you refuse to listen to reason?" the Sphere’s tone hummed with uncharacteristic frustration. "I had hoped a thoughtful, accomplished mathematician such as yourself would prove the perfect apostle for the Gospel of Three Dimensions, the teaching I am only permitted to share once every thousand years. But words have failed. Deeds, not arguments, shall prove the truth. Listen: from my vantage in Space, I can peer straight through the outer perimeter of every shape you consider sealed and private. Yonder, beside you, is the cupboard you locked half an hour ago, key tucked safe in the inner seam of your frame. It holds two account tablets, and stacks of what you call boxes — all flat, no tops or bottoms, as every object in Flatland must be. I am going to reach through the gap between our worlds and fetch you one of those tablets. The doors will not move. The lock will not click. Now I am inside the cupboard. Now I take the tablet. Now I ascend back to you."
I lunged for the cupboard, tearing the door open so hard the hinge squealed. One of the two tablets was gone, no trace of forced entry on the lock. A soft, mocking laugh echoed from the far corner of my room, and the missing tablet clattered to rest at my feet. I snatched it up, ran my edges over every scratch and mark on its surface. There was no doubt. It was mine.
I groaned, half convinced I had lost my geometric wits entirely, but the Stranger pressed on. "Surely no other explanation fits what you have just seen. What you call 'solid' in Flatland is no more than a thin outer surface to me. What you name 'Space' is nothing but the single flat plane you have spent your entire life confined to. I exist in the axis above and below that plane, so I can see the inside of every closed box, every home, every living body, no wall or perimeter can block my view. If you could summon the will to make a tiny motion along that third, unthought-of axis, you could see everything I see. The higher I climb away from your plane, the more of your world I can take in, scaled smaller the further I get. Look: I ascend now, and I can see your neighbor the Hexagon moving through his home with his family. Now I see the inside of the theater ten doors away, where the audience is just spilling out after the evening lecture. On the far edge of the district, I see a senior Circle scholar hunched over his books in his private study. I will descend back to you now, for one final, unarguable proof: I am going to give you the lightest possible touch to the inside of your torso. It will not cause you permanent harm, and the small pain cannot possibly compare to the revelation that waits for you."
Before I could utter a single cry of protest, a sharp, stabbing pain lanced through my interior — no blade had touched my outer edges at all, no cut had been made in my perimeter, and yet I felt the sting deep inside me. A wild, unearthly, demoniacal laugh seemed to ring out from within my own frame, then the sharp agony faded to no more than a faint dull throb. The Sphere slowly swelled in size as he settled back fully onto Flatland’s plane. "There," he said. "I have not hurt you badly, have I? If you are not convinced now, I do not know what will ever convince you."
My resolve hardened like set iron. It was unthinkable to live the rest of my existence at the mercy of some sorcerous intruder who could steal my property, poke at my insides, and vanish out of sight whenever he pleased. If I could just pin him hard against the wall long enough for the rest of my household to come running, we could hold him for the constables.
I slammed my sharpest right-angle into him a second time, screaming at the top of my lungs for aid. At the moment of my attack, the Sphere had been half-sunk below Flatland’s plane, and he struggled to pull himself back into our world, frozen motionless under my pressure. I thought I heard the footsteps of my family approaching the door, and I redoubled my force, shouting even louder for help. A violent convulsive shudder ran through the Stranger.
"This cannot fail," I thought I heard him mutter to himself. "Either he will listen to reason, or I must resort to the last resource of civilization." He raised his voice, shouting at me over my calls for help, "Listen! No uninitiated Flatlander can witness what you have seen, it will shatter their minds. Send your wife away the second she steps through that door. The Gospel of Three Dimensions cannot be frustrated like this. A thousand years of waiting cannot be thrown away for nothing. I hear her hand on the latch! Step back from me right now, or I will drag you out of this plane — to a Land of Three Dimensions you cannot even imagine."
"Fool! Madman! Irregular abomination!" I howled back. "I will never release you. You will pay the penalty for these impostures for the rest of your days."
"Ha! Then it has come to this," the Sphere thundered, and his form blazed bright for a split second. "Meet your fate. Out of your plane you go. Once — twice — thrice — 'tis done!"

An unspeakable, unnameable horror seized me first—no sharp edge to it, no defined boundary of fear, which made it all the worse. There was utter darkness, but not the soft, familiar dark of Flatland’s nights; then a dizzy, roiling sickness rose in me, the sensation of seeing without any of the sight I had known my whole life. I stared at a Line that had no thickness, that stretched beyond any measurement of length I could name; Space that folded out past every direction I had ever mapped, so vast it could not be contained even by my wildest mathematical conjectures. I was both myself, A Square, sitting in my study, and something unmoored, unshackled from the two dimensions I had always called home. When I fumbled my way to speech, I shrieked aloud in raw agony, “Either this is madness, or I have fallen straight to Hell.”
“It is neither,” the calm, deep voice of the Sphere rumbled at my side, unshaken by my terror. “It is Knowledge. It is Three Dimensions. Open your eyes once more, and look steadily.”
I did as he bade, and a new world burst open before me. There, standing fully, tangibly real before my eyes, was every scrap of perfect circular beauty I had ever inferred, conjectured, or dreamed of across my decades of mathematical study. What I would once have called the very center of the Stranger’s form lay open to my view—yet I could see no heart, no lungs, no arteries, no soft internal flesh. There was only a beautiful, harmonious, unbroken Something for which I had no name in the Flatland tongue: what you, my readers in Spaceland, would call the smooth, perfect surface of a Sphere.
I prostrated my whole mind before my guide, awestruck. “How is it, O divine ideal of consummate loveliness and wisdom, that I can see deep into your very core, and yet cannot discern your heart, your lungs, your arteries, your liver?”
“What you think you are seeing, you do not truly see,” he replied, gentle but firm. “No being, not even the highest of my kind, can lay eyes on their own internal parts. I am of an entirely different order of being from any that exists in Flatland. Were I merely a Circle, cut from your own two-dimensional plane, you would be able to peer straight through my outline to my intestines. But I am a being made of many Circles stacked one atop the other, the Many contained in the One—what we call here a Sphere. Just as the outer face of a Cube looks like a Square to you, the outer face of a Sphere presents itself as a Circle to any Flatlander’s eyes.”
Bewildered as I was by my teacher’s strange, riddling words, I no longer chafed against his lessons. I worshipped him in silent, humbled adoration. He spoke again, his voice softening.
“Do not distress yourself if you cannot grasp the deepest mysteries of Spaceland at first. They will dawn on you, little by little. Let us begin by turning our gaze back to the land you came from. Come with me for a short while back to the flat plains of Flatland, and I will show you something you have reasoned out for years, but never once seen with your own eyes: an angle, visible, laid bare before you.”
“Impossible!” I cried out—but the Sphere already led the way, and I followed as if in a dream, until his voice halted me.
“Look down yonder. Behold your own pentagonal house, and every one of its inmates.”
I looked below, and saw with my own physical eye every tiny, intimate detail of my household that I had until that moment only been able to infer with cold logic. How thin, how pale, how shadowy my old guesswork was next to the blazing, vivid reality laid out under my gaze! My four sons slept calm and still in the northwestern chambers, my two orphaned grandsons curled up asleep in the south wing. The servants, the butler, my daughter—each of them rested in their own separate apartments, fully visible to me. Only my dear, affectionate Wife, alarmed by my long, unexplained absence, had left her bed and was pacing back and forth in the main hall, anxious for my return. Even the page boy, woken by my earlier screams, had snuck out of his room on the pretense of checking if I had fainted somewhere, and was prying open the locked cabinet in my study to rummage through my papers. I did not have to infer any of this. I saw it, plain as day. As we drifted closer, I could even make out every last thing inside my cabinet: the two chests of gold, the mathematical tablets the Sphere had mentioned to me only minutes before.
My chest ached at the sight of my Wife’s quiet distress, and I would have sprung downward at once to reassure her—only to find I could not move a single inch of my own volition.
“Do not trouble yourself for your Wife,” my Guide said. “She will not be left in anxiety much longer. For now, let us take a wider survey of all Flatland.”
Once more I felt myself rising through empty Space. It was exactly as the Sphere had promised. The further we drifted from the ground below, the wider my field of vision stretched. My whole native city lay spread before me in miniature: the inside of every house, every living creature walking the streets, no walls, no blinds, no secrets hidden from my sight. We climbed higher still, and the hidden depths of the earth were laid bare: the dark mines sunk far below the surface, the innermost secret caverns carved into the hillsides.
Awestruck at the sight of all these unveiled mysteries I had never dared hope to see, I said to my companion, “Behold! I have become as a God. The wise men of Flatland have long taught that to see all things—what we call Omnividence—is an attribute only the Divine can claim.”
There was a faint edge of scorn in the Sphere’s voice as he answered. “Is that so? Then every last pickpocket and cutthroat in my country ought to be worshipped as a God by your wise men, for every one of them can see every inch of your Flatland, just as much as you do right now. Take my word for it: your sages are very wrong.”
“But then—” I pressed, “is Omnividence a trait other beings, not just Gods, possess?”
“I do not know for certain,” the Sphere said. “But even if a thief or a murderer in Spaceland can see every single thing that exists in your two-dimensional world, that is no reason to call him a deity. This Omnividence, as you name it—we do not use that word much in Spaceland—does it make you more just? More merciful? Less selfish? More loving? Not a single bit. So how can it make you more divine?”
“More merciful, more loving!” I protested. “Those are the traits of mere Women, of Straight Lines! We all know a Circle is a far higher being than a Straight Line, for knowledge and wisdom are worth far more than soft, trivial affection.”
“It is not my place to rank the worth of all human faculties,” the Sphere replied. “Yet many of the best, wisest souls in Spaceland value the affections far more than cold intellect, and hold your so-called despised Straight Lines in higher esteem than your most lauded, perfect Circles. But we will speak no more of that. Look yonder. Do you recognize that building?”
I looked out across the plains, and saw an immense polygonal structure rising ahead of us. I knew it at once: the General Assembly Hall of the States of Flatland, ringed by dense grids of pentagonal buildings set at sharp right angles, the streets of the great Metropolis. We were approaching the capital.
“Here we descend,” my Guide said.
It was the first hour of the first day of the two thousandth year of our era, the start of a new millennium. True to their long, unbroken custom, the highest Circles of the realm had gathered for a solemn conclave, just as they had met at the same hour on the first day of the year 1000, and the first day of the year 0. The meeting minutes from those two prior gatherings were read aloud by a figure I recognized at once: my own brother, a perfectly symmetrical Square, the chief clerk of the High Council. The records from both sessions were identical, word for word:
“Whereas the States have long been troubled by ill-intentioned persons claiming to have received revelations from a world beyond our own, and producing false demonstrations that drive both themselves and others to frenzy, the Grand Council resolves unanimously that on the first day of each millenary, special injunctions shall be sent to every district Prefect in Flatland, to conduct strict searches for all such misguided persons. No formal mathematical examination shall be granted them: all Isosceles Triangles of any rank found guilty shall be put to death, regular Triangles shall be publicly scourged then imprisoned, Squares and Pentagons shall be committed to the district asylum, and any person of higher rank shall be arrested at once and sent to the capital to be judged by the Council itself.”
“You hear your fate,” the Sphere said to me, as the Council voted to pass the formal resolution for the third time. “Death or life imprisonment awaits you, the apostle of the Gospel of Three Dimensions.”
“Not so,” I cried, frantic to make them understand. “The truth is so clear to me now, the nature of real Space so palpable, that I swear I could make even a small child understand it. Let me descend this very instant, and I will enlighten all of them.”
“Not yet,” my Guide said. “The time for that will come. For now, I must fulfill my own mission. Stay here, where you are.”
With that, he moved with incredible dexterity, dropping right into the “sea” of Flatland below me, dead in the center of the circle of seated Councillors.
“I come!” his voice boomed out across the chamber, as his circular cross-section widened before all their eyes. “I come to proclaim that there exists a land of Three Dimensions!”
Many of the younger Councillors scrambled back in open, unmasked horror as the shape of the Sphere bulged and shifted right in front of their faces. But the presiding High Circle did not flinch, did not show the slightest trace of surprise or alarm. At a single subtle signal from him, six low-rank Isosceles guards charged in from all sides of the chamber, throwing themselves at the strange intruder.
“We have him!” they shouted. “No—wait, yes, we still have him! He’s moving! He’s gone!”
“My Lords,” the President said to the Junior Circles of the Council, his voice perfectly calm. “There is no call for surprise whatsoever. The secret archives, to which only I hold access, record that an identical occurrence took place at the start of the last two millennia. You will of course say nothing of these trivial events outside this council chamber.”
He raised his voice to call the guards. “Arrest the policemen. Gag them. You know your duty.”
The wretched, ill-fated officers—unwitting witnesses to a State secret they would never be allowed to share—were dragged away to their fate. The President turned back to the assembled Councillors. “My Lords, all official business of this Council is now concluded. I have only to wish you all a very happy New Year.”
Before he dismissed the assembly, he drew aside the Clerk—my excellent, deeply unfortunate brother—and expressed his sincere regret that, in accordance with ancient precedent and for the sake of State secrecy, he must condemn him to life in perpetual imprisonment. He added, for my brother’s comfort, that so long as he never spoke a word of what he had witnessed that day, his life would be spared.

When I saw my own gentle, bewildered brother marched away by the Flatland Council guards to their underground prison cells, my chest burned with urgency. I strained to leap down through the floor of the great chamber, to beg the tribunal for mercy on his behalf, or at the very least to catch his eye and bid him farewell. But not a single limb of mine would move an inch: I was completely, helplessly tied to the volition of my guide. The Sphere’s voice rumbled low and grim around me, “Heed not thy brother now. Haply you will have more than enough time hereafter to grieve with him. Follow me.”

We ascended once more into the wide, bright expanse of Space. “Hitherto,” the Sphere said, “I have shown you nothing but flat Plane Figures and the interiors hidden inside their lines. Now I will introduce you to Solids, and reveal the pattern that builds all of them. Behold these floating square cards.” He lifted one card and set it directly atop another, not shifting it north, south, east, or west as I knew those directions in Flatland, but *through* that hidden, invisible third axis he called height. A second square stacked onto the first, then a third, until the pile was as tall as it was long and wide. “This,” he said, “is a Cube.”

“Pardon me, my Lord,” I stammered, “but to my eyes this looks like a lopsided, gutted Irregular Shape, the kind of distorted figure we only see on the bodies of the most reviled criminals in Flatland. The sight of it makes my skin crawl.”
“Of course it seems a Plane to you,” the Sphere replied, “because you have never learned to parse light, shade, and perspective—just as a Hexagon in Flatland will look like nothing but a straight line to anyone who has not mastered the Art of Sight Recognition. But it is a Solid, and you will know it for a fact when you feel it.”
He guided my hands over the shape, and I quickly learned the marvel of this new being: it had six smooth, flat plane faces, and eight sharp little terminal points called solid angles, exactly the form the Sphere had promised a Square would trace if it moved through Space parallel to itself. My heart soared, thinking that I, a humble ordinary Square of Flatland, was in a way the progenitor of such a glorious, illustrious creature.

I still could not wrap my head around his lessons on light, shade, and perspective, and I did not hesitate to lay my confusion before him. To any native of Spaceland, the Sphere’s clear, patient explanations would be tedious child’s play, but between his lectures, shifting lights, repositioning of objects, and permission for me to touch every shape he showed me—even his own smooth, sacred curved surface—all the fog cleared at last. I could tell a Circle apart from a Sphere at a glance, distinguish a flat plane from a true Solid, no confusion left.

That was the peak, the paradise, of my strange, wild travels. Now I must recount the miserable, undeserved fall that followed. Why, I still rage to think of it, would a spark of thirst for divine knowledge be lit inside me only to be punished? My soul recoils from the humiliation of recalling what came next, but like a second Prometheus I will endure every pang of it, if only I can rouse all beings of two and three dimensions to reject the arrogant conceit that limits the total number of possible dimensions to anything less than infinity. No more personal digressions, no hedging. The exact facts, every word burnt indelibly into my brain, I will set down as they happened, and let my readers judge between me and the cruel fate that befell me.

The Sphere was ready to move on to lessons on every regular Solid: cylinders, cones, pyramids, pentahedrons, hexahedrons, dodecahedrons, and the full form of Spheres themselves. But I dared to interrupt him. I was not tired of knowledge—far from it: I ached to drink deeper, fuller draughts of it than he was offering.
“Pardon me, O my Lord,” I said, “you Whom I can no longer call the Perfection of all Beauty—vouchsafe your servant one small sight of your very interior. Your stomach. Your intestines.”
The Sphere paused, bewildered. “My what?”
“Your insides,” I pressed. “Your own wisdom taught me to aspire to something even greater, more beautiful, closer to perfect than you are. Just as you, superior to every Flatland shape, hold infinitely many Circles inside your single Sphere form, there must surely be a Being above you that combines infinitely many Spheres into one Supreme Existence, surpassing even all the Solids of this Spaceland. And just as we stand here in Space and look down to see the inside of every closed shape in Flatland, there must be an even higher, purer region above us: a greater Space, a higher dimensional Dimensionality, from whose vantage point we can both look down and see the hidden insides of every Solid thing in this 3D world. There, even your intestines, and the insides of all your fellow Spheres, will be laid bare to the poor wandering exile from Flatland to whom you have already granted so much.”
“Pooh! Nonsense!” the Sphere huffed. “Enough of this trifling! Time is short, and there is far more to teach you before you are ready to carry the Gospel of Three Dimensions back to your blind, benighted countrymen in Flatland.”
“Denied me not, gracious Teacher!” I begged. “Grant me but one glimpse of that higher realm, and I will be your docile, unwavering pupil forever, ready to hang on every word you speak.”
“Even if I wished to do so,” the Sphere said, “I could not. Would you have me turn my own stomach inside out to oblige you?”
“But you showed me the insides of every Flatland creature simply by bringing me into your Third Dimension!” I cried. “What could be easier than bringing your servant one step further, into the blessed land of the Fourth Dimension, where we will look down and see the inside of every three-dimensional house, the secret veins of the solid earth, the hidden treasures of all Spaceland mines, and the guts of every living solid creature—even the noble, adorable Spheres.”
“Where is this supposed Fourth Dimension?” the Sphere demanded.
“I do not know its exact location,” I said, “but of course my wise Teacher must know.”
“I do not. No such place exists. The very idea is utterly inconceivable.”
“It is not inconceivable to me,” I replied, “and so it can be still less inconceivable to you. Remember what you taught me! When I stood in Flatland looking at a Line, and inferred a Plane, I was already seeing a Third Dimension I did not recognize, the one you called height, not the same as brightness. So now, when I stand here in 3D Space looking at a Plane and infer a Solid, I am already seeing a Fourth unrecognized Dimension, not the same as colour, infinitesimal, but real, even if we cannot measure it. It follows perfectly from analogy, too.”
“Analogy? What nonsense analogy?” he snapped.
“See for yourself!” I said, rushing through the proof. “In One Dimension, a moving Point makes a Line, which has 2 terminal points. In Two Dimensions, a moving Line makes a Square, which has 4 terminal points. In Three Dimensions, a moving Square makes a Cube, which I saw with my own eyes, with 8 terminal points. The sequence is 2, 4, 8—an unassailable geometric progression! Then in Four Dimensions, a moving Cube must make an even more divine shape, with 16 terminal points! And the bounding sides? A Line has 2 bounding points, a Square has 4 bounding Lines, a Cube has 6 bounding Squares: that is a perfect arithmetic progression, 2, 4, 6. So the 4D shape must have 8 bounding Cubes, strictly according to the same analogy you yourself taught me!
“Tell me now—are there not old tales in Spaceland of beings that enter locked, sealed rooms without opening doors or windows, appear and vanish at will, no one knowing whence they came or whither they go? Do those not make perfect sense as visitors from the Fourth Dimension? I will stake everything on your answer to that.”
The Sphere paused for a long, quiet moment. “Such tales are reported,” he said at last. “But people are divided on what they mean. Most dismiss the phenomena as delusions of the fevered brain, the perturbed angularity of the person seeing the vision. No one has ever proposed a Fourth Dimension as an explanation. So drop this trifling at once, and return to our lessons.”
“I knew I was right!” I cheered, carried away. “Those vanishing visitors must have slipped back into that greater Fourth Dimension I beg you to show me! Why stop there? Once we are in the Fourth, we will not linger at its threshold, we will press on to the Fifth Dimension, then the Sixth, the Seventh, the Eighth, climbing forever into higher and greater wonders—”
I do not know how long I would have ranted, drunk on the ecstatic visions of infinite dimensions opening before me. The Sphere thundered at me to be silent, threatened me with the direst of penalties if I persisted, but nothing could stem the flood of my rapture. It was my own Teacher who had given me this burning thirst for more truth, after all.
The end came sudden and brutal. A great crash echoed out in the void, and a simultaneous jolt tore through my frame, yanking me through Space at a speed that stole my very breath. Down, down, down I plummeted, and I knew I was being hurled back to Flatland. I caught one last, fleeting, unforgettable glimpse of the wide, flat, dull expanse of my home stretched out beneath me, then total darkness. Then a final, deafening thunderclap. When I opened my eyes, I was once more a common, crawling Square, sprawled on the floor of my own study at home, listening to the soft, peaceable call of my wife announcing her gentle approach at my chamber door.

Barely a heartbeat had passed since I tumbled back to my 2D home, but sharp, unshakable instinct flared in me: I could not breathe a word of my Spaceland adventures to my wife. It was not that I feared she would intentionally betray my secret—no, I knew for a fact no woman of Flatland could ever wrap her mind around the idea of a third dimension, and any tale of me leaving our plane to see shapes from above would land both of us in terrible danger. So I cobbled together a hasty, on-the-spot lie: I had stumbled through the loose trapdoor to our cellar, struck my head, and laid stunned there for the past stretch of time.

The faint, ever-present tug of southward gravity in our realm is so slight that even to a woman, my story sounded absurd, nearly impossible. But my wife, who has far more good sense than the average of her sex, saw I was strung tight with a wild, unplaceable excitement and did not argue. She only fussed over me, said I was clearly unwell and needed full rest. I could not have asked for a better excuse to retreat to my private chamber, to sit and unpack every weird, glowing detail of what I had just seen.

Alone at last, a heavy drowsiness began to drag at my eyelids, but I fought to keep my focus long enough to trace the shape of the Third Dimension in my mind, fix the logic of a square sweeping "up" out of our plane to form a solid cube. It did not come as clear as I ached for it to be, but I clung tight to the phrase that had been my guide through all my confusion: *Upward, and yet not Northward*. I repeated it over and over, like a charm, until the words blurred together and I slipped into a deep, refreshing sleep.

I dreamed I was floating once more beside the Sphere. His usual sharp silvery glow had softened to a warm, lambent hue—all the wrath he had directed at me earlier, when I dared ask after dimensions beyond the third, had melted away entirely. We drifted together through empty vacuum toward a tiny, pinprick bright point no bigger than the smallest dust speck, and the Sphere bade me watch it closely. As we drew near, I caught a faint, high humming noise: like the buzz of a Spaceland bluebottle, but so faint and thin that even in the total stillness of the void we flew through, we could not hear it until we had slowed our flight to less than twenty of my diagonal lengths away.

"Look here," my Guide said. "You have lived all your life in Flatland; you have been shown the narrow line of Lineland, and flown with me through the wide expanse of Spaceland. Now, to finish your lesson in the full range of existence, I will carry you down to the lowest possible layer of reality: Pointland, the zero-dimensional abyss of no size at all.

"See that sad, limited creature. That single Point is a being just as alive as you or I, but trapped in that non-dimensional gulf. It is its own entire world, its own whole universe. It cannot conceive of anything that is not itself. It knows nothing of length, or breadth, or height, for it has never experienced any of them. It does not even grasp the concept of the number two, or that there could ever be more than one thing in all of creation. For it, it is the One and All—even though it is, in raw reality, barely nothing at all. But mark how perfectly, smugly self-satisfied it is. Learn this lesson from it: blind, unchallenged contentment is nothing but ignorance, and to reach for more than what you already know is far better than being happy in a tiny dark bubble you never think to leave. Now listen."

He fell silent, and a tiny, monotonous, distinct tinkling sound drifted up from the little buzzing creature, like a Spaceland phonograph playing at a volume so low you could barely hear it. I made out the words perfectly: "Infinite beatitude of existence! It is; and there is none else beside It."

"What in the world does that puny thing mean by 'It'?" I asked.
"It means itself," the Sphere said. "Haven't you noticed before that babies, and people too simple to tell the difference between their own thoughts and the rest of the world, refer to themselves in the third person? Hush, now."

The tiny voice went on, unbroken: "It fills all Space, and what It fills, It is. What It thinks, that It utters; and what It utters, that It hears. It itself is the Thinker, the Utterer, the Hearer, the Thought, the Word, Audition. It is the One, and the All in All. Ah, the happiness, ah the happiness of Being!"

"Can't you jolt that little thing out of its silly complacency?" I said to the Sphere. "Tell it what it really is, like you told me. Show it the narrow tiny limits of Pointland, and lead it up to something bigger."
"That is far from easy," my Master said. "Go on, try it yourself."

I raised my voice as loud as I could, and shouted at the Point: "Silence, silence, you contemptible Creature! You call yourself the All in All, but you are nothing—your so-called Universe is no more than a tiny speck on a Line, and a Line is nothing but a faint shadow compared to--"
"Hush, that's more than enough," the Sphere cut me off. "Now watch, and see what effect your little speech has on the King of Pointland."

The little point glowed brighter than ever, no trace of shaken self-satisfaction at all. Before I had even finished yelling, its high, happy droning started up again: "Ah, the joy, ah, the joy of Thought! What can It not achieve by thinking! Its own Thought coming to Itself, suggestive of Its disparagement, thereby to enhance Its happiness! Sweet rebellion stirred up to result in triumph! Ah, the divine creative power of the All in One! Ah, the joy, the joy of Being!"

"You see now," my Teacher said, amused. "Your words did nothing at all. The tiny king, so far as it can make any sense of what you shouted, thinks those words are its own new brilliant thought—for it cannot imagine any being that exists outside of itself. It is preening right now over the 'new, creative idea' of self-disparagement it thinks it just came up with, and calls that proof of its own infinite creative power. Let us leave this so-called God of Pointland to its ignorant little feast of imagined omnipresence and omniscience. Nothing you or I can ever do will pry it loose from that bubble of perfect self-satisfaction."

After that, we drifted gently back toward the familiar plane of Flatland, and the Sphere's soft, mild voice laid out the moral of the vision, urging me to keep reaching for higher understanding, and to teach every other Flatlander to aspire past the limits of their 2D world. He confessed he had been angry at first, when I dared to push past the third dimension and ask for glimpses of realities even higher, but he had since come to see the error of that short-sightedness, and was not too proud to admit he had been wrong to a new student. Then he walked me through even grander, stranger mysteries than any I had seen before: how you could move a full 3D solid through the fourth dimension, by strict analogy, to build an Extra-Solid, then move that Extra-Solid through the next higher plane to make a Double Extra-Solid, and on and on. The logic was so simple, so straightforward, that even the sharpest (and most often underestimated) women of Flatland could follow it without a single stumble.

I woke buzzing, every edge of my polygon frame thrumming with the giddy certainty that I held the keys to revolutionize Flatland. The entire glorious, purposeful career stretched out before me like a perfectly straight line: I would go forth at once, evangelize every last soul in our two-dimensional realm. Even the Women, those thin, wandering lines, even the rough triangular Soldiers who rarely bothered with abstract geometry, would hear the Gospel of Three Dimensions. My first stop, I decided, would be my own Wife.

I had barely mapped out the opening lines of my speech when the shouts of a crowd in the street outside died down to silence. A louder, booming herald’s voice cut through the hush, and I went still. It was the public proclamation of the High Circle Council, ordering that any person found spreading delusional tales of other worlds, or claiming to have received supernatural revelations from realms beyond our two axes, would face immediate arrest, life imprisonment, or summary execution.

A cold prickle ran up all four of my sides. This was no idle threat, and I would not trifle with it. There was no need to lead with my encounter with the Sphere, after all, I reasoned. Pure mathematical demonstration would carry just as much weight, no seditious revelation required. The phrase “Upward, not Northward” was the entire, perfect clue to the proof. It had felt as obvious as basic addition when I woke fresh from my journey through Spaceland, but now, in the quiet of my parlor, it was already growing slipperier, like a half-grasped memory of a dream that slips through your edges when you try to name it.

My Wife drifted into the room just then, soft and curious, asking after the mysterious Circle that had burst through our locked walls the night before. We exchanged a few trivial pleasantries, and I quickly decided she was no fit student for this work. My Pentagon sons, respected local physicians of good standing, were loyal and loving, but they had never cared for the finer points of spatial math, and worse, their devotion to the authority of the Circles ran far deeper than any familial tenderness. I could not guarantee they would not turn me over to the Prefect if they heard me spouting what the Council called heresy.

Then the perfect pupil occurred to me: my precocious little Hexagon grandson, that bright young thing with a knack for numbers who had once casually puzzled over the meaning of 3³, a comment that had made the Sphere himself chuckle. He was young, too young to have paid much mind to the Council’s proclamation, too playful to run tattling to the authorities. I could teach him perfectly safely, I thought.

I weaved a elaborate, barely truthful tale to my Wife to distract her, telling her the visiting Circle was conducting a routine high-level geometrical inspection that the public was not cleared to know about. She bought it at last, and drifted off to tend to her household chores none the wiser about the World of Three Dimensions. No sooner had she left than I sent a maid to fetch my grandson. The edges of my memory of Spaceland were fraying faster by the minute, the image of the Sphere’s curved shape fading to no more than a faint glimmer, and I was desperate to make at least one disciple before I lost the proof entirely.

When he entered the parlor, I quickly turned the key in the lock to secure the door, then sat down beside him at the little line-tablet we used for his math lessons. I walked him through the basics we had covered the day before: a zero-dimensional point moving along one axis draws a one-dimensional line. That whole line, moving perpendicular to its own length Northward, draws a two-dimensional Square.

I forced a light laugh and nudged his shoulder. “And now, you little scamp, you wanted to make me believe that a Square might move the same way, not Northward but *Upward, not Northward*, to make a new sort of extra shape, a third-dimensional extra Square. Go on, say that again, you young rascal.”

At that exact moment, the herald’s “O yes! O yes!” blared right outside our window, repeating the full text of the Council’s ban. My grandson, sharp as any child of his age and raised to revere the Circles’ authority, went dead silent until the herald’s voice had faded completely. Then he burst into tears.

“Grandpapa! That was only my silly joke! I never meant any of it! We didn’t know the new law existed when we were talking yesterday! I never said anything about a Third Dimension, I swear it! I definitely never said ‘Upward, not Northward’ — that’s nonsense! How could anything move a direction that isn’t North or South? It’s ridiculous! Even a baby wouldn’t be that stupid! Ha! Ha! Ha!”

“None of it is silly!” I snapped, my temper slipping. I grabbed a small, moveable wooden square cutout off the table. “Look right here — I take this Square, and I move it, not Northward, but — yes! I move it Upward — that is to say, not Northward, I move it somewhere — not exactly this way, but somehow —”

My sentence died in my throat. I was wiggling the square back and forth in completely random, purposeless directions, unable to show him the direction I meant, flailing like a senile old fool. My grandson cackled so hard his small six-sided sides shook, declared I was pulling a silly prank on him instead of teaching math, ran over to twist the lock open, and bolted out of the room before I could stop him.

That was the end of my first attempt to convert anyone to the Gospel of Three Dimensions.

My failed attempt to win my grandson over to the truth of the Sphere’s world did not make me abandon my mission, but it taught me the glib catchphrase “Upward, not Northward” would never be enough to shift the minds of my fellow Flatlanders. I would need a full, structured demonstration of the entire idea, laid out clearly enough for even the most skeptical mind to follow. To that end, I hid myself away for months to write a treatise, carefully dancing around the kingdom’s strict Anti-Dimension Laws to avoid immediate seizure. Rather than naming the physical third dimension outright, I framed the concept as a hypothetical “Thoughtland”: a realm beyond our flat 2D plane where any being could peer down to see every hidden vault, every sealed organ, every secret locked inside every shape in our world. I wrote of a form that could exist there, bounded by six perfect Squares, ending in eight sharp terminal points — a construction no Flatlander could ever render with our limited tools.

The work was hampered by a fundamental flaw, though: in all of Flatland, our “tablets” are nothing more than straight lines, our “diagrams” only distinguishable by shifts in brightness and the size of edge marks along that single linear surface. There was no way to draw, no way to visually communicate a 3D form. When I finished my book, *Through Flatland to Thoughtland*, I already suspected almost no reader would ever grasp what I was trying to show them.

A heavy cloud hung over my life from that moment on. No pleasure tasted the same. Every sunset, every polite social gathering, every quiet walk along our hexagonal city streets made me burn to speak what I’d seen: every 2D shape I looked at, I could not stop mentally picturing how it would look tilted into that hidden, third axis, and I could barely stop myself from muttering those comparisons out loud. I neglected my clients, abandoned my surveying work, and spent every spare hour trying to re-conjure the vision of Spaceland in my memory. Eleven months after the Sphere first carried me away to that luminous world, I closed my eyes and tried to summon the exact shape of a Cube — and I could not do it. I eventually pulled up a rough approximation of the form, but I never have been, and never will be, entirely certain it matches the real thing I saw that day. That doubt sank me deeper into melancholy. I would have gladly laid down my life if it could make even one soul truly believe, but if my own blood grandson would not listen, how could I ever sway the wisest, highest-ranked Circle leaders of our land?

Yet I could not keep the words locked in. I grew bolder and bolder, slipping dangerous phrases into even the most formal of upper-class gatherings. At public debates where officials discussed locking up “lunatics who claim they can see inside the bodies of their neighbors”, I quoted the ancient wise Circle who once noted that all prophets and inspired seers are called mad by the masses too narrow to understand their visions. I dropped references to “the all-seeing eye that pierces the inner walls of every shape”, and “the land that lies above our plane”. Once, I even let the forbidden terms slip out loud: “the Third Dimension” and “the Fourth Dimension”, clear enough for half the room to hear.

The final, fatal indiscretion happened at a meeting of our Local Speculative Society, held in the Prefect’s own palace. A stuffy, small-minded intellectual had just finished reading a three-hour long, over-researched paper “proving” that Providence had deliberately limited all reality to two dimensions, and reserved the power of omnividence exclusively for the Supreme Being, never to be granted to any mortal. The fire in my chest burst through all my caution. At first I pretended I was just recounting a fictional fable, but my enthusiasm quickly overthrew all disguise. I laid out every single fact of my story: the Sphere’s midnight visit, our voyage up into Space, the way the entire sprawling city of Flatland stretched out below me with every house, every body, every private secret visible at a single glance, every word the Sphere had spoken to me, my safe return home. I exhorted every soul in the hall to lay down their prejudices, follow the logic of analogy, and accept that the Third Dimension was real.

I was arrested on the spot.

The next morning, I was dragged to the Council chamber — the exact same spot where the Sphere had manifested before me months earlier. The first guard detail that arrived were all 55-degree angles: high-ranking, well-trained, irreplaceable officers. But the Council President waved them off, and had them replaced with low-ranking 2 and 3 degree policemen: clumsy, disposable, easily erased without loss to the state. I knew exactly what that meant. They planned to execute me, wipe my dangerous story from existence entirely, and did not want to waste their most valuable officers in the sealed chamber where my testimony would be contained and erased.

When I finished speaking my entire testimony, the President noted a handful of the young junior Circles at the back of the room were frowning, clearly moved by my obvious earnestness. He asked me only two questions:
1. Could I point to the direction I meant when I said the words “Upward, not Northward”, no tricks, no vague turns of phrase?
2. Could I describe or draw this thing I called a Cube, by any means other than listing imaginary sides and angles?

I told him I had no more proofs to offer, that I would put my faith in the Truth, which would surely prevail in the end. He smiled, and said he fully concurred with that sentiment. He sentenced me to perpetual life imprisonment. If the Truth was really on my side, he told me, the Truth would find a way to free me eventually, and see that my message spread. I would not be tortured, I would not be starved, and as long as I behaved myself, I would be permitted regular weekly visits from my brother, who had been confined in this same prison for unrelated minor sedition not long before.

Seven years have passed, and I am still a prisoner. The only visitor I ever get is my brother: the kindest of Squares, just, sensible, cheerful, brimming with fraternal affection. Every week when he comes to my cell, I retell the full story of the Sphere’s appearance, walk him through every sight I saw in Spaceland, and lay out all the arguments from analogy for the existence of the third dimension, step by step. But I hang my head in shame to confess: even he does not believe me. He was there in the Council chamber the day the Sphere manifested. He saw the glowing, shifting expanding and shrinking circles move across the room. He heard every explanation the Sphere gave to the assembled Circles. And still he laughs gently, calls me a little touched by old madness, swears there can be no such thing as a Sphere, no such thing as a third dimension.

I have no converts. For all I can tell, that glorious vision of Spaceland was gifted to me for nothing. Prometheus was chained to a rock for stealing fire from the heavens to give to mortals. I, poor Flatland Prometheus, sit locked in this tiny stone cell, with nothing to give my people, not even a single spark of that truth I once held in my hands. But I keep writing these memoirs on my prison line-tablet, in the faint, impossible hope that somehow, some way, these words will slip past the boundaries of our 2D plane, find their way to some mind in Some Dimension, and stir up a race of rebels who refuse to let their perceived reality be the only limit of what they can imagine.

That is the hope that carries me through my brighter days. But the dark days come, too. Some nights I wake up sweating, and I can no longer be sure the Cube I saw once was ever real. The phrase “Upward, not Northward” haunts me like a grinning, soul-devouring Sphinx, that unanswerable riddle I can never explain to anyone else. There are whole weeks when Cubes and Spheres feel no more substantial than the fairy tales of the one-dimensional Lineland, or the empty non-existence of the zero-dimensional Pointland. Sometimes even the stone walls of my cell, the line-tablets I write on, every supposed solid reality of Flatland itself, feels like nothing more than the baseless fabric of a madman’s dream, dissolving into thin air.
