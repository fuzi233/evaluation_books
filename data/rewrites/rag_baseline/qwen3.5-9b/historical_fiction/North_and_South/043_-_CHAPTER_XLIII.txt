The air in Milton hung heavy, a suffocating blanket of coal smoke and industrial grit that seemed to seep into the very marrow of the street. To Mrs. Shaw, a woman whose gentleness usually dictated her actions, this environment was an assault. The noise was deafening, the poverty of the working classes stark and repulsive, and the ostentatious display of the wealthy equally distasteful. Most grievous of all was the sight of the men—whether lord or laborer—all clad in ill-fitting garments that betrayed a lack of care, a symptom of a society unraveling under the weight of its own indifference.

"It is unbearable," Mrs. Shaw declared, her voice tight with anxiety. "Margaret cannot possibly recover her health here. The very atmosphere is hostile to her constitution. We must leave immediately."

Her urgency was born of genuine terror; she feared a recurrence of her own nervous afflictions, a shadow that had haunted her family for generations. She pressed her plea upon Margaret until the younger woman, drained by weeks of grief and exhaustion, finally capitulated. With a sigh that seemed to deflate her entire spirit, Margaret promised to depart the moment Wednesday concluded. That day was already etched in tragedy: the funeral of Mr. Hale, a man who had died far from the homes he knew, leaving behind a widow isolated among strangers. This separation weighed heavily on Margaret, who chided herself bitterly for having succumbed to a stupor during those initial days of sorrow. Had she remained lucid, she reasoned, she might have orchestrated a different outcome.

Before the mournful rites could begin, a letter arrived from Mr. Bell, bearing news that would alter the trajectory of their departure.

"My Dear Margaret," the letter began, its tone oscillating between paternal concern and the casual authority of a man who believed he held the keys to her destiny. "I intended to return to Milton on Thursday, but fate, in its capricious way, has summoned the Plymouth Fellows to a sudden duty. I cannot be absent from my post. Fortunately, Captain Lennox and Mr. Thornton are here. The former appears to be a sensible, well-intentioned soul; he has volunteered to travel to Milton to assist you in your futile search for a will—which, naturally, does not exist, unless you have ignored my previous instructions. Furthermore, the Captain insists on transporting you and his mother-in-law home. Given his wife's fragile state, I foresee no delay beyond Friday. Your steward, Dixon, is trustworthy; he can manage the household finances and the disposal of your belongings until my arrival. Should no will surface, I shall entrust the matter to my local attorney, for I suspect this 'smart' Captain lacks the acumen for business, despite his magnificent moustaches. A sale of your possessions is inevitable; therefore, select what you wish to save, or send a list later.

"There are two final points, and then I shall conclude. You are aware, or your late father certainly was, that upon my death, you inherit my fortune and goods. Do not mistake this for a morbid premonition; I mention it merely to clarify the terms of our future arrangement. The Lennoxes appear fond of you now, though sentimentality is a fickle commodity. Prudence dictates a formal agreement: you shall remit two hundred and fifty pounds annually to them, provided you find the arrangement congenial. (Do not be swindled into paying more for Dixon's services.) This stipulation ensures that if the Captain eventually desires solitude, you possess the means to relocate with your capital and dignity intact—or, heaven forbid, if I have not already claimed you to serve as my housekeeper. Regarding attire, domestic staff, and personal luxuries—including the confections that all young ladies consume until wisdom arrives with age—I intend to consult a trusted confidante to determine your allowance based on your father's estate.

"Now, Margaret, have you not already fled the page, wondering what right this elderly man possesses to dictate your affairs with such cavalier disregard? I am certain you have. Yet, he holds a rightful claim. He has cherished your father for thirty-five years, stood witness to his union, and closed his eyes in death. Moreover, he is your godfather. While his spiritual guidance may be limited by a humble awareness of your superior nature, he seeks to compensate by endowing you materially. Having no kin on earth to mourn him—who will lament the passing of Adam Bell?—his entire existence bends toward this singular purpose. And Margaret Hale is not the type to deny such devotion. Reply within the hour with a mere two lines indicating your decision. But remember: no thanks."

Margaret picked up her pen, her hand trembling violently. She scrawled, *"Margaret Hale is not the girl to say him nay."* In her weakened state, no other words formed in her mind, yet the phrasing gnawed at her conscience. Exhausted by the sheer effort of composition, she collapsed onto the bed, forcing her mind into silence.

"My dearest child," Mrs. Shaw asked softly, sensing the turmoil beneath her daughter's composure, "has this letter distressed you?"

"No!" Margaret whispered, her voice frail. "I shall be stronger once tomorrow passes."

"I am convinced, darling, that you will not find peace until we escape this foul air. I cannot comprehend how you have endured it for two years."

"Where else could I go? I cannot abandon Papa and Mama."

"Do not torment yourself further, my dear. It was undoubtedly for the best, though I lacked the insight to perceive your suffering. Even our butler's wife resides in a finer dwelling than this."

"It is occasionally lovely—in summer. One cannot judge by its current decay. I have been quite happy here," Margaret lied, closing her eyes to halt the conversation.

The house, once a sanctuary, now teemed with a forced comfort. Fires burned brightly in every chamber at Mrs. Shaw's command, and she doted on Margaret with lavish purchases of delicacies and soft linens, items she herself would have coveted. Yet, Margaret remained detached, viewing these gestures solely as evidence of her aunt's sacrifice. Restless despite her weakness, she wandered the rooms throughout the day, methodically separating objects she wished to keep. Dixon followed, ostensibly to gather instructions, but privately tasked with soothing her into rest.

"These books, Dixon, I shall retain. Send the remainder to Mr. Bell; he will appreciate them for their own sake and for my father's memory." She paused, selecting a specific volume. She resumed her perusal of the house, touching familiar objects with a tender reluctance, knowing they were destined for the auction block. Yet, she spoke little. Dixon reported to Mrs. Shaw that Miss Hale appeared oblivious to her surroundings, listening to nothing while talking incessantly to distract herself. The result of such physical exertion was a crushing fatigue by evening, accompanied by a sleep more restorative than she had experienced since hearing of Mr. Hale's death.

By breakfast the following day, Margaret expressed a desire to bid farewell to a few acquaintances. Mrs. Shaw initially objected, citing propriety and the need for church attendance.

"But today is my only opportunity," Margaret insisted. "If Captain Lennox arrives this afternoon, and if we are compelled to leave tomorrow..."

"Oh, yes, we shall depart tomorrow. I am increasingly convinced this air is detrimental to your complexion. Besides, Edith awaits us, and you cannot be left alone at your age. If you insist on these calls, I shall accompany you. Dixon can arrange a carriage, I presume?"

Thus, Mrs. Shaw prepared to escort her niece, bringing Dixon along to manage the shawls and air-cushions. Margaret's face remained a mask of sorrow, refusing to soften into a smile despite the preparations. She dreaded visiting one particular location: Nicholas Higgins' cottage. She hoped her aunt would refuse to alight and traverse the narrow court, where damp laundry hung on lines stretching from house to house, threatening to slap her face with cold water with every gust of wind.

A silent conflict raged within Mrs. Shaw between maternal instinct and social convention; ultimately, convenience prevailed. With repeated warnings to avoid catching a fever, her aunt granted Margaret permission to visit places she had frequented freely in the past.

Nicholas was absent, leaving only Mary and a few of the Boucher children. Margaret reproached herself for her poor timing. Mary possessed a blunt intellect, though her heart was warm; upon understanding Margaret's purpose, she broke down into unrestrained weeping. Confronted with such raw emotion, Margaret found her carefully crafted pleasantries useless. She could only offer vague hopes of reunion and urge Mary to convey her message to her father: that she wished, if possible, for him to visit her after his evening labors.

As she departed, Margaret paused, looking around the modest interior. "I would like a small token to remind me of Bessy."

Mary's generosity flared instantly. "What could we give?" she cried. When Margaret pointed to a simple drinking cup, the one always placed beside Bessy's feverish lips, Mary protested, "Oh, take something better! That only cost fourpence!"

"That will suffice, thank you," Margaret replied, hastening away. The faint glow of satisfaction lingered on Mary's face, a fleeting warmth in the gray day.
