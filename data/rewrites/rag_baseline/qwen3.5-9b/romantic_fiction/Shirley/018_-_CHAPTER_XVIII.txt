The evening hung heavy and still, a suffocating warmth promising a sultry surrender to the heat. Around the descending sun, clouds bloomed in violent purples, casting an Indian rather than an English hue across the horizon. Rosy reflections washed over the hillside, the front of the house, the trunks of ancient trees, the winding roads, and the undulating pastures, turning the landscape into a canvas of fevered light. From the fields, the two girls descended slowly, their footsteps echoing against the silence until they reached the churchyard. Here, the bells had ceased their clamor; the multitude had retreated into the sanctuary, leaving behind a solitude that felt almost sacred.

"How pleasant and calm it is," Caroline murmured, her voice barely rising above the rustle of leaves.

"And how stifling it will be inside the church," Shirley replied, her eyes scanning the gathering twilight. "What a dreary, endless sermon Dr. Boultby will drone on! And how the curates will hammer out their rehearsed orations with mechanical precision! Personally, I would rather rot here than enter that tomb of hypocrisy. Yet, my uncle's anger would be a heavy price to pay for our absence."

"I shall bear the brunt of his wrath; he cannot devour me," Shirley declared, her tone shifting from playful to defiant. "But I shall mourn missing his pungent speech. It will be all sharp logic for the church and all biting satire for the schismatics. He will not forget the Battle of Royd Lane. Nor can I deny myself the chance to hear Mr. Hall's sincere, friendly homily, replete with its racy Yorkshire dialect. But here I must remain. This gray church and its grayer tombs look divine under this crimson glow. Nature is performing her evening prayers; she kneels before those red hills. I see her prostrate on the great steps of her altar, offering supplication for fair nights for sailors on the stormy seas, for travelers lost in deserts, for lambs on the moors, and for unfledged birds in the dense woods. Caroline, I see her clearly now. She is like Eve when she and Adam stood alone upon the earth."

"That is not Milton's Eve, Shirley," Caroline corrected gently.

"Milton's Eve! Milton's Eve! I repeat it. No, by the pure Mother of God, she is not!" Shirley's voice sharpened, cutting through the ambient hum of the insects. "Cary, we are alone; we may speak the truth. Milton was a giant of intellect, but was he good? His brain was right, but what of his heart? He beheld heaven; he looked down upon hell. He saw Satan, and Sin his daughter, and Death their horrific offspring. Serried angels presented their battalions before him; the long lines of adamantine shields reflected the unutterable splendor of heaven back upon his blind eyeballs. Devils gathered their legions in his sight; their dim, discrowned, and tarnished armies marched rank and file before him. Milton attempted to envision the first woman, but Cary, he failed. He saw nothing but a projection of his own mind."

"You are bold to say such a thing, Shirley."

"Not more bold than faithful. It was merely his cook he saw; or perhaps Mrs. Gill, as I have witnessed her, making custards in the sweltering summer heat within the cool dairy, surrounded by rose-trees and nasturtiums framing the latticed window. She prepared a cold collation for the rectors—preserves and 'dulcet creams'—puzzled over which choice offered the greatest delicacy, arranging flavors so they would not clash, creating an elegant sequence of tastes upheld by kindliest change."

"All very well, Shirley," Caroline conceded.

"I must remind him that the first men of the earth were Titans, and Eve was their mother. From her sprang Saturn, Hyperion, Oceanus; she bore Prometheus..."

"Pagan that you are! What does that signify?"

"I say, there were giants on the earth in those days—giants who strove to scale heaven. The first woman's breast, heaving with life on this world, yielded the daring to contend with Omnipotence, the strength to endure a thousand years of bondage, the vitality to feed that vulture-like death through uncounted ages. It was an unexhausted life and uncorrupted excellence, sisters to immortality, capable, after millennia of crime, struggle, and woe, of conceiving and bringing forth a Messiah. The first woman was heaven-born. Vast was the heart from which gushed the wellspring of the blood of nations, and grand the undegenerate head where rested the consort-crown of creation."

"She coveted an apple and was cheated by a snake; but you have mixed Scripture and mythology into such a chaotic stew that no sense remains. You have not yet told me what you saw kneeling on those hills."

"I saw—I now see—a Woman-Titan. Her robe of blue air spreads to the very outskirts of the heath, where yonder flock grazes; a veil white as an avalanche sweeps from her head to her feet, edged with arabesques of lightning. Beneath her breast, I perceive her zone, purple like the horizon itself; through its blush shines the star of evening. I cannot picture her steady eyes, yet I know them: clear, deep as lakes, lifted and full of worship, trembling with the softness of love and the lustre of prayer. Her forehead possesses the expanse of a cloud, paler than the early moon that rises long before darkness gathers. She reclines her bosom on the ridge of Stilbro' Moor; her mighty hands are joined beneath it. Thus kneeling, face to face, she speaks with God. That Eve is Jehovah's daughter, as Adam was His son."

"She is very vague and visionary," Caroline sighed, pulling herself back to reality. "Come, Shirley, we ought to go into the church."

"Caroline, I will not. I will stay out here with my mother Eve, in these days we call Nature. I love her—an undying, mighty being! Heaven may have faded from her brow when she fell in paradise, but all that is glorious on earth shines there still. She is taking me to her bosom, revealing her heart. Hush, Caroline! You will see her and feel as I do if we remain silent together."

"I will humour your whim," Caroline said, though she knew Shirley would likely resume her monologue before ten minutes elapsed.

Miss Keeldar, upon whom the soft excitement of the warm summer evening seemed to work with unwonted power, leaned against an upright headstone. She fixed her eyes on the deep-burning west, sinking into a pleasurable trance. Caroline, moving slightly apart, paced to and fro beneath the rectory garden wall, dreaming in her own quiet way. Shirley had spoken the word "mother." That single syllable ignited a different spark in Caroline's imagination—not the mighty, mystical parent of Shirley's visions, but a gentle human form, the shape she ascribed to her own mother: unknown, unloved, yet desperately longed for.

"Oh, that the day would come when she would remember her child! Oh, that I might know her, and knowing her, love her!" Such was her aspiration.

The longing of her childhood flooded her soul once more. The desire that had kept her awake in her crib, and which the fear of its falsity had nearly extinguished in recent years, relit suddenly, glowing warm in her heart. She imagined her mother coming some happy day, sending for her to her presence, looking upon her fondly with loving eyes, and saying tenderly in a sweet voice, "Caroline, my child, I have a home for you; you shall live with me. All the love you have needed, and not tasted, from infancy, I have saved for you carefully. Come; it shall cherish you now."

A noise on the road shattered Caroline's filial hopes and Shirley's Titan visions. They listened intently, hearing the tramp of hooves. They looked up and saw a glitter through the trees, catching glimpses through the foliage of martial scarlet; helms shone, plumes waved. Silent and orderly, six soldiers rode softly by.

"The same we saw this afternoon," whispered Shirley. "They have been halting somewhere until now. They wish to be as little noticed as possible, seeking their rendezvous at this quiet hour while the people attend church. Did I not say we should witness unusual things ere long?"

Scarcely had the sight and sound of the soldiers vanished when another, somewhat different disturbance broke the night's hush—a child's impatient scream. They turned to look. A man emerged from the church carrying in his arms an infant—a robust, ruddy little boy of some two years old—roaring with all the power of his lungs. He had presumably just awakened from a church-induced slumber. Two little girls, aged nine and ten, followed closely. The influence of the fresh air and the attraction of some flowers gathered from a grave soon quieted the child. The man sat down with him, dandling him on his knee as tenderly as any woman; the two little girls took their places, one on either side.

"Good-evening, William," Shirley said after scrutinizing the man. He had seen her before and appeared to be waiting to be recognized. He now removed his hat and grinned a smile of genuine pleasure. He was a rough-headed, hard-featured personage, not old but very weather-beaten. His attire was decent and clean; that of his children was singularly neat. It was their old friend Farren. The young ladies approached him.
