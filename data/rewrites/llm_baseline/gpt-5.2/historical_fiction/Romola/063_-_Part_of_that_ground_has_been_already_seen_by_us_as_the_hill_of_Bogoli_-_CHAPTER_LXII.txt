About ten o’clock on the morning of the twenty-seventh of February, Florence began to flow as if the stones themselves had been tipped toward one point, and the people—tailors with their shears still in their pockets, bakers with flour ghosting their sleeves, women who had left dough to rise beneath damp linen, boys smelling of smoke and orange peel—were carried in a steady current to San Marco.

It was the last morning of Carnival. Everyone knew a second Bonfire of Vanities was being built before the Old Palace—another hungry pyre to lick up masks and mirrors, ribands and books, the bright trifles that had made the streets sing only yesterday. Yet at this hour the city’s appetite had turned from flame to voice. The Piazza of San Marco was choked, not with dancing, but with bodies pressed close as wheat in a granary, all the more compact for the quiet that held them. The only motion was the swelling pressure of latecomers at every opening, and the stubborn resistance of those already wedged in front, braced shoulder to shoulder as if against a flood.

A semicircular barrier had been set before the church. Within it the Dominican brethren of San Marco were gathering, black mantles folding and unfolding like ravens’ wings as they took their places. The temporary wooden pulpit—fresh boards, resin-scented where the sun warmed them—stood above the church door, empty still, like a baking peel held out waiting for bread.

It was to be climbed by the man whom the Pope’s command had driven from the pulpit of the Duomo; the man whom the other clergy were forbidden to consort with, whom Florentines were forbidden to hear under threat of excommunication—and whom, despite all that, they still came to listen to in his own church, and from whose hands they still, on this very morning, would accept the Communion.

He had said aloud what many had only muttered in kitchens and botteghe, between the scrape of a knife on a board and the thud of a pestle: that a wicked Pope bought into office by bribery was no Christ’s vicar; that curses from such a mouth were broken swords, hilts without blades; that commands contrary to the Christian life must be disobeyed—nay, could not lawfully be obeyed.

But words, however sharp, were not enough when they began to cut into bread.

So long as believing in the Frate promised no visible calamity—so long as it felt like an amulet pinned inside the doublet, a special safety in a dangerous world—Florence asked for no sign. His preaching had been music to which the people marched willingly, in step with their own wishes. Now belief meant immediate blows: merchants hearing their contracts fray; bankers imagining foreign coin refusing their touch; artisans fearing the shutters would come down; the city’s stance among the Italian States trembling; an interdict hanging over Florence like a lid over a pot, suffocating not only trade but marriage, burial, baptism—every domestic rite by which memory was kept warm.

And so, like hunger rising in a house where the larder is failing, one demand had begun to swell in the public throat: What miracle showest thou?

It had come to Savonarola’s ear slowly at first, then faster and faster, until it could be heard even beneath psalmody. Outwardly he answered with assurance: the miracle would come in its fitting time. Inwardly, faith stirred and wavered as all living things do. Yet his speech did not waver. Those who must speak for crowds often speak on yesterday’s faith, hoping it will return tomorrow; and if the crowd’s belly is empty, it cannot be told to wait on a philosopher’s hunger.

The pulpit had been built for this—wood and nails in answer to impatience for a supernatural guarantee.

While the brethren filed in, the crowd’s faces were not yet fixed on the pulpit. They watched the monks instead—picking out familiar cheekbones and family resemblances, remembering whose son had once hawked figs at a corner, whose brother had stitched doublets in the Mercato Vecchio, whose cousin kept a little vineyard beyond the city walls. In Florence, even holiness had kinship lines; even Latin was carried on Tuscan breath.

Then the number of monks was complete. Books fluttered open. The chant began, a thin, persistent wail that threaded itself through the piazza like the keening of wind between cypresses. It was not the chant alone that made the great mass of bodies settle into stillness; it was expectation—felt, shared, and passed along like a quiet signal. People began to say under their breath, “Fra Girolamo must be coming now.”

Silence spread as if a pale solemn light had been set over the upturned faces, and all at once eyes were trained on the empty pulpit.

The next instant, it was not empty.

A figure, covered head to foot in black cowl and mantle, had entered and was kneeling, bent low, face turned away. The monks’ chant went on, and to the eager multitude the time seemed to stretch—like waiting for water to boil when thirst makes every second audible. Yet no one broke the hush. The Frate’s audiences with Heaven still carried electric awe for this mixed crowd; even those who had come with stones in their mind felt their arms slacken, as if the air itself had thickened around their wrists.

At last there was a faint vibration among the multitude—each giving his neighbor an aspen-like touch—as when watchers of the sky see what they hoped for disclose itself without sound. The Frate had risen. He turned toward them and pushed back his cowl a little.

The chant ceased, and for those nearest it was as if the sound that had been filling their ears had abruptly merged into something else: the force of Savonarola’s glance sweeping the piazza in silence.

He stretched out his hands.

They were hands of exquisite delicacy, so refined they seemed less made to grasp than to feel—hands that carried their own kind of speech, as if the part of his soul masked by that strong passionate face had found another language. His face, for all its vigor, was written over now with deeper lines around mouth and brow than forty-four years of ordinary living would carve: lines etched by fasts, by vigils, by the repeated heat and cooling of his own emotion.

At the first outstretched gesture, some in the front ranks dropped to their knees, and here and there a devout disciple farther back followed, as if pulled downward by the gravity of belief. But the greater number stood, stiffly erect. Some resisted the impulse to kneel before an excommunicated man—what if judgment fell on them in that very act? Others held themselves up with scorn and hatred of the ambitious deceiver staging another comedy—yet even they felt an unwilling impotence, like a man caught under the tide of a fashion he despises but cannot stop.

Then the voice came: clear, low at first, laying the Latin on the air as one lays salt on meat, with a steadiness meant to preserve. “Misereatur vestri—”

More knees touched stone.

As the words rose higher and yet clearer, the count of erect heads dwindled. And when he cried, “Benedicat vos omnipotens Deus—” it burst forth in a masculine trumpet-note, as if the blessing itself had to protest its right to be spoken under the clutch of a demon trying to stifle it. It rang to the extremities of the piazza. Under it every head bowed, as wheat bends when the wind passes.

After the blessing he fell to his knees again and hid his face, spent for a moment. Such jets of feeling were necessary to him. He had told the people long ago, “Without preaching I cannot live.” Yet it was a life that shattered him, as fire shatters the earthen pot that holds it too long.

After a few minutes some rose, stiff-legged, rubbing numb hands; but many stayed kneeling, and all watched him with an intentness that made the piazza seem a single creature holding its breath.

Savonarola took up a crystal vessel containing the consecrated Host. The sunlight, struggling through greyness, struck it now and then with pale glints, like a knife flashing above a board. He was about to speak.

“You remember, my children,” he said, “three days ago I besought you—when I should hold this Sacrament in my hand in the face of you all—to pray fervently to the Most High that if this work of mine does not come from Him, He will send a fire and consume me, that I may vanish into the eternal darkness away from His light which I have hidden with my falsity. Again I beseech you to make that prayer, and to make it now.”

A breathless moment followed. Perhaps no one truly prayed; perhaps some tried, in devout obedience, as one tries to chew when the mouth is dry with fear. Most consciousness was seized by the sense that Savonarola was praying—his voice not loud, but distinctly audible in the wide stillness.

“Lord, if I have not wrought in sincerity of soul, if my word cometh not from Thee, strike me in this moment with Thy thunder, and let the fires of Thy wrath enclose me.”

He stopped. He stood motionless with the Mystery in his hand, eyes lifted, the whole of him quivering as if his flesh were a string drawn too tight. No one moved. No one spoke. Even the piazza seemed to listen, and in that listening every small sound—cloth settling on a shoulder, a distant hoof on stone, the faint crackle of a brazier somewhere behind shutters—took on the weight of omen. The city’s own acoustic life, usually so braided with calling vendors, clattering carts, and the cheerful metallic din of work, was reduced to a thin thread. In that thread people heard their fate.

Sunlight, for the last quarter of an hour only piercing the grey in fitful streaks across the convent wall, made some spectators start timidly, as if Heaven were testing its match. Then the light widened. With a gentle quickness, like a smile, brightness poured itself directly onto the crystal vase—set it burning white—and spread over Savonarola’s face with mild glorification, as if the day had chosen him.

An instantaneous shout surged through the piazza: “Behold the answer!”

The warm radiance thrilled through Savonarola’s frame, and so did the shout. In that pulse he tasted untroubled triumph—his last. For a moment the future opened in him like a door: a grander scene to come, an audience representing all Christendom, where he would be sealed again as messenger of supreme righteousness, charged full with Divine strength.

But the strain was more than his body could bear. While the shout still rang, he turned away and disappeared within the church.

When he was gone, the sunlight seemed to lose its singled-out intention. It spread impartially over all things clean and unclean, saint and skeptic, velvet and patched wool. And with the crowd’s movement—knees unbending, feet shuffling, people pushing back toward the openings—voices rose into a confusion in which strong discords and varying scales of laughter told plainly that, in the kneeling and silence, hostility and scorn had only submitted unwillingly to a momentary spell.

From an upper loggia in one of the houses opposite the church, Tito had watched the whole scene with attentive eyes, safe above the press, as if in a theater-box where one could hear the roar below without being touched by it.

“It seems to me the plaudits are giving way to criticism,” he said, turning slightly toward the man beside him. “Nevertheless it was a striking moment, eh, Messer Pietro? Fra Girolamo is a man to make one understand there was a time when the monk’s frock was a symbol of power over men’s minds rather than over the keys of women’s cupboards.”

“Assuredly,” said Pietro Cennini. “And until I have seen proof that Fra Girolamo has much less faith in God’s judgments than the common run of men, instead of having considerably more, I shall not believe he would brave Heaven in this way if his soul were laden with a conscious lie.”
