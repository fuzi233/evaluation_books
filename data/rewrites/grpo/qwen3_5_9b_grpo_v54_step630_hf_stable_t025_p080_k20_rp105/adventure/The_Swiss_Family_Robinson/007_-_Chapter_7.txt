The following day, our first priority was to retrieve the sledge laden with the crockery and baskets we had crafted, located deep within the Calabash Wood. I insisted on keeping the younger boys with their mother, deeming a large party unsuitable for the expedition I intended to lead. Fritz volunteered to accompany me, eager to explore beyond the chain of rocky hills that bordered our known territory.

As we traversed the grove of evergreen oaks, we spotted our sow feasting on acorns. The fright we had inflicted upon her the previous day seemed to have vanished; she appeared not only unharmed but unusually friendly, perhaps viewing us as deliverers from the savage dogs that had threatened her. The forest was teeming with life, the birds undisturbed until Fritz, in a burst of enthusiasm, fired his gun and brought down a blue jay and two parakeets—one scarlet, the other green and gold. Just as he began reloading, a strange, dull thumping sound, reminiscent of a muffled drum, struck our ears. It triggered an immediate alarm, reminding us of the ever-present danger of savages.

We approached the sound with extreme caution, hiding among the thick undergrowth until we reached an open glade. There, perched atop a fallen log, stood a magnificent bird, roughly the size of a rooster, with rich chestnut-brown plumage mottled with dark grey. Distinctive tufts of velvety black feathers, glossed with green, adorned his shoulders. He strutted and wheeled with a strange, stately grace, performing for a small audience of similar birds lacking these elaborate ruffs. After a time, he spread his tail like a fan, stiffened his wings, and began beating them in rapid, rhythmic strokes. The resulting rumble, amplified by the strikes against the hollow log, created a thunderous drumming effect that had initially startled us.

My interest was piqued by this rare spectacle until a shot rang out from behind me. The performance ended abruptly; my overzealous son had transformed a delightful comedy into a tragic slaughter. The enthusiastic drummer fell dead, and his admiring companions scattered in panic. I was deeply annoyed by the cruelty of the interruption and reproached Fritz for firing without permission, lamenting the loss of such a fine specimen of the ruffed grouse.

We placed the bird on the ass, which waited patiently for our return, and pressed on. The sledge remained secure where we had left it. Seizing the opportunity, I resolved to fulfill my original intent: to explore the coastal cliffs and rocky hills that extended along the shoreline, searching for any potential opening into the interior or confirming our isolation. Leaving the Calabash Wood, we advanced across fertile ground rich with manioc, potatoes, and unknown flora, watered by gentle streams.

My attention was drawn to bushes laden with small, white, wax-like berries that stuck to my fingers. Recognizing them as *Myrica cerifera*, I explained to Fritz that by melting and straining these berries, we could produce candles. This prospect brought great joy to my mind, imagining the relief it would offer my wife, who currently had to retire to rest the moment the sun set. Though the greenish wax would be more brittle than beeswax, it burned well and emitted a pleasant fragrance. We gathered enough to fill a canvas bag and continued our journey.

Soon, we encountered another natural wonder: a colony of birds, resembling yellowhammers but plain in brown plumage, sharing a single dwelling. Their nests were clustered around the trunk and branches of a solitary tree, covered by a roof of grass and fibers that shielded them from the elements. The structure resembled a massive bath-sponge, with numerous openings serving as apartments for the community. Twigs protruded from the walls, acting as perches for the young. Thousands of these birds swarmed in and out, including many beautiful parrots that seemed to vie for space with the rightful owners.

Fritz, an expert climber and eager to inspect the nests, ascended the tree. He reached into several empty holes until, finally, his hand was seized and sharply bitten by a strong beak deep within a nest. With a cry, he withdrew his hand, shaking it to alleviate the pain. Recovering, he seized the offending bird, drew it from its retreat despite its shrieks, and stuffed it into his pocket, buttoning his coat quickly. He slid down the tree pursued by a swarm of relatives, who screeched and pecked at him in rage. His prize was not one of the sociable grosbeaks, but a small, green parrot, which he delighted in, planning to tame and teach it to speak.

This colony sparked a lively discussion about social behavior in nature. I compared their communal living to beavers, bees, wasps, ants, and even the coral insect, marveling at their united efforts. Fritz recalled watching ants in his homeland, correcting me gently on the distinction between ant eggs and larvae. I expanded on this, describing the impressive mounds of foreign ants, their internal architecture, and their destructive nature. I detailed the ant-eater, or tamanoir, a formidable creature with a long, toothless snout and a sticky tongue designed to devour the ants, contrasting it with the smaller, arboreal variety. My explanation captivated Fritz, occupying us for a considerable time as we traveled.

Eventually, we reached a grove of tall trees with broad, thick leaves bearing round, sour fruits. Fritz noticed gummy resin exuding from the bark, reminiscent of cherry-tree gum he had collected as a child. Upon scraping some and attempting to soften it, he discovered it possessed elasticity, springing back to its original shape.

"Oh father, only look! This gum is quite elastic! Can it possibly be india-rubber?" he exclaimed.

"What!" I cried, examining it. "A valuable discovery indeed! You are perfectly right!"

"India-rubber," I explained, "or caoutchouc, is a milky resinous juice from trees indigenous to Brazil, Guiana, and Cayenne. The natives used it to coat earthen flasks to create waterproof bottles. It can be molded into various forms, and I am delighted to have it here; I intend to manufacture boots and shoes."

Shortly after, we reached the coconut wood and saw the bay and the promontory we named Cape Disappointment. Passing through, I noticed a smaller palm with a mealy pith, identifying it as the renowned sago-palm. Inside the pith, I found fat worms, recalling that they were eaten as a delicacy in the West Indies. Curiosity overcoming repugnance, I roasted a few over a fire with salt. They smelled temptingly rich and fat. I swallowed one boldly, and to my surprise, found it delicious. Fritz, summoning courage, joined me, and the worm became a savory addition to our baked potatoes.

Encountering a dense thicket blocking our direct path, we diverted toward the sugar-brake near Cape Disappointment, cutting a bundle of sugar-canes for the donkey to carry alongside the bag of wax berries. Returning to the Calabash Wood, we loaded the sledge. The ass, now relieved of his burden, readily dragged the load. We arrived at Falconhurst in the evening to a warm welcome. Our tale was listened to with interest, and the newly acquired green parakeet enchanted the boys. After a grateful supper, we ascended to our tree-castle, exhausted but content.

The idea of candle-making seized the boys' imaginations. The next morning, "candle" was the sole topic of conversation. I agreed to become "chandlers," though privately I harbored doubts regarding the lack of animal fat for a steady flame and the complexity of the process. Nevertheless, I hid my reservations. Under my direction, the boys picked the berries, melted the green wax in a shallow iron vessel, skimmed the pure wax, and dipped wicks prepared by my wife. Fritz hung them on a bush to dry. By repeating the dipping process, the wicks grew into sturdy candles. We hung them to harden, and that same night, three hours after sunset, Falconhurst was brilliantly illuminated for the first time.

My wife praised my ingenuity but requested a method to preserve the cream that constantly went bad. I proposed a plan attributed to the Hottentots. Using a hollowed gourd bottle, we filled it with cream and corked it tightly. The boys then rolled the gourd back and forth in a cradle of sailcloth for half an hour. When opened, a large lump of fresh butter emerged, delighting my wife. Meanwhile, I constructed a clumsy but sturdy cart to replace the sledge, which proved immensely useful for harvesting.

We then turned our attention to landscaping. We planted walnut, cherry, and chestnut trees in parallel rows to form a shady avenue from Falconhurst to Family-bridge, laying down a road to reach Tentholm. We also planted vines around the mangrove roots and other trees to adorn Tentholm. My primary goal for Tentholm was not beauty, but safety; I planted a thick, prickly hedge and mounted two guns on nearby hillocks to defend against wild beasts or savages.

Six weeks of hard labor passed. The boys developed remarkable strength, though their clothes became increasingly ragged. Determined to replenish our wardrobe, I led three boys and myself back to the wreck in the pinnace. We rummaged the hold thoroughly, loading the pinnace with chests of cloth, linen, guns, tables, shutters, and pitch. Before departing, I declared my intention to blow up the brave ship that had carried us from Switzerland. I salvaged a large copper cauldron, securing it to empty casks to ensure it floated. As the fuse burned, a pillar of fire rose from the black waters, and the ship exploded in a sullen roar. We felt a profound sadness, as though losing a dear friend, yet we knew the destruction was necessary.

The next morning, the shore was lined with the remnants of the wreck—planks and beams. I recovered the floating cauldron and stored it under the powder casks. My wife brought news that ducks and a goose had successfully reared families in the river reeds. The sight of these birds inspired Ernest to dream of eating duck and peas again, feeling like civilized mortals. This prompted me to visit Falconhurst, where we found several young trees in our avenue bent by the wind. Resolving to cut bamboos for support, I organized an expedition with my wife and the younger boys.

We brought the cart, drawn by the cow and ass, laden with supplies for a multi-day trip. Passing through our plantations, we revisited the nests of the sociable grosbeak and gathered more wax berries to replenish our candle stock. At the caoutchouc tree, we tapped the bark to collect sap for waterproof boots. Moving forward, we reached a delightful plain bounded by sugar-cane and bamboo, with the calm sea stretching before us. Jack suggested pitching our tent there permanently, but I wisely declined, citing the danger of wild beasts lurking in the nearby thickets. Instead, we made it our headquarters.

While the boys cut bamboo and collected sugar-cane, they decided to gather coconuts. Without the aid of monkeys or land-crabs, they struggled up the smooth, slippery trunks. I intervened, providing them with buskins made of shark's skin and binding ropes around their bodies and the trees. Equipped thus, they climbed successfully. My wife and I watched as they harvested fruit. Ernest, initially lying on the grass, sprang up at a sarcastic remark, climbed a tree, and severed the leafy crest of a cabbage-palm. He offered the prized palm-cabbage to his father, explaining it was worth more than all the nuts combined. He then descended with a nutshell filled with a clear, rosy liquor, which he presented to his mother as a precious wine. We toasted to his health and enjoyed the drink.

As evening fell, our donkey suddenly brayed, pricked up his ears, and galloped off into the bamboo thicket. We chased him briefly, sending the dogs after him, but he vanished into the darkness. Alarmed by the loss of such a valuable beast, I ordered a large fire and instructed the family to sleep with their weapons at hand.

At dawn, finding no sign of the ass, I organized a search party with Jack and the dogs, leaving the elder boys to guard their mother. We followed the hoof prints for an hour before they disappeared, mingled with those of a larger herd. Undeterred, Jack urged me to continue, suggesting that from a hilltop we might spot the herd. We pushed through bushes and swamps until we reached a wide plain where a herd of buffaloes grazed. I resolved to approach them stealthily through a bamboo marsh.

Emerging onto the plain, we faced the herd. They stared inquisitively but did not move. Jack raised his gun, but I checked him, ordering a retreat. However, our dogs, unrestrained, dashed forward and seized a buffalo calf. This triggered a stampede; the herd charged, bellowing and tearing the ground. The leader closed in on us, rendering my gun useless. I fired a pistol, killing the leader, which caused the rest to halt, snuff the air, and flee. The dogs held the calf, struggling to bring it down.

Jack devised a plan. He unwound his lasso, cast it around the calf's hind legs as it reared, and brought it to the ground. We fastened the rope to a bamboo and subdued the animal. I then pierced the cartilage of its nose with a knife and passed a cord through the hole to secure it. Subdued and overawed, the young bull followed us without resistance.

We attended to the dead buffalo, saving the tongue and steaks for later, while the dogs feasted on the rest. Birds of prey soon descended to scavenge, leading to a chaotic contest between the dogs and the intruders. Jack wanted to shoot, but I forbade it, knowing the birds would be useless and the shots ineffective. Satisfied, we decided to return to camp, leaving the search for the ass for another day.

On our way back, I cut down a small bamboo reed, noting its potential for making barrels and candle molds. The young bull, now a beast of burden, carried our goods.
