Louis Moore’s quiet certainty that Mr. Sympson would not be packing his family off from Fieldhead immediately, after their vicious fight over Sir Philip Nunnely, proved perfectly correct. The very next day after their screaming row, a fragile truce was patched up between uncle and niece. Shirley, who could never bear to be anything but warm and hospitable to anyone who crossed her threshold (save for the odious Mr. Donne), begged the whole party to extend their stay a little longer. She asked with such earnest, unmistakeable purpose that they took her at her word without further argument. Mr. Sympson, for his part, could not bring himself to leave her completely unsupervised, free to marry Robert Moore the very second that gentleman managed to make enough of a name for himself to dare press his supposed claim to her hand – a prospect the old man prayed devoutly would never come to pass. So they all stayed.

In the heat of his all-consuming rage against every member of the Moore family, Mr. Sympson had treated Louis with such crude, unprovoked insolence that Louis – who would bear any amount of backbreaking labour or quiet suffering without complaint, but would never tolerate unearned vulgar abuse – had handed his notice in at once. Only the combined pleas of Mrs. Sympson, his own soft, steady affection for his young pupil Henry, and a third, far stronger motive he refused to name, convinced him to resume his post long enough to see the Sympson party safely off Yorkshire soil. It would have been almost impossible, he knew, to tear himself away from Fieldhead just now.

For weeks after that, things settled into a soft, easy calm. Shirley’s health bounced back fully, her old bright, giddy spirits flooding back. Louis had chased every last lingering nervous fear out of her mind; the moment she had laid all her worries in his keeping, every last dark thought had taken wing. She grew as lighthearted and unguarded as a small child who dumps all responsibility for its safety onto its parents, never stopping to wonder if it will ever be hurt. He and William Farren, through whom he made quiet inquiries about the supposedly mad dog Phoebe, both agreed the creature was not rabid at all – it was prolonged, vicious abuse from its master that had driven it feral. The grooms and gamekeepers swore the opposite, insisting the dog was a textbook case of hydrophobia, but Louis turned a deaf ear to their warnings, and only told Shirley the hopeful parts. She believed him, and for all the fear surrounding the bite, it left no lasting mark on her at all.

November slipped by in frost and mist, December arrived. The Sympsons were truly preparing to depart now; they had to be home in time for Christmas. Their trunks were half packed, their travel arrangements made, and they were due to leave in only a few days. One cold winter evening, in the final week of their stay, Louis Moore pulled out the small, blank leather notebook he kept tucked in his desk, and poured all the thoughts he dared not say out loud onto its pages:

* * * * *

She is lovelier than I have ever known her. All the faint, temporary pallor and wasted sharpness that clung to her after the quarrel with her uncle has vanished completely. It is nothing short of magic, watching the boundless energy of youth lift her back to bloom, elastic and bright as a newly unfurled rose.

After breakfast this morning, after I had sat with her, listened to her laugh, and felt her presence hum in every last sentient nerve in my body, I stepped out of her sunlit parlour into the chill of the empty drawing room. I picked up a small gilt volume of lyrical poetry, read one or two short pieces, and I do not know if the magic was in the verse or in me, but my heart swelled soft and warm, my pulse picked up, and I glowed even through the sharp frost seeping through the window panes. I am still young, after all. Though she teases me endlessly for being old and stuffy, I am barely thirty. There are moments where life, for no other reason than my own unspent youth, glows around me in streaks of soft, perfect colour.

It was time to make my way to the schoolroom. That room is a quiet, pleasant place on a winter morning. Sun slants through the low lattice windows, the books are all neatly stacked on their shelves, no stray papers litter the tables, the fire burns bright and clear with no spilled cinders or cold ash piling up. I found Henry there already, and to my quiet delight, he had brought Miss Keeldar with him.

I said she is lovelier than ever, and it is true. A fine, delicate rose flush unfurls across her cheeks, her dark, clear, speaking eyes hold a language no mortal words can translate – the kind of silent wordless communion angels used to share, in the days of "silence in heaven." Her hair was always dark as midnight, fine as spun silk, her neck always fair, smooth, and supple, but now both carry a new, unnameable charm. The tresses drape soft as shadow, the shoulders they fall across move with the effortless grace of a goddess. Once I only saw her beauty, now I feel it, settled deep in my bones.

Henry was reciting his lesson to her, for practice, before he brought it to me to grade. One of her hands rested on the open book, the boy held the other. That child gets far more than his fair share of privileges. He dares to cuddle her, and she lets him, returns his affection a hundredfold. The kindness, the soft indulgence she showers on him is almost too generous. If it went on like this, in a few years, when Henry’s heart was fully grown, he would lay it on her altar just as I laid mine there, long ago.

I saw her eyelid flutter when I stepped into the room, but she did not look up. Lately, she barely ever meets my eye. She has grown quiet, too – she rarely speaks directly to me, and when I am in the room, she says very little to anyone else. On my darker, more bitter days, I tell myself this change is simple indifference, even aversion. On the bright, soft days, I dare to read another meaning into it. I tell myself, if I were her equal in birth and fortune, I could read this shyness as coyness, and this coyness as love. As things stand now, how dare I hope for that? And what could I do with that love, even if I found it?

This morning, I dared to will an hour of uninterrupted time alone with her. I dared to send Henry away, to call solitude to stand guard over us. I walked to the door, called the boy to me, and said, very plainly: "Go play anywhere you like, but do not come back here until I call for you."

Henry did not like being dismissed, I could see it. The boy is young, but thoughtful; his quiet, meditative gaze rests on me strangely sometimes, and I know he half senses what binds me to Shirley, half guesses that the quiet reserve she shows me holds a far dearer pleasure than all the open affection she lavishes on him. That young, half-grown lion would growl at me now and then, for taming his lioness and acting as her keeper, if the habit of our long years of discipline, and his own deep affection for me, did not hold him still. Go, Henry. You must learn to take your share of life’s bitter with every other child of Adam that has come before or will come after. Your fate will not be an exception. Be grateful your love is so small right now, before it can harden into real passion. An hour of petty frustration, a sharp little stab of envy, is all you feel. The searing, tropical heat of full jealousy, the rage of a storm that can break the world – those have not yet touched the quiet, mild clime of your young feelings.

I took my usual seat at the desk, carrying my usual calm, unhurried air. I am blessed with the gift of hiding every wild internal upheaval behind a perfectly still, unreadable face. No one looking at my slow, steady expression could ever guess at the whirlpool raging in my chest, swallowing all my careful prudence and shattering every sensible plan. It is a precious thing, to move peacefully, powerfully, toward what you want, without alarming anyone with a single odd or reckless gesture. I had no intention of uttering a single word of love to her today, no intention of showing even a flicker of the fire that is slowly burning me up. I have never been presumptuous, and I never will be. I would sooner stand up right now, strap my bags to my back, leave Fieldhead forever, and sail across the Atlantic to build a new life cold and barren as the rock the salt tide washes every day, than seem selfish, or greedy, like I am only chasing her fortune. All I wanted this morning, before I left for good, was to look at her close, to read one single line written in the page of her heart. I was determined to know exactly what it was I was walking away from.

I had a handful of quills to sharpen into pens. Most men’s hands would have trembled, with their hearts beating as fast as mine was, but mine worked steady, and my voice, when I spoke, was perfectly firm.

"This day week, you will be completely alone at Fieldhead, Miss Keeldar."

"Yes. I believe my uncle’s plan to leave is settled now, for good."

"He leaves you dissatisfied."

"He is not pleased with me, no."

"He departs exactly as he arrived, no better for this whole journey. That must be mortifying for him."

"I hope the complete failure of all his plans takes away every last urge to try and make new ones for me."

"For all his faults, Mr. Sympson honestly did wish you well, in his own way. Everything he did, everything he planned, he truly believed was for your own good."

"It is very kind of you to defend a man who allowed himself to treat you with such unprovoked insolence."

"I never get upset, or hold a grudge, for someone acting perfectly true to their own nature. That vulgar, violent outburst he had against me, right after he lost his fight with you, was perfectly in character for him."

"So you will cease to be Henry’s tutor, now?"

"I will be parted from Henry for a time – if we both live, I know we will meet again somehow, for we love each other – and I will be cast out of the Sympson family circle for good. Fortunately, this change does not leave me stranded. It is only pushing plans I have been making for years into action earlier than I expected."

"No change ever catches you off guard. I was sure, in your quiet, steady way, you would be prepared for any sudden shift of fate. I always think of you as a lone, watchful, thoughtful archer standing in the middle of a wood. The quiver on your back holds more than one arrow, your bow always has a second string ready to be strung. Your brother is the same way, too. The two of you could go out as homeless hunters into the loneliest western wilderness, and everything would turn out well. You would hew down trees to build huts, clear the forest to plant fields, the buffalo would fall to your rifle shot, and bow its horned head to you before you even had to ask."

"And would any tribe of Blackfoot or Flathead natives offer us a wife, do you think, as part of the bargain?"

"No," she said, hesitating for a second. "I do not think so. The savage is too sordid. I think – that is, I hope – that neither of you would ever share your hearth with someone you could not give your whole heart to."

"How did the wild west get into your head, Miss Keeldar? Have you been walking in my daydreams, without me seeing you? Have you watched my brain work, building that whole future out of nothing but hope?"

She was tearing a spill – a strip of paper for lighting tapers – into tiny little fragments, throwing each morsel one by one into the fire, standing pensively watching them burn to ash. She did not speak.

"How did you learn everything you seem to know about my plans?"

"I know nothing at all. I am only hearing them for the first time right now. I spoke completely at random."

"Your random guess sounds like divination. I will never be a tutor again. I will never take another pupil after Henry, after – well, after the time I have spent here. I will never again sit every day at another man’s table, never be the hanger-on of another family. I am thirty years old now, and I have not been free for a single day since I was ten years old. I have such a burning thirst for freedom, such a deep passion to meet her, know her, call her mine, such a hunger that haunts my days and keeps me up at night, to win her and keep her, that I will not hesitate to cross the Atlantic for her sake. I will follow her deep into virgin, untouched woods. I will never take a savage girl as a slave – she could never be a real wife. I do not know a single white woman I love who would come with me out there – but I am certain Liberty will be waiting for me, sitting under a pine tree. When I call her, she will come to my log house, and I will hold her in my arms."

She could not hear me speak like that and stay completely unmoved, and she was moved. That was what I wanted, to stir her. She could not answer me, she could not lift her eyes to meet mine. I would have been sorry if she could. Her cheeks glowed as if a crimson rose, with the sun shining straight through its petals, had cast all its light across her skin. On the white lid and dark lashes of her downcast eye, trembled all the soft, graceful shame of a feeling that is half pain, half pleasure.

Very soon, she reined her emotion in, pulled every feeling back under perfect control. I could see she had felt her heart rise in rebellion, and was already pulling it back into line, to rule over it. She sat down. I could read the look on her face perfectly. It said: I see exactly where my limit is. Nothing on earth will make me cross it. I know exactly how far I can let my feelings show, and exactly when I have to clasp the book shut and hide them away. I have come as far as a true, proud, undegraded woman can go. Now I will stand rooted here. My heart can break if it is denied what it wants – let it break. It will never dishonour me. It will never dishonour the sisterhood of women I belong to. Suffering before degradation. Death before betrayal.

For my part, I thought to myself: If she were poor, I would be on my knees to her right now. If she were lowly, I would pull her straight into my arms. Her fortune, her high station, are two griffins standing guard over her on either side. Love looks, longs, and dares not come close. Passion hovers around her, and is kept at bay. Truth and devotion are both scared off. There is nothing to lose in winning her, no sacrifice to make. It is all clear, perfect gain – and that is what makes it unimaginably, impossibly hard.

Hard or not, something had to be said. I could not, and would not, sit there silent, with all that beautiful quiet feeling radiating from her, right in front of me. I spoke, still perfectly calm, quiet as my words were, I could hear they fell in a tone that was clear, rounded, and deep.

"Still, I know I will find myself in a very strange place with that mountain nymph Liberty. I suspect she is very much akin to that Solitude I used to woo, back in the days before I knew her, and now I am so desperate to divorce myself from. These Oread nymphs are very odd creatures. They come to you with an unearthly charm, like a starlight evening, and they fill you with a wild but not warm delight. Their beauty is the beauty of spirits, not the beauty of living people – the grace of seasons, of wild landscapes, not of flesh and blood. They have the dewy bloom of dawn, the soft flush of dusk, the quiet peace of the moon, the ever-shifting shape of clouds. I want something different than that. This elfin, unearthly splendour looks cold to my eyes, feels frozen under my touch. I am no poet. I cannot live with abstractions. You, Miss Keeldar, have teased me with your laughing satire before, calling me a material philosopher, saying I only care for solid, tangible things. It is true. I feel material from head to toe, and for all the glory of nature, for all the deep reverence I hold for the wild world, I would far rather look out at it through the soft, human eyes of a loving, lovely wife, than through the wild, unblinking orbs of the greatest goddess on Olympus."

"Juno would never be able to cook a buffalo steak exactly the way you like it," she said.

"She could not. But I will tell you who could: some young, penniless, friendless orphan girl. I wish I could find one just like that – pretty enough for me to fall in love with, with a mind and a heart that suits my taste, not uneducated, honest, and modest. I do not care about fancy accomplishments. But I want the raw, sweet natural goodness inside her that no amount of schooling can ever copy. I can handle any temper Fate throws at me, no matter how hot. For a girl like that, I would be tutor first, then husband. I would teach her my language, my habits, my principles, and then I would reward her with all my love."

"Reward her, lord of creation! Reward her!" she burst out, her lip curling in that sharp, teasing way I adore.

"And I would be repaid a thousand times over."

"If she chose to be, monseigneur."

"And she would choose it."

"You said you would handle any temper Fate gives you. Compulsion is flint and steel to the metal of some souls."

"And love is the spark it strikes out."

"Who cares for a love that is only a spark – that you see for a second, then watch fly up into the air and vanish forever?"

"I have to find my orphan girl. Tell me how, Miss Keeldar."

"Put an ad in the paper. And make sure you add, when you list the required qualifications, that she has to be an excellent plain cook."

"I will find her. And when I do find her, I will marry her."
