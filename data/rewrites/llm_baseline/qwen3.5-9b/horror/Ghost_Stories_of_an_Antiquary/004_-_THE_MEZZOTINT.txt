Some time ago, I believe I told you of an adventure involving a friend named Dennistoun, who pursued works of art for the Cambridge Museum. He kept his experiences private upon returning to England, yet the tale inevitably spread among his acquaintances, particularly reaching the ears of a gentleman presiding over an art museum at another university. It was natural that such a story would weigh heavily on a man whose profession mirrored Dennistoun's; he would be eager to find explanations that rendered the event improbable, thereby sparing himself future anxiety. Indeed, it offered a measure of comfort to reflect that his duties did not involve acquiring ancient manuscripts—that was the domain of the Shelburnian Library. While those authorities might scour the obscure corners of the continent for such treasures, Mr. Williams was content to focus on expanding his museum's already unparalleled collection of English topographical drawings and engravings. Yet, even such a humble and familiar department possesses its dark corners, and to one of these, Mr. Williams was unexpectedly led.

Those with even a passing interest in acquiring topographical prints know that one London dealer is indispensable to their research. Mr. J.W. Britnell publishes, at frequent intervals, admirable catalogues of a vast and shifting stock of engravings, plans, and old sketches of English and Welsh mansions, churches, and towns. These catalogues formed the alphabet of Mr. Williams' trade. However, since his museum already held an immense accumulation of topographical pictures, he was a regular rather than a copious buyer. He relied on Mr. Britnell primarily to fill gaps in the ranks of his collection rather than to supply rare masterpieces.

In February of the previous year, a catalogue from Mr. Britnell's establishment appeared on Mr. Williams' desk, accompanied by a typewritten note from the dealer itself:

> DEAR SIR, We beg to call your attention to No. 978 in our accompanying catalogue, which we shall be glad to send on approval.
>
> Yours faithfully, J. W. BRITNELL.

Turning to number 978 in the catalogue was a task of mere moments for Mr. Williams. The entry read:

*978.—Unknown. Interesting mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. £2 2s.*

It was hardly exciting, and the price seemed steep. Nevertheless, trusting Mr. Britnell's judgment of both his business and his client, Mr. Williams sent a postcard requesting the item on approval, alongside other engravings listed in the same catalogue. Thus, he moved on to his daily routine without much anticipation.

Parcels invariably arrive a day later than expected, and Mr. Britnell's delivery proved no exception. It reached the museum via the Saturday afternoon post, after Mr. Williams had finished his work. Consequently, the attendant brought it to his rooms in the college so that he need not wait over the weekend to inspect it and return unwanted items. When Mr. Williams entered for tea with a friend, there it lay.

The sole item of interest was the large, black-framed mezzotint described above. Further details must be given, though I cannot hope to convey the image's appearance as vividly as it remains in my memory. Nearly identical duplicates can be found in many old inn parlors or the passages of untouched country houses today. It was a mediocre mezzotint—a form of engraving arguably the worst of all. It depicted a full-frontal view of a modest manor house from the previous century, featuring three rows of plain sash windows flanked by rusticated masonry, a parapet adorned with balls or vases at the corners, and a small central portico. Trees stood on either side, and a broad expanse of lawn stretched before the house. The legend *A. W. F. sculpsit* was engraved on the narrow margin, with no other inscription. The entire composition suggested the handiwork of an amateur. Mr. Williams could scarcely fathom how Mr. Britnell dared attach a price of £2 2s. to such an object. Turning it over with considerable disdain, he discovered a paper label on the reverse; however, the left half had been torn away. Only the ends of two lines remained: the first bore the letters *ngley Hall*; the second, *ssex*.

Perhaps it would be worthwhile to identify the location using a gazetteer, allowing him to return the item to Mr. Britnell with comments on the dealer's poor judgment.

He lit the candles, as darkness had fallen, prepared tea, and served his companion, with whom he had been playing golf earlier (a pursuit indulged in by the authorities of the university I describe). Tea was consumed amidst a discussion of strokes and luck, topics familiar to golfers but tedious to the non-participant.

Eventually, the conclusion was reached that certain shots could have been improved and that neither player had enjoyed the requisite degree of fortune. It was then that the friend—let us call him Professor Binks—picked up the framed engraving and asked, "What's this place, Williams?"

"Just what I'm trying to determine," replied Williams, retrieving a gazetteer from the shelf. "Examine the back. Something like 'Somethingley Hall,' located in either Sussex or Essex. Half the name is missing. Do you recognize it?"

"It comes from that fellow Britnell, doesn't it?" said Binks. "Is it for the museum?"

"Well, I'd probably buy it for five shillings," admitted Williams. "But for some inexplicable reason, he demands two guineas. I can't understand why. It's a dreadful engraving, lacking even figures to lend it life."

"It's certainly not worth two guineas," Binks countered. "Though I don't think it's poorly executed. The moonlight seems quite effective to me. Besides, I thought there were figures, or at least one, just on the edge in the foreground."

"Let's check," said Williams. "True, the lighting is surprisingly skillful. Where is your figure? Ah, yes! Just the head, right at the very front of the picture."

Indeed, there it was—scarcely more than a black smudge on the extreme edge of the print. It was the head of a man or woman, heavily wrapped, facing away from the viewer and gazing toward the house.

Williams had failed to notice it previously.

"Still," he remarked, "even if it is more competent than I realized, I cannot spend two guineas of museum funds on a depiction of a place I do not know."

Professor Binks had work to attend to and departed shortly thereafter. For nearly an hour prior to Hall time, Williams vainly attempted to identify the subject of his picture. "If the vowel preceding the 'ng' had survived, it would have been simple," he mused. "As it stands, the name could range from Guestingley to Langley, and there are countless possibilities ending similarly; moreover, this rotting book lacks an index of terminations."

College Hall convened at seven. Needless to dwell on this, especially as he encountered colleagues who had also played golf, exchanging words entirely unrelated to the supernatural. Following dinner, an hour or more passed in the common room. Later, a few retreated to Williams' rooms, undoubtedly engaging in whist and smoking. During a pause in these activities, Williams casually picked up the mezzotint from the table, ignoring it visually, and handed it to a guest mildly interested in art, recounting its provenance.

The gentleman handled it carelessly, glanced at it, and then spoke with genuine interest: "It's actually a very fine piece of work, Williams; it captures the essence of the Romantic period perfectly. The light is admirably managed, and the figure, though somewhat grotesque, is remarkably imposing."

"Yes, isn't it?" replied Williams, busy serving whisky-and-soda to others and unable to approach the view again.

By this time, it was late, and the visitors began to depart. Once they were gone, Williams attended to correspondence and minor tasks. Finally, well past midnight, ready for bed, he extinguished his lamp after lighting his bedroom candle. The picture lay face-up on the table where the previous guest had placed it. As he lowered the lamp, it caught his eye. What he saw nearly caused him to drop the candle; he declared that had he been plunged into darkness at that instant, he would have suffered a fit. Fortunately, clarity returned, allowing him to place the light on the table and examine the print closely. It was undeniable—absolutely certain, though rankly impossible. In the center of the lawn before the unknown house stood a figure absent at five o'clock that afternoon. It crawled on all fours toward the structure, enveloped in a strange black garment bearing a white cross on the back.

Uncertain of the proper course of action, Mr. Williams simply acted. He seized the picture by one corner and carried it across the passage to a second set of rooms he occupied. There, he locked it inside a drawer, barred the doors of both sets of rooms, and retired to bed. Before sleeping, he drafted and signed a detailed account of the extraordinary transformation the picture had undergone since arriving in his possession.

Sleep came late, but he found solace in knowing the picture's behavior was corroborated by another witness. If the previous observer had not seen the same phenomenon, he might have suspected a defect in his own vision or sanity.
