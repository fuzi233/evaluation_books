March brought a chill that cut deeper than the winter frost, settling into the marrow of Florence as the Carnival faded into memory. One morning, near the end of the month, Tito descended the marble stairs of the Old Palace, driven by a mission of critical weight toward San Marco. Yet, he eschewed the direct route—a simple, slightly curved line connecting the palace to the basilica. Instead, he chose a circuitous path leading through the Piazza di Santa Croce, anticipating a surge of humanity spilling forth from the great church following the early Lenten sermon.

Inside Santa Croce, the atmosphere had grown electric. For weeks, the daily sermons had drawn the largest crowds the city had seen in months. Even within the confines of San Marco, where Savonarola preached, the air had grown thick with hostility; the Signoria, obeying a stern papal directive threatening immediate interdict, had silenced the friar, branding him a "wretched worm" and a "monstrous idol," demanding he seek pardon in Rome. Consequently, the most thrilling Lenten spectacle was no longer the friar's own words, but the vitriolic rebuttals hurled against him. This fervor peaked at Santa Croce, where the Franciscan assigned to deliver the Quaresimal sermons had proposed a terrifying gambit: to validate his arguments by walking through fire alongside Fra Girolamo. The schismatical Dominican had boldly claimed his prophetic doctrine would be proven by a miracle at the opportune moment. Now, that moment had arrived. If Savonarola emerged unscathed, the divine origin of his teachings would be undeniable; if the flames consumed him, his falsehood would be exposed. To ensure the logical premise held, the Franciscan had declared himself willing to share the pyre, offering his own flesh as the necessary minor premise for this high-stakes logic.

Savonarola, true to his nature, remained oblivious to these pulpit assaults. However, the zealot presiding at Santa Croce was none other than Fra Francesco di Puglia. The previous year, at Prato, he had faced a similar challenge from Savonarola's devoted follower, Fra Domenico, but had been recalled by superiors before the rhetoric boiled over into violence. Upon hearing of this renewed threat, honest Fra Domenico—who was currently preaching to women in the Via del Cocomero—immediately seized the gauntlet for his master, declaring his readiness to walk through the fire with Fra Francesco. The populace, initially captivated by what appeared to be a swift and conclusive method of debate, began to lose interest once Savonarola intervened. Keenly aware that mere discussion posed a tangible danger, he commanded Fra Domenico to retract his acceptance and sever ties with the affair. The Franciscan accepted this withdrawal gracefully, noting he had challenged the Friar directly, not a subordinate.

Following this resolution, public enthusiasm for the Lenten sermons waned. Yet, that morning, as Tito threaded his way through the Piazza di Santa Croce, he found exactly what he anticipated: a dense throng exiting the church. Rather than dispersing, the crowd coalesced near the entrance of the Franciscan monastery. Tito moved with a careless, leisurely gait, yet his eyes scanned the monastic doors with predatory precision, expecting a significant development.

The crowd's focus, however, rested elsewhere. Dominating the foreground was a massive placard, affixed by order of the Signoria and covered in legible official script. Curiosity was momentarily stifled by the fact that the document was primarily in Latin. While nearly everyone knew the gist of its contents, a gnawing desire for exactitude drove them to scrutinize the text, creating an irritating sense of intellectual superiority over their neighbors' inability to decipher the learned tongue. Mere familiarity with Latin phrases picked up from sermons offered little aid when confronted with written syntax, especially given the chaotic state of orthography among the literate masses. Furthermore, those closest to the board often lacked the requisite education entirely.

"It is the Frate's doctrines that he must prove by being burned," observed Goro, a prominent local figure standing among the foremost observers. "The Signoria has taken matters into its own hands; the writing confirms it. It aligns perfectly with what the Padre has been preaching."

"Nay, Goro," corrected a sleek shopkeeper with a tone of pity. "Thou hast twisted thy legs into hose. The Frate must prove his doctrines by *not* being burned. He is to walk through the flame and emerge sound and whole on the other side."

"Yes, yes," chimed in a young sculptor, wearing his white-streaked cap and tunic with jaunty confidence. "But Fra Girolamo objects to the ordeal. Being sound and whole already, he sees no necessity to walk through fire to remain in that condition. He leaves such trivialities to Fra Domenico."

"Then I say he flinches like a coward!" Goro retorted in a wheezy treble. "Suffocation! That was his trick at the Carnival. He had us gathered in the Piazza to witness lightning strike him, and nothing happened."

"Cease that bleating," interrupted a tall shoemaker, stepping forward with bunches of slippers draped over his shoulders. "It appears to me, friend, that you possess the wisdom of a calf with water on its brain. The Frate will flinch from nothing. Perhaps he says nothing beforehand, but when the hour strikes, he will walk through the fire without requesting a grey-frock to accompany him. Though, I would give a shoestring to understand what this Latin actually states."

"There is far too much of it," the shopkeeper sighed. "Unless I'm mistaken, I can guess the gist. Is there no scholar present?" he added, his voice tinged with disdain.

A collective shift of heads revealed Tito approaching from behind.

"Here is one," the young sculptor smiled, lifting his cap.

"He is the secretary of the Ten," the shopkeeper announced, bowing respectfully—a gesture rarely afforded Florentines save to the highest dignitaries. "He is undoubtedly heading to the convent. Make way." The exceptional reverence was owed to the splendor of Tito's appearance; his black mantle, fastened with a golden fibula, resembled a royal robe, and his velvet cap seemed an entirely unique headgear. The hardening of his cheeks and mouth, the primary alteration to his visage since arriving in Florence, lent his beauty a sharper, more masculine edge. Tito acknowledged the greeting with a nod and a lift of his own cap.

"Thank you, my friend. I merely wished, as you did, to inspect the text at the base of this placard," Tito said smoothly. "Ah, as I suspected. I was informed that the government permits anyone wishing to subscribe their name as a candidate to enter the fire—an act of liberality worthy of the magnificent Signoria—while reserving the right to select the participants. Doubtless, many believers will be eager to sign. What is it to enter the fire for one whose faith is firm? A man fears fire because he believes it will consume him; but if he believes otherwise?" Tito lifted his shoulders, pausing for rhetorical effect. "For this reason, I have never hesitated to trust the Frate when he stated he would enter the fire to prove his doctrine. In his place, if you believed the fire would not burn you, which of you, my friends, would not step into it as readily as you would walk along the dry bed of the Mugnone?"

As Tito surveyed his audience, a transformation rippled through them, akin to an eager dog sensing a pungent scent. With the prospect of burning becoming practical, few rushed to commit to a broad philosophical stance on faith and fire. The scene might have overwhelmed someone lacking Tito's command of gravity.

"Then, Messer Segretario," the young sculptor ventured, "it seems to me Fra Francesco is the greater hero, for he offers to enter the fire for the truth, certain that the fire will burn him."

"I do not deny it," Tito replied blandly. "Yet, if it transpires that Fra Francesco is mistaken, he will have been burned for the wrong cause, and the Church has never counted such victims among martyrs. We must suspend judgment until the trial truly commences."

"It is true, Messer Segretario," the shopkeeper murmured with subdued impatience. "Will you favor us by interpreting the Latin?"

"Assuredly," Tito smiled. "It expresses the specific conclusions or doctrines the Frate teaches, which the trial by fire aims to verify as true or false. They are doubtless familiar to you. First, 'Ecclesia Dei indiget renovatione': the Church of God requires purification or regeneration."

"It is true," several voices chorused.

"That implies priests ought to lead better lives; no miracle is needed to prove that. That is precisely what the Frate has always proclaimed," the shoemaker noted.

"_Flagellabitur_," Tito continued. "It will be scourged. _Renovabitur_: it will be purified. _Florentia quoque post flagellam renovabitur et prosperabitur_: Florence also, after the scourging, shall be purified and shall prosper."

"That means we regain Pisa," the shopkeeper declared.

"And recover the wool from England as we once did, I should hope," added an elderly man in a traditional beretta, breaking his silence. "There has been sufficient scourging with the collapse of our trade."
