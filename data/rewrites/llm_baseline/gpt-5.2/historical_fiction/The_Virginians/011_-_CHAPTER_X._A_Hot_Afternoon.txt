General Braddock and the other gentlemen of consequence, being at last bestowed in the several chambers of Castlewood, the house settled into that post-feast hush when the very boards seem to sweat with heat and wine. In the boys’ room, however, the air was quick with indignation. Through the open casement came the night’s small rural choir—tree-frogs keeping time in the wet grass, a far whippoorwill calling as if it owned the dark, and, nearer, the steady breathing of the plantation: a cart-wheel creak, a bucket on a well-chain, the soft pad of bare feet where the servants moved like shadows. All those honest sounds—soothing in ordinary seasons—only sharpened the brothers’ sense that something monstrous had been spoken at their own table.

They would not bear such a marriage—no. Was the representative of the Marquises of Esmond to marry the younger son of a colonial family, a man bred to measuring acres like so much cloth upon a counter? Castlewood itself—Castlewood, and they at nineteen—handed over to the tender mercies of a stepfather of three-and-twenty! Harry, whose whole nature ran to ardour, was for bursting straight into his mother’s chamber that instant, where her black maids were even now taking from her throat and wrists the simple jewels she had worn in compliment to the feast, and protesting the match in language that would scorch. He would tell her they would ride home, live upon their little property there, and leave her forever, if this unnatural union came to pass.

George, pale by habit and proud by constitution, advocated another method; and as he unfolded it, his voice had that cold precision with which he would later recite Latin or a grievance.

“Our mother,” he said, “can’t marry a man with whom one or both of us has been out on the field—who has wounded us or killed us, or whom we have wounded or killed. We must have him out, Harry.”

Harry caught at the proposition as a starving man catches at bread. The logic—dark, childish, yet in their code unanswerable—seemed to him the very perfection of sagacity.

“No, George,” he breathed. “You are right. Mother can’t marry our murderer; she won’t be as bad as that. And if we pink him, he is done for. Cadit quaestio, as Mr. Dempster used to say. Shall I send my boy with a challenge to Colonel George now?”

“My dear Harry,” George returned, with a complacent recollection of Quebec and what he had there called an affair of honour, “you are not accustomed to affairs of this sort.”

“No,” Harry owned, with a sigh that was half envy, half worship.

“We can’t insult a gentleman in our own house,” George continued, with the majesty of one laying down law. “The laws of honour forbid such inhospitable treatment. But, sir, we can ride out with him—and as soon as the park gates are closed, we can tell him our mind.”

“That we can, by George!” cried Harry, grasping his brother’s hand. “And that we will, too. I say, Georgy—” Here his face reddened to the roots of his hair, and George, with a lift of his brow, required him to proceed.

“This is my turn, brother,” Harry pleaded. “If you go the campaign, I ought to have the other affair. Indeed, indeed, I ought.” It was promotion he begged—this chance to stand first, even for a moment, in the family’s quarrel.

“Again the head of the house must take the lead, my dear,” George said superbly. “If I fall, my Harry will avenge me. But I must fight George Washington, Hal; and ’tis best I should—for indeed I hate him the worst. Was it not he who counselled my mother to order that wretch, Ward, to lay hands on me?”

“Ah, George,” the more peaceable brother ventured, “you ought to forget and forgive.”

“Forgive? Never, sir, as long as I remember. You can’t order remembrance out of a man’s mind; and a wrong that was a wrong yesterday must be a wrong to-morrow. I never, of my knowledge, did one to any man, and I never will suffer one, if I can help it.” The old Bordeaux of their grandsire had set a flush in George’s ordinarily pale cheeks; and his words came fast, like a knife drawn and used without pause. “I think very ill of Mr. Ward, but I don’t think so badly of him as to suppose he will ever forgive thee that blow with the ruler. Colonel Washington is our enemy—mine especially. He has advised one wrong against me, and he meditates a greater. I tell you, brother, we must punish him.”

Harry, docile as ever before his elder, admired the rapid declamation and the high carriage, and prepared himself to follow where George led. Yet even in that hot room, with the night sounds ticking steadily outside like a clock of crickets, George found breath to issue injunctions: Harry must be civil to all guests while they remained under their mother’s roof on the morrow. The courtesy was to be a sheath for the blade.

Good manners and an aversion to carrying tales beyond the threshold forbid the naming of the first of Madam Esmond’s guests to sink beneath the weight of her hospitality; it is enough to say that in that century gentlemen took drunkenness as lightly as they took snuff. Let one imagine the descendants of Messrs. Talmadge and Danvers—then young aides-de-camp—reeling to their beds, supported by sympathetic negroes, and their vinous General, too stout a toper to be conquered by a half-dozen bottles of Bordeaux, conducted to his chamber by the young gentlemen of the house and soon sleeping the sleep Bacchus grants. The mistress of Castlewood watched all this without surprise or horror; and was up early to provide what the weather and the previous night required—cooling drinks for hot palates, carried by servants to the chambers where men moaned softly like sick children.

At breakfast, one English officer rallied Mr. Franklin, who took no wine and refused the morning’s cool draught of toddy. The young fellow said the disease was pleasant and the remedy delicious, and laughingly proposed to continue repeating them both. Colonel Washington—General Braddock’s new American aide—sat quite sober, serene, and watchful, as if the very clink of glasses were a kind of enemy. The British vowed they must take him in hand and teach him the ways of the English army; but the Virginian answered gravely that he did not care to learn that portion of the military education.

Madam Esmond, occupied with the great dinner and the great breakfast after it, had little leisure to watch her sons; but she saw at least that George was scrupulously polite to her favourite, Colonel Washington, as to all the others under her roof. The politeness was the thinner for being exact.

Before Mr. Braddock took leave, he asked a private audience of the lady of the house, and formally offered to take her son into his family. Arrangements were settled for George’s departure; and Madam Esmond—who had seen bottles do as much damage as bullets in her long life—showed no squeamish terrors about the bottle, though she had witnessed it as among the most certain dangers George would meet.

“Mr. Braddock is a perfect fine gentleman in the morning,” she said stoutly to her aide-de-camp, Mrs. Mountain; “and though my papa did not drink, ’tis certain that many of the best company in England do.”

The jolly General shook hands with George, bade him welcome, and ordered him to be in attendance at Frederick three days hence; shortly after which the expedition would set forth.

Then the great coach was called again; the General’s escort pranced round it; other guests and their servants went to horse. Madam Esmond attended his Excellency to the steps of the verandah; her sons followed and stood on each side the coach-door. The guard trumpeter blew a shrill blast that split the heavy air; the negroes shouted, “Huzzay, and God sabe de King,” as Mr. Braddock took leave and rolled away toward headquarters, the wheels grinding the gravel with that cheerful brutality of vehicles carrying men to war.

As the boys turned back up the steps, there stood the Colonel once more taking leave of their mother. No doubt she had recommended George to his namesake’s care; for Washington said, “With my life. You may depend on me,” as the lads returned to the porch. He was booted and ready to depart.

“Farewell, my dear Harry,” he said. “With you, George, ’tis no adieu. We shall meet in three days at the camp.”

Both young men were going toward danger, perhaps death; and the widow’s face, which could command a dining-room as if it were a battalion, softened and trembled now. Colonel Washington’s own expression was grave with those unspoken things which, in any language, refuse translation into common words—attachment, gratitude, regret.

George Warrington watched his mother’s emotion and interpreted it with a pang of malignant scorn. “Stay yet a moment, and console our mamma,” he said steadily, “only the time to get ourselves booted, and my brother and I will ride with you a little way, George.”

He had already ordered horses. The three young men were soon under way, negro grooms behind them. Mrs. Mountain, who knew she had made mischief between them and trembled for the issue, felt a vast relief that Mr. Washington was gone without open quarrel and, at least, without an open declaration of love to their mother.

No man could be more courteous than George Warrington upon the road. His neighbour and namesake, pleased and surprised at this altered behaviour, yielded to the softening influence of long friendship with the Esmond family, to the necessity of future fellowship, and to the tender adieux just exchanged at Castlewood. He was more than usually friendly. George, for his part, seemed gay and easy, his words falling smoothly as a knife falls smoothly into a ripe peach.

It was Harry who was melancholy now. He rode silent and wistful by his brother, keeping away from Colonel Washington, to whose side he had always pressed eagerly before. If the honest Colonel marked the change, he likely attributed it to Harry’s affection for George and his anxiety on the eve of their parting.

They spoke of the war, and of how the campaign must surely end. None of the three doubted success. Two thousand veteran British troops with their commander must overcome whatever force the French could bring, if only they moved in decent time. The ardent young Virginian soldier held an immense respect for the valour and tactics of regular troops. King George II had no more loyal subject than Mr. Braddock’s new aide-de-camp.

So they rode amicably until they reached a rude log-house called Benson’s, where, according to the custom of the country, the proprietor did not disdain to accept money for the hospitalities provided. A recruiting station was here; some officers and men of Halkett’s regiment assembled; and Colonel Washington supposed the lads would take leave of him at this point.

Their horses being baited, they entered the public room and found a rough meal laid out for such as were disposed to partake. Heat lay upon the place like a blanket. The room smelt of sun-baked pine, sweat-damp leather, horse, and spirits—molasses sweetness from the rum, sharp bite of raw whiskey, and the green bruised scent of mint where some hand had tried to make the drink seem civilized. A great bowl of toddy sat like a small brown pond in the centre, its surface pricked with bits of citrus peel, the sugar not wholly melted, the liquor breathing up a warm, heady steam. If there was any music, it was the clink of metal against wood, boots on planks, a fly’s persistent drone, and outside, the ceaseless rasp of summer insects—the acoustic country’s steady sermon against human quarrels.

George Warrington entered with a particularly lively air; poor Harry’s face was white and woebegone.

“One would think, Squire Harry, ’twas you who was going to leave home and fight the French and Indians, and not Mr. George,” said Benson, leaning in the doorway.

“I may be alarmed about danger to my brother,” Harry answered, forcing steadiness, “though I might bear my own share pretty well. ’Tis not my fault that I stay at home.”

“No, indeed, brother,” cried George, too quickly.

“Harry Warrington’s courage does not need any proof!” said Mr. Washington.

“You do the family honour by speaking so well of us, Colonel,” George returned, with a low bow. “I dare say we can hold our own, if need be.”

While he vaunted, Harry looked—truth to tell—by no means courageous. As his eyes met his brother’s, he read there an announcement that chilled him.

“You are not going to do it now?” he whispered.

“Yes, now,” George replied, very steadily.

“For God’s sake, let me have the turn. You are going on the campaign; you ought not to have everything—and there may be an explanation, George. We may be all wrong.”

“Psha—how can we? It must be done now. Don’t be alarmed. No names shall be mentioned—I shall easily find a subject.”

Under the porch sat two of Halkett’s officers, whom the Warringtons knew, with the Virginian toddy-bowl before them.

“What are you conspiring, gentlemen?” cried one. “Is it a drink?”

By the thickened cheer of their voices and the flushed skin at their temples, it was plain they had already been engaged in drinking that morning.

“The very thing, sir,” George said gaily. “Fresh glasses, Mr. Benson! What, no glasses? Then we must have at the bowl.”

“Many a good man has drunk from it,” said Benson; and the lads, one after another, bowing first to their military acquaintance, touched their lips to the rim. The liquor did not seem much diminished for their drinking, though George especially assumed a toper’s airs and declared it delicious after the ride. He called out to Colonel Washington, who was at the porch, to join his friends and drink.

The lad’s tone had an edge: it resembled the manner lately adopted by him which had so chafed Mr. Washington. The Colonel bowed and said he was not thirsty.

“Nay, the liquor is paid for,” George insisted; “never fear, Colonel.”

“I said I was not thirsty. I did not say the liquor was not paid for,” the Colonel answered, drumming with his foot.

“When the King’s health is proposed, an officer can hardly say no,” cried George. “I drink the health of his Majesty, gentlemen. Colonel Washington can drink it or leave it. The King!”

This was a point of military honour. Captain Grace and Mr. Waring, both of Halkett’s, drank “The King.” Harry Warrington drank “The King.” Colonel Washington, with eyes that seemed to harden and brighten at once, swallowed a slight draught from the bowl.

Then Captain Grace proposed “The Duke and the Army,” which toast likewise had no gainsaying. Colonel Washington had to swallow “The Duke and the Army.”

“You don’t seem to stomach the toast, Colonel,” George said.

“I tell you again, I don’t want to drink,” the Colonel replied. “It seems to me the Duke and the Army would be served all the better if their healths were not drunk so often.”

“You are not up to the ways of regular troops as yet,” Captain Grace said, thickly.

“May be not, sir.”

“A British officer,” continued Captain Grace, with great energy and doubtful articulation, “never neglects a toast of that sort, nor any other duty. A man who refuses to drink the health of the Duke—hang me, such a man should be tried by a court-martial!”

“What means this language to me? You are drunk, sir!” roared Colonel Washington, starting up and striking the table with his fist so that the bowl’s surface quivered and the scent of spirits rose in a fresh, aggressive wave.

“A cursed provincial officer say I’m drunk!” shrieked Grace. “Waring, do you hear that?”

“I heard it, sir!” George Warrington said promptly. “We all heard it. He entered at my invitation—the liquor called for was mine; the table was mine—and I am shocked to hear such monstrous language used at it as Colonel Washington has just employed towards my esteemed guest, Captain Waring.”

“Confound your impudence, you infernal young jackanapes!” bellowed Colonel Washington. “You dare insult me before British officers, and find fault with my language? For months past I have borne with such impudence from you, that if I had not loved your mother—yes, sir, and your good grandfather and your brother—I would—I would—”

His words failed him. He stood for a moment quivering, purple with wrath, speechless before his young enemy, while the tavern’s summer noises went on outside with cruel indifference—cicadas sawing, a horse stamping, a distant axe-blow in some clearing like the tick of fate.

“You would what, sir?” George asked very quietly. “If you did not love my grandfather, and my brother, and my mother. You are making her petticoat a plea for some conduct of yours—you would do what, sir, may I ask again?”

“I would put you across my knee and whip you, you snarling little puppy, that’s what I would do!” cried the Colonel, breath found again for fury.

“Because you have known us all our lives, and made our house your own, that is no reason you should insult either of us!” Harry burst out, starting up. “What you have said, George Washington, is an insult to me and my brother alike. You will ask pardon, sir!”

“Pardon?”

“Or give us the reparation that is due to gentlemen,” Harry insisted, his voice quivering as his hand clenched.

The stout Colonel’s heart smote him. To be at mortal quarrel with lads he had loved—boys whose voices he had heard break, whose heads he had patted, whose mother’s table had been his refuge—was misery. As Harry stood with fair hair and flushing cheeks, an immense tenderness filled Washington.

“I—I am bewildered,” he said. “My words, perhaps, were very hasty. What has been the meaning of George’s behaviour to me for months back? Only tell me, and perhaps—”

But the evil spirit was awake and victorious in young George Warrington. His black eyes shot scorn and hatred at the simple, guileless gentleman before him.

“You are shirking from the question, sir, as you did from the toast just now,” he said. “I am not a boy to suffer under your arrogance. You have publicly insulted me in a public place, and I demand a reparation.”

“In Heaven’s name—be it!” said Mr. Washington, and the grief in his face made the words heavy as iron.

“And you have insulted me,” Captain Grace began, reeling forward. “What was it he said? Confound the militia captain—colonel, what is he? You’ve insulted me! Oh, Waring! to think I should be insulted by a captain of militia!” Tears actually ran upon the noble Captain’s cheek at this harrowing thought.

“I insult you, you hog!” the Colonel yelled—humour being little in him, and rage making him blind to the ridiculousness of his adversary.

At that instant a fourth voice came in, brisk and affronted.

“Great powers, sir!” said Captain Waring. “Are three affairs not enough for you, and must I come into the quarrel too? You have a quarrel with these two young gentlemen.”

“Hasty words, sir!” poor Harry tried again.

“Hasty words, sir!” Waring echoed, indignant. “A gentleman tells another gentleman he will put him across his knees and whip him, and you call those hasty words? Let me tell you if any man were to say to me, ‘Charles Waring,’ or ‘Captain Waring, I’ll put you across my knees and whip you,’ I’d say, ‘I’ll drive my cheese-toaster through his body,’ if he were as big as Goliath I would. That’s one affair with young Mr. George Warrington. Mr. Harry, of course, as a young man of spirit, will stand by his brother. That’s two. Between Grace and the Colonel apology is impossible. And now—run me through the body!—you call an officer of my regiment—of Halkett’s, sir!—a hog before my face! Great heavens, sir! Mr. Washington, are you all like this in Virginia? Excuse me, I would use no offensive personality, as, by George, I will suffer none from any man! but, by Gad, Colonel, give me leave to tell you you are the most quarrelsome man I ever saw in my life. Call a disabled officer of my regiment—for he is disabled, ain’t you, Grace?—call him a hog before me! You withdraw it, sir—you withdraw it!”

“Is this some infernal conspiracy in which you are all leagued against me?” shouted the Colonel. “It would seem as if I was drunk, and not you, as you all are. I withdraw nothing. I apologise for nothing. By heavens! I will meet one or half a dozen of you in your turn, young or old, drunk or sober.”

“I do not wish to hear myself called more names,” George Warrington said evenly. “This affair can proceed, sir, without any further insult on your part. When will it please you to give me the meeting?”

“The sooner the better, sir!” said the Colonel, fuming.

“The sooner the better,” hiccupped Captain Grace, rising, swearing (as gentlemen then seasoned all speech), and reeling toward his sword by the door—only to fall as he reached it. From the floor he waved the weapon, knocked his own hat over his eyes, and cried again, “The sooner the better!”

“At any rate, this gentleman’s business will keep cool till to-morrow,” the militia Colonel said, turning to the other King’s officer. “You will hardly bring your man out to-day, Captain Waring?”

“I confess that neither his hand nor mine are particularly steady,” Waring said.

“Mine is!” George Warrington cried, glaring.

Washington, hot and savage still, answered with a sternness that tried to cover sorrow. “Be it so—with what weapon, sir?”

“Not with small-swords, Colonel. We can beat you with them. You know that from our old bouts. Pistols had better be the word.”

“As you please, George Warrington—and God forgive you, George! God pardon you, Harry! for bringing me into this quarrel,” said the Colonel, the sadness breaking through his anger like rain through smoke.

Harry hung his head; but George continued as calmly as if he were calling for salt at table.

“I, sir? It was not I who called names, who talked of a cane, who insulted a gentleman in a public place before gentlemen of the army. It is not the first time you have chosen to take me for a negro, and talked of the whip for me.”

The Colonel started back, turning very red, struck by remembrance as by a blow.

“Great heavens, George! is it that boyish quarrel you are still recalling?”

“Who made you the overseer of Castlewood?” George hissed, teeth grinding. “I am not your slave, George Washington, and I never will be. I hated you then, and I hate you now. And you have insulted me, and I am a gentleman, and so are you. Is that not enough?”

“Too much—only too much,” Washington said, with genuine grief. “Do you bear malice too, Harry? I had not thought this of thee!”

“I stand by my brother,” Harry replied, turning away from the Colonel’s look, and grasping George’s hand—an oath made without words.

The sadness did not leave Washington’s face. “Heaven be good to us! ’Tis all clear now,” he muttered to himself, as if speaking some private dialect of conscience which no other man could quite understand. Aloud, he said, “The time to write a few letters, and I am at your service, Mr. Warrington.”

“You have your own pistols at your saddle. I did not ride out with any; but will send Sady back for mine. That will give you time enough, Colonel Washington?”

“Plenty of time, sir.”

Each gentleman made the other a low bow. George put his arm through Harry’s, and they walked away together, their boots sounding on the boards with a dry finality that drowned, for a moment, even the insects outside.

The Virginian officer looked toward the two unlucky captains, now helpless with liquor. Captain Benson, master of the tavern, was propping the hat of one of them over his head with the rough tenderness of men who have seen too many foolish quarrels.

“It is not altogether their fault, Colonel,” Benson said, with a grim look of humour. “Jack Firebrace and Tom Humbold of Spotsylvania was here this morning, chanting horses with ’em. And Jack and Tom got ’em to play cards; and they didn’t win—the British Captains didn’t. And Jack and Tom challenged ’em to drink for the honour of Old England, and they didn’t win at that game neither, much. They are kind, free-handed fellows when they are sober, but they are a pretty pair of fools—they are.”

“Captain Benson,” said Colonel Washington, “you are an old frontier man, and an officer of ours, before you turned farmer and taverner. You will help me in this matter with yonder young gentlemen?”

“I’ll stand by and see fair play, Colonel. I won’t have no hand in it, beyond seeing fair play. Madam Esmond has helped me many a time—tended my poor wife in her lying-in, and doctored our Betty in the fever. You ain’t a-going to be very hard with them poor boys? Though I seen ’em both shoot: the fair one hunts well, as you know, but the old one’s a wonder at an ace of spades.”
