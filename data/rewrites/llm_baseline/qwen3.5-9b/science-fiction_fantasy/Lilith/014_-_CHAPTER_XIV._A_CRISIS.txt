For a fleeting moment, my only desire was to dedicate my entire existence to the Little Ones. Yet, soon, other currents of thought and feeling began to stir within me. First arose a vague, nagging conviction that I ought to be accomplishing something greater—that I was not destined merely for the fattening of boors! Then the realization struck me: I stood in a marvelous world, and it was undoubtedly my duty to uncover its ways and laws. If I wished to repay the children's boundless goodness, I must learn more about them than they could possibly tell me. To achieve this, I needed freedom.

Surely, I reasoned, no suppression of their growth could be essential to their loveliness, truth, and purity! In no conceivable world could such a discord exist between a constitution and its natural outcome! Life and law could not be so fundamentally at variance that perfection required thwarting development! But the growth of the Little Ones *was* arrested. Something interfered with it. What was it?

Lona appeared to be the eldest, though perhaps no older than fifteen, yet she had long overseen a multitude—children in semblance and behavior—who regarded her as their mother. Were they actually growing? I deeply doubted it. They possessed scarcely any concept of time; they knew nothing of their own age. Lona herself believed she had lived forever. Full of wisdom yet empty of knowledge, she served simultaneously as their Love and their Law. But what I perceived as her ignorance might simply be my own lack of insight. Her singular anxiety was clear: that her Little Ones should not grow, lest they change into "bad giants!"

Their "good giant" was bound to do his best for them, yet without deeper knowledge of their nature and some understanding of their history, he could do nothing. Consequently, he must leave them. They remained exactly as they were; they had not become dependent on me. They were still my protectors, not mine. My presence, paradoxically, only increased their danger from their idiotic neighbors.

I longed to teach them myriad things, but I realized I must first understand more of those I intended to teach. Knowledge no doubt made bad people worse, but it must make good people better! I was convinced they could learn mathematics; might they not also be taught to write down the dainty melodies they murmured and forgot? The conclusion was inevitable: I must rise and continue my travels, hoping to encounter some elucidation regarding the fortunes and destiny of these bewitching little creatures.

My design, however, would not have passed swiftly into action had it not been for what transpired next. Preparing them for my temporary absence, I spent a day working among them, telling them that I would long ago have fled the bad giants, but that I loved the Little Ones so dearly. Suddenly, as if by one accord, they rushed and crowded around me. They scrambled over one another, climbed the tree, and dropped onto my head until I was nearly smothered. With three tiny ones in my arms, one clinging to each shoulder and gripping my neck, another standing upright on my head, four or five holding me fast by the legs, and a multitude climbing and descending upon this chaotic mass, I was helpless, overwhelmed like a rock buried by lava.

Absorbed in the merry struggle, none of them noticed my tyrant approaching until he was almost upon me. With a single cry of "Take care, good giant!" they scattered. They ran from me like mice, dropped from me like hedgehogs, and flew up the tree like squirrels. In that exact same moment, the bad giant emerged sharply around the trunk and struck me on the head with a stick, sending me crashing to the ground.

The children later explained that they had driven him away with "such a many bumps of big apples and stones" that he was frightened and ran blundering home.

When I regained consciousness, it was night. Above me hung a few pale stars waiting for the moon. I thought I was alone. My head throbbed violently, and I was terribly thirsty. Turning wearily onto my side, the instant my ear touched the earth, I heard the gushing and gurgling of water. The soft noises made me groan with longing. Immediately, I was surrounded by a multitude of silent children, and delicious little fruits began to visit my lips. They came again and again until my thirst was quenched.

Then, I became aware of sounds I had never heard there before: the air was filled with little sobs. I attempted to sit up, and instantly a pile of small bodies heaped itself against my back. Struggling to my feet amidst much pushing and pulling from the Little Ones—who were wonderfully strong for their size—they spoke to me.

"You must go away, good giant," they said. "When the bad giants see you hurt, they will all trample on you."

"I think I must," I answered.

"Go and grow strong, and come again," they urged.

"I will," I replied, sitting down.

"Indeed you must go at once!" whispered Lona, who had been supporting me and now knelt beside me.

One of the larger boys added, "I listened at his door and heard the bad giant say to his wife that he found you idle, talking to a lot of moles and squirrels. And when he beat you, they tried to kill him. He said you were a wizard, and they must knock you down, or they would have no peace."

"I will go at once," I promised, "and return as soon as I have discovered what is needed to make you bigger and stronger."

"We don't want to be bigger," they answered, their faces grave. "We *WON'T* grow bad giants! We are strong now; you don't know how much strong!"

It was futile to hold out a prospect that held no attraction for them. I said nothing more, rose, and moved slowly up the slope of the valley. Instantly, they formed themselves into a long procession. Some led the way, some walked alongside helping me, and the rest followed behind. They continued to feed me as we traveled.

"You are broken," they observed, noting the blood staining my clothes. "Much red juice has run out of you: put some in."

Upon reaching the edge of the valley, the moon was just lifting her forehead over the horizon's rim.

"She has come to take care of you, and show you the way," Lona declared.

As we walked, I questioned those around me and learned of a great place inhabited by a giant-girl who ruled as queen. When I asked if it was a city, they shook their heads, saying they did not know. Neither could they tell how far away it was, in what direction, nor even the giant-girl's name. All they knew was that she hated the Little Ones and desired to kill them, though she could never seem to find them.

"How do you know that?" I asked.

Lona replied simply, "She has always known it."

"If the giant-girl comes to look for them, they must hide hard," she warned.

When I told them I intended to go and ask her why she hated them, they cried out in unison, "No, no! She will kill you, good giant! She will kill you! She is an awful bad-giant witch!"

Desperate for guidance, I asked where I was to go instead. They pointed toward the land beyond the baby-forest, away in the direction where the moon originated. There lay a smooth green country, pleasant to the feet, devoid of rocks or trees.

"But how shall we set out for it?" I asked.

"The moon will tell you, we think," they responded.

They were leading me up the second branch of the riverbed. When they saw the moon reach its zenith, they stopped to turn back.

"We have never gone so far from our trees before," they admitted solemnly. "Now mind you watch how you go, so that you may see inside your eyes how to come back to us."

"And beware of the giant-woman that lives in the desert," cautioned one of the older girls as they turned away. "I suppose you have heard of her!"

"No," I answered honestly.

"Then take care not to go near her. She is called the Cat-woman. She is awfully ugly—and she scratches."

As soon as the older ones halted, the younger ones began running back. The remaining group looked at me gravely for a moment before walking slowly away. Last to depart, Lona held up the baby for a kiss, gazed deep into my eyes, whispered, "The Cat-woman will not hurt *you*," and vanished without another word.

I stood for a while, gazing after them through the moonlight, then turned and, with a heavy heart, began my solitary journey. Soon, the laughter of the Little Ones overtook me, sounding like innumerable sheep-bells rippling through the air and echoing off the surrounding rocks. I turned again and again to gaze after them; they gambled along with never a care in their sweet souls. But Lona walked apart, accompanied by her baby.

Pondering as I walked, I recalled many traits of my little friends. Once, when I suggested leaving their country to join me in finding another, they had answered, "But that would be to *not* ourselves!" So profound was their love of place that their homeland seemed essential to their very being! Devoid of ambition, fear, discomfort, or greed, they had no motive to desire change.
