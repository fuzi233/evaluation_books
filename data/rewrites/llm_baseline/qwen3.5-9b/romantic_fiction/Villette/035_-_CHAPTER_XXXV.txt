"Let us forget the Professors," Madame Beck advised. She was a woman of wisdom, yet in saying this, she committed a grave error. That very night, she should have left me calm—indifferent, isolated in my own estimation and in theirs, untethered from any connection, even intellectual, to the second person I was commanded to erase from my mind.

*Forget him?* Ah! How foolishly they devised this "wise" plan to make me forget him—the so-called experts! They displayed his virtues; they transformed my dear little man into a stainless, heroic figure. Then, they prattled endlessly about his capacity for love. Before this day, what grounds did I possess to be certain whether he loved at all?

I had witnessed him jealous, suspicious. I had observed around him certain tender fits and capricious moods—a softness arriving like warm air, a compassion passing like morning dew, only to evaporate in the heat of his irritabilities. *That* was all I had seen. Yet, Père Silas and Madame Beck (working in perfect concert, I could not doubt it) tore open the sanctuary of his heart. They revealed to me a grand, singular love: the offspring of a Southern soul's youth, born so robust and perfect that it had laughed at Death, despised his petty ravaging of matter, clung to the immortal spirit, and, in victory and faith, had kept vigil beside a tomb for twenty years.

This revelation was not idle; it was no hollow indulgence of sentiment. He had proven his fidelity by dedicating his finest energies to an unselfish cause, attesting it through limitless personal sacrifice. For those once dear to her whom he cherished, he had laid down vengeance and taken up a cross.

As for Justine Marie, I knew exactly who she was, as clearly as if I had seen her every day. I knew she was merely adequate; there were girls like her in Madame Beck's school—phlegmatics, pale, slow, inert, yet kind-natured, neutral toward evil, and indistinguishable in goodness.

If she wore angel's wings, I knew whose poetic fancy bestowed them. If her forehead glowed with the reflection of a halo, I knew in whose fiery iris that circle of holy flame originated.

Was I, then, to be terrified by Justine Marie? Should the image of a pale, dead nun rise before me as an eternal barrier? And what of the charities that consumed his worldly goods? What of his heart sworn to virginity?

Madame Beck and Père Silas—you should not have prompted these questions. They became simultaneously the deepest puzzle, the strongest obstacle, and the sharpest stimulus I had ever known. For a week of nights and days, I slept and dreamed, waking only to confront these two inquiries. There was no answer in the entire world to them, save where one small, dark man stood, sat, or lectured beneath the brim of a Greek bandit hat and within the frayed confines of a tattered coat, stained with ink and dust.

Following my visit to the Rue des Mages, I desperately desired to see him again. Knowing what I now knew, I felt his countenance would reveal a page more lucid, more fascinating than ever before. I yearned to trace upon his face the imprint of that primitive devotion, the marks of that half-knightly, half-saintly chivalry which the priest's narrative attributed to his nature. He had become my Christian hero; under that character, I wished to view him.

Opportunity did not wait long to test my new impressions. The following day, I was granted an audience with my "Christian hero"—an encounter neither particularly heroic, sentimental, nor biblical, yet remarkably lively in its own right.

Around three o'clock in the afternoon, the tranquility of the first class—which seemed securely established under the serene rule of Madame Beck, who, in her own person, was delivering one of her orderly and instructive lessons—suddenly fractured. The breach was caused by the wild intrusion of a paletôt.

No one was quieter than I at that moment. Freed from responsibility by Madame Beck's presence, soothed by her steady tones, and delighted by her clear exposition of the lesson (for she taught well), I sat bent over my desk, drawing. Specifically, I was copying an intricate line engraving, tediously refining my copy to match the original's finish, for that was my practical understanding of art. Strangely, I derived great pleasure from the labor, capable even of producing curiously delicate Chinese facsimiles of steel or mezzotint plates—works perhaps as valuable as needlepoint, though highly esteemed by me in those days.

Then, disaster struck. My drawing, my pencils, and my precious copy were gathered into one crushed handful and vanished from my sight. I felt as if I were shaken or emptied from my chair, much like a solitary, withered nutmeg expelled from a spice box by an agitated cook. My chair and desk, seized by the frantic paletôt—one under each sleeve—were carried away. In a second, I followed the furniture; in two minutes, we were all fixed in the center of the grand hall—a vast adjoining room rarely used except for dancing and choral instruction—immobilized with an emphasis that seemed to forbid any hope of movement again.

Regaining my scattered wits, I found myself facing two men, gentlemen, I suppose I should call them. One was dark, the other light; one possessed a stiff, semi-military demeanor and wore a braided overcoat, while the other bore the careless aspect of a student or artist. Both flourished in the full magnificence of moustaches, whiskers, and imperial beards. M. Emanuel stood slightly apart from them; his face and eyes radiated intense anger, and he gestured with his hand in a triumphant manner.

"Mademoiselle," he declared, "your task is to prove to these gentlemen that I am no liar. You will answer, to the best of your ability, any questions they pose. You will also write on any topic they choose. In their eyes, it appears, I occupy the position of an unprincipled impostor. I compose essays and, with deliberate forgery, sign my pupils' names to them, boasting of their work as their own. You must disprove this charge."

Good heavens! Here was the trial, so long avoided, descending upon me like a thunderclap. These two fine, braided, mustachioed, sneering figures were none other than the dandy professors of the college—Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte—a pair of cold-blooded fops and pedants, skeptics, and scoffers. Apparently, M. Paul had rashly exhibited something I had written—something he had never once praised or even mentioned in my hearing, and which I believed forgotten. The essay was not remarkable in itself; it only *appeared* extraordinary compared to the average output of foreign schoolgirls; in an English institution, it would have gone largely unnoticed. Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte deemed it necessary to question its authenticity and insinuate fraud. Now, I was to bear witness to the truth and submit to the torture of their examination.

A memorable scene unfolded.

They commenced with classics. The result was a complete blank. They moved on to French history. I could barely distinguish Mérovée from Pharamond. They tested me in various sciences, receiving only a shake of the head and an unvarying "Je n'en sais rien."

After an expressive pause, they turned to matters of general information, broaching one or two subjects I knew reasonably well and on which I had often reflected. M. Emanuel, who had thus far stood watching, dark as the winter solstice, brightened somewhat; he evidently expected me to demonstrate that I was at least not a fool.

He learned his error quickly. Although answers surged up rapidly, my mind filling like a rising well, ideas were present but words eluded me. I either *could* not, or *would* not speak—I am unsure which: partly, I suspect, my nerves were deranged, and partly my humor was crossed.

I heard one of my examiners—the one in the braided overcoat—whisper to his colleague, "Is she therefore an idiot?"

"Yes," I thought, "she is an idiot, and always will be, for someone like you."

Yet I suffered—cruelly. I saw the dampness gather on M. Paul's brow, and his eye spoke a passionate yet sorrowful reproach. He refused to believe in my total lack of social cleverness; he thought I *could* be prompt if I *would*.

Finally, to relieve him, the professors, and myself, I stammered:

"Gentlemen, you had better let me go; you will gain nothing from me; as you say, I am an idiot."

I wish I could have spoken with calm and dignity, or that my senses had sufficed to silence me; that treacherous tongue tripped and faltered. Seeing the judges cast a hard look of triumph at M. Emanuel and hearing the distressed tremor of my own voice, I burst forth in a fit of choking tears. The emotion was far more anger than grief; had I been a man and strong, I could have challenged that pair on the spot—but it *was* emotion, and I would sooner be scourged than betray it.
