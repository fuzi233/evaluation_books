“Mistress, they all say he is dead.”

“Not so,” Margaret answered, though the words scraped her throat as if they were dry crusts. “They feed me still with hopes.”

“Ay—before thy face.” Luke’s mouth twisted; his honest bluntness had no salve on it. “But behind thy back they all say he is dead.”

At that, the tears she had held like water behind a dam broke through. They ran hot over her cheeks and dropped on her hands, which smelt faintly of bread and baby—of flour dust rubbed into skin, and the warm sour-sweetness that clung to linen after a child had lain there. Luke, who had the shoulders of a man and the soft heart of a maid, began to whimper too, as if sorrow were a tune and he could not keep from joining the chorus.

“Prithee, weep not so, sweet mistress,” he pleaded. “I’d bring him back to life an I could, rather than see thee weed so sore.”

“I think I weep,” said Margaret, swallowing, “because they are double-tongued with me.” And then, as if shame at crying before him stiffened her spine, she wiped her face with the back of her hand and laid that hand—light, grave—on Luke’s shoulder. “Luke, he is not dead.”

“Who can tell?” he whispered.

“Dying men,” she said, and her voice changed, as if she were speaking not from the kitchen, but from some place where the air ran colder, “are known to have a strange sight. Listen, Luke. My poor father—when he was a-dying—and I, simple fool, so happy, thinking he was to get well altogether—he said to mother and me—he was sitting in that very chair where thou art now, and mother was as might be here, and I was yonder making a sleeve—said he: ‘I see him! I see him!’ Just so—clear—none of thy failing mutter. All o’ fire. ‘Sore disfigured—on a great river—coming this way.’”

Luke sat still, as if her words had set a weight on him. Outside, the town had its daily sounds—the distant clack of a cartwheel on cobbles, the cry of a vendor, the thin hammering of a cooper’s mallet like a heart trying to keep time. Inside, Luke’s breath and Margaret’s grief filled the room.

“Ah, Luke,” she went on, and now the plea came naked, without pride, “if thou wert a woman, and had the feeling for me thou thinkest thou hast, thou wouldst pity me, and find him for me. Take a thought! The father of my child!”

“Alack, I would if I knew how,” Luke said helplessly. “But how can I?”

“Nay, of course thou canst not,” she answered quickly, the old humility snapping into place like a lid. “I am mad to think it.” Then the lid slipped. “But oh, if any one really cared for me, they would; that is all I know.”

Luke’s brow furrowed. He was slow—she knew it; he knew it too—and his slowness was a kind of honesty. He chewed the matter like tough meat, unwilling to swallow a lie merely because it was easier.

“The old folk all say dying men can see more than living wights,” he said at last. “Let me think: for my mind cannot gallop like thine. On a great river… Well, the Maas is a great river.”

Margaret’s eyes clung to his face, hungry as a starving mouth.

He pondered on, lips moving soundlessly. “Coming this way? Then if ’twas the Maas, he would have been here by this time—so ’tis not the Maas. The Rhine is a great river, greater than the Maas; and very long. I think it will be the Rhine.”

“And so do I, Luke,” she breathed, eager despite herself; hope, when it came, came like the first scent of yeast in a cold house. “For Denys bade him come down the Rhine. But even if it is, he may turn off before he comes anigh his birthplace.” Her face tightened. “He does not pine for me as I for him; that is clear. Luke—do you not think he has deserted me?”

She asked it as if she begged for contradiction, for some spoonful of comfort to be pressed into her mouth. Luke, however, had never learnt to sweeten truth.

“It looks very like it,” he said, and then, in sudden indignation on her behalf, “what a fool he must be!”

“What do we know?” she pleaded, clutching at uncertainty as if it were a handhold on a slippery bank.

“Let me think again,” Luke muttered. “I cannot gallop.”

Out of that slow grinding of thought came his plan. He knew a station some sixty miles up the Rhine where all the public boats put in. He would go there—stand among the coming and going, under the cries of boatmen and the slap of water on timber—and try to cut the truant off. He did not know Gerard by sight; but as each boat landed, he would slip among the passengers and ask if one Gerard was there.

“And, mistress,” he added, “if you were to give me a bit of a letter to him; for, with us being strangers, mayhap he won’t believe a word I say.”

“Good, kind, thoughtful Luke,” she said, and a blush rose—shame and gratitude mingled, like wine poured into milk. “I will. How I have undervalued thee! But give me till supper-time to get it writ.”

All that afternoon she moved about her small tasks with the letter burning in her mind like a coal in a palm. There was broth to mind and bread to cut; the child to hush; the little domestic world to keep from falling into ruin. Even so, her senses would not leave her: the smell of onions softening, the scrape of a ladle, the familiar hush-and-sigh of the hearth. Grief did not stop the pot from boiling; it only made the steam taste salt.

At supper she put the letter into Luke’s hand. It was long, tied round with silk after the fashion of the day, and sealed over the knot. Luke weighed it, and a shadow crossed his face—discontent, not with her, but with the fate he was helping to knead.

“Say your father was not dreaming,” he said very gravely, “and say I have the luck to fall in with this man, and say he should turn out a better bit o’ stuff than I think him, and come home to you then and there—what is to become o’ me?”

Margaret coloured to the very brow.

“Oh, Luke,” she whispered, “Heaven will reward thee. And I shall fall on my knees and bless thee; and I shall love thee all my days, sweet Luke, as a mother does her son. I am so old by thee: trouble ages the heart. Thou shalt not go—’tis not fair of me. Love maketh us to be all self.”

“Humph!” said Luke, and there was a hurt boy in the sound.

“And if,” he went on, still in that same plain, deadly sense, “yon scapegrace shall read thy letter, and hear me tell him how thou pinest for him, and yet, being a traitor, or a mere idiot, will not turn to thee—what shall become o’ me then? Must I die a bachelor, and thou fare lonely to thy grave, neither maid, wife, nor widow?”

The question struck her like the sudden clang of an iron pot dropped on stone: it rang, it stunned, it could not be ignored. Her breath came quick; fear made her taste metal.

“If—” she began, and stopped, and started again, “which our Lady be merciful to me, and forbid—Oh!”

“Well, mistress?”

“If he should read my letter, and hear thy words—” Her voice broke and recovered. “And, sweet Luke, be just, and tell him what a lovely babe he hath—fatherless, fatherless. Oh, Luke, can he be so cruel?”

“I trow not—but if?”

“Then,” she said, and each word came like a pin drawn from flesh, “he will give thee up my marriage lines, and I shall be an honest woman, and a wretched one, and my boy will not be a bastard. And of course, then, we could both go into any honest man’s house that would be troubled with us; and even for thy goodness this day, I will—I will—ne’er be so ungrateful as go past thy door to another man’s.”

“Ay, but will you come in at mine?” Luke demanded. “Answer me that!”

“Oh, ask me not!” she cried. “Some day, perhaps, when my wounds leave bleeding. Alas, I’ll try. If I don’t fling myself and my child into the Maas. Do not go, Luke! do not think of going! ’Tis all madness from first to last.”

But Luke was as slow to forego an idea as to form one; and love, working in him like yeast, had begun to lift him from soft boy into stubborn man.

“Well,” he said, wiping his eyes with his sleeve as if ashamed of them, “madness is something, anyway; and I am tired o’ doing nothing for thee; and I am no great talker. To-morrow, at peep o’ day, I start. But hold—I have no money. My mother, she takes care of all mine; and I ne’er see it again.”

Margaret went to the little hoard of her hardships and hopes and drew out Catherine’s gold angel—the coin that had escaped so often, like a blessed thing that refused to be spent on folly. She pressed it into Luke’s hand. The gold lay heavy there, as if it carried not only value, but all the prayers that had touched it.

So, at dawn, Luke set out on his mad errand.

It did not seem so mad to him as it would to those who lived by colder measures. It was a superstitious age, and men acted upon a dying man’s vision much as later folk act upon respectable tidings. But when Catherine heard of it, she flamed like a pan when fat catches.

“To send the poor lad on such a wild-goose chase!” she cried. “But you are like a many more girls; and mark my words: by the time you have worn that Luke fairly out, and made him as sick of you as a dog, you will turn as fond on him as a cow on a calf, and ‘Too late’ will be the cry.”

Twelve hours after Luke started up the Rhine, two friars reached Holland from the south. Thus, wild-goose chase or no, the parties were nearing each other—quickly, too. Jerome, unable to preach in low Dutch, now pushed on towards the coast, anxious to win to England as soon as might be; and having the stream with them, the friars would, in truth, have missed Luke by passing him in full current below his station, but for an incident that turned their course like a hand on a tiller.

About twenty miles above the station Luke was making for, Clement landed to preach in a large village. The place had the ordinary noises of rural life—hens fussing, children calling, the steady creak of a well-rope—but over it lay the hush that gathers when a voice speaks in earnest to many souls. Towards the end of his sermon Clement noticed a grey nun weeping. Her sobs were small sounds swallowed by the crowd, but he saw them as one sees a candle in daylight.

When the people broke up, he spoke to her gently and asked her grief.

“Nay,” said she, “’tis not for myself flow these tears; ’tis for my lost friend. Thy words reminded me of what she was, and what she is, poor wretch. But you are a Dominican, and I am a Franciscan nun.”

“It matters little, my sister,” said Clement, “if we are both Christians, and if I can aid thee in aught.”

She searched his face, as if weighing whether charity could indeed wear another order’s hood. “These are strange words,” she murmured, “but methinks they are good; and thy lips are—oh, most eloquent. I will tell thee our grief.”

Then she told him: a young nun, the darling of their convent, had been lured away from her vows. Sin had led to sin, as one bad dish spoils the next; and now the girl lived in a small inn as chambermaid, in truth as decoy, selling her favours to wealthier customers.

“Anywhere else,” the nun said, “we might, by kindly violence, force her away from perdition. But this innkeeper is servant to the fierce baron on the height there, and hath his ear still; he would burn our convent to the ground, were we to take her by force.”

“Moreover,” Clement answered, “souls will not be saved by brute force.”

As they spoke, Jerome came up; Clement persuaded him to lie at the convent that night. But in the morning, when Clement told Jerome he had talked long with the abbess and had promised to try to win back the wandering nun, Jerome frowned.

“It is not our business,” he said. “It is a waste of time.”

Clement, however, was no longer a mere pupil. He stood firm; and at last they agreed: Jerome should go forward and secure their passage in the next ship for England, and Clement should be allowed time for his well-meant, perhaps idle, experiment.

About ten o’clock that day, a figure in a horseman’s cloak, great boots, and a large flapping felt hat stood near the auberge where the apostate nun lived. Disguised thus, Clement was in that moment truly wretched. Such ardent natures undertake wonders; but when they come hand to hand with the sickening difficulties, their nerves shake. Yet their hearts are steel; panting and dispirited, they struggle on.

He hesitated long at the door, prayed for help and wisdom, and at last entered. He sat down faint at heart, his body in cold perspiration. Then, as if he had stepped over a threshold within himself, he became another man.

He called lustily for a cup of wine. The landlord brought it. Clement paid for it with money the convent had supplied him, and made show of drinking.

“Landlord,” said he, “I hear there is a fair chambermaid in thine house.”

“Ay, stranger,” chuckled the man, “the buxomest in Holland. But she gives not her company to all comers—only to good customers.”

Clement dangled a massive gold chain in the landlord’s sight. The metal caught the light as if it had stolen it.

The landlord laughed and shouted, “Here, Janet! here is a lover for thee would bind thee in chains of gold; and a tall lad into the bargain, I promise thee.”

“Then I am in double luck,” came a woman’s voice, careless and used to being summoned. “Send him hither.”

Clement rose, shuddering, and passed into the room where Janet sat with a piece of work. She laid it down every minute to sing a broken scrap of a song; in her life she had not the patience to carry anything out—not stitch, not tune, not virtue.

After a few words of greeting, the disguised visitor asked if they might be more private.

“Why not?” she said, rising with a smile that had been practised until it seemed natural. She tripped before him. He followed, groaning inwardly, sore perplexed.

“There,” she said, leading him to an upper room. “Have no fear! Nobody ever comes here but such as pay for the privilege.”

Clement looked round and prayed silently for wisdom. Then he went softly and closed the window-shutters with care, one after another, until the room dimmed like a conscience.

“What on earth is that for?” Janet asked, uneasiness pricking through her boldness.

“Sweetheart,” he whispered, putting a mystery into his voice, “it is that God may not see us.”

“Madman,” she snapped. “Think you a wooden shutter can keep out His eye?”

“Nay, I know not,” he said, as if wavering. “Perchance He has too much on hand to notice us. But I would not the saints and angels should see us. Would you?”

“My poor soul,” she retorted, “hope not to escape their sight! The only way is not to think of them; for if you do, it poisons your cup. For two pins I’d run and leave thee. Art pleasant company in sooth.”

“After all, girl,” he said, “so that men see us not, what signify God and the saints seeing us? Feel this chain! ’Tis virgin gold. I shall cut two of these heavy links off for thee.”

“Ah! now thy discourse is to the point.” She handled the chain greedily, and in that touch her past brushed against her present like cloth against a sore. “Why, ’tis as massy as the chain round the Virgin’s neck at the conv—”

She did not finish the word.

“Whisht! whisht! whisht!” Clement hissed. “’Tis it. And thou shalt have thy share. But betray me not.”

“Monster!” cried Janet, drawing back, repugnance sharpening her face. “What—rob the blessed Virgin of her chain, and give it to an—”

“You are none!” Clement cried exultingly, and the trap sprang shut. “Or you had not recked for that—Mary!”

“Ah! ah! ah!”

“Thy patron saint, whose chain this is, sends me to greet thee.”

She ran screaming to the window and began to undo the shutters. Her fingers trembled. In those instants Clement had time to rid himself of his boots and hat; then he let the cloak fall. When light streamed in, he stood revealed: a Dominican friar, calm and majestic as a statue, holding his crucifix aloft over her—his look loving, sad, and solemn. It relieved her of mere bodily fear and crushed her with religious terror and remorse. She crouched against the wall as if it were a cliff over a pit.

“Mary,” he said gently, “one word. Are you happy?”

“As happy,” she whispered through clenched teeth, “as I shall be in hell.”

“And they are not happy at the convent; they weep for you.”

“For me?” Her voice cracked.

“Day and night. Above all, Sister Ursula.”

“Poor Ursula!” The name, spoken aloud, opened a seam in her, and she began to weep too, not for herself, but for the friend whose grief she had earned.

“The angels weep still more,” Clement said. “Wilt not dry all their tears, in earth and heaven, and save thyself?”

“Ay! would I could,” Mary sobbed. “But it is too late.”

“Satan, avaunt!” cried the monk sternly. “’Tis thy favourite temptation; and thou, Mary, listen not to the enemy of man, belying God and whispering despair. I who come to save thee have been a far greater sinner than thou. Come. Sin, thou seest, is not so sweet, e’en in this world, as holiness; and eternity is at the door.”

She stared at him through tears, as if he had spoken a word she thought dead.

“How can they ever receive me again?”

“’Tis their worthiness thou doubtest now. But in truth they pine for thee. ’Twas in pity of their tears that I, a Dominican, undertook this task; and broke the rule of my order by entering an inn; and broke it again by donning these lay vestments. But all is well done, and quit for a light penance, if thou wilt let us rescue thy soul from this den of wolves, and bring thee back to thy vows.”

She gazed, shaken. “And thou—a Dominican—hast done this for a daughter of St. Francis! Why, the Franciscans and Dominicans hate one another.”

“Ay, my daughter,” Clement said quietly; “but Francis and Dominic love one another.”

The answer struck her with a simplicity she could not argue with. He reminded her then of how she had recoiled at the thought of robbing the Virgin.

“But see now,” he said, “the convent, and the Virgin too, think ten times more of their poor nun than of golden chains; for they freely trusted their chain to me, a stranger, that peradventure the sight of it might touch their lost Mary and remind her of their love.”

He showed her—plainly, terribly—the end of her present course; and on the other side he revived her dormant memories and better feelings, like embers coaxed back into flame. She sank to her knees at his feet, sobbing, confessing she had known no happiness nor peace since she betrayed her vows. She would go back—if he would go with her. Alone she dared not; could not. Even if she reached the gate she could never enter. How could she face the abbess and the sisters?

“I will go with thee,” Clement said, “as joyfully as the shepherd bears a strayed lamb to the fold.”

But when he urged her to go at once, there sprang up those prodigiously petty difficulties that entangle like silken nets and hold like iron cobwebs.

“How can I walk beside thee in this habit?” she faltered.

“I have brought the gown and cowl of thy holy order,” he answered. “Hide thy bravery with them. And leave thy shoes as I leave these.” He pointed to his horseman’s boots cast aside.

She gathered her jewels and ornaments, hands shaking.

“What are these for?” Clement asked.

“To present to the convent, father.”

“Their source is too impure.”

“But,” she pleaded, “it would be a sin to leave them here. They can be sold to feed the poor.”

“Mary,” he said, lifting the crucifix nearer, “fix thine eye on this, and trample those devilish baubles beneath thy feet.”

She hesitated; then with a sharp sob she flung them down and stamped on them, as if stamping on the life she had lived.

“Now open the window,” Clement commanded, “and fling them out on that dunghill.”

She did so. The jewels tumbled into filth with a sound oddly small, and the act itself seemed to crack a chain about her heart.

“’Tis well done,” he said. “So pass the wages of sin from thy hands, its glittering yoke from thy neck, its pollution from thy soul. Away, daughter of St. Francis; we tarry in this vile place too long.”

She followed him out.

Yet they were not clear. In the kitchen, the landlord, seeing a black friar and a grey nun come through from within, gaped and muttered, “Why, what mummery is this?” Then comprehension struck him, and he sprang between them and the door.

“What ho! Reuben! Carl! Gavin!” he yelled. “Here is a false friar spiriting away our Janet!”

Men came running in, faces hard, hands ready. Clement rushed at them with crucifix in hand.

“Forbear!” he cried, voice like a bell over a marsh. “She is a holy nun returning to her vows. The hand that touches her cowl or her robe to stay her—it shall wither; his body shall lie unburied, cursed by Rome, and his soul shall roast in eternal fire.”

They shrank back as if they had felt heat.

“And thou—miserable panderer!” Clement’s sentence ended not in words but in action. He seized the man by the neck, and strong as a lion in his hot excitement, hurled him from the door. The landlord flew across the room and pitched head foremost upon the stone floor, bleeding, half stunned.

Clement tore the door open and carried the screaming nun out into the road.

“Hush, poor trembler,” he gasped. “They dare not molest thee on the highroad. Away!”

Mary, though she looked back again and again, saw no more of them. The landlord lay terrified, blood on stone; his men, cowed by fear of curse and of the strange authority in a friar’s voice, did not follow.

On the road Clement spoke to her of his own fault, as if confession were the only fair payment for hers.

“Hitherto,” said he, “we have spoken of thy faults: now for mine. My choler is ungovernable—furious. It is by the grace of God I am not a murderer. I repent the next moment; but a moment too late is all too late. Mary—had the churls laid finger on thee, I should have scattered their brains with my crucifix. Oh, I know myself. Go to; and tremble at myself. There lurketh a wild beast beneath this black gown of mine.”

“Alas, father,” Mary said, and in it was a strange gratitude, “were you other than you are I had been lost. To take me from that place needed a man wary as a fox; yet bold as a lion.”

Clement reflected, and the fierce energy in him settled into humility. “This much is certain: God chooseth well His fleshly instruments; and with imperfect hearts doeth His perfect work. Glory be to God!”

When they were near the convent, Mary stopped suddenly, seized his arm, and began to cry—not loud, but deep, like a spring under stone.

He looked at her kindly. “Thou hast nothing to fear,” he told her. “It will be the happiest day thou hast ever spent.”

Then he made her sit down and compose herself while he went within. He entered the convent and desired to see the abbess.

“My sister,” he said when she came, “give the glory to God: Mary is at the gate.”

The abbess’s astonishment and delight were unbounded. She yielded at once to Clement’s earnest request that the road of penitence should, at first, be smoothed for this unstable wanderer; and after some opposition she entered heartily into his counsel as to Mary’s reception.

To give time for their small preparations, Clement went slowly back. He sat by Mary, soothed her, and heard her confession, letting her words fall as quietly as drops into earth.

“The abbess has granted me,” he told her, “that you shall propose your own penance.”

“It shall be none the lighter,” Mary said, as if she feared mercy more than punishment.

“I trow not,” Clement answered; “but that is future. To-day is given to joy alone.”

He led her round the building to the abbess’s postern. As they went they heard musical instruments and singing within—voices rising and falling like birds in a sheltered orchard.

“’Tis a feastday,” Mary whispered. “And I come to mar it.”

“Hardly,” said Clement, smiling, “seeing that you are the queen of the fête.”

“I, father? what mean you?”

“What, Mary—have you never heard there is more joy in heaven over one sinner that repenteth, than over ninety-nine just persons which need no repentance? Now this convent is not heaven; nor the nuns angels; yet are there among them some angelic spirits; and these sing and exult at thy return. But here methinks comes one of them; for I see her hand trembles at the keyhole.”

The postern was flung open. In a moment Sister Ursula clung to Mary, sobbing and kissing her round the neck, as if she would fasten her back to life with embraces. The abbess followed more sedately, but little less moved.

Clement bade them farewell.

They entreated him to stay, but he refused, regretful. He had already tried Brother Jerome’s patience and must hasten to the river; perhaps sail for England on the morrow.

So Mary returned to the fold, and Clement strode briskly on towards the Rhine—and England.

This was the man for whom Margaret’s boy lay in wait with her letter.

That letter was one of those simple, piercing appeals only a woman can write to the man who has used her cruelly and is still, against all reason, beloved. Margaret began with the birth of the little boy, and the comfort he had been to her through the long distress of mind Gerard’s strange silence had brewed in her—like a pot left on the edge of the hearth, never boiling clean, never cooling.

She described little Gerard minutely, as if words could carry flesh across water; she did not forget the mole on his little finger.

“Know you any one that hath the like on his?” she wrote. “If you only saw him you could not choose but be proud of him; all the mothers in the street do envy me; but I the wives; for thou comest not to us. My own Gerard, some say thou art dead. But if thou wert dead, how could I be alive? Others say that thou, whom I love so truly, art false. But this will I believe from no lips but thine. My father loved thee well; and as he lay a-dying he thought he saw thee on a great river, with thy face turned towards thy Margaret, but sore disfigured. Is’t so, perchance? Have cruel men scarred thy sweet face? or hast thou lost one of thy precious limbs? Why, then thou hast the more need of me, and I shall love thee not worse, alas! thinkest thou a woman’s love is light as a man’s? but better, than I did when I shed those few drops from my arm, not worth the tears, thou didst shed for them; mindest thou? ’tis not so very long agone, dear Gerard.”

So the letter ran—tender, yearning, without a word of reproach at the end, for she dared not set a scolding tone upon it; doubt might frighten him away, and she needed him near enough even to blame.

Soft Luke was made the bearer of that sealed silk-tied hope.

At the station on the Rhine, Luke mingled with the passengers of two boats and heard nothing of Gerard Eliassoen. That did not surprise him. He had half expected the river to mock him. But at the third boat, a black friar spoke to him, somewhat severely.

“And what would you with him you call Gerard Eliassoen?”

Luke stared. “Why, father, if he is alive I have got a letter for him.”

“Humph!” said Jerome. “I am sorry for it. However, the flesh is weak. Well, my son, he you seek will be here by the next boat, or the next boat after. And if he chooses to answer to that name—after all, I am not the keeper of his conscience.”

“Good father,” Luke burst out, “one plain word, for Heaven’s sake. This Gerard Eliassoen of Tergou—is he alive?”

“Humph! Why, certes, he that went by that name is alive.”

“Well, then, that is settled,” Luke said drily; and then, in the next breath, the dryness broke. He turned, ran out of sight, and blubbered like a child.

“Oh, why did the Lord make any women?” he sobbed to himself. “I was content with the world till I fell in love. Here his little finger is more to her than my whole body, and he is not dead. And here I have got to give him this.”

He looked at the letter as if it were a coal meant to burn him. He dashed it on the ground—then snatched it up again in spiteful loyalty, as if he could not bear to see even paper wronged for her sake.

He went to the landlord with tears still on his face and begged for work.

The landlord declined at first. He had his own people.

“Oh, I seek not your money,” Luke said hoarsely. “I only want some work to keep me from breaking my heart about another man’s lass.”

“Good lad! good lad!” the landlord cried, struck with rough pity, and found him barrels to mend—on terms agreed.

So Luke coopered with fury between the boats coming down the Rhine, hammering staves into place while the river ran on, bearing toward him the man who might mend Margaret’s life—or split it wider.
