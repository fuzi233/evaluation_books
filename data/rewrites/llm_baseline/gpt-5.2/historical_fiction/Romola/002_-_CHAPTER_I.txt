The Loggia de’ Cerchi lay folded into the very ribs of old Florence, where streets ran narrow as knife-cuts behind the Badia, and the stranger seldom threaded them unless he hunted—half in shame, half in reverence—for a certain severely simple doorway bearing the boastful sentence: Qui Nacque Il Divino Poeta. Once, in Dante’s hearing, those stones had answered back with iron and fury—family against family, the clash of steel as familiar as prayer. Now, in the fifteenth century, the same alleys gave out a different music: the hoarse teasing of woolcarders, the slap of hands on wet cloth, the unhistorical quarrels and fat jokes that rose from San Martino and Garbo like steam from a boiling pot.

Under the loggia, in the early morning of the ninth of April, 1492, two men held one another with their eyes as if a thread were drawn tight between them. One stood over the other, stooping, looking downward with the patient scrutiny of a man who had learned to appraise life by touch and bargain; the other lay on the pavement and stared up as if he had been woken from a dream by a sudden trumpet.

The standing man spoke first. Grey-haired, broad-shouldered, built in the Tuscan manner that seems hewn with the fist and polished with the pickaxe, he had a gravity cut deep about brow and mouth—as if he meant to refute, in advance, any contempt a passer-by might take from his rough workmanship. On the stones beside him he had set down a great bag of skins, swollen with its contents, and from his front hung a pedlar’s basket where small women’s-wares—thread, pins—lay cheek by jowl with jagged bits of glass, likely taken in trade.

“Young man,” he said, pointing with a thick finger, “when your chin has got a stiffer crop on it, you’ll know better than to take your nap in street-corners with a ring like that on your forefinger. By the holy ’vangels! if it had been anybody but me standing over you two minutes ago—but Bratti Ferravecchi is not the man to steal. The cat couldn’t eat her mouse if she didn’t catch it alive, and Bratti couldn’t relish gain if it had no taste of a bargain. Why, one San Giovanni, three years ago, the Saint sent a dead body in my way—a blind beggar, with his cap well-lined with pieces—but, if you’ll believe me, my stomach turned against the money I’d never bargained for, till it came into my head that San Giovanni owed me the pieces for what I spend yearly at the Festa; besides, I buried the body and paid for a mass—and so I saw it was a fair bargain. But how comes a young man like you, with the face of Messer San Michele, to be sleeping on a stone bed with the wind for a curtain?”

To the newly roused listener the man’s deep gutturals came as through water; yet the warning needed no translation. He looked down, saw the ring, and—obedient as a body trained by peril rather than politeness—slipped it off and thrust it under his doublet. As he rose he stretched himself, as if to coax blood back into limbs chilled by dawn-stone.

“Your tunic and hose match ill with that jewel, young man,” Bratti said, weighing him as he would a garment held up to the light. “Anybody might say the saints had sent you a dead body; but if you took the jewels, I hope you buried him—and you can afford a mass or two for him into the bargain.”

At those words a quick pain—like a needle under the nail—ran through the young man’s frame, arresting the careless lift of his chest. For one instant he turned on Bratti with a sharp frown that was more than sleep-shaken irritation. Then his face smoothed itself as a cook smooths flour over a board to hide last night’s knife-marks. He took off the red Levantine cap that hung like a great purse over his left ear, pushed back long dark-brown curls, and glanced down at his own weathered dress.

“You speak truth, friend,” he said, smiling as if the world were a jest worth hearing twice. “My garments are as weather-stained as an old sail, and they are not old either—only, like an old sail, they have had a sprinkling of the sea as well as the rain. The fact is, I’m a stranger in Florence; and when I came in footsore last night I preferred flinging myself in a corner of this hospitable porch to hunting any longer for a chance hostelry, which might turn out to be a nest of blood-suckers of more sorts than one.”

“A stranger, in good sooth,” Bratti answered, narrowing his eyes as if the young man’s vowels were coins whose stamp he did not trust. “For the words come all melting out of your throat, so that a Christian and a Florentine can’t tell a hook from a hanger. But you’re not from Genoa? More likely from Venice, by the cut of your clothes?”

“At this present moment,” the stranger said, still with that easy smile, “it is of less importance where I come from than where I can go to for a mouthful of breakfast. This city of yours turns a grim look on me just here. Can you show me the way to a more lively quarter, where I can get a meal and a lodging?”

“That I can,” said Bratti; and he looked pleased with the power of being needed. “And it is your good fortune, young man, that I have happened to be walking in from Rovezzano this morning, and turned out of my way to Mercato Vecchio to say an Ave at the Badia. That, I say, is your good fortune. But it remains to be seen what is my profit in the matter. Nothing for nothing. If I show you the way to Mercato Vecchio, you’ll swear by your patron saint to let me have the bidding for that stained suit of yours, when you set up a better—as doubtless you will.”

“Agreed, by San Niccolò,” the other laughed. “But now let us set off to this said Mercato, for I feel the want of a better lining to this doublet of mine which you are coveting.”

“Coveting? Nay,” said Bratti, and swung his skin-bag up onto his back with the practiced jerk that settles weight into muscle. He set off, and as he walked his voice broke open into the marketplace cry—harsh, creaking, like a cart-wheel complaining on dry axles, yet pitched to travel through stone corridors and morning air.

“Chi abbaratta—baratta—b’ratta—chi abbaratta cenci e vetri—b’ratta ferri vecchi?”

The street answered him with other sounds: a shutter lifted and thumped, a donkey’s hoof beat a hollow note, a woman called a child by some endearing name that held its own local flavor—one of those words that lose their savor when carried beyond their native quarter. Bratti’s cry kept its rhythm, the way a kitchen keeps its rhythm even under grief: the knife still falls, the pot still sings, the hands still know what they are doing.

“It’s worth but little,” he said presently, letting his voice drop back into talk. “Hose and altogether, your clothes are worth but little. Still, if you’ve a mind to set yourself up with a lute worth more than any new one, or with a sword that’s been worn by a Ridolfi, or with a paternoster of the best mode, I could let you have a great bargain, by making an allowance for the clothes. For, simple as I stand here, I’ve got the best-furnished shop in the Ferravecchi, and it’s close by the Mercato. The Virgin be praised! it’s not a pumpkin I carry on my shoulders. But I don’t stay caged in my shop all day: I’ve got a wife and a raven to stay at home and mind the stock. Chi abbaratta—baratta—b’ratta? ... And now, young man, where do you come from, and what’s your business in Florence?”

“I thought you liked nothing that came to you without a bargain,” said the stranger. “You’ve offered me nothing yet in exchange for that information.”

“Well, well; a Florentine doesn’t mind bidding a fair price for news: it stays the stomach a little though he may win no hose by it. If I take you to the prettiest damsel in the Mercato to get a cup of milk—that will be a fair bargain.”

“Nay; I can find her myself, if she be really in the Mercato; for pretty heads are apt to look forth of doors and windows. No, no. Besides, a sharp trader like you ought to know that he who bids for nuts and news, may chance to find them hollow.”

“Ah! young man,” Bratti said, giving him a side glance—half suspicion, half admiration—“you were not born of a Sunday—the salt-shops were open when you came into the world. You’re not a Hebrew, eh?—come from Spain or Naples, eh? Let me tell you the Frati Minori are trying to make Florence as hot as Spain for those dogs of hell that want to get all the profit of usury to themselves and leave none for Christians; and when you walk the Calimara with a piece of yellow cloth in your cap, it will spoil your beauty more than a sword-cut across that smooth olive cheek of yours. Abbaratta, baratta—chi abbaratta?—I tell you, young man, grey cloth is against yellow cloth; and there’s as much grey cloth in Florence as would make a gown and cowl for the Duomo, and there’s not so much yellow cloth as would make hose for Saint Christopher—blessed be his name, and send me a sight of him this day!—Abbaratta, baratta, b’ratta—chi abbaratta?”

“All that is very amusing information you are parting with for nothing,” the stranger returned, with a little edge of scorn. “But it happens not to concern me. I am no Hebrew.”

“See, now!” Bratti cried, delighted as if he had made a clean trade without putting hand to purse. “I’ve made a good bargain with mere words. I’ve made you tell me something, young man, though you’re as hard to hold as a lamprey. San Giovanni be praised! a blind Florentine is a match for two one-eyed men. But here we are in the Mercato.”

They came out of the close alleys into the broad piazza men called the Mercato Vecchio, the Old Market. It had been the belly of Florence for longer than anyone could count: provision-traffic from time immemorial, so that fond minds said the very ancestors from Fiesole once descended here from their high fastness to trade with the valley’s rustics. Wealth had not fled it in those earlier decades of the century: the Medici and other powerful houses of the popolani grassi had lived there, their ears perhaps not much offended by the loud roar of mingled dialects, their eyes not much shocked by the butchers’ stalls that old Antonio Pucci praised as a chief dignity of any market worth the name.

Yet that particular glory of mutton and veal was absent now: Lent still lay over Florence like a thin veil over bright food. The butchers’ “Art” stood in abeyance, and it was the harvest-time of those who trafficked in what the season allowed—market-gardeners with their heaps of greens, cheesemongers, sellers of macaroni, corn, eggs, milk, dried fruits. The change pulled women’s voices to the fore, so that the piazza rang with a sharper treble, the calls rising and falling like birds over a field.

Even so, the market had its perennial chorus: the experimental ringing of pots and pans; the chink and slap of money at the changers’ boards; the tempting cries from old-clothes stalls; the dice-players’ challenges; the vaunting of new linens and woollens, of wooden-ware, kettles, frying-pans. Narrow inlets choked with mules and carts, and the air thickened with remonstrance—those uncomplimentary phrases that seem to remain unchanged from age to age when bodies are crowded and tempers warmed.

And beneath the uglier sights—rags, beggary, the open-air drama of gaming where eagerness trembled and despair went blank, where sobs and blasphemy and blows came in their bitter sequence—there were still gentler offerings. Brood-rabbits with wide astonished eyes; doves and singing-birds for children’s gifts; kittens for sale; and here and there a handsome gattuccio, a Tom-cat with the highest character for mousing, his whiskers twitching at every scent. There were young cheeks, softly rounded and freshened by the start from far-off castelli at daybreak; and older faces, too, with the unfading charm of honest goodwill—like the steady fragrance of bread that persists even when the oven is cold.

High on a pillar in the center—an old pillar fetched from San Giovanni—stood Donatello’s stone Plenty, with a fountain near it where, as Pucci said, the good wives freshened their utensils and their throats also; not because wine was beyond them, but because they preferred to save the money for their husbands. The water’s trickle made its own mild music under the louder human din, a small clean sound that could steady the mind if one listened for it.

But on this particular morning the market’s face looked altered, as if some gust had turned every head at once. The deschi were only partly dressed with their goods, and purchasers had indeed assembled, alert for the finest greens, the most unexceptionable butter; yet when Bratti and the stranger entered, attention had slid away from bargaining into talk. Traders had turned their backs on their wares and joined knots of speakers that clustered at different points. An old-clothes vendor, caught mid-action in hanging out a pair of long hose, had in his distraction slung them round his own neck and hurried after the nearest group. A cheesemonger, oratorical by trade, stood with a piece of marzolino in one hand and a knife in the other, scoring his emphatic pauses straight into the cheese as if it were paper. Elderly market-women, their egg-baskets at dangerous angles, contributed a wailing fugue of invocation that rose and broke like wind over reeds.

In the general distraction, the Florentine boys—sour crabs, and proud of it—saw opportunity as quickly as a rat scents an unguarded pantry. Some darted at nuts and dried figs; others chose the farinaceous Lenten delicacies at cooked provision-stalls. Dogs, too, who had learned to take kindly to meatless fare, nosed with discriminating hunger and then vanished under the nearest shelter. Mules, kicking and plunging among impeding baskets, stretched their muzzles toward aromatic green-meat, snatching at parsley and fennel; the torn stems released their sharp scent in little bursts, as if the air itself were bruised.

“Diavolo!” Bratti muttered as he and his companion came, quite unnoticed, upon the noisy scene. “The Mercato is gone as mad as if the most Holy Father had excommunicated us again. I must know what this is. But never fear: it seems a thousand years to you till you see the pretty Tessa and get your cup of milk; but keep hold of me, and I’ll hold to my bargain. Remember, I’m to have the first bid for your suit—specially for the hose, which, with all their stains, are the best panno di garbo—as good as ruined, though, with mud and weather stains.”

He turned toward an old woman on the outside of the nearest group and shouted close to her ear, as if he meant to drive his words in like a nail.

“Ola, Monna Trecca! Here are the mules upsetting all your bunches of parsley: is the world coming to an end, then?”

Monna Trecca wheeled round, half fierce, half bewildered—eyes darting from Bratti to her disarranged goods and back again. Then she rushed toward her stall, flinging her first volley of rage at him as if he had personally loosened the mule’s halter.

“A bad Easter and a bad year to you, and may you die by the sword!”

Bratti, untroubled, slipped into the place she had left, within hearing of the narrative that had been holding her. He made a sign to the stranger to keep close.

“I tell you I saw it myself,” said a fat man, gripping a bunch of newly bought leeks as if the green shafts were proof in his hand. “I was in Santa Maria Novella and saw it myself. The woman started up and threw out her arms, and cried out and said she saw a big bull with fiery horns coming down on the church to crush it. I saw it myself.”

“Saw what, Goro?” asked a slim man with a roguish twinkle, dressed in a close jerkin, a skull-cap lodged carelessly over his left ear as if it had fallen there by chance. A delicate linen apron was tucked up on one side, and a razor sat in his belt. “Saw the bull, or only the woman?”

“Why, the woman, to be sure; but it’s all one, mi pare,” the fat man answered, contemptuous of such distinctions. “It doesn’t alter the meaning—va!”

“Meaning? no, no; that’s clear enough,” several voices broke in at once—and then the market’s speech became a hailstorm of phrases: “Lights shooting over San Lorenzo for three nights together”—“Thunder in the clear starlight”—“Lantern of the Duomo struck with the sword of Saint Michael”—“Palle”—“All smashed”—“Lions tearing each other to pieces”—“Ah! and they might well”—“Boto caduto in Santissima Nunziata!”—“Died like the best of Christians”—“God will have pardoned him”—words crossing and striking, each speaker needing utterance more than reply.

Perhaps only three were silent: Bratti, piecing scraps into sense; the barber with the razor; and a thin-lipped, eager-looking man in spectacles with a pen-and-ink case at his belt—the sort who carried words like tools and made his living by their exactness.

“Ebbene, Nello,” Bratti said, edging near enough to speak to the barber. “It appears the Magnifico is dead—rest his soul!—and the price of wax will rise?”

“Even as you say,” Nello answered; and then, with a sudden grave speed that made the words tumble like beans on a table, he added, “and his waxen image in the Nunziata fell at the same moment, they say—or at some other time, whenever it pleases the Frati Serviti, who know best. And several cows and women have had still-born calves this Quaresima; and for the bad eggs that have been broken since the Carnival, nobody has counted them. Ah! a great man—a great politician—a greater poet than Dante. And yet the cupola didn’t fall, only the lantern. Che miracolo!”

A sharp lengthened “Pst!” cut across the guttural pelting. It came from the pale man in spectacles. The effect was immediate: the noise dropped, and all eyes fixed on him, waiting—as mouths wait for salt.

“’Tis well said you Florentines are blind,” he began, in an incisive high voice. “It appears to me, you need nothing but a diet of hay to make cattle of you. What! do you think the death of Lorenzo is the scourge God has prepared for Florence? Go! you are sparrows chattering praise over the dead hawk. What! a man who was trying to slip a noose over every neck in the Republic that he might tighten it at his pleasure! You like that; you like to have the election of your magistrates turned into closet-work, and no man to use the rights of a citizen unless he is a Medicean. That is what is meant by qualification now: netto di specchio no longer means that a man pays his dues to the Republic: it means that he’ll wink at robbery of the people’s money—at robbery of their daughters’ dowries; that he’ll play the chamberer and the philosopher by turns—listen to bawdy songs at the Carnival and cry ‘Bellissimi!’—and listen to sacred lauds and cry again ‘Bellissimi!’ But this is what you love: you grumble and raise a riot over your quattrini bianchi; but you take no notice when the public treasury has got a hole in the bottom for the gold to run into Lorenzo’s drains. You like to pay for footmen to walk before and behind one of your citizens, that he may be affable and condescending to you. ‘See, what a tall Pisan we keep,’ say you, ‘to march before him with the drawn sword flashing in our eyes!—and yet Lorenzo smiles at us. What goodness!’ And you think the death of a man, who would soon have saddled and bridled you as the Sforza has saddled and bridled Milan—you think his death is the scourge God is warning you of by portents. I tell you there is another sort of scourge in the air.”

“Nay, nay, Ser Cioni,” Nello put in, light as a hand that flicks flour from a sleeve, “keep astride your politics, and never mount your prophecy; politics is the better horse. But if you talk of portents, what portent can be greater than a pious notary? Balaam’s ass was nothing to it.”

“Ay, but a notary out of work, with his inkbottle dry,” said another bystander, ragged at the elbows. “Better don a cowl at once, Ser Cioni: everybody will believe in your fasting.”

Ser Cioni turned away and left the group, his indignation stiff as dried paste. As he moved, he uncovered the sallow yet mild face of a short man who had been standing behind him, shoulders bent by some sedentary labor, as if his back had learned to bow over parchment.

“By San Giovanni, though,” the fat man with leeks said, as if his beliefs had been jostled out of their accustomed basket, “I am not sure there isn’t some truth in what Ser Cioni says. For I know I have good reason to find fault with the quattrini bianchi myself. Grumble, did he say? Suffocation! I should think we do grumble; and let anybody say the word, I’ll turn out into the piazza with the readiest, sooner than have our money altered in our hands as if the magistracy were so many necromancers. And it’s true Lorenzo might have hindered such work if he would—and for the bull with the flaming horns, why, as Ser Cioni says, there may be many meanings to it. It may have more to do with the taxes than we think. For when God above sends a sign, it’s not to be supposed he’d have only one meaning.”

“Spoken like an oracle, Goro!” Nello said. “Why, when we poor mortals can pack two or three meanings into one sentence, it were mere blasphemy not to believe that your miraculous bull means everything that any man in Florence likes it to mean.”

“Thou art pleased to scoff, Nello,” said the sallow, round-shouldered man, now visible, “but it is not the less true that every revelation, whether by visions, dreams, portents, or the written word, has many meanings, which it is given to the illuminated only to unfold.”

“Assuredly,” Nello answered. “Haven’t I been to hear the Frate in San Lorenzo? But then, I’ve been to hear Fra Menico in the Duomo too; and according to him, your Fra Girolamo, with his visions and interpretations, is running after the wind of Mongibello, and those who follow him are like to have the fate of certain swine that ran headlong into the sea—or some hotter place. With San Domenico roaring è vero in one ear, and San Francisco screaming è falso in the other, what is a poor barber to do—unless he were illuminated? But it’s plain our Goro here is beginning to be illuminated, for he already sees that the bull with the flaming horns means first himself, and secondly all the other aggrieved taxpayers of Florence, who are determined to gore the magistracy on the first opportunity.”

“Goro is a fool!” said a bass voice, dropping into the chatter like a great bell among little tinklings. “Let him carry home his leeks and shake his flanks over his wool-beating. He’ll mend matters more that way than by showing his tun-shaped body in the piazza, as if everybody might measure his grievances by the size of his paunch. The burdens that harm him most are his heavy carcass and his idleness.”
