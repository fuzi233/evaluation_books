Arthur Pendennis's reticence on matters of the heart puzzled his friend Warrington, despite their easy camaraderie. Though Arthur freely discussed his escapades with his tutor Smirke, his wife, and the peculiarities of their Anglo-Norman church, he evaded any mention of Blanche. When pressed for details, Arthur described her as a kind and intelligent young woman, capable of being a suitable partner, but insisted he had no intentions of marriage, his romantic inclinations now tempered by his maturity. He claimed contentment with his current circumstances, and left it at that.

Meanwhile, elegant satin envelopes bearing cryptic messages arrived at Lamb Court, Temple, adorned with an intricate cipher that would have revealed the identity of the mysterious correspondent – a young lady whose initials were B.A. Arthur responded with witty letters, replete with clever wordplay and poetic flourishes, which he penned in his most gallant tone. These epistles were likely inspired by Blanche's own poetic efforts, whose name, much like "branch," "stanch," and "launch," held a certain musical quality. One couldn't help but wonder if Arthur's skill with language had led him to craft verses that would eventually find publication in the esteemed 'Roseleaves' annual, edited by the illustrious Lady Violet Lebas and illustrated by the renowned artist Pinkney.

As Arthur scribbled away at the club, awaiting dinner, his uncle, the elder Pendennis, espied the young man's creative endeavors and cautioned him. "Verses are well and good, Arthur, and a harmless correspondence between old friends is acceptable, but be mindful of your words. Who knows what unforeseen consequences may arise? The safest approach is to maintain discretion. I've learned from experience – and I'm not one to lecture, having had my share of entanglements in high society." He regaled Arthur with stories of his own misadventures, including the tale of young Spoony, who, through overly ardent expressions in a poem to the widow Naylor, found himself bound to a woman twice his age, courtesy of Colonel Flint's disapproving visit. He also shared the tale of Louisa Salter, whose ill-advised letters to Sir John Bird ultimately led to her own heartbreak. The elder Pendennis's words served as a reminder to Arthur to exercise caution in his correspondence, lest he suffer a similar fate.
