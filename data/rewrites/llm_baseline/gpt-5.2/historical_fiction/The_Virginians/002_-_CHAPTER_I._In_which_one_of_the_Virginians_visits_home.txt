On the paneled wall of a certain American scholar—one who made his fame not by steel but by sentences—there hang two swords crossed in mute argument. One was drawn for a king, the other for a republic; both are now only bright shapes catching light in a quiet room. I have seen, too, in an English parlor, two portraits facing one another across a fireplace: a colonel in scarlet, a general in blue and buff. In that household they have long been called, with a family fondness that turns history into a nickname, “The Virginians.”

Their story survives because a woman saved it the way a careful housewife saves seed-corn and preserves fruit against winter. Madam Esmond—so she was styled ever after Castlewood in Virginia burned and she took refuge in Richmond—kept her sons’ letters as if each sheet were a cloth laid over something perishable. The earliest is a child’s scrawl with duty and love; the last came “from my beloved son” only hours before her own death. Between are pages stained with travel, with tears, with camp-smoke ground into the paper; one is pierced by a bullet, another blotched by blood so that half the inked words are swallowed up, like a voice drowned by a sudden roar. The letters themselves are often scanty—hints, not full pictures. So, bending over the yellowed folds, I have tried to hear the rooms they were written in, to catch the clink of glasses after a ball, the crackle of a fire by a bivouac, the hush of a sick-chamber, and to set down what the writers only half-said.

It was a summer morning in 1756, in the reign of King George the Second, when the Young Rachel, Virginian ship—Edward Franks, master—came up the Avon with the tide, returning, as she did yearly, from the Potomac. The river’s brackish breath mixed with the sweeter smell of tar and wet rope; the rigging spoke in small creaks and complaints as the ship steadied herself for Bristol. She moored in the stream as near to Trail’s wharf as might be, and Mr. Trail—part owner, factor, and a man who could look from his counting-house window and count his profit by the angle of a mast—took boat and came aboard.

He was large, grave, and neatly self-possessed, wearing his own hair and a demure face as if piety were part of his cargo. He shook Captain Franks’s hand and congratulated him upon a quick and fortunate passage, pausing to acknowledge Heaven’s mercies with the same air he used in balancing a ledger. But gratitude in Mr. Trail was a short prayer that led directly to business.

“Cargo? Passengers?” he asked.

Franks was a broad, easy fellow, with a sailor’s relish for a jest and a sailor’s bluntness in offering it.

“We have,” said he, “but yonder ugly negro boy, fetching the trunks, and a passenger who has had the state cabin to himself.”

Mr. Trail’s face fell, as if Providence had been stingy.

“Confound you, Franks, and your luck! The Duke William, in last week, brought fourteen—and she’s not half our tonnage.”

“And this passenger,” Franks went on, enjoying the pinch he gave, “who has had the whole cabin, don’t pay nothin’.”

Mr. Trail stared as if he had been told the ship had sailed without ballast.

“A passenger take the whole cabin and not pay? Gracious mercy, are you a fool, Captain Franks?”

“Ask the passenger himself—for here he comes.”

As the words were spoken, a young gentleman came up from below: nineteen, straight-limbed, handsome in that clear, fresh way youth has before it learns to guard its face. He carried a cloak and a sword under his arm. His clothes were deep mourning, which made his skin look the fairer and his eyes the more alive. Behind him, in black likewise, followed the boy called Gumbo, struggling with baggage.

“Gumbo, you idiot,” the young man cried, quick with impatience, “why don’t you fetch the baggage out of the cabin? Well, shipmate, our journey is ended. You will see all the little folks to-night you’ve been talking about. Give my love to Polly and Betty and little Tommy—not forgetting my duty to Mrs. Franks. Yesterday I thought the voyage would never be done, and now I am almost sorry it is over. That little berth in my cabin looks very comfortable now I am going to leave it.”

Mr. Trail scowled at this passenger who had cost him a cabin and brought him no coin. He was barely civil with his nod; but Franks, with the pride of a man introducing a friend, said:

“This here gentleman is Mr. Trail, sir, whose name you have a-heerd of.”

“It’s pretty well known in Bristol, sir,” Mr. Trail said, drawing himself up with a merchant’s majesty.

“And this,” Captain Franks continued, “is Mr. Warrington—Madam Esmond Warrington’s son, of Castlewood.”

The effect was as immediate as the change of wind in a sail. Mr. Trail’s hat flew off as though snatched by reverence; he bowed again and again, his face smoothing itself into cordiality.

“Gracious powers! Mr. Warrington! This is a delight indeed! What a crowning mercy that your voyage should have been so prosperous! You must have my boat to go ashore. Let me cordially and respectfully welcome you to England—let me shake your hand as the son of my benefactress and patroness, Mrs. Esmond Warrington, whose name is known and honoured on Bristol ’Change, I warrant you. Isn’t it, Franks?”

“There’s no sweeter tobacco comes from Virginia, and no better brand than the Three Castles,” said Franks, and brought out a great brass tobacco-box with the air of a man producing a family treasure. He lifted the lid; the scent sprang up—dark, warm, almost fruity, with that clean bitterness that sits at the back of the tongue like a promise. He packed a quid into his mouth as if he were stowing cargo. “You don’t know what a comfort it is, sir. You’ll take to it, bless you, as you grow older. Won’t he, Mr. Trail? I wish you had ten shiploads of it instead of one. You might have ten shiploads—I’ve told Madam Esmond so; I’ve rode over her plantation; she treats me like a lord when I go to the house; she don’t grudge me the best wine, nor keep me cooling my heels in the counting-room as some folks does”—and here he looked squarely at Mr. Trail—“She is a real born lady, she is; and might have a thousand hogsheads as easy as her hundreds, if there were but hands enough.”

Mr. Trail, who heard “hands” as a bell hears its own ringing, leaned in with eager softness.

“I have lately engaged in the Guinea trade, and could supply her ladyship with any number of healthy young negroes before next fall,” he said, as though offering a new kind of barrel or rope.

The young Virginian’s face tightened; a different cold entered his voice.

“We are averse to the purchase of negroes from Africa. My grandfather and my mother have always objected to it, and I do not like to think of selling or buying the poor wretches.”

Mr. Trail lifted his hands in a pious flutter.

“It is for their good, my dear young sir! for their temporal and their spiritual good! And we purchase the poor creatures only for their benefit; let me talk this matter over with you at my own house. I can introduce you to a happy home, a Christian family, and a British merchant’s honest fare. Can’t I, Captain Franks?”

“Can’t say,” growled Franks. “Never asked me to take bite or sup at your table. Asked me to psalm-singing once, and to hear Mr. Ward preach: don’t care for them sort of entertainments.”

Mr. Trail, pretending not to hear, lowered his tone into something meant for confidential wisdom.

“Business is business, my dear young sir, and ’tis only my duty—the duty of all of us—to cultivate the fruits of the earth in their season. As the heir of Lady Esmond’s estate—for I speak, I believe, to the heir of that great property?—”

The young gentleman bowed, but it was the bow of someone enduring, not receiving.

“I would urge upon you, at the very earliest moment, the propriety—the duty—of increasing the ample means with which Heaven has blessed you. As an honest factor, I could not do otherwise; as a prudent man, should I scruple to speak of what will tend to your profit and mine? No, my dear Mr. George.”

“My name is not George,” the young man said, turning his head sharply away. His eyes filled, in spite of himself, and a tear slid down as if grief had been waiting behind the talk of profits. “My name is Henry.”

“Gracious powers!” Mr. Trail gasped, struck at last by the mourning and the tears. “What do you mean, sir? Did you not say you were my lady’s heir? and is not George Esmond Warrington, Esq.—”

“Hold your tongue, you fool!” Captain Franks cried, and cuffed the merchant’s sleek side with a sailor’s hard hand. “Don’t you see the young gentleman a-swabbing his eyes, and note his black clothes?”

Mr. Trail recoiled, offended at being handled, yet still bewildered by the correction.

“What do you mean, Captain Franks, by laying your hand on your owners? Mr. George is the heir; I know the Colonel’s will well enough.”

“Mr. George is there,” said Franks, and jerked his thumb upward, toward the deck and the sky beyond.

“Where?” Mr. Trail cried, looking about him as if the dead might step from behind a coil of rope.

“Mr. George is there!” Franks repeated, pointing higher, past mast and rigging. “He is dead a year, sir, come next ninth of July. He would go out with General Braddock on that dreadful business to the Belle Riviere. He and a thousand more never came back again. Every man of ’em was murdered as he fell. You know the Indian way, Mr. Trail?” And with his hand he made, quick and dreadful, a circle round his head.

The deck seemed to hush for a moment, as if even the river had stopped its small lap against the hull. Franks’s voice, rough as it was, had the tremor of honest pity.

“Horrible, ain’t it? horrible! He was a fine young man—the very picture of this one; only his hair was black, which is now hanging in a bloody Indian wigwam. He was often on board the Young Rachel, and would have his chests of books broke open on deck before they was landed. A shy and silent young gent— not like this one, which was the merriest, wildest young fellow, full of songs and fun. He took on dreadful at the news; went to his bed; had that fever which lays so many of ’em by the heels along that swampy Potomac, but he’s got better on the voyage. The voyage makes every one better; and in course the young gentleman can’t be for ever a-crying after a brother who dies and leaves him a great fortune. Ever since we sighted Ireland he has been quite gay and happy, only he would go off at times, when he was most merry, saying, ‘I wish my dearest Georgy could enjoy this here sight along with me,’ and when you mentioned the t’other’s name, you see, he couldn’t stand it.”

And now the Captain’s own eyes brimmed; he turned his head away, ashamed, like a man caught praying.

Mr. Trail arranged his face into a proper sorrow, ready with condolences that would offend no customer; but Henry Warrington answered him curtly, declined the offered hospitality, and stayed in the merchant’s house only long enough to swallow a glass of wine and take up money he needed. If Mr. Trail’s wine was honest, it had no welcome in it; it went down like duty, not comfort.

With Captain Franks the parting was altogether different. There were warm grips of the hand, messages repeated, promises made; and as Henry left the ship, the little crew of the Young Rachel cheered him from the side until the sound scattered over the water.

He and his dead brother had pored over maps as boys pore over cookbooks—hungry with imagination, tasting places before they ever see them. They had traced a pilgrimage through England: Bristol, Bath, Salisbury, Winchester, Hexton—Home. They had named the great sights as if naming dishes on a grand table: London with its enormous churches; the Tower with its cold stories; Whitehall’s fatal window; parks and playhouses; Shakespeare’s resting-place by Avon among the Warwickshire pastures; the battlefields where loyalty had bled and failed. Yet there was one point more sacred than all—a house in Hampshire, old Castlewood, spoken of in Virginia as “home” with that affectionate wrongness the colonies still used for the mother country.

Henry traveled like a man drawn by a cord. He was young, and health made a kind of music in his blood; he had money; and he had grief that flared and dulled by turns like a coal on the hearth. The post-chaise took him through an English summer that seemed to him almost extravagant in its abundance. Where his Virginian journeys had been through swamp and pine solitude from one rough ordinary to another log-house, here every mile offered a new arrangement of comfort: hedgerows thick as walls, meadows shining, villages tucked like careful garnishes along the road. The inns were loud with life; the highroad itself was no deserted strip but a busy artery. Heavy wagons went by, bells chiming; post-coaches rattled with practiced haste; packhorses still threaded along with patient feet; a nobleman’s chaise-and-six flashed past with outriders; squires rolled ponderously; farmers jogged toward market; a parson bumped along on Dumpling with his wife on the pillion.

Through open windows came the clatter of dishes and the thick, nourishing smells that make a traveler suddenly aware of his own hunger: roasting meat, bread cooling, ale frothing, onions sweating in fat. In kitchens he glimpsed copper pans bright as shields and heard the music of work—knife on board, spoon on pot, the hiss when something met hot iron. Those sounds were not noise, but the ordered acoustics of a living place: wheels on gravel, a smith’s hammer, a child’s cry, a distant church bell measuring the day. Even the great elms had their own soft speech overhead.

At each stop, eyes followed him. The plump landlady, surrounded by china and punch-bowls and stout gilded bottles of strong waters and glittering rows of silver flagons, looked kindly after the young gentleman in black. The obsequious chamberlain bowed him upstairs to the Rose or the Dolphin. The chambermaid made her best curtsey for the fee that would sweeten her evening. Gumbo, down in the kitchen among the drinkers by the great fire, bragged in his rough way of his master’s splendid house in Virginia and the immense wealth to which he was heir; and the townsfolk listened, weighing the boy’s dark face against the strange boast, and then peering more curiously when Henry passed.

Henry barely gave Bath Abbey more than a passing glance, nor Salisbury Minster more than a moment’s wonder; not because they were small, but because his desire was larger than sight-seeing. Until he beheld Home, he felt as if he had no eyes for any other place.

At last the post-chaise drew up at the rustic inn on Castlewood Green—an inn his grandsire had spoken of so often that Henry knew it before he saw it. From an elm near the porch swung its sign: the Three Castles of the Esmond family. The same cognisance appeared over the gateway of Castlewood House, but there it was joined with the dark symbolism of death: a hatchment showing the arms and, now, an earl’s coronet.

That hatchment was for Francis, Lord Castlewood, newly laid in the chapel hard by; his son reigned in his stead. Henry had heard of Frank all his life—how wild he had been, how bravely he had fought under Marlborough, how he had married abroad and taken up the wrong religion, how loyalty in the family ran so strong that even when he wavered between Prince and King it was still called loyalty, only turned to a different star. He had returned to the Established Church upon his second marriage, had supported the King and the Minister of the hour, and for those services had died an English peer. So much for a life: a coronet painted on a board above a gate, and then the board itself weathering under English rain.

Colonel Esmond—Henry’s grandfather—had corresponded with his stepson briefly but affectionately, and loved him in memory; Madam Esmond had always spoken of him with a cool contempt. “Dull,” she said, “except when he drank too much wine—which was every day at dinner.” A fine tall animal, yes, good-looking; but she’d rather her boys took a different model. The boys themselves, brought up on their mother’s staunch Jacobite talk (respectful to the reigning Majesty, but firm that right was right), had never learned to reverence the dead lord very much.

Now Henry, heart beating as if it were knocking on the gate before he did, left the inn and walked toward the house where his grandsire’s youth had been spent. Castlewood Green sloped down to a river crossed by an old bridge of a single broad arch; beyond, the ground rose gently toward the house—grey, gabled, buttressed, backed by a darker wood that seemed to hold its breath.

At the wicket sat an old man on a stone bench before the great arched entrance. An old dog crouched at his feet, keeping, as dogs do, a small warm vigil over age. Above the sentry at the gate was an open casement with homely flowers in the window, and behind it good-humoured girls’ faces peeped out, curious. They watched the young gentleman in black and the ebony attendant in black following him; and Henry noticed, with a start that made his grief feel suddenly shared, that the man at the gate wore mourning too, and when the girls came out they had black ribbons.

To Henry’s surprise, the old man spoke to him at once, naming him as if he had been expected.

“You have had a nice ride to Hexton, Master Harry, and the sorrel carried you well.”

“I think you must be Lockwood,” Henry said, his voice trembling in spite of his effort to steady it. He held out his hand. He had heard of Lockwood a hundred times in Virginia—how he had followed the Colonel and the young Viscount through Marlborough’s wars forty years before, how he had been faithful as a hound and stubborn as a gatepost.

The veteran looked puzzled at the offered hand, as though affection were a language he could no longer translate cleanly. The old dog lifted its eyes, regarded Henry, and then tucked its head between its knees again, as if refusing to be hurried into friendship.

“I have heard of you often. How did you know my name?” Henry asked.

“They say I forget most things,” the old man answered, smiling; “but I ain’t so bad as that quite. Only this mornin’, when you went out, my darter says, ‘Father, do you know why you have a black coat on?’ ‘In course I know why I have a black coat,’ says I. ‘My lord is dead. They say ’twas a foul blow, and Master Frank is my lord now, and Master Harry’—why, what have you done since you’ve went out this morning? Why, you have a-grow’d taller and changed your hair—though I know—I know you.”

By this time one of the young women had tripped out from the porter’s lodge and dropped Henry a pretty curtsey. Her cheeks were wholesome, her eyes bright; she had the look of someone who knows every sound a house makes at night and can tell by a creak whether it is wind or a thief.

“Grandfather sometimes does not recollect very well,” she said, and tapped her head gently. “Your honour seems to have heard of Lockwood?”

“And you—have you never heard of Colonel Francis Esmond?”

“He was Captain and Major in Webb’s Foot,” Lockwood cried at once, memory flaring up like a match in dry straw, “and I was with him in two campaigns, sure enough. Wasn’t I, Ponto?” The old dog’s tail thumped once, as if the name of war were still a summons.

“The Colonel as married Viscountess Rachel, my late lord’s mother?” the girl said eagerly. “And went to live amongst the Indians? We have heard of him. Sure we have his picture in our gallery, and hisself painted it.”

“Went to live in Virginia,” Henry said, “and died there seven years ago—and I am his grandson.”

The girl’s face changed; astonishment loosened into warmth.

“Lord, your honour! Why, your honour’s skin’s as white as mine,” she cried—blurting the thought as simply as a country girl speaks of cream. Then she turned to the old man. “Grandfather, do you hear this? His honour is Colonel Esmond’s grandson that used to send you tobacco, and his honour have come all the way from Virginia.”

“To see you, Lockwood,” Henry said, and the words, meant kindly, came out with a thickness in his throat. “And the family. I only set foot on English ground yesterday, and my first visit is for home. I may see the house, though the family are from home?”

The girl—Molly, as it seemed by the ease with which Lockwood’s speech had named her—said she dared say Mrs. Barker would let his honour see the house. And Henry Warrington crossed the court as if his feet remembered what his life had never known, while Molly followed, and Gumbo behind them made her a profusion of bows and speeches, his voice fluttering and over-earnest in the new acoustics of England, where every word of his sounded like a foreign coin struck on a familiar table.
