The Knights of the Temple

In the realm of antiquated colleges, schools, and Inns of Court, a stubborn respect for the past clings like mildew to the bricks. While modern workhouses and prisons boast sanitary marvels and running water, these venerable halls remain stuck in the Stone Age of hygiene. A mechanic in Spitalfields commands a cistern; the "Gentlemen" of the Inns endure a regime where water is fetched in jugs by laundresses who seem to enjoy the chore, and where sleeping quarters are priced at the cost of suburban villas. It is a curious paradox: the ancestors of these "Knights of the Temple" were, by all accounts, the Great Unwashed, yet today they occupy rooms built before the concept of decency existed. One can almost hear the ghost of Old Grump, a veteran of the Norfolk Circuit, cursing the heavens every time a drop of shower-bath water trickled through his roof. Grump, now a grizzled relic of half-a-century, had never touched a bath in his life, declaring the whole affair a dandified folly. He dreamed of a benevolent knight devising "Hummums"—portable baths—for the benefit of the legal fraternity, but the Temple historian remains silent on such philanthropy. Instead, we have Pump Court and Fountain Court, where the water runs clear but the souls of the lawyers within seem perpetually dry.

Yet, despite the lack of plumbing, the Inns possess a rough charm that binds their inhabitants together. To the student of law, passing by chambers where Eldon lived or where Coke mused upon Littleton, offers a thrill of poetic reminiscence. The air is thick with the ghosts of Sir Roger de Coverley and Mr. Spectator, debating hoops and patches amidst the greenery of the Temple Gardens. It is a stage where the Bard, Fielding, and Johnson have all played their parts, turning the dusty corridors into a theater of literary history. If one could capture a single day in the four-story houses of the dingy courts where Pen and Warrington dwell, the result would be a volume as queer as an Asmodeus's diary.

Consider the drama unfolding on the ground floor. A parliamentary counsel, now a gentleman driving off to Belgravia, was recently a starving soul in a garret, surviving on stealthy literature and the patience of creditors. Now, the wheel of fortune has spun, and he enjoys a clerk's salary that his former self could barely afford for boot-black. Above him, on the first floor, resides a venerable recluse, a brain-full scholar who has amassed a fortune and a library of classics. He returns from the club, a godless old man, knowing that when he dies, his heirs will burn half his books. Further up, Mr. Doomsday enjoys his nightly ritual of Catullus and a steady rubber of whist with three seniors, following a heavy dinner of three bottles of Port. They are seen asleep in the Temple Church on Sundays, safe in their small fortunes, untouched by attorneys' troubles.

On the same side, where Pen and Warrington reside late into the night, sits Mr. Paley. This young man, a Fellow of his college, is a machine of industry. He reads and notes cases until two in the morning, rises at seven, and works until an hour before dinner, only to repeat the cycle until dawn. He is a fierce grasping intellect, deliberately excluding everything that is not law—from philosophy and poetry to love, beauty, and even the sound of a sweet song. Where Pen and Warrington could indulge in friendship and kindness, Paley must remain selfish, blind to the world outside his reading lamp. He shudders when meeting his old companion Warrington on the stairs, viewing him as doomed to perdition, unable to comprehend why anyone would waste time on anything other than the endless legend of the law.

Despite Paley's grim determination, Pen finds his life in the Temple to be a delightful adventure. Though his family believes he is reforming, studying law with the seriousness of a regular student, Pen is actually enjoying the rough pleasures of a London bachelor. He has shed his aristocratic airs and the dandyish pretenses of his college days. He walks in the Park watching horses instead of riding them himself; he pulls on the river and stretches up to Hampstead. His routine involves a morning's study, a tavern dinner, a bachelor night of joviality, and a quiet evening with a pipe and a humble potation of British spirits, whose quality Mrs. Flanagan, the laundress, always tests. He attends Hall regularly, finding the scene of the Upper Temple Hall fascinating. It is a microcosm of the era: stout grey-headed attorneys, dandies in rowing shirts, swarthy colonials, and lively Irishmen.

The dinner in the Hall is a spectacle of abundance. Messes of four share a joint of beef or mutton, ample pie, and a bottle of port. The "honest habitues," however, employ harmless arts to improve their feast. Enter Mr. Lowton, a Temple gourmand and captain of the mess. Lowton is a man of keen strategy, aiming to secure the thirteenth glass of port and the choice cuts of meat. With a wink and a tug on Pen's gown, he guides the newcomer toward the secrets of the hall. He points out the notables: Boosey the bencher, likely eating turtle; Balls and Swettenham, the "regular swells"; and the mysterious trio who sit opposite—the "High Table" guests who claim to dine but merely pretend, avoiding wine so that the messmates can help themselves to the bottle.

"Why do you like to mess with them if they don't eat?" Pen asks, puzzled.
"How green you are!" exclaims Lowton. "They don't drink any wine, and a fellow gets the bottle to himself if he likes it."

As the meal proceeds, a strange figure enters the fray. Dressed in old, faded clothes and a gown that hangs behind him like a tattered sail, he strides across the table to sit beside Pen. His shirt is clean but ragged, a stark contrast to the magnificent pink raiment of Molloy Maloney in the adjacent mess. He smiles at Pen, recognizing a kindred spirit. "We ought to know each other," he says. "My name's Warrington."

Arthur Pendennis is delighted. "Are you St—— Warrington?" he asks, recalling the hero who thrashed the bargeman Bill Simes.
"Warrington—yes," laughs the stranger. "I recollect you in your freshman's term. But you appear to have quite cut me out."

Pen speaks of college legends, mentioning the bargeman and the Miss Notleys, before Warrington hushes him gently. "Glad to make your acquaintance, Pendennis. Heard a good deal about you."

An instant bond forms. The two young men fall deep into college talk, ignoring the formalities of the Hall. Seeing Warrington take his share of the port with gusto, Pen abandons his earlier pretense of dislike for the vintage, much to Lowton's dismay. After dinner, Warrington invites Pen back to his chambers in Lamb Court for a short pipe. Pen agrees, leaving Lowton sighing behind them.

Back in Lamb Court, Pen and Warrington become sworn friends. Warrington's cheerful temperament, his good sense, and his rough welcome charm Pen far more than the silent politeness of the Polytechnic. Soon, Pen moves out of his expensive lodgings in St. James's, finding it far more economical to share Warrington's room. Even Pidgeon and Flanagan divide their allegiance between the two friends. Together, they navigate the chaotic, humorous, and sometimes grim world of the Temple, bound by shared memories and the simple joy of a pipe and a joke.
