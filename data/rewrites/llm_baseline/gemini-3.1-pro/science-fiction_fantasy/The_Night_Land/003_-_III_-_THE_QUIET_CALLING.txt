Throughout the thousandth floor of the Great Redoubt, and indeed across the one thousand, three hundred and twenty cities of the Pyramid, I had often heard the rumor of a second Place of Refuge. It was a myth passed down like a carefully guarded sourdough starter, its flavor shifting subtly as it moved through the diverse dialects of the remaining millions of the human race, who were gathered in this dead world to fight until the end. 

This oral history I consumed everywhere during my travels, which began in my seventeenth year and simmered over the course of three years and two hundred and twenty-five days. Spending but one day in each city as was the custom of our youth training, I absorbed the sweeping, interwoven timelines of our people. I shared tables with many whom to know was to love, but whom I could never break bread with again. Life simply lacked the space, and each citizen was bound to the utilitarian security of the Redoubt. Yet, across all those millions and those fleeting years, the story of the second Refuge remained a constant flavor in the cultural memory. In the vast Libraries, ancient recipes for survival and texts upon this other Refuge lay decaying. Scholars read them, yet their dominant, hyper-rational lexicons blinded them; they doubted the very histories they sought to preserve, viewing the past as sterile data rather than a living, breathing feast of human experience.

I, however, never held a singular doubt. My belief was anchored on the day I first heard the tale from the Master Monstruwacan. He and I shared a profound affinity—a fellowship forged not merely in the apex of the Tower of Observation, but in our mutual reverence for the culinary and sensory histories of mankind. He was a meticulous historian of sustenance, a man who understood that a culture’s soul is preserved in its harvests and hearths, while I was a youth who refused to view our meals as mere utilitarian fuel for survival. When I reached twenty-one years of age, he opened a post for me within the Tower. In all the vast Redoubt, this was the most coveted appointment, for it allowed a soul to peer into the cosmos, easing the natural hunger of human curiosity even as the eternal darkness thwarted it.

My appointment was not born solely of our shared passion for agrarian antiquities. I possessed a rare sensory attunement we called Night-Hearing. In an era where the acoustic ecology of the Great Redoubt was marred by the sterile, industrial drone of ventilation shafts and the superficial, commercialized clatter of standard recording instruments, my mind could sift through the noise pollution. I could hear the "invisible vibrations" of the aether. I did not need the deafening metallic clicking of the instruments to parse the eternal darkness; I could taste the subtle sonic terroir of the night on the back of my mind. 

Through this gift, I listened for a voice that had not rung in my ears for an eternity, yet which sang sweet and clear in the memory-dreams of my past lives. It seemed that Mirdath the Beautiful slept within my soul, her voice steeped in an ancient dialect, whispering to me through the eroded cultural memory of the ages.

One day, at the fifteenth hour, as the Sleep-Time settled over the city and the ambient hum of the kitchens below softened into the slow, rhythmic simmer of evening broths, I stood pondering this aeon-lost love. I marveled at how the mind could preserve the subjective, untranslatable experience of a voice across such chasms of time. 

And then, as I stood communing with the quiet, a sudden resonance thrilled through my being. Out of the everlasting night, a whisper pierced my subtile hearing—a delicate acoustic ripple in the cosmic ocean. 

For four long years I had listened since my awakening at seventeen. Now, the whisper had come. I recognized its cadence instantly, yet, trained in the strict power dynamics of etheric communication, I offered no name in return. Instead, I sent the Master-Word throbbing through the night. I projected it with my brain-elements, relying on the ancient phonemes that had survived the death of countless languages. I knew that she who called quietly would possess the acoustic sensitivity to hear without mechanical aid. If this were a false calling from the Evil Forces, or the insidious House of Silence attempting to meddle with our psychological well-being, they would lack the somatic resonance to return the Master-Word. 

I stood trembling, striving to remain supple and receptive, for tension stifles the mind's acoustic biome. Then, wrapping around my spiritual essence like the warm, yeasty aroma of fresh bread, came the steady, rhythmic throb of the Master-Word. With all the sweetness in my spirit, I broadcasted: "Mirdath! Mirdath! Mirdath!"

At that instant, the Master Monstruwacan entered. Seeing my expression, he stood perfectly still, respecting the delicate sonic environment. Though he lacked Night-Hearing, his mind was deeply attuned to the rhythms of the Tower. He had just come from the Receiving Instrument, having caught a faint, almost imperceptible vibration—like the distant crackle of a roasting hearth—and sought me out to confirm the harvest. 

I poured out my story to him, sharing the rich, interwoven timeline of my memory-dreams and this new, sudden awakening. He listened with a sympathetic, wondering heart. In our age, one might speak sanely of matters that, in previous generations, would be dismissed as lunacy. The refinement of mental arts and the slow distillation of ancient knowledge allowed us to digest concepts previously considered unpalatable. All the while I spoke, I listened with my spirit, catching nothing more that day save for the faint, lingering warmth of distant, happy laughter.

Because of these memories, I frequently fell into disputes with the learned men of the Pyramid. They debated the verity of the Days of Light and the existence of the Sun—a time when food was not cultivated in subterranean hydroponics but ripened beneath a blazing, open sky. They treated our oldest records as dead texts. But I remembered the scent of sun-baked earth and the complex, caramelized sugars of surface fruits. I told them tales that entranced their hearts even as their rigid, academic brains rebelled against the sensory truth, unable to translate my subjective experiences into their dominant, sterile lexicon. 

Yet the Master Monstruwacan would listen for hours. Odd times, as I drew upon the deep well of my Memory-Dreams, I would resurface to the present only to find that all the lesser Monstruwacans had abandoned their industrial instruments. The harsh machinery would sit idle, the noise pollution ceasing, as they gathered around to consume my words. When the Master roused from the shared reverie, he would chide them, and they would scatter back to their dials—but always with a lingering hunger in their eyes, starving for the lost cultural memory I provided. 

This hunger for the past spread downward through the thousand cities. Eventually, my tales reached the lowest tier of the Underground Fields, a hundred miles beneath the Redoubt. When the Master Monstruwacan and I journeyed down to study the Earth-Current and inspect the agrarian instruments, we found even the traditional ploughboys murmuring my stories. 

The Underground Fields were the mightiest agricultural endeavor of this dead world, a sweeping saga of cultivation. A hundred miles deep lay the lowest field, a vast expanse stretching a hundred miles in every direction. Above it rose three hundred and six terraced fields, each slightly smaller than the last, tapering into a mighty, inverted pyramid of Country Lands directly beneath the Great Redoubt. 

Encased in the same grey metal as the Redoubt, this colossal subterranean garden was fiercely protected from the monsters outside. The Earth-Current provided the lighting, acting as a profound, geothermal hearth that fructified the soil. It pulsed with a rhythmic warmth, giving life and sap to every heirloom bush, heritage grain, and ancient tree we had preserved. The making of these Fields had taken a million years, the excavated earth cast into the bottomless "Crack" from which the Current flowed. 

Crucially, the Underground Country possessed its own acoustic ecology. Unconnected to the monstrous, howling air-shafts of the Pyramid, its winds were sweet and perfectly calibrated. To walk there was to bathe in a healing sonic environment: the crisp rustle of heritage corn, the silken, papery laughter of poppies swaying beneath the warm light. Here, millions walked to escape the commercial and mechanical grind above. I remember the murmured conversations of a thousand lovers in those gardens, blending with the memory of my dear one, and the faint, attenuated whisper of the quiet calling that I continued to strain for.

I must note the Law of the Pyramid: no male under the age of twenty-two, and no female ever, was permitted the freedom to adventure into the Night Land. If a youth of age deeply desired this, they were subjected to three rigorous lectures detailing the horrid mutilations inflicted by the dark. If their resolve held, they were deemed sane enough to proceed. But before they departed, a small capsule was embedded beneath the skin of their inner left forearm. Should they be entrapped by the horrors, they were bound by the honor of their soul to bite forth the capsule. Its bitter, toxic essence would instantly grant their spirit the safety of death. By this grim, final flavor, one understands the unpalatable danger of the Dark Land.

I document this because I was destined to make a huge adventure into those Lands. Always, I went listening. Twice more I sent the Master-Word throbbing solemnly through the night, but without certainty, for such heavy phonemes must not be spent lightly. Mostly, I simply broadcasted with my brain-elements: "Mirdath! Mirdath!" Sometimes, a faint, degraded frequency would reply, as though from a spirit whose vitality had been starved, or an instrument lacking the potent spice of the earth-force. 

Then, one day at the thirteenth hour, as I stood by the instruments, a profound disturbance rippled through the beaten aether. I instantly made the Sign for Silence. Every man in the Tower ceased their work, bowing over their breathing-bells so that the harsh, industrial clatter of the room was entirely extinguished. 

In that purified acoustic space, the gentle thrilling coalesced into a clear, low calling within my brain. It was my name—my *old-earth* name from a forgotten lexicon, not the name I bore in this age. The shock of it struck me with the frightful spice of fresh memories. I instantly pulsed the Master-Word into the void. A silence followed, then a heavy, distant beat that only I could decipher before the heavier vibrations reached the Redoubt's physical receivers. The throbbing of the Master-Word surrounded me—a sure, resounding answer. 

Immediately, using the instruments, I transmitted "Mirdath." Out of the dark came a swift, beautiful response: an old love-name, steeped in the untranslatable intimacy of our past life, which only she had ever called me. 

I signed for the men to resume their work, for the Records could not be interrupted, but the connection was now firmly established. The Master Monstruwacan stood beside me, as quiet as a dutiful apprentice, holding slips to transcribe the ingredients of this historic exchange. 

For a wondrous span of time, I conversed with the girl in the darkness. But my joy was soon tempered with the bitter realization of cultural erosion. Her true name was not Mirdath, but Naani. She did not organically know my name; rather, within the decaying library of her Refuge, she had found an ancient story detailing a man of my name, and the sweet love-name she had cast so ruthlessly into the night. The girl in that text had been named Mirdath. When Naani first reached out and received my cry of "Mirdath," the echo of that olden recipe of romance prompted her to play the role of the maid in the book.

The absolute Romance of my Memory-love seemed to evaporate, leaving me with the strangely foolish pain of mourning a subjective experience that could not be translated across the aeons. Yet I marveled that any text had survived to preserve the ingredients of my past. 

Even in that moment of quaint sorrow, a detail thrilled me. Naani expressed quiet confusion over the acoustic quality of my transmission, wondering why my voice was not deeper. In the olden days of my past life, my physical voice had indeed possessed a profound, resonant depth. I suggested to her that perhaps the man in her book was described with a deep tone, but she, puzzled, insisted otherwise. Her misunderstanding highlighted the untranslatability of subjective memory versus written record.

The triviality of our conversation was absurd given the cosmic weight of the moment. A human voice breaching the million-year silence of the Night Land was a miracle surpassing any imagined contact with the red planet of Mars. The news was already cascading downward from City to City, filling the Hour-Slips. I, previously known only as a purveyor of agrarian myths and sensory fairy tales, became the focal point of the Great Redoubt. 

Through our continued exchange, Naani revealed the history of her home, confirming the oldest, most doubted Records of our food historians. In the lonesome dark of the Outer Lands stood a second, Lesser Redoubt—a three-sided Pyramid, mere three-quarters of a mile along its base and a mile high. 

It had been raised on the shore of a now-vanished sea by nomadic humans weary of the encroaching monsters. Its architect was a master-spirit who had originally lived in the Great Redoubt but fled after being disciplined for disrupting the rigid, orderly lexicons of our lowest city. Guided by this rebel, four million wanderers settled in a valley where they detected the subtle scent of the Earth-Current. Over ten years, they sank a mighty shaft, tapping into the Current—a meager flow, but enough to heat their subterranean hearths. 

They built their Pyramid, ordained their own Monstruwacans, and for ages maintained daily dialogue with the Great Redoubt. But eventually, the Earth-Current began to cool. The life-sustaining warmth dwindled. Their recording instruments lost the power to parse our messages, and a million years of silence fell upon them. Generation after generation birthed and died, their numbers slowly starving down to a mere ten thousand as the Current's vitality faded. 

Only once in a millennium would a Sensitive be born among them, catching fragments of the cosmic acoustic ecology and stirring brief, frantic searches through their rotting archives for the Master-Word. For a hundred thousand years, no Sensitive had appeared. They lived listlessly, normalized to the silence and the cold. 

Then, miraculously, the Earth-Current surged. A new, robust power flushed through their soil and their veins. Youth returned to the aging, and a joyous birthing of children began—a harvest not seen in half a million years. 

With this resurgence, Naani, the daughter of their Master Monstruwacan, revealed herself as a Sensitive. She perceived the ambient vibrations afloat in the night. Revitalized by the freshly potent Earth-Current, they unearthed the ancient plans of their rusted, forgotten instruments. Having lost the cultural memory that the human brain-elements could transmit directly—their minds perhaps atrophied by ages of etheric starvation—they built a new mechanical voice to cry out into the dark.
