Margaret had braced herself for Mr. Bell’s coming as for a duty done for her father’s sake rather than for her own pleasure; yet when the familiar step sounded on the stair, and the door opened upon that keen, kindly face under its scholar’s gravity, she found herself slipping, as easily as into her old place by the hearth, into the simplest friendship in the world.

He chose the warmest corner by the fire, holding out his hands to the glow as if it were a good thing to be thanked for.

“You’ve no merit in pleasing me, child,” said he. “It’s hereditary. You come in and take possession of my regard as if it were your rightful room.”

“And you,” she answered, with a look that made his mock dignity crumble, “have no right to look so fresh and young under that Fellow’s cap and gown.”

“Fresh and young?” He lifted his brows. “You mean fresh and young in warmth and kindness, I suppose; for I’m afraid I must own I think your opinions are the oldest and mustiest I have met with this long time.”

“Hear this daughter of yours, Hale!” cried Mr. Bell, turning upon Mr. Hale with relish. “Her residence in Milton has quite corrupted her. She’s a democrat, a red republican, a member of the Peace Society, a socialist—”

“Papa, it’s all because I’m standing up for the progress of commerce,” said Margaret. “Mr. Bell would have had it keep still at exchanging wild-beast skins for acorns.”

“No, no. I’d dig the ground and grow potatoes. And I’d shave the wild-beast skin and make the wool into broadcloth. Don’t exaggerate, missy. But I’m tired of this bustle. Everybody rushing over everybody, in their hurry to get rich.”

“It is not everyone who can sit comfortably in a set of college rooms and let his riches grow without any exertion of his own,” Mr. Hale said quietly. “No doubt there is many a man here who would be thankful if his property would increase as yours has done, without his taking any trouble about it.”

“I don’t believe they would,” said Mr. Bell. “It’s the bustle and the struggle they like. As for sitting still, and learning from the past, or shaping out the future by faithful work done in a prophetic spirit—Why! Pooh! I don’t believe there’s a man in Milton who knows how to sit still; and it is a great art.”

“Milton people, I suspect, think Oxford men don’t know how to move,” said Margaret. “It would be a very good thing if they mixed a little more.”

“It might be good for the Miltoners. Many things might be good for them which would be very disagreeable for other people.”

“Are you not a Milton man yourself?” Margaret asked. “I should have thought you would have been proud of your town.”

“I confess, I don’t see what there is to be proud of. If you’ll only come to Oxford, Margaret, I will show you a place to glory in.”

“Well!” said Mr. Hale, with that gentle air of bringing contrary minds together as if they were both children to be made friends. “Mr. Thornton is coming to drink tea with us to-night, and he is as proud of Milton as you of Oxford. You two must try and make each other a little more liberal-minded.”

“I don’t want to be more liberal-minded, thank you,” said Mr. Bell.

“Is Mr. Thornton coming to tea, papa?” Margaret asked, lowering her voice.

“Either to tea or soon after. He could not tell. He told us not to wait.”

Mr. Thornton had said nothing at home—had not even asked his mother whether she had carried out her purpose of speaking to Miss Hale. He could imagine too plainly what he should hear: not Margaret’s words as they were, but as they would be strained and sharpened through another mind. And yet her name, her image, haunted him with a kind of tyranny. He blamed her—he was jealous of her—he had renounced her; and, in spite of all, he loved her sorely, with a savage, unwilling fidelity.

In his sleep she came towards him with outspread arms, light and gay, the very air about her seeming to dance; and the vision allured him while it filled him with loathing, because it was Margaret’s form with Margaret’s truth and gravity torn out of it, as if something false had taken her place. Waking, he could hardly separate the Una from the Duessa; and the dislike he felt for the latter clung like a foul mist round the former.

Still, pride kept him from avoiding the sight of her. He would neither seek her nor run from her. To prove his command over himself, he drew the afternoon out into a long chain of deliberate acts—this paper examined twice, that direction given with painful slowness, each movement forced into unnatural composure—until it was past eight when he reached Mr. Hale’s.

There were matters of business to talk over with Mr. Bell in the study. Mr. Bell, after the figures were put away, sat on by the fire, talking on—pleasantly enough at another time, but now to Thornton’s fretful impatience as wearisome as a sermon when a man’s heart is elsewhere. Thornton would not suggest going upstairs; he would not betray himself by eagerness. So he chafed in silence, and called Mr. Bell prosy in his thoughts, while Mr. Bell, in equal secrecy, judged Thornton brusque beyond endurance, and (what was worse) curter in manner and thinner in intelligence than he had once been.

At last a faint stir overhead—a chair moved, the soft fall of something in the sitting-room—made even Mr. Bell concede that they might as well join the others. They went up together.

Margaret sat with a letter open before her; her face was lit with that eager hopefulness which comes seldom and is therefore the more like a sudden flame. She was speaking quickly to her father. At the entrance of the gentlemen she put the letter aside at once; but Mr. Thornton’s quick ear caught Mr. Hale’s half sentence to Mr. Bell—

“A letter from Henry Lennox. It makes Margaret very hopeful.”

Mr. Bell nodded, as one who understood.

Margaret was red as a rose when she lifted her eyes; and in that instant Mr. Thornton felt the wild desire to rise and go out, and never cross the threshold again. He kept his place by sheer force, as if he had clenched his hands upon the arms of the chair.

“We were thinking,” said Mr. Hale, anxious to smooth the evening into cheerful commonplaces, “that you and Mr. Thornton had taken Margaret’s advice, and were each trying to convert the other, you were so long in the study.”

“And you thought there would be nothing left of us but an opinion, like the Kilkenny cat’s tail,” said Mr. Bell. “Pray whose opinion did you think would have the most obstinate vitality?”

Mr. Thornton, who had not the least notion what they were laughing at, disdained to ask. Mr. Hale, with his considerate politeness, explained.

“Mr. Thornton, we were accusing Mr. Bell this morning of a kind of Oxonian mediæval bigotry against his native town; and we—Margaret, I believe—suggested that it would do him good to associate a little with Milton manufacturers.”

“I beg your pardon,” said Mr. Bell. “Margaret thought it would do the Milton manufacturers good to associate a little more with Oxford men. Now wasn’t it so, Margaret?”

“I believe I thought it would do both good to see a little more of the other,” she replied, very low. “I did not know it was my idea any more than papa’s.”

“And so you see, Mr. Thornton, we ought to have been improving each other down-stairs, instead of talking over vanished families of Smith’s and Harrison’s. However, I am willing to do my part now. I wonder when you Milton men intend to live. All your lives seem to be spent in gathering together the materials for life.”

“By living, I suppose you mean enjoyment,” said Mr. Thornton, with a stiffness that made the word sound like a challenge.

“Yes, enjoyment,—I don’t specify of what, because I trust we should both consider mere pleasure as very poor enjoyment.”

“I would rather have the nature of the enjoyment defined.”

“Well! enjoyment or leisure—enjoyment of the power and influence which money gives. You are all striving for money. What do you want it for?”

Mr. Thornton was silent; his face grew darker, as if a deep thought had been touched and resented. At length he said—

“I really don’t know. But money is not what I strive for.”

“What then?”

“It is a home question. I shall have to lay myself open to such a catechist, and I am not sure that I am prepared to do it.”

“No!” said Mr. Hale. “Don’t let us be personal in our catechism. You are neither of you representative men; you are each of you too individual for that.”

“I am not sure whether to consider that as a compliment or not,” said Mr. Bell. “I should like to be the representative of Oxford, with its beauty and its learning, and its proud old history. What do you say, Margaret; ought I to be flattered?”

“I don’t know Oxford,” she answered, trying to smile. “But there is a difference between being the representative of a city and the representative man of its inhabitants.”

“Very true, Miss Margaret. Now I remember, you were against me this morning, and were quite Miltonian and manufacturing in your preferences.”

Mr. Thornton’s eyes went quickly to her face at those words. The look was so swift, so sharp with interpretation, that Margaret felt her cheeks burn again—less with colour than with anger at the meaning he might put upon what was mere talk. Mr. Bell, unconscious, went on—

“Ah! I wish I could show you our High Street—our Radcliffe Square. I am leaving out our colleges, just as I give Mr. Thornton leave to omit his factories in speaking of the charms of Milton. I have a right to abuse my birth-place. Remember I am a Milton man.”

Mr. Thornton, already strung too tight, was more galled than he ought to have been. He was not in a mood for banter. The old friend’s half-testy, half-playful disparagement would have amused him at another time; now it pricked like salt upon a raw place.

“I don’t set up Milton as a model of a town,” he said.

“Not in architecture?” asked Mr. Bell, slyly.

“No! We’ve been too busy to attend to mere outward appearances.”

“Don’t say mere outward appearances,” said Mr. Hale gently. “They impress us all, from childhood upward—every day of our life.”

“Wait a little while,” replied Mr. Thornton, with a kind of vehement restraint. “Remember we are of a different race from the Greeks, to whom beauty was everything, and to whom Mr. Bell might speak of a life of leisure and serene enjoyment, much of which entered in through their outward senses. I don’t mean to despise them, any more than I would ape them. But I belong to Teutonic blood; it is little mingled in this part of England to what it is in others; we retain much of their language; we retain more of their spirit; we do not look upon life as a time for enjoyment, but as a time for action and exertion. Our glory and our beauty arise out of our outward strength, which makes us victorious over material resistance, and over greater difficulties still. We are Teutonic up here in Darkshire in another way. We hate to have laws made for us at a distance. We wish people would allow us to right ourselves, instead of continually meddling, with their imperfect legislation. We stand up for self-government, and oppose centralisation.”

“In short, you would like the Heptarchy back again,” said Mr. Bell. “Well, at any rate, I revoke what I said this morning—that you Milton people did not reverence the past. You are regular worshippers of Thor.”

“If we do not reverence the past as you do in Oxford,” answered Mr. Thornton, pressing on as if he could not stop, “it is because we want something which can apply to the present more directly. It is fine when the study of the past leads to a prophecy of the future. But to men groping in new circumstances, it would be finer if the words of experience could direct us how to act in what concerns us most intimately and immediately; which is full of difficulties to be encountered; and upon the mode in which they are met and conquered—not merely pushed aside for the time—depends our future. But no! People can speak of Utopia much more easily than of the next day’s duty; and yet when that duty is all done by others, who so ready to cry, ‘Fie, for shame!’”

“And all this time I don’t see what you are talking about,” said Mr. Bell. “Would you Milton men condescend to send up your to-day’s difficulty to Oxford? You have not tried us yet.”

Mr. Thornton laughed outright—an unwilling burst, as if some tension snapped.

“I believe I was talking with reference to a good deal that has been troubling us of late,” he said. “I was thinking of the strikes we have gone through, which are troublesome and injurious things enough, as I am finding to my cost. And yet this last strike, under which I am smarting, has been respectable.”

“A respectable strike!” cried Mr. Bell. “That sounds as if you were far gone in the worship of Thor.”

Margaret felt, rather than saw, how the jest fretted Mr. Thornton. The talk had drifted to a matter about which one man’s mind could play, while the other’s was bound up in pain and loss. She tried to draw it away, tried—awkwardly enough—to bring them back to the safer shallows of ordinary news.

“Edith says she finds the printed calicoes in Corfu better and cheaper than in London.”

“Does she?” said Mr. Hale. “I think that must be one of Edith’s exaggerations. Are you sure of it, Margaret?”

“I am sure she says so, papa.”

“Then I am sure of the fact,” said Mr. Bell. “Margaret, I go so far in my idea of your truthfulness, that it shall cover your cousin’s character. I don’t believe a cousin of yours could exaggerate.”

“Is Miss Hale so remarkable for truth?” said Mr. Thornton—bitterly.

The words had hardly left his mouth before he would have given much to recall them. What was he doing—what right had he to fling, before her and her father and her friend, that sharp reminder of a shame he himself had chosen to keep alive? Ill-humour possessed him—ill-humour at being kept so long from her, at hearing another man’s name, at being unable to meet Mr. Bell’s easy cheerfulness, at the sense of his own helplessness in the very room where he had once been master of himself.

Margaret did not rise and leave the room, as she had done at other times when his temper had wounded her. She sat quite still. For a moment she looked at him with grieved surprise—so childlike in its pain that it was like seeing a child struck unexpectedly. Then her eyes slowly widened and darkened into mournful, reproachful sadness; then they fell. She bent her head over her work and spoke no more.

But he could not keep from looking at her. He saw a sigh pass over her whole frame, as if a chill had reached her. He felt, in a strange way, like a mother who has been scolding, and is called away before the child’s slow, trusting smile can come and make peace. He answered with short, sharp words; he mistook jests for taunts, earnestness for reproach. He wanted one glance, one syllable from her—something that would give him leave to throw himself down, in heart at least, and confess his remorse. Yet she neither looked nor spoke. Her round taper fingers went in and out of her sewing with steady, swift precision, as if that narrow strip of cloth and those even stitches were the one business left in life.

He told himself she could not care—else his passionate will would have forced her, if only for an instant, to lift her eyes and read his late repentance. The thought stung him into a fierce desire to do something—anything—that would make his penitence unavoidable; as if some overt rudeness could purchase him the right to explain. It was well that, soon after, he rose and went away. The long walk through the cold open air steadied him into a grave resolve: henceforth he would see as little of her as possible, since the very sight of her, the very sound of her voice—soft as pure melody—had such power to unbalance him. He had learned what love was: a sharp pang, a fierce experience. Through the furnace he would fight his way out into the serenity of middle age—richer, perhaps, and more human for having known this passion.

*

When the door had closed upon him, Margaret rose and began folding her work without a word. The long seams felt heavier than they had any right to feel; her arms ached as if she had been labouring all day. The soft roundness of her face seemed drawn into straighter lines; she looked like one who has been worn with fatigue rather than with any sudden grief.

As they prepared to go to rest, Mr. Bell muttered his condemnation.

“I never saw a fellow so spoiled by success. He can’t bear a word; a jest of any kind. Everything seems to touch on the soreness of his high dignity. Formerly, he was as simple and noble as the open day; you could not offend him, because he had no vanity.”

“He is not vain now,” said Margaret, turning from the table and speaking with quiet distinctness. “To-night he has not been like himself. Something must have annoyed him before he came here.”

Mr. Bell shot one of his sharp glances over his spectacles. Margaret met it steadily; there was nothing to flinch from. When she had left the room, he asked suddenly—

“Hale! did it ever strike you that Thornton and your daughter have what the French call a tendresse for each other?”

“Never!” cried Mr. Hale, startled, then flurried by the thought as if it were a sacrilege. “No, I am sure you are wrong, I am almost certain you are mistaken. If there is anything, it is all on Mr. Thornton’s side. Poor fellow! I hope and trust he is not thinking of her, for I am sure she would not have him.”

“Well! I’m a bachelor, and have steered clear of love affairs all my life; so perhaps my opinion is not worth having. Or else I should say there were very pretty symptoms about her!”

“Then I am sure you are wrong,” repeated Mr. Hale, as if repetition could lay the fancy. “He may care for her, though she really has been almost rude to him at times. But she!—why, Margaret would never think of him, I’m sure. Such a thing has never entered her head.”

“Entering her heart would do,” said Mr. Bell. “But I merely threw out a suggestion of what might be. I dare say I was wrong. And whether I was wrong or right, I’m very sleepy; so, having disturbed your night’s rest (as I can see) with my untimely fancies, I’ll betake myself with an easy mind to my own.”

Mr. Hale resolved he would not be disturbed by any such nonsense; and lay awake, determined not to think about it.

*

Mr. Bell left the next day. He took Margaret’s hand in both of his, holding it a moment longer than mere farewell required.

“Look to me,” he said, “as one who has a right to help and protect you in all your troubles, of whatever nature they might be.”

To Mr. Hale he spoke warmly, as if he must get the feeling out.

“That Margaret of yours has gone deep into my heart. Take care of her, for she is a very precious creature,—a great deal too good for Milton—only fit for Oxford, in fact. The town, I mean; not the men. I can’t match her yet. When I can, I shall bring my young man to stand side by side with your young woman, just as the genii in the Arabian Nights brought Prince Caralmazan to match with the fairy’s Princess Badoura.”

“I beg you’ll do no such thing,” said Mr. Hale, half amused, half anxious. “Remember the misfortunes that ensued; and besides, I can’t spare Margaret.”

“No; on second thoughts, we’ll have her to nurse us ten years hence, when we shall be two cross old invalids. Seriously, Hale! I wish you’d leave Milton; which is a most unsuitable place for you, though it was my recommendation in the first instance. If you would, I’d swallow my shadows of doubts, and take a college living; and you and Margaret should come and live at the parsonage—you to be a sort of lay curate, and take the unwashed off my hands; and she to be our housekeeper—the village Lady Bountiful—by day; and read us to sleep in the evenings. I could be very happy in such a life. What do you think of it?”

“Never,” said Mr. Hale decidedly. “My one great change has been made and my price of suffering paid. Here I stay out my life; and here will I be buried, and lost in the crowd.”

“I don’t give up my plan yet. Only I won’t bait you with it any more just now. Where’s the Pearl? Come, Margaret, give me a farewell kiss; and remember, my dear, where you may find a true friend, as far as his capability goes. You are my child, Margaret. Remember that, and God bless you!”

After he was gone, life fell back into its quiet monotony. There was no longer an invalid to watch, no daily rise and fall of hope and fear. Even the Higginses—so long their vivid, pressing interest—seemed to recede from the front of thought. But the Boucher children, left motherless, drew Margaret often into the poorer streets. She went to see Mary Higgins, who had charge of them. The two families were living in one house. The elder children were sent to humble schools; the younger ones were tended, while Mary was away at work, by the sensible neighbour who had impressed Margaret on the day of Boucher’s death. She was paid for her trouble; and, in all the plans made for the orphans, Nicholas Higgins showed a sober judgment, a regulated method, very unlike his former abrupt, eccentric jerks of action.

He was so steady at his work that Margaret did not often see him through the winter; but when she did, she noticed how he winced away from any mention of the dead father whose children he had so heartily taken for his own charge. And he did not speak easily of Mr. Thornton.

“To tell the truth,” he said once, with his head a little aside, as if the puzzle would not fit his mind, “he fairly bamboozles me. He’s two chaps. One chap I knowed of old as were measter all o’er. T’other chap hasn’t an ounce of measter’s flesh about him. How them two chaps is bound up in one body, is a craddy for me to find out. I’ll not be beat by it, though. Meanwhile he comes here pretty often; that’s how I know the chap that’s a man, not a measter. And I reckon he’s taken aback by me pretty much as I am by him; for he sits and listens and stares, as if I were some strange beast newly caught in some of the zones. But I’m none daunted. It would take a deal to daunt me in my own house, as he sees. And I tell him some of my mind that I reckon he’d ha’ been the better of hearing when he were a younger man.”

“And does he not answer you?” Mr. Hale asked.

“Well! I’ll not say th’ advantage is all on his side, for all I take credit for improving him above a bit. Sometimes he says a rough thing or two, which is not agreeable to look at at first, but has a queer smack o’ truth in it when yo’ come to chew it. He’ll be coming to-night, I reckon, about them childer’s schooling. He’s not satisfied wi’ the make of it, and wants for t’ examine ’em.”

“What are they”—began Mr. Hale; but Margaret touched his arm and showed him her watch.

“It is nearly seven,” she said. “The evenings are getting longer now. Come, papa.”

She did not breathe freely till they were some distance from the house. Then, as her heart grew calmer, she was vexed with herself for having hurried him away so abruptly; for, somehow, they saw Mr. Thornton very seldom now; and he might truly have come only to see Higgins, and for the old friendship’s sake she should have liked to have met him to-night.

Indeed, he came very seldom—even for the dull, cold purpose of lessons. Mr. Hale was disappointed in his pupil’s lukewarmness about Greek, which not long ago had been so eager and animated. Now it often happened that a hurried note arrived at the last moment—Mr. Thornton so much engaged he could not come that evening. Other pupils filled the hours; none filled the place in Mr. Hale’s heart. He grew depressed by the partial cessation of an intercourse that had become dear to him, and sat pondering what could have caused the change.

One evening, while Margaret sat at her work, he startled her by asking suddenly—

“Margaret! had you ever any reason for thinking that Mr. Thornton cared for you?”

He almost blushed as he spoke; Mr. Bell’s half-mocking suggestion had returned to him, and the words escaped before he was aware.

Margaret did not answer at once; but by the droop of her head he read what was coming.

“Yes; I believe—oh, papa, I should have told you.” She let her work fall, and hid her face in her hands.

“No, dear; don’t think that I am impertinently curious,” said Mr. Hale, deeply moved. “I am sure you would have told me if you had felt that you could return his regard. Did he speak to you about it?”

For a moment there was no sound but her quiet breathing; then, after a pause, came a little gentle, reluctant—

“Yes.”

“And you refused him?”
