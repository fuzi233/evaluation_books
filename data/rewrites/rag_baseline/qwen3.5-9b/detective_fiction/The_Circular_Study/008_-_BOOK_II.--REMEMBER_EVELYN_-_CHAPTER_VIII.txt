The morning light filtered through the high windows of the police station, casting long, dusty beams across the desks where the city's weary detectives huddled. Among them sat Mr. Gryce, a man whose face was a map of concentrated skepticism, waiting for the inevitable tide of gossip that invariably followed a murder. Almost simultaneously, two distinct communications arrived, sealing the fate of the investigation in ink and paper.

Miss Butterworth's letter was brief, written with a composure that bordered on the theatrical: *You were quite correct. So far as appears, I was the only person to lean over Mr. Adams's study table after his unfortunate death. I have had to clip the ends of my boa.* Her tone suggested a performance, a deliberate display of propriety that masked the turmoil beneath. In stark contrast, Mr. Gryce's reply was a cold, clinical dispatch: *My compliments, madam! Mr. Adams's jaws have been forced apart. A small piece of paper was found clinched between his teeth. This paper has been recovered, and will be read at the inquest. Perhaps a few favored persons may be granted the opportunity of reading it before then, notably yourself.*

While Miss Butterworth's letter sparked a flutter of speculative whispers among the domestic staff, it was Gryce's revelation that ignited a frenzy at Headquarters. The initial silence of the public broke instantly, replaced by a cacophony of would-be informants eager to trade fragments of observation for the promise of glory. Yet, as the crowd swelled, the substance of their contributions evaporated into the humid air. One garrulous old woman described a lady of genteel appearance ushering in an elderly gentleman in a great-coat; a fashionable young woman recounted a collision with a pale youth and a ghostly figure wearing a hat adorned with a magnificent gray feather. These details, vivid in their specificity, dissolved into the fog of collective imagination when pressed for identification, leaving the investigators with nothing but the comforting certainty that everyone knew someone who saw something, but no one knew exactly what.

Night fell, bringing with it the usual stagnation of unsolved cases, until the next day demanded the finality of a funeral. With no kin to claim the body, the landlord assumed the role of officiant, while the banker stood as the chief mourner, a tableau of detached grief. As the coffin was wheeled out, a phalanx of detectives moved through the throng, their eyes scanning the faces of the mourners, but the procession offered no clues, only the hollow echo of a life extinguished. The case remained a locked box, forcing Mr. Gryce to abandon the safety of the precinct. Excusing himself from his colleagues, he ventured alone into the twilight, walking the familiar path to the dark, shuttered house where the tragedy had unfolded.

Inside, the atmosphere was heavy with the scent of dust and decay. Gryce unlocked the door, the key turning with a reluctant click, and switched on the gas jets, flooding the interior with a sickly yellow luminescence. He inspected the study: the cross had been reset on the wall, the birdcage rehung, and the furniture arranged with meticulous care, save for the gaping wounds in the doorframe where the steel plate had been removed. The blue glint of the hidden mechanism peeked through the jagged holes in the plaster, an ugly scar on the room's facade. With the practical efficiency of a housekeeper repairing a breach, Gryce dragged two heavy rugs from the parlor and draped them over the exposed sections, concealing the evidence of the intrusion.

It was nearly nine o'clock when he settled behind the heavy drapes of the parlor window, his heart beating a steady rhythm against his ribs. True to his word, the carriage rolled up moments later, and Miss Butterworth descended, her silhouette framed by the lantern light.

"Madam, your punctuality is equal to my own," Gryce said, stepping forward to greet her. "Have you ordered your coachman to drive away?"

"Only as far as the corner," she replied, her voice steady despite the dim surroundings. "There he will await the call of your whistle."

Gryce gestured toward the hallway. "Nothing could be better. Are you afraid to remain for a moment alone, while I watch from the window the arrival of the others? At present, there is no one in the house but ourselves."

"If I were subject to fear in a matter of this kind, I should not be here at all," she countered, her gaze drifting to the study. "Besides, the house is very cheerfully lighted. I see you have chosen a crimson light for illuminating the study."

"Because a crimson light was burning when Mr. Adams died," Gryce explained, his voice dropping to a whisper.

Suddenly, a soft coo echoed from the study—"Remember Evelyn!"—followed by the chirping of a bird.

"Oh, you have brought back the bird!" Miss Butterworth exclaimed, her eyes widening. "That is not the cry with which it greeted me before. It was 'Eva! Lovely Eva!' Do you suppose Eva and Evelyn are the same?"

"Madam, we have so many riddles before us that we will let this one go for the present," Gryce interrupted gently. "I expect Mr. Adams's valet here in a moment."

The tension in the room shifted palpably. "Sir, you relieve me of an immense weight," she breathed. "I was afraid that the privilege of being present at the test you propose to make was not to be accorded me."

"Miss Butterworth, you have earned a seat at this experiment," Gryce assured her. "Bartow has been given a key, and will enter as of old in entire freedom to do as he wills. We have simply to watch his movements."

"In this room, sir?" Miss Butterworth recoiled slightly. "I do not think I shall like that. I had rather not meet this madman face to face."

"You will not be called upon to do so," Gryce reassured her, his tone firm. "We do not wish him to be startled by encountering any watchful eye. Irresponsible as he is, he must be allowed to move about without anything to distract his attention. Nothing must stand in the way of his following those impulses which may yield us a clew to his habits and the ways of this peculiar household. I propose to place you where the chances are least in favor of your being seen by him—in this parlor, madam, which we have every reason to believe was seldom opened during Mr. Adams's lifetime."

"You must put out the gas, then, or the unaccustomed light will attract his attention," she urged.

"I will not only put out the gas," Gryce said, moving to the controls, "but I will draw the portières close, making this little hole for your eye and this one for mine. A common expedient, madam; but serviceable, madam, serviceable."

As the curtains were pulled shut, plunging Miss Butterworth into a narrow shaft of darkness, she let out a sharp intake of breath. The sensation was complex, a mixture of duty and unease. If she possessed a spark of lively curiosity, she might have found the situation intriguing, but driven solely by obligation, she felt a flush rise to her cheeks behind the fabric barrier. Just then, the front door creaked open, signaling the arrival of the critical moment. The deaf-and-dumb valet had entered.

A faint, urgent "Hush!" from Gryce silenced her, and she strained her ears, watching the shadowy antechamber. Bartow appeared sooner than anticipated, dropping his hat on a table near the entrance before advancing toward the portière he had undoubtedly lifted dozens of times a day. Yet, this time, he hesitated. His hand hovered over the fabric but did not touch it. Something unseen held him back—perhaps the memory of the previous night, or the strange placement of the rugs masking the broken frames. With a meek, mechanical expression, he turned away, picked up his hat, and retreated silently upstairs.

"I will follow him," Gryce whispered, leaning closer to the slit in the curtain. "Don't be afraid, ma'am. This whistle will bring a man in from the street at once."

"I am not afraid. I would be ashamed..." she began, but the sentence died in her throat as Gryce slipped away into the hall.

Moments later, he returned, shaking his head. "The experiment is likely to be a failure; the old man has gone to his own room and is preparing for bed." He led Miss Butterworth into the study, where he idly pressed a button on the table, bathing the room in a new, eerie violet glow. It was Miss Butterworth's first exposure to this spectral illumination, and she stared in awe at the way the light transformed the portraits and furnishings, her admiration cut short by the distant sound of footsteps descending the stairs.

True to form, Bartow hurried back into the study, his busy demeanor unchecked. He paused on the threshold, seemingly aware of a disturbance, but without looking at the rugs that now covered the entryway, he proceeded to perform his nightly rituals. He fetched a bottle of wine, set it on the table, wiped a glass with scrupulous care, and placed his master's dressing gown and slippers nearby before disappearing into the bedroom.

Creeping forward, Gryce and Miss Butterworth observed the valet's actions. He stooped over a comb Gryce had left on the floor, a gesture that clearly surprised him. After a confused shake of the head, he returned the object to the dresser and turned down the bed.
