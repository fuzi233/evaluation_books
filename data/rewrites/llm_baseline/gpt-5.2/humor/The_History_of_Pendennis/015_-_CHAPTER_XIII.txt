At Fairoaks, the household began to take the Major’s absence personally, as if Chatteris had kidnapped him for ransom and was sending back only vague excuses. Dr. Portman and his lady, rattling homeward to Clavering with the tidy regularity of people who never mislay a sermon, stopped at Helen’s lodge-gate and delivered a note from Major Pendennis.

He would remain at Chatteris another day. He was anxious to have a talk with Messrs. Tatham, the lawyers. He would meet them that afternoon. He added nothing—nothing at all—about the delicate little explosion he had spent the morning arranging, as though the less said about a fire, the quicker it might go out.

Pen, meanwhile, contrived to be wonderfully busy at doing nothing in particular that might take him into the town. He could not endure the idea of walking past that odious Dean’s grass-plat with the polished boots potentially planted there—his guardian’s boots—shining like moral surveillance. How was a man to sigh properly, or to look meaningfully at a young lady, while an uncle might be within spying distance? Pen’s love, like a shy dish sent up too soon, turned watery in public. It could only show itself by his eyes, which—poor boy—did all the hard labour, rolling and gazing in a way that must have given them muscle-ache; but his tongue, before witnesses, became as useless as a fork in soup.

And indeed, for those not engaged in the cookery of it, lovers’ talk is much like a recipe written in a dead dialect: plenty of words, no key; quantities, no taste. People keep old love-letters the way they keep stale cake in a tin—out of sentiment—and when they open it later, the thing is dry, crumbly, and astonishing that it ever nourished anyone. Six absurd lines once fed you for a week. Then you were famished again until the next batch of folly arrived, hot from the oven of delusion. For this reason—pure tenderness for Pen’s future complexion—we decline to serve up his verses from this period. Young ladies should not be asked to eat such rich spoon-meat cold. We will keep the papers tied up in newspapers with Miss Fotheringay’s string, and sealed with Captain Costigan’s great silver seal, like a ham wrapped for winter and best left hanging.

The Major, for his part, emerged from his morning’s interview with Captain Costigan in a condition of fury so concentrated you might have lit a candle from it. The “impudent bog-trotting scamp” had dared to threaten him; had dared to speak as if a Costigan might be permitted to marry into the Pendennises; had even, with the swagger of a man who thinks himself a whole regiment, hinted at a challenge. The Major’s mind performed its usual swift arithmetic: if the fellow could find any person resembling a gentleman to carry such a message, the Major felt a violent temptation not to balk him—then immediately recoiled from the picture of himself going out to fight a tipsy mountebank over an actress in a barn. The Major loved honour, but he loved the right sort of honour, properly laundered, scented, and spoken of discreetly.

When he met Dr. Portman, who inquired anxiously—like a parson testing the temperature of a moral fever—how the battle with the dragon had gone, Major Pendennis would not repeat the General’s insolences. He said only that the affair was very ugly, very disagreeable, and by no means finished. He begged Dr. and Mrs. Portman to say nothing at Fairoaks, and contented himself with sending Helen the note already mentioned.

Then he returned to the George, where he relieved his feelings upon Morgan his valet with a series of explosions which were heard upstairs and downstairs, and were afterwards described by Morgan, over his own dinner in the servants’ room, to Mr. Foker’s man, with professional pride. That news, like smoke, went where smoke always goes: into other people’s rooms.

Mr. Foker had finished his breakfast—at two o’clock in the afternoon, which was his notion of early—and remembered he wished to know what had happened between his two friends. So, wrapped in a brocade dressing-gown and wearing an embroidered skull-cap that had been stitched with maternal devotion, he skipped to the Major’s sitting-room and knocked.

Major Pendennis was indeed engaged: papers, red tape, and the solemn machinery of money and land lay spread out before him. Old Mr. Tatham, the lawyer—white-headed, composed, smelling faintly of parchment and caution—sat opposite, like a crow who had learned to read.

Foker, seeing business, began to retreat with a modest “Oh, you’re busy—call another time.” But the Major, who wanted an audience and whose anger had been longing for somewhere safe to rest, begged him to enter. Foker removed his fez with an air as if he were saluting royalty, advanced, bowed, and installed himself in an arm-chair, spreading his crimson skirts as though he meant to be framed and hung in a gallery.

“You seem to like my dressing-gown, sir,” he said to Mr. Tatham, with a bland smile. “Pretty thing, isn’t it? Neat, but not in the least gaudy.”

Mr. Tatham stared as a countryman stares at a foreign fruit—uncertain whether it is edible or merely ornamental.

“And how do you do, Major Pendennis, sir, and how does the world treat you?”

“There,” said the Major, glad to speak, “I have had an interview with that Irishman—you may speak before my friend Mr. Tatham, who knows all the affairs of the family—and it has not been satisfactory. He won’t believe that my nephew is poor. He says we are liars. He did me the honour to hint that I was a coward as I took leave. And when you knocked I thought you might be the gentleman I expect with a challenge from Mr. Costigan. That is how the world treats me, Mr. Foker.”

“You don’t mean that Irishman—the actress’s father?” cried Mr. Tatham, who, being a dissenter, did not patronise the drama and spoke of it as of an infection.

“The very man. Have not you heard what a fool my nephew has made of himself about the girl?”

Mr. Tatham had heard nothing—he never went within a theatre wall, as if painted scenery might stain his conscience—so the Major was obliged to recount his nephew’s loves from the beginning, with Mr. Foker chiming in, in his familiar idiom, as though narrating a farce at a club.

Mr. Tatham listened in increasing astonishment. Why had not Mrs. Pendennis married a serious man, he wondered—Mr. Tatham was a widower, and saw serious men everywhere, like useful vegetables—who might have kept the boy from perdition? As for the daughter, he said nothing: her profession was enough.

Mr. Foker, always ready to be generous where it amused him, declared he had known excellent people in the booths—by which he meant the Temple of the Muses.

“Perhaps,” Mr. Tatham allowed, with a sigh, “perhaps. But the father, Major—I know him. A man of the worst character. A wine-bibber. An idler in taverns and billiard-rooms. A notorious insolvent.”

And then, with a sort of grim relish, the lawyer related that his office had a writ out against Costigan and another disreputable fellow—one of the play-actors—for a bill given to Mr. Skinner of the city, a respectable grocer and wine and spirit merchant and a Member of the Society of Friends.

“This Costigan came crying to Mr. Skinner,” said Mr. Tatham, offended at the memory, “crying in the shop, sir—and we have not proceeded, as neither was worth powder and shot.”

It was while he spoke that a third knock came—heavier, like a fist trained to applaud tragedians and to threaten creditors. An athletic gentleman entered in a shabby braided frock, holding a letter sealed with a large blotched red seal. He cleared his throat as if about to deliver doom.

“Can I have the honour of speaking with Major Pendennis in private?” he began. “I have a few words for your ear, sir. I am the bearer of a mission from my friend Captain Costigan—”

But his voice faltered. His face went pale. His eyes fixed upon Mr. Tatham’s red and well-remembered countenance, and his courage, which had been marching bravely, suddenly remembered it had no shoes.

“Hullo, Garbetts, speak up!” cried Mr. Foker, delighted, like a boy hearing the first crack of a squib.

“Bless my soul,” said Mr. Tatham. “It is the other party to the bill!”

“I say, sir; stop, I say—” began the lawyer, rising.

But Garbetts, with a face as blank as a man who has seen a ghost in a bailiff’s hat, gasped something unintelligible and fled out of the room.

The Major’s gravity collapsed. He burst into laughter—sharp, relieved, almost musical. Mr. Foker laughed too, rocking in his brocade like a delighted ornament. Even Mr. Tatham, though professionally serious, smiled.

“By Jove, it was a good ’un,” said Foker. “I don’t think there’ll be any fight, Major.” And he began mimicking the tragedian’s stricken look, to their renewed entertainment. “If there is, the old gentleman—your name Tatham? very happy—may send the bailiffs to separate the men.”

Mr. Tatham promised he would.

The Major, though he disliked vulgarity, was not sorry to see the quarrel end in such a ridiculous puff of smoke.

“It seems to me, sir,” he said to Mr. Foker, “that you always arrive to put me into good-humour.”

And Mr. Foker, by one of those idle impulses which in his case sometimes fell accidentally into usefulness, went later in the afternoon to Captain Costigan’s lodgings to hear what had passed in the morning. Costigan was not at home. His daughter had permitted—indeed encouraged—him to go to the convivial club at the Magpie Hotel, where, no doubt, he was at that very moment giving his courage an airing, like a horse pranced before friends, bragging of his intention to murder a ruffian, and enjoying the sound of his own heroism over beer.

But Miss Fotheringay was at home, washing the tea-cups, while Mr. Bows sat opposite—hands in pockets, eyes observant, as fiddlers’ eyes often are, taking the measure of a room as if it were a tune.

“Just done breakfast, I see—how do?” said Mr. Foker, popping in his little head.

“Get out, you funny little man,” cried Miss Fotheringay.

“You mean come in,” said he, and came in at once. He folded his arms and began twirling his head with immense rapidity like Harlequin on a stage the instant he breaks out of his cocoon. Miss Fotheringay laughed with all her heart. A wink of Foker’s could set her off, where the bitterest joke of Bows’s would not win so much as a smile, and the finest speeches poor Pen delivered only puzzled her, like French cookery described to a person who has never tasted butter.

When the harlequinade ended, Foker sank on one knee and kissed her hand. She called him “the drollest little man,” and gave him a great good-humoured slap. Pen used to tremble when he kissed that hand. Pen would have died of a slap. Different temperaments, different sauces.

They talked. Foker, delighted with his own story, recounted Garbetts’s discomfiture, and thus they learned for the first time how far the General had carried his wrath. Foker spoke sturdily in favour of the Major’s honour and veracity, describing him as a tip-top swell, moving in the upper circle, and incapable of deceit—especially incapable of deceiving such a charming young woman as Miss Foth. He touched, with all the delicacy he could command, upon the delicate marriage question; yet he could not help letting it be seen that he held Pen rather cheap. Pen’s sentimentality was a flavour Foker never cared for—too sweet, too frothy, too easily upset by the slightest draught.

“I knew it wouldn’t do, Miss Foth,” said he, nodding. “Couldn’t do. Didn’t like to put my hand into the bag, but knew it couldn’t do. He’s too young for you: too green: a deal too green: and he turns out to be poor as Job. Can’t have him at no price, can she, Mr. Bo?”

“Indeed he’s a nice poor boy,” said Miss Fotheringay rather sadly.

“Poor little beggar,” said Bows, with a queer look at her, as if wondering how women pat a man’s head and then send him out into the rain.

Bows had no objection to declare that Miss Fotheringay was perfectly right in giving up Arthur Pendennis, and that the match had always been absurd. She owned she thought so herself; only she couldn’t send away two thousand a year.

“It all comes of believing Papa’s silly stories,” she said. “Faith I’ll choose for meself another time.” And very likely Lieutenant Sir Derby Oaks—large as life, large as income—strode across her mind at that instant.

After praising Major Pendennis—“a proper gentleman entirely,” she declared, “smelling of lavender, and as neat as a pin”—and after Bows pronounced him the right sort of fellow, though rather too much of an old buck, Mr. Foker suddenly proposed a stroke of reconciliation.

“Come and meet the Major this very evening at dinner at my apartment at the George. He agreed to dine with me—and after the—after the little shindy this morning, in which I must say the General was wrong—it would look kind, you know. I know the Major fell in love with you, Miss Foth: he said so.”

“So she may be Mrs. Pendennis still,” said Bows with a sneer. “No, thank you, Mr. F. I’ve dined.”

“Sure, that was at three o’clock,” said Miss Costigan, who had an honest appetite, “and I can’t go without you.”

“We’ll have lobster-salad and champagne,” said the little monster, who could not construe a line of Latin nor do a sum beyond the Rule of Three, but could always summon a feast like a conjurer pulling ribbons from his mouth.

For lobster-salad and champagne in an honourable manner, Miss Costigan would have gone anywhere. And so it happened that at seven o’clock Major Pendennis found himself seated at dinner with Mr. Bows, a professional fiddler, and Miss Costigan—whose father had wanted to blow his brains out a few hours before.

To complete the comedy, Mr. Foker, who knew Costigan’s haunts as a cook knows the nearest market, sent Stoopid—his man—to the Magpie club, where the General was in the act of singing a pathetic song, and brought him off to supper.

Captain Costigan arrived flushed with drink and sentiment, as if he had been marinated in both. To find his daughter and Bows at the board struck him into a sort of astonished delight. Major Pendennis laughed and held out his hand cordially. The General grasped it avec effusion, and called him his dearest friend. He burst into tears more than once—tears already started by his own song before he joined them—and toasted everybody with reckless tenderness. The champagne went off with an agreeable hiss, the lobster-salad with that sharp sea-sweetness which makes even peace taste possible, and the room at the George rang with voices and laughter, the very clink of glasses sounding like a treaty.

Stoopid and Mr. Foker walked home with the General afterwards, while the Major gallantly gave his arm to Miss Costigan. When he called the next day he was received with friendliness; civilities passed between the gentlemen like coins exchanged at a counter. On taking leave, the Major expressed his anxious desire to serve Miss Costigan whenever useful; and he shook hands with Mr. Foker most cordially, saying the young gentleman had done him the very greatest service.

“All right,” said Mr. Foker; and they parted with mutual esteem.

Major Pendennis returned to Fairoaks next day and said nothing of the previous night—no mention of lobster-salad, no mention of champagne, no mention of the company in which he had eaten and laughed. Yet he asked Mr. Smirke to stop to dinner, and any person accustomed to watch him might have noticed something constrained in his hilarity—an unusual watchfulness in his kindness to his nephew. He gave Pen an emphatic God-bless-you at bedtime; and when he and Mrs. Pendennis were about to part for the night, he seemed on the point of saying something, but checked himself, fearing to spoil her rest, and let her sleep.

Next morning he was down earlier than usual, greeting everyone with marked cordiality. The post generally arrived toward the end of breakfast. When old John entered and discharged the bag of letters and papers, Major Pendennis looked hard at Pen as the lad took his own. Arthur blushed and put a letter down. He knew the handwriting; it was Costigan’s. He did not want to read it before the table.

Major Pendennis knew it too. He had posted it himself in Chatteris the day before.

He told little Laura to go away. The child, who had a thorough dislike to him, went gladly enough. When the door closed, the Major took Mrs. Pendennis’s hand, gave her a look full of meaning, and pointed to the letter lying under the newspaper Pen was pretending to read.

“Will you come into the drawing-room?” he said. “I want to speak to you.”

Helen followed, wondering and nervous.

“What is it?” she asked.

“The affair is at an end,” Major Pendennis said. “He has a letter there giving him his dismissal. I dictated it myself yesterday. There are a few lines from the lady too, bidding him farewell. It is all over.”

Helen ran back to the dining-room with her brother close behind. Pen had seized the letter the instant they were gone. He read it with a stupefied face, as if the paper were written in an unfamiliar tongue and yet somehow cut him like a knife.

Costigan wrote that he was most gratified by Arthur’s kindness to his daughter, but that he had only now been made aware of Mr. Pendennis’s—spelled in Costigan’s manner—peecupiary circumstances. They were such that marriage was out of the question at present; and considering the disparity of age, future union impossible. Under these circumstances, and with deepest regret and esteem, Captain Costigan bade Arthur farewell and suggested he cease visiting, for some time at least, at his house.

A few lines from Miss Costigan were enclosed. She acquiesced in Papa’s decision. She pointed out she was many years older than Arthur and that an engagement was not to be thought of. She would always be grateful for his kindness and hoped to keep his friendship, but until the pain of separation was over, she entreated they should not meet.

Pen read mechanically, as though his eyes had become mere instruments. Then he looked up wildly and saw his mother and uncle watching him. Helen’s face was all tender anxiety.

“What—what is this?” Pen cried. “It’s some joke. This is not her writing. This is some servant’s writing. Who’s playing tricks upon me?”

“It comes under her father’s envelope,” said the Major. “Those letters you had before were not in her hand: that is hers.”

“How do you know?” Arthur asked fiercely.

“I saw her write it,” said the uncle, as the boy started up.

Helen moved forward and took Pen’s hand. He shook her off.

“How came you to see her? How came you between me and her? What have I ever done to you that you should—Oh, it’s not true! it’s not true!” Pen broke out with a wild execration. “She can’t have done it of her own accord. She can’t mean it. She’s pledged to me. Who has told her lies to break her from me?”

“Lies are not told in the family, Arthur,” Major Pendennis replied coldly. “I told her the truth—that you have no money to maintain her, for her foolish father had represented you to be rich. And when she knew how poor you were, she withdrew at once, and without persuasion of mine. She was quite right. She is ten years older than you are. She is perfectly unfitted to be your wife, and knows it. Look at that handwriting, and ask yourself, is such a woman fitted to be the companion of your mother?”

“I will know from herself if it is true,” Arthur said, crumpling the paper. “I’ll hear it from herself.”

The Major, with the faintest possible sneer, drew out a billet. “Her letters were written by a confidant of hers, who writes better than she can. Look here—here’s one from the lady to your friend Mr. Foker. You have seen her with Miss Costigan, as whose amanuensis she acted.”

“It’s not that,” Pen said, burning with shame and rage. “I suppose what you say is true, sir—but I’ll hear it from herself.”

“Arthur!” his mother pleaded.

“I will see her,” Arthur insisted. “I’ll ask her to marry me once more. I will. No one shall prevent me.”

“What, a woman who spells affection with one f?” said the Major. “Nonsense, sir. Be a man, and remember that your mother is a lady. She was never made to associate with that tipsy old swindler or his daughter. Be a man and forget her, as she does you.”

“Be a man and comfort your mother, my Arthur,” Helen said, and embraced him. Seeing both were deeply moved, Major Pendennis went out and shut the door, judging wisely that they were best alone.

He had won a complete victory. He had even brought away Pen’s letters in his portmanteau from Chatteris. When he returned them to Costigan, he complimented the Captain by giving him the little promissory note that had disquieted Garbetts; and the Major settled it with Mr. Tatham.

Pen rushed off wildly to Chatteris that day, but could not see Miss Fotheringay. He left a letter for her enclosed to her father. The enclosure came back, returned by Costigan, who begged that all correspondence might end. After one or two further attempts, the indignant General desired their acquaintance to cease altogether. He cut Pen in the street.

One day, as Arthur and Foker paced the Castle walk, they met Emily on her father’s arm. She passed without nod or sign, as if Pen were a stranger on the pavement. Foker felt the boy trembling on his arm.

The uncle wished Pen to travel, to quit the country awhile; his mother urged him too, for he was growing ill and suffered severely. But he refused, point-blank. He would not go. He would not obey in this instance; and his mother was too fond, his uncle too wise, to force him.

Whenever Miss Fotheringay acted, he rode over to the Chatteris Theatre and saw her. One night there were so few people that the manager returned the money. Pen came home, went to bed at eight, and had a fever. If this continues, his mother will be going over and fetching the girl, the Major thought in despair. As for Pen, he thought he should die.

We are not going to set down a dreary journal of his despair and passion. Other gentlemen besides Mr. Pen have been baulked in love—yes, indeed—and few die of it, however convincingly they insist upon the point.
