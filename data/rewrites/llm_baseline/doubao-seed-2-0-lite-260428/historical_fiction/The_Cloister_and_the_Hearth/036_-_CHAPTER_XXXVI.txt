“In prison, sir? Good lack, what wickedness could she have committed?”

“Ah, she’s a witness. A necessary one, at that.”

“Now, Messire Bailiff,” Denys cut in, grinning, “you don’t clap every last witness in irons, I’d wager?”

The alderman, flattered to be given the courtesy title of bailiff, grew far more talkative. “In a case of blood, we detain any soul that’s like to give us leg bail and cheat justice — that’s why we’ve held all the womenfolk connected to this band. A man might hide a secret in his own mind for a week, off and on, but a woman? If she does her duty to the realm on a Friday, she’ll undo the whole of it before Sunday, mark me. If you saw that girl Manon now, you’d find her blubbering her eyes out, guilty that she betrayed five men to the gallows. Had they been other women, we’d have trusted a simple subpoena — they despise one another, and that shows no small share of sense. But now I think on it, there’s more reason than that to lock her up. Hand me those depositions, young man.” He slid his spectacles on, flipping through the pages. “Aye, here it is. She was mixed up in the lot of them, part of the band.”

Denys and Gerard both burst out in loud, immediate denial.

“Save your breath, no need to din my ears,” the alderman waved them off. “It’s all here in black and white. ‘Jean Hardy, one of the thieves, under questioning, confessed that — humph, aye, here it is. And that the girl Manon was their decoy, and her sweetheart was Georges Vipont, one of the band, hanged last month; that she’s been dejected ever since, and openly blamed the rest of the band for his death, swearing if they hadn’t been rank cowards he’d never have been taken, and it is his opinion she only betrayed them out of pure spite, and—’”

“His opinion?” Gerard cried, indignant. “What value has the word of a cutthroat burning to revenge himself on the woman who delivered him to justice? Why should his testimony count? Is a thief ever anything but a liar? And here he has the strongest motive in the world to lie: revenge, the most ungovernable of all human passions. Oh sir, what madness to question a caught felon, and take his word over an innocent life — as if he were an honest man swearing in open court, his true hand laid on the Gospel!”

“Young man,” the alderman said, frowning, “restrain that heat in front of authority! Your tone tells me you are a stranger here. Know that in this land we question everyone. We are not so foolish as to hope to find truth by stopping up either our left or our right ear.”

“So you would listen to Satan himself slandering the saints!”

“Tush, tush! The law only deals with men and women, and no man alive can spin a tale that is all lies, no matter how hard he tries. We do not bar the barn door against any man’s grain, as the saying goes. Once we’ve taken it in, we winnow it, and sift it clean. And who told you I swallowed that thief’s story whole like plain spring water? I only credited the parts of it that were borne out by far better proof.”

“Better proof?” Gerard stared, bewildered. “Why, who but the thieves would breathe a word against her?”

“Marry — herself.”

“Herself, sir? You questioned her too?”

“I told you, we question everyone. Here is her deposition. Can you read? Then read it for yourself.”

Gerard glanced at Denys, and read the simple, trembling words in Manon’s own hand:
“I am a native of Epinal. I left my home two years ago because I was unfortunate: I could not bear the man my father bade me marry. He beat me for it, so I ran away. I went into service, but left when my mistress grew jealous of me, and turned me out for being saucy. Last year I stood in the marketplace to be hired with the other girls, and the landlord of The Fair Star inn took me on. I stayed eleven months with him. A young man courted me, and I fell in love with him. Then I found out that travellers who came to the inn never left again. I told my lover, and he bade me hold my peace, threatened me when I would not. I learned my lover was one of the band of thieves. When travellers were meant to be robbed, the landlord would slip out and call the band in. I wept, and prayed for the travellers’ souls, but I never spoke a word. A month ago, my lover died.
“The soldier [Denys] reminded me of him — he wore a beard just like the one my love had. I do not know if I would have run for help if that soldier had not had a beard. I am sorry I told now.”

The paper nearly slipped out of Gerard’s fingers. For the first time, he saw that Manon’s life hung by a thread, that the stubborn, unyielding local law would hang her without a second thought. He dropped hard on his knees at the alderman’s feet. “Oh, sir! Think of the difference between those brutal men and this poor weak girl! Could you find it in your heart to send her to the same death as the thieves? Could you condemn us to stand and watch her slaughtered — when she risked her own life to save ours, and would never be in any danger at all if not for that? Show Denys and me pity, at least, if you have none for her, poor soul. We are both honest men, and you will break our hearts if you kill that simple, helpless girl. What else could we do but cut our own throats at the foot of her gallows?”

The alderman was tough, but he was still human. Gerard’s desperate pleas first stunned him, then softened him, in his own gruff way. He grew peevish, waving a hand. “There, get up off the floor, will you. I doubt anyone would waste half that many words begging for me. Daniel! Go fetch the town clerk at once.” When the clerk stepped in from the next room, he nodded at Gerard. “This foolish lad is fretting himself ill over that Manon girl. Can we stretch the rules a little? Let her be admitted as a witness, question her gently, no harshness.”

The town clerk was one of those men whose whole job is to find impossibilities. “Nay sir, we cannot do that. She was not party to the attack on your two travellers. If she had been an accessory, we could legally offer her a pardon in return for giving evidence.”

Gerard burst in, “But she did far better than being an accessory! She stopped the murder from happening, and ran all the way here to report the crime of her own free will!”

“Tush, young man, this is a matter of written law.” The alderman and the clerk bickered for a long while, the first arguing that Manon’s actions put her in the exact same legal position as if she had been an accessory, the other repeating that every step was against the rules. That was Manon’s luck: irritated past endurance at the clerk’s endless list of things he “could not” do, the alderman thundered that he would show him he could do whatever he chose in this case. He had Manon brought out, arranged for the landlord of The White Hart to stand as her bondsman, and Denys deposited five gold pieces with the court to guarantee she would appear when summoned. The alderman set her free, but eased his own conscience by explaining his “leniency” bluntly: “The town has to buy a new rope for every hanged man, and give it to the hangman, or pay him the cost in coin. She is not worth that municipal expense, not like the hardened rogues she once ran with.”

So Denys and Gerard led her away, Gerard dancing around her for joy, Denys cheering her up by telling her all her old bad acquaintances were soon to be dead, while she wept weakly. On the walk back to The White Hart, the crowd spotted her — the archers had already spread the full story, praising her for her courage, and the public followed them cheering, shouting words of encouragement, until Manon, finding herself surrounded by well-wishers, plucked up her courage. The inn landlord saw at a glance that her presence would draw crowds of curious customers, and welcomed her politely, giving her a private upper chamber. She shut herself inside, and as soon as she was alone, the tears came pouring down again. Poor little soul, her spirits were as unsteady as a ripple on water, rising and falling, falling and rising, no stillness at all. Gerard and Denys bade the landlord treat her gently, keep her effectively “prisoner” but never let her feel confined, and then stepped out into the street.

Right in front of them, two processions were coming from opposite directions. One was huge, noisy, full of howls and the wild, raw cries rude folk give voice to when they let the beast beneath their civilized manners show itself. The other was small, quiet, attended only by a handful of nuns and friars, moving slow and silent.

The thieves were being paraded on a cart to the marketplace, to be put on public display before hanging. The bones of their murdered victims were being carried to the churchyard, for Christian burial.

The two groups met in the narrow street, right at the door of the inn, and could not pass one another for a long time. The bier holding the mortal remains of the dead travellers wedged tight against the cart that carried the men who had murdered them — men who would themselves be dead before the sun set. The mob did not grasp the grim poetry of the moment at first, but then a woman screamed out, “Look at your work, you dogs!” and the crowd took up the cry like wildfire, a horrible yell that made the culprits groan, trying to hide their faces against their chests, though their bound hands would not let them. There they stood, pale, hollow-eyed, images of utter despair, staring at the bier, envying the men they had sent ahead down the dark road they were about to walk themselves. Even Manon, hearing the commotion, crept to the window, hid half her face, and peeped trembling through her fingers, as women so often do.

That strange, stark meeting parted Denys and Gerard. Denys, drawn by curiosity and quiet revenge, drifted off to follow the thieves’ cart to the marketplace. Gerard pulled his bonnet off his head, and piously followed the bier carrying the poor souls whose fate he and Denys had so narrowly escaped. For a while he was the only lay mourner, but when they reached the suburbs, far away from the bigger spectacle the gallows promised, more than one artisan dropped his tools, more than one shopkeeper left his shop, touched by pity and common humanity, and drawn in no small part by Gerard’s bareheaded example. They followed the bones, uncovered, until the priests laid them in hallowed ground with the full prayers of the Church.

When the funeral rites were done, Gerard stepped respectfully up to the cure, and offered to pay for a mass to be said for the dead travellers’ souls. Gerard, son of Catherine, always looked at both sides of a penny, and he tried to haggle the price down a little, citing the pitiable circumstances of the deaths. But the good cure gently, adroitly parried his every attempt at a discount, and blandly pushed him to pay the full standard market price.

As they talked, they realized they shared a striking similarity of sentiment. Piety and worldly prudence are not rare companions, but it is uncommon to see both carried so far as these two men carried them. Their little collision in the “prayer market” sparked a mutual respect, like two knights meeting in the lists, each finding the other a worthy opponent. The cure loved a good bit of gossip, and when he learned his customer was one of the two men who had fought off the thieves at Domfront, he dragged Gerard back to his parlour to hear the whole story from his own lips. His heart warmed to Gerard. “God was good to you,” he said. “I thank Him for that, with all my soul. You are a good lad.” He added drily, “You should have told me that tale in the churchyard. I doubt I would have charged you a sou for the mass, out of affection. But” — his tone cooled sharply — “it is bad luck to go back on a bargain once struck. But I will broach a bottle of my very oldest Medoc for you. Few guests in this world get that honour.” He rummaged in his cupboard for the precious bottle, and muttered under his breath, “At their old tricks again!”

“Pardon, your reverence?” Gerard said.

“I said nothing. Aye, here it is.”

“Nay, you spoke. You said, ‘At their old tricks again!’”

“Did I, in truth?” The cure smiled. He poured them each a full cup, tossed an extra log on the fire — for Burgundy had no stoves, back then — and leaned back. “So I said ‘At their old tricks’? Come, sip the good wine, and while it lasts, story for story. I will not deny you a little tale of my own.”

Gerard’s eyes sparkled. “I love a good story more than life itself.”

“Do not raise your hopes too high, mind you. It is but a silly trifle, next to your own adventures.”

“Once upon a time, in the kingdom of France, in the duchy of Burgundy, less than a day’s ride from the very town where we sit sipping this old Medoc, there lived a cure. I say he ‘lived’ — he barely scraped by. His parish was small, the parishioners were greedy, and never gave their cure a single doit more than he could force them to pay. The closer they starved him to a disembodied ghost, the holier they believed his prayers for them would be. I do not know if that was their exact creed, but their behaviour certainly made it look that way.

“At last, the cure thought up a trick to teach them a lesson. One fine day, the richest farmer in the parish had twins to baptize. The cure was invited to the christening feast, as was customary — but before he would baptize the children, he demanded not just the standard christening fee, but their burial fees, too. ‘Saints defend us, parson!’ the new mother cried. ‘Do not talk of burying! I never saw two healthier children more likely to live long lives.’ ‘Nor did I,’ the cure said. ‘Praise be to God. But none the less, they are sons of Adam, same as you and I, and they will die one day. Whenever that day comes, their burial will cost them nothing — the fees are paid already, and written down in my book.’ ‘Nay, that will cost them something else,’ the miller cut in, the biggest joker and the biggest rogue in the parish. The whole parish laughed loud at his jest, and in the middle of all the merriment, they paid the fees, and the twins were baptized.

“But soon every new child born in the parish had their burial fee paid at christening too, or they could not be entered in the parish register, making them ineligible for heaven. Resentment simmered, secret, through the whole village. One day they met in secret, and sent their churchwarden to the bishop with a formal complaint. A summons arrived for the cure at dinnertime: he was to bring all his parish books to the episcopal palace, and answer the charges against him. His appetite vanished instantly. He left his half-eaten food on the table, took the books, and rode to the palace, quaking.

“The bishop greeted him with a frown, laid the complaint out on the table. ‘Monseigneur,’ the cure said, humbly, ‘does the parish lay many charges against me, or just this one?’ ‘Only this one,’ the bishop said, softening a little. ‘First, monseigneur, I admit the facts are true.’ ‘Good,’ the bishop said. ‘That saves us time. Now, give me your excuse, if you have one.’ ‘Monseigneur,’ the cure said, ‘I have been cure of that parish seven years. In that time, I baptized fifty children, and buried fewer than five. At first I thought “Heaven be praised, the air of this village is so healthy!” But when I checked the registers, I saw that it had always been that way. I dug into the matter, and found that almost every child born in Domfront left the village, went to Aix, and got himself hanged. They did that not just to cheat their own cure of fees, but the whole Church — for hanged men are buried out in the open air, and never pay funeral dues. Knowing how greedy my parishioners are, how they grudge the Church every single sou, I laid that little trap for them. Greed against greed, you see. I knew that rather than pay the Church nothing for their funeral, they would stay home, die peacefully in their beds, like honest men.’

“The bishop laughed so hard the tears ran down his face. He called in the churchwarden, who had to admit that far too many of the parish’s young men did indeed end up hanged at Aix. ‘I approve your action,’ the bishop told the cure. ‘I will make sure all my successors approve it too — until your parishioners mend their ways, and start dying in their beds like decent folk.’ The next day, the ringleaders of the complaint came to the cure, crestfallen. ‘Parson, you have always been good to us, save for this one silly quarrel. Let there be no hard feelings between us, over nothing.’ And the cure said, ‘My children, I would not be worthy to be your pastor if I could not forgive a small wrong. Go in peace. Now go and have as many children as you possibly can, so that between the double fees, the cure you love will not starve to death.’

“The bishop told that story for years, and never forgot that cure. At last he moved him to a decent, generous parish, where that cure can offer a bottle of his old Medoc to any guest worthy of it. And worthy guests, as you can guess, are few and far between.”
