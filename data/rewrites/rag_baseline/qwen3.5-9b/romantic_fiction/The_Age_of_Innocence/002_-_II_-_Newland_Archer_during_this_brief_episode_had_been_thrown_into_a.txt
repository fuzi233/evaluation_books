Newland Archer found himself suspended in a peculiar suspension of embarrassment. It was an irritating anomaly that the box commanding the undivided attention of masculine New York should contain his betrothed, seated between her mother and aunt. For a fleeting instant, the figure in the Empire dress remained an indistinct silhouette, a mystery he could not solve until clarity descended like a sudden, sharp light. With it came a rush of indignation, however brief. No, certainly; no one would have presumed the Mingotts would attempt such a maneuver. Yet they had. The low murmurs drifting from the corridor confirmed it beyond doubt: the young woman was May Welland's cousin, the perpetual subject of pity known to the family as "poor Ellen Olenska."

Archer knew she had arrived from Europe only days prior. He had even heard from Miss Welland—a report delivered without disapproval—that she had visited her cousin, who was currently residing with the elderly Mrs. Mingott. Archer possessed a genuine approval for familial solidarity; one of the traits he most revered in the Mingotts was their resolute defense of the few black sheep their otherwise impeccable lineage had produced. There was nothing base or stingy in the young man's heart. He was content that his future wife should not be shackled by false prudery from offering kindness, in private, to her unfortunate relative. To receive Countess Olenska within the domestic sphere was one thing; to parade her publicly at the Opera, specifically in the box occupied by the very girl whose engagement to him was imminent, was quite another.

No, he felt precisely as old Sillerton Jackson must feel: the Mingotts would never have tried it on. Of course, he acknowledged that whatever a man dared within the bounds of Fifth Avenue, old Mrs. Manson Mingott—the Matriarch of the line—would inevitably dare. He had always held admiration for the formidable old lady. Despite her origins as Catherine Spicer of Staten Island, a background marked by a father mysteriously discredited and a lack of wealth sufficient to erase that stain, she had allied herself with the head of the wealthy Mingott dynasty. She had married two of her daughters to "foreigners"—an Italian marquis and an English banker—and capped her audacity by constructing a massive house of pale cream-colored stone in an inaccessible wilderness near Central Park, a stark contrast to the brown sandstone that served as the only acceptable attire for afternoon wear.

Old Mrs. Mingott's foreign daughters had become a local legend. They never returned to visit their mother. And the mother, like many minds of active will and dominating nature, remained sedentary and corpulent at home, philosophically accepting her confinement. Yet the cream-colored mansion, supposedly modeled after the private hotels of the Parisian aristocracy, stood as tangible proof of her moral courage. Within it, surrounded by pre-Revolutionary furniture and souvenirs of the Tuileries under Louis Napoleon—where she had shone in her middle age—she reigned as placidly as if living above Thirty-fourth Street or possessing French windows that swung open rather than sliding up were entirely normal phenomena.

Every observer, including Mr. Sillerton Jackson, concurred that old Catherine lacked beauty—a gift that, in New York's eyes, justified every success and excused numerous failures. The unkind whispered that, like her Imperial namesake, she had secured her triumphs through sheer force of will, a hardness of heart, and a haughty effrontery that was somehow sanctified by the extreme decency and dignity of her private life. Mr. Manson Mingott had died when she was merely twenty-eight, having "tied up" the money with an additional caution born of the general distrust of the Spicers. But his bold young widow proceeded fearlessly. She mingled freely in foreign societies, married her daughters into heavens-know-what corrupt and fashionable circles, hobnobbed with Dukes and Ambassadors, associated familiarly with Papists, entertained opera singers, and maintained intimate friendships with figures like Mme. Taglioni. All the while, as Sillerton Jackson was the first to proclaim, there had never been a breath against her reputation. The only distinction, he noted, lay in the fact that unlike the earlier Catherine, Mrs. Manson Mingott had successfully untied her husband's fortune and lived in affluence for half a century.

However, memories of her early straits had rendered her excessively thrifty. While she ensured that any dress or piece of furniture purchased was of the highest quality, she could not bring herself to expend much on the transient pleasures of the table. Thus, for entirely different reasons, her food matched Mrs. Archer's in poverty, and her wines failed to redeem either. Her relatives deemed this penury a discredit to the Mingott name, historically synonymous with good living. Yet people continued to flock to her despite the "made dishes" and flat champagne. In response to the remonstrances of her son Lovell—who attempted to restore family credit by employing the finest chef in New York—she would laugh and declare, "What's the use of two good cooks in one family, now that I've married the girls and can't eat sauces?"

As Newland Archer mused on these matters, he turned his gaze back to the Mingott box. He observed that Mrs. Welland and her sister-in-law faced their semicircle of critics with the Mingottian aplomb instilled in them by old Catherine. Only May Welland betrayed, through a heightened flush—perhaps exacerbated by the knowledge that he watched her—a sense of the situation's gravity. As for the source of the commotion, she sat gracefully in her corner, eyes fixed on the stage. As she leaned forward, she revealed a fraction more shoulder and bosom than New York was accustomed to witnessing in ladies who wished to remain invisible.

Few things seemed more awful to Newland Archer than an offense against "Taste," that distant divinity of whom "Form" was merely the visible representative. Madame Olenska's pale, serious face appealed to his imagination as suitable for the occasion and her unhappy circumstances. But the manner in which her dress, lacking a tucker, sloped away from her thin shoulders shocked and troubled him. He hated the thought of May Welland being exposed to the influence of a young woman so careless of the dictates of Taste.

"After all," he heard a younger voice begin behind him, cutting through the Mephistopheles-and-Martha scenes, "just what happened?"

"Well—she left him; nobody attempts to deny that."

"He's an awful brute, isn't he?" continued the young inquirer, a candid Thorley, clearly preparing to enter the lists as the lady's champion.

"The very worst; I knew him at Nice," said Lawrence Lefferts with authority. "A half-paralysed white sneering fellow—rather handsome head, but eyes with a lot of lashes. Well, I'll tell you the sort: when he wasn't with women he was collecting china. Paying any price for both, I understand."

There was a general laugh, and the young champion pressed on. "Well, then—"

"Well, then; she bolted with his secretary."

"Oh, I see." The champion's face fell. "It didn't last long, though: I heard of her a few months later living alone in Venice. I believe Lovell Mingott went out to get her. He said she was desperately unhappy. That's all right—but this parading her at the Opera's another thing."

"Perhaps," young Thorley hazarded, "she's too unhappy to be left at home."

This was greeted with an irreverent laugh, and the youth blushed deeply, attempting to look as though he had intended to insinuate what the cognoscenti called a "double entendre."

"Well—it's queer to have brought Miss Welland, anyhow," someone said in a low tone, casting a side-glance at Archer.

"Oh, that's part of the campaign: Granny's orders, no doubt," Lefferts laughed. "When the old lady does a thing she does it thoroughly."

The act concluded, and a general stir rippled through the box. Suddenly, Newland Archer felt himself impelled to decisive action. The desire to be the first to enter Mrs. Mingott's box, to proclaim to the waiting world his engagement to May Welland, and to stand beside her through whatever difficulties her cousin's anomalous situation might entail, had abruptly overruled all scruples and hesitations. It sent him hurrying through the red corridors to the farther side of the house.

Upon entering the box, his eyes met Miss Welland's. He saw that she had instantly understood his motive, though the family dignity both considered a supreme virtue prevented her from voicing it. The persons of their world lived in an atmosphere of faint implications and pale delicacies, and the fact that he and she understood each other without a word seemed to the young man to bring them nearer than any explanation ever could. Her eyes said: "You see why Mamma brought me," and his answered silently: "I would not for the world have had you stay away."

"You know my niece Countess Olenska?" Mrs. Welland enquired as she shook hands with her future son-in-law.

Archer bowed without extending his hand, adhering to the custom of introduction to a lady; and Ellen Olenska bent her head slightly, keeping her own pale-gloved hands clasped on her huge fan of eagle feathers. After greeting Mrs. Lovell Mingott, a large blonde lady in creaking satin, he sat down beside his betrothed and said in a low tone, "I hope you've told Madame Olenska that we're engaged? I want everybody to know—I want you to let me announce it this evening at the ball."
