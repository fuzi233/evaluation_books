The look exchanged between the amiable spinster and the detective was a silent dialect of mutual bewilderment, a language neither fully understood, perhaps because the lady had rarely presented herself to the world burdened by anything other than a serene, unshakeable complacency.

"Miss Butterworth! What does this mean? Have you—"

"There!" Her voice cut through the air with sudden sharpness. "You have cornered me at my old tricks, and I am correspondingly ashamed, while you stand triumphant. The gray parasol you so kindly dispatched to my residence belongs to no one of my acquaintance; I was merely in the room where you discovered it, as your deduction proves correct. Since evasion offers no defense against your perspicacity, I have come here to confess."

"Ah!" The detective leaned forward, his interest piqued instantly. He pulled a chair close to Miss Butterworth and sat. "You were there!" he repeated, his tone measured. "And when? I do not presume to inquire of your purpose."

"But I shall be forced to explain my motives before I find myself at a severe disadvantage," she replied, her demeanor shifting to one of grim resolve. "It is not that I relish exposing my own frailty, but I recognize the exigencies of this moment. I fully anticipate your surprise that Amelia Butterworth, a stranger to Mr. Adams and lacking the pretext that justified my previous interventions in police affairs, should have allowed myself to be so utterly compromised as to appear before you in this posture. This was no incident involving my immediate neighbor, nor did it seek me out. I sought it, sir, and in this manner. I wish I had journeyed to Jericho first; it would have entailed greater travel and expense, yet it would have spared me this humiliation and potential public spectacle. Mr. Gryce, I never intended to become embroiled in another murder investigation. I have demonstrated my aptitude for detective work and received, on numerous occasions, marks of your approval; yet my head was not turned by these accolades—at least, I believed it was not—and I remained sincerely determined to adhere strictly to my own profession, refusing to let myself be seduced by any inducements you might offer into exercising gifts that had once earned me praise but had certainly never brought me happiness. However, the temptation arose not from you, for I might have resisted it, but from a convergence of circumstances that found me weak and, to a degree, unprepared. In essence, I was surprised into taking an interest in this affair. Oh, I am ashamed of it, so consumed by shame that I made every effort to conceal my participation, congratulating myself on my precautions until I found that parasol thrust in my face, realizing that you, if no one else, knew that Amelia Butterworth had been in Mr. Adams's chamber of death prior to your arrival. Yet I believed I had left no trace. Could you have seen..."

"Miss Butterworth, you dropped five small spangles from your gown. You wore a dress encrusted with black sequins, did you not? Furthermore, you displaced the inkstand. Well, I shall never again place faith in circumstantial evidence. I observed these tokens of a woman's presence, listened to the boy's account of the well-dressed lady who sent him to the drugstore with a message for the police, and drew the conclusion—I must admit it—that it was this woman who wielded the assassin's dagger, rather than the deaf-and-dumb butler, who has thus far borne the weight of the accusation. Consequently, I was eager to locate her, little anticipating the outcome of my search or that I would soon need to offer my most humble apologies."

"Do not apologize to me. I had no business to be there, or at least, no business to leave the five spangles you mention scattered across Mr. Adams's wretched floor. I was simply passing by the house; and had I been the woman I once was—a woman who had never ventured into the labyrinth of mystery—I would have continued on my path instead of turning aside. Sir, it is a peculiar sensation to find oneself, despite innocence, cast by an entire city as the catalyst or motive for a terrible murder, particularly when one has spent considerable time studying crime and pursuing criminals. I confess I do not enjoy the experience. But I have invited it upon myself. Had I not been so curious... But it was not curiosity I felt. I will never claim to be subject to mere curiosity; it was the expression on the young man's face. But I forget myself. I am rambling in all directions when I ought to be recounting a consecutive narrative. Not my usual habit, sir; you know this; but I am not quite myself at this moment. I declare I am more distressed by this discovery of my indiscretion than I was by Mr. Trohm's declaration of affection in Lost Man's Lane! Grant me time, Mr. Gryce; in a few moments I shall be more coherent."

"I am granting you time," he returned with one of his lowest bows. "The half-dozen questions I long to ask have not yet escaped my lips, and I sit here, as you must acknowledge, a monument of patience."

"So you deduced this deed was perpetrated by an outsider," she interrupted abruptly. "Most of the journals—I read them with meticulous care this morning—ascribed the crime to the man you have mentioned. And there appears to be sound reasoning for such a conclusion. The case is not simple, Mr. Gryce; it possesses complications—I recognized that immediately, and that is why—but I shall not waste another moment on apologies. You have a right to any fragment of fact I may have gathered during my unfortunate visit, and there is one detail omitted from every account of the murder. Mr. Adams had other visitors besides myself in those few fatal minutes preceding his demise. A young man and a woman were with him. I witnessed them emerge from the house. It was at the exact moment I was passing..."

"Tell your story more directly, Miss Butterworth. What initially drew your attention to the house?"

"There! That is the second time you have reminded me to be more concise. You will not have to do so again, Mr. Gryce. To begin, I noticed the house because I invariably notice it. I never pass it without contemplating its ancient history and speculating, to varying degrees, regarding its current inhabitants. Thus, when I found myself standing before it yesterday afternoon on my way to the art exhibition, I naturally looked up, and—whether by divine intervention or chance, I cannot say—it was precisely at that instant the inner door of the vestibule burst open, and a young man appeared in the hall, carrying a young woman in his arms. He seemed to be in a state of intense agitation, while she lay in a dead faint; but before they could attract the attention of the crowd, he had helped her to her feet, taken her onto his arm, and dragged her down the steps and into the throng of passersby, among whom they promptly vanished. I, as you may imagine, stood rooted to the spot in sheer astonishment, striving not only to discern their direction but lingering long enough to peer into the time-honored interior of this venerable house, which had been left ajar by the young man's oversight in failing to close the front door behind him. As I did so, I heard a cry emanating from within. It was muffled and distant, yet unmistakably a sound of terror and anguish: and, driven by an impulse I may live to regret, as it seems likely to plunge me into considerable trouble, I rushed up the steps and entered, shutting the door behind me to prevent others from following.

"So far, I had acted purely on instinct; but once inside that dimly lit hall, I paused to question what business I had there and what excuse I should offer if I encountered any of the household. However, a repetition of the cry, which I am prepared to swear originated from the farthest room on the parlor floor, coupled with a sharp recollection of the wandering eye and drawn countenance of the young man I had seen staggering forth a moment earlier, bearing an almost fainting woman, propelled me onward in defiance of my feminine instincts; and before I knew it, I was in the circular study before the prostrate form of a seemingly dying man. He lay as you presumably found him shortly thereafter, with a cross upon his breast and a dagger in his heart; yet his right hand trembled violently, and when I stooped to lift his head, he gave a shudder before settling into eternal stillness. I, a stranger from the street, had witnessed his final breath while the young man who had departed..."

"Can you describe him? Did you encounter him close enough for identification?"

"Yes, I believe I would recognize him again. I can at least describe his appearance. He wore a checked suit, exceedingly neat, and was taller and finer-looking than most. But his defining characteristic lay in his expression. I have never seen upon any face—not even on the stage at the climax of the most harrowing tragedy—a greater accumulation of mortal passion struggling against the imperative necessity for restraint. The young girl whose blond head rested upon his shoulder resembled a saint trapped in the grasp of a demon. She had witnessed death, but he... But I prefer not to interpret that expressive countenance. It was lost to my view almost immediately, and likely calmed itself in the face of the throng he entered, or we would be hearing about him today. The girl seemed devoid of almost all feeling. I should not remember her."

"And was that all? Did you merely observe that recumbent figure and vanish? Did you not encounter the butler? Do you possess no definitive knowledge concerning him that would exonerate or condemn him?"
