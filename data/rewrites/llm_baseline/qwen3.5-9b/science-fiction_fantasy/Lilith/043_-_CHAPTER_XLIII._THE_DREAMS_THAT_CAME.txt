I became aware of existence, and simultaneously, of a profound, infinite cold. I felt intensely blessed—more blessed, I know, than my heart can now recall imagining. I could not conceive of warmth with even the slightest suggestion of pleasure. I knew I had once enjoyed it, yet I could not remember how. This cold had soothed every care, dissolved every pain, and comforted every sorrow.

*Comforted?* No. Sorrow was swallowed up in the life drawing near, ready to restore every good and lovely thing a hundredfold. I lay at peace, filled with the quietest expectation, breathing the damp odors of Earth's bountiful bosom. I was aware of the souls of primroses, daisies, and snowdrops, patiently waiting within it for the Spring. How to convey the delight of that frozen, yet conscious sleep! I had no need to stand upright; I only needed to lie stretched out and still. How cold I was—words cannot tell—and yet I grew colder and colder, welcoming the chill more and more.

I became continuously less conscious of myself, and continuously more conscious of a bliss unimaginable yet deeply felt. I had neither created this state nor prayed for it; it was mine simply by virtue of existing, and existence was mine by virtue of a Will dwelling within me.

Then the dreams began to arrive, crowding in upon me.

I lay naked on a snowy peak. Below, the white mist heaved like a billowy sea. The cold moon hung in the air beside me, and above the moon and me lay a colder sky, in which the moon and I dwelt. I was Adam, waiting for God to breathe the breath of life into my nostrils.

*I was not Adam,* I realized, *but a child nestled in the bosom of a mother white with radiant whiteness.*

For centuries I dreamed—or perhaps chiliads? Or merely one long night? Why ask? For time had nothing to do with me. I was in the land of thought, farther in, higher up than the seven dimensions and the ten senses. I think I was where I am—in the heart of God.

I dreamed away dim cycles in the center of a melting glacier, watching the spectral moon draw nearer and nearer, while the wind and the welter of a torrent grew louder in my ears. I lay and listened: the wind, the water, and the moon sang a peaceful waiting for a redemption drawing near. I dreamed of cycles, but for all I knew or could tell, they were the solemn, aeonian march of a single second, pregnant with eternity.

Then, suddenly—but without disturbing my conscious bliss—all the wrongs I had ever committed, from far beyond my earthly memory down to the present moment, were brought fully before me. Within every wrong lived the conscious "I," confessing, abjuring, and lamenting the dead. I made atonement with every person I had injured, hurt, or offended. Every human soul to whom I had caused a troubled thought now grew unspeakably dear to me. I humbled myself before them, agonizing to cast the clinging offense from between us.

I wept at the feet of the mother whose commands I had slighted. With bitter shame, I confessed to my father that I had told him two lies long ago, forgotten them, and then kept them in memory solely to crush them at his feet. I became the eager slave of all whom I had wronged. Countless services I devised to render them! For one, I would build a house never before grown from the ground; for another, I would train horses unseen in any world; for a third, I would create a garden never before bloomed, haunted by still pools and alive with running waters. I would write songs to make their hearts swell and tales to make them glow. I would turn the forces of the world into channels of invention so vast they would laugh with the joy of wonder.

Love possessed me! Love was my life! Love was to me, as to Him who made me, all in all!

Suddenly, I found myself in a solid blackness, upon which the ghost of light dwelling in the caverns of the eyes could cast not one fancied glimmer. Yet my heart, which feared nothing and hoped infinitely, was full of peace. I lay imagining what the light would be when it came, and what new creation it would bring with it.

And then, suddenly, without conscious volition, I sat up and stared about me.

The moon was looking in through the lowest, horizontal, crypt-like windows of a death-chamber, her long light slanting across the fallen, but still ripening, sheaves of the harvest of the great husbandman.

*But no!* That harvest was gone! Gathered in, or swept away by chaotic storm, not a sacred sheaf remained! My dead were gone! I was alone!

Desolation lay in depths yet deeper than I had hitherto known. Had there never been any ripening dead? Had I but dreamed them and their loveliness? Why then these walls? Why the empty couches?

No; they were all up! They were all abroad in the new eternal day, and had forgotten me! They had left me behind, and alone! Tenfold more terrible was the tomb with its inhabitants departed! The quiet ones had made me quiet with their presence, having pervaded my mind with their blissful peace; now I had no friend, and my lovers were far from me!

A moment I sat, horror-stricken. I had been alone with the moon on a mountain top in the sky; now I was alone with her in a huge cenotaph. She too was staring about, seeking her dead with a ghastly gaze!

I sprang to my feet and staggered from the fearful place. The cottage was empty. I ran out into the night. No moon was there! Even as I left the chamber, a cloudy rampart had risen and covered her. But a broad shimmer came from far over the heath, mingled with a ghostly murmuring music, as if the moon were raining a light that plashed as it fell.

I ran stumbling across the moor and found a lovely lake, margined with reeds and rushes. The moon, hidden behind the cloud, gazed upon the monster's den—a pool of clearest, brightest water, very still. But the musical murmur went on, filling the quiet air and drawing me after it.

I walked round the border of the little mere and climbed the range of hills. What a sight rose to my eyes! The whole expanse where, with hot, aching feet, I had crossed and recrossed the deep-scored channels and ravines of the dry river-bed was now alive with streams, with torrents, with still pools—"a river deep and wide"!

How the moon flashed on the water! How the water answered the moon with flashes of its own—white flashes breaking everywhere from its rock-encountered flow! And a great jubilant song arose from its bosom, the song of new-born liberty.

I stood a moment gazing, and my heart also began to exult: my life was not all a failure! I had helped to set this river free! My dead were not lost! I had but to go after and find them! I would follow and follow until I came whither they had gone. Our meeting might be thousands of years away, but at last—*at last*—I should hold them!

Wherefore else did the floods clap their hands? I hurried down the hill; my pilgrimage was begun! In what direction to turn my steps I knew not, but I must go and go until I found my living dead.

A torrent ran swift and wide at the foot of the range. I rushed in; it laid no hold upon me. I waded through the next; I sprang across the third; I swam the fourth; I waded the fifth. I stopped to gaze on the wondrous loveliness of the ceaseless flash and flow, and to hearken to the multitudinous broken music. Every now and then, some incipient air seemed about to draw itself clear of the dulcet confusion, only to merge again in the consorted roar. At moments, the world of waters would invade as if to overwhelm me—not with the force of its seaward rush or the shouting of its liberated throng, but with the greatness of the silence wandering into sound.

As I stood lost in delight, a hand was laid on my shoulder. I turned and saw a man in the prime of strength, beautiful as if fresh from the heart of the glad Creator, young like Him who cannot grow old. I looked: it was Adam. He stood large and grand, clothed in a white robe, with the moon in his hair.

"Father," I cried, "where is she? Where are the dead? Is the great resurrection come and gone? The terror of my loneliness was upon me; I could not sleep without my dead. I ran from the desolate chamber. Whither shall I go to find them?"

"You mistake, my son," he answered, in a voice whose very breath was consolation. "You are still in the chamber of death, still upon your couch, asleep and dreaming, with the dead around you."

"Alas! When I but dream, how am I to know it? The dream best dreamed is the likest to the waking truth!"
