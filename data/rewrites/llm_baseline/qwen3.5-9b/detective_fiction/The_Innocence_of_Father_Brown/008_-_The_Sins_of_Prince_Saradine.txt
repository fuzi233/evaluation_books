Flambeau decided to take his monthly leave from his Westminster office by spending it aboard a tiny sailing vessel—so diminutive it spent far more time acting as a rowboat than a yacht. He chose the narrow rivers of the Eastern Counties, where the craft appeared magical, gliding over land through meadows and cornfields. The boat held comfortably only two souls and their absolute necessities. Flambeau's "special philosophy" dictated four essentials: tins of salmon for sustenance, loaded revolvers for combat, a bottle of brandy in case of fainting, and a priest, presumably to handle death.

With this light luggage, he navigated the little Norfolk rivers toward the Broads, pausing to admire overhanging gardens, mirrored villas, and quiet pools. Like a true philosopher, he had no fixed destination, yet he possessed a valid excuse. Years prior, during his reign as a king of thieves in Paris, he had received countless letters of approval, denunciation, or love. One, however, remained etched in his memory: a visiting card sent via English post. On the reverse, written in French and green ink, read: *"If you ever retire and become respectable, come and see me. I want to meet you, for I have met all the other great men of my time. That trick of yours of getting one detective to arrest the other was the most splendid scene in French history."* The front bore the engraving: *Prince Saradine, Reed House, Reed Island, Norfolk.*

At the time, Flambeau had merely noted the sender was a brilliant, fashionable figure from Southern Italy who had once eloped with a high-ranking married woman—an act that led to the alleged suicide of the insulted husband in Sicily. The Prince had since lived in Vienna and traveled restlessly until settling in England. Seizing the opportunity, Flambeau planned a surprise visit to this eminent exile. Little did he know how soon he would arrive.

That night, they moored beneath a bank draped in high grasses and pollarded trees. Exhaustion brought sleep quickly, and they awoke just before dawn. A large lemon moon set in the forest of grass above them, leaving the sky a vivid violet-blue. Standing amidst the towering weeds, the daisies seemed giant, the dandelions colossal, evoking the whimsical wallpaper of a nursery.

"By Jove!" exclaimed Flambeau. "It's like being in fairyland."

Father Brown sat bolt upright, crossing himself abruptly. "The authors of medieval ballads," the priest replied, "knew more about fairies than you do. It isn't only nice things that happen in fairyland."

"Oh, bosh!" dismissed Flambeau. "Only nice things occur under such an innocent moon. Let us push on; we may die and rot before seeing such a moon again."

"All right," agreed Father Brown. "I never said it was wrong to enter fairyland. Only that it is always dangerous."

As they drifted up the brightening river, the violet sky and golden moon faded into the colorless cosmos preceding daybreak. Soon, the horizon split with streaks of red, gold, and grey, obscured by the black silhouette of a town. Under the hanging roofs of this riverside hamlet, the houses resembled huge grey and red cattle drinking from the river. Though the dawn had turned to working daylight, the wharves remained empty save for a single, placid man in his shirt sleeves. He had a face as round as a recently sunken moon, framed by red whiskers, leaning against a post. Without analysis, Flambeau rose and shouted his inquiry regarding Reed Island. The man smiled expansively and pointed upstream.

Following the direction, they rounded a sharp angle into a secluded pool. In the center lay a long, low islet fringed with rushes, hosting a bungalow constructed of bamboo and tropical cane. Pale yellow walls contrasted with a darker, reddish-brown roof, creating a rhythmic monotony. The morning breeze rustled the reeds, singing through the ribbed structure like a giant panpipe.

"By George!" cried Flambeau. "Here it is! Reed Island, Reed House! I believe that fat man with whiskers was a fairy."

"Perhaps," remarked Father Brown impartially. "If he was, he was a bad fairy."

Impulsively, Flambeau grounded the boat in the rattling reeds. They stepped onto the islet beside the silent house, which faced away from the river. The main entrance overlooked a long garden, accessible only by a path circling nearly three sides of the building. Peering through windows on three different facades, they glimpsed the same long, well-lit room: light wood paneling, numerous mirrors, and a setup prepared for an elegant lunch.

The front door, flanked by turquoise flowerpots, was opened by a butler of the dreariest type—long, lean, grey, and listless. He murmured that Prince Saradine was away but expected hourly, noting the house was kept ready for him and his guests. Seeing the card with its green scrawl, the butler's parchment-like face flickered with life. "His Highness may be here any minute," he said shakily. "He would be distressed to miss any gentleman he invited. We have orders to keep a cold lunch ready."

Curiosity moved Flambeau to accept. The butler, Mr. Paul, ushered them into the long, lightly paneled room. Its defining feature was the alternating rows of low windows and oblong mirrors, lending the space an airy, unsubstantial quality, as if dining outdoors. Pictures hung in the corners: a grey photograph of a young man in uniform and a red chalk sketch of two long-haired boys. When Flambeau asked if the soldier was the prince, Paul curtly denied it, identifying him as the prince's younger brother, Captain Stephen Saradine. Afterward, the butler dried up, losing all conversational inclination.

Once the exquisite coffee and liqueurs were consumed, they toured the garden and library and met the housekeeper—a dark, handsome woman of considerable majesty, resembling a plutonic Madonna. She and Paul were the sole survivors of the prince's original foreign staff; the others were new recruits from Norfolk. The housekeeper, Mrs. Anthony, spoke with a slight Italian accent, suggesting her surname was a Norfolk adaptation of a Latin origin. Paul, while trained in English, retained a faintly foreign air.

Despite its beauty, the place radiated a curious, luminous sadness. Hours stretched like days within the sun-drenched rooms, yet the light felt dead. Beneath the chatter and clinking glasses, the melancholy murmur of the river persisted.

"We have taken a wrong turning and come to a wrong place," Father Brown observed, gazing at the grey-green sedges. "Never mind; one can sometimes do good by being the right person in the wrong place."

Though usually silent, Father Brown possessed an oddly sympathetic nature. In those endless hours, he unconsciously delved deeper into Reed House's secrets than his companion. His friendly silence allowed him to glean information effortlessly. Paul, naturally uncommunicative, revealed only that his master had been badly treated, primarily by his brother, Captain Stephen, whom he described with a sneer as a ne'er-do-well who had drained the prince's fortune, forcing him into this quiet retreat.

Mrs. Anthony was more forthcoming, her tone faintly acidic yet awestruck. While Flambeau examined the red sketch of the two boys, the housekeeper swept in. The room's array of mirrors meant anyone entering was multiplied in reflection. Father Brown paused mid-sentence, but Flambeau, studying the drawing, loudly declared, "The brothers Saradine, I suppose. They both look innocent enough. It would be hard to say which is the good brother and which the bad." Realizing the lady's presence, he deflected with trivialities and exited to the garden. Father Brown, however, continued staring at the sketch. Mrs. Anthony returned the gaze.

Her large, tragic brown eyes and olive face glowed with painful wonder. "He is right enough in one way," she whispered, addressing the priest as if plotting together. "Your friend says it would be hard to pick out the good and bad brothers. Oh, it would be hard... mighty hard... to pick out the good one."

"I don't understand you," Father Brown replied, beginning to withdraw.

The woman stepped closer, her brow furrowed, posture savage like a bull lowering its horns. "There isn't a good one," she hissed. "There was badness enough in the captain taking all that money, but I don't think there was much goodness in the prince giving it. The captain's not the only one with something against him."

A light dawned on the cleric's face; his lips formed the silent word "blackmail." Just then, the woman spun around, her face turning stark white, and nearly fell. The door had opened soundlessly, revealing pale Paul standing like a ghost. Due to the reflective walls, it appeared as though five Pauls had entered simultaneously.

"His Highness," he announced stiffly, "has just arrived."

Instantly, a figure passed outside the first window, crossing the sunlit pane like a lit stage prop. Moments later, he appeared at the second window, the mirrors multiplying the image of an eagle profile and marching stride. He was erect and alert, with white hair and an odd ivory-yellow complexion.
