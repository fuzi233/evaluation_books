I cannot pretend to describe how the agonizing interval of suspense in which I now found myself might affect another man. As for me, the mere prospect of two hours' uncertainty rendered me physically incapable of remaining still in any single spot and morally unable to utter a word to any living soul until I had heard everything Ezra Jennings had to say.

Driven by this feverish state, I abandoned my planned visit to Mrs. Ablewhite and even shrank from meeting Gabriel Betteredge. Returning to Frizinghall, I left a note for Betteredge stating that an unexpected summons had detained me for a few hours, but assuring him I would return around three o'clock. I instructed him to dine at the usual hour and to pass the time as he saw fit, knowing full well that his many friends in the village would keep him occupied until my arrival at the hotel.

With that settled, I hurried out of town again, wandering the lonely moorlands surrounding Frizinghall until my watch signaled the time to return to Mr. Candy's house.

Upon my arrival, I found Ezra Jennings waiting for me.

He sat alone in a sparsely furnished room adjoining a surgery via a glass door. The barren, buff-colored walls were adorned with grotesque colored diagrams depicting the ravages of terrible diseases. A bookcase brimmed with dusty medical texts, topped not by a bust but by a skull; a large deal table was heavily stained with ink; wooden kitchen-style chairs stood scattered about; a threadbare rug lay in the center of the floor; and a rough sink with a basin and waste pipe jutted from the wall, grimly suggesting its purpose for surgical operations. These items comprised the entirety of the room's furniture. Outside, bees hummed among potted flowers, birds sang in the garden, and the faint, intermittent tinkling of a tuneless piano drifted from a neighboring house. In any other setting, these mundane sounds would have welcomed the visitor. Here, they intruded upon a silence broken only by the echoes of human suffering. I glanced at the mahogany instrument case and the massive roll of lint on the shelves, shuddering inwardly at the familiar sounds associated with the daily work of Ezra Jennings.

"I offer no apology, Mr. Blake, for the quarters in which I receive you," he said. "It is the only room available at this hour where we can be assured of privacy. My notes are ready, and here are two books we may need to consult. Please bring your chair to the table, and we shall examine them together."

I moved to the table, and Ezra Jennings handed over his manuscript notes. They consisted of two large folio sheets. One bore writing only at irregular intervals; the other was densely packed with red and black ink from top to bottom. In my irritated state of curiosity, I set aside the second sheet in despair.

"Have some mercy on me!" I exclaimed. "Tell me what to expect before I attempt to read this."

"Willingly, Mr. Blake! May I ask you one or two more questions?"

"Ask whatever you wish!"

He regarded me with a sad smile and kind, soft brown eyes.

"You have already told me," he said, "that you have never, to your knowledge, tasted opium."

"To my knowledge," I repeated.

"You will soon understand why I phrase it thus. Proceed. Are you unaware of ever having taken opium? Last year, at this time, you suffered from nervous agitation and slept terribly. However, on the night of the birthday, there was an exception—you slept soundly. Is that correct?"

"Quite right!"

"Can you attribute your nervous distress and insomnia to any specific cause?"

"I cannot. I recall Old Betteredge guessing at a cause, but it was hardly significant."

"Pardon me. Anything is significant in a case like this. Betteredge attributed your sleeplessness to something. What was it?"

"My quitting smoking."

"An habitual smoker were you?"

"Yes."

"Did you break the habit abruptly?"

"Yes."

"Betteredge was entirely correct, Mr. Blake. A man of ordinary constitution cannot quit a habit like smoking suddenly without temporarily damaging his nervous system. Your sleepless nights are explained. My next question concerns Mr. Candy. Do you recall entering into any dispute with him at the birthday dinner or afterward regarding his profession?"

The question instantly triggered a dormant memory. The foolish argument between Mr. Candy and myself, detailed extensively in Betteredge's narrative, had faded from my mind. All I could now recall was that I had attacked the art of medicine at the dinner table with such rashness and persistence that it angered Mr. Candy momentarily. I remembered Lady Verinder intervening to halt the dispute, and that shortly thereafter, the little doctor and I had "made it up" and became friends again before shaking hands that evening.

"There is one final matter," Ezra Jennings continued. "Of great importance: Did you have any reason to feel particular anxiety about the Diamond at this time last year?"

"I had the strongest reasons. I knew it was the target of a conspiracy and had been warned to protect Miss Verinder, the possessor."

"Was the safety of the Diamond discussed between you and anyone else immediately before retiring on the birthday night?"

"It was the subject of a conversation between Lady Verinder and her daughter..."

"...heard by you?"

"Yes."

Ezra Jennings picked up his notes and placed them in my hands.

"Mr. Blake," he said, "if you read these notes now, illuminated by the light of our current discussion, you will make two astonishing discoveries about yourself. First, that you entered Miss Verinder's sitting room and took the Diamond while in a trance induced by opium. Second, that the opium was administered to you by Mr. Candy without your knowledge, serving as a practical refutation of the opinions you had voiced to him at the dinner."

I sat holding the papers, completely stupefied.

"Try to forgive poor Mr. Candy," the assistant said gently. "He caused dreadful mischief, I admit, but he did so innocently. Look at the notes; you will see that but for his illness, he would have returned to Lady Verinder's the morning after the party, confessed the trick, and the truth, hidden for a year, would have surfaced in a day."

My composure began to return. "Mr. Candy is beyond my resentment," I said angrily. "Yet the trick he played on me remains an act of treachery. I may forgive, but I shall not forget."

"Every medical practitioner commits acts of treachery in the course of their practice, Mr. Blake," Ezra replied. "The ignorant distrust of opium in England is not limited to the lower classes. Every busy doctor occasionally finds themselves obliged to deceive patients, just as Mr. Candy deceived you. I do not defend the folly of the trick under the circumstances, but I plead for a more accurate and merciful judgment of his motives."

"How was it done?" I asked. "Who gave me the laudanum without my knowing?"

"I cannot tell you. Nothing regarding that aspect of the matter escaped Mr. Candy's lips throughout his illness. Perhaps your own memory can identify the suspect."

"No."

"If that is the case, further inquiry is futile. The laudanum was secretly administered somehow. Let us leave that and turn to matters of immediate importance. Read my notes if you can, acquaint yourself with the past, and prepare for a bold and startling proposal regarding the future."

Those last words roused me.

I examined the papers in the order Ezra had arranged them. The sheet with fewer writings was on top. Disconnected words and sentence fragments, dropped by Mr. Candy in his delirium, appeared as follows:

*"... Mr. Franklin Blake ... and agreeable ... down a peg ... medicine ... confesses ... sleep at night ... tell him ... out of order ... medicine ... he tells me ... and groping in the dark mean one and the same thing ... all the company at the dinner-table ... I say ... groping after sleep ... nothing but medicine ... he says ... leading the blind ... know what it means ... witty ... a night's rest in spite of his teeth ... wants sleep ... Lady Verinder's medicine chest ... five-and-twenty minims ... without his knowing it ... tomorrow morning ... Well, Mr. Blake ... medicine today ... never ... without it ... out, Mr. Candy ... excellent ... without it ... down on him ... truth ... something besides ... excellent ... dose of laudanum, sir ... bed ... what ... medicine now."*

"That is what you heard at his bedside?" I asked.

"Literally and exactly what I heard," he answered. "Except that I omitted the repetitions. He reiterated certain phrases dozens, perhaps fifty times, attaching varying degrees of importance to the ideas they represented. These repetitions aided me in piecing together the fragments. Do not assume," he added, pointing to the second sheet, "that I claim to have reproduced the exact phrasing Mr. Candy would have used if he could speak coherently. I merely assert that I have pierced through the barrier of disjointed expression to reach the coherent thought underlying it. Judge for yourself."

I turned to the second sheet, now known to be the key to the first.
