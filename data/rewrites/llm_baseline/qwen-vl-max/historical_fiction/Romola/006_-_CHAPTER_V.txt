The Via de’ Bardi, a street etched into the memory of Florence, lies in Oltrarno—the southern bank of the Arno River. It stretches from the Ponte Vecchio to the Piazza de’ Mozzi near the Ponte alle Grazie. On its right, it is backed by the steep slope once known as the hill of Bogoli, famed for its stone quarries that supplied much of the city’s pavement—though unstable when rain soaked through. To the left, the buildings line the riverbank, their irregular façades forming a picturesque, uneven front mirrored softly in the water as the sun begins its descent toward the western hills. Though these structures are charming, some seem too modern to the historian’s eye—replacements for the grand homes of the Bardi family, razed by popular fury in the mid-fourteenth century.

The Bardi were proud, fierce, and driven. They stood at the forefront of Florence’s earliest civil conflicts, when noble towers loomed over narrow streets and blood stained the gutters. Mars, the old god of war, might have smiled, seeing how little reverence Florentines truly held for him, given their endless devotion to the Baptist. Yet the Bardi were not merely warriors; they had hands that grasped swords with strength, but also fingers that loved the feel of minted coin. Their eyes, sharp and true, saw power not only in conquest, but in commerce and influence. By the mid-1300s, they had risen from humble *popolani* to landowners and feudal Counts of Vernio—a rank that stirred envy among their republican neighbors.

Their wealth came from trade, and soon they stood at the peak of European finance—much like the Rothschilds of later centuries. They funded Edward III’s wars, receiving revenues in kind, especially wool, the most valuable cargo for Florentine galleys. But when the English king defaulted and Sicilian creditors demanded repayment, their credit collapsed. The shock rippled across the Mediterranean coast, devastating their business empire. Yet unlike many ruined men, the Bardi did not crumble. Instead, they stood taller, more defiant than ever—so arrogant that in 1343, during a time of civic unrest chronicled by Giovanni Villani, they provoked the wrath of the people. Fortifying themselves between the two bridges, they resisted the advancing banners of the mob until attacked from behind on the hill. Then, their twenty-two riverfront houses—*palagi e case grandi*—were sacked and burned. Many Bardi fled the city. But roots ran deep in Florence. Over time, the name reappeared in public life, sometimes in dignity, sometimes in disgrace—proof of a lineage marked by cycles of fortune and fall.

By 1492, the Bardi no longer owned the old street along the river. It had passed to other families, notably the Neri, who held a long stretch of homes on the hillside.

Yet within one of those Neri houses lived a descendant of the Bardi—of the very branch that had become Counts of Vernio. He carried the family pride, the hunger for distinction, the desire to leave an indelible mark upon the world. But his world was different. He was not a warrior, nor a lord of castles, nor a merchant bold enough to risk empires on trade routes. He was a scholar—blind, poor, and alone. His hands, once strong and sure, were now cramped from years of copying manuscripts. He ate simple meals, wore worn clothes—first by choice, then by necessity. He sat surrounded by books and ancient fragments, living in the light of memories from youth, which still burned brightly in his mind. This was Bardo de’ Bardi—the man whom Nello, the barber, had promised to introduce to the young Greek scholar, Tito Melema.

Bardo’s house stood on the hillside side of the street, one of those massive, somber stone buildings with small windows and a roofed terrace or loggia, common in old Florence. A heavy door with iron hinges opened onto a vaulted courtyard, bare except for a large iron lamp hanging from the ceiling. A smaller door led to the stone staircase and ground-floor rooms, used as warehouses. Both floors stored goods destined for distant shores—the Scheldt, Africa, the Aegean, the Black Sea. Maso, the elderly servant, returned from the market with vegetables and climbed slowly to the second floor, where he entered the master’s chamber. We follow him through the antechamber to the left-hand door, which he opens.

He glances inside and nods. From the far end of the room comes a clear, young voice: “Ah, you’re back, Maso. Good. We’ve needed nothing.”

The speaker stands before a carved reading-desk—such as one finds in Italian church choirs—her long hair catching the dim light. She is seventeen or eighteen, tall and graceful, her golden-red hair, wavy and unbroken like autumn clouds at sunset, bound loosely by a black ribbon above her ears. It falls forward, framing her neck beneath a square-cut black gown of coarse serge. Her eyes are fixed on a large book open before her. One hand rests lightly on the desk; the other grips the back of her father’s chair.

Her father sits with head raised, turned slightly toward her—as if he sees her. His face, pale and lined, is crowned with white hair and a black velvet cap. The resemblance between them is striking: both share a refined brow, delicate nostrils, and a firm mouth beneath a powerful chin—an expression of quiet strength and stubborn resolve. The girl mirrors this in the set of her head, the curve of her neck and shoulders. Her face holds a tension—between nobility and fire—that makes one hesitate to say whether she inspires love or awe tinged with fear. Only the eyes can reveal the truth—and hers are bent on the Latin pages of Politian’s *Miscellanea*, reading aloud at Chapter Eighty:

“There was a nymph of Thebes named Chariclo, beloved by Pallas. She was the mother of Teiresias. Once, in summer heat, Pallas, bathing with Chariclo in the Heliconian Hippocrene, was seen unveiled by Teiresias, who came to drink. The sight caused him to lose his sight, for the Saturnian laws decree that anyone who beholds the gods without their consent must pay a heavy price... When Teiresias fell into this misfortune, Pallas, moved by Chariclo’s tears, granted him prophecy and long life. Even after death, his wisdom endured, and an oracle spoke from his tomb. She gave him a staff, so he could walk without stumbling... Thus, Nonnus, in Book Five of the *Dionysiaca*, has Acteon exclaim that Teiresias is blessed—for though blind, he has seen Minerva unveiled, and thus carries her image forever in his soul.”

At this point, Romola’s hand slips from the chair and touches her father’s, which he has just raised. She does not turn, but continues reading, her voice slightly altered—something held back. The old man speaks:

“Stay, Romola. Bring me my copy of Nonnus. It is better than any Poliziano possesses. I made corrections no one else has seen. I finished it in 1477, when my sight began to fail.”

Romola walks to the far end of the room, moving with the natural grace of a queen—unselfconscious, yet regal. She steps with ease, her frame tall and finely shaped.

“Is it in the right place, Romola?” Bardo asks, always needing confirmation that reality matches the precise image in his mind.

“Yes, father,” she replies. “On the west end, third shelf from the bottom, behind the bust of Hadrian, above Apollonius Rhodius and Callimachus, below Lucan and Silius Italicus.”

As she speaks, a trained ear might detect the faintest strain in her voice—a weariness beneath patience. But when she sees her father’s arms outstretched, trembling with anticipation, her hazel eyes fill with compassion. She hurries to lay the book in his lap and kneels beside him, looking up as if love alone could pierce the darkness that veils his world. In that moment, the sharpness of her features softens—pride and passion give way to tenderness. The deepest part of her, usually hidden, flows through her gaze. She is no longer merely beautiful—she is profoundly human.

But Bardo does not see it. He is flushed, agitated, his fingers tracing the edges of the book.

“The vellum has yellowed in thirteen years,” he says.

“Yes, father,” Romola answers gently. “But your letters at the back are still dark and clear—fine Roman script. And the Greek…” She opens the book on his knee. “It is more beautiful than any manuscript you bought.”

“Indeed,” Bardo murmurs, running his finger over the page as if seeking form. “What hired scribe can match one who loves the words? To such a man, an error is worse than blindness itself. Even these new printers—those who threaten to make learning vulgar—must rely on our manuscripts, where scholars have read deeply, almost divinely, into the poet’s soul. Without us, they would flood the world with nonsense, turning Parnassus into a poisoned stream. Now find the passage in Book Five—Poliziano refers to it. I know it well.”

Romola takes the book, seating herself on a low stool near his knee. She reads the four lines where Acteon declares Teiresias fortunate.

“It is true,” Bardo says when she finishes. “A true vision. For what is the narrow light men use to see only their own petty world, compared to the broad, enduring light that reveals centuries of thought, nations’ lives, and the minds of immortals? I lived with the dead even when I could see. The living seemed mere shadows—lifeless, empty. Unlike the Lamiae Poliziano mocks—Florentines who put on their eyes when they go out and take them off when they return—I come from the streets as from a dream, and return to my books, saying with Petrarch, ‘Books delight me, speak with me, counsel me, and bind me in a living, wise companionship.’”

“And in one thing you are happier than Petrarch,” Romola says, smiling softly. “He looked at his Homer and mourned that the Greek was a dead letter to him. He suffered inner blindness—worse than yours.”

“True,” Bardo replies. “I carry within me the fruit of my fervent study under Crisolora, Filelfo, Argiropulo. I wished to weave all my discoveries into a single work—the harvest of my life. But blindness stole it. And I lacked a fitting assistant. The endurance needed for such paths is less compatible with feminine nature than with frail bodies.”

“Father,” Romola says sharply, flushing. “I will read anything you wish. I will find passages, make notes—whatever you need.”

Bardo shakes his head, smiling with a bitter pity. “Like a pentathlete trying to win five contests with the limbs of a nymph. Have I forgotten your fainting while searching for references to explain a single line of Callimachus?”

“But father, it was the weight of the books. Maso can help. It was not lack of attention.”

He shakes his head again. “I do not need limbs. I need a sharp mind—to cut through the fog of my failing senses. Blindness acts like a dam, forcing thought backward, blocking progress. If my son had stayed—if he had not abandoned me, seduced by fanatical dreams fit only for madmen who dwell among tombs—I might have continued. He was gifted. But now… now the path is closed. All but the narrow track left to me—alone in my blindness.”

Romola rises abruptly, carrying the book back to the shelf. The pain of his words stings her. She returns, standing apart, arms lowered, fingers tightly clasped. Her face is sorrowful as she looks at the silent objects around her—the parchment spines, the broken marble, the rusted bronze and clay.

Bardo, lost in memory, does not notice her retreat.

“Yes,” he continues. “With my son, I might have shared in the triumphs of this age. The names of Bardi—father and son—might have been honored by future scholars, not for idle poetry or pretentious philosophy, but because we offered a lamp to illuminate the greatest works of the past. Why should Poliziano, born after I debated Thomas of Sarzana, be remembered as a great commentator? Why should Ficino, whose Latin offends me and who wanders blindly through superstition, be called the high priest of Platonism—while I, who surpass them, have done nothing but scattered work, claimed by others? Because my son—whom I raised to renew my learning with youth—left me to wander with fanatics, to scream at midnight, to preach on pilgrimages fit only for men who know no past beyond the missal and crucifix—left me when the night had already begun to fall.”

His voice trembles, falling into a tone of sorrow so raw it moves Romola. She turns, her heart swelling with pity. She sits beside him, placing her hand on his knee—not to speak, but to be near, to comfort without words.

“Yes, Romola,” Bardo says, letting his heavy hand, ringed with protective bands, rest too firmly on her blue-veined wrist. She bites her lip to keep still. “If Florence remembers me, it will be as Niccolò Niccoli did—because I chose knowledge over wealth, collected ancient wisdom, and left it, as the Romans did, for my city’s eternal good. But why limit myself to Florence? If Florence remembers me, will not the world?”

He pauses, voice softening. “Yet Lorenzo’s early death has brought new trouble. He promised—would have sworn—that my collection would bear my name, never be sold. But there is enough for the harpies to seize—enough for you too, Romola. Besides, you will marry. Bernardo scolds me for not seeking a proper *parentado* for you. We will delay no longer. We shall consider it.”

“No, father,” Romola says quickly. “What could you do? And it’s useless. Wait until someone seeks me.”

“Nay, child,” Bardo replies. “That is not a father’s duty. The ancients did not think so. And Florentines have not fallen from their ancestors’ ways.”

“But I will study,” Romola insists, her eyes wide with anxiety. “I will become as learned as Cassandra Fedele. I will serve you as if I were a son. Perhaps some great scholar will want to marry me—someone who won’t care about dowry. He’ll want to live here, to be your son in place of mine… and you’ll not regret that I was a daughter.”

A sob rises in her voice. The last words touch something deep in Bardo. He reaches upward, searching for her hair. She leans her head into his hand, and he strokes it gently, leaning close as if he sees a faint glow in her presence.
