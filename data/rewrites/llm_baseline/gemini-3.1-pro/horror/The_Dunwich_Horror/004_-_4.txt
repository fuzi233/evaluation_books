For a decade, the annals of the Whateleys folded seamlessly into the circular, generational rhythms of a morbid agrarian community. The village, long deafened by the slow creep of modernity, treated the family's May Eve and All-Hallow rites as mere seasonal routines. Twice a year, fires bloomed atop Sentinel Hill, smelling richly of charred sage and rendering marrow, whilst the mountain rumblings provided a deep, subterranean bass note to the valley's acoustic ecology. Yet, beneath the domestic facade of the lonely farmhouse, there was a meticulous, almost culinary precision to their dark husbandry. Callers who caught the heavy, coppery olfactory notes of freshly drained blood wondered how swiftly or lingeringly a cow or bullock was processed and prepared in the sealed upper story. Talk of animal cruelty arose, but Dunwich folk, clinging to their isolated ways, kept their mouths shut. 

Around 1923, when Wilbur was a boy of ten—though bearing the bearded, towering stature and deep-timbered voice of full maturity—a second great siege of carpentry commenced. Discarded lumber told a tale of radical architectural hollowing: Wilbur and his grandfather had gutted the upper partitions, creating a single, vast wooden resonator beneath the peaked roof. Most violently, they tore down the great central chimney, the ancestral hearth where generations had preserved meats and simmered bone broths, replacing it with a flimsy tin stove-pipe that rattled with a discordant, metallic tininess against the howling winds.

In the spring following this upheaval, Old Whateley attuned his fading senses to the organic symphony of the glen. He noted the increasing flocks of whippoorwills emerging from Cold Spring Glen to chant beneath his window. As a traditional agrarian elder rooted in the deep lore of sustenance and soil, he found profound meaning in this localized acoustic phenomenon. "They whistle jest in tune with my breathin' naow," he murmured, his native dialect an eroding vessel of old-world truths that sanitized modern English could never fully parse. "An' I guess they're gittin' ready to ketch my soul. They know it's a-goin' aout, an' dun't calc'late to miss it. Yew'll know, boys, arter I'm gone, whether they git me er not. Ef they dew, they'll keep up a-singin' an' laffin' till break o' day. Ef they dun't, they'll kinder quiet daown like. I expeck them an' the souls they hunts fer hev some pretty tough tussles sometimes."

On Lammas Night of 1924, the cycle of the harvest reached its darkest iteration. Wilbur lashed his final horse through the oppressive, loam-scented darkness to telephone Dr. Houghton from Osborn's store. Arriving from Aylesbury, Houghton brought the sterile, clinical detachment of the industrialized world into an environment dripping with sensory history. He found Old Whateley steeped in the sour, lactic scent of approaching death, his stertorous breathing rattling through the room. By the bed stood the shapeless albino daughter and the towering, bearded grandson. Above them, the hollowed void of the second floor emitted a terrifying acoustic signature: a rhythmical surging and lapping, much like the slow, wet churn of a colossal fermentation vat. Yet Houghton was most distressed by the natural world’s invasive chorus. A seemingly limitless legion of whippoorwills blanketed the farmhouse, their cries timed with diabolical precision to the wheezing gasps of the dying patriarch. To Houghton, whose ears were accustomed to the soulless, mechanical hum of Aylesbury’s clinics, this weaponized acoustic ecology felt unnatural—too deeply rooted in a landscape he despised.

Near one o'clock, Old Whateley rallied, his gasps carrying the heavy weight of untranslatable agrarian wisdom. He passed on the terrible recipe of his lineage to his grandson. "More space, Willy, more space soon. Yew grows—an' _that_ grows faster. It'll be ready to sarve ye soon, boy. Open up the gates to Yog-Sothoth with the long chant that ye'll find on page 751 _of the complete edition_, an' _then_ put a match to the prison. Fire from airth can't burn it nohaow!" The birds outside shifted their tempo instantly, matching his altered cadence. The elder continued his grotesque instruction in husbandry and dietary exactitude: "Feed it reg'lar, Willy, an' mind the quantity; but dun't let it grow too fast fer the place, fer ef it busts quarters or gits aout afore ye opens to Yog-Sothoth, it's all over an' no use. Only them from beyont kin make it multiply an' work.... Only them, the old uns as wants to come back...." 

As the throaty death-rattle finally came, Lavinia screamed, her voice tangling with the bird-cries. Dr. Houghton closed the glazing eyes as the tumult of whippoorwills faded imperceptibly into the silence of a failed hunt. While Lavinia sobbed, Wilbur merely chuckled, the low timbre of his bass voice harmonizing with the faint mountain rumblings. "They didn't git him," he muttered.

Wilbur evolved into a meticulous historian of forbidden lore, corresponding with distant librarians to source rare, archaic volumes the way a passionate chef seeks lost, ancient recipes of sustenance. He was despised locally, shrouded in the olfactory dread of unexplained youthful disappearances, yet he silenced all inquiry with old gold, continually purchasing vast herds of cattle—the literal raw ingredients required for the ceaseless, unseen consumption upstairs. His physical form expanded grotesquely; by 1925, when a pale, uncomprehending scholar from Miskatonic University—a man trapped in a dominant, sterile academic lexicon—visited him, Wilbur stood an imposing six and three-quarters feet tall.

Wilbur’s contempt for his half-deformed mother grew, a harsh power dynamic that eventually exiled her from the visceral, sensory-rich rituals on the hills. By 1926, the erosion of their shared familial dialect was complete; Lavinia confessed her isolation to Mamie Bishop. "They's more abaout him as I knows than I kin tell ye, Mamie," she lamented, mourning the untranslatability of her own home. "An' naowadays they's more nor what I know myself. I vaow afur Gawd, I dun't know what he wants nor what he's a-tryin' to dew." 

That Hallowe'en, the acoustic balance of the hills shattered. Sentinel Hill burned, but the villagers were captivated by the unnaturally belated flocks of whippoorwills, whose pandemoniac cachinnation around the unlighted Whateley house drowned out all other sounds until dawn. Following this terrifying sonic harvest, the birds fled southward, fully a month overdue. What this meant remained unspoken until the realization settled over the village: no townsfolk had died, but poor Lavinia Whateley, the twisted albino, was never seen again—consumed, it seemed, in a dark, domestic satiation.

By the summer of 1927, Wilbur undertook the final preparation of his environment. He repaired two outbuildings, shifting his meticulously sourced texts and historic tools out of the house. Earl Sawyer soon reported the heavy, rhythmic thud of carpentry echoing from the Whateley farm. Wilbur was sealing every ground-floor door and window, hollowing out the lower partitions just as he and his grandfather had gutted the upper floor four years prior. Living entirely in a shed, smelling of ancient dust and nervous sweat, Wilbur appeared tremulous. The village, quietly recognizing the grim realities of his mother’s disappearance, avoided him entirely as his stature stretched beyond seven feet, driven by the ceaseless, interwoven timelines of growth and an unyielding, cosmic hunger.
