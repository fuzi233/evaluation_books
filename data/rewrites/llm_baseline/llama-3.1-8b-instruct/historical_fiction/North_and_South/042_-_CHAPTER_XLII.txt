As the stillness enveloped her, Margaret's eyes remained shut, her breathing shallow and labored. The sudden loss had left her prostrate, unable to summon the strength to speak or even shed tears. Her slender frame lay limp on the sofa, a testament to the overwhelming grief that had consumed her. Mr. Bell sat beside her, his eyes fixed on her pale face, his mind racing with thoughts of what to do next. He dared not leave her, nor could he persuade her to join him on the journey back to Oxford, where they had planned to return. Her exhaustion was too pronounced, and the thought of facing the reality of their situation was too daunting.

The savory aroma of dinner wafted through the room, but Mr. Bell's stomach churned with unease. He couldn't muster the appetite to eat the deviled chicken, which tasted like sawdust on his tongue. He knew better than to try to tempt Margaret with food, so he instructed Dixon to prepare a light broth instead. When she attempted to feed Margaret, the girl's weak shake of the head indicated that even the gentle offering would only cause discomfort.

Mr. Bell rose from the sofa, his sturdy frame creaking with stiffness from the long journey. He followed Dixon out of the room, his eyes never leaving Margaret's still form. "I must write to Oxford," he said, his voice low and resolute. "They can continue preparations without me. Perhaps Mrs. Lennox can come to visit her?" He scribbled a hasty note, his hand moving swiftly across the paper as he asked Mrs. Lennox to come and comfort Margaret.

Dixon's voice trembled as she delivered the news that Mrs. Lennox was too close to her own confinement to travel. Mr. Bell's expression turned stern, his eyes flashing with determination. "Then we must send for Mrs. Shaw," he declared. "She's returned to England, hasn't she?" Dixon nodded, her eyes welling up with tears. "But, sir, perhaps she won't want to leave Mrs. Lennox at such a delicate time."

Mr. Bell's face darkened, his voice taking on a hint of acidity. "Delicate time? Ha! That woman has experienced far more delicate times than this. I'll not have Margaret left alone, friendless and helpless, like a statue on an altar. Mrs. Shaw will come, and she will come soon." With that, he dispatched a letter, his words firm and commanding.
