The grey, biting twilight had just settled over Florence when the pair of Compagnacci set out on their errand, and on the far opposite bank of the Arno, another ragged figure slipped out of hiding to wander down to the river’s edge. His purpose seemed entirely unconnected to the armed men’s: he made his way to a stretch of shore tucked within the city walls, overlooked by no cottage windows, only bordered by tall warehouses and granaries that turned their blank, forbidding backs to the water a short distance inland. Sloping down to the current was a wide stretch of long, dank grass and rushes, made all the soggier by open gutters that emptied their refuse steadily into the river.

That loneliness and those trickling gutters were exactly what drew Baldassarre to hunker down in the reeds, bending to watch the thin runlets rush down the sloped bank beside him. More than once before, those friendly streams had carried him a full loaf of discarded bread, a raw carrot, even scraps of apple parings. It was worth waiting for such unlooked-for charity where no one could see him—he often snuck out here long before dawn, when restlessness would not let him lie still, to save himself from the humiliating silent begging that meant sitting motionless on a church step just outside the Porta San Frediano.

Baldassarre hated begging so fiercely he would likely have chosen death over that quiet appeal, if not for one single reason that made him cling to life. It was no longer a clear, defined hope, only that stubborn, unshakable possibility that clings to any idea that has entirely possessed a man’s mind: the same irrational certainty that makes a widow stand for years on a headland watching for a ship all her neighbors know sank decades before.

After the San Miniato monks had nursed him back from the edge of death in their convent hospital, after he had searched in vain for Romola and convinced himself she must have perished in the great pestilence that had blotted out all memory of the night he and Tito had parted, he had been unable to find any way to exact the sacred vengeance that burned at the core of him. His knife was long gone, his body too broken and weak to earn another through work, his mind too fogged and bewildered even to plot a course if he had possessed a blade. He was a shattered, lonely old man, and yet he could not bear to die. He waited for something he could not name, something dim and formless that stirred a wild, sharp thrum in his chest, the same unaccountable restlessness that rouses us from deep sleep even when no sound or touch has disturbed us. So he crept out in the grey half-light, settled in the long grass, and watched the water, faint as a half-remembered promise.

Meanwhile, the Compagnacci went about their work. The great armed bands the Signoria was too fearful (if not openly complicit) to restrain had split into two groups, both making their way toward the Arno by separate routes. The smaller band headed for the Ponte Rubaconte, the larger for the Ponte Vecchio, the same whispered signal passing from mouth to mouth through every throng: every last man knew they were bound for the Via de’ Bardi to sack a house there. What better reason could a mob demand? The leaders of both factions had made perfect plans to converge on a palazzo near the eastern end of the street, where the master of the house would still be fast asleep, caught unawares at dawn.

But that master, Tito Melema, was neither abed nor asleep. He had not rested a wink all night. The chaos of the previous day had spurred his desperate urge to flee Florence; he knew official investigations would follow that might summon him for questioning and delay his departure, and in every moment of delay he sensed a cold, sharp danger. His web of lies had grown thick and strong over the years, but it had spawned an equal, twin life: Fear. He no longer wore armor, no longer cowered from Baldassarre, but the corpse of that old fear had birthed an undying habit of dread. He knew he would not be truly safe until he was far away from this fierce, roiling city, and every last detail of his escape was arranged. Maso was to hand the palazzo over to its new tenant that very day; his horses and mules waited stabled outside the San Gallo gate; Tessa and their children had been lodged overnight in the Borgo just beyond the walls, already dressed and ready to mount their mules and ride away with him. He stepped down the stone steps into his courtyard, out through the great oak doorway, nearly as bright and easy in his bearing as he had been the day he first walked into that house and made the foolhardy choice to marry Romola. That old mistake was done now. His old life was cast off, soon to vanish far behind him.

He turned toward the Piazza dei Mozzi, meaning to cross the quieter Ponte Rubaconte—until a roar of distant sound drifted to his ears, and he spun on his heel to hurry the opposite way. The mob was pouring into the Oltrarno district, it seemed. A nuisance. He would have to take the busier Ponte Vecchio instead. He pulled his mantle tight around his shoulders and broke into a run, thankful no one was abroad to spot him in the dim twilight. But before he had reached the far end of the Via de’ Bardi, those same sounds hit his ears again, far louder and much closer. Could he have misheard before? The mob must be coming across the Ponte Vecchio, too. A surge of blind, unreasoning fear made him turn again—only to see the throng was already entering the street from the end he had just fled from.

He decided not to run back to his house. Surely they would not attack *him*. But he carried valuables on his person, and reason and order never hold sway over a mob. Necessity lent him the illusion of courage, and he pushed onward toward the Ponte Vecchio, the roar of trampling feet and shouting voices swelling so loud ahead that he stopped hearing any sound at his back. The crowd pouring off the bridge met him at the street’s corner and hemmed him in entirely.

He had no time to wonder at a sudden scream before armed Compagnacci surrounded him. His cap was knocked from his head, and he was shoved violently forward into the rabble, jostled down the narrow passageway of the bridge. The shouts rang clear a second later: “Piagnone! Medicean traitor! Piagnone! Throw him over the rail!” They tore at his mantle, the pressure near throttling him until the iron fibula clasping the fabric snapped clean in two. A hand snatched at his scarsella, the leather pouch at his belt, but the crowd dragged him onward before they could tear it free. Half-moteless, rabid execration thundered in his ears, spreading even to men at the back of the throng who had not even seen him, who only knew there was a man to revile. Tito’s most horrifying fear was that he would be beaten or trampled to death before they could hurl him over the bridge arches. His only flickering hope was that they would throw him over before they could break his strength, and his entire soul narrowed to that single desperate wish, and the terror of the opposite.

They were at the central arches now. In that moment of utmost extremity, a spark of sharp self-preservation flared in his brain. With a wild, heaving effort, he ripped the clasp of his belt clean open, and hurled the heavy belt and its bulging scarsella out toward the only empty patch of space against the bridge parapet, screaming at the top of his voice: “Diamonds! Gold! There is treasure here!”

The hands clutching him slackened instantly, as the whole crowd surged forward to grab for the flying pouch. Tito threw himself at the parapet in a desperate leap, and the next second he had plunged, hitting the dark Arno far below with a great echoing splash.

It was his chance, and it was a good one. His powerful swimming had saved his life once before, when he escaped the galley years earlier, and as he broke the surface after a long dive, he felt a surge of deliverance. He struck out with every ounce of the strength of his prime, the current sweeping him swiftly along. If he could only swim past the Ponte alla Carrara, he could land in a remote, unguarded stretch of the city shore and still make his escape to San Gallo. Life was still his for the taking. The screaming, bellowing idiots on the bridge would think he had drowned.

They did. They leaned over the parapet, staring down into the dark, rippling water, but they could not spot the moving black blur of his floating hair, or the heavy velvet of his tunic sleeves bobbing below the surface. Only from the riverbank could his pale olive face be seen, bleached white above the dark current, its square forehead, long low arch of eyebrows, and lustrous agate eyes unmistakeable even to a stranger. His nostrils flared and quivered, the blue veins on his temples standing out as he was carried onward. He passed the Ponte Santa Trinita. Should he risk landing now, instead of pushing his exhausted strength further? No. He thought he could still hear the yells of the mob pursuing him. He feared his fellow men far more than the unknown risks of the river, so he swam on, panting and straining. He was far less fresh than he would have been if he had slept the night through. He passed the last bridge, his muddled mind only barely registering that he had cleared it, that he might soon be safe to land. But he did not know where the current was carrying him. Exhaustion was creeping over him in the hazy, dreamlike daze that precedes unconsciousness.

But there were eyes watching him, aged eyes sharp to spot small things at a distance. Baldassarre, who had been staring blankly at his trickling runlet after finding no scraps in it that morning, lifted his head and spotted a pale white bobbing shape far down the broader stream. Could this be the lucky chance he had been waiting for? He stared and stared until the shape resolved, and he lurched forward so hard the reeds rustled around him, a wild new light flooding his eyes. He did not move, only watched. Something was being brought to him.

The next instant, the current hurled Tito’s limp body hard onto the grass barely two yards from where he sat. Baldassarre launched himself forward like a starved panther, clutching the shoulder of Tito’s velvet tunic as he fell on top of him, his eyes flashing down into the man’s face.

Dead? Was he dead? Tito’s eyes were rigid and glazed. No, that could not be. Justice would not cheat him like that. Men often looked dead when they had only fainted, and life would return to them. In that moment, Baldassarre felt no trace of his old feebleness. He knew exactly what he must do. He slipped his large, calloused fingers inside the neck of Tito’s tunic, held fast, and knelt on one knee beside the body, never taking his eyes from his face. A fierce, trembling hope blazed in his chest; every last spark of the slow-burning life left in him had leaped into flame.

Rigid. Still rigid. Those half-lidded eyes were locked shut against his vengeance. Could he really be dead? There was no way to mark the passage of time, and it stretched on so long that hope began to freeze into despair. At last, the eyelids quivered. The eyes were no longer glassy. A flicker of living light danced in them, and they flew wide open.

“Ah! Yes. You see me. You know me.”

Tito recognized him instantly. He could not tell if this was life or death, if the chill gloom surrounding him was some hell where the face of the hideous past hung over him for all eternity. But Baldassarre had no fear now but one: that the strong young man beneath him would somehow slip away. He pressed his knuckles hard against Tito’s round throat, driving all the weight of his aged frame down onto the younger man’s chest. Death could come now, and welcome.

He never took his eyes off Tito’s face. Even when the eyes went rigid and glazed a second time, he did not trust the stillness. He would never loosen his grip until someone came to find them, a witness for justice. Then he would tell the world that he had killed the traitor he had once raised like a son. They would believe him now, at last. Then he would be content, let justice work its work on earth, and die still clutching the body, to follow the traitor straight to hell and hold fast to him there.

So he knelt, and so he pressed his knuckles to Tito’s throat, never trusting the apparent stillness, until the sun climbed high and he could kneel no more. Then he sat on the body, his grip still tight on the neck of the tunic. The hours dragged by, and no witness came. No one spotted the two figures half hidden in the tall riverside grass. Florence was busy with far greater affairs that day, and the preparation of a far deeper tragedy. Not long after the two bodies lay still in the reeds, Savonarola was strapped to the rack in the Bargello, screaming in his agony: “I will confess!”

It was not until the sun hung low in the western sky that a peasant leading a gentle grey ox wandered to the grassy bank, there to gather the heap of paving stones he had left ready to cart away. Leaning down to scoop the stones up, he spotted the strange shape half hidden in the rushes. The old man had fallen forward, his dead hands still locked fast around the other man’s tunic. It was impossible to pry them apart. The peasant decided it would be better to load both bodies onto his waggon as they were, and carry them into the main Piazza to alert the Eight.

As the waggon trundled through the crowded streets, a growing crowd gathered to follow it, drawn by the strange, silent burden. No one recognized the bodies for a long while, for the old man’s face had fallen forward, half hiding the younger man’s features. But before they had been carried far, they were unmistakeable.

“I know that old man,” Piero di Cosimo testified, pushing through the crowd. “I painted his likeness once. He is the prisoner who clutched Melema on the steps of the Duomo all those years ago.”

“It is likely the same old man who turned up uninvited at supper in my gardens once,” said Bernardo Rucellai, one of the Eight. “I had forgotten him. I thought he died in prison long ago. There is no way to untangle the full truth now.”

Who can point a finger to any single act of men and say, “There—there is perfect, plain justice, made real for all to see”? Justice is like the Kingdom of God. It does not exist out in the world as a neat, tangible fact we can lay hands on. It lives within us, as a great, burning, unquenchable yearning.
