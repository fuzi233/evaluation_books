These days, East India Dock Road is nonstop chaos: tram bells jingle nonstop, omnibuses rattle so hard their rivets look ready to pop, and thousands of feet pound the pavement at the same time, like the whole city is late for a very important appointment. Back when I was a kid, though? It was practically a ghost street. On one side, there was the echoing miles-long dock wall, and on the other, random tiny houses dotted between vegetable patches, laundry lines strung between trees, and piles of rubbish that looked like they might have been there since the Romans popped by for a visit. Only one little leftover from those days still stands (or did, last time I wandered past): the single-story brick cottage right at the start of the bridge, the old toll-house. I remember that rickety little building like it was yesterday, because that’s exactly where my childhood fell right off my shoulders, leaving me sad, jittery, and staring at the big scary world like it had just shown up uninvited to my tea party.

I can’t explain it any clearer than that. That afternoon I’d trundled all the way to Plaistow to visit our family dentist. The guy set up shop in the middle of nowhere for a very good reason: the half-crown my mom sent me with never actually left my pocket.

“Hold that half-crown out in your hand the second you get in the door,” Mom would tell me, double-checking that the coin purse it was in was tucked all the way at the bottom of my knickerbockers so I couldn’t lose it, “but if he won’t take it? Bring it right back home, no questions asked.”

I think he was some distant cousin, if not exactly related, definitely the kindest man with a sharp pointy tool ever to exist in human history. I’d plop down in his fancy velvet dentist chair, he’d unroll his case of instruments and gush over the most brutal-looking one like it was a prize-winning puppy, begging me to pick my favorite. But the second I opened my mouth to say “uh, maybe the little one that doesn’t look like it could yank a tree root out?” a pair of pliers would zip out from under his sleeve and before I could even say “wait, hold on!” the bad tooth was out, no fuss no pain. Then we’d have tea.

He was an old bachelor, his house surrounded by a huge overgrown garden (Plaistow back then was a proper little country village, not the crowded mess it is now), and his housekeeper made jam and jelly out of all that fruit that tasted like pure heaven. Those teas were legendary. Half the time he’d tell me stories about Mom, back when she was a tiny kid — nothing at all like the sensible, no-nonsense mom I knew! A chaotic, prank-loving little gremlin, by the sound of him. I’d repeat all the tales back to Mom later, and she’d laugh and say she had no memory of any such nonsense, and scold me for gossiping about grown-ups, then immediately lean in and go “…wait, what else did he say about me, specifically?”

Tea done, he’d bring out his giant microscope, and it was like peeking through a door straight into fairyland. Pond dragons, tiny slimy monsters the size of a pinhead, all wiggling around. I decided he was a totally harmless magician, no questions asked. Looking back, I always figured his obsession with tiny tiny things made perfect sense, because he was a very little man, with the biggest heart that ever fit into a human chest.

I’d hand him the half-crown on my way out, “with mamma’s compliments”, he’d take it very formally, and the second I stepped outside his gate? I’d dig in my jacket pocket and there that exact same half-crown would be, grinning back at me like it had snuck out just to hang out. The first time I brought it back to him, and he looked at me with a perfectly straight face and said “Must be a different half-crown! Those things are slippery little rascals, they wander right back into pockets all the time. Happens every day.”

On that fateful day I was trundling home, I stopped on the bridge for a minute to watch the lazy barges drift slow between the piers. It was one of those quiet summer evenings where even the gross, grimy city air feels full of soft little whispers, and the second I walked through that white toll-gate? I felt like I’d left the little 10-year-old version of me leaning on the bridge railing, staring at the sun on the water. The feeling was so strong I actually turned around to check, half expecting to see him still there. It sounds daft, I know, but I swear that little kid never came back. He was gone, like he’d wandered off somewhere and forgot to return for his body.

I tried for weeks to play his games, dream his silly little dreams, but none of it felt real anymore. I was just a tall, thin ghost pretending to be a kid, and it made me sad enough to cry. I didn’t want to grow up! Could no one put a stop to this? I’d much rather stay small forever, running around the streets with my broomstick horse, not heading down this weird dark road no one would tell me anything about. Did I have to go forward? Slowly, over months and years, a new kind of me grew in his place, all these new silly feelings and weird daydreams, and that little Paul faded out of my life for good. I have to let him fade out of this book too, before he sticks around and messes up all the stories later. Before I say goodbye to him for good, though, let me get the rest of his best memories down on paper, so we can be properly quit of him.

First up, Aunt Fan. She’d be crouched over the kitchen fire, her skirt and crinoline rolled all the way up to her waist, leaving only her petticoat on for decency’s sake, swaying back and forth like a metronome while she stroked her own knees affectionately. I’d stand opposite her, waving a paper knife like a sword or riding a broomstick steed, yelling dramatic lines at the top of my lungs. Sometimes I was a knight and she was a wicked ogre, so she’d growl and curse dramatically, get “killed”, and immediately turn into the beautiful princess I was supposed to rescue. The rescuing part was always make-believe, though, because Aunt Fan would never get up out of that chair by the fire, she was way too busy rubbing her knees.

Other times, I’d grab the meat chopper and declare myself an Indian brave, so she’d be Laughing Water or Singing Sunshine, and we’d go off “scalping” the dinner potatoes together. When I was the Fairy Prince, though, she made a terrible Sleeping Beauty — that role was way too boring for her. Her absolute best role was Cannibal Chief, when I was Captain Cook sitting on top of an upturned table, and she’d smack her lips and go “I’ll skin him, hang him in the larder for a week, then he’ll be perfect — not too tough, not too gamey.” Aunt Fan was always incredible at adding the tiny, ridiculous details no one else thought of.

Don’t get me wrong, Aunt Fan wasn’t just humoring me to be nice. No, she was living for this stuff. Half the time she’d make up the game and the plot all by herself, and immediately cast herself as the heavy: dragons, wicked uncles, fussy wizards, random annoying fairies. She could have written a best-selling ogre cookbook, no contest. All the boring fairy tales just say “boil the prince, roast the princess for supper, stick them in a pie” — Aunt Fan invented whole new recipes. “We’ll stuff him with chestnuts, serve him on toast,” she’d direct me, “don’t forget the giblets, they make the best sauce.” For imprisoned maidens? “Don’t feed them too much fish, it ruins the taste when you roast them later.”

The stuff she’d turn people into! Kings’ sons, rightful princesses, she’d turn them into a glyptodon or a hippocrepian without a second thought, and later when I looked those weird ancient creatures up in the illustrated dictionary, I realized I’d never be able to kiss the transformed princess ever again, no matter what the fairy tale said. Warriors turned into plaice, queens into Brussels sprouts. She’d plan out every last detail of the big battle: sharpen the knives, get the mops and buckets ready to clean up the mess afterwards. Total realist, that woman.

Her dramatic death scene at the end of every afternoon was a whole production, groaning and writhing so hard it sometimes scared me. I’d cheer out loud when she did her final death gurgle, and only then would she let her skirt fall back down, like she was dropping the theatre curtain, and stand up to start making the tea.

Another memory: me, in my little glazed peaked school cap, strutting through the grimy streets explaining absolutely everything to a tiny curly-haired girl who stared up at me like I was the smartest person who ever lived. Every so often she’d run ahead, turn around, and look at me like I was a hero fresh off a horse, ever since the day she decided I was the most famous, impressive kid in Poplar.

I loved having her around, for way more reasons than she knew. First off, she stopped me from being a coward. With her next to me, I couldn’t run away from a random stray dog or a gang of older kids, we’d both go looking for adventure together — the constant chance of getting attacked makes walking way more fun than it is for boring grown-ups, after all. But that trusting little face next to me made me feel 10 feet tall, and in the quiet dark lanes I’d even let her hold my hand, which was peak kid chivalry back then.

The other perk? No one would shove me into the gutter when she was around. She had a vocabulary on her that could make a sailor blush. Any idiot who tried to walk straight through us like we weren’t there (everyone did that back then, no one bothered stepping around kids) would get a full earful. “Oi, glass-eye! Where d’you think you’re going? We’re not ghosts, can’t you see us?” If they tried to argue back, her tiny shrill voice would only get louder: “Garn, they ran out of heads when they made you! That thing on top of your shoulders is just a turnip!” No one ever dared smack her for it, it’d be like yelling at a terrier for barking, it just made the whole street laugh at them. Half the time I was the one staring at her like she was the hero, not the other way around. She’d wait for hours for me outside my house, her little face pressed to the iron railings, until I came out or waved no from my bedroom window, then she’d run off, the sound of her little feet pattering away making me feel sad for no reason.

I sort of liked her, but she never made it into my daydreams. That meant she was only a real-world friend, not a princess from my head. And honestly? Her dad ran a fried fish shop. I searched every fairy tale I owned, and not a single one had a princess whose dad sold battered cod out of a shopfront. If he’d been a wood chopper? Easy, that’s classic fairy tale stuff, she was clearly a lost royal baby the fairies accidentally dropped off there. Even a pork butcher? You could rebrand that as a swineherd, no problem. But fried fishmonger? There was no precedent. Our meetings got fewer and fewer, and once the glorious Barbara showed up (the girl with golden hair I’m getting to soon) I stopped hanging out with her entirely. I still see her in my head, though: that tiny little girl who thought I hung the moon. Some quiet summer afternoon soon I’m gonna walk into that little shop where she works now, go say hi. I traced her ages ago, I know she’s a mom with a whole bunch of kids, she’s gotten stout, and her tongue is even sharper than it was back then. But I know under all that grown-up stuff, I’ll still see that little curly-haired maid staring up at me like I’m a hero. It took me years to realize how rare it is to find someone who thinks you’re amazing just for existing. If we all wrote the real truth in our diaries, wouldn’t we admit our favorite thing about other people is how much they admire us? If you want me to be a good person? Tell me how great I am. Praise is sunlight, indifference makes you boring, and contempt turns you into garbage. That’s the truth, no one will admit it out loud.

Then there were all the “general” maids that cycled through our house. Back then, any woman who actually wanted to be a proper servant moved out to the rich west end, so the local maids were all girls running away from bad breakups, or mad at their families, or heartbroken and looking for a quiet place to hide instead of joining a convent. They kept quitting the second they found a new boyfriend or patched things up with the old one, so my poor mom was forever running around trying to find a new one. I was friends with all of them, since I had no other kid friends. Most of them weren’t very good at make-believe, but they’d always squeeze 10 minutes out of their mopping schedule to run around and play with me, which was way better for me than any fancy fantasy game. The bad part? They were absolutely blown away by how much random book learning I had, so they all thought I was some sort of tiny child prophet, and they’d listen to me ramble for hours, wide-eyed. Sometimes they’d ask Mom if they could bring me home to tea with them, just so all their friends could hear me talk. I’d stand on their kitchen table and hold forth, loving the sound of my own voice, and everyone would go “ain’t he as good as a whole book?” One old greengrocer even said I was “as good as a play”. I was totally convinced I was one of the immortals at 10 years old.

One girl named Janet stayed with us for months, and would have stayed forever, if she hadn’t sworn like a barge sailor. She was the only daughter of the captain of the barge Nancy Jane, that ran between Purfleet and Ponder’s End, and her conversation was equal parts terrifying and hilarious. “Janet!” Mom would yell, clapping her hands over her ears, “how can you say such terrible words?” “What words, mum?” “The things you just called the gas man!” “Him! Did you see him walk straight into my clean kitchen without wiping his boots? The — “ and before Mom could stop her, Janet would yell the whole curse again, completely confused why Mom was panicking.

We were best mates, so I took it upon myself to reform her. I told her straight up, blunt as anything, that if she kept swearing she’d go straight to hell. “Then where’s my dad going?” she asked immediately. I asked her if her dad swore. Turns out her dad made Janet’s swearing look like a nursery rhyme. “It’s the only way he knows how to talk!” she said. “He doesn’t mean anything by it.” I sighed, stuck to my guns, told her all swearers go to hell. But Janet wasn’t having any of it. “God send my sweet old dad to hell just cause he can’t talk like a prissy posh gentleman? Don’t you believe it, Master Paul. He’s got more sense than that.”

Those words stuck with me forever. Back then I was going through a whole phase of being absolutely terrified of hell, you see. All the stupid children’s books they gave you back then showed pictures of devils shoveling burning coals onto little sinners, who screamed forever. I’d burned my hand once pretty bad when a shovel of hot coals tipped on me, so I knew exactly what that felt like — but forever? A thousand years of burning, a million years of burning, and you still weren’t even a tiny bit closer to it stopping. I couldn’t sleep for weeks. The grown-ups all kept telling me “just don’t do bad things! Just repent! Just believe!” but it was so easy to do bad things, and no one could explain to me how to properly repent, or how to make sure you actually believed, or what the unforgivable sin even was. I was too scared to ask, because I was 100% sure I’d accidentally committed it when I snuck an extra biscuit last Tuesday. I’d kneel in the dark for hours, whispering over and over “I believe! I believe! I do believe!” then stand up, my knuckles white, wondering if I’d done it right. But whenever I got stuck thinking about all that nonsense, Janet’s voice would pop back into my head: “God’s got more sense than that.” And she was right.
