The sun crested the horizon, painting the sky in hues of bruised purple and burning gold. I climbed to the ridge once more, peering back into the hollow I had traversed under the silver moon. It was empty now, a void scrubbed clean of all life. A cold dread settled in my stomach: did the vast, shimmering plain ahead teem with ravenous things, invisible to the naked eye?

I turned my gaze forward, scanning the landscape that lay between me and the distant, darker patch I had identified as a forest. It stretched out like a desolate desert, barren and vast. There was no smoke, no dust clouds, no sign of cultivation—only the endless, clear blue of a cloudless sky. The air was so perfectly still it felt heavy, pressing against my ears.

I descended from the ridge and pushed onward toward that imagined green sanctuary. If there was any sign of life, it had to be within those trees; this arid wasteland could not possibly harbor anything.

By the time I reached the edge of the plain, my vision confirmed what I had feared: a wide, ancient riverbed, now a graveyard of stone. The ground was a chaotic mosaic of flat, channeled rock and jagged, pinnacled spires, carved by the relentless flow of waters long gone. Innumerable dry runs scored the earth, devoid of moisture. Here and there, a patch of dry moss clung to a channel, or a lichen, hard as iron, stained a grey rock. The silence was absolute, a suffocating quiet that felt less like peace and more like the breath held before a scream.

It took the entire day to reach the forest, yet not a single trickle of water had I crossed. Even as the sun hammered down, baking the stones to white heat, I was haunted by an auditory hallucination. I could hear it so clearly—the roar of a thousand waters, the rush of a mighty river—that I struggled to reconcile the sound with the parched reality before my eyes.

As the sun began its descent, I left the silent riverbed and stepped into the woods. The canopy blocked the direct rays, casting the forest floor in a tapestry of blessed, dappled shadows. I had expected a familiar pine grove, but this was a chaotic garden of strange flora. Some trees bore a haunting resemblance to Earth's giants, while others defied description entirely. I collapsed beneath the sprawling boughs of a tree that looked like a blooming eucalyptus, though its flowers were grotesque. Each bloom possessed a hard, skull-like calyx, the top lifting like a lid to reveal a frothing, brain-like interior. From the shelter of its falchion-shaped leaves, I gazed deeper into the gloom.

Soon, the forest began to close in. The aisles narrowed, the glades vanished, and the night descended with terrifying speed. The cold was instant and biting. How do I convey the wild, spectral chill of this place to you?

The great tree I leaned against rose high before branching, yet its limbs drooped low, threatening to cage me in as I rested against its smooth trunk. My eyes, drifting in a listless stare, watched the foliage begin to shift. Under the fading twilight, the clumps of leaves started to suggest shapes other than their own. A light wind stirred, rocking a neighboring tree. Its branches swayed, every twig and leaf syncing in a rhythmic dance. Among the shifting greens, I saw a pack of wolves, straining desperately against an invisible wizard's leash. They were not merely mimicking movement; they were fighting with a savage intensity that greyhounds could not match.

My interest sharpened as the wind grew stronger. Another mass of foliage, denser and more compact, resolved into a group of horses' heads and forequarters, caparisoned in their stalls. Their necks bobbed up and down with impatience, a rhythm broken by the wilder, side-to-side sway of the wind. What strange, gaunt heads they were! Several were bare skulls; one had skin pulled tight over its bones, another had lost its lower jaw, hanging low in unutterable weariness, yet occasionally jerking upward as if trying to bite the air. Above them, floating on the very tip of a branch, stood the form of a woman, waving her arms in an imperious, commanding gesture.

The clarity of these visions first startled me, then unsettled me. What if my mind could not withstand the illusion of reality? But the twilight dissolved into darkness, the wind died away, and the shapes were swallowed by the night. I fell asleep.

I awoke to a far-off, confused rushing, mingled with faint, human cries. The sound grew, swelling into a tumult of gathering multitudes that seemed to fill the entire wood. I froze, not daring to move a muscle lest I betray my presence to hostile entities.

Slowly, the moon crested the tree line and spilled her pale light into the forest. With her first gleam, the noises erupted into a deafening uproar, and dim shapes materialized around me. As the moon climbed higher, the chaos sharpened into a furious battle raging inches from my hiding spot. Wild cries, roars of rage, the shock of collision, and prolonged struggles surged in my ears, interwoven with articulate words. Curses, creeds, snarls, sneers, laughter, mockery, sacred names, and howls of hate collided in a chaotic maelstrom.

Skeletons and phantoms fought in a mad confusion. Swords swept through the ghosts, causing them only to shiver; maces crashed upon the skeletons, shattering them hideously, yet not one ceased to fight as long as a single joint held two bones together. Bones of men and horses lay scattered and heaped, ground underfoot by the combatants. Everywhere, the bone-gaunt white steeds charged; everywhere, on foot or riding misty battle-horses, the indestructible spectres raged and ravened. Weapons and hoofs clashed and crushed, while skeleton jaws and phantom throats swelled the deafening tumult with the war-cry of every opinion, good or bad, that had ever bred strife, injustice, or cruelty. The holiest words accompanied the most hateful blows. Distorted truths flew hurtling through the wind of javelins and bones.

Moments passed where a warrior would turn against his comrades, fighting more wildly than before, shouting, "THE TRUTH! THE TRUTH!" One figure I noted wheeled in a perpetual circle, striking on all sides. Occasionally, a weary pair would sit side by side for a minute before rising to renew the fierce combat. No one stooped to comfort the fallen; no one stepped wide to spare him.

The moon shone until the sun rose, and all night long I watched a woman move at her will above the strife-tormented multitude. She appeared on this front, then that, one arm outstretched urging the fight, the other pressed against her side. "Ye are men: slay one another!" she shouted. I saw her dead, glassy eyes and the dark spot upon her brow, and my memory returned to the vision of the previous night.

This was the battle of the dead, witnessed as I lay hidden beneath the tree.

Just before sunrise, a breeze swept through the forest, carrying a voice that cried out, "Let the dead bury their dead!" At the word, the contending thousands dropped noiselessly. When the sun finally looked in, it saw no bone, but only here and there a withered branch.

I rose and resumed my journey through a wood as quiet as any that grows from the quiet earth. The morning wind had ceased, and the trees were silent. No bird sang, no squirrel, mouse, or weasel showed itself, and not a single moth flew athwart my path. Yet, I kept a vigilant watch over myself, refusing to let my eyes rest on any forest shape. All the time, faint sounds of mattocks and spades and hurling bones echoed in my mind. Any moment, my eyes might open to things I would not see. Prudence whispered that perhaps ten thousand phantoms waited only for my consenting fancy to manifest them.

In the middle of the afternoon, I emerged from the wood to find myself before a second net of dry water-courses. I initially thought I had wandered from my intended line and reversed direction, but soon realized I had not. I concluded I had simply reached another branch of the same ancient riverbed. I began to cross it immediately, finding myself in the bottom of a wide channel just as the sun set.

I sat down to await the moon, and as sleepiness overtook me, I stretched out on the moss. The moment my head touched the ground, I heard the sounds of rushing streams—all sorts of sweet, watery noises. The veiled melody of molten music sang me into a dreamless sleep. When I awoke, the sun was already up, illuminating the wrinkled country in a wide, stark visibility. Covered in shadows, the land lay striped and mottled like the skin of a wild beast. As the sun rose higher, the shadows diminished, as if the rocks were re-absorbing the darkness that had oozed from them during the night.

Until now, I had loved my Arab mare and my books more than any living man or woman. Now, at last, my soul was athirst for human presence. I longed even for those inhabitants of this alien world whom the raven had so vaguely described as nearest to my own kind. With a heavy yet hopeful heart, and my mind haunted by a doubt as to whether I was traveling in any direction at all, I kept wearily moving "north-west and by south."
