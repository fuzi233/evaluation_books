The new pope, favoring the Dominican order, summoned a capable friar to teach at the University of Basle. Clement, versed in languages and once a pupil of Guarini the younger, was chosen. His journey to England delayed, he set out for Basle, preaching along the way.

Barefoot, eyes lowered, his soul wrapped in contemplation, Clement walked a path marked by contrasts—love, despair, impiety, remorse, and now, resignation. The world that had once threatened him now bowed before his piety. Thieves pressed stolen coins into his hands, begging prayers. Yet this pilgrimage, though safe, was not without its trials—not of the body, but of the spirit.

On the first Sunday of his journey, he did what he had never dared before: he prayed for Margaret’s soul. For months, religious despair had gripped him, convinced his sins were beyond redemption. But now, a fragile peace settled upon him. He no longer felt unworthy to intercede for her. And so, daily, he whispered her name in his prayers, the last earthly thread binding him to the past.

The road brought encounters that tested his spirit. In one village, he found a young man convulsing from a tarantula’s bite—or perhaps the fear of one. The musicians’ frantic tunes only worsened his agony. Clement, recalling the healing power of music, plucked a psaltery and played until the man’s own whispered melody emerged. Learning the strange tune, Clement played it tirelessly until the sufferer slept, his life spared.

Elsewhere, he intervened in a dispute over a mulberry branch. An Englishman, ignorant of the tree’s sacred status in Italy, had plucked leaves to shield himself from the sun. The podesta, enraged, would have imprisoned him, but Clement brokered peace, explaining the foreigner’s ignorance. Grateful, the Kentish squire—Rolfe of Betshanger—offered boundless hospitality. "Come to England," he urged, pressing a purse into Clement’s hands. "We sit forty to dinner daily. What’s one more?"

Their exchange grew deeper. Rolfe confessed, lamenting his neglect of faith abroad. Clement, in turn, revealed his mission to England. "But how shall I learn your tongue?" he asked. Rolfe, ever practical, bartered his book of hours—Latin and English side by side—for Clement’s prayers. "Not a bargain," the friar corrected gently. "A gift."

Days later, Clement met two Germans laboring under a broken-down cart—a printing press in tow. At his call, "Sweynheim! Pannartz!" they fled, fearing witchcraft. But reason prevailed. They were Fust’s disciples, their craft the very wings of knowledge. Clement, once an enemy of the press, now blessed it. "Print no ill books," he warned. "They would fly like locusts, laying waste souls."

In Tuscany, a masked lady knelt to wash his feet, her penance for a crime—love twisted to murderous intent. She confessed in whispers: a youthful passion, rejection, a hired blade. "He never returned. I sent him to his grave."

Clement listened, then spoke softly. "The man you sought to kill lives."

She trembled. "Lives?"

"Gerard, the Hollander. He survived—not by your mercy, but by Heaven’s. He is now a friar, dead to the world you knew."

Recognition struck. She lifted her head, staring through the mask—then gasped. The penitent collapsed at his feet, her guilt and redemption crashing together in silence.

Thus Clement journeyed, his path crossing souls in need, each encounter a thread in the tapestry of his own redemption. The road to Basle was long, but every step carried him further from the man he had been, nearer to the servant he had vowed to become.
