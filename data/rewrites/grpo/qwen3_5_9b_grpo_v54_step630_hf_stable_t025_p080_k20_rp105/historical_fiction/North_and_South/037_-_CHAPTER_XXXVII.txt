A pickaxe or a bill!
A hook to reap, or a scythe to mow,
A flail or what ye will—
And here's a ready hand
To ply the needful tool,
And skill'd enough, by lessons rough,
In Labour's rugged school."

The following day, Higgins's door remained barred when Margaret and her father arrived to pay their respects to the widow Boucher. An officious neighbor informed them that Higgins had departed for his daily labor before visiting the widow, though he had made the attempt. The visit proved deeply unsatisfying. Mrs. Boucher viewed herself as a victim of her husband's suicide, a sentiment rooted in enough truth to make refutation nearly impossible. Yet, her grief was entirely self-absorbed, extending even to her children, whom she regarded as burdens despite her animalistic affection for them. Margaret attempted to connect with the children, while Mr. Hale tried to elevate the widow's thoughts beyond her helpless querulousness. He discovered that the children were far more sincere mourners than their mother; each could stammer eagerly of specific acts of kindness and indulgence from their lost father.

"It is that thing upstairs, isn't it? It doesn't look like him. I'm frightened, and I never was frightened of Daddy."

Margaret's heart ached to hear that the mother, in her desperate need for sympathy, had dragged her children upstairs to view their dead, disfigured father. She felt it was a cruel mingling of coarse horror with profound natural grief. She tried to redirect their thoughts toward practical duties—what they could do for their mother, or, more effectively, what their father would have wished of them. The children, seeing their small tasks within reach, began to attempt cleaning the slatternly room. Mr. Hale, however, set a standard too high and too abstract for the indolent invalid. He could not rouse her torpid mind to imagine her husband's misery before the final step; she could only view the tragedy through the lens of her own suffering. She could not comprehend the enduring mercy of God who had not intervened to save her prostrate husband. Though she secretly blamed her husband for his despair and denied any excuse for his rash act, she remained inveterate in her abuse of anyone who might have driven him to such desperation. The masters—Mr. Thornton in particular, whose mill had been attacked and for whom the rioting warrant was later withdrawn—the Union, represented by Higgins, the numerous hungry children; all constituted a vast army of personal enemies responsible for her current helplessness.

Margaret heard enough of this unreasonableness to be disheartened, finding it impossible to cheer her father upon their return.

"It is the town life," she said. "Their nerves are quickened by the haste and bustle of everything around them, not to mention the confinement of these pent-up houses, which alone induces depression. In the country, people live much more outdoors, even children, even in winter."

"But people must live in towns. And in the country, some develop such stagnant habits of mind that they become almost fatalists."

"Yes; I acknowledge that. Each mode of life produces its own trials. The town dweller finds it as difficult to be patient as the country-bred man finds it to be active and equal to unwonted emergencies. Both struggle to realize a future: the one because the present is so living, hurrying, and close; the other because his life tempts him to revel in mere animal existence, caring for no pungent pleasure requiring planning and self-denial."

"And thus, the necessity for engrossment and the stupid contentment in the present produce similar effects. But this poor Mrs. Boucher! How little we can do for her."

"And yet we dare not leave her without effort, though it may seem useless. Oh, Papa! It is a hard world to live in!"

"So it is, my child. We feel it acutely just now, but we have been very happy even amidst our sorrow. What a pleasure Frederick's visit was!"

"Yes, it was," Margaret said brightly. "Such a charming, snatched, forbidden thing." She suddenly stopped. She had spoiled the memory of Frederick's visit through her own cowardice. Of all faults, she most despised in others the lack of bravery; the meanness of heart that leads to untruth. And here she had been guilty of it. Then came the thought of Mr. Thornton's knowledge of her falsehood. She wondered if she would mind detection less from anyone else. She imagined facing her Aunt Shaw, Edith, her father, Captain and Mr. Lennox, and Frederick. The thought of Frederick knowing, even in his own behalf, was most painful given their fresh mutual regard, but even a fall in his opinion was nothing compared to the shrinking shame she felt at the prospect of meeting Mr. Thornton again. Yet, she longed to see him, to resolve where she stood. Her cheeks burned as she recalled how proudly she had implied an objection to trade, citing the deceit of passing off inferior goods or assuming credit for wealth not possessed. She remembered Mr. Thornton's look of calm disdain as he explained that in the great scheme of commerce, all dishonorable ways proved injurious in the long run, and that testing actions by the poor standard of success revealed folly rather than wisdom in all deceit. She remembered herself, then strong in her untempted truth, asking if buying cheap and selling dear lacked transparent justice, using the word "chivalric"—corrected by her father to "Christian"—drawing the argument upon himself while she sat silent with a slight feeling of contempt.

No more contempt for her! No more talk of the chivalric! Henceforth, she must feel humiliated and disgraced in his sight. But when would she see him? Her heart leaped at every ring of the doorbell, yet sank with sadness at each disappointment. It was evident her father expected him and was surprised by his absence. The truth was, their previous conversation had left points unexpanded, with an understanding that they should meet the following evening if possible. Mr. Hale had looked forward to this meeting since they parted. Having not yet resumed his instruction to his pupils due to his wife's serious illness, he had fewer occupations than usual, and the recent tragedy of Boucher's suicide had driven him back to his speculations with renewed eagerness. He was restless all evening, muttering, "I quite expected to see Mr. Thornton. I think the messenger who brought the book last night must have had a note and forgot to deliver it. Do you think there has been any message left today?"

"I will go and inquire, Papa," Margaret said. "Stay, there's a ring!" She sat instantly, bending her head over her work. She heard a step on the stairs, but it was only one, and she knew it was Dixon's. She lifted her head and sighed, believing she felt glad.

"It's Higgins, sir. He wants to see you, or else Miss Hale. Or perhaps Miss Hale first, then you, sir; he's in a strange kind of way."

"He had better come up here, Dixon; then he can see us both and choose his listener."

"Oh! Very well, sir. I have no wish to hear what he has to say, I'm sure; only, if you could see his shoes, I'm sure you'd say the kitchen is the fitter place."

"He can wipe them, I suppose," said Mr. Hale. Dixon flung off to bid him walk upstairs. She was slightly mollified when Higgins looked at his feet with a hesitating air, then sat on the bottom stair, removed the offending shoes, and walked upstairs without a word.

"Servant, sir!" he said, slicking his hair down upon entering the room. "If you'll excuse me (looking at Margaret) for being in my stockings; I've been tramping all day, and the streets are none of the cleanest."

Margaret thought fatigue might account for the change in his manner; he was unusually quiet and subdued, evidently struggling to say what he came to say. Mr. Hale's ever-ready sympathy with shyness or hesitation came to his aid.

"We shall have tea up directly, and then you'll take a cup with us, Mr. Higgins. I am sure you are tired if you've been out much this wet relaxing day. Margaret, my dear, can't you hasten tea?"

Margaret could only hasten tea by taking the preparation into her own hands, thereby offending Dixon, who was emerging from her sorrow for her late mistress into a touchy, irritable state. But Martha, like all who came in contact with Margaret—even Dixon herself in the long run—felt it a pleasure and honor to forward her wishes. Her readiness and Margaret's sweet forbearance soon made Dixon ashamed of herself.

"Why, Master and you must always be asking the lower classes upstairs since we came to Milton; I cannot understand it. Folk at Helstone were never brought higher than the kitchen; and I've let one or two of them know before now that they might think it an honor to be even there."

Higgins found it easier to unburden himself to one than to two. After Margaret left the room, he ensured the door was shut, then approached Mr. Hale closely.

"Master," he said, "you wouldn't guess easy what I've been tramping after today. Especially if you remember my manner of talk yesterday. I've been seeking work. I have," he continued. "I said to myself, I'd keep a civil tongue in my head, let who would say what they would. I'd set my teeth into my tongue sooner than speak in haste. For that man's sake—you understand," jerking his thumb back in some unknown direction.

"No, I don't," said Mr. Hale, seeing he waited for assent and completely bewildered as to who "that man" could be.

"That chap as lies there," he said with another jerk. "Him as went and drowned himself, poor chap! I didn't think he'd got it in him to lie still and let the water creep over him till he died. Boucher, you know."

"Yes, I know now," said Mr. Hale. "Go back to what you were saying: you'd not speak in haste..."

"For his sake. Yet not for his sake; for wherever he is and whatever, he'll never know other clothing or cold again; but for the wife's sake, and the bits of children."

"God bless you!" said Mr. Hale, starting up; then, calming down, he said breathlessly, "What do you mean? Tell me out."

"I have told you," said Higgins, a little surprised at Mr. Hale's agitation. "I would not ask for work for myself; but them's left as a charge on me. I reckon I would have guided Boucher to a better end; but I set him off of the road, and so I must answer for him."

Mr. Hale got hold of Higgins's hand and shook it heartily without speaking. Higgins looked awkward and ashamed.

"There, there, Master! There's never a man, to call a man, amongst us, but what would do the same; ay, and better too; for, believe me, I've never got a stroke of work, nor yet a sight of any. For all I told Hamper that, let alone his pledge—which I would not sign—no, I could not, not even for this—he'd never have such a worker on his mill as I would be; he'd have none of me—no more would none of the others. I'm a poor black feckless sheep—children may claim for ought I can do, unless, Parson, you'd help me?"

"Help you! How? I would do anything—but what can I do?"

"Miss there"—for Margaret had re-entered the room and stood silent, listening—"has often talked grand of the South, and the ways down there. Now I don't know how far off it is, but I been thinking if I could get 'em down there, where food is cheap and wages good, and all the folk, rich and poor, master and man, friendly like; you could, maybe, help me to work. I'm not forty-five, and I've a deal of strength in me, Master."

"But what kind of work could you do, my man?"

"Well, I reckon I could spade a bit..."

"And for that," said Margaret, stepping forward, "for anything you could do, Higgins, with the best will in the world, you would, maybe, get nine shillings a week; maybe ten, at the outside. Food is much the same as here, except that you might have a little garden..."

"The children could work at that," he said. "I'm sick of Milton anyways, and Milton is sick of me."

"You must not go the South," said Margaret. "You could not stand it. You would have to be out all weathers. It would kill you with rheumatism. The mere bodily work at your time of life would break you down. The fare is far different to what you have been accustomed to."

"I'm nought particular about my meat," he said, as if offended.

"But you've reckoned on having butcher's meat once a day if you're in work; pay for that out of your ten shillings, and keep those poor children if you can. I owe it to you—since it's my way of talking that has set you off on this idea—to put it all clear before you. You would not bear the dulness of the life; you don't know what it is; it would eat you away like rust. Those that have lived there all their lives are used to soaking in the stagnant waters. They labour on from day to day in the great solitude of steaming fields—never speaking or lifting up their poor, bent, downcast heads.
