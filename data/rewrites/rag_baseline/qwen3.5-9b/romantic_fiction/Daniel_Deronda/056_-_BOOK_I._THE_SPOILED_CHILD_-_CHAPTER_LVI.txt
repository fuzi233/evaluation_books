The pain, the curse with which they died, had never passed away; Deronda could not draw his eyes from hers, nor lift them up to pray. Yet that night, he did not remove his clothes. Gwendolen, after insisting on seeing him once more before consenting to undress, had fallen into a perfectly quiet stillness, whispering with a repressed, frantic eagerness that he must come to her when she summoned him in the morning. Even so, a foreboding gnawed at him—the possibility that a change might seize her, the danger of a supervening fever, the suspicion that the late catastrophe was already seeping into her speech. He instructed her attendant that he stood ready to be called at any alarming shift in her symptoms, making it clear to everyone that he was in constant contact with her friends in England and felt bound to take every precaution for her sake—a role he assumed all the easier because he was well-known to Grandcourt's valet, the sole old servant who had survived the voyage.

When the strange, variegated emotions of the day finally claimed him, Deronda slept, undisturbed until the morning dreams arrived as a tangled web of yesterday's events, waking him with an image drawn by his pressing anxiety. It was morning, and there had been no summons—an augury that cheered him as he dressed, leading him to reflect that it was too early to inquire further. Later, he learned she had endured a too-wakeful night but showed no violent signs of agitation, eventually succumbing to sleep. He marveled at the force dwelling in this creature, so acutely alive to dread; he carried an irresistible impression that even under the shock of severe physical trauma, she was mastering herself with a terrifying determination of concealment. In contrast, he felt his own sensibilities blunted by the ordeal of meeting his mother; he seemed to himself merely fulfilling obligations, his deeper, more passionate sympathy lying in abeyance. Having lived so keenly in an experience apart from Gwendolen's lot, his current cares for her felt like a revisiting of familiar scenes, lacking the complete revival of inward response they once commanded.

In the interim, he sought a formal, legally recognized statement from the fishermen who had rescued Gwendolen. Few details emerged. The boat in which Grandcourt had departed was found drifting with its sail loose, towed safely inland. The fishermen believed he had been knocked overboard by the flapping canvas while turning the vessel, unable to swim. Yet, though they were nearby, their attention had first been arrested by a cry resembling that of a man in distress. As they hastened with their oars, they heard a shriek from the lady and saw her leap into the water.

Upon returning to the hotel, Deronda was informed that Gwendolen had risen and desired to see him. He was led into a room darkened by blinds and heavy curtains, where she sat wrapped in a white shawl, gazing toward the opening door with the uneasy vigilance of one awaiting judgment. Her long hair was gathered and coiled with careful precision, and the blue stars in her ears retained their place. As she started impulsively to her full height, encased in the shawl, her face and neck stark white save for a purple line beneath her eyes, her lips slightly parted with the peculiar expression of the accused and helpless, she appeared as the unhappy ghost of the Gwendolen Harleth Deronda had once seen turning from her losses at the gaming table with firm lips and proud self-possession. The sight pierced him with pity, stirring the effects of all their past relations anew.

"I beseech you to rest—not to stand," Deronda said as he approached, and she obeyed, falling back into her chair.

"Will you sit down near me?" she asked. "I want to speak very low."

She settled into a large armchair, and he drew a small one close to her side. The gesture touched her peculiarly; turning her pale face fully toward his, which hovered inches away, she whispered in the lowest audible tone, "You know I am a guilty woman?"

Deronda himself grew paler as he replied, "I know nothing." He dared not say more.

"He is dead." She uttered this with the same undertoned decisiveness.

"Yes," Deronda answered, in a mournful suspense that made him reluctant to continue.

"His face will not be seen above the water again," Gwendolen said, her voice not louder but charged with suppressed urgency, her hands clenched tight.

"No."

"Not by any one else—only by me. A dead face... I shall never get away from it."

With an inward voice of desperate self-repression, she spoke these final words while averting her gaze from Deronda toward something distant on the floor. Was she viewing the entire event—including her own acts—through an exaggerating medium of excitement and horror? Was she in a state of delirium, entering a realm of concealment and necessary self-suppression? Such thoughts flashed through Deronda as a fragile hope. But consider the conflict of feeling that kept him silent. She was bent on confession, and he dreaded hearing it. Against his better will, he shrank from the task laid upon him; he wished, and yet rebuked the wish as cowardly, that she could bury her secrets in her own bosom. He was not a priest. He feared the weight of this woman's soul flung upon his own with imploring dependence. Then she spoke again, hurriedly, looking at him.

"You will not say that I ought to tell the world? You will not say that I ought to be disgraced? I could not do it. I could not bear it. I cannot have my mother know. Not if I were dead. I could not have her know. I must tell you; but you will not say that any one else should know."

"I can say nothing in my ignorance," Deronda murmured, "except that I desire to help you."

"I told you from the beginning—as soon as I could—I told you I was afraid of myself." There was a piteous pleading in the low murmur to which Deronda turned his ear exclusively. Her afflicted face weighed upon him too heavily. "I felt a hatred in me that worked like an evil spirit—contriving things. Every means I could devise to free myself came to mind; and it got worse—all things got worse. That is why I asked you to come to me in town. I thought then I would tell you the worst about myself. I tried. But I could not tell everything. And *he* came in."

She paused, a shudder passing through her frame, before continuing.

"I will tell you everything now. Do you think a woman who cried, and prayed, and struggled to be saved from herself, could be a murderess?"

"Great God!" Deronda exclaimed, his voice deep and shaken. "Do not torture me needlessly. You have not murdered him. You threw yourself into the water with the impulse to save him. Tell me the rest afterward. This death was an accident that you could not have hindered."

"Don't be impatient with me." The tremor, the childlike beseeching in these words, compelled Deronda to turn his head and look at her face. The poor, quivering lips continued. "You said—you used to say—you felt more for those who had done something wicked and were miserable; you said they might get better—they might be scourged into something better. If you had not spoken in that way, everything would have been worse. I *did* remember all you said to me. It came to me always. It came to me at the very last—that was the reason why I—But now, if you cannot bear with me when I tell you everything, if you turn away from me and forsake me, what shall I do? Am I worse than I was when you found me and wanted to make me better? All the wrong I have done was in me then—and more—and more—if you had not come and been patient with me. And now—will you forsake me?"

Her hands, so tightly clenched moments before, were now helplessly relaxed and trembling on the arm of her chair. Her quivering lips remained parted as she ceased speaking. Deronda could not answer; he was obliged to look away. Taking one of her hands, he clasped it as if they were walking together like two children; it was the only way he could answer, "I will not forsake you." All the while, he felt as though he were signing his name to a blank paper that might be filled up terribly. Their posture, his averted face bearing an expression of suffering he was solemnly resolved to endure, might have told half the truth of the situation to a stranger who had suddenly entered.

That grasp was an entirely new experience for Gwendolen. She had never before received from any man a sign of tenderness that her own being desperately needed, and she interpreted its powerful effect on her as a promise of inexhaustible patience and constancy. The stream of renewed strength allowed her to proceed as she had begun—with that fitful, wandering confession where the sameness of experience seemed to nullify the sense of time or order in events. She resumed in fragments.
