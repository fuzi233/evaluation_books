I quit London once with drums thumping in my chest and victory flags flapping around my brain, the sort of grand dramatic send-off a 20-something lad with delusions of knighthood and a play in his pocket swears he’s earned. A little over a year later, I slunk back on a foggy, drippy December evening, properly defeated — and that’s nothing, you can bounce back from a loss with a pint and a new plan. No, I was disgraced. The kind of disgraced you don’t just brush off. I returned to London like it was a big, overcrowded haystack I could lose myself in, planning to waste my unravelling days unnoticed until I could summon the nerve to finally put an end to the dead, useless life I’d accidentally built for myself.

I had been ambitious, back when I still thought I was clever. I’d wanted to make a name for myself, carve out a spot in the world — and not just any spot, mind you. I was going to be the good great man. The world wouldn’t just admire me, it would respect me. I’d be Galahad, not Launcelot: the knight without fear, and far rarer, without reproach. Turned out I was just a spineless, backboneless little fighter who folded the first time evil tapped him on the shoulder, a creature ruled by petty cowardice who jumped every time the devil cracked his tiny whip, happy enough to snuffle around the trough with the pigs.

I’d learned my “genius theatrical partner” Urban Vane was just a regular, unremarkable swindler. That “original play” of his? He’d nicked it straight off the desk of a well-known dramatist he’d met hanging around Deleglise’s kitchen. When the dramatist fell ill, Vane had popped round every other day with broth and flattery, and the second the poor guy left for Italy to recover (everyone thought he’d cark it there, which would’ve made the theft airtight), Vane swiped the whole manuscript. We were halfway through a run in some tiny northern town when word reached us: the guy wasn’t dead. He’d bounced back and was already on a boat home to England.

Vane broke the news to me over a bottle of wine, puffing on his cigar like he was telling me a funny anecdote about a stray cat he’d seen steal a fish off a market stall, not admitting we were both about to get caught for grand theft script. Six months of hanging around with Vane hadn’t quite killed off every last scrap of decency in me — thank the Lord for small mercies — but it had definitely wilted all its leaves, so I didn’t blow up nearly as hard as I should have.

The man was clever, in the way a guy who can lie his way out of a train fare is clever. It still baffles me that he didn’t work out earlier that honesty is the easiest scam for people with two brain cells to rub together. Last I heard he’s making thousands now running perfectly legitimate “respectable” businesses, the sort of upstanding citizen people invite to charity galas. Chicanery in the blood has to burn off eventually, I suppose. You meet Urban Vanes in every line of work: they start out as unapologetic scamps, then 10 years later you run into them and everyone’s calling them an ornament to their profession. We all sow our wild oats, it just depends what soil they land in.

At first I did storm, of course. Vane just sat there smiling like I was a toddler throwing a tantrum over a broken toy.

“Your language, my dear Kelver,” he said, once I’d run out of swear words, “might wound me if I considered you any sort of authority on this tedious morals business. Everyone preaches one thing and does the other, I’ve seen it a hundred times. It’s sad, sure, but no one’s sermons interest me anymore. You’re furious I stole another man’s ideas? It happens every single day. You were happy enough to take top billing on this play, weren’t you? Not 200 lines of it are yours, and they’re very good lines, but they don’t make a full five-act drama. Vane told me he didn’t want his own name on it, remember? Private family reasons, you said! You jumped at the chance to call yourself a famous playwright with zero effort.”

That angle had never occurred to me. “But you said it was translated from the French!” I stammered. “Adaptors put their own names to plays all the time, that’s just the industry custom!”

He waved cigar smoke in my face like I was being very silly. “Oh, I know. It’s always made me laugh. Our sick friend the dramatist we’re all so pleased is not dead? He’s done that exact thing at least twice, and made a pile of money off it. I’d bet good money this very play he wrote is mostly stolen from someone else too. What’s the difference between him nicking from a foreigner and me nicking from him? The only difference is the copyright laws are so daft stealing from a Frenchman is totally legal and everyone claps you for it, while stealing from a living Englishman makes people side-eye you over tea.”

“And the consequences are going to be nasty,” I said, panicking.

He laughed. “You mean the consequences for you, dear boy.”

I stared at him, dumbfounded.

“Don’t look so shocked,” he said, patting my shoulder. “You can’t be that thick. The original manuscript registered at the Lord Chamberlain’s office? All in your handwriting. You visited the sick playwright right alongside me. You’ve managed every single stop on this tour, all the contracts, all the bookings. No one’s going to believe you were just the stooge.”

All the anger drained right out of me, replaced by cold, dumb fear. “But you said it was just a formality! You said—”

He looked genuinely surprised I’d bought that line. That was the worst part. I suddenly felt like the biggest idiot in the whole north of England. “I’m sorry, Kelver. I genuinely thought you were a man of the world, and that you just chose not to ask questions.”

To my everlasting shame, fear was the only feeling in my whole body. “You’re not going to pin the whole thing on me?” I pleaded.

He stood up and gave my shoulder a friendly little squeeze. And I was grateful for it, the pathetic wretch — he was the only person I thought I could turn to for help.

“Don’t be daft, it’s not that bad,” he said. “He’ll just think he lost the manuscript. Half the writers I know can’t even remember what ideas they’ve had and what they’ve only thought about having. None of the cast are going to say anything, it’s not their problem. We’ll rewrite the whole thing in a fortnight. Change all the character names, keep the one scene that’s actually good, tweak every line so even if he saw it he’d just think we saw a snippet of his idea and beat him to staging it. No one will ever know.”

For a few weeks after that I kept hearing the voice of my last remaining good angel, telling me I should go find the dramatist, confess everything, take whatever lumps came to me. But Vane was glued to my elbow 24/7, dropping little reminders that if I ratted him out, he’d tell everyone I was the lying little thief who’d tried to frame him, and every theatre director in the country would blacklist me before I turned 25. And then he’d spin the other side: what harm were we actually doing? The play had only run in a handful of tiny towns where no one would remember it in six months. The original guy would put on his version and make a fortune, ours would vanish without a trace. The whole “world is fair and honest” thing was just a bunch of bourgeois conventions, anyway. The only real rule was don’t get caught.

So I caved. I fell right out of my own good graces, and I worked side by side with Vane to rewrite that stolen play, every line I wrote feeling like it was being dragged out of my skin. Even now I’m glad I didn’t enjoy a single second of it. I tried to beg Vane to let me write a completely new, original play from scratch, offered to give it to him for free, but he wouldn’t hear of it. “Later, dear boy, later. This one’s making us money right now, why fix what isn’t broken?”

Three weeks later we had a cobbled-together mess that was technically original as far as dialogue and characters went, and some of it was even decent. In any other circumstances I might have been proud of it. Instead, I felt like a burglar tiptoeing past a constable every single night. When the audience laughed at my jokes, I didn’t feel clever, I just felt like I’d cheated them. There was one grand, dramatic line the hero spits at the villain: “Yes, I love her. But there’s something I love far more — my own self-respect!” Our juvenile lead, a man who spent most of his time hiding from bailiffs sent after him by his very angry ex-wife for unpaid alimony, would yell that line while slapping his chest like it was a treasure chest full of virtues, and bring the house down every single night. Listening to the roaring applause, I’d sit in the wings and shudder, because I’d written that exact line while hiding under my desk terrified someone would break down the door and arrest me.

Vane had been bugging me for weeks to play the bit part of the vicious old rascal in the play, and once my willpower had completely dried up I agreed. It was a zippier part than the noble country lad I’d been playing, but every cheap, leering laugh I got from the crowd chipped another little piece of decency off of me. I started leaning into the gross little suggestive gags Vane suggested, making the part as unpleasant as we could get away with, and he kept telling me I was a genius, that I “understood human nature.” Worst of all, I started to like Vane.

Looking back now, he was just a regular, boring scammer with no interesting quirks at all, but to my dumb 20-something self he seemed like the coolest, most enlightened man alive. He’d wander around acting like he was far too clever to bother following all the silly little rules us regular chumps stuck to, and call anyone who had a scrap of basic decency “suburban” or “bourgeois” like that was the worst insult you could possibly come up with. He was one of those guys who rants about Hedonism and how all morality is just made up nonsense, the exact sort of boring edgelord you get cornered by at a house party at 2 a.m. that everyone rolls their eyes at now, but I was a kid who’d never heard anyone talk like that before, and I ate it all up. For a while I actually thought being a dissolute layabout was a sign you were a great artist.

Of course, the whole “mysterious elegant villain who does evil for aesthetic pleasure” schtick is great in novels, but in real life they’re just delusional fools. And for all his talk, Vane turned out fine in the end — I’ve heard he’s an excellent husband and dad, a kind boss, the sort of guy who hosts fantastic dinner parties. He genuinely thought he was doing me a favour, “freeing me from the chains of convention.” Idiot.

Then there was our leading lady, a big, handsome, brusque woman who’d started out as a music hall singer, then run off with a very promising young sensitive novelist who’d thrown his whole career away for her. By the time we met her, she was already bored of him. He was hanging around our troupe playing tiny, terrible bit parts, too wrapped up in being in love with her to leave even when he wasn’t getting paid.

Vane decided it’d be a great little scheme to set us two up. He told her I was a rich, upcoming young writer who could keep her in all the fancy gowns she wanted, and told me she was madly in love with me. I wasn’t attracted to her beyond thinking she looked as nice and robust as a well-fed cart horse, but my stupid, fragile vanity ate the whole thing up. Just like I’d forced myself to learn to smoke cigars and drink whiskey because I thought it made me look like a real writer, I started trying to force myself to fall for her, convinced that all great artists had to be a little bit of a cad. The fact her lingering touches made me feel cold instead of flustered, the fact her so-called “sultry” smiles just annoyed me, felt like a personal failure on my part.

Then fate stepped in, thank God, and saved me from making a complete fool of myself. The dramatist we’d stolen the play from released his version earlier than anyone expected, and word got around our tiny touring circuit that some other troupe had already been running a nearly identical knockoff. People started asking questions. Vane kept saying “it’ll blow over,” but it was very much not blowing over.

We were playing in a tiny town in the north of Ireland, and I was the one who always handed out salaries on a Friday night after the show. I hadn’t seen Vane all day, and the second I got out of my costume the box office guy handed me a crumpled note. Vane had grabbed all the evening’s takings and legged it on the 7:50 train. He wrote he regretted the inconvenience, but life was full of little comedies and you shouldn’t take any of them seriously, and he was sure we’d meet up soon and laugh about the whole mess over a drink.

I looked up from the note and saw every member of the troupe staring at me, all their faces full of suspicion. I tried to tell them the truth, but none of them believed it. Vane had spent months making sure every single one of them thought I was the sole manager and the only person in charge. Half of them were talking about giving me a “good hiding,” but I was so numb and listless at that point I don’t think I would have noticed if they’d beaten me within an inch of my life. Someone said I was obviously just very drunk, and everyone bought it. There were no more trains out of town that night, so they told me to go back to my lodgings and they’d come “sort things out” first thing in the morning.

I got back to my tiny rented room and found our leading lady sitting by my fire, waiting for me. Her husband was off working as our advance agent in the next town, so there was no one else around.

“Well?” she said, giving me the one smile she’d been telling me for months was absolutely soul-shattering. It had never once made my pulse speed up.

“Vane ran off with all the week’s takings,” I said, and laughed. “There’s no money for anyone’s salaries, no money for anyone’s fares back to London.”

She stood up so fast she knocked her chair over. “Wait a second. Are you the rich idiot Vane told me you were, or just his broke little accomplice?”

“Both,” I said. “No money attached. The whole tour was his idea, he just put my name on the paperwork because he didn’t want his respectable family to find out. He stole the whole play, I was just the idiot who wrote the transcript for the Lord Chamberlain.”

She sat back down and laughed so loud the walls shook, long and loud and genuinely amused. “Well I’ll be damned! He’s the first man who’s ever managed to take me for a ride. I never would’ve signed on for this rickety grift if I’d known what a crook he was.” She stood up to leave, then stopped leaning on the doorframe, cackling too hard to leave. “You know why I really turned up here tonight? You’d asked me to run away with you last night, remember? I came to tell you yes.”

I stared at her. That felt like it had happened 100 years ago. I’d completely forgotten the whole stupid, dramatic speech I’d slurred at her outside the stage door after three whiskeys.

She snort-laughed again. “Well, thank God I decided to sleep on it! What a lucky escape for both of us. Goodnight, kid.”

She paused at the step. “Got any money to get yourself out of here?”

I dug in my pocket. “One pound seventeen shillings. It’s either enough to get me the fare back to London, or buy a dinner and a secondhand revolver. Haven’t decided which yet.”

“Go back to London and pull yourself together, you’re just a kid,” she said, and left.

I stuffed a handful of my things in a tiny bag and walked 30 miles straight through the night all the way to Belfast, then caught the first ferry back to England. I rented a tiny, anonymous garret in Deptford, where I was unlikely to run into a single person I’d ever met before. I scraped by for four months teaching sixpence half-hour singing lessons, 18 pence a week evening French and German classes to ambitious teen shop boys (may God forgive my terrible accent), and doing bookkeeping for local tradesmen. I never wandered outside my tiny neighbourhood, and whenever I saw a familiar London street corner in the distance I’d turn and run back to my little corner of dim, smoky Deptford. For months I existed in a sort of numb, dull daze, eating, sleeping, walking for hours and staring at the ground, not really seeing a single thing going on around me. I was half-convinced I’d already died and no one had bothered to tell me.
