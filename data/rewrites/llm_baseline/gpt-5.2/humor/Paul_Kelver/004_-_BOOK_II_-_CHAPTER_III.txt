“Louisa!” my father’s voice came booming up the kitchen stairs like a kettle-drum in a church tower. “Are you all asleep? Here have I had to answer the front door myself!”

The house, which had been murmuring all morning with the small homely music of knives on boards and peas rattling into a basin, went still at that. Then came the slam of his office door—sharp as a cleaver on bone.

A quarter of an hour later his bell began to jangle: brisk, authoritative, and entirely out of tune with the kitchen’s gentler clinks. My mother, wrestling with potatoes in stiff wash-leather gloves, looked up; my aunt Fan, with her thumbs in a pile of pods, cocked her head like a fowl hearing thunder.

“Once for Louisa, twice for James, isn’t it?” my aunt observed, as though the bell were a code worth decoding.

The bell rang again, louder, angrier—metal scolding metal.

“You go, Paul,” said my mother. “Say that Louisa—” But the words stuck halfway; a flush ran over her face as if someone had opened the oven door. In a flutter she stripped off her gloves, and before I could set down my slate she had swept past me. “No—don’t stop your lessons. I’ll go myself.”

She went; and the kitchen resumed its work with a nervous haste. The potatoes, freed from their skins, looked pale and patient; the peas in my aunt’s bowl lay like green beads, silent witnesses.

Then, very softly, the kitchen door opened. My mother’s hand appeared through the crack and beckoned to me as though she were summoning an accomplice to a crime.

“Walk on your toes,” she whispered, and demonstrated at once.

I walked on my toes. The stairs, being old and having a conscience, creaked louder than usual—as if offended by secrecy and determined to publish it to the street. Up we went all the same, and into my parents’ bedroom, where the wardrobe—relic of grander days, smelling faintly of lavender and camphor—kept, like a shrine, my best suit; or, to be exact, my better.

Never had I worn it on a weekday morning. My mother brushed aside every question that wasn’t directly concerned with getting into it quickly. Buttons were attacked; collars were coaxed; and when I was complete, down to the new shoes—Bluchers, we called them then—she took my hand and we crept down again, silent, alert, and absurdly solemn.

“Shan’t I want my cap?” I breathed.

My mother shook her head as though caps belonged to ordinary life, and ordinary life had been suspended. She shut the street door with a bang; and at once I understood the pantomime. This sort of “business”—comic, tragic, or both—was becoming familiar in our house.

With my mother’s hand on my shoulder, we entered my father’s office.

To me, as a child, my father’s profession never had the look of anything serious. It seemed a game with props: big books that might as well have been castle walls, bundles of documents lavishly labelled and suspiciously blank, japanned tin boxes with thunderous titles and empty insides. “Sutton Hampden, Esq.” was, in practice, my mother’s work-box. “Drayton Estates” produced, so far as I could see, only apples; and “Mortgages,” I discovered much later, had nothing whatever to do with poems, though in my mind the word had always sounded as if it ought to rhyme with “gorgeous.”

Now my father rose to meet us and came forward, trying to look carved out of law and gravity, though his hair stood up in those familiar tufts that came of rumpling it while he talked. He always reminded me—more than I liked to admit—of an overgrown schoolboy playing at “business” for the amusement of smaller children.

“This is the youngster,” he said, and handed me along as if I were an exhibit. “Tall for his age, isn’t he?”

The man who received me was very fat, very prosperous, and very certain of it. He shifted a cigar of the most offensive smell from one corner of his thick mouth to the other, and held out a hand that closed round mine with a damp, confident grip. Its rings flashed; its nails were not as clean as the rings suggested they ought to be. He flapped me on the head with the other hand; the gesture was meant kindly, but it felt like a seal’s fin. In my natural history book a walrus had a face I had long distrusted; this gentleman’s hand gave me the same impression.

“Like ’is mother,” he remarked, his small eyes bright as two polished beads. “Wonderfully like ’is mother, ain’t ’e?” Then he winked one of those eyes at my father. “And that’s about the ’ighest compliment I can pay ’im, eh?”

When his eyes came back to me, I tried to think quickly of something pleasant about him. I had the uncomfortable feeling he could see straight through my thoughts, as through the frosted glass in a door.

“And where are you thinkin’ of sendin’ ’im?” he went on. “Eton or ’Arrow?”

“We haven’t quite made up our minds,” replied my father. “At present we are educating him at home.”

“You take my tip,” said the fat man, wagging the cigar as if it were a pointer on a blackboard, “and learn all you can. Look at me! If I’d ’ad the opportunity of being a schollard, I wouldn’t be here offerin’ your father an extravagant price for doin’ my work; I’d be able to do it myself.”

“You seem to have got on very well without it,” my father laughed; and indeed the man’s waistcoat rose and fell like a well-fed tide beneath a massive gold chain, and his fingers sparkled with rings as though his hands had been dipped in a jeweller’s tray.

“I’d ’ave done better with it,” he grunted.

“But you look very clever,” I said, which was meant to please him and also, in a child’s way, was true: cleverness, I then believed, was simply the quality of getting what you wanted and looking as if you deserved it.

He roared with laughter, and his whole body shook, wobbling like a great bowl of blancmange.

“Well, old Noel Hasluck’s not exactly a fool,” he conceded. “But I’d like myself better if I could talk about somethin’ else than business, and didn’t drop my aitches. And so would my little gell.”

“You have a daughter?” asked my mother, instantly warmed. A child, to my mother, was always a bridge flung across any strangeness.

“I won’t tell you about ’er,” Mr. Hasluck answered, with a sudden softening that looked odd on so much flesh. “But I’ll just bring ’er to see you now and then, ma’am, if you don’t mind. She don’t often meet gentle-folks, an’ it’ll do ’er good.”

My mother glanced at my father; and before her glance could become a question, Mr. Hasluck waved it away with easy mastery.

“You needn’t be afraid, ma’am, that she’s anythin’ like me,” he said, good-temperedly. “Nobody ever believes she’s my daughter, except me and the old woman. She’s a little lady, she is. Freak o’ nature, I call it.”

“We shall be delighted,” said my mother, in the tone she used when offering second helpings: firm kindness, no condescension.

“Well, you will when you see ’er,” Mr. Hasluck replied, as if the matter were settled by the laws of the universe.

As I was being ushered out he pressed a half-crown into my hand with the cheerful tyranny of a man who paid for whatever he touched, including other people’s pride.

“No squanderin’ it on the ’eathen,” he called after me. “You spend that on a Christian tradesman.”

It was the first money I ever remember having that was truly mine to spend. Up to then, any coin that floated into my small life had been immediately tethered by improving visions: Sunday gloves, a Latin grammar with my own name inside, books with good boys in them who did good things with tidy faces. Now, however, I was left free.

Freedom, I discovered, is an anxious thing.

For nights I lay awake, the half-crown burning in my mind as if it had been heated on the range. I tramped the streets with it in my pocket, feeling it drag my trousers down by sheer responsibility. I stared into shop windows until my breath fogged the glass; I asked prices I had no intention of paying; I made up my mind and unmade it again in the space of a single paving-stone.

Advice, when I begged for it, was denied.

“You must learn to think for yourself,” said my father, who could talk for ten minutes on “commercial aptitude” without once noticing that I was only eight and hated commerce.

“No, dear,” said my mother. “Mr. Hasluck wanted you to spend it as you like. If I told you, that would be spending it as I liked. Your father and I want to see what you will do.”

The good little boys in books bought gifts for their parents or gave away money to people in distress. For this I hated them with the clean, unmixed malice the lower nature always feels towards the higher.

I tried my aunt Fan.

“If somebody gave you half-a-crown,” I asked, “what would you buy with it?”

“Side-combs,” said my aunt promptly. She was forever losing or breaking her side-combs, as if her hair were engaged in a private war.

“But I mean if you were me.”

“Drat the child!” said my aunt. “How do I know what he wants if he don’t know himself? Idiot!”

So the half-crown grew into a family affliction. Even my mother’s patience began to show small cracks, like a pie-crust that has been handled too much.

Then, one day, I ended it.

In a back street there was a plumber’s shop, smelling of metal and damp wood, with brass taps and rolls of lead piping and cistern contrivances displayed as if they were jewels. Among them, propped neatly, were squares of coloured glass—cheap stained panes such as you saw in lavatory doors and staircase windows. Some held stars at their centres; others had severe little patterns that tried to be grand and succeeded in being merely tidy.

I bought a dozen.

The plumber, an affable man, seemed glad to see me in the way that tradesmen are glad to see foolish boys with money. He wrapped the panes in paper with practised hands and threw in two extra out of generosity—or out of the belief that I was the sort of customer who, once started, might some day build a house entirely out of lavatory windows.

Why I bought them I did not know at the time, and I do not know now. They pleased me—perhaps because they made ordinary daylight look like something from a story, because they turned the common into the coloured without changing anything but the way you saw it. Or perhaps because, like many of Mr. Hasluck’s gifts, they carried their own peculiar curse and I was merely obedient to it.

My mother cried when she saw them. Not loudly; she simply sat down as if her knees had turned to dough and covered her face with her hands.

My father got no further than: “But what are you going to do with them?” and, since my mind had purchased them long before my reason arrived, I could not tell him.

My aunt Fan alone attempted comfort.

“If a person fancies coloured glass,” she said, “then he’s a fool not to buy coloured glass when he gets the chance. We haven’t all the same tastes.”

In the end I cut myself badly with them and, after bleeding in a manner that made everyone feel justified, consented to their being thrown into the dust-bin. Yet looking back I think less of folly than of Fate. I have met many people since who were recipients of old Hasluck’s half-crowns—men who slapped their pockets and blessed the day they first met that “Napoleon of Finance,” as later his friends came to call him. But it ever ended so: coloured glass and cut fingers. Fairy gold, perhaps: bright in the hand, awkward in life.

The next time Mr. Hasluck knocked at our door a maid in cap and apron opened it.

That was only the beginning.

New oilcloth appeared in the passage and shone like a wet fish. Lace curtains—those neighborhood banners of sudden respectability—hung in our windows and announced our prosperity to every passer-by. Greatest marvel of all, at least to my awed eyes: my father’s Sunday clothes came down into weekday service, while new ones took their place in the wardrobe, that old stronghold of gentility where we had always gone for comfort when the outer walls looked thin.

“Seeing that everything was all right,” my mother would say, as if “all right” were a condition you could lay out on the bed and stroke smooth.

She would spread the lilac silk gown and soothe down its rustling waves; she would linger over its deep frosted Honiton flounces as lovingly as over a good pie-crust that had turned out exactly as planned. She might enter the room looking tired and pinched, but as she folded and refolded the Indian shawl—the one that could go through a wedding-ring but never would when you asked it—her spirits rose like bread in warmth. By the time the great cameo brooch was pinned and the poke bonnet had been shaken and petted into decency, she would be humming, sometimes even singing softly to herself, nodding to words that were usually about somebody too old and somebody too bold and another too cold, “so he wouldn’t do for me,” and stepping lightly as if money had taken a little weight off her shoulders.

I remember one evening—before the advent of this Hasluck—when I climbed out of bed because trouble was within me.

Creatures, indescribable but heavy, had sat upon my chest; after which I had fallen downstairs in a dream, slowly and reasonably for the first few hundred flights, then with indecent haste for the next million miles or so, until I found myself in the street with nothing on but my nightshirt. Personally, I was shocked; but nobody else in the dream seemed to mind. I hailed a two-penny ’bus and climbed in, and then discovered I had no pockets with which to pay. I jumped out and ran, and the conductor came after me. My feet were like lead; with every step he gained—until, with a scream, I made one mighty effort and woke.

Feeling the need of comfort, I wrapped some clothes round me and crept downstairs. The office was dark; but to my surprise a light shone from under the drawing-room door. I opened it.

The candles in the silver candlesticks were lit. In state, one in each easy-chair, sat my father and mother in their best clothes: my father in buckled shoes and frilled shirt, which I had never before seen him wear; my mother with the Indian shawl about her shoulders and on her head the cap of ceremony that lay, three hundred and sixty days of the year, in its round wicker nest lined with silk. They started guiltily when I came in—like children caught playing at grown-up.

I congratulate myself that I had enough sense—or instinct—not to ask questions.

Three hours earlier they had been cleaning the ground-floor windows, my father outside, my mother within, with all lights carefully extinguished. Now their whole manner had changed. Their voices were altered: softer, more measured, as if they had stepped into another dialect of themselves—one with longer vowels and fewer worries.

My father brought sherry and sweet biscuits from the sideboard. He poured a glass for my mother and handed it as if presenting a prize. They drank to each other, while I sat between them and ate the biscuits, which were crisp and sugary and tasted of occasion. The conversation was of Byron’s poems and the great glass palace in Hyde Park.

I wonder, am I disloyal setting this down? Perhaps to others it shows only a foolish man and woman dressing up in their own lives. But the memory is tender to me. Virtues are what we expect from those we love; failings we try to forget. It is over their follies that affection smiles and lingers.

To me personally, old Hasluck’s coming—and all that followed—made perhaps more difference than to anyone else. My father became busy all day; if not in his office then away in what I still thought of as the grim city of giants. My mother’s days filled with new social and domestic duties, and for a time I was left much to my own resources.

Rambling—“bummelling,” as the Germans call it—was my bent. My mother would have checked it, but my father said:

“Don’t molly-coddle him. Let him learn to be smart.”

“I don’t think the smart people are always the nicest,” my mother demurred. “I don’t call you at all smart, Luke.”

My father looked surprised, then considered as if weighing evidence.

“I should call myself smart—in a sense,” he explained at last.

“Perhaps you are right, dear,” my mother said. “And of course boys are different from girls.”

Sometimes I wandered towards Victoria Park, which then was edged with small cottages set in leafy gardens, where you could hear birds and children and the kind of quiet that feels like a clean tablecloth. Sometimes I reached as far as Clapton, where old red-brick Georgian houses stood behind high palings and tall elms lent the wide road, on sunny afternoons, an air of peace that seemed borrowed from another century.

But, strangely, those narrow squalid streets nearer home had a greater hold on me.

Not the main thoroughfares with their dull deep throbbing, as of some tireless iron machine—the place where the endless human files streaming and crossing and recrossing looked like chains of flesh working on unseen wheels. What drew me were the dim, weary, lifeless streets—the dark, tortuous roots, as I imagined them, of that grim forest of brick. There was a hush there that felt less like quiet than like listening. Far-off footsteps would rise suddenly into sound and then fade, leaving the air to settle back into its own uneasy breathing. A door might bang two streets away and the noise would travel along the walls as though the houses passed it on to one another in whispers.

In those streets, silence had a taste: stale, smoky, with a hint of damp plaster. And in that silence, fear could grow fat.

One day a cry came from behind a curtained window—long, piercing, not like a street-seller’s shout but like something torn out of a throat. I stood still for an instant, then ran. Before I could get far, it came again, fiercer before it ended, and I ran faster, not heeding where I went, until I found myself in a raw unfinished street that ended in black waste land bordering the river.

The air there was different—open, wet, smelling of mud and rot and the river’s cold breath. The city’s clatter did not quite reach it; what you heard instead was a thin, restless soundscape: wind worrying at loose boards, a distant chain clinking, some far, unseen creak that might have been a rope or a conscience.

I stopped, panting, and sat on the doorstep of an empty house to recover myself. And then there came dancing down the road with a curious half-running, half-hopping step—like a water-wagtail’s—another child, a boy about my own age. He eyed me oddly and sat down beside me.

For a few minutes we watched each other. His mouth opened and shut though he said nothing, as if he were chewing words too tough to swallow. Then, edging closer, he spoke in a thick whisper, as though his mouth were full of wool.

“Wot ’appens to yer when yer dead?”

“If you’re good you go to Heaven,” I answered promptly. “If you’re bad you go to Hell.”

“Long way off, both of ’em, ain’t they?”

“Yes. Millions of miles.”

“They can’t come after yer? Can’t fetch yer back again?”

“No. Never.”

All the while his eyes stared towards the other end of the street, now darkening. He did not look away for an instant.

“Ever seen a stiff ’un—a dead ’un?”

“No.”

“I ’ave—stuck a pin into ’im. ’E never felt it. Don’t feel anythin’ when yer dead, do yer?”

As he talked he swayed and twisted, arms and legs wriggling in queer curves, pulling faces. Those gingerbread men bakers used to sell—comical, with bent limbs and grinning mouths—came into my mind so strongly that, absurdly, my stomach tightened with hunger.

“Of course not,” I said, glad to have something solemn ready. “When you are dead you’re not there, you know. Our bodies are but senseless clay.”

I was pleased with myself for remembering that line. I tried to summon the next, about food for worms, but it evaded me.

“I like you,” he announced; and, making a fist, he punched me in the chest.

It was the local token of fellowship. Gravely, feeling the sudden fierce warmth of instant friendship—children’s friendships being made like matches, with a scratch and a flare—I punched him back.

He wriggled up on the step, suddenly serious.

“Yer won’t tell?” he demanded.

I had no notion what I was not to tell, but a secret oath was obviously required of me.

“Say ‘I swear.’”

“I swear.”

This pleased me: it made him seem like a hero out of my books, only dirtier and nearer.

“Good-bye, cockey,” he said.

Then he turned back, fished in his pocket, and thrust an old knife into my hand. Without another word he ran off across the mud, that odd hopping gait carrying him towards the river.

I watched him, bewildered. He stumbled where the mud turned softer and deeper; he sank nearly to his knees, wrenched himself free, went on. I shouted, but he did not look back. Every few yards he sank again, floundered forward again, like something being slowly swallowed and refusing, with stubborn little kicks, to be eaten.

Then, still some distance from the water, he fell on his face and did not rise.

I saw his arms beating—feebler, feebler—until the oily slime closed over him. For a moment there was only a faint heaving beneath the mud, as if the earth itself were chewing. Then even that ceased.

It was late when I reached home, and fortunately my father and mother were still out. I did not tell anyone what I had seen; I had sworn not to, and the oath felt heavier than the knife in my pocket. In time the incident haunted me less and less until, as often happens, it became something I could order about in my mind—summon, dismiss, place on a shelf.

But it cured me, for a while, of my taste for those silent streets. From behind still walls I would hear again that long cry; down the narrow vistas I would see that writhing figure—like an animated gingerbread man—hopping, springing, falling.

Yet in the more crowded streets another trouble waited for me—one more tangible, and more cruel because it came with laughter.

Have you ever seen a flock of sparrows round some crumbs, and suddenly the rest fall upon one? There is a ridiculous hubbub, tiny wings and heads tossed, a babel of shrill chirps. It looks comical.

“Spiteful little imps,” people say, amused.

So good-tempered men and women would call out, laughing, when boys set upon a single boy.

“There go those young devils chivvying that poor little beggar again; ought to be ashamed of theirselves.”

But oh, the anguish of the poor little beggar.

Reduced to facts, what was it? Jeers that broke no bones; pinches, kicks, slaps; at worst a few hard knocks. Yet the dread beforehand made every street a trap. Terror hid round each corner, waiting for me like a boy with his hands in his pockets and a grin already loaded.

The half-dozen crouched over marbles—had they seen me? The boy whistling outside the print shop—would I get past without his noticing, or would he swing round and give the shrill whoop that called them from doorways as if the very bricks were breeding tormentors?

Then the shame when caught and cornered: the grinning faces that stopped to watch; the jokes of passers-by, treating it as no more than sparrows squabbling; worst of all, the rare pity, which landed like cold porridge on pride.

And afterwards, released at last, I would dart away, followed by laughter and shouted taunts. Every eye seemed turned to watch me shrink by, my whole small carcass shaking with dry sobs of bitterness and rage.

If only I could have turned and faced them! Pain I could bear; I knew it. Any number of stand-up fights would have hurt less than the hot choking misery of being hunted. For in my mind I was the comrade of a hundred heroes. Nightly I rode with Richard Coeur de Lion; I couched my lance against Sir Lancelot himself and was not wholly unsuccessful; damsels in distress looked to me as naturally as kettles look to the fire.

And yet, when the whoop went up, the joyous shout died on my pale lips, and the thing that scattered to the wind was myself, slipping into the first opening like a rabbit that knows its own burrow.

Sometimes, blood boiling, I would turn, determined to go back and at all risk defy them—prove to myself I was no coward. But before I had retraced a dozen paces the whole scene would leap up in my imagination: the laughing crowd, the halting passers-by, the spiteful little faces wherever I turned; and I would creep home instead, stealing up to my room, and cry my heart out in the dark upon my bed.
