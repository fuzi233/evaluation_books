West Country Song.

The following morning, Amyas Leigh was nowhere to be found. He had not fled to drown his sorrows by the Torridge, nor ridden off in a frenzy of despair. Frank discovered that he had simply departed for Stow to visit Sir Richard Grenville. His mother, sensing the truth immediately—that he had gone to seek a commission in the Irish army—dispatched Frank to retrieve him and urge him to reconsider.

Frank mounted his horse and rode the ten miles to Stow, but finding no inn on the road, he turned back toward Clovelly Court to seek hospitality from Mr. Cary, the local squire.

Entering the long, dark hall of the court, where the walls were lined with heavy wainscoting, Frank found the scene chaotic. Seated at the long table was the massive figure of Amyas, alternately burying his face in a pasty and the pasty in his face, his grief seemingly sharpening his appetite. Opposite him, kneeling on the bench with elbows on the table, was young Will Cary, laying down the law fiercely in a low voice.

"Hillo! lad," cried Amyas, "come here and rescue me from this fire-eater. I believe he intends to kill me, or at least someone else."

"Ah! Mr. Frank," said Will Cary, regarding the visitor with the high honor due to a young gentleman of fashion, "welcome. I was longing for your advice on half a dozen matters. Sit down and eat; there is the ale."

"None so early, thank you."

"Ah no!" Amyas muttered, burying his head in the tankard, then mimicking Frank's tone. "Avoid strong ale in the morning. It heats the blood, thickens the animal spirits, and clouds the pure reason with frenzied idols. Eh? Young Plato, young Daniel, come to judgment! Yet, though I cannot see the bottom of the tankard, I see plainly enough that Will shall not fight."

"Shall I not, eh? Who says that? Mr. Frank, I appeal to you; only listen."

"We are in the judgment-seat," Frank said, settling into the pasty. "Proceed, appellant."

"Well, I was telling Amyas that Tom Coffin of Portledge... I will stand him no longer."

"Let him be," said Amyas. "He could stand very well by himself when I last saw him."

"Plague on you, hold your tongue. Does he have any right to look at me as he does whenever I pass?"

"That depends on how he looks; a cat may look at a king, provided she doesn't mistake him for a mouse."

"Oh, I know how he looks, and what he means. He shall stop, or I will stop him. And the other day, when I spoke of Rose Salterne—" "Ah!" groaned Frank, "Here comes the apple again!"—"(never mind what I said) he burst out laughing in my face. Is that not a fair quarrel? And I know he wrote a sonnet and sent it to her via a market woman. What right has he to write sonnets when I cannot? It is not fair play, Mr. Frank, or I am a Jew, a Spaniard, and a Papist; it is not!" Will smote the table until the plates danced.

"My dear knight of the burning pestle," Frank said, suddenly shifting his tone to one of winning sweetness, "do not imagine I cannot feel for you, or that I, like you, have not known the stings of love and the bitterer sting of jealousy. But oh, Mr. Cary, does it not seem an awful waste to squander that divine wrath—which Plato calls the root of all virtues—upon your own petty quarrel? That wrath has been given to you to spend in the service of her whom all bad souls fear and all virtuous souls adore: our peerless queen. Who dares, while she rules England, claim his sword or courage as his own, rather than hers? Are there no Spaniards to conquer, no wild Irish to deliver from oppression, that two gentlemen of Devon can find no better place to test their blades than in each other's hearts?"

"By heaven!" cried Amyas. "Frank speaks like a book. I think Christian gentlemen may leave love quarrels to bulls and rams."

"And that the heir of Clovelly," Frank smiled, "may find nobler examples than the stags in his own deer park."

"Well," said Will, penitently, "you are a great scholar, Mr. Frank, and you speak like one; but gentlemen must fight sometimes, or where would be their honor?"

"I speak," Frank said, with quiet pride, "not merely as a scholar, but as a gentleman who has fought ere now, and to whom it has happened, Mr. Cary, to kill a man (on whose soul may God have mercy). But it is my pride to remember that I have never yet fought in my own quarrel, and my trust in God that I never shall. For as there is nothing more noble than to fight for those we love, so to fight for ourselves is forbidden to a Christian man, unless refusal means utter loss of life or honor; and even then, it is better to overcome evil with good."

"And I can tell you, Will," said Amyas, "I am not troubled by ghosts; but when I cut off the Frenchman's head, I told myself, 'If that braggart had been slandering me instead of her gracious majesty, I should expect to see that head on my pillow every night.'"

"God forbid!" said Will, shuddering. "But what shall I do? To the market tomorrow I will go, even if it were choke-full of Coffins and a ghost in each coffin."

"Leave the matter to me," said Amyas. "I have a device, as does Scholar Frank. If there is a quarrel in the market tomorrow, see if I do not—"

"Well, you are two good fellows," said Will. "Let us have another tankard."

"And drink the health of Mr. Coffin and all gallant lads of the North," Frank added. "Now to my business. I must take this runaway youth home to his mother; if he will not go quietly, I have orders to carry him across my saddle."

"I hope your nag has a strong back," said Amyas. "But I must go on to see Sir Richard, Frank. It is all very well to jest, but my mind is made up."

"Stop," said Cary. "You must stay here tonight; first, for fellowship's sake; and next, because I want the advice of our Phoenix here, our oracle, our paragon. There, Mr. Frank, can you construe that for me? Speak low, though; there comes my father."

"Eh, company here?" the old gentleman asked, settling onto a great bench inside the chimney. "Young men, you are always welcome. How is your good mother, Frank? Where have I been, Will? Round the house-farm, looking at the beeves. That sheeted heifer of Prowse's is all wrong; her coat stares like a hedgehog's. Tell Jewell to bring her in before night. And then up the forty acres; sprang two coveys, picked a leash. The Irish hawk flies as wild as any haggard and will never make a bird. I had to hand her to Tom and take the little peregrine. Give me a Clovelly hawk against the world, after all! Heigh ho, I am very hungry! Half-past twelve and dinner not served? What, Master Amyas, spoiling your appetite with strong ale? Better have tried sack, lad; have some now with me."

The worthy old gentleman finished his oration, settled himself by the warm peat-ashes, and placed his hawk on a perch over his head, while his son pulled off his boots amid warnings to take care of his corns.

"Come, Master Amyas, a pint of white wine and sugar, and a bit of a shoeing-horn to it ere we dine. Some pickled prawns, or a rasher off the coals?"

"Thank you," quoth Amyas. "I have drunk a mort of outlandish liquors in the last three years, better and worse, and never found aught to come up to good ale, which needs neither shoeing-horn before nor after, but takes care of itself and all honest stomachs."

"You speak like a book, boy," said old Cary. "And after all, what a plague comes of these newfangled hot wines and aqua vitae, which have come in since the wars, maddening the brains and fevering the blood?"

"I fear we have not seen the end of that yet," said Frank. "My friends write from the Netherlands that our men are falling into a swinish trick of swilling like the Hollanders. Heaven grant they may not bring the fashion home."

"A man must drink, they say, or die of the ague in those vile swamps," said Amyas. "When they get home here, they will not need it."

"Heaven grant it," said Frank. "I should be sorry to see Devonshire a drunken county; and there are many of our men out there with Mr. Champernoun."

"Ah," said Cary, "there, as in Ireland, we are proving her majesty's saying true, that Devonshire is her right hand, and the young children thereof like the arrows in the hand of the giant."

"They may well be," said his son, "when some of them are giants themselves, like my tall school-fellow opposite."

"He will be up and doing again presently," said old Cary.

"And that I shall," quoth Amyas. "I have been devising brave deeds; and see in the distance enchanters to be bound, dragons choked, empires conquered, though not in Holland."

"You do?" asked Will, sharply, suspecting more was meant than met the ear.

"Yes," said Amyas, turning serious. "I go to what Raleigh calls the Land of the Nymphs. Another month, I hope, will see me abroad in Ireland."

"Abroad? Call it rather at home," said old Cary. "For it is full of Devon men from end to end. George Bourchier from Tawstock has the army in Munster; Warham St. Leger is marshal; George Carew is with Lord Grey of Wilton. After the defeat last year, when that villain Desmond cut off Herbert and Price, the companies were made up with six hundred Devon men, and Arthur Fortescue at their head. So the old county holds her head as proudly in the Land of Ire as she does in the Low Countries and the Spanish Main."

"And where," asked Amyas, "is Davils of Marsland, who used to teach me how to catch trout when I stayed at Stow? Is he in Ireland too?"

"Ah, my lad," said Mr. Cary, "that is a sad story. I thought all England had known it."

"You forget, sir, I am a stranger. Surely he is not dead?"

"Murdered foully, lad! Murdered like a dog, and by the man whom he had treated as his son, and who pretended, the false knave, to call him father."

"His blood is avenged?" said Amyas, fiercely.

"No, by heaven, not yet! Stay, don't cry out again. I am getting old—I must tell my story my own way. It was last July. Over comes to Ireland Saunders, one of those Jesuit foxes, as the Pope's legate, with money, bulls, and a banner hallowed by the Pope. With him was James Fitzmaurice, the same fellow who had sworn on his knees to Perrott in Kilmallock to be a true liegeman to Queen Elizabeth, confirmed by all his saints and a world of Irish howling. Well, he had been practicing with the King of France but got nothing but laughter, so he went over to the Most Catholic King, promising to join Ireland to Spain and set up Popery again. He fits him out and sends him off on such another errand as Stukely's—though I will say, for the honor of Devon, if Stukely lived like a fool, he died like an honest man."

"Sir Thomas Stukely dead too?" said Amyas.

"Wait a while, lad, and you shall have that tragedy afterwards. Well, where was I? Fitzmaurice and the Jesuits land at Smerwick with three ships, choose a place for a fort, bless it with holy water, and purify it from the stain of heretic dominion. Meanwhile, a Courtenay of Haccombe, lying at anchor near by in a ship of war, cuts out the three ships and cuts off the Dons from the sea. John and James Desmond, with some small rabble, go over to the Spaniards. Earl Desmond will not join them, nor fight them, but stands by to take the winning side. Then in comes poor Davils, sent down by the Lord Deputy to charge Desmond and his brothers in the queen's name to assault the Spaniards. Folks say it was rash of his lordship; but I say, what could be better done? Every one knows there was never a stouter or shrewder soldier than Davils; and the young Desmonds used to look on him as their father. But he found out what it was to trust Englishmen turned Irish. The Desmonds found out on a sudden that the Dons were such desperate Paladins that it was madness to meddle, though they were five to one. Poor Davils, seeing there was no fight in them, goes back for help and sleeps that night at Tralee.
