Desire sets the sails, and circumstance merely provides the wind to catch them. While Grandcourt rode his magnificent black stallion, Yarico, with his groom trailing behind on Criterion, enjoying the pleasant journey from Diplow to Offendene, Gwendolen sat before her mirror. Her mother was busy gathering the long, light-brown tresses she had been meticulously brushing.

"Only gather it up easily and make a coil, mamma," Gwendolen instructed.

"Gwen, let me bring you some earrings," Mrs. Davilow offered, as they both gazed into the glass.

It was impossible for either of them not to notice that Gwendolen's eyes shone with a brightness absent lately, and that a shadow seemed lifted from her face, restoring its lines to a placid youthfulness. The mother drew an encouraging inference, her voice brightening.

"Do you want your earrings?"

"No, mamma. I won't wear any ornaments. I'll put on my black silk. Black is the only attire suitable when one intends to refuse an offer," Gwendolen replied, offering one of her characteristic smiles before rising to shed her dressing gown.

"Suppose the offer isn't made after all?" Mrs. Davilow ventured, with a sly glint in her eye.

"Then that will simply be because I refused it beforehand," Gwendolen countered proudly. "It amounts to the same thing."

There was a small, defiant toss of her head as she spoke. When she descended the stairs in her long black robes, she possessed that firm poise of head and elastic grace that had recently been missing, like vitality returning to a parched plant. Her mother watched her, thinking, *She is quite herself again. It must be the joy of his coming. Could her mind truly be made up against him?*

Gwendolen would have been furious if that thought had been spoken aloud, especially since, over the last twenty hours with scant sleep, she had been torn between alternating images and arguments regarding her potential marriage to Grandcourt. The resolution she had formed earlier had slipped from her grasp; the constant ebb and flow of counterbalancing thoughts, born of conflicting desires, had left her in a state where no conclusion appeared fixed. She intended to refuse Grandcourt. From the moment she received his letter, she had decided to reject him. Yet, this very resolve forced her to confront the unwelcome realities head-on, stripping away some of her awe. Her imagination, fueled by uncertainty, filled in gaps in her knowledge, reshaping what she knew into various possibilities. By staring at a doubtful object with a constructive mind, one can conjure twenty different forms.

Her vague hesitations from the previous day at the Whispering Stones now counted for nothing, submerged beneath a final, overwhelming repulsion. *If it hadn't been for that day at Cardell Chase,* she told herself, *there would be no barrier to marrying Grandcourt.* On that day and those following, she had not reasoned or weighed options; she had acted on an impulsive force against which all questioning was drowned out like a whisper against a torrent. That impulse stemmed not only from maidenly pride and jealousy, nor solely from the shock of witnessing another woman's misery, but from a profound dread of wrongdoing. Though vague and distant from her daily life, this fear was potent. Whatever aligned with being a "lady" posed no scruple for her, but from the dim realm of the disgraceful, the wrong, the guilty, she recoiled with a mixture of pride and terror. Apart from shame alone, her instinct would have placed any deliberate harm inflicted on another squarely within the category of guilt.

But now—did she truly know the extent of the situation concerning Mrs. Glasher and her children? She had given a sort of promise, saying, *"I will not interfere with your wishes."* Would another woman marrying Grandcourt actually be the decisive obstacle to those wishes, or cause real injury to the mother and son? Might it not be just as well, or even better, for Grandcourt to marry? After all, what couldn't a woman achieve once married, if she knew how to assert herself? Here, however, lay purely constructive imagination. Gwendolen possessed as accurate a concept of marriage—the mutual influences, demands, and duties of man and woman—as she did of magnetic currents or storm laws.

*"Mamma managed badly,"* was her summary of her mother's experience; she assured herself she would manage quite differently. The trials of matrimony were the last topic Mrs. Davilow wished to explore deeply with this daughter.

*I wonder what mamma and my uncle would say if they knew about Mrs. Glasher!* Gwendolen mused internally, debating silently. Not that she imagined revealing it, even if she weren't bound by silence. *I wonder what anyone would say; or what they'd say to Mr. Grandcourt marrying someone else and fathering other children!* Considering what "anyone" would think liberated her from the difficulty of judging where everything remained obscure to her when emotion had ceased to be decisive. She merely collected her memories, which convinced her that society viewed illegitimate children as more deserving of shunning and deprivation of advantage than illegitimate fathers. The verdict of "the public" seemed to absolve her of significant concern for Mrs. Glasher and her offspring.

Yet, there was another dimension to their impact on her. Public opinion could not erase a feeling best described initially as indignation and loathing—to be expected to unite herself with an exhausted life, heavy with backward secrets that must have been acutely felt by the woman involved, yet alien to her own existence. True, the question of love on her part had played almost no role in her assessment of Grandcourt. The desirability of marriage had always seemed rooted in feelings other than love; to be enamored was the province of the man initiating the advances. Gwendolen found no fault with Grandcourt's manner of being enamored until she glimpsed his past, which she resented as a deliberate affront. His advances to *her* had been calculated, and she felt a retrospective disgust toward them. Perhaps other men's lives were similarly constructed—filled with secrets that reduced the innocent assumptions of the women they sought to marry to a farce, performed while they laughed in their sleeves.

These feelings of disgust and indignation had sunk deep. Although subsequent troubling experiences in recent weeks had dulled them from passionate fire to mere memory, it was chiefly their lingering resonance that kept her steadfast in her understanding: she would not accept Grandcourt. She had never intended to form a new determination; she had only been contemplating what might be thought or said. If anything could have swayed her, it would have been the prospect of making life easy for "poor mamma"—a genuine temptation. But no! She was going to refuse him.

Meanwhile, the anticipation of refusing him invigorated her. She held the white reins once more; a fresh current flowed through her frame, reviving her from the defeated consciousness left by her interview with Klesmer. She was no longer going to seek validation of her capabilities; she was going to exercise her power. Was this what caused her heart to beat anxiously when she heard the horse's hooves on the gravel? When Miss Merry opened the door to announce that Grandcourt was in the drawing-room?

The hours of preparation and the triumph of the situation seemed futile; she might as well have encountered Grandcourt suddenly amidst her despair. Walking into the drawing-room required concentrating every ounce of energy into self-control, allowing her to appear gravely gracious. She extended her hand to him and answered his inquiry about her health in a voice as low and languid as his own.

A moment later, seated on two wreath-painted chairs—Gwendolen upright with lowered eyelids, Grandcourt sitting two yards away, leaning an arm over the back of his chair and studying her while holding his hat in his left hand—any observer would have concluded they were in a suspended moment of romantic tension. Indeed, the courting had begun. She already felt herself being wooed by this silent man seated at an agreeable distance, surrounded by a subtle atmosphere reminiscent of attar of roses, with an attention bent entirely on her. He, too, considered himself to be wooing. He was not a man to believe his presence carried no weight, and he was precisely the type to derive the utmost spice from a girl he had not yet deemed fully calculable.

"I was disappointed not to find you at Leubronn," he began, his usual broken drawl tinged with a shade of amorous laziness. "The place was intolerable without you. A mere kennel of a location. Don't you agree?"

"I cannot judge what it would be without myself," Gwendolen replied, turning her eyes onto him with a recovering sense of mischief. "*With* myself, I like it well enough to have stayed longer, if I could. But I was obliged to return home due to family troubles."

"It was very cruel of you to go to Leubronn," Grandcourt remarked, ignoring the troubles—a detail Gwendolen, for reasons she barely understood, wished to clarify immediately. "You must have known it would ruin everything. You knew you were the heart and soul of everything that transpired. Are you quite reckless about me?"

It would be impossible to say "yes" in a tone taken seriously; equally impossible to say "no." What else could she say? In her difficulty, she lowered her eyelids again and blushed across her face and neck. Grandcourt observed her in a new phase, interpreting it as a display of inclination.
