It is an odd trade for a man of my years—fifty-five last birthday—to set his hand to paper and try to make a history out of what has mostly been dust, sweat, gunpowder, and bad water. A history ought to have the comfort of a hearth in it: a pot that simmers to its conclusion, a table laid, the same faces returning like seasons. My own life has been more like a trek-knife—always out, always cleaned and put away again, never quite done with blood.

And yet here I am in Durban, with my left leg aching as if the lion’s tooth were still in it. I have been trading, hunting, fighting, and mining since I was scarcely old enough to know the taste of my own bread from another man’s. Only eight months ago I made what folks call a “pile”—how large I cannot rightly say, for money has a way of slipping into corners, like mealie-meal in a wagon-crack. But I would not go through the last fifteen or sixteen months again for it; not even if an angel promised me safe passage out the far end. I am a timid man, and I do not relish violence, and I have grown near sick of adventure.

Then why write?

Because Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good asked me.

Because my leg keeps me housebound and short-tempered, so that if I do not give my thoughts a road to travel they will trample my mind like oxen in a mealie field. Ever since that confounded lion got hold of me I have had this trouble. Wounds close, and yet, when the weather turns to the very season of the mauling, they wake again and burn. There must be something in a lion’s mouth besides teeth—some poison or memory that will not die. It is a hard thing, too, when one has shot five-and-sixty lions, that the sixty-sixth should take to chewing one’s leg as if it were tobacco. It is disorderly, and I am an orderly man; I like the routine of the world to keep its promises.

And because I want my boy Harry—over there in London, in the hospital, learning to cut and stitch and name what is inside a man—to have something with more life in it than dead flesh and disinfectant. There are smells in a hospital that cling like wet cloth: carbolic, boiled linen, cold metal. A young fellow needs, now and then, the scent of open air and a story that bites back.

Lastly—because this is the strangest tale I know. There is no woman in it to speak of, unless you count Foulata; and Gagaoola—if she was a woman, and not a wicked old spirit with skin stretched over bones—was nearer a hundred than anything decent, so I do not count her either. There is not a petticoat in the whole business. It is mostly men, thirst, old names, and the kind of silence that makes you hear your own blood moving.

That is my yoke, and it sits stiff on the neck. But “sutjes, sutjes,” as the Boers say—softly does it. A strong team will pull through in the end, if the oxen are not poor. You can do nothing with poor oxen, and you can do nothing with a poor tongue either.

I was tempted to begin like a deposition—“I, Allan Quatermain, of Durban, Natal, Gentleman, make oath and say”—the same heading I used before a magistrate about poor Khiva and Ventvögel’s deaths. But a book is not a court, and besides, what is a gentleman? I have known natives who were gentlemen in the only sense that matters, and I have known white men fresh from home, with money in their belts and hardness in their mouths, who were not. The words we use are often the whips we do not notice in our hands. I do not like certain old names people fling about as if they were harmless; they sour the mouth, and once a man learns to speak of another as less than a man, the next step comes too easily. Still, I was born a gentleman, and I have tried—God knows I have tried—to behave like one, though I have lived most of my days as a wandering trader and hunter.

I have killed men, yes. But never for sport, never for pleasure, never for the mere taste of power. Only when there was no other way to keep my own life or another’s. The Almighty gave us breath; I suppose He meant us to defend it. I have never stolen, either—though once I cheated a Kaffir out of cattle, and the thing has sat on my conscience like bad meal. He had done me a dirty turn, true, but a wrong does not season a wrong into right; it only makes a stew you cannot swallow without remembering what went into it.

It was about eighteen months ago that I first met Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good. I had been beyond Bamangwato elephant-hunting and had the kind of luck that turns a man’s stomach: spoor that vanished like smoke, waterholes that tasted of rot, and then fever—hot as a forge in the bones, cold as river stones in the sweat. When I was fit to sit a wagon without falling off, I trekked down to the Diamond Fields, sold what ivory I had, sold the wagon and oxen too, paid off my hunters, and took the post-cart to the Cape.

Cape Town is a place where a man can be overcharged for a bed and still be told, politely, that he ought to be grateful. I stayed a week, saw the botanical gardens—useful, those, for there is more future in a seed than in a speech—and saw the new Houses of Parliament, which I fancy will grow plenty of talk and little else, like mealies planted in sand.

Then I booked myself on the Dunkeld to Natal. She lay at the docks, waiting for the Edinburgh Castle due in from England. I took my berth, went aboard, and that afternoon the Natal passengers from the Edinburgh Castle transhipped. We weighed and put to sea.

Among those who came aboard were two men who caught my eye at once, not because they sought attention, but because the world itself seemed to make room around them.

One was about thirty, and built as if the Creator had begun with a broad chest and long arms and only afterwards remembered the rest. Yellow hair, thick yellow beard, grey eyes set deep, and a face cut clean as if from a hard-grained wood. He looked like one of those old sea-raiders you see in pictures—men with horn cups in their fists and iron on their shoulders, laughing at storms. I thought of a modern Dane I once met who cheated me of ten pounds, and then of an ancient Dane I’d only ever seen in an illustration—white Zulus, I called them in my mind, for what else are they but a nation of tall fighters, only born under a colder sky? As he stood there by the companion-ladder, I could almost smell tar and salt and hear the hollow boom of a drum in some long-ago hall. Afterwards I learned that his blood did run Danish, which is a curious thing; a man’s past will speak through his bones even when his mouth is shut.

The other man was stout, darker, and so neat that he looked as if he had been shaved and polished like a parade gun. I knew, without knowing why, that he was Navy. Perhaps it was the set of his shoulders—as if he measured space by deck-planks—or the way he took the ship’s movement into his body, instead of fighting it. I have gone shooting with several naval men in my time, and for all their fondness for language that would blister paint, I have found them the best fellows alive. If anyone asks me again what a gentleman is, I shall answer: a Royal Naval officer is, in a general way—though there are black sheep everywhere. I believe it is the wide sea and the wind of God that scours them clean and keeps their minds from turning small.

His name, I found from the passenger list, was Good—Captain John Good. He was medium height and broad, clean-shaven to the last hair, and wore an eye-glass in his right eye that seemed to belong there like an extra organ. There was no string to it; he only removed it to wipe it, as carefully as a man wiping the rim of a good wineglass. Later I discovered he did not sleep in it—as I first suspected—but put it in his trouser pocket at night, along with his false teeth. He had two beautiful sets, which, considering my own are no great affair, has often tempted me into the tenth commandment.

By evening the weather turned dirty. A keen breeze sprang up off land and brought with it one of those Scotch mists that do not fall so much as press themselves into your face and clothes, dampening everything—rope, wood, skin, temper. The Dunkeld is a flat-bottomed punt, and going up light she rolled like a drunken man trying to walk a straight line. It seemed she would go clean over, yet she never did. There is a sort of stubbornness in ships, the way there is in old oxen: they will lurch and groan, but they will keep their feet.

Walking was impossible, so I stood near the engines where it was warm. The thump and hiss there had a steady comfort to it—an honest sound, like a mortar and pestle in a kitchen at dawn. A ship has its own music: the deep breathing of steam, the slap of water, the complaining creak of timber. Out at sea, away from carts and stamping yards and the harsh clatter of town-work, those sounds settle a man. There is less of the modern racket that makes thinking difficult—no endless hammering, no shriek of iron, no cheap music shouted at you by streets. The ocean keeps its own order, and if you listen properly you can hear where you are in the world.

Opposite me a pendulum was fixed, swinging back and forth as the vessel rolled, marking the angle at each lurch.

“That pendulum’s wrong; it is not properly weighted,” said a testy voice at my shoulder.

I turned and saw Captain Good.

“Indeed,” I said, “and what makes you think so?”

“Think so? I don’t think at all,” he snapped. “Why, there—” as she righted herself after a roll—“if the ship had really rolled to the degree that thing pointed to, she would never have rolled again, that’s all. But it’s just like these merchant skippers; they are always so confoundedly careless.”

At that moment the dinner-bell rang, and I was not sorry. It is a dreadful thing to hear a naval man let himself loose on merchant skippers, unless you are equally fond of hearing merchant skippers speak candidly of naval men. Captain Good and I went down together, and found Sir Henry already seated. He and Good were placed together, and I sat opposite.

The saloon smelled of damp wool and varnished wood, with the stew’s heavy warmth trying to do battle against sea-air that had crept in on everyone’s coats. There is a particular sound at a ship’s dinner: cutlery clicking in time with the roll, glasses sliding a hair’s breadth and being steadied, men’s voices rising and falling like waves. It is a small domestic world afloat, pretending it is not surrounded by deep water.

Good and I soon fell into talk—shooting, veldt, beasts—and he asked questions in his quick way, the way a man checks the knots in a rope: tug, tug, tug, to see where it will give. I answered as well as I could. Presently he got onto elephants.

“Ah, sir,” called out somebody near me, “you’ve reached the right man for that. Hunter Quatermain should be able to tell you about elephants if anybody can.”

Sir Henry, who had been listening quietly, started as if the name were a hand laid sudden on his shoulder.

“Excuse me, sir,” he said, leaning forward, his voice low and deep, as suited his great lungs. “Excuse me, but is your name Allan Quatermain?”

I said that it was.

He made no further remark, but I heard him mutter “fortunate” into his beard, as though he had been walking in the dark and found a door.

After dinner, as we were leaving the saloon, Sir Henry strolled up and asked if I would come to his cabin and smoke a pipe. I accepted, and he led the way to the Dunkeld deck cabin—a very good one. It had once been two cabins, but when Sir Garnet Wolseley, or one of those big swells, travelled down the coast, they knocked out the partition and never put it up again. There was a sofa, and a little table in front of it. Sir Henry sent the steward for a bottle of whisky.

We sat down, lit our pipes, and the lamp was lit too, the flame steadying itself against the ship’s motion like a patient cook coaxing a reluctant fire.

“Mr. Quatermain,” said Sir Henry, when the steward had gone, “the year before last about this time, you were, I believe, at a place called Bamangwato, to the north of the Transvaal.”

“I was,” I answered, surprised that a gentleman should be so well acquainted with my movements, which I had never considered worthy of notice beyond the price of ivory.

“You were trading there, were you not?” put in Captain Good.

“I was. I took up a wagon-load of goods, made a camp outside the settlement, and stopped till I had sold them.”

Sir Henry sat opposite in a Madeira chair, his great arms resting on the table. He lifted his eyes and fixed them on my face. There was anxiety there—something held tight behind the grey.

“Did you happen to meet a man called Neville there?”

“Oh yes,” I said. “He outspanned alongside of me for a fortnight, resting his oxen before going on to the interior. I had a letter from a lawyer some months back asking if I knew what had become of him, and I answered as best I could.”

“Yes,” said Sir Henry. “Your letter was forwarded to me. You said the gentleman called Neville left Bamangwato at the beginning of May in a wagon, with a driver, a voorlooper, and a Kaffir hunter called Jim. You said he meant to trek, if possible, as far as Inyati—the extreme trading post in the Matabele country—sell his wagon there, and go on foot. You also said he did sell the wagon, because six months after you saw it in the possession of a Portuguese trader, who told you he had bought it at Inyati from a white man whose name he had forgotten; and that he believed the white man and his native servant had gone off into the interior on a shooting trip.”

“Yes,” I said again.

There was a pause in which you could hear the ship’s bones talking: the creak of wood, the deep pulse of engines, the faint rattle of a glass on the table as she rolled. A man’s secrets often come out in pauses; speech is only the knife, silence is the board you cut on.

“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said suddenly, “I suppose you know or can guess nothing more of the reasons of my—of Mr. Neville’s journey northward, or as to what point that journey was directed?”

“I heard something,” I began, and stopped. It was not a subject I cared to season with talk. Some matters, once spoken aloud, are like certain native words—full of a taste you cannot translate, and once your mouth has shaped them you cannot pretend you did not.

Sir Henry and Captain Good looked at each other. Good nodded once, sharply, as if giving a command.

“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry went on, “I am going to tell you a story, and ask your advice—and perhaps your assistance. The agent who forwarded me your letter told me I might rely on it implicitly, as you were,” he said, choosing the phrase carefully, “well known and universally respected in Natal, and especially noted for your discretion.”

I bowed and took whisky and water, partly to hide my confusion. I am a modest man, and praise sits awkwardly on me, like a borrowed coat.

“My brother,” said Sir Henry, and his voice thickened on the word, “Mr. Neville was my brother.”

“Oh,” I said, and started; for now I knew whom he had reminded me of the first moment I saw him. Neville had been smaller, darker-bearded, but the eyes—those grey eyes with the same keen look—were the same; and the bones beneath the face were the same family’s.

“He was,” Sir Henry continued, “my only and younger brother, and until five years ago I do not suppose we were ever a month apart. But about five years ago a misfortune befell us, as sometimes happens in families. We quarrelled bitterly, and I behaved unjustly to my brother in my anger.”

Captain Good nodded his head vigorously to himself, so that I noticed it even as the ship gave a big roll and the looking-glass opposite us swung up till it was almost over our heads.

“As you may know,” Sir Henry went on, “if a man dies intestate and has no property but land—real property, it is called in England—it descends to the eldest son. It so happened that when we quarrelled our father died intestate. He had put off making his will until it was too late. The result was that my brother, who had not been brought up to any profession, was left without a penny. Of course it would have been my duty to provide for him, but at the time the quarrel was so bitter that I did not—” he drew breath, and the lamp made a small, steady halo on his beard “—to my shame I say it—offer to do anything. Not that I grudged him justice; but I waited for him to make advances, and he made none.”

“I am sorry to trouble you with this,” he added, “but I must, to make things clear—eh, Good?”

“Quite so, quite so,” said Captain Good. “Mr. Quatermain will, I am sure, keep this history to himself.”

“Of course,” I said. Discretion is like salt: it ought to be there, but if you can taste it, there is too much.

“Well,” Sir Henry continued, “my brother had a few hundred pounds at his bank. Without saying anything to me, he drew out that paltry sum, took the name of Neville, and started for South Africa in the wild hope of making a fortune. I learned this afterwards. Three years passed, and I heard nothing of him, though I wrote several times. Doubtless the letters never reached him. But as time went on I grew more and more troubled. I found out, Mr. Quatermain, that blood is thicker than water.”

“That’s true,” I said, thinking of my boy Harry and the tug a father feels even across oceans.

“I found out,” Sir Henry said, “that I would have given half my fortune to know that my brother George—the only relation I possess—was safe and well, and that I should see him again.”

“But you never did, Curtis,” Captain Good broke in, quickly, glancing at Sir Henry’s face as though to steady him by naming the hurt.

Sir Henry’s mouth tightened. Then he went on.

“As time passed I became more anxious to learn if my brother was alive or dead; and if alive, to bring him home. I set enquiries on foot, and your letter was one result. So far as it went it was satisfactory—it showed that until lately George was alive—but it did not go far enough. So, to cut the matter short, I made up my mind to come out and look for him myself, and Captain Good was so kind as to come with me.”

“Yes,” said Good. “Nothing else to do, you see. Turned out by my Lords of the Admiralty to starve on half pay, with the barren honour of a commander’s rank because it was ‘impossible’ I should be promoted. And now perhaps, sir—” and he swung his eye-glass a fraction, fixing me with it “—you will tell us what you know or have heard of the gentleman called Neville.”
