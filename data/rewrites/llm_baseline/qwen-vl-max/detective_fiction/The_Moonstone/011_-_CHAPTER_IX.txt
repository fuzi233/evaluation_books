June twenty-first, the day of Miss Rachel’s birthday, began with a cloudy and unsettled sky at dawn, but by noon the clouds had broken, and the sun shone brightly. In the servants’ hall, we marked the occasion as we always did—by presenting our small gifts to Miss Rachel, accompanied by the annual speech delivered by me, the head servant. Like the Queen’s opening of Parliament, my address followed the same pattern year after year: familiar words, carefully rehearsed, yet met each time with eager anticipation, as if it were something entirely new. When it was finally spoken, and revealed to be no more than the usual repetition, there would be a mild grumble—but then, already, hopes would turn toward next year’s version. A simple people, easy to manage, whether in the halls of government or in the kitchen. That’s the lesson.

After breakfast, Mr. Franklin and I held a private meeting about the Moonstone. The time had come to remove it from the bank at Frizinghall and place it in Miss Rachel’s hands. Whether Mr. Franklin had once again attempted to win his cousin’s affection and been rebuffed—or whether his restless nights were deepening the odd inconsistencies in his nature—I couldn’t say. But one thing was clear: on this morning, he was not himself. He vacillated wildly over the Diamond, changing his mind every few minutes. For my part, I clung to the facts. Nothing had occurred to justify alarming my lady; nor could anything alter the legal duty now resting upon Mr. Franklin to deliver the jewel to his cousin. That was my position—and though he twisted and turned, he ultimately had to accept it. We agreed that he would ride to Frizinghall after lunch to retrieve the Diamond, likely accompanied by Mr. Godfrey and the two young Ablewhite sisters on the return journey.

With that settled, Mr. Franklin returned to Miss Rachel. They spent the entire morning and much of the afternoon decorating the front door—Penelope standing by, mixing paints as directed. My lady kept coming in and out, handkerchief pressed to her nose (for they used an excessive amount of Mr. Franklin’s vehicle that day), trying in vain to pull the artists away from their work. It wasn’t until three o’clock that they finally took off their aprons, released Penelope (who looked decidedly worse for wear), and cleaned themselves up. But they had achieved what they set out to do—the door was finished, and they were proud of it. The griffins, cupids, and other figures were, I must admit, magnificent to look at—though so numerous, entangled in floral designs, and so absurdly posed that one felt their chaos linger long after the viewing ended. And if I mention that Penelope ended her morning by being sick in the back-kitchen, it is not out of malice toward the paint. No indeed! It ceased to smell once dry. And if Art demands such sacrifices—even from my own daughter—then let Art have them.

Mr. Franklin grabbed a quick bite from the luncheon table and rode off to Frizinghall, telling my lady he was escorting his cousins. In truth, he was going to fetch the Moonstone—a fact known only to him and me.

Being one of the grand occasions when I stood at the sideboard, overseeing the dinner service, I had plenty to attend to while he was gone. After checking the wine and reviewing the staff who would serve, I retired to compose myself before the guests arrived. A brief whiff of—well, you know what—and a few pages from a certain book I’ve mentioned before—sufficed to steady both body and mind. I was roused from what I suspect was not a nap, but a reverie, by the sound of horses’ hooves outside. Rushing to the door, I found a cavalcade arriving: Mr. Franklin and his three cousins, escorted by one of old Mr. Ablewhite’s grooms.

Mr. Godfrey struck me as oddly subdued—much like Mr. Franklin in spirit, though not in manner. He greeted me warmly, shook hands as usual, and praised my appearance with polite concern. But beneath the courtesy, I sensed a shadow, a cloud I couldn’t explain. When I asked how his father was, he replied curtly, “Much as usual.” Yet the two Miss Ablewhites were radiant—so full of life, so exuberant, that they more than made up for their brother’s gloom. Nearly as tall as their brother, with golden hair, rosy cheeks, and boundless energy, they seemed to overflow with health and high spirits. Their weight nearly brought the horses to their knees, and when they dismounted without help, they bounced to the ground like balls of rubber. Everything they said began with a wide “O”; everything they did was done with a crash; and they giggled and shrieked at the slightest provocation. Bouncers—that’s what I call them.

Amid the din, I managed a quiet word with Mr. Franklin in the hall. “Have you got the Diamond safe, sir?”
He nodded and tapped his breast pocket.
“Have you seen any sign of the Indians?”
"Not a glimpse," he answered. Then he asked for my lady, and, learning she was in the small drawing-room, went straight there.

The bell rang almost immediately. Penelope was sent to inform Miss Rachel that Mr. Franklin Blake wished to speak with her.

About half an hour later, passing through the hall, I froze at the sound of screams from the drawing-room. I wasn’t alarmed—only because I recognized the signature “O” of the Ablewhite girls. Still, I stepped inside, pretending to seek instructions about dinner, to see if anything serious had occurred.

There stood Miss Rachel at the table, transfixed, holding the Colonel’s diamond. On either side of her knelt the two Bouncers, staring at the jewel with rapturous eyes, screaming each time it caught the light. Across the table, Mr. Godfrey clapped his hands like a child, murmuring softly, “Exquisite! exquisite!”
Mr. Franklin sat by the bookcase, pulling at his beard, his gaze fixed on the window.
And at the window stood my lady, facing away from the room, reading the extract from the Colonel’s will.

She turned to me when I entered. Her brow furrowed, her lips twitching with suppressed irritation. “Come to my room in half an hour,” she said. “I shall have something to say to you.”

Then she left.

It was evident she faced the same dilemma that had troubled Mr. Franklin and me at the Shivering Sand: Was the legacy of the Moonstone proof that she had wronged her brother? Or was it proof that he was even worse than she’d ever imagined? Heavy questions for a mother to weigh, while her daughter stood innocent, unaware of the past, cradling the very gift that had haunted the family.

Before I could leave, Miss Rachel stopped me—always thoughtful toward the old servant who had served the house since her birth. “Look, Gabriel!” she said, holding the stone aloft in a shaft of sunlight.

Lord bless us! It *was* a diamond! As large as a plover’s egg, or nearly. The light it cast was like that of the harvest moon. Looking into it, one saw a deep yellow glow that drew the eye inward, so completely that nothing else existed. It seemed endless—this tiny gem, held between thumb and forefinger, yet vast as the heavens. We placed it in the sun, then shut the light out, and still it glowed with a pale, ghostly radiance, like a moon in darkness. No wonder Miss Rachel was entranced—no wonder the girls screamed. Even I, hardened servant that I am, burst forth with a shout as loud as theirs.

Only Mr. Godfrey remained composed. He put an arm around each sister, looked from the jewel to me with pity, and said, “Carbon, Betteredge! Mere carbon, my good friend, after all!”

His intention, I suppose, was to instruct me. All it did was remind me of dinner. I hurried downstairs to my waiting staff.

As I left, Mr. Godfrey called after me, “Dear old Betteredge, I have the truest regard for him!”—while embracing his sisters and ogling Miss Rachel. What a reservoir of affection! Mr. Franklin seemed positively primitive beside him.

At the appointed time, I presented myself in my lady’s room. Our conversation echoed the earlier discussion with Mr. Franklin—except that I chose to remain silent about the jugglers, since nothing had occurred to justify alarming her. When I was dismissed, I could see she had taken the darkest possible view of the Colonel’s motives, and was determined to take the Moonstone from her daughter as soon as possible.

On my way back, I met Mr. Franklin. He asked if I had seen his cousin Rachel. I had not. Did I know where Godfrey was? I didn’t—but I began to suspect he might not be far from Rachel. Mr. Franklin’s thoughts seemed to follow the same path. He tugged fiercely at his beard, then slammed the library door behind him with a force that spoke volumes.

No further interruptions came until it was time to prepare for dinner. Just as I fastened my white waistcoat, Penelope appeared at my dressing table, ostensibly to brush my thinning hair and adjust my cravat. She was in high spirits, clearly with news.

She kissed my bald head and whispered, “News for you, father! Miss Rachel has refused him.”

“Who’s ‘him’?” I asked.

“The ladies’ committee-man, father,” she said. “A nasty sly fellow! I hate him for trying to supplant Mr. Franklin!”

Had I breath, I would have protested—such language toward a man of such noble philanthropy! But at that moment, she was tightening my cravat, and all her passion flowed into her fingers. I felt my windpipe compress. Never had I been so nearly strangled.

“I saw him take her away alone into the rose-garden,” she continued. “I waited behind the holly to watch. They went out arm-in-arm, laughing. They returned walking apart, solemn as statues, avoiding each other’s eyes. I never was so delighted, father! There’s one woman in the world who can resist Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite—at least, if I were a lady, I’d be another!”

Again, I opened my mouth to reprove her—but she had seized the hairbrush now, and all her emotion poured into that. If you are bald, you understand the sacrifice. If not, skip this part and thank your lucky stars you have some defense between your head and a bristle.

“Just beyond the holly,” she went on, “Mr. Godfrey stopped. ‘You prefer,’ he said, ‘that I should stay here as if nothing happened?’ Miss Rachel turned on him like lightning. ‘You’ve accepted my mother’s invitation,’ she said. ‘You’re here to meet her guests. Unless you wish to cause a scandal, you’ll remain.’ She walked on a few steps, then softened. ‘Let us forget what passed, Godfrey,’ she said. ‘Let us remain cousins.’ She offered her hand. He kissed it—which, I should say, was taking liberties—and she left him. He stood alone, head down, heel grinding a hole in the gravel. You never saw a man look more humbled. ‘Awkward!’ he muttered, looking up. ‘Very awkward!’”

If that was his self-assessment, he was right. Awkward enough, I thought. And the end of it, father,” Penelope concluded, delivering the final blow with a flourish, “is what I told you all along—Mr. Franklin’s the man!”

I wrested the brush from her and opened my mouth to deliver the rebuke her words and actions deserved. But before I could speak, the crash of carriage wheels outside silenced me. The first guests had arrived.

Penelope dashed off. I put on my coat and glanced in the mirror. My face was crimson, but otherwise, I was as neatly dressed as any man need be for such a night. I reached the hall just in time to announce the first arrivals. Not worth much attention—just the philanthropist’s parents, Mr. and Mrs. Ablewhite.
