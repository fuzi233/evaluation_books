The afternoon sun slanted through the window as Gerard lay restless in his bed, his fevered mind clinging to the thought of a lemon—something tart and cool to cut through the dryness in his throat. Denys, ever the faithful guardian, had set out to find one, though lemons were scarce in these northern lands. Left alone, Gerard drifted in irritation, his body aching, his thoughts frayed.

Then the door creaked open, not with Denys’ boisterous return, but with the measured step of an elderly physician. The man was a portrait of learned dignity: a long fur-trimmed gown, cherry-colored hose, and shoes so pointed they might have been weapons. His white beard flowed like a scholar’s scroll, and behind him trailed a boy bearing a basket of vials and instruments, the tools of his trade.

“How fares my patient?” the doctor murmured, his voice soft as if afraid to disturb the air.

Gerard, wary but polite, admitted his head was heavy, his throat parched. The doctor’s eyes sharpened at the mention of a wound.

“A bite?” he mused, inspecting the mark. “A dog, surely. No bear would leave so neat a puncture.”

“It was the paw,” Gerard corrected.

“Ah! A grave matter indeed,” the doctor intoned, shaking his head. “Claws carry a venom worse than teeth. We must act swiftly—cautery and bloodletting.”

Gerard flinched. “Must it burn?”

“Science demands it,” the physician declared, launching into a lecture on the humors, the evils of corrupted blood, and the infallibility of his methods. He spoke of Greek anatomists and Arabian quacks, of veins and vital essences, his voice swelling with the pride of Montpellier-trained wisdom.

Then Denys returned, lemons in hand, and halted at the sight.

“What’s this?” he asked, eyeing the physician’s tools.

“He means to bleed and burn me,” Gerard said weakly.

Denys’ face darkened. “I’ve seen enough battlefields to know what follows when fevered men are drained. The strong grow weak, the weak die.”

The doctor bristled. “You would trust a soldier’s superstition over centuries of learning?”

“I trust my eyes,” Denys shot back. “And they’ve seen more men saved by rest and clean air than by leeches and hot irons.”

Gerard, caught between them, groaned. “Must I choose between two tyrants? One would boil my blood with words, the other with arguments.”

The debate raged—Denys scoffing at the physician’s pretensions, the doctor dismissing Denys as an ignorant brute. Voices rose, tempers flared, until Gerard, driven to desperation, flung his pillow at the old man’s head.

Chaos followed. The doctor toppled, his basket of phials scattering, his dignity shattered. The boy yelped. And then—the final humiliation—the physician sat upon the hot coals meant for Gerard’s wound.

A hiss. A yowl. The scent of singed wool.

Denys roared with laughter as the doctor scrambled up, his robes stained, his pride in tatters. Gerard, despite himself, let out a weak chuckle.

“Perhaps,” he gasped, “the cure is worse than the disease.”

The physician, red-faced and sputtering, stormed out, hurling dire prophecies of convulsions, black vomit, and death. The door slammed.

Silence.

Denys wiped his eyes. “Well, little monk, it seems we’ve made an enemy of medicine.”

Gerard sighed, sinking back. “At least we still have lemons.”

And with that, he closed his eyes, exhausted but—for the moment—unburned.
