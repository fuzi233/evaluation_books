A woman named Catherine is a formidable force in a stranger's home. Her influence is immediate and absolute. Hardly has she crossed the threshold before the pots gleam, the hearth sweeps itself clean, and the windows seem to widen, flooding the room with light. Yet, this domestic alchemy is driven by a restless spirit—a "Busy Body" with no sense of discrimination. She seeks to correct everything and everyone, believing that order is always virtue. While many things improve under her scrutiny, not everything can bear such correction; save for the grave, nothing stands still enough to remain untouched by her rearranging hands.

Catherine moved every chair and table in Margaret's house, perhaps improving the layout, but her ambition soon turned to the living furniture itself. As Margaret's confinement drew near, Catherine cunningly enlisted the help of Denys and Martin. When the two simple men earnestly asked how they might serve, she directed them to take a walk, hinting that the longer the stroll, the better. This was not a generous offer. The dynamic of the strong being commanded by the weak would have enraged a she-bear with three cubs, let alone two weary travelers. Consequently, they slipped out at dawn and returned like thieves at dusk. If caught inside, they moved with the caution of cats walking on glass, their faces etched with awe and their spirits crushed by submission.

Yet, their presence remained a nuisance. Their very existence felt ill-timed, jarring against Catherine's nerves. Did an instinct whisper that a pair of depopulators had no place in a house destined to multiply? The breastplate offered no shield against a woman's tongue, and Catherine drove them away with relentless verbal pricks. By the time Margaret descended the stairs, the kitchen was cleared of its male occupants. Martin, old and stiff, had retreated no further than the street, carrying off his prize: a stool, upon which he sat suspended in the air. Margaret, seeing him bathed in sunlight, assumed he was merely resting, unaware he had become a fixture of the sun itself. She called for Denys, expecting his cheerful return.

Catherine, wiping a bowl with exaggerated vigor, lied smoothly. "Denys is gone to his friends in Burgundy," she declared. "High time, too; he hasn't been near them in three years."

"What? Gone without bidding me farewell?" Margaret asked, her eyes lifting like tender violets.

Catherine flushed. The accusation pricked her conscience, but she dismissed it with a casual toss of her head. "Oh, you were asleep then. I would not have woken you."

"Poor Denys," Margaret sighed, and visible dew gathered on her violet eyes.

Catherine, catching the sentiment from the corner of her eye, turned her attention to the remaining man. Suddenly, she showered Martin with hospitality and tenderness. He was old and stiff in more ways than one.

"No, thank you, dame," he replied, accustomed to the outdoors. "I dislike changing and changing. I meddle with nobody here, and nobody meddles with me."

"Oh, you nasty, cross old wretch!" Catherine screamed, shifting instantly from syrup to vinegar. She flounced back into the house.

Later, in quiet reflection, she felt a pang of regret. To soothe her conscience, she vowed that Margaret would never miss her "plagues of soldiers." Still uneasy, she dispelled the lingering doubt with a simple, sovereign act: she took the child and washed him.

From head to foot, she scrubbed the infant in tepid water, and the grievances of heroes dissolved like dust in an ocean of soap. During this celestial ritual, Margaret could not remain silent. She hovered around the woman performing the task, eager for even an apparent hand in the process. She dipped her finger into the water, ostensibly to pave the way for her son, though she knew Catherine would not allow her to judge the temperature. Kneeling opposite the little Gerard, Margaret prattled with amazing fluency, careful not to use adult pronunciations that a cherub might find ridiculous.

"I wish you could wash out THAT," she said, fixing her gaze on the boy's hand.

"What?"

"What? Have you not noticed? On his little finger."

Granny looked, and there, indeed, was a small brown mole.

"Eh, but this is wonderful!" she cried. "Nature, my lass, is strong and meddling to boot. Have you noticed such a mark on anyone else? Tell the truth, girl!"

"On him? Nay, mother, not I."

"Well, then he has; and on the very spot. You never noticed that much. But, dear heart, I forgot; you haven't known him from child to man as I have. I have had him hundreds of times on my knees, just as this, and washed him from top to toe in luke-warm water." She swelled with conscious superiority, and Margaret looked up meekly, as a woman facing a competitor beyond reach.

Catherine looked down from her dizzy height of experience and moralized. Unlike other busybodies, she occasionally reflected, though not deeply.

"It is strange," she said, "how things come round and about. Life is but a whirligig. At least, for us poor women, our lives follow one pattern. Wasn't I, in my youth, trying to wash out my Gerard's mole? 'Oh, fie! Here's a foul blot,' I would say, scrubbing until the poor wight cried, until I thought it time to stop. And now you say to me, 'Mother, do try and wash out my Gerard's finger.' Think on't!"

"Wash it out?" Margaret cried. "I wouldn't for all the world. It is the sweetest bit of his little darling body. I'll kiss it morning and night until he who owns it first returns to us three. Oh, bless you, my jewel of gold and silver, for being marked like your own daddy, to comfort me."

She kissed the little mole, but could not stop there. Soon, she had the boy sprawling on her lap, kissing his back over and over again, seeming to worry him like a wolf worrying a lamb. Catherine watched, smiling. She had seen many such savage onslaughts in her day.

This little sketch captures the tenor of Margaret's life for several months. A few small incidents occurred during this time, but they are reserved for another string of beads. While her boy's father endured fearful tempests of the soul, ending in the dead calm of the monastery, Margaret's life could be summed in one great, blissful word: Maternity.

You, who know the weight of that word, expand my sketch. See the young mother nursing, washing, dressing, and undressing; crowing and gambolling with her first-born. Then, swifter than lightning, dart your eye to Italy. See the cold cloister and the monks passing like ghosts, eyes downcast, hands meekly crossed over bosoms dead to earthly feeling.

One of these cowled ghosts is the man whose return, full of love, youth, and joy, the radiant young mother awaits.

In the valley of Grindelwald, the traveler sees on one side the perpendicular Alps—rock, ice, and everlasting snow, towering above the clouds and piercing the sky. On the other hand lie little, everyday slopes, green as emeralds, studded with cows and pretty cottages, teeming with life. Those lofty neighbors stand leafless, lifeless, inhuman, yet sublime. Elsewhere, the sweet commonplaces of nature pass unnoticed; but facing the grim Alps, they soothe and gently strike the mind by contrast with their tremendous opposites.

Such, in their way, are the two halves of this story, rightly viewed. On the Italian side: rugged adventure, strong passion, blasphemy, vice, penitence, pure ice, holy snow, soaring directly at heaven. On the Dutch side: all on a humble scale and womanish, yet ever green. And as a pathway parts the ice towers of Grindelwald, aspiring to the sky, from its little sunny braes, so here is but a page between "the Cloister and the Hearth."
