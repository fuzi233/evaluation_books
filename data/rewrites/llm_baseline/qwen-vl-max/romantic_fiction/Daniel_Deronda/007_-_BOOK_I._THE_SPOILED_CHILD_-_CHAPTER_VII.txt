“_Perigot_. As the bonny lass passed by, _Willie_. Hey, ho, bonny lass! _P_. She roode at me with glauncing eye, _W_. As clear as the crystal glasse. _P_. All as the sunny beame so bright, _W_. Hey, ho, the sunnebeame! _P_. Glaunceth from Phoebus’ face forthright, _W_. So love into thy heart did streame.” —SPENSER: _Shepard’s Calendar_.

“The kindliest symptom, yet the most alarming crisis in the ticklish state of youth; the nourisher and destroyer of hopeful wits; * * * the servitude above freedom; the gentle mind’s religion; the liberal superstition.”—CHARLES LAMB.

The first hint of the unexpected snowstorm was like a translucent white cloud that seemed to enhance the depth of the blue sky. Anna knew the truth of Rex’s feelings—though for the first time in their lives, he had said nothing about what mattered most to him, assuming she already understood. And for the first time, Anna could not speak her own thoughts aloud. Perhaps it would have hurt her to think that he might soon care for someone else more than for her—but such pain was softened by doubt and worry over his well-being. Anna admired her cousin deeply—she would have said, simply and sincerely, “Gwendolen is always very good to me,” and felt it natural to place herself entirely under her cousin’s influence. Yet she watched her with a mixture of awe and unease, as if gazing upon a creature both wondrous and mysterious—a beautiful animal whose nature remained hidden, perhaps even capable of devouring all the small, cherished things that belonged to Anna alone. And now, a heavy certainty settled within her: Gwendolen would never return Rex’s affection. What Anna held sacred—what she loved and honored—seemed indifferent to Gwendolen. It was easier to imagine her scorn than tenderness. And then there was the way Gwendolen always longed to be extraordinary. Poor Rex! Papa would be furious if he knew. And of course, Rex was far too young for such feelings. Anna had imagined that years would pass before anything like this came along—that she would be his housekeeper for a long time still. But what kind of heart must be so cold as to ignore such devotion? In the shadow of his possible suffering, Anna began to resent her dazzling cousin.

To both of them, the weeks had felt full of quiet excitement, though outwardly calm. If asked, Rex would have said he had no reason to hide what he hoped would become an engagement—one he meant to tell his father immediately. Yet for the first time, he kept his emotions close, and even his actions were guarded—something especially noticeable to Anna. She, too, grew anxious whenever her parents spoke to her privately, fearing they might mention Rex and Gwendolen. But the adults remained oblivious to the unfolding drama, which unfolded more through silent gestures than words—clear enough to those involved, but easily missed by others who were absorbed in reading the _Guardian_ or the _Clerical Gazette_, seeing the younger generation’s stirrings much as one might watch ants scurrying across a page.

“Where are you going, Rex?” Anna asked one gray morning after their father had left for the sessions in his carriage, Mrs. Gascoigne beside him. She noticed her brother had on his antigropelos—the closest thing he owned to hunting gear.

“Going to see the hounds throw off at the Three Barns.”

“Are you taking Gwendolen?” Anna asked, timidly.

“She told you, did she?”

“No—but I thought—Does Papa know you’re going?”

“Not that I’m aware of. I don’t suppose he’d care.”

“You’re using his horse?”

“He knows I do when I can.”

“Don’t let Gwendolen ride after the hounds, Rex,” Anna pleaded, her fears sharpening her intuition.

“Why not?” Rex asked, smiling with a teasing edge.

“Papa, Mama, and Aunt Davilow all wish her not to. They think it’s improper.”

“Why should you assume she’ll do something wrong?”

“Gwendolen doesn’t always listen to anyone,” Anna said, gaining courage in her irritation.

“Then she won’t listen to me,” Rex replied, turning her anxiety into jest.

“Oh, Rex, I can’t bear it. You’ll make yourself miserable,” she whispered, breaking down in tears.

“Nannie, Nannie, what’s wrong?” Rex asked, impatient now, hat on, whip in hand.

“She won’t care for you at all—I know she never will!” the child sobbed, losing all composure.

Rex flushed and hurried out of the hall, leaving Anna to suffer the bitter knowledge that she had only made herself unwelcome.

He thought of her words as he rode. They carried the sting of prophecy—even when laughed off. But he quickly dismissed them as born of Anna’s affection, feeling sorry he couldn’t stay to comfort her. Yet another emotion surged forward: a firm belief that she was wrong, accompanied by a new resolve to prove it. This certainty, tinged with doubt, pushed him toward confession faster than pure confidence ever could.

Gwendolen was already mounted, riding up and down the avenue when Rex arrived at the gate. She had arranged for the groom to wait nearby, just in case he didn’t show—she wouldn’t have waited longer than necessary. Now, the groom was sent away, and the two rode off together, free and unbound. Gwendolen was radiant, and Rex found her more beautiful than ever. Her figure, her long white throat, the graceful curve of her cheek and chin—all were perfectly framed by the simple elegance of her riding attire. He couldn’t imagine a more perfect girl. To a young lover like Rex, the harmony of goodness, truth, and beauty seemed already present in the object of his devotion. Most people would likely feel the same about Rex—his handsome face lacked the faint, unsettling trace of darkness that sometimes made observers hesitate before admiring Gwendolen.

It was a lovely January morning—no threat of rain, just a soft gray sky that deepened the charm of the winter landscape: grassy lanes, hedgerows dotted with red berries and filled with bird song, bare elms in purple shadow, rich brown furrows. The horses’ hooves chimed like music, echoing their young voices. She laughed at his modest outfit—he was no dandy—and he relished her laughter. The freshness of the air blended with the freshness of youth. Every sound from their clear throats, every glance exchanged, felt like water bubbling from a spring of joy. For them, it was all morning—inside and out. And to witness them in these moments, one cannot help wishing—if only things had been slightly different then, so that everything afterward might have been profoundly different—if only these two radiant young souls could have pledged themselves to each other right then, and never once turned away from that vow! Some of the goodness Rex believed in was truly present. Goodness is a vast word, often looking ahead—like a harvest buried beneath the soil, uncertain whether the seed will grow. At one stage, it’s invisible, promising life; at another, green shoots appear, and soon delicate flowers tremble, vulnerable to wind or rain. Each phase carries its own risk, and life may be choked by the poisoned ground around it—or by corruption from afar.

“Anna thought you’d want to ride after the hounds today,” Rex said, his mind still haunted by her earlier words.

“Did she?” Gwendolen laughed. “What a little clairvoyant!”

“Will you?” Rex asked, unsure if she’d defy her family’s wishes, but trusting she had reasons.

“I don’t know. I can’t say until we get there. Clairvoyants are often wrong—they predict what’s likely. I don’t like what’s likely—it’s always dull. I do what’s unlikely.”

“Ah, there’s your secret. Once I knew what most people would do, I’d know you’d do the opposite. So you’d follow your own likelihood. I could count on you. You couldn’t surprise me.”

“Yes, I could. I’d turn around and do what’s likely for everyone else,” Gwendolen replied, laughing musically.

“You see—you can’t escape some sort of likelihood. Contradiction makes the strongest likelihood of all. You must give up your plan.”

“No. My plan is to do what pleases me.” (Let any young woman who wishes to imitate Gwendolen consider her posture: had her head been set differently, her chin jutting, her neck curved too sharply, her words might have sounded harsh to sweet-natured Rex. But in her speech, even the oddness was charm, playful banter he longed to draw toward one purpose.)

“Can you really feel only what pleases you?” he asked.

“Of course not—that comes from what others do. But if the world were kinder, we’d only feel pleasure. Girls’ lives are so boring—they never do what they want.”

“I thought men suffered more. They’re forced into hard work, bored, broken down. And when we love a girl deeply, we want to please her—so in the end, you get your way.”

“I don’t believe it. I’ve never seen a married woman who had her own way.”

“What would you like to do?” Rex asked, genuinely curious.

“Oh, I don’t know—go to the North Pole, ride steeplechases, or become a queen in the East like Lady Hester Stanhope,” Gwendolen said lightly. Her words came swiftly, but she couldn’t explain their deeper meaning.

“You don’t mean you’d never marry?”

“No, I didn’t say that. Only that if I did, I wouldn’t live like other women.”

“You could do whatever you liked if you married a man who loved you more than anything,” Rex said, unknowingly stepping beyond the limits of his usual conversation. “I know one who does.”

“Don’t talk about Mr. Middleton, for heaven’s sake,” Gwendolen said quickly, her cheeks flushing. “That’s Anna’s favorite topic. I hear the hounds. Let’s go.”

She urged her chestnut into a canter, and Rex had no choice but to follow. Still, he felt encouraged. Gwendolen knew Rex was in love with her—but she saw it as a harmless game, a passing fancy. She had never known true sorrow in love. She wanted the little romance of his devotion to fill his time at Pennicote, avoiding explanations that might end it too soon. And she recoiled, almost physically, from being directly courted. Though she delighted in being adored, there was a fierce pride in her maidenhood.

But soon, all else faded for her in the thrill of the scene at the Three Barns. Several gentlemen of the hunt knew her, and she exchanged cheerful greetings. Rex couldn’t get near her. The color, the movement, the energy of the field took hold of Gwendolen with a force not born of habit—she had never ridden with the hounds before, only said she wished to, sparking a prohibition. Her mother feared danger, her uncle declared such violent exercise unsuitable for women, and insisted no respectable lady followed the Wessex hunt—except Mrs. Gadsby, wife of the yeomanry captain, who had once been a kitchenmaid and still spoke like one. That argument stirred Gwendolen, making her hesitate between desire and dread of being compared to Mrs. Gadsby.

Some of the neighborhood’s most respected women occasionally watched the hounds set off, but none were present this morning. Mrs. Gadsby was also absent. Thus, Gwendolen felt no restraint. The animal excitement—the baying of the hounds, the pawing of horses, the shifting voices, the flashes of color against the still green and gray—overwhelmed her. It was the ultimate anticipation of the chase: the feeling of becoming part dog, part horse, with the added rush of social vanity and the sensation of centaur-like power.

Rex would have enjoyed it more had he stayed closer to Gwendolen, but he saw her constantly engaged with acquaintances, or drawn by others on lively horses that swirled around her like a revolving lever.

“Delighted to see you here this fine morning, Miss Harleth,” said Lord Brackenshaw, a middle-aged peer in stained pink, with easy manners that made disaster seem inconsequential. “We’re in for a splendid run. A shame you didn’t join us. Have you ever tried your little chestnut over a ditch? You wouldn’t be afraid, eh?”

“Not at all,” Gwendolen replied. And it was true—she never feared action or companionship. “I’ve taken him over rails and ditches near—”

“Ah, by Jove!” his lordship murmured, signaling that something had interrupted their talk. As he pulled back, Rex brought his steady hackney alongside Gwendolen—then the hounds bayed, and the entire field surged forward as if the earth itself were spinning. Gwendolen went with the tide—no warning to Rex, who without hesitation followed. Could he let her go alone? Under other circumstances, he might have relished the chase. But now, he was troubled by the sudden halt in his longing to declare his love and receive an answer. His pursuit had been abruptly redirected—not into a romantic chase, but into a real one, atop his father’s old gray nag—a good horse, but slow, dignified, and thoroughly clerical.

Gwendolen, astride her spirited chestnut, raced with the best, feeling as secure as an immortal goddess. Had she considered danger, she’d have trusted her luck—she believed nothing ill would happen to her. But she didn’t think of danger, nor of her cousin. If she had, she might have found amusement in imagining him slowly falling behind, searching for gates—this lean, eager youth, his heart pounding like a beagle’s, trapped on a stiff, old-fashioned horse, frozen by a wizard’s spell. That image would have made her laugh, her sense of fun too strong to pity his embarrassment. But Gwendolen usually thought of those who watched her, not those she couldn’t see. Soon, Rex was so far behind that even if she’d looked, she wouldn’t have seen him. And alas, on a recently repaired lane, Primrose stumbled, broke his knees, and threw Rex over his head.

Fortunately, a blacksmith’s son—also following the hunt, though on foot, a practice some deemed immoral—was nearby and witnessed the fall. He ran to help. Rex was badly stunned, and when sensation returned, it came with pain. Joel Dagge proved himself invaluable—his knowledge suited the moment. He not only diagnosed the horse’s injury, knew their distance from the nearest inn and Pennicote Rectory, and confirmed Rex’s shoulder was merely dislocated, but offered skilled aid.

“Lord, sir, let me pop it back in! I’ve seen Nash, the bone-setter, do it—and done it myself twice for our little Sally. Same thing, shoulders are. Just trust me and tighten your mind a bit, and I’ll fix it in no time.”

“Come on, old fellow,” Rex said, able to focus better than sit still. Joel performed the procedure—painfully, but effectively. Rex turned pale, struggling to endure it, and Joel remarked, “Ah, sir, you aren’t used to it. I’ve seen plenty of joints out. Once saw a man with his eye pushed out—now that was a proper mess. But it went back in. I’ve swallowed three teeth myself, as sure as I’m alive. Now, sirrey” (to Primrose), “come along—you can’t pretend you can’t move.”

Joel, clearly of humble means, need not be further described for the refined reader—save that he helped Rex home as quickly as possible. There was no choice but to return, though Rex was consumed with worry for Gwendolen, more distressed by the thought that she might have been hurt than by his own pain or the trouble he’d cause his father. He comforted himself by believing others would protect her, and someone would surely escort her home.

Mr. Gascoigne was already home, writing letters in his study, when Rex entered with a face pale and strained, yet still strikingly handsome. Secretly, Rex was his favorite son—his living portrait. But Mr. Gascoigne treated him with strict fairness, even rigor. Having questioned Anna, he already knew Rex had gone to the meet with Gwendolen.

“What’s happened?” he asked sharply, not putting down his pen.

“I’m sorry, sir. Primrose fell and broke his knees.”

“Where did you take him?”

“To the Three Barns to see the hounds set off.”

“And you were foolish enough to follow?”

“Yes, sir. I didn’t jump any fences, but the horse stepped into a hole.”

“And you injured yourself, I suppose?”

“My shoulder’s dislocated, but a blacksmith put it back. I’m just bruised.”

“Well, sit down.”

“I’m sorry about the horse, sir. I knew it would upset you.”

“And what of Gwendolen?” Mr. Gascoigne asked abruptly. Rex, who hadn’t expected inquiries about himself, blushed—more remarkable given his paleness. Then, nervously, he said:

“I’m worried—I’d like to go or send to Offendene. But she rides well—I think she’ll be fine. There’ll be others around her.”

“I suppose it was she who led you on?” Mr. Gascoigne asked, laying down his pen, leaning back, and studying his son closely.

“It was natural for her to want to go—she didn’t plan it. She was swept up by the moment. And of course, I followed.”

A silence followed. Then Mr. Gascoigne spoke quietly, with irony:

“But now you see, young gentleman, you’re not equipped with a horse fit to chaperone your cousin. You must give up this pastime. You’ve spoiled my nag, and that’s enough mischief for one vacation. I’ll ask you to leave tomorrow for Southampton, to join Stilfox until you go up to Oxford with him. It will help your bruises—and your studies.”

Poor Rex felt his heart swell as if it were no stronger than a girl’s.

“I hope you won longer insist on my leaving now, sir.”

“Do you feel too ill?”

“No—not that—but—” He bit his lip, feeling tears rise, to his great frustration. Then he rallied, trying to speak firmly: “I want to go to Offendene, but I can go tonight.”

“I’m going there myself. I can bring news of Gwendolen, if that’s what you want.”

Rex broke down. He thought he saw a fate worse than death—fatal to his happiness, perhaps his life. He trusted his father’s insight and expected firmness. “Father, I can’t leave without telling her I love her—and knowing she loves me.”

Mr. Gascoigne inwardly reproached himself for not being more cautious. He truly pitied the boy, but duty demanded wisdom. Calmly, he replied:

“My dear boy, you’re too young for such decisive steps. This is a fleeting fancy, born in idle days. You must apply yourself to something serious and forget it. There are many reasons against it. An engagement at your age would be reckless and unjustifiable. And alliances between cousins are undesirable. Accept this disappointment. Life is full of them. We all must be shaped. This is a mild beginning for you.”

“No—mild? I can’t bear it. I’ll be useless. I wouldn’t mind anything if it were settled between us. I could do anything then,” Rex said, impetuously. “But pretending I’ll obey you is pointless. I can’t. If I said I would, I’d break my word. I’d see Gwendolen again.”

“Wait until tomorrow morning—we’ll discuss it again. You’ll promise me that,” Mr. Gascoigne said quietly. And Rex, unable to refuse, nodded.
