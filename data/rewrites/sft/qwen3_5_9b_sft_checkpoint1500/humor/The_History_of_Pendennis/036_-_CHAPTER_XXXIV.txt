Captain Shandon, spurred on by his formidable wife—who rarely interfered with business matters but struck fear into the hearts of debt collectors—had successfully lobbied for John Finucane, Esquire, of the Upper Temple, to become the sub-editor of the *Pall Mall Gazette*. The spirited proprietor of the *Daily Journal* promptly conferred this honor upon Mr. Finucane. Truly, Finucane deserved such kindness; his devotion to the Captain and his family was legendary, matched only by his desperate eagerness to render a service. It was within Finucane's chambers that Captain Shandon, in days gone by, hid whenever danger lurked or bailiffs prowled. Those rooms became so famous a sanctuary that the sheriff's officers began visiting the Captain on Finucane's staircase with the same regularity as they did at his own front door. It was likewise to these chambers that poor Mrs. Shandon flocked repeatedly to unburden herself of her sorrows and scheme ways to rescue her adored husband. How many meals Finucane provided for her and the little child! To visit his modest rooms with such a distinguished lady was an immense honor. As she descended the staircase, her veil drawn low, Fin would lean over the balustrade, watching her retreat, perhaps secretly wishing that some rogue "Temple Lovelace" might ambush her, so he could rush forward to break the villain's bones—a fantasy born of genuine, if slightly theatrical, affection. It was a sincere delight to Mrs. Shandon that arrangements were finally made for her champion to serve as her husband's aide-de-camp in the newspaper world.

Finucane would have sat with Mrs. Shandon past the hour when prisons usually closed, often witnessing the tucking away of little Mary, who slept in a crib in the room. Before saying grace, Finucane, though a Roman Catholic himself, had offered a sympathetic "Amen" to the child's prayers asking for her father's blessing. However, destiny intervened: he had an appointment with Mr. Bungay concerning the future of their paper, necessitating a quiet dinner discussion. At precisely six o'clock, he departed from Mrs. Shandon, yet immediately appeared at the Fleet Prison the following morning. For this occasion, he had dressed in his finest garb and ornaments; while cheap in price, they blazed with brilliant colors. In his pocket lay four pounds and two shillings—the entirety of his weekly salary at the *Daily Journal*, minus two shillings spent on a pair of gloves purchased en route to the prison.

Having "cut his mutton" with Mr. Bungay, as the latter grandiloquently termed it, Finucane had previously met with Mr. Trotter, Bungay's reader and business manager, at Dick's Coffee-House. They had delved deeply into the strategy for conducting the *Pall Mall Gazette*. With masterful flair, Finucane outlined the sub-editorial architecture: the precise typography required for various articles, the reporters needed for the markets, the turf, the ring, church intelligence, and fashionable gossip. He knew intimately the gentlemen cultivating these diverse fields of knowledge and knew their weekly earnings. Furthermore, he was a connoisseur of a thousand dodges and ingenious economic contrivances by which spirited capitalists could save coin. He simultaneously dazzled and mystified Mr. Bungay, who was notoriously slow of comprehension, by the rapidity of his calculations scribbled on paper as they sat in the box. Bungay later confessed to his subordinate, Mr. Trotter, that the Irishman seemed remarkably clever.

Now, having impressed Mr. Bungay, the faithful Finucane maneuvered subtly toward his ultimate objective: the liberation of his admired friend and chief, Captain Shandon. He knew to the exact shilling the amount of detainers against the Captain at the Fleet Porter's Lodge and, indeed, professed to know all his debts, though this was impossible, for no man in England—not even the Captain himself—truly knew his liabilities. Finucane pointed out Shandon's existing engagements and argued persuasively that the Captain would work far better if released from confinement. Though Mr. Bungay initially denied this, claiming that a locked-up Captain was always "sure to be found at home," whereas a free one vanished like smoke, Finucane played his final card. He painted a vivid picture of Mrs. Shandon pining away and the child falling ill within the gloom of the prison. This emotional appeal proved decisive; the publisher agreed to consider the matter if Mrs. Shandon visited him in the morning. The colloquy concluded with a second round of brandy-and-water. Although Finucane, holding four guineas, would have happily settled the tavern bill, Bungay declared, "No, sir, this is my affair, sir, if you please. James, take the bill, and eighteenpence for yourself," handing the necessary funds to the waiter. Thus, when Finucane retired to his chambers in the Temple after dinner, he found himself actually possessing his full week's salary on the following Saturday morning.

He bestowed a wink upon Mrs. Shandon, so knowing and joyful, that the kind creature sensed imminent good news. Hastening to retrieve her bonnet and shawl, she asked if Finucane might accompany her for a walk, granting her some fresh air. Little Mary leaped for joy at the prospect of a holiday, for Finucane never neglected to procure toys or tickets to shows, carrying newspaper orders for London diversions in his pocket to amuse the child. Indeed, he loved them with all his heart and would willingly have sacrificed his rambling brains to serve them.

"May I go, Charley?" Mrs. Shandon asked, referring to the Captain, "or shall I stay with you? You're poorly, dear, this morning. He's got a headache, Mr. Finucane. He suffers from headaches, and I persuaded him to stay in bed."

"Go along with you, and Polly," the Captain replied with perfect good humor, despite writing. "Jack, take care of 'em. Hand me over Burton's Anatomy, and leave me to my abominable devices." He frequently drew upon that wonderful repertory of learning, extracting Greek and Latin quotations from it for his public writings.

So Fin gave his arm to Mrs. Shandon, and Mary skipped down the passages, through the gate, and into the free air. The journey from Fleet Street to Paternoster Row was short. Upon reaching Mr. Bungay's shop, Mrs. Bungay was entering via the private door, clutching a paper parcel and a manuscript volume bound in red—an account of her butcher transactions. She was attired in a gorgeous shot-silk dress flamed with red and purple, wearing a yellow shawl, red flowers in her bonnet, and a brilliant light-blue parasol.

Mrs. Shandon wore an old black watered silk; her bonnet had seen fewer brilliant days of prosperity than its owner, yet she possessed an innate dignity that made her look like a lady regardless of her attire. The two women curtsied to one another, each according to her fashion.

"I hope you're pretty well, mum?" said Mrs. Bungay.
"It's a very fine day," said Mrs. Shandon.
"Won't you step in, mum?" said Mrs. Bungay, staring so intently at the child that she nearly frightened her.

"I—I came about business with Mr. Bungay," stammered timid Mrs. Shandon, "I—I hope he's pretty well?"
"If you go to see him in the counting-house, couldn't you leave your little gurl with me?" Mrs. Bungay asked in a deep, tragic voice, extending a finger toward the child.
"I want to stay with mamma!" cried little Mary, burying her face in her mother's dress.
"Go with this lady, Mary, my dear," said the mother gently.
"I'll show you some pretty pictures," Mrs. Bungay cooed, her voice momentarily softening before turning to the ominous tone of an ogress. Opening her brown-paper parcel, she revealed choice sweet biscuits, a treat Mrs. Bungay savored after her wine. Little Mary, entranced, followed the attraction, and the whole party entered the private entrance, leading to a side door into Mr. Bungay's commercial apartments. Here, as the child prepared to separate from her mother, her courage faltered, and she clung again to the maternal petticoat. Seeing the look of disappointment in Mrs. Bungay's face, the kind Mrs. Shandon graciously offered, "If you will let me, I will come up too, and sit for a few minutes." Thus, the three females ascended the stairs together. A second biscuit charmed little Mary into perfect confidence, and within moments she prattled away without restraint.

Meanwhile, Faithful Finucane found Mr. Bungay in a mood far sterner than the previous night, when two-thirds of a bottle of port and two large glasses of brandy-and-water had warmed the publisher's soul into generosity. His impetuous wife had rebuked him upon his return home. She ordered that no relief be given to the Captain, dismissing him as a "good-for-nothing fellow" whom money could not help. She disapproved of the entire plan for the *Pall Mall Gazette*, predicting that Bungay would lose his money as they had lost it over the way at the Whitehall Journal. Her decree was absolute: let Shandon remain in prison and do his work; it was the best place for him.
