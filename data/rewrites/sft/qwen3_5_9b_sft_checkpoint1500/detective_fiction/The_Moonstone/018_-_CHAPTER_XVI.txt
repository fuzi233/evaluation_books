The room was dim, lit only by the harsh glare of a single reading-lamp whose shade was drawn tight, casting a shadow over Lady Verinder's countenance. She sat hunched at the desk, eyes locked stubbornly on the open pages of a book, refusing to lift them as we entered.

"Officer," she said, her voice steady despite the gloom, "does the nature of your inquiry depend upon knowing if anyone currently residing in this house intends to depart?"

"It is of paramount importance, Madam," I replied.

"I must inform you," she continued, "that Miss Rachel Verinder has resolved to travel to her aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite, at Frizinghall. Her departure is scheduled for the first hours of tomorrow."

Sergeant Cuff cast a sharp glance at me, and I felt a sudden, crushing weight in my chest. I stepped forward to defend my young mistress, yet fear seized me, and I retreated, silence falling between us.

"When did Miss Verinder disclose this intention to you?" the Sergeant asked.

"Approximately an hour ago," my mistress answered.

Cuff looked at me again, his gaze piercing. "I have no authority to command Miss Verinder's movements," he stated plainly. "However, I implore you to delay her departure until later today. I intend to ride to Frizinghall myself in the morning and return by noon. If she remains here until that hour, I wish to speak to her unexpectedly before she leaves."

Lady Verinder turned to me. "Go to the coachman," she ordered, "and instruct him that no carriage is to approach for Miss Rachel until two o'clock." After giving these orders, she returned her attention to the Sergeant. "Is there anything further you require?"

"One final request, Madam," Cuff said. "If Miss Verinder is startled by this alteration in plans, do not attribute it to me. Please keep the cause of the delay entirely from her."

As if steeling herself against a terrible revelation, Lady Verinder raised her head from the book, checking her impulse to speak with a visible struggle. She looked back at the page and dismissed us with a cold wave of her hand.

"That is a remarkable woman," Cuff murmured as we crossed the threshold into the hall. "Had she not mastered her composure, the mystery that baffles you, Mr. Betteredge, would have unraveled tonight."

Those words struck a spark in my weary brain. For a fleeting moment, sanity abandoned me. I lunged forward, seizing the Sergeant by his coat and pinning him against the wall.

"Curse you!" I roared. "Something is dreadfully wrong with Miss Rachel, and you have been concealing it from me all along!"

Cuff stared up at me, unmoving, his melancholy features impassive.

"Aha," he said calmly, "so you have deduced it at last."

My grip loosened, and I sank onto my knees, shame burning my cheeks. Consider the lengths I have served this family; consider the times little Rachel climbed upon my lap, tugging at my whiskers. To me, she was the dearest, sweetest young mistress a loyal servant could love. I begged forgiveness, my voice thick with tears, utterly humbled.

"Do not distress yourself, Mr. Betteredge," the Sergeant said, his tone softening with a kindness I rarely expected. "In my profession, quick tempers are useless. If it comforts you, seize me again. You lack the skill to do so effectively, but I shall overlook your clumsiness given your distress."

A faint, grim smile touched his lips, suggesting he viewed his own predicament as a dark jest.

I ushered him into my small sitting room and barred the door.

"Speak the truth, Sergeant," I demanded. "What do you suspect? Spare me no more secrets."

"I do not suspect," Cuff replied firmly. "I know."

"My blood boils!" I shouted, my temper flaring anew. "Do you imply that Miss Rachel has stolen her own diamond?"

"Yes," the Sergeant affirmed. "Precisely that. Miss Verinder has secretly possessed the Moonstone from the beginning. She enlisted Rosanna Spearman into her confidence solely to ensure that we would suspect Rosanna of the theft. That is the entirety of the case. Collar me again if it pleases you, Mr. Betteredge, though I doubt it will offer you relief."

"Give me your reasons!" I pleaded, desperate for understanding.

"You shall hear them tomorrow," Cuff said. "Should Miss Verinder refuse to postpone her trip—which I anticipate she will do—I shall be compelled to present the full case to your mistress tomorrow. I request your presence to witness the proceedings. Let this matter rest for tonight. No, Mr. Betteredge, you shall not extract another word regarding the Moonstone from me. Behold, your supper is ready. That is a human frailty I always treat gently. Ring the bell, and I shall say grace: 'For what we are about to receive...'"

"Wish you a hearty meal, Sergeant," I muttered, my appetite vanishing. "Mine is gone. I will watch you eat, and then I shall beg leave to retire, seeking solitude to cope with this horror."

I observed him devour the finest fare, wishing it might choke him. Just then, the head gardener, Mr. Begbie, entered with his weekly accounts. Immediately, the conversation shifted to the virtues of grass versus gravel paths. I departed, leaving them to their trivial debate, my heart heavy with despair.

This was the first tribulation in decades that tobacco smoke could not dispel, nor could the solitude of *Robinson Crusoe* assuage.

Restless and wretched, I wandered the terrace, letting the storm brew around me. The air grew chill, the sky darkening with ominous clouds racing over a pale, watery moon. The dogs whined uneasily. I pondered my failing age, wondering when God would finally release me from this weariness. Yet, amidst my misery, I clung tenaciously to my faith in Miss Rachel. Even if Sergeant Cuff, wise as King Solomon, accused her of guilt, my answer would remain unchanged: "You do not know her, and I do."

My reverie broke when Samuel arrived with a note from my mistress. Entering the house to secure a candle, I noted the shift in the atmosphere—the wind moaning low, the air growing damp. The sky was turning black, heralding wild weather.

Reading the note, I learned that the magistrate at Frizinghall had written to remind Lady Verinder of the three Indians. They were to be released early next week, free to roam at will. If further questioning was necessary, time was short. Having overlooked this detail during her previous audience with the Sergeant, my mistress now tasked me with reminding him. Though the subject of the Indians had faded from my mind, I complied with her order.

Returning to the house, I found Sergeant Cuff and Mr. Begbie engaged in a fervent debate over roses, sharing a bottle of Scotch whiskey. The Sergeant held up his hand, signaling me not to disturb their discourse. Their contention centered on whether the white-moss rose required budding onto the dog-rose to thrive. Mr. Begbie asserted "yes"; the Sergeant insisted "no." They appealed to me with the intensity of schoolboys. Lacking botanical knowledge, I adopted a neutral stance, mimicking the caution of high court judges when justice hangs by a thread. "Gentlemen," I declared, "much may be argued on either side."

During the brief pause created by my impartiality, I placed Lady Verinder's note on the table beneath the Sergeant's gaze.

Within seconds, he scanned the paper, recalled Superintendent Seegrave's report, and identified the section concerning the Indians. "A certain traveler, expert in Indian languages, appeared in Mr. Seegrave's findings, correct?" he asked. "Do you know his name and address?" Upon receiving confirmation, he instructed me to write them on the back of the note. "I shall inquire of this gentleman when I arrive at Frizinghall tomorrow."

"Do you expect results?" I asked. "Superintendent Seegrave deemed them innocent."

"Superintendent Seegrave has erred in every conclusion thus far," Cuff countered. "Tomorrow, we shall verify if he was mistaken regarding the Indians as well." Turning back to Mr. Begbie, he resumed the argument on roses as if nothing had occurred.

I slipped out of the room, closing the door behind me to escape the din. In the hallway, I encountered Penelope hovering anxiously.

"What awaits you?" I asked.

"She waits for my young lady's summons to resume packing," Penelope explained. "Miss Rachel claims the house is unbearable and cannot endure the presence of a policeman under the same roof. Half an hour ago, upon learning her departure was delayed until two, she erupted into a furious rage. My lady sternly rebuked her and then sent me away, presumably for a private conversation." Penelope's spirits were crushed. "Nothing functions correctly, Father," she whispered. "Some calamity looms over us all."

So did I. But I masked my sorrow before her. Suddenly, Miss Rachel's bell rang. Penelope scrambled up the stairs. I proceeded to the hall to check the barometer.

As I approached the swinging doors connecting the servants' quarters to the hall, they burst open violently. Rosanna Spearman fled past me, her face etched with pain, clutching her heart as if agony tore through it.

"What troubles you, child?" I asked, stepping to block her path
