A month after his return from the Sudan, Durrance arrived in London and found a letter waiting for him at his club. It was from Ethne, brief and quiet, telling him she was staying with Mrs. Adair in Hill Street and would be pleased if he could call. But the paper and envelope were edged in black—a sign of mourning.

The next afternoon, he went to see her. She was alone when he arrived.

"I didn’t write to Wadi Halfa," she said at once, "because I thought you’d already be on your way home before my message could reach you. My father died last month—towards the end of May."

Durrance felt a pang. "I feared that might be the news," he replied. "I’m deeply sorry. You must miss him terribly."

"More than I can say," she answered, her voice low but steady. "He passed away one morning early. I think I should tell you how it happened—if you don’t mind hearing."

She described the slow decline that had led to Dermod’s death—not from any sudden illness, but from a quiet wasting away. Yet there was something remarkable about the final hours. Just before he slipped into silence, he seemed to reclaim a fragment of his old strength.

"We both knew he was dying," Ethne said. "He did too. At seven o’clock that evening—after speaking to me for a little while—he called our dog by name. The dog came instantly to the bed, though his voice was barely a whisper. He crouched close, pushing his muzzle under my father’s hand with a soft whine. Then he told me to leave him and the dog entirely alone. I was to shut the door and not open it again until the dog gave the signal. I obeyed. I waited outside—six long hours—until one o’clock, when a sudden, mournful howl echoed through the house."

She paused. That was the only sign of emotion she showed. After a moment, she continued, her tone calm and clear.

"It was hard to stand there in the corridor as the day faded into night. He wouldn’t allow a lamp in his room. I imagined him lying still in the great four-poster bed, facing the hills, the light fading slowly around him. The room growing dark. The windows growing larger in the dimness. The dog beside him, and no one else—right to the very end. He wanted it that way. But it was cruel—for me."

Durrance said nothing. He offered her what she needed most: the quiet understanding of silence. He could picture her standing there, listening, heart pounding, hands clenched, enduring those endless hours. And he felt a sharp sorrow for Dermod’s stubborn choice—to die alone, even if it meant leaving his daughter to suffer in silence.

It was Ethne who broke the quiet.

"I mentioned that my father spoke to me just before he sent me away. Do you know whom he talked about?"

She looked directly at him, her eyes steady, her voice level. A sudden warmth surged through Durrance—hope, quick and bright—before he realized the danger of it.

"Tell me," he said, leaning forward.

"Mr. Feversham," she said simply.

He recoiled slightly. Not the name he had hoped for. He turned his gaze down to the carpet, so she wouldn’t see the disappointment—or the anger—on his face.

"My father always admired him," she went on gently. "And I wonder… do you know anything about where he is now? Or what he’s doing?"

Durrance did not answer at once. He walked to the window and stood looking out, his back to her. Even so, the bitterness in his voice was unmistakable.

"No," he said, his tone tight. "I’ve never seen him. And I never want to." He took a breath, then spoke more sharply. "I know he was your friend. But I can’t remember that friendship now. All I can think is—if he had been the man we believed him to be, you wouldn’t have had to wait alone in that dark hall for six hours."

He turned abruptly. "You’re going back to Glenalla?"

"Yes."

"You’ll live there alone?"

"Yes."

For a moment, silence filled the room. Then Durrance moved behind her chair.

"You once said you might tell me why your engagement ended."

"But you already know," she said. "What you said earlier—that look—proved it."

"No," he insisted. "Only a few words my father dropped. He asked me about Feversham the last time I saw him. But I don’t know anything certain. Please—tell me."

Ethne shook her head, resting her elbows on her knees. "Not now," she whispered.

Again, silence.

Then Durrance spoke again, his voice suddenly urgent. "I have only one more year in Halfa. It would be wise to leave Egypt then. I don’t expect much will happen in the Sudan soon. I don’t think I’ll stay—no matter what."

Ethne didn’t pretend not to understand. "We’re not children," she said quietly. "You have your whole life ahead. We should be prudent."

"Yes," Durrance replied, his voice rising with frustration. "But the right kind of prudence—the kind that knows it’s worth risking everything for what matters."

She remained still, her back to him, silent and unmoving. At last, she spoke—softly, almost to herself.

"Do you want me so very much?"

Before he could answer, she turned quickly toward him.

"Try not to," she pleaded earnestly. "Just this one year—try not to. You have important things to focus on. Try to forget me completely."

There was a hint of sorrow in her voice—not grief, but regret—for losing a dear friend. It was enough to ease the sting of her words and deepen Durrance’s illusion: that if not for her fear of harming his future, she might have said yes.

Mrs. Adair entered the room just then, cutting off any reply. Durrance left, carrying that false hope with him.

That evening, he dined at his club and sat afterward beneath the great tree where he had spent so many restless nights a year before, searching in vain for news of Harry Feversham. The sky was clear and star-strewn, the air warm and still. Lights flickered from the windows, making the leaves glow like spring green. Carriages rolled past, their rumble like distant waves.

And on this night, another man came into the courtyard—someone who knew Durrance. But unlike others, he did not hesitate. He walked straight up and sat beside him.

Durrance dropped his newspaper and reached out his hand.

"How are you?" he asked.

It was Captain Mather.

"I wondered if I’d find you here," Mather said. "I read the evening paper and thought you might be in town. Have you seen the news?"

"What news?" Durrance asked.

Mather picked up the paper and flipped through it. "Remember our last reconnaissance from Suakin?"

"Of course."

"We stopped at the Sinkat fort at midday. There was an Arab hiding in the trees behind the glacis."

"Yes."

"Have you forgotten what he told us?"

"The story about Gordon’s letters and the wall in Berber? No—I haven’t forgotten."

"Well," Mather said, folding the paper carefully and handing it over, "here’s something that might interest you."

Durrance read the short paragraph between puffs of his cigar. It said only that letters exchanged between the Mahdi and General Gordon—summons to surrender Khartum and invitations to convert, along with copies of Gordon’s curt replies—had been found hidden in a wall in Berber and delivered safely to Captain Willoughby at Suakin.

"The fellow must have gone back to Berber after all," Durrance remarked. "A dangerous thing. Abou Fatma—that was his name."

The article made no mention of Abou Fatma. Only Captain Willoughby was named.

"They weren’t worth risking a life for," Mather said.

"Perhaps not," Durrance replied hesitantly. "But I’m glad they’re recovered. Maybe the copies are in Gordon’s own handwriting. They’re historically significant."

"In a way," Mather agreed. "But they don’t shed any real light on the siege. They won’t change history—or anyone’s life."

"That’s true," Durrance said—and yet, in truth, it wasn’t. For here, in the same place where he had once sought news of Feversham, news had come to him. Only he didn’t know it. He was blind to its meaning.

He set the paper aside and returned to thoughts of Ethne. She had mentioned Feversham—yes—but likely only because her father had spoken of him. Her voice had been steady. She had spoken his name without tremor. Perhaps that meant nothing stirred in her heart anymore.

And then there was her request—that he try to forget her for one year. To Durrance, it seemed almost like a promise. As if she might give him what he longed for—if only he could endure the waiting.

Days passed. Then he returned to Mrs. Adair’s house. But Ethne was gone—back to Donegal.

Mrs. Adair said she had left suddenly, and she didn’t know why.

But Durrance understood. She was following her own advice. If he was to forget her, she would help by disappearing from his sight.

And he was right.

Yet there was one thing Ethne had overlooked. She hadn’t told him to stop writing.

So, in the autumn, the letters began again—from the Sudan.

She welcomed them, yet they troubled her. Though he wrote carefully, avoiding direct feelings, sometimes a phrase would slip through—perhaps repeating a word she had said years ago, long forgotten. And she couldn’t help seeing: he still carried her in his thoughts.

There was also a new brightness in his words—a sense of hope, of color returning to his world. As if life had become musical again.

Ethne had never escaped the guilt that one man’s life had been ruined because of her. She had sworn that no other man would suffer the same fate. But now, reading his letters, she faced a new question: How could she protect him?

Two paths lay before her. Which one would let her keep her vow?

She was no longer certain that his career was everything. The question haunted her. She took the letters with her to the hillside, sat beneath the wind-swept sky, and wrestled with it—without finding an answer.

Even her violin failed to soothe her.
