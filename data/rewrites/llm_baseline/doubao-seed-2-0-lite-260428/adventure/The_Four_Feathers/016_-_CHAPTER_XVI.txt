Colonel Durrance had ceased to exist for Ethne Eustace the moment Captain Willoughby dropped that crumpled, once-white feather, now stained pale yellow with desert dust and years of sun, into the cup of her hand. She half-dragged him past the thick hawthorn hedge into the walled waterside enclosure, and every word of his story of Harry Feversham’s years in the Sudan swallowed her whole. For five long, stiff years she’d held her head high, smiled through every polite inquiry, played the part of the stoic, unbetrothed heiress, and all that time she’d buried every memory of Harry’s laugh, his quick half-embarrassed grin when he teased her, the way his voice softened when he spoke of his west-country home. But Willoughby’s narrative dug all those memories up again, sharp and bright, filling in every gap of his account until she could almost see Harry tramping across the sun-scorched Soudanese sands herself.

She’d been so completely swept away from that sun-drenched August garden, from the weight of those five years, that when she lifted her head and spotted Durrance standing right at the enclosure’s single hedge entrance, a stab of pain spasmed across her face and her eyes squeezed shut for a split second. There was no time to grieve for the lost, unguarded minutes of Willoughby’s story. The small stolen holiday was over. If she could never actually manage to forget Harry — and right now she knew, with absolute brutal certainty, that she never would — she had to play the part perfectly, never letting Durrance suspect that the “friendship” she offered him as his fiancée was nothing more than hard-won loyalty. Durrance was far too sharp a guesser to ever find Willoughby here, or even learn he’d come.

She scanned the enclosure for an escape, and knew instantly it was hopeless. The creek lapped at the stone lip of the ground before them, three high, thick hedges walled them in from behind and the sides, and the only way out was blocked entirely by Durrance, who was stepping slowly into the clearing now.
“Keep still,” she breathed into Willoughby’s ear.
“You know him?” he whispered back.
“Of course. We served together three years at Suakin. I heard he’d gone blind. I’m glad that’s rubbish,” he added, watching Durrance move with steady, unhesitating grace.
“Lower your voice. It’s no rubbish. He is blind.”
“Nobody would ever tell. It’s impossible he can’t see us right here,” Willoughby protested, staring at Durrance who seemed to be looking directly at their bench.
“He sees nothing at all.”
“Ethne?” Durrance called, his voice so quiet, so sure, the illusion of sight was almost unbreakable.
“He’s not even sure where we are,” Ethne whispered, her fingers clamping hard around Willoughby’s arm. “Not a sound. Don’t move an inch.”
“Why can’t he know I’m here?” Willoughby mouthed, but the desperate tightness in Ethne’s face silenced him before he could ask more. They both sat rigid on the wooden bench, holding their breath, every muscle frozen, as Durrance walked steadily closer, stopped dead directly in front of them, his gaze fixed straight on their faces. Even Ethne, who knew perfectly well his eyes could not distinguish a single shape, felt a flicker of doubt.

Then he shrugged, called her name one last time, and turned toward the creek. His back was to them now, but Ethne, who had learned to read every tiny shift of his posture since he lost his sight, could tell instantly he was listening — straining every nerve to pick up the smallest rustle of a dress, the quietest huff of breath. A full minute dragged by, and he spun on his heel, taking a sharp step back toward the bench. Not a single thread of Ethne’s skirt shifted. Willoughby did not twitch. Durrance sighed, muttered that he had no idea where she’d got to, and walked slowly out of the enclosure, the hedge swallowing him up.

Ethne only released her bruising grip on Willoughby’s arm once Durrance’s footsteps had faded entirely.
“That was a near thing,” Willoughby huffed, once he was allowed to speak. “Might’ve sat right on top of us if he’d made it two more steps.”
“But he didn’t,” Ethne said, calm as still water. “You’ve told me everything? All of Harry’s story?”
“Every last part I can recall. It all happened last spring.”
Ethne’s chest ached sharp. “Why didn’t you bring that feather to me last summer? Your leave can’t have been that short.”
“Was, though. Spent the whole of it hunting ibex in the hills north of Suakin. Never thought there was any rush — Harry never said it was urgent. Figured I’d probably get back to England and find he’d beaten me home anyway.”

A desperate, unspoken cry screamed in Ethne’s throat — last summer, she had been free. No quiet engagement to Durrance weighing on her, no honor-bound promise trapping her, and Willoughby had wasted that whole precious time chasing mountain goats in the desert. But her face did not shift. She only smiled faintly, asked him if he’d had good sport, and heard him grumble that it was decent enough.
“Two more feathers, right?” she said. “He still has to win them back from Castleton and Trench, before he’ll come home to me.”
Willoughby shook his head. “He’ll never get the chance. Castleton was killed when the Dervish square broke at Tamai.”

Ethne laughed, a short, bright, satisfied little laugh that made Willoughby stare at her in shock. She was Celt through and through — death felt no great weight to her, and the three men who had sent Harry the white feathers, who had ruined five years of his life and hers, she hated every last one of them, no pity left at all. She did not even spare a thought for the fact she’d handed Harry the fourth feather herself, back when she’d believed him a coward. “And Trench?” she demanded, sharp.
“Alive, but worse than dead. Captured on a reconnaissance, locked up in Omdurman prison. You have no idea what that kind of captivity does to a man,” Willoughby said, his voice severe.
“I don’t want to hear,” Ethne said, stubborn as iron. “You have to go now. First — where did Harry go after he gave you that feather?”
“Hopped the first steamer out of Suakin for Suez. Had more than enough money, too — his father’s allowance still came through regular.”

Ethne sent him to wait hidden in the enclosure while she checked the garden was clear. The whole estate was drowsing in the midday heat, the birds fallen quiet, only bees humming over the flower beds, a distant farm boy yelling across the creek far away. No sign of Durrance anywhere.
“You can go,” she said, leading Willoughby back out toward the moored boat. “I don’t expect you to be my friend, but thank you for coming. I need you to promise me you’ll never tell Durrance you were here, or a single word of what you told me. He’s Harry’s oldest friend, but he has no idea the feathers ever existed, or why our engagement broke off. I can’t let him find out.”
“Why not?” Willoughby frowned, stubborn as only an army man could be.

“Because I’m engaged to him,” Ethne said, simple as day. She knew perfectly well Durrance would never keep the engagement once he learned Harry had redeemed every scrap of his honor. He was far too good, far too honorable, to chain her to a blind man when the man she truly loved was free to come home. And she would not let him throw away the only bit of happiness he had left, not when she had bound herself to him by her own word. She would not break her own honor, not when Harry had fought so hard to win his back.

Willoughby whistled low. “Engaged to Jack Durrance! Guess I wasted my whole trip bringing you this feather, huh?” He nodded at the crumpled yellowed thing in her palm. Ethne flinched, pulling her hand away like he meant to snatch it back.
“Not wasted. I’m grateful. It’s all a mess, isn’t it?” Willoughby said, glancing between her face and the path to the water. “Rough on Harry. Rough on Jack. And if I’m any judge, it’s going to be roughest on you.”

Ethne did not answer. She hurried him down the bank to the boat, desperate to get him away before anyone spotted him — but she had forgotten all about Mrs. Adair, who had been glued to the drawing room curtain all morning, watching every step. She’d seen Ethne meet the strange man at the boat, seen Durrance follow them into the enclosure, seen him come back alone, and she’d burned with curiosity, half-convinced the stranger was Harry Feversham himself. She’d seen Ethne and Willoughby walk back to the water now, and she ran all the way across the lawn to catch them, just as Willoughby was about to cast off the rope.

“Captain Willoughby!” she called, smiling sweetly, and trapped him so neatly he admitted he was part of the North Surrey regiment — Harry Feversham’s old unit, of course. She tried to invite him in for lunch, but Ethne cut in sharp, lied that he had to get back to Kingsbridge immediately, and sent him pushing off from the shore before he could say a word that would give the whole tangled game away.
“Bit unfair to leave me starving, isn’t it?” Willoughby called back, laughing, as his boat drifted out into the creek.

Ethne laughed too, turning back toward the terrace at Mrs. Adair’s side. Once, twice, she opened her palm to the sun, staring down at that once-white, yellowed feather, and laughed a low, clear, private laugh. She offered no explanation, and none was asked. This was her secret, and her burden, and hers alone to carry.
