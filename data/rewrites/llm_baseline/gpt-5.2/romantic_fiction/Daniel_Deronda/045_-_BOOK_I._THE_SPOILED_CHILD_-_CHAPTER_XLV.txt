The Grandcourts had barely settled again into their white-and-gold rooms in Park Lane when the first invitation arrived—Lady Mallinger’s musical evening, neat as a visiting-card and yet heavy with all the unseen hands that pushed and pulled in Sir Hugo’s world. It was only the third night after their return; but for Gwendolen, the new ceilings and the pale crimson hangings were like a set hastily painted and already wearisome. Her thoughts kept circling back—like a refrain that would not be silenced—to a certainty: she would speak with Deronda again, and she would see the girl he had spoken of, the Miss Lapidoth who had “submitted to anything in the form of duty.”

That phrase—duty—lay in Gwendolen’s mind like a bitter spice too strong for the dish. It did not blend; it asserted itself. She repeated it with a kind of inward mockery, for she knew, even if she would not name it plainly, that her own submission had not the clean taste of obligation. It was submission to a yoke she had drawn over her own neck with an act she could not bear to look at, and the motives that had pulled it on were so selfish that they left no room for duty to take hold and lift.

Lady Mallinger’s drawing-rooms were already full of sound when the Grandcourts entered—first the measured stream of instrumental music, then that pause in which voices swelled up as if relieved from restraint. The air was warm with perfumes shaken from hair and fur, and with the faint resinous breath of polished wood: the scraped sweetness of violin-bow, the deeper, almost edible richness of old mahogany and beeswax, like a pantry warmed by a fire. Outside, far off, London rolled its iron wheels; inside, the acoustics were padded and gilded, the city’s harsher roar turned into a distant, dulled surf. Even so, the room had its own ecology of sound: the quick bright clink of bracelet against glass, the hush that people practiced as a kind of art, and under it the steady humming of many lives pressed together.

Klesmer sat already at the piano, his hands ready, his profile keen as a blade; and beside him stood Mirah Lapidoth—conspicuous not by boldness but by stillness, like a candle-flame that will not flicker. Klesmer, in his generous zeal for her, had chosen to accompany her in Leo’s “O patria mia,” a piece he said was more her own than the songs that had been worn smooth by a hundred fashionable throats.

Gwendolen, magnificent in pale green velvet, her diamonds like cold drops of poison, was ushered to a seat placed so that she could be seen and could see. On entering a room she had always possessed the hunter’s power—quick to distinguish faces and positions, to take the measure of a scene without betraying the act of measuring. She let her glance brush Mirah, and as she passed, she bowed to Klesmer. His answering smile flashed back on her with the cruelty of a memory: there had been a morning when it was her ambition to stand where this “little Jewess” stood, to be lifted above the crowd by talent rather than jewels. Now she was one of the crowd—silk, gems, trained grace—and all she could do was admire, or find fault, or sit with a face that said she was used to being there.

“He thinks I am in the right road now,” her resentment whispered, not loud enough to be heard, but sharp enough to sting her own heart.

She did not catch sight of Deronda at first. While she made her way through the proper interchange with Sir Hugo, her eyes traveled with practiced ease around the room, bowing here and there, careful not to let any anxious searching show itself—careful especially that her husband should not detect it and afterward brand it with that slow contempt of his as “damnably vulgar.”

But even the smoothest glance can stumble. Among the eyes that met hers and compelled a slight bow were those of Mr. Lush—the man she had pushed out of her mind as if will were a curtain. He stood near Grandcourt. Grandcourt, with a shoulder turned and his attention apparently for Lord Pentreath, let Lush hover at the edge of his importance like a servant who had once been an accomplice.

And then, for the first time, a sensation went through Gwendolen as sudden and disagreeable as tasting something rancid in a dish that looked perfect: Lush knew. He knew the old life, the hidden life—whatever there was that made her marriage feel like a locked room with no window. He had been absent, and in his absence her inner drama had shifted to other figures—Deronda, the girl singer, her own shame. Yet here Lush reappeared at her husband’s elbow, and in an instant her mind fabricated the certainty of his connection with the secrets that made her wretched.

She turned her head away with effort, continuing her survey as if she had seen nothing more noteworthy than a portrait. And then she found Deronda.

He stood not far from the door, with Hans Meyrick beside him, as if he had been careful to bring the young painter into Lady Mallinger’s orbit. Both had the look of men whose enjoyment is hitched to another’s success; they were anxious—anxious in that particular way artists and true listeners can be anxious—lest Mirah should be swallowed by talk, lest her voice should be treated like a decorative dessert set down among heavy roasts.

Deronda had come away from Lady Pentreath, who had spoken in her violoncello voice as if she were weighing an object she had bought: “Well, your Jewess is pretty—there’s no denying that. But where is her Jewish impudence? She looks as demure as a nun. I suppose she learned that on the stage.”

The word “Jewess” had struck him like a careless slap. In it he heard not merely ignorance but that fashionable habit of stamping human beings as if they were bales of imported silk with lettering that justified disdain. He winced the more because he knew how Mordecai would feel it—how that single word could carry a whole dominion of condescension, a dominant lexicon reducing a person to a label.

In this mood he saw the Grandcourts enter. Hans, delighted as always by a beauty he could frame into metaphor, leaned toward him. “That Vandyke duchess of a beauty!” he murmured.

Deronda’s response came with a sarcasm not quite good-natured. “I thought you could admire no style of woman but your Berenice.”

“That is the style I worship—not admire,” Hans returned, with his usual airy candor. “Other styles of women I might make myself wicked for, but for Berenice I could make myself—well, pretty good, which is something much more difficult.”

“Hush,” Deronda said, as if the music were about to begin—which it was.

He had never heard Mirah sing this “O patria mia.” He knew Leopardi’s ode—Italy as a mother in chains, hiding her face and weeping—and the few chosen words were filled, for him, with the greatness of all that lay unsaid: the untranslatability of grief that belongs to one’s native tongue and does not survive being carried into another. In Mirah’s singing, the syllables had the dark sweetness of something slowly reduced over fire—concentrated, essential; and the room itself seemed to alter its breath to receive it. The chatter stilled. Fans paused. Even the small noises—the rustle of silk, the click of a ring—became cautious, as if the listeners sensed that sound could bruise what was being offered.

Certain lines not in the song rang within Deronda nevertheless, as if they had been struck in his mind like a bell: “Non ti difende Nessun de’ tuoi! L’armi, qua l’armi: io solo Combatterò, procomberò sol io—” The heroic vow sounded to him not like vanity but like the pure, strange nourishment of unselfish love—love that people call “in vain” because they measure it by victory, not by what it reveals in the soul. And now that passion came to him in the image of a man who was dying away from battle, dying in a small room while his spirit demanded a battlefield.

Mirah was equal to every hope. When the applause rose, Klesmer’s praise—worth all the clapping—was given for her alone: “Good, good—the crescendo better than before.” But her chief anxiety, as always, was whether she had satisfied Mr. Deronda. She looked toward him, and he saw it; yet he remained where he was, watching as admirers gathered round her in little currents of compliment.

Presently those currents parted to let Gwendolen through. Mrs. Klesmer introduced her, and Gwendolen seated herself near Mirah on a red divan. The contrast between them—splendor and simplicity, the one armored with fashion, the other bare of it and therefore, in a way, more clothed—caught Klesmer’s amused eye. He lingered, smiling more with his gaze than his lips, as if he saw an odd duet beginning.

All the solicitude was on the side of the splendid one.

“You must let me say how much I am obliged to you,” Gwendolen began, her voice smooth, her manner carefully gracious. “I had heard from Mr. Deronda that I should have a great treat in your singing, but I was too ignorant to imagine how great.”

“You are very good to say so,” Mirah answered, her mind far less occupied with her own triumph than with studying this grand lady close at hand—this real creature of diamonds and trained ease, like someone who had stepped out of a drama Mirah had never been taught to read. There was in Gwendolen’s beauty, even under all its finish, a hint of tragedy—like a fine fruit with a bruise hidden on the side turned away.

“We shall all want to learn of you—I, at least,” Gwendolen went on. “I sing very badly, as Herr Klesmer will tell you.” She glanced up at Klesmer with an arch look and continued, “But I have been rebuked for not liking to be middling, since I can be nothing more. I think that is a different doctrine from yours?”

Klesmer answered quickly, with his brisk kindness. “Not if it means it would be worth while for you to study further, and for Miss Lapidoth to have the pleasure of helping you.” Then he moved away.

Mirah, taking everything with naïve seriousness, leaned toward Gwendolen. “If you think I could teach you, I shall be very glad. I am anxious to teach, but I have only just begun. If I do it well, it must be by remembering how my master taught me.”

Gwendolen had not expected such simple promptitude; it embarrassed her, because it was too real for the little play of phrases she had meant. She shifted, trying to change the subject, and her next question slipped into a tone that would have been rude to an equal, yet sounded, in her mind, like condescension made kindly.

“You have not been long in London, I think?—but you were perhaps introduced to Mr. Deronda abroad?”

“No,” Mirah said. “I never saw him before I came to England in the summer.”

“But he has seen you often and heard you sing a great deal, has he not?” Gwendolen asked—partly from an aching wish to hear anything about Deronda, partly from that helplessness which seizes even the ready talker when her mind is full and empty at once. “He spoke of you to me with the highest praise. He seemed to know you quite well.”

“Oh, I was poor and needed help,” Mirah answered, and at once her voice altered, warmed, as if gratitude itself were a hearth. “And Mr. Deronda has given me the best friends in the world. That is the only way he came to know anything about me—because he was sorry for me. I had no friends when I came. I was in distress. I owe everything to him.”

Gwendolen, who had once wanted to be a struggling artist herself, felt a prickle of humiliation she could not wholly understand. A question that would have been offensive to her, she had offered easily to this girl—this Jewess who might teach her singing. Yet Mirah’s reply did not plead; it only shone with a delicate insistence, as if she were guarding Deronda from any misunderstanding. There was in her gratitude a kind of strictness: she would not let anyone imagine a relation between herself and Deronda that was less purely generous than it was.

Deronda and Hans, watching from a distance, would have felt anger if they had known that Mirah had presented herself so simply as needy, to satisfy the curiosity of the fashionable world. But Mirah’s motive held a refinement she could not have explained: she would not allow anyone to place herself on equal terms with Deronda in imagination, as if his kindness were a casual acquaintance.

Gwendolen, however, heard only what pleased her most: that Deronda’s compassion had been ready, that he could bend down without despising. The thought soothed her with a sweetness that was almost pain. When Klesmer signaled he was about to play again, she moved away contentedly, without the least foreboding that this protégé would one day matter to her more than the possible improvement of her singing—if a Mrs. Grandcourt could ever have leisure and spirit for any lessons beyond those the world gave, charging dearly for every bitter mouthful.

Then, under the pretext of moving farther from the instrument, Gwendolen chose not to return to her former seat. With that alternation she had—careful for appearances, then suddenly reckless with impulse—she placed herself on a settee where she could have only one neighbor. It brought her nearer to Deronda.

He came up, naturally, in time to shake hands before the music began. He stood for a while by the empty end of the settee; then Klesmer’s playing rose—bass and treble rushing together like a tide—and in the storm of sound, in that brief license when art made all small social calculations seem ridiculous, Deronda sat down beside her.

But when the playing ended and talk burst forth like released steam, Gwendolen saw that Mr. Lush had placed himself within hearing, leaning against the wall close by them. His presence was like a coarse noise introduced into a delicate silence. She felt anger rise hot in her throat; yet she could not fling it out. She forced herself into a tone of polite indifference.

“Miss Lapidoth is everything you described her to be.”

“You have been very quick in discovering that,” Deronda said, with an irony that was more like a guard put up than a jest.

“I have not found out all the excellencies you spoke of—I don’t mean that,” Gwendolen hurried on. “But I think her singing is charming, and herself, too. Her face is lovely—not in the least common; and she is such a complete little person. I should think she will be a great success.”

The words grated on Deronda. He would not answer; his eyes fixed gravely ahead.

She knew he was displeased, and with Lush so near, blocking her as a wall blocks a voice, she could not say what she had come burning to say. The constraint stretched out, absurdly long—two people seated close, each locked in separate silence, while the room’s sounds flowed around them: laughter like broken glass, a low murmur like bees, the occasional hard edge of a man’s loud remark cutting through. At last Lush shifted from the wall and drifted away toward another group, as if his very leisure were a kind of insolence.

Gwendolen spoke at once, like someone snatching a moment before a door shuts.

“You despise me for talking artificially.”

“No,” Deronda said, looking at her coolly. “That is quite excusable sometimes. But I did not think what you were last saying was altogether artificial.”

“There was something in it that displeased you,” she insisted, her voice tightening. “What was it?”

“It is impossible to explain such things,” he answered. “One can never communicate niceties of feeling about words and manner.”

“You think I am shut out from understanding them,” Gwendolen said, a tremor betraying itself in spite of her effort. “Have I shown myself so very dense to everything you have said?”

Her eyes turned on him with a look that was like suppressed tears held down by pride, as one holds down a cry with one’s hand.

“Not at all,” Deronda said, and his voice softened. “But experience differs for different people. We don’t all wince at the same things. I have had plenty of proof that you are not dense.” He smiled at her.

“But one may feel things and not be able to do anything better for all that,” she said. She did not smile back. His words seemed to push her away into a cold distance. “I begin to think we can only get better by having people about us who raise good feelings. You must not be surprised at anything in me. I think it is too late for me to alter. I don’t know how to set about being wise, as you told me to be.”

“I seldom find I do any good by my preaching,” Deronda said, and there was sadness in it—his mind going back to the necklace, that unlucky interference which might lead only to his own pain, watching her hardened into another kind of gambling. “I might as well have kept from meddling.”

“Don’t say that.” Her words came hurriedly, as if she feared interruption, feared her own agitation would swell beyond control. “If you despair of me, I shall despair. Your saying that I should not go on being selfish and ignorant has been some strength to me. If you say you wish you had not meddled—that means you despair of me and forsake me. And then you will decide for me that I shall not be good. It is you who will decide; because you might have made me different by keeping as near to me as you could, and believing in me.”

As she spoke she did not look at him. She stared instead at the closed fan in her hand, as if the little carved handle were the only thing steady in the room. With the last words she rose and left him, returning to her former seat, which had been left vacant.

People settled into quiet again. Mirah’s voice began—Per pietà non dirmi addio—soft, searching, the melody seeming not a display but the shape of emotion itself, as if feeling had found a throat to pass through. In Deronda’s ear the strain became, for a moment, a continuation of Gwendolen’s pleading—a drawn-out, aching urgency, impossible to answer without cruelty, impossible to satisfy without betrayal of other claims.

He saw more clearly than ever the strange mixture in her: resolute pride and a precocious air of knowing the world, and yet a precipitate, almost guileless indiscretion. Sir Hugo’s hints had made him alert to dangers his own habit of pity might neglect; but it was plain as morning that Gwendolen’s reliance on him held no dream of trapping him, no notion that he might misread her. That trust, innocent in intention and terrible in consequence, wrestled with his sense of peril—and with the foreboding that her mind was fastening on him with an incompatible claim.

A collision seemed foreshadowed: on one side, Mordecai’s dying hand, grasping him with all the ideals and prospects it awakened; on the other, this fair creature in silk and gems, wounded in secret, dreading herself, stretching out her trust as if it were something she must lean on to keep from falling. It was as if he saw himself on a shore, arms reaching to him with cries, while the waves seized him and forced him toward a vessel already sailing for a far coast.

When Mirah’s song ended, he moved away, reproaching himself for exaggerating his own importance—accepting too readily Gwendolen’s view of him as if he could truly decide her fate.

Hans Meyrick came up with his irrepressible commentary. “What an enviable fellow you are,” he said. “Sitting on a sofa with that young duchess, and having an interesting quarrel with her!”

“Quarrel with her?” Deronda repeated, uncomfortable.

“Oh, about theology, of course; nothing personal,” Hans said, with airy assurance. “But she told you what you ought to think, and then left you with a grand air which was admirable. Is she an Antinomian? If so, tell her I am an Antinomian painter, and introduce me. I should like to paint her and her husband. He has the sort of handsome physique that the Duke ought to have in Lucrezia Borgia—if it could go with a fine baritone, which it can’t.”

Deronda devoutly hoped Hans’s version of what had been visible from afar was no more than the painter’s fancy, which turned even private pain into a scene.

Gwendolen, meanwhile, had her own afterthoughts. Had her husband watched her? Had his eyes extracted from her movements something to reprove—some failure of that perfect public equability which was her own ideal of dignity? Her conscience told her she had not kept it. Yet Grandcourt made no remark on her behavior.

Only when they were driving home, the carriage rocking with that subdued, expensive smoothness that seemed meant to deaden reality, did he speak.

“Lush will dine with us among the other people to-morrow. You will treat him civilly.”

Gwendolen’s heart began to beat violently, as if the words had struck it. The sentence she wanted to fling back—like a blow returned—rose in her throat: You are breaking your promise to me—the first promise you made me. But fear held her silent: fear of quarrel, as if she foresaw it might end not in harsh words but in throttling fingers.

After a pause she said, and the tone was more defeat than resentment, “I thought you did not intend him to frequent the house again.”

“I want him just now,” Grandcourt answered. “He is useful to me; and he must be treated civilly.”

Silence filled the carriage, but it was not quiet. It had its own sound—the tight, inward roar of a woman swallowing what she dared not say, and the steady, indifferent breath of a man who knew that his will set the household’s tune.

There may come a moment, even in a marriage that began with vows, when an excellent husband who once dropped smoking out of tenderness takes up his cigar again between himself and his wife, as if the air she must breathe were now his to flavor. Mr. Lush was, so to speak, a very large cigar. And if these are the sorts of lovers’ vows at which Jove laughs, he must have a merry time of it.
