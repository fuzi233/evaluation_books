"Truth will fail thee never, never! Though thy bark be tempest-driven, Though each plank be rent and riven, Truth will bear thee on for ever!"

These words rang hollow in Margaret's ears. The "bearing up better than likely" was becoming a terrible strain upon her spirit. Occasionally, amidst what passed for cheerful conversation with her father, a sudden, sharp thought pierced her defenses: she realized with a jolt that she no longer had a mother. Beyond this grief lay the heavy cloud surrounding Frederick. The Sunday post had failed to deliver their London correspondence, and by Tuesday, Margaret was disheartened to find silence once more. She was left in total darkness regarding his plans, while her father suffered miserably under the weight of this uncertainty. It shattered his recently acquired habit of sitting quietly in one armchair for hours; instead, he paced restlessly up and down the room, then out into the hall, only to return and bang bedroom doors shut with no discernible purpose.

Margaret attempted to soothe him by reading aloud, but it became clear he could not concentrate for long. In those moments, she felt profound gratitude that she had kept secret the additional source of anxiety stemming from her encounter with Leonard. Her relief was compounded when Mr. Thornton arrived. His presence alone served to redirect her father's troubled thoughts. He approached Mr. Hale immediately, taking his hands and wringing them tightly without uttering a word. For a minute or two, holding them within his own, his face and eyes conveyed a depth of sympathy that transcended language.

Turning then to Margaret, he found her looking far from "better than likely." Her stately beauty was dimmed by sleeplessness and weeping. An expression of gentle, patient sadness settled upon her features—a sadness that bordered on immediate physical suffering. He had intended to greet her with the studied coldness of his recent demeanor, yet he could not resist stepping toward her as she stood slightly apart, rendered timid by his unpredictable behavior lately. With a voice tender enough to bring tears to her eyes, he spoke only the necessary commonplace words, causing her to turn away to conceal her emotion.

She retreated to her work, sitting down in profound silence. Mr. Thornton's heart beat quickly and strongly; for the moment, he entirely forgot the events of Outwood Lane. He attempted to converse with Mr. Hale, finding unusual delight in the interaction. As Margaret watched, she noted how Mr. Hale, usually pleased by Thornton's power and decisiveness, found in him today a particularly safe and sure harbor.

Suddenly, Dixon appeared at the door, his manner so flustered that Margaret felt a sickness rise in her stomach. Something had happened to Fred. There was no doubt of it. Fortunately, both her father and Mr. Thornton were deeply engrossed in their conversation.

"What is it, Dixon?" Margaret asked the moment she had closed the drawing-room door behind her.

"Come this way, miss," Dixon replied, ushering her into what had formerly been Mrs. Hale's bedchamber, now serving as Margaret's quarters since her father refused to sleep there following his wife's passing.

"It's nothing, miss," Dixon choked out. "Only a police inspector. He wishes to see you, miss. But I dare say, it concerns nothing serious."

"Did he name—" Margaret began, her voice barely audible.

"No, miss; he named nothing. He merely asked if you resided here and if he might speak with you. Martha admitted him and showed him into Master's study. I went to inquire if that would suffice, but no—it is you, miss, whom he seeks."

Margaret remained silent until her hand rested on the study door lock. Turning then, she warned, "Take care Papa does not come down. Mr. Thornton is with him now."

The inspector was nearly intimidated by the haughtiness of her manner as she entered. Indignation flickered in her countenance, yet it was so suppressed and controlled that it lent her an air of superb disdain. There was no surprise, no curiosity. She simply awaited the opening of his business. She asked no questions.

"I beg your pardon, ma'am, but my duty compels me to ask you a few plain questions," the inspector stated. "A man died at the Infirmary due to a fall sustained at Outwood Station between five and six o'clock on Thursday evening, the twenty-sixth. Initially, the fall seemed inconsequential, but physicians state it proved fatal due to an underlying internal condition and the victim's habitual drinking."

Her large, dark eyes widened slightly as they locked onto his face. Otherwise, she remained immovable. Her lips curved into a fuller line than usual, a result of muscle tension, though he lacked the knowledge of their normal state to recognize the sullen defiance in their sweep. She neither blanched nor trembled. She fixed him with an unblinking gaze. When he paused, she encouraged him, almost as if inviting him to finish his tale: "Well—go on!"

"It is suspected an inquest will be required," the inspector continued. "There is slight evidence suggesting the blow, push, or scuffle that caused the fall was provoked by the deceased's half-drunk insolence toward a young lady walking with the man who shoved him off the platform. This was witnessed by someone on the platform, who, however, deemed the incident minor and paid it no further heed. Furthermore, there is reason to identify the lady as yourself; in which case—"

"I was not there," Margaret interrupted, maintaining her expressionless stare with the vacant look of a sleepwalker.

The inspector bowed but said nothing. The woman before him displayed no emotion, no fluttering fear, no anxiety, and no desire to conclude the interview. The information he possessed was vague: a porter, rushing to prepare for a train, had seen a scuffle at the far end of the platform between Leonard and a gentleman accompanied by a lady, hearing no sound. Before the train reached full speed, the porter was nearly knocked down by the headlong rush of the enraged, half-intoxicated Leonard, who swore and cursed violently. The porter had not dwelt on it until the inspector unearthed his testimony. Subsequent inquiries at the station revealed that a young lady and gentleman had indeed been present around that hour; the lady was described as remarkably handsome and identified by a grocer's assistant as Miss Hale of Crampton, whose family shopped at his establishment. While certainty was lacking, the probability was high.

Leonard himself, driven mad by rage and pain, had fled to the nearest gin palace for solace. His drunken ravings went unnoticed by the busy waiters, who only recalled him rising abruptly and cursing himself for failing to utilize the electric telegraph sooner, for some purpose unknown. They believed he departed intending to use it. Overcome by pain or drink en route, he collapsed in the street, where police found him and transported him to the Infirmary. He never regained sufficient consciousness to provide a coherent account, though brief lucid intervals prompted authorities to summon a magistrate in hopes of securing a dying deposition. However, the magistrate found Leonard rambling about being at sea, confusing the names of captains and lieutenants with those of his fellow porters. His final words were a curse against the "Cornish trick," which he claimed had cost him a hundred pounds.

The inspector reviewed these facts in his mind: the vagueness of the evidence placing Margaret at the station, contrasted with her unflinching, calm denial. She stood awaiting his next word with a composure that seemed supreme.

"Then, madam, I have your denial that you were the lady accompanying the gentleman who struck the blow, or gave the push, resulting in the death of this poor man?"

A quick, sharp pain shot through Margaret's brain. *Oh God! that I knew Frederick were safe!*

A deep observer of human faces might have perceived the momentary agony flashing through her gloomy eyes, akin to the torture of a creature cornered. Yet, the inspector, though keen, was not profound. Still, he was struck by the nature of her response, which sounded like a mechanical repetition of her initial denial, unchanged and unmodified to address his specific query.

"I was not there," she said slowly and heavily. Throughout this exchange, she never closed her eyes or ceased that glassy, dream-like stare.

His quick suspicions were aroused by this dull echo of her former denial. It was as if she had forced herself into a single falsehood and been stunned into an inability to vary it. He deliberately closed his notebook of notes, then looked up. She had not moved an inch, appearing as solid as a great Egyptian statue.

"I hope you will not deem me impertinent when I say I may need to call upon you again. I may have to summon you to appear at the inquest to prove an alibi should my witnesses"—he gestured to the single individual who had recognized her—"persist in testifying to your presence at this unfortunate event."

He scrutinized her sharply. She remained perfectly quiet, showing no change of color or darkening shadow of guilt upon her proud face. He thought he saw a wince, but he did not truly know Margaret Hale. He felt somewhat abashed by her genuine composure. It must have been a mistake of identity.

"It is very unlikely, ma'am, that I shall have to proceed to such measures," he added. "I trust you will excuse me for performing my duty, even if it appears impertinent."

Margaret bowed her head as he turned toward the door. Her lips were stiff and dry; she could not muster even the common words of farewell. Suddenly, however, she stepped forward, opened the study door, and preceded him
