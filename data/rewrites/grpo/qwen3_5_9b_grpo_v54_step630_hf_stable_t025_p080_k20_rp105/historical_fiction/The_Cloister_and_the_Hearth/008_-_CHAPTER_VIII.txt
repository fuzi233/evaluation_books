While the burgomaster of Tergou laid his traps for Gerard, a quieter, yet no less desperate storm brewed within the walls of Sevenbergen. Margaret found herself in a predicament born of a housewife's deepest shame: hunger. She stood before Martin Wittenhaagen, the veteran soldier, her eyes wet with unshed tears.

"Martin," she whispered, her voice trembling, "there is nothing left in the larder. Gerard returns tonight, and he is so careless; he forgets to eat at home until his labor is done, and then he comes to me, poor soul, nearly fainting. How can I offer food to the one who loves me so dearly when my cup is empty?"

Martin scratched his beard, his brow furrowed in concern. "What remedy do I have?"

"It is Thursday," she insisted, "your appointed day for the hunt. I had counted on your return today."

"Nay," the soldier replied, shaking his head. "I dare not draw my bow when the Duke or his retinue are out in the chase. Read this." He produced a parchment sealed with the grand insignia of Philip, Duke of Burgundy. It was a stipend and license granting Martin, one of the Duke's archers, a yearly allowance of four merks for past service and a wound sustained at the Duke's side. The license permitted him to shoot three arrows weekly—specifically on Thursdays—in the Duke's forests of Holland, provided the game was limited to a seven-year-old buck or a doe with a fawn. However, the proviso was absolute: if the Duke or his friends were hunting, Martin was forbidden from disturbing the woods, lest he forfeit his salary, his life, and pay a fine of a penny.

Margaret sighed, the weight of the law pressing down on her.

"Cheer up, mistress," Martin said gently, though his tone was grave. "For your sake, I will risk my carcass. I have done worse for women of lesser worth. It is not a great danger; I shall merely step to the edge of the forest. Odds are they will drive a hare or a fawn within range."

"If I permit you to go," Margaret pleaded, "promise me you will not stray far and remain unseen. Far better that Gerard goes supperless than that harm should come to you, faithful Martin."

With the promise extracted, Martin took his bow and three arrows, slipping cautiously into the woods, barely a furlong from the house. The faint cry of horns echoed in the distance, signaling the Duke's party; all the game was now on the move.

"Come," Martin thought, "I shall soon fill the pot, and none the wiser." He positioned himself behind a thick oak overlooking an open glade, drawing his weapon—a formidable instrument of English yew, six feet two inches tall and thick as a man's thigh. Martin, broad-chested with arms of iron and cord, had drawn this bow since infancy. He could pull a three-foot arrow to its head, and when released, the arrow flew so fast the eye could scarcely track it, the string twanging with a musical snap. This bow had claimed many a stout soldier in the wars of the Hoecks and Cabbel-jaws, where battles were fought with few combatants but many deaths, for they faced their enemies clearly rather than through the haze of modern smoke.

A hare cantered into view, then sat alert, its ears forming a perfect 'V'. Martin leveled his massive weapon. The arrow flew, the string sang, but in his haste to secure the kill, he missed by an inch. The shaft struck the earth beside the startled hare, passing beneath her belly like a flash before vanishing into the grass. The hare leaped three feet vertically and bolted at full speed.

"Bungler!" Martin muttered. A true proof he was not an habitual bungler, for he would have blamed the hare if he were. He fitted another arrow just as a wood-pigeon settled on the very branch above him. "Aha!" he thought. "Small, but dainty." This time he took care, drawing smoothly and releasing with precision. The arrow appeared to pierce the bird cleanly, sending feathers skyward like dust. Yet, the bird, its breast torn rather than pierced, fluttered weakly away. By sheer effort, it rose above the canopy, flew fifty yards, and fell dead, hidden by the thick foliage.

" Luck is against me," Martin said despondently. He fitted a third arrow and scanned the glade. Suddenly, he heard a commotion behind him and turned just in time to see a noble buck crossing the open space—too late to shoot. He dashed his bow to the ground in frustration.

At that precise moment, a long-spotted animal glided swiftly across the path, its belly skimming the earth. Martin snatched up his bow instantly. He recognized the Duke's leopard.

"The hunters will not be far behind," he realized. "I must vanish. Gerard must go supperless tonight."

He plunged into the woods, following the trail of the buck and the leopard, the only path back to safety. Soon, he heard an unnatural sound ahead: leaves rustling violently, the ground trampled. Hurrying forward, he discovered the leopard atop the buck, tearing at its flesh with teeth and claws. The deer ran in a frantic circle, convulsing as blood poured from its hide.

Martin formed a desperate resolve. He needed the venison for Margaret. Drawing his arrow to the head, he buried it deep in the deer. Despite the predator on its back, the buck bounded high into the air and collapsed, dead. The leopard continued to tear at the carcass as if nothing had happened.

Martin hoped the beast would gorge itself and then leave the meat. Minutes passed. He walked resolutely forward and placed his hand on the buck's leg. The leopard gave a frightful growl, pausing its feeding. She sensed his presence, sulky and wary. What was to be done? Martin recalled an old tale: wild creatures could not withstand the steady gaze of a human. Standing erect, he fixed his eyes upon the beast. The leopard returned a savage glare, never breaking contact.

Continuing to stare down the animal, the leopard, brutally ignorant of natural history, lunged at his head with a terrifying yell, her eyes flaming, jaws distended. Martin had but a split second to catch her by the throat before her teeth crushed his face. One claw seized his shoulder, ripping the fabric; the other, aimed at his cheek, would have been fatal. Martin was old-fashioned, wearing no hat, only a scapulary of rough leather over his jerkin. He had thrown it over his head like a hood, and the brute's claw snagged in the loose material.

He struggled to keep her teeth from his face, gripping her throat with iron force while she tore at his shoulder. It felt like blunt reaping-hooks grinding against bone. The pain was excruciating, yet instead of cowering, it ignited his blood. He gnashed his teeth in rage almost as fierce as hers, squeezing her neck with unyielding strength. Their eyes flared in a silent duel—the man's fury matching the brute's. Realizing she was being throttled, she made a wild attempt to break free, dragging his hood across his face, blinding him, and tearing her claw from his shoulder, taking flesh and bone with it. Still, he held her.

Presently, her long tail, raised high in the air, dropped. "Aha!" Martin cried joyfully, gripping her like death. Next, her body lost its elasticity. He held a choked, powerless thing, maintaining his grip until all motion ceased. Only then did he dash her to the earth. Panting, he removed his hood. The leopard lay mute at his feet, tongue protruding, paws bloody. For the first time, terror seized Martin.

"I am a dead man," he whispered. "I have slain the Duke's leopard."

Hastily, he gathered handfuls of leaves and threw them over the carcass, then shouldered the heavy deer and staggered away, leaving a trail of mingled blood—his own and the deer's. He burst into Peter's house a horror-stricken figure, bleeding and stained, and flung the deer's carcass onto the floor.

"There," he gasped, refusing questions. "Broil me a steak on it; I am faint."

Margaret did not see his wounds; she assumed the blood belonged solely to the deer. She busied herself at the fire, while the stout soldier stanching and binding his own wound apart. Soon, Martin, Gerard, and Margaret sat supping royally on the broiled venison.

They were merry indeed. Gerard, with wonderful thoughtfulness, had brought a flask of Schiedam, and under its influence, Martin revived, recounting the tale of the hunt. They laughed over the exploit, the tension momentarily forgotten.

Then, their mirth was shattered. Margaret's gaze became fixed, fascinated, her cheeks paling with sudden fear. She gasped, unable to speak, pointing a trembling finger toward the window. Their eyes followed hers. There, in the twilight, crouched a dark form with eyes like glowing worms.

It was the leopard.

As they stood petrified, hypnotized by the green fire of its eyes, a single, deep bay echoed from the woods. Martin trembled at the sound.

"They have lost her," he realized, dread filling his heart. "They have laid muzzled bloodhounds on her scent. They will find her here, and the venison. Good-bye, friends. Martin Wittenhaagen ends here."

Gerard seized his bow and pressed it into the soldier's hands.

"Be a man!" he cried. "Shoot her and fling her into the wood before they arrive. Who will know?"

More voices of hounds broke out, growing louder, closer.

"Curse her!" Martin roared. "I spared her once; now she must die, or I, or both of us more likely." He reared his bow, drawing the arrow to the head.

"Nay! Nay!" cried Margaret, seizing the arrow. It snapped in half; the pieces fell on either side of the bow. Simultaneously, the air filled with the tongues of the hounds; they were hot upon the scent.

"What have you done, wench?" Martin screamed, despair clawing at his throat. "You have put the halter round my neck!"

"No!" Margaret cried. "I have saved you! Stand back from the window, both! Your knife, quick!"

She seized his long-pointed knife, almost tearing it from his girdle, and darted from the room. The house was now surrounded by baying dogs and shouting men.

The glowworm eyes moved not.
