The dust hung in the air, thick and choking, as the three columns of Zulu warriors crept forward with a terrifying, deliberate slowness. There was no panic, no frantic energy, only the grim, rhythmic approach of inevitable doom. When they reached roughly five hundred yards from our position, the central column halted abruptly at the base of a narrow ridge of open plain that jutted upward into the hill. This pause was tactical, a brief respite to allow the flanking divisions to complete their encirclement of our horseshoe-shaped defensive perimeter, ensuring that the triple assault would strike simultaneously.

"Oh, for a Gatling gun!" Good groaned, his voice tight with frustration as he stared down at the serried ranks of enemies below. "I'd clear that plain in twenty minutes."

"We haven't one," Sir Henry replied, his tone pragmatic despite the gravity of the situation. "So yearning is useless. But suppose you try a shot, Quatermain?" He gestured toward a tall figure standing slightly apart from the main mass, clearly the commander. "Aim for him. Two to one you'll miss, and if you hit, I promise you an honest sovereign once we're out of this hell."

The wager sparked a competitive fire in me. Loading the express rifle with solid ball, I waited until the officer stepped ten yards forward, accompanied only by his orderly, giving me a clearer line of sight. I dropped to the ground, bracing the rifle against a jagged rock. The weapon was sighted only to three hundred and fifty yards, so I had to calculate the ballistic drop, aiming halfway down the officer's torso where I believed his chest lay. He stood perfectly still, oblivious to the predator watching him. Whether it was the adrenaline, the wind, or simply the sheer distance, the shot went wide. My target remained upright, unharmed, while his orderly, standing mere paces to the left, crumpled instantly, seemingly dead.

The officer spun around, alarm flashing across his face as he fled back to his men.

"Bravo, Quatermain!" Good shouted, his relief palpable. "You've frightened him."

Anger surged through me, hot and irrational. I detested missing in public; a man masters only one art, and he must guard his reputation in it fiercely. Humiliated by my failure, I acted rashly. As the general turned to run, I snapped the rifle up and fired the second barrel. The man threw his arms up in a futile gesture of surrender and collapsed onto his face. This time, the kill was clean. A cold, brutal satisfaction washed over me—not for the death itself, but for the restoration of my competence.

The regiments witnessing the feat erupted in wild cheers, interpreting the display of white man's magic as an omen of victory. Simultaneously, the force the general had commanded fell back in chaotic disorder. Seizing the momentum, Sir Henry and Good raised their rifles, the latter methodically suppressing the dense mass of attackers with his Winchester repeater. I added a few shots of my own, managing to knock several men out of the fight before they could close the distance.

Just as we ceased firing, an ominous roar erupted from our far right, quickly echoed by a similar thunderous sound on our left. The other two divisions had engaged.

At the sound, the line of defenders before us spread out, charging uphill toward the bare grassland at a slow trot, their voices rising in a deep-throated chant. We returned fire steadily, Ignosi joining in occasionally, accounting for several stragglers, yet our efforts amounted to nothing against the tidal wave of armed humanity rushing toward us. Like stones thrown against a crashing breaker, our bullets made no dent in their momentum.

They surged forward, screaming and clashing spears, driving in the pickets we had anchored among the rocks at the hill's foot. Their advance slowed as they climbed, conserving breath for the final push. Our defenses were tiered: the first line half-way down the slope, the second fifty yards back, and the third occupying the plateau's edge.

"They come!" the cry rang out as they launched themselves up the incline, shouting their war-cry: "*Twala! Twala! Chiele! Chiele!*"—*Smite! Smite!*

"*Ignosi! Ignosi! Chiele! Chiele!*" our men roared back.

Now close enough to touch, the *tollas*, or throwing knives, flashed in a deadly arc. With an awful, guttural yell, the battle closed in. The mass of warriors swayed back and forth, bodies falling as easily as leaves in an autumn storm. But soon, the superior weight of the attacking force became undeniable. Our first line was slowly crushed backward, merging into the second. The struggle there was ferocious, yet our defenders were driven back again, retreating step by agonizing step until, within twenty minutes of the fight's start, our third line was forced into action.

By now, the assailants were exhausted and bleeding heavily. Breaking through that third, impenetrable wall of spears proved beyond their power. For a while, the seething lines of savages oscillated in the fierce ebb and flow of combat, the outcome hanging in the balance. Sir Henry watched the desperate melee with a kindling look in his eye, then, without a word, he charged into the thickest part of the fray, followed closely by Good.

As for me, I stayed put.

Our soldiers spotted his tall form plunging into the chaos and let out a roar: "*Nanzia Incubu! Nanzia Unkungunklovo!*"—*Here is the Elephant!*

From that moment, the tide turned. Fighting with splendid gallantry, the attacking force was gradually pushed back down the hillside until they retreated in confusion. Just as the left flank reported a successful repulse, I began to believe the crisis was over. Then, to our horror, we saw our men on the right being driven across the plain toward us, pursued by swarms of the enemy who had finally broken through.

Ignosi, standing beside me, assessed the situation in a single glance and issued a rapid command. Instantly, the reserve regiment surrounding us, the Greys, extended outward. Another word from Ignosi, relayed by the captains, and in a heartbeat, I found myself swept up in a furious onslaught. Clinging to the rear of Ignosi's massive frame, I did my best to survive, stumbling along as if I welcomed the end. Within moments, we were plunging through the fleeing groups of our own men, who were scrambling to reform behind us. Then, memory fractured.

All I recall is a terrible rolling noise—the clash of shields—and the sudden appearance of a hulking giant, his eyes bulging from their sockets, lunging straight at me with a bloody spear. But I rose—or rather, sank—to the occasion. Most men would have collapsed utterly; instead, seeing that standing meant death, I dove flat on the ground just as the monster struck. Unable to stop, he crashed headlong over my prostrate form. Before he could recover, I sprang up and dispatched him with my revolver.

Someone knocked me down shortly after, and darkness swallowed me.

When consciousness returned, I found myself back at the kopje, Good leaning over me with a gourd of water.

"How do you feel, old fellow?" he asked, anxiety etched on his face.

I sat up, shaking off the daze. "Pretty well, thank you."

"Thank Heaven!" he breathed. "When I saw them carry you in, I felt sick. I thought you were finished."

"Not this time, my boy. Just a rap on the head knocked me out. How has it ended?"

"For a while, they've been repulsed everywhere," Good said, pointing to long lines of men advancing in fours. "But the loss is dreadful. We have nearly two thousand killed and wounded; they must have lost three times as many. Look at the sight!"

In the center of every quartet marched a kind of hide-tray, a standard carried by Kukuana forces, with loops for handles at each corner. Upon these endless trays lay the wounded. As they arrived, medicine men hurriedly examined them. If the wound wasn't fatal, the sufferer was taken away for care. But if the injury was hopeless, the procedure that followed was horrific, though perhaps the ultimate mercy. A doctor would swiftly open an artery with a sharp knife under the guise of examination, and within minutes the victim would expire painlessly. Many such cases occurred that day. Since the broad Kukuanan spears usually made recovery impossible, this practice was common. Often the victims were already unconscious, or the arterial nick was so swift and precise they didn't even feel it. It was a ghastly spectacle, one we were grateful to escape, though nothing else I witnessed compared to the sight of those gallant soldiers being put out of their misery by the red-handed healers, nor the later discovery of a Swazi force burying their hopelessly wounded alive.

Hurrying to the far side of the kopje, we found Sir Henry, still gripping a battle-axe, consulting deeply with Ignosi, Infadoos, and a few chiefs.

"Thank Heaven you're here, Quatermain!" Sir Henry exclaimed. "I can't quite grasp what Ignosi intends. Though we've beaten off the attack, Twala is bringing in massive reinforcements and shows signs of investing us, intending to starve us out."

"That's awkward."

"Yes, especially since Infadoos reports the water supply has failed."
