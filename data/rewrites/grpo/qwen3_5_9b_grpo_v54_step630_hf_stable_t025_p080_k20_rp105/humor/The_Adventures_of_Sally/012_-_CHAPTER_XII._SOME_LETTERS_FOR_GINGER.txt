Laurette et Cie.,
Regent Street,
London, W.,
England.

January 21st.

Dearest Ginger,

I'm feeling better. Since it's been three months since I last wrote, you'd probably think I'm a weak-minded creature if I weren't. One *ought* to recover from anything in three months. Unfortunately, I haven't quite succeeded, but I have managed to stash my troubles in the cellar and stop dragging them out to inspect them daily. That counts for something, doesn't it?

I should probably give you my impressions of London, but I've grown so accustomed to it that I've lost all sense of novelty. It feels like I've lived here for decades.

You'll notice from the address that Mr. Faucitt hasn't sold his inheritance yet. He expects to soon; there's a whiskered man with a predatory eye he lunches with constantly, and I suspect big deals are brewing. Poor dear! He's desperate to escape to the countryside, grow ducks, and settle down. London has failed him. It's not the place it used to be. Until recently, he wandered disconsolately trying to locate the landmarks of his youth. (He hasn't been to England in nearly thirty years!) Apparently, every thirty years or so, London suffers a collective amnesia and repaints a shop-front red instead of blue, which drives the returned exile mad. Mr. Faucitt feels like Rip Van Winkle. First shock: the Empire Theatre is now a music-hall. Second shock: the Tivoli music-hall has been demolished entirely. Third shock: the baker's shop in Rupert Street, where he lodged in the eighties, is now a dressmaker's. He only cheered up slightly when a lovely magenta fog rolled in, reminding him that some things remain unchanged.

Thank goodness, I am kept quite busy at Laurette et Cie. (Not being a French scholar like you—do you remember Jules?—I initially thought "Cie" was the junior partner and looked forward to shaking his hand: *"Miss Nicholas, shake hands with Mr. Cie, one of your greatest admirers."*) I hold down the female equivalent of your job at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd.; essentially, I'm the right-hand woman. I hover near the entrance, sidle up to customers, and exclaim, *"Chawming weather, moddom!"* (usually a lie), then pass them to the staff who actually do the work. I wouldn't mind continuing this for years, but Mr. Faucitt is determined to sell. I don't know if you share this trait, but every Englishman I've met seems obsessed with owning a house in Loamshire, Hants, or Salop. Their sole ambition is to make money and "buy back the old place"—which, of course, was sold at the end of Act One to pay the heir's gambling debts.

Mr. Faucitt, as a boy, lived in a tiny Gloucestershire village near Cirencester—or rather, Cissister, which I bet you didn't know. After forgetting it for fifty years, he's suddenly bitten by the desire to die there, surrounded by pigs and chickens. He took me to see it the other day. Oh, Ginger, the English country! I can't fathom why any of you live in towns. Old, grey stone houses, yellow haystacks, squelchy muddy lanes, great fat trees, and blue hills in the distance. The peace! If I ever sell my soul, I'll demand the Devil grant me forty years in an English country place in exchange.

Perhaps you think I'm too busy to remember you. To prove otherwise, let me tell you: a week ago, while reading the paper, I saw the headline "International Match." It meant nothing at first, then I remembered. This was the sport you once snipped for! So I headed to Twickenham to witness the spectacle. There was an enormous crowd, and I was nearly crushed, but I endured it for your sake. I learned the English team wore white and the Welsh wore red. After a neighbor yelled himself hoarse, I asked, "Could you kindly inform me which is the English scrum-half?" Just then, players rushed past, and a dozen men in red threw themselves violently onto a meek little fellow who had just fallen on the ball. Ginger, you are safe! That was the scrum-half, and I gathered that such brutality is merely routine for him. Stopping a rush, it is called, and he does it constantly. The idea of you ever engaging in such daredevil feats! You thank your stars for your safe stool in Fillmore's outer office, where you can summon a cop if anyone jumps on you. Did you really perform these stunts? You must have hidden depths I never suspected.

While riding Piccadilly on a bus, I saw someone familiar: Mr. Carmyle. He's back in England. Thank goodness he didn't see me. I don't wish to encounter anyone reminding me of New York just now.

Thanks for the news, but please stop sending it. It makes me remember, and I don't want to. Here's the deal, Ginger: let me write to you because it relieves me, but do not answer. Do you mind? I'm sure you'll understand.

So Fillmore and Gladys Winch are married! From what I've seen of her, it's the best thing that ever happened to Brother F. She's splendid. I must write to him...

Laurette et Cie.,
London.

March 12th.

Dearest Ginger,

I saw in a Sunday paper last week that "The Primrose Way" was produced in New York to great success. Well, I'm glad. But I don't think newspapers should print such things; it's unsettling.

The next day, feeling blue and lonely and far from everyone, I did something foolish: I visited your club and asked for you! A nice old man in uniform at the desk spoke in a fatherly tone, saying you hadn't been in lately and perhaps were out of town, but offered me a seat while he inquired. He then summoned a tiny boy, also in uniform, who skipped off chanting, *"Mister Kemp! Mister Kemp!"* in a shrill treble. Hearing your name echo in the distance gave me such an odd feeling. I felt ashamed to cause them trouble, and when the boy returned, I slipped twopence into his palm. I suppose that violates the rules, though he seemed to enjoy it.

Mr. Faucitt has sold the business and retired to the country, leaving me rather at a loose end...

Monk's Crofton,
(Meaning unknown)
Much Middleford,
Salop,
(Slang for Shropshire)
England.

April 18th.

Dearest Ginger,

What's the use? What is the use? I try to escape New York, and New York tracks me down to my hiding place. A week or so ago, walking aimlessly down the Strand, who do you think appeared? Fillmore, arm in arm with Mr. Carmyle! I couldn't dodge. First, Mr. Carmyle had spotted me; second, it's a day's journey to evade poor dear Fillmore now. I blushed for him. Ginger! Right there in the Strand, I blushed for him. In my worst dreams, I never pictured him so enormous. Upon what meat does this giant Fillmore feed? Poor Gladys! When she looks at him, she must feel like a bigamist.

Apparently, Fillmore is still full of grand schemes. He talked airily about buying English plays. He's come over, as you likely know, to arrange "The Primrose Way" here. He's staying at the Savoy, and they took me off to lunch, whooping joyfully like a strayed lamb. It was the worst possible thing. Fillmore talked Broadway without pausing until, after working his way past French pastry and lolling back, breathing stertorously, waiting for coffee and liqueurs, he had filled me with such homesickness that, if I hadn't wanted to make a public scene, I would have broken down and howled. It was insane of me to go near the Savoy. Of course, it's simply an annex to Broadway. Americans sat at every table as far as the eye could reach. I might as well have been at the Astor.

Well, if Fate insists on bringing New York to England for my discomfiture, I suppose I must endure it. I let events take their course and have been drifting ever since. Two days ago, I drifted here. Mr. Carmyle invited Fillmore—and he seems to love Fillmore—and me to Monk's Crofton. I had no excuse to refuse, so I came. Now I sit writing to you in an enormous bedroom with an open fire, armchairs, and every luxury. Fillmore is out golfing. He sails for New York on Saturday aboard the Mauretania. I am horrified to learn that, in addition to his other schemes, he is now promoting a light-weight championship fight in Jersey City, guaranteeing enormous sums to both boxers. Arguing with him is futile; he simply quotes figures showing how others profited. Besides, it's too late anyway. As far as I can tell, the fight is in a week or two. Still, it makes my flesh creep.

Let's change the subject. Do you know Monk's Crofton? Probably not, as I recall hearing it was a recent purchase. Mr. Carmyle bought it from a lord who lost money on the Stock Exchange. I hope you haven't seen it, because I want to describe it at great length. I want to pour out my soul about it. Ginger, what has England ever done to deserve such paradises? I thought Mr. Faucitt's Cissister place was wonderful, but it doesn't even begin to compete. Of course, his is just an ordinary country house, whereas this is a Seat. Monk's Crofton is the sort of place described in English novels: *"The sunset was falling on the walls of G---- Castle, in B----shire..."* I can imagine Tennyson's Maud living here. It is one of England's stately homes; how beautifully they stand, and I'm crazy about it.

You motor up from the station, drive about three miles, then turn into a big iron gate flanked by stone posts with stone beasts. Close by is a cute little lodge with an old man who pops out to touch his hat. You think you've arrived, ready to jump out, but the car rolls on for another fifty miles through beech woods full of rabbits and open meadows with deer. Finally, just as you think you'll go forever, you whizz around a corner, and there it is. You don't see it until then because the trees are too thick.

It's very large, low, and square, with a tower on one side and a fascinating upper porch with battlements. I suppose in old days you stood there dropping molten lead on visitors' heads. Wonderful lawns surround it, with shrubberies and a lake visible where the ground dips beyond the fields. It's too early for flowers, but to the left, a million roses await June. Along the side of the rose garden is a high wall of old red brick shutting off the kitchen garden. I explored it this morning. It's enormous, with hot-houses and a cunning farm at one end featuring a stable yard full of puppies that tear your heart out with their sweetness. And a big, sleepy cat sitting in the sun, blinking, letting the puppies run over her. There's a lovely stillness, and you can hear everything growing. Thrushes and blackbirds... Oh, Ginger, it's heavenly!

But there's a catch. It's a case of "Where every prospect pleases and only man is vile." At least, not exactly vile, but terribly stodgy. I now see why you couldn't hit it off with the Family. Because I've seen them all! They're here! Yes, Uncle Donald and everyone else. Is it a habit for your family to collect in gangs, or did I stumble into an accidental Old Home Week? When I came down to dinner the first evening, the drawing-room was bursting—not just because Fillmore was there, but because uncles and aunts were everywhere. I felt like a small lion in a den of Daniels. I know exactly what you meant about the Family. They look at you! Of course, it's fine for me since I'm snowy white clear through, but I can imagine your time with your permanently guilty conscience. You must have had an awful time.

By the way, getting this letter through will be delicate—like carrying dispatches through enemy lines in a Civil War play. You're supposed to leave letters on the hall table for collection by bicycle later. But if I do that, some aunt or uncle will surely see it, and I'll be an object of loathing. It is no light matter, my lad, to be caught corresponding with a human Jimpson weed like you. It would blast me socially. At least, so I gather from last night's dinner. Someone mentioned you, and a terrible roasting session erupted. Uncle Donald acted as cheerleader. I feebly admitted I'd met you and found you part-human, resulting in an awful silence until they all started correcting me simultaneously, showing how cruelly my girlish inexperience had deceived me. A young, innocent half-portion like me apparently cannot suspect the true infamy of the dregs of society. You aren't fit to speak to people like us, being at best a blot on the human race. I tell you this in case you imagine you're popular with the Family. You're not.

So I must exercise snaky craft to smuggle this letter through. I'll take it to the village myself if I can sneak away. It will be difficult, as for some reason I seem to be a center of attraction. Except when I hide in my room, hardly a moment passes without an aunt or uncle popping out for a "cosy talk." It sometimes seems as though they're weighing me in the balance. Well, let them weigh!

Time to dress for dinner. Good-bye.

Yours in the balance,

Sally.
