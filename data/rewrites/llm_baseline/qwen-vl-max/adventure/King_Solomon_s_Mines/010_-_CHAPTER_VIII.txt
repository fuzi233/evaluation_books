The afternoon sun cast long shadows as we traveled along the magnificent roadway, stretching steadily toward the northwest. Infadoos and Scragga walked beside us, while their men marched ahead—about a hundred paces distant, like a silent guard escorting royalty. The road itself was an engineering marvel, smooth and broad, carved through the land with precision that spoke of ancient hands.

“Infadoos,” I said at length, “who built this road?”

“It was made long ago, my lord,” he replied, his voice grave. “No one knows when or by whom—not even Gagool, the wise woman who remembers generations past. We are too young to recall its making. No one today could fashion such a path. But the king commands that no grass grow upon it—so it remains clear and strong.”

“And those carvings on the cave walls?” I asked, recalling the Egyptian-like figures we’d seen earlier. “Who inscribed them?”

“The same hands that laid the stones wrote the words,” Infadoos answered. “We do not know who they were.”

I turned to him again. “When did your people, the Kukuana, come into this land?”

“Ten thousand thousand moons ago,” he said, pointing northward, beyond the snow-capped peaks. “They came down like a storm from the great lands beyond. They could go no further—the high mountains encircle this country, as our ancestors told us, and as Gagool confirms. The land was fertile, so they settled, grew strong, and now their numbers are as countless as the sands of the sea. When Twala calls his regiments, their plumes cover the plain as far as the eye can see.”

“But if the mountains surround you,” I pressed, “who do your warriors fight?”

“Not all is walled in, my lord,” he said. “To the north, the land opens. From time to time, warlike strangers descend upon us in great numbers—from lands we do not know. We meet them and destroy them. It has been three lifetimes since the last war. Thousands died, but we broke their strength. Since then, peace has held.”

“Then your warriors must grow restless,” I observed.

“There was one war,” Infadoos admitted, “after the great battle. But it was among ourselves—a civil war. Dog ate dog.”

“How so?” I asked.

“My lord, the king—my half-brother—had a twin born at the same hour, from the same mother. Our custom forbids twins to live; the weaker must die. But the queen hid the younger child, for her heart could not bear to lose him. That child is Twala, the king. I am his younger brother, born of another wife.”

“And what happened after?”

“Kafa, our father, died when we came of age. My brother Imotu became king. He ruled well and had a son by his favorite wife. When the boy was three, just after the great war, famine struck. The land failed. People starved, and murmured like lions denied prey.

Then Gagool—the wise, the terrible, the immortal—rose and proclaimed: ‘Imotu is not your king!’ At that time, Imotu lay wounded, unable to move.

Gagool entered a hut and brought forth Twala—my half-brother, hidden since birth. She stripped away his waistcloth and revealed the sacred mark of the coiled snake around his belly—the sign of the eldest son. And she cried out: ‘Behold your king! I have saved him for you!’”

The people, mad with hunger and reasonless, roared: “_The king! The king!_” But I knew it was false. Imotu was older. He was the rightful ruler.

Just then, though gravely ill, Imotu crawled from his hut, hand in hand with his wife—and behind them, their son, Ignosi—meaning ‘Lightning’ in our tongue.

‘What is this noise?’ he demanded. ‘Why cry ye _The king!_?’

Twala ran to him, seized his hair, and stabbed him through the heart with his knife.

The people, fickle and quick to worship new power, clapped and shouted: ‘_Twala is king!_ Now we know it!’

“And what of Imotu’s wife and child?” I asked.

“She fled,” Infadoos said. “When she saw her husband fall, she took the boy and ran. Two days later, she reached a kraal, hungry and broken. None gave her food—no one welcomes the fallen. But a little girl crept out at night and brought her corn. She blessed the child and vanished into the mountains before dawn. No one has seen her since. Nor Ignosi.”

“So if Ignosi lived, he would be the true king?”

“Yes, my lord. The sacred snake marks him. If he lives, he is king. But alas—he is long dead.”

He pointed ahead. Below us, nestled in the plain, lay a vast kraal—huts clustered within a fence, surrounded by a deep ditch. “That is where the queen and her son were last seen,” Infadoos said. “We shall rest there tonight—if, indeed, my lords sleep at all.”

“We do as the Kukuanas do,” I replied with dignity, turning to address Good, who trudged behind, preoccupied with his flapping shirt.

But I nearly collided with Umbopa, who stood directly behind me—listening intently. His face bore a strange expression, as though some forgotten memory stirred beneath the surface.

We continued forward. The mountains we had crossed now rose above us, their peaks veiled in mist. Sheba’s Breasts loomed softly, draped in gauzy clouds. The landscape grew ever more beautiful—lush, yet not tropical; warm under the sun, but never harsh. A gentle breeze carried scents of earth and wild herbs. This land felt like paradise—richer, fairer, more perfect than any I had known. The Transvaal was grand, but Kukuanaland surpassed it.

Infadoos had sent a runner ahead to warn the kraal of our coming. The man had sprinted off at incredible speed—running, he explained, was a common and valued skill among his people.

Now, as we neared the kraal, we saw companies of warriors emerging from its gates, marching toward us in orderly ranks. Sir Henry touched my arm. “Looks like a warm welcome,” he remarked, his tone cautious.

Infadoos noticed. “Do not fear, my lords,” he said quickly. “This regiment is under my command. They come to greet you.”

I nodded, though unease lingered.

Half a mile from the gate, the ground sloped gently upward. There, the companies formed—each about three hundred strong. They charged up the rise, spears flashing, plumes waving, taking position along the road.

By the time we reached the slope, twelve companies—three thousand six hundred men—had lined the path. Then came the first unit.

We stopped. Before us stood the most magnificent warriors I had ever seen. All were mature men—veterans of forty or more—none shorter than six feet, many towering at six-three or four. On their heads, heavy black plumes of Sakaboola feathers. Around their waists and knees, circlets of white ox tails. In their left hands, round shields—twenty inches across—made of iron plates covered in milk-white ox-hide.

Their weapons were simple but deadly: short, thick, double-edged spears—meant only for close combat. Each blade measured six inches wide. These were not thrown, but thrust like Zulu *bangwan*—inflicting terrible wounds.

Each warrior carried three large knives—called *tollas*—weighing two pounds each. One hung at the hip, two fastened to the back of the shield. These replaced the throwing assegai. The Kukuana could hurl them fifty yards with deadly accuracy. As they charged, they would release a volley before closing in.

Each company stood motionless—like bronze statues—until we passed. Then, at a signal from their commander—distinguished by a leopard-skin cloak—they raised their spears and roared: “_Koom!_”—the royal salute.

Then they fell in behind us, marching in formation. Soon, the entire regiment—the “Greys,” named for their white shields—marched in our wake, their tread shaking the earth.

At last, we branched off from Solomon’s Great Road and approached the wide fosse surrounding the kraal—over a mile in circumference, protected by a strong palisade of tree trunks. At the gate, a primitive drawbridge lowered for us to cross.

Inside, the kraal was impressively laid out. A wide central path crossed at right angles with others, dividing the area into square blocks—each housing a company. The huts were dome-shaped, built of wattle and beautifully thatched with grass. Unlike Zulu huts, these had doorways—large enough for men to walk through—and were much larger, surrounded by verandas paved with hard-packed lime.

Along the central path, hundreds of women stood—drawn by curiosity. For a native race, they were strikingly beautiful—tall, graceful, with fine figures. Their hair was short but curly, not woolly. Faces often aquiline, lips not overly thick. But what amazed us most was their quiet dignity. They watched us with calm eyes, no expressions of shock or rudeness. Even when Infadoos quietly pointed to Good’s pale legs—his skin so white it seemed almost luminous—they stared, then looked away—without comment.

It was enough for Good, who blushed deeply.

At the center of the kraal, Infadoos halted before a large hut, ringed by smaller ones.

“Enter, Sons of the Stars,” he declared in a grand tone. “Deign to rest in our humble homes. Food will be brought—milk, honey, an ox or two, sheep. Not much, my lords—but enough.”

“It is good,” I said. “We are weary from journeying through realms of air. Let us rest.”

We stepped inside. The hut was prepared—couches of tanned hides, water for washing. Soon, voices shouted outside. At the door, we saw lines of girls bearing milk, roasted mealies, honey. Behind them, youths drove a fat young ox.

We accepted the gifts. A youth drew his knife and swiftly cut the ox’s throat. Within ten minutes, it was dead, skinned, and jointed. The best cuts were set aside for us. The rest, I offered to the warriors, who took it reverently and distributed it as a gift from the “white lords.”

Umbopa, with the help of a charming young woman, began boiling our portion in a large earthen pot over a fire outside. When it was nearly ready, I sent word to Infadoo and Scragga, inviting them to join us.

They arrived shortly, sitting on small stools—unlike the Zulus, who squat. They ate with us. Infadoos was courteous and affable. But Scragga watched us with wary eyes. At first, he had been awed by our pale skins and strange ways. But now, seeing us eat, drink, and smoke, his awe faded—replaced by suspicion. It unsettled me.

During dinner, Sir Henry suggested we ask about his brother. But I thought it best to wait. How could we explain a man lost from “the Stars”?

After supper, we lit our pipes. The sight stunned Infadoos and Scragga. Tobacco-smoke was unknown here. Though they grew the plant, they used it only as snuff. They stared, baffled, at the rising curls of smoke.

Later, I asked Infadoos when we would continue.

“We leave at dawn,” he said. “Messengers have already gone to Twala. He is at Loo, preparing for the great annual feast—held in early June. Then all regiments gather, except those guarding the borders. The great witch-hunt also takes place. You will reach Loo by nightfall on the second day—unless rivers swell or accident delays.”

They bid us goodnight. We arranged watches—three slept, one kept vigil—for the night.

And so, under the stars, we rested—exhausted, yet alive in a world both wondrous and perilous.
