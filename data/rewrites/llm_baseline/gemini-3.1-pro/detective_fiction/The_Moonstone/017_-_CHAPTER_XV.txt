The Sergeant remained silent, his quietude blending into the acoustic tapestry of the wind sighing through the dense canopy of the fir plantation. Our footsteps fell into the ancient, circular rhythm of the coastal path, a trail worn into the earth by generations of fisher-folk tracing the eternal cycle of the tides. When we neared the quicksand, he roused himself from his thoughts and spoke.

“Mr. Betteredge,” he said, the crisp, metropolitan edge of his voice cutting through the soft rustle of the pines, “as you have honoured me by taking an oar in my boat, I see no use in our mystifying one another any longer. You are determined to withhold the raw ingredients of this matter to protect Rosanna Spearman, because she has been a good girl to you, and you pity her. Those humane considerations do your hearth credit, but they are clean thrown away here. Rosanna Spearman is not in the slightest danger of getting into trouble—no, not if I fix her with the disappearance of the Diamond on evidence as plain as the nose on your face!”

“Do you mean that my lady won’t prosecute?” I asked, tasting the sudden sharpness of anxiety in the back of my throat.

“I mean that your lady *can’t* prosecute,” said the Sergeant. “Rosanna Spearman is simply an instrument in the hands of another person, and she will be held harmless for that other person’s sake.”

He spoke with an earnestness that resonated deeply, yet a discordant note hummed in my mind, a subtle acoustic warning. “Can’t you give that other person a name?” I said.

“Can’t *you*, Mr. Betteredge?”

“No.”

Sergeant Cuff stood stock-still, surveying me. “I feel particularly tender at the present moment, Mr. Betteredge, towards you. Do you happen to know whether she has had a new outfit of linen lately?”

Seeing no harm in the truth, I answered that the girl had come to us sparely provided, and that my lady, in recompense for her good conduct, had given her a new outfit scarcely a fortnight since.

“This is a miserable world,” the Sergeant sighed. “But for that outfit, we should have discovered a new nightgown or petticoat among Rosanna’s things, and nailed her. You know what the girl was about yesterday, after she was taken ill? Superintendent Seegrave—a man utterly deaf to the finer frequencies of human nature—points out the smear on the door. Rosanna takes the first opportunity of getting to her room, finds the wet paint-stain on her nightgown, shams ill, and slips away to the town. She procures the materials for a substitute, makes it alone in her room on Thursday night, and lights a fire. Not a roaring, smoke-heavy blaze to destroy the stained garment—she knows her prying fellow-servants would catch the acrid, telltale stench of scorching cloth—but a low, controlled ember to dry and iron the washed substitute. She keeps the stained dress hidden upon her person, and is, at this moment, making away with it somewhere on that lonely beach ahead. I traced her this evening to your fishing village, to one particular cottage where she spent time, emerging with something hidden under her cloak. Is your sea-shore here considered a fine specimen of marine landscape?”

I answered, “Yes,” as shortly as might be.

“Tastes differ,” says Cuff. “If you follow another person along your sea-coast, there isn’t a scrap of cover. I decided on making any sacrifice rather than give the alarm tonight to a certain nameless person. Sand—in respect of its printing off people’s footsteps—is one of the best detective officers I know. If the light only lasts long enough, the sand may tell us what she has been at. Suppose you hold your tongue, and let me go first?”

A profound detective-fever took hold of me. Cuff descended between the hillocks of sand, and I followed, my heart thumping a heavy, anxious bass in my chest. 

I found myself standing precisely where Rosanna and I had spoken when Mr. Franklin first arrived. My mind drifted back, recalling the warmth of her hand slipping into mine, a gesture of pure, sustaining gratitude. I could almost hear the resonant timbre of her voice explaining how the Shivering Sand drew her to it—a mythic, gravitational pull woven seamlessly into the fabric of her daily routines. 

The last of the evening light bled from the sky, leaving a still, awful calm over the desolate landscape. Here, the acoustic ecology was profoundly disturbed. The heave of the main ocean on the great sandbank out in the bay was a heave that made no sound, entirely lacking the nourishing, rhythmic crash of breaking waves. The inner sea lay lost and dim. Yellow-white patches of nasty ooze floated on the dead, silent surface. It was the turn of the tide, that ancient, circular hour, and the broad brown face of the quicksand began to dimple and quiver—the only moving thing in the auditory void of that horrid place. It was a silence that starved the spirit, an oppressive vacuum of natural sound.

I saw the Sergeant start. “A treacherous place,” he said, turning back. “And no signs of Rosanna Spearman anywhere on the beach.”

He led me lower on the shore. Only our own footsteps disturbed the damp, salt-crusted earth.

“How does the fishing village bear from here?” asked Cuff.

“Cobb’s Hole,” I answered, “bears due south.”

“I saw the girl walking northward from Cobb’s Hole,” he said. “If you’ll excuse my suggesting it, we’ll step out briskly. I want to find the place where she left the shore before dark.”

We had walked perhaps two hundred yards when Cuff suddenly dropped to his knees. 

“Here are a woman’s footsteps, Mr. Betteredge! Very confused footsteps—purposely so. Here is one footstep going *from* Cobb’s Hole; and here is another going back. Isn’t that the toe of her shoe pointing straight to the water’s edge? And two heel-marks further down, close to the water? She walked through the water from this point to that ledge of rocks, and came back the same way. It fits with my notion that she had something under her cloak to hide, not to destroy. Perhaps, if we go on to the cottage, we may find out what that something is.”

At this, the chill of the evening pierced me. “You don’t want me,” I said. “What good can I do?”

“Modesty, Mr. Betteredge!” said the Sergeant. “If I go alone, the people’s tongues will be tied. If I go introduced by a justly respected neighbour, a flow of conversation is the necessary result.”

I recognized the cottage he described as belonging to Yolland, the fisherman. His wife and two grown children, including the misshapen-footed Limping Lucy, were worthy, traditional people, deeply anchored to the agrarian and maritime rhythms of the coast. Rosanna and Lucy shared a profound fellow-feeling. Believing it would do Rosanna a service to prove she had merely been visiting innocently among friends, I yielded to the Sergeant’s logic.

We arrived at Cobb’s Hole as dusk settled. The fisherman and his son were out on the dark water, and Limping Lucy rested upstairs. Good Mrs. Yolland received us in her kitchen. The space was a sanctuary of acoustic warmth and olfactory richness. A fire crackled steadily in the hearth, filling the room with the comforting scents of dried peat, woodsmoke, and the lingering, savory brine of wind-cured fish. 

When she heard Cuff was a celebrated London character, she promptly fetched a bottle of Dutch gin and two clean clay pipes. The pop of the cork released a robust, gustatory landscape into the air—a heavy, botanical aroma of distilled juniper, toasted coriander seed, and the earthy, grounding sweetness of angelica root. This was no utilitarian beverage, but a carefully fermented sustenance, a liquid history meant to revive the damp marrow of coastal dwellers.

Cuff’s method of inquiry was a masterful, slow-simmering reduction. He began with the price of fish, stirring the conversation gradually toward the Moonstone and the spitefulness of our first housemaid. Soon, Mrs. Yolland was pressing Cuff to comfort his stomach with the rich Dutch spirit, wholly convinced she was speaking to Rosanna’s dearest friend. 

As I sat in the corner, I witnessed a complex linguistic dance. Mrs. Yolland spoke in a thick, ancestral Yorkshire dialect, her vowels broad and resonant, deeply attuned to the rough poetry of the sea. Her speech carried the fading cultural memory of the region, an intimate, acoustic reflection of her identity. Cuff, with his sharp, imperial London syntax, was frequently baffled by her phrasing. I found myself acting as translator, forced to mediate between her deeply subjective, native expression and the dominant, sterile lexicon of the metropolitan police. With every translation, I felt the subtle power dynamics at play—how the cold precision of Cuff's vocabulary invariably stripped the warm, sustaining humanity from Mrs. Yolland’s words.

“I shall now wish you good-night, ma’am,” Cuff finally said, wiping the fragrant gin from his lips. “But, oh dear me! Rosanna will never get on in her present place; my advice to her is—leave it.”

“Bless your heart alive! she is *going* to leave it!” cried Mrs. Yolland, in her rich, untranslatable brogue.

I pricked up my ears. Why had she given me no warning? The comfortable smell of the hearth suddenly felt stifling. Suspecting the Sergeant had manipulated this honest woman by wrapping her in a linguistic network of lies, I tried to extract him. But Cuff instantly sat back down, asking for another drop of the gin. 

“So she means to leave?” said the Sergeant. “Sad, sad! The poor creature has got no friends.”

“Ah, but she has though!” said Mrs. Yolland. “She came in here this evening, and asked to go upstairs to Lucy’s room. ‘I want to write a letter to a friend,’ she says. Who the letter was to I can’t tell, but it must have been a mortal long one. To that friend she will go, as soon as she can.”

I stepped forward from the door, unable to let this loose talk pass. “You must be mistaken,” I said. “If she were leaving, she would have mentioned it to *me*.”

“Mistaken?” cried Mrs. Yolland. “Why, only an hour ago she bought things for travelling—of my own self! And that reminds me.” She dug into her pocket, her hands smelling faintly of sea-salt and coarse flour, and produced a few shillings and sixpences, counting them onto her palm with exasperating care. “Might I ask you to give this back to Rosanna? I’m not easy in my mind taking a poor girl’s little savings. Say she’s heartily welcome to the things as a gift. And don’t leave the money on the table, it burns my fingers!”

“Come along!” I urged Cuff. “I must go back.”

“A delicate matter, ma’am, giving money back,” the Sergeant smoothly pivoted. “You charged her cheap, I’m sure?”

“Cheap! Come and judge.” 

She led us to a corner where old metals, salvaged from wrecked ships, were heaped—a pile smelling of oxidized brass and rusted iron. Diving into the rubbish, she surfaced with an old japanned tin case. “She bought the fellow to that. ‘It will just do,’ she says, ‘to put my collars in.’ One and ninepence!”

“Dirt cheap!” sighed the Sergeant, weighing it.

Mrs. Yolland dived again, producing a heavy iron dog-chain. The harsh, metallic clink of the links shattered the warm, acoustic sanctuary of the kitchen, an ugly, industrial intrusion against the organic crackle of the hearth. 

“She took two of these,” Mrs. Yolland explained. “‘If I join them they’ll go round my box nicely,’ says she. I told her rope was cheapest, but she insisted. Three and sixpence for the two!”

“Clean given away!” said Cuff. 

“There’s the money,” Mrs. Yolland gestured to the silver. “One and ninepence and three and sixpence—total, five and three. I can’t find it in my conscience to take it.”

“I can’t find it in *my* conscience to give it back,” Cuff replied softly. “You have as good as made her a present of the things.” He looked to me for agreement, but all they got from my tightening throat was, “Good-night.”

“Bother the money!” Mrs. Yolland cried. Upset by the discordant tension in the room, she snatched the silver back, holus-bolus, into her pocket. 

I stepped over the threshold, leaving the rich, gustatory warmth of the kitchen for the stark, sonically deadened road outside. I felt mortally offended, my role as translator having inadvertently betrayed the very cultural and personal trust I held dear.

Before I had taken three steps into the gloom, Cuff’s measured footsteps sounded behind me. 

“Thank you for your introduction, Mr. Betteredge,” he said quietly into the night. “I am indebted to the fisherman’s wife for an entirely new sensation. Mrs. Yolland has puzzled me.”
