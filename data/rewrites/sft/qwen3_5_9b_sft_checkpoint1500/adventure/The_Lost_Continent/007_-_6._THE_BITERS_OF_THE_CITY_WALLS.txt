Thus ended my welcome back to the capital of Atlantis, offering me a first, unsettling glimpse of Her Majesty's new policies. I confess openly to a certain lethargy and inaction on my part; yet, it was a deliberate stance. The ancient bonds of duty seemed severed, or at least hopelessly entangled. Formerly, to serve the King was synonymous with serving the Clan of the Priests, from whom He was chosen and whose head He effectively constituted. But Queen Phorenice was self-made, appearing to be a sovereign unto Herself. If the words of Zaemon were to be believed, he served as the mouthpiece of the Priests, a faction that had deliberately set Her at defiance. And how could a man of simple honesty, such as I, instantly choose between these two opposing forces?

Cold logic, however, prevailed. Governments exist for the welfare of the nation as a whole, not for the preservation of specific factions. I resolved within myself that I must determine which regime offered the best hope for Atlantis and dedicate my efforts to strengthening it. Just then, another path emerged from the labyrinth: I had heard significant rumors of rebels, a faction of Atlanteans strong enough to besiege the capital. Before making a final judgment, it seemed prudent to weigh their grievances against the status quo. So, on the very night I had re-established my footing upon the soil of my homeland, I set out to investigate these matters personally.

Inside the Royal Pyramid, no one dared challenge me. The banquet had concluded abruptly with the horrific scene I have chronicled above; with Tarca writhing on the floor, displaying the gruesome scars of his leprosy, even the most voracious guests had lost all appetite for further gluttony. Phorenice glowered at the remnants of the feast in silent fury for a while longer, uttering no further word, until finally her gaze turned to me, though softened slightly.

"You may be an honest man, Deucalion," she said at length, "but you are a monster of coldness. I wonder when you will thaw?" A smile played upon her lips. "I suspect it will be soon. But for now, I bid you farewell. Tomorrow, we shall seize this country by the shoulders and arrange it anew."

She departed the hall, followed closely by Ylga. Taking precedence according to my rank, I exited immediately, while the others stood to salute. However, I paused first by Tarca and laid my hand upon his filthy flesh. "You are an unfortunate man," I said, "but I can admire a brave soldier. If relief can be procured for your plague, I shall use my influence to obtain it for you."

Gratitude emerged from his ruined mouth in a mumble, causing several nearby nobles to shudder in feigned disgust. I turned upon them with a dark brow. "Your charity, my lords, appears to be of as little value as your courage. You pretended a disdain for Zaemon's teachings and a mocking contempt for his priesthood, yet when it came to touching him, you displayed a discretion that, in the old days, we would have termed ugly. I would sooner be Tarca, with all his uncleanness, than any of you standing there now."

With this dismissal, I waited silently for their salutations and then walked out of the banqueting hall without casting another glance at any soul. I proceeded to the grand gate of the pyramid, summoned the officer of the guard, and demanded exit. The man was sufficiently obsequious, though he hesitated.

"My lord's attendants have not yet arrived?"

"I have none."

"My lord knows the condition of the streets?"

"I did twenty years ago. I shall navigate my way."

"My lord must recall that the city is under siege," the officer persisted. "The people are starving. They prowl in packs after dark, and—while I make no question of your ability to conquer any odds—you—"

"Quite correct. I seek no street brawl tonight. Provide me with a sufficient escort. You will know best what is required. I am accustomed to the tranquility of a quiet street."

A dozen sturdy men were detached for my protection. Flanked by them in a double file, I marched from the heavily perfumed air of the pyramid into the cool, silver moonlight of the city. My intention was to tour the walls and ascertain the disposition of these rebels.

However, the Gods deemed it fitting to provide me with another lesson first. As I walked, I observed that the city was no longer the ancient capital I had known, nor the gradual accumulation of ages blending comfort and splendor. The splendor remained, magnified. Entire districts had been razed to make room for new palaces and towering pyramids for the wealthy, and I could not help but admire the skill and intellect required to construct such magnificent monuments. Indeed, gazing at them under the moonlight, I could almost comprehend the emotions of the Europeans and other barbarians who worship such structures as gods, deeming them too wonderful and majestic to be erected by human hands alone.

Nevertheless, while easy to admire, it was equally simple to perceive the staggering cost at which these works were built. From every parcel of ground where one of these stately piles gleamed silver under the moon, a hundred families had been evicted and left to wander wherever they pleased in the open. Consequently, now every niche held its share of sleepers, and every shadow concealed a squad of fierce wild creatures, ready to rush out and rob or slay any traveler lacking their own strength.

I am, by nature, not a pamperer of the common folk. I believe that if a man is left to hunger and cold, he will labor to secure food and clothing; and if not, he may die, thereby relieving the State of a worthless burden. Yet, as we marched through numerous wards, I witnessed marks of blind oppression: starved corpses, bones protruding from gaunt skin, sprawled in the gutters. It was evident that, save for the favored few, the people of this great capital lived under a crushing weight of misery.

Despite my deep regret, I could make no loud complaint. By ancient law, all the people, great and small, were servants of the King, to be employed at His pleasure without question. Phorenice stood in the King's place. Thus, I refrained from accusing treason, sighing softly as I kept my eyes fixed above the misery and squalor of the roads, sending my thoughts upward to the stars hanging in the purple night and to the High Gods dwelling among them, seeking guidance for my future policies.

Eventually, the winding streets brought us to the walls. Coursing alongside them and responding appropriately to the sentries beating their drums as we passed, we arrived at the great gate, charged to the Captain of the Garrison.

Here, a peculiar commotion was evident. Laughter echoed into the still night air, intermingled with the snarl and roar of a great beast, occasionally broken by the shriek of a wounded man. Whatever transpired, it was not a scene to encounter suddenly. The entrance gates of our great capital, designed by ancient builders, were no weaker than the walls themselves. Four pairs of valves existed, each a monstrous block of stone two men-high square and a man-high thick, embedded in a wall doubled to receive them, enclosing an open circus between the two layers. The four gates were set—one at the inner wall, one at the outer—and a hidden machinery connected them so that neither set could open until the other was closed. To force them without war engines would be as futile as trying to push down the Royal Pyramid with bare hands.

My escort sounded the horn hanging from the wall, summoning a warder who emerged from an arrow-slit to inspect our persons and business. His inquiry followed the ancient, lengthy formula, rendered even more tedious by the constant noise from within, which periodically drowned out all speech.

Finally, after the formalities were completed, he shot back the massive bars and bolts of stone and threw ajar one monstrous stone valve. I and my fellows crowded into the chamber within—the space created by the thickness of the wall between the two doors. Then the warder, operating his machines, pulled the valve that had been opened and approached me through the press of my escort, bowing low.

"I have no warrant to give you," I said abruptly. "Proceed with your duty. Open that other door."

"With respect, my lord, it would be better if I first announced your presence. There is a spectacle proceeding in the circus, and the tigers are currently mere savages, respecting no persons."

"The what?"

"The tigers, if you'll permit them that name. They are baiting a batch of prisoners with the two great beasts the Empress—whose name be adored—has sent to aid us in holding the gate. But if you please, there are wardrooms leading off this passage, and galleries running out from them commanding the circus. From there, my lord can observe the sport undisturbed."

Now, mere lust for killing excites only disgust within me, but I suspected the Empress's orders behind this affair and possessed a curiosity to see her scheme. Stepping into the warder's lodge and then onto the galleries overlooking the circus, I positioned myself behind the arrow-slits. The old builders intended these as a second line of defense; assuming the outer doors were forced, an enemy could be swiftly shot down in the circus without retaliation, marching only into a death trap. Yet, as a viewing platform for spectacles, they proved no less useful.
