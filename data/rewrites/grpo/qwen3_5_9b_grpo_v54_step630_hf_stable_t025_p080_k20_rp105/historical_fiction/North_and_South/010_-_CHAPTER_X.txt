"We are the trees whom shaking fastens more."

Mr. Thornton departed from the house without returning to the dining-room, driven by a sense of urgency that bordered on anxiety. He feared that any delay might be construed as disrespect toward his new acquaintance. As he hurried toward Crampton's residence, the church clock tolled half-past seven. He paused at the door, watching Dixon's inevitable slowness; she was invariably tardy whenever the duty of answering the bell fell upon her. Finally admitted into the small drawing-room, he was greeted warmly by Mr. Hale, who guided him to his wife. Margaret sat there, her pallor and the shawl draped loosely around her shoulders offering a silent apology for her cool reception. She was lighting the lamp as he entered, the twilight deepening outside.

The lamplight cast a soft glow into the center of the room, a space left open to the night sky and the encroaching darkness—a stark contrast to the heavy, mirror-less dining room he had just left. That other room felt designed solely for consumption, dominated by his mother's presence and her absolute will. This drawing-room, however, was vastly finer, though far less comfortable. It lacked mirrors, devoid of glass to catch the light, and held no gilding. Instead, it possessed a sober, warm breadth of color, softened by the familiar Helstone chintz curtains and chair covers. An open davenport stood by the window, while another held a tall white china vase overflowing with English ivy, pale green birch, and copper-toned beech leaves. Baskets of needlework were scattered about, and books lay open on a table, seemingly abandoned mid-read. Behind the door, a second table was set for tea, draped in white linen and laden with coconut cakes, oranges, and red American apples resting on leaves.

To Mr. Thornton, these graceful preparations seemed habitual to the family, particularly to Margaret. She stood by the tea table in a light muslin gown touched with pink, appearing entirely absorbed in the arrangement of the cups. Her round, ivory hands moved with a dainty, noiseless precision among the porcelain. A bracelet adorned one slender arm, threatening to slide down over her wrist. Mr. Thornton found himself watching the movement of this ornament with more fascination than he paid to Mr. Hale's words. It seemed to captivate him to see her push the band up impatiently until it constricted her soft flesh, only to watch it loosen and fall again. "There it goes again!" he almost whispered. Once the tea service was complete, he felt a pang of regret that the necessity of eating and drinking would soon interrupt his observation of Margaret. She served him his cup with the proud demeanor of a reluctant servant, yet her eyes betrayed her eagerness to refill his cup. He longed to ask her to perform the task he saw her father compelling her to do for him—holding her tiny fingers and thumbs as sugar tongs. He watched her beautiful eyes lift to her father, filled with a mixture of light, laughter, and love during this silent pantomime, unaware that they thought themselves unseen. Though her head still ached, betraying itself through her pale complexion and silence, Margaret resolved to fill any awkward pauses rather than allow her father's guest to feel neglected.

After the tea things were cleared, Margaret retreated to a corner near her mother with her work, allowing her thoughts to wander freely. Meanwhile, Mr. Thornton and Mr. Hale remained deeply engrossed in their discussion, a topic carried over from their previous meeting. Margaret was only reminded of the present moment by a casual remark from her mother. Looking up from her work, she was struck by the profound difference in appearance between her father and Mr. Thornton, a reflection of their opposing natures. Her father was slight, appearing taller than he was when not contrasted with the massive, towering frame of the other man. His face bore soft, wavering lines, traversed by a constant, trembling movement that revealed every shifting emotion. His eyelids were large and arched, lending his eyes a languid, almost feminine beauty, while his brows were finely curved but raised high by the weight of those dreamy lids.

In stark contrast, Mr. Thornton's straight brows fell low over clear, deep-set, earnest eyes that seemed intent on penetrating the very core of his subject. The lines on his face were few but firm, carved in marble, concentrated mainly around his lips. These lips were slightly compressed, framing teeth so perfect they resembled sudden sunlight. When a rare, bright smile flashed across his face, transforming his severe, resolved expression into one of keen, honest enjoyment, it was a joyfulness seldom displayed so fearlessly by adults. Margaret admired this smile; it was the first quality she had found admirable in her father's new friend. The visible opposition of their characters seemed to explain the magnetic attraction they clearly felt for one another.

She rearranged her mother's worsted work and sank into her own reverie, completely forgotten by Mr. Thornton, who was fully occupied explaining the magnificent yet delicate power of the steam-hammer to Mr. Hale. He spoke of the machine's ability to stretch from earth to sky, filling the horizon, and then obediently compress into a vessel small enough for a child to carry.

"This imagination of power, this practical realization of a gigantic thought, sprang from one man's brain in our town," Mr. Thornton declared. "That very man possesses the capacity to ascend, step by step, to higher marvels. I am certain there are many among us who, if he were gone, could spring into the breach and carry on the war that compels all material power to yield to science."

"Your boast reminds me of the old lines:
'I've a hundred captains in England,' he said,
'As good as ever was he.'"

At her father's sudden shift from cogwheels to *Chevy Chase*, Margaret looked up in wondering surprise. How had they leaped so far from industry to romance?

"It is no boast of mine," Mr. Thornton replied, his tone matter-of-fact. "I need not deny that I am proud to belong to a district where necessity births such grandeur of conception. I would rather be a man toiling, suffering—even failing—here, than lead a dull, prosperous life in the old, worn grooves of the aristocratic South, with its slow days of careless ease. One may become clogged with honey and lose the ability to rise and fly."

"You are mistaken," Margaret interrupted, her defense of the beloved South bringing color to her cheeks and angry tears to her eyes. "You know nothing of the South. If there is less adventure or progress—perhaps less excitement, driven by a gambling spirit of trade required to force out such inventions—there is also less suffering. I see men here walking the streets who look ground down by some pinching sorrow or care; they are not only sufferers but haters. In the South, we have our poor, but there is not that terrible expression of sullen injustice I see here. You do not know the South, Mr. Thornton," she concluded, collapsing into a determined silence, furious with herself for speaking so freely.

"And may I say you do not know the North?" he asked, his voice possessing an inexpressible gentleness as he sensed he had wounded her. She remained resolutely silent, yearning for the lovely haunts of Hampshire, feeling her voice would tremble if she attempted to speak.

"At any rate, Mr. Thornton," Mrs. Hale interjected, "you will allow that Milton is a much more smoky, dirty town than you will ever encounter in the South."

"I'm afraid I must concede its cleanliness," Mr. Thornton said, a quick, gleaming smile breaking across his face. "But we are bidden by Parliament to burn our own smoke; so I suppose, like good little children, we shall do as we are bid—eventually."

"But I thought you told me you had altered your chimneys to consume the smoke, did you not?" Mr. Hale asked.

"My chimneys were altered by my own will before Parliament intervened," Mr. Thornton corrected. "It was an immediate expense, but it repays me in coal savings. I am not sure I would have done it had I waited for the Act. At least, I would have waited to be informed against and fined, giving all the legal trouble possible. But all laws relying on informers and fines become inert due to the odiousness of their machinery. I doubt a single chimney in Milton has been reported in the last five years, despite some constantly emitting what we call unparliamentary smoke."

"I only know it is impossible to keep the muslin blinds clean here above a week," Mrs. Hale noted. "At Helstone, we have had them up for a month or more, and they remain unstained. And as for hands—Margaret, how many times did you wash them this morning before noon? Three times, was it not?"

"Yes, Mamma."

"You seem to have a strong objection to Acts of Parliament and legislation affecting your management here in Milton," Mr. Hale observed.

"Yes, I do; and many others share this view, with justice, I believe. The entire machinery of the cotton trade is so new that it is no wonder it does not function perfectly everywhere simultaneously. Seventy years ago, what was it? And now, what is it not? Raw, crude materials converged; men of similar education and station suddenly found themselves divided into masters and workers based on foresight regarding the future potential of Sir Richard Arkwright's model. This rapid development of a new trade granted those early masters enormous power over wealth and command—not just over workmen, but over purchasers and the global market. Consider an advertisement inserted in a Milton paper not fifty years ago, stating that a calico-printer would close his warehouse at noon, dictating that all buyers must arrive before that hour. Imagine a man dictating the hours of sale! Today, if a good customer chose to come at midnight, I would rise and stand hat in hand to receive their orders."

Margaret's lip curled, yet she was compelled to listen; she could no longer retreat into her own thoughts.

"I mention such things to illustrate the almost unlimited power manufacturers held at the beginning of this century. Success in ventures rendered them dizzy. Because a man prospered in his ventures, there was no reason his mind should remain balanced in other matters. On the contrary, his sense of justice and simplicity were often smothered under the glut of wealth descending upon him. They tell strange tales of the wild extravagance indulged in by those early cotton lords on gala days. There can be no doubt of the tyranny they exercised over their workpeople. As the proverb says, 'Set a beggar on horseback, and he'll ride to the devil'; some of these early manufacturers rode to the devil in magnificent style, crushing human bone and flesh under their horses' hooves without remorse. But eventually, a reaction occurred. More factories arose, more masters emerged, and more labor was needed. The balance of power between masters and men became more even, and now the battle is fairly waged between us. We will hardly submit to the decision of an umpire, much less to the interference of a meddler with only a smattering of knowledge, even if that meddler is called the High Court of Parliament."

"Is there necessity for calling it a battle between the two classes?" Mr. Hale asked, noting that the term accurately reflected his son-in-law's perception.

"It is true, and I believe it is as necessary as the conflict between prudent wisdom and ignorance. It is one of the great beauties of our system that a working-man may raise himself into the power and position of a master through his own exertions. Every one who rules themselves with decency, sobriety, and attention to duty joins our ranks; it may not be as a master, but as an overlooker, cashier, bookkeeper, or clerk—one on the side of authority and order."

"You consider all who fail to raise themselves in the world, from whatever cause, as your enemies, then, if I understand you rightly," Margaret said in a clear, cold voice.

"As their own enemies, certainly," he replied quickly, stung by the haughty disapproval in her tone. Yet, his straightforward honesty soon compelled him to realize his words were a poor, quibbling answer. Regardless of her scorn, it was a duty he owed himself to explain his meaning truthfully. It was difficult to separate her interpretation from his intent. He could have illustrated his point better by recounting his own life, but was it too personal for strangers? Still, it was the simplest way to clarify. Putting aside the momentary flush of shyness that colored his dark cheek, he spoke:

"I speak without book. Sixteen years ago, my father died under miserable circumstances. I was taken from school and forced to become a man in a few days. I had a mother of rare strength and firm resolve. We moved to a small country town where living was cheaper than in Milton, and I found employment in a draper's shop—an excellent place for learning about goods. Week by week, our income was fifteen shillings, from which three people had to live. My mother managed so that I saved three shillings regularly. This was the beginning; this taught me self-denial. Now that I can afford my mother such comforts as her age requires rather than her own wishes, I thank her silently for that early training. When I feel that in my own case it was no luck, merit, or talent, but simply the habits of life that taught me to despise indulgences not thoroughly earned—and never to think twice about them—I believe the suffering Miss Hale observes on the faces of Milton's people is merely the natural punishment for dishonestly enjoyed pleasure in some former period of their lives. I do not look upon self-indulgent, sensual people with hatred; I simply regard them with contempt for their poverty of character."

"But you have the rudiments of a good education," Mr. Hale remarked. "The zest with which you now read Homer shows you do not approach it as an unknown book; you have read it before and are only recalling old knowledge."
