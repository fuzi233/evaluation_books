"I would speak with you," Baldassarre said, breaking the silence as Romola regarded him with quiet anticipation. It was evident he had trailed her, waiting patiently for this moment. Finally, she intended to uncover the secret that weighed upon him.

"Yes," she replied, offering a submission akin to one accepting a penance imposed by authority. "Yet you wish to go where no ear can catch our words?"

"Where *he* will not stumble upon us," Baldassarre corrected, turning his head to glance nervously over his shoulder. "Out—into the open air—far from the crowded streets."

"I occasionally visit San Miniato at this hour," Romola offered. "If you wish, I shall go now, and you may follow. The journey is long, yet it offers solitude."

He nodded his agreement. Romola set off immediately. To lesser minds, venturing to a secluded spot with a man displaying the outward signs of the madness Tito had described might seem terrifying. But Romola possessed no such personal fears; instead, she welcomed the distance, viewing it as a necessary buffer before the next blow struck her spirit.

By late afternoon, the sun dipping low in the western sky, Romola paused on rough terrain beneath the shadows of towering cypress trunks, scanning for Baldassarre. He was close, yet when he finally joined her, he collapsed onto the edge of a stony bank, utterly spent. The thickset frame that had once displayed sturdy vigor during his initial appearance in the Duomo, bound in ropes, now bore the marks of decay. Even the brief exertion of climbing the hill had left his eyes clouded with a helpless vagueness.

"The ascent is steep," Romola observed, her tone soft with compassion as she sat beside him. "Do you fear you have been weakened by hunger?"

He turned his gaze toward her, silent and unable to formulate the words he sought. She remained perfectly still, terrified that any sign of impatience might shatter his fragile composure. In this light, he resembled nothing more than a neglected, ordinary old man. Yet, Romola was accustomed to the proximity of the suffering and often pondered their hidden burdens. Slowly, a clarity returned to his stare.

"Aha!" he exclaimed abruptly, his voice gaining weight. "You would have been my daughter!"

A swift crimson flush swept across Romola's cheeks and vanished instantly, leaving her lips parted in a pale, marble-like expression of horror. The revelation crashed over her mind. From that single phrase, she deduced the truth lurking behind the years of silence. Driven by her acute understanding of Tito's character, her impulse was immediate and unguarded. Her emotional reaction served as fuel for Baldassarre; for the first time, his words struck their intended mark.

With growing eagerness and resolve, he placed a heavy hand upon her arm. "You possess noble blood, do you not? You attend the sermons of the preacher; you despise baseness—the kind that smiles and triumphs. Do you hate your husband?"

"Oh, God! Were you truly his father?" Romola whispered, her mind consumed by visions of the past, oblivious to his direct inquiry. "Or did he lie? Did you take him as an infant?"

"You believe me! You understand what he is!" Baldassarre cried triumphantly, tightening his grip as if physical contact restored his fading strength. "Will you aid me?"

"Yes," Romola answered, though she did not interpret his request as he intended. She laid her palm gently over his rough, grasping hand, tears welling in her eyes as she gazed at him. "Oh, it is pitiful! Speak—were you a great scholar? Did you teach him? What became of it?"

She hesitated. Tito's accusation of Baldassarre's insanity echoed in her mind, and where were the traces of former refinement? Still, she maintained her composure, keeping her hand steady while listening with newfound caution.

"It is lost! All of it is gone!" Baldassarre lamented. "They refused to believe me because he lied, claiming I was mad, and had me dragged to prison. I am old; my mind will not return. The world conspires against me."

He paused, his eyes sinking under a wave of despair, before lifting them again with renewed fervor. "But *you* stand with me. He manipulated you into loving him, yet he has betrayed you; you hate him. Indeed, he made *me* love him too. He was beautiful and gentle, while I was a lonely man. I took him when they beat him. He slept in my bosom as a child, and I watched him grow, pouring all my knowledge into him, intending to give him everything I owned. I possessed much—money, books, jewels. He took my jewels and sold them; he left me in servitude. He never sought me out. When I returned, broken and wretched, he denied me. He called me a madman."

"He told us his father died—drowned," Romola murmured faintly. "Surely he must have believed that then. Oh! He could not have been so vile *then*!"

A vision rose before her of Tito in those early days, innocent in her eyes, regarding no evil as a child regards poison in flowers. The yearning regret of that memory offered a fleeting respite from the horror. With a great sob, tears burst forth.

"You are young," Baldassarre chided, showing a hint of impatience. "Tears flow easily, but they serve no purpose. They extinguish the inner fire, and it is the fire that matters. Tears will only hinder us. Listen to me."

Romola shifted slightly, startled. Once again, the specter of his madness pierced her thoughts, checking the flood of belief. Was this merely a deranged assassin? Yet, her profound conviction in his story remained, outweighing her fear. She approached him not with dread, but with sympathy, avoiding any display of doubt that might wound him.

"Tell me," she asked as softly as possible, "how did you lose your memory, your scholarship?"

"I fell ill. I cannot say how long—a total void. I recall nothing until I found myself sitting in the sun among the stones, surrounded by darkness. Slowly, gradually, I felt a longing for something unknown that never arrived. Later, aboard a ship upon the waters, I began to comprehend what I desired: the return of the Boy, the restoration of my thoughts. I felt locked out of my own mind. Now, I exist outside it. I perceive only walls and darkness."

Baldassarre drifted into a reverie, resting his head in his hands, until Romola's pity submerged her remaining cautions. The sorrow in his words resonated with an ancient pain of her own. Had she not witnessed daily how her father mourned for Dino and the future he had envisioned for that son?

"It all returned once," Baldassarre continued. "I mastered everything. I saw the world anew, my gems, my books. I believed I held him in my power, so I went to expose him where the lights shone and the trees stood tall. He lied again, calling me mad, and they dragged me away to prison... Wickedness is mighty, and he wears armor."

Fierceness flared within him once more. Speaking with his former intensity, he seized Romola's arm again. "But you will assist me? He has betrayed you as well. He has taken another wife, bearing children. He convinces her he is her husband, rendering her a foolish, helpless creature. I will show you where she resides."

The first shock that coursed through Romola was pure anger. The woman's sense of indignation surged to the forefront. Instinctively, Baldassarre sensed her alignment with his cause.

"You hate him," he pressed on. "Is it not true? No love exists between you. I know women can hate; you possess proud blood. You abhor falsehood, and you crave revenge."

Romola sat paralyzed by the collision of conflicting emotions, unaware that his grip was bruising her tender arm.

"You shall devise a plan," Baldassarre whispered eagerly. "I have memorized that you are his rightful wife. You are a noble woman. You frequent the preacher of vengeance; you will uphold justice. But you must think for me. My mind fails—sometimes everything vanishes except the fire. That fire is God; it is justice; it cannot die. Do you believe this? If they refuse to hang him for stealing from me, you must strip him of his armor. You will make him defenseless, and I shall strike him. I carry a knife, and my arm remains strong enough."

He slid his hand beneath his tunic, drawing out the concealed blade, tracing its edge absently as if needing the tactile sensation to sustain his delusions. To Romola, every passing hour of her life seemed destined to grow harder than the last. Her judgment was too sharp and rapid to waste breath on futile protests against a man in Baldassarre's state. Instead of answering his final plea, she chose to buy time, hoping to let his agitation subside by asking a different question. Trembling slightly, she spoke:

"You claim she is foolish and helpless—that other wife—who believes him to be her true husband. Perhaps he is; perhaps he wed her before marrying me."
