Mrs. Adair sat quietly in her room, her mind restless with the weight of what she had confessed to Durrance. She could not shake off the uneasiness that followed her words—what would he do now? Would he confront Ethne with the truth? Would he reveal how he had come to understand the depth of Ethne’s feelings for Feversham, and how her own affection for him had been mistaken all along? If he did, it would be impossible to hide her role in his discovery. She would have to face Ethne directly, and the thought of that moment filled her with dread—the quiet gaze, the unblinking honesty in those eyes, the question that would hang in the air: *Why?*

For a time, relief washed over her. Nothing changed between Ethne and Durrance. They met as before, spoke as if nothing had happened—no trace of the night Willoughby arrived at the garden, no echo of the evening Ethne played the Musoline Overture on the violin. The past seemed wiped clean.

But when the immediate fear passed, Mrs. Adair began to feel a different kind of unease. Her interference had done nothing. And then came irritation—then disappointment. Why had Durrance not acted? Why had he not broken through the pretense?

Durrance, however, had made up his mind quickly. Ethne didn’t want him to know. In her sorrow, she clung to the belief that she had brought happiness to someone—someone she truly cared for. That belief was her comfort. And Durrance saw it. He chose not to destroy it—not yet. He needed certainty. He had to know whether Ethne and Feversham were truly separated by misunderstanding or by an irrevocable rift. He had to know what had become of Harry Feversham. Until then, silence was his weapon.

So he feigned ignorance. He dropped his old habit of careful observation. He even let go of his usual attentiveness—because there was no longer any need. Instead, he forced himself into cheerfulness, making light of his blindness, pretending it was no great burden. He smiled often, joked freely, and said things like:

“You see, one can get used to blindness—it becomes the natural thing. But one never gets used to you, Ethne. Every time I meet you, I find something new to delight me. Besides,” he added with a grin, “there’s always the chance of a cure.”

And it worked. Ethne believed him. She saw the effort behind his smile, and it gave her strength. She could now bear her own pain, knowing that he did not suspect her secret grief.

But her pain was deep. For a brief moment, she had tasted the joy she once knew before the three white feathers reached Ramelton. Now, that joy was gone, replaced by a familiar sorrow—sleepless nights, the heavy ache in her head, the endless waiting for dawn. She could no longer pretend Harry Feversham was dead. He was alive—and trapped somewhere in the heat and darkness of Omdurman. She longed to know his fate, yet dreaded the truth.

Once, her impatience broke through. “I suppose escape is possible from Omdurman?” she asked casually, forcing her voice flat.

“Possible? Yes,” Durrance replied easily. “But difficult. It takes time. Attempts have been made to rescue Trench and others—but none have succeeded. The problem is the go-between.”

Ethne turned sharply. “The go-between?”

She hesitated, then whispered, “I think I begin to understand.” Then stopped herself abruptly.

“A man who moves between Omdurman and the Egyptian frontier,” Durrance explained. “Usually a Dervish trader or peddler, someone who slips into Wadi Halfa or Assouan. He takes money and promises to deliver messages—or prisoners. But he’s risky. If caught, he’d be killed. So often, he backs out at the last moment. Or worse—he’s a fraud. You pay him. He returns months later, alone, with excuses: bad season, tight guards, suspicion. He asks for more money. You give it. He comes back again—alone.”

Ethne nodded slowly. “Exactly.”

It was a revelation. She had known Harry meant to help Colonel Trench—but how? Now she understood. Harry would be the go-between. He would use his own capture as a means to reach Trench. The idea filled her with hope. He had planned this carefully. He wouldn’t stay behind if he could free Trench. He must return. The second feather would come back to her.

They sat in silence on the terrace. The sunset painted the creek in crimson and gold.

“Life wouldn’t be easy in Omdurman’s prison,” Ethne said, trying to sound indifferent.

“Easy?” Durrance laughed softly. “No. A hovel—crowded, dark, without air or light. The roof barely two feet above your head. Locked in from dusk till dawn. Prisoners standing all night, packed like animals. Imagine it here, on a night like this. Now imagine it in August in the Sudan—suffocating heat, no breath, no water. And if you remember places like this… well, the memory makes it worse.”

Ethne looked out across the garden. At that very moment, Harry might be gasping for air in that foul hole, throat dry, fever burning, dreaming of Ramelton’s green slopes, hearing the murmur of the Lennon River in his ears.

“One would pray for death,” she murmured, “unless—”

She stopped. She almost said, *unless one went there on purpose, with a purpose to fulfill.* But she cut herself off.

Durrance finished the sentence: “Unless there was a chance of escape. And there is a chance—if Feversham is in Omdurman.”

He regretted speaking so frankly about the prison. He added quickly, “Of course, what I’ve told you is just hearsay. We don’t really know. The prisoners may not suffer as badly as we think.” Then he changed the subject.

Ethne never mentioned it again.

But sometimes she wondered—how had Durrance known about her sudden departure from the drawing-room the night he told her of meeting Harry in Wadi Halfa? He never referred to it. She decided not to either. His manner had changed—no longer cautious, no longer tense. He seemed to have found a simple explanation. Maybe he thought she had merely fainted, or been startled. It eased her fears.

Still, she puzzled over why he had told her about the meeting at all. Why reveal that Harry had gone south? She found a strange answer—one that satisfied her: perhaps the news wasn’t really his to give. It was a message meant only for her. Durrance was merely the instrument. Some higher force had compelled him to speak.

For the rest of September, they kept up the act. Each morning, Durrance crossed the fields to visit Ethne at The Pool. Mrs. Adair watched them laugh, talk, walk together—as if nothing had ever shaken their bond. And each day, her anger grew. She wanted to speak, to stop the lie. But she held her tongue.

It was a month of tension—for all three. Relief came when Durrance left for London to see his oculist. The visits increased in frequency and length. Even Ethne welcomed them. She could finally rest, shed the mask, and gather strength. There were moments when despair threatened to break through. *Could I keep this up after marriage? When we are always together?* she asked herself. But she pushed the thought away. To look ahead was to risk collapse.

After the third trip, Durrance said:
“Do you remember I once mentioned a famous oculist in Wiesbaden? It seems advisable that I go there.”

“You’re recommended to go?”

“Yes. And alone.”

Ethne looked up, sharp and quick. “You think I’d be dull in Wiesbaden?”

“No fear. I can find some relative to accompany me.”

“No,” he said firmly. “It’s for my own sake. I may have to enter a private home. It’s better to be quiet, unseen.”

“You’re sure?” she asked. “Because I’d hate to think you were doing this because you believed I’d be happier at Glenalla.”

“No,” he answered honestly. He needed space. He too was exhausted by the constant performance. But he couldn’t say it. Not yet. He knew how much Ethne valued her resolve—to protect both their futures. So he stayed silent.

“I’ll return next week,” he added. “Then I’ll know whether I’m going to Wiesbaden.”

He was glad he’d mentioned it—before Calder’s telegram arrived.

That afternoon, the telegraph came. Durrance took it to The Pool that evening and handed it to Ethne. Only four words:

He stepped back instinctively—this new sensitivity born of suffering and reflection. He folded the paper neatly, so that by the time Ethne read it, she was alone on the terrace.

She remembered his description of the prison. Her imagination ran wild. The stillness of a September evening settled over the fields. A mist rose from the creek, creeping across the lawn. Already, in the hot land where the Niles meet, the prison doors were shut.

“He will pay tenfold for his fault,” she whispered, furious at the injustice. “And the fault was not his—his father’s, mine too. Neither of us understood.”

She blamed herself for giving him the fourth feather. She leaned against the stone railing, eyes closed, wondering if Harry would survive the night. Would he live to see another dawn?

The cool stones under her hands felt like reproach.

“Something can now be done,” Durrance said, stepping onto the terrace. “He was my friend. I cannot leave him there. I’ll write to Calder tonight. No expense spared. He is my friend, Ethne. You’ll see. From Suakin or Assouan—something will be done.”

He placed the entire effort at his own feet. He wanted her to believe he was acting purely out of friendship. Not out of love for her, or any lingering connection to Harry.

Ethne turned sharply. “Major Castleton is dead?”

“Castleton?” Durrance blinked. “There was a Castleton in Feversham’s regiment. Is that him?”

“Yes. He is dead?”

“He was killed at Tamai.”

“You’re sure?”

“I was in the square with the Second Brigade. On the edge of the gulley. Osman Digna’s men burst from the ground. Castleton was within the square. I saw him fall.”

“I am glad,” Ethne said simply.

She meant it.

The first feather had returned with Captain Willoughby. Perhaps Colonel Trench would bring the second. Harry had succeeded once—under terrible danger. Now the danger was greater, the task harder. But she believed in him. He had planned carefully. He had resources. And she—she had faith.

Besides, she was relieved. The sender of the third feather could never be approached. And she hated him. That was the end of it.

Durrance stared at her. He was not a man of emotion. He was a soldier—quiet, disciplined, gentle but firm. Yet this cold declaration—gladness at the death of a man she had never met—struck him deeply.

“You disliked Major Castleton so much?” he asked.

“I never knew him.”

“Yet you’re glad he’s dead?”

“I am quite glad,” she insisted.

Another slip. Another clue.

Durrance remembered it. Later, in his gun-room at Guessens, he pondered it. It fitted into the puzzle he was slowly assembling.

Captain Willoughby’s visit had given him the key. A white feather—only one meaning: cowardice. But Durrance had never seen cowardice in Harry Feversham. The charge stunned him. Yet it had happened. Something occurred at the ball at Lennon House. After that, Harry vanished.

Suppose a white feather had been sent to Lennon House—and opened in Ethne’s presence? Or more than one?

She had returned from her talk with Willoughby clutching that feather as though it were priceless. Mrs. Adair had told him that.

So the cowardice had been atoned. That explained her renewed youth, her peace. She treasured the feather not as a symbol of shame, but of redemption.

But Harry had not returned. He remained hidden. Willoughby was not the only one who had accused him. Two others.

One, Durrance already knew. When he suggested Harry might be taken to Omdurman, Ethne had said instantly: *Colonel Trench is in Omdurman.*

She didn’t need explanation. She knew Harry had gone deliberately—to be captured. To reach Trench.

And she had spoken of the unreliable go-between. That helped Durrance. There was a duty on Feversham to aid Trench. Suppose Harry had planned to rescue him—and became the go-between himself?

Then a second feather must have been sent. Trench had sent it.

Now, Durrance connected Castleton to the others. Ethne’s relief at Castleton’s death—why? Because if Castleton had lived, Harry would have owed him the same obligation. The same debt.

So likely—another feather had come. Sent by Castleton.

Durrance pieced it together. It made sense. One man could have told him the truth—but refused. That man would still refuse.

But Durrance intended to make him talk. And to do so, he must arrive with the story complete—no questions, no demands.

“Yes,” he said aloud, “after my next visit to London, I think I’ll call on Lieutenant Sutch.”
