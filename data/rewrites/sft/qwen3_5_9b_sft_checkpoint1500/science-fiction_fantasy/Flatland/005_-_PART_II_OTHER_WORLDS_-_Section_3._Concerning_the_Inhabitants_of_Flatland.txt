To gauge the stature of a fully grown citizen in Flatland, measure roughly eleven inches across; twelve inches marks the absolute ceiling. Yet, within this two-dimensional realm, true distinction lies not merely in width, but in geometry.

Our females exist as straight lines—featureless, silent, and dangerous due to their inability to turn corners safely. Above them stand the soldiers and the lowest laborers, shaped as isosceles triangles. They possess two legs of approximately eleven inches, joined by a base so negligible, often less than half an inch, that they form a terrifyingly sharp vertex. When reduced further to mere eighths of an inch, these figures become indistinguishable from the line-women they guard, their intellects so stunted that they appear point-like rather than triangular. These we term Isosceles.

Ascending the hierarchy, the middle class comprises equilateral triangles—figures of three perfectly equal sides. Next follow the professionals and gentlemen, represented by squares and pentagons. The nobility begins with hexagons, climbing steadily in complexity until the figures become polygons, their sides so minute they blur into circles. This final state, the Circular order, holds the highest sanctity, reserved for the priesthood.

Nature here adheres to a rigid arithmetic law: a male child must inherit exactly one additional side from his father. A square fathers a pentagon; a pentagon, a hexagon. Each generation naturally ascends the ladder of nobility, moving ever closer to the perfection of the circle.

However, this cosmic equation falters among the artisans and soldiers. Lacking equal sides, they are biological anomalies who cannot strictly obey nature's progression. An isosceles father invariably produces an isosceles son. Yet, hope persists in the shadows of their degradation. Through relentless military valor or meticulous, humble labor, the sharpest minds among the lower orders may slowly widen their base and shorten their legs. Intermarriage, orchestrated by the priestly circles, accelerates this evolution, occasionally yielding a child whose symmetry approaches that of the equilateral triangle.

True miracles occur rarely—a genuine equilateral born of isosceles parents. To the uninitiated eye, this seems impossible, a violation of natural law. But the priests argue otherwise: a "certificate" is required before any woman of standing will accept a husband who lacks perfect regularity. While history records instances where irregularity was passed down through generations, it usually manifests catastrophically in the third generation, causing the child to fail reaching pentagonal status or regress entirely back to the triangle. Achieving such a birth demands not just genetic lottery, but centuries of disciplined frugality, self-mastery, and a slow, agonizing cultivation of intellect across multiple dynasties.

When a True Equilateral finally emerges from the serf-ranks, the entire nation erupts in jubilation. Following a rigorous audit by the Sanitary and Social Board, the infant, if deemed regular, is solemnly inducted into the class of Equilaterals. Immediately thereafter, a heartbreaking severance occurs: the child is adopted by a childless noble, bound by sacred oath to sever all ties with the former home. The parents, proud yet shattered, are forbidden from seeing their progeny again. The priests fear that mere proximity to the low-born bloodline could cause the newly elevated soul to revert, collapsing under the gravitational pull of heredity.

These singular ascensions serve a dual purpose. They offer a faint, flickering beacon of hope to the suffering masses, illuminating the bleak monotony of their existence. Simultaneously, they act as a vital damper for the aristocracy. The higher orders understand that while these anomalies do not threaten their privilege directly, the sheer potential for upward mobility among the common folk fuels the fires of revolution. If the rabble believed they were utterly doomed to remain acute-angled, they would find charismatic leaders capable of marshaling their superior numbers and lethal angles against the wisdom of the Circles.

Instead, a profound law of compensation governs Flatland. As the working class grows in intelligence, virtue, and knowledge, their physical form simultaneously degrades. Their acute angles sharpen, making them physically more terrifying but mentally less coherent. The brute strength required for penetration correlates inversely with the capacity for strategy. In the most savage soldiers, those closest to the line-women in wit, the penetrating power is immense because the mind is simple. Conversely, as a soldier's intellect matures enough to wield his terrible shape with cunning, his angle softens, approaching the harmless stability of the equilateral. His mind expands, and his danger diminishes.

"How exquisite is this balance!" the author marvels, praising what feels like divine providence in the design of the state. By manipulating this natural trade-off, the Polygons and Circles effortlessly smother sedition in its infancy. They exploit the irrepressible hopefulness of the human spirit, turning ambition against itself.

Artistry also aids the machinery of order. State physicians, wielding subtle techniques of artificial compression and expansion, can reshape the most promising rebel leaders into perfect geometries overnight, elevating them instantly to the privileged classes. Hundreds more, though still imperfect, are lured by the promise of eventual ennoblement into State Hospitals, where they live out honorable, confined lives. Only the most stubborn, foolish, and hopelessly irregular few meet the guillotine.

Ultimately, the wretched mass of Isosceles men, left planless and leaderless, face a grim fate. Either they are swiftly transfixated by the elite guards retained by the Chief Circle for such emergencies, or, more frequently, they are torn apart by internal strife. The Circular party skillfully stirs up jealousies and paranoia, driving neighbors to strike down one another with their own treacherous angles. History books record no fewer than one hundred and twenty major rebellions, alongside two hundred and thirty-five minor outbreaks, all ending in this tragic silence.
