We often weave fanciful dreams of radical change, yet our actual lives are spun in a loom where busy passion drives the shuttle back and forth, weaving the familiar patterns of our actions.

Gwendolen's note, arriving "just too soon and just too late," reached Klesmer precisely as he was departing Quetcham. Driven by a desire to honor her plea for kindness, he chose to spend the night in Winchester, despite the inconvenience it caused himself. There were specific reasons preventing him from staying at Quetcham.

That magnificent mansion, built with extravagant expense, had become stiflingly hot for him. Its owners, much like certain grand politicians, were shocked by an uprising against the established order that ordinary people, seeing it clearly in hindsight, recognized had been brewing openly beneath their very noses.

As usual, the house was filled with guests. Among them was one whom Miss Arrowpoint anticipated as a new suitor: a political man of good standing who confidently awaited a peerage and believed, on public grounds, that he required a substantial fortune to uphold the title properly. Heiresses vary greatly; those interested in them beforehand are prepared to find them too pale or too flushed, tall and lanky, or short and stout, volatile and whimsical, or dreamy and bland. Yet, in every instance, it is assumed that the woman will view herself merely as an accessory to her wealth and marry wherever society dictates her fortune should lead. However, nature, accommodating itself poorly to our favored customs by creating "only-child" daughters, occasionally endows such misplaced daughters with a clear mind and a strong will. The Arrowpoints had already felt anxiety regarding these gifts in their Catherine. She refused to accept the social duty dictating a marriage to a needy nobleman or a commoner climbing the ladder toward nobility. They worried about her persistent rejection of suitable offers. As for the possibility that she was in love with Klesmer, they were entirely unconcerned—a very common form of blindness. Generally, mortals are astonishingly surprised by effects they have actively created, yet baffled by the absence of effects they have neglected to pursue. Parents marvel at their sons' ignorance despite investing immense time and money to ensure it; spouses are stunned by the loss of affection they failed to nurture; and everyone is prone to wondering why their neighbors do not admire them. Thus, the truth appears highly improbable. Truth differs vastly from the lazy combinations born of our desires. The Arrowpoints' moment of astonishment had finally arrived.

When a passion exists between an heiress and a proud, independent-minded man, reaching an understanding is difficult. However, obstacles usually dissolve unless the proud man constantly constructs an *alibi*. Brief reunions following calculated absences reveal much; yet, frequent companionship, marked by shared tastes and admirable qualities on both sides, is far more potent—especially when one acts as a teacher and the other delights in their own receptivity, which in turn brings joy to the instructor. This scenario is legendary in history, possessing no less charm today than it held in the days of Abelard.

Yet, this comparison never crossed the minds of the Arrowpoints when they first invited Klesmer to Quetcham. Having a first-rate musician in one's household is a privilege of wealth; Catherine's talent demanded every advantage, and she specifically wanted to utilize her quiet country time for rigorous study. Klesmer was not yet a Liszt, understood to be adored by ladies across Europe (with the exception of Lapland); even with that reputation, it did not imply he would propose to an heiress. No honorable musician would dare. Still less could it be conceived that Catherine would provide him the slightest pretext for such boldness. The large check Mr. Arrowpoint intended to draw in Klesmer's name seemed to render him as safe an inmate as a footman. Where marriage is deemed inconceivable, a girl's sentiments are considered secure.

Klesmer was indeed a man of honor, but marriages rarely commence with formal proposals, and furthermore, Catherine's limits of the conceivable did not align perfectly with her mother's.

Outsiders might have judged Klesmer's position as dangerously precarious if Miss Arrowpoint had been an acknowledged beauty, failing to realize that the most powerful beauty is that which reveals itself only after sympathy, not before. There is a charm in the eyes and lips that accompanies every phrase demonstrating delicate perception or fine judgment, every modest word or smile indicating a heart awake to others; no gesture of clothing or movement is more satisfying than that which restores the confidence that one is in the presence of someone who notices nothing lost. What profound meaning accumulates in frowns and laughter never witnessed in the wrong places; what radiant adorableness fills a human frame where a mind flashes with comprehension and hands execute with finesse! The more obvious beauty, also adorable sometimes—one may say it without blasphemy—often begins as an apology for folly and ends, like other apologies, becoming tiresome through repetition. Therefore, Klesmer's passionate attachment to Miss Arrowpoint, though he was very susceptible to such beauty, was no more a paradox than any other triumph of manifold sympathy over monotonous attraction. We object less to being accused of enslaving excesses of passion than to our deficiencies in wider passions; but if the truth were known, our reputed intensity is often the dullness of not knowing what else to do with ourselves. Tannhäuser, one suspects, was a knight of ill-equipped imagination, hardly possessing broader discourse than a heavy Guardsman; Merlin had certainly seen his best days and was merely repeating himself when falling into that hopeless captivity; and we know Ulysses felt such a manifest ennui under similar circumstances that Calypso herself facilitated his departure. Indeed, there is a report that he later left Penelope; but since she was habitually absorbed in woolen work, and it was likely from her that Telemachus acquired his petty, litigious disposition, always anxious about property and daily meat consumption, no valid inference can be drawn from this dubious scandal regarding the relationship between companionship and constancy.

Klesmer was as versatile and fascinating as a young Ulysses upon sufficient acquaintance—one whom nature seemed to have created generously first, then endowed with music as a dominant power, utilizing all abundant rest. Like Mendelssohn, he found expression not only in the highest finish of execution but in a fervor of creative work and theoretical belief that pierced the entire future of a life with the delight of congruent devotion. His foibles of arrogance and vanity did not exceed those found in the best English families, and Catherine Arrowpoint possessed no corresponding restlessness to clash with his; notwithstanding her innate kindness, she was perhaps too coolly firm and self-sustaining. Yet, she belonged to those satisfactory creatures whose interaction holds the charm of discovery; whose integrity of faculty and expression inspires a desire to know what they will say on all subjects or how they will perform any undertaking, thereby ending in raising not only continuous expectation but a continuous sense of fulfillment—the systole and diastole of blissful companionship. In such cases, the outward presentation easily becomes what the image is to the worshipper. It was not long before the two realized each was interesting to the other, though the extent of this interest remained doubtful. Klesmer did not conceive that Miss Arrowpoint viewed him as a potential lover, and she was not accustomed to thinking of herself as likely to stir more than a friendly regard, nor to fear expressing more to any man who was not enamored of her fortune. Each was content to endure some unshared sense of denial for the sake of loving the other's society a little too well; under these conditions, there had been no need to restrict Klesmer's visits for the past year, whether in the country or the city. He knew very well that if Miss Arrowpoint had been poor, he would have made ardent love to her instead of sending storms through the piano or folding his arms to pour out hyperbolic tirades about impersonal topics like the North Pole. Nor was she less aware that if it had been possible for Klesmer to wish for her hand, she would have found overwhelming reasons to grant it. Here lay the security of full cups, which are as secure from overflowing as half-empty ones, provided no disturbance occurs. Naturally, silent feelings had not remained static, any more than the stealthily moving clock hand. During this current visit to Quetcham, Klesmer had begun to think he would not return, while Catherine was more sensitive to his frequent brusqueness, which she resented as a needless attempt to assert his superiority in every sense except the conventional.

Meanwhile entered the expectant peer, Mr. Bult, an esteemed party man who, though neutral in private life, held strong opinions regarding the districts of the Niger, was equally at home in Brazil, spoke decisively of affairs in the South Seas, studied his parliamentary and itinerant speeches, and possessed the general solidity and suffusing pinkness of a healthy Briton on the central plateau of life. Aware of a tacit understanding that he was an undeniable match for an heiress, Catherine had nothing against him save that he was thoroughly tiresome. Mr. Bult was amiably confident and had no notion that his insensitivity to counterpoint could ever be counted against him. He barely regarded Klesmer as a serious human being entitled to a vote, and he did not mind Miss Arrowpoint's addiction to music any more than her probable expenses in antique lace. Consequently, he was somewhat amazed by an after-dinner outburst from Klesmer regarding the lack of idealism in English politics, which reduced all mutual relations between distant races to the simple necessity of a market.
