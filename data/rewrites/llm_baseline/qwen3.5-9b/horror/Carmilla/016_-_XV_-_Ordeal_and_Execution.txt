As I spoke, a figure emerged from the chapel doors—the same archway through which Carmilla had vanished. He was a strikingly peculiar man, towering yet narrow-chested, hunched under high, sloping shoulders. Clad entirely in black, his skin was a weathered brown, etched with deep, drying furrows. Upon his head sat an oddly shaped hat adorned with a broad leaf, while long, grizzled hair cascaded past his shoulders. Gold-rimmed spectacles perched on his nose, framing a face that wore a perpetual, enigmatic smile. He moved with a slow, shuffling gait, alternating between gazing upward at the sky and bowing low toward the earth. His long, thin arms swung wildly, clad in lanky black gloves far too large for his hands, gesturing in a state of utter abstraction.

"The very man!" cried the General, stepping forward with evident delight. "My dear Baron, what joy to see you! I dared not hope to meet you so soon."

He signaled to my father, who had just returned, and guided the eccentric elder—whom he addressed as the Baron—to join them. Formal introductions were exchanged, and immediately, serious discourse ensued. The stranger produced a rolled sheet of paper from his pocket, unspooling it across the worn surface of a nearby tombstone. Holding a pencil case, he traced imaginary lines across the document, his gaze darting frequently between the paper and specific points within the chapel structure. To me, this appeared to be a schematic plan of the sanctuary. Accompanying his lecture, he occasionally read aloud from a grimy, small book, its yellow pages densely inscribed.

They strolled together down the side aisle, directly opposite my position, conversing intently. Soon, they began pacing out distances, eventually halting before a section of the sidewall. There, they examined the masonry with minute scrutiny, stripping away clinging ivy and tapping the plaster with the ends of their sticks, scraping and knocking rhythmically. Finally, they confirmed the presence of a broad marble tablet bearing carved relief lettering. With the aid of the woodsman, who promptly returned, a monumental inscription and a carved coat of arms were revealed. They belonged to the long-lost monument of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein.

Though I suspected the General was not inclined toward prayer, he nonetheless raised his hands and eyes to the heavens in silent gratitude for several moments.

"Tomorrow," I overheard him declare, "the commissioner shall arrive, and the Inquisition will proceed according to law." Turning to the old man with the gold spectacles, the General grasped his hands warmly. "Baron, how can I repay your service? How can we all thank you? You have liberated this region from a plague that has ravaged its people for over a century. Thank God, the terrible enemy is finally tracked."

My father led the stranger aside, followed closely by the General. I knew they had withdrawn to speak privately regarding my condition, for I watched them exchange furtive glances in my direction as their discussion continued. When my father returned, he embraced me repeatedly, kissing me again and again before guiding me from the chapel.

"It is time to depart," he said gently. "But before we leave, we must summon the good priest, who resides nearby, and persuade him to accompany us to the castle."

Our efforts succeeded. Exhausted beyond words, I was relieved to reach home, yet my satisfaction curdled into dread upon realizing there was no news of Carmilla. No explanation was offered for the chaotic events within the ruined chapel; it was clear my father intended to withhold this secret from me for the present. The sinister silence surrounding Carmilla's absence rendered the memory of the scene even more horrifying.

That night, the arrangements were singular. Two servants and Madame were to remain awake in my chamber, while the clergyman and my father kept vigil in the adjacent dressing room. The priest had performed certain solemn rites earlier that evening, the meaning of which remained obscure to me, as did the rationale behind these extraordinary precautions for my safety during sleep. Only a few days later would the full significance become clear.

With Carmilla gone, my nightly torments ceased abruptly.

You are likely familiar with the appalling superstitions prevalent throughout Upper and Lower Styria, Moravia, Silesia, Turkish Serbia, Poland, and even Russia—the enduring belief in the Vampire. If human testimony, gathered with utmost care and solemnity before countless judicial commissions composed of numerous members selected for their integrity and intellect, holds any weight, then it is difficult to deny—or even doubt—the existence of such a phenomenon. For my part, having witnessed and experienced exactly what I describe, I possess no alternative theory to explain my ordeal save the ancient, well-attested beliefs of my homeland.

The following day, the formal proceedings commenced within the Chapel of Karnstein. The grave of the Countess Mircalla was opened. Both the General and my father recognized their treacherous and beautiful guest in the visage now exposed to the light. Though a century and a half had passed since her burial, her features retained the warmth of life. Her eyes were wide open; no cadaverous odor emanated from the coffin. Two physicians—one official, the other representing the prosecutor—attested to the marvel: a faint yet perceptible respiration and a corresponding heartbeat. Her limbs were perfectly supple, her flesh elastic, and the leaden coffin floated in a pool of blood, in which her body lay submerged to a depth of seven inches.

Here were all the admitted signs and proofs of vampirism. Consequently, in accordance with ancient custom, the body was raised, and a sharp stake was driven through the vampire's heart. She let out a piercing shriek at that precise moment—a sound indistinguishable from the final agony of any living soul. Immediately after, her head was severed, and a torrent of blood gushed forth from the wound. The body and head were then placed upon a pyre of wood, burned to ash, and cast into the river to be carried away. Since that day, the territory has never again suffered the visitations of a vampire.

My father possesses a copy of the Imperial Commission's report, complete with the signatures of all witnesses present, verifying this account. From this official document, I have summarized the details of this final, shocking event.
