To recount the specifics of how a handful of disciplined fighters shattered a rebel horde fifteen times larger would be tedious. What mattered was the psychological collapse that followed. The rebels, numbering in the thousands, had poured into the city via a secret mine, confident in their victory. Instead, they found silence. Not a single man emerged from the tunnels. Within moments, the initial whispers of terror inside the walls erupted into a cacophony of triumph, turning the besiegers' confidence into a sudden, malignant panic.

These were not professional soldiers; they were Atlanteans, yes, bred for courage, but stripped of leadership by their own "silly principles" which declared every man equal to his neighbor. With no commanders and arms of vile quality, they were a chaotic mass. When the panic struck, it spread like a plague through their ragged ranks. There were no officers to rally the fleeing, no voices to direct the desperate few who stayed to fight.

My strategy was brutally simple: I never played defense. My men and I became a relentless hammer. We turned one flank, smashed through the center, and harassed the other, hacking a path through the solid mass. By constantly keeping them running and guessing where the next blow would fall, we fermented their terror until the outer lines fled toward the forests and scorched fields, dragging the inner packs with them.

Breaking this siege was not a feat of martial genius, nor did it require the skill of a veteran legionnaire. It was merely seizing the momentum of the moment. Had these rebels faced us in open battle on their own soil, their sheer ferocity and numbers would have overwhelmed any trained troop. They were Atlanteans, just as the citizens within the walls were, and their oppression had pushed them to a savagery born of desperation. Yet, scattered as they were, the rebellion crumbled. I saw clearly that the uprising could be ended without excessive cruelty; too great a severity would only drive the malcontents to further despair.

Chasing the fugitives to ensure their rout had taken us miles from the city walls. Exhausted by the burning heat of the day, I halted among some half-ruined houses, establishing a temporary fortification for the night. Fortune smiled upon us when a drove of frightened horses, scared by the rebels' flight, blundered into our lines. After killing five, we had supper, and quickly fires were lit. Sentries paced the perimeter, shouting warnings, while the wounded tended their injuries by the flames. Around the embers, the officers discussed the day's engagements with the gusto that soldiers alone possess, though the details held no interest for anyone outside the profession.

As sleep drew near, snores rose from every corner. My officers, weary to the bone, continued talking only out of deference to their commander. Yet I harbored a feverish dread of solitude, pressing conversation onto them remorselessly until they were spared the rudeness of falling silent. Then, a sentry approached, saluting sharply. "My lord," he reported, "there is a woman who came from the city attempting to enter the bivouac."

"How is she named?"

"She will not say."

"Has she business?"

"She says none. She demands only to see my lord."

"Bring her here to the fire." On second thought, recalling that whatever news she carried would likely be meant for my private ears, I intervened. "Wait," I commanded. Rising from beside the fire, I followed the sentry to the outer line.

"Where is she?" I asked.

"My comrades are holding her. She might be a wench of the rebels plotting to stab my lord in the heart, and then we sentries would pay for it," the soldier explained simply. "The Empress seems to value my lord greatly at present, and we know the cunning of her tormentors."

"You are frank in your thoughtfulness," I replied. He revealed the figure. Though muffled in a heavy hood and cloak, the silhouette betrayed itself instantly to anyone who loved Nais as deeply as I did. It was Ylga, her twin sister. I signaled the sentries to release her without question and led her away from the camp beyond the reach of their scrutiny.

"It is something of the most pressing nature that brings you here, Ylga?"

"You know me, then? There must be something warmer than the ordinary between us, Deucalion, if you could guess who walks beneath all these mufflings."

I let that pass. "What is your errand, girl?"

"Aye," she said bitterly, "there is my reward. Your concern lies solely with the message, not the carrier. Well, good my lord, you are no longer husband to the dainty Phorenice."

"This is news."

"And true enough. She divorces you, spurns you, thrusts you aside. After the first splutter of wrath, come the pains and penalties."

"The Empress can do no wrong. I demand you speak respectfully of the Empress."

"Oh, be done with that old fable! It sickens me. The woman was mad for love of you, and now she is mad with jealousy. She knows you gave Nais some of your priest's magic, causing her to sleep until you choose to wake her. To enlighten her on the method, I will tell you plainly: There is another airshaft next to the one where you cooed and billed, leading to another cell containing another prisoner. The wretch heard everything and tried to bargain for freedom by selling the story.

"But his news came a trifle stale. Due to the pressure of the morning's ceremonies, they forgot to bring his ration. When the gaoler finally remembered him, it was too late; Phorenice had publicly tied herself to a husband, and poor Nais had doubtless consumed her green drug. The fools tried to trade the tale for what it would fetch, and naturally, such a silly head was chopped off for his pains. After that, your Phorenice behaved as you may guess. Now, you may thank me, sir, for warning you not to return to Atlantis."

"But I shall go back. And if the Empress chooses to cut my head from its column, that is as the High Gods will."

"You are more sick of life than I thought. But I believe Phorenice judges your case accurately. I heard her outburst. 'Shall I hew off his head?' she said. 'Pah! Shall I give him to my tormentors? He would not wrinkle his brow at their fiercest efforts. No, he must have a heavier punishment... I shall lop off his right hand and left foot, crippling him, and drive him forth into the dangerous lands where he may learn Fear.' She speaks of beasts hunting him, fires spoiling his rest, hunger, and bad air. All the while, she claims she holds Nais near her, living and locked in stone, to play with as she chooses." She paused, eyes searching mine. "Will you go back to meet her vengeance?"

"No," I said firmly. "It is no part of my plan to be mutilated and left to live."

"So, being a woman of some sense, I judged. Moreover, having some small kindness left, I have made a plan for your movement. Does the name of Tob come back to your memory?"

"One who was Captain of Tatho's navy?"

"That same Tob. A gruff, rude fellow, smelling of tar, but possessing a sturdy honesty. Tob sails tonight for parts unknown, presumably to found a kingdom with himself as king. He struggles to earn a living in Atlantis these days and has scruples about leaving his family hungry. At the harbor, when I placed my neck under the axe to secure transport, he became loose-lipped, revealing he sees you as the perfect recruit. Whether you join him or not, he vows to serve you before the nether Gods."

"By his side, I gained experience fighting the greater sea beasts."

"Well, go and do it again. Believe me, it is your only chance. I bargained with Tob to slip past the harbor forts before the chains are raised. He is a daring fellow, fearless of navigating in darkness, and awaits you at a point of shore we agreed upon. Come, Deucalion, let me lead you."

"My girl," I said, "I see I owe you many thanks for what you have done."

"Oh, your thanks!" she said. "Keep them. I did not venture into the dark and danger for mere thanks, though I knew little else would be offered." She plucked at my sleeve. "Show me your walking pace, sir. They will want your countenance in the camp directly, and we need to avoid too narrow inquiries."

Together, we departed, leaving the lights of the bivouac behind. Ylga led the way, I bearing my weapons ready for beasts or men. Words were scarce, limited to warnings of natural dangers. Only once did we touch, crossing a rivulet of scalding water fed by a boil-spring.

"Are you sure of footing?" I asked, for the night was dark and the water hot enough to peel flesh from bone.

"No," she said, reaching out and taking my hand. I helped her across, then loosened my grip. She sighed and slowly withdrew her hand. Then, side by side, we walked in silence for hours and leagues.

Finally, we crested a rise, and below us, through the trees, I saw the gleam of the great estuary where Atlantis stood. The ground was soggy, the trees bristling with barbs, the path monstrous. Ylga's breath came in labored pants.
