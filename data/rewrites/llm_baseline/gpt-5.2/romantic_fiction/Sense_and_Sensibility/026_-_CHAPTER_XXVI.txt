Elinor could not seat herself beside Mrs. Jennings, and feel the carriage-wheels begin their long urging toward London, without a quiet astonishment at the turn her life had taken. It seemed but yesterday that their acquaintance with this bustling, benevolent woman had begun; that their ages, their habits, their very manner of thinking had appeared so ill matched; that Mrs. Jennings herself had raised objections—loud, repeated, and to her mind unanswerable—against carrying off the Dashwood ladies to town. Yet objections, like yesterday’s crust, had been softened by warmth and time. Mrs. Dashwood and Marianne, with that ardour which makes youth mistake eagerness for certainty, had smiled away every doubt. Elinor, though she could not always still the small, persistent misgiving that Willoughby’s attentions were too brilliant to be safely trusted, could not look on Marianne’s bright, expectant rapture—her whole soul leaning forward as if London already held out its arms—without feeling how pale her own prospect was beside it. How easily, she thought, could she embrace anxiety, if only she might also possess hope; how willingly exchange her cheerless composure for the keen suspense that animated Marianne.

A short time—very short—must decide what Willoughby intended; he was, in all likelihood, already in town. Marianne’s impatience to be gone declared plainly where she expected her happiness to be waiting. Elinor resolved, therefore, not merely to gather whatever light London might throw on his character, but to observe his behaviour to Marianne with such steady attention as should soon determine what he was, and what he meant. If her judgement proved unfavourable, she would at any cost try to open her sister’s eyes. If it proved otherwise, she must school herself into a more generous mind—must banish the selfish comparisons which stole in even when she condemned them, and allow Marianne’s joy to be unclouded by her own secret regrets.

They were three days upon the road; three days of cramped sitting, cold starts, and warmed inns; of the ceaseless accompaniment of hoof and wheel, and the changing music of England as they passed it—the thin winter wind worrying at hedges, the cawing of rooks over bare fields, the sudden roar and then hush of a coaching-yard as they swung in beneath an archway and the world became voices, chains, and stamping horses. Mrs. Jennings, hearty as a good kitchen fire, found something to remark upon in every meal and every bedchamber. If the inn’s hallway smelt of wet wool and smoke, she would lament that nobody kept a proper clear flame now; if the broth were thin, she would declare that people had grown stingy with bones; if the butter were sweet, she praised the dairy; if it were rank, she prophesied ruin to the farmer. She fretted, with the affectionate tyranny of a hostess by anticipation, that she could not make them choose their own dinners, nor extort a confession of preferring salmon to cod, boiled fowl to veal cutlets; and she treated such preferences as a matter of character, as if a woman’s heart might be read in her appetite.

Marianne, meanwhile, was a pattern of what future companionableness to Mrs. Jennings might be expected—if pattern meant a beautiful absence. She sat silent almost the whole way, wrapped in meditation and expectation, speaking scarcely at all unless a sudden glimpse of picturesque beauty—a pale river under leafless trees, a church tower rising over mist—drew from her an exclamation addressed to Elinor alone. To atone for this, Elinor took immediate possession of the post of civility she had assigned herself. She talked with Mrs. Jennings; she listened; she laughed when laughter was expected; she answered question after question about Barton and its neighbourhood, about people Mrs. Jennings half knew and wholly delighted in discussing. The very clatter of the road seemed to insist on common talk, and Elinor obliged it. If Marianne would not be drawn into the ordinary comfort of companionship, Elinor would at least make the journey tolerable to their friend.

They reached London by three o’clock on the third day, glad to escape the long confinement and to surrender stiff limbs to the luxury of a good fire. Mrs. Jennings’s house was handsome, and handsomely fitted up; they were shown at once into a comfortable apartment which had formerly belonged to Charlotte. Over the mantelpiece hung a landscape wrought in coloured silks—patient hills and a slow river—evidence that seven years at a great school in town had not passed without its expected accomplishments. Elinor looked at it with a kind of melancholy amusement; so much labour, so little life, and yet it was valued, spoken of, framed and displayed, like a proof of worth in a language some women were forced to learn.

Dinner was not to be ready in less than two hours; and Elinor, feeling that her mother must be made easy by some account of their arrival, sat down to write home. Marianne, in a few moments, took up pen and paper also.

“I am writing home, Marianne,” said Elinor. “Had not you better defer your letter for a day or two?”

“I am not going to write to my mother,” Marianne replied hastily, as if the words were a door shut to further inquiry.

Elinor said no more; but her mind made its own quick step. Marianne, then, wrote to Willoughby. The conclusion that followed—instant, almost involuntary—was that, however mysteriously they wished to manage the affair, they must be engaged. The conviction did not satisfy all her prudence; but it did, in spite of herself, give her pleasure. An engagement would explain so much; it would give honourable shape to Marianne’s confidence, and purpose to Willoughby’s apparent devotion. She returned to her letter with more alacrity.

Marianne’s was finished in a very few minutes; in length it could be no more than a note. It was folded, sealed, directed with eager rapidity. Elinor fancied she distinguished, in the bold sweep of the address, a large W; and no sooner was it complete than Marianne rang the bell and requested the footman to have it conveyed to the two-penny post. That decided the matter as far as outward signs could decide it.

Marianne’s spirits remained high, yet they fluttered with a nervous lightness that made them less pleasurable to witness. As evening came on, her agitation increased; she could scarcely eat any dinner, though Mrs. Jennings lamented the smallness of her appetite with all the earnestness of one who believed nourishment a remedy for every trouble of the heart. When they returned to the drawing-room, Marianne seemed to listen anxiously to every passing sound. In London, even in a good neighbourhood, the air is never still: there are distant wheels, the calling of chairmen, the rise and fall of voices in the street, the occasional sharp report of a carriage-door, like a hand knocking at the heart. Marianne started at each, her attention strained, as if the very city were a great instrument playing only one awaited note.

It was a great satisfaction to Elinor that Mrs. Jennings, being much engaged in her own room, saw little of what passed. The tea-things were brought in. The kettle’s low murmur, the small clink of porcelain, the comfortable breath of hot water and leaves—these homely sounds and smells, which in quieter circumstances might have settled the mind, only made Marianne’s vigilance more painful. Already she had been disappointed more than once by a rap at a neighbouring door, when a loud one was suddenly heard—so distinct, so near, that it could not be mistaken for any house but their own. Elinor felt secure it announced Willoughby’s approach. Marianne started up and moved toward the door.

Everything fell silent. Such silence could not be borne. Marianne opened the door, advanced a few steps toward the stairs, listened half a minute with her whole being, and returned to the room in all the agitation which a conviction of having heard him would naturally produce. In the ecstasy of that instant she could not help exclaiming, “Oh, Elinor, it is Willoughby—indeed it is!” and seemed almost ready to throw herself into his arms—when Colonel Brandon appeared.

The shock was too great to be met with calmness. Marianne immediately left the room.

Elinor, too, was disappointed; the hope she had not acknowledged even to herself had leapt at that rap. But regard for Colonel Brandon ensured him welcome, and something like pain followed close upon it: pain that he, so partial to Marianne, should be made to feel—must see at once—that her first emotion at the sight of him was nothing but grief and mortification. She perceived instantly that it was not unnoticed. He even looked after Marianne as she quitted the room, with such astonishment and concern as almost robbed him of recollection for what civility demanded.

“Is your sister ill?” said he.

Elinor answered, in distress, that she was; and then, hurriedly, she spoke of headaches, low spirits, fatigue—of everything to which she could decently attribute Marianne’s behaviour. He listened with earnest attention; then, as if recollecting himself and forcing a gentler composure into his features, said no more of it. He began to speak of his pleasure at seeing them in London, and made the usual inquiries about their journey, and the friends they had left behind. The talk went on in that calm way which is meant to be friendly and becomes only formal when hearts are elsewhere. Both were out of spirits. Elinor’s thoughts pursued one name; Brandon’s, she suspected, pursued another, with a heaviness he would not expose.

Elinor wished very much to ask whether Willoughby were in town; but she feared to give Colonel Brandon pain by naming his rival. At length, merely to say something, she asked if he had been in London ever since she had seen him last.

“Yes,” he replied, with some embarrassment, “almost ever since. I have been once or twice at Delaford for a few days, but it has never been in my power to return to Barton.”

The words and the manner brought back to Elinor’s mind all the circumstances of his quitting Barton, and the uneasiness and suspicions they had caused to Mrs. Jennings. She was fearful her question had implied more curiosity than she had ever truly felt.

Mrs. Jennings soon came in, full of noise and cheerfulness, as if she brought with her a whole bustling household.

“Oh! Colonel,” cried she, “I am monstrous glad to see you—sorry I could not come before—beg your pardon, but I have been forced to look about me a little, and settle my matters; for it is a long while since I have been at home, and you know one has always a world of little odd things to do after one has been away; and then I have had Cartwright to settle with. Lord! I have been as busy as a bee ever since dinner! But pray, Colonel, how came you to conjure out that I should be in town to-day?”

“I had the pleasure of hearing it at Mr. Palmer’s, where I have been dining.”

“Oh, you did? Well, and how do they all do? How does Charlotte do? I warrant you she is a fine size by this time.”

“Mrs. Palmer appeared quite well, and I am commissioned to tell you, that you will certainly see her to-morrow.”

“Ay, to be sure, I thought as much. Well, Colonel, I have brought two young ladies with me, you see—that is, you see but one of them now, but there is another somewhere. Your friend, Miss Marianne, too—which you will not be sorry to hear. I do not know what you and Mr. Willoughby will do between you about her. Ay, it is a fine thing to be young and handsome! Well! I was young once, but I never was very handsome—worse luck for me. However, I got a very good husband, and I don’t know what the greatest beauty can do more. Ah! poor man! he has been dead these eight years and better. But Colonel, where have you been to since we parted? And how does your business go on? Come, come, let’s have no secrets among friends.”

He replied with his customary mildness to all her questions, but without satisfying her in any. Elinor began to make the tea—measuring leaves, pouring the water, watching the steam rise with a momentary absorption in the small exactness of the task, as if the senses could be persuaded to quiet the mind. Marianne was obliged to appear again. After her entrance, Colonel Brandon grew more thoughtful and silent than before, and Mrs. Jennings could not prevail on him to stay long.

No other visitor came that evening. The ladies agreed, with unusual unanimity, to go early to bed. The house, settling for the night, had its own soft language: doors eased to, coals shifted and sighed, footsteps above and below grew fewer. Yet for Marianne, silence was not rest; it was only the pause in which expectation could speak loudest.

She rose the next morning with recovered spirits and happy looks. The disappointment of the evening before appeared forgotten, absorbed into the confidence of what must surely happen to-day. They had not long finished breakfast when Mrs. Palmer’s barouche stopped at the door; and in a few minutes she came laughing into the room, delighted beyond measure—so delighted that it was hard to say whether she took most pleasure in meeting her mother or the Miss Dashwoods again. She was surprised at their coming to town, though it was what she had rather expected all along; angry that they had accepted her mother’s invitation after declining her own, though she declared, with perfect sincerity, that she never would have forgiven them if they had not come.

“Mr. Palmer will be so happy to see you,” said she. “What do you think he said when he heard of your coming with Mama? I forget what it was now, but it was something so droll!”

After an hour or two spent in what Mrs. Jennings called comfortable chat—meaning, in truth, every variety of inquiry concerning all their acquaintance on her side, and laughter without cause on Mrs. Palmer’s—it was proposed by Mrs. Palmer that they should all accompany her to some shops where she had business that morning. Mrs. Jennings and Elinor readily consented, as they too had purchases to make. Marianne declined at first, but was induced to go.

Wherever they went, Marianne was evidently on the watch. In Bond Street especially, where much of their business lay, her eyes were in constant inquiry, sweeping faces, doorways, the passing carriages; and in whatever shop the party were engaged, her mind was equally abstracted from everything actually before them. There were counters bright with ribbon and gloves, cabinets where perfumed soaps were stacked like little carved blocks of luxury, baskets of oranges breathing out a sharp sweetness that cut through the city’s damp—yet Marianne saw none of it, or saw it as one sees a table set when the appetite is not for food but for news. Elinor asked her opinion on an article that concerned them both; Marianne scarcely heard. Mrs. Palmer’s attention, by contrast, was caught by everything pretty, expensive, or new; she was wild to buy all, could determine on none, and dawdled away her time in rapture and indecision. Elinor, who could have smiled at such folly on another day, found it almost unendurable now, because every delay lengthened Marianne’s suspense.

It was late in the morning before they returned home. No sooner had they entered the house than Marianne flew eagerly upstairs; and when Elinor followed, she found her turning from the table with a sorrowful countenance that declared no Willoughby had been there.

“Has no letter been left here for me since we went out?” she asked the footman, who entered at that moment with parcels.

He answered, No.

“Are you quite sure of it?” she insisted. “Are you certain that no servant, no porter, has left any letter or note?”

He repeated that none had.

“How very odd!” Marianne said in a low, disappointed voice, turning away to the window.

“How odd, indeed,” repeated Elinor within herself, and uneasiness sharpened her thought. If Marianne had not known him to be in town, she would not have written as she did; she would have written to Combe Magna. And if he was in town—how strange that he should neither come nor write! Oh, my dear mother, you must be wrong to permit an engagement—if engagement there is—between a daughter so young and a man so little known, to be carried on in so doubtful, so mysterious a manner. Elinor longed to inquire outright; and yet she foresaw how such interference would be borne. After some consideration she determined that if appearances continued, for many days longer, as unpleasant as they now were, she would represent to her mother, in the strongest manner, the necessity of serious inquiry.

Mrs. Palmer and two elderly ladies—intimate acquaintance of Mrs. Jennings, met and invited in the morning—dined with them. Mrs. Palmer left soon after tea to fulfil her evening engagements, and Elinor was obliged to assist in making a whist-table for the others. Marianne was of no use on these occasions; she would never learn the game. Though her time was thus her own, the evening brought no more pleasure to her than to Elinor. It was spent in the same anxious expectation and the same pain of disappointment.

She sometimes attempted to read; but the book was soon thrown aside, as tasteless as food to a fevered palate. She returned to the more interesting employment of walking backwards and forwards across the room, pausing whenever she came to the window, listening, listening always—her whole being drawn toward the outer sounds of London, as if somewhere in that restless tide of wheels and footsteps there must at last be the long-expected rap.
