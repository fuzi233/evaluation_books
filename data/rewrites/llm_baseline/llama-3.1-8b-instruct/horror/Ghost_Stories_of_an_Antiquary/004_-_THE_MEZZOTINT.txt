In the quiet, hushed atmosphere of the university's art museum, Mr. Williams often found solace in the familiar rhythms of his daily routine. His task, to curate the vast collection of English topographical drawings and engravings, was a labor of love that brought him joy and satisfaction. While his colleagues at the museum might be drawn to the thrill of acquiring rare and exotic artifacts, Mr. Williams was content to focus on the more mundane yet no less significant pursuit of enriching the museum's existing collection.

Among his trusted suppliers was Mr. J. W. Britnell, a London dealer renowned for his meticulous catalogues of topographical prints and sketches. Mr. Williams had long relied on these catalogues to guide his acquisitions, but his museum's already impressive holdings meant he was a selective buyer, rather than a voracious one. Still, he appreciated Mr. Britnell's dedication to his craft and valued the dealer's expertise.

One February afternoon, a package arrived at the museum, bearing Mr. Britnell's latest catalogue and a handwritten note. The letter was brief, merely pointing out a particular mezzotint, numbered 978, which the dealer thought might interest Mr. Williams. The note read:

Dear Sir,

We draw your attention to No. 978 in our accompanying catalogue, which we would be delighted to send on approval.

Yours faithfully,
J. W. Britnell

Mr. Williams turned to the catalogue, and his eyes scanned the entries until he came to the one marked 978. There, he found a simple description of a mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. £2 2s. The image didn't particularly leap out at him, and the price seemed steep. Still, he recalled Mr. Britnell's reputation for discernment, and so he penned a postcard requesting that the mezzotint be sent on approval, along with a few other items from the catalogue that caught his eye.

As the days passed, Mr. Williams returned to his daily routine, largely forgetting about the mezzotint and the promise of new additions to the museum's collection. It wasn't until the afternoon post on a Saturday that the parcel arrived, delivered to the museum after Mr. Williams had left for the day. The attendant brought it to his rooms in college, where it awaited his inspection, along with the rest of the contents.
