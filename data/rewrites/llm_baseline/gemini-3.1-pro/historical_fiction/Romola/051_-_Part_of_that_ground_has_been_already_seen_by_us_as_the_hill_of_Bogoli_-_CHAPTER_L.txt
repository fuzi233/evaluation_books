The rhythmic, wooden *clack-whir* of Monna Lisa’s spinning wheel established the morning’s acoustic baseline, a steady, domestic heartbeat within the airy new house north of the Porta Santa Croce. Here, the air was entirely distinct from the acrid, overcrowded alleys of San Giorgio. It carried the olfactory tapestry of agrarian survival: the deep, earthy aroma of dried winter chestnuts, the sharp tang of garlic hanging in braided clusters, and the rich, starchy steam of coarse farro simmering gently over the hearth fire. For Tessa, whose understanding of the world was profoundly physical, rooted in the tactile and the gustatory, this house was a sanctuary of sustenance. 

Tito had been with her the evening before, escaping the dissonant, commercialized clamor of the Florentine political sphere. He had sunk into the carved, straight-backed armchair he had procured for his own comfort, shedding the weariness of a man forced to constantly translate his soul into the deceitful, dominant lexicons of the republic. Here, no such translation was necessary. The sonic environment was pure, unpolluted by ambition. Tessa spoke in the soft, rounded vowels of her native *contadina* dialect, a language inherently untranslatable in its simple, fiercely protective maternal grace. He had sat with one arm wrapped around the sturdy Lillo, while his other hand steadied the tiny Ninna, listening to the high, sweet notes of their voices calling him *Babbo*. For Tito, these round-cheeked children were like the tender green shoots of spring garlic breaking through dark soil—pure, breathing an uncorrupted vitality.

“Ninna is very good without me now,” Tessa began, letting the toddler seat herself heavily on the terracotta floor like a plump melon finding its resting angle. The scent of sweet milk and warm wool clung to the child. “I can leave her with Monna Lisa any time. If she cries, Lillo is as sensible as can be—he goes and thumps Monna Lisa.”

To prove his comprehension of the household’s percussive ecosystem, Lillo, whose dark eyes shone beneath curls the color of toasted wheat, bounded from his father’s knee and began rhythmically thumping the deaf old woman, seamlessly integrating his own drumbeat into the hum of her wheel. 

“A wonderful boy!” Tito laughed, the tension melting from his face. 

“Isn’t he?” Tessa said eagerly, leaning in so Tito could catch the scent of wild rosemary she had crushed between her palms that morning. “And I might go and see the Carnival to-morrow, just for an hour or two, mightn’t I?”

Tito pinched her cheek, an affectionate gesture of ownership. “Oh, you wicked pigeon. What have you to do with carnivals, now that you are an old woman with two children?”

“But old women like to see things,” Tessa said, her lower lip trembling slightly. In her memory, the Carnivals of past generations were a feast for the senses—a mythic procession of kings and queens entwined with fragrant, heavy-headed roses, singing ancient melodies that resonated with the turning of the seasons. It was better than the sterile, utilitarian spectacles the city had lately favored. 

Tito gently warned her of the bonfire, of the crowds, of the shifting, dangerous tide of the city’s mood, and of the fierce men with feathers and swords. But he could not entirely extinguish the bright, sensory hunger in her blue eyes. When Tessa whispered, begging to at least bring back *confetti* for the children, Tito drew several small coins from his *scarsella* and poured them into her palm. The clinking metal was a sudden, sharp sound, a promise of culinary delights. 

“That will buy no end,” Tessa breathed, envisioning the ancient, labor-intensive confections. She would bring back the taste of celebration to her children.

The February sunshine was bright as Tessa ventured toward the Piazza de' Signori. The morning air was a crisp acoustic space, still free from the overwhelming roar of the afternoon crowds. She wore her green woolen dress and her only treasure: a new clasp and necklace mounted with silver. The cold metal rested against her collarbone, a tactile reminder of Tito’s provision. As she navigated the streets, she sought the comforting, familiar sounds of country folk gathering, anticipating the vibrant hum of dialect and the smell of roasted meats that usually accompanied feast days. 

Yet, she was unaware that her steps were being tracked. Baldassarre followed her, his body bent beneath a heavy package of yarn. He was a man hollowed out, surviving on the meager bread his labor bought, his internal landscape dominated by the starving, sacred fire of vengeance. The city’s noise was a grating torment to his enfeebled mind, a harsh industrial friction against the echoes of his past. He remembered the grand, acoustic perfection of ancient Greek symposiums, the rich, complex lexicons of scholars dining on figs and honeyed wine—a cultural memory now buried under the rubble of his betrayal. His fierce purpose had become stealthy. He recognized Tessa as the peasant wife, the keeper of Tito's hidden domestic hearth, and he studied her with the desperate scrutiny of a starving man deciphering a faded, ancient recipe.

Tessa’s search for festive sights yielded only a peddler carrying a basket of red crosses. It was Bratti Ferravecchi, an elder of the Mercato whose very presence was a living archive of Florence's agrarian past. Bratti smelled of cured salt pork, old copper, and the raw, earthy dust of the Tuscan hills. 

“Young woman,” Bratti called, his voice a gravelly, resonant bark that cut through the street’s morning murmur. “Four white *quattrini* is a small price to pay for your soul—prices rise in purgatory, let me tell you.”

When she turned, Bratti’s sharp eyes recognized her beneath her hood. He spoke in the old, robust dialect of the market, a language of survival and bartering, mocking her old master Nofri and celebrating her apparent prosperity. He peddled his blessed crosses with the pragmatic worldview of a man who knew that both spiritual salvation and physical hunger required immediate, practical appeasement. Tessa, intimidated by the aggressive hum of his salesmanship and desperate to preserve her coins for Pippo’s sweet shop, hastily accepted his cross on trust and fled.

Entering the Via de' Libraj, Tessa found her true sanctuary. Pippo’s shop was a cathedral of sensory preservation. The air here was profoundly evocative, thick with the scent of boiling sugar, toasted coriander seeds, and the distillate of bitter orange blossoms. She exchanged her coins for a basketful of crisp *confetti*—plump Apulian almonds encased in fragile, crystalline shells of hardened sugar, their creation requiring days of meticulous, rhythmic layering by passionate confectioners. Holding the small basket, Tessa felt a childlike contentment, a profound meaning found through the simple act of providing sustenance and joy.

Then, the acoustic landscape of the street abruptly fractured. 

A procession approached, but it lacked the organic, joyous melodies of the old Carnivals. Instead, a sharp, dissonant chant pierced the air. White-robed youths marched beneath a banner of the Holy Mother, carrying baskets. To Tessa, who naturally integrated the mythic into the everyday, they appeared as literal angels stepped down from a fresco. But as they wheeled and surrounded her, their presence became a terrifying auditory and physical assault. 

“Sister, you carry the Anathema about you,” a treble voice demanded. 

“It is those vanities that are the Anathema,” a taller youth added, pointing to her silver necklace. “Take off that necklace and unclasp that belt, that they may be burned in the holy Bonfire of Vanities.”

They spoke in the flattened, dominant lexicon of Savonarolan zealotry—a sterilized, theological vocabulary designed to strip away individual identity and erode cultural memory. Words like *Anathema* and *Vanities* were foreign weapons to Tessa, completely untranslatable to her subjective experience. To her, the necklace was not a vanity; it was the physical manifestation of her husband’s affection, a necessary warmth against the coldness of the world. She could not bridge the chasm between her native dialect of love and their rigid, theological decrees. Her pouting lips quivered. Tears blurred her vision, her profound terror drowning out the harsh noise of the boys’ demands.

Suddenly, a gentle hand fell upon her arm. A soft, wonderfully modulated voice—a resonant acoustic balm—cut through the boys' aggressive chanting. 

“Do not be afraid; no one shall harm you.”

Tessa looked up into the loving hazel eyes of a tall, noble lady in black. This was Romola. She possessed a stately grace and an authoritative command of the dominant lexicon, easily navigating the subtle power dynamics the boys wielded. 

“Do not ask her for them,” Romola instructed the white-robed youths, her tone mild but unyielding, a perfect counter-frequency to their zealotry. “It answers no good end for people to give up such things against their will. That is not what Fra Girolamo approves: he would have such things given up freely.”

The white train, rebuffed by a lexicon of higher authority, moved on. The jarring, industrial-like noise of their march faded, leaving Tessa weeping in relief. Romola guided her into the quiet shelter of an archway, intuitively understanding the need to protect the vulnerable artifacts of Tessa's life. 

“Now, can we find room for your necklace and belt in your basket?” Romola asked. She looked into the basket, recognizing the delicate, culinary heritage within. “Ah! your basket is full of crisp things that will break: let us be careful, and lay the heavy necklace under them.”

Romola unfastened the silver and gently buried it beneath the *confetti*. The contrast was stark: the cold, unyielding metal of earthly wealth hidden beneath the fragile, labor-intensive sweets of a mother’s devotion. For Tessa, it was a sudden shift from a nightmare of erasure back to floating safety. She explained the sweets were for Lillo and Ninna, her voice returning to its comfortable, rhythmic dialect.

“And you would rather go home to them than see any more of the Carnival?” Romola smiled, pointing Tessa toward the straight road back to the Porta Santa Croce, back to the simmering farro and the steady hum of the spinning wheel. Tessa murmured a reverent farewell and hurried away, undesirous of any further spectacle that sought to burn the beautiful, tactile things of the world.

From the shadows, Baldassarre watched the tall, gliding figure of Romola vanish around the stonework. He tightened his grip on his bundle of yarn. A new, complex recipe for vengeance was finally taking shape in his mind. He had seen the two wives together. The tall wife, Romola, carried the proud, ancient bloodlines; she possessed the deep linguistic heritage, the buried treasure of classical scholarship that Baldassarre so violently mourned. If he could prove her husband's infidelity, her noble resentment would ignite. She would understand the untranslatable pain of a shattered worldview. She would become the fierce, compounding ingredient he needed—another mind, another will, ready to crush Tito beneath the weight of his own deceits. Baldassarre retreated into the city's noise, his patience solidifying, waiting for the harvest of his hatred.
