Two weeks had slipped by, leaving Newland Archer in a state of abstracted idleness within the private confines of his office at Letterblair, Lamson, and Low. The silence of the room, usually a sanctuary for the meticulous lawyer, felt heavy with unspoken tension until the head of the firm summoned him.

Old Mr. Letterblair sat throned behind his mahogany desk, a figure of established authority and perplexity. As he stroked his close-clipped white whiskers and ran a hand through the rumpled grey locks atop his jutting brows, Archer observed him with a mixture of respect and amusement. The senior partner looked remarkably like the Family Physician, a caricature of paternal anxiety, annoyed by a patient whose symptoms refused to fit into any neat medical classification.

"My dear sir," Letterblair began, addressing Archer with the formal deference reserved for a junior, "I have called you to discuss a delicate matter. For the moment, I prefer not to mention it to Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood." He gestured vaguely toward the other senior partners, men whose names adorned the firm's letterhead but whose physical forms had long since departed the mortal coil. In the old New York legal world, where lineage often outweighed tenure, Letterblair was effectively his own grandfather.

Leaning back, his brow furrowed in deep concern, the elder partner continued, "For family reasons..."

Archer looked up, his curiosity piqued.

"The Mingott family," Letterblair said, offering an explanatory smile that didn't quite reach his eyes. "Mrs. Manson Mingott sent for me yesterday. Her granddaughter, the Countess Olenska, wishes to sue her husband for divorce. Certain papers have been placed in my hands." He paused, drumming his fingers rhythmically on the polished wood. "Given your prospective alliance with the family, I believe it is incumbent upon me to consult you—to consider the case with you—before taking any further steps."

A sudden heat rose in Archer's temples. He had seen the Countess only once since their brief encounter at the Opera, where she had appeared less vivid and importunate, receding from his mental foreground as May Welland had effortlessly reclaimed her rightful place in his life. He had not heard the word divorce spoken seriously since Janey's casual, random allusion, dismissing it as mere gossip. Theoretically, the concept of divorce held a distaste for him, second only to his mother's prejudices. He was annoyed that Letterblair, perhaps prompted by the formidable old matriarch, should so evidently be maneuvering to draw him into the fray. After all, there were plenty of Mingott men capable of handling such legal entanglements, and as yet, he was not even a Mingott by marriage.

He waited for the senior partner to elaborate. Letterblair unlocked a drawer and produced a packet of documents. "If you will run your eye over these papers..."

Archer frowned, his composure slipping. "I beg your pardon, sir; but precisely because of the prospective relationship, I should prefer your consulting Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood."

Letterblair looked surprised, perhaps slightly offended. It was highly unusual for a junior partner to reject such an opening.

"I respect your scruple, sir," Letterblair bowed smoothly. "But in this specific instance, I believe true delicacy requires you to do as I ask. Indeed, the suggestion is not mine, but that of Mrs. Manson Mingott and her son. I have seen Lovell Mingott, and also Mr. Welland. They all named you."

Archer felt his temper rising. For the past fortnight, he had drifted languidly through events, allowing May's radiant nature to obliterate the pressing claims of the Mingott clan. But this summons from old Mrs. Mingott roused him to a sharp awareness of what the family believed they had the right to exact from a prospective son-in-law. He chafed at the role.

"Her uncles ought to deal with this," he said firmly.

"They have," Letterblair replied. "The matter has been thoroughly discussed by the family. They are opposed to the Countess's idea; yet she is firm and insists on a legal opinion."

The young man fell silent, refusing to open the packet in his hand.

"Does she intend to marry again?"

"I believe it is suggested, but she denies it."

"Then—"

"Will you oblige me, Mr. Archer, by first looking through these papers? Afterward, when we have discussed the case, I will give you my opinion."

Reluctantly, Archer withdrew with the unwelcome documents. Since their last meeting, he had half-consciously collaborated with fate in shedding the burden of Madame Olenska. His solitary hour by the firelight had created a momentary intimacy, shattered providentially by the Duke of St. Austrey's intrusion with Mrs. Lemuel Struthers and the Countess's joyous greeting of them. Two days later, he had witnessed the comedy of her reinstatement in the van der Luydens' favor. He had told himself, with a touch of tartness, that a lady who knew how to thank all-powerful elderly gentlemen so effectively for a bunch of flowers did not require the private consolations or public championship of a young man of his limited stature. Viewing the matter in this light simplified his own position and surprisingly restored faith in his domestic virtues. He could not imagine May Welland, in any conceivable emergency, hawking her private difficulties or lavishing her confidences on strange men. She had never seemed finer or fairer than in the week that followed. He had even yielded to her wish for a long engagement, since she had offered the one disarming answer to his plea for haste.

"You know, when it comes to the point, your parents have always let you have your way ever since you were a little girl," he had argued.

And she had answered, with her clearest look: "Yes; and that's what makes it so hard to refuse the very last thing they'll ever ask of me as a little girl."

That was the old New York note; that was the kind of assurance he desired to hear from his wife. If one breathed the New York air habitually, there were times when anything less crystalline seemed stifling.

The papers he retreated to read told him little in terms of facts, yet they plunged him into an atmosphere where he choked and spluttered. They consisted mainly of an exchange of letters between Count Olenski's solicitors and a French legal firm to whom the Countess had applied for the settlement of her financial situation. There was also a short letter from the Count to his wife. After reading it, Newland Archer rose, jammed the papers back into their envelope, and reentered Mr. Letterblair's office.

"Here are the letters, sir. If you wish, I'll see Madame Olenska," he said in a constrained voice.

"Thank you—thank you, Mr. Archer. Come and dine with me tonight if you're free, and we'll go into the matter afterward: in case you wish to call on our client tomorrow."

Newland Archer walked straight home that afternoon. It was a winter evening of transparent clearness, with an innocent young moon hovering above the rooftops. He felt a desperate need to fill his soul's lungs with the pure radiance of the night, wishing to exchange no words with anyone until he and Mr. Letterblair were closeted together after dinner. It was impossible to decide otherwise; he must see Madame Olenska himself rather than allow her secrets to be bared to other eyes. A great wave of compassion had swept away his indifference and impatience. She stood before him as an exposed and pitiful figure, to be saved at all costs from further wounding herself in her mad plunges against fate.

He remembered what she had told him of Mrs. Welland's request to be spared whatever was "unpleasant" in her history, and he winced at the thought that it was perhaps this attitude of mind that kept the New York air so pure. "Are we only Pharisees after all?" he wondered, puzzled by the effort to reconcile his instinctive disgust at human vileness with his equally instinctive pity for human frailty.

For the first time, he perceived how elementary his own principles truly were. He passed for a young man unafraid of risks, knowing that his secret love affair with poor, silly Mrs. Thorley Rushworth had not been too secret to invest him with a becoming air of adventure. But Mrs. Rushworth was "that kind of woman"—foolish, vain, clandestine by nature, far more attracted by the secrecy and peril of the affair than by the charms and qualities he possessed. When the fact had dawned on him, it had nearly broken his heart; now, it seemed the redeeming feature of the case. The affair, in short, had been of the kind most young men of his age had experienced, emerging from it with calm consciences and an undisturbed belief in the abysmal distinction between the women one loved and respected and those one merely enjoyed—and pitied. In this view, they were sedulously abetted by their mothers, aunts, and other elderly female relatives, who all shared Mrs. Archer's belief that when "such things happened," it was undoubtedly foolish of the man, but somehow always criminal of the woman. All the elderly ladies Archer knew regarded any woman who loved imprudently as necessarily unscrupulous and designing, viewing the mere simple-minded man as powerless in her clutches. The only solution, they believed, was to persuade him, as early as possible, to marry a nice girl and then trust to her to look after him.

In the complicated old European communities, Archer began to guess, love-problems might be less simple and less easily classified. Rich, idle, and ornamental societies must produce many more such situations; and there might even be one in which a woman, naturally sensitive and aloof, would yet, from the force of circumstances, from sheer defenselessness and loneliness, be drawn into a tie inexcusable by conventional standards.

Upon reaching home, he wrote a line to the Countess Olenska, asking at what hour the next day she could receive him, and dispatched it by a messenger boy. The boy returned shortly with a message that she was going to Skuytercliff the next morning to stay over Sunday with the van der Luydens, but that he would find her alone that evening after dinner. The note was written on a rather untidy half-sheet, without date or address, but her hand was firm and free. He was amused at the idea of her week ending in the stately solitude of Skuytercliff, but immediately afterward felt that there, of all places, she would most keenly feel the chill of minds rigorously averted from the "unpleasant."

He arrived at Mr. Letterblair's punctually at seven, glad of the pretext to excuse himself soon after dinner. He had already formed his own opinion from the papers entrusted to him and did not particularly wish to delve deeper with his senior partner. Mr. Letterblair was a widower, and they dined alone, copiously and slowly, in a dark, shabby room hung with yellowing prints of "The Death of Chatham" and "The Coronation of Napoleon." On the sideboard, between fluted Sheraton knife-cases, stood a decanter of Haut Brion and another of the old Lanning port—a gift from a client that the wastrel Tom Lanning had sold off a year or two before his mysterious and discreditable death in San Francisco, an incident less publicly humiliating to the family than the sale of the cellar.

After a velvety oyster soup came shad and cucumbers, then a young broiled turkey with corn fritters, followed by a canvas-back with currant jelly and a celery mayonnaise. Mr. Letterblair, who lunched on a sandwich and tea, dined deliberately and deeply, insisting on his guest's doing the same. Finally, when the closing rites had been accomplished, the cloth was removed, cigars were lit, and Mr. Letterblair, leaning back in his chair and pushing the port westward, said, spreading his back agreeably to the coal fire behind him: "The whole family is against a divorce. And I think rightly."

Archer instantly felt himself on the other side of the argument. "But why, sir? If there ever was a case—"

"Well—what's the use? *She's* here—he's there; the Atlantic is between them. She'll never get back a dollar more of her money than what he's voluntarily returned to her: their damned heathen marriage settlements take precious good care of that. As things go over there, Olenski has acted generously; he might have turned her out without a penny."

The young man knew this and remained silent.

"I understand, though," Mr. Letterblair continued, "that she attaches no importance to the money. Therefore, as the family says, why not let well enough alone?"

Archer had gone to the house an hour earlier in full agreement with Mr. Letterblair's view; but put into words by this selfish, well-fed, and supremely indifferent old man, it suddenly became the Pharisaic voice of a society wholly absorbed in barricading itself against the unpleasant.

"I think that's for her to decide."

"H'm—have you considered the consequences if she decides for divorce?"

"You mean the threat in her husband's letter? What weight would that carry? It's no more than the vague charge of an angry blackguard."

"Yes; but it might make some unpleasant talk if he really defends the suit."

"Unpleasant—!" said Archer explosively.

Mr. Letterblair looked at him from under enquiring eyebrows, and the young man, aware of the uselessness of trying to explain what was in his mind, bowed acquiescently while his senior continued: "Divorce is always unpleasant."

"You agree with me?" Mr. Letterblair resumed, after a waiting silence.

"Naturally," said Archer.

"Well, then, I may count on you; the Mingotts may count on you; to use your influence against the idea?"

Archer hesitated. "I can't pledge myself till I've seen the Countess Olenska," he said at length.
