I awoke from a deep slumber to the throbbing agony of my right hand. The back of it was swollen, marked by a triangular wound that resembled the bite of a leech. By evening, the swelling had receded, the pain almost vanished, leaving only a faint scar. I scoured the cave, lifting every stone, searching for any creature capable of such an attack, but found nothing.

Days stretched into weeks. The figure in the corner remained motionless, eyes sealed shut. Yet, it was not dead; there was no scent of decay, only the crisp purity of the cave air. More subtly, the sharp angles of the skeleton seemed to soften, the form rounding out, the skin losing its parchment-like tension. If this transformation was real, then life had returned—the tide that had receded toward the infinite was flowing again. I watched with feverish hope, twenty times a day seeking proof of revival, twenty times doubting, sometimes despairing entirely. Yet, whenever I recalled the moment I discovered her, hope surged anew.

Weeks passed. One night, unable to sleep, I rose to breathe the cooler air of the cavern entrance. The moon was full, casting a silver clarity over the shadows. Before leaving, I glanced at my charge. "Eternal bliss!" I whispered, my heart hammering against my ribs. "Do I see her eyes?" They appeared to glow—great orbs, dark as the void between stars, luminous from an excess of darkness, set against the pale whiteness of her face. I crept closer, terrified my heartbeat might startle her. I leaned down. Alas, her lids were closed tight. Hope and imagination had conspired to deceive me. My desire would remain unfulfilled. I turned away, collapsed onto the cave floor, and wept. Then, a thought struck me: her eyes had been slightly open before, a tiny slit through which nothingness had peered. Perhaps she had opened them for a fleeting moment and fallen back asleep; perhaps she was awake, holding them shut. In either case, life, however faint, must have commanded them. Comforted by this logic, I slept.

That night, I was bitten again. I awoke with a burning thirst.

Morning brought another search, equally fruitless. The wound was identical, healing swiftly by evening. I reasoned that a large leech occasionally ventured from the hot stream. "But if its goal is blood," I told myself, "so long as I guard this place, my treasure is safe."

That same morning, after peeling a grape as my ritual, I placed it in her mouth. Her lips twitched, accepting the fruit. I *knew* then that she lived.

Hope ignited within me. She must be able to rise at will. I ventured into the forest, seeking materials. Fibrous skeletons, reminiscent of the prickly pear, suggested themselves immediately. I gathered them, dried them in the sun, separated the reticulated layers, and began weaving two loose garments—one for her waist, one for her shoulders. Using the stiletto-point of an aloe leaf and various filaments, I stitched three thick layers of the tissue together.

For the following week, the only change was that she consumed grapes more readily. Signs grew clearer: she was becoming plumper, her skin fairer. Yet her eyes remained closed. A terrible fear would seize me, that her growth was some grotesque fungal expansion, the grapes insufficient to explain such rapid change.

The bites became regular, occurring every three days. The creature, whatever it was, visited only at night, striking my neck or arm with a single bite while I slept. I lay awake for hours, watching, hearing nothing, seeing nothing. I never felt the sting. Despairing of ever catching it, I ceased my searches. I knew I was losing blood dangerously, yet I cared little. Death was yielding to life before my eyes; a soul was gathering strength to save me from solitude. We would depart together, and I would recover.

The garments were finished. Satisfied with my work, I matted the fibers into sandals.

One night, I woke suddenly, breathless and faint, craving air. I rose to crawl from the cave when a faint rustle in the bedding leaves froze me.

"I caught the vile thing," a weak voice murmured, speaking my mother tongue. "I caught it in the very act!"

She was alive! She spoke! I held my breath, terrified that my transport would terrify her.

"What creature?" I breathed.

"The creature that was biting you," she answered.

"What was it?"

"A great white leech."

"How big?" I forced myself to remain calm.

"Nearly six feet long, I should think."

"You have saved my life, perhaps! But how could you touch such horror! How brave!"

"I did!" was her only reply, and I thought I heard a shudder.

"Where is it? What did you do?"

"I threw it in the river."

"It will return, I fear!"

"I doubt I could have killed it, even if I knew how. I heard you moaning, rose to check, saw the monster at your neck, and pulled it away. I could not hold it, nor throw it far. I only heard it splash into the water!"

"We'll kill it next time!" I declared, then turned pale, sought the open air, and collapsed.

When consciousness returned, the sun was high. The lady stood a distance away, looking, even in the clumsy attire I had crafted, both grand and graceful. I *had* seen those glorious eyes! Through the night they had shone! Dark as primordial darkness, they now outshone the day. She stood erect as a column, regarding me. Her pale cheek showed no emotion, only inquiry. I rose.

"We must go!" I said. "The white leech..."

I stopped. A strange smile flickered across her beautiful face.

"Did you find me here?" she asked, pointing to the cave.

"No; I brought you here," I replied.

"You brought me?"

"Yes."

"From where?"

"From the forest."

"What have you done with my clothes—and my jewels?"

"You had none when I found you."

"Then why did you not leave me?"

"Because I hoped you were not dead."

"Why should you have cared?"

"Because I was very lonely, and wanted you to live."

"You would have kept me enchanted for my beauty!" she said, with proud scorn.

Her words and gaze roused my indignation.

"There was no beauty left in you," I said.

"Then why did you not let me alone?"

"Because you were of my own kind."

"Of *your* kind?" she cried, in a tone of utter contempt.

"I thought so, but find I was mistaken!"

"Doubtless you pitied me!"

"Never had woman more claim on pity, or less on any other feeling!"

With an expression of pain, mortification, and unutterable anger, she turned from me and stood silent. Starless night lay profound in the gulfs of her eyes; hate for the one who restored it had slain their splendor. The light of life had gone from them.

"Had you failed to rouse me, what would you have done?" she asked suddenly, without moving.

"I would have buried it."

"It? What? You would have buried *this*?" she exclaimed, flashing around me in a white fury, arms thrown out, eyes darting forks of cold lightning.

"Nay; that I saw not! These weary weeks of watching and tending have brought you back," I answered—for with such a woman, I must be plain. "Had I seen the smallest sign of decay, I would have buried you instantly."

"Dog of a fool!" she cried. "I was but in a trance—Samoil, what a fate! Go and fetch the she-savage from whom you borrowed this hideous disguise."

"I made it for you. It is hideous, but I did my best."

She drew herself up to her tall height.

"How long have I been insensible?" she demanded. "A woman could not make that dress in a day!"

"Not in twenty days," I rejoined, "hardly in thirty!"

"Ha! How long do you pretend I have lain unconscious? Answer me at once."

"I cannot tell how long you lay when I found you, but there was nothing left of you but skin and bone. That was more than three months ago. Your hair was beautiful; nothing else! I have done for it what I could."

"My poor hair!" she said, bringing a great armful of it around from behind her. "It will be more than a three-months' care to bring *you* to life again! I suppose I must thank you, though I cannot say I am grateful!"

"There is no need, madam: I would have done the same for any woman—yes, or for any man either!"

"How is it my hair is not tangled?" she said, fondling it.

"It always drifted in the current."

"How? What do you mean?"

"I could not have brought you to life but by bathing you in the hot river every morning."

She gave a shudder of disgust, standing for a while with her gaze fixed on the hurrying water. Then she turned to me.

"We must understand each other!" she said. "You have done me the two worst of wrongs—compelled me to live, and put me to shame. Neither can I pardon!"

She raised her left hand and flung it out as if repelling me. Something ice-cold struck me on the forehead. When I came to myself, I was on the ground, wet and shivering.
