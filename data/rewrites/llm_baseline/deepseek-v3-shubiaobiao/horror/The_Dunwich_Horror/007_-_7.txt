The quiet unease that had settled over Dunwich was but a prelude to the true horror yet to come. Officials, bewildered and hesitant, went through the motions of their duties—inspecting the property of the late Wilbur Whateley, filing reports in Aylesbury, and avoiding the reeking, boarded-up ruin of his farmhouse at all costs. The air itself seemed thick with something foul, and from the hollow shell of the house came wet, slithering sounds, as if something vast stirred within.

Earl Sawyer, who had tended the livestock in Wilbur’s absence, was a wreck of frayed nerves. The men sent to assess the estate dared not linger long. They left quickly, grateful to escape the oppressive dread that clung to the place. Among the relics left behind was a massive ledger filled with cryptic symbols—a madman’s journal, perhaps, or something far worse. Sent to Miskatonic University for study, it defied all translation.

Then, on the night of September ninth, the earth itself seemed to revolt. The hills groaned, dogs howled in frenzy, and by dawn, a sickening stench tainted the air. Luther Brown, George Corey’s hired boy, stumbled back from the meadow wild-eyed and gasping.

“Up there—past the glen—” he choked out to Mrs. Corey. “Somethin’s been there! The bushes—torn up like a house was dragged through. And the *prints*—” His voice dropped to a whisper. “Big as barrels, sunk deep… but no four-footed thing made ’em. They had *marks*—like great big fans pressed into the dirt. And the stink—like *his* place. Like *Whateley’s*.”

Word spread like a brushfire. Sally Sawyer, over at Seth Bishop’s, had her own tale: Chauncey, her boy, had seen the Whateley house blown apart, timbers scattered like kindling, the lower floor smeared with a black, tarry slime. And in the yard—tracks. Enormous, sticky, leading off into the fields where the grass was flattened in a swath wider than a barn.

“Somethin’ ain’t right,” Sally whispered into the phone. “It ain’t human. Never was.”

By noon, the men of Dunwich gathered, grim-faced, to follow the trail. The prints gouged into the earth, the shattered trees, the stench—all led down into Cold Spring Glen. None dared descend. Whatever had emerged from the Whateley ruin had returned there, and the glen waited, silent and reeking, like the maw of some unseen beast.

That night, doors were barred, guns loaded. But no barricade could keep out the terror. At Elmer Frye’s farm, just east of the glen, the family awoke to a stench so thick it choked them. Then came the sounds—splintering wood, the cattle screaming, a *crushing* weight moving in the dark. By dawn, the Frye barn was in ruins, half the livestock gone, the rest torn apart.

The next day, fresh tracks scarred the road near Sentinel Hill. The thing had climbed straight up the cliffs, leaving a trail of crushed stone and that same foul stickiness upon the ancient ritual slab where the Whateleys once chanted to things best left unnamed.

Then, on the third night, the phones rang—a single, shattered plea: *“Help, oh, my Gawd!—”* before silence. By morning, the Frye farm was gone. Not ruined. *Erased.* The house collapsed inward, nothing left but stench and slime.

The Elmer Fryes had vanished from Dunwich.

And the horror was only beginning.
