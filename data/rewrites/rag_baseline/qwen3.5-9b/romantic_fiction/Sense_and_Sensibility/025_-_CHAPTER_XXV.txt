January brought a shift in the atmospheric pressure of Barton Cottage, mirroring the internal climate of its inhabitants. Mrs. Jennings, though accustomed to drifting between the homes of her children and friends, maintained a permanent anchor in a residence near Portman Square. As the frost thickened, she directed her thoughts toward this haven and, with sudden decisiveness, summoned the elder Misses Dashwood to join her.

Elinor, failing to register the storm clouds gathering in Marianne's expression—the animated intensity that betrayed a fervent desire to comply—immediately issued a polite yet absolute refusal. She framed it as a unified maternal duty: they could not abandon their mother during the coldest season. Mrs. Jennings, taken aback, doubled down with characteristic vigor. "Oh, Lord!" she exclaimed, her voice rising in pitch. "Your mother can spare you in a heartbeat! I beg you to grant me this favor; I have set my heart upon it. Do not imagine you will be a burden. I shall send Betty in the coach—I trust my purse can handle that—and we three shall travel comfortably in my chaise. Should you tire of following my lead in town, simply tag along with one of my daughters. I am confident your mother will agree; after all, I have successfully dispatched my own offspring, making me the ideal custodian for yours. Unless I manage to marry one of you before I'm done, it won't be my fault. Rest assured, I shall advocate strongly for you among the eligible youth."

Sir John, sensing an opening, interjected with a conspiratorial whisper. "I suspect Miss Marianne would welcome such a scheme if her elder sister would join. It is a tragedy that she is denied pleasure solely due to Miss Dashwood's caution. I urge you both to depart for London once you've grown weary of Barton, keeping Miss Dashwood entirely in the dark."

"Nonsense," Mrs. Jennings retorted, clapping her hands. "I shall be ecstatic with Miss Marianne's company regardless of Elinor's presence. The more the merrier! Besides, it is far more comforting for them to remain together; if they grow bored of me, they can confide in one another and mock my eccentricities behind my back. But one or both must come. Good heavens, how can I endure another winter poking about alone, deprived of Charlotte? Come, Miss Marianne, seal the bargain with a handshake. If Miss Dashwood changes her mind later, so much the better."

Marianne responded with radiant warmth. "Thank you, madam, sincerely. Your invitation secures my gratitude forever. To accept would bring me nearly the greatest happiness I possess. Yet, my mother—my dearest, kindest mother—I acknowledge the validity of Elinor's plea. Were her peace compromised by our absence... Oh, no! Nothing could tempt me to leave her. It must not be a struggle."

Mrs. Jennings reiterated her assurance that Mrs. Dashwood could spare them effortlessly. Elinor, now fully comprehending her sister's state—a mind consumed by the desperate longing to reunite with Willoughby—ceased her direct opposition. Instead, she deferred the matter to their mother, anticipating no support for her efforts to block a journey she deemed detrimental to Marianne's sensibilities and personally inconvenient for herself. She knew Mrs. Dashwood would champion any endeavor Marianne desired, unable to instill caution regarding an affair where she had never learned to trust. Furthermore, Elinor dared not reveal her own aversion to London, knowing that Marianne, despite her fastidious nature, would overlook every indignity of Mrs. Jennings' company in pursuit of this singular goal. Such blindness to social friction proved the overwhelming power of Marianne's obsession, a phenomenon Elinor found impossible to reconcile with her sister's usual refinement.

When informed of the invitation, Mrs. Dashwood, convinced the trip promised immense amusement and perceiving the depth of Marianne's yearning through her daughter's distracted gaze, refused to entertain the idea of decline. "We shall go," she declared cheerfully. "Margaret and I shall benefit equally. In your absence, we shall retreat into our books and music, living quietly and happily. Upon your return, you will find Margaret transformed! I have alterations planned for your bedrooms that can now proceed without disruption. It is imperative that women of your station acquaint themselves with the manners and amusements of London. You will be under the care of a motherly, kind-hearted woman, and you will undoubtedly encounter your brother. Despite his faults—or those of his wife, considering his lineage—I cannot bear the thought of you remaining estranged from him."

"Though you act with your customary zeal for our happiness," Elinor countered gently, "there remains an objection I deem insurmountable." Marianne's face fell instantly. "And what," Mrs. Dashwood asked, feigning innocence, "does my prudent Elinor propose? Is it the cost?"

"My concern is this," Elinor stated firmly. "While I hold Mrs. Jennings' heart in high regard, her society offers neither genuine pleasure nor social consequence."

"That is true," her mother conceded, "yet you will rarely interact with her in isolation; you will almost invariably appear alongside Lady Middleton."

"If Elinor is deterred by her distaste for Mrs. Jennings," Marianne interjected, her voice sharp with defiance, "it need not hinder *my* acceptance. I harbor no such scruples and could endure every unpleasantness with minimal effort."

Elinor suppressed a smile at this display of indifference toward the social graces she had tirelessly cultivated in Marianne. Resolving internally that she could not permit her sister to navigate London's complexities alone, nor allow Mrs. Jennings to dominate Marianne's domestic hours unchecked, she decided to accompany them. This resolve was bolstered by the knowledge that Edward Ferrars, according to Lucy Steele, would not arrive in town until February, allowing ample time to conclude their visit early.

"I insist you *both* go," Mrs. Dashwood announced, dismissing Elinor's reservations as nonsense. "London awaits, and the prospect of your reunion promises delight. Perhaps Elinor, if she could anticipate enjoyment, would foresee pleasures in expanding her acquaintance with her sister-in-law's family."

Elinor, wishing to soften her mother's reliance on the Ferrars connection before the inevitable revelation of Lucy's deception, seized the moment. "I admire Edward Ferrars greatly and shall always welcome seeing him," she said calmly. "However, regarding the rest of the family, it is a matter of perfect indifference to me whether I am ever introduced to them."

Mrs. Dashwood smiled enigmatically, offering no comment. Marianne gazed upward in astonishment, realizing too late that Elinor had spoken her mind. After further discussion, the decision was finalized: the invitation was accepted.

News of the departure filled Mrs. Jennings with jubilation, accompanied by numerous pledges of kindness. Sir John was equally delighted; for a man terrified of solitude, acquiring two new companions in London was a triumph. Even Lady Middleton managed a performance of delight, stretching her social muscles to accommodate the occasion. The Miss Steeles, particularly Lucy, experienced a euphoria unknown to them, their spirits soaring at the intelligence.

Elinor submitted to the arrangement with less resistance than anticipated. Regarding her own attendance, she felt a profound unconcern. Observing her mother's evident satisfaction and Marianne's exuberant restoration to her former animation—elevated to a gaiety surpassing her usual baseline—Elinor could not fault the outcome. Marianne's joy bordered on manic agitation; her only tether to composure was the impending separation from her mother, a thought that induced excessive grief at the moment of parting. Mrs. Dashwood shared this sorrow, while Elinor remained the sole observer who viewed the separation as anything less than eternal.

The party departed in the first week of January. The Middletons followed shortly thereafter, while the Miss Steeles remained stationed at the park, intending to leave only upon the family's return.
