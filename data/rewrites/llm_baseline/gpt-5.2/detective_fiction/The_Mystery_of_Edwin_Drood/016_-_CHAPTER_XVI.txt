When John Jasper came back from the fit—whether swoon, seizure, or the mere collapse of a body overdriven—he opened his eyes upon a little domestic court assembled to keep him in the world.

Mr. and Mrs. Tope had been summoned in haste, and stood over him with the practised industry of people who have seen too many men refuse sustenance until their nerves exact it in a lump. Mrs. Tope’s cheeks were wet; her apron was already half a tray. Mr. Tope hovered with that anxious stiffness which makes a room feel smaller. And in a chair set squarely as a judge’s bench sat Mr. Grewgious, wooden in aspect, hands laid flat upon his knees, watching recovery as if it were a document to be witnessed.

“There! you’ve come to nicely now, sir,” sobbed Mrs. Tope. “Thoroughly worn out, and no wonder!”

“A man,” said Mr. Grewgious, with his habitual air of quoting himself as though he were the only reliable authority, “cannot have his rest broken, and his mind cruelly tormented, and his body overtaxed by fatigue, without being thoroughly worn out.”

Jasper was helped into his easy-chair. His voice, when it came, seemed to have been scraped down to a faint apology. “I fear I have alarmed you?”

“Not at all, I thank you,” returned Mr. Grewgious.

“You are too considerate.”

“Not at all, I thank you,” said Mr. Grewgious again, as if the phrase had been prepared beforehand and he was resolved to use it twice.

Mrs. Tope would not allow the air to settle. She moved as though the kitchen itself were her body and her limbs its utensils. “You must take some wine, sir, and the jelly that I had ready for you, and that you wouldn’t put your lips to at noon—though I warned you what would come of it, you know—and you not breakfasted; and you must have a wing of the roast fowl that has been put back twenty times if it’s been put back once. It shall all be on table in five minutes, and this good gentleman belike will stop and see you take it.”

The “good gentleman” replied with a snort—one of those brief sounds that mean yes, no, and nothing, according to the hearer’s necessity—and Mrs. Tope, having no leisure to interpret, bustled to set the cloth as if she were laying down a charm against death.

When the food came, it came with the smells that belong to an old English house: the faint sweetness of cooling jelly set with care, the savory fat of roast fowl re-warmed again and again until it had given up and taken back its own juices, the sharp comfort of wine poured with a steady hand. Even the plates had an odour—warm crockery, clean linen, the ghost of soap.

“You will take something with me?” Jasper asked, in the manner of a host who knows he is not hosting but being kept alive by ceremony.

“I couldn’t get a morsel down my throat, I thank you,” answered Mr. Grewgious.

Jasper ate and drank almost voraciously. Yet it was not the appetite of a man tasting; it was the haste of a man fortifying. The wine might have been water, the jelly mere cold sweetness, the fowl so much fibre and salt. He swallowed as if every mouthful were a sandbag thrown up against another collapse. The very ordinary house-sounds—the scrape of knife, the soft clink of glass, Mrs. Tope’s brisk movements—felt too loud in that small room, and then, in the pauses, the silence pressed in with the thick weight of Cloisterham itself: an old town that listened.

Mr. Grewgious sat upright, expressionless, a hard politeness set upon him like a coat: as though, if invited to talk of weather, murder, music, or the price of flour, he would reply, “I couldn’t originate the faintest approach to an observation on any subject whatever, I thank you.”

At length Jasper pushed away his plate and glass and sat with his hands together, meditating as if he were counting beads.

“Do you know,” he began, slowly, “that I find some crumbs of comfort in the communication with which you have so much amazed me?”

“Do you?” returned Mr. Grewgious, plainly implying that he did not, and thanked no one for it.

“After recovering from the shock—after having time to think—yes.” Jasper leaned forward. His eyes had that eager brightness which hope lends to desperate men because it is a stimulant and not a peace. “Is there not—or is there—if I deceive myself, tell me so, and shorten my pain—Is there not, or is there, hope that, finding himself in this new position, and becoming sensitively alive to the awkward burden of explanation—in this quarter, and that, and the other—with which it would load him, he avoided the awkwardness, and took to flight?”

“Such a thing might be,” said Mr. Grewgious, pondering.

“Such a thing has been,” Jasper pressed. “I have read of cases in which people, rather than face a seven days’ wonder and have to account for themselves to the idle and impertinent, have taken themselves away, and been long unheard of.”

“I believe such things have happened,” said Mr. Grewgious, still pondering.

Jasper seized the assent as though it were a rope thrown to a drowning man. “When I had, and could have, no suspicion—most of all, of such a leading matter as this—what gleam of light was there for me in the whole black sky? When I supposed his intended wife was here, and the marriage close at hand, how could I entertain the possibility of his voluntarily leaving this place in a manner so unaccountable, capricious, and cruel? But now—now that I know what you have told me—is there no little chink through which day pierces? Supposing him to have disappeared of his own act, is not his disappearance more accountable and less cruel? The fact of his having just parted from your ward is in itself a sort of reason for his going away. It does not make his mysterious departure the less cruel to me, it is true; but it relieves it of cruelty to her.”

Mr. Grewgious could not but assent.

“And even as to me,” Jasper continued, brightening with an ardour that was painful to witness, “he knew you were coming to me. He knew you were entrusted to tell me what you have told me. If your doing so has awakened a new train of thought in my perplexed mind, it reasonably follows that, from the same premises, he might have foreseen the inferences that I should draw. Grant that he did foresee them; and even the cruelty to me—and who am I?—John Jasper, Music Master—vanishes!”

Once more Mr. Grewgious could not but assent.

“I have had my distrusts, and terrible distrusts they have been,” said Jasper, with a shiver of truth that ran under the hopeful words. “But your disclosure—overpowering as it was at first—showing me that my own dear boy had had a great disappointing reservation from me, who so fondly loved him—kindles hope within me. You do not extinguish it when I state it, but admit it to be a reasonable hope. I begin to believe it possible—” Here he clasped his hands, as if praying to his own conclusion—“that he may have disappeared from among us of his own accord, and that he may yet be alive and well.”

At that moment Mr. Crisparkle came in.

Jasper, as if the words were an incantation that must be repeated to hold their power, said at once, “I begin to believe it possible that he may have disappeared of his own accord, and may yet be alive and well.”

Mr. Crisparkle took a seat, his strong, open face troubled, his kind eyes quick. “Why so?”

Jasper repeated the reasoning he had just set forth. And if it had been less plausible than it was, the good Minor Canon’s mind was already ready to receive anything that exculpated his unhappy pupil. Moreover, he too attached weight—real weight—to the lost young man having been so immediately before his disappearance thrust into a new and embarrassing relation towards all who knew his affairs. The fact altered the light.

“I stated to Mr. Sapsea, when we waited on him,” said Jasper—truthfully—“that there was no quarrel or difference between the two young men at their last meeting. We all know their first meeting was unfortunately far from amicable; but all went smoothly and quietly when they were last together at my house. My dear boy was not in his usual spirits; he was depressed—I noticed that—and I am bound henceforth to dwell upon the circumstance the more, now that I know there was a special reason for his being depressed: a reason, moreover, which may possibly have induced him to absent himself.”

“I pray to Heaven it may turn out so!” cried Mr. Crisparkle.

“I pray to Heaven it may turn out so!” Jasper echoed, and the echo was so exact it sounded like rehearsal. “You know—and Mr. Grewgious should now know likewise—that I took a great prepossession against Mr. Neville Landless, arising out of his furious conduct on that first occasion. You know that I came to you, extremely apprehensive, on my dear boy’s behalf, of his mad violence. You know that I even entered in my Diary, and showed the entry to you, that I had dark forebodings against him. Mr. Grewgious ought to be possessed of the whole case. He shall not, through any suppression of mine, be informed of a part of it, and kept in ignorance of another part of it. I wish him to be good enough to understand that the communication he has made to me has hopefully influenced my mind, in spite of its having been, before this mysterious occurrence took place, profoundly impressed against young Landless.”

This fairness—this sudden insistence on the entire truth—troubled Mr. Crisparkle more than any accusation would have done. It cast a light backward upon his own omissions. For he had been keeping back two fragments: Neville’s second strong outbreak of temper against Edwin Drood, and the flame of jealousy which, to Mr. Crisparkle’s certain knowledge, had leaped in Neville against the very man who now defended him. He was convinced Neville was innocent of the ugly disappearance; yet the little circumstances that clung around Neville like burrs were already heavy enough. To add two more felt like weighing truth until it resembled falsehood.

But here was a model of openness set before him. He hesitated no longer.

Addressing Mr. Grewgious—who became, if possible, more surpassingly Angular as he found himself in the unexpected position of authority in this matter—Mr. Crisparkle bore testimony to Mr. Jasper’s strict sense of justice. Then, with a plainness that cost him, he expressed his absolute confidence that his pupil would sooner or later be entirely cleared from the least taint of suspicion, and yet avowed that this confidence had been formed in spite of his confidential knowledge that Neville’s temper was of the hottest and fiercest; that it had been directly incensed against Jasper’s nephew; and that it had been inflamed by the romantic fancy of being enamoured of the same young lady.

Even this unlooked-for declaration could not shake Jasper’s sanguine reaction. It whitened him; but he said he would cling to the hope he had derived from Mr. Grewgious; and that if no trace were found leading to the dreadful inference that his dear boy had been made away with, he would cherish, unto the last stretch of possibility, the idea that Edwin might have absconded of his own wild will.

Then followed, out of this conference, a night walk by Mr. Crisparkle—memorable because, like many such turns in human lives, it seemed at the time to have no plan at all.

Uneasy, troubled for the young man he held as a kind of prisoner in his own house, he wandered without choosing. Cloisterham’s sounds guided him: the muted cough of winter trees, a late cart far off on frozen ruts, the town’s old stones taking and returning footfalls as if they were remembered. He often walked to the Weir; there was nothing remarkable in his footsteps tending that way. But so preoccupied was his mind, that his first clear consciousness of being near the Weir came not from sight, but from hearing—the falling water close at hand, steady as breath.

“How did I come here!” was his first thought, stopping short.

“Why did I come here!” was his second.

And then he stood listening, as if the water had a dialect of its own, and tonight might choose to speak in it.

A familiar passage of his reading, about airy tongues that syllable men’s names, rose so unbidden to his ear that he put it away with his hand, as though it were tangible and he could brush it aside. It was starlight. The Weir lay full two miles above the spot where the young men had repaired to watch the storm on Christmas Eve. No search had been made up here; the tide had been running strongly down at that time of night, and the likeliest places for the discovery of a body—if fatal accident had happened—lay between the storm-watching place and the sea, whether the tide ebbed or flowed.

The water came over the Weir with its usual sound on a cold starlight night. Little could be seen of it. Yet Mr. Crisparkle had a strange idea that something unusual hung about the place.

He reasoned with himself, sternly, as if he were examining a witness: What was it? Where was it? Which sense did it address? No sense reported anything unusual. He listened again. The water answered with the same voice as ever—unbroken, indifferent, filling the dark with a kind of ceaseless speech.

Knowing well that the mystery preying on him might itself haunt the scene, he strained his hawk’s eyes to correct his mind. He drew closer and peered at the Weir’s familiar posts and timbers. Nothing. No shadow of anything.

But he resolved he would come back early.

The Weir ran through his broken sleep all night, like a refrain you cannot stop humming. At sunrise he was there again.

Now it was bright frost. The air rang with a clean, tight silence in which every small sound—his own breathing, his boots on hard ground—seemed too distinct, too sharply outlined. The whole structure lay before him in clear detail. He stood where he had stood last night and surveyed it closely; and, when his eyes were about to withdraw, they were snatched back—caught keenly by one spot.

He turned his back upon the Weir and looked far away at the sky and the earth, as if to cleanse his sight; then looked again. The spot took him again at once. He concentrated. It was hardly more than a speck in the landscape, yet it fascinated him: a glisten that did not move with the glistening water-drops, but stayed—stationary—at a corner of the Weir.

His hands began stripping off his coat before he quite admitted to himself what he meant to do. He assured himself of it. He threw off his clothes. He plunged into the icy water, which struck him like a blow, and swam for the place.

Clambering the timbers, his fingers found what his eyes had promised: caught among the interstices by its chain, a gold watch. On its back were engraved two letters, clear as accusation: E. D.

He took it, brought it to the bank, then swam again to the Weir and climbed once more. He dived off. He knew every hole and corner of the depths there, and he dived and dived and dived until the cold drove pain into his bones and he could bear no more. His notion was that he would find the body. He found no body. Only a shirt-pin, stuck in mud and ooze like a small, spiteful fact that would not be washed away.

With these discoveries he returned to Cloisterham; and taking Neville Landless with him, went straight to the Mayor.

From that moment the machinery of suspicion began its grinding.

Mr. Jasper was sent for. The watch and the pin were identified. Neville was detained. And the town—always ready to fatten on a mystery, always hungry for a story it could repeat in its own approved words—rose in the wildest frenzy and fatuity of evil report against him.

The reports had a particular flavour: the flavour of a place that does not listen to what is said, but only to how it can be repeated. Cloisterham’s talk moved like the Weir’s water—continuous, cold, and self-justifying—carrying names as if names were driftwood. And every telling rubbed Neville’s identity into a thinner, harsher shape, until he was less a man than a convenient foreign syllable to be blamed.

He was described as vindictive and violent; as a nature that, but for his poor sister—who alone had influence over him, and out of whose sight he was never to be trusted—would be in the daily commission of murder. Before coming to England he had, it was said, caused to be whipped to death sundry “Natives”—nomadic persons, encamping now in Asia, now in Africa, now in the West Indies, and now at the North Pole—vaguely supposed in Cloisterham to be always black, always of great virtue, always calling themselves Me, and everybody else Massa or Missie (according to sex), and always reading tracts of the obscurest meaning in broken English, but always accurately understanding them in the purest mother tongue. He had nearly brought Mrs. Crisparkle’s grey hairs with sorrow to the grave. He had repeatedly said he would have Mr. Crisparkle’s life. He had repeatedly said he would have everybody’s life, and become, in effect, the last man. He had been brought down to Cloisterham, from London, by an eminent Philanthropist, and why? Because that Philanthropist had expressly declared: “I owe it to my fellow-creatures that he should be, in the words of BENTHAM, where he is the cause of the greatest danger to the smallest number.”

Those were Mr. Sapsea’s original expressions; and they were flung about the town like kitchen-salt—cheap, sharp, and making everything taste the same.

These dropping shots from the blunderbusses of blunderheadedness might not have struck Neville vitally; but there was a trained and well-directed fire of arms of precision too.

He had notoriously threatened the lost young man; and by the showing of his own faithful tutor—who strove hard for him—he had had a cause of bitter animosity, created by himself and stated by himself, against that ill-starred fellow. He had armed himself with an offensive weapon for the fatal night. He had gone off early in the morning after making preparations for departure. He had been found with traces of blood upon him—traces that might have been wholly caused as he represented, but might not also. When a search-warrant issued for the examination of his room, clothes, and so forth, it was discovered he had destroyed all his papers and rearranged all his possessions on the very afternoon of the disappearance.

The watch found at the Weir was challenged by the jeweller as one he had wound and set for Edwin Drood at twenty minutes past two on that same afternoon. It had run down before being cast into the water; and it was the jeweller’s positive opinion it had never been re-wound. That justified an ugly hypothesis: that the watch was taken from Edwin not long after he left Mr. Jasper’s house at midnight, in company with the last person seen with him, and was thrown away after being kept some hours.

Why thrown away? If he had been murdered, and so artfully disfigured or concealed—or both—that the murderer hoped identification would be impossible except by something he wore, assuredly the murderer would remove from the body the most lasting, the best known, the most easily recognisable things upon it. Those things would be the watch and the shirt-pin.

As to opportunities of casting them into the river: if Neville were the suspected man, they were easy. He had been seen by many persons wandering about on that side of the city—indeed on all sides—in a miserable, seemingly half-distracted manner. As to the choice of the spot, obviously such criminating evidence had better take its chance of being found anywhere rather than upon him or in his possession.

Concerning the reconciliatory nature of the appointed meeting between the two young men, very little could be made of that in Neville’s favour. It distinctly appeared that the meeting originated not with him but with Mr. Crisparkle, and was urged on by Mr. Crisparkle; and who could say how unwillingly, or in what ill-conditioned mood, his enforced pupil had gone to it?

So, the more the case was looked into, the weaker it became at every point.

Even the broad suggestion that Edwin Drood had absconded was rendered additionally improbable on the showing of the young lady from whom he had so lately parted. When interrogated, what did she say—earnestly and sorrowfully? That he had expressly and enthusiastically planned with her that he would await the arrival of her guardian, Mr. Grewgious. And yet he had disappeared before that gentleman appeared.

On these suspicions, urged and supported, Neville was detained and re-detained. The search was pressed on every hand. Jasper laboured night and day. But nothing more was found.

No discovery proving the lost man dead could be made; and at length it became necessary to release the person suspected of having made away with him. Neville was set at large.

Then came the consequence Mr. Crisparkle had too well foreseen. Neville must leave the place. The place shunned him and cast him out. Even had it not been so, Mrs. Crisparkle—dear old china shepherdess—would have worried herself to death with fears for her son and with general trepidation occasioned by such an inmate. Even had that not been so, the authority to which the Minor Canon deferred officially would have settled the point.

“Mr. Crisparkle,” quoth the Dean, “human justice may err, but it must act according to its lights. The days of taking sanctuary are past. This young man must not take sanctuary with us.”

“You mean that he must leave my house, sir?” asked Mr. Crisparkle.

“Mr. Crisparkle,” returned the prudent Dean, “I claim no authority in your house. I merely confer with you on the painful necessity you find yourself under of depriving this young man of the great advantages of your counsel and instruction.”

“It is very lamentable, sir,” Mr. Crisparkle represented.

“Very much so,” the Dean assented.

“And if it be a necessity—” Mr. Crisparkle faltered.

“As you unfortunately find it to be,” returned the Dean.

Mr. Crisparkle bowed submissively. “It is hard to prejudge his case, sir, but I am sensible that—”

“Just so. Perfectly. As you say, Mr. Crisparkle,” interposed the Dean, nodding smoothly. “There is nothing else to be done. No doubt, no doubt. There is no alternative, as your good sense has discovered.”

“I am entirely satisfied of his perfect innocence, sir, nevertheless.”

“We-e-ell!” said the Dean, dropping his voice and glancing slightly round as if innocence were a dangerous word to let out of doors, “I would not say so, generally. Not generally. Enough of suspicion attaches to him to—no, I think I would not say so, generally.”

Mr. Crisparkle bowed again.

“It does not become us, perhaps,” pursued the Dean, “to be partisans. Not partisans. We clergy keep our hearts warm and our heads cool, and we hold a judicious middle course.”

“I hope you do not object, sir, to my having stated in public, emphatically, that he will reappear here, whenever any new suspicion may be awakened, or any new circumstance may come to light in this extraordinary matter?”

“Not at all,” returned the Dean. “And yet, do you know, I don’t think”—with a very nice and neat emphasis on those two words—“I don’t think I would state it emphatically. State it? Ye-e-es! But emphatically? No-o-o. I think not. In point of fact, Mr. Crisparkle, keeping our hearts warm and our heads cool, we clergy need do nothing emphatically.”

So Minor Canon Row knew Neville Landless no more; and the young man went whithersoever he would, or could, with a blight upon his name and fame—a stain compounded not only of what he had done, but of what could be easily said of him in the town’s dominant tongue.

It was not until then that John Jasper silently resumed his place in the choir.

He came back among the ancient harmonies like a man returning to a trade after bereavement. Haggard and red-eyed, he bore on his face the mark of nights without sleep, and in his posture the drag of an inward weight. Whatever hope had lit him in that small room with the Tope’s supper smells and Mr. Grewgious’s wooden presence had deserted him now. His sanguine mood was gone; all his worst misgivings had returned, and sat with him like a second self.

A day or two afterwards, while unrobing, he took his Diary from the pocket of his coat. He turned the leaves. Then, with an impressive look, and without one spoken word, he handed Mr. Crisparkle an entry to read:

“My dear boy is murdered. The discovery of the watch and shirt-pin convinces me that he was murdered that night, and that his jewellery was taken from him to prevent identification by its means. All the delusive hopes I had founded on his separation from his betrothed wife, I give to the winds. They perish before this fatal discovery. I now swear, and record the oath on this page, That I nevermore will discuss this mystery with any human creature until I hold the clue to it in my hand. That I never will relax in my secrecy or in my search. That I will fasten the crime of the murder of my dear dead boy upon the murderer. And, That I devote myself to his destruction.”
