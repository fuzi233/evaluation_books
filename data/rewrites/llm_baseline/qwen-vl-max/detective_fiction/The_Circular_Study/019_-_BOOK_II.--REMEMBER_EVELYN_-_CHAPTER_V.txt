What follows is Thomas’s own account, as he confessed it to the detectives:

As I had promised, I brought my young wife to Felix’s house on the day and at the hour we had agreed upon. We walked there—no need for a carriage, since the hotel where we were staying was only a short distance away—and were met at the door by an old servant whose silence and deafness I had been warned about. The moment I saw him, and then the dim, grand hall stretching before us in quiet aristocratic gloom, Eva turned pale and looked at me with a question in her eyes. But I smiled reassuringly, though inside I was trembling with dread of what lay ahead. Taking her arm, I followed the servant down the hall, past the open drawing-room door—where I expected we might pause—into a plain room that made me frown until Bartow, as I later learned he was called, pulled aside a heavy portière and led us into my brother’s study.

At that moment, the room seemed like something out of a dream—fairyland, perhaps—had not Felix, who stood alone within, immediately drawn our attention with the sheer force of his presence, more powerful than ever before.

Eva, who knew little of this brother—nothing that would have prepared her for such a man—looked frightened, slightly awed. Yet she stepped forward bravely toward him, and when I introduced her, she smiled with such grace and warmth that I secretly hoped he might be disarmed.

And perhaps he was. His already pale face grew almost waxy under the strange yellow glow of the lantern above us. His hand, raised in a mechanical greeting, dropped heavily, and he could barely manage a few words of welcome. They sounded inadequate—too thin for the occasion—except for the way his eyes locked onto hers, revealing a depth of feeling she couldn’t possibly understand. For her very life—her happiness, which is life itself—hung in the balance beneath that iron will.

I, who knew—or thought I did—cast him a pleading glance. Then, saying I had explanations to make, I asked if Mrs. Adams might rest while we spoke privately.

He answered with a look so strange I couldn’t read it. Did he sense the rebellion in my tone? Did he understand then, as he would later, the nature of my compliance? I’ll never know. All I know is that he stopped fumbling with some small object on the table, bowed with a sarcastic elegance that made me feel suddenly inferior—even in stature—and led me to a small door I hadn’t noticed before.

"Your wife will find it more comfortable here," he said slowly, pausing between words as if struggling to contain a storm beneath his calm. He opened the door to reveal a small but elegant bedroom. "What we have to say won’t take long. Mrs. Adams won’t find the wait tedious."

"No," she replied, smiling with a natural laugh that, I hope, came from perfect joy. Still, she must have found the whole scene odd—my manner, his, the way I was leaving her with a stranger. It wasn’t the kind of introduction one expects from a groom bringing his bride to meet his only living relative.

"I’ll call you—" I began, but the image of her dimpled face above the bouquet of roses she carried made me forget the rest. The door closed behind me, and I was left face to face with Felix.

He breathed easier now that we were alone, and his manner seemed more natural. Yet he didn’t speak at once. Instead, he cast a curious glance around the room—the most unsettling space I’ve ever seen—and after seeming satisfied with what he saw, led me to the large table that had always held a strange pull over him.

"Come further back," he said. "I need air—space in this confined room. And so do you. Besides, why should she hear what we’re about to say? She’ll learn the truth soon enough. She seems a gentle woman."

"An angel!" I started to say, but he silenced me with a sharp gesture.

"We won’t discuss your wife—Mrs. Adams," he snapped. "Where is John Poindexter?"

"At the hotel," I replied. "Or maybe he’s already gone home. I no longer care about him. Felix, I will never leave my wife. I’d rather break the oath I swore—before I even knew how precious the woman was whose happiness I vowed to destroy. That’s what I’ve come to tell you. Make it easy, Felix. You’ve loved and suffered. Let us bury the past. Let us—"

Had I truly believed I could move him? Perhaps I still clung to childish hopes. But even as those words left my lips—before I’d fully spoken them—I saw his expression harden, as if carved from stone. I could falter; he could not. He stood firm, stronger than ever, beyond appeal.

But what did it matter? At worst, what did I have to lose? A struggle that might bring Eva pain, and me the loss of a fortune I had begun to think of as mine. Trivial concerns. Eva would learn the truth sooner or later—my real name, the story of her father’s guilt. Why not now? And if we had to begin life poor, at least we’d live together. To separate from her—

Felix broke the silence with words I never expected.

"I anticipated this. From the moment you begged me to allow you to marry her, I knew this would happen. I prepared for it. Weakness from her husband was predictable. So I’ve hardened myself. Your oath must be kept!"

The cold authority in his voice crushed me. It was the voice of power, against which any ordinary resistance would fail. I glanced around the room, imitating what I’d seen him do earlier. Nothing seemed threatening. Yet a new fear crept in—the first real distrust I’d felt—sparked by the way he’d scanned the bizarre, Eastern-style interior of the study. Was it his calm confidence? Or the eerie sensation of being surrounded by Oriental splendor, lit like night despite the daylight outside, all while the city’s noise raged just beyond the walls?

From that moment on, I felt a vague terror—not for myself, but for her. Her voice drifted through the wall, humming a cheerful tune in the next room. I forced myself to stay calm. I even smiled—though I felt the gaze of Evelyn’s painted face watching me. Evelyn, whose portrait I had seen the instant I entered, though I hadn’t looked directly at it. Now I stood with my back to it. Felix faced it, yet refused to raise his eyes. He waited for my reply. When I hesitated, he repeated:

"That oath must be kept."

This time, I found words.

"Impossible!" I shouted, throwing doubt, fear, hesitation—all I’d feared—into the wind. "I took that oath under pressure: family loyalty, our father’s dying breath, a thousand calculated influences designed to trap me. But I can’t keep it after four months of real life with a man thirty years removed from his crime, and with his innocent daughter. You can’t force me to abandon my wife because of a wicked vow made before I knew her. If you think your money—"

"Money?" he laughed, with a contempt that mocked both my sincerity and his own. "I’d forgotten I had any. No, Thomas, I wouldn’t trade money for the chance to live with a woman like your wife. But her life? That’s another matter. Carry out your threat—fail to pay John Poindexter the debt we owe—and things will become serious. You’re unprepared. A daughter dead in her honeymoon would break him as much as one dishonored. And either dead or disgraced, he’ll find her when he comes here searching for the child he can’t forget. Which shall it be? Speak!"

Was I dreaming? Was this really Felix? Was this really me? Were these words truly being spoken?

In a flash, I lunged to his side, where he stood near the table, and groaned, more than screamed:

"You wouldn’t kill her! You don’t mean to commit murder—here—on her—the innocent—the good—"

"No," he said quietly. "It will be you who kills her. You who won’t want to see her suffer—fade—go mad. Thomas, I am not the madman you imagine. I am a keeper of oaths. More than that—I have skill. This house proves it. This room—see? It has no exit except those high windows, barely visible, and that doorway, now covered by a curtain. But in an instant—without moving—I can seal this room so tightly that no man, unless armed with a crowbar and pickaxe, could enter, even if they knew we were trapped. And soon, the entire house will be sealed shut by Bartow, my servant, as he leaves."

"God protect us," I whispered, frozen with horror, my eyes darting from his face to the distant windows, then to the door—just steps away, yet infinitely far from the room where Eva waited.

Felix watched me calmly, explaining as if describing something mundane.

"You’re looking for the windows," he said. "They’re hidden behind the goblin faces on the tapestries overhead. As for the door—if you’d looked to your left when you entered—you’d have seen the edge of a massive steel plate flush with the frame. With a touch of my hand on this button, it slides across the opening instantly. Once moved, nothing—not even I—can push it back. I have power to send it forward, but not to return it. Doubt my mechanical skill? Or the electrical system I installed here? Don’t, Thomas. Nor doubt the will that needs only a second to act. Shall I press the button, brother?"

"No! No!" I cried, overcome by terror—not from his words, but from knowing the man I faced. "Let me think. Tell me what you demand. What will she suffer if I comply? What will I?"

His hand retreated—smooth, white, a hand I’d admired before. Then he smiled.

"I knew you wouldn’t be foolish," he said. "Life has its charms, even for hermits like me. And for a *beau garçon* like you—"

"Hush!" I interrupted, driven by rage. "I’m not buying my life. I’m saving hers—possibly yours too. You’ve planned to die with us. Isn’t that it?"

"Certainly," he replied, coldly. "Am I an assassin? Would you expect me to live, knowing you’re perishing?"

I stared, stunned. Such resolve—such sacrifice for an idea—was beyond comprehension.

"Why—what?" I stammered. "Why kill us? Why kill yourself?"

The answer struck me like thunder.

"Remember Evelyn!" shrieked a voice from above.

I froze, struck dumb by a horror I never thought possible. It wasn’t Felix. It wasn’t my conscience. Muffled, strange, echoing from the vaulted ceiling—lit by that golden glow that would forever mean death to me.

"What is that?" I asked. "Another of your machines?"

He smiled. I wished he’d frowned.

"Not exactly. A starling. My favorite bird. Alas, he repeats what he’s heard—echoes from the silence of these rooms. I thought I’d muffled him well enough. But he’s stubborn. This warns me to hurry. Thomas, this is what I require: John Poindexter—he doesn’t know where he is now, but I do—received a telegram moments ago. If he’s a man at all, he’ll arrive here in half an hour. Sent in your name. It says matters demand his immediate attention. That you’re coming to your brother’s—giving him this address—where, if you gain entry, you’ll wait in the study. But if you’re not home, he’s to wait for a message at the Plaza. The house’s appearance will tell him whether you’re here. That’s what I sent in your name."

"Bartow," he continued, "will leave in five minutes. He’s going on a journey. He expects me to follow. He’ll draw every shade, lock every door. After he leaves, the house becomes a tomb. In two weeks, John Poindexter will return—and discover… well, let’s not rush the future. But Thomas—shall Bartow go? Or shall I signal him, using signs he understands better than speech, to wait and open the door for the man we’ve decided to crush in his moment of pride? You need only write a line. I’ve copied the words you must use—so you don’t lose control. Leave it here on the table. You go. Eva stays. He’ll read it, and I’ll add what’s needed. Have you anything to say against this plan? Anything beyond what you’ve already said?"

I picked up the paper. It lay on the table, clearly written just before or during our arrival. I read the words. You know them. But now they carry a terrible weight:

"You want me to sign this—to write it in my own hand—so it’s mine? Mine!" I repeated.

"Yes. And leave it here. He won’t believe anything I say about your intentions."

Horror swept over me. Love, life, friendship, hope—everything—rushed through me in one overwhelming flood. I could never sign such a blasphemy against all that is sacred.

Tossing the paper at him, I yelled:

"You’ve gone too far! Better her death, better mine, better the end of us all—than dishonor the purest thing heaven ever made. I refuse, Felix—I refuse! May God have mercy on us all!"

The moment was unbearable. I saw his face change, his finger tremble over the fatal button. I imagined the steel plate sliding forward—felt the doom closing in—and reached for him, ready to fight, when a sound from another direction caught my ear.

Turning in terror, I saw Eva stepping out of the room where we’d left her.

A deep red light replaced the sickly yellow that had filled the room. But I barely noticed—only that it gave her cheeks a color the roses at her belt lacked.

Fearless, sweet as when she first told me she loved me, she approached us.

"What is this?" she demanded. "I’ve heard words like a nightmare. What does it mean, Thomas? What does it mean, Mr. ——"

"Cadwalader," Felix said, lowering his eyes but not changing his expression.

"Cadwalader?" she echoed. "I’ve heard that name. It was Evelyn’s name—the Evelyn who—"

"Whom you see painted above you," Felix finished. "My sister. Thomas’s sister. The girl your father—well, I spare you, though you’re the child of a man who spared nothing. From your husband, you may learn why a Cadwalader can never be happy with a Poindexter. Why, thirty years after that girl’s death, you—who weren’t born then—are given the choice between death and shame. I give you five minutes to listen. Then you’ll tell me your decision. But stay where you are. One step—either of you—and Thomas knows what follows."

Five minutes. To explain, to decide. I felt dizzy, speechless. But seeing her eyes—raised in silent trust—filled me with strength. I burst into words—excuses, explanations, everything a man might say in such a crisis.

She listened, understanding more from my face than my words. As the minutes passed, and Felix raised a warning hand, she turned to him and said:

"You’re serious? We must part in shame—or die in this prison with you?"

His answer was flat, mechanical, but firm:

"You’ve said it. You have one minute left, madam."

She shrank. Then, a sudden surge of courage—she became a blazing angel where a fragile woman had stood. She glanced at me, testing if I had the strength to strike my own blood. (Alas, I didn’t understand.) Then, grabbing an old Turkish dagger lying nearby, she plunged it sideways into his side, shouting:

"We cannot part—we cannot die—we are too young, too happy!"

It was swift. A single act of a woman shattered the most carefully laid plans of a mind that had mastered every contingency.

But I didn’t see that then. I didn’t think of our escape, or the cost Eva might pay. My world was Felix—whose near-death had awakened the full strength of his spirit.

Though wounded, he didn’t fall. It was as if his will—forged in thirty years of torment—held him upright so he could give Eva one last look—a look I’d never seen, and never will forget. Then, seeing her collapse, her delicate form weakening, he turned to me—and I saw—God help me—a tear rise from his stone-like eyes and fall slowly down his cheek, hollowing under death’s blow.

"Eva! Eva! I love Eva!" shrieked the voice from the vault above.

Felix heard. A faint smile touched his lips—a revelation to me. He loved Eva too.

When he saw I understood, his will failed. He sank to the floor, whispering:

"Take her away. I forgive. Save! Save! She didn’t know I loved her."

Eva, horrified, staring at her deed, didn’t move. But when she saw his head fall back, his limbs grow still, a shock ran through her—so intense I feared it would shatter her mind.

"I’ve killed him!" she murmured. "I’ve killed him!" Her eyes darted wildly, landing on the cross hanging on the wall behind us. It reminded her—perhaps—that Felix was Catholic. "Bring it!" she gasped. "Put it on his chest. It may bring him peace—hope."

As I rushed to obey, she collapsed.

"Save!" came again from lips we thought sealed forever.

I realized her danger—and the need to shield her from this horror. I placed the cross in his hands, lifted her from the floor, and ran out of the house.

But as I saw the busy street, the crowd rushing by, I woke to our peril. I set her down, revived her with my own strength, dragged her down the steps, merged into the throng, breathing for her, living in her until we reached a carriage and fled.

For the silence we’ve kept since, don’t blame Mrs. Adams. When she regained consciousness—days later—she wanted to confess, to take responsibility. But I wouldn’t allow it. I loved her too much to see her name in the papers. When her father learned the truth, he insisted on silence, sharing my duty to guard her.

But alas—his silence came from pride, not love. John Poindexter cares nothing for his daughter, no more than he did for his wife or the long-forgotten child whose grave sparked this tragedy. Had Felix won, he would never have broken that man’s heart. As he once said: when a man cares for nothing, not even himself, there’s no point in cursing him.
