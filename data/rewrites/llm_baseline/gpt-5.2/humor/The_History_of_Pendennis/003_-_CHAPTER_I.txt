One bright morning in the full London season—when the sparrows in Pall Mall must learn to chirp in the key of carriages, and even the wind sounds as if it wore kid gloves—Major Arthur Pendennis came from his lodgings, as punctually as a kettle that has been threatened, to breakfast at his Club.

The Club, being a sort of domestic establishment for men who prefer their homes public and their servants invisible, received him as it always did: with that soft, cushioned hush of feet on thick carpet, of silver laid down without clink, of doors closing as though afraid to disturb important digestion. The waiters bowed before him with the reverence usually reserved for Dukes, Field-Marshals, and persons who can detect a bad bottle at twenty paces.

At a quarter past ten—never at ten, never at half-past, because the Major was the kind of man who believed Time itself ought to keep appointments—he appeared in boots so superbly blacked that they might have been roasted, glazed, and served. His morning cravat sat in crisp checks like a well-pressed napkin that would not consent to be rumpled until dinner. His buff waistcoat carried the crown on its buttons as if the sovereign’s head had taken up residence there; and his linen was so spotless you could almost smell the starch, a clean, dry, papery perfume that suggested both virtue and a very expensive laundress. His whiskers were trimmed as accurately as a border of herbs; his cane looked as if it had been polished with the same devotion one gives to a prized copper saucepan.

From behind, you might have taken him for thirty. From the front, the illusion required more charity: the hair was rich brown with that particular richness which comes from art rather than Providence, and there were crow’s-feet about eyes that had once been brighter, and now looked slightly faded—as if too many chandeliers had shone on them. His nose had the Wellington air of having won battles on principle. His hands were long, pale, and confident, and his wristbands fastened with handsome gold buttons, a gift from the Duke of York—proof that Royalty, like good butter, improves anything it touches. Rings glittered on his fingers, the chief one engraved with the arms of Pendennis, as though the family crest needed constant reminding not to slip off.

He always took possession of the same table in the same corner—by the fire, yet near the window; a station chosen with the strategic genius of a man who had never besieged anything more perilous than a decanter. No one thought now of ousting him. In former days, one or two mad wags had tried it—young fellows who imagined courage consisted in sitting where another man had always sat. But there was a quiet dignity in the Major’s way of taking the next table and simply looking at them—an unspoken, steady gaze like cold gravy setting—that made it impossible to swallow toast under his eye. The interlopers fled; the corner belonged to him by right of long use, polished boots, and social terror.

His letters were already laid out there, as if the post had been trained to obey his habits. Many a young gentleman about town stared, half envious, half hungry, at the number of notes, at the seals and franks—the very wax seeming to exhale perfume and privilege. If you had a question about etiquette, society, who was married to whom, how old a duke really was in private, Major Pendennis was the oracle to consult. Marchionesses drove up and left notes for him, or fetched him out, as one might send for a physician—only that the Major’s remedies were smiles, introductions, and a touch of the hat at precisely the proper angle.

He seated himself, and while the waiters went to bring his toast and his hot newspaper (hot enough to warm the hands and the conscience), he surveyed his correspondence through a gold double eye-glass. He flourished it so jauntily you might not have known it was spectacles in disguise; he examined one pretty note after another, and laid them aside in order, as if arranging courses for a dinner.

There were large, solemn dinner cards suggestive of heavy soups, heavier conversation, and men who talk as if they are chewing. There were neat little confidential notes, with feminine entreaties folded into them like secrets inside a pastry. There was an invitation on thick official paper from the Marquis of Steyne—come to Richmond, to a little party at the Star and Garter, and speak French; the Major spoke French as neatly as he tied a neckcloth, and with as much satisfaction to himself. There was another from the Bishop of Ealing and Mrs. Trail, requesting the honour of Major Pendennis’s company at Ealing House—an engagement he liked, though bishop’s dinners were apt to proceed with the cautious tread of a sermon. He read them all gracefully, and with added relish because Mr. Glowry, the Scotch surgeon, sat opposite eating his breakfast and hating him.

Glowry’s hatred was of the quiet, nutritious kind: it sustained itself on small daily portions. He watched the seals and franks, watched the Major’s easy air of being wanted everywhere, and chewed in the gloom of a man who receives no notes except bills.

The Major, having tasted his importance, took out his pocket-book and began to decide—like a steward planning a week’s provisions—where to expend his presence. He threw over Cutler, the East India Director in Baker Street, in order to dine with Lord Steyne and the French party at the Star and Garter. He accepted the Bishop because—though the dinner might be slow—he liked to dine with bishops. So he went through his list, accepting and declining as fancy and interest dictated, and then turned to breakfast in earnest: toast properly done, not burned into bitterness nor left pale and flabby; butter that spread without sulking; coffee whose aroma rose in a dark, comforting cloud, mingling with the room’s gentle soundscape—murmured greetings, the soft scrape of chair legs, the discreet clatter of crockery carefully trained not to clatter.

Outside, London roared in its usual iron-and-leather way; inside, the Club subdued noise into a kind of gentlemanly purr. It was a refuge from the city’s grinding wheels—a place where even news arrived “hot” but never loud. The Major unfolded the paper, glanced at the gazette, the births and deaths, the fashionable intelligence—making sure his name was present where it should be, like a seasoning that must appear in every respectable dish.

Among all the notes in his morning budget there remained only one unread: it lay solitary and apart, as if it had walked in from muddy lanes and felt shy among all these crested envelopes. It had a country postmark and a homely seal. The superscription was in a delicate female hand; it was marked “Immediate” with a strong dash of anxiety under the word—an underline like a knife-score on a chopping board.

Yet the Major, for reasons of his own, had left this humble rural petitioner to the last. In the antechamber under the slop-bason, as it were, the country letter waited while the grandees of London were admitted and dismissed.

At last it came to be its turn. He broke the seal—“Fairoaks” engraved upon it; “Clavering St. Mary’s” for a postmark—and, being a man who could prolong pleasure or postponement as he pleased, he began by perusing the envelope before he attacked the inner epistle.

“Is it a letter from another Jook?” growled Mr. Glowry inwardly. “Pendennis would not be leaving that to the last, I’m thinking.”

“My dear Major Pendennis,” the letter ran, “I beg and implore you to come to me immediately—”

Very likely, thought Pendennis; and Steyne’s dinner to-day.

“I am in the very greatest grief and perplexity. My dearest boy, who has been hitherto everything the fondest mother could wish, is grieving me dreadfully. He has formed—I can hardly write it—a passion, an infatuation—”

The Major’s mouth twitched into a grin at the words, as if “infatuation” were an over-sweet pudding served by a sentimental cook.

“—for an actress who has been performing here. She is at least twelve years older than Arthur—who will not be eighteen till next February—and the wretched boy insists upon marrying her.”

Whatever sound the Major made at that moment was not one the Club encouraged. Rage and astonishment gathered in his open mouth so visibly that Glowry, alarmed by the look of a man about to burst, felt in his pocket for the lancet he always carried in his card-case. Pendennis’s face had the expression of a person who has bitten unexpectedly on grit in a dish he ordered expensive.

“Hay! What’s making Pendennis swear now?” Glowry asked himself, with the secret hope that it was something irreparable.

“Do, my dear friend,” the grief-stricken lady went on, “come to me instantly on the receipt of this; and, as Arthur’s guardian, entreat, command, the wretched child to give up this most deplorable resolution.”

More entreaties followed, poured out with the helpless urgency of a mother who has discovered that love, like yeast, rises best when you least want it to. The writer ended by signing herself his “unhappy affectionate sister, Helen Pendennis.”

“Fairoaks, Tuesday,” the Major muttered as he read the last words, as if the date itself were an insult. “A d——d pretty business at Fairoaks, Tuesday. Now let us see what the boy has to say.”

He took up the other letter. It was written in a great floundering boy’s hand, sealed with the large signet of the Pendennises—larger even than the Major’s own—and with supplementary wax sputtered all round, like sauce spilled in excitement: unmistakable signs of tremulousness and agitation.

It ran:

“FAIROAKS, Monday, Midnight.

“MY DEAR UNCLE, In informing you of my engagement with Miss Costigan, daughter of J. Chesterfield Costigan, Esq., of Costiganstown, but, perhaps, better known to you under her professional name of Miss Fotheringay, of the Theatres Royal Drury Lane and Crow Street, and of the Norwich and Welsh Circuit, I am aware that I make an announcement which cannot, according to the present prejudices of society at least, be welcome to my family.

“My dearest mother, on whom, God knows, I would wish to inflict no needless pain, is deeply moved and grieved, I am sorry to say, by the intelligence which I have this night conveyed to her. I beseech you, my dear Sir, to come down and reason with her and console her.

“Although obliged by poverty to earn an honourable maintenance by the exercise of her splendid talents, Miss Costigan’s family is as ancient and noble as our own. When our ancestor, Ralph Pendennis, landed with Richard II. in Ireland, my Emily’s forefathers were kings of that country. I have the information from Mr. Costigan, who, like yourself, is a military man.

“It is in vain I have attempted to argue with my dear mother, and prove to her that a young lady of irreproachable character and lineage, endowed with the most splendid gifts of beauty and genius, who devotes herself to the exercise of one of the noblest professions, for the sacred purpose of maintaining her family, is a being whom we should all love and reverence, rather than avoid;—my poor mother has prejudices which it is impossible for my logic to overcome, and refuses to welcome to her arms one who is disposed to be her most affectionate daughter through life.

“Although Miss Costigan is some years older than myself, that circumstance does not operate as a barrier to my affection, and I am sure will not influence its duration. A love like mine, Sir, I feel, is contracted once and for ever. As I never had dreamed of love until I saw her—I feel now that I shall die without ever knowing another passion. It is the fate of my life. It was Miss C.’s own delicacy which suggested that the difference of age, which I never felt, might operate as a bar to our union. But having loved once, I should despise myself, and be unworthy of my name as a gentleman, if I hesitated to abide by my passion: if I did not give all where I felt all, and endow the woman who loves me fondly with my whole heart and my whole fortune.

“I press for a speedy marriage with my Emily—for why, in truth, should it be delayed? A delay implies a doubt, which I cast from me as unworthy. It is impossible that my sentiments can change towards Emily—that at any age she can be anything but the sole object of my love. Why, then, wait?

“I entreat you, my dear Uncle, to come down and reconcile my dear mother to our union, and I address you as a man of the world, qui mores hominum multorum vidit et urbes, who will not feel any of the weak scruples and fears which agitate a lady who has scarcely ever left her village.

“Pray, come down to us immediately. I am quite confident that—apart from considerations of fortune—you will admire and approve of my Emily.

“Your affectionate Nephew,

“ARTHUR PENDENNIS, JR.”

When the Major came to the end, his face took on such a look of outrage and horror that Glowry, still fingering his lancet, believed for a moment that Fashion itself had struck Pendennis down. The intelligence was enough to agitate any guardian who lived by the favourable opinion of other men: the head of the Pendennises going to marry an actress; a headstrong boy plunging into matrimony with the rashness of someone who thinks passion is a permanent income.

“The mother has spoiled the young rascal,” groaned the Major inwardly, with that special bitterness one reserves for other people’s parenting. “With her cursed sentimentality and romantic rubbish. My nephew marry a tragedy queen! Gracious mercy, people will laugh at me so that I shall not dare show my head!”

And in the midst of his moral indignation—like pepper in a dish whose principal ingredient is self—there stung him an inexpressible pang: Lord Steyne’s dinner at Richmond would be lost. The Star and Garter, the French talk, the select society, the agreeable evening he had promised himself—gone. In their place: a night without rest, wedged into an abominable tight mail-coach, rattling through darkness as if the road were determined to saw him into pieces.

Worse yet, this single foolish romance at Fairoaks might swallow not only one dinner, but a whole menu of engagements. Who knew how long it would detain him? Love, in provincial households, had a way of boiling over and sticking to everything.

He quitted his breakfast-table for the adjoining writing-room, and there, with the rueful air of a man canceling a feast because someone at home has upset the larder, he wrote refusals to the Marquis, the Earl, the Bishop, and all his entertainers. The very scratch of his pen seemed louder in that quieter room, a thin, resentful sound—like the last crisp edge of toast being broken in two.

Then he ordered his servant to take places in the mail-coach for that evening—charging, of course, the sum he disbursed to the account of the widow and the young scapegrace of whom he was guardian; for if Romance must be paid for, the Major intended it should never come out of the portion reserved for his own comfort.
