Like the division of the Promised Land among the heirs of Art, every claimant must fight hard to win his share; the promise itself arrives like a prophecy—a title held without possession. Carrying a map of an estate you do not yet own in your pocket is merely a precarious form of tenure. To imagine casting one's shoe over Eden offers no guarantee that one will ever set the sole of one's foot upon a single acre of it.

The most stubborn beliefs humans hold about themselves are those supported by no evidence other than a constant, spontaneous throbbing of self-satisfaction. It is akin to a hidden seed of madness: a confident delusion that one can move the world without understanding where one stands or what leverage one possesses.

"Please go to church, Mamma," Gwendolen said the following morning. "I prefer seeing Herr Klesmer alone." (He had replied to her note stating he would meet her at eleven.)

"That seems hardly proper, I think," Mrs. Davilow said, her voice tinged with anxiety.

"Our affairs are too serious for us to concern ourselves with such nonsensical rules," Gwendolen retorted, her tone dripping with contempt. "They are insulting as well as ridiculous."

"You wouldn't mind Isabel sitting with you? She could read quietly in a corner."

"No; she couldn't. She'd bite her nails and stare. It would be too irritating. Trust my judgment, Mamma; I must be alone. Take everyone else to church."

As usual, Gwendolen got her way. Only Miss Merry and two of the younger girls remained behind, staying inside to give the house the appearance of habitation by sitting at the dining-room windows.

It was a delicious Sunday morning. The melancholy, fading sunlight of autumn rested upon the half-mown grass and filtered gently through the windows in slanting bands of brightness. These beams illuminated the old furniture, the glass panel reflecting it, the tapestried chairs adorned with faded floral wreaths, the dark, enigmatic paintings, and the superannuated organ at which Gwendolen had delighted in playing Saint Cecilia upon her first joyful arrival. Through the open doors of the antechamber, a crowd of pallid, dusty knick-knacks was visible—the very spot where she had once achieved the role of Hermione in her Greek dress. This memory was vividly active in her mind now; surely Herr Klesmer had been struck by admiration for her pose and expression then? Whatever he had actually said, or whatever she imagined he had thought, was currently the object of her keenest scrutiny. Perhaps she had never in her life felt so inwardly dependent, so acutely aware of needing another person's opinion. A new fluttering stirred within her spirit, introducing an element of deliberation into her self-assessment that had previously been a blissful, intuitive gift. Yet, the recurring burden of her inner monologue remained: *Herr Klesmer had seen very little of me.* Any unfavorable conclusion he reached would necessarily rest on too narrow a foundation. She truly felt clever enough for anything.

To pass the time, she gathered her volumes and musical scores, placing them atop the piano, and set herself to classifying them. Catching the reflection of her movements in the glass panel, she was diverted to contemplating her own image. Dressed entirely in black, devoid of ornament, with the warm whiteness of her skin highlighted between her light-brown crown of hair and her square-cut bodice, she might have tempted an artist to attempt the Roman trick of a statue carved in black, white, and tawny marble. Watching her reflection advance slowly, she thought, *"I am beautiful"*—not with exultation, but with grave decision. Beauty, after all, was the condition upon which she most desperately craved external validation. If anyone objected to the turn of her nose or the shape of her neck and chin, she lacked the sense that she could immediately demonstrate her power of attainment in these branches of feminine perfection.

There was little time left to fill before the sound of wheels, the loud ringing of a bell, and the opening of doors assured her that she was not, by any accident, to be disappointed. This slight increase in her internal flutter served only to heighten her dread. Despite her self-confidence, she feared Klesmer as part of that unmanageable world independent of her wishes—something vitriolic that would not cease burning simply because one smiled or frowned at it. Poor creature! She was facing a higher crisis in her woman's fate than in her last experience with Grandcourt. Then, the question had been whether she should marry a particular man. Now, the deepest fold of her questioning was whether she needed to marry at all—whether she could not achieve substantial independence and gratified ambition without bondage.

Herr Klesmer made his most deferential bow in the wide doorway of the antechamber, displaying the deference of the finest gray kerseymere trousers and perfect gloves (for the "masters of those who know" are, happily, altogether human). Gwendolen met him with unusual gravity, extending her hand. "It is most kind of you to come, Herr Klesmer," she said. "I hope you have not thought me presumptuous."

"I took your wish as a command that did me honor," Klesmer replied with matching gravity. He was indeed setting aside his own affairs to give his utmost attention to whatever Gwendolen had to say; yet his temperament was still in a state of excitation from the events of the previous day, likely lending his expressions a sharper, more biting edge than usual.

For once, Gwendolen was under such a strain of feeling that she forgot formalities. Standing near the piano, she waited, and Klesmer took his position near the opposite end, his back to the light and his terribly omniscient eyes fixed upon her. No affectation would serve, and she began without delay.

"I wish to consult you, Herr Klesmer. We have lost all our fortune; we have nothing. I must earn my own bread, and I desire to provide for my mother so as to spare her any hardship. The only way I can think of—and I should like it better than anything else—is to become an actress—to go on the stage. But, of course, I would aspire to a high position. Therefore, I thought—if you believed I could"—here Gwendolen grew slightly nervous—"it would be preferable for me to be a singer. I wish to study singing as well."

Klesmer placed his hat upon the piano and folded his arms as if concentrating intensely.

"I know," Gwendolen resumed, flushing from pale to pink and back again—"I know that my method of singing is very defective; but I have been ill-taught. I could be better taught; I could study. And you will understand my wish: to sing and act, like Grisi, represents a much higher station. Naturally, I wish to attain the highest rank possible. I can rely on your judgment. I am sure you will tell me the truth."

Gwendolen possessed a strange conviction that now, in making this serious appeal, the truth would prove favorable.

Yet Klesmer remained silent. He quickly removed his gloves, tossed them into his hat, rested his hands on his hips, and walked to the other end of the room. He was filled with compassion for this girl and wanted to guard his speech carefully. When he turned back, he looked at her with a mild, inquiring frown and spoke with gentle but rapid utterance: "You have never seen anything, I think, of artists and their lives?—I mean musicians, actors, artists of that kind?"

"Oh, no," Gwendolen replied, unperturbed by a reference to this obvious fact in the history of a young lady who had hitherto been well-provided for.

"You are—in pardon me," Klesmer said again, pausing near the piano—"coming to a conclusion on such a matter as this without considering everything. Are you perhaps twenty?"

"I am twenty-one," Gwendolen said, a slight fear rising within her. "Do you think I am too old?"

Klesmer pursed his lower lip and shook his long fingers upward in a manner totally enigmatic.

"Many persons begin later than others," Gwendolen remarked, betrayed by her habitual consciousness of possessing valuable information to bestow.

Klesmer paid her no heed, instead speaking with even greater studied gentleness: "You have probably not considered an artistic career until now. You did not entertain the notion, the longing—what shall I say?—you did not wish to be an actress, or anything of that sort, until this present trouble arose?"

"Not exactly: but I was fond of acting. I have acted; you saw me, if you remember—you saw me here in charades, and as Hermione," Gwendolen said, genuinely fearing that Klesmer had forgotten.

"Yes, yes," he answered quickly, "I remember—I remember perfectly," and once more walked to the other end of the room. It was difficult for him to refrain from this peculiar movement whenever he was engaged in any argument, audible or silent.

Gwendolen felt that she was being weighed. The delay was unpleasant, but she did not yet conceive that the scales could tip against her. It seemed only graceful to say, "I shall be very much obliged to you for taking the trouble to give me your advice, whatever it may be."
