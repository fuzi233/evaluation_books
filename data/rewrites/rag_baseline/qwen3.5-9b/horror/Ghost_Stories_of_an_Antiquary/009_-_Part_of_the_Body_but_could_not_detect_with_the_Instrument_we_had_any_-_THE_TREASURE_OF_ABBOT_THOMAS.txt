"Verum usque in præsentem diem..."

The Latin flowed across the page, dense and archaic, a testament to the obscurity of the source material. The antiquary paused, his pen hovering over the parchment. He was translating a passage from the *Sertum Steinfeldense Norbertinum*, a rare chronicle of the Premonstratensian abbey in the Eifel region, originally published in Cologne in 1712.

"To the present day," the translation read, "much gossip persists among the Canons regarding a certain hidden treasure of this Abbot Thomas, for which the men of Steinfeld have often searched, though hitherto in vain. The story holds that Thomas, while yet in the vigor of life, concealed a vast quantity of gold somewhere within the monastery. When repeatedly questioned as to its location, he would invariably answer with a laugh: 'Job, John, and Zechariah will tell either you or your successors.' On occasion, he added that he bore no ill will toward those who might eventually uncover it."

The antiquary, Mr. Somerton, sat in the quiet study of his Berkshire manor, the candlelight casting long, dancing shadows against the walls. He was a man of leisure, accustomed to the slow burn of intellectual pursuit, but the passage before him sparked a sudden, sharp interest. Beyond the tale of the gold, the text detailed Abbot Thomas's contributions: the restoration of the abbey lodgings, the digging of a marble-adorned well, and, most notably, the installation of a magnificent stained-glass window in the south aisle of the church. This window featured figures painted with admirable skill, including the Abbot's own effigy and heraldic arms.

Somerton had spent years tracking down fragments of medieval glass from the dissolved abbeys of Germany and Belgium, pieces that now adorned English cathedrals and private chapels. Steinfeld Abbey had been a significant contributor to this trade. While identifying the glass was often straightforward—via inscriptions or recognizable narrative cycles—the current specimen posed a unique challenge.

In a private chapel, Somerton had encountered three large figures, each occupying a full light of a window. They were unmistakably the work of a sixteenth-century German artist. Yet, their grouping was baffling. They depicted JOB PATRIARCHA, JOHANNES EVANGELISTA, and ZACHARIAS PROPHETA. Each held a scroll or book inscribed with a specific text. As Somerton examined the transcriptions, a peculiar discrepancy emerged. The scroll in Job's hand bore the inscription: *Auro est locus in quo absconditur*—"There is a place for gold where it is hidden"—rather than the standard Vulgate reading. John's book read: *Habent in vestimentis suis scripturam quam nemo novit*—"They have on their garments a writing which no man knows," borrowing phrasing from a different verse entirely. Only Zacharias retained an unaltered text: *Super lapidem unum septem oculi sunt*—"Upon one stone are seven eyes."

The connection remained elusive until the discovery of the *Sertum*. The names Job, John, and Zechariah had been constants in Abbot Thomas's mouth, and the window described in the chronicle matched the style and era of the glass Somerton possessed. It was a logical leap, one that demanded verification. With characteristic resolve, Somerton packed his bags, abandoning his leisurely routine for a pilgrimage to the obscure chapel.

His hypothesis proved correct. The craftsmanship aligned perfectly with the late fifteenth or early sixteenth century, and in a separate pane, he found the arms of Abbot Thomas von Eschenhausen. The pieces fit. But as he reviewed every facet of the window, noting the inscriptions with scrupulous care, a darker thread began to weave itself into the tapestry. The first inscription, *Auro est locus in quo absconditur*, was not merely poetic; it was a direct reference to hidden wealth. The Abbot hadn't just commissioned art; he had embedded a cipher.

For weeks, Somerton worked by the light of midnight oil, sketching tracings and cross-referencing texts. The solution crystallized with terrifying clarity: the secret wasn't in the ground, but in the glass. The Abbot had set a riddle for posterity, hiding the key to the treasure within the very images he had created. Satisfied that the trail was hot, Somerton prepared to depart for the continent, leaving his man to pack his belongings for a short journey abroad.

The morning sun filtered through the high windows of the rectory at Parsbury, illuminating dust motes dancing in the air. Mr. Gregory, the Rector, stepped onto the gravel drive before breakfast, intending to catch the postman and breathe in the crisp autumn air. He was not disappointed. Before he could field more than a dozen queries from his energetic children, the postman appeared, laden with the day's mail.

Amongst the correspondence was a single envelope bearing a foreign postmark and stamp, instantly sparking a competitive frenzy among the young Gregorys. Inside, however, lay a letter written in an uneducated but distinctly English hand, signed by William Brown, the confidential valet of Mr. Somerton.

"HONOURED SIR," the missive began, its spelling fractured by haste and distress. "Has I am in a great anxiety about Master I write at is Wish to beg you Sir if you could be so good as Step over. Master Has add a Nastey Shock and keeps His Bedd. I never Have known Him like this but No wonder and Nothing will serve but you Sir. Master says would I mintion the Short Way Here is Drive to Cobblince and take a Trap. Hopeing I Have maid all Plain, but am much Confused in Myself what with Anxiatey and Weakfulness at Night. If I might be so Bold Sir it will be a Pleasure to see a Honnest Brish Face among all These Forig ones. I am Sir Your obedt Servt William Brown."

A postscript clarified the destination: "P.S.—The Village for Town I will not Turm. It is name Steenfeld."

The impact of the letter on the quiet Berkshire parsonage was immediate and profound. By midday, Mr. Gregory had secured passage to Antwerp and a berth on the steamer to Coblentz. From there, the transit to Steinfeld was manageable, though the destination itself remained a mystery. As the narrator later admitted, he had never visited the place, nor could either Somerton or Gregory provide a vivid description. It was a small settlement dominated by a large, stripped church and surrounded by ruinous seventeenth-century structures, remnants of a once-luxurious abbey reconstruction.

Gregory arrived at the sole inn in the village, where Mr. Brown awaited him. The valet, typically the epitome of impassive efficiency in his native Berkshire, was now a study in disarray. Clad in a light tweed suit that seemed utterly inappropriate for the setting, he appeared anxious, irritable, and completely out of his depth. Yet, upon seeing the familiar, honest countenance of the Rector, his relief was palpable, though he struggled to articulate it.

"Well, I ham pleased, I'm sure, sir, to see you. And so I'm sure, sir, will master."

"How _is_ your master, Brown?" Gregory asked, his concern evident.

"I think he's better, sir, thank you; but he's had a dreadful time of it. I 'ope he's gettin' some sleep now, but—"

"What has been the matter?" Gregory pressed. "Was it an accident?"

"It's complicated, sir. Master was very particular that I should be the one to tell you. But there's no bones broken—that's one thing we ought to be thankful for—"

Before Brown could elaborate further, they moved toward the bedroom door. Gregory reached for the handle, his fingers brushing the wood paneling. Suddenly, a terrible cry erupted from within.

"In God's name, who is that?" the voice rang out, trembling with panic. "Brown, is it?"

"Yes, sir—me, sir, and Mr. Gregory," Brown stammered, his voice cracking.

An audible groan of relief followed. They pushed the door open to reveal a room dimmed against the afternoon sun. Sitting upright in the curtained bed, Mr. Somerton looked gaunt, his face pale and damp with sweat. He stretched out a shaking hand toward Gregory.

"Better for seeing you, my dear Gregory," he rasped, his voice thin but recovering strength.

Five minutes of conversation stabilized him sufficiently. Somerton ate a substantial dinner and declared himself fit to travel to Coblentz within twenty-four hours. However, a new urgency seized him.

"But there's one thing," he interrupted, his agitation flaring again. "Which I must beg you to do for me, my dear Gregory. Don't," he commanded, seizing Gregory's hand to forestall any inquiry, "don't ask me what it is, or why I want it done. I'm not up to explaining it yet; it would throw me back—undo all the good you have done me by coming. The only word I will say about it is that you run no risk whatever by doing it, and that Brown can and will show you tomorrow what it is. It's merely to put back—to keep—something—No; I can't speak of it yet. Do you mind calling Brown?"
