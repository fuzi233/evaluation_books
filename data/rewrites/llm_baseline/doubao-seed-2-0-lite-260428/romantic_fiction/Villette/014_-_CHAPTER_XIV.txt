No sooner was little Georgette fully recovered than Madame Beck sent her off to stay with relatives in the country, and I felt a quiet, hollow little loss settle in my chest where the child’s bright chatter had once been. I did not complain, though. The school hummed with robust, thrumming young life all around me; any of the other teachers might have been my companion, but I had long since chosen solitude over forced, half-hearted intimacy. I tried each of their proffered friendships in turn. The first was honest enough, but narrow of mind, coarse of feeling, and entirely wrapped up in her own small concerns. The second, Mademoiselle St. Pierre, the Parisienne, was polished and soft-spoken on the surface, but at her core corrupt: no creed, no fixed principle, no deep affection anchored her. Scratch through her thin crust of decorum, and you found a stagnant, greedy slough beneath. She had an insatiable hunger for gifts, and the third teacher — a quiet, unremarkable creature with little personality to mark her — shared that vice, plus one far stranger for a girl not yet twenty-five: a rabid avarice, a love of money for money’s own sake. The sight of a single gold coin would cast a strange green glint in her eyes, a sight I never forgot. Once, as a special mark of favour, she dragged me up to her garret room, unlocked a hidden cupboard, and showed me her hoard: a great pile of heavy five-franc pieces, some fifteen guineas in all. She loved that little stack of coin as a bird loves its nest of eggs, and would come to me day after day, chattering about her savings with a besotted, doting devotion that felt almost unearthly in one so young.

Mademoiselle St. Pierre, by contrast, was free-spending, reckless in her appetites if not her actions — that dark, secret snake side of hers only peeked out once, very cautiously, from between the pages of a tattered bad novel. I glimpsed just a sliver of its scaly hide, and my hasty flash of sharp wrath sent it recoiling, hissing, back into hiding, and she hated me from that hour forward. She was perpetually in debt, her salary spent months in advance on silken gowns, rare perfumes, fancy confections, and imported condiments. She was the coldest, most callous epicure I ever met: thin of face and figure, sallow of cheek, her features perfectly regular, her teeth white as pearls, her lips a thin sharp line, her chin prominent, and her wide, pale eyes open and frozen, craving pleasure but never grateful for the enjoyment of it. She loathed work, and worshipped what she called “pleasure”: a dull, heartless, brainless dissipation of hours that brought no real warmth to anyone.

Madame Beck knew her character perfectly, of course. She once spoke of her to me, with that odd mix of sharp discernment, total indifference, and quiet antipathy that was so uniquely hers. When I asked why she kept such a woman on staff, she answered plainly: “Because it suits my interests.” She pointed out what I had already observed: Mademoiselle St. Pierre possessed an almost unrivalled gift for keeping order among the most wild, undisciplined ranks of students. A sort of petrifying stillness hung about her; no shouting, no violence, no heated temper, and yet she held the whole crowd in check as a breathless frost stills a brawling, churning stream. She was of little use for teaching actual lessons, but for strict surveillance and upholding rules she was invaluable. “I know full well she has no principles, and perhaps no morals,” Madame admitted frankly, then shrugged philosophically. “But her conduct in the classroom is always proper, even marked with a certain dignity. That is all that matters. The pupils and the parents never look any deeper — and why should I?”

It was a strange, frolicsome, noisy little world, that school: great pains were taken to hide the iron chains of routine under garlands of flowers, a subtle undercurrent of Romanism threading every arrangement. Large, soft physical indulgences were permitted as deliberate counterweight to the jealous, tight spiritual restraints that bound every soul within its walls. Every young mind was being raised in quiet slavery, but to keep anyone from stopping to notice that fact, every possible pretext for games, walks, and recreation was seized and stretched to its fullest. The Church strove there, as elsewhere, to raise her children robust of body, feeble of soul: plump, ruddy, hale, giddy, ignorant, unthinking, and uncomplaining. *Eat, drink, and be merry*, she seemed to say. Tend to your bodies. Leave your souls to me. I hold their cure, I guide their course, I guarantee their final fate. It is a bargain every devout Catholic counts himself the gainer by. Lucifer offers the very same terms.

It was the peak of summer, the air thick and honeyed with golden light, when Madame Beck’s house grew as merry as a boarding school could ever be. All day long the broad folding doors and two-leaved casements stood propped wide open; steady, unbroken sunshine seemed to have settled permanently into the air, while clouds drifted far off across the sea, hanging over that dear, mist-kissed isle of my home, and entirely gone from the drier, brighter continent. We lived more in the garden than under roofs: classes were held out under the great leafy arbor, meals were spread there too, and there was a hum of holiday preparation that nearly tipped the usual rules into total liberty. The long autumn vacation was still two months off, but long before that a great, important day awaited celebration: Madame’s own annual fête.

All the arrangements for the fête fell to Mademoiselle St. Pierre, while Madame herself affected to stand aloof, disinterested and entirely unaware of the honour being planned for her. She never knew, or so the polite fiction went, that every year a small subscription was quietly levied across the whole school to buy her a handsome gift. The reader will be so kind as to pretend he never overhears the short, secret consultation that took place in Madame’s own private chamber one afternoon.

“What would you like this year?” her Parisian lieutenant asked her.

“Oh, never mind all that. Let it be. Let the poor children keep their francs,” Madame said, her face soft and benign, all modesty.

Mademoiselle St. Pierre stuck her chin out, rolling her eyes. She knew Madame’s little acts of “kindness” for the grimaces they were, and never for a moment pretended to respect them. “Quick,” she said, coldly. “Name what you want. Jewellery? Porcelain? Haberdashery? Silver?”

“Ah, very well. Two or three spoons, and the same number of forks, in solid silver.”

And the end result was a handsome engraved case, holding three hundred francs’ worth of fine polished plate.

The fête programme was full: the presentation of the silver, a lavish collation spread in the garden, a dramatic performance with pupils and teachers as the actors, then a dance and a late supper. It seemed to me, then, a dazzling, almost magical spectacle, and Mademoiselle St. Pierre managed every detail with perfect, polished skill.

The play was the centrepiece, and required a full month of prior drilling. Casting the roles demanded careful judgement, then lessons in elocution, posture, then the endless fatigue of dozens of rehearsals. Mademoiselle St. Pierre could not handle all that, of course; the flair and knowledge required belonged to another man entirely: M. Paul Emanuel, the fiery little professor of literature. I never once sat in on his dramatic lessons, but I saw him constantly striding across the square hall that linked the dwelling house to the school, and heard his voice ringing through the open doors on warm summer evenings. His name and endless anecdotes of his temper were on every lip. Most of all, our old acquaintance Ginevra Fanshawe, who had been cast in a prominent role in the play, spent nearly all her free time with me, larding every line of her chatter with tales of his odd sayings and wild doings. She swore he was hideously plain, and claimed she was nearly thrown into hysterics at the sound of his heavy step or sharp voice. He was certainly a small, dark man, pungent and austere: even to me he seemed a harsh, vivid apparition, with his close-shorn black hair, broad sallow brow, thin sharp cheek, wide quivering nostrils, piercing gaze, and quick, hurried bearing. Everyone knew he was irritable; you could hear it in his voice as he apostrophised the clumsy, slow-witted squad of amateur actors under his charge. Sometimes he would burst out at the raw young actresses, furious at their hollow understanding of the parts, their cold dead emotion, their feeble delivery. “Listen!” he would cry, his voice ringing through the whole building like a trumpet, and when Ginevra or Mathilde or Blanche mimicked his shout with their tiny piping voices, it was no wonder a groan of scorn or a fierce hiss of rage rewarded their tame little echo.

“You are nothing but dolls!” he thundered once, loud enough for me to hear clear across the garden. “You have no passions, you girls! You feel nothing at all! Your flesh is made of snow, your blood of ice! I want this whole thing to catch fire, to live, to have a soul!”

It was a hopeless ambition. When he finally accepted that the girls could never breathe life into the grand tragedy he had chosen, he tore the whole script to shreds, and showed up the next day with a short, compact little comic vaudeville farce. That they took to far more easily, and in no time at all he had hammered the lines and gestures straight into their smooth, round young heads.

Mademoiselle St. Pierre always sat in on M. Emanuel’s lessons, and everyone said her polished manners, apparent attention, and soft tact impressed him very favourably. She did have the art of pleasing anyone, for an hour or two — but that charm dried up like dew, vanished like gossamer, the moment her attention wandered.

The day before the fête was almost as much of a holiday as the fête itself, devoted entirely to scrubbing, cleaning, rearranging, and decorating the three main schoolrooms. There was no quiet corner left in the whole house, not upstairs nor down, for a solitary person to rest, so I fled out to the garden, and wandered or sat there alone the entire long day, basking in the warm sun, sheltering under the boughs of the old trees, finding gentle company in my own thoughts. I exchanged only two short sentences with another living soul all that day, and I did not feel lonely for a moment. It was enough to slip through the classrooms once or twice to observe the changes being wrought: the little green room and dressing room being fitted out, the tiny stage with its painted scenery being erected, M. Paul Emanuel and Mademoiselle St. Pierre directing the whole operation, and a crowd of giddy, eager pupils — Ginevra Fanshawe amongst them — working cheerfully under their orders.

The great day arrived at last. The sun rose hot and unclouded, and burned hot and unclouded from dawn till dusk. Every door and every window stood thrown wide open, lending a delicious sense of unbounded summer freedom, and freedom was indeed the order of the day. Teachers and pupils stumbled down to breakfast in their dressing-gowns and curl papers, revelling in the luxury of messy, unkempt ease for the morning, like aldermen fasting in preparation for a grand feast. Around nine o’clock, the most important functionary of the entire day arrived: the hairdresser. Sacrilegious as it sounds, he set up his station in the school oratory, right beside the holy water font, the burning candle, and the crucifix, performing the mysteries of his art right there in the sacred space. Each girl was summoned in turn to his chair, and emerged with her head as smooth as a polished shell, parted by perfect crisp white lines, and wreathed in Grecian plaits that shone as if lacquered. I took my turn with the rest, and could hardly believe what the mirror showed me when I went to look later: the garland of woven brown curls piled upon my head amazed me, I feared it could not all be my own hair, and it took several gentle, reassuring tugs to convince me otherwise. I acknowledged then that the hairdresser was a first-rate artist, who made the very most of even the most unpromising materials.

When the oratory was cleared, the dormitory became the scene of elaborate ablutions, layers of fancy underclothes, and endless bedizenment. It has always been an enigma to me how they contrived to spend three mortal hours putting on the gala uniform, so simple in the end: a clear white muslin dress, a bright blue sash (the colours of the Virgin), and a pair of white or pale straw kid gloves. For all that, the effect was perfect, immaculate in its fashion, fit, and freshness, every head dressed with exquisite neatness, a compact, formal taste that suited the full, soft, firm Labassecourien faces, even if it was too stiff for any more flowing, flexible style of beauty. The whole throng of girls looked like a field of bright, spotless snow, and I felt like a tiny, shadowy dark spot lost in that sea of light. I had not the courage to put on a sheer transparent white dress myself. The weather was far too hot for heavy fabric, so I had searched a dozen shops until I found a soft crape-like material of purple-grey, the exact colour of mist lying low over a moor in bloom. My dressmaker had sewn it as carefully as she could, noting wisely that since it was “so sad, so unnoticeable” the cut needed to be all the more neat and flattering. It was well she took such care, for I had no flowers, no jewellery to set it off, and worse: I had no natural rose of blush upon my pale cheeks.

You forget these small deficiencies in the dull routine of daily drudgery, but they force their unwelcome blankness upon you on bright days when everyone else’s beauty shines.

Yet in that shadowy grey gown I felt entirely at ease, a comfort I never would have found in any brighter, more striking garment. Madame Beck kept me in countenance, too: her own dress was almost as quiet as mine, save for a thin gold bracelet and a large bright brooch set with fine stones. We met on the stairs, and she gave me a nod and a smile of quiet approval. It was not that she thought I looked well — that point was unlikely ever to engage her interest — but that I was dressed properly, decently, and Propriety and Decency were the two calm deities of Madame’s worship. She even paused, laid her gloved hand on my shoulder, her embroidered perfumed handkerchief held in her palm, and whispered a sarcastic remark about the other teachers, whom she had just been complimenting to their faces. “Nothing could be more absurd,” she said, “than grown women dressing themselves like girls of fifteen. As for St. Pierre, she looks like an old coquette pretending to be an innocent young ingénue.”

Dressed a full two hours before anyone else, I slipped away not to the garden, where the servants were propping up long tables, setting out seats, and spreading cloths for the collation, but to the schoolrooms: now empty, quiet, cool, and clean, their walls newly painted, their planked floors freshly scoured and still damp, potted fresh blooms adorning every recess, bright new draperies hanging over the great windows.

I withdrew to the first class, a smaller, neater room than the rest, unlocked the glazed bookcase I kept the key to, pulled out a volume whose title promised a quiet few hours of reading, and sat down. The glass door of the classroom opened right out onto the great arbor; acacia boughs caressed the panes, stretching across to meet a rose bush blooming by the opposite lintel, and bees murmured busily and happily amongst its blossoms. I began to read, and soon the soft hum, the shaded green light, the warm lonely calm of my little retreat were stealing the meaning from the page, blurring the words in my vision, luring me down the slow gentle path of reverie into some deep soft dell of dreamland — when the sharpest peal of the street door bell that much-tried instrument had ever rung snatched me rudely back to full consciousness.

The bell had been ringing all morning, of course, as workmen, servants, hairdressers, and dressmakers came and went on their errands, and it was due to ring all afternoon too, as nearly a hundred external pupils arrived in carriages and hackney cabs, and it would not rest all evening when all the parents and friends thronged in to see the play. A sharp ring was nothing out of the ordinary, but this particular peal had a note all its own, that chased my dream away and sent my book tumbling from my knee.

I was just stooping to pick it up when a firm, fast, determined step came striding straight down the vestibule, along the corridor, across the great hall, through the first and second divisions, and into the main schoolroom. The closed door of my quiet sanctuary offered no resistance; it burst open, and a dark paletot and a Greek-style bonnet filled the doorway. Two dark eyes glanced over me vaguely at first, then dived hungrily right into the very core of me.

“That’s her!” a voice said. “I know her. That’s the Englishwoman. So much the worse. Any Englishwoman, prudish as she might be, will suit my purpose, or I shall know why.”

Then, with a sort of stern politeness (I suppose he thought I had not caught the drift of his uncivil mutterings) he spoke in the most mangled, execrable English I had ever heard. “Miss — you will play. I am placed here, no refusal.”

“What can I do for you, M. Paul Emanuel?” I asked, for of course it was he, in a state of great, heated excitement.

“You must play. I will not have you shrink, or frown, or play the prude. I read your character that night you first arrived; I see your capabilities. You can play. You must play.”

“But how, M. Paul? What do you mean?”

“There is no time to lose,” he went on, now switching to rapid French. “We will push all reluctance, all excuses, all silly coyness right up against the wall. You must take a role.”

“In the vaudeville?”

“In the vaudeville. You have said it.”
