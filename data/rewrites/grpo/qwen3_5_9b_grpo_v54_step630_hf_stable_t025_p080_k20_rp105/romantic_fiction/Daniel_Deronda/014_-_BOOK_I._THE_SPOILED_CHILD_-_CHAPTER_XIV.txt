The morning sun broke upon Gwendolen Harleth with a brilliance that seemed to scrub clean the doubts of the previous night. She stood tall and vibrant, a newly opened lily in a garden of high society, her energy a sharp reaction against yesterday's hesitation. The prospect of the archery match at Cardell Chase filled her with a delightful anticipation; she imagined herself moving through the beech trees like a wood-nymph, graceful and unburdened. While she knew Grandcourt would not be the passionate, lyrical suitor of romance novels, she calculated his advances with a cool, rational satisfaction. She foresaw him making slow, deliberate approaches toward a declaration, and she prepared herself to receive it with the encouragement she had promised her uncle.

Breakfast was a scene of polite domesticity, though the air hummed with unspoken tension regarding the letters waiting on Gwendolen's plate. When she read one with a gathering smile and passed it to her mother, Mrs. Davilow's face lit up with renewed cheerfulness. The mother, sensing her daughter's restored spirits, gently suggested that Gwendolen write to Dover immediately, as the family was departing that very morning. Gwendolen, however, dismissed the urgency with a brusque refusal, insisting she would write tomorrow. Only then, feeling a fleeting pang of guilt, did she soften her tone, cooing affectionately at her "old, beautiful mamma." The exchange was a dance of affectionate deflection; Gwendolen insisted her mother was hardly twenty-five years older, while Mrs. Davilow, buoyed by the certainty of her daughter's engagement, found happiness in the stability of the arrangement. "The sooner I get my palaces and coaches the better," Gwendolen declared merrily, sealing the moment with a saucy pout.

Yet, a shadow fell over the day's festivities when the rector was detained by magistrate's business. Gwendolen cared little that Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna stayed behind, but she felt a distinct loss of momentum without her uncle's presence to validate the decision she was inching toward. The realization of her own desires was becoming a heavy, defining weight. As she wished for more, the potential for what she might get began to shrink into mere limitation. Still, she clung to the comforting illusion that marriage was the gateway to a vast, new freedom.

The meeting place, Green Arbor, offered a sheltering amphitheatre of hanging wood where servants prepared the picnic. The plan was to stroll briefly before luncheon, saving the main expedition for the golden light of the afternoon. The muster was swift, and as the groups scattered into the dappled shadows of the beeches and oaks, the scene was picturesque. The roving archery, unlike the stationary target games, required a different skill, and Gwendolen did not distinguish herself in the early shots. She dressed in white and green, the same attire that had marked her introduction to Grandcourt. Though he remained constantly by her side, their relationship appeared unchanged to the casual observer, yet the air between them crackled with the unspoken understanding that they were on the eve of engagement.

As the groups drifted back toward Green Arbor, chatting aimlessly, the words that sealed her fate were exchanged. Grandcourt asked, "Do you know how long it is since I first saw you in this dress?" Gwendolen laughed, noting the date discrepancy—"nearly three weeks"—and added, "I am not good at calculating." Grandcourt paused, then replied, "That is a great loss of time." When she protested that his knowing her caused the loss, he corrected her: "It is because of the gain that I feel the loss." He pressed further, asking if she liked uncertainty. Gwendolen, suddenly beaming with a playful smile, admitted she did, sensing there was "more in it." Grandcourt's steady gaze into her eyes seemed to strip away her usual self-consciousness. When he asked if she meant more torment, she blushed and turned away, shaking her head. "No, that would make me sorry."

This moment of vulnerability was decisive, yet Grandcourt, aware of the public eye, hesitated to press further as they descended the steep path to Green Arbor. He offered his hand to help her, and they walked in silence, observed by the crowd below, including Mrs. Arrowpoint. The gossip mill churned; Mrs. Arrowpoint openly compared Grandcourt unfavorably to his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger, deeming him too languid for a woman of Catherine's high standards. Mrs. Davilow, however, listened with quiet cheerfulness, her melancholy suspended by the certainty of her daughter's future.

The picnic lunch was a blur of excellent food and champagne, a performance of high-society ease where advantages were taken with a bored, high-bred depreciation. While the gentlemen smoked cigars, Grandcourt was notably absent. Instead, Mr. Lush, usually a blot on the scene to Gwendolen, was particularly serviceable, fetching bows and managing the ladies' needs. When the group prepared to resume the roving, Gwendolen noticed her bow was missing. Fearful that Mr. Lush might take it, she hurried to retrieve it herself. A valet intercepted her, handing over the bow and a letter addressed to her. Written in a delicate, feminine hand, the note commanded her to slip away from the party after passing the Whispering Stones to hear a secret that would decide her fate. It warned of the consequences of disobedience and demanded absolute secrecy.

Gwendolen felt an inward shock, but her youthful absorption in the idea of revelation blinded her to any suspicion of contrivance. She resolved to go unobserved to the stones, thrusting the letter into her pocket. The sense of a secret to guard gave her a bracing quality, helping her maintain composure as she rejoined the company.

To everyone's surprise, Grandcourt did not appear with the group. Lord Brackenshaw joked that they would find him later, attributing his absence to the distraction of a lover absorbed in thought. Gwendolen accepted the hint with quietude, though her mind raced with the terrifying possibility: *Can he too be starting away from a decision?* It was a thought that unsettled her, yet it aligned with a strange, hazy obstinacy she sensed in him. Grandcourt, seemingly suffering from a fit of diseased numbness when an end seemed near, had likely chosen to desist simply because he could, independent of any motive. He sat smoking a second cigar, radiating a vague, hazy defiance.

The rovers set off without depression, leaving behind the less vigorous ladies, including Mrs. Davilow. The afternoon grew exquisite, the forest shifting from roofed groves to open glades as the shadows lengthened. They played an extemporized version of *As You Like It*, and when a compliment was paid to Gwendolen regarding her role as Rosalind, she felt compelled to surpass all expectations in loveliness. An excitement, born of the letter and the uncertainty, fueled her need for adventure and control over her retreat.

Nearly an hour into the roving, they arrived at the Whispering Stones, two tall, conical blocks leaning toward each other like gray-mantled giants. Under the soft sunlight, Gwendolen felt daring. She fell back among the group, then, as they hurried obliquely forward under Mr. Lush's guidance, she spotted her opportunity. She slipped away, moving silently until she was out of sight. Without running, she seemed to fly along the ground, counting the moments until she returned to the stones.

On the other side, she found a stranger. A lady with large dark eyes met hers at a foot's distance. Startled, Gwendolen shrank back but managed to take in the stranger's figure—exceedingly handsome—and noticed two children seated on the grass nearby.

"Miss Harleth?" the lady asked.

"Yes." Wonder filled Gwendolen's consciousness.

"Have you accepted Mr. Grandcourt?"

"No."

"I have promised to tell you something. And you will promise to keep my secret. However you may decide, you will not tell Mr. Grandcourt, or anyone else, that you have seen me?"

"I promise."

"My name is Lydia Glasher. Mr. Grandcourt ought not to marry anyone but me. I left my husband and child for him nine years ago. Those two children are his, and we have two others—girls—who are older. My husband is dead now, and Mr. Grandcourt ought to marry me. He ought to make that boy his heir."

She gestured to the boy, a cherub with brown curls and a hat hanging backward, puffing his cheeks in a failed attempt to blow a tiny trumpet. Gwendolen's eyes followed the gesture, then returned to Lydia Glasher.

"You are very attractive, Miss Harleth. But when he first knew me, I too was young. Since then my life has been broken up and embittered. It is not fair that he should be happy and I miserable, and my boy thrust out of sight for another."

The words were delivered with a biting accent but a determined restraint. Watching the woman's face, Gwendolen felt a sort of terror, as if a ghastly vision had whispered, "I am a woman's life."

"Have you anything more to say to me?" Gwendolen asked in a low, proud tone. The revulsion within her did not soften; everyone seemed hateful.

"Nothing. You know what I wished you to know. You can inquire about me if you like. My husband was Colonel Glasher."

"Then I will go," Gwendolen said, moving away with a ceremonious inclination returned with equal grace.

Minutes later, Gwendolen was back in the beech grove, but her party had vanished, and no search was evident. Solitude enveloped her until she reached the avenue pointed out by the warden. Determined to return quickly, she used rapid movements to suspend the thoughts that might prevent her from behaving with due calm. She had already made up her mind.

Upon her return, Mrs. Davilow was astonished. Gwendolen explained that she had lingered to look at the stones and lost sight of the party, choosing the shorter route back. She feigned relief, claiming she had had enough walking. When Mrs. Arrowpoint hinted that Grandcourt was missing, Gwendolen flashed a look of defiance and a light laugh. "No," she said. "And we didn't see any carvings on the trees, either. Where can he be? I should think he has fallen into the pool or had an apoplectic fit."

Despite her resolve not to betray her agitation, her tone was unusually high and hard. Mrs. Davilow felt sure something unpropitious had occurred. Gwendolen ordered the carriage, citing fatigue. As the horses were fetched, the roving party reappeared, bringing Grandcourt with them.

"Ah, there you are!" Lord Brackenshaw exclaimed to Gwendolen, who was arranging her mother's shawl. "We thought at first you had alighted on Grandcourt and he had taken you home... However, we didn't suppose you could be in any danger."

Grandcourt approached with his usual air, as if he did not conceive that there had been any omission on his part. Lord Brackenshaw moved aside.

"Yes, we are going," Gwendolen said, looking busily at her scarf arranged across her shoulders in a Scotch fashion.

"May I call at Offendene tomorrow?"

"Oh yes, if you like," Gwendolen replied, sweeping him from a distance with her eyelashes. Her voice was light and sharp as the first touch of frost.

Mrs. Davilow accepted Grandcourt's arm to lead her to the carriage. But in that moment, with incredible swiftness, Gwendolen got in advance of them and sprang into the carriage. "I got in, mamma, because I wished to be on this side," she said apologetically. She had successfully avoided Grandcourt's touch; he merely lifted his hat and walked away, carrying the impression that she meant to show herself offended by his neglect.

The mother and daughter drove for five minutes in silence. Then Gwendolen declared, "I intend to join the Langens at Dover, mamma. I shall pack up immediately on getting home, and set off by the early train. I shall be at Dover almost as soon as they are; we can let them know by telegraph."

"Good heavens, child! what can be your reason for saying so?"

"My reason for saying it, mamma, is that I mean to do it."

"But why do you mean to do it?"

"I wish to go away."

"Is it because you are offended with Mr. Grandcourt's odd behavior in walking off today?"

"It is useless to enter into such questions. I am not going in any case to marry Mr. Grandcourt. Don't interest yourself further about it."

"What can I say to your uncle, Gwendolen? Consider the position you place me in. You led him to believe only last night that you had made up your mind in favor of Mr. Grandcourt."

"I am very sorry to cause you annoyance, mamma, dear, but I can't help it," Gwendolen said, her tone hardening with resistance. "Whatever you or my uncle may think or do, I shall not alter my resolve, and I shall not tell my reason. I don't care what comes of it. I don't care if I never marry anyone. There is nothing worth caring for. I believe all men are bad, and I hate them."

"But need you set off in this way, Gwendolen?" Mrs. Davilow asked, miserable and helpless.

"Now mamma, don't interfere with me. If you have ever had any trouble in your own life, remember it and don't interfere with me. If I am to be miserable, let it be by my own choice."

The mother was reduced to trembling silence. She realized the difficulty would be lessened if Gwendolen went away. And she did go. The packing was done carefully that evening, and not long after dawn the next day, Mrs. Davilow accompanied her daughter to the railway station. The sweet dews of morning, the cows and horses looking over the hedges, the early travelers with their bundles—all seemed melancholy and purposeless to them both. The dingy torpor of the railway station was worse still.
