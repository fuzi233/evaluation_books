Before dawn, Xodar and I began to weave our threadbare plan of escape. Using the buckles from my discarded harness and the razor-sharp edge of the stolen green gem, I sketched a crude map of the South Polar regions onto the stone floor of our cell. From this, I calculated the trajectory to Helium and the distance from the cell block to Omean. Next, Xodar drew the layout of Omean, marking Shador and the specific aperture in its dome that led to the outside world. I memorized these diagrams, etching them into my mind until they burned there indelibly. Through Xodar, I also learned the rituals of the guards patrolling Shador. Their rounds were lethargic, taking nearly ten minutes to complete a circuit. This sluggishness created a critical blind spot: for approximately five minutes on either side of the prison, the area remained unguarded as the sentinel traversed the opposite flank.

"This knowledge," Xodar observed, "will serve us well *after* we have escaped, but it does not address the first and most vital hurdle."

"We shall escape," I assured him, a grin tugging at the corner of my mouth. "Leave the mechanics to me."

"When?" he asked.

"The first night a vessel is moored near Shador's shore," I replied.

"But how will you know?" he countered. "The windows are too high."

"Not so, friend Xodar. Look!"

I leaped to the iron bars of the window opposite our cell, scanning the courtyard below. Several small craft and two massive battleships hovered idly within a hundred yards of Shador.

"To-night," I decided aloud, ready to announce the plan, when the heavy door of our cell burst open. A guard stepped inside. If he saw me plotting, my chances of survival would vanish instantly; they would bind me in irons, and my superhuman strength would become a liability rather than an asset. The man stood with his back to me, five feet below the top of the partition wall separating our cells. That gap was my only lifeline. If he turned, I was dead. I could not drop silently to the floor without striking him.

"Where is the white man?" the guard barked at Xodar. "Issus demands his presence." He began to pivot.

That was the signal. I scrambled up the iron grating of the window, planting one boot on the sill, then released my grip and launched myself at the partition wall. Metal grated against stone as I slid over the barrier, landing softly on the floor of the adjacent cell.

"What was that noise?" the guard roared.

"I know not," Xodar lied smoothly. "He was here moments ago. I am not his keeper. Go find him."

The guard grunted unintelligibly and moved to unlock the door of another cell. I listened intently as the lock clicked shut, then vaulted back over the partition to rejoin Xodar.

"Do you see the path now?" I whispered.

"I see how you leap," Xodar admitted, "but I am no lighter than a stone. These walls are insurmountable for me."

Hours passed, and the guard resumed his patrol before returning to our cell. When his gaze met mine, his eyes widened in shock.

"By the shell of my first ancestor!" he screamed. "Where have you been?"

"I have been imprisoned since yesterday," I lied, feigning confusion. "I was in this room when you entered. Your eyesight must be failing."

He stared at me, a mixture of fury and disbelief warring on his face. "Come," he ordered. "Issus summons you."

Outside the prison, Xodar was left behind. I joined several other guards and the red Martian youth from the neighboring cell. We retraced the journey to the Temple of Issus I had made the prior day. The guards kept the red boy and me apart, preventing the conversation we had begun the night before from continuing. Yet, his face haunted me. Every line of his features, his posture, his speech—he was strangely familiar, as if I had known him forever, despite knowing I had never seen him before.

Upon reaching the gardens of Issus, instead of heading toward the temple, we were diverted along winding paths through enchanted parks to a colossal wall rising a hundred feet into the air. Massive gates opened onto a small plain bordered by the same lush forests I had seen at the base of the Golden Cliffs. Crowds of blacks strolled alongside the plant-men and great white apes, treated with the casual indifference of owners to pets. If the animals obstructed the march, the blacks shoved them aside or slashed them with swords; the beasts slunk away in terrified obedience.

Our destination lay beyond the garden walls: a vast amphitheater half a mile away. Through a massive archway, the crowds poured in to take their seats, while our guards led us to a smaller entrance near one end. Inside, beneath the seating tiers, we found ourselves herded into an enclosure with numerous other prisoners. Most wore chains, but the sheer weight of the guards' authority kept them subdued.

Now safe within the paddock, the guards relaxed their vigilance, allowing me to approach the red Martian youth, whom I felt an inexplicable pull toward.

"What is the purpose of this gathering?" I asked. "Are we to fight for the amusement of the First Born, or is this some darker ritual?"

"It is part of the monthly rites of Issus," he explained calmly. "Black men wash their sins in the blood of outsiders. If a black dies, it proves his disloyalty to Issus—an unforgivable crime. If he survives, he is acquitted of the charges that warranted the trial."

"And if we win?" I pressed. "Is there freedom?"

He laughed, a dry, humorless sound. "Freedom? Nonsense. Death is our only liberty. None who enter the domains of the First Born ever leave. Those who fight well are permitted frequent bouts; the weak simply perish sooner or later."

"You have fought often?"

"Too often," he replied. "It is my sole joy. I have claimed the lives of a hundred black devils in nearly a year. My mother would be proud if she knew how faithfully I uphold my father's legacy."

"My father must have been a legendary warrior!" I exclaimed. "I have encountered most warriors of Barsoom; surely I know him."

"My father was—"

"Come, calots!" a guard's rough voice cut in. "To the slaughter!"

We were hustled up a steep incline toward chambers far below, emptying into the arena itself. Like all structures on Barsoom, the amphitheater was built into a massive excavation. Only the uppermost rows formed a low wall surrounding the pit; the arena sank deep underground. Just beneath the lowest tier sat barred cages level with the sandy floor. Unfortunately, my youthful friend was not assigned to my cage.

Directly opposite mine sat the throne of Issus. The hideous creature squatted there, surrounded by a hundred slave maidens sparkling in jeweled trappings. Brilliant, multi-hued cloths cushioned the dais where they reclined. Flanking the throne were three solid ranks of heavily armed soldiers, followed by the high dignitaries of this mock heaven—gleaming blacks adorned with precious stones, their rank denoted by gold circles on their foreheads.

Rows of humanity stretched from the roof to the floor. Women outnumbered men, each clad in intricate harnesses signifying their station. Each black noble, for there was no peasantry among the First Born, was attended by one to three slaves drawn from the domains of the Therns or the Outer World. The First Born did no labor. Men fought—a sacred duty unto death. Women did nothing; they ate, dressed, and fed by slaves, some of whom spoke for them, narrating the arena's carnage to their masters while they sat with closed eyes.

The day's first event was the Tribute to Issus, marking the end of a year of servitude for ten splendid beauties from the courts of mighty Jeddaks and the temples of the Holy Therns. Today, they paid the price of their divine favor with their lives; tomorrow, they would grace the tables of the courtiers.

A huge black entered the arena, inspecting the women, feeling their limbs, poking their ribs. He selected one, led her before the throne, and spoke words I could not hear. Issus nodded. The black raised his hands in salute, seized the girl by the wrist, and dragged her through a small doorway beneath the throne.

"Issus will dine well tonight," a prisoner whispered beside me.

"That was her dinner," the companion confirmed. "Did you not notice how carefully he chose the plumpest?"

I cursed the monster on the throne. "Fume not," my companion warned. "You will witness far worse if you survive a month among the First Born."

Turning back, I saw the gate of a nearby cage swing open. Three monstrous white apes leaped into the arena. The girls shrank into a terrified huddle in the center. One knelt, imploring hands outstretched toward Issus, but the deity leaned forward, anticipation sharpening her gaze. The apes spotted the huddled prey and charged with demoniacal shrieks.
