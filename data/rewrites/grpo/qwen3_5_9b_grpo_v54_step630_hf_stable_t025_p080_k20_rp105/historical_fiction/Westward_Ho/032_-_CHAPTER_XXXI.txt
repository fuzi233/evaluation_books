"Britannia needs no bulwarks,
No towers along the steep,
Her march is o'er the mountain wave,
Her home is on the deep."

Thus sang Campbell of the mariners of England, and now the prophecy was being tested in a drama that would decide the spiritual fate of Europe and the destiny of the New World. For twelve days, the seas churned with a conflict worthy of Homer's thunder, pitting the Protestant cause of liberty against the Catholic yoke of despotism. While the narrative of this epic belongs to the chroniclers of the age, the events themselves unfolded with a ferocity that demanded witness.

The engagement began with a declaration of war. The English Lord High Admiral, commanding the *Defiance*, signaled his intent by firing a warning shot, followed immediately by the *Arkroyall*—also known as the *Triumph*—which closed to musket range and unleashed a storm of ordnance upon what the English admiral believed to be the Spanish flagship. In truth, it was the vessel of Alfonso de Leon. Soon, Sir Francis Drake, Sir John Hawkins, and Sir Martin Frobisher turned their guns upon the rear squadron under Recalde. The Spaniards quickly realized the terrifying nimbleness of their English opponents. Recalde's ships, realizing they were losing the exchange of fire, broke formation to join the main fleet. Admiral Medina, seeing his line scatter, rallied his ships into a defensive crescent. The great Armada pressed forward with the slow, stately dignity of a herd of buffalo ignoring wolves, unaware that these were not beasts of prey but cunning hunters, swift and deadly. Within two hours, the myth of Spanish invincibility was already cracking under the weight of English steel.

One of the four great galliasses was already riddled with shot, her chapels and pulpits ruined, the friars within thrown into chaos. The fleet was forced to circle around her to prevent Drake and Hawkins from sinking her, a maneuver that allowed the "principal galleon of Seville," carrying the blue-blooded Don Pedro de Valdez, to collide with her neighbor. Stripped of her foremast and abandoned by her crew, the proud vessel was left to drift, a testament to the fragility of Spanish chivalry. Yet, the English pressed on, driven by a courage that dismissed such losses as mere trifles compared to the glory awaiting them.

The first day ended with a resounding victory for England. Not a single English ship was seriously damaged, nor was a man lost. The prestige of Spain was shattered in the minds of the English, vindicating the strategy of Lord Howard, who, advised by Raleigh and Drake, had chosen a running fight over a static blockade. Among the prizes was the *Sta. Catharina*, a massive galleon bearing the arms of Don Guzman, the commandant of soldiers. Amyas Leigh, having been called off by the admiral's signal, retired to his hammock, dreaming of revenge.

But sleep was short-lived. Before an hour had passed, Cary roused him. Drake's lantern, the signal for pursuit, was nowhere to be seen, replaced only by the glow of a great fire on a burning Spanish ship. The fleet lay-to for the night, drifting in the dark.

The next morning found them off Torbay. A pinnace brought a letter from Drake, written with the characteristic brevity and wit of the admiral. He recounted how he had let go several captured merchantmen to lure the Spaniards, only to fall upon Valdez's galleon, capturing a host of noblemen. Drake noted that Valdez, initially proud to his last breath, had eventually surrendered, kissing Drake's fist and calling himself fortunate. Drake revealed that Don Guzman was indeed aboard the *Sta. Catharina*, a lofty ship of three tiers of ordnance, sailing in Recalde's squadron. The Armada, Drake wrote, was attempting to smoke them out of Plymouth but had failed; their rigid orders bound them like bulls in a tether, unable to match the English fleet's agility. "Time and light will kill any hare," Drake concluded, confident that the chase would lead the Spaniards far from safety.

Amyas, reading the letter, stamped in frustration. "St. Catherine! Why, that was the galleon we hammered all yesterday!" he exclaimed.

"Cunning old Drake," Cary remarked, "lining his own pockets while keeping the fleet waiting."

"He has given the Lord High Admiral the due," Amyas retorted, though he secretly worshipped Drake and despised the Papists.

The fleet reunited with Lord Howard and Lord Sheffield at nightfall. The loyalty of English Catholics was proven by the Howards' desperate courage, which led only to victory. As they passed a drifting, deserted Spanish galleon, Amyas refused to board her, insisting they had come to fight, not to plunder. The men pulled long faces as their neighbors towed off the *San Miguel d'Oquenda*, the vice-admiral's ship, which had been nearly blown up by a disgruntled Dutch gunner seeking revenge.

The second day saw the wind shift to the north, blowing cool off the white-walled downs of Weymouth Bay. The Spaniards turned to face the English, intending to wait for a change in the wind before attempting to pass the Needles. The English, however, executed a series of maneuvers, slipping close-hauled between the Armada and the land. By noon, they were coming down upon the Spaniards right before the wind, initiating a fierce and confused battle.

The fighting was furious. The English, being fewer and smaller, charged with marvelous agility, firing broadsides and then darting away to reload, targeting the great, unwieldy Spanish ships. Cock, an Englishman of Devon, died with honor in a small ship amidst the enemy. Lord Howard, seeing his captain Fenner in danger, rushed to his aid, shouting, "O George, what doest thou? Wilt thou now frustrate my hope?" Enflamed by this display of loyalty, Howard fought like a valiant captain, as did all his men.

Night fell upon a sea that looked like a floating volcano. The next morning, the fleets had passed Purbeck and Freshwater, pouring out past the Needles to join the chase. From all English havens, the chivalry flocked to the battle: the Lords Oxford, Northumberland, Cumberland, Pallavicin, Brooke, Carew, Raleigh, and Blunt. Spain had staked her noble houses; England's gentlemen measured their strength against the Cavaliers of Spain. Lord Howard had sent light craft to Portsmouth for ammunition, but the wind died, and the two fleets drifted helpless, shouting idle defiance at each other.

The sun set on a glassy sea and rose again on the twenty-fifth, St. James's Day, the patron saint of Spain. Would the saint intervene? The galleys attacked, thrashing the sea with three hundred oars apiece, but it was Lord Howard's *Triumph*, his brother's *Lion*, and the *Elizabeth Jonas*, *Bear*, *Victory*, and *Leicester* that met them with salvos of chain-shot, smashing oars and cutting rigging. Without the wind, the galliasses would have shared the fate of Valdez. Frobisher beat down the Spanish admiral's mainmast, and when attacked by Mexia and Recalde, he was rescued by Lord Howard. After that day, no galliass dared fight again.

The battle raged beneath the cliffs of Freshwater, where sea-fowl rose screaming from the chalk walls, and shepherds hurried down the slopes to gaze in trembling awe at the masts and flags piercing the veil of smoke. St. James's Day passed as Baal's did on Carmel; the only fire answering his votaries was that of English cannon. The Armada, gathering into a roundel, ceased fighting and made for Calais, hoping for French assistance from the Guises and a junction with Parma's forces in Dunkirk.

Before a fair Etesian gale, the fleets glided past Brighton, Beachy Head, Hastings, and Dungeness. Instead of fighting, Lord Howard rewarded his captains with knighthoods. On the cliffs, pike-men and musketeers marched eastward, joined by women, children, and aged folk, raising a cry of mingled faith and fear. The danger was not over. News from Newhaven indicated the Guises would not stir, and Seymour and Winter had reinforced the fleet to one hundred and forty sail—slightly larger than the Spanish force but with less tonnage and fewer men. The Spaniards were battered but unbroken.

As the Armada anchored in Calais Roads on Saturday evening, England's hour had come. The bells that would ring on Sunday morning would be either the death-knell or the triumphal peal of the Reformed faith.

That Sabbath was solemn in country and town. Cowards who had scoffed at the Armada's approach now succumbed to abject fear, while brave men knelt beside their families, hearts sinking at the thought of the Inquisition or the fanaticism of the Spanish soldiery. Memories of the Massacre of St. Bartholomew, the fires of Smithfield, and the extermination of the Moors haunted the English imagination. They knew the hazard: one false step, and the doom of Magdeburg would fall upon every town from London to Carlisle. None knew this better than the guiding spirit of the land, who had devised a daring stroke to anticipate the blow.

But where was Amyas Leigh? For days, he had searched for the *Sta. Catharina* in the thick of the press, unable to find her or hear of her. One moment he feared she had sunk; the next, that she had escaped. Moody, restless, and peevish, he talked of nothing but Don Guzman. He spent his spare moments polishing his sword, muttering to it, treating it as a confidant. This selfish obsession with vengeance consumed his mind; he forgot England's triumph, his own safety, and everything but his brother's blood. Yet, this was the day he had longed for since boyhood, the day to face the invader, Antichrist himself. He had believed he was sent into the world only to fight the Spaniard, and now he was fighting for such a stake, yet he was not content. While the fleet received Communion side by side, rejoicing in Britain's Salamis, Amyas turned away from the holy elements.

"I cannot communicate, Sir John," he told Captain Brimblecombe. "Charity with all men? I hate, if ever man hated on earth."

"You hate the Lord's foes only, Captain Leigh."

"No, Jack, I hate my own as well."

"Don't try to put me off with the same Jesuit's quibble..." Jack protested, but Amyas cut him off, declaring his hatred for a specific enemy that kept him from loving any human being. Jack departed with a sigh.

While the crew communed on deck, Amyas sat below sharpening his sword, then took a boat to Drake's ship to ask for news of the *Sta. Catharina*. He listened scowling to the Spanish chants and bells. Finally, Drake returned with a secret commission, and Amyas helped him until nightfall, returning to his own ship as Sir Amyas Leigh, Knight, to the joy of all but his own moody soul.

On the Sabbath, the two armaments lay scowling at each other, hardly out of gunshot. Messengers hurried to Bruges for Parma, who was not at Dunkirk but at St. Mary of Halle in Hainault, praying for visions of Cardinal Allen crowning him. Parma reported that his victuals were not ready, his Dutch sailors had fled, and his ships were heavily armed and stubborn. While he prayed, the English prayed and acted.

By Monday morning, Lord Henry Seymour had delivered a letter of command from Elizabeth. Eight fire-ships, besmeared with wild-fire, brimstone, pitch, and resin, stole down the wind toward the Spanish fleet, guided by two valiant Devon men, Young and Prowse. The ships were fired, and the sky turned red from Dover Cliffs to Gravelines Tower. Weary Belgian peasants inland leaped from their beds, believing the Day of Judgment had come.

A panic ensued. Shrieks, oaths, and prayers made the night hideous. Remembering Jenebelli's fire-ships at Antwerp, the Spaniards cut their cables and hoisted any sails they could. The Invincible Armada lumbered wildly out to sea, every ship foul of her neighbor. The largest galliass lost her rudder and drifted helplessly. The Duke, having weighed his anchor deliberately, ran in to fire a signal for return, but his truant sheep were deaf to his pipe. Swearing and praying, he ran up the Channel toward Gravelines, picking up stragglers, but Drake and Fenner had arrived first.

When Monday's sun rose on Gravelines, the thunder of cannon recommenced and was not hushed till night. Drake hung coolly in the rear to plunder but fought fiercely when needed. The Armada must never be allowed to reform; if it did, its left wing might hold the English at bay while its right drove off the blockading Hollanders, freeing Parma to sail to the Thames.

Drake weighed anchor and moved up the Channel as soon as the Spanish fleet appeared, with Fenner burning to redeem his honor. Before the others could join, the Devon ships had worried the Spaniards into confusion for two hours. But what was that heavy firing behind them? The great galliass lay stranded on the sands where the Calais pier now stands. Amyas Preston, the future hero of La Guayra, pounded her into submission while a fleet of hoys and drumblers looked on like jackals.

Then, on the southwest horizon, two mighty ships loomed larger, followed by sail upon sail. A shout greeted the *Triumph* and the *Bear* as the Lord High Admiral glided stately into the thickest of the fight. True, they had only twenty-three ships to cope with ninety Spaniards, but they had dash, daring, and the inspiration of utter need. Now, or never, the struggle must end.
