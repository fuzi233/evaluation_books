For a time, I harbored no desire save to spend my life steeped in the domestic rhythms of the Little Ones. But soon, a profound unrest began to permeate my days. It started with a visceral rejection of my daily labor: the realization that I was merely a utilitarian cog, tasked with the grotesque fattening of boors who viewed meals as nothing more than fuel. To those lumbering giants, sustenance was devoid of ritual, stripped of the sensory reverence that connects a creature to its soil. I realized then that I had stumbled into a mythic ecology, one whose cyclical laws and agrarian mysteries it was my duty to record. If I were to honor the deep, unwritten hospitality the children had shown me, I needed to understand the ancestral sourcing of their diet and the fading syllables of their native tongue. To do that, I had to be free. 

Surely, I reasoned, the arrested development of the Little Ones could not be the natural harvest of their pristine environment. Life and law are not so discordant that perfection requires the stunting of the vine. Yet, their growth was unmistakably halted. What blight had interfered? Lona, who possessed the quiet gravity of a traditional agrarian elder despite appearing no older than fifteen, tended to her multitude of charges as though she were the sole custodian of their hearth. Did they age at all? They lacked any concept of linear time, existing instead in a circular pacing dictated by the ripening of fruits and the shifting of the moon. Full of inherited agrarian wisdom yet empty of empirical knowledge, Lona was at once their sustaining love and their cultural law. 

Her singular anxiety was that her Little Ones must not grow, lest they transform into the boorish consumers she so despised. As their “good giant,” I was bound to protect them, but without delving into the food history of their ancestors and the root causes of their stagnation, I could offer no true nourishment. If I left, they would simply revert to their ancient foraging routines; I was not their savior, but a guest partaking in their harvest. My presence only drew the discordant, percussive threat of their idiotic neighbors closer to their sanctuary. I yearned to help them preserve their culture before it eroded entirely—to teach them the architecture of notation, so they might transcribe the dainty, gustatory melodies they murmured while gathering, songs they routinely forgot in the absence of a recorded lexicon. I concluded I must resume my travels, hoping to uncover the lost recipes and timelines that governed the destiny of these bewitching creatures.

My departure, however, was violently accelerated. To prepare them for my absence, I was sitting with them during our daily foraging, attempting to translate my intentions. I explained that I would have long ago abandoned the bad giants, but the rich, sensory life of the Little Ones had anchored me. Suddenly, as if guided by a single, interwoven instinct, they swarmed me. They scrambled over one another, scaling the tree and dropping onto my shoulders until I was enveloped in the scent of crushed moss, sweet milk, and wild mint. With three tiny bodies cradled in my arms, one clinging to my neck, another standing proudly upon my head, and a multitude gripping my limbs, I was blissfully immobilized—overwhelmed by a living, breathing harvest. 

Absorbed in this joyous, tactile struggle, not one of us heard the harsh, industrial tread of my tyrant until he shattered our acoustic equilibrium. With a single, panicked cry of, “Take care, good giant!” the children scattered. They darted like field mice, dropped like hedgehogs, and scurried up the bark like squirrels. In that very second, the bad giant rounded the trunk, his presence a jarring pollution in our quiet wood. He struck me across the head with a heavy branch—a blunt, utilitarian blow that instantly severed my consciousness. 

I learned later that the children had retaliated by pelting him with a fierce barrage of heavy apples and jagged stones, defending their domestic space until the frightened brute blundered back to his sterile home.

When I drifted back to awareness, the air was chilled by the night. Above me hung a scattering of pale stars, waiting in the stillness for the moon. A deep, agonizing ache throbbed in my skull, accompanied by a parching thirst. Wearily, I turned on my side. The moment my ear pressed against the loam, the acoustic environment shifted: I heard the pristine, rhythmic gurgling of a hidden spring. The soft, liquid resonance made me groan with a desperate craving for moisture. 

Instantly, I was surrounded by a multitude of silent shadows. The Little Ones had come, bringing the forest’s bounty to my lips. They fed me meticulously sourced nocturnal fruits—berries bursting with the tart, tannic memory of the first rains, and small, fleshy drupes that yielded a sweet, resinous nectar. They brought them in continuous succession until my thirst was entirely quenched, the rich olfactory landscape of petrichor and citrus reviving my senses. Then, a new sound entered our acoustic ecology: the air filled with the quiet, rhythmic cadence of their collective weeping. 

I tried to sit up. A soft barricade of small bodies immediately braced my back. With immense effort and the surprising, coordinated strength of their pushing and pulling, I staggered to my feet. 

“You must go away, good giant,” they murmured, their voices a synchronized chorus. “When the bad giants see you hurt, they will all trample on you.”

“I think I must,” I answered, tasting the lingering sweetness of the wild juice on my tongue. 

“Go and grow strong, and come again,” they said.

“I will,” I replied, my legs giving way as I sank back down.

“Indeed you must go at once!” whispered Lona, kneeling beside me, her hands smelling of damp earth and medicinal herbs. 

One of the older boys stepped forward, his tone urgent. “I listened at his door. I heard the bad giant tell his wife that he found you idle, talking to moles and squirrels. When he beat you, we tried to kill him. He said you were a wizard, and they must knock you, or they would have no peace.” 

The dominant lexicon of the boors—words like *idle* and *wizard*—was a weapon used to criminalize the Little Ones’ gentle agrarian existence, a violent misunderstanding of their quiet connection to the land. 

“I will go at once,” I said, “and come back as soon as I have found out what is wanted to make you bigger and stronger.”

“We don’t want to be bigger,” they answered, their faces deeply serious in the starlight. “We WON’T grow bad giants!—We are strong now; you don’t know how much strong!”

It was futile to offer them a paradigm of growth that held no flavor, no sensory appeal, and threatened their deeply held worldview. I said nothing more, rising to move slowly up the valley’s slope. Instantly, they formed a solemn, processional line. Some guided my steps, others supported my weight, and the rest followed. As we walked, they continued their sacred domestic ritual of feeding me. 

“You are broken,” they explained, pressing more fragrant, juice-heavy morsels into my hands, “and much red juice has run out of you: put some in.” 

When we reached the valley’s upper ridge, the moon crested the horizon, washing the landscape in a pale, creamy light. 

“She has come to take care of you, and show you the way,” Lona said. 

As we walked, I questioned them about the surrounding lands. They spoke, in their fragmented, native dialect, of a great place ruled by a giant-girl for a queen. They did not know if it was a city, nor its direction, nor even the queen's name. They only knew, with the instinctual certainty of prey, that she despised the Little Ones and wished to eradicate them, thwarted only by her inability to locate their foraging grounds. When I asked how they knew this, Lona replied simply that she had always known it—a piece of inherited cultural memory passed down through generations. If the giant-girl came, they would have to “hide hard.” 

I suggested I might go to her and demand why she hated them. They recoiled in terror. “No, no! She will kill you, good giant; she will kill you! She is an awful bad-giant witch!” 

I asked where I should direct my steps instead. They pointed beyond the baby-forest, toward the horizon where the moon was born. There lay a smooth green country, they said, a terrain pleasant to the feet, absent of rocks or towering trees. When I asked for a path, they deferred to the night sky: “The moon will tell you, we think.”

They were guiding me up the second branch of the dry riverbed. Upon seeing the moon reach its zenith, they halted. 

“We have never gone so far from our trees before,” they murmured, their voices betraying the unease of leaving their known acoustic and culinary boundaries. “Now mind you watch how you go, that you may see inside your eyes how to come back to us.”

“And beware of the giant-woman that lives in the desert,” warned one of the older girls as they prepared to turn back. “I suppose you have heard of her!”

“No,” I answered. 

“Then take care not to go near her. She is called the Cat-woman. She is awfully ugly—AND SCRATCHES.”

As the older children paused, the smaller ones had already begun to retrace their steps toward the familiar comforts of their hearth. The remaining few looked at me with deep, silent gravity for a moment, then walked slowly away. 

Lona, the last to leave, held up her baby to be kissed. She gazed into my eyes, her scent a complex mingling of woodsmoke and crushed leaves, and whispered, “The Cat-woman will not hurt YOU.” Without another word, she turned and departed. 

I stood for a long time, watching their small forms dissolve into the moonlight, before turning with a heavy heart to begin my solitary journey. Soon, their laughter overtook me—a sound like innumerable sheep-bells, rippling the night air and echoing against the rocks in a perfect, harmonious acoustic ecology. I turned one last time to look back. They were gamboling along, entirely devoid of the anxieties that plagued the modern, industrial mind. Only Lona walked apart, cradling her baby in the quiet dignity of her station. 

Pondering as I walked, I recalled the profound untranslatability of their subjective experience. Once, when I had suggested they abandon the territory of the bad giants and accompany me to find new lands, they had replied, “But that would be to NOT ourselves!” It was a phrase that spoke volumes of their connection to the soil; to sever them from the specific roots, herbs, and sonic landscapes of their valley was to erase their very identity. Without ambition, greed, or the noise of commerce, they lacked any motive to alter their circumstances. Except for the care of their babies, they had never known the opportunity to sustain anyone but me. 

How were they to grow? But more importantly, why *should* they grow? In seeking to alter their diet and their world to fit the dominant lexicon of my own, might I not inflict irreversible harm? To force their minds into the linear, utilitarian framework of my society might only distort and wither their rich, sensory existence. Their instinctive fear of growth—of becoming giant consumers—might be a deeply ingrained, necessary defense mechanism against cultural death. The role of the philanthropist is steeped in subtle power dynamics; the man who would nourish his neighbor must first learn how not to poison him, beginning always by pulling the utilitarian beam from his own eye.
