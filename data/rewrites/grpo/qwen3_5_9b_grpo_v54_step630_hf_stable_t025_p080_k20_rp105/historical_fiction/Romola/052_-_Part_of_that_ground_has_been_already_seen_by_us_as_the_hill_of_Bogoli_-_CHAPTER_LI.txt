As Romola spoke of someone expecting her, she referred to her cousin Brigida, yet she could not have guessed how desperately that kindred spirit needed her presence. Walking back toward the Piazza together, they spotted a gathering of youths halting before Tessa. When Romola stepped closer, seeing the simple country girl's distress, she called out, "Wait a moment, cousin." But Monna Brigida, her composure fracturing, replied hastily, "No, I cannot go on. Come with me to Boni's shop; I shall return there."

The truth lay deeper than a mere desire to flee. Monna Brigida was torn between the vanity she wore on her person and a dawning terror that the Piagnoni were correct in their belief that rouge, false hair, and pearl embroidery corrupted the soul. The serious atmosphere of the sect hung over the city like a pervasive odor; nothing tasted or felt as it once had. Here stood her dear cousin Romola, youthful and beautiful, living a life that seemed to demand rigorous sacrifice from a woman. For a widow of fifty-five whose self-worth had long been anchored in her appearance and the admiration of others, maintaining buoyant spirits required immense imagination. Monna Brigida had begun to fight battles at her toilette. Was it truly worth the effort to apply rouge and braid her hair if her soul required such austerity to flourish? Yet, when she gazed into the hand-mirror, she saw a sallow face with sagging cheeks and crow's-feet that no coquettish lip-curl could hide. When she parted her grey hair to lie flat against her temples in the Piagnone style, her courage evaporated. Monna Berta, though only fifty-two, possessed a sharp wit that would surely laugh her to death, calling her an old hag. Every non-Piagnone woman would shrug at her sight, and every man would address her as if she were his grandmother. At fifty-five, she reasoned, she was not ancient—she merely needed a touch of makeup. Thus, the rouge, the braids, and the embroidered berretta remained, and Monna Brigida was content with her familiar reflection. However, today, while preparing for the Piagnone Carnival, the internal war between fear and longing proved too great. She turned back to seek refuge in the druggist's shop rather than face the collectors of the Anathema without Romola beside her. But her retreat was not swift enough. Even before she turned, the white-robed boys trailing behind the crowd that had wheeled toward Tessa had spotted her. Perhaps their eagerness to seize her was fueled by the fact that some of their number had already cornered another figure bearing the heavier burden of the Anathema. Several of these youths were at the tenderest age, undergoing peculiar training. Among them was a small boy of ten, an olive wreath resting above cherubic cheeks and wide brown eyes. His imagination was seized by a hovering awe of existence, sensing that great consequences hinged on every action, yet his longings ran toward mischief and mastery. He was the first to reach Monna Brigida, placing himself squarely in her path.

She felt a surge of anger and scanned for an open door, finding none. To attempt escape now would only worsen her plight. Yet, it was not the cherubic boy who first addressed her, but a youth of fifteen gripping the handle of a wide basket.

"Venerable mother!" he began, his voice ringing with conviction. "The blessed Jesus commands you to surrender the Anathema you carry upon you. That cap embroidered with pearls, those jewels fastening your false hair—give them up and sell them for the poor. Cast the hair itself away from you, for it is a lie fit only for the fire. Doubtless, beneath your silk mantle, you harbor other treasures."

"Yes, lady," chimed in the youth at the opposite handle, who had memorized many of Fra Girolamo's phrases. "They are too heavy for you. They weigh more than a millstone, dragging you toward perdition. Will you adorn yourself with the hunger of the poor and boast of carrying God's curse upon your head?"

"In truth, you are old, *buona madre*," the little boy added in a sweet soprano. "You look frightfully ugly with the red on your cheeks and that black, glistening hair, and those fine things. Only Satan could find pleasure in seeing you. Your Angel is sorrowful; He wishes you to rub away the red."

The small fellow snatched a soft silk scarf from the basket and held it out to Monna Brigida, urging her to use it as her guardian angel desired. Her anger and mortification were rapidly yielding to spiritual alarm. Monna Berta and the cloud of high-society witnesses were nowhere to be seen. Instead, she was surrounded by young monitors whose white robes, wreaths, red crosses, and terrifying candor possessed an awful, unusual power. Her Franciscan confessor, Fra Cristoforo, was absent to bolster her distrust of Dominican teachings, leaving her helplessly shaken by a vague sense that a supreme warning had arrived. With no other course seeming open to her, she accepted the offered scarf and rubbed her cheeks with trembling submission.

"It is well, madonna," said the second youth. "It is a holy beginning. Once you remove those vanities from your head, the dew of heavenly grace will descend upon it." As the infusion of mischief grew stronger, the boy placed his hand on one of the jeweled pins securing her braids to the berretta and drew it out. The heavy black plait fell forward, obscuring Monna Brigida's face and pulling the rest of her headgear down. This became a new reason for haste; she raised her hands quickly, undone the remaining fastenings, and flung her beloved crimson-velvet berretta, with its unsurpassed embroidery of seed-pearls, into the basket of doom. She stood revealed as an unrouged woman, her grey hair pushed back from a face where deep lines of age had triumphed over her former fullness.

Yet, the berretta was not permitted to rest in the basket. With impish zeal, the youngsters lifted it and held it up pitilessly, the false hair dangling limply.

"See, venerable mother," the taller youth declared, "what ugly lies you have delivered yourself from! Now you resemble the blessed Saint Anna, mother of the Holy Virgin."

Thoughts of entering a convent immediately, never again to show her face in the world, rushed through Monna Brigida's mind. There was nothing left for her but to tend to her soul.

Of course, spectators laughed; she did not need to look around to confirm it. Very well, perhaps it would be better to be forced to think more of Paradise. But at the realization that the dear, accustomed world was no longer within her choice, hard tears gathered in her elderly eyes, blurring her vision. She could barely discern a large, rough hand holding a red cross, suddenly thrust before her over the shoulders of the boys, accompanied by a strong, guttural voice.

"Only four quattrini, madonna, blessing and all! Buy it. You will find comfort in it now that your wig is gone. *Deh!* What are we sinners doing all our lives? Making soup in a basket and receiving only the scum for our stomachs. Better buy a blessing, madonna! Only four quattrini; the profit is not even the smell of a coin, and it goes to the poor."

Monna Brigida, her eyes dim with confusion, moved toward the further submission of reaching into her embroidered *scarsella*, currently hidden by her silk mantle, when the group surrounding her—which she had not yet considered escaping—parted before a figure as welcome as an angel releasing prison bolts.

"Romola, look at me!" Monna Brigida cried in a piteous tone, extending both hands.

The white troop was already moving away, with a slight awareness that their zeal regarding the headgear had been superabundant, affording them a dispensation from demanding further penitential offerings.

"Dear cousin, do not be distressed," Romola said, smitten with pity yet hardly able to suppress a smile at the sudden apparition of her kinswoman in a genuine, natural guise, so strangely contrasted with all her memories. She removed the black drapery from her own head and draped it over Monna Brigida's. "There," she continued soothingly, "no one will notice you now. We will turn down the Via del Palagio and go straight home."

They hurried away, Monna Brigida clutching Romola's hand tightly, as if seeking a stronger assurance that her cousin was truly there.

"Ah, my Romola, my dear child!" the short, stout woman cried, hurrying with frequent steps to keep pace with the majestic young figure beside her. "What an old scarecrow I am! I must be good—I mean to be good!"

"Yes, yes; buy a cross!" the guttural voice called out, as the rough hand was thrust once more before Monna Brigida. Bratti was not to be abashed by Romola's presence into renouncing a probable customer; he had quietly followed their retreat. "Only four quattrini, blessing and all—and if there were any profit, it would all go to the poor."

Monna Brigida would have been compelled to pause, even in a less submissive mood. She raised one hand deprecatingly to arrest Romola's remonstrance, while with the other she reached out a *grosso*, worth many white quattrini, saying in an entreating tone, "Take it, good man, and begone."

"You are in the right, madonna," Bratti said, snatching the coin quickly and thrusting the cross into her hand. "I will not offer you change, for I might as well rob you of a mass. What! We must all be scorched a little, but you will come off the easier; better fall from the window than the roof. A good Easter and a good year to you!"

"Well, Romola," Monna Brigida cried pathetically as Bratti left them, "if I am to be a Piagnone, it matters not how I look!"

"Dear cousin," Romola said, smiling at her affectionately, "you do not know how much better you look than you ever did before. I see now how good-natured your face is, like yourself. That red and finery seemed to thrust themselves forward and hide your expression. Ask our Piero or any other painter if he would not rather paint your portrait now than before. I think all lines of the human face possess something either touching or grand, unless they seem to spring from low passions. How fine old men are, like my godfather! Why should not old women look grand and simple?"

"Yes, when one reaches sixty, my Romola," Brigida replied, relapsing slightly. "But I am only fifty-five, and Monna Berta, and everyone else... but it is no use: I will be good, like you. Your mother, had she lived, would have been as old as I am; we were cousins together. One *must* either die or grow old. But it does not matter about being old, if one is a Piagnone."
