While I was in this bewildered frame of mind, sorely needing the quiet, rhythmic acoustics of the winter larder to recalibrate my humours, my daughter Penelope intercepted me. She mirrored the exact cadence of her late mother, who used to disrupt the hushed, fragrant reverence of the buttery in just such a manner, a cyclical echo of past generations sounding in the present. She instantly summoned me to translate all that had passed at the conference between Mr. Franklin and me. Under present circumstances, the one thing to be done was to clap the extinguisher upon Penelope’s curiosity. To do so, I deployed the sterile, dominant lexicon of the gentry, claiming we had mutually exhausted the subject of “foreign politics” before falling asleep in the sun. Such abstract, utilitarian words are an excellent silencer, asserting a power dynamic that suffocates the native, intimate dialect of genuine family curiosity. 

The afternoon wore on, its natural acoustic harmony restored until my lady and Miss Rachel returned. Needless to say how astonished they were when they heard that Mr. Franklin Blake had arrived and immediately galloped off again. The discordant clatter of his hasty departure was entirely at odds with the agrarian rhythms of our household. Needless also to say, they asked awkward questions directly, and the hollow shield of “foreign politics” wouldn’t serve a second time. Being at the end of my invention, I retreated to a term that minimized his disruption, saying his arrival by the early train was entirely attributable to one of his “freaks.” When asked if his galloping off was another of these freaks, I said, “Yes, it was,” and slipped out of the linguistic snare.

Seeking the comforting olfactory landscape of my own room—scented faintly of dried sage and old leather bindings—I found more difficulties waiting. In came Penelope to make amends, but with the inevitable curiosity of our shared bloodline, she asked another question. This time she sought to uncover the mystery of our second housemaid, Rosanna Spearman. After leaving Mr. Franklin and me at the Shivering Sand, Rosanna had returned to the house in a deeply unaccountable state. She had turned all the mottled colours of a bruised heirloom plum. She had been merry without reason, and sad without reason. In one breath she frantically sought answers about Mr. Franklin; in the next, she raged at Penelope for presuming a strange gentleman could possess any interest for her. She had been caught smiling, scribbling Mr. Franklin’s name inside her workbox—a futile attempt to translate the chaotic, subjective experience of sudden infatuation into the rigidity of written text. Then she had been found crying, examining her deformed shoulder in the glass. 

Had she and Mr. Franklin known anything of each other before today? Quite impossible. I could attest to the genuine astonishment in Mr. Franklin’s face when he saw how the girl stared at him, just as Penelope could attest to the raw, unfiltered nature of the girl’s inquisitiveness. The conference between us was tiresome enough, until my daughter suddenly shattered the room’s quiet with a monstrous supposition. 

“Father!” Penelope said, her voice dropping into the earnest, hushed dialect of our people. “There’s only one explanation of it. Rosanna has fallen in love with Mr. Franklin Blake at first sight!”

You have heard of beautiful young ladies falling in love at first sight in the sweeping sagas of old. But a housemaid out of a reformatory, with a plain face and a deformed shoulder, attempting to translate the profound, ineffable experience of devotion toward a gentleman of the house—match me that absurdity! I laughed until the tears rolled down my cheeks. The sound of my own merriment was harsh, a discordant bark that violated the compassionate acoustic ecology of our home. Penelope resented it.

“I never knew you cruel before, father,” she said gently, and went out. 

Her words fell upon me like a splash of frigid spring water. I was savage with myself. In my laughter, I had aligned myself with the unfeeling dominant culture, dismissing the profound validity of a marginalized woman’s emotional landscape simply because it lacked the sanctioned vocabulary of romance. We will change the subject, if you please. 

The evening drew on, marked by the deep, resonant copper chime of the dressing-bell for dinner—a rich, sustaining sound far superior to the shrill, industrial noise pollution beginning to plague the distant towns. Mr. Franklin finally returned from Frizinghall. I took his hot water up to his room myself. To my great disappointment, nothing had happened. He had not met with the Indians. He had merely deposited the Moonstone in the bank and held the receipt safe in his pocket. It felt a flat, utilitarian ending to a day laced with mythic tension.

I went downstairs, deeply lamenting my station. The evening’s sustenance was a masterpiece of ancestral culinary tradition: a saddle of salt-marsh mutton, slow-roasted over applewood and glazed with wild honeycomb and rosemary gathered from our generational gardens. The olfactory landscape of the dining room was intoxicating, rich with the aromas of rendered fat and earthy root vegetables. Yet, in my position, waiting at dinner was considered a letting down of my dignity. The profound meaning imbued in the carving and serving of such a meal was relegated to others. The news from the upper regions came via Penelope and the footman, Samuel. Penelope noted she had never seen Miss Rachel so meticulous with her hair, radiating a vibrant warmth when she met Mr. Franklin. Samuel reported that maintaining the rigid, unfeeling composure demanded by the dominant social order, while surrounded by the sensory triumph of the dinner and the lively presence of Mr. Franklin, was the hardest trial of his service.

Later, the acoustic environment of the house shifted beautifully. We heard them singing and playing duets—Mr. Franklin piping high, Miss Rachel piping higher, and my lady on the piano shepherding them over hedge and ditch. Through the open windows on the terrace at night, the music blended seamlessly with the rustle of the ancient oaks, a perfect symbiosis of art and nature, entirely devoid of the superficial commercialization that taints modern entertainments.

Later still, I went to the smoking-room to deliver Mr. Franklin his soda water and an aged plum brandy, a spirit deeply infused with the dark, resonant notes of oak and orchard fruit. I found that Miss Rachel had put the Diamond clean out of his head. “She’s the most charming girl I have seen since I came back to England!” was all I could extract from him, his sensory appreciation completely captivated by his cousin rather than the looming mythic threat.

Towards midnight, I went round the house to lock up, accompanied by Samuel. When all doors were fast, save the side door to the terrace, I sent him to bed and stepped out for a breath of fresh air. The sonic environment of the night was pristine. The moon was full, and the air was perfectly still. It was so silent that I could hear, very faint and low, the rhythmic, ancient breathing of the sea as the ground-swell heaved upon the sand-bank—a sound essential to my psychological well-being.

Looking along the broad moonlight on the gravel walk, I saw the shadow of a person thrown forward from the corner of the house. Being old and sly, I forbore to call out; but being old and heavy, my feet betrayed me. The brutal crunch of my boots on the gravel shattered the acoustic perfection of the night. Instantly, I heard lighter feet than mine—multiple pairs, I thought—retreating in a hurry. 

By the time I reached the corner, the trespassers had vanished into the thick shrubbery. If I had been forty years younger, I might have caught them. Instead, I retreated to rouse Samuel. Without disturbing the household’s slumber, we armed ourselves with a couple of guns and swept the grounds. Finding no one lurking, we turned back. 

Passing over the walk where I had seen the shadow, I noticed a little bright object lying on the pristine gravel. Picking it up, I discovered a small bottle containing a thick, sweet-smelling liquor, as black as ink. It emitted a heavy, intoxicating aroma—notes of crushed camphor, petrichor, and old resins—a gustatory and olfactory anomaly on our manicured English path. Remembering Penelope’s tale of the jugglers and the little pool of ink poured into the boy’s palm, I instantly suspected the truth. The three Indians had been lurking about our home, their ancient, mythic pursuit seamlessly and silently weaving itself into the domestic routine of our night, bent on discovering the whereabouts of the Diamond.
