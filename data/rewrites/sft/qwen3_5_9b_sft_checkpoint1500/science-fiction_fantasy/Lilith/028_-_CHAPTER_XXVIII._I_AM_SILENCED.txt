The water drained from my lashes, blurring the edges of the world until focus returned. There, perched on the rim of the massive stone basin, sat the raven. The pale, hard light of dawn caught in his glossy feathers, casting a sharp, judgmental gleam as he looked down at me. I lay submerged in the fountain's pool, propped up on my elbows, lifting my face just enough to meet his gaze. This was the heart of the garden, the great fountain my father had built—a towering column of steel-shiny stalks erupting a hundred feet into the sky, exploding into a frothy blossom before falling back around us.

"You told me nothing!" I snapped, the chill of the bird's words settling in my bones.

"I instructed you to ignore anyone you distrusted," the raven corrected, his voice smooth and unyielding. "I told you to do *nothing*."

"Tut!" I protested, shivering slightly despite the warmth of the morning. "How was a mere mortal expected to remember that?"

"You will not forget the consequences of forgetting it!" Mr. Raven replied, stepping closer. He leaned over the marble edge, stretching a hand across the gap between us. The gesture felt ancient, bridging worlds.

I took his hand. Instantly, the distance vanished, and I found myself standing dry on the grass beside him, though still dripping wet from the immersion.

"You must change your clothes at once!" he urged, his tone practical yet stern. "A soaking tells no one where you originate—though currently, such an accident is highly irregular. Here, it merely creates inconvenience."

Before I could respond, the transformation reversed. The raven stepped away, regaining his avian form with a stately stride, heading toward the house where the doors stood wide open.

"I haven't much need to change!" I called after him, laughing. My robes were already discarded, tossed aside as I climbed the trees.

"It has been a long time since I shed a feather," the raven murmured, pausing near the threshold.

Inside, the house slept soundlessly. I retreated to my chamber, donned a dressing gown, and descended to the library. As I crossed the threshold, the librarian emerged from the shadows of a closet. Without a word, I collapsed onto a velvet couch. Mr. Raven followed, drawing a chair close to mine and sitting. For a moment, the silence hung heavy between us, broken only by the hum of the distant city.

"What does it all mean?" I finally asked, the question tearing at me.

"A profound inquiry!" he answered gently. "No man truly knows what anything *is*. A person can only grasp what a thing *means*. And whether they ever do depends entirely on how they choose to use it."

"I have used nothing yet!"

"Not significantly, perhaps—but you possess the awareness of that lack, and that counts for something. Most spend a lifetime believing they have accomplished everything, only to realize too late they have learned nothing and done less. Yet, you have harbored the desire to be useful."

"My desire was to aid the children—the precious Little Ones."

"I knew you intended that—and you chose the wrong path."

"I didn't know the right way."

"That is true. However, you are culpable for failing to seek it."

"I am willing to accept whatever truth you impart, provided I can comprehend its meaning."

"If you had accepted our invitation, you would have known the correct path. When a man refuses to act within his immediate sphere, he must travel vast distances to find his work."

"Indeed, I have traveled far and found nothing. I have not discovered my work! I abandoned the children to learn how to serve them, and in doing so, I have only uncovered the peril they face."

"When you were with them, you were exactly where you could assist. By leaving your post to search for a role, you failed them. It requires wisdom to know when to depart; folly lies in wandering away only to return too late."

"Do you mean..." I hesitated, "that I could have helped the Little Ones simply by staying?"

"Could you have taught them anything by abandoning them?"

"No... but how was I to teach them? I didn't know where to begin. Besides, they were far ahead of me!"

"That is accurate. Yet, you were not a ruler measuring them against your own standards! If they possessed your knowledge—or what you might have known—they would have soared beyond your reach. But you observed that they were not growing, or if they were, so slowly that they hadn't even conceived the idea of growth! They feared growing themselves! Have you never seen children remain children?"

"But surely, I lacked the power to make them grow!"

"You could have removed obstacles blocking their growth!"

"What obstacles? I knew them not. I suspected perhaps it was a thirst!"

"Naturally. They had no tears to cry with!"

"I would have gladly prevented them from needing water for such a purpose!"

"No doubt you would—that being the hallmark of all misguided philanthropy! Tell me, Mr. Vane, if it weren't for their sorrow, would your world ever have been worth saving? You admitted you believed they needed water; why did you not dig wells for them?"

"That thought never crossed my mind!"

"Not when the whispers of subterranean waters echoed in your ears?"

"I believe I heard them once. But I was terrified for them regarding the Giants. That fear compelled me to endure so much suffering alongside those brutish creatures!"

"In truth, you nearly trained these noble little souls to dread the Stupid Bags! While they fed, comforted, and revered you, you submitted to slavery under beasts! You offered the darlings a feigned coward for their hero. A grievous injustice you inflicted. They gave you their hearts; you owe them your soul! By now, you could have commanded the Bags to become woodcutters and water-carriers for the Little Ones!"

"I fear your words hold truth, Mr. Raven! But I was certain that greater knowledge might harm them, rendering them less innocent, less beautiful."

"They offered you no cause for such trepidation!"

"Isn't a modicum of knowledge inherently dangerous?"

"That is one of your world's most cherished deceptions! Does humanity's greatest knowledge surpass a little? Or is it therefore perilous? The delusion that knowledge constitutes greatness in itself renders any degree of knowledge more hazardous than any quantity of ignorance. Knowing all things would not constitute greatness."

"At least, I served the Giants out of love, not cowardice!"

"Conceded. Yet, you should have served the Little Ones, not the Giants! You should have given them water; then, swiftly, they would have taught the Giants their proper place. Meanwhile, you could have ordered the Giants to fell two-thirds of their coarse fruit trees to make space for the delicate ones. You squandered your opportunity with the Lovers, Mr. Vane. You speculated upon them rather than aiding them!"
