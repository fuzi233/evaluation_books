Catherine was of those housewives whose hands held the whole dwelling together as a net holds fish: lift it but for a day, and something slipped through—milk left to sour, dough forgotten, a child’s stocking mislaid, the very kettle seeming to take offence and boil over. She knew it, and watched for a slack tide in chores to go to Sevenbergen; yet still some small domestic fate thwarted her. One afternoon it might truly have been done—if only Peter Buyskens’ mule had not been out of the way.

At last Eli asked her, bluntly and before all the family, whether it was true she had been thinking to visit Margaret Brandt.

“Ay, my man.”

“Then I do forbid you.”

“Oh, do you?”

“I do.”

“Then there is no more to be said, I suppose,” she answered, colouring to the brow.

“Not a word,” said Eli, and his face shut like a chest-lid.

When she was alone with Kate, Catherine turned her wrath—not on her husband, but on herself, as if her own tongue had betrayed her like a careless cook who lifts the cover to boast of a stew and lets all the savor fly.

“Behoved me rather go thither like a cat at a robin,” she whispered, fierce with shame. “But this was me all over. I am like a silly hen that can lay no egg without cackling, and convening all the house to rob her on’t. Next time you and I are after aught the least amiss, let’s do’t in Heaven’s name then and there, and not take time to think about it, far less talk; so then, if they take us to task we can say, alack we knew nought; we thought no ill; now, who’d ever? and so forth. For two pins I’d go thither in all their teeth.”

Defiance so wild and painted in such homely images startled Kate. “Nay, mother; with patience father will come round.”

“And so will Michaelmas; but when?” Catherine’s voice fell, and with it some softer hunger showed—hunger not for bread but for certainty. “And I was so bent on you seeing the girl. Then we could have put our heads together about her. Say what they will, there is no judging body or beast but by the eye. And were I to have fifty more sons I’d ne’er thwart one of them’s fancy, till such time as I had clapped my eyes upon her and seen Quicksands. Say you, I should have thought of that before condemning Gerard his fancy; but there—life is a school, and the lesson ne’er done; we put down one fault and take up t’other, and so go blundering here, and blundering there, till we blunder into our graves.”

“Mother,” said Kate timidly.

“Well, what is a-coming now? no good news though, by the look of you. What on earth can make the poor wretch so scared?”

“An avowal she hath to make,” faltered Kate.

“Now, there is a noble word for ye,” Catherine said, proud in spite of herself. “Our Gerard taught thee that, I’ll go bail. Come then, out with thy vowel.”

Kate’s breath came thin. “Well then—sooth to say—I have seen her.”

“And?”

“And spoken with her to boot.”

“And never told me? After this marvels are dirt.”

“Mother, you were so hot against her. I waited till I could tell you without angering you worse.”

“Ay,” Catherine answered, half sad, half bitter. “Like mother, like daughter; cowardice it is our bane. The others I whiles buffet, or how would the house fare? but did you, Kate, ever have harsh word or look from your poor mother, that you—Nay, I will not have ye cry, girl. Ten to one ye had your reason. So rise up, brave heart, and tell me all, better late than ne’er; and first and foremost, when ever and how ever went you to Sevenbergen wi’ your poor crutches, and I not know?”

“I never was there in my life; and, mammy dear, to say that I ne’er wished to see her that I will not, but I ne’er went nor sought to see her.”

“There now,” said Catherine, disputatious with relief, “said I not ’twas all unlike my girl to seek her unbeknown to me? Come now, for I’m all agog.”

“Then thus ’twas. It came to my ears—no matter how—and prithee, good mother, on my knees ne’er ask me how—that Gerard was a prisoner in the Stadthouse tower.”

“Ah!” Catherine’s sound was like a knife turned in a wound.

“By father’s behest, as ’twas pretended.”

A sigh came from Catherine that was almost a moan. “Blacker than I thought,” she muttered, as if the word itself had a taste like burnt crust.

“Giles and I went out at night to bid him be of good cheer. And there at the tower-foot was a brave lass, quite strange to me I vow, on the same errand.”

“Lookee there now, Kate.”

“At first we did properly frighten one another—through the place his bad name, and our poor heads being so full o’ divels—and we whitened a bit in moonshine. But next moment, quo’ I, ‘You are Margaret.’ ‘And you are Kate,’ quo’ she. Think on’t!”

“Did one ever? ’Twas Gerard! He will have been talking backards and forrards of thee to her, and her to thee.”

Kate kissed her—a single kiss that spoke agreement, gratitude, and a sudden return to their old oneness.

“Now then,” said Catherine, with a catch in her throat, “speak thy mind, child. Gerard is not here. Alas, what am I saying? would to Heaven he were.”

“Well then, mother, she is comely, and wrongs her picture but little.”

“Eh, dear; hark to young folk! I am for good acts, not good looks. Loves she my boy as he did ought to be loved?”

“Sevenbergen is farther from the Stadthouse than we are,” said Kate, thinking it out as if weighing flour, “yet she was there afore me.”

Catherine nodded, understanding.

“Nay, more, she had got him out ere I came. Ay—down from the captive’s tower.”

Catherine shook her head. “The highest tower for miles! It is not feasible.”

“’Tis sooth though. She and an old man she brought found means and wit to send him up a rope. There ’twas dangling from his prison, and our Giles went up it. When first I saw it hang, I said, ‘This is glamour.’ But when the frank lass’s arms came round me, and her bosom did beat on mine, and her cheeks wet, then said I, ‘’Tis not glamour: ’tis love.’ For she is not like me, but lusty and able; and, dear heart, even I, poor frail creature, do feel sometimes as I could move the world for them I love. I love you, mother. And she loves Gerard.”

“God bless her for’t!” cried Catherine, and said it again, as if blessing were a kind of seasoning the heart needed. “God bless her!”

“But—”

“But what, lamb?”

“Her love—is it for very certain honest? ’Tis most strange; but that very thing which hath warmed your heart hath somewhat cooled mine towards her; poor soul. She is no wife, you know, mother, when all is done.”

“Humph! They have stood at the altar together.”

“Ay, but they went as they came—maid and bachelor.”

“The parson, saith he so?”

“Nay, for that I know not.”

“Then I’ll take no man’s word but his in such a tangled skein.” Catherine’s eyes narrowed; her mind, like a careful housekeeper’s, began to sort and stack facts. After a little she said, “Natheless art right, girl. I’ll to Sevenbergen alone. A wife I am but not a slave. We are all in the dark here. And she holds the clue. I must question her, and no one by—least of all you. I’ll not take any lily to a house wi’ a spot, no, not to a palace o’ gold and silver.”

The more Catherine chewed that talk over—slow, persistent chewing, as one works a tough crust—the more she felt drawn toward Margaret; and besides, curiosity, that strong spice which sharpens the dullest broth, tugged her on. At last, one fine day after dinner, when the house still held the smell of pottage and woodsmoke and the last loaf’s warm crumb, she whispered to Kate, “Keep the house from going to pieces, an ye can,” and put on her best kirtle and hood, her scarlet clocked hose and her new shoes, and went briskly off to Sevenbergen, troubling no man’s mule.

In Sevenbergen she asked where Margaret Brandt lived. The first person shook his head: “The name is strange to me.” A little farther a girl of fifteen stood in a doorway. Catherine asked her; the girl called into the house, loud as a jackdaw, “Father, here is another after that magician’s daughter.”

The man came out and said Peter Brandt’s cottage was just outside the east side. “You may see the chimney hence,” and pointed. Then, with a kind of relish in the telling, “But you will not find them there, neither father nor daughter; they have left the town this week, bless you.”

“Say not so, good man, and me walken all the way from Tergou.”

“From Tergou? then you must ha’ met the soldier.”

“What soldier? ay, I did meet a soldier.”

“Well, then, yon soldier was here seeking that self-same Margaret.”

“Ay, and warn’t a mad with us because she was gone?” put in the girl, with the heedless cruelty of youth. “His long beard and her cheek are no strangers, I warrant.”

“Say no more than ye know,” Catherine snapped, the way one slaps a child’s hand from the dough. “You are young to take to slandering your elders. Stay! tell we more about this soldier, good man.”

The man shrugged. “Nay, I know no more than that he came hither seeking Margaret Brandt, and I told him she and her father had made a moonlight flit on’t this day sennight, and that some thought the devil had flown away with them, being magicians. ‘And,’ says he, ‘the devil fly away with thee for thy ill news;’ that was my thanks. ‘But I doubt ’tis a lie,’ said he. ‘An you think so,’ said I, ‘go and see.’ ‘I will,’ said he, and burst out wi’ a hantle o’ gibberish—my wife thinks ’twas curses—and hied him to the cottage. Presently back a comes and sings t’other tune. ‘You were right and I was wrong,’ says he, and shoves a silver coin in my hand. Show it the wife, some of ye; then she’ll believe me; I have been called a liar once to-day.”

“It needs not,” said Catherine, though she took the coin and looked at it, weighing its cold shine.

“And he seemed quiet and sad like, didn’t he now, wench?” asked the man.

“That a did,” said the girl warmly; “and, dame, he was just as pretty a man as ever I clapped eyes on. Cheeks like a rose, and shining beard, and eyes in his head like sloes.”

“I saw he was well bearded,” said Catherine, “but for the rest, at my age I scan them not as when I was young and foolish. But he seemed right civil: doffed his bonnet to me as I had been a queen, and I did drop him my best reverence, for manners beget manners. But little I wist he had been her light o’ love, and most likely the—Who bakes for this town?”

The man’s eyes widened at so swift a turn, like someone interrupted mid-gossip by talk of grain and ovens.

“Well, dame, there be two; John Bush and Eric Donaldson, they both bide in this street.”

Catherine went to them, but her step had lost its cocking briskness; her foot came flatter now, as if the road itself had grown heavy. Bush said the Brandts were not customers. Donaldson said, “Not a stiver: his daughter had come round and paid him the very night they went. Didn’t believe they owed a copper in the town.”

“Can you tell me what sort this Margaret was?” Catherine asked, turning to go.

“Well, somewhat too reserved for my taste. I like a chatty customer—when I’m not too busy. But she bore a high character for being a good daughter.”

“’Tis no small praise,” Catherine said, and could not keep the respect from her voice.

“A well-looking lass, I am told?” she added, though she tried to sound indifferent.

“Why, whence come you, wyfe?”

“From Tergou.”

“Oh, ay. Well you shall judge: the lads clept her ‘the beauty of Sevenbergen;’ the lasses did scout it merrily, and terribly pulled her to pieces, and found so many faults no two could agree where the fault lay.”

“That is enough,” said Catherine. “I see, the bakers are no fools in Sevenbergen, and the young women no shallower than in other burghs.”

She bought a manchet—partly out of habit, for she kept a shop, partly out of justice, and partly so she might carry home, in her hands and nose, a measure by which to praise her own bread. The loaf steamed faintly through the paper; its crust gave a clean, nutty scent, and she breathed it as one might breathe courage. Yet she walked back to Tergou dejected, the town’s sounds behind her—the clack of wooden shoes, the far-off hammering, the stray shout—dulling into her own thoughts.

Kate met her outside the town, eyes bright with expectation.

“Well, Kate, lass,” Catherine said, and her voice broke, “it is a happy thing I went; I am heartbroken. Gerard has been sore abused. The child is none of ourn, nor the mother from this hour.”

“Alas, mother, I fathom not your meaning.”

“Ask me no more, girl, but never mention her name to me again. That is all.”

Kate bowed her head with a humble sigh, and they went home together through the familiar hush of fields, where even the birds seemed to keep their distance from such news.

Within, the hearthlight was alive, and a soldier sat tranquilly by their fire, as if the crackle and the smell of peat belonged to him. When they entered, he rose at once and saluted them civilly.

They stood and looked at him—Kate with a small surprise, but Catherine with a great deal, and indignation rising like yeast.

“What makes you here?” was Catherine’s greeting.

“I came to seek after Margaret.”

“Well, we know no such person.”

“Say not so, dame; sure you know her by name—Margaret Brandt.”

“We have heard of her for that matter—to our cost.”

“Comes, dame, prithee tell me at least where she bides.”

“I know not where she bides, and care not.”

Denys’s jaw tightened; he felt the lie in it as plainly as grit in bread. He bit his lip. “Well, I looked to find myself in an enemy’s country at this Tergou; but maybe if ye knew all ye would not be so dour.”

“I do know all,” Catherine replied bitterly. “This morn I knew nought.” Then, arms akimbo, she lifted her voice and flashed her eyes, wondering at his cheek to sit by that hearth of all hearths.

“May Satan fly away with your hearth to the lake of fire and brimstone,” shouted Denys—in fluent Flemish that rang against their rafters like a struck pan. “Your own servant bade me sit there till you came, else I had ne’er troubled your hearth. My malison on it, and on the churlish roof-tree that greets an unoffending stranger this way!” He strode, scowling, to the door.

“Oh! oh!” cried Catherine, frightened and, beneath it, pricked by conscience. The fierceness drained out of her as if someone had opened the tap of her courage; she dropped into a chair and burst into tears. Kate followed suit quietly, almost at once.

Denys halted at the threshold, dismayed at the ruin his words had made. The house sounded suddenly different: the fire snapped too loud, the bench creaked, even the little hiss of the kettle seemed to reproach him.

“Nay, wife,” he said, turning back, “weep not neither for a soldier’s hasty word. I mean not all I said. Why, your house is your own, and what right in it have I? There now—I’ll go.”

“What is to do?” said a grave, manly voice.

Eli had come in from the shop. The smell of flour clung to him, and cold air with it, as if he brought the outside world on his shoulders.

“Here is a ruffian been a-scolding of your women folk and making them cry,” Denys said, half angry, half ashamed.

“Little Kate, what is’t? for ruffians do not use to call themselves ruffians,” said Eli sensibly.

Ere she could speak, Catherine broke in between sobs. “Hold your tongue, girl. Muriel bade him sat down, and I knew not that, and wyted on him; and he was going and leaving his malison on us, root and branch. I was never so becursed in all my days—oh! oh! oh!”

“You were both somewhat to blame; both you and he,” said Eli calmly, as if settling a quarrel over a measure of grain. “However, what the servant says the master should still stand to. We keep not open house, but yet we are not poor enough to grudge a seat at our hearth in a cold day to a wayfarer with an honest face, and, as I think, a wounded man. So end all malice, and sit ye down!”

“Wounded?” cried mother and daughter together.

“Think you a soldier slings his arm for sport?”

“Nay, ’tis but an arrow,” Denys answered, forcing cheer, “a trifle.”

“But an arrow?” Kate said, horror gathered into one small voice. “Where were our eyes, mother?”

“Nay, in good sooth a trifle,” Denys went on. “Which, however, I will pray mesdames to accept as an excuse for my vivacity. ’Tis these little foolish trifling wounds that fret a man, worthy sir. Why, look ye now, sweeter temper than our Gerard never breathed, yet, when the bear did but strike a piece no bigger than a crown out of his calf, he turned so hot and choleric y’had said he was no son of yours, but got by the good knight Sir John Pepper on his wife dame Mustard; who is this? a dwarf? your servant, Master Giles.”

“Your servant, soldier,” roared the newcomer.

Denys started: he had not counted on meeting a powder-bomb in the shape of a man.

Yet it was Denys’s mention of Gerard that changed the air. All three—Eli, Catherine, and Kate—came creeping nearer him as if drawn by scent from a remembered kitchen: the name of their absent one was like hot bread set on a board. They stared down into him, lips parted, as if he were some marvel set by their fire.

Amazement grew into agitation.

“Now hush!” said Eli. “Let none speak but I.” His voice shook, though his back remained straight. “Young man, in God’s name who are you, that know us though we know you not, and that shake our hearts speaking to us of—the absent—our poor rebellious son: whom Heaven forgive and bless?”

“What, master,” said Denys, lowering his tone, “hath he not writ to you? hath he not told you of me—Denys of Burgundy?”

“He hath writ—but three lines, and named not Denys of Burgundy, nor any stranger.”

“Ay, I mind. The long letter was to his sweetheart, this Margaret, and she has decamped—plague take her—and how I am to find her Heaven knows.”

“What,” Catherine broke in sharply, “she is not your sweetheart then?”

“Who, dame? an’t please you.”

“Why, Margaret Brandt.”

“How can my comrade’s sweetheart be mine? I know her not from Noah’s niece; how should I? I never saw her.”

“Whist with this idle chat, Kate,” Eli said impatiently, and turned full on Denys again, like a judge. “How came you to know Gerard, our son? Prithee now think on a parent’s cares, and answer me straightforward, like a soldier as thou art.”

“And shall,” Denys replied, and sat more squarely by the fire, as if to give an account in a guard-room. “I was paid off at Flushing, and started for Burgundy. On the German frontier I lay at the same inn with Gerard. I fancied him. I said, ‘Be my comrade.’ He was loth at first; consented presently. Many a weary league we trode together. Never were truer comrades—never will be while earth shall last. First I left my route a bit to be with him; then he his to be with me. We talked of Sevenbergen and Tergou a thousand times; and of all in this house. We had our troubles on the road; but battling them together made them light.

“I saved his life from a bear; he mine in the Rhine—for he swims like a duck and I like a hod o’ bricks. And one another’s lives at an inn in Burgundy, where we two held a room for a good hour against seven cut-throats, and crippled one and slew two. And your son did his devoir like a man, and met the stoutest champion I ever countered, and spitted him like a sucking-pig. Else I had not been here.

“But just when all was fair, and I was to see him safe aboard ship for Rome—if not to Rome itself—met us that son of a—the Lord Anthony of Burgundy, and his men, making for Flanders, then in insurrection, tore us by force apart, took me where I got some broad pieces in hand, and a broad arrow in my shoulder, and left my poor Gerard lonesome. At that sad parting, soldier though I be, these eyes did rain salt scalding tears, and so did his, poor soul. His last word to me was, ‘Go, comfort Margaret!’ so here I be. Mine to him was, ‘Think no more of Rome. Make for Rhine, and down stream home.’ Now say, for you know best, did I advise him well or ill?”

“Soldier,” said Eli, and reached out his hand as if to clasp a plank in rough water, “take my hand. God bless thee! God bless thee!” His lip quivered; he could not find more, nor needed to.

Catherine said nothing at all. She darted from the room and bade Muriel bring the best that was in the house. She returned with wood in both arms and heaped the fire till it roared and threw a brighter, kinder light; then she pulled from the press a snow-white cloth, smelling faintly of flax and lavendered airing, and hurried to lay it as if setting a table were a prayer. But suddenly her strength ran out of her body like broth from a cracked pot; she sat down, white-lipped.

“Father!” cried Kate, quick-eyed with love.

Denys sprang up; Eli waved him back and flung a little water sharply in Catherine’s face. She gasped, “So sudden. My poor boy!” Eli leaned and murmured to Denys, “Take no notice. She thinks of him night and day.” They all pretended not to see her weakness; she shook it off and, hands trembling, smoothed the cloth—yet a tear or two fell upon it, darkening the white like drops of wine.

Then the house turned hospitable in earnest. They made much of Denys; they stuffed him, crammed him, pressed him as if feeding him could somehow feed the hollow place Gerard had left behind. Catherine’s pantry gave up its best: bread with a crackling crust, cheese that smelled of cellar and summer pasture, and warm ale that carried the malt’s sweet bitterness. The fire’s talk—its pops and sighs—kept time with their questions; and outside, the night lay listening, the village’s small sounds muffled, as if even the dogs had been told to hush.

They gathered round Denys and kept filling his glass in turn while, by that genial blaze and ruby drink and eager faces, he told all he had said—and far more besides: little turns of speech of Gerard’s, how he cleaned his hands and tended his clothes, how he could not abide coarse linen, how he had a proverb ready as a cook has salt.

Giles, after a while, crept in softly and curled himself before the fire, gazing at the soldier with a reverence almost canine. And when Denys, rough as oak-bark yet full of warmth for their absent one, let fall some proof of his rare affection for Gerard, Kate—timorous as a bird—stole her little hand into his huge brown palm. It lay there a moment like a spoonful of cream spilled on a platter; then, shyly, she pinched the ball of his thumb and served for a measurer of his heart’s beat, as if her fingers could read truth more surely than words.

Denys, who had known louder audiences, learned quickly that here the story’s danger could not be left to stand stark. When Gerard was in too sharp a peril, the women’s faces whitened and their throats made small, wet sounds; and in common humanity he was forced to spoil his own suspense.

“Now, dame, take not on till ye hear the end,” he would say, softening a horror with a jest. “Ma’amselle, let not your cheek blanch so; courage! it looks ugly; but you shall hear how we won through. Had he miscarried, and I at hand, would I be alive?”

Only once did he repay himself for the loss of such fine terrors: when he told of falling into the Rhine and sinking, Kate pinched him—hard. Denys felt it and, in his wicked way, lingered in that watery tale longer than needful, treading the imagined current as he spoke, chaffing the officers, making himself almost a fish, until even Eli’s stoic face tightened. At last he let the river release him, for very shame, and the pressure of Kate’s little fingers eased.

It was eleven o’clock—an hour unheard-of in that house—yet no one had marked time, for grief and hope were keeping their own clock. Denys had still much to tell when the door opened quietly and Cornelis and Sybrandt slipped in, hang-dog, smelling of ale and the last drop of their mysterious funds. Catherine feared Eli would rebuke them before the stranger, but he only looked sadly and motioned them to sit down in silence.

Denys alone seemed discomposed; he knitted his brows and eyed the two thoughtfully, gloomily, as if weighing them the way a man weighs flour and finds it light. Then he turned to Catherine.

“What say you, dame? the rest to-morrow; for I am somewhat weary, and it waxes late.”

“So be it,” said Eli.

Denys rose to go to his inn; but Catherine, all at once herself again where hospitality was concerned, stopped him like a hand on a door.

“And think you to lie from this house? Gerard’s room has been got ready for you hours agone; the sheets I’ll not say much for, seeing I spun the flax and wove the web.”

“Then would I lie in them blindfold,” Denys said gallantly. “Ah, dame, our poor Gerard was the one for fine linen. He could hardly forgive the honest Germans their coarse flax; and whene’er my traitors of countrymen did amiss, a would excuse them, saying, ‘Well, well; bonnes toiles sont en Bourgogne’—that means, there be good lenten cloths in Burgundy. But indeed he beat all for bywords and cleanliness.”
