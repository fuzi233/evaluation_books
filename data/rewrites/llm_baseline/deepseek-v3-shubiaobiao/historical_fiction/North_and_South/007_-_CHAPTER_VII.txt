*"Mist clogs the sunshine,
Smoky dwarf houses
Hem me round everywhere."*

The following afternoon, the train carried them twenty miles from Milton-Northern, branching onto the narrow line that led to Heston. The town sprawled in a single, meandering street, parallel to the seashore, its character distinct from the genteel bathing resorts of southern England—sturdier, more purposeful. The carts bore iron fittings rather than wood, the people moved with brisk intent even in leisure, and the colors of the world seemed muted, enduring.

Margaret noted the absence of smock-frocks among the country folk—too cumbersome for machinery, she supposed. In southern towns, shopkeepers lounged at their thresholds, savoring the air; here, idle hands found occupation in needless tasks, ribbons unrolled and re-rolled. The contrasts pressed upon her as she and her mother searched for lodgings. Two nights at hotels had strained their purse, and they took the first clean rooms available.

For the first time in days, Margaret felt rest—a dreamy repose by the sea, the rhythmic lap of waves, the cries of donkey-boys, the salt-kissed air warm even in late November. She let the scenes drift past, unwilling to anchor them in thought, content to exist in the present. But the future loomed, inevitable.

Milton-Northern announced itself miles before they arrived—a leaden smudge on the horizon, stark against the pale winter sky. As they neared, the air soured with smoke, the grassy sweetness of Heston replaced by soot. The streets unfurled in rigid lines, rows of brick houses interrupted by the hulking factories, chimneys belching black plumes. The thoroughfares clogged with wagons, all laden with cotton—raw in sacks, woven into bales. The crowds were well-dressed but slovenly, lacking the threadbare polish of London’s poor.

Mr. Hale pointed out New Street, Milton’s bustling artery, its growth having enriched their friend Mr. Bell. Margaret scarcely listened, her attention snagged by the oppressive energy of the place. They alighted at a hotel, where a message awaited: Mr. Thornton, a local manufacturer, had called.

When Margaret entered the sitting room, she found not the clergyman her father expected, but a young woman of quiet command—her straw bonnet plain, her shawl draped like an empress’s mantle. Thornton, unprepared for her presence, faltered. She met his gaze without fluster, her poise unshaken by his rough Milton bearing.

Their exchange was brief, strained. He spoke in clipped sentences; she replied with courteous detachment, her pride mistaken for disdain. By the time Mr. Hale returned, Thornton’s pride had hardened into resentment. Only the elder Hale’s warmth thawed the tension, their talk turning to mutual acquaintances and the house in Crampton—a practical choice, though its gaudy wallpaper dismayed Margaret.

Thornton left with a stiff bow, his departure a relief. Margaret, exhausted, spared little thought for him—a man of thirty, unremarkable in feature, yet formidable in presence. "Not a gentleman," she admitted, "but not common." Her father bristled at the implied slight to his friend.

Later, when they moved into the Crampton house, the garish paper was gone. The landlord claimed no credit, but Margaret suspected Thornton’s intervention—a small mercy, unspoken. Milton’s grime and clamor awaited them, but for now, the walls were bare, and the future, though stern, was theirs to shape.
