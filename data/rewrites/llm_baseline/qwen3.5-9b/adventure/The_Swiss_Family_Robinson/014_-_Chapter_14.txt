Scarcely had I finished my pottery when dark, ominous clouds gathered, heralding the arrival of another wet season. The rains poured down relentlessly, forcing us to abandon our daily outdoor excursions. Even within the comfort of our spacious new home, surrounded by our varied projects, time seemed to drag heavily. Morale dipped low; even brief pauses in the storm, offering opportunities for quick rides, failed to lift our spirits.

Fritz and I observed this gloom. "Father," Fritz suggested, "why shouldn't we build a canoe? Something faster and easier to handle than our current vessels? I often dream of a light skiff to skim across the water."

The idea thrilled everyone except my wife. She, who always felt uneasy on the sea, argued that our risk of drowning with the existing pinnace and canoe was already sufficient. Adding another fragile craft seemed unnecessary danger to her. However, my reassurances quickly calmed her fears. I promised that the boat I planned would not be a flimsy shell, but a sturdy, safe vessel modeled after the Greenlander's *cajack*. I resolved to engage the children in the project and create a true masterpiece.

Work began immediately. We constructed a skeleton of whalebone, reinforcing the sides and creating a continuous deck from split bamboo canes. The deck spanned the entire length of the boat, leaving only a central square opening for the rower. The work consumed our total attention until the rain finally ceased and the brilliant sun returned. Since our doorway was narrow, we completed the final assembly outdoors. We then covered the sides and deck with seal-skin, sealing every seam meticulously with rubber (caoutchouc).

The resulting *cajack* looked peculiar, yet it was remarkably light—so light that one person could lift it with a single hand. When we launched her, she bounced upon the waves like a giant rubber ball. Fritz was unanimously named her captain. Before he could venture into the water, however, my wife insisted on providing him with a special swimming suit. "Should his boat leak from a sharp rock or a fish's fin," she reasoned, "he needs a chance to survive."

Though I didn't view the boat as fragile as she imagined, I happily assisted. The result was a curious double-waistcoat made of linen treated with a rubber solution, sealed with caoutchouc, and designed to be inflated with air through a small valve.

Meanwhile, nature flourished around us. Seeds scattered earlier sprouted into magnificent crops, and the verandah draped itself in fragrant, climbing vines. The air buzzed with birdlife; the island teemed with vitality. Soon, Fritz proudly tested his new suit. Donning the inflatable garment, he paddled effortlessly across the bay toward Shark Island, while we followed in our boat. The experiment was a roaring success. Despite initial laughter, Ernest, Jack, and Franz begged for their own suits.

Our visit to the small colony of settlers we had established the previous autumn revealed they were thriving. Footprints confirmed that the antelopes we had protected were utilizing our shelters. We left them supplies of maize and salt, then turned our attention to the shoreline. Here, the boys collected beautiful shells and delicate corals for their museum. My wife directed them to gather colorful seaweed, noting its utility.

As we returned, my wife selected specific seaweed leaves with serrated edges. Back home, she washed and dried them in the oven. Curiosity got the better of me. "Is this a substitute for tobacco?" I asked jokingly. She smiled, revealing her dislike for the habit, and playfully suggested, "Perhaps a mattress stuffed with these leaves would be wonderfully cool in summer?" Her twinkling eyes hinted at a deeper secret.

Later, exhausted from a long expedition, we lounged on the verandah. Suddenly, my wife appeared with a porcelain dish containing a translucent, glittering jelly. "Ambrosia!" Fritz exclaimed upon tasting it. It was divine.

"Aha," my wife laughed. "An excellent substitute for tobacco, far superior to the nasty weed. Behold the product of my mysterious seaweed."

"My dear wife," I pressed, "where did you learn this? How did you obtain it?"

"While visiting my Dutch friends at the Cape," she explained, "I saw this dish and recognized the leaves on Shark Island. The process is simple: soak the leaves in fresh water daily for a week, then boil them with orange juice, citron, and sugar."

Delighted, we thanked her profusely. The boys eagerly vowed to return to the island to gather more leaves. Accompanying them, I inspected our plantations. Palms and mangroves had grown marvelously, and seeds cast randomly among rocky crevices had taken root, beginning to clothe the bare boulders. We also discovered a sparkling spring nestled among the rocks, frequented by thirsty antelopes.

Next, we checked our colony on Whale Island. Rabbits had multiplied, nibbling tender shoots everywhere except the coconut palms. Determined to protect our future food sources, I helped the boys erect protective hedges of thorny bushes around the palm stems. Noticing that rabbits had also gnawed some of the seaweed, I collected samples for later study.

We then turned our gaze to the massive skeleton of a whale, bleached white by sun and rain. Seeing the potential in the vertebrae, I separated a dozen joints and towed them to the boat. A new invention occupied my mind: a crushing machine. Stone was impossible, but whalebones might suffice. I planned to cut wooden blocks to support the mechanism.

Seeking materials, I saddled my horse, Storm, intending to cross the bridge. However, upon reaching the cassava field, I found it devastated. Examining the tracks, which resembled those of wild boar, I pursued the trail. Near our old potato patch, the dogs, Floss and Bruno, alerted me to the intruders. An entire family of fierce pigs stood at bay, snarling savagely. Raising my gun, I fired twice; two pigs fell, and the rest fled. I retrieved the fallen ones and continued my journey.

Soon, I located a suitable tree for my machine and marked it. Returning home, I found Ernest waiting to help skin the young pigs for dinner, hoping the older boys had returned. Late that evening, the sound of hooves announced their arrival. Jack thundered in on his horse, followed by Fritz and Franz carrying heavy game bags. Their haul included four birds, a kangaroo, twenty muskrats, a monkey, hares, beaver rats, and a bundle of thistles.

Jack recounted the thrill of riding an ostrich, describing the terrifying speed and wind pressure. "Father, you must make me a mask with glass eyes, or I'll be blinded!" he pleaded.

"Indeed," I replied calmly. "First, I am under no obligation to fulfill every request. Second, I believe you are capable of crafting such a device yourself. Nevertheless, congratulations on your abundant catch. You must have worked hard."

He protested that he forgot our anxiety at home, but I dismissed his concerns, urging him to check the animals and join us for supper.

That night, while enjoying roasted pig and fragrant mead, Fritz detailed their day's adventures. Traps near Woodlands yielded muskrats and beaver rats, while hunting secured a kangaroo. Fritz presented cuttings from cinnamon and apple trees for the garden. Finally, he showed me the thistles, believing them useful for wool production. Correctly identified as the "fuller's teazle," I noted their sharp thorns, essential for raising the nap of cloth.

Resolved to process the bounty efficiently, I decided to build a machine to flay the animals. Discovering a large syringe in the surgeon's chest, I modified it by fitting valves and perforating the stopper to create a powerful air pump.

The boys erupted in laughter at the prospect of "skinning a kangaroo with a squirt." Undeterred, I hung the kangaroo by its hind legs, made a small incision, and pumped air forcefully between the skin and the carcass. The hide distended, changing the animal's shape entirely. Continuing until the skin was nearly detached, I made a bold cut down the belly, releasing the ligaments. The hide slid off with ease.

"What a splendid plan!" cried the boys.

"For a simple reason," I explained. "Animal skin attaches to flesh via delicate fibers connected to tiny air bladders. By forcing air into these bladders, the fibers stretch and eventually snap. The skin becomes free-floating."

This technique, known to Greenlanders for centuries, proved invaluable. In just two days, all the skins were removed and prepared.

With the skins ready, I summoned the boys to help procure timber for the crushing machine. We spent two days felling a large tree, guiding its fall with ropes and sawing it into precise blocks. Using these blocks alongside the whale vertebrae, I swiftly completed the machine.

Just as preparations neared completion, a surprise awaited us. Our chickens returned from the fields with full crops, indicating they had feasted elsewhere. Rushing to the cornfield, I found the grain perfectly ripe. The sheer volume of the harvest overwhelmed my wife. "What of our potatoes and manioc?" she worried.

"Don't fret," I assured her. "Digging in this light soil is easy, and we can leave the younger plants to regrow. Let us adopt the Italian method of harvesting: it saves time and labor. We aim for two crops a year anyway."
