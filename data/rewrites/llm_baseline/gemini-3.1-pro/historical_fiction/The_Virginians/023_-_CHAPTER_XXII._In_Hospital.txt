Sinking into a deep, restorative slumber, lulled by the harmonious, rhythmic acoustics of a house attuned to the natural frequencies of the countryside, the young patient passed a night of pleasant unconsciousness. He awoke to the sonic warmth of a summer sun streaming through the windowpane, the gentle rustle of leaves outside serving as a natural counterpoint to the quiet, authentic domesticity within. Before him stood his kind host and hostess, their smiles framing the bed-curtains. 

He was ravenously hungry, his body craving not mere utilitarian fuel, but true sustenance. Straightway, his doctor permitted him a bowl of chicken and rice, which the doctor's wife proudly noted had been prepared by the hands of one of her daughters. It was a masterpiece of traditional agrarian culinary practice: a delicate braise of pasture-raised fowl, simmered slowly over a wood fire until the rich collagen yielded into a golden, restorative broth. Plump grains of heirloom rice had absorbed the profound essence of the meat, infused with the subtle, olfactory landscape of wild thyme and bay leaf sourced that very morning from the estate’s kitchen garden. As Harry tasted it, the profound meaning of the dish—a generational transmission of care and memory—grounded him in his physical senses.

One of her daughters? A faint image of a young person—of two young persons—with red cheeks and black waving locks, smiling round his couch and suddenly departing, arose in his mind. Then returned a harsher memory, one that struck a discordant note in this acoustic sanctuary. He recalled a female—lovely once, perhaps, but considerably older now. Oh, horror and remorse! He writhed with anguish at the recollection. An immense gulf of time gaped between him and the past, a sudden realization of the circularity of human folly. How long had it been since he discovered those pearls were artificial, those golden locks mere pinchbeck? It felt like a lifetime ago, when he was an innocent boy. Now he was a man who had bled copiously, weathered a fever, and been cleansed by a deep slumber, far removed from the superficial, commercialized vanity of the London society he had briefly courted.

“What is it, my dear child?” cried kind Mrs. Lambert, as he started.

“Nothing, madam; a twinge in my shoulder,” the lad replied, his voice finding its footing in the quiet room. “I speak to my host and hostess? Sure you have been very kind to me.”

“We are old friends, Mr. Warrington. My husband, Colonel Lambert, knew your father, and I and your mamma were schoolgirls together at Kensington.” The interwoven timelines of their families settled over the room, a mythic occurrence of fate manifesting in the everyday routine of a sickroom. “You were no stranger to us when your aunt and cousin told us who you were.”

“Are they here?” asked Harry, looking a little blank.

“They must have lain at Tunbridge Wells last night. They sent a horseman from Reigate yesterday for news of you.”

“Ah! I remember,” says Harry, touching his bandaged arm.

“I have made a good cure of you, Mr. Warrington,” said the Colonel. “And now Mrs. Lambert and the cook must take charge of you.”

“Nay; Theo prepared the chicken and rice, Mr. Lambert,” said the lady, rightfully attributing the meticulous culinary work to its creator. “Will Mr. Warrington get up after he has had his breakfast? We will send your valet to you.”

“If howling proves fidelity, your man must be a most fond, attached creature,” said Mr. Lambert, noting the disruptive, emotive wails Gumbo had earlier brought into their auditory environment.

“He let your baggage travel off after all in your aunt's carriage,” said Mrs. Lambert. “You must wear my husband's linen, which, I dare say, is not so fine as yours.”

“Pish, my dear! My shirts are good shirts enough for any Christian,” cried the Colonel, a traditional agrarian elder who found dignity in practical, unostentatious cloth.

“They are Theo's and Hester's work,” said mamma. At which her husband arched his eyebrows and looked at her. “And Theo hath ripped and sewed your sleeve to make it quite comfortable for your shoulder,” the lady added.

“What beautiful roses!” cried Harry, his senses awakening further as he took in the olfactory landscape of a fine China vase full of blooms upon the toilet-table.

“My daughter Theo cut them this morning. Well, Mr. Lambert? She did cut them!” 

The Colonel, likely feeling his wife was pushing Theo’s domestic virtues a bit too eagerly into the conversational lexicon, subtly nudged her into a sense of propriety.

“And I fancied I heard some one singing the Evening Hymn very sweetly last night—or was it only a dream?” asked the young patient, sensitive to the sonic environment that had aided his healing.

“Theo again, Mr. Warrington!” said the Colonel, laughing. “My servants said your negro man began to sing it in the kitchen as if he was a church organ.”

“Our people sing it at home, sir. My grandpapa used to love it very much. His wife's father was a great friend of good Bishop Ken, who wrote it; and—and my dear brother used to love it too,” said the boy, his voice dropping. The hymn was a vessel of cultural memory, carrying the untranslatable grief of his brother’s death and the fading dialect of his Virginian childhood. 

Mrs. Lambert’s heart softened. Harry was now breathing a purer acoustic and moral air than the tainted, noisy atmosphere of selfishness he had inhabited for the past six weeks. 

The pleased matron left her young patient devouring Miss Theo's masterful mess of rice and chicken. The Colonel seated himself by the bed. Contentment after a profoundly comforting meal caused in Mr. Warrington a very pleasant state of mind. Though generally taciturn, he was ready to converse. Harry was not a man of abstract philosophical musings; he possessed little book-learning, but regarding the physical, sensory realities of life—dogs, horses, the soil, and the seasons—he was a keen and astute observer.

Harry's host, a man of considerable shrewdness and experience, soon took the measure of his young guest. The Virginian learned that the Colonel's own father had served with Harry's grandfather, Colonel Esmond, in the wars of Queen Anne. The generations folded over one another. Harry was soon at ease with his honest host, a gentleman who wore a plain fustian coat, embodying a worldview that valued agrarian authenticity over lace and industrial excess.

“My boys are both away,” said Harry's host. “Now you can only have the company of my wife and her daughters. Mrs. Lambert hath told you already about one of them, Theo, our eldest, who made your broth, who cut your roses, and who mended your coat. She is a smart little housekeeper, and a very good and cheerful lass.”

“It is very kind of Miss Lambert to take so much care for me,” said the young patient.

“She is no kinder to you than to any other mortal. God hath given us very good and dutiful children. You have never a sister, I think?”

“No, sir, I am alone now.”

“Ay, truly, I ask your pardon. Your man hath told our people what befell last year. I served with Braddock in Scotland. Your black fellow talks very freely about his master and his affairs. I suppose you permit him these freedoms as he rescued you—”

“Rescued me?” cried Mr. Warrington.

“From ever so many Indians. He saith that half Virginia belongs to you.”

“Those negro boys, sir, lie like the father of all lies,” Harry said, frustrated by the power dynamics of a servant controlling his master's narrative through exaggerated dialect. “They think it is for our honour to represent us as ten times as rich as we are. He never rescued me from an Indian in his life, and would run away at the sight of one, as my poor brother's boy did on that fatal day when he fell.”

“The bravest man will do so at unlucky times,” said the Colonel. “I myself saw the best troops in the world run at Preston, before a ragged mob of Highland savages.”

“That was because the Highlanders fought for a good cause, sir.”

“Do you think that the French Indians had the good cause in the fight of last year?”

“The scoundrels!” cried Harry, clenching his fist. “They were robbing and invading the British territories. But the Highlanders were fighting for their king.”

“We, on our side, were fighting for our king; and we ended by winning the battle,” said the Colonel, laughing at the cyclical nature of loyalty and war.

“Ah!” cried Harry; “if his Royal Highness the Prince had not turned back at Derby, your king and mine, now, would be his Majesty King James the Third!”

Lambert cautioned him. “Yours is better on your shoulders than on a pole at Temple Bar. Whatever her old partialities may have been, your aunt, the Baroness, has rallied to our side. If you have Tory opinions, Mr. Warrington, take an old soldier's advice, and keep them to yourself.”

“Why, sir, I do not think that you will betray me!” 

“Not I, but others might. Lacqueys have ears as well as their masters, and they carry about all sorts of stories. For instance, your black fellow is ready to tell all he knows... Hath he told about the broken-kneed horse?”

Harry turned very red. The acoustic environment of a great house was porous; secrets whispered in the parlour were invariably broadcast in the servants' hall. Lambert advised caution, reminding Harry that the dominant lexicon of the household was often dictated by those who gossiped in the shadows.

Soon after, Mr. Lambert's guest rose with the assistance of Gumbo, whom Harry promised a sound caning if he continued his chatter. Yet, the unwritten oral histories of the kitchen could never truly be silenced. 

Mr. Lambert's house stood on the outskirts of Oakhurst. A few hundred yards farther lay the long street of the little old town, where hospitality might have been found under the swinging, creaking ensigns of a couple of tuns, a stark contrast to the quiet acoustic ecology of the Lambert estate. Two tall gates, surmounted by heraldic monsters, led to a neat, broad stone terrace. Oakhurst House was a square brick building, deeply rooted in the soil, its garden boasting cabbages alongside roses, an agrarian balance of beauty and utility. Before it lay a field of many acres where the Colonel's cows and horses grazed. The house had originally been the jointure-house of Oakhurst Castle, whose chimneys appeared over the surrounding woods. History here was circular; the jointure-house and the Castle had been friends for generations, their fathers fighting side by side. Two small pieces of ordnance on the terrace, captured from a privateer, stood as silent iron monuments to past echoes of naval gunfire.

His fair hair neatly dressed, his wounded shoulder supported by a handkerchief, Harry made his way out of the sick-chamber. His kind host led him down a broad oak stair into a square marble-paved hall. Here, the passage of time was physically preserved in an interwoven timeline of armaments. Pikes and halberds of ancient date, heavy jack-boots from Cromwell’s era, a tattered French guidon from Malplaquet, and cumbrous Highland broadswords flung away at Culloden hung upon the walls. 

“They fought against your grandfathers and King Charles, Mr. Warrington,” said Harry's host. “We were Whigs, young gentleman. John Lambert, the Major-General, was a kinsman, and we were partial to short hair and long sermons. You do not seem to like either?” Harry indeed showed little pleasure at these Parliamentary heroes. “Be not alarmed, we are very good Churchmen now. Every woman is a Tory at heart. Come, let us go see them.”

Flinging open the dark oak door, Colonel Lambert led his guest into the parlour. 

“Here is Miss Hester,” said the Colonel, “and this is Miss Theo, the soup-maker, the tailoress, the harpsichord-player, and the songstress, who set you to sleep last night.” 

Miss Theo made a profound, deep ceremonial curtsey—a "cheese"—and blushed modestly. She was not a delicate, ephemeral beauty, nor did Harry instantly fall in love with her to replace his previous infatuation. She possessed a physical reality deeply connected to the earth and the hearth: kind eyes, a ruddy freckled cheek, and a round white neck framed by rich curling clusters of dark brown hair. Her arms, bare from the elbow, were jolly and red, speaking to her passionate, meticulous labor as a chef and caretaker. She was sixteen, grounded, and wholly authentic.

As she rose, her papa tapped her double chin and laughingly hummed a line of French: “Eh bien! que dites-vous, ma fille, de notre hôte?”

“Nonsense, Mr. Lambert!” cried mamma.

“Are you fond of nonsense?” the Colonel asked Harry. “We consume a vast deal of it. Rabelais is my favourite reading. My wife is all for Mr. Fielding and Theophrastus. I think Theo prefers Tom Brown, and Mrs. Hetty here loves Dean Swift.”

“Indeed, sir. I never heard of either one of 'em,” said Harry, hanging his head, feeling the subtle power dynamics embedded in this dominant literary lexicon. “I fear my book-learning was neglected at home. My brother had read every book that ever was wrote. He could have talked to you about 'em for hours.” The untranslatability of his Virginian experience—rooted in soil, seasons, and unspoken grief—left him momentarily adrift in this sea of English literary references.

“Never mind, honesty is better than books any day!” cried the jolly Colonel.

“I know more about horses and dogs than Greek and Latin, sir. We most of us do in Virginia.”

“You are like the Persians; you can ride and speak the truth.”

“Are the Prussians very good on horseback, sir? I hope I shall see their king and a campaign or two,” remarked Harry. 

At this mention of war, Miss Theo looked at her mother, and a sad expression crossed the good woman's face. 

Why? Because in the quiet, humdrum rhythms of country towns, where the sonic environment is attuned to the rustle of wind and the boiling of a pot, young women are inherently attuned to the mythic cycles of human history. They do not require castles of steel or impenetrable forests to understand the gravity of conflict. The fairy tales speak of ivory horns blowing at castle gates, of champions galloping on white chargers—a romanticized, noisy commercialization of love and duty. But in the quiet agrarian reality, the heart's expectancy is far more profound. It listens to the mundane—the farmer driving pigs, the postman's double knock—and translates it into the perpetual, circular longing for connection. Cultural memory insists that every generation experiences this first unspoken inclination, an acoustic resonance within the soul that waits, generation after generation, for a voice to answer in kind.
