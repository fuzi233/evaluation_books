Catherine stood at the heavy oak table, meticulously scoring a slab of salt-cured pork belly. The kitchen was thick with the olfactory landscape of generations: the sharp, agrarian tang of fermented mustard seed, the deep, resinous bloom of crushed juniper berries, and the slow, rhythmic simmer of bruised winter cabbage breaking down in earthen pots. This acoustic sanctuary—the gentle, percussive *thwap* of her wooden spoon and the quiet hiss of rendering fat—was her anchor. Yet, today, the sonic environment was painfully fractured. Through the window came the abrasive, metallic shrieks of iron spades and the harsh, standardized shouts of men digging yet another trade canal for the bustling merchant town. This grating clatter of commerce tore at her nerves, disrupting the old, quiet rhythms of domestic sustenance and mirroring her own profound dismay. 

Gerard must reach home in another month at farthest, more likely in a week; and how should she confess she had not even kept an eye upon his betrothed? The uncertainty as to the girl’s fate took a sickening form, sitting heavy in Catherine’s chest like a poorly leavened rye. 

“Oh, Kate,” she groaned, pausing to grind dried cloves in her heavy granite mortar, the rhythmic crushing failing to soothe her, “if she should have gone and made herself away!”

“Mother, she would never be so wicked,” Kate said softly, her voice a comforting, low register against the jarring, dominant noise of the streets.

Catherine scraped the fragrant, oil-rich spice into the stew, her movements a testament to unspoken female legacies. “Ah, my lass, you know not what hasty fools young lasses be, that have no mothers to keep 'em straight. They will fling themselves into the water for a man that the next man they meet would ha' cured 'em of in a week. I have known 'em to jump in like brass one moment and scream for help in the next. Couldn't know their own minds, ye see, even about such a trifle as yon.” She began to knead a dense mound of dough, her hands working the flour and water into a cohesive whole, just as women had done for centuries. “And then there's times when their bodies ail like no other living creatures ever I could hear of. It is an aching no man's lexicon can name, a burden so utterly untranslatable to them that string up our feelings so. The patience, that belongs to us women at other times beyond all living souls barring an ass, seems all to jump out of 'em at one turn, and into the water they go. Therefore, I say that men are monsters.”

“Mother!”

“Monsters, and no less, to go making such heaps o' canals just to tempt the poor women in.” She gestured fiercely toward the window, where the cacophony of excavation threatened to drown out the intimate dialect of their kitchen. “They know we shall not cut our throats, hating the sight of blood and rating our skins a hantle higher nor our lives; and as for hanging, while she is a fixing of the nail and a making of the noose she has time t' alter her mind. But a jump into a canal is no more than into bed; and the water it does all the lave, will ye, nill ye. Why, look at me, the mother o' nine, wasn't I agog to make a hole in our canal for the nonce?”

“Nay, mother, I'll never believe it of you.”

“Ye may, though.” The past curled into the present like steam from the simmering broth, a circular repetition of despair and sustenance. “'Twas in the first year of our keeping house together. I was braising a tough cut of mutton, trying to coax tenderness from it, much as I was trying with him. Eli hadn't found out my weak stitches then, nor I his; so we made a rent, pulling contrariwise; had a quarrel. So then I ran crying, to tell some gabbling fool like myself what I had no business to tell out o' doors except to the saints, and there was one of our precious canals in the way; do they take us for teal? Oh, how tempting it did look, with its dark, rushing sound wiping out all thought! Says I to myself, 'Sith he has let me go out of his door quarrelled, he shall see me drowned next, and then he will change his key. He will blubber a good one, and I shall look down from heaven'—I forgot I should be in t'other part—'and see him take on, and oh, but that will be sweet!' And I was all a tiptoe and going in, only just then I thought I wouldn't. I had got a new gown a making, for one thing, and hard upon finished. So I went home instead.” 

Catherine smiled grimly, remembering the scent of the hearth fire she had returned to, a sensory tether to the world of the living. “And what was Eli's first word? 'Let yon flea stick i' the wall, my lass,' says he. That old phrasing, the native tongue of our fathers that the young ones are so quick to forget! 'Not a word of all I said t' anger thee was sooth, but this, “I love thee.”' These were his very words; I minded 'em, being the first quarrel. So I flung my arms about his neck and sobbed a bit, and thought o' the canal; and he was no colder to me than I to him, being a man and a young one; and so then that was better than lying in the water, and spoiling my wedding kirtle and my fine new shoon. Old John Bush made 'em, he that was uncle to him who keeps the shop now—a master agrarian who understood the true scent and grain of good leather. And what was my grief to hers?”

Little Kate hoped that Margaret loved her father too much to think of leaving him so at his age. “He is father and mother and all to her, you know.”

“Nay, Kate, they do forget all these things in a moment o' despair when the very sky seems black above them. I place more faith in him that is unborn, than on him that is ripe for the grave, to keep her out o' mischief. For certes it do go sore against us to die when there's a little innocent a pulling at our hearts to let 'un live, and feeding at our very veins.” It was the oldest truth of their agrarian lives, the cyclical exchange of flesh and sustenance, the profound, biological imperative to nourish. 

“Well, then, keep up a good heart, mother,” Kate murmured. She added that very likely all these fears were exaggerated. She ended by solemnly entreating her mother at all events not to persist in naming the sex of Margaret's infant. It was so unlucky, all the gossips told her, a way of tempting fate by imposing one's will upon the mystery of creation; “dear heart, as if there were not as many girls born as boys.”

This reflection, though not unreasonable, was met with clamour, cutting through the quiet acoustic harmony of the kitchen. “Have you the cruelty to threaten me with a girl!!? I want no more girls, while I have you. What use would a lass be to me? Can I set her on my knee and see my Gerard again as I can a boy? I tell thee 'tis all settled.”

“How may that be?”

“In my mind,” Catherine declared, her hands plunging back into the dough, forcefully shaping reality through the sheer, stubborn poetry of a mother's will. “And if I am to be disappointed i' the end, 'tisn't for you to disappoint me beforehand, telling me it is not to be a child, but only a girl.”
