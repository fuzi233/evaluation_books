The brief dispute between George Warrington and his cousin William had, for the time being, severed their connection with Bloomsbury Square. Mr. Will, ever more zealous in his attentions, now carried himself with such unmeasured arrogance that George found it increasingly difficult to restrain his temper. The once cordial visits ceased entirely, though not without a lingering sense of unease—particularly as both men had been bound over to keep the peace, rendering any further confrontation legally perilous.

Lydia Van den Bosch, ever the impulsive and artless child, seemed utterly transformed by the events. Six weeks earlier, she had been consumed by jealousy at George’s evident regard for another; now, however, her manner toward Theo Lambert was marked by an almost maternal warmth. “Miss Theo,” she would declare, “is so quiet, so gentle—such a perfect match for George! I’m truly grateful that the quarrel never came to bloodshed.” She spoke with a softness that belied her usual vivacity. “Had the constables not arrived just in time, dear Mr. George, you might have lost your life. And how foolish Mr. Esmond looked, raging about his ruined duel!”

She added, with a demure smile, “You won’t stay to meet him at dinner today? You don’t like his company? But he can do you no harm—and you’ll do him none.”

Such innocent remarks, spoken aloud in the presence of others, were not calculated to endear George to the family. Yet they carried a subtle weight, especially when uttered by a young woman whose charm had long captivated him.

News of George’s encounter with Mr. Esmond reached Dean Street only after some delay. He had chosen to withhold it from the household, not wishing to disturb their tranquility. But once the truth emerged, the reaction was swift and intense.

“You were actually going to take a man’s life!” exclaimed Theo, appalled. “And you came here, said nothing? Oh, George, it was shocking!”

“I could not let him call us both cowards,” George replied, defending himself. “Could I sit silent while he insulted my brother and me?”

The General sat quietly, his face grave. “You know, papa,” he said, “you’ve often said dueling is un-Christian, and that gentlemen ought to have the courage to refuse.”

“Refuse?” muttered Mr. Lambert, still brooding. “It requires immense strength of mind.”

“And I think,” Jack Lambert added, darkly, “that if any man called me a coward, I’d forget my orders.”

“See?” George retorted. “Brother Jack stands with me.”

“I must not oppose you, Mr. Warrington,” Jack replied stiffly.

“Mr. Warrington!” George blushed. “Would you, a clergyman, have me break the Commandments and commit murder?”

“I am a soldier’s son,” Jack replied dryly. “Besides, Mr. Warrington has committed no murder. We shall soon hear from Canada—the great question of racial supremacy will be decided there!”

He turned away as he spoke, and George watched him with astonishment.

Hetty, pale at this unexpected turn, was discreetly led from the room by her sister. George rose and followed them to the door.

“Great powers, gentlemen!” he declared, returning. “I believe, on my honour, you’re accusing me of shirking this affair with Mr. Esmond!”

The clergyman and his father exchanged glances.

“A man’s nearest and dearest are always the first to insult him,” George snapped.

“You mean to say, ‘Not guilty’?” the General cried, suddenly moved. “God bless thee, my boy! I told thee so, Jack.” He rubbed his eyes, blushed deeply, and gripped George’s hand with fervor.

“Not guilty of what, in heaven’s name?” Mr. Warrington demanded.

“Nay,” the General said, “Mr. Jack brought the story. Let him tell it. I believe ’tis a —— lie, with all my heart.”

And with that, he stormed out of the room.

Jack Lambert looked profoundly embarrassed.

“And what is this—this d——d lie, sir, that someone has been spreading about me?” George asked, grinning.

“To question a man’s courage is always an offence,” Jack replied. “And I rejoice that yours has been misrepresented.”

“Who told the falsehood, sir?” George bellowed. “I insist on knowing the name!”

“You forget,” Jack reminded him, “you are bound over to keep the peace.”

“Curse the peace!” George roared. “We can fight in Holland! Tell me the man’s name!”

“Fair and softly, Mr. Warrington,” Jack said, raising his voice. “My hearing is excellent. It was not a man who told me the story—I confessed it to my father.”

“What?” George exclaimed, realization dawning. “Was it that clever, mischievous little vixen in Bloomsbury Square?”

“Vixen?” Jack protested. “That word applies to no young lady, George Warrington—least of all Miss Lydia. Artful? She is the most innocent creature in Heaven! Wicked? That angel! With heartfelt joy that the quarrel ended, with deep gratitude that consanguineous blood was spared, she praised you for declining the duel—and expressed profound sympathy for your choice of the painful but only method of avoiding it.”

“What method?” George demanded, stamping his foot.

“Why, laying an information, of course!” Jack replied.

George exploded into a torrent of furious language—too violent to repeat—directed squarely at Miss Lydia.

“Do not speak thus!” the parson interjected, now visibly angry. “Do not defile, in my hearing, the most charming, the most innocent of women! If she erred in judging you, and doubted your willingness to commit what, after all, is a crime—homicide, the most dreadful of sins—you have no right to slander her character! You, who are innocent yourself, should honor her purity as well as her beauty! Oh, George, are you to be my brother?”

“I hope to have that honour,” George answered, smiling faintly. He began to grasp Jack’s intent.

“And what, then—though it is too much bliss for sinful man to hope—what if she should one day be your sister? Who could resist her charms? I confess I am enslaved. Those Latin Sapphics in the September issue of *The Gentleman's Magazine*, beginning *Lydicae quondam cecinit venustae*—with an English version by my friend Hickson of Corpus—are mine. I have told my mother what has passed between us. My mother agrees: the most lovely of her sex has deigned to look upon me with favour. I have written a letter—she another. She intends to visit Miss Lydia’s grandfather today, and bring me the answer that will make me either the happiest or most wretched man alive!”

He paused, then added, “In the course of family talk, I mentioned her sentiments to my father. Perhaps I spoke lightly of your courage—which I do not doubt, by Heaven, I do not doubt. But perhaps she, too, has erred. Perhaps the fiend jealousy has gnawed at my heart—and horror!—perhaps I thought my sister’s lover received too much favour from her. I would have it all to myself. Ah, dear George, who knows his own faults? I am beside myself with passion. Confound it, sir! What right have you to laugh?”

At that moment, the General burst in, catching the tail-end of the laughter.

“What, have you two boys made it up?” he asked, grinning.

“I was giving my opinion to Mr. Warrington on laughter—on his laughter in particular,” Jack retorted, fuming.

“George is bound over to keep the peace, Jack! You cannot fight him for two years. In that time, I trust you’ll reconcile. Dinner is ready, boys! Let us drink to absent friends, and put an end to the war—no fighting outside the profession!”

George excused himself with a business engagement and left early. Jack followed shortly after. When George returned to his lodgings and went to Mr. Van den Bosch’s house in Bloomsbury Square, he found Jack already speaking with a servant.

“His master and mistress left town yesterday,” the servant reported.

“Poor Jack! And you had the decisive letter in your pocket?” George asked.

“Well, yes,” Jack admitted. “And my mother has ordered a chair—she’s coming to wait on Miss Lyddy,” he whispered, distraught.

George, too, had a note in his pocket—one he hadn’t shared with Jack. His errand at home had been to write a sharp letter to Miss Lydia, addressed to the gentleman who had spread the tale of his having given information about the duel.

With the family gone, George decided not to leave his note. “If cousin Will was the slanderer,” he thought, “I’ll go and make him recant.”

“Will the family return soon?” he asked the servant.

“They’re visiting the quality,” the man replied.

George read the address on a slip of paper: “The box from Madam Hocquet’s to be sent by the Farnham Flying Coach; addressed to Miss Van den Bosch, at the Right Honourable the Earl of Castlewood’s, Castlewood, Hants.”

“Where?” Jack gasped.

“Their lordship and ladyships have been here often,” the servant said proudly. “The families are quite intimate.”

This was strange. Lyddy had mentioned only one visit from Lady Castlewood.

“And they’ll be staying some time,” the man added. “Miss took a power of boxes and gowns with her!”

The two young men walked away, each crumpling their letters in their pockets.

“What did you say?” George asked Jack.

“Distraction! I am beside myself, George! I—don’t know what I’m saying,” Jack groaned. “She’s gone to Hampshire—and Mr. Esmond is gone with her!”

“Othello could not have spoken better!” George laughed. “And she has a pretty scoundrel in her company!”

“Ha! Here is your mother’s chair!” they heard.

Aunt Lambert appeared, swinging down Great Russell Street, preceded by her footman.

“It’s no use going farther, Aunt Lambert!” George called. “Our little bird has flown.”

“What little bird?” she asked.

“The bird Jack wished to pair with—the Lyddy bird, aunt. Why, Jack, I protest you’re swearing again! This morning it was the Sixth Commandment you wanted to break—and now—”

“Confound it! Leave me alone, Mr. Warrington, do you hear?” Jack growled, striding far beyond the reach of his mother’s bearers.

“What is the matter, George?” Aunt Lambert asked.

George, annoyed by Jack’s behavior all day, replied, “Brother Jack has no fine temper, Aunt Lambert. He tells you I’m a coward and reproaches me for being angry. He finds his mistress gone to the country and begins to swear and stamp. O fie! Oh, Aunt Lambert, beware of jealousy! Did the General ever make you jealous?”

“You’ll make me very angry if you speak to me this way,” she said, distressed.

“I am respectfully dumb. I make my bow. I withdraw,” George said, bowing low, and turned toward Holborn.

His soul burned with anger. He felt the need to quarrel with someone. Had he met cousin Will that night, the sureties might have faced ruin. He sought Will at every haunt—Arthur’s, his own house—but learned only that Mr. Esmond had likely joined the family in Hampshire.

He wrote a letter:
“My dear, kind cousin William,
You know I am bound over and would not quarrel with anyone, least of all a dear, truthful, affectionate kinsman whom my brother insulted by caning. But if anyone says I prevented the meeting by giving information, please tell them I believe it is not I but someone else who is the coward. I also write to Mr. Van den Bosch, informing him and Miss Lyddy that some rascal has been spreading lies about me, and begging them to beware such persons.”

After dispatching these letters, George dressed and attended the play, supping cheerfully at the Bedford.

A few days later, a letter arrived on his breakfast table, franked “Castlewood” and signed by the nobleman himself.

“Dear Cousin,” Lord Castlewood wrote, “our family has suffered much lately. It is time our quarrels ended. Two days ago, my brother William brought me a furious letter signed G. Warrington, and informed me, to my grief, of a quarrel between you—your conduct, to say the least, violent. It is ill to waste good wine on poor uses—such as you applied to good Mr. Van den Bosch. Before an old man, youth should show more respect. I admit William’s words and behaviour are often irritating. He has tried my temper within the past twenty-four hours.

“Ah! Why cannot we all live in harmony? You know, cousin, I have always held you in sincere regard. I admire the admirable young lady to whom you are engaged, and offer my warmest compliments to her. I wish to live in peace with all my family where possible—especially since I hope to welcome a Countess of Castlewood.

“At my age, it is not unusual to choose a young wife. My Lydia (you may divine I am happy to call her the elegant Miss Van den Bosch) will naturally survive me. After soothing my declining years, I shall not begrudge her choosing a worthy successor. Though of noble Dutch descent, she admits her rank is not equal to mine—a fact she is pleased to share. I, in turn, shall welcome descendants to my house, and through my Lady Castlewood, restore its former splendour, diminished by improvident predecessors.

“My Lydia, seated beside me, sends her warmest regards to you and the Lambert family.

“The marriage will take place soon. May I hope to see you at church? My brother will not attend—he took the news poorly, spoke harsh words he will regret, and is visiting neighbours. The Dowager Lady Castlewood keeps the Kensington house; we have our own establishment, where you will always be welcomed.

Yours affectionately,

From the *London Magazine*, November 1759:
“Saturday, October 13th, married at his seat, Castlewood, Hants, the Right Honourable Eugene, Earl of Castlewood, to the beautiful Miss Van den Bosch, of Virginia. 70,000 pounds.”
