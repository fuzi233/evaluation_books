As the warm sunlight filtered through the windows of Castlewood, it cast a gentle glow on the room where Mr. Washington sat, surrounded by the quiet reverence of his surroundings. His youth, good looks, and bravery had captivated the hearts of many, including the kind-hearted Madam Esmond. Her admiration for him only grew stronger, and she often spoke of his sterling qualities to her sons, comparing them favorably to Harry's carefree nature and George's introspective demeanor. But George, though outwardly composed, harbored a growing unease, fueled by his mother's effusive praise of Mr. Washington. His attempts to conceal his envy had become increasingly strained, causing him to utter cryptic remarks that left his family bewildered.

Outside, the world was abuzz with the escalating tensions between the French and English North Americans. The once-local dispute had taken on a national scale, with French reinforcements arriving in Canada and English troops on their way to Virginia. Amidst this turmoil, a letter from Madame la Presidente de Mouchy, written from Quebec, found its way to George Warrington. "Dear friend," she penned, "I fear the path you've chosen will lead you to the very frontiers of war. I tremble at the thought of your safety amidst the chaos. Can we truly wish victory for our nations when it means seeing you among the ranks of the enemy?" Her words conveyed a sense of concern, tinged with a hint of wistfulness.

In Quebec, the atmosphere was charged with anticipation. The arrival of the English troops had sparked a flurry of activity, and the once-peaceful landscape was now alive with the sound of marching drums and the rustling of leaves as soldiers prepared for battle. The Chevalier de la Jabotiere, a skilled swordsman and a long-time adversary of George's, burned to cross swords with his opponent on the field of combat. Meanwhile, M. Du Quesne, a respected member of society, spoke of George in hushed tones at supper gatherings, his words carrying an air of reverence.

As the days passed, the English forces gathered momentum. Commodore Keppel, an experienced commander, anchored his fleet in Hampton Roads, accompanied by General Braddock and a contingent of troops. The arrival of these seasoned warriors marked a significant turning point in the conflict, and the stage was set for a clash of arms that would shake the very foundations of the New World.
