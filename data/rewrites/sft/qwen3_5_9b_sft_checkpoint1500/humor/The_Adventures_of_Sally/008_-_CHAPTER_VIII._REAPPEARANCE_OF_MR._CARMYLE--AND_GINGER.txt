By the time Sally departed Detroit on Saturday, flanked by Fillmore—who was heading back to Manhattan to stake his claim on Broadway—the gloom that had clouded her spirit had evaporated. She felt a pang of guilt for her previous fussiness, realizing that ambition was simply men's trade. Her success was tied irrevocably to Gerald's, and complaining about his energy felt like complaining about the sunrise. The recent whirlwind, particularly the triumphant opening of *The Primrose Way*, had cemented this optimism. Detroit, known for its genial crowds, had devoured the play whole. Critics, usually skeptical of a first-night's fever pitch, had unanimously hailed Gerald as the next big thing and Elsa Doland as the inevitable star. Even Fillmore got a fleeting nod as "the coming manager." Yet, amidst this golden haze, a tiny, jagged stone remained: Gladys Winch. The sole mention of her name in the reviews appeared in the credits, misspelled as "Wunch," listed merely among those the cast "also included."

"One of the greatest character actresses on the stage," Fillmore grumbled later, recounting the slight.

However, life was too radiant to dwell on a single blemish. Business boomed; Elsa improved nightly; a frantic telegram from Mr. Cracknell hinted at buying back the rights, and the formidable Ike Schumann, owner of half New York's theaters, had personally stamped *The Primrose Way* with his seal of approval. On the train ride home, sitting opposite a radiating Fillmore, Sally broke a long silence.

"Yes, do," she said softly.

"Eh?"

"I said, 'Yes, do.' You owe it to your position."

"Do what?"

"Buy a fur coat. Wasn't that what you were dreaming about?"

"Don't be a chump," Fillmore protested, though a blush betrayed him. He had indeed toyed with the idea; a man must keep warm.

"With an astrakhan collar," Sally pressed.

"As a matter of fact," Fillmore declared loftily, his high-minded soul resistant to such frivolity, "what I was truly contemplating was something Ike said."

"Ike?"

"Ike Schumann. He's on the train. I just met him."

"We call him Ike!" Sally whispered.

"Of course I call him Ike," Fillmore snapped. "Everybody does."

"He wears a fur coat," Sally murmured.

Fillmore bristled. "Please stop harping on that coat. And anyway, why couldn't I wear one?"

"Fill..." Sally tried again, weaving a tapestry of fantasy. "'And it isn't only the coat,' I'll say. 'It's what's underneath...' I'll point and say, 'That's my brother!' 'No!' 'He is, really.' 'You're joking.' 'That's the great Fillmore Nicholas.' 'I know. But he really is my brother.'"

"Do leave off!"

"'And it isn't only the coat,'" Sally continued relentlessly. "Tucked away inside that mass of fur, dodging behind a dollar cigar, is a man we point to with pride..."

Fillmore glanced sharply at his watch. "I have to see Ike."

"We are consulting with Ike constantly."

"He wants to talk about the show. Suggests Chicago before New York."

"Oh, no!" Sally gasped, dismayed.

"Why not?"

Sally rallied. She had momentarily feared a Chicago premiere meant another long separation from Gerald, but logic quickly intervened; he wouldn't need to stay with the company after the opening. "You're worried we lack a New York reputation," she reasoned. "A Chicago run could boost us here. I'll have to think it over."

Fillmore adopted a pensive expression, drawing his brows together.

"All wrong," Sally corrected gently. "Compress your lips, Fill. Press your forefinger to your temple in a look of care. You have a lot to learn."

"Oh, stop it!"

"Fillmore Nicholas," Sally said, her voice thick with mock sorrow, "if you knew the pain of joshing my only brother, you'd regret it. But it's for your good. Go and ease Ike's anxiety. He was waiting for you on the platform, watch in hand. 'Will he come, Miss Nicholas?' he asked, his voice trembling with yearning. 'Of course,' I lied. 'My brother is kindness itself.' 'If I could only think so!' he gulped. 'Managers are busy, they brood over coats, they forget everything.' 'Have no fear,' I assured him. 'Fillmore Nicholas keeps his word.'"

Sally would have loved to prolong the charade, but Fillmore stood up, his back rigid against her levity. He moved down the car, leaving her alone. She picked up a magazine, lost in a gripping jungle adventure split across two issues, when a voice cut through the silence.

"How do you do, Miss Nicholas?"

Bruce Carmyle slid into the seat vacated by the departing manager. He possessed an air of deferential restraint that never wavered.

Sally jumped. While everyone traveled these days, finding a man presumed dead in Europe suddenly breathing in America felt like waking from a nightmare.

"Mr. Carmyle!" she exclaimed.

If Sally had haunted Carmyle's thoughts since Paris, he was barely in hers. She searched her memory for recognition before composing herself.

"We meet on trains so often, don't we?" she said, regaining her poise. "I never expected to see you stateside."

"I came over."

Sally hesitated. At their last meeting, she had been dreadfully rude. She was never unkind without immediate regret. She settled for a timid "Yes."

"Yes," Carmyle agreed, "it has been many years since I took a proper holiday. My doctor thought me run down. It seemed a fitting chance to visit America. Everyone," he proclaimed oratorically, trying to convince himself his trip wasn't solely to reconnect with Sally, "ought to visit America. It's part of an education."

"And what are your impressions of our glorious nation?" Sally teased.

Carmyle seemed pleased to lecture on neutral ground. He, too, had stumbled initially, embarrassed by the coincidence. His voice, however, restored him.

"I visited Chicago," he said.

"Oh!"

"A magnificent city."

"I've never seen it. I just left Detroit."

"Sally's eyes widened. "You heard I was in Detroit? Good heavens, how?"

"I called at your New York address and made inquiries," Carmyle admitted awkwardly.

"But how did you know where I live?"

"My cousin—Lancelot—told me."

Silence fell between them. Sally felt the chill of a detective novel protagonist caught in the shadow of a killer. Whether Carmyle had tracked her down or not, it was clear she was a significant curiosity to him. A compliment, perhaps, but unsettling. Seizing on the mention of Ginger as a lifeline, she steered the conversation.

"How is Mr. Kemp?" she asked.

Carmyle's dark face deepened. "We have received no news."

"No news? You sound as if he vanished."

"He has disappeared!"

"When?"

"Shortly after I last saw you."

"Disappeared!"

Carmyle frowned. Sally felt an instinctive dislike flare within her—a certain hardness in this man. "Where has he gone?"

"I don't know. Nor do I wish to know." A flush rose on Carmyle's cheeks, suggesting he shaved twice daily. "The family has washed their hands of him. He must look after himself. I believe he is off his head."

Sally's temper ignited, yet she suppressed it. She felt a strange, protective fury for Ginger, despite his actions. If she wanted answers, she needed to coax them out. "What happened? What was the trouble?"

Carmyle's eyebrows knit together. "He insulted his uncle, Donald. Grossly. The one man he should have courted. He defied him!"

"What did he do?" Sally cried, fighting to keep the thrill from her voice.

"No details. My uncle won't speak. Apparently, Lancelot gambled away some winnings at Roville, which turned his brain. My uncle insists he is mad. I agree. Since that dinner, nothing."

Before Sally could press further, the imposing figure of Fillmore loomed into view. Explanations were due. He eyed the stranger suspiciously.

"Oh, hello, Fill," Sally interjected smoothly. "Fillmore, this is Mr. Carmyle. We met abroad. My brother, Mr. Carmyle."

The introduction satisfied Fillmore, who admired a man who seemed important. "Strange to meet again like this," he noted amiably.

"The porter is ready," Sally said, signaling the end of the social hour. "You two might retire to the smoking room. I'm going to bed."

She needed solitude to process Carmyle's alarming news about Ginger. As the men departed, Sally sank into an empty seat, a surge of curious exhilaration coursing through her. Ginger had followed her advice! Excellent, fierce Ginger! She felt the swelling pride of a creator, having transformed a meek dependent into a ravenous beast. By poetic law, this feat deserved a night's rest.
