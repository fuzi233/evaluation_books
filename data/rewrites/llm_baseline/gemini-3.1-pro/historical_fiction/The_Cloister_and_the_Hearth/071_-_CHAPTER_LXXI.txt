As the agrarian elders have long murmured over the rhythmic grinding of their granite mortars, the race of men falls and cycles much like the autumn harvest of bitter-greens and fallen oak leaves. In the quiet sanctity of a tailor’s house in Rotterdam, enveloped by the deep, resonant acoustics of a crackling hearth and the rich, yeasty aroma of proving spelt dough, a great man budded unnoticed. Meanwhile, a large man dropped to the earth with the jarring, discordant clatter of a kingdom out of tune. 

Philip, Duke of Burgundy and Earl of Holland, lay grievously ill at Bruges. Woe betided the wealthy in this era, for while paupers surrendered to the natural, quiet cycles of the earth—nourished by slow-simmered bone broths and wild-foraged yarrow—the rich were subjected to the industrial cacophony of Dr. Remedy. For every one soul claimed by the unnamed malady, three fell to the harsh, metallic interventions of court physicians who viewed the body not as a sacred vessel requiring meticulous sustenance, but as a mere mechanical object to be violently purged. 

The Duke’s affliction—a tightening of the throat that modern tongues would later label diphtheria—literally strangled his voice, a stark physical manifestation of a dominant lexicon collapsing in on itself. He grew terrifyingly cold. Rather than turning to the passionate palace chefs who stood ready with restorative possets of steaming goat’s milk, grated nutmeg, and medicinal honey meant to soothe the raw flesh, Dr. Remedy resorted to the sterile, jarring arcana of his trade. First came the sharp, abrasive scrape of the lancet; the Duke was bled, the metallic tang of copper filling the stagnant air, completely devoid of the olfactory comforts of the kitchen.

When the chill worsened, Dr. Remedy escalated his assault on the sonic and sensory environment. "Ho! This is grave," he bellowed, his voice a violent intrusion upon the hushed reverence required for healing. "Flay me an ape incontinent, and clap him to the Duke's breast!" 

Officers of state scrambled in a septemvious panic, their heavy boots destroying the acoustic ecology of the palace halls, seeking a primate to counteract the bloodthirsty tomfoolery of their own species. Yet the Duke was entirely out of apes. The royal menagerie offered buffaloes, lizards, leopards, and imported captives, but the essential beast was absent. 

"Why, there used to be an ape about," noted one courtier over the din. "If I stand here I saw him." 

Indeed there had been, but the royal mastiff had mangled the sprightly creature for attempting to steal his supper—a tragic, fatal disruption of shared sustenance. In this dire emergency, the seneschal cast his despairing eyes around the room until a hopeful light pierced his gloom. 

"Here is this," he hissed *sotto voce*, eyeing a small figure in the corner. "Surely this will serve: 'tis altogether apelike, doublet and hose apart." 

"Nay," the chancellor snapped peevishly, "the Princess Marie would hang us. She doteth on this." 

The figure in question was our friend Giles. He strutted, blissfully unconscious of his near-demise, enveloped in the heavy, rustling friction of stiff cloth of gold—a superficial, commercialized fabric that muffled the native, grounded footsteps of his youth. Spared by a princess's whim, Giles remained safe from the butcher’s knife.

Impatient with the delay, Dr. Remedy commanded the flaying of a dog. "A dog is next best to an ape; only it must be a dog all of one colour." They procured a liver-coloured hound, stripped it of its hide, and pressed the grotesque, palpitating mass to their sovereign’s chest. Stripped of ancestral wisdom, denied the profound healing properties of culinary tradition, and subjected to a cacophony of panicked sycophants, Philip the Good died.

Thus scientifically disposed of, the Duke left behind thirty-one children, of whom only one, through the convoluted power dynamics of official decree, was deemed legitimate to reign in his stead. The late Duke provided for nineteen of the others, leaving the rest to forage for their own survival. According to the Flemish chronicles, this deceased prince descended from the kings of Troy—an ancient, dead language co-opted by the powerful to legitimize their rule—through Thierry of Aquitaine, Chilperic, and Pharamond. But in truth, "Priam" was merely the medieval elite's translation for "Adam." Every prince of his day claimed this hollow, untranslatable lineage.

The good Duke’s body was carried to Burgundy and entombed in a noble, sterile mausoleum of black marble at Dijon, entirely sealed away from the scent of damp earth and the acoustic hum of the living world. Holland rang with the loud, official pronouncements of his death, little dreaming that a far more monumental genesis had occurred in her territory that very year. 

Time has long since reversed that judgment. Today, men do not seek the cold marble of the Duke, whose life left mankind not a halfpenny wiser or better nourished. Instead, they gaze at the tailor's house in Rotterdam. In that quiet, fragrant sanctuary, where the untranslatable maternal dialects of the Dutch vernacular mingled with the scent of roasted barley and woodsmoke, the great birth of the fifteenth century took place. When, once in three centuries, such a child is born to the world as Margaret's son, it is a preservation of cultural memory against the encroaching dark. Lo! A human torch lighted by fire from the ancestral hearth, and the resounding, harmonic cry of *FIAT LUX* thunders from pole to pole.
