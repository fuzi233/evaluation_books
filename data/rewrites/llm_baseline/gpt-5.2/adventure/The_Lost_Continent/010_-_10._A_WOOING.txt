A whisper ran through the ring of kneeling bodies like wind through dry reeds, and in a breath it thickened into a growl of voices.

“Kneel.”

“Kneel, beardless one—kneel, or you will be marked.”

Another, nearer, spat the words as if they were bone splinters. “Kneel and do obeisance to the only Goddess, or by the old Gods I’ll make myself her priest and butcher you!”

The air itself seemed to bruise with that roar—until a name began to skip from mouth to mouth, softening the fury as water softens meal into dough.

“Deucalion.”

“Deucalion—”

It was not Deucalion the absent viceroy of Yucatan they feared; that was a story grown thin with distance. It was Deucalion they remembered at Phorenice’s side beneath the writhing canopy of golden snakes, the one who might still have teeth enough to bite back. The shouting sagged, grudgingly, into silence.

When the hush had settled and Phorenice turned, seeing me upright among prostrate worshippers, I stepped from the press. Between two of the great stones I passed, over the trampled ground, and across the circle toward the altar where she stood. I did not kneel. At the proper distance I gave the salutation she herself had required when she made me chief minister, and hailed her with the measured decorum of court.

“Empress.”

Her smile came sideways, like a knife drawn half an inch. “Deucalion, man of ice.”

“I still adhere to the old Gods.”

“I was not referring to that,” she said, and let the words linger as if they were perfume she expected me to breathe.

Then Ylga hurried up, pale as scalded milk, her hand fluttering like a bird’s wing against her breast. She fell into supplication for my life, voice breaking in little jerks.

“If he will not leave the old Gods yet, surely you will pardon him. He is strong—he does not become a convert easily. You may change him later. But think, Phorenice, he is Deucalion; and if you slay him here for this one thing, there is no other man within all the marches of Atlantis who would so worthily serve—”

Phorenice cut her off as one snips a thread. “You slut!” she cried. “I keep you near to appoint my wardrobe and carry my fan, and you dare put a meddling finger on my policies? Back outside the circle, or I’ll have you whipped. Ay—and I’ll do more. I’ll serve you as Zaemon served my captain Tarca. Shall I point a finger and smite that pretty skin with a sudden leprosy?”

The girl’s shoulders caved. She went away as if each step cost her. Phorenice watched her go, then turned her full attention to me.

“My lord,” she said, and there was an eager brightness in her, as though she had only just discovered she owned a body and a will, “I am like a young bird in a nest that has suddenly found its wings. Wings have so many uses that I am curious to try them all.”

“May each new flight be for the good of Atlantis.”

An eye-flash—impatient, amused. “Oh, I know what you have most at heart. We will go back to the pyramid and talk this out at more leisure. Now, my lord, conduct me to my riding beast.”

So the refusal to worship her—publicly, before all—was for the moment swallowed, and Ylga’s intercession counted not as loyalty but as insolence. I did not understand the exact measure of either, but I accepted what advanced the work Zaemon had laid on me.

Phorenice offered her fingers. Warm they were, fine-boned and delicate despite their talent for holding war. I took them gravely and led her out of the great circle she had befouled with her tricks. I had expected the Sun to strike at the profanation in the very act—to scorch, to split stone, to make an example. Nothing came. The Lord chooses His own hour, and His own instrument; my arm is too small to guard His honour.

We walked away and the smell of sacrifice followed us: a heavy, copper-sweet reek underlaid with smoke and hot fat that clung to the back of the throat. It should have been the clean, honest scent of a hearth—meat seared over flame, salt driven into fibre—but this was worship’s kitchen, where piety and cruelty share the same pot.

At the edge of the square the huge red mammoth waited, shaggy as a hill in winter. Its breath steamed out, thick with the animal musk of old hair and warm hide. On its back rose the golden castle with its writhing canopy of royal snakes, metal scales catching the sun like fish in a net.

Phorenice climbed the stair and settled herself on the cushions, then bade me mount beside her. The seat behind—where the fan-girl should have perched—was empty. Our words, as we set off through the streets, had no ears to sip them up and carry them away.

She pressed me at once for what had befallen after the attack on the gate. I told her, and gave weight to Nais’s worth—cleverness, thought, that rare thing which seasons a crowd as a pinch of bitter herb seasons a stew. I said also that with care the girl might be won back to allegiance. Of Zaemon’s commands—spoken between him and me in the sacred tongue—I said nothing. Those matters are not for repetition save before the Ark of the Mysteries in the High Council itself.

“You seem to have an unusual kindliness for this rebel Nais,” Phorenice said.

“She showed herself more clever and thoughtful than the common herd.”

“Ay.” Her sigh sounded real enough, a sound like a harp string touched once and left to die. “An Empress loses much that meaner women get as common due.”

“In what particular?”

“She misses the honest wooing of her equals.”

“If you set up for a Goddess—” I began.

“Pah!” She snapped the word away. “I wish to be no Goddess to you, Deucalion. That was for the common people—power, and convenience, and a leash on their minds. All you Seven higher priests know that trick of calling down fire. It pleased me to filch it. Can you not be generous and admit a woman may be as clever in finding out natural laws as your musty elders?”

“Remains that you are Empress.”

“Nor Empress either. Think only that there is a woman seated beside you on this cushion. Look at her and say what words come first to your lips. Have done with ceremonies and statecraft. Do you wish to wait on till all your manhood withers? It is well not to hurry—yes, I grant that. Yet who but a fool watches fruit grow ripe, and then leaves it till it rots?”

The mammoth’s feet thudded a slow drum against the stone, a deep, living percussion that made the street’s smaller sounds—sandals, murmurs, the clink of weapons—seem like the hiss of frying oil around a great roast. Above it all drifted the city’s constant chorus: distant hammering in the smiths’ ward, donkey brays, the chatter of vendors selling salt fish and dried fruit, and under that, like a sour note that would not resolve, the siege’s far-off groan—creak of timber, twang of cord, the occasional thin whine of something thrown.

Phorenice’s beauty was a torch beside me. Yet, as I live, it left me cold. I remembered the command laid on me and forced a smile as one forces down food one cannot stomach.

“I may have been fastidious,” I said, “but I do not regret waiting this long.”

“Nor I. But I have played my life as a maid time enough. I am a woman—ripe, full-blooded—and the day has come when I should be more than what I have been.”

I let my hand close on hers. “Take me to husband then, and I will be a good man to you. But as I am bidden speak to Phorenice the woman now, and not to the Empress, I offer fair warning: I will be no puppet.”

She gave me that sidelong look again, measuring, tasting. “I have been master so long that I think it will come as enjoyment to be mastered sometimes. No, Deucalion—I promise you shall be no puppet. Indeed, it would take a lusty lung to do the piping if you were to dance against your will.”

“Then as man and wife we will live together in the royal pyramid. We will rule this country with all the wit the High Gods have bestowed. These miserable differences shall be swept aside. The rebels shall go back to their homes and hunt and fight beasts in the provinces. The Priests’ Clan shall be pacified. Phorenice—you and I will throw brain and soul into the government and make Atlantis rise again for peace and prosperity.”

Petulantly she drew her hand away as though I had smeared her with ash. “Oh, your conditions—and your Atlantis! You carry a crudeness in these colonial manners of yours, Deucalion, that palls after the first blunt flavour has worn away. Am I to do all the wooing? Is there no thrill of love under all your ice?”

“In truth, I do not know what love may be. I have had little enough speech with women all these busy years.”

“We were a pair then, when you landed,” she said, “though I have heard sighs and protestations from every bearded man in Atlantis. Some tickled my fancy for a day; none moved me deeper. No—I also have not learned what this love may be from my own feelings. But, sir, I think you will teach me soon, if you go on with your coldness.”

“From what I have seen,” I said, “love is for the poor, the weak, the flighty.”

Her laughter came sharp. “Then I would another woman were Empress, and I some ill-dressed gutter creature a strong man could pick up by force and carry home for sheer passion. Ah—how I could revel in it! How I could respond if he caught my whim!” She shook her curls. “But I should lead him a sad life of it if my liking were not as strong as his.”

“We are as we are made,” I said. “We cannot change the inwards that move us.”

Her glance turned sullen, heavy with threat like a storm cloud pressing low. “If I do not change yours, my Deucalion, there will be more trouble brewed for the Atlantis you set such store upon. There will be ill doings in this household of ours if my love grows for you and yours remains unborn.”

She would have had me fondle her there—on the mammoth’s back, in the golden castle—while the city stared and the street’s smells rose up: fish brine, crushed herbs, the sweet rot of fruit peelings, the smoky breath of cooking fires where poor folk boiled grains in thin broth. Privacy never tasted necessary to her. For the life of me I could not. The Gods know I was earnest about my task; They know also how it repelled me. I had put away personal liking to obey the Council. Had I known the craft of it—how to speak and touch as those jeweled triflers about the court do—I would have followed her mood. But I stuck, tongue and hand, as if the words themselves were stones in my mouth.

There was no help but to leave unsaid all except what I truly felt. Diplomacy I had learned; on most matters my speech could be smooth enough. But to palter with women was an art I had neglected, and if I tried to coin pretty phrases from clumsy inexperience, Phorenice would have weighed them and found them false at once. She had been fed for years on pap of protestations; she could taste the starch of a lie as a cook tastes flour.

And honest confession would not serve either. Plain truth would sour what must, for Zaemon’s bidding, be brought to seeming sweetness. So I held my tongue and submitted to be counted a clown.

“I had always heard,” she said, “that you colonists in Yucatan were far ahead of those in Egypt in arts and graces. But you, sir, do small credit to your vice-royalty. Why, I have had gentry from the Nile come here, and you might almost think they had never left their native shores.”

“They must have made great strides these last twenty years,” I said. “When last I was sent to Egypt to report, the blacks were masters of the land, and our people lived there on sufferance. Their pyramids were puny, their cities nothing more than forts.”

“Oh, they are mere exiles still,” she said, mockery like vinegar, “but they remember manners. My poor face seemed to please them—at least they went into raptures over it. And for ten pleasant words one cut off his own right hand. We made the bargain, my Egyptian gallant and I, and the hand lies dried on a shelf in my apartment to-day as a pleasant memento.”

Before I could answer, chance threw its own interruption between us.

The rebels outside the walls were attacking with vigour and some thought. More than once during our progress lighter missiles from their engines had sung overhead and split against a building, showering splinters that nicked those packed in the streets. It was the common music of a siege—no more to trained nerves than the crackle of a skillet to a cook. But presently the rebels changed their seasoning. They ceased to hurl stone and began to load their engines with wooden carcases lined with throwing fire.

Against stone, such things did little but scorch; yet when they fell among the rude booths and rush shelters of the poorer wards, they took hold at once. Dry reed and old wood drank flame like oil. There was no quenching it—no patient pouring of water, no smothering with earth. It was a fire that fed on itself, blue smoke first, then the pale tongue of flame showing under sun as if the day had bitten its own lip and bled.

Phorenice and I might still have passed it with small concern—trivial incidents of every siege—had the mammoth been properly schooled. But the huge beast knew fire in its bones, older than any collar or chain.

When the first blue whiff of smoke reached us down the street’s windings, the mammoth lifted its trunk and began to sway its head. When the drifts thickened and flame flickered, it stopped and trumpeted, the sound a brutal tearing that drowned human voices and turned the city’s chorus into a frightened muddle. The acoustic order of the street—vendor calls, children, the clean clack of hooves—was ripped apart by that one ancient scream. Even before the beast moved, the noise itself infected every mind around us with panic; men’s shoulders tightened, women clutched their own throats, as if sound were hands.

The guards tugged at the chains that hung from the jagged metal collar round its neck. The spikes bit deep, and blood welled—hot, dark, and sharp-smelling as iron left out in rain. But with each drop, its bought tameness seemed to ooze away. Fire was the mammoth’s oldest enemy. Men have always sheltered behind flame, and mammoths have always learned to dread it.

Phorenice’s brow darkened. She shook her red curls viciously. “Some one shall lose a head for this blundering. I ordered to have this beast trained to stand indifferent to drums, shouting, arrows, stones, and fire. The trainers assured me all was done, and brought examples.”

I slipped my girdle free. “Here—quick. Let me lower you to the ground.”

She turned with a gleam. “Are you afraid for my neck, then, Deucalion?”

“I have no mind to be bereaved before I have tasted my wedded life.”

“Pish! There is little enough danger. I will stay and ride it out. I am not one of your nervous women, sir. But go you, if you please.”

“There is little enough chance of that now.”

The mammoth’s fear mastered the pain of spikes and the tug of men. With wild squeals it turned and charged back down the way it had come. Spearmen were scattered like straw. A swath opened through the crowd as if a scythe had passed. Cries rose—thin, brief, swallowed by the beast’s own bellowing and the thunder of its feet. The golden castle lurched and swayed so that we had much ado not to be shot off like stones from a catapult.

I braced my feet and wrapped an arm around a pillar, then clapped my spare arm around Phorenice, offering my body as a buffer. She lay content enough, her face just beneath my chin, hair dampened with heat and fear-sweat, its scent faintly spicy—like crushed leaves warmed on a stone.

The mammoth ran on, insane with panic. We had outstripped the smoke—the cause—yet the fear remained as a taste remains after bitter herb has been swallowed. It held its trunk aloft and deafened us with screams. We left behind the glittering pageant of guards and courtiers and were carried helpless through ward after ward.

The brute was beyond control. Its pace made leaping suicide: even if we avoided shattered limbs, we would land at a frightful disadvantage, and the mammoth would scent us, turn, and trample us to pulp in moments. Up in the castle we were beyond its reach.

A thought came, cold and distinct: here might be the High Gods’ answer to sacrilege. The mammoth appointed as instrument, I appointed as witness. Yet no direct revelation had been given; I took no initiative. I clung, and held the Empress from bruises.

The streets wound and doubled, confusing the beast, and still it ran. By degrees we left the great palaces and pyramids behind and came among artisans—smiths and weavers gaping from doorways, their forges’ clang momentarily silenced by the passing thunder. Then the merchants’ quarter, where the air smelled of tar, rope, salt, and foreign spices kept in jars: peppery seeds, dried citrus peel, pungent resins. And then, down an open space, water glittered—harbour-water like a sheet of hammered bronze.

Now, I thought, the beast will stop and swerve and charge back. I tightened my grip. But it held straight on, screaming louder, and plunged into the harbour, sending spray up in bright sheets.

Its pace slackened at once. Here the great sewers—devised in old King’s time for the city’s health—vomited their drainings, and the harbour bed was layered with an impalpable sludge. Into that the mammoth sank deeper and deeper. When it finally stopped, bogged beyond hope, it struggled madly. Water and slime churned into a stinking compost—sour, rank, like grain left too long in a wet sack. The golden castle lurched so wildly that we were torn from it and shot out into the water.

There, of course, we were safe, and I was pleased enough to be rid of the bumping.

Phorenice laughed as she swam, the sound bright against the harbour’s foul breath. “You handle yourself like a sore man, Deucalion. I owe you something for lending me the cushion of your body. By my face, there’s more gallant about you at the test than one would guess to hear you talk. How did you like the ride, sir? I warrant it came as a new experience.”

“I’d liefer have walked.”

“Pish, man! You’ll never be a courtier. You should have sworn that with me in your arms you could have wished the bumping had gone on for ever. Ho, the boat there! Hold your arrows. Deucalion, hail me those fools. Tell them if they hurt so much as a hair of my mammoth I’ll kill them all by torture. He’ll exhaust himself directly, and when his flurry’s done we’ll leave him where he is to consider his evil ways for a day or so, then haul him out with windlasses and tame him afresh. Pho! I could not feel myself to be Phorenice if I had no fine red shaggy mammoth to take me out for my rides.”

A ten-slave galley churned toward us from the far side of the harbour, oars biting the water in time to the whips’ persuasion. Soldiers on the foredeck had begun to shoot, but at my shouts they stopped, and the steersman swerved to pick us up. We had been swimming at ease to avoid landing near the sewer outpour; now we stopped and trod water until respectful hands hauled us over the side.

The galley belonged to the captain of the port—a mincing mariner whose chief appetite was to lick the feet of the great. He prostrated himself at once, wishing his eyes had been blinded before seeing the Empress in such deadly peril.

“The peril may pass,” Phorenice said, shaking water from her sleeves. “It is nothing mortal that will ever kill me. But I have spoiled my pretty clothes, and shed a jewel or two, and that is annoying enough as you say, good man.”

The fool repeated his wish to be blinded.

She narrowed her eyes. “If you are tired of them, let me tell you you have gone the way to have them plucked out of their sockets. Kill my mammoth, would you, because he has shown himself a trifle frolicsome? You and your sort want education. Port-captains are easy to come by and small value when got. But my mammoth is mine—mine, do you understand?—property of Goddess Phorenice, and as such sacred.”

The port-captain abased himself lower. “I am an ignorant fellow,” he said, “and heaven was robbed of its brightest ornament when Phorenice came down to Atlantis. But if reparation is permitted, I have two prisoners in the cabin here who shall be sacrificed to the mammoth forthwith. Doubtless it would please him to make sport with them, and spill the last lees of his rage upon their bodies.”

“Prisoners you’ve got, have you?” Phorenice’s voice sharpened with interest. “How taken?”

“Under cover of last night they tried to pass between the two forts that guard the harbour mouth. Their boat fouled the chain, and by torchlight the sentries spied them. They were caught with ropes and put in a dungeon. There is an order not to abuse prisoners before judgment.”

“It was my order. Did these prisoners offer to buy their lives with news?”

“The man has not spoken. Indeed, I think he got his death-wound in being taken. The woman fought like a cat also, so they said in the fort, but she was caught without hurt. She says she has nothing of use to tell. She says she has tired of living like a savage outside the city, and moreover that inside there is a man for whose nearness she craves most mightily.”

“TUT!” Phorenice said, delighted. “Is this a romance we have swum to? You see what affectionate creatures we women are, Deucalion.”

By then the galley had been brought against the royal quay and made fast to its golden rings. I handed the Empress ashore; she turned back to face the boat, garments still dripping a thin thread of harbour-water.

“Produce your woman prisoner, master captain, and let us see whether she is a runaway wife or a lovesick girl mad after her sweetheart. Then I will deliver judgment, and as like as not surprise you all with my clemency. I am in a mood for tender romance to-day.”

The port-captain went into the little hutch of a cabin with a white face. Phorenice’s gaiety frightened him more than her rage. I saw him stoop, knife in hand, to cut a lashing.

“The man appears to be dead, Your Majesty,” he began. “I see that his wounds—”

“Bring out the woman, you fool. I asked for her. Keep your carrion where it is.”

He obeyed. And when he brought her out into the daylight, the harbour’s stink and the salt wind did not disguise her.

It was the girl I had saved from the cave-tigers in the circus—the one who afterward, in those hours of strange companionship, had drawn my thoughts as steadily as hunger draws a man toward bread. The sight of her struck like a remembered word in a dead dialect: not merely sound, but the whole taste of what it once meant.

Phorenice recognised her as well. She did not hide it. She spoke her name aloud and, with a cruel brightness, began to mock her—asking after the rebels’ ménage, and the success of the prisoner’s amours.
