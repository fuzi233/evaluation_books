Neville Landless had risen with such alacrity and marched with such vigor that by the time the bells of Cloisterham tolled for morning service, he was already eight miles distant. Having departed on a mere crust of bread, he required sustenance and halted at the nearest roadside inn to refresh himself.

Visitors seeking breakfast—a rarity at the *Sign of the Tilted Wagon*, save for horses and cattle, for whom ample provisions of trough and hay awaited—were uncommon occurrences. Consequently, securing tea, toast, and bacon proved a laborious endeavor. While Neville sat in the sand-scattered parlor, pondering how long it would take for the damp, sneezing fagots in the hearth to ignite warmth for someone else, the inn itself presented a grim tableau. Perched atop a hill, the establishment's grounds were a mire of damp hoofprints and trampled straw. Inside, a scolding landlady slapped a baby clad in mismatched socks within the bar; cheese lay discarded beside a mold-stained cloth and a green-handled knife resting in a cast-iron canoe, while pale bread seemed to weep crumbs over its own shipwreck in another vessel. Half-washed linens lay exposed to public view, and every beverage was served from a mug, evoking rhymes of rustic simplicity. Despite failing to keep its painted promise of fine entertainment for man or beast, Neville, undeterred by criticism, accepted whatever comfort he could find and resumed his journey after a rest longer than necessary.

Stopping roughly a quarter-mile from the inn, Neville faced a choice: continue on the main road or veer onto a cart-track winding between high hedgerows across a breezy heath, presumably rejoining the road later. Choosing the latter, he labored uphill through deep ruts. Soon, he sensed footsteps behind him. Four figures approached at a brisker pace; he stepped aside against a bank to yield. Yet, their behavior was peculiar. Four passed him, while four others slowed, seemingly intending to follow once he moved forward. A sixth group, perhaps six strong, turned abruptly and retreated rapidly.

Neville observed the four approaching from behind and the four ahead; all returned his gaze. Resuming his path, he noted the four in front constantly glancing backward, while the four in rear steadily closed the distance. Once they emerged from the narrow track onto the open heath, maintaining this formation regardless of his lateral movements, doubt vanished: he was surrounded. Testing their resolve, he halted; they halted instantly.

"Why do you shadow me thus?" Neville demanded of the entire group. "Are you a gang of thieves?"

"Don't answer him!" one voice commanded, though the speaker remained unseen. "Better be quiet."

"Better be quiet?" Neville echoed, incredulous. "Who said that?" Silence answered. "Good advice, whoever this skulking voice belongs to," Neville snapped, anger rising. "I refuse to be penned between four men yonder and four men yon. I intend to pass, and I shall pass those four ahead."

They stood motionless, including Neville. "Whether eight, four, or two set upon one," Neville declared, his rage mounting, "that one has no recourse but to strike first. By the Lord, I will do so if further obstructed!"

Shouldering his heavy staff, he surged forward to bypass the four ahead. Immediately, the largest and strongest of the group shifted to intercept him, skillfully engaging him before Neville's staff could fall.

"Let him be!" the man whispered urgently as they grappled on the grass. "Fair play! His frame is slight compared to mine, and he carries extra weight on his back. Leave him; I'll handle him."

After a brief, bloody roll, the man dismounted Neville's chest and rose. "Now, seize him arm-in-arm, any two of you!"

Compliance was immediate. Addressing Neville, the man spat blood and wiped his face. "Regarding our reputation as thieves, Mr. Landless, you know better than that at noon. We would not have harmed you had you not provoked us. We shall escort you to the high road; you will find aid there against real thieves. Someone, wipe his face; look at the blood trickling down him!"

Once cleaned, Neville recognized the speaker as Joe, the Cloisterham omnibus driver, whom he had glimpsed only once upon his arrival.

"My counsel for the moment," Joe advised, "is silence, Mr. Landless. A friend awaits you at the high road—he went ahead via the alternate route when we split. Say nothing until you reach him. Hand over that stick; let us move!"

Bewildered, Neville fell silent. Flanked by his captors, who gripped his arms, he drifted forward like a dreamwalker until they reached the high road and a small gathering of townsfolk. Among them were the men who had retreated earlier, alongside Mr. Jasper and Mr. Crisparkle. The captors delivered Neville to the Minor Canon, releasing him as a gesture of respect.

"What is this commotion? What has happened? I feel as though I've lost my wits!" Neville cried, as the crowd closed in.

"Where is my nephew?" Mr. Jasper demanded wildly.

"Where is your nephew?" Neville repeated, startled. "Why ask me?"

"I ask," Jasper retorted, "because you were the last seen with him, and he is nowhere to be found."

"Not to be found!" Neville gasped.

"Hold, hold," Mr. Crisparkle intervened calmly. "Allow me, Jasper. Mr. Neville, you are distraught; compose yourself. Your clarity of thought is vital. Attend to me."

"I shall try, sir, but I feel mad."

"You left Mr. Jasper last night with Edwin Drood?"

"Yes."

"At what hour?"

"Twelve o'clock?" Neville asked, hand to his throbbing temple, turning to Jasper.

"Precisely," Crisparkle confirmed. "The hour Mr. Jasper mentioned. You went down to the river together?"

"Undoubtedly, to observe the wind's effect."

"And afterward? How long did you remain?"

"Approximately ten minutes, perhaps less. Then we walked to your house, and he took his leave at the door."

"Did he mention returning to the river?"

"No. He stated he was heading straight back."

The bystanders exchanged glances toward Crisparkle. Turning to Neville, who had been scrutinizing him intently, Jasper lowered his voice, sharp with suspicion. "What are those stains on his garments?"

All eyes fixed on the blood soaking Neville's clothes.

"And here are identical stains on this stick!" Jasper exclaimed, seizing it from the man holding it. "I recognize this stick; he carried it last night. What signifies this?"

"In God's name, explain!" Crisparkle urged.

"That man and I," Neville pointed to his former adversary, "struggled over this stick moments ago. You can see the matching marks on him. When attacked by eight men who offered no explanation, how could I anticipate the truth?"

Admitting their discretion in silence and confirming the struggle, the men watched darkly as the fresh air began to dry the crimson smears.

"We must return, Neville," Crisparkle said. "You will surely wish to clarify this situation?"

"Certainly, sir."

"Mr. Landless shall walk beside me," the Minor Canon announced, surveying the crowd. "Come, Neville!"

They departed, with the others trailing at varying distances. Jasper walked strictly on Neville's opposite side, never breaking contact. Silent throughout, despite Crisparkle's repeated inquiries and speculative theories, Neville remained immovable, his expression fixed. Near the city gates, when Crisparkle suggested visiting the Mayor immediately, Neville nodded sternly but spoke not a word until they entered Mr. Sapsea's parlor.

Upon learning the nature of their visit, Mr. Jasper broke his silence, placing his trust entirely in Mr. Sapsea's discernment. Unless the Mayor could propose a plausible reason for his nephew's sudden flight, Jasper deferred judgment. Similarly, unless drowning in the dark appeared probable, he withheld accusation. Washed clean of direct suspicion, yet tormented by doubt, Jasper entrusted the verdict to Sapsea's mind.

Mr. Sapsea opined the case possessed a "dark look"—an distinctly non-English complexion. Launching into a labyrinth of nonsensical ramblings unsuitable for a mayor, he eventually arrived at the profound realization that killing a fellow creature meant destroying something that did not belong to oneself. Hesitating between issuing a warrant for Neville's imprisonment under grave suspicion, he paused only due to Crisparkle's indignant assurance that the young man would remain at home and present himself voluntarily.

Jasper interpreted Sapsea's hesitation as an instruction to scour the river, inspect its banks rigorously, disseminate notices to outlying areas and London, and publish broadsheets imploring Edwin Drood, should he have fled, to reveal himself to his grieving kin. These measures were enacted immediately.

It was difficult to gauge who bore greater horror: Neville Landless or John Jasper. Yet, Jasper's position compelled activity, whereas Neville's demanded passivity; neither could stand tall. Both were crushed beneath the weight of dread.
