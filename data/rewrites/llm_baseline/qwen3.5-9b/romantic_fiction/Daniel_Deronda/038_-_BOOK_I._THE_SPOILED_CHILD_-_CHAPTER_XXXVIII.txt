Some believe that the deepest tragedies occur not after a man has seized the divine fire and bound it to his staff to bring it to humanity, but *before* that moment. Before he is thrust by the crushing weight of established systems into a lonely exile of rejected ideas, pinned down by the agonizing grip of poverty and sickness—a desolate place where crowds walk past without seeing him. "Second sight" is a banner flying over contested territory. Yet, it is undeniable that there exist individuals whose deepest yearnings, conceptions, and even reasoned conclusions constantly manifest as vivid images possessing a prophetic power. The act they intend to perform rises before them in perfect form, becoming a compelling archetype; the event they crave or fear grows rapidly from a tiny seed, nourished by countless impressions. These individuals are not necessarily less logical or less sane than the ordinary calculators of commerce; indeed, their natures may possess manifold openings, like the city of Thebes with its hundred gates, allowing a vast and diverse influx of experience that cannot enter through a narrow, guarded door.

Of course, there are pathetic examples of the visionary, just as there is a tiny mammal you might trap inside the finger of a glove. This small relative of the elephant causes no harm, yet what great mental or social category is entirely free of specimens whose insignificance is both ugly and harmful? One hesitates to consider everything encompassed by the label "patriot"; or to imagine the jostling that might occur on Judgment Day among those who claimed authorship, carrying volumes in their hands or on carts. This defense of inevitable kinship serves only to introduce the facts concerning Mordecai, whose figure had gnawed its way into Daniel Deronda's mind as a new question he felt compelled to answer. However, Deronda's interest was merely a vague, expectant suspense. The consumptive-looking Jew, appearing to be a fervent student earning his living through a quiet craft, similar to Spinoza, fit into none of Deronda's expectations.

It was quite different with the impact of their meeting on Mordecai. For many winters, while aware of his fading physical life and expanding spiritual isolation, all his passionate desire had concentrated on finding a young ear into which he could pour his mind as a testament. He sought a soul kindred enough to receive the spiritual fruit of his own brief, painful existence as a mission to be executed. Remarkably, the hopefulness often granted as a benign illusion to consumptive patients was in Mordecai entirely diverted from the prospect of bodily recovery and channeled into this yearning for transmission. The desperate longing, panting upward from overwhelming discouragement, had grown into hope, and that hope into a confident belief. Instead of being dampened by his clear awareness of his hastening decline, this belief intensified into an expectant faith in a prophecy destined to fulfill itself in a short remaining time.

Several years had passed since he first began measuring men with a keen glance, searching for a possibility that gradually sharpened into a distinct concept. Initially, this clarity was achieved primarily through contrast: he sought a man who differed from himself. Analyzing the reasons within his own self for the rebuffs he faced and the obstacles that hindered him, he imagined a person who possessed all the elements necessary for sympathy with him, yet embodied them differently. This man must be a Jew, intellectually cultivated and morally fervent—in every way a nature ready to be filled from Mordecai's—but his face and frame must be beautiful and strong. He must be accustomed to the refinements of social life, his voice flowing with a full and easy current, his circumstances free from sordid need. He must glorify the possibilities of the Jew, rather than sitting in wonder as Mordecai did, bearing the mark of his people amidst the signs of poverty and failing breath.

Sensitive to physical characteristics, Mordecai had, both abroad and in England, examined pictures as well as people. In idle hours, he would sometimes linger in the National Gallery seeking paintings that could feed his hopefulness with grave and noble types of the human form, suitable for men of his own race. Yet, he returned disappointed. Instances are scattered thinly across the galleries of Europe where the fortune or selection of even the greatest masters has gifted art with a face that is simultaneously young, grand, and beautiful. Where melancholy exists, it is not a feeble passivity but enters into a foreseen capacity for heroism. Observant souls may recall his emaciated figure and dark eyes set deep in their sockets as he stood before a painting that touched him to new or habitual meditation. He commonly wore a cloth cap with black fur trim, which no painter would have asked him to remove. Spectators likely viewed him as an oddly dressed Jew, perhaps trying to extract money from the artworks; Mordecai was perfectly aware of the impression he made. Experience had made him acutely sensitive to how a man's poverty and other physical disadvantages cheapen his ideas, unless he is a Peter the Hermit ringing a bell for the rabble.

However, he was too sane and generous to blame his spiritual banishment solely on the excusable prejudices of others. Certain incapacities of his own had contributed to the sentence of exclusion. Hence, his imagination constructed another man—one who would be something more ample than the "second soul" bestowed, according to Cabalist notions, to supplement the insufficient first. This figure would be a blooming human life, ready to incorporate all that was worthy in an existence whose visible, tangible part was burning itself away quickly. His inner need for the conception of this expanded, prolonged self was reflected as an outer necessity. The thoughts of his heart—that ancient phrase best shadowing the truth—seemed to him too precious, too intricately woven into the growth of things to lack a further destiny. As the more beautiful, stronger, and more executive self took shape in his mind, he loved it beforehand with an affection that was half-identifying, half-contemplative and grateful.

Mordecai's mind worked so constantly in images that his coherent chains of thought often resembled the significant dreams attributed to sleepers by awake observers in their most creative moments. Nay, they often mimicked genuine dreams in their manner of breaking off the passage from the known to the unknown. For a long while, he habitually conceived of the Being answering to his need as someone distantly approaching or turning their back toward him, darkly silhouetted against a golden sky. The reason for the golden sky lay in one of Mordecai's habits. He was keenly alive to certain poetic aspects of London; a favorite resort, when strength and leisure permitted, was some of the bridges, particularly around sunrise or sunset. Even when bent over watch-wheels and trinkets, or seated in a small upper room overlooking dingy bricks and cracked windows, his imagination spontaneously placed him on a spot offering a far-stretching view. His thoughts operated in wide spaces, and whenever possible, he tried to experience the influences of a large sky in reality. Leaning on the parapet of Blackfriars Bridge, gazing meditatively, the breadth and calm of the river, with its long vista half-hazy, half-luminous; the grand, dim masses of tall buildings signaling world-commerce; the oncoming boats and barges moving from the distant stillness into sound and color—all entered his mood and blended indistinguishably with his thinking, like a fine symphony that acts as a medium supporting our spiritual wings.

Thus, the figure representing Mordecai's longing was mentally seen darkened by the excess of light in the aerial background. But in the inevitable progression of his imagination toward fuller detail, he ceased to see the figure with its back turned. It began to advance, and a face became discernible. The attributes of youth, beauty, refinement, Jewish birth, and noble gravity coalesced into a form and color that were hardly individual but typical. Gathered from his memories of faces seen among the Jews of Holland and Bohemia, and from paintings that revived those memories, this vision took shape. Reverently, it may be said of this mature spiritual need that it was akin to the boy's or girl's picturing of a future beloved; yet, the stirrings of such young desire are feeble compared with the passionate current of an ideal life straining to embody itself, made intense by resistance to imminent dissolution.

The visionary form became a companion and auditor, occupying a place not only in the waking imagination but in those dreams of lighter slumber wherein it is truest to say, "I sleep, but my heart waketh"—when the disturbing trivia of yesterday is charged with the impassioned purpose of years. Recently, the urgency of irremediable time, measured by the gradual choking of life, had turned Mordecai's trust into an agitated watch for the fulfillment that must be at hand. Was the bell on the verge of tolling, the sentence about to be executed? The Deliverer's footsteps must be near—the one who would rescue Mordecai's spiritual toil from oblivion and grant it an abiding place in the finest heritage of his people.

An insane exaggeration of his own value, even if his ideas were as true and precious as those of Columbus or Newton, might lead many to dismiss this yearning as the sublimer part of a man to declare, "If not I, then another," and to hold cheap the meaning of his own life. But the fuller nature desires to be an agent, to create, and not merely to observe; strong love hungers to bless, not merely to behold blessing. While there is warmth enough in the sun to sustain an energetic life, there will still be men who feel, "I am lord of this moment's change, and will charge it with my soul."
