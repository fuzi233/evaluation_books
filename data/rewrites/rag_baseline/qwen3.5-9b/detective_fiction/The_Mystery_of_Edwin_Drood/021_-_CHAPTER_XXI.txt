The night held no wind, and the dove slept undisturbed until the morning light broke the silence. At ten o'clock, Mr. Crisparkle burst into the room, arriving from Cloisterham as if thrown from the river. He spoke of Miss Twinkleton's agitation, explaining that her wonder at receiving Rosa's note had compelled him to seize the first train. He admitted he had wished for Rosa's presence earlier, but concluded that her journey to her guardian was the wiser path.

"I considered you," Rosa replied, noting the proximity of Minor Canon Corner to her guardian. "It was natural."

Mr. Grewgious confirmed he had heard everything Rosa had shared the previous evening. He noted that Mr. Crisparkle's arrival was timely, especially since he had just departed. The conversation turned to Helena and her brother. Mr. Crisparkle confessed a deep perplexity, remarking that if Mr. Grewgious, whose intellect spanned a whole night ahead of his own, remained undecided, he was lost.

At that moment, the Unlimited entered, announcing a visitor seeking Mr. Crisparkle. When informed the Reverend Sir was occupied, the visitor clarified his description: a brown gentleman with blue eyes, not black-haired. Mr. Grewgious suggested admitting him, arguing that in moments of difficulty, one must keep every possible avenue open rather than closing doors prematurely.

The stranger entered with a frank, modest grace, apologizing for interrupting. He smiled and asked, "Who am I?"

"You are the gentleman I saw smoking under the trees at Staple Inn moments ago," Crisparkle answered.

"True," the stranger acknowledged. "There I saw you. Who else am I?"

Crisparkle studied the handsome, sunburnt face, and a faint memory of a departed boy rose in the room. The stranger noticed the flicker of recognition in the Minor Canon's features and continued, "What will you have for breakfast? You are out of jam."

"Wait!" Crisparkle raised his hand. "Tartar!"

They embraced with vigorous heartiness, extending the gesture beyond typical English reserve by placing hands on each other's shoulders and locking gazes.

"My old fag!" Crisparkle cried.
"My old master!" Tartar responded.
"You saved me from drowning!"
"After which you took to swimming, you know!"
"God bless my soul!"
"Amen!"

They resumed shaking hands. Crisparkle, eyes glistening, recounted the image of young Tartar, a small junior, diving to catch him—a large, heavy senior—and dragging him to shore like a water-giant. Tartar affirmed he would not let him sink, recalling how the boy had been his best protector despite the masters' failures. An irrational impulse had once driven Crisparkle to save him, fearing to go down with his friend.

Mr. Grewgious stepped forward, extending his hand with practiced formality. He expressed pride in meeting him, hoping Tartar had not taken cold or swallowed too much water. To Rosa, it seemed miraculous that such courage and skill existed in someone so slight and young. Tartar declined compliments but offered an idea. He mentioned believing he had seen Tartar's name listed as a tenant in the top set of the house adjacent to the corner building.

"Yes, sir," Tartar confirmed. "You are right so far."

Grewgious ticked this off mentally. He then asked for the name of the neighbor in the top set on the opposite side of the party wall.
"Landless," Tartar replied.
Another tick.
"Do you have personal knowledge of him?"
"Slight, but some."
Another tick.
"What is the nature of this knowledge?"
Tartar explained he believed the young man was in poor straits and had recently asked permission to extend his flower garden to include the neighbor's windows.

"Take seats," Grewgious commanded. "I have an idea!"

They sat, with Grewgious in the center, hands on his knees, delivering a rehearsed plan. He outlined the dangers of direct communication between Miss Rosa and either Mr. Neville or Miss Helena due to a local friend who skulked around Staple, possibly aided by informants among porters or watchmen. While Miss Rosa wished to speak with Helena, secrecy was paramount.

"If our local friend has an informant," Grewgious reasoned, "they can only monitor the chambers occupied by Mr. Neville. They cannot watch all of Staple unless... unless it is mine."

"I begin to understand," Crisparkle approved.
"I also understand," Tartar added. "Let me say at once that my chambers are freely at your disposal."

"There!" Grewgious smoothed his head triumphantly. "Now we have the idea. You have it, my dear?"
Rosa blushed slightly. "I think I have."

"The plan is simple," Grewgious instructed. "You go to Staple with Mr. Crisparkle and Mr. Tartar. I move in and out alone as usual. You ascend to Mr. Tartar's rooms, inspect his flower garden, and await Miss Helena's appearance or signal her that you are nearby. No spy can detect this movement."

Rosa hesitated, fearing she might be discovered.
"Not frightened?" Grewgious asked gently.
"No, not that," Rosa whispered. "Only in Mr. Tartar's way. We seem to be appropriating his residence so coolly."
Tartar protested that he would cherish the sound of her voice forever.

Rosa, unsure how to respond, asked if she should put on her hat. Grewgious agreed, and she withdrew. During her absence, Crisparkle summarized the distresses facing Neville and his sister to Tartar. When Rosa returned, Tartar offered his arm, and Crisparkle walked ahead.

"Poor, poor Eddy!" Rosa thought as they moved along. She glanced at Tartar's right hand, waving animatedly above her head. It was not as powerful or sun-browned as it had been when saving Crispaskle, yet it possessed a steady determination. He revealed he had been a sailor, roaming the seas for years.
"When are you going to sea again?" Rosa asked.
"Never!"

She imagined the girls' reaction to seeing her walk across the wide street supported by a sailor's arm. Passersby must view her as small and helpless compared to the strong figure capable of carrying her miles from danger. His far-seeing blue eyes seemed accustomed to watching distant threats without flinching, drawing closer until they were intimate. As she raised her own eyes, she realized he was thinking of her. This confusion accounted for her later inability to recall precisely how she ascended to his garden in the air, entering a marvelous country that bloomed suddenly like the land atop a magic beanstalk. May it flourish forever.
