To my kin in England, I pen these words from distant India. My purpose is plain: to justify my refusal to extend the hand of friendship to my cousin, John Herncastle. My silence on this matter has bred misunderstanding among those whose esteem I value. I ask only that they withhold judgment until they have heard my account. On my honor, every word I write is truth.

The rift between Herncastle and myself began in the shadow of a great battle—the storming of Seringapatam under General Baird on May 4th, 1799. To grasp the full measure of our quarrel, you must first know of the whispers that haunted our camp in those fevered days before the assault: tales of gold and jewels heaped within the Sultan’s palace, and one story stranger than all the rest.

Men spoke of a diamond unlike any other—a cursed yellow gem sacred to the moon-god of Hindoo legend. For centuries, it had been guarded by three Brahmin priests, passed down through generations since the days of Mahmoud of Ghizni’s pillage. They said the stone carried a prophecy: disaster would follow any hand that stole it, and all who inherited it thereafter.

By the time of our campaign, the diamond had found its way into the hilt of Tippoo Sultan’s dagger, locked away in his armory. Yet rumor insisted the three Brahmins still watched in disguise, biding their time. Most of us laughed at such superstitions—but not Herncastle. On the eve of battle, he swore with that reckless bravado of his that if Seringapatam fell, we’d see the gem gleaming on his finger. We mocked him for it. By dawn, we’d forgotten his boast.

We forgot too soon.

The assault was chaos. I lost sight of Herncastle in the fray, and it was only at dusk, when the city was ours and Tippoo lay dead, that our paths crossed again. We’d been tasked with curbing the looting, but the treasury was a riot of drunken soldiers. Amid the chaos, a cry cut through the night—a sound that still chills me. I followed it to an armory, where two slain Indians barred the door. Inside, a third man knelt, bleeding, before a figure with a torch and a bloodied dagger.

The torchlight caught the pommel of that dagger as the man turned—and I saw Herncastle’s face. The dying Indian pointed to the blade and gasped: *"The Moonstone will have its vengeance yet on you and yours!"* Then he fell.

Herncastle’s eyes burned with something worse than battle-madness. He drove me out, barring the door. By morning, when Baird threatened hanging for looters, my cousin met me with a curt greeting. I demanded the truth of what I’d seen. He shrugged. *"The Indian died of his wounds. As for his ravings—who can say?"*

I turned my back on him then. I turn it still.

Let my family weigh this account as they will. I’ve no proof to condemn Herncastle beyond my own certainty—no witness to the killings, only a dying man’s curse. Yet I write this with a soldier’s superstition: evil deeds cast long shadows. If Herncastle keeps that diamond, it will haunt him. If he parts with it, worse will follow.

Mark my words.
