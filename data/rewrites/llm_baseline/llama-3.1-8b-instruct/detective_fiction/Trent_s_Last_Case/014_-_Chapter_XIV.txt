"If you insist," he said, addressing his companion, "I suppose I shall have to compose this epistle now, despite my reservations." He glanced around the room, seeking a suitable writing surface, and finally spotted a blank sheet of paper free from any markings.

His companion smiled sympathetically, handing him the paper. "What shall I say?" Trent asked, his brow furrowed in concentration. "Shall I wax poetic, comparing Mabel and me to a summer's day? What words shall I choose?"

His companion leaned forward, her voice filled with encouragement. "Say what you truly feel."

Trent shook his head, a wry smile spreading across his face. "What I long to say to every person I meet is 'Mabel and I are betrothed, and all is joy and simplicity.' But that wouldn't be suitable for a letter of formal tone, let alone one with a somewhat sinister undertone." He pondered for a moment, his eyes scanning the room. "I've managed to scribble down 'Dear Mr. Marlowe,' but the rest eludes me."

Just then, his companion held up a manuscript, which she had apparently brought with her. "I thought you might find this interesting," she said, her eyes sparkling with curiosity.

Trent's gaze snapped back to the paper, his brow furrowing in concern. "Do you realize that in that sentence, there are only two words with more than one syllable? This letter needs to convey gravitas, not ease the recipient into a sense of familiarity. We require words that convey sophistication."

His companion looked puzzled. "I don't see why. I've received many letters from solicitors and businesspeople, and they often begin with phrases like 'with reference to our previous correspondence' or similar verbiage. Yet when I meet them in person, they speak in plain language. It strikes me as peculiar."

Trent set his pen aside, a hint of relief washing over his face. "It's not peculiar to them, I assure you.
