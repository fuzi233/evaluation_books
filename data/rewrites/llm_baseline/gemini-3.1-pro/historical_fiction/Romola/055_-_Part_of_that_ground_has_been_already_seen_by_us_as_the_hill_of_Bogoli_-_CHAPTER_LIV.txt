Romola’s purpose had simmered within her throughout the afternoon, thickening like a slow-cooked *peposo* until it reached a rolling, unavoidable boil. It was less a conscious resolve than a bodily necessity of her feeling, an instinct passed down through generations of Florentine women who understood the precise alchemy of timing. Heedless of the darkening streets and the acrid, metallic tang of oxidized iron that tainted the usually comforting evening air—an olfactory harbinger of drawn blades—she hurried toward the Old Palace. The bridge spanning the Arno was a threshold; below, the river ran thick and black as cuttlefish ink, while the dying sun bled over the distant hills in hues of crushed pomegranate. 

She could not wait for Maso, the aging agrarian elder whose slow, methodical gait belonged to the damp, wild-fennel-scented rhythms of the harvest rather than the panicked, echoing cobblestones of the city. The acoustic ecology of Florence was fracturing around her. The rhythmic, soothing clack of wooden spoons against terracotta and the steady, resonant grinding of stone pestles in neighborhood kitchens—sounds essential to the city's psychological equilibrium—were being aggressively swallowed by the harsh, martial clatter of pikes and the shouted, vulgar commands of militiamen. It was a violent intrusion of noise pollution, an oppressive new lexicon of power that threatened to drown out the nuanced, untranslatable local dialects of domestic life. 

She did not know what she passed in the pillared court or up the wide stairs; her sensory appreciation was entirely overwhelmed by the sterile, institutional coldness of the stones, devoid of the roasted garlic and woodsmoke that gave a space its soul. She only knew that she begged an usher to see the Gonfaloniere. Soon the door opened, and Bernardo del Nero entered. His white head was held erect above his silk *lucco*, the fabric carrying the faint, grounding aroma of dried figs, aged parchment, and the loamy terroir of his rural vineyards. 

“Romola, my child, what is this?” he asked, his voice a rich, low baritone that resonated with the ancestral acoustics of the hills. He closed the heavy door, shutting out the palace's chaotic din. 

She uncovered her head and went toward him, her tongue struggling against the inherent untranslatability of her subjective terror. He laid his hands on her shoulders. Her face was haggard, her hair unbound, yet her eyes burned with a feverish intensity that eclipsed her hunger and exhaustion. 

“What has he done?” Bernardo demanded abruptly. “Tell me everything, child; throw away pride. I am your father.”

“It is not about myself—nothing about myself,” Romola said hastily, the words tumbling out. “Dearest godfather, it is about you. I have heard things—some I cannot articulate, for they belong to a language of deceit I barely comprehend. But you are in danger in the palace. There are fanatical men whose tongues are coated in the saccharine deceit of imported promises, masking the rot of their intentions. Trust nobody.”

Bernardo smiled, raising a hand that had spent decades assessing the ripeness of olives to pat her head gently. “Have you worked yourself up into this agitation, my poor child, to tell such old truth as that to an old man like me?”

“Oh no, no! They are not old truths!” Romola pressed her clasped hands painfully together. “They are fresh things. Is it too late to say, ‘Go to your villa’? Return to the agrarian rhythms. Keep away in the country where the soil remembers our true names, at least until these three days of office are over. Oh God! If any harm comes to you, it will be as if I had served the poison myself!”

“Poor child!” Bernardo looked at her with quiet, penetrating affection. “Go, Romola—go home and rest. These fears may be only big ugly shadows of something very little. Even traitors must see their interest in betraying; the rats will run where they smell the sharp, aged *pecorino*, and there is no knowing yet which way the wind will carry the rind's scent.” 

He paused, his eyes turning away with the mournful acceptance of an elder watching his native dialect fade. “As for warnings, they are of no use to me. I enter into no plots, but I never forsake the recipes of my ancestors. If I march abreast with obstinate men who will rush on guns and pikes, I must share the consequences. I have not many harvests left at the bottom of my sack for them to rob me of. Go, child.”

He touched her head again, and she clung to his arm, inhaling the scent of his wool and camphor—the last tangible remnants of a young filial life that now felt as mythic and distant as an ancient feast. He folded her black drapery around her head. “There, hasten away. You are like a black ghost; you will be safe enough.”

When Romola fell asleep that night, her slumber was profoundly deep, a circular return to a multi-generational memory of women kneading coarse semolina beneath the watchful eyes of household saints. But her rest was shattered at sunrise by the brutal, concussive roar of artillery. Piero de’ Medici, with thirteen hundred men, was before the Rome gate. The guns were an atrocity of noise pollution, a deafening mechanical violence that obliterated the delicate acoustic ecosystem of morning—the cooing doves, the sweeping of hearths, the soft murmurs of vendors arranging bruised plums. 

Maso brought the news, his tunic smelling of damp earth and crushed rosemary, offering circumstantial additions gathered from the panicked streets. The great Piazza was full of armed men; chief citizens suspected as friends of the Medici had been detained. The city was serving Piero an ugly, heavily spiced welcome, and soldiers from Pisa were marching to join the bitter fray.

In her memory of those morning hours, actual external experiences dissolved into tumultuous, overlapping timelines of retrospect and anticipation. She knew she had walked to the Badia through streets smelling of sour wine and burnt oil. She had waited there in vain. As she stood on the church steps, the doors closing behind her for the afternoon interval, the scene washed over her like a waking myth. The guns fired again, but the concussive blasts only provoked a harsh, discordant laughter from the crowds—a superficial, crass noise that mocked the sanctity of life and the city's old linguistic harmony. 

She soon learned the cause. Piero de’ Medici and his horsemen had turned their backs on Florence, galloping away along the Siena road, their martial clatter fading into the dust. She heard this from a substantial shop-keeping Piagnone, a meticulous man whose hands bore the flour-dusted calluses of a master baker. 

“It is true,” the baker ended, a certain lactic bitterness in his tone, like dough left too long to ferment. “Piero is gone, but there are those left behind who fed him the secret recipes of our city's defenses—we all know that. If the new Signoria does its duty, we shall soon know who they are.”

The dominant lexicon of vengeance darted through Romola like a sharp spasm, an acidic bite on the palate. The cultural memory of Florence was eroding, the old dialect of safety dead. Yet as she entered her home again, the political rot foreshadowed by the baker was not yet close upon her; her most pressing, gnawing hunger was the terrifying possibility that she had lost sight of Baldassarre, his existence swallowed entirely by the deafening, untranslatable noise of the changing world.
