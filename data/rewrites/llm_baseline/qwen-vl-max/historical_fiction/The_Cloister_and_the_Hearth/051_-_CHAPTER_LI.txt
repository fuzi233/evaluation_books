The reader already knows how much there was to say between them. It had been a day of both sweetness and sorrow for Margaret—sweet because she had found a true friend in Denys, bitter because the news he brought left her heart heavy with grief. For the first time, she learned that Gerard was utterly alone in that distant land, cast adrift from all who loved him. She could not believe, as Denys did, that Gerard would return; if he were coming, he would have arrived long before now.

Yet Denys was a comfort—a steady presence in her solitude. He called her his “she-comrade,” and with his lively manner and endless jests, he lifted her spirits when they sank. She cherished him, teased him gently, and kept strict watch over his conduct. She refused to let him dine outside her home, forbade him from using coarse language, and scolded him lightly whenever he slipped: “It spoils you, Denys. Good lack, to hear such ugly words come from so comely a head! Forbear, or I shall be angry. So be civil.” And then, with comic dignity, Denys would straighten himself, struggling between the manners he’d once known and the roughness he’d adopted in the world, his face a mask of forced propriety.

And just as misfortune never comes alone, other visitors now sought Margaret’s company. She had written to Margaret Van Eyck, humbly acknowledging that she was no longer the favorite she once had been, and pledged to keep her distance. Yet she could not forget the kindnesses shown her in the past. In her letter, she briefly recounted the many hardships she had endured to survive, ending with a fervent plea: “I beg you, do not reveal my whereabouts—least of all to *his* people. I hate them. They drove him from me. And even after he was gone, their hearts never turned toward me as if they truly repented their cruelty.”

Margaret Van Eyck was troubled by this message. At last, she confided in Reicht, who passed it on like water through a pipe to Catherine. “Ah,” said Catherine, “and is she so bitter against us now?” “She stole our son from us,” replied the older woman, “and now she resents us for not rushing into her arms. Still, it is a blessing she lives—and no farther away than Rotterdam.”

Meanwhile, the English princess, now Countess Charolois, made a stately journey through the northern provinces of the duchy, accompanied by her stepdaughter, Marie de Bourgogne, the young heiress of Burgundy. The old Duke, most splendid prince in Christendom, outdid himself in magnificence. Knights in dazzling armor, ladies richly dressed, minstrels, jongleurs, bards, musicians, actors, and tumblers followed in their train. Every town they visited rang with music, dance, and jousting. Richart summoned all his people to Rotterdam to witness the spectacle.

After several days in the city, Denys met Catherine by chance in the street. Their greeting was warm and joyful. “Hark!” he exclaimed, “I’ve found your she-comrade at last!” He crowed with pride. But Catherine cooled his ardor. “Had you stayed quietly at Tergou, you might have found her sooner than wandering across Holland like a vagabond.” “And what good does finding her do?” she asked. “Her heart is closed to us now.” “Let that flea stick,” said Denys. “Come with me to her house.” “No,” Catherine replied firmly. “I will not go where I am certain of an unwelcome.” “Them that come unbidden sit unseated,” she added. “Then let me be the mediator,” said Denys. “I’ll bring peace between you.” He accepted the role at once, full of confidence and pomp.

He trotted off to Margaret and announced, “She-comrade, I met a friend of yours today.” “You looked into the mirror, then, and saw yourself,” she retorted. “Nay,” he insisted, “it was a woman—someone who seeks your regard. It was Catherine, Gerard’s mother.” “Oh?” said Margaret, her voice flat. “Then tell her she comes too late. Once I longed for her, but she kept aloof when I needed her most. Now we shall remain as we have been.” Denys tried to soften her resolve. He coaxed, pleaded, urged—but she remained cold and sullen. Then, losing patience, he scolded her sharply. At that, she burst into hysterics. Startled by the sudden turn, Denys faltered. Off guard, he allowed himself to be trapped into a solemn vow never again to mention the matter.

He returned to Catherine crestfallen, reporting the refusal. Catherine flared with anger and told her family what had happened. The household rose in fury. A rift opened between them, growing deeper each day. Only little Kate offered any defense of Margaret.

The very next day, another visitor arrived at Margaret’s door. She found the household in uproar: Martin buried in soap suds, Denys awkwardly ironing, and herself plaiting ruffs with a sharp eye over her charges. Into this scene stepped an old man—venerable at first glance, but upon closer view, sharp-eyed and thin-faced. “Ah!” cried Margaret, turning swiftly away and covering her face as if to hide from some dreadful sight. “Oh, that man! That man!”

“Fear me not,” said Ghysbrecht. “I come on a friend’s errand. I bring you a letter from abroad.” “Do not mock me, old man,” she whispered, turning slowly. “See,” he said, holding out a large, sealed letter. Margaret snatched it from his hands, trembling, her eyes glistening. It bore Gerard’s handwriting. “Oh, thank you, sir,” she breathed, “bless you for this—I forgive you all the harm you ever did me.” With one hand, she pressed the letter to her chest, and in silence, glided from the room.

She did not return. Ghysbrecht left, casting a dark look at Martha as he went. For hours, Margaret remained alone with her letter—alone with the words of the man she loved, carried across oceans and dangers, finally reaching her at last.
