In the quiet sanctuary of the cloister, Gerard found solace in the company of a monk renowned for his ingenuity. This brother, a master craftsman of the divine arts, had erected a grand sundial in the prior's garden, fashioned wheels to lift water from the deep, and devised machines to winnow grain. His workshop brimmed with wonders: he had carved several psalteries and two dulcimers, and was currently striving to construct a set of regales, small organs intended for the choir.

Gerard, playing his humble psaltery with tentative grace, listened as the monk touched the strings with a divine fluency, instantly revealing the novice's lack of experience. While the monk shone brightly in illumination, Gerard surpassed him in the beauty of his script. In an age where accomplishment reflected a genuine spiritual bent, their differences melted away. Youth and Age stood side by side like brothers, and Gerard was warmly invited to remain that night. He sought counsel from Denys, who, with a rueful shrug, granted permission.

That evening, Gerard shared his destination and recounted their recent trials, carefully softening the harsher truths of their journey. "Alas!" sighed the elderly monk, "though I have traveled widely in my time, I was never molested." He warned Gerard against inns, describing them as haunts of rogues and rioters where the soul itself might fall prey to evil, urging him instead to seek refuge in peaceful monasteries for every stage of his travels. Suddenly, his demeanor shifted, his eyes sharpening like needles as he asked, "How long hast thou been shriven?" Gerard blushed, offering a feeble reply, "More than a fortnight." "And thou an exorcist!" the monk exclaimed, alarmed. "No marvel then that perils have beset thee. Thou must be absolved at once." "Yes, Father," Gerard knelt, joining his hands, but the monk held him back with a fretful gesture. "Not unto me! Not unto me! I am as saturated with worldly cares as any living soul. My entire being rests upon these wooden pipes and leather stops, destined to rot alongside those whose hearts are fixed upon such vanities." "Most holy Father," Gerard pleaded gently, "these instruments serve the Church, and surely that sanctifies the labor bestowed upon them?" "Precisely what the Devil whispers in my ear," the monk retorted, placing a hand behind his back and pointing a finger that danced between threat and playfulness. "He reminds me that Solomon built the Lord a house adorned with rare hangings, counting it gracious and sinless. Oh, he quotes Scripture with startling frequency! Yet I am no simpleton, my lad," he declared, turning his gaze upward with sudden defiance, addressing the empty air rather than Gerard. "One toy completed, vigils, fasts, and prayers await me—prayers standing, prostrate upon the chapel floor, and immersed in a basin of icy water." He nudged Gerard, winking knowingly. "Nothing terrifies me more than witnessing monks lost in prayer up to our chins in cold water, for *corpus domat aqua*. Go now, confess thy trifling, pardonable sins of youth and secular life, and leave me to mine, sweet as honey and demanding equal measure of expiation."

Gerard bowed his head, unable to resist murmuring, "Where shall I find a confessor holier and more merciful?" "Within each of these cells," the monk replied simply, guiding him down the corridor, "seek Brother Anselm, yonder." Gerard approached the indicated door, but the cells were clustered so closely together that he feared a mistake. Just as he raised his hand to knock, he heard his old friend calling out in a frantic whisper, "Nay! Nay! Nay!" Turning quickly, he found the monk pacing anxiously outside his own door, beating the air with doubled hands like a woodcutter swinging an axe. Gerard, convinced the adjacent cell harbored some ferocious beast, hesitated, glanced back, and moved to the next door. Immediately, the monk nodded vigorously, his face transforming from agitated distress to a blissful radiance, before darting back into his chamber. He retrieved an hourglass, flipped it, and resumed his work on the regales, muttering to himself, "Well-a-day, how swiftly the sands flow when a man bends over earthly toys."

Brother Anselm was a venerable figure, his ample head crowned with dignity and his countenance radiating a profound, gentle love. As Gerard confessed, answering Anselm's tender yet penetrating inquiries, a thought crossed his mind: "Behold a head! Oh, dearest Father, I wonder if you will permit me to sketch it when our conversation ends." So engrossed was he in admiration that his own thoughts grew hazy, and he momentarily forgot certain crimes. Nevertheless, he offered no false comfort nor diminished the gravity of his past errors. The penance imposed was severe yet symbolic: Gerard was to enter the church, prostrate himself, and kiss the lowest step of the altar three times; then, kneeling, recite three Pater Nosters and a Creed. Upon completion, he was to return immediately.

Having fulfilled this brief mortification, Gerard returned to find Father Anselm applying plaster to his injured leg. "First the soul, then the body," the monk explained. "I am the surgeon here, lacking a better. This is applied to cool the flesh, lest it burn. The Saints forbid otherwise." During this treatment, the monastic physician, having shown interest in Gerard's account of his injuries in Düsseldorf, leaned toward Denys's theory regarding bleeding. "We Dominicans seldom bleed patients nowadays," he mused. "Lay physicians claim it stems from timidity or lack of skill, yet we have long known that simples alone can cure most ailments. Furthermore, remedies never kill in capable hands, whereas others strike like thunderbolts. Regarding blood, the Vulgate explicitly states it is the life of man. To be wiser than the All-Wise in medicine or law is to be a fool. Moreover, simples are mighty. Consider the four-legged creature that slays the poisonous snake; if bitten itself, it finds an herb potent enough to quell the venom, superior in strength and speed to any mortal remedy. Taught by this wisdom and guided by tradition, we still search for the virtues of the plants God scatters across the earth, some to nourish, others to heal. Only in desperate cases do we mingle heavenly with earthly virtue, steeping the hair or bones of a departed saint within the medicine to effect marvellous cures."

"Pray tell, Father, is it truly due to relics?" Gerard asked. "For Peter a Floris, a learned physician and no pagan, denies it stoutly." "What does Peter a Floris know? And what do I know?" Anselm countered gently. "I dare not claim we command the saints, nor can we dictate whether their blessed remains draw forth physical virtue. Yet I observe that a patient receiving such treatment with faith is often restored, as by a charm. Undoubtedly, faith plays a vast role in these healings. Thus it has always been: a sick woman whom Jewish physicians could not save merely touched Christ's garment and was healed instantly. Had she not touched that sacred cloth, she would never have recovered. If she had worn it without faith to her grave, I doubt she would have benefited at all. Yet we do ill to probe these mysteries too curiously. Everything around us demands faith. Exercise a little patience. Soon we shall understand everything. Meanwhile, I, your temporary confessor, strictly forbid you, under penalty of your soul's salvation, to listen to learned laymen on matters of religion. Their arrogance is their ruin; with it, they shut heaven's open door in their own faces. Be mindful, I speak of the learned laic. The unlearned have often been my teachers in humility and may become yours. Your wound is tended; in three days, it shall be but a scar." With words of blessing, wishing God speed and that the saints render Gerard as virtuous and joyful as he was thoughtful and kind, Father Anselm dismissed him.

Gerard lingered, hoping to delay departure for dinner in the refectory, but Anselm, with a faint trace of regret, informed him that he remained in penitential seclusion within his cell for the week. Before closing the door, Anselm held Gerard's head delicately in both hands and kissed his brow. Almost before the cell door swung shut, the monk retreated to his devotions. Gerard stepped out, chilled to the core by the stark solitude of monastic life, his heart heavy with sadness. "Alas!" he thought, "Here lies a kindly face I shall never behold again upon this earth; a gentle voice forever silenced from my ears and my heart. There is nothing but meeting and parting in this sorrowful world. Well-a-day! Well-a-day!" This melancholy reverie was broken by the arrival of a young monk who escorted him to the refectory. There, Gerard found several brethren seated at a table, while Denys stood rigid as a poker, scrutinized regarding the towns he must traverse. The friars pooled their knowledge, charting a safe route and marking every religious house along the path, which they presented to Gerard. After supper, the old monk carried Gerard to his cell, where they engaged in an eager conversation. Incidentally, Denys revealed the cause of his earlier theatrical display in the corridor.
