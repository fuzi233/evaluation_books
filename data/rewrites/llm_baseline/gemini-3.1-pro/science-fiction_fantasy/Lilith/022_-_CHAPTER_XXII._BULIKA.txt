I had lost the rhythmic cadence of my own footsteps, wandering in a dissonance of pure, helpless impatience, when the sonic landscape abruptly shifted. A rushing sound, like the vigorous decanting of a heavy vintage, met my ears, and I found myself wading in the path of the leopardess. The liquid ran against my ankles with the force of a spring brook, emitting a sharp, ferrous aroma that immediately provoked an unshaped suspicion: could this be the blood from her paw? Yet, as I traced the acoustic current of its bubbling flow to guide me toward Bulika, my internal food historian reasoned against the impossible yield. No beast of this world, nor the hugest creatures that grazed the ancient, cyclical epochs of our own, could pump such an endless volume of fluid unless its vessels were perpetually replenished by lakes and forests. I dipped a finger, bringing it near my palate to catch its olfactory notes. It was not blood. It was a softly murmuring rivulet of pure water, flowing without a channel over the grass. Its song was a delicate acoustic harmony, a soothing balm against the chaotic noise of my wanderings, but I dared not taste it. I followed its diminishing melody, my wet feet refreshed, until the aurora’s pale light steeped the horizon like saffron infused in broth, and the wall-towers of a city emerged—steeped in the multi-generational patina of countless ages. 

I looked down to find the brook, but its acoustic signature had faded into silence; the grass lay bent in a frozen choreography of its passing, pooling like spilled whey. The fountain's flow had paused its ancient cycle.

Surrounding the city were sprawling vegetable gardens. As a passionate student of traditional agrarian ecosystems, I eagerly inspected the crops, yet the leaves and roots were of cultivars entirely alien to me—stark, utilitarian greens cultivated without the loving, multi-generational husbandry I so revered. There was no water, no fragrant blossoms to invite pollinators, no symphony of insects. The gardens were violently severed from the city walls by towering heaps of gravel and discarded refuse, a physical manifestation of a culture that had commercialized its soil and discarded its culinary heritage. The gate hung half-closed on corroded hinges, groaning a harsh, industrial dissonance when pushed. The street beyond was a vacuum of acoustic ecology—a dead, silent avenue devoid of the rhythmic clatter of hearths, the chopping of herbs, or the bubbling of morning stews. Had the vibrant dialects of daily sustenance died out entirely here? 

I retreated over the dust-heaps, deciding to wait for the inhabitants to stir. My mind chewed on my purpose. I had to see the woman I had resurrected. It was not companionship I sought; friendship was as impossible between us as emulsifying oil and ash. But I needed to decipher the untranslatable dialect of her existence. Her subjective experience was a lexicon of power and consumption that threatened the Little Ones, whose innocence and agrarian purity I was bound to protect. I needed to learn her native tongue of influence to shield them from her harvest.

When a low, abrasive murmur finally signaled life, I entered a narrow street. The architecture seemed to trap and distort sound. I sat near a pillar topped with a bat-like gargoyle. The citizens who sauntered past possessed an oppressive auditory footprint. I spoke to one, hoping to hear the musical cadence of a local dialect, but he responded with a rude stare and a barked, staccato insult—a dominant, utilitarian slang that actively eroded any sense of communal cultural memory. 

Soon, I encountered a group of young men, reminiscent of the bad giants. Their voices were a cacophony of modern noise pollution, devoid of nuance. They shoved me, their harsh lexicon establishing a cruel power dynamic where poverty and sickness were reduced to taxable refuse. They hurled objects, and their jeers were the very sound of a society that had forgotten the shared table. I fled toward the gate. 

At the threshold, a lumbering guard sat consuming a hunch of bread. His relationship to the food was purely functional—fuel shoveled into a furnace without the slightest sensory appreciation. In his stupid eagerness to join the hostility, he reached for a stone but instead hurled his bread at me. I caught it, and when he cowardly refused to step beyond the walls to reclaim it, I retreated into the fields. 

I threw myself down in the grass and examined the gift. It was a dense, rustic loaf. I broke it open, listening to the deeply satisfying crack of the crust—a sound that resonated with the ancient, cyclical history of grain and fire. The crumb was tight, smelling faintly of wild yeast and toasted barley. I ate it slowly, chewing with deliberate reverence, letting the complex, nutty flavor profile unfold on my tongue, rehydrating my exhausted muscles and replenishing my spirit. To me, this meal was a profound communion with the soil; to the citizens of Bulika, it was mere fodder. Satiated, I fell into a deep sleep as the hot sun baked the earth.

I woke to the cyclical return of night. The moon looked down, a familiar companion from my first terrifying evening in this world, its silvery light a constant across interwoven timelines. A cold wind blew from the gate, carrying an evil odour—a rancid, sulfurous stench of decay that assaulted my olfactory senses—but the solar warmth stored in my body and the sustaining digestion of the rustic bread kept the chill at bay. 

I crept back into the city, where the few people outdoors crouched in corners, shivering. The acoustic environment of the narrow street was eerily muted until, just ahead, a huge white thing bounded across the stones. It vanished with a single lunar flash. I turned down an impossibly narrow lane, guided by the faint scent of musk and the subtle, rhythmic padding of paws. 

In the wider street beyond, I saw the creature on the shadowed side: a leopardess, following what appeared to be a man. Every so often, the man glanced back, but he never spoke—their relationship existed in an untranslatable silence. When he crossed a patch of moonlight, the visual anomaly struck me. He cast no shadow. He was a flat, two-dimensional entity, an opaque silhouette that consumed the light rather than blocking it. In the darkness, he was blacker than the shadow itself; in the light, he appeared as a man who had pulled his shadow around him like a cloak. Meanwhile, the leopardess—a radiant, gleaming contrast—drew her own deep shadow across the ground. 

I walked abreast of them on the opposite side. The sonic contrast was stark: my bare feet slapped rhythmically against the flat paving stones, an acoustic affirmation of life and flesh, while they moved with an unnatural, sweeping silence. The leopardess never twitched an ear toward my auditory presence. But the shadow-man seemed to look at me. For a second, his profile vanished into a sharp, upright line—an untranslatable cipher of a being. 

In that instant, the wind found me. It blew straight through my chest, carrying the stale, suffocating aroma of a forgotten, dead language. I shuddered from head to foot, and my heart rattled against the walls of my ribs like a dry, hollow gourd—a frantic, isolated beat in the deafening silence of Bulika.
