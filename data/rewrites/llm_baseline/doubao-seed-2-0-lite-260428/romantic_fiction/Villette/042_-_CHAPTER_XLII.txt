No mortal can see the future, after all. Love is no crystal-ball oracle, and fear so often conjures phantoms that never come to pass. I sickened for months at the very thought of those three years of separation ahead: the grief they would bring felt as certain and unavoidable as death itself. I could picture the heavy chariot of that fate rolling toward me, Juggernaut atop it a grim, towering mass, its broad wheels sinking deep into soft, crushed soil—me, the prostrate, waiting devotee, bracing already for the annihilating crunch I was sure would tear me to nothing.

Strange as it sounds, stranger still as it proved true (and life holds so many such quiet, surprising mercies), that anticipatory agony was nearly all the torture I would ever bear. The great chariot rumbled past me, lofty and loud and sullen as a thunderhead, but it swept over like a shadow gliding across the noon sky, leaving nothing but a faint, chilling dimness in its wake. I lifted my head, and the demon charioteer and his heavy load were gone. I still breathed. I still was whole.

M. Emanuel was gone for three years. Reader, those were the three happiest years of my life. Do you balk at that paradox? Listen, and you will understand. I opened my little school, and I worked—worked long, hard, happy hours. I saw myself as the steward of the gift he had laid at my feet, and I swore, with God as my witness, that I would tend it well and return him a rich accounting when he came home. Pupils came first from the burgher families of the town, and before long, families of far higher standing sought places for their daughters too. Halfway through the second year, a lucky, unforeseen chance dropped an extra hundred pounds right into my hands: one day, a letter arrived from England holding that exact sum, sent by Mr. Marchmont, the cousin and heir of my dear, long-dead old mistress. He had only just recovered from a near-fatal illness, and the money was a peace offering for his pricked conscience, nagging at him over some papers and notes found after his kinswoman’s death that had named and recommended Lucy Snowe. Mrs. Barrett had passed him my address. I never pressed for the full details of what he felt he owed. I asked no questions, just put that money to the best possible use I could imagine.

With those hundred pounds, I dared rent and renovate the house right next door to my own. I would never leave the rooms M. Paul had chosen for me, the rooms where he had left his things, and where he expected to find me waiting for him when he returned. My small day school expanded into a full, thriving boarding school. And its success was never down to my own wit or skill, not truly. It grew out of a life unshackled, a heart no longer heavy with old loneliness. The spring that fed all my energy lay far across the sea, on a sun-warmed Indian isle. The day he left, he had left me a legacy: a bright, steady thought to carry me through each day, a glowing hope for the future, a reason to be persistent, hardworking, bold, patient, and brave. I could not flag. Almost nothing could shake me now. Almost no petty slight or small mishap had the power to vex me, frighten me, or drag me back into old dark moods. Even trivial little things felt like magic, full of charm.

Do not imagine that that warm, bright flame in my heart fed only on a parting promise. My generous, faraway provider sent me more than enough fuel to keep it burning steady. I was never left cold, never left wanting, never left to fear poverty, never left twisting in the wind of uncertainty. Every ship that docked from his route carried a letter from him. He wrote as he gave, as he loved: full-handed, full-hearted, no skimping, no holding back. He wrote because he wanted to, because he missed me. He never cut pages short, because he had no reason to hold back anything he wanted to say. He would sit down at his desk, pen in hand, simply because he loved Lucy and had a thousand small things to tell her—because he was faithful, thoughtful, tender, true. There was no falseness, no hollow pretense in him. He never coated his words in slippery, apologetic evasions, never sent cowardly excuses or empty nothings instead of news. He would never give me a stone, an excuse, a disappointment. His letters were real, nourishing food for my spirit, living, cool water that refreshed me after every long tiring day.

Was I grateful? God knows I was. I think no living soul who had ever been so well remembered, so steadily sustained, treated with such constant honor and kindness, could ever feel anything else, not until their last breath.

Devout to his own faith (he was never the sort to flip carelessly from one creed to another), he never once tried to pull me from my own quiet beliefs. He never teased me about it, never tempted me to convert. He said, plain and soft: “Stay a Protestant, my little English Puritan. I love that faith in you. I see its severe, gentle charm. There are parts of its ritual I could never embrace for myself, but it is the only creed that fits my Lucy.” All the bigotry of Rome could never take root in him, not even the Propaganda itself could turn him into a cunning, cold Jesuit. He was born honest, not false; artless, not manipulative; a free man, not a slave. His soft tenderness made him pliable in the hands of his priest, sometimes—his affection, his devotion, his sincere, earnest piety blinded his kind eyes sometimes, made him set aside his own good to do the work of crafty people, serve the ends of selfish men—but those are such rare, costly faults, we may well count them one day as precious jewels, not flaws.

And now the three years are done. M. Emanuel’s return date is fixed. It is autumn, and he will be here long before November’s thick mists roll over the town. My school thrives. The house is all ready for him. I have built him a small private library, lined the shelves with all the books he left in my care. I was never much of a gardener by nature, but I tended all his favorite plants out of love, and many of them are still blooming even now. I thought I loved him the day he sailed away—but that love has deepened, grown new roots. He is more mine now than he ever was before.

The sun slips past the equinox. The days draw short, the leaves turn sere and gold. But he is coming.

Night frosts creep over the windowpanes. November sends its fogs ahead to haunt the streets. The wind keens the old autumn moan around the gables. But he is coming.

The skies hang heavy, dark and full. A gale front sails in from the west. The clouds twist into strange, grand shapes: grand arches, broad glowing streaks of light. There are resplendent dawns, glorious and royal, deep purple as a king in his state robes, the whole sky blazed with flame. They are so wild they rival the heat of a thick battle, so bright and crimson they outshine Victory herself, perched on her war chariot. I know the signs of the sky; I have watched and noted them since I was a small child. God, watch over that ship. Oh, guard it, keep it safe.

The wind shifts to the west. Peace, peace, you banshee keening at every windowpane! The gale rises, swells, shrieks out long and loud. I wander from room to room all through that night, sleepless, and I cannot lull that raging blast. The hours slip by, and the storm only grows stronger. By midnight, every wakeful soul in the town hears it, fears it: a wild, screaming south-west tempest.

That storm raged frenzied, unstopping, for seven full days. It did not die until the Atlantic was strewn with wreckage. It did not lull until the deep darks of the ocean had gorged themselves full of what they wanted. Not until the destroying angel of the tempest had finished every last bit of his terrible work did he fold his thunderous wings, the sweep of them carrying storm and roar across the waves.

Peace, be still. Oh, a thousand weeping people, praying in agony on the shores waiting for their loved ones to sail home, strained to hear that quiet fall—but for many, the hush did not come soon enough. When the sun returned at last, its light was no better than dark night for the families of some who never came back.

Pause here. Pause at once. I have said enough. I will trouble no soft, kind heart with more of the dark story. Leave the sunny, tender imaginations to their hope. Let them picture the joy that bursts fresh and bright out of terrible fear, the rapture of being pulled back from the jaws of peril, the wondrous reprieve that erases every last dread, the long-awaited moment he steps off the ship and into your arms at last. Let them paint the picture of our long union, the quiet happy life that stretches ahead for us, full of small daily joys. Madame Beck prospered all the days of her life. So did Père Silas. Madame Walravens lived to see her ninetieth year before she passed away.

Farewell.
