There is no crueler irony than the fate that mocks the most carefully laid plans of those who map every variable to secure their own comfort—Machiavelli marked it well, writing of Caesar Borgia, who had braced for every possible disaster following his father’s death, provided against every ill chance he could name, and never once thought to foresee that his own death would follow fast on his father’s heels.

Sir Grandcourt’s entire standing in the realm rested on that grand, passive power that comes of inheriting thousands of acres of prime land. Wars abroad, political reforms at home, even the worst commercial panics only ever touched him through the line that fed his rental income; his most diligent biographer would never need to parse Bismarck’s policies, trade union demands, or household suffrage debates. He skimmed the top lines of every respectable newspaper, and his views on all these noisy, distressing topics were admirably broad, lumping every German, every tradesman, every voter who might use ill-scented soap under the single neat label “brutes.” He never bothered to speak on them, only peered at anyone who dared bring them up from under half-lowered lids, his silence heavy enough to shake the resolve of any timid thinker who dared argue in his presence.

But within the small, tight circle of people and desires he claimed as his own, Grandcourt had the cold, calculating cunning of a master continental diplomat, the kind who pulled off bloodless, unlooked-for coups no one saw coming.

Not a single movement Gwendolen made around Daniel Deronda escaped his notice. He would have scoffed if anyone called him jealous—jealousy, to his mind, would have implied he harbored some foolish doubt of his own power to stop anything he had set his will against. It would not have pained him a bit if his wife preferred another man’s company to his own; what he required was that she understand her longing was as useless as a hand trapped in a locked pair of cuffs, that not a single whim of hers could ever outweigh his unshakable resolve. He had been fleeting, whimsical even, in his choice to marry, but he left no room for wavering when it came to enforcing the terms of their bond. He had never once regretted the union: it had given his idle, aimless days a new sharp purpose, a new object for his will to press against. He did not regret his choice of Gwendolen, either. His taste was fastidious to the point of absurdity, and she satisfied every last box: he would never have stood a wife who did not gain a lift in social standing from marrying him, who did not turn every head with her proud bearing and her beauty, whose nails were not perfectly shaped, whose earlobes were even slightly too large or too flushed, or who, even with all those physical details correct, was a simpering ninny who could not snap back with a sharp, spirited retort. And far from minding that Gwendolen seethed with a barely restrained urge to defy him, that she had married him out of desperation and not passionate devotion, he found that temper perfectly suited him: for a man who has always preferred absolute command to any messy, reciprocal love, there is no reason to change one’s habits the second the wedding ring is on a finger.

This was not jealousy, he assured himself. But his behavior was identical to the jealous man’s, the exact shade of pale bitter yellow that can be produced by two very different sources.

He had come up to London far earlier than usual that spring to meet with his lawyers, iron out the terms of his new will, arrange the transfer of old mortgages, and finally push his uncle into signing over the succession to the Diplow estate, the deal he had lured the old man into with careful, unpressured offers of ready cash. But the pleasantest side effect of being in town early was parading his new, beautiful wife before every drawing room that mattered. Grandcourt would have sneered if anyone suggested he cared a whit for public admiration—but that very state of not-caring craved its required audience, a crowd of people staring, smiling, envying, for what is the point of stonily ignoring everyone’s praise if no one is there to praise you? For the first time in his life he brought a wife out to society, and he let their days be filled with glittering receptions, conspicuous rides through Hyde Park, public appearances at every distinguished event in the season. He wanted Gwendolen to be sought after, he enjoyed watching “fellows” cluster to talk to her, to escort her within his line of sight; he would not have even objected to a little lofty, playful coquetry on her part. The only thing he could not abide was the way she acted around Daniel Deronda.

After the musical soiree at Lady Mallinger’s, when he had watched the pair talk for ages on the settee, his gaze sharp as Hans Meyrick’s own, he did a very characteristic thing: he added Deronda’s name to the invitation list for their own upcoming party, right alongside the Mallingers, making sure no one could ever suspect him of caring whether Deronda came or went. He said nothing directly to Gwendolen about her behavior that night, too proud to let his disgust show loud enough to betray that it mattered. But a few days later, over breakfast, he remarked offhand, as if he had no particular thought in mind,

“Nothing makes a woman look more like a gawky fool than craning her neck to hunt for people and showing off her temper in public. A wife ought to have good manners. Otherwise it’s absolutely intolerable to be seen with her.”

Gwendolen’s stomach dropped. She knew the line was aimed straight at her, and the thought of being seen as gauche and foolish stung her sharply—even in her deep, unshakable melancholy, she still wanted every admirer around her, still wanted to be the most desired woman in every room. But the constant, hanging threat of his quiet rebuke only made her lean harder on every chance encounter with Deronda, stretching their rare brief conversations out in her mind, certain he could not fail to see how much she relied on him. Deronda never avoided her: far from it, he went out of his way to be gentle, to make it clear he still respected her, that the confidence she had placed in him was never misplaced. He liked being near her, after all: she was far more than a problem to solve, a lovely, sad-eyed woman whose fate felt like a weight on his shoulders, a responsibility he could not set down even when he knew his own future would never intersect with hers for long.

Grandcourt inadvertently pushed her closer to that desperate, clinging trust when he brought Mirah Lapidoth into their conversation. Gwendolen had been half-planning to take singing lessons from the young Jewish singer, a quiet promise to follow Deronda’s advice, but the days slipped by, unmarked by any real work to give them shape, and Grandcourt’s constant silent, languid presence hung over her like thick London fog, flattening every small burst of energy she tried to summon into the same bored, hollow indifference he lived in.

One morning over breakfast, though, she rallied the old sharpness that had once made her the toast of every party, picking at the prawn on her plate without taking a bite.

“I think I’ll make myself more accomplished while we’re in town,” she said, light and playful. “I’ll take singing lessons.”

Grandcourt lifted a languid eyebrow. “Why?”

“Why?” she echoed, feigning sauciness, “Because I can’t force myself to eat pâté de foie gras until I’m sleepy, I can’t smoke, and I can’t loiter at the club until I’m dying to come home. I need new kinds of ennui to keep things interesting. What time would work best for you, when you’re busy with your lawyers, for me to take lessons from that little Jewess everyone’s raving about, the one whose singing is all the rage right now?”

“Whenever you like,” Grandcourt said, pushing his plate away and leaning back in his chair, that cold, unblinking lizard stare fixed on her as he stroked the ears of the tiny spaniel curled on his lap— the dogs Gwendolen had come to hate, for the way they fawned all over him. “I don’t see why a lady needs to sing, anyhow. Amateurs only make fools of themselves. No lady ought to risk looking silly performing in company. And I’ve no interest in hearing squawking going on in my own house.”

“How frank you are,” Gwendolen said, tipping her chin up and turning her eyes away from his cold gaze, fixing them instead on the prawn’s pale boiled eyes, far more pleasant to look at than his. “That’s the greatest charm a husband can have, I think. But I don’t suppose you’d object to Miss Lapidoth singing at our party on the fourth? I thought of booking her. Lady Brackenshaw had her perform, you know, and the Raymonds, who are very particular about their musical entertainment. Mr. Deronda, who’s a wonderful musician and the best judge of singing I know, says no one sings half so tastefully for a drawing room. His opinion carries a great deal of weight with me.”

She had meant the line as a tiny, playful jab at him.

“It’s thoroughly indecent of Deronda to go about the place praising that girl,” Grandcourt said, his tone utterly flat, utterly indifferent.

“Indecent?” Gwendolen exclaimed, her face flushing bright red, staring at him in shocked disbelief, too stunned to stop and question how much truth there was to that casual, offhand claim.

“Of course. Especially when she’s under Lady Mallinger’s patronage. He ought to keep his mouth shut about her. Every man who’s been around town long enough can see exactly what his relations with her are.”

“Only men who judge everyone else by the filth in their own minds,” Gwendolen shot back, the red draining from her face to leave her pale, and a second later she was seized by a cold terror that she had spoken out of turn.

“Naturally. And a sensible woman takes other men’s judgment on these things,” Grandcourt purred, his voice soft as he dug his mental pinchers into that pale, shaken creature across the table from him. “Else she’s very likely to run her head straight into trouble. I suppose you think Deronda’s some sort of saint?”

“Good heavens no,” Gwendolen said, summoning every last scrap of her legendary self-control, her voice high and sharp and hard. “Only slightly less of a monster than everyone else.”

She stood up slow, pushed her chair back without hurry, and walked out of the room moving with the deliberate care of a man trying not to show he has had too much wine. She turned the key in her dressing room door behind her, and sat down, pale and still, as if she had not moved at all from the breakfast table. Even when she had read that terrible poison-pen letter about her family’s shame, she had not felt pain this sharp, this physical, as if a blade was twisting right in her chest. The image of Deronda as something other than the kind, honorable man she had trusted hit her like a hideous apparition, unshakable even before she could stop to ask if it was true. Worse, she suddenly realized how little she knew of him, how thin her faith had always been, how foolish she had been to pour out her secrets to him. His gentle rebukes, his quiet kindness, all the lofty, noble things he had said to her, suddenly felt like a sickening mask, the kind of pretense any selfish, immoral man could wear.

Then a stream of golden London sunlight broke through the gray fog outside her window, wrapping around her where she sat frozen on the edge of her bed. She stretched her arms up over her head, lacing her fingers tight in the old familiar gesture she used to fight off suffocating despair, and paced the room in the sunbeams. It was not true. The hateful dream meant nothing. She would not sit here doubting him, not a second longer.

She rang for her maid, ordered her carriage the second she heard Grandcourt had left for his lawyer appointment, and dressed as fast as her shaking hands would let her. She paced the gilded, empty drawing room like a caged animal, not even recognizing her own reflection in the mirrored panels, not caring that Grandcourt would almost certainly find out where she had gone the second he got home. She did not care what punishment he dreamed up for her, not as long as she got one single scrap of proof that her trust in Deronda had not been misplaced.

Her carriage pulled up outside Mirah’s small lodgings minutes later, and she climbed the stairs, her fine silk skirts swishing around her ankles, her heart hammering so hard she could hear it in her ears. Before the door was even fully opened, she heard Deronda’s low, familiar voice coming from the back room, reading aloud. She fumbled with her gloves, pretending to fix the buttons to hide how violently she was trembling, until Mirah stepped into the doorway, soft and quiet, her sweet face lighting up in recognition.

Relief flooded Gwendolen at the sight of her. She stepped inside, sat down, and leaned in close, her voice barely more than a whisper. “Perhaps you’re surprised to see me. I should have written, but I wanted to ask you a very particular favor. I hoped you would agree to sing at my party on the fourth, the same kind of small performance you did for Lady Brackenshaw. I would be so very grateful.”

Mirah smiled. “I would be happy to. Shall I perform around ten?”

Gwendolen nodded, and could not make herself stand up to leave. Deronda’s voice was still drifting through the wall. The words spilled out of her before she could stop them. “Mr. Deronda is in the next room, isn’t he?”

“Yes,” Mirah said, calm as ever. “He’s reading Hebrew with my brother.”

“You have a brother?” Gwendolen said, she had heard the mention once before but never paid it any mind.

“I do. He’s an invalid, consumptive, and Mr. Deronda is the kindest friend he has ever had, the same way he was kind to me,” Mirah said, her voice softening with the force of her gratitude.

Gwendolen reached out and laid her hand on Mirah’s, her fingers cold and shaking. “Tell me the truth. Are you sure he is completely good? That there is no evil in him at all? That all the cruel, ugly things people say about him are lies?”

Mirah’s eyes flashed bright with indignation. “Who dares speak evil of him? I would not believe a single bad word about him if an angel from heaven came to tell it to me. He found me when I was so miserable I was about to drown myself in the Thames, ragged and homeless, no one in the whole world to care if I lived or died. And he treated me like I was a king’s daughter. He brought me to the kindest woman I ever knew. He found my long-lost brother for me, and honors him even though we were both as poor as beggars. My brother is a very learned, great-minded man, and he adores him. There is no one half so good in all of London.”

It was like drinking cold, clear water after days of wandering in the desert. Gwendolen did not even notice that Mirah was angry at her for daring to doubt him. The ugly lie Grandcourt had planted in her head dissolved completely, vanishing like smoke in the sunlight. Deronda was nothing like the vile picture her husband had painted. She squeezed Mirah’s hand, whispered “Thank you, thank you” in a hoarse rush, and fled out the door before Deronda could step out and see her, suddenly desperate to be gone.

She climbed back into her carriage, and by the time she pulled up to her own door in Grosvenor Square, Grandcourt was just arriving home, a cigar between his fingers. He tossed it aside, handed her out of the carriage, and followed her up the stairs. She ducked into the drawing room to put space between them, sat down, pulled off her gloves, and rubbed a hand over her forehead, acting as if he was not even in the room.

“Might I ask where you’ve been at this very unusual hour?” Grandcourt said, his voice low and cold.

“Oh, yes,” Gwendolen said, not lifting her eyes from her gloves. “I went to Miss Lapidoth’s house, to invite her to sing at our party.”

“And to pump her for details about her relations with Deronda?” Grandcourt said, a cruel, icy sneer in his voice that sounded diabolical to Gwendolen’s overtaxed ears.

For the first time since their wedding, all her fear snapped. She lifted her eyes to his, her voice sharp and biting. “Yes. And what you said is a low, wicked falsehood.”

“Did she tell you that, then?” Grandcourt drawled, his sneer deeper, more contemptuous.

Gwendolen went mute. The fiery anger in her throat curdled into dumb, helpless rage. What proof did she have? All the bright, warm certainty she had felt at Mirah’s lodgings shriveled up under his cold gaze. She had no evidence, nothing but her own stupid, foolish feelings, and he would laugh that right out of existence. She turned her head away, staring furiously at the far wall.

Grandcourt knew he had won. “It makes no difference to me, as far as her singing goes,” he said, his tone lazy and dismissive. “You can have her perform if you like. But you will observe this, from now on: you will never go near that house again. As my wife, you will take my word for what is proper for you. When you married me, you agreed not to make a fool of yourself. That is exactly what you did this morning. If you keep acting this way, every man at every club in London will be whispering about you soon, and you will hate what they say. You do not know how the world works. You married me. You will follow my rules.”

Every slow, soft sentence landed like a weight on Gwendolen’s shoulders, and she was utterly helpless against it. He never mentioned the incident again. He knew exactly how much power his words held. That cold, sharp-faced, white-handed man could have governed the most unruly colony in the empire and won endless praise for it—he would have known far better than to cajole the people he meant to control, and he would never have flinched from making their chains as unbreakable as the ones he had just clamped around Gwendolen’s throat.
