The future breathes sometimes a soft, shuddering sob of warning long before the calamity it carries crashes into our lives. It lingers like a distant, gathering storm: the wind’s low, keening note in treetops, the strange bruised flush of sunset bleeding across the sky, tattered clouds that warn of a blast strong enough to strew the whole sea with wreckage, or to drift a fog thick with the yellow taint of Eastern pestilence over white Western cliffs, dimming the lattice panes of English homes with the poisoned exhalation of far-off plagues. Other times it bursts upon us all unannounced, as if a rock has split open to reveal a long-sealed grave, and the shrouded form of one who slept through the ages steps forth to stand face to face with you before you have time to gasp: a new Lazarus, risen unlooked for, bearing no gift but unforeseen calamity.

Caroline Helstone had walked home from Hollow’s Cottage that evening perfectly well, or so she had thought. When she woke the next morning, though, a strange heavy languor clung to her bones, a weight she could not shake off. At breakfast, and every meal the day after that, she could summon no appetite at all. What had once been her favourite spiced tarts, currant jams, and thick fresh cream all tasted like dry ash and coarse sawdust on her tongue.
“Am I unwell?” she murmured, pausing to stare at her reflection in the glass. Her eyes were bright, pupils blown wide, her cheeks rosier and fuller than they had been in months. “I look well. Why can I not eat?”

A pulse thudded fast and hard at her temples, and her brain hummed with strange, bright scattered energy: hundreds of half-formed, glittering thoughts darted through her head, gilded with the same warm flush that stained her cheeks. Then came a night of parched burning thirst, no rest, no ease. Towards dawn, a terrible nightmare seized her, claws sinking deep like a tiger’s hold, and when she jolted awake she knew, with sharp cold certainty, that she was ill.

She could not fathom how she had caught the fever. Most likely, she thought, some sweet poisoned breeze, thick with honeydew and hidden miasma, had wrapped around her on that late walk home, slipping into her lungs and veins to feed the slow spark of fever she had carried for months: the smouldering excitement of unspoken love, the heavy languor of long quiet conflict and habitual gentle sadness. It had fanned that spark to a bright unquenchable flame.

For days no one feared for her. After two hot restless days and sleepless nights, her symptoms seemed mild, no wild delirium, no violent spikes. Her uncle, the maids, the local doctor, even Shirley Keeldar when she came to call, all agreed a few days of rest would set her right. She’d be up and about picking roses in the rectory garden before the week was out, they all said.

But the few days stretched on, and still she did not recover, though everyone kept insisting health could not be far off. Mrs. Pryor, who had visited her every single day, sat at her bedside for a long quiet watch one morning, two weeks into her illness, holding her thin wrist between her fingers to count the thready racing pulse. Then she slipped quietly out of the room, shut herself in Mr. Helstone’s study, and stayed closeted with him for half the morning. When she came back, she set down her bonnet and shawl, stood by the bedside rocking gently back and forth, one hand folded in the other, that quiet old habit of hers, and spoke soft.
“I’ve sent Fanny over to Fieldhead to fetch my things. I’m to stay here and look after you till you’re well. Your uncle has kindly given his permission. Would that be alright with you, Caroline?”

“I’m sorry you should take such needless trouble,” Caroline had smiled back, faint and soft. “I do not feel very ill, but I cannot refuse resolutely. It will be such comfort to know you are in the house, to see you sometimes in the room. But don’t confine yourself on my account, dear Mrs. Pryor. Fanny nurses me very well.”

Bending over the pale little sufferer, Mrs. Pryor smoothed the soft hair under her cap, and gently fluffed her pillows. Caroline lifted her face to kiss her, smiling still.
“Are you free from pain? Are you tolerably at ease?” the older woman asked in a low earnest voice, returning the caress.
“I think I am almost happy.”
“You wish to drink? Your lips are parched.” She held a glass of cool barley water to her mouth. “Have you eaten anything today, Caroline?”
“I cannot eat.”
“But soon your appetite will return. It must return. That is, I pray God it may.”
When she laid her back on the couch, her arms wrapped around Caroline almost involuntarily, drawing her tight to her chest for a long still moment.
“I shall hardly wish to get well, that I may keep you always,” Caroline laughed.
Mrs. Pryor did not smile. A tremor ran over her features, and it took her minutes to steady it back into calm.
“You are more used to Fanny than to me,” she said at last. “I should think my attendance must seem strange, officious?”
“No. Quite natural, and very soothing. You must have been accustomed to wait on sick people, ma’am. You move about the room so softly, and you speak so quietly, and touch me so gently.”
“I am dexterous in nothing, my dear. You will often find me awkward, but never negligent.”

Negligent she never was. From that hour Fanny and Eliza faded into nonentities in the sick room: Mrs. Pryor made it her whole world, tending to every duty, never leaving it day or night. Caroline protested faintly at first, then not at all. Loneliness and gloom were banished from her bedside; protection and soft solace sat there in their place. The two settled into a wondrous gentle union. Caroline had always hated needing or receiving much care, and Mrs. Pryor had never before had the habit or knack of performing small tender services for anyone, but all of it unfolded so easily, so naturally, that the patient was as willing to be cherished as the nurse was bent on cherishing her. No flicker of weariness ever crossed Mrs. Pryor’s face to remind Caroline she ought to feel guilty for the trouble.

Even with all this tender care, it was strange the girl did not get well. She wasted like a snow-wreath in thaw, faded like a rose left parched in drought. Shirley Keeldar, whose thoughts never lingered on danger or death for long, had feared nothing at all for her friend at first, but as she visited week after week and saw Caroline sink a little further each time, cold alarm clutched at her heart. She confronted Mr. Helstone with such fierce energy that the gentleman was forced, very unwillingly, to admit his niece was suffering from something far worse than a mild migraine. When Mrs. Pryor quietly demanded a second specialist, he told her she might send for two if she liked. One came, delivered a dark vague pronouncement only time would unpack, wrote his prescriptions, issued his directions with crushing authority, pocketed his fee and left, no doubt knowing full well he could do no good but unwilling to say so.

Still no rumour of serious illness spread through the neighbourhood. At Hollow’s Cottage, everyone thought Caroline only had a severe cold, for she had written Hortense a little note to that effect, and Mademoiselle Moore contented herself with sending two pots of homemade currant jam, a recipe for a healing tisane, and a page of gentle advice. Mrs. Yorke, hearing a physician had been summoned, sneered at the hypochondriac fancies of the rich and idle, who, she said, having nothing but themselves to think of, would send for a doctor if their little finger so much as ached.

The “rich and idle” Caroline, meanwhile, was falling fast into a state of prostration whose rapid, wasting weakness puzzled every single person watching her—except one, who alone knew how liable a long undermined structure is to collapse all at once.

Sick people often have fancies no ordinary attendant can unravel, and Caroline had one even her tender nurse could not explain at first. Every single week, on the same day, the same hour, no matter if she was better or worse, she would beg to be helped up, dressed, and settled in the armchair by the window. She would sit there, quiet and patient, until noon had long passed. No matter how pale and exhausted she looked, she would gently put off every plea to lie back down until the church clock had tolled all twelve strokes of midday. The second the last chime faded, she would turn docile, meekly letting herself be helped back to bed. Once settled under the covers, she would bury her face deep in the pillow and pull the blankets tight around her, as if shutting out the sun and the world she was growing tired of. More than once, a faint tremor shook the small sick bed, and a quiet sob broke the stillness, and Mrs. Pryor saw all of it.

One Tuesday morning, as usual, Caroline had asked to rise. She sat wrapped in her soft white dressing gown, leaning forward in the easy chair, gazing steadily, patiently, out the lattice. Mrs. Pryor sat a little behind her, ostensibly knitting, but in truth watching her every breath. A soft change crossed Caroline’s pale mournful brow, animating its languor; a spark shot back into her faded eyes, reviving their old warm lustre. She half rose, craning forward to stare earnestly out. Mrs. Pryor glided softly to her side, glancing over her shoulder. From that window they could see the churchyard, and beyond it the open road. And there, riding sharply past, was a horseman. He was not far enough to be unrecognised, and Mrs. Pryor had long sight. She knew Mr. Robert Moore. Just as a little rise of ground between the road and the house blocked him from view, the church clock struck twelve.

“May I lie down again?” Caroline asked, quiet and small.
Her nurse helped her back to bed, drew the curtain around her, and stood listening near. The little couch trembled, and a suppressed sob stirred the still air. A sharp contraction of anguish twisted Mrs. Pryor’s features; she wrung her hands, half a groan escaping her lips. She remembered then: Tuesday was Whinbury market day. Mr. Moore always rode past the rectory on his way there, just before noon, every single week.

Caroline wore a slender braid of silk around her neck at all times, strung with some small trinket. Mrs. Pryor had seen a glint of gold peep out once or twice, but never got a clear look at it. Caroline never parted with it: when dressed, it was hidden deep in her bodice; when lying in bed, she always held it tight in her hand. That Tuesday afternoon, the thin doze—more like lethargy than true sleep—that sometimes shortened her long days had stolen over her. The weather was hot, and in her restless febrile tossing, she had pushed the blankets a little aside. Mrs. Pryor bent to tuck them back in. The small wasted hand, lying nerveless on her breast, still clasped that jealously guarded treasure. Those thin, attenuated fingers, painful to look at, were lax in sleep. Mrs. Pryor gently disengaged the silk braid, drawing out a tiny locket: a simple little thing, cheap enough for her small girlhood purse to afford. Under its crystal face rested a tight, crisp curl of black hair, far too short and coarse to have been cut from a woman’s head.

A small agitated movement made the silk chain twitch. The sleeper started, jolted awake. Her thoughts were scattered now when she woke, her gaze wandering. Half rising in fear, she cried out:
“Don’t take it from me, Robert! Don’t! It is my last comfort. Let me keep it. I never tell anyone whose hair it is; I never show it.”

Mrs. Pryor had already slipped behind the curtain, withdrawing out of sight. Caroline looked around the empty room, thinking herself alone. Her stray thoughts drifted back slow, like tired birds folding their wings on the sad shore of her mind, and the words she had never spoken aloud in health poured unconsciously from her lips.
“Oh, I should see him once more before all is over! Heaven might favour me thus far!” she cried. “God grant me a little comfort before I die! But he will not know I am ill till I am gone, and he will come when they have laid me out, and I am senseless, cold, and stiff. What can my departed soul feel then? Can it see or know what happens to the clay? Can spirits, through any medium, communicate with living flesh? Can the dead revisit those they leave? Can they come in the elements? Will wind, water, fire, lend me a path to Moore? Is it for nothing the wind sounds almost articulately sometimes—sings as I have lately heard it sing at night—or passes the casement sobbing, as if for sorrow to come? Does nothing, then, haunt it, nothing inspire it? Why, it suggested words to me one night; it poured a strain I could have written down, only I was appalled, and dared not rise to seek pencil and paper by the dim watch-light. What is that electricity they speak of, whose changes make us well or ill, whose lack or excess blasts, whose even balance revives? What are all those influences that are about us in the atmosphere, that keep playing over our nerves like fingers on stringed instruments, and call forth now a sweet note, and now a wail—now an exultant swell, and anon the saddest cadence? Where is the other world? In what will another life consist? Why do I ask? Have I not cause to think that the hour is hasting but too fast when the veil must be rent for me? Do I not know the Grand Mystery is likely to burst prematurely on me? Great Spirit, in whose goodness I confide, whom, as my Father, I have petitioned night and morning from early infancy, help the weak creation of Thy hands! Sustain me through the ordeal I dread and must undergo! Give me strength! Give me patience! Give me—oh, give me FAITH!”

She fell back on her pillow. Mrs. Pryor stole quietly out of the room, then slipped back in again soon after, as composed as if she had not heard a single word of that strange, raw soliloquy.

The next day callers began to arrive. Word had spread that Miss Helstone was worse. Mr. Hall and his sister Margaret came, and both left the sick room in tears, shocked at how much she had altered. Hortense Moore came, too, and Caroline seemed almost revived by her presence. She smiled and assured her she was not dangerously ill, talking low but cheerfully, the flush of excitement bringing colour back to her cheeks so she almost looked well.
“How is Mr. Robert?” Mrs. Pryor asked as Hortense gathered her things to leave.
“He was very well when he left.”
“Left! Is he gone from home?”
It was then explained that urgent police news about the rioters he had been chasing had called him away to Birmingham that very morning, and he would not likely be back for a full fortnight.
“He does not know Miss Helstone is very ill?”
“Oh no! He thought, like me, that she only had a bad cold.”

After Hortense left, Mrs. Pryor did not approach Caroline’s bedside for over an hour, for she heard her quiet weeping and dared not look on her tears. As evening closed in, she brought her a cup of tea. Caroline opened her eyes from a short doze, and looked at her nurse with a blank unrecognising gaze.
“I smelt the honeysuckles in the glen this summer morning,” she said, “as I stood at the counting-house window.”
Strange words like these from pale fevered lips pierce a loving listener’s heart sharper than steel. They sound romantic on a page, but in real life they are harrowing.
“My darling, do you know me?” Mrs. Pryor whispered.
“I went in to call Robert to breakfast. I have been with him in the garden. He asked me to go. A heavy dew has refreshed the flowers. The peaches are ripening.”
“My darling! my darling!” the nurse repeated over and over.
“I thought it was daylight—long after sunrise. It looks dark. Is the moon now set?”
That moon, lately risen, gazed full and mild down upon her, floating unclouded in the deep blue summer sky.
“Then it is not morning? I am not at the cottage? Who is this? I see a shape at my bedside.”
“It is myself—it is your friend—your nurse—your—— Lean your head on my shoulder. Collect yourself.” Her voice dropped to a trembling murmur. “O God, take pity! Give her life, and me strength! Send me courage! Teach me words!”

Several minutes passed in silence. The patient lay mute and passive in the trembling arms, against the throbbing bosom of her nurse.
“I am better now,” Caroline whispered at last. “Much better. I know where I am. This is Mrs. Pryor near me. I was dreaming. I talk when I wake up from dreams; people often do in illness. How fast your heart beats, ma’am! Do not be afraid.”
“It is not fear, child—only a little anxiety, which will pass. I have brought you some tea, Cary. Your uncle made it himself. You know he says he can make a better cup of tea than any housewife can. Taste it. He is concerned to hear that you eat so little; he would be glad if you had a better appetite.”
“I am thirsty. Let me drink.”
She drank eagerly.
“What o’clock is it, ma’am?”
“Past nine.”
“Not later? Oh! I have yet a long night before me. But the tea has made me strong. I will sit up.”
Mrs. Pryor raised her, and fluffed her pillows around her.
“Thank Heaven! I am not always equally miserable, and ill, and hopeless. The afternoon has been bad since Hortense went; perhaps the evening may be better. It is a fine night, I think? The moon shines clear.”
“Very fine—a perfect summer night. The old church-tower gleams white almost as silver.”
