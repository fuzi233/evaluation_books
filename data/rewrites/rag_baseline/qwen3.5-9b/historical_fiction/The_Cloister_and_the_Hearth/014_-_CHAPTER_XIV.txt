The back door yielded to a blow delivered with the crude finality of a gavel strike, sealing their fate within the confines of the house. Yet, amidst the thunderous assault, Margaret seemed to reclaim a fragment of her composure. With a whisper that cut through the chaos, she commanded, "Tell them he was here, but is gone." Seizing Gerard, she nearly dragged him up the rough-hewn steps leading to her father's chamber, her own resting just beyond. The pounding intensified, a relentless rhythm demanding entry. "Who dares knock at this hour?" Martin retorted, his voice steady despite the intrusion. "Open, and you shall see!" came the demand. "I yield not to thieves—honest men sleep now." "Open to the law, Martin Wittenhaagen, or face the consequences." The voice belonged to Dirk Brower. "What brings you so far from Tergou?" "Open, and you shall know." Slowly, deliberately, Martin drew the bolt. In surged Dierich and four others, letting in their companion who had entered through the rear. "Now, Martin, where is Gerard Eliassoen?" "Gerard Eliassoen? He was here but moments ago!" "Was he?" Dierich's face fell, the color draining from his features. "And where is he now?" "They say he has fled to Italy. Pray, what is to be done?" "It matters not. When did he leave? Do not tell me he departed in such a storm!" "A coil indeed surrounds Gerard Eliassoen," Martin sneered, his tone dripping with disdain. He lit the candle, seated himself calmly beside the fire, and began to whet a fine silk cord around his bowstring at the point where the arrow's notch fretted it. "I shall inform you," he said casually. "Do you know his brother Giles? A misbegotten imp, all head and limbs? Well, he tore over here on a mule, bellowing words I could not catch from my distance, only the noise itself. Regardless, he startled Gerard. Immediately after his departure, there was such weeping and kissing, and then Gerard vanished. They claim he has gone to Italy—perhaps you know where that lies, for I do not." Dierich's expression sank further at this report. No flaw marred his tale; a more cunning man might have spun too many lies and invited suspicion, but Martin executed his duty perfectly. He uttered only the single falsehood he was instructed to speak, inventing nothing else from his own mind. "Comrades," Dierich murmured, "I fear he speaks truth. I warned the burgomaster how this would unfold. He encountered the dwarf galloping Peter Buyskens's mule from Sevenbergen. 'They have sent that imp to Gerard,' he declared, 'so Gerard must be at Sevenbergen.' 'Ah, master!' I replied, 'It is too late now. We should have sought Sevenbergen before wasting our time hunting the obscure corners of Tergou for those cursed parchments we shall never find until we locate the man who stole them. If he was at Sevenbergen, and they dispatched the dwarf to him, it must have been to warn him we hunt him. He is leagues away by now,' I concluded. Confound that chalk-faced girl! She has outwitted us bearded men; and so I told the burgomaster, yet he refused to hear reason. A wet jerkin apiece—that is all we shall gain from this venture." Martin grinned coolly in Dierich's face. "However," the latter added, "to satisfy the burgomaster, we must search the house." Martin's demeanor shifted instantly to gravity. This transformation did not escape Dierich's notice. He paused, reflecting. "Station two of you outside, one on each flank of the house, to ensure no one leaps from the upper windows. The rest follow me." Taking the candle, he ascended the stairs, trailed by three comrades. Martin was left alone. The stout soldier bowed his head. Everything had proceeded smoothly at first; now came this fatal reversal. Suddenly, a spark of hope ignited. Gerard must be in Peter's room or Margaret's; neither was excessively high from the ground. Gerard would leap. Dierich had left a guard below, but what then? For half a minute, Gerard and he would stand two to one, and in that fleeting interval, anything might occur. Martin held the back door ajar and watched. Light spilled into Peter's room. "Curse the fool!" he muttered. "Is he allowing them to capture him like a maiden?" The illumination drifted into Margaret's bedroom. Still, no window opened. Had Gerard intended to flee that way, he would not have waited until the men occupied the room. Martin grasped this immediately and descended to the foot of the stairs, listening intently. He began to believe Gerard must have escaped via the window while the men searched the interior. The longer the silence persisted, the stronger this conviction grew. Then, it was shattered abruptly and rudely. Faint cries emanated from the inner bedroom—Margaret's. "They have taken him," Martin groaned. "They have secured him." Instantly, it flashed across his mind that if they removed Gerard, his life would be worthless; and if harm befell him, Margaret's heart would shatter. Casting his eyes wildly around like a savage beast seeking an exit, he formed a resolution in a twinkling, terribly characteristic of those iron times and a soldier driven to the horns. He moved to each door in turn, mimicking Dierich Brower's voice sharply, "Watch the window!" Then, quietly, he closed and bolted both doors. He picked up his bow and six arrows; fitting one to the string, he stowed the others in his quiver. His knife he placed upon a chair behind him, hilt facing inward, and there he waited at the foot of the stairs with the calm determination to slay those four men, or perish by their hands. Two, he knew, he could dispatch with his arrows before they could approach, leaving Gerard and him to face the remaining pair hand-to-hand. Moreover, he had witnessed men panic-stricken by such a sudden assault. Should Brower and his men hesitate even an instant before engaging him, he would shoot three instead of two, tipping the odds decisively in their favor. He need not wait long. Heavy footsteps echoed in Margaret's room, drawing nearer and nearer. The light advanced, accompanied by voices. Martin's heart, though stout, hammered violently to hear men marching thus to their deaths—and perhaps his own; more likely so than not: for four against one in a battlefield ten feet square was long odds, and Gerard might be bound, powerless to aid. Yet this man, whom we have seen tremble in his shoes at the mere mention of a Giles-o'-lantern, never wavered in this awful moment of peril. He stood there, body braced for combat, eye glowing, equally prepared to take life and lose it. A desperate gamble! To win meant instant exile for life; to lose meant death upon that very floor. Dierich Brower and his men discovered Peter in the first stages of sleep. They ransacked his cupboards, drove knives into an alligator he had nailed to his wall, and peered beneath his bed. It was a spacious room, seemingly replete with hiding places, yet Gerard was nowhere to be found. Proceeding to Margaret's room, the sight proved disheartening—it was small and bare, devoid of cupboards, save for a large fireplace and chimney. Dierich's eye fixed immediately upon these. Here they found the beauty of Sevenbergen sleeping on an old chest scarcely a foot high, unshielded by any covering; yet the sheets were snow-white, as was Margaret's own linen. There she lay, resembling a lily fallen into a rut. Soon she awoke, sitting upright in bed, amazed; then, spotting the intruders, she began to scream faintly, praying for mercy. Her plea made Dierich Brower ashamed of his mission. "Here is a disturbance," he said, slightly flustered. "We intend no harm to you, my pretty maid. Lie still, shut your eyes, and dream of your wedding night while I inspect this chimney to see if Master Gerard hides therein." "Gerard! In my room?" "Why not? They say you and he—" "Cruel! You know they have driven him away from me—expelled him from his native land. This is a ruse. You are thieves; you are wicked men; you are not men of Sevenbergen, or you would know Margaret Brandt better than to seek her lover in this room of all others in the world. Oh, brave! Four great hulking men, armed to the teeth, to insult one poor honest girl! The women dwelling in your own homes must be naught, or you would respect them too much to defile a girl of good character." "Enough! Depart, before we hear worse," Dierich hastened, saying. "He is not in the chimney. Plaster may mend what a cudgel breaks, but a woman's tongue is a double-edged dagger, and a girl retains her mother's milk." He beat a hasty retreat. "I told the burgomaster how this would play out."
