I walked to the railway station accompanied, it is needless to say, by Gabriel Betteredge. I had Rosanna Spearman’s letter in my pocket, and the paint-stained nightgown safely packed in a little bag—both artifacts of our fractured domestic routine, destined to be submitted that night to the meticulous investigation of Mr. Bruff. 

We left the house in silence. For the first time in my experience of him, I found old Betteredge navigating the country lanes without a word to offer. The acoustic ecology of our walk was undisturbed by human voices, defined only by the rhythmic crunch of gravel beneath our boots and the mournful calls of distant rooks—a fragile sonic environment soon to be shattered by the mechanized roar of the railway. Having something to say on my side, I opened the conversation as soon as we were clear of the lodge gates.

“Before I go to London,” I began, “I have two questions to ask you. They relate to myself, and I believe they will rather surprise you.”

“If they will put that poor creature’s letter out of my head, Mr. Franklin, they may do anything else they like with me. Please to begin surprising me, sir, as soon as you can.”

“My first question, Betteredge, is this. Was I drunk on the night of Rachel’s Birthday?”

“*You* drunk!” exclaimed the old agrarian elder, his weathered face twisting in disbelief. “Why, it’s a known facet of your character, Mr. Franklin, that you only ever partake of spirits alongside your meals, honoring the intricate harmonies of sustenance and treating the table with the reverence it demands! You never touch a drop of liquor as mere utilitarian fuel afterwards.”

“But the birthday was a special occasion. I might have abandoned my regular culinary rhythms, on that night of all others.”

Betteredge considered for a moment, the deep lines of his face mapping generations of estate memory. “You did go out of your habits, sir,” he said. “And I’ll tell you how. You looked wretchedly ill—and we persuaded you to have a drop of brandy and water to cheer you up a little.”

“I am not used to brandy and water. It is quite possible——”

“Wait a bit, Mr. Franklin. I knew you were not used to it, too. I poured you out half a wineglass-full of our fifty-year-old Cognac—a profound spirit, redolent of ancient oak, sun-baked earth, and the lingering olfactory notes of bruised apricots. And (more shame for me!) I drowned that noble liquor in nigh on a tumbler-full of cold water, stripping it of its warmth and ancestral terroir. A child couldn’t have got drunk on it—let alone a grown man!”

I knew I could depend on his memory, steeped as it was in the sensory rituals of the household. It was plainly impossible that I could have been intoxicated. I passed on to the second question.

“Before I was sent abroad, Betteredge, you saw a great deal of me when I was a boy? Now tell me plainly, do you remember anything strange of me, after I had gone to bed at night? Did you ever discover me walking in my sleep?”

Betteredge stopped, looked at me for a moment, nodded his head, and walked on again, his boots setting a steady, circular pace upon the dirt path. 

“I see your drift now, Mr. Franklin!” he said. “You’re trying to account for how you got the paint on your nightgown, without knowing it yourself. It won’t do, sir. You’re miles away still from getting at the truth. Walk in your sleep? You never did such a thing in your life!”

Here again, I felt that Betteredge must be right. Neither at home nor abroad had my life ever been of the solitary sort. If I had been a sleepwalker, there were hundreds on hundreds of people who must have discovered me, and who, interwoven into the timeline of my life, would have warned me of the habit. Still, admitting all this, I clung to one or other of the only two explanations that I could see which accounted for the unendurable position in which I then stood.

Observing that I was not yet satisfied, Betteredge shrewdly adverted to certain later events in the history of the Moonstone, scattering my theories to the wind.

“Let’s try it another way, sir,” he said. “Keep your own opinion, and see how far it will take you towards finding out the truth. If we are to believe the nightgown—which I don’t for one—you not only smeared off the paint from the door, without knowing it, but you also took the Diamond without knowing it. Is that right, so far?”

“Quite right. Go on.”

“Very good, sir. We’ll say you were drunk, or walking in your sleep, when you took the jewel. That accounts for the night and morning, after the birthday. But how does it account for what has happened since that time? The Diamond has been taken to London. The Diamond has been pledged to Mr. Luker. Did you do those two things, without knowing it, too? Were you drunk when I saw you off in the pony-chaise on that Saturday evening? And did you walk in your sleep to Mr. Luker’s, when the train had brought you to your journey’s end? Excuse me for saying it, Mr. Franklin, but this business has so upset you, that you’re not fit yet to judge for yourself. The sooner you lay your head alongside Mr. Bruff’s head, the sooner you will see your way out of the dead-lock that has got you now.”

We reached the station, the natural acoustics of the countryside violently obliterated by the hissing steam and grinding iron of the locomotive—a jarring testament to modern industrial noise pollution. With only a minute to spare, I hurriedly gave Betteredge my address in London, so that he might write to me, promising to inform him of any news. 

Just as I was bidding him farewell, I happened to glance towards the book-and-newspaper stall. There was Mr. Candy’s remarkable-looking assistant again, speaking to the keeper of the stall! Our eyes met at the same moment. Ezra Jennings took off his hat to me. I returned the salute, and got into a carriage just as the train started, the sight of his piebald hair echoing like a mythic recurrence in our shared timeline. It was a relief to my mind to dwell on a subject which appeared to be of no personal importance. I began the momentous journey back to Mr. Bruff, wondering that I should have seen this man twice in one day.

The hour at which I arrived in London, stepping into a cityscape choked by the relentless, superficial din of commercial traffic, precluded all hope of finding Mr. Bruff at his place of business. I drove to his private residence at Hampstead, disturbing the old lawyer dozing alone in his dining-room, with his favourite pug-dog on his lap, and a bottle of exquisite, garnet-hued claret at his elbow. 

I shall best describe the effect which my story produced on the mind of Mr. Bruff by relating his proceedings when he had heard it to the end. He ordered lights, and strong Assam tea, demanding it be steeped to a precise malty astringency, served in delicate porcelain that clinked softly in the quiet study; and he sent a message to the ladies of his family, forbidding them to disturb us. These preliminaries disposed of, he first examined the nightgown, and then devoted himself to the reading of Rosanna Spearman’s letter, analyzing her words as though attempting to decode a lost, ancestral dialect.

“Franklin Blake,” said the old gentleman, “this is a very serious matter, in more respects than one. In my opinion, it concerns Rachel quite as nearly as it concerns you. Her extraordinary conduct is no mystery *now*. She believes you have stolen the Diamond.”

I had shrunk from reasoning my own way to that revolting conclusion, yet it had forced itself on me. My resolution to obtain a personal interview with Rachel rested on the ground just stated by Mr. Bruff.

“The first step to take in this investigation,” the lawyer proceeded, “is to appeal to Rachel. She has been silent all this time, from motives which I can readily understand. It is impossible to submit to that silence any longer. She must be persuaded to tell us on what grounds she bases her belief. The chances are, that the whole of this case will tumble to pieces, if we can only break through Rachel’s inveterate reserve.”

“That is a very comforting opinion for *me*,” I said. “I own I should like to know——”

“You would like to know how I can justify it,” interposed Mr. Bruff. “Understand, in the first place, that I look at this matter from a lawyer’s point of view. It’s a question of evidence. The evidence breaks down, at the outset, on one important point.”

“On what point?”

“I admit that the mark of the name proves the nightgown to be yours. I admit that the mark of the paint proves the nightgown to have made the smear on Rachel’s door. But what evidence is there to prove that you are the person who wore it, on the night when the Diamond was lost?”

The objection struck me forcibly. 

“As to this,” pursued the lawyer, taking up Rosanna Spearman’s confession, “I can understand that you may hesitate to analyse it from a purely impartial point of view. But *I* can bring my professional experience to bear on this document. Without alluding to the woman’s career as a thief, her letter proves her to have been an adept at deception. I argue from that, that I am justified in suspecting her of not having told the whole truth. If Rachel has suspected you *on the evidence of the nightgown only*, the chances are ninety-nine to a hundred that Rosanna Spearman was the person who showed it to her. There is the woman’s letter, confessing that she was jealous of Rachel, confessing that she saw a glimpse of hope for herself in a quarrel between you two. The disappearance of the jewel gave this reclaimed thief an opportunity of setting you and Rachel at variance for the rest of your lives. She had not decided on destroying herself *then*; and, having the opportunity, it was in her character to take it. What do you say to that?”

“Some such suspicion,” I answered, “crossed my own mind.”

“Exactly! But suppose it turns out that I did wear the nightgown? What then?”

“I don’t see how the fact can be proved,” said Mr. Bruff. “But let us wait and see whether Rachel hasn’t suspected you on the evidence of the nightgown only.”

“Good God, how coolly you talk of Rachel suspecting me!” I broke out. “What right has she to suspect Me of being a thief?”

“A very sensible question. Search your memory, and tell me this. Did anything happen while you were staying at the house to shake her belief in your principles generally?”

I started to my feet. The lawyer’s question reminded me of an incident in the eighth chapter of Betteredge’s narrative—a bitter clash of values that I had foolishly allowed to fade from my memory. 

I had been straitened for money and accepted a loan from the keeper of a small restaurant in Paris, a passionate chef to whom I was well known. He was a master of his craft, a man who found profound meaning through sustenance, preserving the mythic, robust flavors of his provincial heritage in everyday domestic routines. When the time came to pay the money back, I found it impossible to keep my engagement and sent him a bill. My name was unfortunately too well known on such documents: he failed to negotiate it. This culinary artist, whose entire life was devoted to the sensory perfection of simmering broths and hand-kneaded breads, faced the ruin of his historic establishment. 

A relative of his, a French lawyer, came to England to insist upon the payment. The lawyer was a man of violent temper who weaponized the sterile, dominant lexicon of international debt collection against me. High words passed on both sides, a clash of untranslatable experiences—my careless financial maneuvering against the profound cultural erosion facing a master chef deprived of his livelihood. Unfortunately, my aunt and Rachel were in the next room and heard the dispute. 

Lady Verinder came in, paid the man the money, and sent him off. She knew me better than to take the Frenchman’s view, but she was shocked at my carelessness. Rachel, however, took her own romantic, high-flown view. To her, I was “heartless” and “dishonourable.” I had failed to appreciate the sanctity of the artisan's labor, treating his life's work as a mere utilitarian transaction. She said some of the severest things to me which I had ever heard. The breach lasted for the whole of the next day, though I had eventually made my peace. 

Had Rachel reverted to this unlucky accident, at the critical moment when my place in her estimation was again assailed? 

Mr. Bruff answered in the affirmative. “It would have its effect on her mind. However, we have discovered that there *was* a predisposing influence against you. Our next step must be the step that takes us to Rachel.”

He rose, pacing thoughtfully. “The grand difficulty is how to make her show her whole mind in this matter, without reserve. Have you any suggestions?”

“I have made up my mind, Mr. Bruff, to speak to Rachel myself.”

“You!” He stopped, looking at me as if I had lost my senses, then considered it. “In cases of this extraordinary kind, the rash way is sometimes the best way. Nothing venture, nothing have. I don’t trust your discretion, but I do trust in Rachel’s still preserving a certain perverse weakness for *you*. Touch that—and trust to the consequences! The question is—how are you to see her?”

“She has been a guest of yours. May I venture to suggest that I might see her here?”

“Cool!” said Mr. Bruff. “In plain English, my house is to be turned into a trap to catch Rachel; with a bait to tempt her, in the shape of an invitation to share a meal with my wife and daughters. As things are, I firmly believe Rachel will live to thank me. Consider me your accomplice. Rachel shall be asked to spend the day here.”

“When? Tomorrow?”

“Say the day after. Stay at home all the morning and expect me to call on you.”

I thanked him and returned to my lodgings in London. Of the day that followed, I have only to say that it was the longest of my life. The relentless, mechanical roar of the city outside my window felt like a physical weight against my mind. I caused myself to be denied to every visitor, venturing out only under cover of night when the oppressive sonic environment finally quieted. 

The next morning, amidst the comforting aromas of steeped tea and warm toast at the breakfast-table, Mr. Bruff surprised me. He handed me a large key.

“She is coming today, to lunch and spend the afternoon with my wife and my girls.”

“Are Mrs. Bruff, and your daughters, in the secret?”

“Inevitably. The end being to bring you and Rachel together again, my wife and daughters pass over the means employed to gain it.”

“What is this key?”

“The key of the gate in my back-garden wall. Be there at three this afternoon. Let yourself into the garden, and make your way in by the conservatory door. Cross the small drawing-room, and open the door in front of you which leads into the music-room. There, you will find Rachel—alone.”

With those words, he left. To while away the weary hours, I looked at my letters. Among them was one from Betteredge. I opened it eagerly, tracing the old man's fading, native dialect in the ink. It began with an apology. In the next sentence, the everlasting Ezra Jennings appeared again! He had stopped Betteredge on the way out of the station. Mr. Candy, hearing of this, had himself driven over to Betteredge, wishing particularly to speak to me when I was next in the neighbourhood of Frizinghall. The warm-hearted, faithful old man acknowledged that he had written “mainly for the pleasure of writing to me.” I crumpled up the letter in my pocket, consumed by the interest of my coming interview.

As the clock of Hampstead church struck three—its resonant chime a pure, acoustic marker cutting through the distant industrial haze—I put Mr. Bruff’s key into the lock of the door in the wall. Stepping into the garden, I felt a certain guilty doubtfulness. I looked furtively on either side. Nothing appeared to justify my apprehensions. The walks were solitudes; the gentle hum of bees and the rustle of leaves were the only witnesses, weaving a serene natural soundscape that soothed my frayed nerves. 

I passed through the garden, entered the conservatory, inhaling the damp, rich scent of potting soil and blooming jasmine, and crossed the small drawing-room. As I laid my hand on the door opposite, I heard a few plaintive chords struck on the piano in the room within. The notes hung in the air, speaking an untranslatable language of melancholy and subjective longing. She had often idled over the instrument in this way, when I was staying at her mother’s house. I was obliged to wait a little, to steady myself. The interwoven timelines of past and present rose side by side at that supreme moment, and the contrast shook me. After the lapse of a minute, I roused my manhood, and opened the door.
