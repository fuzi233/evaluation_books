Few men could claim more sympathy than one who is brave, young, handsome, and unfortunate. Madam Esmond might have had twenty sons, yet she still found reason to admire her youngest—the soldier boy whose name was spoken with such pride in every corner of Castlewood. Mr. Washington’s room had never felt more like his own; it was as if he had taken root in the very heart of their home. She spoke of him everywhere, praising him before all guests, pointing out his virtues to her sons with a warmth that left no doubt of her regard. She contrasted his steadfast character with Harry’s restless love of pleasure—“the wild boy!”—and George’s quiet absorption in books, which she deemed too much of a retreat from duty.

But George did not share her admiration. His jealousy grew with each compliment, until it became a constant shadow over his thoughts. He turned bitter jokes into barbs so sharp they cut through the air, though his mother remained oblivious, confused by his sarcasm and baffled by his dark moods. To her, he was still the thoughtful son; to him, the world had grown colder, and he saw only the weight of expectation pressing down upon him.

Meanwhile, the wider world stirred with events that would soon reshape the lives of this modest family. The conflict between French and English settlers in North America had ceased to be merely local—it had become a war of nations. Reinforcements from France had arrived in Canada, and now English troops were expected in Virginia. It was a time of rising tension, when peace could no longer be taken for granted.

From Quebec came a letter addressed to George Warrington, written by Madame la Présidente de Mouchy, a lady of noble bearing and deep affection for the young Virginian. “Alas, my dear friend,” she wrote, “how cruelly fate has divided us! I see you leaving your beloved mother to rush into the arms of Bellona. I imagine you wounded after fierce battles. I hesitate even to wish victory to our lilies, knowing you stand beneath the banners of the Leopard. There are enmities the heart does not recognize—ours surely are at peace amidst the storm. All here send their love and greetings, especially Monsieur le Chasseur d’Ours, your brother (that cool Hippolyte, who preferred the hunt to the gentle talk of our ladies!). Your friend, your enemy, the Chevalier de la Jabotière, burns to meet you on the field of Mars. Last night, M. Du Quesne spoke of you at dinner. And I, your husband, send warm remembrances to your young friend, joined always with those of your sincere Présidente de Mouchy.”

The “banner of the Leopard” mentioned in her letter was indeed unfurled across the colonies. The king’s forces were being mustered, determined to reclaim the territories seized by the French. Two regiments were raised and funded by the Crown in America, while another fleet carried two additional regiments from England under a seasoned commander.

In February 1755, Commodore Keppel anchored his ship, the *Centurion*—once commanded by Anson on his famous circumnavigation—in Hampton Roads. With him came two warships, General Braddock, his staff, and part of the army. Braddock had been appointed by the Duke of Cumberland, a man known then as “The Duke par excellence.” Though not without controversy, he was a figure of great influence in England, and his presence signaled serious intent.

Soon after, a fleet of transports followed, bringing supplies, soldiers, and funds. Braddock landed his troops at Alexandria on the Potomac and journeyed to Annapolis, Maryland, where he summoned colonial governors to a council. He urged them to mobilize their provinces in support of the common cause, warning that the moment for action had come.

His arrival sent waves of excitement through the colonies, none greater than at Castlewood. Harry, ever eager for adventure, rode immediately to Alexandria to see the encamped soldiers. He marveled at their orderly lines, the crisp music of fifes and drums, and the disciplined bearing of the officers. He quickly made friends among them, longing to join their mission. They welcomed him at their mess, and he returned home full of stories and dreams.

Madam Esmond approved. She believed these English gentlemen offered her sons a chance to mingle with men of true breeding—unlike the horse-racing, cock-fighting squires of Virginia or the petty lawyers and flatterers at the lieutenant-governor’s table. She had an eye for sycophants and mocked the petty rivalries of Williamsburg’s courtiers. But while she praised the officers’ manners, neither Harry nor George fully shared her enthusiasm. If she had known the truth—heard their drunken songs, seen their disorder, witnessed how many of them staggered home after nights of revelry—she might have thought twice about recommending them to her sons.

Yet the soldiers’ behavior grew worse. They swaggered through the countryside, frightening farmers and villagers alike. Braddock raged against their lack of discipline and accused the colonists of cowardly reluctance to aid the Crown. The troops began to act like conquerors, mocking the locals and even insulting the wives of Indian allies who had joined the English ranks. In outrage, the chiefs withdrew their support—and within months, Braddock lost the vital help he would have needed most.

Some whispers reached Madam Esmond, but she dismissed them. Soldiers were soldiers, she said. Those who visited Castlewood were polite, well-mannered men, and so they were. She received them graciously, offering them the best hunting and hospitality the land could provide.

Then came word from General Braddock himself. He sent courteous messages to Castlewood, recalling that his father had once served alongside Colonel Esmond under the famed Marlborough. The name of Colonel Esmond was still respected in England, and Braddock wished to pay his respects to the daughter of such a distinguished officer. He asked permission to visit Castlewood.

Had she known why he sought her favor, perhaps Madam Esmond would not have been so touched. But she did not know. Instead, she accepted with dignity, writing back in elegant script, thanking him and asking when he might honor her home with his presence.

At Alexandria, Braddock held levees, and among those who paid homage were the twin sons of Castlewood. Dressed in their finest London suits, mounted on their best horses, and attended by their two servants in smart liveries, they rode in procession to meet the general.

Braddock was in a sour mood. He barely acknowledged the young men, asking only casually of his aide-de-camp, “Who are those young Squire Gawkeys in blue and gold and red waistcoats?”

Mr. Dinwiddie, the Lieutenant-Governor of Virginia, replied, “Those are the sons of the Princess Pocahontas.”

With a furious oath, Braddock demanded, “Who the deuce was she?”

Dinwiddie, who had little respect for Madam Esmond—having endured her sharp tongue and lofty airs—gave a mocking account of her, ridiculing her pretensions and telling tales that amused the general so thoroughly that he fell asleep.

When he awoke, Dinwiddie was gone, but a gentleman from Philadelphia remained, engaged in conversation with the officers. Braddock resumed the discussion, speaking harshly of Madam Esmond, calling her an old fool and expressing contempt for the colonial gentry.

But Benjamin Franklin of Philadelphia stepped in. He corrected the general’s impression, explaining that Madam Esmond owned vast lands, cattle, and stores—all of which could prove invaluable. He advised Braddock to treat her with care and respect.

Impressed by Franklin’s intelligence, Braddock ordered an aide to invite the two young Virginians to dinner the next day. When they arrived, he was far more cordial, and the officers treated them with warmth. They conducted themselves with grace and humility, returning home pleased with the experience—and their mother delighted by the general’s civility.

She replied to Braddock’s message with a letter of equal elegance, accepting his invitation and requesting a date for his visit.

In Castlewood, talk of war and the coming campaign filled every hour. For Harry, it was the dream of a lifetime. He lived for battle, spent his days with the officers in Williamsburg, polished every gun and sword in the house, and revived childhood games—ordering the negroes into mock formations. He was ready, eager, and burning to serve.

His mother, though proud, felt a deep sorrow. She knew one of her sons would have to go. She admired George, but loved Harry with all her heart. Yet Harry, though desperate to leave, dared not speak openly. When he brought up the subject with George, his elder brother’s face darkened.

Harry, loyal to the core, looked up to George as his natural leader. He feared what George might say—and dreaded what he might decide.

And indeed, George was not untouched by the call of duty. He studied the campaigns of Eugene and Marlborough, read his grandfather’s military texts, and re-read Plutarch’s biographies of the greatest warriors. He and Dempster, the old Scotchman, resumed fencing lessons—though Dempster never revealed where he’d learned his skill.

When Braddock’s offer came—would one of the young gentlemen care to join the campaign?—Harry’s eyes lit up. He cried out, “I would go with all my heart!”

But George looked at him steadily and said, “One of us will be proud to serve his Excellency. But the other must stay—to care for our mother.”

Harry remained silent. He trusted George’s judgment. Even in his deepest desire, he would not speak until George had decided.

They rode home in silence, each thinking the same thoughts, afraid to voice them. Only when they returned did they tell their mother.

“I knew it would come,” she said softly. “At such a time, our family must do its duty. Have you decided… which of you will leave me?”

“Of course I should go, Mother,” Harry blurted, turning crimson. “The youngest ought to go.”

Mrs. Mountain, present in the room, nodded firmly. “There! Mountain says so. I told you so!”

“But the head of the family ought to go,” George said quietly. “No—you’re ill. You haven’t recovered from your fever. Should he go, Mountain?”

“You would make the better soldier, I know it, dear Hal,” Mrs. Mountain replied. “You and Mr. Washington are great friends. You could travel together. And he doesn’t care for me—nor I for him, though he is admired. But it is the law of honour, my Harry.”

She turned to George with extraordinary tenderness. “The grief I feel is that I must refuse you. I must go. Had Fate given you just half an hour more life than I had, it would have been your turn—and you would have claimed your right to go first.”

“Yes, George,” Harry whispered. “I would.”

“You will stay,” George said, “and take care of Castlewood and our mother. If anything happens to me, you are here to fill my place. I would give way—I know you would lay down your life for me. But we must each do our duty. What would our grandfather say if he were here?”

Their mother looked at them proudly. “My papa would say his boys were gentlemen,” she murmured, then left the room, unable to hide her emotion.

Word spread quickly through the household. Dinah, George’s foster-mother, wept bitterly at losing him. Phillis, Harry’s nurse, grumbled that Master George was favored again. Sady, George’s servant, boasted of the feats he would perform. Gumbo, Harry’s boy, pretended to mourn—but at home, he was no warrior.

Yet none was angrier than Mrs. Mountain. She insisted George stay. “You don’t know what you’re doing!” she exclaimed. “You’ll bring nothing but trouble.”

George was surprised by her persistence. “I know Harry would make the better soldier,” he said. “But duty calls me.”

“To stay is your duty!” she shouted, stamping her foot.

“Why didn’t Mother say so earlier?” George asked.

“Your mother?” Mrs. Mountain laughed grimly. “Your mother, my poor child?”

“What do you mean?” George asked, blood rushing to his face.

“She wants you away, George,” she continued, wagging her head. “It may be that when you return, you’ll find a father-in-law waiting.”

George stared. “What in heaven do you mean?”

“I see everything,” she said. “Colonel Washington wants a rich wife. When you’re gone, he’ll ask your mother to marry him. And you’ll come back to find him master here.”

“But he’s going on the campaign too,” George protested.

“He’s going on the marrying campaign, child!” she insisted. “General Braddock told me he accepted the post of aide-de-camp.”

“An artifice!” she snapped. “A trick to blind you! He’ll be wounded and return—just wait and see. I have proof—look!”

She pulled from her pocket a sheet of paper in Washington’s handwriting.

“How did you get this?” George asked, pale.

“I found it in the Major’s chamber,” she admitted, embarrassed.

“You read private letters of a guest?” George shouted. “For shame! I won’t look at it!”

He threw it into the fire.

“I couldn’t help it,” she said. “By chance—pure chance. Governor Dinwiddie is taking the Major’s room, and I was moving things. This paper fell out. I just glanced—only to see what it was. Then I knew I had to read it.”

“Oh, you are a martyr to duty, Mountain,” George said coldly.

“Don’t mock me,” she pleaded. “I’ve watched you, nursed you, carried you when you were sick. I’m your mother in every way—your real mother, perhaps.”

“Stop!” George said sharply. “Don’t speak like that.”

But she did not stop. “You are ungrateful,” she said. “All of you. My Mr. Mountain was a wretch—and you are no better.”

The fire burned low. The paper lay in ashes, safe from harm—or so she thought.

George sat in silence, consumed by jealousy and dread. When his mother asked him at dinner why he looked so pale, he replied, “Do you suppose, madam, that leaving a mother like you does not cause me great pain?”

She could not understand his words, his strange laughter, his sudden bursts of anger. He drank heavily, joked wildly, called to the servants, laughed at them. Each time the door opened, he turned toward it—just as Mrs. Mountain did, fearing Mr. Washington would appear.
