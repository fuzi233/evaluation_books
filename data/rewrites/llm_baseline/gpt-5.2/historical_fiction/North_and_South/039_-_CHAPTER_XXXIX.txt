Margaret shut the door of her own room with a care that was almost tenderness. The house was slight-built; it had that thin, listening quality of many Milton dwellings, where the very boards seemed trained by habit to carry a footfall along the joists as a loom carries vibration along its frame. She began, in the first gust of hurt pride, to pace; then checked herself, remembering how her agitation would sound—step answering step—through plaster and lath, till it reached Mrs. Thornton’s chamber like an accusation. She forced herself down into the chair by the window and waited, motionless, until she heard the outer door and the faint jar of departure; only then did she draw breath, as if the air in the room had been kept back with her.

When she was alone with silence, she made herself go over every word that had passed, speech by speech, as one might turn over slices of bread to see where the heat had scorched them. At the end she rose and spoke aloud, her voice low and bitterly steady.

“Her words do not touch me. They fall off from me—because I am innocent of what she attributes to me. And yet it is hard—hard—that any one, any woman, should believe such things of another so quickly.” She pressed her fingers hard together. “Where I have done wrong, she does not accuse me—she does not know. He never told her. I might have known he would not.”

A strange, involuntary pride lifted her head for an instant—pride in his forbearance, in that reserve which kept her falsehood locked within himself. And then, like a knife turning in a wound, a new thought entered; she clasped her hands tighter.

“He too must take poor Frederick for some lover.” The word brought a hot colour up into her face, and she hated herself for it. “I see it now. It is not only that he knows of my untruth—he believes that some one else cares for me; and that I—Oh, what shall I do? what do I mean? Why do I care what he thinks, beyond the mere loss of his good opinion as regards my telling the truth?”

The room seemed close; she went to the glass, not to look at herself, but because the movement saved her from sobbing. “I cannot tell,” she whispered, and the confession sounded like a foreign phrase in her own mouth—untranslatable into the steady, dutiful language she had always used for her life. “But I am very miserable. Oh, how unhappy this last year has been! I have passed out of childhood into old age. I have had no youth—no womanhood. The hopes of womanhood have closed for me—for I shall never marry; and I anticipate cares and sorrows as if I were an old woman, with the same tearful spirit.”

Outside, some distant machinery gave a dull, intermittent beat, like a giant’s pulse; and nearer, a hawker’s cry rose and fell in the street—sound after sound that belonged to Milton, that harshly made itself master of the air. It seemed to her that even noise had power here, and that her own heart must beat to the town’s rough measure whether it would or no. She was weary of being braced and tightened like a string.

“I am weary of this continual call upon me for strength,” she said. “I could bear up for papa—that is a natural duty. I could bear up against—yes, I could resent—Mrs. Thornton’s unjust suspicions. But it is hard to feel how completely he must misunderstand me.” She stopped, bewildered by the intensity of her own pain. “What has happened to make me so morbid to-day? I do not know. I only know I cannot help it. I must give way sometimes.”

Then, with a sudden impatience at her own softness, she sprang up. “No, I will not.” She spoke as if rebuking a child. “I will not—I will not think of myself and my position. I won’t examine my feelings. It would be no use now. Some time—if I live to be an old woman—I may sit over the fire, and looking into the embers, see the life that might have been.”

All this time she had been putting on her things with hurried fingers, as if dressing herself could dress her mind into composure. Twice she stopped to wipe her eyes, angry at the tears that came back with the same persistence as the town’s unceasing clang.

“I dare say there are many women who make as sad a mistake as I have done, and only find it out too late,” she went on, as she fastened her bonnet-strings too tightly. “And how proudly I spoke to him that day! I did not know then. It has come upon me little by little, and I don’t know where it began. Now I won’t give way. I shall find it difficult to behave in the same way to him, with this miserable consciousness upon me; but I will be calm and quiet, and say very little. But perhaps I may not see him at all—he keeps out of our way. That would be worse than all. And yet no wonder he avoids me, believing what he must about me.”

She went out at once, turning her face towards the fields beyond the smoke, and walked rapidly, as if swiftness could outstrip thought. The country air met her with a colder, purer smell—damp earth, raw hedgerow, and the faint sweetness of something green bruised underfoot. It was not the rich, domestic fragrance of bread in an oven or herbs crushed on a board; it was the austerity of winter soil. Even so, it soothed her, because it was a different world from heated rooms and sharp words.

When she returned and stood on the doorstep, her father came up the path with that gentle, trusting look which always pierced her most.

“Good girl!” said he. “You’ve been to Mrs. Boucher’s. I was just meaning to go there, if I had time, before dinner.”

“No, papa; I have not,” she answered, and felt the red shame rise at once—shame not for having neglected the poor, but for the strange fact that she had not even remembered them in her first rush of misery. “I never thought about her. But I will go directly after dinner; I will go while you are taking your nap.”

Accordingly she went.

Mrs. Boucher was very ill—really ill, not merely complaining. The close room smelt of stale fire, of damp clothes hung too near the hearth, and of that sickly sweetness that comes when breath is laboured and the body is failing. A kind, sensible neighbour—the same woman Margaret had noticed the other day—seemed to have taken charge with the quiet authority of one who knows how to measure out spoonfuls and make order in disorder. Some of the children had been sent away to neighbours. Mary Higgins had come for the three youngest at dinner-time; and since then Nicholas had gone for the doctor. He had not returned. The neighbour spoke in a low voice and with a certain plain reverence, as if death were already in the room and must not be startled by loudness. There was nothing to do but wait.

Margaret thought she should like to hear the doctor’s opinion, and that she could not do better than go to the Higginses in the meantime. Perhaps she might learn whether Nicholas had been able to make his application to Mr. Thornton.

At the Higginses’ she found, to her surprise, a domestic scene almost cheerful. Nicholas stood by the dresser, busily engaged in making a penny spin, setting it off with a practised finger so that it whirled with a humming, silver sound upon the worn wood. Three little children clung to him without fear, their eyes fixed on the trembling circle of coin as if it were a conjurer’s trick. The penny’s small, bright music—its thin, eager ring against the dresser—was oddly pleasant after the heavy noises of Milton; it was a sound that belonged to home, not to the mill.

Nicholas smiled, and the children smiled with him, and Margaret—watching the absorption in his face—thought the look of interest was a good sign, as if his own heart had not grown altogether hard.

When the penny’s spin slackened and fell into stillness, “lile Johnnie” began to cry, wailing as if the world had ended with the motion.

“Come to me,” said Margaret, and lifted him down. She held him close and, to divert him, put her watch to his ear, letting him hear the small, steadfast tick that spoke a quieter language than either crying or scolding. The child hushed a little, listening, his moist mouth open with wonder at the imprisoned sound.

In that softened moment she asked, “Have you seen Mr. Thornton?”

The change on Nicholas’s face was instant; the smile went as if a door had shut.

“Ay,” said he. “I’ve seen and heerd too much on him.”

“He refused you, then?” Margaret’s voice fell.

“To be sure. I knew he’d do it all along. It’s no good expectin’ marcy at th’ hands o’ them measters. Yo’r a stranger and a foreigner, and aren’t likely to know their ways; but I knowed it.”

“I am sorry I asked you. Was he angry? He did not speak to you as Hamper did, did he?”

“He weren’t o’er-civil!” said Nicholas, and he spun the penny again—half to occupy his hands, half for the children, though his eyes now looked past them. “Never yo’ fret. I’m only where I was. I’ll go on tramp to-morrow. I gave him as good as I got. I telled him I’d not that good opinion on him that I’d ha’ come a second time o’ mysel’; but yo’d advised me for to come, and I were beholden to yo’.”

“You told him I sent you?” Margaret asked quickly, alarmed.

“I dunno as I ca’d yo’ by your name. I dunnot think I did. I said a woman as knew no better had advised me for to come and see if there was a soft place in his heart.”

“And he—?” Margaret could hardly force out the question.

“Said I were to tell yo’ to mind yo’r own business.—That’s th’ longest spin yet, my lads.—And them’s civil words to what he used to me. But ne’er mind. We’re but where we was; and I’ll break stones on th’ road afore I let these little uns clem.”

Margaret set the struggling Johnnie back on the dresser. The child’s sobbing had turned to a hiccuping watchfulness, his eyes following the penny as if it might give him comfort.

“I am sorry I asked you to go to Mr. Thornton’s,” she said, quietly. “I am disappointed in him.”

There was a slight noise behind her—no more than a shift of weight, a brush of cloth against the doorpost—yet it struck both her and Nicholas into stillness. They turned at the same instant.

Mr. Thornton stood in the doorway.

His face had that look of displeased surprise which seemed to harden every line. For one second Margaret felt as if she had been caught doing something petty and malicious, when she had only spoken out of hurt. Her impulse was swift as pain. Without a word, she passed out before him, bowing low—not in courtesy, but to hide the sudden paleness she felt must have rushed over her face.

He bent equally low in return, and then shut the door after her.

As she hurried away towards Mrs. Boucher’s, she heard the door-clang behind her, the sharp iron sound of it striking through the air; it seemed to fill up the measure of her mortification. He too was annoyed to find her there.

And indeed he was. He had tenderness in him—a “soft place,” as Nicholas had called it—but he had the pride of keeping it hidden, as if pity were a treasure to be locked away from common handling. He was jealous of every circumstance that tried to enter and name his kindness for him. Yet if he dreaded the exposure of his feeling, he was equally desirous that all men should recognise his justice; and all day the thought of Higgins’s five hours’ waiting had lain on him like a weight. He had not five hours to spare himself; but he spent an hour—two hours—of his hard, penetrating labour, not at the mill, but in going about Milton, collecting evidence as to the truth of Higgins’s story, the nature of his character, the tenor of his life.

He tried not to be convinced; and then he was convinced. And when conviction came, it went in like a charm and touched what was warmest under the sternness. Higgins’s patience, the simple generosity of his motive—made plainer by what Thornton learnt of the quarrel between Boucher and Higgins—broke down, for a moment, the mere reasoning of justice. Thornton’s mind overleaped it by an instinct that felt almost like grace. He came to tell Higgins he would give him work.

That was why Margaret’s presence nettled him more than her last sentence; for now he understood that she was the “woman” who had urged Higgins to come, and he hated—yes, hated—the idea that any thought of her might be supposed to have moved him, when he wished to do this solely because it was right.

“So that was the lady you spoke of as a woman?” he said sharply to Higgins. “You might have told me who she was.”

“And then, maybe, yo’d ha’ spoken of her more civil than yo’ did,” returned Higgins, with a grim satisfaction. “Yo’d getten a mother as might ha’ kept yo’r tongue in check when yo’ were talkin’ o’ women bein’ at th’ root o’ all th’ plagues.”

“Of course you told that to Miss Hale?”

“In coorse I did. Leastways, I reckon I did. I telled her she weren’t to meddle again in aught as concerned yo’.”

“Whose children are those—yours?” Thornton asked, though he had a pretty good notion from what he had heard. He felt awkward, and shifted ground.

“They’re not mine, and they are mine.”

“They are the children you spoke of to me this morning?”

“When yo’ said,” Higgins answered, turning with ill-smothered fierceness, “that my story might be true or might not, but it were a very unlikely one. Measter, I’ve not forgotten.”

Thornton was silent for a moment; then he spoke with effort, in a voice that had been made blunt by long habit of command.

“No more have I. I remember what I said. I spoke to you about those children in a way I had no business to. I did not believe you. I could not have taken care of another man’s children myself, if he had acted towards me as I hear Boucher did towards you. But I know now you spoke truth. I beg your pardon.”

Higgins did not turn round at once. The penny spun on, making its thin ring and whirr; the children watched it, ignorant of the meanings being exchanged above their heads. At last Higgins spoke, his tone softened though the words remained gruff.

“Yo’ve no business to go pryin’ into what happened between Boucher and me. He’s dead, and I’m sorry. That’s enough.”

“So it is,” said Thornton. “Will you take work with me? That’s what I came to ask.”

Higgins’s obstinacy wavered, gathered itself, and stood firm. He would not speak. Thornton would not ask again. There was a pause; the penny fell; one child breathed out a disappointed “Oh!” as if it mattered more than any quarrel.

Higgins’s eye dropped to the children. “Yo’ve called me impudent, an’ a liar, an’ a mischief-maker, and yo’ might ha’ said wi’ some truth as I were now and then given to drink. An’ I ha’ called you a tyrant, an’ an oud bull-dog, an’ a hard, cruel master. That’s where it stands. But for th’ childer, Measter—do yo’ think we can e’er get on together?”

“Well!” Thornton said, half laughing in spite of himself, “it was not my proposal that we should go together. But there’s one comfort on your own showing. We neither of us can think much worse of the other than we do now.”

“That’s true,” Higgins said reflectively. “I’ve been thinkin’ ever sin’ I saw you, what a marcy it were yo’ did na take me on, for that I ne’er saw a man whom I could less abide. But that’s maybe been a hasty judgment; an’ work’s work to such as me. So, Measter, I’ll come; an’ what’s more, I thank yo’; and that’s a deal fro’ me.”

As he spoke he turned fully round and faced Thornton for the first time. Thornton met him, and gave his hand a strong, honest grip.

“And this is a deal from me,” Thornton answered.

Then, as if ashamed of softness, he resumed the master.

“Now mind you come sharp to your time. I’ll have no laggards at my mill. What fines we have, we keep pretty sharply. And the first time I catch you making mischief, off you go. So now you know where you are.”

“Yo’ spoke o’ my wisdom this morning. I reckon I may bring it wi’ me; or would yo’ rayther have me ’bout my brains?”

“’Bout your brains if you use them for meddling with my business; with your brains if you can keep to your own.”

“I shall need a deal o’ brains to settle where my business ends and yo’rs begins.”

“Your business has not begun yet, and mine stands still for me. So—good afternoon.”

Not long after, as Thornton approached Mrs. Boucher’s door, Margaret came out. She did not see him. He followed her for several yards, watching—against his will—the light, easy manner of her walk, the tall grace of her figure moving through the mean street like something finer than the place. For a moment the pleasure in looking at her was simple, and it softened him; then, suddenly, jealousy—poisonous, unreasonable—tainted it. He wished to overtake her and speak, to see how she would receive him now that he believed her attached elsewhere. He wished, too—though he was ashamed of the wish—that she should know he had justified her advice, had taken Higgins on, had repented of the morning.

He came up to her. She started.

“Allow me to say, Miss Hale, that you were rather premature in expressing your disappointment. I have taken Higgins on.”

“I am glad of it,” she replied coldly.

“He tells me he repeated to you what I said this morning about—” Thornton hesitated.

“About women not meddling,” Margaret took it up, the words brisker than her heart. “You had a perfect right to express your opinion, which was a very correct one, I have no doubt. But”—and here she spoke more eagerly, as if snatching at a chance to set one thing straight—“Higgins did not quite tell you the exact truth.”

The word truth struck her like a blow. It carried her back to that other truth she could not speak, and to the untruth she had uttered to shelter Frederick. She stopped short, feeling the discomfort spread, as if the very air had become heavy.

Thornton at first looked puzzled at her sudden silence; then he remembered—remembered the lie, remembered what it meant, and remembered what it made him believe.

“The exact truth!” he said. “Very few people do speak the exact truth. I have given up hoping for it. Miss Hale—have you no explanation to give me? You must perceive what I cannot but think.”

Margaret said nothing. In her mind loyalty to Frederick rose like an old, stern law; she could not break it without doing him harm.

“Nay,” Thornton went on, his voice changing, “I will ask no farther. I may be putting temptation in your way. At present—believe me—your secret is safe with me. But you run great risks—allow me to say—in being so indiscreet. I am only speaking as a friend of your father’s. If I had any other thought or hope, of course that is at an end. I am quite disinterested.”

“I am aware of that,” Margaret answered, forcing herself into an indifferent, careless tone that tasted like ash. “I am aware of what I must appear to you. But the secret is another person’s, and I cannot explain it without doing him harm.”

“I have not the slightest wish to pry into the gentleman’s secrets,” he said, and anger rose as if it were a defence against pain. “My own interest in you is—simply that of a friend. You may not believe me, Miss Hale, but it is—in spite of the persecution I’m afraid I threatened you with at one time—but that is all given up; all passed away. You believe me, Miss Hale?”

“Yes,” said Margaret quietly, sadly.

“Then, really, I don’t see any reason for us to go on walking together,” he replied. “I thought perhaps you might have had something to say, but I see we are nothing to each other. If you’re quite convinced that any foolish passion on my part is entirely over, I wish you good afternoon.”

He walked off very hastily, his step striking the pavement with a sharp decisiveness that seemed to cut the air behind him.

“What can he mean?” Margaret thought, standing still for an instant, as if her mind had to catch up with her body. “What could he mean by speaking so, as if I were always thinking he cared for me, when I know he does not; he cannot. His mother will have said all those cruel things about me to him.” A fierce effort rose in her. “But I won’t care for him. Surely I am mistress enough of myself to control this wild, strange, miserable feeling, which tempted me even to betray my own dear Frederick, so that I might but regain his good opinion—the good opinion of a man who takes such pains to tell me that I am nothing to him.”

She set her lips into a sort of trembling smile, as if coaxing her own heart. “Come, poor little heart! be cheery and brave. We’ll be a great deal to one another, if we are thrown off and left desolate.”

That morning her father was almost startled by her merriment. She talked incessantly, forcing her natural humour up into an unusual pitch; and if there was a tinge of bitterness in much she said—if her accounts of the old Harley Street set came out a little too pointed, a little too sharp—Mr. Hale could not bear to check her, as he might have done at another time. He was only glad to see her shake off care, and her voice, even when it laughed, was a sound of life in the house.

In the middle of the evening she was called down to speak to Mary Higgins; and when she came back, her father fancied he saw traces of tears upon her cheeks. But that could not be, he thought; for she brought good news—Higgins had got work at Mr. Thornton’s mill.

Yet her spirits were damped notwithstanding, and she found it difficult to go on talking at all, much more in that wild, forced way. For some days her mood varied strangely; and her father began to feel anxious, when news arrived from one or two quarters that promised change and variety.

Mr. Hale received a letter from Mr. Bell, in which that gentleman volunteered a visit. Mr. Hale imagined the promised society of his old Oxford friend would give an agreeable turn to Margaret’s ideas as it did to his own. Margaret tried to take interest in what pleased her father; but she was too languid to care about any Mr. Bell, even though he were twenty times her godfather.

She was more roused by a letter from Edith—full of sympathy about their aunt’s death; full of details of herself, her husband, and the baby; and at the end saying that the climate did not suit the child, and that as Mrs. Shaw talked of returning to England, she thought it probable Captain Lennox might sell out, and they might all go and live again in the old Harley Street house—which, however, would seem very incomplete without Margaret.

At that, Margaret’s heart yearned after the old house and the placid tranquillity of that well-ordered, monotonous life. She had found it occasionally tiresome while it lasted; but since then she had been buffeted about, and now felt so exhausted—exhausted by this recent struggle with herself—that she thought even stagnation would be rest and refreshment. She began to look forward to a long visit to the Lennoxes on their return as to a point—not of hope, she told herself, but of leisure, where she might regain power and command.

For at present it seemed as if all subjects tended towards Mr. Thornton, and that she could not forget him with all her endeavours. If she went to see the Higginses, she heard of him there. Her father had resumed their readings together, and quoted Thornton’s opinions perpetually. Even Mr. Bell’s proposed visit brought the tenant’s name upon the tapis; for he wrote word that he believed he must be occupied for some part of his time with Mr. Thornton, as a new lease was in preparation, and the terms must be agreed upon. So his name kept sounding through her days, now harsh as the mill’s beat, now soft as a watch ticking in a child’s ear; and in neither language could she find a word that exactly told what she felt.
