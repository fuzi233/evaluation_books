Mr. Harry Warrington, the so-called Fortunate Youth of Virginia, now resided in splendid lodgings on Bond Street, living lavishly upon the finest fare London could offer, his table never lacking the best wines, his company ever filled with the loftiest names of the town. Youth, wealth, good looks, and luck—these were his companions, and society welcomed him with open arms.

Let us not too harshly condemn the world for favoring such a one while turning a cold shoulder to those less blessed. If a man may choose his friends, why should society—being but the sum of all men—not do the same? Harry was liked because he was likable: rich, handsome, well-born, and well-mannered. He drank jovially with those who loved a song and a bottle; he rode boldly with gentlemen of the field; he blushed becomingly in ladies’ company; and to those beneath him in station, he was ever generous, sparing them any humiliation his rank might permit.

True, our young Virginian carried himself with lofty pride, but in those days, when distinctions of birth were sharply observed, such hauteur was expected, not resented. A Secretary of State knelt before his King; an Under-Secretary stood in his superior’s presence. There were lines then drawn which few dared cross—lines now blurred, to the dismay of many a gentleman who finds himself clapped on the back by men who ought to know their place.

For Harry, London in the winter of 1756-57 offered every diversion. There were operas, though he cared little for them (despite the outcry against their foreign frivolity, the houses remained full). There were plays, with Garrick and Pritchard at one theater, the divine Clive at another. There were masquerades, ridottos, assemblies where cards ruled the evening—though Harry preferred White’s, where the play ran higher and fairer.

One day, his cousin Lord Castlewood presented him at Court. The King, newly arrived from Kensington, received him coldly.

"Heard of you from Lady Yarmouth," muttered His Majesty in German to Castlewood. "Tell him he plays too deep."

With that, the royal back turned.

"What did he say?" Harry asked.

"His Majesty disapproves of the stakes at White’s," whispered Castlewood, pale.

"Then we shan’t trouble him again," said Harry, shrugging. "I never thought much of German kings for English thrones."

"For God’s sake, hold your tongue!" hissed Castlewood. "These walls have ears!"

"And what of it?"

"Look around you! An hour ago, these men bowed and smiled. Now they avoid me like the plague!"

A broad-shouldered gentleman in a scarlet-laced waistcoat thrust out a hand. "Shake, nephew. I heard you. My name’s Sir Miles Warrington. Your father’s brother."

Castlewood, paling further, confirmed it.

"Might’ve visited us in Norfolk instead of dawdling at Tunbridge Wells," growled Sir Miles. "Or do you call yourself Esmond now?"

"My mother is proud of her father’s name, and so am I," Harry retorted.

"Well said! Come dine with us in Hill Street—roast mutton, none of your White’s kickshaws. And don’t fret, my lord," he added, seeing Castlewood’s unease. "I’ll keep your secrets."

At dinner, Lady Warrington—stately at first—soon confessed she had expected a far worse rogue, given his reputation. The young ladies offered polite curtsies, while the heir, young Master Miles, blurted:

"Cousin Harry, are you truly wicked? Papa says you drink and game and keep bad company!"

"A little pitcher with big ears," laughed Sir Miles, dispatching the boy.

Over port, the Baronet grew frank. "My wife’s taken to Methodists of late. One Ward, a preacher, filled her head with tales of you and your brother."

"Speak of me as you please," Harry flared, "but if that fellow slanders my brother George again, I’ll thrash him!"

"Loyalty becomes you," said Sir Miles, refilling his glass. "To your mother’s health!"

Harry drank, though the toast pricked his conscience. Later, he sent trinkets to the girls "from their aunt in Virginia," a kindness that warmed him—until Colonel Lambert arrived one afternoon, grim-faced.

"Your gifts are too costly for a man of modest means," said the Colonel, laying them aside. "My daughters cannot accept what was won at cards."

Harry stiffened. "You question my honor?"

"I question nothing but the wisdom of gaming. At Court today, they spoke of your winnings—thousands here, thousands there. Such talk follows you."

"Must I account for every shilling?" Harry’s voice turned icy.

"Only to yourself. But Theo and Hetty cannot wear trinkets bought with dice."

The words struck like a blow. "You return my kindness with insult?"

"With truth. Keep your gifts for those who do not love you."

"Then take your leave, sir!" Harry’s hand trembled as he pointed to the door. "I’ll not endure such reproach from any man!"

The Colonel’s face fell, but he turned without another word.

Alone, Harry stared into the fire, the taste of triumph ashen in his mouth.
