For months, our existence at the fort was a weary cycle of conflict and reconciliation between the commandant and myself. Our days were filled with greasy card games, dismal duets featuring his wheezing flute and my cracked guitar, and the grim reality of survival. Poor Fawn endured the blows and bottles of liquor dispensed at her "lord and master"'s whim, yet she tirelessly cared for her papoose, her master suffering from gout, and our prisoner battling fever. This routine persisted until early autumn, when a hunter arrived bearing news that electrified our tiny garrison.

The Marquis de Montcalm had dispatched a substantial force to reinforce French-held forts and expand their territory into British lands. Troops had departed Quebec and Montreal, traveling up the St. Lawrence and across the lakes in bateaux, laden with artillery and vast supplies. Rumors swirled that Commandant Museau would soon be replaced by a higher-ranking officer—one who might exchange me for a prisoner or surrender me to the Indians as retaliation for atrocities committed by our forces against enemy soldiers. The men of the fort grew restless with anticipation; they dreamed of advancing into Pennsylvania and New York, seizing Albany and Philadelphia, driving the "Rosbifs" into the sea, and claiming all America from the Mississippi to Newfoundland.

Yet, despite this triumphant outlook, the prospect of French victory offered no solace to Mr. Museau.

"Eh, commandant!" I remarked. "'Tis *fort bien*, but where, meanwhile, are your farm in Normandy, your pot of cider, and your *trippes à la mode de Caen*?"

"Yes," he replied, "all very well, my boy. But consider: when poor old Museau is superseded, where will you be? Other officers lack the humanity I possess. Few men in the world can match my kindness. If a large garrison arrives, will my successors grant you the indulgences honest Museau provides? You will be kept in a sty like a pig awaiting slaughter. Just as surely as one of our officers falls into the hands of your frontier brigands and suffers, so too must you pay with your skin for his misfortune. You will be handed over to our red allies—to the brothers of La Biche yonder. Do you recall last year? What did they do to your countrymen captured at Braddock's defeat? Roasting was merely the least of their punishments, *ma foi*. Was it not, La Biche?"

Museau launched into a graphic description of tortures—eyes burned out, teeth and nails wrenching, limbs and bodies slashed. You turn pale, dear Miss Theo! Well, I shall exercise mercy and spare you the horrors Museau recounted so cheerfully as potential fates.

La Biche, however, was hardly shaken by this tale. Ladies, she had witnessed such brutality firsthand. Hailing from the Senecas, whose villages lay near the great cataract between Lake Ontario and Erie, her people fought both for and against the English. In the endless wars between our factions, it is difficult to declare whether white or red skin proves the more savage.

"They may chop me into cutlets and broil me," I said calmly. "But again, you will never have your farm in Normandy."

"Fetch the whisky bottle, La Biche," Museau commanded.

"And it is not too late, even now," I insisted. "I will reward the guide who brings me home generously. Furthermore, as a man of honor, I promise ten thousand livres to whoever brings me a token—say, my watch and seal engraved with my grandfather's arms, which I believe lies in a chest somewhere in this fort."

"Aha, scélérat!" roared the commandant, laughing hoarsely. "You have eyes, you do! Everything is fair prize in war."

"Think of a house in your village, a fine field nearby with six cows, a lush orchard heavy with fruit..."

"And Javotte at the door spinning, with a couple of rascally children, their cheeks as red as the apples! O my country! O my mother!" Museau whimpered. "Quick, La Biche, the whisky!"

That night, the commandant pondered deeply, while La Biche sat apart, silent and melancholy, nursing her child. Whenever my gaze met hers, I found her eyes fixed upon me. The infant cried, and Museau, using his customary foul language, ordered the child away to the hut where the unfortunate La Biche resided. Once she was gone, we spoke freely. I presented arguments to Monsieur that his greed could not ignore.

"How do you know," he asked, "that this hunter will serve you?"

"That is my secret," I replied. Yet, since we are not bound by honor tonight, let me explain. When hunters enter the settlements for trade, they often linger for days, seeking rest, drink, and camaraderie. Our new friend loved these things. He gambled with the men, traded furs for liquor, and reveled in the fort, singing and dancing. I suspect they enjoyed listening to my songs; lacking better entertainment, I frequently sang and scraped my guitar. We held many concerts, the men joining in chorus or dancing to my rustic tunes until interrupted by the drums and the retreat call.

Our guest, the hunter, attended one or two of these gatherings. I decided to test if he understood English. After exhausting our supply of French songs, I declared, "My lads, I shall sing an English song." To the melody of "Over the hills and far away"—a favorite air my grandfather once hummed in Marlborough's camp—I improvised some doggerel verses: *"This long, long year, a prisoner drear / Ah, me! I'm tired of lingering here: / I'll give a hundred guineas gay / To be over the hills and far away."*

"What is it?" the hunter asked. "I don't understand."

"'Tis a girl to her lover," I answered. But I noticed a twinkle in his eye; he understood.

The following day, when no men were within earshot, the trapper confirmed my suspicion. As he passed me, he hummed softly, yet clearly, in perfect English, the refrain of my previous evening's verse.

"If you are ready," he said, "I am ready. I know your people and the path to them. Speak to the Fawn; she will tell you what to do. What! You will not play with me?" He produced some cards just as two soldiers approached. "Milor est trop grand seigneur? Bonjour, my lord!"

The man bowed mockingly and sauntered off, shrugging his shoulders, offering to play and drink elsewhere.

I realized then that La Biche was to be the agent of this affair, and my offer to Museau had been accepted. The poor Fawn executed her role with remarkable faithfulness and skill. I required no further words with Museau; the understanding was complete. For a long time, she had been permitted free communication with me. She had tended to me during my wounds and illnesses, assisted with the chores of my small chamber, and cooked for me. She was free to leave the fort, venturing to the river and fields where the garrison's corn and garden produce were gathered.

Having squandered most of the money earned from selling his pelts, the trapper assembled his store of flints, powder, and blankets, then departed. Three days later, Fawn signaled that the time for my attempt at freedom had arrived.

When first wounded, I had been taken by my kind Florac and placed on his bed in the officers' room. When the fort was stripped of all officers save the old lieutenant left in command, I remained in my quarters, sometimes left relatively free, sometimes locked up and fed prisoners' rations, and occasionally invited to share the gaoler's mess while drunk.

This officers' house, or room, was constructed of logs, similar to the half-dozen others within the fort, mounting only four small-caliber guns. One cannon stood on the bastion directly behind my cabin. Looking westward over this gun, one could see a small island at the confluence of the Ohio and Monongahela rivers, where Fort Duquesne stood. On the shore opposite this island, there were some trees.

"You see those trees?" my poor La Biche asked me the day prior, speaking in her French jargon. "He waits for you behind those trees."

During the day, the door to my quarters remained open, and La Biche moved freely. On the eve of my escape, she returned from the fields carrying a pickaxe and a basket of vegetables and herbs for soup. Sitting on a bench at my door, the pick leaning against it and the basket beside her, I stood talking to her for a while. I suppose I was so foolish that I never would have considered using the pick if she had actually thrust it into my open doorway, causing it to fall inside.

"Hide it," she instructed. "You will need it soon." That afternoon, she indicated the trees to me.

The next day, she appeared feigning anger, shouting, "My lord! My lord! Why do you not come to the commandant's dinner? He is very ill! *Entendez-vous*?" Peering into the room as she spoke, she tossed a coil of rope to me.
