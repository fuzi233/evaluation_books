The evening air inside the Archer household was thin, punctuated only by the clink of silver against china. Janey Archer announced the news with a crispness that betrayed none of the tremor in her voice. "Your cousin the Countess called on mother while you were away."

Newland Archer looked up from his solitary meal. His mother sat opposite, her posture rigid, her gaze lowered to the porcelain rim of her plate as if reading a menu written in invisible ink. She did not hide her displeasure; she merely refused to acknowledge the world outside her immediate circle. Newland understood the calculation instantly: she was annoyed that he found the intrusion surprising, that he treated the arrival of Madame Olenska as a breach of protocol rather than a social inevitability.

"She wore a black velvet polonaise," Janey continued, her description precise, almost forensic. "Jet buttons, and a tiny green monkey muff. I never saw her so styled." She paused, letting the image settle. "She came alone, early on Sunday afternoon. Fortunately, the fire was already lit in the drawing-room. She possessed one of those new card-cases. She stated clearly that she wished to know us because you had been so good to her."

Newland allowed a brief, dry laugh to escape him. "Madame Olenska always adopts that tone regarding her friends. She finds comfort in being among her own people again."

"Yes, she told us that," Mrs. Archer confirmed, her lips tightening into a line of disapproval. "One must say she makes a distinct effort to please, even when visiting an elderly woman."

"Mother does not consider her simple," Janey interjected, her eyes narrowing as she scrutinized her brother's face.

"It is merely my old-fashioned sensibility," Mrs. Archer replied, dismissing the notion with a wave of her hand. "May represents my ideal."

"Ah," Newland said, the word hanging in the silence between them. "They are not alike."

Later, Archer descended upon Old Mrs. Mingott at St. Augustine. The old lady received him with a warmth that bordered on effusiveness, grateful for his role in dissuading the Countess from pursuing a divorce. When Archer confessed that he had abandoned his office without permission, rushing south solely to see May, Mrs. Mingott emitted a soft, adipose chuckle. She patted his knee with a hand that felt like a puff-ball.

"So you kicked over the traces, did you?" she cooed. "And I imagine Augusta and Welland pulled long faces, behaving as though the apocalypse had arrived? But little May... she knew better, surely?"

"I hoped she did," Archer admitted. "Yet she refused to grant what I came to request."

"And what was that?"

"I sought a promise that we marry in April. What utility is there in wasting another year?"

Mrs. Manson Mingott screwed up her mouth into a grimace of mock prudery, her eyes twinkling with malicious intelligence. "'Ask Mamma,' I suppose—the familiar narrative. Ah, these Mingotts—all identical! Born into a rut, and impossible to extract. When I constructed this house, you would have believed I was migrating to California! No one ever built above Forty-second Street—or even the Battery—before Christopher Columbus discovered America. No, no; none of them desire difference; they are terrified of it, more so than smallpox. Ah, my dear Mr. Archer, I thank my stars I am nothing but a vulgar Spicer. Yet not one of my own children resembles me except my little Ellen." She broke off, still flashing those knowing glances, and asked with the casual irrelevance of advanced age: "Now, pray, why in the world did you not marry my little Ellen?"

Archer laughed. "For one thing, she was absent."

"No—to be certain; more's the pity. And now it is too late; her life is concluded." She spoke with the cold-blooded tranquility of the aged burying youthful aspirations. A chill settled in Archer's chest, and he hurriedly added, "Cannot I persuade you to exert your influence with the Wellands, Mrs. Mingott? I was not fashioned for protracted engagements."

Old Catherine beamed upon him with approval. "No; I perceive that. You possess a keen eye. When you were a small boy, I am certain you preferred assistance." She threw back her head, her laughter causing her chins to ripple like gentle waves. "Ah, here is my Ellen now!" she exclaimed as the portières parted.

Madame Olenska emerged, her face vivid and bright. She extended her hand gaily to Archer while bowing to receive her grandmother's kiss.

"I was just telling him, my dear: 'Now, why did you not marry my little Ellen?'"

Olenska looked at Archer, her smile unwavering. "And what was his response?"

"Oh, my darling, I leave you to discover that! He has traveled to Florida to visit his sweetheart."

"Yes, I know." Her gaze remained fixed on him. "I visited your mother to inquire where you had gone. I sent a note that you never acknowledged, and I feared illness."

He mumbled something about departing abruptly, in a great hurry, intending to write from St. Augustine.

"And naturally, once you arrived, you ceased thinking of me!" She continued to beam at him with a gaiety that suggested a carefully cultivated indifference.

"If she requires me further, she is determined not to reveal it," he thought, stinging beneath her manner. He desired to thank her for visiting his mother, yet under the ancestral scrutiny, he found himself tongue-tied and constrained.

"Observe him—in such frantic haste to wed that he took French leave and rushed below to beg the silly girl on his knees! That approximates a lover—that is how handsome Bob Spicer abducted my poor mother; then grew weary of her before I was weaned—though they required only eight months to produce me! But there—you are not a Spicer, young man; fortunately for you and for May. It is only my poor Ellen who retains any trace of their wicked blood; the remainder are model Mingotts," the old lady cried scornfully.

Archer noted that Madame Olenska, seated beside her grandmother, was still examining him thoughtfully. The gaiety had drained from her eyes. With great gentleness, she said, "Surely, Granny, we can persuade them jointly to act as he desires."

Archer rose to depart. As his hand touched Madame Olenska's, he sensed she awaited an allusion to her unanswered letter.

"When may I see you?" he asked as she accompanied him to the door.

"Whenever you wish; provided it is soon if you intend to view the little house again. I am relocating next week."

A sharp pang struck him at the recollection of his lamplight hours in the low-ceilinged drawing-room. Though few in number, they were dense with memory.

"Tomorrow evening?"

She nodded. "Tomorrow; yes; but early. I am going out."

The following day was Sunday. If she were "going out" on a Sunday evening, it could only mean Mrs. Lemuel Struthers's. A slight irritation stirred within him—not primarily at her destination, for he tolerated her freedom despite the van der Luydens—but because it was a venue where she was certain to encounter Beaufort, a meeting she must have anticipated, perhaps arranged.

"Very well; tomorrow evening," he repeated, resolving internally to arrive late. By reaching her door after the initial hour, he would either intercept her before she entered the Struthers's or arrive after she had departed—a solution that seemed, all things considered, the simplest.

It was only half-past eight when he rang the bell beneath the wisteria. Not as late as planned by thirty minutes, yet a singular restlessness had propelled him to her threshold. He reasoned that Mrs. Struthers's Sunday evenings lacked the formal rigidity of a ball, and her guests, seeking to minimize their transgression, typically departed early.

What he had not anticipated upon entering Madame Olenska's hall was the presence of hats and overcoats. Why bid him come early if hosting a dinner? Upon closer inspection of the garments alongside Nastasia's placement of his own coat, his resentment curdled into curiosity. These overcoats were peculiar, the strangest he had witnessed under a polite roof. One was a shaggy yellow ulster of "reach-me-down" cut; the other, an ancient, rusted cloak with a cape resembling a French "Macfarlane." This garment, seemingly tailored for a figure of prodigious bulk, bore the marks of long, arduous wear, its greenish-black folds emitting a moist, sawdust scent evocative of prolonged nights against bar-room walls. Resting atop it were a ragged grey scarf and an odd felt hat of semi-clerical shape.

Archer raised his eyebrows questioningly at Nastasia, who returned the gesture with a fatalistic "Gia!" before swinging the drawing-room door wide.

The young man observed immediately that his hostess was absent. To his surprise, he discovered another lady standing by the fire. This woman was long, lean, and loosely assembled, clad in raiment intricately looped and fringed, with plaids, stripes, and bands of plain color arranged in a design whose key seemed lost. Her hair, attempting to turn white and succeeding only in fading, was crowned by a Spanish comb and a black lace scarf. Silk mittens, visibly darned, covered her rheumatic hands.
