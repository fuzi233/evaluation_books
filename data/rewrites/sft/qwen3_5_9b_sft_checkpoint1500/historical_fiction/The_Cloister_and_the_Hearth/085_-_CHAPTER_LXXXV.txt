The service had commenced just as Margaret stepped across the threshold of the vast, unfinished cathedral of St. Laurence. It was a monumental undertaking, clearly not destined for completion within a single year. While the side aisles bore roofs, the central nave and the sanctuary remained open to the elements; yet the stone pillars and soaring arches stood with a majestic perfection, some already washed in bright white lime. Of the windows, only one held glass; the remaining apertures were mere jagged wounds in the masonry.

And today, these raw imperfections transformed the space into something transcendent. A glorious summer afternoon bathed the interior, casting sunlight through the irregular gaps in fragments of miraculous geometry. The beams danced playful, bewitching patterns across the rough surfaces, streaming through the gaping walls to carve the dark, cool side aisles with rivers of radiance. They dazzled upon the white columns, making them glow with an inner life, while the great central aisle became a checkerboard of shifting light and shadow. These shades offered a coolness more profound than any artificial chill, and the lights appeared as patches of amber diamonds ignited with heavenly fire. Above, the azure sky vaulted the lofty aisle, pressing down intimately from west to east.

A sea of white bonnets contrasted exquisitely with this vivid canopy of blue. Though the massive central aisle was packed to capacity, a hushed silence reigned. The words and the mellifluous, earnest cadence of the preacher held the multitude mute.

Margaret stood entranced by the sheer beauty, the depth of devotion, and "the great calm" that permeated the air. Seeking shelter, she slipped behind a pillar in the north aisle. Even though the distance rendered the words indistinct, a sweet, languid peace settled over her, stirred by the sanctity of the place and the musician quality of the voice. Like warm oil trickling over troubled waves, it smoothed the turmoil within her heart. Leaning wearily against the stone, her eyes half-closed, everything around her softened into a dreamlike haze. It felt, simply, good to be there.

Soon, she observed a lady rising from a prime seat opposite to escape the scorching rays pouring directly onto her head from the high window. Seizing the opportunity, Margaret moved swiftly and quietly, securing the spot. Now positioned beside a pillar in the south aisle, less than fifty feet from the rostrum, she sat at his side, slightly behind, able to catch every syllable.

However, her focus fractured moments later by the sight of a man's head and shoulders bobbing up and down with a peculiar, comical rhythm, tempting a smile despite herself. Yet, beneath the surface, it was no longer essentially droll.

It was the sexton, diligently digging.

She realized this instantly by peering backward through the window gap, tracing the shadow to its source. Just then, a specific inflection of the preacher's voice struck her ear and mind with such distinct force that it seemed to literally vibrate her very bones. Her hand flew instinctively to her bosom, startled by the sudden, strange thrill. Turning sharply, she sought the speaker. His back was to her, revealing nothing but the shaved crown of his tonsure. She sighed, feeling a contradiction; the physical reality of the tonsure effectively undermined the profound emotional resonance of the tone.

Still, she leaned forward slightly, eyes cast downward, praying for that accent to return. It did not. Yet the entire voice grew strangely potent within her, rising and falling as the preacher warmed to his subject. It seemed to awaken faint echoes of a thousand happy, long-lost memories. She refused to look away, unwilling to dispel the melancholy, intoxicating pleasure this voice evoked.

Suddenly, midway through an eloquent passage, the preacher halted.

An involuntary sigh escaped her lips; a soothing melody had abruptly ceased. Was the sermon finished? No. Glancing around, she saw the congregation remained seated, unmoving.

Then, a number of faces began to turn toward her. She whipped her gaze backward, finding nothing there. Startled expressions nearby fixed themselves upon the preacher. Following their stares, she froze. In the pulpit, a face stared back, pale and rigid as a corpse. The friar's eyes, naturally large and accentuated by the hollows of his cheeks, were dilated to a supernatural size, blazing outward from a bloodless visage, locked directly upon her.

Margaret cringed, spinning about in terror, convinced some dreadful entity stood beside her. But there was nothing; she was merely an external figure within the listening throng.

In that instant, the church erupted into commotion. Figures surged upward, craning necks forward; agitated countenances swayed from the friar to Margaret, and back again. The rapid turning of so many caps created a deafening rustle. Shrieks from nervous women and the buzzing murmur of men filled the air. Seeing hundreds of eyes leveled at her, Margaret shrank terrified behind the pillar, stealing one frantic, hurried glance at the speaker before burying herself in the shadows.

That fleeting glimpse captured an expression on the stricken face that sent a shiver racing down her spine.

She swooned, collapsing onto a pile of woodchips left by the laborers, burying her face in her hands. The sermon resumed, and she heard the sound of it, but the sense eluded her. Her mind spun in a vortex, unable to coalesce into any form other than this terrifying truth: *upon that prodigy-struck face, I had seen a look.* And the memory of that look now made her tremble from head to toe.

For that look was "RECOGNITION."

After wavering uncertainly, the sermon concluded in a strain of exalted, feverish eloquence, driving the crowd to forget the preacher's strange pause and ghastly stare. Margaret merged hastily with the departing throng and exited the church.

Outside, they dispersed toward their homes, but she paused at the door, turning instead into the churchyard to Peter's grave. Though poor, she had secured him a slab and a headstone. Sitting upon the stone, she pressed a kiss to it, then drew her apron over her head to conceal her hair from any passersby.

"Father," she whispered, "you have often heard me say I am wading in deep waters; but now I begin to think God alone knows the bottom of them. I will follow that friar round the world, but I shall keep him at arm's length. And he shall tell me why he looked toward me like a dead man awakened, with no soul standing behind me. Oh, Father; you often praised me here: speak a word for me there. For I am wading in deep waters."

Her father's tomb commanded a commanding view of the church door. There, with her face veiled, she sat upon the stone, waiting to intercept the holy preacher.
