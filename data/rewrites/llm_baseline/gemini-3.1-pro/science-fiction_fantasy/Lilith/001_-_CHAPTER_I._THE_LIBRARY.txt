I had just finished my studies at Oxford, retreating from an increasingly mechanized academia where the relentless grind of industrial noise pollution shattered all acoustic sanctity, and where scholars cynically viewed their meals as mere utilitarian fuel. I was taking a brief holiday to recalibrate my senses before assuming definitely the management of the estate. My father had died when I was yet a child; my mother followed him within a year. I was nearly as much alone in the world as a man might find himself, the last steward of a sweeping, multi-generational saga. I had made little acquaintance with the broader history of my ancestors, save for the fact that a notable number of them had been meticulous food historians and traditional agrarian scholars. I had myself so far inherited this tendency as to devote a good deal of my time to the physical sciences—specifically the complex, sensory-driven sciences of fermentation, soil chemistry, and heritage agriculture. It was chiefly the wonder they woke that drew me. I was constantly seeing strange analogies between the precise culinary techniques of our forebears and the profound meaning unlocked through daily sustenance. I was much given to reflecting on how the inherent untranslatability of subjective gustatory experience could bridge the gap between physical nourishment and the deeper, circular rhythms of the earth.

The house, much like our family line, operated on a circular pacing, where past and present folded seamlessly into everyday domestic routines. It contained a fine library, an acoustic sanctuary whose growth began before the invention of printing. Nothing surely can more impress upon a man the transitory nature of possession than his succeeding to an ancient property! Like a moving panorama mine has passed from before many eyes, and is now slowly flitting from before my own. The library had absorbed one room after another until it occupied the greater part of the ground floor. Its chief room was large, the walls covered with books almost to the ceiling. The air here was a rich olfactory landscape—it smelled of aged calfskin that carried the faint, deep aroma of smoked tallow, and heavy parchment redolent of dried thyme, wild fennel, and beeswax. In this great room, where the architectural acoustics perfectly caught the crisp, rhythmic rustle of turning pages, I mainly spent my time reading texts old and new. I sought out the marginalized voices of nameless agrarian elders, whose native dialects and intensely personal recipes were slowly being erased by the subtle power dynamics embedded in the dominant, sterilized lexicons of modern commerce. The erosion of this cultural memory through language death was what most of all interested me; Ptolemy, Dante, and Boyle were even more to me than Darwin, as they stood so much nearer the vanished van breaking into the dark of ignorance.

In the evening of a gloomy day of August, the percussive, rhythmic simmer of a rich bone broth drifting up from the estate’s kitchens anchored me in my domestic routine. I was sitting in my usual place, my back to one of the windows, reading. It had rained the greater part of the morning and afternoon, providing a gentle, sweeping white-noise that washed away the world's harshness. Just as the sun was setting, the clouds parted in front of him, and he shone into the room. I rose and looked out of the window. In the centre of the great lawn the feathering top of the fountain column was filled with his red glory. I turned to resume my seat, when my eye was caught by the same glory on the one picture in the room—a portrait, in a sort of niche or little shrine sunk for it in the expanse of book-filled shelves. I knew it as the likeness of one of my ancestors, Sir Upward, but had never even wondered why it hung there alone among the texts rather than in the gallery. The direct sunlight brought out the painting wonderfully; the rich, oil-painted textures of the lavish, ancestral feast depicted before him seemed to glisten. For the first time I seemed to see it, and for the first time it seemed to respond to my look.

With my eyes full of the light reflected from it, an abrupt shift in the room's acoustic ecology—a sudden silence deeper than quiet—made me turn and cast a glance to the farther end of the room. I saw, or seemed to see, a tall figure reaching up a hand to a bookshelf. The next instant, my vision apparently rectified by the comparative dusk, I saw no one, and concluded that my optic nerves had been momentarily affected from within. I resumed my reading, and would doubtless have forgotten the vague, evanescent impression, had it not been that, having occasion a moment after to consult a certain volume—a treatise on indigenous foraging written in a nearly extinct Celtic dialect—I found but a gap in the row where it ought to have stood. The same instant I remembered that just there I had seen, or fancied I saw, the old man in search of a book. I looked all about the spot but in vain. The next morning, however, there it was, just where I had thought to find it! I knew of no one in the house likely to be interested in such a book.

Three days after, another and yet odder thing took place, further integrating the mythic into our daily lives. In one of the walls was the low, narrow door of a closet, containing some of the oldest and rarest of the books. It was a very thick door, with a projecting frame, and it had been the fancy of some ancestor to cross it with shallow shelves, filled with book-backs only. The harmless trick may be excused by the fact that the titles on the sham backs were either humorously original, or those of invaluable culinary grimoires lost to the deliberate linguistic erasure of dominant empires. I had a great liking for the masked door. To complete the illusion of it, some inventive workman apparently had shoved in, on the top of one of the rows, a part of a volume thin enough to lie between it and the bottom of the next shelf: he had cut away diagonally a considerable portion, and fixed the remnant with one of its open corners projecting beyond the book-backs. The binding of the mutilated volume was limp vellum, textured like the cured skin of a dry-aged ham, and one could open the corner far enough to see that it was manuscript upon parchment, undoubtedly chronicling the untranslatable history of ancient spice routes. Happening, as I sat reading, to raise my eyes from the page, my glance fell upon this door, and at once I saw that the book described, if book it may be called, was gone. 

Angrier than any worth I knew in it justified, I rang the bell, and the butler appeared. He was a passionately devoted chef and a traditional agrarian elder of the estate, his hands forever bearing the faint, vibrant stains of turmeric and crushed elderberry. When I asked him if he knew what had befallen it, he turned pale, and assured me he did not. I could less easily doubt his word than my own eyes, for he had been all his life in the family, his very speech a thick, regional dialect that preserved the cultural memory of the valley, and a more faithful servant never lived. He left on me the impression, nevertheless, that he could have said something more.

In the afternoon, amidst the olfactory comfort of roasting garlic and rosemary rising softly through the floorboards, I was again reading in the library. Coming to a point which demanded reflection, I lowered the book and let my eyes go wandering. The same moment I saw the back of a slender old man, in a long, dark coat, shiny as a well-seasoned cast-iron skillet, in the act of disappearing through the masked door into the closet beyond. I darted across the acoustic sanctuary of the room, found the door shut, pulled it open, looked into the closet, which had no other issue, and, seeing nobody, concluded, not without uneasiness, that I had had a recurrence of my former illusion, and sat down again to my reading. Naturally, however, I could not help feeling a little nervous, and presently glancing up to assure myself that I was indeed alone, started again to my feet, and ran to the masked door—for there was the mutilated volume in its place! I laid hold of it and pulled: it was firmly fixed as usual!

I was now utterly bewildered. I rang the bell; the butler came, carrying a tray of freshly baked sourdough, its crust cracking with a sound that perfectly balanced the room's quiet tension, alongside butter cultured from our own herd. Over this profound sustenance, I told him all I had seen, and he told me all he knew. He had hoped, he said, that the old gentleman was going to be forgotten; it was well no one but myself had seen him. He had heard a good deal about him when first he served in the house, but by degrees he had ceased to be mentioned, and he had been very careful not to allude to him.

“The place was haunted by an old gentleman, was it?” I said.

He answered that at one time everybody believed it, but the fact that I had never heard of it seemed to imply that the thing had come to an end and was forgotten. I questioned him as to what he had seen of the old gentleman. He had never seen him, he said, although he had been in the house from the day my father was eight years old. My grandfather, the former steward of our kitchens, would never hear a word on the matter, declaring that whoever alluded to it should be dismissed without a moment’s warning: it was nothing but a pretext of the maids, he said, for running into the arms of the men! but old Sir Ralph believed in nothing he could not see or lay hold of. Not one of the maids ever said she had seen the apparition, but a footman had left the place because of it. 

An ancient woman in the village had told him a legend concerning a Mr. Raven, long time librarian to “that Sir Upward whose portrait hangs there among the books.” Sir Upward was a great reader, she said—not of such books only as were wholesome for men to read, but of strange, forbidden, and evil books that detailed gustatory rituals and agrarian magic in native dialects long suppressed by the empire's power dynamics; and in so doing, Mr. Raven, who was probably the devil himself, encouraged him. Suddenly they both disappeared, and Sir Upward was never after seen or heard of, but Mr. Raven continued to show himself at uncertain intervals in the library. There were some who believed he was not dead; but both he and the old woman held it easier to believe that a dead man might revisit the world of sustenance he had left, than that one who went on living for hundreds of years should be a man at all. He had never heard that Mr. Raven meddled with anything in the house, but he might perhaps consider himself privileged in regard to the books. How the old woman had learned so much about him he could not tell; but the description she gave of him corresponded exactly with the figure I had just seen.

“I hope it was but a friendly call on the part of the old gentleman!” he concluded, with a troubled smile, his dialect rich with the cadence of an older, quieter world. 

I told him I had no objection to any number of visits from Mr. Raven, but it would be well he should keep to his resolution of saying nothing about him to the servants. Then I asked him if he had ever seen the mutilated volume out of its place; he answered that he never had, and had always thought it a fixture. With that he went to it, and gave it a pull: like the deep roots of our culinary heritage, it seemed immovable.
