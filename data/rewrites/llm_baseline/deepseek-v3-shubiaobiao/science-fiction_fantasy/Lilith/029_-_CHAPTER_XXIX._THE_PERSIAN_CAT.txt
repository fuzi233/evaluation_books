I sat in silence, burning with shame. Every word he spoke was true—I had failed the Little Ones. Worse, I had failed myself.

Mr. Raven regarded me with unsettling calm. "You wronged them twice over. For creatures like them, a little discipline might have been a mercy. A lesson or two from their own branches, and they might have stood a chance against what’s coming."

"Cowards," I muttered, though the word tasted bitter.

"Cowardice in others doesn’t justify cruelty in you," he said. "A man who preys on weakness is weaker still." His voice darkened. "And now, because of your dealings with *her*—"

"I despise her!" I snarled.

"Did you ever let her know it?"

My silence was answer enough.

His gaze sharpened. "Not even hatred could make you honest. But quiet now—we weren’t alone at the fountain."

"I saw nothing. Only a wretched cat slinking into the bushes."

"A Persian," he corrected, "though you’d never know it by the state of her. Fur matted, eyes wild—hardly the regal creature she pretends to be."

A chill gripped me. "What are you saying?"

"We’ll see soon enough," he murmured. "Cats have their tells, and I know a few." He rose, crossed to the closet, and returned with the book—the *whole* book, intact, no longer torn.

I stared. "But the other half—"

"Lodged in my library," he said, as if that explained everything.

Perhaps it did. I held my tongue. Some questions had no safe answers.

He opened the ancient volume, its pages yellowed, one stained dark as old blood. His fingers traced the lines, then he began to read—not in any language I knew, yet every word seared into my mind. Fragments took shape in my memory:

A hiss slithered through the room. I jerked upright, scanning the shadows. Nothing.

He turned a page.

Another turn. Another verse.

A shriek tore through the air, raw and guttural. Not human. Not wholly animal.

Mr. Raven didn’t flinch. He read on, relentless.

The scream came again. This time, movement—a streak of white, a blur of matted fur. A massive cat, spine arched, claws splayed, scrambled toward the hearth.

He slammed the book down in its path. The creature froze, eyes locked on the pages.

Still, his voice rolled on, as if the words themselves were a cage:

The cat convulsed. Its yowl was a sound of rending flesh.

Mr. Raven stood, took a charred stick from the fireplace, and drew a symbol on the floor. Then he faced the trembling creature.

"Lilith," he said, quiet as a grave. "You didn’t expect *me* here, did you?"

The cat’s pupils swallowed its eyes.

He continued, merciless. "When God shaped me, He gave me a wife—not a servant, not a slave. A queen. But you wanted worship, not love. So you bled yourself dry to flee me, wed the Shadow instead, and crowned yourself in Hell."

The air thickened. The cat’s form shuddered, stretched—

A woman crouched on the carpet, naked, glorious, venom in her smile. "I am *beautiful*," she hissed. "And I am *eternal*."

"You are a burning bush," he said. "All ash, no flame."

Her hand flew to her side. A spasm twisted her face.

He stepped closer. "What’s festering under your ribs, Lilith? A leopard’s spot? Or the rot of your choices?"

She bared her teeth. "I will *drink* the blood of your child."

His voice changed. Not louder. *Deeper.* As if the earth itself spoke.

"**Down.**"

She collapsed. Fur rippled over skin. The leopard writhed, then shrank—

A scrawny gray cat again.

He seized her by the neck, hauled her to the closet, and flung her inside. With a final mark on the threshold, he shut the door. The lock clicked.

When he turned back to me, he was just an old librarian, weary and sad.

But his eyes were still full of storms.
