In Flatland, just as in your own realm, the compass points are fixed: North, South, East, and West. Yet, unlike your world, we possess no sun, no moon, and no stars to mark the sky. Determining direction here requires a unique reliance on the laws of our nature. A subtle, constant gravitational pull draws everything inexorably toward the South. While this force is negligible in our temperate zones—allowing a healthy woman to wander several furlongs northward with ease—it serves as our primary navigational anchor across most of the land. This pull is compounded by the rain, which descends at regular intervals exclusively from the North, offering a wet warning of our orientation. Within our cities, architecture reinforces this guidance; our dwellings are aligned so their walls run North-South, shielding interiors from the northern downpour. In the open countryside, where structures are absent, the trunks of trees stand as silent sentinels pointing the way. Despite these aids, navigation remains a challenge. In the vast, featureless plains of our temperate regions, devoid of both shelter and timber, I have known myself stranded for hours, helpless until the first drop of rain fell from the North to reveal my bearing.

The burden of this southern attraction is not borne equally. It weighs heavily upon the frail, the elderly, and particularly upon the delicate Females of our sex. Consequently, it is a fundamental rule of breeding that any man encountering a lady in the street must immediately yield the North side of the path to her. This act of courtesy is far from simple, especially when one is in robust health and the climate renders the distinction between North and South a matter of guesswork.

Our dwellings offer no windows, for light permeates our existence uniformly, day and night, from a source we cannot perceive. In ancient times, our scholars obsessed over the origin of this illumination, their futile inquiries filling our asylums with the insane. The Legislature eventually banned such research entirely, yet I alone bear the terrible truth: I know the source of light lies in a third dimension. My insight isolates me, marking me as the maddest of the mad, while my fellow citizens remain blind to the geometry of higher spaces.

Turning back to our homes, the standard design is pentagonal. As illustrated, the two northern sides, RO and OF, form the roof and typically lack doors. The eastern side holds a narrow entrance reserved for women, while the western side features a broad doorway for men; the southern base remains doorless. Square and triangular residences are strictly prohibited. The sharp angles of squares and triangles pose a lethal threat to travelers, whose lines are less distinct than those of living beings. A sudden collision with the corner of a square or triangle could prove fatal. Recognizing this danger, the law banned triangular homes as early as the eleventh century, permitting them only for military fortifications and state arsenals. Squares were tolerated longer but taxed heavily. Three centuries ago, the Legislature decreed that in any town exceeding ten thousand souls, the pentagon became the minimum allowable shape for public safety. Today, the pentagonal form dominates even our rural landscapes, leaving the rare square house as a relic found only in the most isolated, backward districts.
