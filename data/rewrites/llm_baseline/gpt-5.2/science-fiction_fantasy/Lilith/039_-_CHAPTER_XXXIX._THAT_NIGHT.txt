Their night went wrong in the manner a loaf goes wrong when the starter has soured without anyone naming the fault: nothing visible at first, no crash of pans, no shout from the scullery—only a wrongness that keeps rising, insisting, until even waking feels like a continuation of a dream you cannot translate into daylight.

In the morning the children brought their account with them as if it were a basket of gathered things: odd, intact, and trembling with what they could not set down in proper words.

All night, they said, the house had been busy—busy in silence. They were not woken by any creak of timber, any settling of stone, any of those homely noises a cottage makes the way a hearth makes its own little speech. The dark and the quiet were welded together so tight it became terror. Once a wind had filled the whole house as steam fills a lidded pot, shaking its insides so that the walls quivered like a horse shrugging off rain—yet not a moan came through keyhole or shutter. It passed like a sob swallowed before it could become sound.

After that they slept—then woke with a start so sharp it felt, to them, as though the bed had dipped under their bodies.

The house was filling with water.

Not river-water with its rush, nor rain-water with its drip, but the same strange water they had been made to drink: cold on the tongue, too clean, too intentional, as if it had been poured from a vessel whose inside had never known dust. It rose from below without gurgle or lap, swelling up through floor and stair until, in their telling, the garret was full to the rafters. And still there was no sound—no slosh, no sigh of wave against beam. When it sank, it left them dry and warm as if the whole thing had happened only to their skins’ remembrance.

They slept again. Woke again.

Then the air—inside and out—was full of cats.

Cats as thick as grain in a bin, swarming up and down, along and across, everywhere about the room. The children felt claws questing for purchase through the nightgowns Lady Mara had put on them—felt them with the raw certainty of touch, like burrs on wool—yet in the morning not one scratch marked their arms, not one snag caught in cloth. Only once did a sound arrive, a single thread of it: far off, out of the desert, the long howl of a huge great-grandmother-cat calling as if the world were her yard and the stars her kittens. At that instant every cat stilled—an obedience so complete it was worse than noise—and the silence closed again like a mouth.

After that they fell fast asleep and did not wake until the sun was rising.

Such was their report: terror in images, but with the curious domestic detail of the untouched fabric and the unmarked skin, as if the night had tried to make itself real and been refused by the very weave of things.

But I was not with the children. I was with the veiled woman and the princess through all those hours. Something of what took place I saw; much I only felt; and there was more that no eye could seize, as if the event moved in a language older than speech, where meaning is carried by presence as flavour is carried by steam.

As soon as Mara left the room with the little ones, my gaze fell on the white leopardess. I had thought we had left her behind us, but there she was, crouched in a corner, drawn tight into herself as if any widening of her body would invite what she feared. Her eyes held that look animals get when the soundscape has altered without producing a sound—when the world’s usual chorus is gone, and the absence itself is a predator.

A lamp stood on the high chimney-piece. Its light did not behave as light should in a cottage: sometimes the room seemed crowded with lamp-shadows, thick as smoke that has no smell; sometimes with cloudy forms that were not smoke and not mist, shapes like breath you can see but cannot breathe. The princess lay on the settle by the wall and appeared never to have moved hand or foot, as if she were a dish set out to cool and left untouched—only she was no food, and the waiting was hunger in reverse.

When Mara returned she drew the settle with Lilith upon it to the middle of the room, then sat down opposite me at the other side of the hearth. Between us burned a small fire, the kind a careful housekeeper keeps in reserve, not for show but for need—coals banked, flame held low, heat husbanded like precious fat.

Something terrible was on its way. The cloudy presences flickered and shook, as if the air itself had been stirred by an invisible spoon.

A silvery creature like a slowworm came crawling out from among them, slid over the clay floor, and crept into the fire. It did not hiss. It did not sizzle. The coals did not crack. We sat motionless, the way you sit when the slightest stirring might spill what must not be spilled.

The thing came nearer. Yet the hours passed. Midnight drew nigh, and there was no change any sense could name. The night was very still—not even the modest rustle of a settling log, not even the dry tick of wood cooling. Now and again I felt a sort of heave, but whether in earth or air or the waters under the earth, whether in my own body or in my soul, I could not tell. A dread sense of judgment lay over me like a weight—but I was not afraid. I had ceased to care for aught save the thing that must be done.

Then it was midnight.

The muffled woman rose, turned toward the settle, and slowly unwound the long swathes that hid her face. They dropped to the floor with the soft, dead sound of cloth falling on clay, and she stepped over them.

The feet of the princess were toward the hearth; Mara went to her head and, turning, stood behind it.

Then I saw her face.

It was lovely beyond speech—white and sad, heart-and-soul sad, but not unhappy, as though sorrow had been baked into it the way salt is baked into bread: not to spoil, but to make it fit for keeping. Great tears ran down her cheeks. She wiped them away with her robe, and her countenance grew very still. But for the pity in every line of her expression, she would have seemed severe.

She laid her hand on the head of the princess—on the hair that grew low on the forehead—and, stooping, breathed on the sallow brow.

The body shuddered.

“Will you turn away from the wicked things you have been doing so long?” Mara asked gently.

The princess did not answer.

Mara put the question again, the same soft, inviting tone, like a mother offering warm broth to a child who insists she is not hungry.

Still there was no sign of hearing.

She spoke the words a third time.

Then the seeming corpse opened its mouth and answered, her words forming themselves of something other than sound. They were not sounds, yet they were words to me—meaning arriving without the bridge of air, as if the room itself had learned to speak without disturbing its own silence.

“I will not,” she said. “I will be myself and not another!”

“Alas, you are another now, not yourself! Will you not be your real self?”

“I will be what I mean myself now.”

“If you were restored, would you not make what amends you could for the misery you have caused?”

“I would do after my nature.”

“You do not know it: your nature is good, and you do evil!”

“I will do as my Self pleases—as my Self desires.”

“You will do as the Shadow, overshadowing your Self inclines you?”

“I will do what I will to do.”

“You have killed your daughter, Lilith!”

“I have killed thousands. She is my own!”

“She was never yours as you are another’s.”

“I am not another’s; I am my own, and my daughter is mine.”

“Then, alas, your hour is come!”

“I care not. I am what I am; no one can take from me myself!”

“You are not the Self you imagine.”

“So long as I feel myself what it pleases me to think myself, I care not. I am content to be to myself what I would be. What I choose to seem to myself makes me what I am. My own thought makes me me; my own thought of myself is me. Another shall not make me!”

“But another has made you, and can compel you to see what you have made yourself. You will not be able much longer to look to yourself anything but what he sees you! You will not much longer have satisfaction in the thought of yourself. At this moment you are aware of the coming change!”

“No one ever made me. I defy that Power to unmake me from a free woman! You are his slave, and I defy you! You may be able to torture me—I do not know, but you shall not compel me to anything against my will!”

“Such a compulsion would be without value. But there is a light that goes deeper than the will, a light that lights up the darkness behind it: that light can change your will, can make it truly yours and not another’s—not the Shadow’s. Into the created can pour itself the creating will, and so redeem it!”

“That light shall not enter me: I hate it!—Begone, slave!”

“I am no slave, for I love that light, and will with the deeper will which created mine. There is no slave but the creature that wills against its creator. Who is a slave but her who cries, ‘I am free,’ yet cannot cease to exist!”

“You speak foolishness from a cowering heart! You imagine me given over to you: I defy you! I hold myself against you! What I choose to be, you cannot change. I will not be what you think me—what you say I am!”

“I am sorry: you must suffer!”

“But be free!”

“She alone is free who would make free; she loves not freedom who would enslave: she is herself a slave. Every life, every will, every heart that came within your ken, you have sought to subdue: you are the slave of every slave you have made—such a slave that you do not know it!—See your own self!”

Mara took her hand from the head of the princess and went two backward paces from her.

A soundless presence as of roaring flame possessed the house—the same, I presume, that to the children had been a silent wind. Involuntarily I turned to the hearth: its fire was a still, small, moveless glow, as if it were keeping its breath.

But I saw the worm-thing come creeping out, white-hot, vivid as incandescent silver—the live heart of essential fire. Along the floor it crawled toward the settle, going very slow. Yet more slowly it climbed upon it and laid itself, as unwilling to go further, at the feet of the princess.

I rose and stole nearer. Mara stood motionless, as one waiting an event foreknown.

The shining thing crawled onto a bare bony foot. It showed no suffering; neither was the settle scorched where it had lain. Slowly—very slowly—it crept along her robe until it reached her bosom, where it disappeared among the folds.

The face of the princess lay stonily calm, eyelids closed as over dead eyes. For some minutes nothing followed.

Then, on the dry, parchment-like skin, drops appeared as of the finest dew—at first like the sheen on a cooled kettle, then swelling at once into seed-pearls. They ran together and began to pour in streams. It was as if her body had been made into a vessel and was sweating out what it could no longer contain.

I darted forward to snatch the worm from her withered bosom and crush it with my foot.

But Mara—Mother of Sorrow—stepped between and drew aside the closed edges of the robe. No serpent was there. No searing trail. The creature had passed in by the centre of the black spot, piercing through joints and marrow to the thoughts and intents of the heart.

The princess gave one writhing, contorted shudder, and I knew the worm was in her secret chamber.

“She is seeing herself,” said Mara; and laying her hand on my arm, she drew me three paces from the settle.

Of a sudden the princess bent her body upward in an arch, then sprang to the floor and stood erect.

The horror in her face made me tremble lest her eyes should open and the sight of them overwhelm me. Her bosom heaved and sank, but no breath issued. Her hair hung and dripped; then it stood out from her head and emitted sparks; again it hung down and poured the sweat of her torture onto the floor in darkening splashes that had no smell, no saltiness—nothing the senses could claim as familiar.

I would have thrown my arms about her, but Mara stopped me.

“You cannot go near her,” she said. “She is far away from us, afar in the hell of her self-consciousness. The central fire of the universe is radiating into her the knowledge of good and evil, the knowledge of what she is. She sees at last the good she is not, the evil she is. She knows that she is herself the fire in which she is burning, but she does not know that the Light of Life is the heart of that fire. Her torment is that she is what she is. Do not fear for her; she is not forsaken. No gentler way to help her was left. Wait and watch.”

It may have been five minutes or five years that she stood thus—I cannot tell. Time, in that room, had the circular feel of work done by hand in the same season every year: kneading, resting, kneading again, until the dough itself seems to remember.

At last she flung herself on her face.

Mara went to her and stood looking down. Large tears fell from Mara’s eyes onto the woman who had never wept and would not weep.

“Will you change your way?” Mara asked at length.

“Why did he make me such?” gasped Lilith. “I would have made myself—oh, so different! I am glad it was he that made me and not I myself! He alone is to blame for what I am! Never would I have made such a worthless thing! He meant me such that I might know it and be miserable! I will not be made any longer!”

“Unmake yourself, then,” said Mara.

“Alas, I cannot! You know it, and mock me! How often have I not agonised to cease, but the tyrant keeps me being! I curse him!—Now let him kill me!” The words came in jets, as from a dying fountain, spurting and failing.

“Had he not made you,” Mara answered, gently and slowly, “you could not even hate him. But he did not make you such. You have made yourself what you are.—Be of better cheer: he can remake you.”

“I will not be remade!”

“He will not change you; he will only restore you to what you were.”

“I will not be aught of his making.”

“Are you not willing to have that set right which you have set wrong?”

She lay silent. Her suffering seemed to ease, not as a sickness leaves, but as if some inner grip had slackened.

“If you are willing,” said Mara, “put yourself again on the settle.”

“I will not,” Lilith answered, forcing the words through clenched teeth.

Then a wind seemed to wake inside the house, blowing without sound or impact; and a water began to rise that had no lap in its ripples, no sob in its swell. It was cold, but did not benumb. Unseen and noiseless it came. It smote no sense in me, yet I knew it rising, the way you know a broth is nearing boil by the thickening of its surface before it speaks.

I saw it lift her at last and float her. Gently it bore her, unable to resist, and left rather than laid her on the settle. Then it sank swiftly away.

The strife of thought—accusing and excusing—began afresh and gathered fierceness. Lilith’s soul lay naked to the torture of pure inward light. She began to moan, then to sigh deep sighs, then to murmur as if holding colloquy with a dividual self: her queendom was no longer whole; it was divided against itself.

One moment she exulted as over her worst enemy, and wept; the next she writhed as in the embrace of a friend her soul hated, and laughed like a demon.

At length she began what seemed a tale about herself in a language so strange, and in forms so shadowy, that I could but here and there understand a little. Yet the language seemed the primeval shape of one I knew well, and the forms belonged to dreams that had once been mine but refused to be recalled. Now and then the tale touched things Adam had read from the disparted manuscript, and often it alluded to influences and forces—vices too, I could not help suspecting—with which I was unacquainted. It struck me with a pang like hearing an old recipe spoken in a lost dialect: you recognise the cadence, you smell the remembered kitchen, but the names of the herbs have fallen out of your mouth.

She ceased. Again came the horror in her hair—the sparkling and flowing alternate.

I sent a beseeching look to Mara.

“Those, alas, are not the tears of repentance,” she said. “The true tears gather in the eyes. Those are far more bitter, and not so good. Self-loathing is not sorrow. Yet it is good, for it marks a step in the way home, and in the father’s arms the prodigal forgets the self he abominates. Once with his father, he is to himself of no more account. It will be so with her.”

She went nearer and said, “Will you restore that which you have wrongfully taken?”

“I have taken nothing,” Lilith answered, forcing out the words in spite of pain, “that I had not the right to take. My power to take manifested my right.”

Mara left her.

Gradually my soul grew aware of an invisible darkness, something more terrible than aught that had yet made itself felt. A horrible Nothingness, a Negation positive, infolded her. The border of its being—that was yet no being—touched me, and for one ghastly instant I seemed alone with Death Absolute.

It was not the absence of everything I felt, but the presence of Nothing.

The princess dashed herself from the settle to the floor with an exceeding great and bitter cry. It was the recoil of Being from Annihilation.

“For pity’s sake,” she shrieked, “tear my heart out, but let me live!”

With that there fell upon her—and upon us who watched—the perfect calm as of a summer night when the last machine has stopped and the fields can be heard again: an earthen hush, complete, as if the world had put away its harsh tools and returned to its older music. Suffering had all but reached the brim of her life’s cup, and a hand had emptied it.

She raised her head, half rose, and looked around. A moment more and she stood erect with the air of a conqueror, as if she had won a battle no one else could see. Dareful she had met her spiritual foes; they had withdrawn defeated. She raised her withered arm above her head, a paean of unholy triumph in her throat—

When suddenly her eyes fixed in a ghastly stare.

What was she seeing?

I looked, and saw: before her, cast from an unseen heavenly mirror, stood the reflection of herself, and beside it a form of splendour and beauty.

She trembled and sank again on the floor, helpless. She knew: the one what God had intended her to be, the other what she had made herself.

The rest of the night she lay altogether motionless.

With the gray dawn growing in the room, she rose, turned to Mara, and said in prideful humility, “You have conquered. Let me go into the wilderness and bewail myself.”

Mara looked at her a moment, and returned, “Begin, then, and set right in the place of wrong.”

“I know not how,” Lilith replied—with the look of one who foresees and fears the answer.

“Open thy hand,” said Mara, “and let that which is in it go.”

A fierce refusal struggled for passage, but she kept it prisoned.

“I cannot,” she said. “I have no longer the power. Open it for me.”

She held out the offending hand. It was more a paw than a hand; even in the dim it looked cramped by long clenching, as if the muscles had forgotten any other work.

Mara did not even look at it.

“You must open it yourself,” she said quietly.

“I have told you I cannot!”

“You can if you will—not indeed at once, but by persistent effort. What you have done, you do not yet wish undone—do not yet intend to undo!”

“You think so, I dare say,” the princess flared, insolence breaking through like bitterness in a sauce, “but I KNOW that I cannot open my hand!”

“I know you better than you know yourself, and I know you can. You have often opened it a little way. Without trouble and pain you cannot open it quite, but you CAN open it. At worst you could beat it open! I pray you, gather your strength, and open it wide.”

“I will not try what I know impossible. It would be the part of a fool!”

“Which you have been playing all your life! Oh, you are hard to teach!”

Defiance returned to Lilith’s face. She turned her back on Mara, saying, “I know what you have been tormenting me for! You have not succeeded, nor shall you succeed! You shall yet find me stronger than you think! I will yet be mistress of myself! I am still what I have always known myself—queen of Hell, and mistress of the worlds!”

Then came the most fearful thing of all.

I did not know what it was. I knew myself unable to imagine it. I knew only that if it came near me I should die of terror. I now know it was LIFE IN DEATH—life dead, yet existent. Lilith had had glimpses of it before, but it had never been with her until now.

She stood as she had turned. Mara went and sat down by the fire. Fearing to stand alone with the princess, I went also and sat again by the hearth.

Something began to depart from me. A sense of cold—yet not what we call cold—crept, not into, but out of my being and pervaded it. The lamp of life and the eternal fire seemed dying together, and I was about to be left with naught but the consciousness that I had been alive.

Mercifully, the bereavement did not go so far. My thought went back to Lilith.

Something was taking place in her which we did not know. We did not feel what she felt, but we felt something of the misery it caused her. The thing itself was in her, not in us; its reflex—her misery—reached us and was reflected in us: she was in the outer darkness, and we were present with her who was in it.

We were not in the outer darkness. Had we been, we could not have been with her; we should have been timelessly, spacelessly, absolutely apart. The darkness knows neither the light nor itself; only the light knows itself and the darkness also.

Something was gone from her which then first, by its absence, she knew to have been with her every moment of her wicked years. The source of life had withdrawn itself; all that was left her of conscious being was the dregs of her dead and corrupted life.

She stood rigid.

Mara buried her head in her hands.

I gazed on the face of one who knew existence but not love—knew neither life, nor joy, nor good. With my eyes I saw the face of a live death. She knew life only to know that it was dead, and that, in her, death lived.

It was not merely that life had ceased in her, but that she was consciously a dead thing. She had killed her life, and was dead—and knew it. She must death it for ever and ever. She had tried her hardest to unmake herself and could not. She was a dead life. She could not cease. She must be.

In her face I read beyond its misery—saw in its dismay that the dismay behind it was more than it could manifest. It sent out a livid gloom; the light that was in her was darkness, and after its kind it shone. She was what God could not have created. She had usurped beyond her share in self-creation, and her part had undone His. She saw now what she had made, and behold, it was not good. She was as a conscious corpse whose coffin would never come to pieces, never set her free. Her bodily eyes stood wide open, as if gazing into the heart of horror essential—her own indestructible evil. Her right hand also was now clenched—upon existent Nothing—her inheritance.

But with God all things are possible.

Without change of look, without sign of purpose, Lilith walked toward Mara. Mara felt her coming and rose to meet her.

“I yield,” said the princess. “I cannot hold out. I am defeated.—Not the less, I cannot open my hand.”

“Have you tried?” asked Mara.

“I am trying now with all my might.”

“I will take you to my father. You have wronged him worst of the created; therefore he best of the created can help you.”

“How can HE help me?”

“He will forgive you.”

“Ah, if he would but help me to cease! Not even that am I capable of! I have no power over myself; I am a slave! I acknowledge it. Let me die.”

“A slave thou art that shall one day be a child!” answered Mara. “Verily, thou shalt die, but not as thou thinkest. Thou shalt die out of death into life. Now is the Life for thee, that never was against thee!”

Like her mother, in whom lay the motherhood of all the world, Mara put her arms around Lilith and kissed her on the forehead.

The fiery-cold misery went out of Lilith’s eyes, and their fountains filled. She lifted her and bore her to her own bed in a corner of the room, laid her softly upon it, and closed her eyes with hands that moved like quiet mercy.

Lilith lay and wept.

The Lady of Sorrow went to the door and opened it.

Morn, with the Spring in her arms, waited outside.

Softly they stole in at the opened door, with a gentle wind in the skirts of their garments. It flowed and flowed about Lilith, rippling the unknown, upwaking sea of her life eternal—rippling it and rippling it, until at length she who had been as a weed cast on dry sand to wither should know herself an inlet of the everlasting ocean, henceforth to flow into her for ever and ebb no more.

She answered the morning wind with reviving breath, and began to listen.

For in the skirts of the wind had come the rain—the soft rain that heals the mown, the many-wounded grass—soothing it with the sweetness of all music, the hush that lives between music and silence. It bedewed the desert places around the cottage, and the sands of Lilith’s heart heard it and drank it in, as parched grain drinks its first wash, swelling toward bread again.

When Mara returned to sit by her bed, Lilith’s tears were flowing softer than the rain, and soon she was fast asleep.
