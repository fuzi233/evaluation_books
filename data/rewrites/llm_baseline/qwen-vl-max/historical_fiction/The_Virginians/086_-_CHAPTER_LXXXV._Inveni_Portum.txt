When my mother learned that I had accepted a position in England, I sensed her displeasure. It was not beyond her to cut off my allowance—perhaps even to starve me into submission, forcing me back to Virginia and under her control. Yet we never spoke plainly of the financial rift between us. She withdrew her support; I said nothing. I lived on what I could manage without her aid. I never heard her express regret or acknowledge injustice—except through Harry’s quiet words to me later. Years passed, and when we met again, she treated my wife with such unexpected kindness and affection that I thought it might be her way of admitting she had been wrong. But she never apologized, nor did I ask for one.

Harry, now secure in his own life, had always been a concern to her. With him settled, all her savings and careful plans were directed toward me, her heir. Once, a few guineas would have meant more than hundreds I might one day inherit. But by the time Madam Esmond and I crossed paths again, necessity was long behind me. I no longer needed to pinch pennies or begrudge the doctor’s fee. I had enough—and more could only add to it. I suspect she suffered privately, knowing her grandchildren had gone hungry while she turned away from them, proud and unyielding. Proud? Was she prouder than I? A simple word of understanding might have healed us years sooner—but neither of us spoke. When I do wrong and later see it clearly, I often seek forgiveness—not for others’ sake, but for my own peace. To apologize is to reclaim something lost within myself. And so, perhaps out of pride in that inner self, I’ve rarely stooped to acts of humility.

I showed no sign of surrender to my mother in Virginia. We lived apart for years, exchanging only brief messages—births, deaths, little things—passed between my wife and her. After our troubles in America over the Stamp Act, new trials came to me in London. Though I stood with the Tory side in the dispute—believing the Crown had full right to tax the colonies—I wrote an outspoken letter to a member of the House of Burgesses in Virginia. It questioned the long-standing arrogance of Britain toward its colonies, and expressed views so unpalatable to those in power that I lost my post as hackney-coach licenser. My uncle was horrified. He could never abide anyone disgraced. He adored my wife and our son, but toward me he held a pitying disdain that amused me deeply. He loathed poverty and admired success above all. Any deviation from convention frightened him; truth-telling made him pale. Yet despite everything I did to shock him, he continued to visit Theo and the child—and even me, offering a solemn nod when we met. He saw me not as a man, but as a broken creature beyond redemption.

Still, I pressed on. The repayment of Harry’s debt gave me breathing room. I could live, at least for a season. How strange youth seems now! How bold we were, so poor and yet so unshaken.

Then came the death of His Majesty’s royal uncle, the Duke of Cumberland. Strangely, his passing marked a turning point in my fortune. Sir Miles Warrington, ever eager for courtly affairs, missed no ceremony. He attended christenings, balls, funerals—any event where nobility gathered. When the duke died, he insisted on attending the funeral, standing bareheaded in the rain. He caught a chill, developed throat inflammation, and soon after, a lawyer arrived at my lodgings in Bloomsbury—bearing a formal salutation: “Sir George Warring-ton.”

The storm had passed. The rocks were avoided. The ship had found harbor. We laid Sir Miles to rest beside his young son in the family churchyard, where generations of our name lie buried. Little Miles and I were chief mourners. The tenants bowed low and curtsied, eager to win favor from the new master. The dowager and her daughter retreated to Bath. I took possession of the house—a home I would rule for thirty years.

“Do not hurry, my son,” I once said. “Have patience. One day, I too shall sleep beneath those yew trees, and the people will cheer for Sir Miles.”

The records of a peaceful country life are simple. The steward’s ledgers show rents paid and forgiven, crops grown in rotation. Visitors who came, how long they stayed. The pensioners my wife cared for, their illnesses, their deaths. The year I served as sheriff, the hunts near our gates—all these are written in our household journals, waiting for any heir who chooses to read.

We couldn’t afford the grand mansion in Hill Street my predecessor had occupied. Instead, we chose a smaller house, though we spent more on it. We made less display—no liveries, no lavish carriages, no silver plate like my uncle’s. But our beer was stronger, and my wife’s charities more generous than the dowager’s ever were. No doubt she believed there was no harm in spoiling the Philistines. She charged exorbitant prices for the goods left behind—potted plants in the greenhouse, a dreadful old spinet in the drawing-room, framed needlework by Dora and Flora, which she valued as if they were Titian masterpieces. Still, I paid without complaint. We were kinder to the poor than she had been. Yet for a time, we earned the worst reputation in the county. Whispers followed me—stories of cruelty, of mismanagement.

I had hoped to succeed my uncle in Parliament. But I discovered, somehow, that I was seen as dangerously radical. I refused to bribe. I refused to force my tenants to vote for me in the election of ’68. A man from Whitehall arrived with a pocketful of banknotes—proof I had no chance against my opponent.

Ah, well! Now that we were secure, now that tenants bowed before us and we rode in the great family coach with four fat horses—did we not sometimes miss poverty? Did I not long for the dear little cottage at Lambeth, where Want always lingered at the door? Did I not wish to return to bear-leading, or to translate books for publishers—work I once thought drudgery?

When we visited London, we made sentimental pilgrimages to our old haunts. My wife embraced every landlady she remembered. We invited friends from those days to share our new life. The Reverend Mr. Hagan and his wife came more than once. His sermon at B------ caused quite a scandal. Sampson, another unfortunate Levite, was welcomed as long as he wished to stay. Mr. Johnson mentioned coming—but delayed, then disappeared. Perhaps our house was dull. I know I grew silent for days. My moodiness must have tried even the sweetest woman in the world.

I cared little for field sports. Killing one partridge seemed no different from another. I marveled how men could spend days chasing such slaughter. Their fox-hunting tales began at four o’clock, lasting until supper. I sat silent. I listened. Day after day, I fell asleep. No wonder I was unpopular.

What confession am I making? Here, the storm is past. The ship has reached port. And yet, am I truly content? Was Susan—the woman I longed for across the sea—really the beauty I expected? Let her look in William’s logbook. I won’t write secrets there. No, Susan, I never kept secrets from you. I never loved another. I’ve seen greater beauty, but none who suited me as you did. I’ve met Mrs. Carter, Miss Mulso, Mrs. Thrale, Madam Kaufmann, the angelic Gunnings, the Duchess of Devonshire—and many others far less divine. But none dazzled me.

Yes, young folks, I may have made your mother’s life weary. I may have been a Bluebeard—though I had no other heads in the closet. But once the crown was placed upon my head, I soon grew tired of wearing it. The captain wears it differently. He rides five days a week, finds sport thrilling. He’d hear the same sermon fifty times and not yawn. But I—sweet Joan, beloved Baucis—your faithful husband and true lover—am ashamed of being testy. Arrived at happiness, Philemon asks pardon for falling asleep so often after dinner.

There came a time when I reached the peak of joy—and found myself bored. I yawned in Eden. “Is this all?” I asked. “No lions to bite? No rain to fall? No thorns in the rosebush? Only Eve, forever sweet, and figs for breakfast, dinner, supper—week after week?”

Shall I confess? Listen.

I understand the rites and practices of the Roman Church. But raised in a different faith—and skeptical by nature—I cannot help questioning whether Catholics truly confess all. Do Protestants? Has education made them so different? Among us, we don’t assume Catholic priests or laymen are more open than ourselves. Which brings me back: does any man confess all?

Does the woman beside me know everything about my life? She has shared it for thirty years. Whenever I’ve spoken of sorrow, she’s soothed it with gentle grace. When I’ve been silent, she’s remained still—or worn smiles, pretending ease, so I might believe she felt no care. Oh, the dear hypocrite! Have I not watched her hide the boys’ mischief from me? Have I not known she secretly spent from the household to cover their small debts? She’d talk to me with an artless face, as if unaware our revered captain had dealings with gentlemen in Duke’s Place, or our learned collegian had urgent reasons to wear oak at term’s end against university tradesmen.

From the very beginning, you wise woman, you’ve concealed things from me—this one stealing jam, that one failing at school, that rebellious boy throwing an inkstand at mamma in rage, while I was told only the gown and carpet were ruined by accident.

We all hide. We all carry secrets. We are all alone. We sin in silence—and, let us hope, repent.

She would give her foot to spare mine a twinge of gout. But when I suffer, the pain is in my slipper.

At the end of novels or plays, heroes marry or die—and the poet huzzas, or shovels them into the grave. But when Mr. Random and Mr. Thomas Jones wed, is it truly over? Are there no quarrels at home? No Lady Bellastons abroad? No constables to outrun? No temptations to conquer—or be conquered by?

The Sirens sang after Ulysses long after his marriage. The suitors whispered in Penelope’s ear. They endured doubt and fear—and so do we all.

Money troubles ended with my inheritance. But does Atra Cura sit behind baronets as well as equites? My London friends congratulated me on my happiness. Who wouldn’t want a good house, a good estate? But can Gumbo shut the hall door on blue devils? Can claret drown them forever? Does a man sleep better with twenty-four hours to doze? Do his thoughts brighten after a sermon from the dull vicar, ten minutes of flattery from the village apothecary, or the conversation of Sir John and Sir Thomas with their ladies, who travel muddy miles to eat a haunch and play a rubber?

It’s pleasant to have tradesmen bow at your carriage door, a place at quarter-sessions, your wife taken down second or third to dinner. But pleasures fade. They bring inconveniences.

For seven years after we came to Warrington Manor, our two best neighbors were Lord Tutbury and Sir John Mudbrook. We are older than the Mudbrooks—so Lady Tutbury fell to my lot when we dined together. She was deaf and slept after dinner. If I had Lady Mudbrook, she chattered incessantly, so foolishly that even my wife—consummate hypocrite as she is—could barely keep her composure, knowing I seethed.

In London, I consulted Dr. Heberden. I listed my complaints. “Psha, my dear Sir George!” he said. “Headaches, languor, bad sleep, bad temper—” (“Not bad temper,” my wife interjected. “Sir George has the sweetest temper in the world—only sometimes melancholy.”) “Bad sleep, bad temper,” the doctor repeated. “My dear lady, his inheritance has been his ruin. A little poverty and much work would do him more good than all this.”

No, Harry should have been squire—then my son Miles. Harry’s letters were full of joy. His estate thrived. His slaves multiplied. His crops were bountiful. He was a member of the House of Burgesses. He adored his wife. If only he could have a child, his happiness would be complete.

Had Hal been master here, he would have been loved throughout the land. He’d have been steward at races, the liveliest hunter, welcome at every mansion where my glum face was scarcely greeted.

As for my wife—everyone liked her. Everyone pitied her. Somehow, the rumor spread: I treated her cruelly, jealous as Bluebeard. Ah, me. It is true—I have had dark hours. Days of silence. Conversations with fools make me irritable. When I feel contempt, I often fail to conceal it.

I hope I grow more charitable with age. Because I do not love bawling after a fox like the captain, I am not his superior—but I humbly admit he is mine in that regard. He feels joys I cannot understand. Because I am blind, the world is not dark.

Now I try to listen when Squire Codgers speaks of the hunt. I strive to laugh at Captain Rattleton’s stories. I step up to the harpsichord with old Miss Humby and pretend to enjoy her ancient songs. I play whist, laboriously. Am I not doing my duty? And may I not be garrulous and egotistical, having read Montaigne all morning?

I was not surprised, knowing Harry’s inclinations, to find his name among the Virginia burgesses who declared that the sole right to tax the colony rests legally and constitutionally with the House of Burgesses—and called upon other colonies to pray for royal intervention in defense of American rights.

And it was then, after three years in our English home, that correspondence began between us and Madam Esmond. It was my wife who, with some feminine pretext, rekindled the connection. Our son Miles contracted smallpox—miraculously recovering without losing his beauty. Upon his recovery, his grandmother wrote the most charming letter to the child’s grandmother, filled with modest phrases and humble goodwill. She recounted anecdotes of the boy’s precocious genius (what became of that brilliant mind, I wonder?) and sent a lock of his hair—surely smuggled past Williamsburg customs.

In return, a gracious letter came from Lady Castlewood. She lamented the dangerous spirit spreading in the colony. She feared her son was associating with rebels. She could not blame him, given his advisers. How could a wife from a menial background sympathize with noble persons devoted to the Crown? If monarchy faced hard times—and the Americans refused taxes, demanding everything free—she recalled how the Esmonds, especially her father the Marquis, had always stood loyal.

She didn’t know what opinions were fashionable in England—though she might judge from my admiration for Lord Chatham. She prayed at least one of her sons was not on the side of rebellion.

Years later, reviewing old papers in Virginia, we found “Letters from my daughter Lady Warrington,” tied with ribbon. Theo insisted I not open them. The truth, I think, is they praised her husband so lavishly, she feared my vanity might suffer.

When Madam began writing, she shared brief news of Harry and his wife. “The two women,” she wrote, “still govern everything at Fannystown—how he calls his house. They must save money; I hear their hospitality is shabby. The Mount Vernon gentleman remains his close friend, and he votes as advised. Why he is so stingy, I cannot understand. Of five negroes sent with his equipages to Lord Bottetourt’s, only two wore shoes. I saved, because I had sons to provide for. He has no children—spared grief, though Heaven surely means our good through the trials it sends via our children.”

She added: “His mother-in-law has been ill. Since his marriage, poor Henry has become the puppet of these two clever women. They rule him entirely. Nothing, my dear daughter, is more contrary to sense and Scripture than this. Are we not told, ‘Wives, obey your husbands’? Had Mr. Warrington lived, I would have enforced that sacred law. Nothing becomes a woman more than humility and obedience.”
