While we struggled to recover from our terror and examined the gruesome remains of the Place of Death, Gagool was busy with her own sinister agenda. With surprising agility for someone of her age, she scrambled onto the massive table and approached the body of our fallen comrade, Twala, lying beneath the dripping ceiling. Whether she intended to inspect his decomposition or served some darker ritualistic purpose, she bent down to kiss his frozen lips with feigned affection before hobbling back. Pausing intermittently to mutter unintelligible phrases toward the shrouded figures nearby, she completed this macabre ceremony and settled directly beneath the "White Death" statue. There, she began to pour forth what sounded like fervent prayers—or perhaps curses directed at the ultimate adversary of humanity.

The sight of this vile creature beseeching evil powers sent us scrambling to finish our investigation.

"Now, Gagool," I whispered, unable to raise my voice above a murmur in that oppressive atmosphere, "guide us to the chamber."

The witch descended instantly. "Are my lords not afraid?" she leered, grinning up at me.

"Lead on," I commanded.

"Very well, my lords," she replied, circling to the rear of the great statue. "Here lies the entrance. Light your lamps and enter." She set a gourd of oil on the floor and leaned casually against the cave wall.

I struck a match from our dwindling supply, igniting a rush-wick, and scanned the area for a doorway. Instead, I found only solid rock. Gagool chuckled. "The way is there, my lords. *Ha! Ha! Ha!*"

"Do not joke with us!" I warned sternly.

"I jest not, my lords. Behold!" she declared, pointing to the rock face.

As she spoke, raising our lamp, we witnessed a miracle of engineering: a massive slab of stone rose slowly from the floor, sliding upward into a cavity concealed within the rock above. The monolith was roughly ten feet high, five feet thick, and wide enough for a large door, weighing an estimated twenty to thirty tons. It operated on a simple counter-weight system, much like a modern window sash. We did not witness the mechanism's trigger, as Gagool wisely avoided revealing it, but I suspected a subtle lever hidden somewhere, activated by slight pressure, which tipped the balance and lifted the stone.

Slowly, silently, the barrier vanished, revealing a dark maw leading into the depths. Our excitement was palpable; trembling with anticipation, we wondered if this was a final hoax or if Da Silvestra's warnings of immense wealth were true. Could vast hoards await us here, making us the wealthiest men on Earth? We would know shortly.

"Enter, white men from the Stars," Gagoo called out, stepping into the darkness. "But first, listen to your servant, Gagool the aged. The brilliant stones you will encounter were excavated from the pit guarded by the Silent Ones and stored here, though I know not by whom, for this predates my memory. Yet, this chamber has been breached only once since those ancient stowaways fled in haste, abandoning their treasures. Rumors of the riches spread through the ages, but no one knew the location or the secret of the door until a white man arrived from over the mountains—perhaps another traveler 'from the Stars.' He was welcomed by the King of that era."

She gestured to the fifth figure seated at the table of the dead. "It came to pass that he and a local woman journeyed here. By chance, the woman discovered the secret of the door—a mystery that would elude a thousand years of searching. Together, they entered, found the stones, and filled a small goat-skin, meant for food, with the gems. As he departed, he seized one final, large stone in his hand."

She paused, sensing our rapt attention. "Well," I breathed, "what befell Da Silvestra?"

The old hag recoiled at the name. "How dost thou know the dead man's name?" she snapped, then continued without waiting for an answer. "No one knows the outcome. However, the white man became terrified. He discarded the goat-skin laden with stones and fled, clutching only the single stone in his hand. The King claimed that stone, and it is the very gem you took from Twala's brow, Macumazahn."

"Has anyone entered since?" I asked, peering into the abyss.

"No, my lords. Only the secret of the door has remained intact. Every King has opened it, yet none have entered. There is a prophecy: those who step inside shall die within a moon, just as the white man perished in the mountain cave where you found him, Macumazahn. Therefore, the Kings keep their distance. *Ha! Ha!* These are my true words."

Our eyes locked, and a chill ran through me. How did this crone possess such forbidden knowledge?

"Enter, my lords," she urged mockingly. "If I speak truth, the goat-skin lies upon the floor. If death awaits those who enter, you shall learn that soon. *Ha! Ha! Ha!*"

With a lantern in hand, she limped through the doorway. Hesitating briefly, I was spurred onward by Good's impatience. "Confound it all!" he shouted. "I'm not letting this old devil scare me." Followed closely by Foulata, who trembled violently despite her courage, he plunged into the tunnel. We followed suit.

A few yards down the narrow, rock-hewn passage, Gagool halted. "Behold, my lords," she illuminated with her light. "Those who hid the treasure fled in haste, lacking time to seal the way completely." She indicated large square blocks of stone laid across the passage, forming a partial wall. Beside them sat more blocks, alongside a heap of mortar and two trowels—tools strikingly similar to those used by laborers today.

Foulata, overcome by fear and fatigue, collapsed. "I feel faint... I cannot go further," she whispered. We lowered her onto the unfinished wall beside her basket of provisions and waited while she recovered.

Proceeding another fifteen paces, we encountered an elaborately painted wooden door, standing ajar. Someone had either forgotten to close it or lacked the time. Lying across the threshold was a goatskin bag, bulging with pebbles.

"Hee! Hee!" Gagool snickered as the lamplight caught the bag. "Did I not tell you the white man fled in panic and dropped the woman's bag? Behold! Look within; you will find a water-gourd among the stones."

Good knelt, lifting the heavy, jingling sack. "By Jove! It feels full of diamonds," he murmured in awe. Indeed, the image of a tiny leather pouch stuffed with diamonds was enough to stun anyone.

"Hurry," Sir Henry urged impatiently. Taking the lamp from Gagool, he stepped through the doorway, holding it high. We pressed in behind him, momentarily forgetting the diamond bag, and found ourselves in King Solomon's treasure chamber.

Initially, the dim light revealed merely a ten-foot-square room carved from living rock. Then, stacked against the arched ceiling, came into view a magnificent array of elephant tusks. Hundreds of them, pristine and gleaming, lined the walls. Alone, this stockpile represented a fortune for a lifetime. Perhaps this was the source of Solomon's legendary "throne of ivory."

On the opposite wall stood approximately twenty wooden crates, resembling oversized ammunition boxes, painted red.

"There are the diamonds!" I cried. "Bring the light."

Sir Henry complied, illuminating the top crate. Its lid, rotted by time even in this dry environment, had been smashed inward—likely by Da Silvestra himself. Reaching through the breach, I pulled out handfuls not of diamonds, but of gold coins of an unknown design, stamped with Hebrew-like characters.

"Aha!" I replaced the coin. "We leave not empty-handed. There must be two thousand pieces per crate, and eighteen crates total. Likely payment for workers and merchants."

"Well," Good interjected, "that seems to be the extent of it. I see no diamonds, unless the old Portuguese pocketed them all in his bag."

"If my lords wish to find the stones, look where it is darkest," Gagool advised, reading our expressions. "Yonder lies a niche containing three stone chests; two sealed, one open."

Before addressing Sir Henry, I couldn't suppress my curiosity. "How do you know this, if no one has entered since the white man centuries ago?"

"Ah, Macumazahn, the Night Watchman," she mocked. "Dwellers of the stars, do you not know that some live long, and some possess eyes that pierce through rock? *Ha! Ha! Ha!*"

"Look in that corner, Curtis," I instructed, pointing to the spot she indicated.

"Hullo, fellows," Curtis called out. "Here's a recess. Great heavens, look here."

We rushed to the alcove, shaped like a small bow window. Against the back wall sat three stone chests, each about two feet square. Two were capped with stone lids; the third lay open, its lid propped against the side.

"See!" Curtis rasped, holding the lamp over the open chest.
