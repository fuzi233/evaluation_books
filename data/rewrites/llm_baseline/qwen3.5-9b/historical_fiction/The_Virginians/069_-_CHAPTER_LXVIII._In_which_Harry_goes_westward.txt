Human hearts recoil from the thought of parting; thus, I shall omit any description of Harry Warrington's sorrow as he prepares to leave his brother and friends. Are there not thousands of men in precisely the same predicament? Did not Mr. Wolfe have a mother to kiss—his brave father having passed away during his son's absence on the glorious campaign at Louisbourg—and a sweetheart to hold in a final embrace? Did not the sturdy Admiral Holmes, before sailing westward with his formidable squadron—the *Somerset*, the *Terrible*, the *Northumberland*, the *Royal William*, the *Trident*, the *Diana*, and the *Seahorse*—take leave of Mrs. Holmes and her daughters, with his own flag flying proudly from the *Dublin*? Was even Admiral Saunders, who departed the following day, immune to human feeling?

Away went William and his crew of jovial sailors, cutting through the tumbling waves, while poor Black-eyed Susan remained on shore, watching the vessel shrink until it vanished into the sunset. It dwindled toward the West. Night fell darkly over the ocean. They were gone, yet their hearts lingered at home for a while longer. In silence, with spirits inexpressibly soft and tender, how each man must have thought of those left behind! What a chorus of pitiful prayers rose to the Father, both at sea and on shore, on that night of departure: by the vacant bedside where a wife knelt in tears; around the hearth where mothers and children poured out their supplications; or on the deck, where the sailor gazed upward at the stars as his ship cleaved through the roaring midnight waters.

Tomorrow, the sun would rise upon ordinary life again, and we would resume our daily tasks of toil and duty. George accompanied his brother, staying with him at Portsmouth while they waited for a favorable wind. He shook Mr. Wolfe's hand, looked at his pale face one last time, and watched the ships depart amidst the clamor of bells and the thunder of cannon from the shore. The next day, George returned home to the business that is surely one of the world's most selfish and absorbing pursuits—a trade to which nearly every man of thirty has apprenticed himself by this time.

He felt a pang of sadness as he glanced into the lodgings at the little room Harry had occupied, seeing his half-burned papers still lying in the grate. Soon, however, he was on his way to Dean Street again, whispering words of comfort to his clinging sweetheart by the fitful firelight. She was exceedingly happy—oh, so happy! at his return, yet ashamed of her joy. Was it not heartless to be so glad when poor Hetty was so melancholy? Poor little Hetty! Indeed, it seemed selfish to rejoice while she suffered. It made one quite wretched to behold her distress. "Don't, sir!" Theo exclaimed. "Well, I ought to be wretched, and it is very, very wicked of me if I am not." One could understand her soft-hearted repentance. What she meant by "Don't" remains unclear; I had noted earlier that the room was dark and the fire burned fitfully—"Don't" was likely whispered during one of those shadowy moments.

Servants entered with supper and lights. The family arrived, and conversation became general. The destination of the fleet was now known throughout the realm. The force aboard was deemed sufficient to defeat the French in Canada, and under an officer of Wolfe's caliber, to correct the blunders and disasters of previous campaigns. He looked dreadfully ill, indeed, but possessed a great soul within a feeble body. Ministers and the nation pinned their highest hopes upon him.

After supper, as was customary, Mr. Lambert assembled his modest household, of whom George Warrington formed a significant part. As he prayed for all travelers by land and water, Theo and her sister knelt together. Thus, as the ship sped farther and farther into the West, these fond thoughts pursued it; the night passed, and the sun rose. Within a day or two, everyone returned to their books or usual work. As for George Warrington, that celebrated dramatist, he was already busy composing another piece.

When the tragedy of *Carpezan* had run some thirty or forty nights, other persons of genius took possession of the theater. There may have been those who marveled how the town could grow so fickle as to tire of such a masterpiece, unable to bear seeing actors in different costumes reciting others' verses. But George, of a skeptical turn of mind, accepted the fate of his tragedy with philosophical calm, pocketing the proceeds with quiet satisfaction. From Mr. Dodsley, the bookseller, he received the usual sum of one hundred pounds; from the theater manager, two hundred or more; and with such praise from critics and friends, he set to work preparing a new piece, hoping to achieve even greater success.

Over these studies and other charming occupations, months slipped away. What happy business! The happiest time of youth and life, when love is first spoken and returned; when dearest eyes shine daily with welcome, and fondest lips never tire of whispering sweet secrets; when the parting glance accompanying "Good night!" offers delightful promise of tomorrow; when the heart overflows with love and happiness, leaving something to spare for all the world; when days close with glad prayers and open with joyful hopes; when doubt seems cowardice, misfortune impossible, and poverty merely a sweet trial of constancy!

Theo's elders, thankfully recalling their own prime, sat softly by to witness this pretty comedy performed by their young people. In one of his later letters, dutifully written to his wife during a temporary absence, George recorded how he had looked up at the windows of the dear old house in Dean Street, wondering who sat in the chamber where he and Theo had been so happy.

Meanwhile, we can learn how time passed and what occupied our friends through excerpts from George's letters to his brother:

*'Why have you returned to rugged rocks, bleak shores, burning summers, and nipping winters, when you might have been cropping countless laurels in Germany?
