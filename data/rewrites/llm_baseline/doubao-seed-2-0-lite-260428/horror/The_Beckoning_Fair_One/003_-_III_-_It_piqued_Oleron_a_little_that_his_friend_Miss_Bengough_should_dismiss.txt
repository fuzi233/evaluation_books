It niggled at Oleron, sharp as a splinter under the skin, that his old friend Elsie Bengough should dismiss the house he’d come to think of as his private, secret miracle with nothing more than a flick of her fair lashes. She barely lifted her eyes to the high carved cornices, to the way afternoon light pooled thick as cream on the floorboards where no one else had trod for decades. But that was Elsie all over: indifferent to quiet graces, careless of fripperies, far more at home gnawing biscuits straight from a crinkly paper bag than sitting through stuffy dinner parties where everyone pretended to care about social niceties. At thirty-four, she was an unapologetically freelance journalist, broad-shouldered and blooming, butter-gold hair, cheeks flushed the soft pink of a wild dog-rose, like a prize bloom plump straight from a florist’s display. She moved in sweeping, unselfconscious gestures, her voice booming warm and sudden, trailing veils and scarves that left little whirlwinds of displaced air spinning in her wake. She “hauled a better living out of the print pool” as she liked to say, than Oleron ever had, penning sly glowing little mentions of drapers and haberdashers in the society pages in exchange for nearly the entirety of her vast, ever-changing wardrobe.

Oleron heard the rustle of her skirts on his creaking staircase, the single loud unapologetic knock at his door, exactly one month to the day after he’d moved in. She brought the sharp cold tang of the city’s autumn streets in with her, dumping a stack of glossy ladies’ journals unceremoniously on his best chair.
“Don’t stop work for me,” she mumbled around a mouthful of giant hatpins, working them loose one by one to yank off her heavy fur hat and trailing veil. “I had no idea if you were even settled in yet, so I brought ham sandwiches for lunch. You’ve got coffee, right? No, don’t you dare get up – I know where kitchens are, I’ll rummage around and find the kettle myself.”
“Honestly, I’m glad for the interruption,” Oleron told her, half-relieved, gathering up the half-scrawled, half-blank pages of his manuscript scattered across his desk to tuck them out of sight. He’d been staring at the same half-written line for the better part of three hours, words slipping through his fingers like water every time he tried to pin them down.

She was already in his tiny kitchen; he could hear her twisting the tap on, water gurgling into the dented old kettle. He joined her, and ten minutes later they were back in the sitting room, coffee and sandwiches balanced on a rickety little table between them.
“So,” Elsie said, pouring him a cup that steamed thickly, “what do you think of this new hole of yours? Anybody’d swear you were about to get hitched, Paul, the way you’ve been gushing about it.”
He huffed a laugh. “Hardly. But it’s a sight better than the last few dumps I lived in, isn’t it?”
“Is it? I guess. I liked the last one, even with the soot-black ceiling and no running tap. How’s *Romilly* coming along?”
Oleron rubbed at the stubble on his chin, the empty leaden weight in his chest he’d been pretending wasn’t there for weeks. “Ah. I’m almost too embarrassed to tell you. I haven’t exactly made great headway. But it’ll all sort itself out, like you always say the night before a deadline.”
“Stuck?”
“More than stuck.”
“Got any bits you’d let me read?”

For years, he’d made a habit of reading snippets of his work out loud to Elsie. Her feedback was sharp, unvarnished, practical, sometimes directly useful, sometimes sparking ideas he’d never have landed on alone. She never let him see a line of her own work, though – said his was “real, proper writing,” while hers was just filler, barely even grammatically correct half the time.
“I don’t have anything new to show you,” he admitted, still stroking his chin, then blurted the truth before he could stop himself. “Elsie, I haven’t written a single new line of it, not for weeks. But – it’s not like I haven’t been thinking. I’ve been thinking so hard it’s almost alarming. I’m planning to rebuild the whole story from the ground up.”
Elsie gasped so loud her coffee sloshed over the edge of the cup. “Rebuild it?!”
“Make Romilly a different kind of woman entirely. I swear, I’m not pulling every last bit of potential out of her the way she is now. I’ve lost almost all interest in the version of her I had written already.”
“But Paul,” she protested, round-eyed, “she was so real you could almost touch her! You made her breathe.”
Oleron smiled faintly. He’d fully expected her to hate the idea. Of course she’d love the old Romilly – half the fictional woman was based on Elsie herself, whether she’d ever noticed it or not. Of course she’d think that version was alive.
“Are you actually serious about this?” she asked, after a long pause.
“Dead serious.”
“You’re going to scrap those fifteen whole chapters? That lush gorgeous love scene? That bit where she’s standing on the shore, the wind tangling her hair, you wrote it so well I could taste the salt?”
Oleron shifted in his seat, suddenly uneasy, the faint cloying rose-mold scent in the air thickening all at once. “They wouldn’t have to go to waste, exactly.”
Elsie threw up her hands in that big dramatic gusty gesture of hers, and let loose. “You are impossibly infuriating sometimes! I wish you’d remember you’re just a human man who lives in the real world! I’d never ask you to drop your standards an inch, but this isn’t raising them – it’s making the book so obtuse no normal person will ever care to parse it. You’re being far too much of a self-absorbed god about this. Destroying those fifteen chapters would be a criminal waste of the talent you’ve spent twenty years nurturing. You’re almost to the finish line, Paul. I don’t need you to tell me your bank account’s running dry, I can read the papers the same as anyone else. This book was supposed to make your name, and now you’re throwing it away to write something ten to one no one will ever want to publish? You’re testing every last scrap of patience I have.”

Oleron shook his head slowly. This was an old familiar fight between them. The loud brilliant practical journalist was a wonderful friend, up to a very specific point – past that point, every last one of us is entirely alone. Elsie liked to say if she had a tenth of Oleron’s talent she could conquer the entire industry, like talent was some kind of measurable powder you could spoon out into recipes. She never understood that it wasn’t something you could split, or add, or subtract – it was something that lived in your bones, indivisible, and no amount of sharp practicality could manufacture. Their two spirits diverged right there, and Oleron knew it. She never seemed to.
“Alright, alright,” he said, a little tired. “You’re completely right, every word. If I could hand *Romilly* over to you, you’d turn it into a massive bestseller. But I can’t. And for my part? I’m starting to seriously wonder if she’s even worth my time at all. You know what that means.”
“What does it mean?” she demanded bluntly.
He smiled, thin and weary. “When you’re certain a thing isn’t worth doing? You stop doing it.”
Elsie rolled her eyes up at the water-stained ceiling, as if begging it for patience with this impossible man. “That’s utter garbage! The last time I saw you, you were practically dripping *Romilly*. You were cranking out four chapters a week. If you’d never moved into this weird old house you’d be three quarters done by now. What possessed you to pick up and relocate right in the middle of the most important work of your life?”

Oleron tried to brush her off, making jokes about the drafty old lodgings he’d lived in before, but she wouldn’t buy it. He was twenty years deep in garrets, and roof-chambers, and dingy little flats, and he was bone tired of the shabbiness. The reward he’d chased for decades was as far out of reach as it ever was – and lately, he didn’t even have the urge to reach for it. Telling a man at the end of his rope to just make one more effort does nothing if he no longer has the strength to move.
“Anyway,” he said, wrapping the argument up, “I’m happier here than I’ve been in ten years. That’s justification enough, isn’t it?”
“And you’re doing no work,” she said, sharp as a tack.
That was the last straw, and the petty irritation he’d been holding back spilled over. “Why shouldn’t I get to be happy instead of working every waking minute? What has all this work ever gotten me? I don’t claim I don’t love it, once it’s done – but the act of writing it? It’s a burden, an intolerable weight I want to throw off half the time. Once every few weeks I get that one spark, that thrill of creation, and I remember when the whole of it used to feel that way. I’m forty-four now. It’s drudgery. No one buys it, I don’t even want to write it anymore, and if any normal sensible man asked me if I wasn’t a fool to keep going, I’d agree with him.”

Elsie’s pink blooming face went soft and serious. “But you knew all that decades ago, Paul. You chose this life.”
“Did I, though?” he snapped back. “I was told I knew. My heart, if you want to call it that, told me, and youth always thinks it knows everything. Then one day you wake up and you’re almost fifty –”
“Forty-four, Paul.”
“Forty-four, then. And you find out all the magic you thought was waiting for you up ahead was all behind you. I thought I knew what I was choosing, but no one tells you how expensive that choice is, when you’re young and stupid.”
Elsie stared down at the scuffed floorboards, her voice quiet. “You’re not regretting it, are you?”
“Am I not?” he said, and the words were out before he could think. “Lately? I absolutely think I am. What have I ever gotten in return for all this sacrifice?”
“You know exactly what you’ve gotten,” she said, soft, and suddenly he realized he could hear what she wasn’t saying. That if he’d asked her to marry him any time in the last ten years, she would have said yes, no questions, no hesitation. He’d never once thought to ask. “Your work is real. People like me, the hacks, we couldn’t do our jobs without people like you. You and a handful of others carry all the real art on your shoulders.”

They sat in silence for a long minute, and Oleron’s face heated up. He was never the type to mope and grumble like this. He stood up abruptly, stacking the coffee cups back onto the tray.
“Sorry you had to catch me in such a sorry mood, Elsie,” he laughed, a little shaky. “I’ll carry these out to the kitchen, then we can go for a walk, if you like, get some fresh air.”
He dumped the tray in the kitchen sink, then led her on a tour of the rest of the empty flat. She barely commented on any of it, until they got back to the small back bedroom where the window seat sat, its old cushion still propped against the wall. She pointed at the faded reddish scrap of frieze Oleron had found tucked under the seat the first week he moved in, that Mrs. Barrett used as a little footrest for her old wooden chair.
“That thing? I’ve been trying to figure out what it even is,” Oleron told her, unfolding it for her to see, recounting how he’d dug it out from under the splintered old wood of the window seat.
Elsie’s face went pale, suddenly. “I know what that is. It’s wrapping cloth, for a small harp. People used to sew frieze linings to wrap their harps in before tucking them away in their cases to keep the wood from scratching.”
“By God, that makes sense,” Oleron said, a little taken aback. He’d never thought of that. The faint distant harp note he swore he heard drifting through the empty halls after dark made a horrible new kind of sense all at once.

They wandered back to the sitting room, and Elsie leaned back against the mantel. “Who else lives in the rest of the big empty house, then?”
“Hardly anyone. A tramp sometimes crashes in the cellar, I think. No one else.”
“Mm. Let me tell you what I think of this place, Paul.”
“Go on.”
“You will never finish *Romilly* here. You will never write a single good thing in this house. I can’t explain why, but I know it. You’re going to have to leave, before you ever get that book done.”
He blinked, thrown off. “That’s a little prejudiced, isn’t it? There’s no logic to that at all.”
“Not a single leg to stand on, logically,” she agreed, already shoving the big hatpins back in her hat to fasten it in place.
Oleron reached up to the hook by the door to grab his own hat and coat, laughing to play off the cold prickle that had just crawled down his spine. He could swear, for half a second, he felt a warm soft breath against the back of his neck, just over his collar, as if something standing behind him was reading the words off Elsie’s lips.
“I do hope you’re completely wrong,” he said, and his voice shook just a tiny bit, too quiet for her to notice. “If *Romilly* isn’t out by autumn, I’m going to be in a very serious mess indeed.”
