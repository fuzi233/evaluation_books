From our vantage point just below the crest of the Sacred Mountain, the city of Atlantis spread out far beneath us like a hand-drawn battle map. Its harbour and the wide silver estuary glinted at the far edge of the plain, the ring of smouldering hills beyond puffing their familiar plumes of smoke and sulphur into the dusk, and the great weathered stone circle of our Lord the Sun jutted stark and bare from the city’s centre. Closer still rose the massive bulk of the royal pyramid, its gilded outer tiers catching the last of the day’s light to glow like stacked ingots of raw sunfire. And there, in the wide open square before the pyramid, the great granite throne sat, shrunk by distance to the size of a molehill—the very tomb where the woman I loved had slept sealed away for nine long years.

Old Zaemon followed my gaze, his weathered priest’s face softening with a quiet sigh. “Aye. I know exactly where your thoughts have run. Deucalion, when he first landed fresh from the wars in Yucatan, was a hard, unbreakable man. The king we’ve crowned for this new realm—for all his virtues—carries soft spots you never did.”
“Soft spots make no weakness here,” I shot back. “Give me Nais, alive and warm at my side to fight for, and I will be ten times the cold, unfeeling viceroy and soldier you once knew.”
“I gave my word to that already, and you shall have her,” Zaemon said, his tone hardening. “But this work will shake the foundations of our new kingdom to its roots. We might even rid ourselves of Phorenice and her whole poisonous court in the process—though I won’t count on it. She is too wily, too sharp. The second we set our plan in motion she will guess exactly who is behind it, how it will end, and slip away before a single hand can close around her. The High Gods, who sent all these trials to temper our people, saw fit to grant her a rare, dangerous knowledge of how the earth’s deep tremors can be roused to do her bidding.”
“I’ve seen her juggle those tremors like a child’s toy,” I said. “Tell me your scheme.”
“All will be shown to you soon enough. For now, sleep. Tonight, you will slip back down into the city, collect your woman—my daughter—when the tomb breaks open, and bring her back up here as best you can. For that, you will need every scrap of strength a man can muster.” He stepped back, raking his eyes over the shaggy, dust-caked figure I’d become in our months hiding in the high peaks, and huffed a rare, dry laugh. “Not a soul in that city would take you for the neat, clean-shaven king Deucalion now. You are hairy enough, ragged enough, naked and grime-caked enough to pass for some feral savage fresh off a raider ship from Europe. That is your armour tonight, when we wake the city with earth-shocks and terror. Only pray no greedy citizen takes a fancy to your broad thews and tries to drag you home as his personal servant.”
“I pity that man his household the second he tries.”
Zaemon chuckled again, a rough, rare sound. “By the old gods, so do I.”
His face fell grave as fast as it had lightened—laughter was a stranger to old Zaemon. “Go rest now, my King. I am off to the Ark of Mysteries, to work the rites that will rouse the deep fires with one of the other Three. Handling those powers beneath the earth is no trivial work. There is as much danger for us as there is for you. If we do not meet again until we stand behind the stars, giving account of our lives to the High Gods—fare you well, Deucalion.”

I slept that day like a soldier before a battle, dragging full rest out of every hour, no fretting thoughts allowed to gnaw at my mind. Only the weak let their sleep break when the stakes are highest. When full dark had settled over the city, I woke, roused the men holding the thick hemp rope, and let them lower me over the cliff edge. The moon was dark that night, the sky shrouded in thick cloud, the air black enough that I could not see my own hand before my face—perfect cover.
I landed soft on the jumbled boulders at the foot of the cliff, and moved with extra care: Phorenice’s sentries squatted around their campfires, restless, jumpy, as if they could feel the tremors building beneath their boots. I slipped through the deepest shadows, quiet as a hunting cat, until I reached the mouth of the old secret tunnel I had used once before to pass under the city walls. The small ritual lamp I had left there still burned, and I wound through the narrow galleries fast. The air reeked worse than I remembered, thick with hot sulphur and noxious fumes, proof that Zaemon had already begun to stir the deep earth powers.
I struggled longer than I expected to find an exit: not because anyone was patrolling the passages, but because the whole masonry layout had shifted. Once, the Priests’ Clan oversaw every architect’s plan, making sure no new construction sealed off their secret tunnels and chambers. But in Phorenice’s reign, the priests had been stripped of all power, and new builders had carelessly bricked up half the old outlets by accident. I was forced to claw my way out of the network of tunnels where I could, not where I planned, and emerged on the far eastern edge of the city, almost where the outer walls met the harbour. I closed the exit behind me, wiping away all traces of it, and froze: the ground thrummed under my bare feet like the deck of a ship in a long, slow swell. No full tremor had hit yet, the citizens slept on undisturbed in their homes—but the solid, still deadness of the earth was gone. At any second, the whole ground could rip open into devastating, rolling waves of shock.
A cold, sharp panic coiled in my chest. What if I was too late? What if Nais’s tomb broke open before I got to the square, and panicking crowds of strangers laid their rude, careless hands on her helpless sleeping body? My throat went dry at the thought.
I ran. Full tilt, through the narrow winding dark streets, no thought in my head but to reach the square before the full tremors hit. I was the one who had sealed her alive into that tomb under the granite throne nine years prior with my own two hands, and if speed and brute strength were enough, I would pull her free again with those same hands.
Hardly anyone tried to stop me. A few gutter drunks yelled “Thief!” and demanded I toss them a share of whatever plunder I was carrying, but one look at my broad, feral frame and blistering speed made any would-be pursuers think twice. Soon the ground began to buck harder, stone walls groaned and grated, showers of dust poured down from rooftops, and people poured screaming out of their doorways. None of them spared a second glance for the ragged, shaggy, dust-caked savage sprinting past, flinging mud from his bare feet as he went.
I skidded into the great square none too soon. The whole space was filling with panicking people fleeing the narrow collapsing streets, thick with darkness, dust, and the bitter stench of sulphur. The ground rippled under our feet like an open sea, each slow wave of shock growing sharper and more violent by the second.
Whole houses collapsed unseen in the gloom, adding their roar to the chaos. The old royal pyramid, master-built by the ancient architects, held firm for now: its stones were dovetailed into one another with unmatched craft, clamped tight with massive bars of solid bronze. Its builders had boasted you could dig half its foundations out from under the earth and it would still stand square and unbroken, more enduring than the hills.
Flickering torchlight showed its massive stone doors hanging open, frightened courtiers scurrying out and vanishing into the dark. I did not care about the pyramid’s fate then, did not even spare a thought for whether Phorenice would be trapped inside or escape to cause more trouble. I crouched low beside the granite throne at the centre of the square, and heard its great carved stones grate together like splintering bone as it rocked to the earth-waves.
In that dust-choked dark I could barely see my own palm held up before my face, but I swear the High Gods granted me a special sharpness of sight, a reward for nine years of aching love. I watched the great carved capstone of the throne jerk once, then again, then a third time, shifting a little to one side with every pulse of the earth. Zaemon’s rite was working exactly as planned.
I scrambled up the throne’s steps to the side where the capstone was sliding, clinging tight to its edge, burning with wild, thrumming expectation. The tremors ramped up faster and faster, the din of falling masonry and the shrieks of the wounded climbing higher into the night. The dust grew so thick I coughed and choked just breathing it, blotting out the few faint stars, wrapping the whole square in deeper black. I clung to the corner of the throne, hunkered on the step just below the capstone, barely able to keep my balance against the force of the shocks.
Still that great carved stone held my love locked in her living tomb, and I could have bitten the cold granite in my frustration at my own helplessness. The crowds flooding the square were mad with terror, and every new person who stumbled close made my situation more desperate. “Phorenice, Goddess, save us!” some wailed, and when the prayer brought no relief, they began babbling the old childhood prayers to the forgotten old gods they had spent decades spurning.
The crashes of splitting masonry grew louder, and from the royal pyramid itself, unseen through the dark, came the grinding of stone and the crack of snapping bronze bars—proof even that unmatched masterpiece of ancient engineering had a breaking point. I remembered Zaemon’s thundering threat in Phorenice’s perfumed banqueting hall, how he had screamed that she would see the pyramid she had polluted with her debaucheries torn apart tier from tier, stone from stone, scattered like feathers before a gale.
The earth’s surging grew heavier, the great square’s pavement gaped and upheaved, the crowd’s screams rising sharper as flagstones ground together and crushed their feet. Now the pyramid itself began to split, gape, and topple. The roofs of its grand chambers caved in, the tons of masonry above crashing down to bury everything inside. Thunder boomed overhead, adding its roar to the chaos, lightning tearing open the dust clouds in sudden, jagging bursts of white light.
One flash lit up Phorenice, standing calm as ever no more than a dozen paces from where I clung to the throne. She swayed her lean, supple body with the shocks like an old sailor on a ship’s deck, her face perfectly composed, her beauty untouched by the years. She brushed dust off her short curly red hair every few seconds, no flicker of fear on her face—only faint weariness, faint contempt, and a spark of amusement. Not even the crumbling of her royal capital could shake Phorenice’s nerve.
The sight of her cool indifference made my blood boil. I could have strangled her with my bare hands if she had been within reach. Crouched at her feet on the same great flagstone was Ylga, her acolyte, doubled over and green with motion sickness. I remembered Ylga was Nais’s twin sister, owed me an old kindness, but I had no time to spare for her then. The great capstone of the throne slid farther and farther out of its bed with every new tremor, my love drawing closer to me by the inch.
A knot of panicking townsfolk stumbled toward the throne, certain the massive granite structure was the only safe spot in the bucking square, dragging themselves up the steep steps on hands and knees, their feet crushed by the heaving flagstones below. I beat at them with my fists, hauling them off, hurling them back down the steps. When they yelled and demanded to know what right I had to claim the spot over them, I answered only with blows and bared teeth. I did not care what they thought of me. I only wanted them gone. They scattered wailing, screaming that I was some devil of the dark, with no fight left in them to stand their ground.
The stone slid so slow I almost went mad with frustration. All my years of learned patience vanished. I rammed my shoulder and my head against the massive block, clawing at it with my hands until the muscles on my arms knotted like thick rope, and I swear the High Gods laughed at my useless fury. Their work moved slow, but it was sure.
At last, when the whole city’s population was numb with terror, incapable of any more shock—save only Phorenice, who still stood unshaken—the promised moment came. The capstone slid all the way to the edge of its bed, balanced for one heartbeat, and the next tremor sent it roaring and clattering down to the pavement below.
A cold, sharp tremor ran through me. After nine years of waiting, after all the scheming, the risk, the pain, the tomb was open. But my guts turned over: what if Phorenice had found a way into the hidden tomb long before this? What if she had murdered Nais, left her corpse there as a grisly taunt for me to find? I could not guess, dared not imagine. I had never felt so torn apart by emotion in all my years of hard fighting.
The roar of the tremors was already slackening, as if Zaemon had called back the deep fires the second the work was done, to give the broken city a chance to breathe. I steeled myself, clambered onto the flat surface of the throne’s inner slab. The lightning had faded, the dark was thick again, but at any second the sky could light up once more, and if Phorenice caught sight of me, ragged and unkempt as I was, she would see through my disguise in a heartbeat.
My hands shook as I fumbled over the metal clamps I had fastened with my own two hands nine years before. My fingers slipped between them, and I felt the familiar curves of Nais’s still body resting right where I had left her. Wild, blazing joy exploded in my chest. Dead or alive, I had her. She was mine.
I clung to the slab with one hand as the earth still rocked, working the clamps free with the other. When the top half of the metal fastenings were loose, I stooped, lifted her soft, still body up onto the slab beside me, and pressed a hundred burning kisses to her face, hidden in the dark. I half-madly believed the sheer heat of my love would wake her right there. But she lay limp in my arms, cold as marble, no breath, no pulse.
This was no place for us. The tremors were dying, dawn was approaching, and as soon as the panicking crowds recovered their wits, every bit of work we had done would be for nothing. I slung her light weight over my shoulder, keeping my right arm free to fight if I needed to, and blundered off through the dust and dark. The old secret tunnels under the walls would all be caved in by the shocks, useless—but the crowds were all screaming the same thing: “The city is cursed! The walls will fall on us! Run for the open fields outside!”
I stumbled through the ruins, over heaps of fallen masonry, the ground tossed and tumbled like a frozen storm sea. The great outer walls of Atlantis were gone, flattened to rubble by the force of the tremors, no gates left standing. The once-great walled city was as defenceless as an open village. The soldier in me felt a faint twinge of grief for those mighty ramparts I had once defended, but it passed fast. I had a far more important love to protect now.
To anyone who stumbled too close to us, I yelled that the woman I carried had the plague, and they scattered out of my path, none daring to stop me. At last I reached the base of the Sacred Mountain cliff, where the hemp rope dangled down. The priests hauled Nais up first, then pulled me up after.
We laid her on a soft cot in a warm stone hut on the mountain, and for the first time I saw her clearly by lamplight. Her beauty was drawn thin and pale, her eyelids so thin and transparent I could almost see the brown of her pupils beneath them. Her hair had grown thick and long, spread like a soft cushion under her head. No breath stirred, no pulse beat under her skin—but she did not look like ordinary death. The woman I had sealed in the tomb nine years prior had been full-bodied, strong, half again her current weight. Her fingernails had grown long, twisted into tight spirals, her hands translucent enough that you could see the outline of bone beneath the waxy skin. Her lips were as white as snow, the familiar carmine gone, hollows of dust catching in the gaunt curves of her neck and shoulders.
I set to work at once, moving with reverent care. I heated water, filled a great wooden tub, and laced it heavily with the concentrated life essences of wild beasts the priests had stored away for emergencies, rare brews no common citizen could ever get their hands on. I lowered her into the bath chin-deep, tended the fire under it to keep the water at exact blood heat, and sat down beside her, watching, waiting, as the High Gods willed her to wake.
