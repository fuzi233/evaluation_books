Gerard and Margaret rode to Sevenbergen with light hearts, the fresh air of freedom filling their lungs after their narrow escape. The joy of liberty, however, soon gave way to heavier thoughts. Gerard was still a fugitive—a man hunted by law and vengeance—and though they had fled together, they could not stay together. He must leave, at least for a time, if he were to avoid capture and the gallows.

Margaret’s heart sank at the thought of parting. She longed for him to remain close, safe beside her in the quiet village where no one knew his name or crime. Yet when she heard of Italy—of the cities where artists were honored, where skill was rewarded—she felt a strange hope stir within her. Gerard spoke of it as destiny: that there, he would earn wealth and standing, and finally win the right to marry her without shame or fear.

He had told her everything—the words the demoiselle Van Eyck had whispered to him, how the gold coins were meant only for a journey across the sea. “It is not just my fate,” he said, “but ours. In Italy, I shall be seen for what I am—a craftsman, an artist.” And so, though her eyes filled with tears, she nodded. It was not for herself she consented, but for him—for the future he might build beyond this backward land.

They sat together through the long evening, hands clasped, speaking in hushed tones. At times they dreamed aloud of Venice, Florence, Rome—cities of marble and music, where men like Gerard would be celebrated. Then, without warning, Margaret would weep, and Gerard, young and tender-hearted as she, would fall silent, his voice breaking into sobs. They asked each other again and again: *What have we done? Why does every soul seem set against us?*

Night deepened, but neither could bring themselves to speak of departure. Time stretched on, soft and cruel. Perhaps they would never part—had it not been for Giles.

At about three in the afternoon, he arrived, riding hard from afar, his face grim, his breath ragged. He had taken a wide path to avoid detection. As soon as he saw them, he leapt from his horse and shouted, “Brother Gerard! Kate sends word—run for your life! They accuse you of theft. You’ve given them cause. Do not try to explain. Justice is not found in Tergou. Those parchments you took—they are merely a cover. But she has seen death in their eyes. A price is on your head. Fly! For Margaret’s sake, for all who love you—do not delay!”

The words struck like lightning. Gerard and Margaret stared at each other, pale and stunned. Then Giles, who until now had spoken only what Catherine had commanded, added quietly, “All the constables came to our house looking for you. So did Dirk Brower. Kate knows these men. Listen to her counsel, Gerard. Fly.”

“Oh, yes!” Margaret cried, clutching at his sleeve. “Go now—this very instant! Oh, those parchments—why did I let you take them? My mind warned me!”

“Hush,” Gerard said, though his own voice trembled. “They’re only a blind. Giles says so. Still—I will not leave before I hide what belongs to no one but the earth. That old villain shall never see them again.”

With a final thanks to Giles, he bade him farewell. “Tell Kate I’m gone,” he said. “I’ll be far away before you reach home.” Then he called for Martin.

Martin came quickly, bow in hand. Gerard told him of the danger, and begged him to go toward Tergou and watch the road. “If you see Dirk Brower or any of his men,” he said, “shoot an arrow into the oak tree in our garden. That will be the signal. Then I’ll run into the forest and meet you at the weird hunter’s spring. You’ll guide me through.”

Martin nodded. “Aye,” he said. “And if I hear footsteps, I’ll make sure you know.”

Relieved by this plan, Gerard turned to Margaret. While she stood trembling by the window, watching the oak tree, fearing every rustle of wind or leaf, he dug a deep hole behind the cottage. One by one, he placed the parchments inside—ancient charters, records of the burgh, deeds of land. Among them lay a private contract between Floris Brandt, father of Peter, and Ghysbrecht.

“This is yours as much as his,” Gerard murmured. “Let me read it.”

“No!” Margaret gasped. “Not now! Every second you waste fills me with dread. Look—raindrops already fall. The sky darkens.”

He obeyed, but slipped the deed into his chest, beneath his shirt. Then he covered the hole with dirt and stamped it down firmly.

As he worked, a flash lit the heavens, followed by a distant rumble of thunder. Moments later, rain fell in sheets.

They ran inside, followed by Martin. “The road is clear,” he reported. “And a storm is coming.”

True enough, the thunder grew louder, crashing overhead. Lightning split the sky like a whip, and the rain poured down in torrents. Margaret buried her face in her hands, unable to bear the sight. Gerard shut the rough shutter and lit a candle.

Together, they sat in silence, holding each other’s hands. The storm gave them a few more hours—precious, stolen moments. The sun set unnoticed. Thunder rolled on. Rain lashed the roof. Supper was brought, but neither touched it. The thought that this might be their last meal together choked their throats.

The storm eased slightly. Peter went to bed. But Gerard would leave at dawn. Neither dared sleep.

Martin stayed a while, carefully fitting a new string to his bow—a task he performed with great care. Beside him, the lovers whispered their griefs and their love.

Suddenly, Martin raised a hand. “Be still,” he hissed.

They listened. Nothing.

Then—faintly—a footstep cracked the dry leaves in the garden behind the house. Soft, deliberate. Trying to be silent.

To anyone unafraid, it meant nothing. To them, it was a blade at the throat.

Martin nocked an arrow. With a swift motion, he blew out the candle.

At once, another sound—more than one step—crept near the front door. Not quite silent, but cautious, creeping forward. Then—stillness.

Their blood turned cold.

“Oh, Kate! Oh, Kate!” Margaret sobbed. “You told him to flee instantly. Why did I hold him back? Why did I let him stay?”

“Hush, girl,” Martin whispered, fierce and low.

Then—a heavy knock.

On the door.

And on the hearts within.
