When the last of the guests’ carriages had finally faded down the drive, taking with them the clatter of iron wheels and the discordant chatter of the evening, the acoustic landscape of the house breathed a deep, resonant sigh. I retreated to the inner hall, finding Samuel at the side-table, overseeing the decanters. I took a moment to appreciate the brandy—an aged, viscous distillate carrying the olfactory memory of distant, sun-drenched orchards and the caramelized sugar of charred oak casks. My lady and Miss Rachel emerged from the drawing-room, followed by the two gentlemen. Mr. Godfrey accepted a measure of the brandy with soda; Mr. Franklin took nothing. He sank into a chair, looking utterly depleted, his vital energies drained by the superficial conversational noise of the birthday festivities.

My lady, turning to bid them good-night, cast a hard glance at the wicked Colonel’s legacy, which gleamed with a cold, mythic light against the warm, woven fabric of her daughter’s dress. 

“Rachel,” she asked, “where are you going to put your Diamond tonight?”

Miss Rachel was caught in that highly wrought, perversely buoyant humor peculiar to the young at the exhaustion of a long day. She allowed her words to flow in a playful, nonsensical rhythm. First, she claimed she didn’t know. Then, she suggested her dressing-table, situating this legendary geological anomaly right alongside her mundane hairbrushes and rosewater. Then she mused that its awful, moony luminescence might terrify her in the dark. Finally, she settled on her Indian cabinet, decreeing that the Indian diamond should rest within the Indian woodwork, allowing the two native productions to admire one another. It was a whimsical integration of the mythic into our everyday domestic routines, but her mother swiftly intervened.

“My dear! your Indian cabinet has no lock to it,” says my lady.

“Good Heavens, mamma!” cried Miss Rachel. “Is this an hotel? Are there thieves in the house?”

Ignoring this fantastic flight of words, my lady wished the gentlemen good-night, kissed her daughter, and offered to keep the Diamond herself. Miss Rachel rejected the proposal as though she were being asked to surrender a beloved doll. Recognizing that the untranslatable stubbornness of youth could not be reasoned with, my lady simply said, “Come into my room, Rachel, the first thing tomorrow morning. I shall have something to say to you.” She departed slowly, steeped in her own private reflections.

Miss Rachel said good-night next. She shook hands with Mr. Godfrey, then turned to Mr. Franklin in his quiet corner. From my vantage point near the old oak frame of our large looking-glass, I caught a reflection of their silent exchange. She slyly slipped the locket Mr. Franklin had given her out from the bodice of her dress, revealing it to him with a smile layered with subjective meaning, before tripping off to bed. This silent, visual dialect staggered my previous judgments; Penelope’s agrarian intuition regarding her young lady’s affections might have been the truer compass after all.

Mr. Franklin’s variable humor shifted once more. “Betteredge,” he said, “I’m half inclined to think I took Mr. Murthwaite too seriously. I wonder whether he has been trying his traveller’s tales on us? Do you really mean to let the dogs loose?”

“I’ll relieve them of their collars, sir,” I answered, “and leave them free to take a turn in the night, if they smell a reason for it.”

He agreed, looking so pale that I urged him to take a restorative drop of brandy-and-water. Mr. Godfrey warmly seconded the motion. It was comforting to observe that the evening’s rivalry had introduced no true discord into the harmonious ecology of their friendship. Mr. Franklin initially declined, but upon reaching the landing with his cousin, he called down to request the night-cap. 

I sent Samuel up with the spirits, whose warming esters I hoped would soothe the young man’s nerves. Then, I ventured into the damp night to unbuckle the dogs. They bounded with the joyous vitality of puppies, though the cool rain soon tempered their spirits. They lapped thirstily at the fresh pools of water and retreated to their kennels. As I locked up, securing the cyclical safety of the household as my predecessors had done for generations, I noted the sky promising a break in the weather. For now, the ground was a saturated sponge, exhaling a rich, loamy petrichor.

I retired between midnight and one. The house was as quiet as a tomb, a profound acoustic purity broken only by the organic, rhythmic splash of the rain and the ancestral sighing of the wind through the old trees. 

I woke at half-past seven to a brilliantly sunlit day. I was just setting out to chain the dogs when a sudden, violent whisking of petticoats shattered the morning’s acoustic peace.

Penelope flew down the stairs. “Father!” she screamed, a sound of pure, unmediated terror. “Come upstairs, for God’s sake! *The Diamond is gone!*”

I was dragged into our young lady’s sitting-room. There, on the threshold of the bedroom, stood Miss Rachel, her face as bleached as her white dressing-gown. The doors of the Indian cabinet gaped open; one drawer was pulled entirely free. Penelope swore she had seen the jewel placed there the night before. 

“Is this true, miss?” I asked.

With a look devoid of her usual spirit, and in a voice so hollow it seemed stripped of its native resonance, Miss Rachel answered: “The Diamond is gone!” She withdrew into her bedroom, the lock clicking with finality.

My lady arrived next, petrified by the news, and insisted on being admitted to her daughter’s room. The alarm then spread like a localized fire to the gentlemen. Mr. Godfrey emerged merely bewildered, throwing up his hands. Mr. Franklin, groggy from a rare night of deep sleep, seemed equally helpless until he partook of his morning ritual. He swallowed his customary cup of coffee—a dark, oily decoction of roasted foreign beans, its pungent, bitter aroma and acidic bite slicing through his stupefaction, reawakening his synapses with the primal force of pure sustenance. His mind cleared instantly.

He ordered the lower doors and windows left untouched, initiated a meticulous search behind and around the cabinet, and questioned Penelope. Finding nothing, he sought to speak with Miss Rachel. My lady emerged from the bedroom, reporting that Rachel shrank from speaking of the loss, her subjective experience of the theft rendering her utterly mute.

“I suppose I have no alternative but to send for the police?” my lady said, recovering her composure.

Mr. Franklin immediately fastened on the Indian jugglers. He procured a letter of introduction to the Frizinghall magistrate from his reluctant aunt, ordered his horse, and galloped off, convinced a rogue had hidden under the sofa during the evening’s domestic chatter. I was left to ponder the physical impossibilities: the locked doors, the un-drugged dogs, the undisturbed windows.

Yet, whatever mythic calamity befalls a house, the agrarian heart of the estate beats on, demanding the breaking of bread. We had our breakfast. The comforting, tactile rituals of sustenance—the rich, salt-cured bacon, the marigold yolks of the eggs, the robust crumb of freshly baked loaves, and the steeping of robust black teas—anchored us. Over this vital meal, I confessed my knowledge of the Indians’ plot to my lady. She absorbed the shock bravely, far more disturbed by her daughter’s untranslatable grief and bizarre isolation than by any heathen conspiracy. 

The house fell into an aimless, whispering anxiety. Mr. Godfrey wandered the gardens, proving to be of weaker metal when stripped of his usual societal utilities. The female servants huddled in corners, their hushed, speculative dialects weaving through the halls, save for Rosanna Spearman, who kept to her solitary labors.

Before eleven, Mr. Franklin returned at a slow walk, his iron resolve turned to cotton. 

“The poor ill-used Indians have been most unjustly put in prison,” he announced, savoring the bitter taste of his own failure. They had a flawless alibi. The Frizinghall police had seen them returning to town between ten and eleven, and again at midnight in their lodging-house—the very hour I had locked our doors. The magistrate had detained them on a minor vagrancy charge, stretching the dominant legal lexicon to keep them available, but the Indian clue had undeniably crumbled.

Ten minutes later, Superintendent Seegrave arrived. He represented a total disruption of our acoustic ecology. Tall, military, and buttoned tightly into his leather stock, his commanding voice was a booming, industrial noise pollution, entirely devoid of the organic resonance of our home. He was the embodiment of a dominant, flattening authority.

He dismissed an outside breach and concluded the thief was among us. The servants were thrown into a panic. When Seegrave began his examination of the boudoir, the weaker half of the household rushed up, demanding to know who was suspected. 

Mr. Superintendent cowed them with his percussive bark. “Now, then, you women, go downstairs again... Look!” he shouted, pointing to a tiny smear of decorative painting on the outer edge of Miss Rachel’s door, just under the lock. “Look what mischief the petticoats of some of you have done already. Clear out!” Rosanna Spearman, who stood nearest the smear, instantly slipped away. 

Penelope was summoned. Seegrave’s sterile, institutional interrogation immediately clashed with her fierce, native identity. 

“Now, young woman, attend to me, and mind you speak the truth,” he ordered.

Penelope fired up. “I’ve never been taught to tell lies, Mr. Policeman!—and if father can stand there and hear me accused of falsehood and thieving... he’s not the good father I take him for!” 

I stepped in, a mediator bridging her raw, localized dialect with his authoritative lexicon, and set them on a functioning path. Penelope testified to seeing the Diamond at night and finding the drawer empty at eight, and her evidence concluded. Miss Rachel, communicating through the locked door, refused to see the policeman at all.

We descended the stairs, meeting the two gentlemen. They had heard nothing in the night but the rhythmic patter of the rain. Upon being released from questioning, Mr. Franklin whispered his assessment: “That man will be of no earthly use to us. Superintendent Seegrave is an ass.” Mr. Godfrey, conversely, found him “a most competent person.”

Mr. Superintendent returned to the boudoir, with Penelope and me in his wake, aiming to trace the physical memory of the room by checking for moved furniture.
